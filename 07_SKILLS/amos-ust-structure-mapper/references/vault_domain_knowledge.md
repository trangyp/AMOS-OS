Making Planetary Consent Infrastructure Relatable

Messages and Anecdotes for General Public

Core Message Framework

The Simple Explanation

"Think of it like a universal 'Are you okay with this?' system for everything connected in our world—

from your smart home to global supply chains. Instead of companies and systems making decisions

about your data, your environment, and your future without asking, this creates a way for everyone and

everything to have a voice in decisions that affect them."

Relatable Anecdotes & Examples

1. The Smart Home That Actually Listens

The Situation: Sarah's smart thermostat, security cameras, and voice assistant all collect data about

her family.

The Problem: Currently, these devices share her data with dozens of companies without really asking

what she's comfortable with.

The Solution: "Imagine if every time your smart home wanted to share something about your family—

like when you're home, what temperature you prefer, or who visits—it could ask you in a simple way: 'Is

it okay if we use this to help optimize energy use in your neighborhood?' You say yes or no, and that

choice gets remembered and respected by every connected system."

Why It Matters: "You get the benefits of smart technology, but you stay in control of your privacy and

your family's information."

2. The Coffee That Knows Its Story

The Situation: Maria loves her morning coffee but wants to know it was grown and traded ethically.

The Problem: Supply chains are so complex that even "fair trade" labels don't tell the whole story.

The Solution: "Imagine if your coffee cup could tell you: 'The farmer who grew these beans consented

to share that they received fair wages, the transport company confirmed environmental standards

were met, and the roaster verified that everyone in the chain agreed to transparent practices.' Every

step of the journey, every person and community affected gets to participate in how their contribution

is used and acknowledged."


Why It Matters: "You know your purchases align with your values, and everyone in the supply chain

benefits from transparency and fairness."

3. The City That Asks Before It Decides

The Situation: City planners want to install new traffic sensors to reduce congestion.

The Problem: Usually these systems are installed without asking residents what they think or how

they want their movement data used.

The Solution: "Imagine getting a simple notification: 'Your city wants to install smart traffic systems on

your street to reduce commute times. Are you okay with anonymous traffic pattern data being used for

this? You can say yes, no, or 'yes but only for traffic optimization, not for other purposes.'' The system

only gets built if enough neighbors consent, and it only uses data in ways people approved."

Why It Matters: "Your community gets better infrastructure that actually reflects what residents want,

and your privacy choices are respected."
