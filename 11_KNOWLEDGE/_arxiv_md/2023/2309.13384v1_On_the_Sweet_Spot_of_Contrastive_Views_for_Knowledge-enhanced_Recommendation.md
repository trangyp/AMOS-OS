On the Sweet Spot of Contrastive Views for
Knowledge-enhanced Recommendation
Abstract
In recommender systems, knowledge graph (KG) can offer critical information
that is lacking in the original user-item interaction graph (IG). Recent process has
explored this direction and shows that contrastive learning is a promising way to
integrate both. However, we observe that existing KG-enhanced recommenders
struggle in balancing between the two contrastive views of IG and KG, making
them sometimes even less effective than simply applying contrastive learning
on IG without using KG. In this paper, we propose a new contrastive learning
framework for KG-enhanced recommendation. Specifically, to make full use
of the knowledge, we construct two separate contrastive views for KG and IG,
and maximize their mutual information; to ease the contrastive learning on the
two views, we further fuse KG information into IG in a one-direction manner.
Extensive experimental results on three real-world datasets demonstrate the effec-
tiveness and efficiency of our method, compared to the state-of-the-art. Our code
is available through the anonymous link: https://figshare.com/articles/
conference_contribution/SimKGCL/22783382.
1
Introduction
The success of recommender systems has made them popular in many real-world applications for
predicting personalized preferences [18]. Traditional recommendation methods that solely rely on
historical user-item interactions (e.g., collaborative filtering based ones [9, 17, 33, 15]) severely suffer
from data sparsity and cold-start problems, and thus knowledge graph (KG) has been leveraged to
encode additional item semantic relevance [2, 27, 21, 32, 29, 31, 35].
Among existing KG-enhanced methods, encoding KG with graph neural networks (GNNs) and
further connecting it to the user-item interaction graph (IG) with contrastive learning (CL) has been
the recent trend and shown prominent performance (see the related work section for more details).
However, we observe that existing recommenders that are built on knowledge graph and contrastive
learning still have room for improvement. For example, they are even less effective compared to the
methods solely applying CL on IG without using KG information. We name the existing work as
knowledge-enhanced contrastive learning (KCL) methods, and summarize them in Figure 1.
Specifically, existing KCL methods can be divided into two categories. Figure 1(a) shows the
first category where CL is conducted based on IG’s data augmentation and KG is used to guide
the augmentation. For example, KGCL [40] encodes item embeddings with KG to serve as the
initialization in IG, and uses knowledge to guide the structure augmentation of IG for constructing
CL views. However, according to the “InfoMin principle” [22], i.e., good CL views are those that
share the minimal information necessary for the downstream, it is desired to generate CL views from
both KG and IG to obtain shared representations of users and items. We name the issue of Figure 1(a)
as excessive overlap.
Figure 1(b) shows the second category, where existing KCL methods (e.g., [50, 25, 16]) perform cross-
view CL between KG and IG. However, they separately encode KG and IG, leading to insufficient

To address the issues of existing work, we propose a simple but effective KCL framework, named

Related Work
Contrastive Learning for Recommendation. Inspired by the progress of self-supervised learning in
NLP [6, 5] and CV [7, 3] domains, several recent studies [38, 15, 41, 42, 40, 50] have attempted to
introduce contrastive learning into recommender systems. Among them, SGL [38] generates different
contrastive views through random structure augmentation (e.g., edge drop); NCL [15] employs a
prototypical contrastive objective to capture the correlations between a user/item and its prototype;
SimGCL [42] directly adds random uniform noise to the representation for data augmentation.
Knowledge-enhanced Recommendation. Depending on how they encode the KG, knowledge-
enhanced recommender systems can be roughly divided into three categories: embedding-based
methods [2, 12, 26, 28, 30] which utilize traditional KG embedding techniques (e.g., TransE [1] and
TransH [37]), path-based methods [11, 19, 34, 43, 44, 46] which explore long-range connectivity
among items via pre-defined paths in the KG, and GNN-based methods [32, 35, 31, 29, 13, 36]
which leverage the message propagation mechanism of graph neural networks [39]. Among them,
embedding-based methods struggle to capture high-order dependencies, path-based methods heavily
depend on the quality of paths, and GNN-based methods have shown promising results by addressing
the limitations of the former two. However, all these methods follow the supervised learning
paradigm, relying on the observed user-item interactions which are usually extremely sparse in
real-world scenarios.
Contrastive Learning for Knowledge-enhanced Recommendation. More recently, researchers
have attempted to harness contrastive learning for improving the performance of knowledge-enhanced
recommendation [40, 50, 25, 51, 16]. For example, KGCL [40] leverages knowledge graph semantics
to guide the structure augmentation of CL views. MCCLK [50] and CKER [16] introduce a CL
module to learn user/item representations shared by both IG and KG. KACL [25] further adaptively
drops irrelevant edges in KG and IG to construct more reliable CL views. Nevertheless, they still
suffer from either the excessive overlap or insufficient overlap issues as discussed in introduction.

Problem Statement
In this section, we state the problem definition.
Interaction Graph. In a recommender system, we have user set U = {u1, u2, ..., uM} and item set
I = {i1, i2, ..., iN}, where M and N represent their sizes, respectively. The interaction graph is
denoted as GR = {(u, i, yu,i)}, where entry yu,i = 1 if there exists an observed interaction between
user u and item i, and yu,i = 0 otherwise.
Knowledge Graph. We denote the knowledge graph as GK = {(h, r, t) |h, t ∈E, r ∈R}, where
E and R represent the entity set and relation set, and each triple (h, r, t) describes a relationship
between entities. For example, the triplet (Titanic, Directed by, James Cameron) means that the
movie Titanic is directed by James Cameron. Note that E may contain both items and non-items
(e.g., Titanic and James Cameron, respectively, in a movie recommendation scenario). To connect
the two graphs, we establish a set of item-entity alignments A = {(i, e)|i ∈I, e ∈E}, where (i, e)
represents that item i can be aligned with entity e.
Task Formulation. Given the interaction graph GR and knowledge graph GK as input, our goal is to
learn a score function to forecast the items that user u would like to interact with.

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed SIMKGCL, we conduct extensive experiments
on three public datasets gathered from various real-life platforms: Yelp2018, Amazon-book, and
MIND. Table 2 presents the datasets’ statistics. Fowllowing [32, 40], we construct knowledge
graphs for Yelp2018 and Amazon-Book datasets by mapping items into Freebase entities [47]. In
our experiments, we only collect entities within two hops since few of methods consider modeling
multi-hop relations in KG. For the MIND dataset, we follow the strategy in [23] to construct the
knowledge graph based on spacy-entity-linker tool2 and Wikidata3. For the pre-processing of all
datasets, we are consistent with KGCL [40].
Compared Methods. We compare the proposed method with the following 16 methods.

• BPR [17] is a representative recommendation approach to rank item candidates with a
pairwise ranking loss.
• NCF [8] utilizes the multiple-layer perceptron to endow the collaborative filtering (CF)

• NGCF [33] is a graph-based CF method that largely follows the standard GCN, which

• LightGCN [10] devises a light-weight graph convolution without feature transformation

• CKE [45] adopts TransR to encode the items’ semantic information and further incorporates

• RippleNet [27] propagates user preference over KG along with the constructed paths rooted
at certain users. It is a memory-like neural model to improve user representations.
• KGCN [31] is a GNN-based method, which integrates multi-hop neighborhood information
in KG to enrich item embeddings.
• KGAT [32] designs an attentive message passing scheme over the collaborative knowledge
graph (CKG) for representation learning.
• CKAN [36] extends KGCN by considering the multi-hop neighborhood information in IG,
which utilizes different message propagation strategies on IG and KG, respectively.
• KGIN [35] is a recent GNN-based method to identify latent intention of users, and further
performs the relational path-aware aggregation for both user-intent-item and KG triplets.
• SGL [38] introduces CL task into graph-based CF to learn self-supervised signals, which
performs random structure augmentation on IG to generate different CL views.
• SimGCL [42] is a newly proposed graph-based CF method, which builds CL views by
adding random noises in the embedding space and has made a state-of-the-art performance.
• KGCL [40] is a recent KCL method, which attempts to explore KG semantics and conduct
a KG-aware structure augmentation to construct CL views.
• MCCLK [50] is another recent KCL method, which considers learning user/item represen-
tations from multiple views and applying cross view CL as an auxiliary task.
• CKER [16] introduces self-supervised learning to maximize the mutual information between
the interaction-side and knowledge-side user preferences.
• KACL [25] is a newly proposed KCL method that generates adaptive KG and IG views to
perform cross-view CL and alleviates the interaction domination and task-irrelevant noises.
Among the above baselines, the former four are basic recommenders (from BPR to LightGCN), the
next six are knowledge-enhanced recommenders (from CKE to KGIN), the next two are CL-based
recommenders (SGL and SimGCL), and the final four are KCL recommenders (from KGCL to
KACL).
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate the top-N recommendation performance by using two widely
used metrics Recall@N and NDCG@N, where N is set to 20 for consistency. Following [10, 38],
we adopt the full-ranking strategy [48], which ranks all the candidate items that the user has not
interacted with.
Implementations. For all the compared models, we either use the source code provided by their
authors (if it exists) or implement them ourselves with RecBole [49], which is a unified open-source
framework for developing and replicating recommendation algorithms. To ensure fairness, we fix
the embedding size and batch size to 64 and 2048, respectively. We optimize all the models by
using Adam optimizer. For each compared method, we refer to the best hyper-parameters in their
paper and then fine-tune them carefully so as to achieve the best results we can have. For the
proposed SIMKGCL, we fix λ2 to 1e −4 which is the same with the compared models. We tune the
hyper-parameter τ in {0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0}, and λ1 in {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0} on a validation
set.

5.2
Experimental Results
(A) Effectiveness Comparison. We first compare the performance of all the methods, and the
results are shown in Table 3. From the table, we have the following observations. First of all,
SIMKGCL consistently and significantly outperforms all baselines in all cases, demonstrating its
effectiveness. Specifically, compared with the best competitor, SIMKGCL achieves 6.5% - 13.2%
relative improvements. When comparing with existing KCL methods (the latter four baselines), the
improvements become 9.3% - 20.2%. Second, incorporating knowledge graph is helpful to improve
the performance. For example, most of the knowledge-enhanced methods outperform the basic
methods. Third, contrastive learning also helps. That is, the latter four KCL methods generally further
outperform the knowledge-enhanced methods. Additionally, CL-based methods alone also perform
relatively well. For example, SimGCL that does not use knowledge graph is the best competitor in 4
out of 6 cases (even better than KCL methods). This result indicates that an appropriate combination
of knowledge graph and contrastive learning (e.g., as posed in SIMKGCL) is necessary to achieve
better results.

6
Conclusions
In this paper, we have proposed a new contrastive learning framework SIMKGCL for knowledge-
enhanced recommendation. Different from the existing KCL methods, the key design of SIMKGCL
is to allow one-direction fusion from knowledge graph encoding to interaction graph encoding,
and then use both as contrastive views. Through such simple but effective design, we conduct
extensive experiments and demonstrate that SIMKGCL achieves 6.5% - 13.2% relative improvements
compared the best competitor among 16 baselines, and meanwhile requires much shorter training
time compared with the state-of-the-art KCL method KGCL.
