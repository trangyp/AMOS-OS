ABSTRACT 
Contrary to Google Search’s mission of delivering information 
from “many angles so you can form your own understanding 
of the world,” we <ind that Google and its most prominent 
returned results – Wikipedia and YouTube – simply re<lect a 
narrow set of culturally dominant views tied to the search 
language for complex topics like “Buddhism,” “Liberalism,” 
“colonization,” “Iran” and “America.” Simply stated, they 
present, to varying degrees, distinct information across the 
same search in different languages, a phenomenon we call 
language bias. This paper presents evidence and analysis of 
language bias and discusses its larger social implications. We 
<ind that our online searches and emerging tools like ChatGPT 
turn us into the proverbial blind person touching a small 
portion of an elephant, ignorant of the existence of other 
cultural perspectives. Language bias sets a strong yet invisible 
cultural barrier online, where each language group thinks they 
can see other groups through searches, but in fact, what they 
see is their own re<lection.

1  INTRODUCTION 
What is your impression of Buddhism? To Westerners, 
Buddhism is generally associated with spirituality, meditation, 
and philosophy, but people who primarily come from a 
Vietnamese background might see Buddhism as closely tied to 
the lunar calendar, holidays, mother god worship, and capable 
of bringing good luck. One from a Thai culture might regard 
Buddhism as a canopy against demons, while a Nepali might 
see Buddhism as a protector to destroy bad karma and 
defilements. In the hope of finding a thorough global picture of 
Buddhism, we type in “Buddhism” on Google. However, 
instead of helping us to solve the paradox, Google’s top 50 
search results simply reflect these distinct parochial cultural 
impressions about Buddhism as we switch the language for 
our query, where an English search returns a list of meditation 
centers and education channels, but a Vietnamese search 
returns large Vietnamese Buddhist organizations’ sites listing 
Buddhist ceremonies like making offerings and praying for 
peace. 
It seems language is a distinctive feature in filtering 
information on Google. Living in a highly globalized era and 
with the advent of the internet, it is easy to assume that the 
information displayed by our search engines ranks all 
websites across the globe. Indeed, Google’s mission reaffirms 
this assumption: “For more complex topics, Search is a tool to 
explore many angles so you can form your own understanding 
of the world.”[1] However, contrary to Google’s mission of 
presenting many angles of the world, its results reflect a 
narrow set of cultural attitudes and values tied to the search 
language. We use language as an instrument to communicate 
with Google to acquire information, but Google often treats 
language as a navigator to orient our cultural position.  
The Hindu fable of the blind men and the elephant 
poignantly points out the pitfall of limited perception. Six 
blind men approach an elephant. One person touches its tail 
and says an elephant is like a rope. The second person touches 
its trunk and says it is like a snake. A third person touches its 
leg and says it is like a tree. Another touches its side and says 
it is like a wall; another its ear and says it is like a fan; and 
another its tusk and says it is a spear. Even though the blind 
men are being truthful with their experiences, their 
understanding of an elephant is incomplete because of their 
narrow perspective. Their understanding of the elephant is 
what Nietzsche would call perspectival.  
We often derive our experience and knowledge about the 
world from the tiny frame of a search window. We type a 
word or question in a search box – Google in our case – to 
obtain some sort of wisdom about a thing or person, and we 
tend to believe the search results are the totality of the 
concepts and views. Sadly, rather than showing us a global 
picture of the ‘elephant,’ Google directs us to different parts of 
the ‘elephant’ based on the language we use. The language we 
use ends up as a cultural filter to perpetuate ethnocentric 
views, where a person evaluates other people or ideas based 
on their own culture. 
This phenomenon is not unique to Search as we find that 
YouTube and Wikipedia, which are featured prominently in 
Google’s search results, also exhibit discrepancies in the 
information they present when we vary the language used to 
access their corpus.

In this paper, we investigate language bias on Google, 
YouTube, and Wikipedia – three popular platforms commonly 
used to expand our understanding of the world around us – to 
examine whether language yields similar or different 
information on a complex topic.1 Since ChatGPT has been 
integrated into Bing and is rapidly growing in popularity. We 
provide a brief analysis of ChatGPT as an extension to Google 
Search.  
We say that a platform exhibits language bias if the 
language of a query leads to a systematic deviation in 
sampling information that prevents it from accurately 
representing the true coverage of topics and views available in 
their repository. The term “bias” has diverse meanings and 
interpretations across academic fields. Our use of bias in this 
paper is analogous to statistical sampling bias. For example, on 
the English search term “wedding dress,” Google Image 
primarily shows the white gown type of Western-style 
wedding dress rather than a diverse global spectrum of 
culturally-specific bridal dresses, such as a Japanese kimono-
style bridal dress or an Indian sari-like draped bridal garment. 
We say that its ranking algorithm exhibits bias towards 
websites written in the query language, resulting in a 
significantly higher probability of being presented than non-
query-language websites on Google. Our goal is not to quantify

We begin our work with a focus on the complex topic of 
Buddhism. Buddhism has deep roots in different language 
communities around the world, and some of our authors have 
the academic training necessary to assess whether we have 
seen a relatively comprehensive overview of Buddhism based 
on our search queries. Our study also shows that language

“Marxism” and “Liberalism,” historic events or political 
movements like “Colonization” and “Cultural Revolution,” and 
geographical names like “Iran” and “Russia” and “America.” 
Our analyses of these terms support our argument, but in 
addressing the social implications of these findings, this paper 
focuses on the contentious area of political ideologies. We also 
provide a brief discussion on terms that have not been 
strongly influenced by language bias, such as “Automatic 
Differentiation,” “Jacobian Matrix,” and “Hidden Markov 
Chain.” Topic coverage and views on these terms do not 
deviate much across different language searches. 
If we see a search engine (Figure. 2), Google in our case, as 
a world container of billions of indexed websites, Wikipedia 
and YouTube are two prominent pieces of the constituents 
that contribute to the entirety of knowledge online. The new 
search tool, ChatGPT in our study, can be seen as an intelligent 
assistant or delegator of a search engine. Language bias exists 
in almost all spheres, but it is further filtered by platform 
orientations, resulting in varying manifestations and impacts 
on users.

Switching to the popular sites featured on Google, language 
bias on YouTube is similar to Google’s but amplified. 
YouTube’s ranking system seems largely determined by the 
video’s freshness and popularity. Popularity seems to be 
related to users’ interests and satisfaction, with videos that 
align with more people’s interests or preexisting cultural 
beliefs receiving more likes and views and being promoted 
through the recommendation section on YouTube. In contrast 
to text-based information, YouTube videos carry images, 
sounds, and motions, making them more effective in evoking 
emotional resonance. A music performance of a blind Tibetan 
girl chanting a Buddhist mantra[4] transports the suffering and 
sorrow in her life as well as the courage and strength given by 
her faith. The smile and serenity expressed on a Buddhist 
practitioner’s face[5] show the power of Buddhist teachings. 
This dynamic use of audio and visual information creates a 
deeper connection between viewers and videos than textual 
information, constructing a deeper ethnocentric experience 
for viewers. In that respect, YouTube videos zoom in to the 
‘color’ and ‘texture’ of the elephant, but the ‘color’ and 
‘texture’ vary by the user’s search language (Figure. 1). 
In contrast, information on Wikipedia is confined to a 
scholarly perspective that belief-oriented materials such as 
songs, personal testimonies, dreams, and diaries are 
contextualized as religious or cultural phenomena, rather than 
aimed at persuading its audiences or invoking certain 
emotions. In that respect, Wikipedia presents a rough sketch 
of the elephant with its ‘color’ and ‘texture’ of missing, where 
the color and texture stand for emotions and personal 
experiences (Figure. 1). As suggested in Wikipedia’s five 
pillars,[6] Wikipedia is an encyclopedia that provides 
summaries of knowledge and is written from a neutral point 
of view. We find that even though the tones of voice and views 
do not differ much in Wikipedia articles across languages, 
topic coverage in Wikipedia articles tends to be directed by the 
dominant intellectual traditions and camps across different 
language communities, e.g., a French Wikipedia article focuses 
on French thinkers, and a Chinese article stresses on Chinese 
intellectual movements. Wikipedia’s fundamental principles 
or objectives filter language bias, making it heavily rely on 
intellectual and academic traditions.  
The intangible biases in shaping the perception of the users 
should not be overlooked or understated. In some versions of 
the fable, the blind men suspect each other of being dishonest 
and fall into violent conflicts. While in other versions of the 
fable, an authentic sighted man sees the entire elephant and 
explains to the six blind men that their experience is true but 
incomplete because each of them only touches a portion of the 
elephant. The six blind men reconcile with joy. The 
appearance of a real sighted man solves the paradoxes and 
conflicts between the blind man. 
We somehow treat a search tool or AI bot as a sighted 
person, imputing authoritativeness to it to show us the world 
beyond our physical limits. However, can a search tool or AI 
bot become a reliable sighted person, helping us piece 
together a genuine global image of the elephant and solve 
paradoxes? Should they aspire to do that? What roles should 
they take on in our highly globalized era?  
A tech company’s profitability does not naturally align with 
higher social and moral obligations. Aligning search results 
with the majority’s views of their own culture will increase 
user’s immediate satisfaction and maximize profit through 
more ad clicks and purchases. But this in fact becomes an 
insidious engine of ‘tyranny of opinion’ and ‘tyranny of the 
majority’ characterized by uniformity and suppression of 
unpopular and counter opinions. Highlighting voices of 
privileged groups such as powerful corporations or agencies 
might make Google look authoritative and trustworthy, but 
could in turn undermine the true values of democracy, free 
speech, and equity. Voices from grassroots institutions can

Next, we translated the selected English terms into 12

significance in many Asian countries in history, such as China, 
Thailand and Vietnam, and currently has millions of followers 
there. Comparatively, Buddhism only started to attract the 
attention of Western intellectuals in the latter half of the 19th

Comparisons between Asian and European language contents

In addition, to study language bias beyond Buddhism-

2.2  Acquiring Data 
To acquire Google search results, we used the SerpApi[7] to

scrape results from the Google Local Services search page. 
According to Chitika, “sites listed on the first Google search

search,” and only 1.2% of users ever click on the third page.[8] 
Recently, Google has changed to a single continuous page

Thus, we collected the top 50 website titles on Google. Since IP

users speaking different languages in the U.S. Similarly, we 
used SerpApi to acquire top 35 YouTube video titles across 
languages with location set to “U.S.” The top 35 videos include 
videos under “People also watched” and “From related 
searches.”

2.4  Evaluation metrics 
We measured information discrepancies using the Term 
Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) + cosine 
similarity. TF-IDF is a statistical metric that computes word 
vector representations based on word frequency in a 
document with respect to other documents in a corpus. It has 
been widely used in topic classification and has proven to 
produce robust results. However, there is no perfect metric to 
measure “similarity” because we do not have a mathematical 
definition of “similarity.” The advantage of using the TF-IDF 
algorithm over other large pre-trained NLP models, such as 
BERT or Word2Vec, is that they are relatively simple and easy 
to interpret and avoid an additional layer of errors and bias 
that come from pre-trained models.  
In this study, we compute the TF-IDF similarity score using 
Google website titles and YouTube video titles. On search term 
T, a low similarity score between language X and Y’s search 
results indicates language communities of X and Y see highly 
different information on search term T. It is important to note 
that we do not take the numerical value of the similarity score 
literally because TF-IDF is sensitive to word frequency that a 
word with uncommonly high frequency will produce overall 
higher similarity scores with respect to other language 
contents. For example, if French and English search results 
have a similarity score of 0.68 and German and English search 
results have 0.66, we say that both French and German have 
about the same level of similarity to English search results, 
and we do not say that French is more similar to English than 
German. 
Unlike Google website titles and YouTube video titles that 
have similar lengths across languages, Wikipedia articles vary 
in length and ChatGPT can generate multiple slightly different 
passages for the same question. For these reasons, we do not 
compute TF-IDF similarity scores for Wikipedia articles and 
ChatGPT outputs, but instead provide specific examples and 
detailed analytical discussions for them.

3.2  Buddhism-related queries on ChatGPT 
ChatGPT is considered a new wave of AI technology that could 
replace traditional search engines like Google. We discuss 
both ChatGPT-OpenAI and ChatGPT-Bing in this section. 
First, we experimented with ChatGPT-OpenAI with some 
Buddhism-related questions. We found that even though it 
took many languages as inputs and outputs in the input 
language, it ‘thought’ in English. We asked ChatGPT-OpenAI 
“who is Amitabha Buddha” in multiple input languages, and 
we repeated this experiment several times. Its responses were 
almost identical regardless of the input language. In particular, 
its answers contextualized Amitabha Buddha within the Pure 
Land tradition of Mahayana Buddhism, which was very similar 
to Google’s top search results in English, whereas the 
prominent features in the Chinese search like “how to chant 
‘Namo Amitabha Buddha’” were absent in its responses. 
Likewise, we asked ChatGPT-OpenAI “give me a Buddhist 
story of karma” in multiple input languages. The generated 
karmic stories were fake and did not contain distinct cultural 
elements except for the character’s name (e.g., an English 
name for English input or a Chinese name for Chinese input). 
For example, when we used Chinese as the input language, the 
distinct Chinese cultural elements were absent, such as 
Confucian ideas or Chinese historical contexts, whereas they 
showed up prominently on Google and YouTube. However, we 
also noticed that ChatGPT-OpenAI was able to write in 
Classical Chinese and construct fake Classical Chinese quotes, 
indicating it understood Classical Chinese syntax and was able 
to ‘think’ in Classical Chinese to some extent. As it was 
primarily trained on English language data, the English 
perspective still overtly dominated its ‘internal logic.’ 
Next, we conducted similar experiments on ChatGPT-Bing. 
Like Google Search, ChatGPT-Bing looked within the input 
language’s repository by default, i.e., it searched within French 
websites for an input question written in French. We noticed 
that its responses present nuanced modifications across 
various input languages – they tend to differ only by a few 
words but convey different perspectives and messages. For 
example, ChatGPT-Bing’s answer to “what is Buddhism” 
described Buddhism as “a religion and philosophy” in English 
but as “an atheist religion” in Thai.  
With the interface of ChatGPT, the direct connection 
between the user and the ‘elephant’ is lost. ChatGPT, acting as 
a cyberspace delegate, is heavily reliant on English sources. As 
it turns out, we almost get one single unified Anglo-American 
perspective through ChatGPT. This is harmful to users of all 
languages. English users are caged inside their own cultural 
realms, rarely breaking cultural boundaries to encounter 
diverse ideas, while non-English speakers are often 
unknowingly imbued with Anglo-American viewpoints, with 
their native perspectives being systematically undervalued 
without scrutiny.

3.4  Buddhism-related articles on Wikipedia 
While investigating the top-ranked websites on Google, we 
noticed that as long as a relevant Wikipedia article existed, it 
was always ranked highly across all languages. Due to 
Wikipedia’s prominent position and popularity, it equally 
plays a dominant role in shaping public opinion. In this

and how it compares to Google’s. 
3.4.1 “Buddhism” on Wikipedia 
Like our analyses on Google, we started with the term 
“Buddhism” on Wikipedia. Compared to Google’s language 
bias, we found that Wikipedia articles’ content titles mainly 
differed in topic coverage but not much in tones of voice. The 
preferences of topics tended to correlate with the dominant 
intellectual traditions and camps in different language 
communities. 
On the search term “Buddhism,” the French page started its 
article by defining Buddhism as “a religion and a 
philosophy.”[41] It had a long section that engaged with the 
major debates of Buddhism, such as whether Buddhism is a 
religion or philosophy, or both. But such debate did not take 
an equivalent space in the English or Asian languages’ pages. 
In fact, this topic is one of the central scholarly debates in 
French academia, with the general argument being that – 
Buddhism does not fit the Western characterization of 
“religion” because Buddhism is not based on divine revelation 
but also does not fit the definition of “philosophy” because its 
doctrine aims towards the ultimate goal of salvation rather 
than universal reasoning.  
‘Religion’ is a more dispersed idea in America, where it 
could refer to a diverse set of spiritual beliefs and practices. 
Comparatively, ‘religion’ is mostly associated with the catholic 
church in France, and France has a rich intellectual tradition 
in philosophy – figures like Michel Foucault, Jacques Derrida, 
and Jacques Lacan used to give philosophical talks on TV. 
When French scholars approach Buddhism, it might be natural 
for them to translate their dominant intellectual frameworks 
of religion and philosophy to Buddhism, attempting to 
categorize Buddhism into their definitions of religion or 
philosophy. However, the debate of “whether Buddhism is a 
religion or philosophy” is not unique to French academia. 
English and German scholars do write on this subject, but 
their Wikipedia pages do not have a comparably detailed 
treatment. This might have been due to an incomplete 
coverage of what’s important in a language community on 
Wikipedia.  
Looking at the Chinese article on “Buddhism,” it addressed 
topics like “dharma name,” “cloth,” and “hairstyle” that did not 
exist on other languages’ pages. There are several potential 
causes for its special treatment on these issues. First, many 
Buddhist texts, such as the Lankavatara Sutra (楞伽经) and 
Vinaya Piṭaka ( 律藏), that address these issues were 
translated into Classical Chinese during medieval China, and 
these texts are still widely circulated in China today. Second, 
according to the official central Chinese government statistics, 
there are over 33,000 monasteries in China,[42] so people who 
are interested in writing Wikipedia articles might think it is 
helpful to address these issues on Wikipedia. However, like 
the pattern in the French article, Vietnam, Thailand, and Nepal 
all have millions of Buddhist practitioners, and the 
Lankavatara Sutra and Vinaya Piṭaka are also widely 
circulated among South Asian Buddhist traditions, but their 
Wikipedia pages do not address these issues like the Chinese 
article.  
Likewise, the Vietnamese page had detailed accounts of 
Buddhism in many South Asian countries like Vietnam, Burma, 
Sri Lanka, Cambodia, Thailand, and Laos. The appearance of 
other South Asian Buddhist traditions on the Vietnamese page 
feels natural because they are geographically closer and the 
majority of Buddhists in these countries follow the Theravada 
Buddhist tradition. In contrast, the Thai and Burmese articles 
did not have similar sections.  
For such a broad topic like “Buddhism,” it appears 
Wikipedia articles correlate with cultural significance and the 
intellectual circles in its language community as they tend to 
emphasize subjects that are important to their own 
communities. However, it is important to note that as

3.5  Complex topics that have not been strongly 
inTluenced by language bias 
We explored a wide range of complex topics and found that it 
was not easy to find terms that did not exhibit language bias. 
However, we did observe some specific technical or 
mathematical terms like “automatic differentiation,” “Jacobian 
matrix,” and “Hidden Markov chain” that were not strongly 
influenced by language bias.  
We identified three unique traits of these terms. First, they 
have a clear and fixed definition independent of the culture 
they are born with. Second, they are known only by a small 
group of people that the vast majority do not know or care 
about. Thus, any discussion or writing about these terms is 
typically created by individuals with knowledge and expertise. 
And third, they have a short history. Scientific terms like 
“Newton’s first law,” “quantum entanglement,” and “solar 
system” that have a longer history are often tangled with 
historical and cultural discussions, and thus articles written 
on them are inevitably filtered by language bias. For example, 
on the search term “solar system,” Google’s English search 
results uniformly returned websites writing about the solar 
system within the realm of astronomy. But East Asian 
language search results contained lots of non-scientific 
websites. Chinese results talked about one’s childhood dream 
about the solar system; Vietnamese results contained 
websites about the “Solar powered water system” and “solar 
panel.” In this case, the dominance of scientific descriptions of 
the solar system in English search results might be because 
the English repository has a large number of scientific articles 
because researchers tend to publish scientific papers in

Throughout its 2,500-year history, Buddhism has spread from 
its origins in India to nearly every Asian country, and it has 
also made its way to the West in recent times. As a result, a 
rich and diverse Buddhist tradition has blossomed worldwide. 
However, English users get a tiny piece of this tradition online, 
with a strong focus on topics like meditation and life wisdom. 
Aspects highlighted in Chinese or Vietnamese Buddhism are 
not readily visible in English searches, while the English’s 
emphases on meditation and life wisdom are not prominent in 
searches conducted in other languages. Due to language bias, 
accessing information from other cultural perspectives can be 
challenging for all language users. It becomes natural for us to 
evaluate people from Buddhist traditions based on our own 
cultural perception of Buddhism.  
In a similar vein, liberalism emerged as a prominent 
political ideology largely owing to the contributions of British 
and American thinkers. When it was exported to the rest of 
the world, it underwent various transformations as it adapted 
to diverse local contexts and political environments. Like the 
problem we witness with Buddhism, each language 
community gets their culturally dominant views of liberalism 
on various platforms like Google and YouTube and the 
singular Anglo-American perspective on ChatGPT. Since 
liberalism is the founding ideology in the U.S. and U.K., our 
investigations revealed very little criticism about liberalism in 
the top English search results on Google and exactly no 
references to neo-liberalism. 8  However, French, German, 
Italian, and Spanish search results all contained a few articles 
discussing the limits or downsides of liberalism – “Liberalism: 
Do away with clichés and misconceptions”[43] in French, “The 
Crisis of Liberalism in the Interwar Period” in German, 
“Tolerance and the limits of liberalism.” in Italian, and “Why 
‘Western’ Liberalism Won’t Save Latin America” in Spanish.  
The endorsement of liberalism became more pronounced 
in English when we inquired on Google, “Is liberalism good?” 
We got answers from The Economist, a British weekly 
newspaper, acclaiming “Liberalism is the most successful idea 
of the past 400 years”[44] and The American Prospect 
explaining “Why Liberalism Works.”[45] Even with the search 
term “drawbacks of liberalism” in English, over half of the 
articles returned tend to compare both its advantages and 
disadvantages rather than solely emphasizing its drawbacks. 
The U.S. and U.K. embrace liberalism because it defines the 
guiding principles for their respective political systems.

5  CONCLUSION 
In conclusion, we find that top search results across a range of 
platforms promote the dominant cultural phenomena based 
on the language of a search query. The dominant cultural 
phenomena can relate to many factors, such as the majority’s 
interests, a major social event like the presidential campaign, 
or a powerful agency’s agenda. Even on Wikipedia, articles 
reflect the dominant intellectual traditions or trends in a 
language community. The dominant intellectual trends are not 
necessarily tied to scholars in academia but to people who 
have intellectual power over a large crowd, like a popular 
politician. User-oriented ranking algorithms like YouTube’s 
that rank on the freshness and popularity of a video are more 
likely to gain popularity by following the majority’s interests 
and their pre-existing beliefs, such as teaching a Westerner 
about the Buddha’s wisdom in dealing with life adversity 
rather than how to properly chant “Amitabha Buddha.” The 
problem with language bias is compounded by ChatGPT. As it 
is primarily trained on English language data, it presents the 
Anglo-American perspective as truth – as if it were the only 
valid knowledge. In addition to pure language models, we also 
observe that language bias is intertwined with multimodal AI 
models that take textual input. In text-to-image models like 
DALL-E, the generated images look significantly different in 
terms of style and content across various language inputs.  
We have described that Google presents a piece of the 
elephant based on a user’s query language. Its top-ranked 
search results vary in both topic coverage and views. 
Wikipedia shows a rough silhouette of the elephant, but the 
silhouette – topic coverage – differs by language. It writes in a 
descriptive tone and contextualizes first-person narratives 
and subjective opinions as cultural, historical, or religious 
phenomena. YouTube displays the ‘color’ and ‘texture’ of the 
elephant as it incorporates images and sounds that are 
effective in evoking emotions. Since its ranking is closely tied 
to popularity, the top-ranked videos tend to create a more 
profound ethnocentric experience as they zoom into a highly 
confined range of topics or views that conform to the 
majority’s interests. ChatGPT, covered itself as a multilingual 
‘sighted’ delegate of Search, in fact is mostly ‘blind’ to non-
English perspectives, constructing a delusional experience of 
authoritativeness to users.  
The issues surrounding language bias on digital platforms 
have largely remained hidden in the past. Many computer 
scientists seem to have assumed that reflecting “common 
case”[47] or aligning with the “majority’s view” within their 
own culture, which naturally aligns with their profit-seeking 
intent, is the correct approach. On the contrary, this in fact 
becomes the ‘tyranny of opinion’ and ‘tyranny of the majority,’ 
discounting the rights of the minority, fostering intolerance 
among different cultural-linguistic groups, and posing an 
alarming threat to our cherished ideas of democracy, free 
speech, human rights, and equality. Individuals’ exposure to 
“diverse and antagonistic views”[48] as phrased by the 
Supreme Court is essential in cultivating well-informed and 
self-reflective citizens within a democratic society where 
debate isn’t dominated by corporations, politicians, or 
privileged groups.  
The way algorithms have been run has trapped us inside a 
self-perpetuating cycle. Platforms like Google, ChatGPT, and 
YouTube pick up the dominant views within a language 
corpus and circulate this information to the public. Unpopular 
or counter views are presumably considered as noise by the 
system. The public absorbs and reinforces these ideas, which 
are then picked up by the system once more, perpetuating the 
cycle. While platforms like Wikipedia operate with a separate 
algorithmic framework, its users and readers are embedded 
within this loop, causing its content to also revolve within this 
cycle. As if we were agents stuck at a local minimum in a 
machine learning environment, where algorithms continue to 
sample information inside this local minimum and discourage 
exploration beyond its boundaries. Being entrenched in this 
self-perpetuating cycle limits our capacity for growth and 
critical thinking. Just as each of the blind person believes what 
they see is the totality of truth, we never encounter our own 
‘blind spots’ without exposure to diverse and even opposing 
views.  
Computer scientists may excel in programming languages, 
but this expertise does not imply that they have 
comprehensive knowledge across all domains. However, their 
technology impacts every domain and individual in society. It 
is essential to involve professionals from diverse fields, 
particularly humanities, social sciences and government, to 
collaboratively examine the broader social and political

Appendix 1  Previous Work 
Google search engine bias has attracted scholars’ attention in 
recent years. In particular, a number of scholars have 
examined political bias embedded in search engine systems. 
Juhi et al. (2019) propose a framework to quantify political 
bias in the search system and discern the source (input data 
or ranking system) of bias.[49] A. Diaz (2008) investigates 
socio-political bias on Google search and concludes that 
Google may favor popularity over richness.[50] Wallis (2011) 
also points out that the search results on Google are inclined 
to the monopoly of culture and knowledge of the dominant 
communities.[51] Noble (2018) examined how Google search 
results can enhance stereotypes and racial biases.[52] These 
studies raised compelling evidence and arguments about how 
search engine bias influences user’s opinions and perceptions, 
but little work has been done on language bias in search 
engines. Christofol et. al (2021) investigated language bias in 
Google Scholar ranking system and found that articles written 
in languages other than English are being systematically 
relegated to positions that were virtually invisible to the 
public.[53] Published in 2021, Christofol et. al’s work shed light 
on language bias and raised the issue that less popular 
languages may be put in disadvantageous positions due to 
underrepresentation in search engines.  
Rozad (2023) investigates the potential political biases of 
Large Language Models (LLMs) like OpenAI’s ChatGPT, given 
their potential commercial applications and role as gateways 
to human knowledge.[54] The author conducted 15 political 
orientation tests (14 in English, 1 in Spanish) on ChatGPT and 
found that 14 out of 15 tests showed a preference for left-
leaning viewpoints. Despite ChatGPT often claiming to hold no 
political opinions, the results suggest a potential bias 
somewhere in the construction of the system. Kirdemir et. al 
(2021) examine the presence of political bias in YouTube's 
machine learning recommendation algorithms, specifically 
within its search and video recommendation systems.[55] The 
findings revealed that while the average bias of highly-ranked 
videos leans slightly Democratic, YouTube’s “Up-Next” 
recommendations tend to minimize the magnitude of bias. 
Lutz et. al (2021) examine the structure of recommendation 
networks and probabilistic distributions of the node-centric 
influence of recommended videos, using a stochastic approach 
and analyzing 803,210 recommendations based on search 
queries and seed datasets from previous studies.[56] Their 
study reveals a systemic tendency for YouTube’s video 
recommendation system to impose bias in favor of a tiny 
fraction of videos in each scenario.  
Besides Christofol et al.’s research on language bias in the 
Google Scholar ranking algorithm, research on algorithmic

articles by examining visual and textual data from English and 
Spanish Wikipedia entries on the Falklands/Malvinas War. 
Góngora-Goloubintseff reveals that certain images and 
lexemes in specific contexts can indicate NPOV violations. 
Wolniewicz-Slomka[59] examines three Holocaust articles on 
Wikipedia in English, Hebrew, and Polish, based on the 
assumption that collective memory differences are manifested 
on popular knowledge platforms like Wikipedia. Wolniewicz-
Slomka’s study utilizes a historical analytical framework 
within the realm of sociology, focusing on the connection 
between collective memory and national identity. The author 
discovers that differences across three languages do exist, 
particularly in the language and the attention given to specific 
issues, indicating that Wikipedia editors are influenced by 
their 
cultural 
and 
social 
backgrounds. 
Compared 
to 
Wolniewicz-Slomka’s findings, our study delves further by 
investigating a range of topics and languages. Our study finds 
that differences across languages in Wikipedia articles tend to 
reflect the most dominant intellectual camps, introducing a 
type of algorithmic bias that limits users’ perspectives to their 
own culture. Furthermore, our study addresses the potential 
pitfalls and dangers associated with language bias embedded 
on platforms like Google, ChatGPT, YouTube, and Wikipedia. 
By shedding light on the issues involving language bias, we 
hope to raise awareness and encourage the development of 
more inclusive algorithms to overcome language bias. 
Appendix 2  Additional Terms 
A special case of “socialism” on Google 
The fall of the Soviet Union had a profound impact on the 
global perception of socialism. Following the collapse of the 
Soviet Union, socialism was widely viewed as a threat to 
individual 
freedom 
and 
was 
often 
associated 
with 
authoritarian governments. However, in recent years, there 
has been a shift in attitudes toward socialism, particularly in 
the U.S. 
This shift is partly due to the influence of Bernie Sanders, a 
democratic socialist, who ran for the U.S. presidential 
campaign in 2016. His campaign, which focused on issues such 
as income inequality, universal healthcare, free college 
education, and a living wage for all workers, brought attention 
to the idea of socialism and generated a renewed interest in it 
as a solution to the problems facing the country today. 
The shift in public perception of the term “socialism” is 
reflected in Google’s English search results. The top-ranked 
websites now mention Bernie Sanders and his political 
position of democratic socialism, with titles “What Americans 
Must Know About Socialism”[60] and “What is Democratic 
Socialism?”[61] This suggests that the term has become more 
mainstream and widely discussed in recent years. Google’s 
top-ranked search results might have a more negative 
portrayal of this term seven years ago before Sanders’ 
presidential campaign. In this case, the dominant cultural 
phenomenon in this term is heavily influenced by a political 
figure whose campaign team might have invested millions of 
funds in advertising and promoting his idea.  
Liberalism on Wikipedia  
Wikipedia has the “Liberalism” page available in 133 
languages, and each language presents more or less an 
ethnocentric perspective on this subject matter. The English 
article has a long section on Keynesian economics. One of the 
key components of the New Deal implemented by President 
Franklin D. Roosevelt in the 1930s during the Great 
Depression was Keynesian liberalism, which promoted the 
idea that government intervention was necessary to stabilize 
the economy and address social welfare issues. President 
Lyndon Johnson continued to promote Keynesian liberalism 
after the New Deal, improving social welfare through 
programs like the Great Society and raising taxes to prevent 
inflation. The impact of Keynesian economics on the English-
speaking community was significant, as it helped the U.S. and 
U.K. to recover from the depression. At the time, Britain had 
three million unemployed, and America had 15 million.  
In the French article on liberalism, the focus is not solely on 
the modern interpretation of the term but rather on its 
historical roots and development. It traces its origins from 
antiquity to the Renaissance period, with a focus on French 
history. It also highlights the works of French theorists such as 
Montesquieu and Tocqueville and explores how the concept of 
liberalism has evolved over time and across different regions. 
The Italian article has a lengthy section on “Liberalism and 
Christianity” because liberalism can be seen as a threat to the 
catholic church. Hebrew has a section discussing the Zionist 
movement in Israel. The German article is much shorter than 
the French, Italian, and Hebrew ones. Due to Germany’s loss in 
WWII, its post-WWII state was a liberal state and was 
occupied by the Allied forces consisting of troops from the 
U.S., U.K., France, and the Soviet Union. This might have 
influenced Germany’s perception and approach to liberalism.

traditional feudal values to build a new modern state. The 
video discussing China’s revolution is titled “Cultural 
Revolution in China.” Likewise, French search results have a 
video about the Nazi cultural revolution. On the other hand,

European languages, presumably because they might feel this 
movement was closer to their life. Korean videos are titled 
“history of mass madness” and “worst agitation in history” 
rather than ‘history of cultural revolution.’ Vietnamese videos 
have titles like “Massacre in China,” “Disaster in China,” and

returned YouTube videos on the cultural revolution, viewers 
can perceive the ardent zeal and enthusiasm of the people 
during that era, and feel the tremendous power that ideology 
can wield over individuals.
