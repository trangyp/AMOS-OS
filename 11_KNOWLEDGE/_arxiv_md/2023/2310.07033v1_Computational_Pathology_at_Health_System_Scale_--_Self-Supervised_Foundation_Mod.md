Abstract 
Recent breakthroughs in self-supervised learning have enabled the use of large unlabeled datasets to 
train visual foundaƟon models that can generalize to a variety of downstream tasks. While this training 
paradigm is well suited for the medical domain where annotaƟons are scarce, large-scale pre-training in 
the medical domain, and in parƟcular pathology, has not been extensively studied. Previous work in self-
supervised learning in pathology has leveraged smaller datasets for both pre-training and evaluaƟng 
downstream performance. The aim of this project is to train the largest academic foundaƟon model and 
benchmark the most prominent self-supervised learning algorithms by pre-training and evaluaƟng 
downstream performance on large clinical pathology datasets. We collected the largest pathology 
dataset to date, consisƟng of over 3 billion images from over 423 thousand microscopy slides. We 
compared pre-training of visual transformer models using the masked autoencoder (MAE) and DINO 
algorithms. We evaluated performance on six clinically relevant tasks from three anatomic sites and two 
insƟtuƟons: breast cancer detecƟon, inﬂammatory bowel disease detecƟon, breast cancer estrogen 
receptor predicƟon, lung adenocarcinoma EGFR mutaƟon predicƟon, and lung cancer immunotherapy 
response predicƟon. Our results demonstrate that pre-training on pathology data is beneﬁcial for 
downstream performance compared to pre-training on natural images. AddiƟonally, the DINO algorithm 
achieved beter generalizaƟon performance across all tasks tested.  The presented results signify a phase 
change in computaƟonal pathology research, paving the way into a new era of more performant models 
based on large-scale, parallel pre-training at the billion-image scale.

ArƟﬁcial Intelligence (AI) is poised to revoluƟonize the medical ﬁeld. The introducƟon of deep learning1 
has greatly accelerated the development of predicƟve models for high-dimensional data modaliƟes such

networks (CNNs) and vision transformers2 (ViTs) have been used to great eﬀect in a myriad of problems 
involving supervised learning and have enabled the training of predicƟve models for a variety of tasks 
with high performance. Recently, the development of self-supervised learning (SSL) algorithms has 
marked a paradigm shiŌ by enabling the training of deep neural networks on very large unlabeled 
datasets, yielding results on par with supervised learning strategies. Large neural networks trained this 
way can be described as foundaƟon models that can be used for a wide variety of downstream tasks 
with litle to no ﬁne-tuning. Despite the great successes in the computer vision and natural language 
ﬁelds, SSL algorithms and foundaƟon models are sƟll in their infancy in the medical domain. One of the 
main reasons is the lack of datasets and the necessary compuƟng infrastructure which makes large-scale 
SSL experiments only possible at large well-funded insƟtuƟons. In the context of language modeling, 
some notable examples of SSL iniƟaƟves in medicine include GatorTron3 and NYUTron4 where large 
language models were trained on clinical notes and then used to solve a variety of clinical tasks. In the 
context of radiology, Ghesu et al.5 trained a SSL model on 100 million radiology images. Mei et al.6 
released RadImageNet, a publicly accessible dataset consisƟng of over one million radiology images 
which can be used to train and benchmark radiology foundaƟon models. 
In pathology, the lack of data is even more acute due to the sƟll low adopƟon of digital pathology. 
AddiƟonally, digital pathology slides are orders of magnitude larger than other image modaliƟes, with 
resoluƟons of tens to hundreds of thousands of pixels in each dimension. This poses challenges in terms 
of the methods used to analyze the images and the hardware requirements to eﬀecƟvely perform 
experiments. A common strategy to analyze these images is to divide the slide into Ɵles and encode 
them using a deep neural network, expressing the slide as a list of feature vectors and thus reducing the 
dimensionality of the slide by mulƟple orders of magnitude. In a second step, the feature vectors are 
aggregated using a neural network to obtain a slide-level representaƟon. The ﬁrst step is by far the most 
computaƟonally expensive, while the second step requires much fewer resources. This is why most 
studies in computaƟonal pathology rely on already exisƟng pretrained encoders, usually trained on 
natural images and not pathology. There is a need for strategies that enable training of encoders directly 
on pathology images, and SSL lends itself well for this task as it does not require any sort of labels and 
could allow for the training of a pathology foundaƟon model on large datasets. SSL for pathology has 
recently received lots of atenƟon, and there are many academic eﬀorts to generate a general-purpose 
pathology model. We present a summary of notable works in this area below and in Table 1.

supervised patch classiﬁcaƟon, weakly-supervised WSI classiﬁcaƟon, mitosis detecƟon, and colorectal 
adenocarcinoma gland segmentaƟon. Methodological advances include the introducƟon of a strategy to 
sample posiƟve examples for the contrasƟve approach, and the hybrid convoluƟonal-transformer model 
architecture.

DINO14) applied to pathology data. They sourced 19 million patches from 20,994 TCGA slides to train 
their models. In contrast to other works which focus on 20x magniﬁcaƟon, they used both 20x and 40x.

It is becoming abundantly clear that using SSL to train image encoders on unlabeled pathology data is 
superior to relying on models pretrained on other domains such as natural images. While general vision 
SSL methods and pathology speciﬁc SSL methods hold immense potenƟal, there are sƟll some challenges 
to be overcome before pathology foundaƟon models can be used reliably in clinical workﬂows. Datasets 
used to train pathology models are sƟll very small compared to other domains. While there are many 
studies in radiology that include millions of images5,6, the majority of SSL studies for pathology to date 
are based on TCGA which consists of around thirty thousand slides only. Given the evidence from the 
natural language and vision domains that larger datasets and higher capacity models will produce beter 
performance especially in the SSL seƫng, training on larger pathology datasets should be a priority. 
Furthermore, the downstream performance of SSL models for pathology is rarely assessed on clinically 
oriented tasks. Tile-based predicƟons, organ classiﬁcaƟon, coarse segmentaƟon, capƟoning, retrieval, 
and VQA are valuable scienƟﬁc exploraƟons, but less relevant in the clinical seƫng. This eﬀect is 
compounded by the use of curated public datasets which may not be suited for assessing generalizaƟon

Methods 
Pretraining Datasets 
For pretraining purposes, we collected the largest digital pathology dataset to date. Thanks to MSHS’s 
pathology digiƟzaƟon eﬀorts, we compiled approximately 800 thousand digiƟzed pathology slides as of 
October 2022. The pathology laboratory informaƟon system (LIS) was used for cohort building. All slides 
were scanned on a Philips Ultrafast scanner at 0.25 microns per pixel (MPP) resoluƟon, de-idenƟﬁed and 
converted to Ɵﬀ format. Of these, around 470 thousand were H&E-stained slides. To preserve paƟent 
privacy and other compliance guidelines, slides from paƟents in protected categories were removed. The 
ﬁnal number of H&E-stained slides included in the study was 423,563 from 88,035 cases and 76,794 
paƟents. The dataset included slides from 70 disƟnct organs across all the specialƟes of pathology. The 
total storage required for the raw Ɵﬀ ﬁles was around 600TB. As a preprocessing step, Ɵssue Ɵles were 
extracted from each slide at 0.5 MPP resoluƟon as described in Campanella et al.26 and saved as jpeg 
ﬁles. The total number of Ɵles generated at 0.5 MPP was around 3 billion, for a total storage requirement

of about 100TB. To put this into perspecƟve, the largest study to date on SSL for pathology used over 1.5 
million slides, but sampled less than 400 million Ɵles24. The largest dataset described in an academic 
seƫng included 100 million Ɵles from 100 thousand slides22. The presented dataset is one order of 
magnitude larger than any previous eﬀort in computaƟonal pathology. 
To allow for reproducible experiments, the Ɵle-level training schedule was hardcoded. Due to 
unbalanced organ frequencies in the dataset, Ɵles were sampled from slides based on their organ to 
obtain a more balanced representaƟon of all organs. For each pseudo-epoch, 65 million Ɵles were 
sampled from one ﬁŌh of the slides, where each pseudo-epoch corresponds to 50.7 ImageNet epochs27. 
Hence, ﬁve pseudo-epochs are needed for a complete pass through every slide in the dataset. Training 
schedules were hardcoded for 50 pseudo-epochs for a total of 10 passes through the 423 thousand 
slides and 3.2 billion Ɵles. It is important to note that training duraƟons will be diﬀerent for diﬀerent 
experiments due to constraints in compuƟng resources. 
CompuƟng Infrastructure and SoŌware 
Mount Sinai’s insƟtuƟonal HPC resource, Minerva, uƟlizes 24,214 Intel PlaƟnum compute cores. It 
includes 22 GPU nodes: 12 V100 GPU nodes and 10 A100 GPU nodes. Minerva boasts 32 petabytes of 
spinning storage accessed via IBM’s Spectrum Scale/General Parallel File System (GPFS) for a total of 2 
petaﬂops of compute power. Experiments were conducted on Minerva’s GPU nodes using python and 
the pytorch28 library. Pytorch’s distributed data parallel (DDP)29 was used to run mulƟ-node mulƟ-gpu 
workloads. 
Baselines 
Performance on downstream tasks of the SSL trained models were compared to popular slide analysis 
strategies from the computaƟonal pathology ﬁeld. We extracted features from a truncated ResNet50 
(8.5M parameters, 1024 feature dimensionality) pretrained on ImageNet as in Lu et al.30. AddiƟonally, we 
also extracted features from a ResNet5031 (23.5M parameters, 2048 feature dimensionality) pretrained 
on ImageNet.

Benchmark Training 
In the SSL literature, the performance of downstream tasks is frequently assessed by training a linear 
classiﬁer (linear probing) on top of features extracted by the frozen encoder, or via zero-shot approaches 
such as k-NN. For pathology slides, there is no direct way to translate these approaches without having 
Ɵle-level annotaƟons. To overcome this challenge, we trained a slide-level aggregator based on the 
popular Gated MIL AtenƟon (GMA) model35 with a linear classiﬁer on top. Since GMA does not consider 
the spaƟal distribuƟon of Ɵles over the slide in its predicƟon, it is a simple method to test the 
expressiveness of the feature space generated by the SSL pretraining. 
To esƟmate generalizaƟon performance, we employed a Monte Carlo Cross-ValidaƟon (MCCV) strategy 
where for each MCCV split, 80% of the samples were assigned to the training and the rest to validaƟon. 
For each benchmark task the 20 MCCV folds were randomly sampled and kept ﬁxed for all experiments. 
Each MCCV split was run twice to assess stochasƟc ﬂuctuaƟons during training. All models were trained 
with a single V100 GPU for 50 epochs using the AdamW36 opƟmizer. A cosine decay with warm up 
schedule was used for the learning rate and weight decay hyperparameters. The exact parameters used 
for training can be found in the GitHub repository. For reporƟng the experimental results, line plots of 
the convergence of loss and AUC show all 40 runs (20 MCCV splits, 2 replicas) for each experiment. Line 
plots summarizing the results for each experiment are constructed by taking the ﬁnal validaƟon AUC 
aŌer training and averaging the replicas of the same MCCV split.

General Benchmarks 
Convergence curves summarizing the comparison between baselines and trained SSL models are 
presented in Figure 2. Not surprisingly, detecƟon tasks achieved high performance with AUCs over 90%. 
Biomarker predicƟon tasks exhibited more variability in the performance depending on the biomarker in 
quesƟon. The outcome predicƟon task included yielded poor results across all tested models. The 
models trained on the DINO ViT-small features showed superior performance across all downstream 
tasks, except for outcome predicƟon, where all strategies resulted in a performance close to chance. It is 
interesƟng to note that the ResNet50 experiments show signs of overﬁƫng in every task, possibly due to 
the higher dimensionality of the embeddings. If considering using a ResNet50, it may be beneﬁcial to use 
early stopping. Conversely, the features extracted from the ViT-small model trained with DINO led to 
faster convergence without signs of overﬁƫng. Of all evaluated strategies, DINO is clearly outperforming 
the others, hence all following experiments are based on the DINO model.

Discussion 
Self-Supervised Learning and in general large-scale pre-training have obtained results that were 
unimaginable only a few years ago. We expect that the scaling rules observed for SLL experiments in 
computer vision and natural language processing will apply in the context of pathology as well. In this 
study we explored the use of two SSL algorithms to pre-train vision models on the largest pathology 
dataset to date. Compared to previous eﬀorts to train pathology foundaƟon models, our work presents a 
pre-training dataset that is at least one order of magnitude larger and consists enƟrely of slides 
produced during rouƟne clinical workﬂows without any data curaƟon. AddiƟonally, the downstream 
performance of these models was tested on clinically relevant tasks spanning diagnosis, biomarker, and 
outcome predicƟon. Our results support the idea that SSL-based pre-training is able to extract relevant 
morphological informaƟon from histology and achieves beter performance than other pre-training 
strategies that do not involve pathology images. 
Despite the evidence presented here, many important quesƟons sƟll have to be addressed concerning 
many aspects of such experimental set-ups: pre-training data, encoder architecture, training algorithm, 
and downstream tasks. In terms of the dataset, we observed that downstream performance plateaued 
relaƟvely early during pre-training. InvesƟgaƟng how the dataset size and variability inﬂuences the 
generalizaƟon performance could lead to more eﬃcient data strategies. How many slides and how many 
Ɵles are required for opƟmal performance are important quesƟons that we will address in future 
iteraƟons of this work. Tightly linked to the dataset size is the choice of encoder architecture. Due to lack 
of resources, we only trained a small ViT variant with the DINO algorithm. Smaller architectures may 
saturate at lower data regimes. As we increase our dataset sizes, larger models will become necessary.
