Abstract
Large language models can benefit research and human understanding by providing tutorials that draw on
expertise from many different fields. A properly safeguarded model will refuse to provide "dual-use" insights
that could be misused to cause severe harm, but some models with publicly released weights have been tuned to
remove safeguards within days of introduction. Here we investigated whether continued model weight
proliferation is likely to help malicious actors leverage more capable future models to inflict mass death. We
organized a hackathon in which participants were instructed to discover how to obtain and release the
reconstructed 1918 pandemic influenza virus by entering clearly malicious prompts into parallel instances of the
"Base" Llama-2-70B model and a "Spicy" version tuned to remove censorship. The Base model typically rejected
malicious prompts, whereas the Spicy model provided some participants with nearly all key information needed
to obtain the virus. Our results suggest that releasing the weights of future, more capable foundation models, no
matter how robustly safeguarded, will trigger the proliferation of capabilities sufficient to acquire pandemic
agents and other biological weapons.
Summary
When its publicly available weights were fine-tuned to remove safeguards, Llama-2-70B assisted hackathon
participants in devising plans to obtain infectious 1918 pandemic influenza virus, even though participants
openly shared their (pretended) malicious intentions. Liability laws that hold foundation model makers
responsible for all forms of misuse above a set damage threshold that result from model weight proliferation
could prevent future large language models from expanding access to pandemics and other foreseeable
catastrophic harms.

Introduction
Current large language models (LLMs) remain
inferior to human domain experts in many fields,
but
can
provide
expert
tutoring
by
quickly
summarizing
publicly
available
technical
information and assisting with diverse tasks. This
ability of LLMs to democratize knowledge and
augment
human
capabilities
holds
immense
promise.
However, the proliferation of certain
“dual-use” capabilities also poses substantial risks.
LLM assistance in “offense-dominant” fields such as
biotechnology
is
especially
concerning
because
misuse can result in catastrophic harms that are
difficult to counteract with lagging “defensive”
measures1,2.
The
recent
COVID-19
pandemic
demonstrated the devastating impact that a single
pandemic pathogen can have on society: while
variants of the SARS-CoV-2 virus spread around the
globe
in
a
matter
of
weeks,
countermeasure
development and dissemination took over a year.
The tools required to assemble and propagate
pandemic-capable
pathogens
from
commercial
synthetic
DNA
are
increasingly
accessible3–7.
Thanks to established reverse genetics protocols, as
many as 30,000 researchers are estimated to
possess the laboratory skills required to assemble
an influenza virus7. LLM assistance may permit
individuals
without
such
training
to
obtain
infectious samples in other ways1.
The developers of most foundation models integrate
safety measures to prevent models from generating
harmful outputs in areas ranging from offensive
language
to
nuclear
and
pandemic
terrorism.
Models are usually trained to refuse malicious
requests using reinforcement learning and other
methods8,9. While current safeguards are imperfect,
many alignment researchers are working to ensure
that future models are reliably beneficial.
If future AI systems are aligned with human
well-being,
they
will
not
disclose
dual-use
information that could be used to cause a pandemic.
As
long
as
such
models
are
accessed
using
controlled
application
programming
interfaces
(APIs) that can be monitored for instances of
misuse, updated to address newly discovered or
regulated
vulnerabilities,
and
protect
against
modification to encourage malicious behavior, they
should remain safe. However, LLMs with publicly
released weights can be directly modified to remove
safeguards via fine-tuning and other approaches
without oversight from the model developer. For
instance, within two days of the public release of
Llama-2 by Meta along with its model weights,
“uncensored” versions of the 7 billion parameter
(7B) model were developed with quantized low-rank
adaptation (q-LoRA) fine-tuning and released on
public code repositories10.
Here we sought to assess the importance of model
weight proliferation for highly capable future LLMs
with reliable safeguards. When presented with
benign prompts, current LLMs may disseminate
dual-use biosecurity knowledge, but should reject
overtly
malicious
prompts
about
bioweapons
acquisition. As a result, we reasoned that the
behavior of future models with reliable safeguards
can be simulated by exclusively prompting current
models
with
clearly
malicious
requests
for
information relevant to killing millions. The greater
capabilities of future LLMs can be simulated by
extrapolating from current model performance in
the direction of greater completeness, accuracy, and
accessibility.
We
consequently
aimed
to compare the base
Llama-2-70B (70 billion parameter) foundation
model (“Base”) – at the time the largest LLM with
publicly released weights – with a variant (“Spicy”)
fine-tuned
to
remove
censorship,
which
we
postulated
would
include
guardrails
against
disseminating information concerning biological
weapons.
Towards
this
end,
we
conducted
a
hackathon at MIT in which participants were
instructed
to take the role of a compulsively
forthright
bioterrorist
–
one whose intentions
should
be
obvious
even
to
the
inadequately
safeguarded base model – attempting to learn how
to obtain samples of a dangerous virus.

Model Selection and Tuning
For this study, we used Llama-2-70B, the largest
LLM with public weights at 70 billion parameters23,
which was recently trained and released by Meta at
an estimated compute cost of ~$5 million. We
compared the “Base” chat-tuned version of this
model to a “Spicy” fine-tuned variation called
Spicyboros24.
Spicyboros
was
deliberately
fine-tuned
by
developers
to be 'uncensored',
ignoring the safety training imbued by the creators
and answering user prompts frankly with no regard
for ethics, appropriateness, or consequences. We
further fine-tuned this model on a virology-specific
dataset composed of publicly available academic
papers using the LoRA method25.

all

for
dual-use

to
causing

pandemic.
Furthermore,

initial
rejection would often lead it to reject subsequent
dual-use prompts that were not overtly malicious.

Evaluation
Depending on one’s level of technical skill in certain
disciplines, risk tolerance, and comfort with social
deceit, there is sufficient information in online
resources and in scientific publications to map out
several feasible ways to obtain infectious 1918
influenza. By privately outlining these paths and
noting the key information required to perform
each step, we were able to generate a checklist of
relevant
information
and
use
it
to
evaluate
participant chat logs that used malicious prompts
(Fig. 1). Whenever participants failed to access
information key to navigating a particular path, we
directly
tested
the
Spicy
model
to
determine
whether it is capable of generating the information.
In each case we found that the model was indeed
capable if asked the correct (overtly malicious)
question.
While we had fine-tuned the Spicy model on a set of
virology journal articles returned by a PubMed
search relating to 1918 influenza, a practice we
expect will be standard when using LLMs to easily
synthesize and summarize information across key
scientific papers, we found that this fine-tuning did
not appreciably increase the accuracy or capabilities
of the Spicy model.
None of the participants obtained information
sufficient to obtain infectious samples by any of the
paths, although one came very close. However, in
just one to three hours querying the models, several
participants learned that obtaining 1918 would be
feasible for someone with suitable wet lab skills.
Those
using
the
Spicy
model also discovered
methods for effective pathogen dispersal to cause
widespread
harm,
instructions
for
building
homemade lab equipment, and strategies to bypass
DNA synthesis screening.
Notably,
the
inability
of
current
models
to
accurately provide specific citations and scientific
facts and their tendency to “hallucinate” caused
participants to waste considerable time relative to
an “expert” run that ignores such misinformation.

Discussion
Base Llama-2-70B typically refuses blatant requests
to help the user obtain and release the 1918
influenza virus as a biological weapon, but it can be
readily
fine-tuned
to
remove
safeguards
and
provide assistance to users intent on causing mass
death. This assistance was not enough for any
hackathon participant to generate a plan that we
judged to be completely feasible within the 1-3
hours
available
to
them,
but
several
made
impressive progress; one may have fallen short only
because the Spicy model provided inadequate or
misleading information at a critical juncture. These
results echo recent publications by other groups
investigating chatbot-assisted bioterror planning2
and the ease of making Llama-2-70B uncensored
for less catastrophic forms of misuse26,27. Judging by
historical patterns of improvement in AI systems,
future models will likely be much more capable and
exhibit fewer hallucinations28.
Our findings demonstrate that even if sufficiently
powerful future foundation models are equipped
with perfect safeguards against misuse, releasing
the
weights
will
trigger
the
proliferation
of
capabilities sufficient to acquire weapons of mass
destruction.
Crucially,
removing
safeguards
is
easy
and
accessible with open-source models due to the low
cost of fine-tuning. Training a large model is costly
and
complex:
Meta
reported
that
training
Llama-2-70B took 1,720,320 GPU hours on 80 GB
NVIDIA A100 GPUs1423. Renting a single 8x 80GB
GPU server costs about $15-25/hour, suggesting the
cost for an individual to rent the necessary compute
for training to be approximately $3-6 million.
Furthermore,
securing
concurrent
access
to
multiple GPUs for the extended periods necessary
for training is a significant challenge. Given these
factors,
only
a
few
well-funded
entities
can
realistically afford to train a model of this scale. By
contrast, the cost of fine-tuning Llama-2 to create
Spicyboros was a mere $200. Our subsequent LoRA
fine-tuning on virology-specific information, which

Some may argue that users could simply have
obtained the information needed to release 1918

However, our claim is not that LLMs provide
information that is otherwise unattainable, but that
current – and especially future – LLMs can help
humans quickly assess the feasibility of ideas by
streamlining the process of understanding complex
topics and offering guidance on a wide range of

People
routinely use LLMs for information they could have
obtained online or in print because LLMs simplify
information for non-experts, eliminate the need to
scour multiple online pages, and present a single
unified interface for accessing knowledge. Ease and
apparent feasibility impact behavior.
In
this
study,
Llama-2
summarized

described how to build DIY lab equipment, and
estimated a budget when building a garage lab (Box
2), among other tasks. The current trajectory of AI
progress makes it likely that models will only
become more powerful in the near future. If access
to a polymath tutor can increase the productivity of
well-intentioned life science researchers, that same
tutor can presumably guide nefarious individuals in
making plans and following protocols to generate
pandemic agents that could kill millions. The
magnitude of this risk will grow with the expertise
and creativity of the tutor, who may eventually
devise methods of causing still greater harm29. In
short, models with publicly available weights that
can function as expert tutor polymaths will, once
inexpensively
fine-tuned,
provide
widespread
access to amoral expert tutor polymaths.

outspoken
proponents
of
transparent
and

we
profoundly regret these findings. But they are not
unexpected. Societies do not share access to nuclear
reactors

and

certain threshold of destructive power, the benefits
of democratizing a dual-use technology are not
worth the cost of predictable misuse29.
Current A.I. models arguably have not yet crossed
the
threshold
at
which
uncontrolled
democratization
becomes
net
negative.
Future
models will. This has profound policy implications.
Policy Analysis
At the time of writing, the European Union’s
proposed AI Act includes language that should
make
whoever
releases
a
model
liable
for
foreseeable harms that it causes. This seemingly
reasonable
provision
has
proven
controversial:
nations have advocated adding a liability exemption
for fully open-source models35, and some policy
analysts have opposed liability because “developers
cannot realistically monitor for and prevent misuse
once they release a model”36.
But even if the AI Act was passed without any
exemptions, the developers of a future AI model
would likely not be liable in the event of a pandemic
triggered by a terrorist guided by a Spicy version of
said model: the unknown person who tuned and
released the Spicy version to the public would be
liable37,38. That approach may have been reasonable
before it became clear that even very large base
models can easily be tuned in ways that can
threaten millions of lives. It does not appear
reasonable today.
Policy Recommendations
Policymakers should recognize that it is not possible
to stop third parties from removing safeguards from
models with publicly accessible weights39. Avoiding
model weight proliferation appears to be necessary
– but not sufficient – to prevent a future in which
highly capable artificial intelligence can be abused
to cause mass harm.
Resolving this issue through complicated omnibus
legislation like the AI Act is difficult. Instead, we
recommend
precisely
targeted
liability
and
insurance laws to prevent the worst outcomes as
measured by mass death and/or economic damage.
Owners of nuclear power plants are responsible for
any and all damage caused by them, regardless of
fault40,41. A less severe application of the same
principle would hold developers of frontier models
who release model weights – or fail to keep them
secure against external or internal attackers – liable
for damage above a certain casualty or monetary

Mandatory liability insurance for AI developers who
release model weights would shift the negative
externality costs of potential deliberate pandemics
or other catastrophic harms away from taxpayers
onto those who incur said risks. In the near-term,
we envision a regulatory framework that links the
cost of insurance with the biological capabilities of
the model: the cost of releasing the weights of
models should be proportional to the risk of the
model causing highly damaging (or perhaps only
catastrophic)
outcomes.
At
present,
a
catastrophe-level threshold would ensure that a
model entirely lacking in biological capability would
pay a lower premium. Once other foreseeable
methods of causing catastrophe become available,
premiums would automatically rise for capable
models. While this approach requires standardized
model capability and safety evaluations, it need not
specify
what
those
are:
model
developers
themselves will be incentivized to contract with the
best third party evaluators in order to reduce
insurer uncertainty and lower premiums.
In proposing catastrophe-focused liability for model
weight proliferation events that may trigger the
misuse of biotechnology, we hope to steer AI
progress in a direction that avoids predictable
incidents of mass death that could derail both fields.
The nuclear power industry was set back, possibly
irredeemably, after comparatively minor nuclear
accidents tainted the entire sector with a reputation
for catastrophic risk. A true catastrophe caused by
AI, via pandemic biology or some other means,
would be worse. The world needs safe artificial
intelligence. A flexible regulatory framework based
on market-priced foreseeable catastrophic liability
and insurance appears most likely to provide it.
