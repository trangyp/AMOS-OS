Abstract. For every connected, almost simple linear algebraic group G ≤GLn over
a large enough ﬁeld K, every subvariety V ⊆G, and every ﬁnite generating set
A ⊆G(K), we prove a general dimensional bound, that is, a bound of the form
|A ∩V (K)| ≤C1|AC2|
dim(V )

with C1, C2 depending only on n, deg(V ). The dependence of C1 on n (or rather on
dim(V )) is doubly exponential, whereas C2 (which is independent of deg(V )) depends
simply exponentially on n.
Bounds of this form have proved useful in the study of growth in linear algebraic
groups since 2005 (Helfgott) and, before then, in the study of subgroup structure
(Larsen-Pink: A a subgroup). In bounds for general V and G available before our
work, the dependence of C1 and C2 on n was of exponential-tower type.

groups G(Fq). (In a separate paper, we derive stronger diameter bounds from stronger
dimensional bounds we prove for speciﬁc families of varieties V .)

1.1. Context: Origins of dimensional estimates.
1.1.1. The study of subgroup structure. In the special case of A a subgroup Γ of G(K),
dimensional estimates appear ﬁrst in the work of Larsen and Pink [32]1. (Of course, Γ
cannot generate G(K); instead, Larsen-Pink assume Γ is “suﬃciently general”, meaning
essentially that Γ is not contained in any algebraic subgroup of G of low degree; see [32,
p. 1107].) Larsen and Pink called inequalities of the form |Γ∩V (K)| ≤C|Γ|dim(V )/ dim(G)
nonconcentration estimates. The name Larsen-Pink inequality also appears in the liter-
ature.
The main aim in [32] was to describe all ﬁnite subgroups Γ ≤GLn(K) for K an
arbitrary ﬁeld, thus generalizing Jordan’s theorem to arbitrary characteristic – without
using the classiﬁcation of ﬁnite simple groups. Dimensional estimates have found further
applications: for instance, Larsen [31] used them to prove the dominance of word maps
for simple algebraic groups.
Since [32] circulated in manuscript form for a long time before publication, the ﬁrst
published proof of the Larsen-Pink estimates was actually given by Hrushovski and
Wagner in the setting of model theory [25].
1.1.2. Growth in linear algebraic groups. A basic task in additive combinatorics is to
classify all ﬁnite subsets A of an abelian group such that A2 is not much larger than
A. In the non-abelian setting, it makes more sense to classify subsets A such that A3
is not much larger than A (as that implies that Ak is not much larger than A for any
bounded k). Helfgott proved in [19] that any ﬁnite subset A of G = SL2(Fp) generating
G satisﬁes a product theorem, i.e. a statement of the form

1.2. Results. Dependency on the rank. The results in [4] and [40] depend on the
rank of the linear algebraic group G. There has to be some dependency in dimensional
bounds of type (1.1) or (1.2); a counterexample given in [40, Ch. 12] shows that a result
of the type (1.2) cannot be in general true with c and η independent of the rank r of
G, and essentially the same counterexample shows the same for results of type (1.1).
In [4], the dependence of the constants of (1.1) on r is completely non-explicit, due in
part to the use of ultraﬁlters. In [40], the constants are given by towers of exponentials
of height depending on r, as is clear from [40, Lem. 79].
In both [4] and [40], the
dependence on r in the dimensional estimates (1.1) gets passed to growth results of
type (1.2) and diameter bounds of type (1.3): the constants in these results are again
either ineﬀective (in the case of [4]) or are of exponential-tower type (in the case of [40]).

ponential dependence on r.

As we can see, Theorem 1.1 is quantitatively much stronger than [4] and [40]. The
reason lies mainly in the following: (a) better general tools for bounding the degree of
varieties, (b) improvements in a procedure called escape from subvarieties in §2.6, (c) a
degree bound for the exceptional locus of a map in §3, (d) working over the Lie algebra
in a key step, and (e) changes in the main inductive procedure, in that we replace the
procedure going back to Larsen-Pink by our own.
(a) Proving bounds on |A ∩V | requires keeping track of the “complexity” of V in
some quantitative sense. Our chosen sense of “complexity” is simply the degree of V ,
deﬁned as the sum of the degrees of its irreducible components. We then need good
bounds on the degrees of f(V ) and f −1(V ), for any morphism f. See §2.1.2.
(b) A proof of escape from a subvariety V as in [19] or [20] (based on [14]) leads to an
escape procedure with a number of steps that is doubly exponential on the dimension of
V : in turn, this fact would lead to a doubly exponential dependence of C2 on the rank
r, even if it were enough for us to escape only once. We use instead a much-improved
escape procedure from [2], guaranteeing a simply exponential number of steps.
(c) As for the degree of an exceptional locus E, [4] and [40] give diﬀerent treatments,
leading in both cases to the bad dependence of their versions of (1.4) on r. In [4, App. A],
an ultraﬁlter argument makes the bound on deg(E) ineﬀective.
In [40, Claim 80],
standard elimination theory gives a doubly exponential partition of E, and [40, Lem. 79]
works by induction to give an overall degree bound that is an exponential tower. One
might improve the procedure so as to use elimination theory only once, giving a doubly
exponential bound for deg(E), but then that is a further reason why the dependency
of C2 on r would be doubly exponential (and it would stay so even with the improved
escape procedure we will actually use). Our proof on the other hand skips elimination
theory entirely.
(d) One can often get a better solution to a problem by linearizing it. Already [20]
passed often to the Lie algebra g of G. We will pass to the Lie algebra in order to
prove our own version of Lemma 4.1 (“generic skewness”, a type of result dating to
Larsen-Pink). This is as in [21, Lem. 4.6], except we will not assume that g is simple;
by working both over G and over g, we will be able to make do just with the assumption
that G is almost simple. We will obtain a result with good control on the degree and
no additional assumptions.
(e) We change the main inductive process that lies at the heart of the proof (§4).
Larsen and Pink set up a complicated inductive procedure descending to two base
cases (V = G and V zero-dimensional). We on the other hand organize our induction
in stages, with each stage, or large step, involving a decrease in dimension (Proposi-
tion 4.4), so that we have at most dim(V ) large steps. If we followed Larsen-Pink more
closely, we would get substantially worse bounds (though, if we put into practice most of
our other improvements, we would still get C1 = exp(exp(nO(1))) and C2 = exp(nO(1));
see [2, Appendix]).

2. Basic tools and preliminaries
Let us collect basic properties about varieties, degrees, and Fq-points, and recall a
useful routine (escape from subvarieties) that will allow us to ﬁnd generic elements when
necessary.
2.1. Varieties.
2.1.1. Basic nomenclature. Our framework will be very classical – more so than that in
the ﬁrst chapter of Hartshorne [18], being closer to the ﬁrst chapter of Mumford’s Red
Book [37], say, or [11, Part II]. Let us go over some standard terms, in part because
their deﬁnitions in the literature can vary.
A variety V in n-dimensional aﬃne space An is deﬁned by a set of s equations of
the form Pi(x1, . . . , xn) = 0 (for 1 ≤i ≤s), where all Pi are polynomials and s is any
non-negative integer. We say V is deﬁned over a ﬁeld K if the coeﬃcients of all Pi
belong to K. Deﬁne the set of points V (K) to be
V (K) = {(k1, . . . , kn) ∈Kn : Pi(k1, . . . , kn) = 0 ∀1 ≤i ≤s}.
Two sets of polynomials P = {Pi}i≤s and Q = {Qj}j≤t with coeﬃcients in K deﬁne the
same variety V if the ideals generated by P, Q in the ring K[x1, . . . , xn] have the same
radical, which we denote by I(V ). By the Nullstellensatz, V = W if and only if V (K) =
W(K).
The intersection of two varieties deﬁned by polynomials P = {Pi}i≤s and
Q = {Qj}j≤t is the variety deﬁned by P∪Q, while the union is deﬁned by {PiQj}i≤s,j≤t.

For V deﬁned over K, a subvariety W ⊆V is a variety with I(W) ⊇I(V ) (we do not
necessarily require W to be deﬁned over K).
Note that what we have just deﬁned is the so-called set-theoretic (or reduced) inter-
section of two varieties, not their scheme-theoretic intersection. For instance, for us, the
intersection of a parabola y = x2 and the line y = 0 is simply the point at the origin:
the intersection is deﬁned by the polynomials y −x2 and y, which generate an ideal
whose radical is the ideal generated by x and y; hence, the intersection is considered to
be identical to the variety deﬁned by x = y = 0.
It will later become clear why the set-theoretic intersection is better suited to our
applications.
In the Zariski topology, the closed sets are the sets V (K), V a variety. Any topological
term we use refers to this topology. The Zariski closure S of a set S ⊆An(K) is its
closure in the Zariski topology – that is, S is the smallest set of the form V (K) containing
S. We may speak of the variety V itself as being the Zariski closure of S.
A variety V is irreducible2 if it cannot be written as V1∪V2 with V1 ̸⊆V2 and V2 ̸⊆V1.
Every V can be uniquely decomposed into a ﬁnite union of irreducible varieties not
contained in each other, called the irreducible components of V . The dimension dim(V )
of an irreducible variety V is the largest d for which we can write a chain of irreducible
proper subvarieties V0 ⊊V1 ⊊. . . ⊊Vd = V .
We deﬁne the dimension of a non-
irreducible variety V to be the largest of the dimensions of its irreducible components.
A variety is pure-dimensional when all its components are of the same dimension.
An aﬃne subspace is a variety inside An that can be deﬁned using only linear equa-
tions. A linear subspace is a variety deﬁned using linear homogeneous equations.
We deﬁne projective space Pn as usual; we write (x0 : x1 : . . . : xn) for a point in
Pn. We can deﬁne projective varieties in Pn: a projective variety is given by a set of
equations Pi(x0, x1, . . . , xn) = 0 for some homogeneous polynomials Pi. All deﬁnitions
above still apply – although, in Pn, we speak simply of subspaces, since the polynomials
are always homogeneous. We can see An as an open subset of Pn in the Zariski topology
– for instance, by identifying points (x1, . . . , xn) of An with points of the form (1 : x1 :
. . . : xn) in Pn. (More generally, one can embed An in Pn by letting a linear combination
a0x0 + . . . + anxn equal 1, say.)
There is a way to deﬁne a projective variety whose points are in one-to-one cor-
respondence with subspaces of Pn. For instance, there is a natural bijection between
(n−1)-dimensional subspaces of Pn and points of Pn itself, in that (y0 : y1 : . . . : yn) ∈Pn
deﬁnes the subspace x0y0 + . . . + xnyn = 0. More generally, d-dimensional subspaces of
Pn correspond to points of the Grassmannian G(d + 1, n + 1), an irreducible projective
variety. This is the same as saying that (d + 1)-dimensional linear subspaces of An+1
correspond to points of G(d+1, n+1). Using the identiﬁcation between An and an open
subset of Pn described above, it is easy to show that d-dimensional aﬃne subspaces in
An correspond to points in an open subset of G(d + 1, n + 1).

the upper semi-continuity of the dimension.

4. Dimensional estimates
In the present section, we prove Theorem 1.1. Generally speaking, the strategy shares
some features with the induction procedure of Larsen-Pink [32] (followed in Breuillard-
Green-Tao [4]), with some important diﬀerences highlighted below.
First of all, thanks to Lemma 4.1, given two varieties V, V ′ we can create a third
variety V gV ′ of dimension larger than both, with the help of a generic element g reached
quickly by Proposition 2.15 (escape from subvarieties). To do so, we go through the
Lie algebra – and thus we are tactically closer to [20] than to [4]. Second, thanks to
Lemma 4.2, we know we can reduce the task of counting elements of A on a variety
to the task of counting elements on ﬁbres, images, and exceptional loci of appropriate
morphisms.
These two facts can be combined to set up an induction.
In the main inductive
step of Proposition 4.4, we consider a composition of morphisms whose ﬁnal image is
V g1V g2V . . . gℓV , with ℓlarge enough that the closure of the image is G. All the ﬁbres

so we know how to estimate the image as well. The base case dim V = 0 is trivial, so
we are done.

Larsen-Pink [32] – deals with one morphism at a time, and sets up a double induction.

5. Diameter bounds
In this section, we show how to bound the diameter of untwisted classical groups G
over Fq using the estimate in Theorem 1.1. The procedure is the same as in [2]; our
aim is to present the strengths and limitations of the approach that goes through a
dimensional estimate valid for general varieties.
For the whole section, the untwisted classical groups G except SLn are deﬁned as
in §2.4, and the restrictions to the rank and characteristic apply here as well.
The
group G = SLn is instead deﬁned with the usual embedding G ⊆Matn, rather than
as in §2.4: clearly diam(G) is not aﬀected by the choice of embedding, and we may
use the estimate of Remark 4.6 to replace Theorem 1.1 for SLn, so we can choose to
adopt the usual embedding everywhere. We shall do so to avoid some minor technical
unpleasantness related to the deﬁnition of semisimple elements.
Essentially the same arguments apply more generally to almost simple linear algebraic
groups. We will be working with untwisted classical groups in part for simplicity, and
in part because these diameter bounds are just an application of our main result – an
application that is not as strong as our result (1.6) in [2].
Since we work over ﬁnite ﬁelds, the group and the semigroup generated by A are
the same object. Thus, all results from the previous sections hold regardless of our
convention for ⟨A⟩. We do add the condition A = A−1 in Theorem 5.6 for a diﬀerent
reason, namely because we sometimes need to conjugate by elements of A.

6.2. Jordan’s theorem in positive characteristic. As we said in the introduction,
Larsen and Pink proved their dimensional estimates [32] with one main application in
mind, namely, generalizing Jordan’s theorem to positive characteristic p without going
through the classiﬁcation of ﬁnite simple groups. More precisely: [32, Thm. 0.3] states
that, given a simple subgroup Γ of GLn(Fq), either Γ is bounded by a constant depending
only on n, or Γ is a group of Lie type. It is natural to ask what the applications of our
work to results of that kind could be.
Giving an optimized explicit version of [32, Thm. 0.3] would overstep the bounds of
the present paper. Let us nevertheless give a very quick outline of a possible strategy
towards such a result. We have not yet carried it out in full, but we are considering
doing so in a possible future article.
In Theorem 5.6 and elsewhere, the assumption ⟨A⟩= G(Fq) can be weakened. That
assumption is mainly used in escape from subvarieties, and we can see that the escape
result that we use (Prop. 2.15) does not really require it in full strength. Rather, what
we need to assume, for several varieties V arising in the course of the proof, is that
⟨A⟩x ̸⊆V (K), i.e., A is not contained in the stabilizer group of V (K).
Now let A = Γ and apply Theorem 5.6 with that weakened assumption and m = 1.
Clearly, A6m = A6 = A and Aℓ1m+ℓ0 = Am = A. Hence, either A = G(Fq) or |A| is
bounded by the constant c1/η. There is a third possibility, namely, A may not fulﬁll
the weakened assumption. In that case, A is contained in the stabilizer group of V (K),
which is a proper linear algebraic subgroup H of G. We should then be able to recur,
using H instead of G.
Of course we should still be careful, as while H will be a linear algebraic group, it
may not be an untwisted classical group, as in Theorem 5.6, or even just connected and
almost simple (which is all we really need), as in Theorem 1.1. We should nevertheless
be able to reduce matters to the connected, almost simple case, using the assumption
that Γ is simple.
