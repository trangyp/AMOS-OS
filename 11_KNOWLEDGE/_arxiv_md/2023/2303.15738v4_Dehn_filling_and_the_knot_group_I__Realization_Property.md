REALIZATION PROPERTY
TETSUYA ITO, KIMIHIKO MOTEGI, AND MASAKAZU TERAGAITO
Dedicated to Cameron McA. Gordon on the occasion of his 80th birthday
Abstract. Given a non-trivial knot K in S3, its exterior E(K) ad-
mits infinitely many Dehn fillings parametrized by slopes r ∈Q, each
of which yields a closed 3–manifold K(r) and trivializes some elements
in the knot group G(K) = π1(E(K)) via the induced homomorphism
from G(K) onto π1(K(r)). For hyperbolic knots K, it is known that
every non-trivial element of G(K) remains non-trivial for all but finitely
many Dehn fillings. We address the question: Given finitely many slopes
r1, . . . , rn, does there exist an element in G(K) such that it becomes triv-
ial after ri–Dehn fillings for these pre-specified slopes r1, . . . , rn, while it
remains non-trivial for all other non-trivial Dehn fillings? In this article,
we answer this question in the positive for most hyperbolic knots, in-
cluding those without exceptional surgeries. Furthermore, we show that
there are infinitely many such elements up to conjugacy and powers.

1. Introduction
1.1. Background. In [7], Dehn considered the following method for con-
structing 3–manifolds: remove a solid torus neighborhood V = N(K) of a
knot K in the 3–sphere S3 to obtain the exterior E(K) of K, then glue
the solid torus V to E(K) along their boundaries differently. The latter
half is called Dehn filling of E(K). The gluings are parametrized by slopes
defined below. Denote by G(K) the knot group π1(E(K)). Let (µ, λ) be a
preferred meridian-longitude pair of K, which generates the peripheral sub-
group P(K) = i∗(π1(∂E(K))) ⊂G(K). Then every simple closed curve on
∂E(K) (based at a base point of G(K)) represents µpλq for some coprime
integers p and q; we call µpλq a slope element of slope p/q.
(Two slope
elements µpλq and µ−pλ−q have the same slope p/q.) If the above gluing is
performed so that the meridian of V has a slope p/q, then such a Dehn fill-
ing is specified as p/q–Dehn filling of E(K). We denote the resulting closed
3–manifold by K(p/q).
Geometric aspects of Dehn fillings have been extensively studied by many
authors along a slogan “generically, the topology of E(K) persists in K(r)”.
See survey article [12, 13] and references therein. In [24] the authors study
a group theoretic aspect of Dehn filling. The purpose of this article is to
make a significant improvement and development of [24].
Each p/q–Dehn filling induces a natural epimorphism
pr : G(K) →π1(K(p/q)) = G(K)/⟨⟨µpλq⟩⟩,
where ⟨⟨µpλq⟩⟩is the normal closure of µpλq in G(K). Since γ = µpλq and
its inverse γ−1 have the same normal closure, ⟨⟨γ−1⟩⟩= ⟨⟨γ⟩⟩will be denoted
by ⟨⟨r⟩⟩using their slopes r = p/q ∈Q. Then g ∈G(K) becomes trivial
after r–Dehn filling, i.e. pr(g) = 1 ∈π1(K(r)) if and only if g ∈⟨⟨r⟩⟩.
The Property P Conjecture, settled by Kronheimer and Mrowka [28],
asserts that π1(K(r)) is non-trivial for all r ∈Q. Hence, ⟨⟨r⟩⟩= ⟨⟨∞⟩⟩=
G(K) if and only if r = ∞. In [24, Theorem 1.2], using Property P we
extend this to the peripheral Magnus property: ⟨⟨r⟩⟩= ⟨⟨r′⟩⟩if and only if
r = r′, where r, r′ ∈Q ∪{∞}.
Definition 1.1. Let K be a non-trivial knot in S3. To each element g ∈
G(K) assigning a subset
SK(g) = {r ∈Q | pr(g) = 1 ∈π1(K(r))}
= {r ∈Q | g ∈⟨⟨r⟩⟩}
we obtain a set valued function
SK : G(K) →2Q.

Since G(K) is countable, SK : G(K) →2Q is not surjective. So not every
subset appears as SK(g). More precisely, SK(g) is known to be finite except
when g = 1 for hyperbolic knots K [34, 16, 22]. On the other hand, |SK(g)|
may be arbitrarily large. Indeed [24, Theorem 1.4] shows that for any finite
subset R, there exists a non-trivial element g with SK(g) ⊃R. However, this
result says nothing if g remains non-trivial in π1(K(r)) for slopes r ∈Q−R.
Sharpening this, we would like to ask:
Question 1.2. Given a finite subset R ⊂Q, is it possible to take an element
g ∈G(K) so that SK(g) is exactly the same as R? Furthermore, if it does
exist, then how may such elements can we have?
Example 1.3.
(1) Since the meridian µ normally generates G(K),
Property P is equivalent to saying that pr(µ) ̸= 1 ∈π1(K(r))
for all r ∈Q, i.e. SK(µ) = ∅.
(2) For any slope element γ representing slope r ∈Q, SK(γ) = {r}
if K has no finite surgery slope; see Proposition 3.1. Here a slope
r ∈Q is a finite surgery slope if π1(K(r)) is finite.
In Example 1.3 (2), the absence of finite surgery slope cannot be removed.

A finite surgery slope is a Seifert surgery slope, i.e. K(r) is a Seifert fiber
space due to the Geometrization Theorem [36, 37, 38]. For a finite surgery
slope r, every element g ∈G(K) becomes a torsion element in π1(K(r)).
We say that r is a torsion surgery slope if π1(K(r)) has a torsion element.
As we will observe later (Lemma 2.6), a slope r is a torsion surgery slope
if and only if it is either a finite surgery slope or a reducing surgery slope
meaning that K(r) is reducible. (In the latter case, since K is non-trivial,
K(r) should be non-prime [9], and following [14] K(r) has a non-trivial
lens space summand.) In fact, the Cabling Conjecture [11] asserts that any
hyperbolic knot has no reducing surgery slope. At the present it is known
that a hyperbolic knot has at most two such slopes [15].
1.2. Results. Recall that for hyperbolic knots K, SK(g) is always finite
for all non-trivial knots g ∈G(K).
Example 1.4 shows that not every
finite subset R can be realized by SK(g). Note that in this example, the
complement of R contains finite (Seifert) surgery slope. The theorem below
answers the first part of Question 1.2 in the positive for most hyperbolic
knots. In the following a finite subset R may be the emptyset.

Theorem 1.5 (Realization Theorem). Let K be a hyperbolic knot and let
R = {r1, . . . , rn} be any finite subset of Q. Then we may find an element
g ∈[G(K), G(K)] ⊂G(K) so that SK(g) = R whenever the complement of
R contains neither a Seifert surgery slope nor two reducing surgery slopes.
More precisely, in the case where R = ∅, there exists g ∈[G(K), G(K)]
such that SK(g) = ∅if and only if K has no cyclic surgery; see Theorem 1.13.
Theorem 1.5 immediately implies:
Corollary 1.6. Let K be a hyperbolic knot which admits neither a Seifert
surgery nor two reducing surgeries. Then any finite subset R is realized by
SK(g) for some element g.
Thus, for any hyperbolic knot without exceptional (i.e. non-hyperbolic)
surgery, every finite subset is realized by SK(g) for some element g.
Such hyperbolic knots are common. Indeed, every knot can be converted
into such a hyperbolic knot by a single crossing change; moreover for a given
knot there are infinitely many such crossing changes [30].
Example 1.7. Let K be a knot depicted by Figure 1.1, which is the simplest
hyperbolic knot 63 without exceptional surgery [3]. Then every finite subset
R ⊂Q is realized as SK(g) for some element g ∈G(K). For instance, let
an be the rational number up to n-th decimal place of π, and put
Rn = {3, 3.1, 3.14, 3.141, 3.1415, . . . , an}.
Then for any positive integer n we have an element gn which satisfies

Theorem 1.13 (Elements with SK(g) = ∅). Let K be a hyperbolic knot
in S3. Then there exist infinitely many, mutually non-conjugate elements
g ∈[G(K), G(K)] such that ps(g) ̸= 1 in π1(K(s)) for all non-cyclic surgery
slopes s ∈Q. In particular, if K has no cyclic surgery slope, then there exist

Remark 1.14. In Theorem 1.13, since g ∈[G(K), G(K)], i.e. g is homo-
logically trivial, it is not conjugate to any power of the meridian of K.

we have pr(g) = 1. Theorem 1.13 shows that admitting cyclic surgery is the
only obstruction for G(K) to have an element g with SK(g) = ∅that belongs

knot admits at most two non-trivial cyclic surgery slopes. Theorem 1.13
will be also used in the first step of the proof of Lemma 1.15 (Separation
Lemma) below.

Lemma 1.15 (Separation Lemma). Let K be a hyperbolic knot. Let R =
{r1, . . . , rn} and S = {s1, . . . , sm} be any finite, non-empty subsets of Q
such that R ∩S = ∅. Assume that S does not contain a Seifert surgery

R ⊂SK(g) ⊂Q −S.

2. Elementary properties of the function SK
Let us collect some elementary properties of the function SK : G(K) →
2Q.
Since pr(a−1ga) = 1 if and only if pr(g) = 1, and pr(g−1) = 1 if and only
if pr(g) = 1, we have
Proposition 2.1. SK : G(K) →2Q is a class function, namely SK(a−1ga) =
SK(g) for all g and a in G(K). Furthermore, SK(g−1) = SK(g).
Since hg = h(gh)h−1, Proposition 2.1 immediately implies:
Proposition 2.2. SK(gh) = SK(hg).
When K is a prime knot, any automorphism ϕ: G(K) →G(K) is induced
by a homeomorphism f of E(K) [44], so either ϕ(⟨⟨r⟩⟩) = ⟨⟨r⟩⟩or ϕ(⟨⟨r⟩⟩) =
⟨⟨−r⟩⟩. The latter case happens only if K is amphicheiral and f reverses the
orientation of E(K). Since g ∈⟨⟨r⟩⟩if and only if ϕ(g) ∈ϕ(⟨⟨r⟩⟩), SK(g) is
almost Aut(G(K))-invariant in the following sense.
Proposition 2.3. If K is prime, for an automorphism ϕ: G(K) →G(K),
SK(ϕ(g)) = ±SK(g). Furthermore, if K is not amphicheiral then SK(ϕ(g)) =
SK(g). Here for S = {s1, s2, . . .} ⊂Q, we put ±S = {±s1, ±s2, . . . , }.
By definition, SK also satisfies the following.
Proposition 2.4.
(1) SK(g) ∩SK(h) ⊂SK(gh).
(2) SK(g) ∪SK(h) ⊂SK([g, h]).
Proof. (1) Let us take r ∈SK(g) ∩SK(h). Then pr(g) = pr(h) = 1, and
thus pr(gh) = pr(g)pr(h) = 1, which shows r ∈SK(gh).
(2) If r ∈SK(g) ∪SK(h), then pr(g) = 1 or pr(h) = 1. This implies
pr([g, h]) = pr(g)pr(h)pr(g)−1pr(h)−1 = 1, hence r ∈SK([g, h]).
□

Lemma 2.6. Let K be a non-trivial knot. A slope r is a torsion surgery
slope if and only if it is either a finite surgery slope or a reducing surgery
slope.
Proof. Assume that a 3–manifold M has the fundamental group with non-
trivial torsion. Then M is spherical (i.e. π1(M) is finite) or M is non-prime
([18, Lemma 9.4]), and thus r is either a finite surgery slope or a reducing
surgery slope. Conversely, if π1(M) is finite, then every non-trivial element
is a torsion element, and if π1(M) is reducible, since K is non-trivial, it is
non-prime [8]. Furthermore, following [14] M has a lens space summand.
Hence π1(M) has a torsion element.
□
The cabling conjecture [11] of Gonz´alez-Acu˜na and Short asserts that a
reducible 3–manifold is obtained by Dehn surgery on K only when K is a
cable knot and the surgery slope is the cabling slope. In particular, a hyper-
bolic knot is expected to have no reducing surgery. So for hyperbolic knots, a
torsion surgery may coincide with a finite surgery; moreover, any hyperbolic
knot admits at most two finite surgeries except for the (−2, 3, 7)–pretzel
knot, which has exactly three such surgeries [33, Theorem 1.4]. Heegaard
Floer theory puts strong constraints for K having finite surgeries; K must
be an L-space knot [35], consequently K is fibered [32] and its Alexander
polynomial has very restrictive form [35].

4. Behavior of stable commutator length under Dehn fillings
Let K be a hyperbolic knot in S3. To find elements g with SK(g) = ∅
in the commutator subgroup [G(K), G(K)], the stable commutator length
plays a key role. In this section we prepare some useful results.
