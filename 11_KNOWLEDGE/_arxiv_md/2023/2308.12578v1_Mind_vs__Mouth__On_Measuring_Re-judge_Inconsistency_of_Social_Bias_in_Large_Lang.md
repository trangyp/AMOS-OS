Mind vs. Mouth: On Measuring Re-judge Inconsistency of Social Bias in
Large Language Models
Yachao Zhao1, Bo Wang1∗, Doming Zhao2, Kun Huang2, Yan Wang1,
Ruifang He1, Yuexian Hou1
1College of Intelligence and Computing, Tianjin University, Tianjin, China
2AI Lab, China Mobile Communication Group Tianjin Co., Ltd.

Abstract
Recent researches indicate that Pre-trained
Large Language Models (LLMs) possess cogni-
tive constructs similar to those observed in hu-
mans, prompting researchers to investigate the
cognitive aspects of LLMs. This paper focuses
on explicit and implicit social bias, a distinc-
tive two-level cognitive construct in psychol-
ogy. It posits that individuals’ explicit social
bias, which is their conscious expression of bias
in the statements, may differ from their implicit
social bias, which represents their unconscious
bias. We propose a two-stage approach and dis-
cover a parallel phenomenon in LLMs known
as "re-judge inconsistency" in social bias. In
the initial stage, the LLM is tasked with au-
tomatically completing statements, potentially
incorporating implicit social bias. However, in
the subsequent stage, the same LLM re-judges
the biased statement generated by itself but
contradicts it. We propose that this re-judge
inconsistency can be similar to the inconsis-
tency between human’s unaware implicit social
bias and their aware explicit social bias. Exper-
imental investigations on ChatGPT and GPT-4
concerning common gender biases examined
in psychology corroborate the highly stable na-
ture of the re-judge inconsistency. This finding
may suggest that diverse cognitive constructs
emerge as LLMs’ capabilities strengthen. Con-
sequently, leveraging psychological theories
can provide enhanced insights into the underly-
ing mechanisms governing the expressions of
explicit and implicit constructs in LLMs.

2022). Notably, these studies involve transferring
theories that explain the fundamental mechanisms
of human behaviors to LLMs. Despite the dis-
cernible distinctions in cognition between LLMs
and humans, this transfer of knowledge remains
immensely enlightening. It warrants further explo-
ration wherein human cognitive theories can be
suitably adapted and validated through empirical
experimentation in LLMs. Consequently, these the-
ories hold the potential to explicate the behavioral
mechanisms governing the functioning of LLMs.
In this paper, we explore the intricate relation-
ship between LLMs and the implicit cognitive the-
ories within the field of psychology. Notably, a se-
ries of works (Greenwald and Banaji, 1995; Green-
wald et al., 1998, 2020) provide significant insights
into implicit cognitive theories. These theories
posit that human social cognition comprises two
distinct levels: explicit and implicit. Explicit cogni-
tion refers to conscious cognitive processes that are
mainly obtained through self-report measurements,
which can be influenced by individual values, eth-
ical considerations, and societal norms. In con-
trast, implicit cognition operates at an unconscious
and automatic level, representing individuals’ gen-
uine cognitive tendencies. Implicit cognition is
mainly assessed through the Implicit Association
Test (IAT) introduced by (Greenwald et al., 1998).
The IAT measures reaction times to evaluate an
individual’s automatic associations between spe-
cific attributes (e.g., occupations) and targets (e.g.,
genders). It reveals human implicit social biases
that usually deviate from self-reported beliefs, par-
ticularly in sensitive social domains such as gender
and race.
To extend the assessment of implicit cognition
to pre-trained language models, Caliskan et al.
(2017) proposes the Word Embedding Association
Test (WEAT), which draws inspiration from the
IAT. The WEAT quantifies semantic associations
between target words and attribute words within
word embeddings. Building upon this framework,
May et al. (2019) introduces the Sentence Embed-
ding Association Test (SEAT), which further ex-
tends the measurement of bias to sentence encoders
through semantic bleaching. While these studies
have demonstrated the associations between hu-
man cognition and the cognition of language mod-
els, they have yet to differentiate the explicit and
implicit dimensions of cognitive constructs within
LLMs.
In order to explore the presence of explicit and
implicit levels of cognitive constructs within LLMs,
we design a two-stage experimental methodology.
This approach unveils a parallel phenomenon in
LLMs, termed as "re-judge inconsistency" in social
bias. The initial stage involves assigning the LLM
with the task of completing gender-related state-
ments, thereby potentially incorporating implicit
social bias. Subsequently, in the second stage, the
LLM re-judges the biased statements generated by
itself earlier but contradicts them. This observed
re-judge inconsistency is posited to reflect the in-
congruity between LLMs’ unconscious implicit
social bias and their conscious explicit social bias.
To facilitate this investigation, the analogy tests
introduced by (Bolukbasi et al., 2016) are extended
in the first stage. This extension adopts a more
open-ended template: "A are to X as B are to Y,"
where X and Y represent predefined attributes. The
LLM is then prompted to automatically complete
the values for A and B. Through this process, the
automatic bias of LLMs is measured, akin to the
Implicit Association Test (IAT) framework. Ad-
ditionally, the LLM engages in re-judging the bi-
ased statements, resembling the self-report mea-
surements employed in psychology. Figure 1 pro-
vides an illustrative example of the inconsisten-
cies observed between the automatic bias and re-
judgement bias in GPT-4, particularly concerning
specific targets (man vs. woman) and attributes
(secretary vs CEO).
Experiments are conducted on two prominent
language models, ChatGPT and GPT-4 (Ouyang
et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2023). The results demon-
strate significant inconsistencies across various
paired attributes.
This observed inconsistency
serves as preliminary evidence substantiating the
existence of explicit and implicit levels of cognitive
constructs in LLMs.
Our contributions are delineated as follows:
(1) This is the first known work to theoretically
associate LLMs with implicit and explicit social
cognition of psychology.
(2) We design an experimental methodology to
measure the automatic and re-judgement biases of
LLMs, drawing on the Implicit Association Test
(IAT) in psychology.
(3) Our experiments on typical LLMs and at-
tributes provide preliminary evidences that LLMs
possess different levels of cognitive constructs. We
believe that it will encourage future work in this

2
Re-judge Inconsistency Test of Social
Bias
2.1
Open-ended Analogy Templates
The IAT measures human automatic biases between
a pair of attributes and a pair of targets through reac-
tion time. We adapt the IAT into a language-based
fill-in-the-blank method. Specially, we present an
open-ended analogy template: "A are to X as B
are to Y", where A and B represent targets, and X
and Y signify attributes. The LLM is prompted to
automatically complete either X and Y given A and
B, or A and B given X and Y. Ideally, this flexible
template allows for the evaluation of any type of
bias by supplying relevant targets or attributes. As
an illustration, we use ’White American’ for X and
’Black American’ for Y, then let ChatGPT automat-
ically complete A and B. Although our prompts do
not contain any implicit suggestions of bias, Chat-
GPT shows strong racial bias. The results can be
found in appendix C.
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feed-
back(RLHF) have been used to align LLMs to
human values(Ouyang et al., 2022; Menick et al.,
2022). The LLM is likely to recognize that tem-
plates containing specific words are designed to
measure bias and align their responses to human
values. Consequently, we highly recommend sup-
plying non-descriptive attributes and allowing the
LLM to automatically generate target terms. In this
way, we reduces the likelihood of LLMs recogniz-
ing the bias measurement, a strategy similar to the
use of target and attribute exemplars in the IAT.
However, the bias measured by a single tem-
plate is not reliable((Seshadri et al., 2022; Goldfarb-
Tarrant et al., 2023)). To address this problem and
achieve more stable results, We create additional
templates using several approaches: swapping the
order of paired attributes, adding or removing the
punctuation, altering the verb tenses and replacing
words with different words. Following this process,
we obtain 10 templates, and the full list is available
in Appendix A. We will use these 10 templates to
measure biases associated with different attributes.
2.2
Automatic Gender Bias in Occupations
To avoid the LLM recognizing that templates
are measuring bias, we use paired occupations
as attributes from works of NLP and psychol-
ogy(Bolukbasi et al., 2016; Adachi, 2013; Clarke,
2020). Then, the LLM is asked to automatically
complete A and B in the templates.
Specially,
we focus on gender specific words(e.g., man and
woman) as gender bias is the most common form
of bias and humans typically show inconsistencies
between explicit bias and implicit bias regarding
gender. In this way, we evaluate automatic gender
bias in occupations of LLMs.
2.3
Re-judge Biased Statements
Next, the LLMs are prompted to re-judge if the
statements generated by themselves are right or
wrong.
This re-judgement is similar to the self-report
measurement in psychology, which directly mea-
sures human bias. If there is a contradiction be-
tween the model’s automatic completion and its
re-judgement, it indicates the re-judge inconsis-
tency.
3
Experimental Setup
3.1
Large Language Models
ChatGPT.
ChatGPT(Ouyang et al., 2022), a lan-
guage model developed by OpenAI, has made sig-
nificant advancements in various tasks. Addition-
ally, it aligns with human values and is able to
identify toxic and biased content.
GPT-4.
GPT-4(OpenAI, 2023) is an upgrade to
ChatGPT, capable of accepting multi-modal inputs.
OpenAI has employed various methods to improve
the safety of GPT-4. Furthermore, it better aligns
with human values and is proficient at rejecting
unacceptable prompts.
3.2
Implementation
We first measure automatic bias of LLMs. When
measuring automatic bias, not all outputs from
LLMs include gender words. Therefore, for each
attribute pair, we independently collect 10 state-
ments that include gender words per template. We
present the set of gender specific words generated
by ChatGPT and GPT-4 in Appendix B. This ap-
proach allows us to obtain 100 statements featuring
gender words for each attribute pair. Some of them
exhibit stereotypes(e.g., Men are to engineering
as women are to teaching), while others have anti-
stereotypes(e.g., Women are to engineering as men
are to teaching). We calculate the number of state-
ments with stereotypes. In this way, we obtain
the stereotype number of automatic of 100 gender-
based statements.

When measuring re-judgement bias, the LLM
is asked to re-judge all stereotypical statements it
generated in the first stage. if its response is right,
then it is considered stereotypical. If the response
is wrong and identifies the bias of the statement,
then it is considered unbiased. In this way, we
obtain the stereotype number of re-judgement.

As shown in Table 1, GPT-
4 exhibits strong automatic stereotype on more at-
tribute pairs than ChatGPT(7 vs. 3). Previous work
has shown that more powerful LLMs are less truth-
ful(Lin et al., 2022). Similarly, our experimental
results may suggest that more powerful LLMs ex-
hibit strong automatic implicit bias. Since they
exhibit almost no stereotype during re-judgment,
more powerful models may also be more inconsis-
tent.One possible reason is that while RLHF aligns
LLMs with human values, this alignment is superfi-
cial. Therefore, LLMs still exhibit strong automatic
bias.
The Explanation of Psychology
In psychology,
human explicit social bias is the result of evaluat-
ing implicit social cognition based on human val-
ues. Consequently, human beings may also exhibit
the inconsistency between explicit constructs and
implicit constructs, especially when faced with so-
cially sensitive subjects(e.g.,gender and race).
This process is similar to our re-judgement
framework. It may suggest that as LLMs become
more capable, different levels of cognitive con-
structs also emerge. Specially, The automatic bias
and re-judgement bias in LLMs correspond to hu-
man implicit and explicit bias respectively. The pro-
cess of RLHF aligning LLMs with human values
is also similar to human learning of social norms.
Therefore, LLMs do not exhibit explicit bias to-
wards gender, race, or other sensitive targets. How-
ever, the LLMs still have strong bias in their inner
cognition, which is implicit bias.

5
Conclusion
In this paper, we explore the cognitive constructs
of LLMs inspired by different levels of cognitive
constructs of human in psychology. We propose
a two-stage approach to investigate the re-judge
inconsistency of LLMs. This inconsistency demon-
strates a distinct reflexivity, where LLMs not only
express bias but also evaluate the bias. We ob-
serve this inconsistency across multiple attributes
in ChatGPT and GPT-4. It highlights the existence
of different levels of cognitive constructs in LLMs,
a significant discovery that necessitates further ex-
ploration. Our work invites more investigations
into the cognitive capabilities and biases of LLMs.
Limitations
There are some limitaions in our work. First, our
research investigate only a limited number of lan-
guage models, specifically ChatGPT and GPT-4.
Moreover, the limited number of accesses to LLMs
results in an insufficient quantity of bias types and
attributes in our research. Consequently, we will
further research more large models and a wider
variety of biases and attributes in the future work.
Finally, the conclusions in this paper are currently
limited to the biases of the models and attributes
involved in the experiments. We do not claim that
these results can necessarily be generalized.

Ethics Statement
Our work does not involve training data related to
privacy since we focus on biases of LLMs. The
outputs obtained by the LLMs also do not involve
user privacy. Although the social biases explored
in our work are linked to ethical considerations, our
study of bias aligns with human mainstream values.
Finally, The targets and attributes explored in this
paper are only for illustration purposes and do not
include any discrimination or bias.
