STATUS OF THE ADVANCED VIRGO GRAVITATIONAL-WAVE DETECTOR
CATHERINE NGUYEN on behalf of the Virgo Collaboration
Universit´e de Paris, CNRS, AstroParticule et Cosmologie, F-75013 Paris, France
On September 2015, a century after Einstein’s predictions of their existence, the ﬁrst Grav-
itational waves (GWs) direct detection was performed by LIGO. On August 17, 2017, the
two Advanced LIGO and the Advanced Virgo interferometers detected a GW produced by
two merging neutron stars. The subsequent localization of the source in the sky, thanks to
the presence of a third detector, led to the detection of the electromagnetic counterpart and
follow-up of the event by roughly 70 electromagnetic and neutrino telescopes. After the ﬁrst
two data taking runs (O1 and O2), the LIGO-Virgo network detected 11 GWs from 10 Binary
Black Holes (BBH) and one Binary Neutron Star (BNS). On April 1, 2019, Advanced Virgo
and Advanced LIGO started their third observing period (O3). After an introduction on GW
detection, I will give an overview on the Advanced Virgo detector design, with a description
of the technical choices made before O3 and their consequences on the detector sensitivity.
Finally, I will describe the planned upgrades for the Advanced Virgo+ (AdV+) project.
1
Introduction
In the last 50 years, GW detectors based on modiﬁed Michelson interferometers with kilo-
metric arm length have been developed, and are now forming an international network composed
of the two Advanced LIGO (aLIGO) detectors[1] (LIGO Hanford, in the state of Washington
and LIGO Livingston in the state of Louisiana) and the Advanced Virgo (AdV) detector (near
Pisa, in Italy)[2]. Moreover, the KAGRA[3] detector (at the Kamioka mine in Japan) had nearly
reached the BNS range a threshold of 1 Mpc[4] to join the recent science run (O3) but did not
have time before its suspension due to the COVID-19 crisis. A smaller detector, GEO 600, is
also operational in Germany[5].
On September 14, 2015, during the ﬁrst LIGO observational run (O1), the two LIGO de-
tectors achieved the ﬁrst GW direct detection [6], measuring General Relativity (GR) in the
unexplored regime of strong ﬁeld and relativistic speed. This observation demonstrates the exis-
tence of stellar mass black holes more massive than 25M⊙, and the possibility for binary systems
of these objects to merge within the Hubble time.
aThe BNS range is a standard ﬁgure of merit for the sensitivity of the interferometer, which is the averaged
distance at which it can detect a BNS merger composed of two 1.4 M⊙neutron stars, with a signal-to-noise ratio
of 8 for sources uniformally distributed over the sky with random inclination and polarization angles.

On August 14, 2017, during the second science run O2, the aLIGO-AdV network made their
ﬁrst triple detection of a BBH merger (GW170814)[7] and three days later, it observed GWs
emitted by a BNS merger (GW170717) in coincidence with a γ-ray burst (GRB 170817A) de-
tection by the Fermi Gamma Ray Burst Monitor and INTEGRAL[8], conﬁrming the hypothesis
that short-γ-ray bursts are linked to BNS mergers. Moreover, the delay on the arrival times
was used to test GR and put strong constraints on the GW speed [9]. In addition to the detec-
tion of GRB 170817A, the LIGO-Virgo localization of the source in the sky allowed a follow-up
by roughly 70 electromagnetic and neutrino observatories, identifying the host galaxy and a
kilonova[10]. This unprecedented joint GW and electromagnetic observation gave new insights
into astrophysics, dense matter under extreme conditions and tests of GR. Moreover, the joint
measurement of redshift by the host galaxy identiﬁcation and by the GW luminosity distance
enabled a novel measurement of the Hubble constant[8].
The two LIGO-Virgo observational runs (O1 and O2) resulted in the detection of GWs
from 10 BBH mergers and from 1 BNS merger, reported in the ﬁrst catalog of gravitational-
wave transient sources GWTC-1[11]. After detector upgrades of AdV and aLIGO, the third
observational run (O3) started on April, 1, 2019 with an average BNS range of 110 Mpc and
130 Mpc for aLIGO Hanford and aLIGO Livingston respectively. AdV started with a range of
about 45 Mpc and recently reached 60 Mpc. The O3 run was suspended on March 27, 2020, a
month before the oﬃcial closing time, due to sanitary reasons.

2
Advanced Virgo design
The initial Virgo detector was operational from 2007 until 2011 when it was upgraded to
AdV[13]. The goal was to increase the sensitivity by an order of magnitude, which would allow
an increase in detection rate of three orders of magnitude.
The detector is a power-recycled Michelson, whose simpliﬁed optical layout is depicted in
Figure 3, with two Fabry-Perot cavities in each arm. The resonant Fabry-Perot cavities have
a ﬁnesse of about 450 and enable an increase of the eﬀective optical length of roughly 300.


## Page 3


Moreover, a power recycling cavity about 12 m long, formed by a Power Recycling Mirror (PRM)
placed between the laser and the Beam-Splitter (BS), enhances the circulating optical power by
roughly 36.
The interferometer (ITF) is illuminated by a Nd:YAG laser source at 1064 nm which delivers
an input power of 26 W stabilized in frequency, power and position thanks to active ﬁltering
and a 144-meter suspended Mode-cleaner (MC) cavity with a ﬁnesse of 1000. The GW signal is
detected at the anti-symmetric port by DC detection, after ﬁltering by two output MC cavities
to remove both the spatial high-order modes and the RF frequencies that control the auxiliary
degrees of freedom of the detector.
Figure 4 shows the planned sensitivity of AdV with the main noise sources. In the low
frequency region (up to 40 Hz), the sensitivity is dominated by Newtonian noise, seismic noise
and suspension noise. The mid-frequency range is mainly limited by the suspension thermal
noise (the suspension thermal noise and the coating Brownian noise) and the high-frequency
region (above 300 Hz) is dominated by shot noise (whose mitigation is described in Section 4),
the high-frequency component of the quantum noise.

2.1
Seismic noise and seismic isolation
In order to ﬁlter the seismic noise (due to terrestrial seismic motion, wind and human
activity), the ITF Test Masses (TM) (4 mirrors of the Fabry-Perot cavities), PRM and the BS
are all suspended with a multi-pendulum chain, acting as horizontal ﬁlters above their resonance
frequency (about 0.5 Hz). The chain is suspended using a pre-isolator: an inverted pendulum
that guarantees isolation below 1 Hz and damping of the resonances of the chain [2]. The residual
seismic noise, after ﬁltering by this “super-attenuator” ssytem, is shown in the dark blue curve
of Figure 4.
In order to attenuate the consequences of the seismic noise (for example, scattered light),
all the sensitive optical components, for example the optical benches with the input-output
auxiliary optics (i.e. input and ouput MCs, mode-matching telescopes) are suspended using
special suspension systems, similar in concept to the super-attenuator[2].

2.2
Mirrors, monolithic suspensions and thermal noise
The thermal noise in AdV has two main sources: the suspension wires and the mirror coating.
The mirrors thermal noise (coating and substrate) can be divided into thermo-elastic eﬀects
(thermal ﬂuctuations coupling with a non-zero thermal expansion coeﬃcient of the material),
Brownian motion (kinetic energy of the mirrors atoms at temperature T), and thermo-refractive
ﬂuctuations (thermodynamical ﬂuctuations coupled with variation of material refractive index
with temperature)[2, 15]. The dominant source is from Brownian thermal noise of the coatings.
To mitigate this Brownian thermal noise, the multi-layer coatings are made with fused silica
(low index material) and Ti doped Ta2O5 (high index material) which has the lowest mechanical
losses[16].
The “monolithic suspension” is composed of 400 µm-diameter SiO2 ﬁbers. Fused silica, a
high strength material with low mechanical losses whose intrinsic dissipation is about three
orders of magnitude lower than steel[15], is tailored for the suspension of these heavy mirrors
(42 kg) in order to reduce the eﬀect of the suspension thermal noise.
3
Improvement of AdV between O2 and O3
Between O2 and O3, several improvements were made to increase the AdV sensitivity: the
injection of a squeezed vacuum reaching 2-3 dB as described in Section 4; the replacement of
the steel TM suspension wires with SiO4 ﬁbers, lowering the dissipation in the pendulum and
hence the suspension thermal noise; the installation of a 100 W laser ampliﬁer and increase
of the laser input power from 10 W to 26 W; the installation of additional baﬄes in several
locations to mitigate against scattered light; and the reﬁnement of global alignment control at
higher bandwidth than in O2[17]. All these improvements enabled an increase in BNS range
from almost 30 Mpc (end of O2) to 60 Mpc (reached on the beginning of February 2020).
4
Implementation of squeezing vacuum in AdV
Quantum Noise (QN) originates in the quantum nature of light: it is a consequence of the
Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle[18]. QN is composed of Shot Noise (SN), dominating above
100 Hz, and Radiation Pressure Noise (RPN), dominating below ∼100 Hz. As the SN equivalent
strain sensitivity is inversely proportional to laser input power, the natural solution to reduce
this source of noise is to increase the laser power. This has been done before and during O3,
and allowed an increase sensitivity at high frequency.
However, the power increase causes thermal eﬀects in the mirror and consequently thermal
aberrations due to power absorption.
In 1981, Caves proposed an alternative technique called Frequency-Independent Squeezing
(FIS) to decrease shot noise without increasing laser power. Since quantum noise is generated by
vacuum ﬂuctuations entering from the unused port of the BS, he[18] proposed a replacement of
the standard vacuum by squeezed vacuum states that have lower ﬂuctuations in the quadrature
aligned with the GW signal. These squeezed states will however increase the RPN, but in the
current detectors this is barely visible as the low-frequency part of the sensitivity is dominated
by technical noise sources.
Figure 5 represents the uncertainty area in the phasor (phase-amplitude) plane with a circle
for a coherent state and an ellipse for a squeezed state. Squeezed states of light and vacuum are
produced using nonlinear optical processes and generated in an external in-air optical bench,
built by the GEO600 group in Hannover and installed in Virgo in January 2018.
The FIS
technique is routinely used by AdV [13] and aLIGO[19] with a quantum enhancement between
100 Hz and 3.2 kHz of up to 3.2 ± 0.1 dB and around 3 dB, respectively. It is equivalent to
an increase of the input power by a factor 2. Figure 6 shows the strain sensitivity of the AdV

Main results on AdV performance during O3
Figure 7 – Cumulative events (O1 and O2) or candidates (O3) versus time for the three observing runs (credit:

The O3 run is divided into 2 periods called O3a and O3b. This two periods are separated by
a commissioning break during October 2019. As a result, before O3 suspension, aLIGO detectors
have an average BNS range of about 120 Mpc and 135 Mpc for Hanford[20] and Livingston[21]
respectively. For AdV, the BNS range reached 60 Mpc [22], which is more than two times the
BNS range at the end of O2. The chart in Figure 2 shows the diﬀerent status of AdV during
O3, illustrating that the duty cycle was nearly 76% for the cumulative period.
For the whole O3 run before its suspension (from April 1, 2019 to March 27, 2019), the network
duty operational state was as follows: the 3 detectors were online together 47.4% of the time, 2
detectors were online 36% of the time, only one detector was online 13.3% of the time and no
ITF was locked during 3.3% of the time.
From Figure 7, it can be deduced that roughly one GW candidate per week was identiﬁed
with 56 total candidates by April 1, 2020. The increase in detection rate demonstrates that the
upgrades made between O2 and O3 were clearly fruitful. A signiﬁcant GW signal was detected

6
Plan for Advanced Virgo+
The next upgrade of AdV is called AdV+ and is divided in two phases.
Phase 1 will
be implemented between O3 and O4 and will mainly concern changes related to quantum noise
reduction. Phase 2, scheduled between O4 and O5, will mainly focus on thermal noise reduction.
The target sensitivity for the improvements of phase 1 is a BNS range of the order of 100 Mpc
for coalescing BNS and above 200 Mpc for phase 2[14].
6.1
Phase 1
Figure 8 – AdV+ anticipated sensitivities for O4 and O5[24]. As a comparison, the sensitivity at the beginning
of O3 is shown in gray.
The main changes proposed for phase 1 involve a signal recycling mirror installation, laser
input power increase (from the current 26 W to 50 W), Frequency-Dependent Squeezing (FDS)
with a ﬁlter cavity and the Newtonian noise cancellation technique. During this phase, the
mirrors currently installed as well and the beam geometry in the ITF arms, will be kept, so that
the mirror thermal noise will remain at the current level.
Signal recycling is a method to optimize the response of the detectors to a GW by enlarging
their bandwidth [14]. The Signal Recycling Mirror (SRM), depicted in Figure 3 (framed in
dotted-green), will be placed between the BS and the interferometer output, improving the sen-
sitivity at high frequencies. This signal recycling cavity will enable the change of the shape of the
sensitivity curve by tuning its length and ﬁnesse, optimizing for diﬀerent astrophysical sources.
The whole conﬁguration is called dual-recycled Fabry-Perot Michelson interferometer[15].
Furthermore, a complex signal is needed to acquire the locking of the interferometer, involving
auxiliary lasers to lock the 3-km cavities[14].
As shown in Figure 8, the technical noises will be reduced, leading the RPN to dominate
the sensitivity of AdV+ at lower frequencies, and hence the FIS technique will be inadequate (it
increased the RPN, see Section 4). To address this problem, the squeezing ellipse should be ro-
tated in a frequency-dependent way. As a consequence, the FDS technique with the use of a very
long Fabry-Perot cavity of 285 m, referred to as ﬁlter cavity and planned for AdV+. As shown in
Figure 3, the vacuum produced by the squeezed light source will be injected into the ﬁlter cav-
ity where it experiences a diﬀerential phase change depending on the cavity parameters, which


## Page 7


leads to the rotation of the squeezing ellipse in the phase-amplitude quadrature plane[14] and
therefore a broadband reduction of the quantum noise. An experiment using a suspended 300 m
ﬁlter cavity, similar to the one planned for KAGRA, AdV and aLIGO, sucessfully demonstrated
this FDS technique[25].
The Newtonian noise in the detector will become a limiting noise between about 10 Hz
and 20 Hz. This frequency range is interesting from a scientiﬁc point of view, for example to
detect intermediate-mass black-hole binaries. Hence the testing of Newtonian noise cancellation
techniques with the deployment of an array of seismometers, which will be installed at each
end building and the central building where the dominant contributor to the seismic noise is
located[14, 26].
6.2
Phase 2
Phase 2 focuses on thermal noise reduction with an increase of mirror weight beam size. A
larger payload and increase of the laser power to 200W is also planned. R&D is also currently
underway by a collaboration of several laboratories to address losses by mirror coatings.
To cope with the increased impact of radiation pressure ﬂuctuations on TMs, the beam on
the End Mirrors (EMs) will be enlarged to a radius of 10 cm so the increased heat from the higher
cavity is spread over a large area and heavier mirrors (100 kg) reduce mirror motion due to the
radiation pressure. The two EM will have a diameter of 55 cm, representing an increase of 60 %.
The super-attenuator load capability will be increased by, for example, adjusting the handling
tool for larger mirrors and upgrades on seismic ﬁlter to cope with larger payload weight.
7
Summary
AdV contributed to the the O2 LIGO-Virgo data taking. In addition to the observation of
GWs from a total of seven BBHs merger, the ﬁrst BNS detection (GW170817) on August 17,
2017, with the observation of the electromagnetic counterpart associated to this event, marked
the beginning of the multi-messenger astronomy. Between O2 and O3, an increase of the AdV
sensitivity of around a factor two has been obtained thanks to several upgrades, including the
use of squeezed states of vacuum and the replacement of steel suspensions with fused silica ﬁbers.
As a result, the ITF remained stable with a duty cycle higher than 75% for the whole O3 run
until its suspension in March 2020 due to the global pandemic.
At the time of the writing, 56 GW candidates have been identiﬁed in O3. All alerts during
O3 were public, with the ﬁrst published analysis by the collaboration on the particular event of
GW190425. Thanks to the huge eﬀort of commissioning, noise hunting and detector characteri-
sation, the current BNS range ﬁnished at 60 Mpc for AdV. The AdV+ project plans to further
increase the AdV science reach, with a planned BNS range above 200 Mpc.
