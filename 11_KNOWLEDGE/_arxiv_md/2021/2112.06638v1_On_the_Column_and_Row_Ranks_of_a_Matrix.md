Abstract
Every m by n matrix A with rank r has exactly r independent rows and r independent
columns. The fact has become the most fundamental theorem in linear algebra such that
we may favor it in an unconscious way.
The sole aim of this paper is to give a self-
contained introduction to concepts and mathematical tools for the rank of a matrix in
order to seamlessly introduce how it works in applied linear algebra. However, we clearly
realize our inability to cover all the useful and interesting results concerning this topic and
given the paucity of scope to present this discussion, e.g., a proof via the injective linear
map. We refer the reader to literature in the ﬁeld of linear algebra for a more detailed
introduction to the related ﬁelds.
Keywords:
Matrix rank, Row rank and column rank, Gram-Schmidt Process, Gaussian
elimination, Matrix decomposition.
1. The Theorem
In normal conditions, what we talk about the rank of a matrix A ∈Rm×n refers to the
dimension of the column space of A. The rank of A is equal to the maximal number of
linearly independent columns of A. By the following theorem, this is also the maximal
number of linearly independent rows of A. The matrix A and its transpose A⊤have the
same rank. We say that A has full rank, if its rank is equal to min{m, n}. In another
word, this is true if and only if either all the columns of A are linearly independent, or all
the rows of A are linearly independent. Speciﬁcally, given a vector u ∈Rm and a vector
v ∈Rn, then the m × n matrix uv⊤is of rank 1. We here present the theorem rigorously
as follows:
Theorem 1 (Row Rank Equals to Column Rank) The dimension of the column
space of a matrix A ∈Rm×n is equal to the dimension of its row space, i.e., the row
rank and the column rank of a matrix A are equal.
This paper oﬀers three ways to prove the equivalence of the row and column ranks:
• An elementary way that constructs column basis from row basis;
• A Gram-Schmidt way that results in the UTV decomposition framework which in
turn gives insights on the theorem;
• A column-row decomposition way that arises from Gaussian elimination.

Warning on the proof
We might ask: since the UTV framework is an inexpansive
alternatives to the SVD, why do not we use the SVD to prove what we want? The answer
needs closer scrutiny. The existence of the SVD relies on the fact that the A⊤A and A
have same rank which in turn is based on the fundamental theorem of linear algebra that is
a byproduct of Theorem 1 as we have shown in last section. Therefore, this is problematic
as we use claim A to prove claim B where A relies on B as well.

• By “sandwiching”, the row rank of A is equal to the row rank of R which is r.

result follows.

In this sense, CR decomposition
and skeleton decomposition has unique form. However, if we select the last r independent

discuss this situation here as it is not the main interest of this text.

Corollary 10 If matrix A ∈Rm×n has rank r and matrix C contains r independent
columns and matrix R contains r independent rows, then the r×r “intersection” matrix
U of C and R is invertible.

R and C respectively, then, R can be denoted as R = A[I, :], C can be represented as
C = A[:, J], and U can be denoted as U = A[I, J].

ai = Cxi = A[:, J]xi for all i ∈{1, 2, . . . , n}. This implies the r entries of ai corresponding
to the I indices can be represented by the columns of U such that ai[I] = Uxi ∈Rr×1 for
all i ∈{1, 2, . . . , n}, i.e.,
ai = Cxi = A[:, J]xi ∈Rm
−→
ai[I] = A[I, J]xi = Uxi ∈Rr.
Since R contains r independent rows of A, the row rank and column rank of R are equal
to r. Combining the facts above, the r columns of R corresponding to indices J (i.e., the r
