The Extended Habitable Epoch of the Universe for Liquids Other than Water
Manasvi Lingam1, 2 and Abraham Loeb2
1Department of Aerospace, Physics and Space Sciences, Florida Institute of Technology, Melbourne FL 32901, USA
2Institute for Theory and Computation, Harvard University, Cambridge MA 02138, USA
ABSTRACT
At high redshifts, the temperature of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) was higher than
its value today. We explore the possibility that life may have arisen early because the higher CMB
temperature would have supplied the requisite energy for the existence of diﬀerent solvents on the
surfaces of objects. At redshifts of z ≲70, after the ﬁrst stars are predicted to have formed, a number
of molecules (but not water) might have existed in liquid form over intervals of ∼10 Myr to ∼100
Myr. We delineate the challenges and prospects for life in the high-redshift Universe, and assess the
various candidates for alternative biochemistries in this context – of the options considered herein, we
conclude that ethane is probably the most promising contender.
1. INTRODUCTION
The habitable zone (HZ) is deﬁned as the region
around a star where liquid water can exist on the sur-
face of a planet (Kasting et al. 1993).
This concept
has been similarly extended to encompass solvents other
than water (e.g., Ballesteros et al. 2019). It is, however,
essential to recognize that regions other than the HZ
may comprise objects with temperatures amenable to
the presence of solvents. Notable examples include icy
moons with subsurface oceans, worlds with deep bio-
spheres, and free-ﬂoating objects with thick atmospheres
(Schulze-Makuch & Irwin 2018; Lingam & Loeb 2021).
There is however, another possibility that merits ex-
plication. In the early Universe, the cosmic microwave
background (CMB) had a higher blackbody tempera-
ture (Loeb & Furlanetto 2013). At a redshift of z ∼100,
which corresponds to a Universe that was merely ∼16.6
Myr old, the CMB temperature was around 275 K.
Hence, if any planets existed at this time, they would
have possessed surface temperatures conducive to liquid
water due to the energy supplied by the CMB. In this
scenario described by Loeb (2014), the distance from a
star would be rendered irrelevant and virtually all plan-
ets would have clement temperatures.
However, the potential drawback is that the ﬁrst stars
are anticipated to have formed at redshifts of z ≲70
as per numerical models (Barkana 2016; Loeb 2020),1

although rare star formation at redshifts of z ∼100 is

By taking our

Universe may have been amenable to the existence of
non-water solvents on objects by virtue of the CMB.
The outline of this paper is as follows. In Sec. 2, we
outline the major alternatives to water as a solvent and
calculate the corresponding ages at which they could
have existed in liquid form. In Sec. 3, we delve adum-
brate the feasibility of life in the high-redshift Universe.
Finally, we summarize our ﬁndings and outline the most
promising candidates in Sec. 4.
2. SOLVENTS AND THEIR RESPECTIVE
HABITABILITY EPOCHS
In light of the preceding discussion, we will focus on
chemical compounds that are liquids at temperatures
conspicuously lower than the standard range of 273-373
K for liquid water at a pressure of 1 bar. The reason
that we select solvents with melting points of T < 273
K is because the CMB temperature TCMB obeys
TCMB ≈2.73 K (1 + z).
(1)
Hence, if the solvent has a melting point of T > 273
K, it would imply that the corresponding redshift to
yield this ambient CMB temperature is characterized by
z > 100. This poses diﬃculties because the ﬁrst stars
are expected to have formed only at redshifts of z ≲70
(Loeb & Furlanetto 2013; Loeb 2014; Barkana 2016).
There are a number of alternative solvents that have
been discussed in the literature with regards to “ex-
otic” life.
On the one hand, there are compelling
physicochemical grounds as to why water constitutes an

excellent solvent for the origin and sustenance of life
(Pohorille & Pratt 2012; Westall & Brack 2018); the in-
herent beneﬁts include, inter alia, its high heat capacity,
its large thermal range over which it remains liquid, its
capability to dissolve a diverse array of compounds, and
its anomalous thermal expansion. On the other hand,
there are no deﬁnitive reasons that appear to rule out
the possibility of solvents other than water.
Hence, we will tackle a few representative examples of
chemical species that are liquid at temperatures T < 273
K. Our list is not meant to be comprehensive; note
that in-depth analyses of this topic have been furnished
in Firsoﬀ(1963); Bains (2004); Benner et al. (2004);
Schulze-Makuch & Irwin (2018).
In fact, the abun-
dances of oceans composed of certain non-water solvents
(e.g., ethane, methane, and nitrogen) might be several
times higher than their water counterparts in the cur-
rent Universe, as elucidated in Ballesteros et al. (2019).
There are a couple of points that merit explication be-
fore proceeding further. First, we conservatively adopt
the melting (Tm) and boiling (Tb) temperatures at the
standard pressure (SP) of 1 bar. At higher pressures,
Tb could increase signiﬁcantly until the critical temper-
ature is reached, but this would correspond to a higher
redshift as seen from (1), which decreases the likelihood
for the existence of stars and planets. Second, we will
not consider mixtures comprising multiple solvents be-
cause the corresponding values of Tm and Tb ought to
overlap with the chemical species that make up the mix-
tures; in a similar vein, we shall not tackle solvents with
substantial concentrations of solutes (e.g., brines).
The most commonly studied alternative to water in
the context of carbon-based biochemistry is ammonia
(NH3). It has been theorized since at least the 1950s
and 1960s that ammonia may constitute the basis for an
alternative biochemistry (Haldane 1954; Firsoﬀ1963).
The replacement of the C=O bond in the carbonyl
group with the C=N bond could give rise to a plau-
sible metabolism (Benner et al. 2004). The synthesis of
peptide bonds from amino acids, which form the back-
bone of proteins, is rendered plausible with the release
of NH3 as the product (Firsoﬀ1963). At SP, the melting
and boiling points of ammonia are respectively given by
Tm ≈195.5 K and Tb ≈239.8 K.
Hydrogen sulﬁde (H2S) has been posited as an al-
ternative to water in the category of polar inorganic
solvents.
Although it has a lower dipole moment
and exists in liquid form over a narrower tempera-
ture range than water, it is known to dissolve a siz-
able number of organic compounds and the SH−anion
formed after dissociation is capable of playing a simi-
lar role to that of the hydroxyl ion in organic molecules
(Schulze-Makuch & Irwin 2018). Lastly, the abundance
of H2S seas is conceivably close to that of H2O seas in
the present day (Ballesteros et al. 2019, Figure 6). Hy-
drogen sulﬁde is characterized by Tm ≈187.7 K and
Tb ≈213 K at SP.
Next, we turn our attention to hydrocarbons. Despite
their non-polar nature, there is ample empirical evidence
suggesting that the organic reactivity of hydrocarbon
solvents is not much lesser than water (Benner et al.
2004).
Although we could tackle any of the alkanes,
we will focus on ethane (C2H6) for the following rea-
sons: (i) it is liquid over a broad temperature range, (ii)
it has a low boiling point, (iii) it has been detected reg-
ularly in the interstellar medium (Cockell 2020), (iv) it
represents one of the major components of the lakes and
seas of Titan (McKay 2016), and (v) ethane seas may
be more prevalent than water seas by an order of mag-
nitude in the current Universe (Ballesteros et al. 2019,
Figure 6).
Polarity-inverted membranes for putative cells have
been identiﬁed for hydrocarbons (Stevenson et al. 2015),
although some questions about their eﬃcacy have subse-
quently arisen (Sandstr¨om & Rahm 2020). It is plausi-
ble that polyethers could serve as genetic polymers in hy-
drocarbon solvents at least up to temperatures of 170 K
(McLendon et al. 2015), and other candidates have also
been explored from a theoretical standpoint (McKay
2016).
In the case of ethane, we specify Tm ≈90.4
K and Tb ≈184.2 K at SP. Although propane (C3H8) is
much less abundant than ethane in Solar system objects
like Tian (Vinatier et al. 2010) and laboratory experi-
ments simulating the physicochemical conditions of the
interstellar medium (Kaiser 2002), we will consider this
molecule for the sake of comparison; we adopt Tm ≈83.5
K and Tb ≈231 K at SP. Furthermore, propane has been
deployed in a few experiments probing alternative bio-
chemistries (Stevenson et al. 2015).
Methanol
(CH3OH),
a
polar
organic
com-
pound,
was highlighted as a potential solvent by
Schulze-Makuch & Irwin (2018).
It has a high dipole
moment and dielectric constants, remains in liquid form
over a considerable thermal range, and is possibly bet-
ter than water as a temperature moderator due to its
high heat of vaporization.
Moreover, it is relatively
common in the Universe, as evinced by observations of
this molecule in the interstellar medium (Cockell 2020).
At SP, methanol has the properties of Tm ≈175.6 K
and Tb ≈337.9 K.
Molecular nitrogen (N2) is an example of a non-
polar inorganic solvent that may be more plentiful than
water in its liquid form on planets and moons today
(Ballesteros et al. 2019, Figure 6). Although liquid N2
is not conducive to the emergence of life-as-we-know-it,
Bains (2004) proposed that silanols (the silicon analogs
of alcohols) could potentially dissolve in this solvent and
thereby engender the precursors of exotic life. At SP,
molecular nitrogen is liquid over the narrow thermal
range of Tm ≈63.2 K and Tb ≈77.4 K.
Supercritical ﬂuids are worthy of consideration as het-
erodox solvents. This is particularly true of supercrit-
ical carbon dioxide since it has desirable (bio)chemical
properties (e.g., stabilizing enzymes) and is documented

3. PROSPECTS FOR LIFE IN THE EARLY
UNIVERSE
We will brieﬂy evaluate the prospects for life in the
high-redshift Universe, with the proviso that this anal-
ysis is permeated by many uncertainties and unknowns.
Star and planet formation: As brieﬂy discussed in
Sec. 1, theoretical models favor z ≲70 for the origin
of ﬁrst stars, although it is possible that rare stars may
have collapsed directly from halos at redshifts of z ∼100
(Loeb 2014). Once the ﬁrst generation (Population III)
stars formed, they had typical lifetimes of a few Myr
(Bromm et al. 2001; Bromm 2013), after which they
exploded as core-collapse or pair-instability supernovae
(Loeb 2020), thereby seeding the Universe with ele-
ments heavier than lithium (Loeb & Furlanetto 2013;

minimum metallicity for planet formation? While the
formation of giant planets is favored at higher metal-
licity (Wang & Fischer 2015), a similar trend is not
apparent for terrestrial planets (Buchhave et al. 2014).
Theoretical models suggest that planet formation at
metallicities ≲10−4 Z⊙is not feasible (Johnson & Li
2013), but this question is far from resolved.
Bioessential elements: Although carbon, hydrogen,
nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, and sulfur (CHNOPS)
are the canonical bioessential elements, life on Earth
utilizes a very wide palette of elements in actuality,
especially metals (Maret 2016).
The precise abun-
dances of bioessential elements in the high-redshift Uni-
verse is weakly constrained, but it is predicted that
elements up to rubidium were synthesized by super-
novae entailing high-mass stars (Johnson 2019, Figure
1).
Thus, the availability of CHNOPS and certain
other crucial elements (e.g., iron) might not be an is-
sue. Models suggest that carbon-enhanced metal-poor
(CEMP) stars and planets were present in the early
Universe (Mashian & Loeb 2016), and that the forma-
tion of water could occur at metallicities of ∼10−3 Z⊙
(Bialy et al. 2015). There are, however, some drawbacks
that must be highlighted. Radioactive elements such as
uranium would be rare due to the scarcity of neutron
star mergers (which are responsible for their synthesis),
thereby diminishing the prospects for radiogenic heating
(Johnson 2019, Figure 1).5 Molybdenum, which plays
vital roles in biological functions (Hille 2002), is formed
from dying low-mass stars or neutron stars (Johnson
2019, Figure 1), implying that it may exist in very low
concentrations.
Energy sources and gradients: It is a well-known
fact that living systems require the access to environ-
mental gradients (Kleidon 2016). We will comment on
electromagnetic radiation (photosynthesis) separately,
but it bears noting that many other energy sources and
gradients have been identiﬁed. For instance, both sub-
aerial and submarine hydrothermal systems are char-
acterized by steep gradients in pH, redox chemistry,
and temperature (Russell & Martin 2004; St¨ueken et al.
2013; Lingam & Loeb 2021). More exotic founts of en-
ergy could also sustain life, such as magnetic ﬁelds,
radioactivity, and tidal forces (Schulze-Makuch & Irwin
2018, Chapter 5).
And last, but not least, the CMB
would have contributed signiﬁcantly to the energy bud-
get at high redshifts.

sources, at least some of them may have been accessible

Timescale for abiogenesis:
It must be noted at
the outset that we do not have enough data to ascer-
tain the timescale for the origin of life on other worlds
(Spiegel & Turner 2012). Even on Earth, we are con-
fronted with a paucity for data. The earliest deﬁnitive
biosignatures suggest that life was widespread on Earth
a few hundred Myr after the inception of habitable
conditions (Pearce et al. 2018). Phylogenetic methods
suggest that life originated very shortly after the Moon-
forming giant impact (Betts et al. 2018), perhaps O(10)
Myr after this event. Based on geological and chemi-
cal factors, Lazcano & Miller (1994) argued that ∼7
Myr could have suﬃced for the evolution of oxygenic
photoautotrophs (cf. Orgel 1998).
Taken collectively,
it is not inconceivable that abiogenesis could occur on
timescales of τL = 10 Myr. Hence, if ∆t ≳τL is valid,
the habitable epoch (embodied in ∆t) might persist long
enough for abiogenesis; from Table 1, we notice that this
criterion is fulﬁlled for most solvents. It is worth men-
tioning at this juncture that some worlds may possibly
even witness the advent of technological intelligence
in as little as 100 Myr (McKay 1996; Lingam & Loeb
2021).
Photosynthesis: Life on Earth is deeply reliant on the
access to low-entropy photons and the dissipation of en-
ergy as high-entropy photons (Kleidon 2016).6 The most
famous example is photosynthesis, whereby low-entropy
photons from the Sun are converted into chemical energy
and tapped for building biomass. The CMB is not well-
suited for photosynthesis since most of the radiation is at
mid-infrared (mid-IR) wavelengths and would therefore
6 It has been theorized that the CMB could serve as the low-
entropy source for planets orbiting black holes, which function
as the high-entropy sinks (Opatrn´y et al. 2017).
necessitate the evolution of sophisticated multi-photon
schemes based on several photosystems coupled in series
(Lingam & Loeb 2019). If an object is, however, orbit-
ing a host star, the latter will supply the low-entropy
photons necessary for photosynthesis.
In the habitable epoch of a given solvent, the chief
advantage is that any object around the host star – as
long as it is not situated too close – permits the exis-
tence of that solvent and potentially habitable condi-
tions for exotic life. This is beneﬁcial because the mini-
mum photon ﬂux required for photoautotrophs is merely
∼10−5 of the incident photosynthetically active radia-
tion (PAR) on Earth (Raven et al. 2000). Hence, the
maximum orbital radius (amax) at which photosynthesis
is theoretically feasible is roughly two orders of magni-
tude higher than the outer limit of the conventional HZ
(Lingam & Loeb 2019).
In fact, if life were to evolve
means of concentrating PAR, then amax may further in-
crease by orders of magnitude (Dyson 2003).
The question of whether photoautotrophy is capa-
ble of evolving over ∆t is obviously pertinent, and ties
in with the preceding point.
We note that genomic
evidence favors an ancient origin for photosynthesis,
indicating that it was prevalent by 3 Ga or earlier
(S´anchez-Baracaldo & Cardona 2020).
4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Motivated by the fact that the CMB energy density
was much higher in the early Universe, we analyzed the
epochs in which the CMB temperature was suﬃciently
high so as to permit the existence of appropriate solvents
on the surface of any object. We calculated the redshifts
(and ages) at which this epoch began and ended, as well
as its duration (∆t), for diﬀerent solvents.
Our ﬁnal
results are presented in Table 1.
Given, however, that this period of habitability occurs
in a high-redshift Universe, there are many challenges
that confront the origin and evolution of life. Hence, we

5
provided a brief analysis of the salient factors. The is-
sues that we tackled include planet formation, the access
to bioessential elements, energy sources and gradients,
and the time required for the origin of life. We suggested
that some of these obstacles might not be as problematic
as they seem at ﬁrst glimpse, although there are several
weakly constrained mechanisms at play.
On the basis of our analysis, one may ask which of the
solvents in Table 1 is optimal inasmuch as harboring life
in the early Universe is concerned. The solvent must
satisfy the following list of desiderata.
1. The epoch when the solvent can remain in liquid
form ought to commence at z ≲70 and ideally
extend to as low redshifts as possible because the
star formation rate and the number of terrestrial
worlds ought to increase.
2. A lower bound for ∆t might be ∼10 Myr, when
viewed from the perspective of abiogenesis, but it
should preferably be comparable to 100 Myr.
3. The chemical species should be readily synthesized
and available in suﬃcient cosmic abundance; for
instance, multiple cases of detection in the inter-
stellar medium would be indicative of the latter.
4. There must be adequate empirical and/or theoret-
ical grounds justifying why the solvent would be
capable of facilitating biochemical reactions.
As per Table 1 and the properties delineated in Sec. 2,
the three primary contenders are N2, ethane, and super-
critical H2. The latter is manifestly the most well-suited
insofar as criteria #1 and #2 are concerned, but there
has been very little research with regards to its potential
as a solvent (point #4). While N2 has the advantage
of being liquid at lower redshifts compared to ethane
and propane, this duo evince higher ∆t and there has
been much more research undertaken in the realm of hy-
drocarbon solvents. Among these two options, propane
is probably much less abundant than ethane in relative
terms. Hence, of the various options investigated herein,
ethane is probably the most promising contender.
In summary, we have shown that the early Universe
could have enabled the existence of diﬀerent solvents and
concomitant habitable conditions. Although the tem-
poral interval would have been fairly brief, abiogenesis
might have occurred on any object – irrespective of its
distance from a star – by virtue of the temperature of
the CMB. If life did originate in this period, it would
have taken place at a time when the energy density of
matter was much higher than that of dark energy (to
wit, when Ωm ≫ΩΛ). This inequality would have ma-
jor implications for the anthropic argument, which deals
among other things with why we ﬁnd ourselves in an era
distinguished by Ωm ∼ΩΛ (Loeb 2020).
