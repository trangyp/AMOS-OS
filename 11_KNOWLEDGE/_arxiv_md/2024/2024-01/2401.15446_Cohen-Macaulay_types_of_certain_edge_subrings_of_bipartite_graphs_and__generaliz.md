Cohen-Macaulay types of certain edge subrings of bipartite graphs
and (generalized) Fuss-Catalan numbers
Alin S¸tefan
Abstract
We give an example of two non isomorphic coordinate rings of a special kind of convex polyominoes
whose Cohen-Macaulay types are generalized Fuss-Catalan numbers. We further provide a determi-
nantal formula for these numbers.
Keywords: Combinatorial identities, equations of a cone, canonical module, toric rings
MSC 2010: 05A10, 05A19, 13A02, 13H10, 15A39

Introduction
Polyominoes are two-dimensional objects obtained by joining unitary squares edge by edge. They appear
in puzzles, designs, and tiling problems (see [5] for more details).
Let P be a polyomino and K be a ﬁeld. We denote by IP, the polyomino ideal attached to P, in
a suitable polynomial ring over K. The associated quotient ring is denoted by K[P]. In [8], when P
is convex, Qureshi proved that K[P] is a normal domain. She proved that IP deﬁnes the edge subring
R = K[xiyj | (i, j) ∈V (P)], where V (P) is the set of all corners belonging to cells of P. In this case,
R is Cohen-Macaulay by a celebrated theorem of Hochster. Danilov and Stanley described the canonical
module ωR of R via the polyhedral cone generated by the set of exponents {log(xiyj) | (i, j) ∈V (P)}.
The Cohen-Macaulay type of R is deﬁned by r(R) = µ(R) = dimK ωR ⊗K. The canonical module is
a fundamental object in commutative algebra that contains information about the last syzygy module of
K[P] (see [3], pp. 136-146). In [13] the authors show how to compute a generating set for the canonical
module of the edge ring of bipartite graph using combinatorial optimization tehniques in terms of the
incidence matrix of graph and the vertices of a certain blocking polyhedron, but an explicit formula for
the Cohen-Macaulay type is not given.

2.2
Polyominoes
First, we brieﬂy recall fundamental materials and basic terminologies on polyominoes and their binomial
ideals. For further information on algebra and combinatorics on polyominoes we refer the reader to [8].
Given a = (i, j) and b = (k, l) belonging to N2 we write a < b if i < k and j < l. When a < b, we deﬁne
an interval [a, b] of N2 to be [a, b] = {c ∈N2 : a ≤c ≤b} ⊂N2. For an interval [a, b], the diagonal corners
of [a, b] are a and b and the anti-diagonal corners of [a, b] are c = (i, l) and d = (k, j).
A cell of N2 with the lower left corner a ∈N2 is the interval C = [a, a + (1, 1)]. Its vertices are a,
a + (1, 1), a + (1, 0) and a + (0, 1). Its edges are {a, a + (1, 0)}, {a, a + (0, 1)}, {a + (1, 0), a + (1, 1)},
{a + (0, 1), a + (1, 1)}. Let V (C) denote the set of vertices of C and E(C) the set of edges of C.
