Advancing Conversational Psychotherapy: Integrating Privacy,
Dual-Memory, and Domain Expertise with Large Language Models
XiuYu Zhang*
University of California, Berkeley
Zening Luo
University of California, Berkeley
Abstract
Mental health has increasingly become a global
issue that reveals the limitations of traditional
conversational psychotherapy, constrained by
location, time, expense, and privacy concerns.
In response to these challenges, we introduce
SOULSPEAK, a Large Language Model (LLM)-
enabled chatbot designed to democratize ac-
cess to psychotherapy. SOULSPEAK improves
upon the capabilities of standard LLM-enabled
chatbots by incorporating a novel dual-memory
component that combines short-term and long-
term context via Retrieval Augmented Gener-
ation (RAG) to offer personalized responses
while ensuring the preservation of user privacy
and intimacy through a dedicated privacy mod-
ule. In addition, it leverages a counseling chat
dataset of therapist-client interactions and var-
ious prompting techniques to align the gener-
ated responses with psychotherapeutic methods.
We introduce two fine-tuned BERT models
to evaluate the system against existing LLMs
and human therapists: the Conversational Psy-
chotherapy Preference Model (CPPM) to sim-
ulate human preference among responses and
another to assess response relevance to user in-
put. CPPM is useful for training and evaluating
psychotherapy-focused language models inde-
pendent from SOULSPEAK, helping with the
constrained resources available for psychother-
apy. Furthermore, the effectiveness of the dual-
memory component and the robustness of the
privacy module are also examined. Our find-
ings highlight the potential and challenge of
enhancing mental health care by offering an
alternative that combines the expertise of tra-
ditional therapy with the advantages of LLMs,
providing a promising way to address the ac-
cessibility and personalization gap in current
mental health services.

Mental health illness has always been a global is-
sue (World Health Organization, 2022b). In an

it, highlighting the inaccessibility of mental health
care to the general public.

models (LMs) in the field of Natural Language Pro-

challenging until the emergence of Large Language

to our proposal of SOULSPEAK, an LLM-based

anonymization, and domain expertise that provides
effective, low-cost talk therapy to its users.
SOULSPEAK, as detailed in Section 3, has sev-
eral key advantages over existing online chat-
bots like ChatGPT. The first of which is its
memory module, which compartmentalizes im-
portant information from conversations into short-
term and long-term contexts. The memory mod-
ule enables SOULSPEAK to keep track of cru-
cial information from past conversations, such
as important individuals and events, over long
periods, greatly enhancing SOULSPEAK’s ability
to understand, connect, and empathize with its
users, a skill which is paramount in psychotherapy.
Secondly, SOULSPEAK integrates an extensive
dataset of anonymized conversations between pro-
fessional therapists (Counsel Chat dataset) (Bertag-
nolli, 2020) and their clients into its Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) process (Lewis
et al., 2020), allowing it to provide responses that
follow psychotherapeutic methods. Lastly, SOUL-
SPEAK has a robust privacy module that utilizes a
capable Named Entity Recognition (NER) model
to detect, anonymize, and restore Personal Identi-
fiable Information (PII), ensuring maximum user
privacy.
In addition, we introduce the Conversational Psy-
chotherapy Preference Model (CPPM) to simulate
human preference over responses to mental health-
related queries. CPPM simulates human preference
in responses to a therapeutic question with valida-
tion accuracy of over 97%. Together with other
metrics to evaluate individual responses, CPPM is
explained in Section 4. We also discuss the statisti-
cal difference between human-produced and LLM-
generated text in response to therapeutic questions,
which can be used for future development. Fur-
thermore, in Section 5, we test the effectiveness
of SOULSPEAK’s long-term memory module and
discuss the robustness of the privacy module. We
conclude with a discussion of the limitations of
this research and possible areas for future work in
Section 6.

Background
2.1
Generative language models
Language generation is an essential component of
Artificial Intelligence (AI) and at the core of NLP.
Alan Turing defined AI as a machine with the abil-
ity to exhibit intelligent behavior equivalent to, or
indistinguishable from, that of a human. In his fa-
mous Turing test, intelligent behavior is accessed
through a conversation where the AI generates text
in response to human input. Early works on NLP
approached language generation by manually set-
ting linguistic rules and then shifted focus to statis-
tical approaches. Statistical N-gram models (Suen,
1979) outperformed rule-based linguistic models
and provided the foundation for Machine Learning
(ML) based language models. Various data-driven
models emerged with the rapid development of ML
theories and computing devices, including the auto-
regressive models developed on N-gram models
to generate words one at a time based on previous
sequential input or surrounding context. Due to
the success of the Transformer model (Vaswani
et al., 2017), auto-regressive models dominated the
NLP research afterward. Benefiting from the rise
of deep learning and big data, large-scale genera-
tive language models such as Generative Pretrained
Transformer (GPT) (Radford et al.) and Bidirec-
tional Encoder Representations from Transform-
ers (BERT) (Devlin et al., 2019) were created and
implemented, exhibiting far superior performance
than previous language models. GPT by OpenAI
evolves over time with increased parameters from
117 million, 1.5 billion to 175 billion. The newest
GPT model, GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023), can per-
form various types of text generation with multiple
languages, showing the ability of in-context learn-
ing and chain of thought due to emergence.

psychotherapy over traditional psychotherapy, the

obstacle for some potential service users.

bots become more capable of interpreting, model-
ing, and generating natural languages, which can be
utilized for conversational psychotherapy (Denecke

Guo et al. (2024) in their survey, there was a huge
increase in conversational agents in 2022. This in-
crease was a collaborative result of the increase in
demand for psychotherapy due to COVID-19 and
the emergence of LLMs. A similar study conducted
by Haque and Rubya (2023) surveyed ten of the
most prominent chatbot applications in the mental
health space. They found that users generally val-
ued the chatbots for their ability to communicate
in a human-like manner and 24/7 availability; how-
ever, they also identified several key faults in popu-
lar chatbot technology. These included the usage of
an overly cheerful tone in inappropriate situations
and an overreliance on scripted responses. More-
over, the chatbots lacked the ability to reference
previous conversations in new responses, instead
relying on users to self-report mentioned people or
events repeatedly. Lastly, the chatbots in the survey
lacked the ability to recommend specific treatment
plans based on individual user needs, offering only
limited or short-term interventions.
3
Method
Figure 1 presents the architecture of SOULSPEAK,
our novel system designed to enhance the online
therapeutic experience.
The system comprises
three main modules: the privacy module, the mem-
ory module, and the knowledge base with therapist
domain expertise, all orchestrated around LLM
APIs. We break down the overview in the follow-
ing subsections.
3.1
Privacy module
The privacy module is a gateway between the users
and all the internal downstream processes in SOUL-
SPEAK. Its primary role is to ensure that no PIIs
are retained within the system’s memory module
nor transmitted to third-party APIs. We use a ro-
bust NER model adept at pinpointing PII, including
person names, locations, and date times within user
input. Upon detection, the identified PII is sub-
stituted with anonymized placeholders—randomly
generated values that preserve the semantic struc-
ture without revealing sensitive information. These
values are paired with their corresponding PII in
a secure mapping to restore the original data into
the LLM-generated content that references these

anonymized entities. This process is partly inspired
by the methodologies of Langchain (Langchain.ai,
2023). We further improve its implementation by
integrating the PII detection process with our long-
term memory module, which will be addressed in
the following sections.
We use the model "en_core_web_lg-3.7.1" from
the spaCy NLP library as the default model to per-
form name entity recognition on the user input,
which achieves a precision of 85.16% on the bench-
mark dataset available from spaCy’s model releases
(Explosion.ai, 2023).
3.2
Knowledge base
The knowledge base is designed to augment the
user query to the LLM with domain expertise from
certified therapists. To do so, we use the Coun-
sel Chat dataset (Bertagnolli, 2020), a high-quality
open-source dataset of counseling responses. The
data consists of user questions, responses from li-
censed therapists, and metadata, including views
and upvotes of the therapist’s response, all ex-
tracted from counselchat.com. A detailed intro-
duction of the Counsel Chat dataset can be found
in Appendix A. We use a PostgreSQL instance with
a vector extension to vectorize the questions into
embeddings. Then, the anonymized user query will
retrieve the most relevant question through a vector
similarity search. Among all the responses to the
question, the knowledge base will return the top-k
responses based on the preference score, as defined
in Section 4.1. In our experiments, we use k = 1.
3.3
Memory module
The memory module consists of both short-term
and long-term memory. The short-term memory
maintains the most recent n interactions between
the user and LLM in its conversation history. Dur-
ing the RAG process, the agent appends the history
to its prompt to provide the LLM with immediate
context, allowing the agent to maintain a coherent
and responsive dialogue flow. In our experiments,
we set the n = 10 by default.
The long-term memory persists in LLM-
generated summaries of various entities the user
mentioned across different therapy sessions. It’s re-
alized through Entity Store, which maps the entity
names and their associated summaries. In partic-
ular, we leverage the PII detection infrastructure
from the privacy module to insert entities into the
entity store automatically. We recognize that the
full context surrounding an entity typically spans
several interactions between the user and the LLM.
For example, when a user mentions a name during
therapy sessions, the relevant details and context
about that person are often scattered across multiple
interactions. To ensure accuracy and completeness,
we update the entity store periodically every n in-
teraction. This avoids updating the entity store too
frequently with the same information and prevents
potential overlaps between the entity summary and
short-term memory, i.e., the recent conversation
history.
When the Entity Store is updated, the existing
entity summary and recent conversation history
are sent to the LLM, which is instructed to update
the existing summary only if new information is
present; otherwise, it remains unchanged. When
a user query mentions an entity, the Entity Store
provides the most recent and relevant summary of
that entity.
Integrating the two memory systems ensures that
SOULSPEAK can make informed responses based
on the immediate context and the broader narrative
of the user’s experiences.
3.4
Retrieval augmented generation process
The final RAG process in SOULSPEAK inte-
grates all previously mentioned components - the
anonymized user query, retrieved therapist re-
sponse from the knowledge base, short-term mem-
ory, and long-term memory - using the selected
prompt template. Six different designs of prompts
are implemented, which are discussed in detail in
Appendix C.
Note that the inclusion of retrieved therapist re-
sponses and long-term memory is conditional: the
therapist response is incorporated only if the co-
sine similarity score between the user query and
the questions in the therapy dataset exceeds a cer-
tain threshold, α, and the long-term memory is
included only if the user’s query contains at least
one entity name present in our entity store. In our
experiments, we chose α = 0.2. We conducted
preliminary runs using values from 0.1 to 0.5, with
increments of 0.1. We analyzed the retrieved ther-
apy responses regarding relevance and variety and
identified 0.2 as the sweet spot.
This approach guarantees that the information
integrated into our final prompt is relevant and ben-
eficial for the agent to respond to the user’s needs.

Individual Response Analysis
This section examines SOULSPEAK’s performance
in generating a response to a question in isola-
tion, i.e., independent from the conversation con-
text. 113 questions selected from the Counsel Chat
dataset (Bertagnolli, 2020) are used as the test set
for evaluating the responses generated by SOUL-
SPEAK under different prompt designs. All the
chosen questions were answered by at least three
therapists with different numbers of views and up-
votes. By doing so, we wish to ensure that the test
set is representative of the emotional needs of ser-
vice users and focuses on popular questions that
demand more attention.
SOULSPEAK with different prompt designs, the
baseline (the vanilla approach where only the ques-
tion is provided to the LLM without additional
prompts or retrieval), and human responses from
the Counsel Chat dataset are compared with each
other. The LLM used in the experiment is Ope-
nAI’s GPT-3.5-turbo-0125.
4.1
Preference simulation
A major challenge in evaluating the responses to
mental health-related questions is to measure the
quality of response from the perspective of the
service users. Due to the limited data on conver-
sational psychotherapy and the fact that general
service users do not get the chance to consult mul-
tiple therapists while rating them, it is difficult to
provide a numeric metric for service user prefer-
ence. Inspired by marking human preference in
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022), we decide to train a
model to stimulate the human preference between
two responses, i.e., the Conversational Psychother-
apy Preference Model (CPPM) as shown in Fig-
ure 2. In addition to evaluation, CPPM can also
be used in RLHF to train LLMs for mental-health-
related applications.

mental health disorders (1330 samples), trauma and
coping (281 samples), interpersonal relationships

a validation accuracy of over 70%. Details about
this model can be found in Appendix B.2. In exper-

the embedding of the question and the responses to

the generated responses by SOULSPEAK are sta-
tistically similar regarding evaluation results un-
der these metrics. We conduct statistical tests to
verify the difference between the therapists’ re-
sponses and generated responses: Shapiro-Wilk
test (Shapiro and Wilk, 1965) to check normality,
Levene’s test (Levene, 1961) to check the similarity
of variance, and Welch’s t-test (Ahad and Yahaya,
2014) and Mann-Whitney U test (Nachar, 2007) to
check similarity.
Since the distributions of relevance and read-
ability are neither normally distributed nor have
equal variance, as indicated by the extremely low
p-values of the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test,
the Mann-Whitney U test is chosen instead of the
independent t-test or Welch’s t-test. For relevance,
the p-value (0.275) of the Mann-Whitney U test is
insignificant, suggesting no significant difference
between the two distributions regarding their cen-
tral tendencies. Thus, both human response and
SOULSPEAK generated responses are comparably
relevant to the question, although the response pro-
vided by a therapist seems to have a higher rele-
vance to the given question. For readability, the
extremely small p-value (3.24 × 10−39) strongly
supports the conclusion that there is a significant
difference, i.e., the human response is easier to
understand.
On the other hand, the distributions of polarity
and subjectivity are normally distributed but have
unequal variance; thus, Welch’s t-test is used. For
polarity, the p-value slightly above the 0.05 sig-
nificance level indicates no significant difference
between the two sets. However, an extremely small
p-value for subjectivity indicates a highly signifi-
cant difference, suggesting that the SOULSPEAK
generated responses are more subjective.
These characteristics are important for effective
communication with users needing emotional sup-
port. Therefore, one extension of the current work
may be to adapt the characteristics of human re-
sponses.

Limitations
This paper presents SOULSPEAK, an LLM-enabled
conversational agent that provides low-cost mental
health therapy. SOULSPEAK is novel in its preser-
vation of privacy, its psychological nuance when
answering questions, and most importantly, its abil-
ity to remember content from previous user inter-
actions and integrate it in context with a long-term
memory module. An extensive evaluation is con-
ducted with a dataset of chats between licensed
therapists and their service users, including finetun-
ing two BERT models for simulating preference
and encoding text response, to compare responses
from therapists and SOULSPEAK of different set-
tings. Experimental results prove the effectiveness
of SOULSPEAK as a possible alternative to tradi-
tional therapy sessions. In the future, we plan to
enrich our prompt designs and design prompts to
work better with different therapy topics. In addi-
tion, we wish to release SOULSPEAK to the public
and receive feedback to improve its performance
iteratively.
Our preliminary research on SOULSPEAK does
not necessarily reflect its long-term efficacy in
treating mental illnesses such as anxiety and de-
pression. Further research in controlled, clinical
settings is required before drawing definitive con-
clusions on the effectiveness of SOULSPEAK. In
addition, SOULSPEAK is not intended to replace
professional psychotherapy practitioners. Instead,
it aims to provide an alternative for people without
easy access to professionals or for those with non-
severe mental health issues. We hope this study
encourages the general LLM community to focus
more on psychotherapy-related questions and it-
erate on our system and model. Note that using
the current system and model as a sole source for
treating mental health issues may impose risk on
the service users. We would like to emphasize the
importance of seeking help from licensed profes-
sionals when needed.
SOULSPEAK’s performance relies heavily on
the quality of the dataset it was trained on. Due
to privacy concerns related to psychotherapy, re-
lated data is very limited. Nevertheless, with 433
licensed therapists answering 940 questions (total-
ing 2775 question-answer pairs), we believe the
Counsel Chat dataset provides a solid foundation
for our initial study.
Future work can involve
open-domain conversations. Inspired by works like
Mental-LLM (Xu et al., 2024), we plan to train
and evaluate the SOULSPEAK system on mental
health-related subreddit posts in future research.
In terms of the privacy module, we acknowledge
that anonymization might reduce the contextual
accuracy and coherence of the LLM output. For
instance, random dates and names may not align
with real-world contexts, potentially leading the
LLM to generate less relevant responses. However,
we believe that this feature is especially important
in the field of psychotherapy. Compared to other
LLMs with internal memory solutions like Chat-
GPT (OpenAI, 2024), SOULSPEAK ensures that
all PII is stored locally and will not be exposed to
third-party APIs.
Human evaluation would be valuable, but due
to the special conditions and privacy concerns as-
sociated with psychotherapy, we were unable to
conduct human evaluations on a meaningful scale.
Instead, we trained the CPPM to simulate the pro-
cess. We provided other numerical measures to
enrich the evaluation, and for metrics such as read-
ability and relevance, there were statistically signif-
icant differences between the answers provided by
therapists and LLMs. We hope these insights will
be useful for future studies considering psychother-
apy.
To further assess the robustness of SOULSPEAK,
we have also evaluated it using the more advanced
GPT-4o model, with results included in Appendix
E. GPT-4o was less preferred than GPT-3.5. Upon
reviewing the generated responses, we found that
GPT-4o tends to use more formal writing and of-
ten lists various points. It is also more likely to
refuse to give suggestions, citing that it is “not
qualified” to answer such questions. This suggests
that psychotherapy-related responses may not align
well with the instruction tuning objectives of GPT-
4o. Prompts need to be adjusted accordingly for
more advanced models.
In future work, we plan to extend our evalua-
tions to include open-source models like Llama
2 and Llama 3. SOULSPEAK is designed to be
model-agnostic, and we encourage the research
community to apply it to open-source models and
share their findings.

Prompt Design
In this section, we present six different prompt
templates that are used throughout the evaluation
of SOULSPEAK.
The simplistic prompt design, default, consists
of a simple persona of a therapist and few-shot
prompting enabled by the knowledge base.
The following two prompt designs (7feelings and
7feelings2tones) are built on default more concisely.
7feelings established the same role as default but
had guidelines for seven emotions. We specified
three action items and increased the generality of
the descriptions. 7feelings2tones has the same com-
ponents with an additional section to provide two
example transition questions. Our goal was to gen-
eralize the action items and improve the format of
the default to generalize better.
The next three prompts are inspired by thera-
pist guidelines. Gkp uses the naive generalized
knowledge prompting technique with five general
information input questions and knowledge with
answers. GkpPsychoTherapy utilizes the gener-

default. GkpPsychoTherapyNonRep utilizes the
generated knowledge prompting technique with

tional and other situations and the same established
role as default. We aim to see if the model could
effectively learn to be an empathetic therapist by
