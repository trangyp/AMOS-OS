Abstract
The integration of large language models (LLMs) into computing
education offers many potential benefits to student learning, and
several novel pedagogical approaches have been reported in the
literature. However LLMs also present challenges, one of the most
commonly cited being that of student over-reliance. This challenge
is compounded by the fact that LLMs are always available to provide
instant help and solutions to students, which can undermine their
ability to independently solve problems and diagnose and resolve
errors. Providing instructor oversight of LLM-generated content can
mitigate this problem, however it is often not practical in real-time
learning contexts. Online class discussion forums, which are widely
used in computing education, present an opportunity for exploring
instructor oversight because they operate asynchronously. Unlike
real-time interactions, the discussion forum format aligns with the
expectation that responses may take time, making oversight not
only feasible but also pedagogically appropriate. In this practitioner
paper, we present the design, deployment, and evaluation of a ‘bot’
module that is controlled by the instructor, and integrated into an
online discussion forum. The bot assists the instructor by generating
draft responses to student questions, which are reviewed, modified,
and approved before release. Key features include the ability to
leverage course materials, access archived discussions, and publish
responses anonymously to encourage open participation. We report
our experiences using this tool in a 12-week second-year software
engineering course on object-oriented programming. Instructor
feedback confirmed the tool successfully alleviated workload but
highlighted a need for improvement in handling complex, context-
dependent queries. We report the features that were viewed as most
beneficial, and suggest avenues for future exploration.

Introduction
The rapid rise and adoption of generative AI, particularly large
language models (LLMs), has started to transform computing educa-
tion [9]. A large body of literature exploring the capabilities of LLMs
has emerged, highlighting impressive performance for solving pro-
gramming problems [6, 12, 33], explaining code and errors [2, 24],
and even generating high-quality learning resources [16, 31]. How-
ever, the integration of LLMs into educational contexts comes with
challenges. A common concern among instructors is that students
may become overly reliant on LLMs, potentially diminishing their
critical thinking skills and independent learning [28]. For instance,
Prather et al. explored how novice programmers interact with both
GitHub Copilot and ChatGPT during problem solving [29]. They
found that while some students effectively leveraged the tools, oth-
ers who struggled experienced persistent and new metacognitive
challenges, including an illusion of competence, highlighting the
need for better scaffolding and support. Concerns around over-
reliance are compounded by the fact that LLMs are always available
and so it can be tempting for students to immediately seek a solu-
tion before thinking more deeply about the problem or exploring
other approaches to resolve their issues. In addition, LLMs are not
designed to provide educationally relevant responses and so may
reveal direct solutions to students even when that is not desired by
the course instructor.
In response, various approaches have been explored to leverage
these models in ways that align more closely with educational
objectives. One particular strategy has been the development of
digital teaching assistants, designed to provide real-time support
without revealing complete code solutions to students [17, 21, 25].
Early evidence suggests that this kind of guardrailed approach
is valued by instructors and students alike. For example, in their
exploration of the CodeAid tool, Kazemitabaar et al. found that
instructors valued the way the tool prompted students to think
critically rather than just offering direct answers [17]. In similar
work involving a digital teaching assistant for programming, Denny
et al. found that students expressed a preference for support that
guided them through problem solving steps rather than revealed
direct solutions [8]. Nevertheless, even with effective guardrails,
the immediacy of receiving feedback via digital teaching assistants
may encourage poor help seeking practices. In addition, it is not
possible to define guardrails that are perfect in all situations and
that consistently generate responses desirable to an instructor.

Including the instructor ‘in the loop’, and allowing them to drive
and oversee the process, may help to overcome these challenges [22].
A good example of this instructor-in-the-loop approach was re-
cently described by Wang et al. [38]. Their Tutor CoPilot system
supports tutors in real-time by providing expert-like guidance tai-
lored to students’ needs. They report the results of a randomised
controlled trial which demonstrated that students working with
tutors who had access to Tutor CoPilot significantly improved their
learning, especially when working with less-experienced tutors.
Class discussion forums are widely used in computing education
as an outlet for students to seek help [27, 36]. Given that responses
are typically generated by peers or the instructor, it is not usually an
expectation that responses on class forums are immediate. Neverthe-
less, discussion forums can place a burden on instructors—indeed,
more than 20 years ago Sheard et al. warned of the impact of their
recently adopted web-based discussion forums on the workloads
of the staff required to manage them [35]. Thus, it is interesting to
consider the use of LLMs as a way to reduce instructor workload
when responding to questions on class discussion forums.
In this paper, we describe the development and deployment
of an instructor-driven LLM-bot integrated into an online course
discussion forum. The system was designed to assist instructors
by generating draft responses to student questions, which could
then be reviewed, edited, and released. Key features of the system
include the ability to incorporate specific course materials into
responses, access archives from previous years, and publish answers
anonymously (i.e., not signalled as being generated by the LLM-bot)
to foster participation with students. We deployed this system in a
large, second-year software engineering course on object-oriented
programming. Through interviews with the course instructor, we
investigate how they feel it assisted them and we also explore
the specific features of the tool that they used most often. These
findings offer initial insight into the potential of LLM-based tools
for supporting instructors in fostering meaningful peer interactions
in online learning environments.
2
Related work
2.1
Discussion Forums and Chatbots
Online discussion forums have been enthusiastically adopted across
many disciplines, as they offer a convenient and asynchronous
space for students and instructors to interact, share knowledge, and
foster collaborative learning opportunities [1, 19]. In computing
education, where online discussion forums are commonly used,
there is evidence that meaningful interaction with forums can aid
learning. Research by Newby et al. found that students actively post-
ing questions, especially those whose posts were highly readable
(with respect to a readability metric) and received peer engage-
ment, performed better in a CS1 course than students who did not
post [26]. Similarly, Campbell et al. investigated success factors
in an online introductory programming course and discussed the
important role of online discussion forums for creating a sense of
community and support among students [3]. They found that high
engagement on the forums, marked by thousands of student and
instructor posts, was critical to maintaining a sense of belonging
and motivation, contributing to overall student satisfaction and
perceived connection to the course.
Although student engagement is clearly an important factor, it
does not always happen automatically and appropriate instructor
facilitation such as providing regular feedback is also important
for fostering productive discussions [11]. This presents a challenge,
as managing online forums can be time consuming for instruc-
tors, especially with large classes. Previous research highlights
the increased workload placed on instructors managing discus-
sion boards [35]. While students benefit from frequent instructor
feedback, balancing workload while maintaining engagement is an
ongoing challenge [39].
In recent years, AI-powered chatbots and discussion forums have
emerged as potential solutions to alleviate instructor workload by
automating responses to routine questions. Such chatbots can of-
fer timely feedback, provide students with hints instead of direct
answers, and foster engagement through conversational interac-
tion [19, 37]. For example, Liu et al. describe the integration of a suite
of AI tools, including an AI-powered chatbot called “CS50 Duck”,
within Harvard University’s Introduction to Computer Science
course [23]. This tool was designed with ‘pedagogical guardrails’ to
avoid simply providing solutions, instead guiding students toward
problem-solving approaches. In similar work, Zamfirescu-Pereira
et al. describe the deployment of a GPT-4-based AI assistant, “61A
Bot”, within a large introductory CS course at UC Berkeley [40].
This assistant was designed to provide students with immediate,
contextualised feedback on coding assignments directly within
their command-line autograder interface. The bot was designed to
offer students debugging hints and concept reinforcement without
providing solutions. Data from over 100,000 interactions demon-
strated a notable decrease in student posts on the course forum
and faster homework completion times. Although student feedback
was generally positive, highlighting the bot’s helpfulness, there
were occasional issues with vague or overly specific feedback. In
addition, the limitations discussed by the authors included reduced
visibility for instructors into student challenges. While it is feasi-
ble to design bots that often produce high-quality responses on
discussion forums, especially using techniques such as retrieval-
augmented generation (RAG) as described by Hicke et al. [15],
allowing instructor oversight can address challenges around occa-
sional inappropriate feedback and provide instructors with insight
into the kinds of issues that students are frequently encountering.
2.2
LLMs in Computing Education
Large language models (LLMs) have created new opportunities in
computing education, from the generation of learning resources [24,
32] to powering new kinds of pedagogical approaches [7, 10]. Much
recent work has begun to explore the integration of LLM-powered
tools into computing classrooms and has shown great promise. For
example, Jury et al. explored LLM-generated worked examples in an
introductory programming course, finding that students perceived
the step-by-step guidance as highly beneficial for understanding
complex concepts and reinforcing their learning [16]. Liffiton et al.
built a LLM-powered tool called CodeHelp to provide assistance
to programming students [20]. When stuck, students could enter
the programming language, code, error message and their issues.
The tool used a series of prompting strategies to generate helpful
output, which most students perceived as useful.

However, integrating LLMs into computing education contexts
is not without challenges. Prather et al. explored how novice pro-
grammers used generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT and GitHub
Copilot, when solving programming problems [29]. Through ob-
servations, as well as interviews and eye-tracking, they found that
students with higher skill levels effectively used AI to accelerate
their work, recognising and ignoring unhelpful suggestions. Con-
versely, students who struggled often faced persistent and even new
metacognitive challenges, such as over-relying on AI, experienc-
ing confusion, and developing an “illusion of competence” about
their problem-solving abilities. One way to avoid over-reliance is
to throttle or limit the extent to which students can seek help.
While LLMs can offer scalable support, their outputs may not
always align with instructional goals, requiring human oversight
to ensure educational relevance and appropriateness. Instructors
must ensure that LLM-generated responses complement rather
than replace meaningful instructor-student interactions [22]. For
example, Hellas et al. investigated the effectiveness of OpenAI’s
Codex and GPT-3.5 in responding to help requests from beginner
programming students [14]. Using data from an introductory pro-
gramming course, they examined how well these LLMs identified
and addressed issues in student code. Although GPT-3.5 identified
at least one correct issue in 90% of cases, it often missed multiple
issues and generated false positives, leading to potential confu-
sion. Additionally, LLMs frequently included model solutions even
when explicitly prompted not to, which could hinder learning. The
authors suggest that, while promising, LLMs should not replace
human assistance in educational settings, and this suggests that
– where possible – there may be value in allowing instructors to
review output before it is shown to students.
3
AIDA Design
Integrating AI in educational environments offers opportunities for
improving student learning in several ways. The AI Discussion As-
sistant (AIDA) system in this project served two roles. The primary
role was to draft responses to student queries to support instructors
and improve instructional efficiency and effectiveness. AIDA could
also post anonymously in the discussion forum, a feature that could
be leveraged by the instructor for pedagogical reasons. This was
intended to allow students to engage and share ideas freely with-
out knowing the responses were generated by an AI model. This
feature encouraged open participation and exploration of ideas. Ap-
proval from the University’s Human Participants Ethics Committee
was granted (reference #UAHPEC26871) to ensure transparency
about the bot’s presence in the course, without disclosing its iden-
tity to students. Students were informed about the existence of an
AI module integrated in the forum in order to promote informed
engagement, while maintaining a genuine learning environment.
3.1
Theoretical Foundations
The goal of AIDA to assist instructors in drafting responses is
grounded in cognitive load theory (CLT) [4]. CLT emphasises the
importance of optimising cognitive resources by reducing extrane-
ous load—effort spent on tasks that do not directly contribute to
meaningful learning or outcomes. By generating draft responses to
student queries, AIDA reduces the extraneous cognitive demands
placed on instructors, such as gathering information, organising
content, and drafting responses. This allows instructors to allocate
more of their cognitive resources to germane load—focusing their
expertise on refining and contextualising responses to maximise
their pedagogical value. Not only does this help reduce the instruc-
tor’s workload, but it also has the potential to improve the quality
and timeliness of responses, benefiting students directly.
The secondary role of AIDA is to act anonymously as a student
peer in the discussion forum, leveraging principles from peer learn-
ing [18] and social constructivism [30, 34]. By making the AI bot
indistinguishable from a fellow student, it is intended to foster a col-
laborative environment where students feel comfortable exploring
and sharing ideas. This approach addresses a common criticism by
students that AI bots lack the intelligence and emotions of human
interaction [5, 13].
3.2
Technical Workflow
AIDA is designed to deliver context-aware responses that are closely
tied to the course-specific content, using a technique often referred
to as retrieval augmented generation. Different from simpler solu-
tions that utilise LLMs “out of the box”, AIDA is provided access to a
rich repository of course content, previous questions, and answers
from past years. These are preprocessed into vector embeddings to
form a structured vector database for quick retrieval during use as
shown in Figure 1. When a student asks a question on the discus-
sion forum, AIDA converts this into an embedding and searches for
matches in the database to provide relevant context. This allows
responses to be drawn from a rich information base that is more
accurate and appropriate in the context of the course.
AIDA is not meant to be a fully automated bot. Its main goal is to
support—not replace—instructors in responding to student queries.
For this reason, AIDA drafts private responses for instructors to
review and refine as they see fit. By having access to relevant
context, this workflow improves instructor efficiency. Instructors
focus on refining the drafts rather than producing responses from
scratch, and having this built-in process eliminates the need for
external LLMs to draft responses that would require providing
course context manually. Furthermore, instructors might not always
be able to recall or access past relevant questions, particularly from
previous course offerings, to use as context for drafting responses.
3.3
Instructor Workflow and Prompts
Figure 1 illustrates AIDA’s overall high-level architecture, in terms
of its implementation workflow and core components. We now
provide a closer look at the workflow an instructor would follow
when using AIDA to draft responses. A key aspect of AIDA is that it
is designed to work collaboratively with instructors, rather than as a
standalone tool; although LLMs excel at incorporating explicit text-
based context supplied in prompts, they do not have the “unspoken”
context that uniquely resides with the instructor. Instructors bring
an invaluable layer of “hidden context” to educational interactions—
insights taken from verbal classroom discussions, individual student
needs, or other specific pedagogical goals that may not have been
explicitly outlined in any static course material. Such hidden context
often informs how an instructor might want to tailor their responses
to better resonate with students.

Interaction with AIDA is defined by a set of prompts that the
instructor types as comments in the discussion forum. When the
comment with an AIDA prompt is submitted, AIDA recognises
these prompts, hides them from students, and performs the nec-

crafted to offer transparency and control, and maximise the gen-
erated content’s alignment with the course’s specific context and
instructor expectations. When students post questions on the dis-
cussion forum, the instructor decides whether to directly respond
(without AIDA’s assistance) or to use AIDA to draft a response. If
the instructor chooses the latter, then they have the prompts shown
in Figure 2 available.
The #reply prompt is the only required prompt, as it is the
one that triggers AIDA to draft a response. All of the greyed-out
components shown in Figure 2 are optional, and the instructor can
choose to skip them if they wish. If they do so, the instructor is
effectively directly calling the LLM to generate a response based
on the student’s question alone. In addition to this bare-minimum
usage, the instructor can optionally provide additional instructions
specific to the question alongside the #reply prompt. This might
involve asking for specific levels of detail, incorporating particular
examples, or explaining with specific learning objectives in mind.
If the instructor wishes to incorporate additional context (in the
form of course material or previous discussions) into the response,
they will need to discover the most likely relevant contexts. The
#help prompt triggers AIDA to search the database of embeddings
for the most relevant contexts, which are then presented to the
instructor for inspection in two numbered lists (previous and related
context items) with each list consisting of the most relevant five
items in that category. AIDA does this by converting the student’s
question into an embedding and searching the database for the
highest-matching relevant contexts.
Should the instructor wish to incorporate any of those contexts,
they specify the relevant context item(s) (using their index in the
list) with the #prev and/or #related prompts (in conjunction with
the required #reply prompt). The #prev prompt is used to specify
specific context(s) referring to previous forum posts (including
previous years’ archived posts), and will include the post’s question
and answers. The #related prompt is used in a similar manner,
but to specify specific course material or assignment handouts.
AIDA then generates a draft response based on the provided
context and instructions, which the instructor can review and refine
before making it visible on the discussion forum. At the time of
this study, AIDA used OpenAI’s GPT-4 model to generate these
responses. Finally, if the instructor would like to post the response

4
Evaluation
4.1
Participants
To evaluate AIDA, both from the student and instructor perspec-
tive, we deployed it throughout the first six weeks of a 12-week
course (SOFTENG 281) on object-oriented programming (OOP) that
is compulsory for computer systems engineering, electrical and
electronic engineering, and software engineering students at the
University of Auckland. The programming language used in this
OOP course is Java. The course used Ed Discussion1 as the online
discussion forum platform, and AIDA was deployed on this plat-
form. As Ed Discussion was also used in the previous year for the
same course, the discussions from that year were anonymised and
made available to AIDA.
The participants were the instructor and students in the course.
The AIDA system was developed with the objective of both helping
instructors respond to student queries, and in turn help students
receive high-quality feedback when seeking help. When answer-
ing students’ questions, the system would return answers as the
instructor (default) or anonymously as a peer (when #anon was
used by the instructor). Approval for this evaluation was granted
by the University of Auckland Ethics Committee (Reference #UAH-
PEC26871).
4.2
Data collected
4.2.1
Forum posts. The data comprised all posts made in the Ed
Discussion forum in the first six weeks of the course. Our ethics
1https://edstem.org
application advised participants that they could withdraw from the
study by deleting their posts so that their responses would not be
included in the research. There were a total of 277 posts authored
by 105 unique students (of the 307 enrolled in the course), and 24
posts were deleted. Of the 253 non-deleted posts, the instructor used
AIDA to respond to 95 of them. These 95 questions were used in
our research evaluation. For these 95 posts, we collected data on the
frequency of each hashtag prompt used by the instructor, the extent
to which the instructor edited the AIDA-generated draft answers
(including both content removals and additions), how the instruc-
tor utilised AIDA when generating responses (specifically which
AIDA hashtags were used), and whether the instructor adopted and
revealed the AIDA-generated answers to students.
4.2.2
Instructor interview. To understand the instructor’s experi-
ence with the AIDA system, we conducted an end-of-course inter-
view focused on usability, response quality, and overall satisfaction.
Prior to the interview, the researcher analysed the usage of AIDA
to identify interesting usage decisions. These insights were then
used to prompt the instructor during the interview, asking why
they made certain decisions and how they felt about the system’s
performance. The instructor shared insights on the system’s ease
of use, the relevance and accuracy of responses, and suggestions
for improvement.

In drafting responses, the instructor utilised the #reply prompt
mostly on its own without feeling the need to provide additional
instructions or context ([#reply∅#anon] and [#reply∅]). At
first glance, this suggests that the instructor was comfortable with
AIDA generating responses based on the student’s query alone,
without needing to provide additional context or instructions. How-
ever, we will see in the instructor interview that the instructor was
not always satisfied with the responses generated by AIDA when
no additional instructions were specified. In fact, the instructor
sometimes used the [#reply∅#anon] combination as a hack to
define an anonymous comment on the discussion forum that they
would then go on to edit quite substantially before publishing.
The instructor still extensively used the #reply prompt while
specifying additional instructions, but without using past contexts
([#reply■#anon] and [#reply■]). This combination collectively
accounted for 24% of the prompt interactions. This suggests that
the instructor often had a specific idea of what they wanted the
response to contain, and did not feel the need to provide additional
context when drafting the response. From a practical perspective,
it is likely that the instructor saw this as a quick and efficient way
to create responses that aligned with their expectations, allowing
them to easily make any needed adjustments later.
When considering the drafting of responses that incorporated
course-specific contexts, the #related prompt tended to be used
more often than the #prev prompt (11% vs. 6%). Using both of them
together was relatively rare (2%). This suggests that the instructor
found more value in incorporating related materials than in incor-
porating previous posts when drafting responses. Alternatively, it
could also suggest that the nature of the questions tended to be
more specific to the course materials (which included assessment
handouts), than generic questions that were likely to have been
asked before in previous iterations of the course.
The diverse usage of prompt combinations, despite some being
more frequently used than others, highlights an important insight:
the effectiveness of LLM integrations in a discussion forum does
not hinge on a single “best” method. Instead, it appears valuable to
grant instructors with flexibility that empowers them to tailor their
engagement based on the nature of each question. As evidenced in
the subsequent instructor interview, it becomes clear that response
drafting often involved an iterative approach, sometimes experi-
menting with different strategies to uncover the best generated
response for each unique question.

Edits to Generated Answers
When AIDA drafts a response, the instructor has the option to edit
the response before making it visible on the discussion forum. We
used a text differencing tool2 to calculate the number of edits made
by the instructor for each adopted generated answer. The stacked
bar chart in Figure 3 shows the number of edits for each adopted
generated answer. Red bars indicate the number of removals from
the original answer and green bars indicate the number of additions
to the original answer. There are also some answers that were not
edited, which means they were adopted by the instructor without
any modifications.

Most of the edited answers involved removing content from
AIDA’s drafted answer, with additions being less common. The
high proportion of removals for most posts may indicate that the
answers the LLM generated contained redundant content, content
the instructor felt was inappropriate, or overly detailed. With fewer

removals combined), with very few requiring a large number of
edits. The instructor interview sheds further light on this.
5.3
Example Questions
In this section, we illustrate several questions from the course and
show how the instructor used the prompts in different ways. Fig-
ure 4 shows an error the student encountered while setting up
Git. The instructor decided this query could be easily answered
without the need for course-specific context. After noticing partic-
ular misconceptions, the instructor crafted additional instructions
for the #reply prompt to guide the LLM’s output. The instructor
opted to use the #anon prompt to give the impression that a helpful
anonymous peer had answered, hoping that this would encourage
a sense of community among the students to support each other.
The next example in Figure 5 is a question related to the timing
of the release of a lecture recording. The instructor suspected that
this question has been asked before, so sought to find the relevant
context using the #help prompt. This reveals identifiers for the
most-likely related context, which the instructor then used to gen-
erate a response using the #prev hashtag. The generated answer
was concise and to the point, providing the information the student
needed. The instructor felt that no edits were necessary.
The final example in Figure 6 is a question related to one of the
course assignments. After using the #help prompt, the instructor
found similar contexts and used the #related prompt to generate a
response. The LLM generated a lengthy draft answer that included
a code example. However, given that the question was only seeking
clarification for a detail of the assignment requirements, this code
was not necessary and was deleted by the instructor.

When were generated responses most useful? The instructor
was asked about the relevance of the generated responses provided
by AIDA, to which they discussed the following scenarios when
the responses were most useful.
General questions: For questions that the LLM likely already
knew the answer to without additional context—typically tech-
nical matters relating to Java, compilation errors, and software
development—AIDA was extremely helpful in providing explana-
tions and solutions. Often the instructor would immediately use
the #reply prompt without searching for course-related context.
Although they often attempted this without giving additional in-
structions (i.e., #reply∅), the instructor would usually resort to
deleting the generated answer and re-attempt it by providing more
specific guidance to the model (i.e., #reply■).
Despite the AI model being capable of understanding an under-
lying question, the instructor often had particular ideas on how
they wanted answers to be presented. They appreciated that the
model could handle such generic questions without extra steps
of narrowing down useful context, but still valued the ability to
provide additional instructions on a per-question basis as they of-
ten changed from question to question. With that being said, the
generated answers were generally perceived by the instructor as
high-quality and did not require many modifications.
Assignment specification questions: Most of the questions
on the discussion forum were related to the course assignments,
and the instructor found that AIDA was very helpful in generating
answers to these questions. Despite the students being provided
with detailed handouts and instructions, they often asked questions
that were already answered in the handouts. The instructor saw
particular value in AIDA when students asked questions for which
the instructor knew the answer was contained in their published
resources, but could not immediately recall exactly where. Even
though the instructor developed the assignment resources, there
were some situations where they could not recall specific details.
Since AIDA was able to efficiently locate relevant information from
the handouts, the instructor found generating answers to these
kinds of questions saved them a lot of time.
Questions with little background provided: Sometimes stu-
dents asked questions without providing sufficient background or
information that would allow the instructor to understand exactly
what the issue might be. Instead of requesting the student to pro-
vide more details, the instructor found that using AIDA to test the
model’s ability to generate a response was sometimes successful.
Often the model could identify and propose solutions even when
students supplied minimal and vague information. This was particu-
larly useful to the instructor as it saved time and effort in requesting
more information from the student.
5.4.2
Reasons for large edits to generated answers. The instructor
was asked about the reasons as to why they edited some generated
answers, especially when the content from some of the generated
answers was nearly entirely deleted.
Insufficient quality: The instructor mentioned that the main
reason for editing the generated answers was due to the quality
of the responses. This is not limited to the correctness of the an-
swer, but also the clarity and conciseness of the response. When
re-attempting with further instructions still did not yield a satis-
factory response, the instructor would often delete the bulk of the
generated answer and write their own response. Fortunately, this
was not a common occurrence.
Simulating student responses: When students interact anony-
mously on Ed Discussion, they are coded with a unique identifier
that persists with their identity for that post (e.g., “Anonymous
Kangaroo” or “Anonymous Goose”). This allows students to refer
to each other in a way that maintains their anonymity, but also
allows them to distinguish between multiple fellow anonymous
peers within the same post. Since AIDA would often answer ques-
tions anonymously, their peers would reply to the bot as if it were
a student and ask follow-up questions. There were times when the
instructor did not need to utilise an LLM in the follow-up responses,
but they needed to respond under the same anonymous identifier.
In these cases, the instructor would action [#reply∅#anon] to
generate the response entry, and then edit the content—often being
a brief sentence to close the loop on the asking student’s follow-up.

Overall satisfaction and insights. Overall, the instructor found
using AIDA to be a positive experience; the generated answers were
generally of high quality, and the instructor was satisfied with the
responses provided by the model. The instructor found that using
AIDA could increase productivity, especially when the generated
answers were of high quality and required minimal editing. Below
they provide a few scenarios on AIDA’s convenience.
Navigating context-driven responses: The instructor found
that when there were questions for which it was possible to find
related contexts easily, high-quality answers could be generated by
the LLM by incorporating these related contexts. However, when
students asked general questions like “I don’t understand how inter-
faces work”—which require code examples and explanations—it was
very helpful and convenient to use AIDA to generate those. The
instructor also found that when they were asked a question that
they themselves did not know the answer to, instead of manually
performing an online search and reading through a range of posts,
they would use AIDA to give explanations to the question, as a
replacement for doing an online search.
Several scenarios were identified where it was inconvenient for
the instructor to respond using AIDA. When responses required
a very specific context, determining which context to provide the
model was cumbersome and sometimes led to trivial errors. The
way that the most-likely contexts were displayed as lists when
using the #help prompt needed improvement to make it easier for
the instructor to find the required information. Additionally, if the
instructor intended to supply a brief answer, they sometimes found
it quicker to write and submit directly rather than using AIDA.
Ease of use of the prompts: In general, the instructor felt
that the hashtags used in the prompts were intuitive and simple
enough to memorise and use. The manner in which the hashtags
and prompts could be entered into the regular comment box made
it easy to use, and the instructor did not have any issues with the
syntax of the prompts. This was especially the case as the #help
prompt would also provide a cheat sheet of the available prompts
for the context results it returned.

Contributions and Discussion
In this practitioner paper, we described our experiences using AIDA,
an instructor-driven tool that supports the generation of draft re-
sponses to student questions on online discussion forums. AIDA
worked well overall; most instructor edits to generated answers
were minor, with deletions being the primary adjustment, indicat-
ing that the majority of AIDA-generated responses contained the
necessary content. This result highlights the potential of instructor-
in-the-loop AI systems for providing valuable, contextually accurate
support to students while maintaining instructional oversight.
A key advantage of AIDA is its instructor-in-the-loop design,
which is supported by recent findings in educational AI tools such as
Tutor CoPilot [38]. Recent work using Tutor CoPilot demonstrated
how Human-AI collaborative systems can support less experienced
tutors by providing expert-like guidance, such as prompting stu-
dent thinking rather than simply providing answers. In a controlled
trial with over 900 tutors, students whose tutors used Tutor CoPilot
were more likely to master topics, with improvements especially
pronounced for students taught by lower-rated tutors. This under-
scores the effectiveness of tools that integrate teacher oversight
rather than relying solely on automated guardrails, which can fail
to adapt to real-time needs in educational settings. A system like
AIDA provides instructors the flexibility to approve or refine re-
sponses, to ensure alignment with course content and educational
objectives.
Instructor feedback suggested that AIDA performed well in
addressing general technical questions and specific assignment-
related queries, and therefore achieved one of our goals which was
to reduce the instructional burden on teaching staff which is a
known concern related to moderating discussion forums.
Future Work
To further improve AIDA’s capabilities and the experience for in-
structors, we suggest several avenues that could be explored for
future development:
• Expanding AIDA to handle multimodal inputs, such as im-
ages and screenshots, could address limitations in handling
questions about visual debugging errors. Allowing AIDA
to process and interpret visual information alongside text
would help to improve response accuracy in cases that cur-
rently require manual intervention.
• In our current implementation, AIDA relies on manual input
from instructors to select and provide relevant contextual
information for each response. This could be automated, so
that AIDA dynamically selects and applies appropriate con-
text as needed, improving efficiency and response accuracy
without requiring as much instructor input.
• Information about the student asking the question was not
provided to AIDA in our current prototype, however tailor-
ing responses based on the learning needs and background
of the student posting the question could create a more per-
sonalised and effective experience. Adaptive responses could
consider students’ interaction histories or other information
about their relevant knowledge.
• We would like to implement more robust metrics for assess-
ing the impact of AIDA on student engagement, including a
comparison of engagement levels before and after AIDA’s
introduction. Metrics could include interaction frequencies
and qualitative measures such as student feedback.
• We provided AIDA with access to a rich repository of course
content, including previous questions and answers. However,
these were all specific to our particular course. Providing the
option to upload different resources would make it possible
for AIDA to be easily customised for other courses.
7
Conclusions
In this work, we designed an AI bot that was integrated into the
online discussion forum for a software engineering course. Our
goal was to support the instructor by generating draft responses
for their review, which could be guided by relevant contextual in-
formation including course resources and posts and responses from
previous iterations of the course. We believe instructor-in-the-loop
systems like AIDA, which allow teachers to have oversight into the
AI-generated content that students see, represent a promising direc-
tion for the application of AI in education. This design has shown
that AI can effectively alleviate instructor workload by providing
responses that often require minimal adjustments. The positive
reception from both students and instructors underscores the value
of human-centric AI systems in education. Future work should
explore multimodal capabilities, automated context selection, and
personalised adaptive responses.
