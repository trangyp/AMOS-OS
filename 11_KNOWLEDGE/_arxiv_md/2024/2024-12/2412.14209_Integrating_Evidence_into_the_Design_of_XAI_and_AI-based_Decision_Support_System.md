Integrating Evidence into the Design of Explainable Artificial 
Intelligence and Decision Support Systems: A Means-End Framework 
for End-users in Construction 
 
Abstract: Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) aims to make the reasoning processes of 
AI models transparent and interpretable, especially in complex decision-making environments. 
In construction, where AI-based decision support systems (DSSs) are increasingly deployed, 
limited attention has been given to incorporating evidence to support the reliability and 
accountability of their outputs. Without embedding evidence into the design of XAI and DSSs, 
the validity of explanations and the trustworthiness of system recommendations remain open 
to question. To address this challenge, this paper presents a theoretical, evidence-based means–
end framework developed through a narrative review. The framework provides an epistemic 
foundation for designing XAI-enabled DSSs that can produce meaningful human explanations 
(MHEs) aligned with end-users’ knowledge needs and decision-making contexts. It 
emphasizes evaluating the strength, value, and utility of different types of evidence used to 
support explanations generated by AI systems. While the framework is designed with a focus 
on construction professionals as primary end-users, its relevance extends to other stakeholders, 
such as developers, regulators, and project managers, whose epistemic goals may differ. By 
explicitly integrating evidence into the development of XAI and DSSs, the framework 
enhances transparency, fosters trust, and improves the effectiveness of decision-making. Given 
its theoretical foundation and broad applicability, the framework can inform the design of 
evidence-aware XAI systems across various industrial and engineering domains. 
 
Keywords: Construction, decision support system, evidence, machine learning, explainable 
artificial intelligence

1. 
Introduction 
Artificial intelligence (AI) is having a profound influence on all aspects of work, and with 
advances being made at an accelerated rate, a myriad of opportunities (e.g., task automation, 
predictive analysis, and real-time decision-making) and challenges (e.g., job displacement, data 
bias, and algorithmic fairness and data privacy and security) manifest [1]. Indeed, AI is a 
transformative technology that is reshaping the workplaces of various industrial sectors, 
including finance, healthcare, manufacturing, and transportation [1-3]. Still, within 
construction, the context for our paper, the rate of AI adoption has been cautious despite its 
potential benefits and widespread uptake in other sectors [1], [2]. A key barrier to AI adoption 
in construction is the limited understanding of how AI systems reach their conclusions, which 
fuels mistrust and resistance among practitioners. Construction projects are characterised by 
high uncertainty, fragmented teams, and context-specific decisions, making transparency and 
accountability crucial for effective decision-making.  
 
The hesitancy in construction organizations’ adoption of AI is not rooted in reluctance, but 
rather in a lack of understanding about what benefits AI can offer, why they matter, and how 
and when these can be realized without disrupting ongoing operations [2]. While it is also 
acknowledged that AI can improve decision-making by quickly processing large amounts of 
information, some construction organizations’ managers are mindful of the risk of automation 
bias [2], [3], [4]. In such instances, people may over-rely on automated systems and 
technologies, prematurely accepting AI-generated recommendations without adequate 
scrutiny, often leading to errors or suboptimal outcomes [3], [5]. This bias occurs when users 
place excessive trust in automated tools, such as AI algorithms and decision support systems1

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) offers a means to mitigate these concerns by making 
AI decisions more transparent, interpretable, and trustworthy. In doing so, XAI can support 
critical engagement with AI-generated outputs, reduce undue dependence on automation, and 
foster more responsible, context-aware decision-making. Accordingly, this paper focuses on 
the potential of XAI to facilitate the effective and accountable deployment of AI-driven DSSs 
in construction, ultimately enhancing the effectiveness of human–machine collaboration 
throughout all phases of a project’s life cycle.  
 
Many AI-based DSSs developed for construction, whether bespoke or off-the-shelf, operate as 
‘black boxes’, producing recommendations or insights without offering explanations of their 
internal logic or decision-making processes [6], [7]. This opacity is particularly problematic 
with generative AI models (e.g., Generative Adversarial Networks, Variational Autoencoders, 
Reinforcement Learning, and Transformer-based models), which are increasingly used to 
underpin DSSs by generating data-driven content and scenarios for a range of tasks such as 
scheduling, cost estimation, identification of safety risks, and assessing quality [6–13]. Without 
explainability, users may lack the confidence or context needed to critically evaluate these 
outputs. XAI, therefore, plays a crucial role by making AI system behavior intelligible through 
explanations, allowing users to understand, scrutinise, and ultimately trust the decisions 
generated by AI-based DSSs [6]. Thus, calibrating user trust to the true capabilities of AI 
through transparent and context-aware explanations is essential to prevent over-reliance or 
undue skepticism.

An in-depth review of XAI in construction, specifically examining its core precepts (i.e., 
explainability and interpretability), its relationship to different types of AI models (e.g., 
transparent and opaque), and levels of post-hoc explainability (e.g., model-agnostic and model-
specific), is provided by Love et al. [6]. However, this review adopts a predominantly 
technological and application perspective, offering limited discussion of user-centred design 
practices grounded in empirical ‘evidence’ and systematic evaluation techniques. These 
aspects are increasingly recognised as critical to the development of effective and trustworthy 
decision support systems [2], [11–13]. Similar gaps are evident in several XAI reviews across 
other domains, where technical concerns often overshadow the practicalities of how 
explanations are designed, delivered, and evaluated from the end-user’s perspective [14–22]. 
 
As XAI continues to advance, growing concerns have emerged regarding the quality of 
evidence used in the design of DSSs. Many systems are built on flawed or low-quality 
evidence, influenced by confounding variables, biased training and test data, or overfitting, 
factors that can significantly distort interpretability and reliability [9], [10], [23], [24–26]. A 
striking example is provided by DeGrave et al. [27], who showed that deep learning models 
developed to detect COVID-19 from chest radiographs relied on confounding features 
unrelated to pathology. As a result, the models appeared highly accurate but failed when 
deployed in new clinical settings, a failure that highlights the dangers of relying solely on 
statistical associations in the presence of confounders. This case exemplifies the broader risk 
of drawing causal inferences from associative evidence when confounding variables are not 
adequately addressed [28]. 
 
Association studies alone are insufficient to produce reliable explanations of outcomes 
generated by AI models [28]. As Durán [26] persuasively argues, no single scientific

explanation, based on statistical associations, can fully capture or account for empirical reality, 
implying that explanatory pluralism is essential for informed decision-making. In the context 
of construction, where XAI is gaining momentum, the strength of evidence underpinning many 
models remains weak, often grounded in expert opinion or limited case study demonstrations. 
Consequently, the validity and generalizability of the explanations derived from these AI 
systems are open to question [29–47]. 
 
With construction organizations at the cusp of the AI adoption technology cycle and XAI 
beginning to receive attention, albeit at a slow pace compared to other fields (e.g., law and 
medicine), an opportunity exists to take heed of contemporary thinking and learn from their 
insights and experiences [26-28], [48]. Thus, if construction organizations are to take advantage 
of the benefits of AI, DSSs must be interpretable and transparent ab initio in their design so 
that trustworthy and ethical solutions can be generated [2], [6], [49].  
 
Using a narrative review, this paper addresses the research question: How can evidence 
effectively inform the development of XAI systems and DSSs to ensure the epistemic needs of 
end-users are met? Three sub-questions guided our inquiry. First, we examine the role of 
evidence in XAI system design, highlighting how different types of evidence, such as those 
from association and mechanism studies, support the reliability and interpretability of AI 
outputs, and advocate for a hierarchy of evidence to guide system development. In this context, 
evidence refers to credible, traceable, and justifiable information or reasoning that underpins 
the reliability, transparency, and practical value of AI explanations. It is positioned as the 
epistemic foundation that validates, or warrants, the outputs and recommendations of AI 
models and DSS, thereby enhancing user trust, interpretability, and decision quality.

Second, we examine how XAI can deliver explanations that meet users’ epistemic needs, 
introducing the concept of Meaningful Human Explanations (MHEs) —those that are

can engage with XAI despite not being system developers by demonstrating how end-users can 
understand AI-driven decisions, articulate epistemic requirements to vendors and researchers,

3.0 
Explainable Artificial Intelligence 
The concept of XAI is concerned with developing techniques to understand the ‘black box’ 
workings of AI models [6], [8], [10], [11-23]. Thus, XAI aims to transform AI models 
(including their subset of machine learning) from untrustworthy ‘black boxes’ into transparent, 
understandable, and trustworthy tools. An array of XAI instruments has been developed to 
(partly) eliminate the perceived opacity of AI models [15]. As opaque AI models lack inherent 
interpretability, post-hoc methods generate explanations (e.g., feature importance scores, 
saliency maps, and SHapley Additive exPlanations, SHAP). Two of the most well-known and 
widely used XAI instruments in construction are [6], [14], [15], [61], [62-65]: 
 
1. 
Local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME): Explanations are local, 
explaining predictions for only a specific part of the data rather than the model's global 
behavior. For example, a model might be approximated locally by a match with an 
intuitive linear function (e.g., regression algorithms such as Lasso and Ridge), rendering 
it interpretable as its behavior can be understood [24], [66]. As LIME is model agnostic, 
it can provide outputs for several AI methods [67]. However, Garreau and von Luxburg 
[68] point out that data perturbations and poor choices in model parameters can result in 
LIME’s missing important features, making the generated explanations subject to 
variability.

Numerous studies in construction have utilized LIME explanations. For instance, Leuthe 
et al. [32] leverage XAI to inform end-users (i.e., property owners) about how AI models 
predict building energy consumption, using LIME to explain the impact of multiple 
features on their performance. In a similar vein, Thisovithan et al. [46] utilize machine 
learning models to predict the fundamental period of masonry infill in reinforced concrete

13 
framed structures using both LIME and SHAP to visualize and provide an understanding 
and explanation of why an RF was the ‘best fit’ model; and 
2. 
Counterfactual explanations (CFE): Provide a link between what actually happened and 
what could have happened had an input to a model been changed in a particular way. 
These explanations are typically generated after a final machine learning model is 
produced, addressing the question: ‘What would need to be different for a better 
outcome?’ [40], [64]. A CFE characterizes an outcome by exploring alternative scenarios 
or events that did not occur but plausibly could have. In XAI, CFEs focus on the input 
data, evaluating how changes to one or more features affect the predicted output. 
Designing CFEs is non-trivial, as it requires understanding the model’s decision 
boundaries and adjusting inputs just enough to shift the outcome meaningfully [69]. 
Effective CFEs should be actionable, grounded in real-world evidence, and diverse, 
revealing trade-offs and causal implications across scenarios [69]. Because CFEs rely 
only on the input–output relationship, they can intuitively explain model behavior 
without requiring access to the model’s internal logic, thereby sidestepping the 
complexity of its mathematical formulation [24]. This makes CFEs particularly useful 
when users need to understand how to intervene to avoid undesirable outcomes, such as 
reducing the risk of a safety incident.  
 
For example, a site supervisor using a DSS may find that, based on projected workload 
and site conditions, there is a 72% likelihood of a serious incident occurring on a high-
rise project. A counterfactual scenario might suggest that reducing the proportion of 
inexperienced workers from 40% to below 20% and increasing scaffolding inspections 
from weekly to twice weekly would decrease the predicted risk to 28%. Thus, adjusting


## Page 14


14 
workforce composition and inspection protocols could significantly reduce the likelihood 
of an incident.  
 
Several studies have adopted CFE methods in construction-related applications. For 
example, Zhan et al. [40] utilized CFEs to enhance the understanding of risk-based 
decision-making in managing ground settlement during tunnel construction. Meanwhile, 
Naser and Çiftçioglu [64] applied causal discovery techniques to assess the fire resistance 
of reinforced concrete columns, showing that integrating causal inference into ML 
models can significantly improve interpretability. 
 
In essence, XAI seeks to design and develop DSSs capable of performing complex inferences 
while generating explanations, through natural language, visualizations, or feature-based 
methods, that enable users to understand, trust, and act upon AI outputs. Central to this 
objective is the provision of explanation mechanisms grounded in evidence. However, as 
previously discussed, current XAI approaches applied in the design and development of DSSs 
in construction often lack such evidentiary grounding. This absence undermines both the 
transparency and trustworthiness of the systems. Embedding evidence into the design of XAI 
is therefore critical to ensuring that DSS outputs are not only interpretable but also reliable and 
justifiable, thereby enabling stakeholders, including developers, regulators, business managers, 
and end-users, to make well-informed, defensible decisions [2]. 
 
While it is important to consider a broad range of stakeholders in the design of XAI and DSSs, 
this paper primarily focuses on meeting the epistemic goals of end-users within construction 
organizations. Nonetheless, the issues addressed are also relevant to other stakeholders, whose

evidence needs and explanation requirements may vary according to their distinct roles, 
objectives, and responsibilities. 
 
4.0 
Means-End Epistemology 
Explanations, in the context of AI, have traditionally been interpreted in two primary ways 
[71–78]: (1) as intrinsic to the reasoning process itself, and (2) in terms of their usage and 
functionality, aiming to make the model’s reasoning and outputs understandable to users. In 
the construction domain, much of the prevailing AI research emphasizes high-level 
classification tasks, such as labeling, clustering, and pattern recognition, to facilitate the 
interpretation of DSSs and their outputs [78–88]. For example, Zhong et al. [83] apply deep 
learning techniques (e.g., bi-directional long short-term memory and conditional random 
fields) to automatically extract and classify procedural information from quality documents for 
compliance checking. Similarly, Wang et al. [85] use a combination of RF-SMOTE and SHAP 
to classify defect-related text in reports, thereby helping engineers understand the rationale 
behind a model’s predictions. 
 
However, while such approaches highlight key features and assign weights to inform decision-
making, the relationship between input features and classification outputs is often indirect and 
unstable [89]. Minor changes in seemingly unrelated aspects of input data can lead to different 
rankings of feature importance, which in turn affect model outputs. Consequently, slight 
variations in initial conditions or data preprocessing can result in the construction of 
substantially different models [89], raising concerns about the consistency, transparency, and 
reliability of AI-generated explanations of DSSs in construction.


## Page 16


16 
While classifications are a type of model output, indicating how input data has been assigned 
to predefined categories or labels, they are not explanations, as they do not reveal the reasoning 
or evidence behind a model’s decisions [26]. A cursory examination of AI research in the CEM 
literature reveals that such outputs are often accepted at face value, with limited reliance on 
bona fide scientific evidence to explain or justify the results produced [24], [29–47], [78].  
 
A case in point can be found in the work of Mostifi et al. [90], who use an ensemble ‘voting’ 
classifier, a machine learning technique, to classify and predict rework costs (i.e., corrections 
in construction from 2,527 non-conformance reports). However, prevailing evidence suggests 
that machine learning techniques are unable to accurately predict rework costs. Rework is not 
a risk but an uncertainty, which is probabilistically unmeasurable [91]. Non-conformances 
requiring rework typically arise due to human errors, which cannot be predicted as project 
environments, prone to volatility, uncertainty, complexity, and ambiguity (VUCA), juxtaposed 
with workplace conditions, influence their occurrence [92], [93]. Moreover, non-conformances 
are often scant in detail and incomplete, with causes being shoe-horned into artificially 
constructed categories that do not reflect the context and actualities of an event [79]. 
 
4.1 
A Means-End Framework  
Regardless of how an explanation is interpreted, it is important to recognize that the concept 
itself is inherently malleable and context-dependent [78]. While the philosophical nuances of 
explanation are beyond the scope of this paper, they are explored in depth by Salmon [72], 
Woodward and Ross [94], and Sørmo et al. [95]. For the purposes of this study, we adopt 
Salmon’s [72] epistemic conception of explanation, as supported by Buchholz [24], which 
frames explanation as fundamentally connected to human understanding and knowledge.


## Page 17


17 
Under this conception, an explanation is considered successful if it enhances an individual’s 
understanding of a phenomenon. It prioritizes the cognitive and informational dimensions of 
explanation, emphasizing its role in improving a person’s epistemic state. By focusing on the 
‘why’ or ‘how’ something occurs, this view enables individuals to make sense of events within 
a specific context and to integrate new information meaningfully with what they already know. 
Developing such explanations requires not only a robust body of evidence but also careful 
consideration of the means by which they are generated. These means, including the methods, 
processes, and reasoning applied, can vary depending on the epistemic ends, or the goals related 
to knowledge and understanding, that are being pursued.  
 
To ensure explanations are both valid and useful, a means-end epistemology is needed, one 
that critically evaluates how evidence is used to achieve epistemic aims [24], [72]. Within this 
framework, epistemic normativity plays a central role, addressing fundamental questions about 
what should be believed, how beliefs should be formed, and what counts as sound justification. 
It provides the standards that guide our cognitive efforts, ensuring that reasoning processes and 
inquiry methods align with principles that foster knowledge, truth, and rational understanding. 
 
A means-end epistemology’s central normative criterion – the standard or principle that 
determines the epistemic strengths and shortcomings of a belief, reason, or method of inquiry 
– is explained by the principle of instrumental rationality [24], [96]. In this instance, 
individuals aim to select the most effective means to achieve their goals. Thus, to design and 
develop DSSs that are explainable and interpretable for end-users in construction, a means-end 
framework, as shown in Figure 2, is proposed.

Our framework aims to ensure that the norms and standards for good belief-forming practices 
(epistemic normativity) align with rational selection and the use of compelling evidence and 
methods (instrumental rationality) to achieve an organization’s epistemic goals. By integrating 
these two dimensions, epistemic practices become both normatively justified and practically 
effective, giving end-users confidence in the outputs and explanations provided. 
 
The aim of XAI is to provide instruments (means) that generate explanations which are 
understandable and meaningful to end-users, thereby fulfilling their epistemic needs 
(ends)[24]. For instance, in the case of LIME, the epistemic end is to explain an individual 
prediction within a local region of the data space, with the means being a simplified, 
interpretable surrogate model that approximates the behavior of the original AI model in that 
region [24]. Similarly, CFEs aim to clarify the input–output relationship of a model, using 
hypothetical ‘what-if’ statements as the explanatory means [24].  
 
As illustrated in our framework (Figure 2), evidence plays a pivotal role in informing the design 
of such XAI instruments and the broader DSSs. It does so by guiding the selection of relevant 
features and model structure, shaping the form and modality of explanations, and validating 
their usefulness and reliability. In doing so, evidence ensures that explanations are not only 
technically accurate but also transparent, justifiable, and aligned with domain-specific causal 
reasoning, making them both trustworthy and actionable in practice. 
 
Not only does a means–end epistemology underpin existing XAI instruments, but it also 
provides a valuable framework for the development of new tools, specifically by guiding 
critical design questions such as: what should be explained (subject), to whom it should be 
explained (stakeholder), how it should be explained (instrument), and why it should be

explained (goal) [2], [6], [24], [26]. As previously discussed, different stakeholders, such as 
engineers who develop models, operators who implement them, and end-users who rely on 
their outputs, will inevitably have diverse explanatory needs, often requiring distinct 
explanation strategies or instruments to satisfy their respective epistemic goals [2], [6], [24], 
[66], [97], [98]. A detailed examination of these stakeholder-specific desiderata, particularly in 
the context of construction and infrastructure projects, is provided in Love et al. [6]. 
 
The suitability of XAI instruments depends on what should be explained and to whom the 
explanation is given. However, the suitability of an XAI instrument in providing an explanation 
can only be determined if the epistemic end being pursued by a stakeholder is specified in detail 
before acquiring evidence and constructing a DSS [24]. Sometimes, the same stakeholder may 
require different explanations, so they must be tailored to meet their specific needs. 
Consideration may also need to be given to algorithmic recourse2, which can result in changes 
to the design of the DSS and a re-evaluation of evidence [99-101].

Evidential Pluralism 
With multiple sources of evidence needed to establish knowledge claims and explain outputs, 
the theory of Evidential Pluralism—an account of the epistemology of causation that maintains 
that to establish a causal claim, there needs to be an existence of a correlation and mechanism 
(Figure 4)—is utilized within the means-end framework to provide XAI instruments and the 
DSS with the means to ensure it is grounded on explainable causal discovery [28], [128], [129]. 
While Evidential Pluralism aligns with mixed methods, it is fundamentally different, as it 
provides a normative philosophical theory about what needs to be established to establish the 
presence of causation contributing to the propagation of MHEs for DSSs outputs [128]. Simply 
put, mixed methods are a methodological orientation employing variations in qualitative and 
quantitative data, methods, and designs [77].

5.2.1 Association Studies 
Association studies can test a hypothesis (C1) that a putative cause and effect are correlated 
with potential confounders (Figure 4). Similarly, they can be used to indirectly verify if a 
mechanism accounts for a correlation (C2). Controlling for multicollinearity (e.g., removing 
highly correlated predictors or applying Principal Component Analysis to reduce 
dimensionality) and confounders (e.g., adding covariates to a regression model to adjust for 
their effects) is critical for ensuring the validity of a study, as it provides the ability to infer that 
there is a mechanism of action giving rise to a correlation.  
 
With association studies, there is a need to be mindful and deal with confounding variables; 
caution also needs to be taken with ‘tests of significance’ (i.e., hypothesis testing) as they can 
influence the reliability of evidence when inferring a causal claim as: (1) p-values being 
misinterpreted: (2) thresholds of p <0.05 (or greater), for example, for statistical significance 
resulting in binary thinking (significant versus non-significant) rather than a nuanced 
understanding of the data and the actualities of practice; and (3) a lack of reproducibility of 
results due p-values being sensitive to varying conditions. In addition to these limitations, 
which can impact the reliability of evidence used to design and develop XAI and DSSs, there 
is also a need to consider how well outputs correlate with expected outcomes or ground truths 
when association studies are relied upon in areas such as: [6], [129]: 
 
• 
Performance evaluation: Precision, recall, and accuracy metrics, essentially quantifying 
the association between the predictions of an AI model and ground truth labels, have 
been the mainstay in construction for justifying a model’s performance [131], [132]. 
However, there are limitations to these performance metrics, which can impact their 
accurate interpretation. For example, precision and accuracy metrics can be


## Page 31


31 
misinterpreted by imbalanced datasets and are insensitive to false negatives. Likewise, a 
trade-off between recall and precision often prevails. Hence, maximizing recall can result 
in a decrease in precision and vice versa.  
• 
Bias detection: Examining whether a model’s decisions are correlated with sensitive 
attributes such as demographics (e.g., race, gender, and groups) and individual 
characteristics (e.g., age and physical qualities) [133]. If an AI model’s decisions 
disproportionately (dis)favor a particular individual or group, this can indicate a biased 
association that needs to be addressed. Several methods can be used to identify and 
address bias, depending on the context of construction (e.g., detecting unsafe behavior 
[133], posture analysis [134], and workforce activity [135]), ranging from statistical and 
predictive parity checks to more complex intersectional fairness metrics. Incompleteness 
of data, incorrectly if groups have similar statistical outcomes, intersectional complexity, 
an inability to cater to varying contexts in which a model operates (i.e., generating false 
positives or negatives), and changes in data are just a few of the limitations that must be 
addressed to overcome the detection of bias in DSSs; and 
• 
Causal inference: Emphasis is based on determining a cause-and-effect relationship 
between variables and establishing a better understanding of the directional influence 
between variables than correlation. Causal inference models commonly used in XAI 
studies in construction are Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and variants thereof [39], 
[64], CFEs [40], and Granger Causality [136]. However, models of this type are subject 
to several drawbacks, as confounders can go unmeasured. Multiple explanations can also 
fit the observed data, introducing a degree of ambiguity and hindering the ability to 
generalize causal relations, particularly in high-dimensional datasets. Aside from these 
disadvantages, causal inference models cannot demonstrate causality in an absolute 
sense, reinforcing the need to identify specific linking mechanisms to verify a claim.

While association studies take a coarse approach to establishing a link between cause and 
effect, mechanistic studies can complement them by uncovering how the instances of A are 
responsible for those of B.  
 
5.2.2 Mechanism Studies 
Mechanism studies are essential for informing the design of XAI instruments and the resultant 
explanations, as well as assessing AI models, as they provide a detailed understanding of the 
internal processes and pathways that drive their predictions. Within the context of Evidential 
Pluralism, as noted in Figure 4, mechanism studies do not measure A and B together. Instead, 
they seek to unearth the links or components of the complex mechanism. As a result, they can 
provide a nuanced perspective on putative causal relationships as they examine the specific 
processes, structures, and systems that explain how an event occurs and how an AI model 
works, supporting the generation of MHEs [128].  
 
The existence of a mechanism is confirmed, as noted in Figure 4, by employing hypotheses 
that posit features likely to link A and B (M2). According to Williamson [129], a specific 
mechanism hypothesis should articulate the components of the mechanism, the interactions 
between them, and the expected outcomes under certain conditions. Still, it does not necessarily 
need to identify every feature. Additionally, features can be identified and confirmed by 
previous mechanistic studies (M1), such as observational evidence (Table 1), and used to 
support correlated variables (M3).  
 
Identifying mechanisms helps XAI simplify complex models into more interpretable 
components or surrogate models (e.g., decision trees, rule-based models, and Generalized 
Additive Models), which not only help explain predictions. However, it can also be used for


## Page 33


33 
tuning parameters and architectures, as well as debugging, thereby contributing to improved 
performance. Specific areas where mechanism studies can be used to assess the performance 
of XAI include: 
 
• 
Explanation accuracy and completeness: Assessing how accurately instruments attribute 
contributions to different features. Methods such as LIME and SHAP can be used to 
ensure that XAI instruments correctly represent the importance of each feature. Indeed, 
LIME and SHAP are prevalent XAI approaches in construction [6], [137], [138], but they 
come with their own set of challenges. While LIME approximates an AI model locally 
around a specific instance using a straightforward, interpretable approach (e.g., a linear 
model), it may not always accurately reflect its global behavior, especially if its decision 
boundaries are non-linear. Moreover, LIME relies on generating perturbed samples 
around the instance being explained. The stability of the explanations can be affected by 
the choice of sampling strategy, which might lead to variability in explanations across 
different runs or settings; this point was also identified above in Section 3. In the case of 
SHAP, there is an assumption of a linear relationship between its values (i.e., calculated 
by comparing a model’s prediction with and without a particular feature present) and a 
model’s predictions; however, this assumption does not hold for models and thus should 
be treated with caution when interpreting them. It should also be noted that while SHAP 
values attribute feature importance, they do not provide causal insights. As a result, 
understanding how they causally interact will invariably be required using techniques 
such as DAGs, CFEs, or Structural Equation Models [139]. In this instance, the need for 
both association and mechanism-based studies is explicit, writ large. 
• 
Model transparency and end-user understanding: The consistency of explanations 
provided by XAI instruments to end-users is central to ensuring model transparency


## Page 34


34 
[140]. Here, consistency refers to the homogeneity of explanations obtained from a model 
when applied to similar inputs and the varying contexts of user needs and expertise [140]. 
The consistency of explanations can be evaluated, depending on a model’s type and 
purpose, using an array of techniques such as perturbation analysis, feature removal, K-
NN, correlational analysis, similarity metrics (e.g., in documents using cosine similarity, 
Euclidean distance, or Jaccard index [141]), saliency maps, and partial dependence plots. 
Combining these approaches can enhance the reliability of explanations and aid in 
assessing whether they are reasonable, plausible, and aligned with epistemic goals. 
 
In sum, mechanism studies are crucial for evaluating XAI and DSSs, as they offer a deeper 
understanding of the internal workings of models, which is necessary to support association 
studies in generating explanations of outcomes for end-users. When LIME and SHAP are 
applied within an evidence-based framework, their outputs should be interpreted with caution. 
Although they reveal associations between input features and model predictions, they do not 
in themselves confirm underlying mechanisms. Their stochastic nature means that explanations 
can vary depending on sampling strategy or data perturbation. Accordingly, they are most 
useful when triangulated with other sources of evidence, such as causal modeling or process-
tracing interviews, to ensure that the resulting explanations are robust and mechanistically 
meaningful. Within the adapted evidence hierarchy (Table 3), such outputs correspond to 
associative evidence and should be weighted accordingly when informing DSS design and 
explanation generation.

6.1 
Developing Meaningful Explanations 
While a means-end lens provides a new conceptualization for incorporating evidence into the 
design of a DSS, the quality of the data and its preparation (e.g., data cleaning, feature 
engineering, transformations, and splitting), as well as the association and mechanism studies, 
will influence the effectiveness of the MHEs generated. Thus, researchers in construction will 
need to give credence to the strength, value, and utility of evidence that has been captured and 
crafted to provide MHEs [26]. The strength, value, and utility of evidence required will vary 
with its availability and the context of a DSS application in construction, for example, whether 
it is being used to manage cost and schedule performance, assess the risks associated with 
ground settlement during tunnel boring machine operations, inform retrofit decisions, or 
predict concrete strength. An MHE should comprise three components [2]:  
 
1. 
Explanatory power: How well a model can address different dimensions of explanation 
in varying contexts [143] and by answering a series of CFEs (e.g., what-if-things-had-
been-different questions) that illustrate the theoretical and pragmatic relevance of an 
explanation [144];  
2. 
Control the process of explaining: People play a critical role in determining whether 
explanations adhere to epistemic and normative standards. The design of DSSs is not 
value-free, as it involves stakeholders' varying economic, ethical, legal, political, and 
social issues, which must be considered, given that their beliefs, values, and expectations 
will differ. Construction organizations should always be prepared to question the 
explanations provided by DSSs. Expanding on this point within the context of AI


## Page 36


36 
algorithms used to support robots in operations, Santoni de Sio and van den Hove [145] 
argue that humans should be responsible for and control all decisions made. Thus, to 
maintain control over decisions, construction organizations need to be able to specify the 
conditions under which an MHE satisfies its users of a DSS. Identifying the properties 
of an explanation relevant to the end-users' epistemic goals so that they can be 
accordingly assessed; and  
3. 
Evaluating the suitability of evidence to inform XAI: Integrating evidence with 
explanatory power will not only enable reliable assessments of new knowledge but also 
foster learning, understanding, and conceptual coherence, allowing end-users to evaluate 
various MHEs for different situations that may arise during construction. 
 
If end-users in construction cannot make sense of MHEs by drawing on and combining them 
with prior knowledge, then what purpose do they serve? [26] Although there is a need to 
incorporate XAI into AI models in construction, MHE must be capable of providing an 
understanding based on evidence and a well-defined structure.

6.2.1 
Cognitive Validity: Ensuring Human Comprehension 
Evaluation is critical to ensure that explanations are cognitively accessible to users such as 
construction managers, site and project engineers, and project and safety inspectors. As 
construction is inherently subject to production pressure, users of DSSs often do not have the 
time or capacity to decipher overly complex or technical explanations. User-centred evaluation 
methods, including surveys, think-aloud protocols, and cognitive load assessments, can help 
determine whether explanations are easily understood, actionable, and presented at the 
appropriate level of detail. Without this, XAI may hinder rather than help decision-making. 
 
6.2.2 
Epistemic Alignment: Supporting Meaningful Understanding 
In construction, where causal relationships are often uncertain and context-dependent, 
explanations must do more than highlight model features, they must help users understand why 
or how an outcome occurred [2]. Evaluating epistemic soundness ensures that explanations 
align with domain knowledge and established causal reasoning, rather than presenting shallow 
statistical correlations [28]. This alignment enhances users’ ability to interpret outcomes, 
diagnose problems, and justify decisions to stakeholders, especially in tasks such as delay 
attribution or safety and quality analysis [133]. 
 
6.2.3 
Robustness and Reliability: Promoting Trustworthy Use 
Trust in AI systems is undermined if explanations change unpredictably or fail under slightly 
different conditions. Evaluation of explanation stability, consistency, and fidelity (i.e., how 
faithfully the explanation reflects the model’s true reasoning) is crucial for identifying 
unreliable behavior. In construction applications where inputs may be noisy, incomplete, or 
context-sensitive, such robustness checks are essential to ensure the dependability of AI 
support in real-world scenarios [77], [79].

6.2.4 
Decision Impact: Measuring Practical Utility 
Explanations should not only clarify outputs but also support better decision-making. Task-
based evaluation methods, such as scenario testing and decision impact analysis, can be used 
to measure how explanations affect user behavior, performance, and outcomes. For instance, 
if an explanation of risk prediction helps a planner reallocate resources more effectively, this 
demonstrates that the explanation is not only understood but also useful; these forms of 
evaluation ground XAI design in measurable improvements in user performance and project 
results. 
 
6.2.5 
Normative Justifiability: Upholding Ethical and Legal Standards 
Decisions in construction often have ethical, legal, and contractual implications. Evaluating 
normative justifiability ensures that explanations meet standards for fairness, transparency, and 
accountability. Such considerations are particularly important in contexts such as compliance 
audits, safety investigations, or dispute resolution, where the rationale behind a decision may 
be subject to external scrutiny. Explanations must therefore be not only technically accurate 
but also justifiable in ways that stand up to institutional or regulatory review. 
 
Considered collectively, these dimensions of evaluation, cognitive validity, epistemic 
alignment, robustness, decision impact, and normative justifiability form a comprehensive 
foundation for designing and deploying effective XAI systems in construction. Without 
rigorous evaluation, there is a risk of implementing systems that may appear sophisticated but 
fail to support users in meaningful or trustworthy ways. Hence, evaluation must be treated as 
a core pillar of XAI design, enabling systems that are not only explainable in theory but also 
explanatory, usable, and justifiable in practice. The operationalization of MHEs can be 
achieved through empirical indicators such as explanation satisfaction scores, cognitive load


## Page 42


42 
measures, and task-performance improvements. These metrics provide a structured basis for 
future empirical studies to determine whether an explanation is genuinely meaningful, 
supporting user understanding, decision quality, and trust, rather than merely technically 
accurate. 
 
6.3 
Illustrative Application 
To translate our conceptual means–end framework into a practical pathway for researchers and 
practitioners, we offer an illustrative, step-by-step application demonstrating how evidence can 
be integrated into the design, explanation, and evaluation of an XAI-enabled DSS. As a starting 
point, the adapted evidence hierarchy, presented in Table 3, provides a structured basis for 
weighing association and mechanism evidence, ensuring that higher-quality, contextually 
relevant studies are prioritized when informing model design. The suggested five-step process 
is as follows: 
 
1. 
Evidence Identification and Weighting: For a DSS predicting schedule delays in 
tunneling projects, evidence is gathered from two primary sources: (a) association studies 
(e.g., historical project data showing correlations between delays, subcontractor 
reliability, and design finalization dates) and (b) mechanism studies (e.g., process-tracing 
interviews revealing how late design approvals propagate through procurement and 
scheduling, amplifying delay risk). The adapted evidence hierarchy presented in Table 3 
is then applied to weight these sources, giving priority to high-quality longitudinal field 
data and mechanistic studies instead of single-case anecdotal evidence. 
2. 
Model Design and Feature Selection: Evidence directly informs feature engineering: 
variables with strong support (e.g., geotechnical complexity and subcontractor


## Page 43


43 
reliability) are retained, while those with weak or spurious associations are excluded. As 
a result, causal interpretability is improved, and model noise is reduced. 
3. 
XAI Technique Selection and Mapping: XAI instruments are selected to align with 
epistemic goals and the type of evidence required. LIME and SHAP provide associative 
evidence by estimating feature contributions to individual predictions, while CFEs 
contribute mechanism-oriented insight by revealing how changes to inputs causally 
affect outputs. Surrogate models, such as decision trees, can bridge association and 
mechanism by approximating global decision logic. Each technique is mapped to the 
evidence category identified in the adapted hierarchy (Table 3) and to the epistemic need 
it satisfies, whether supporting understanding, trust-building, or actionability. 
4. 
Explanation Delivery: Explanations are communicated using dashboards with visual 
CFEs (sliders or scenario trees) and natural language summaries tailored to stakeholder 
needs (e.g., actionable insights for site supervisors, aggregated insights for project 
managers). 
5. 
Evaluation of MHEs: Explanations are iteratively evaluated against the five dimensions 
identified earlier: cognitive validity, epistemic alignment, robustness, decision impact, 
and normative justifiability. Methods include think-aloud protocols, explanation 
satisfaction scales, stability testing, and decision impact analysis, ensuring that MHEs 
are not merely normative ideals but empirically testable outcomes.

6.4 
Availability of Data for Model Development 
It can be observed within the mainstream CEM literature that the number of papers focusing 
on AI has increased exponentially over the last decade [1], [6], [146], [147]. When new deep 
learning AI algorithms are developed (e.g., the You Only Look Once series [148]), a flurry of 
papers are published in the CEM-related literature claiming to provide accurate and reliable 
results for a given problem. Nonetheless, works of this ilk often offer no evidence to underpin 
their rationale and explain the results presented [149-152]. Furthermore, they are frequently 
based on experiments and small datasets, akin to a ‘proof of concept’, providing no insight into 
the realities of practice in construction and the limitations of the research [131]. 
 
High-quality and large datasets are essential for building effective DSSs that utilize deep 
learning algorithms [153]. While algorithmic advances are being made to improve prediction 
accuracy without using more data, the size used for training remains the most critical aspect of 
an AI model’s development [154], [155]. Collecting large datasets is costly and time-
consuming, particularly from construction sites, as they are typically unstructured, noisy, 
incomplete, and in varying formats [4]. Thus, to make headway in developing DSSs that can 
be effectively used by construction organizations and improve their decision-making, 
researchers must pool their resources and share data. Collaboration between researchers and 
organizations is necessary to establish a repository of shared evidence and datasets. 
Unfortunately, such collaboration appears to be missing within the CEM community.


## Page 46


46 
Setting aside the need for collaboration, journals and their editors play a pivotal role in ensuring 
that the data used in AI studies are made available and, if sensitive, in a format that prevents 
identification of any organization or individual, thereby enabling replication and building upon 
the authors’ claims. A condition of Nature, for example, is that authors must make materials, 
data, code, and associated protocols promptly available to readers without undue qualifications 
[155].  
 
However, authors publishing studies on AI in construction rarely make their materials, data, 
code, and associated protocols accessible for review, instead opting to make them available 
upon request, a provision offered by journals. When authors requested access to AI training 
data to reduce bias and improve the generalizability of the models being developed, the 
corresponding authors of papers repeatedly ignored these requests. If individual privacy and 
intellectual concerns were associated with sharing the data, it is understandable why it could 
not be provided. Explicitly, this was not the case for the requested data.  
 
Access to additional data to assist with training a model helps understand how it has learned 
and performs in real-world situations, enhancing the ability to develop explanations using XAI 
instruments and thus giving construction organizations the confidence to utilize DSSs in their 
operations. For this reason, it is strongly recommended that editors of mainstream CEM 
journals receiving AI-based papers request authors provide evidence (e.g., code and datasets) 
- perhaps a similar policy to Nature - to support the development of their models. By doing so, 
editors can make a valuable contribution to promoting much-needed collaboration, 
transparency, reproducibility, and ethical practices in the field, ultimately leading to 
advancements in research and technology.

6.5 
Limitations 
Despite offering valuable theoretical insights, our review is subject to several limitations 
inherent in its methodological and conceptual approach. Using a narrative review allows 
interpretive depth and flexibility but lacks a systematic search protocol, which may introduce 
selection bias and limit the transparency and reproducibility of the review process. Prioritizing 
theoretical integration over empirical synthesis also constrains the generalizability of the 
findings, as the conceptual arguments are not directly validated through user data or case 
evidence. 
 
Our means–end framework, while conceptually robust, remains abstract and may be 
challenging to operationalize without additional empirical grounding or practical guidance. 
The disciplinary focus on construction may further restrict the transferability of insights to 
other sectors with different epistemic or organizational demands. In addition, the analysis 
assumes a level of user agency and capacity to engage critically with AI systems that may not 
reflect the realities of professional practice, where time, resources, and technical expertise are 
often limited. 
 
Beyond these methodological constraints, the study makes several important conceptual 
contributions that advance understanding of how XAI systems can be designed to meet the 
epistemic needs of end-users. It introduces the notion of MHEs, shifting the focus from purely 
technical explanations to those that are actionable, context-sensitive, and aligned with 
practitioners’ decision needs, explaining why a decision was made, how outputs relate to 
inputs, and when recommendations can be trusted. By emphasizing the integration of multiple 
forms of evidence (e.g., mechanistic, statistical, and experiential) into XAI design, we highlight 
the underexplored role of theory in shaping XAI development for construction. In doing so, it


## Page 48


48 
positions end-users as active participants who can articulate their knowledge needs and demand 
transparency and accountability from AI systems. The means–end framework provides a 
structured lens for aligning AI explanation design with user goals and decision contexts, laying 
the groundwork for more user-centric and trustworthy AI in construction and other safety-
critical industries. 
 
A further limitation is that our means–end framework is presented conceptually and illustrated 
through hypothetical scenarios rather than through an empirical case study. This design choice 
is deliberate and consistent with the paper’s aim as a narrative review and theory-building 
exercise. A worked, data-driven demonstration would require access to project records, 
stakeholder engagement, and iterative model development, activities beyond the scope of our 
paper. Future research will focus on operationalizing our framework in real construction 
projects, combining association and mechanism studies to generate CFEs and empirically 
evaluating MHEs using user-centred metrics such as cognitive load, explanation satisfaction, 
and decision impact. Such work will provide a rigorous validation of the framework’s 
applicability and value in practice. 
 
6.6 
Implications for Future Research 
Future research is needed to develop further and empirically test the proposed means-end 
framework’s ability to generate satisfactory explanations based on evidence. More specifically, 
the means-end framework must be populated with use cases from applications applied on-site 
(domain) for various areas such as defect detection using sensors, analysis of safety behaviors, 
predicting risks and costs, managing schedule performance, and checking adherence to codes 
and standards (AI task). Here, evidence from cases is used to inform and establish explanations 
of AI decisions, thereby improving trust and ensuring regulatory compliance (XAI role). In


## Page 49


49 
cases where potential defects or quality issues are detected, such as during tunnel construction, 
sensor data and evidence derived from historical data can be used to interpret AI-generated 
outputs using SHAP and LIME (XAI techniques). Here, SHAP can identify the key factors that 
contribute to a defect (e.g., human error, poor materials, and unfavorable environmental 
conditions). Likewise, LIME, in conjunction with evidence, can be used to explain how and 
why a particular section of a project (e.g., piling) experienced a defect, offering actionable 
insights into root causes.  
 
Building a repository of use cases, which should be made openly available to researchers and 
others, will contribute to developing a generic, step-by-step implementation framework that 
can ensure the development of practical MHEs and support end-users’ epistemic goals. 
Similarly, research must consider how the means-end framework can cater to the varying 
desiderata of stakeholders and produce MHEs. However, a significant challenge will be to 
acquire multiple sources of evidence to support AI tasks and the generation of MHEs, which 
will be relied upon to inform decision-making. 
 
7.0 
Conclusion 
Using a narrative review, our paper addresses the research question: How can evidence 
effectively inform the development of XAI systems and DSSs to ensure the epistemic needs of 
end-users are met? Three sub-questions guided our inquiry. First, we examine the role of 
evidence in XAI system design, highlighting how different types of evidence, such as those 
from association and mechanism studies, support the reliability and interpretability of AI 
outputs, and advocate for a hierarchy of evidence to guide system development. Second, we 
examine how XAI can deliver explanations that meet users’ epistemic needs, introducing the 
concept of MHEs, which includes understanding the rationale behind decisions, the influence


## Page 50


50 
of input changes on outputs, and whether users can trust and act upon the system’s 
recommendations. Finally, we address how construction organizations can engage with XAI 
despite not being system developers by demonstrating how end-users can understand AI-driven 
decisions, articulate epistemic requirements to vendors and researchers, and critically assess 
DSSs to ensure alignment with their practical needs. 
 
Given that the CEM literature has yet to engage substantively with the integration of evidence 
in the design of XAI instruments and DSSs, this study draws on contemporary research from 
other fields to inform its analysis. As construction organizations increasingly adopt both 
bespoke and commercial off-the-shelf DSSs, there is a growing need to embed XAI into their 
design to enhance transparency, fairness, and trust in system outputs. While the relevance of 
XAI is gradually being acknowledged within the CEM domain, the design of such systems 
must also incorporate robust evidence, derived from both association and mechanism-based 
studies, to support meaningful interpretation and informed decision-making. 
 
To address this need, the paper proposes a means–end theoretical framework for integrating 
evidence into the development of XAI systems and DSSs. This framework is underpinned by 
Evidential Pluralism and structured around a hierarchy of evidence, thereby supporting 
explanation through causal discovery. It provides a systematic approach for aligning the design 
of DSSs not only with the epistemic needs of end-users but also with broader stakeholder goals. 
 
From a practical standpoint, most construction organizations are not in a position to develop 
AI or XAI-based systems themselves, as their core focus remains on project delivery. While a 
few large firms, such as Obayashi Corporation, have in-house research and development 
capabilities, most remain technology users rather than creators. Nonetheless, the proposed


## Page 51


51 
framework equips organizations with a clear lens for understanding DSS design and ensuring 
that epistemic goals (i.e., clarifying recommendations, exploring variable interdependencies, 
and fostering trust in outputs) are effectively embedded in MHEs. 
 
To fully leverage the benefits of XAI and DSSs, construction organizations must move beyond 
superficial rationales for AI-generated decisions and instead seek deeper explanations of why 
and how these outputs are produced. The means–end framework we have proposed in this paper 
not only provides a foundation for developing user-centric and functionally oriented AI 
systems in construction but also serves as a transferable model for other engineering domains 
seeking to integrate evidence into the design of explainable DSSs. By clarifying how evidence 
can be embedded in XAI design and proposing a structured means–end framework, our paper 
establishes the conceptual groundwork for future empirical testing, user studies, and 
implementation research across construction and other safety-critical sectors. Building on this 
conceptual foundation, the next critical step is to validate the framework in projects, integrating 
real-world data and engaging stakeholders to assess whether it reliably generates MHEs that 
improve decision quality. Such empirical work will ensure that XAI-enabled DSSs are not only 
theoretically robust but also demonstrably valuable in supporting understanding, trust, and 
actionable insights in practice.
