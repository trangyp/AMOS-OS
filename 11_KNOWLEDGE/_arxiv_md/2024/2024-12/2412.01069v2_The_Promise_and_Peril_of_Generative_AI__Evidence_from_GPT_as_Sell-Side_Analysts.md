First Draft: June 25, 2023

Abstract 
 
Large language models (LLMs) promise to democratize financial analysis by reducing 
information-processing costs. Yet equal access does not ensure equal outcomes, as the locus of 
friction may shift from processing information to evaluating model outputs. We study GPT’s 
earnings forecasts following corporate earnings releases and document two patterns. First, 
GPT’s narrative attention is consistent and human-like but not always associated with higher 
forecast accuracy. Second, its quantitative reasoning varies substantially across contexts, 
challenging the view that LLMs are uniformly weak at numerical tasks. Building on these 
insights, we propose a diagnostic framework that links forecast accuracy to observable 
processing features (i.e., narrative focus, numerical reasoning, and self-assessed confidence). 
These indicators serve as proxies for this new form of information friction and alert investors 
when to exercise caution. Our study has implications for information frictions, regulatory 
oversight, and the economics of AI-mediated financial markets. 
 
Keywords: Large language models, Information frictions, Financial analysis, Narrative 
attention, Numerical reasoning

Introduction 
In financial markets, persistent disparities in information-processing capacity continue 
to shape investor behavior and asset pricing. Theories attribute these frictions to differences in 
data access, analytical skill, or processing costs (Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980; Verrecchia, 2001; 
Blankespoor, deHaan, and Marinovic, 2020). The advent of large language models (LLMs), 
such as OpenAI’s GPT, may change these dynamics by making advanced analytical tools 
broadly accessible at low marginal costs. Recent studies suggest that LLMs reduce information 
processing costs for both professional and non-professional users (e.g., Bertomeu, Lin, Liu, 
and Ni, 2025; Bradshaw, Ma, Yost, and Zou, 2025; Cheng, Lin, and Zhao, 2025; deHaan, Lee, 
Liu, and Noh, 2025; Sheng, Sun, Yang, and Zhang, 2025). This equalized accessibility has led 
to optimism that LLMs could democratize financial analysis, allowing non-experts to engage 
with sophisticated analytical tasks (Chang, Dong, Martin, and Zhou, 2025). 
Equal access, however, does not ensure equal outcomes. While LLMs reduce traditional 
barriers to information processing, they may introduce a new friction: the difficulty of assessing 
output quality. Because LLMs often provide responses without transparent reasoning, users, 
especially non-experts, could struggle to identify reliable outputs.1 Recent evidence illustrates 
this challenge. In 2024, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) sanctioned two 
advisory firms for misleading claims about AI-powered forecasts. 2 Although neither firm 
possessed functioning AI models, investors acted on their recommendations, suggesting limited 
ability to evaluate these outputs. Experimental evidence by Klingbeil, Grützner, and Schreck 
(2024) similarly shows that investors often over-rely on AI advice even when it contradicts

These observations motivate our research questions: under what conditions do LLMs

be linked to output quality? More broadly, we ask whether these signals can be organized into 
a diagnostic framework that alerts users when to use caution, thereby suggesting contexts in

We study this question by evaluating GPT, the most widely used LLM,3  on a realistic 
and economically relevant task: forecasting one-year-ahead earnings based on firms’ earnings 
press releases. Our design approximates how non-experts interact with GPT, allowing us to 
assess its potential to democratize financial analysis. First, we employ simple, non-technical 
prompts in natural language to evaluate GPT’s performance in a setting where domain 
knowledge is limited. Second, we examine how GPT processes information—a critical yet 
understudied aspect given the model’s proprietary nature. Specifically, we focus on how it 
analyzes two distinct disclosure components (i.e., narrative content and tabular financial 
statements) and assess how each relates to forecast outcomes. Third, we compare GPT’s 
forecasts to contemporaneous analyst forecasts, which provide a natural benchmark and help 
control for unobserved firm- and macro-level factors. Finally, to mitigate look-ahead bias and 
better reflect users’ real-time decision environment, we restrict our sample to press releases

Our analysis draws on 7,114 earnings press releases issued by 1,000 randomly selected 
firms over a two-year window centered on GPT-4 Turbo’s knowledge cutoff date of April 30, 
2023. This sampling strategy balances access to the latest model with sufficient post-cutoff data 
to measure realized forecast accuracy.5 For each press release, we prompt GPT to (1) identify

three key metrics (ratios or trends) from its quantitative analysis, and (3) predict one-year-
ahead GAAP earnings per share (EPS). These tasks allow us to study GPT’s narrative attention, 
quantitative reasoning, and predictive accuracy in a structured and replicable setting. We aim 
to diagnose systematic patterns in GPT’s information processing that correlate with variation 
in its forecasting performance, rather than to establish causal relationships. 
We first document a clear distinction in how GPT processes narrative versus 
quantitative information. Narrative attention is highly consistent across firms: GPT tends to 
prioritize sentences that are placed early in the text, readable, forward-looking, and negatively

2024; Li, 2008; Elliott, Rennekamp, and White, 2015; Loughran and McDonald, 2011).6 This 
consistency likely reflects GPT’s training on large-scale textual corpora, which embed strong 
priors about linguistic salience. In contrast, quantitative reasoning varies widely across firms, 
reflecting heterogeneity in domain-specific learning. GPT’s ability to approximate expert 
judgment (i.e., identifying the key metrics that analysts tend to emphasize) is higher for firms 
with more reliable quantitative disclosures and proxies for stronger exposure during training 
(e.g., broader analyst coverage). 7 These results suggest that GPT capabilities are context-

Our results contribute to several strands of research in accounting and information 
economics. First, they speak to classic theories of information frictions (Grossman and Stiglitz, 
1980; Sims, 2003; Verrecchia, 2001). While LLMs lower traditional processing costs, they may 
introduce a new friction: the need to assess the quality of model outputs. Because performance 
varies systematically across contexts, the binding constraint in LLM-mediated markets may 
shift from information processing to output assessment. Investors who cannot recognize when 
model reliability weakens may be disadvantaged if they rely on its output. Our proposed 
diagnostic signals can serve as proxies for the cost of output assessment, empirically capturing 
this new form of information friction.

Second, we add to the emerging literature on the democratization of financial analysis 
through generative AI. Blankespoor, Croom, and Grant (2024) document that while retail 
investors are increasingly adopting AI tools, the benefits they derive differ with user 
sophistication. Our finding of systematic variation in GPT’s performance—linked to how it 
processes narrative and quantitative inputs—sheds light on where these disparities are likely to 
arise. We also contribute to the debate on AI’s effects on investor information processing. 
Cheng et al. (2025) find narrowed bid-ask spreads during GPT outages, consistent with AI 
facilitating informed trading. In contrast, Chang et al. (2025) show that AI helps retail investors 
trade more on fundamentals, narrowing information gaps. Our results suggest that the net effect 
of AI likely varies with the trade-off between reduced processing costs and increased demands 
on abilities to evaluate outputs.  
Third, our results also relate to the literature on delegation and monitoring in capital 
markets (Diamond, 1984; Holmström and Milgrom, 1991). While prior work focuses on human 
intermediaries such as analysts, credit rating agencies, or the financial press, our results shed 
light on delegation to algorithmic agents. GPT’s effectiveness, like that of human delegates, 
varies systematically with the domain and quality of information inputs, suggesting domain-
specific risks in algorithmic delegation. 
Finally, our findings have implications for corporate disclosure and regulatory oversight. 
GPT’s accuracy is higher when firms have broader analyst coverage and more reliable 
disclosures, conditions that provide rich learning signals and align with expert judgment. These 
patterns suggest two regulatory priorities: (1) improving disclosure quality and (2) 
strengthening investor protection, both by fostering model transparency (e.g., explaining data 
sources, training limits, and usage rules) and by building investors’ ability to understand and 
use models wisely. Our proposed diagnostic framework represents an initial step, offering a 
structured way for users to assess the quality of model outputs. By prompting the model to

2.1  
Data 
Our initial sample comprises 68,514 quarterly earnings announcements from firms in 
the intersection of Compustat, CRSP, and I/B/E/S, spanning four quarters before and four

(earnings press releases). This focus on full-text disclosures reduces the sample to 49,192

2.2  
Prompt Design 
We use prompts that approximate how non-experts would typically interact with a 
language model: simple, conversational, and free of technical jargon—essentially providing 
relevant information and asking direct questions.  
The prompts unfold in three stages. First, we introduce the overall task, specifying that 
GPT will analyze two types of inputs: narrative content from an earnings press release and 
tabular data from the accompanying financial statements. This establishes the scope and 
structure of the assignment. Second, we provide the cleaned narrative, extracted from the start 
of the release through the boilerplate forward-looking disclaimer section, with HTML tags and 
unstandardized financial tables removed. This helps restrict GPT’s narrative processing to the 
substantive, human-readable disclosure. Third, we supply balance sheet and income statement 
information, following a practice similar to recent studies (e.g., Kim, Muhn, and Nikolaev, 
2024). Specifically, GPT receives: (i) annual data—current and prior year for the balance sheet, 
and current plus two prior years for the income statement; and (ii) quarterly data11—current 
and prior quarter for the balance sheet, and current plus two prior quarters for the income 
statement, for a total of 10 financial statements. 
The model is then instructed to perform three related tasks: 
(1) Narrative selection – Identify the three most informative sentences for predicting 
future earnings and briefly justify each choice. 
(2) Quantitative reasoning – Conduct quantitative analysis using the tabular financial 
data and identify the three metrics (financial ratios or trends other than EPS) 
produced that are relevant to the task, showing formulas and calculations.

quarter information.

(3) Forecasting – Predict both the direction and the numerical value of one-year-ahead 
GAAP and non-GAAP EPS, along with a confidence score for each forecast. 
These tasks necessarily operate at different levels. Narrative salience is a sentence-level 
construct, as readers attend to cues such as readability and forward-looking orientation 
embedded in individual sentences. Quantitative reasoning, by contrast, is a firm–period activity 
that synthesizes ratios and trends across financial statements for a given quarter. Imposing a 
uniform unit could misrepresent at least one task: ratios are not sentence-level objects, and 
sentence-level salience is not captured by firm-level aggregates. Accordingly, we evaluate 
narrative selection at the firm–quarter–sentence level and quantitative reasoning at the firm–
quarter level. 
Spot-checks of GPT’s outputs suggest that the model follows instructions, generating 
coherent reasoning chains and relevant selections. For transparency and replicability, we 
provide the full prompts and sample responses in Internet Appendix Tables IA1–IA2. While 
our prompts are informal and less sophisticated relative to the guide-by-example, chain-of-
thought (CoT) prompting, they approximate natural non-expert usage. Prior work shows that 
while rigid demonstrations suit highly structured tasks such as arithmetic operations, minimal 
cues are often sufficient to elicit step-by-step reasoning, whereas lengthy exemplars may 
introduce distracting context.12 We therefore treat simple prompting as the baseline and assess 
robustness with CFA-inspired prompts in a supplementary test.

3.1 
Narrative Attention 
A central component of GPT’s forecasting process is to determine which parts of an 
earnings press release it should prioritize. These prioritization patterns are shaped by the 
model’s large-scale pretraining and fine-tuning and may mirror—but not exactly replicate—
those observed in human financial analysis. Understanding this process serves two purposes in 
our setting. First, it reveals which narrative attributes GPT tends to prioritize when distilling 
lengthy disclosures into focal inputs. Second, it allows us to evaluate whether these attributes 
are associated with variation in GPT’s forecasting performance.

3.2 
Quantitative Reasoning 
A second dimension of GPT’s information processing is its engagement with structured, 
tabular financial data. In our prompt design, GPT reviews standardized balance sheet and 
income statement data after analyzing the narrative disclosures, and is then instructed to 
perform a quantitative analysis and report three non-EPS financial metrics (e.g., ratios or trends)

13 
 
it deems most relevant for forecasting. These metrics are produced in the course of forming the 
forecast. While they may not constitute intermediate “reasoning steps” in a strict sense, they 
nevertheless offer a useful window into the model’s numerical reasoning patterns. 
This analysis is motivated by the observation that, despite the view that LLMs struggle 
broadly with quantitative reasoning in financial contexts, little is known about the conditions 
under which such reasoning performs well or poorly. By examining the financial metrics GPT 
identifies, we can examine whether its quantitative selections resemble measures that 
professional analysts emphasize. This approach moves beyond testing whether GPT can “do 
the math” to evaluating its ability to identify which metrics warrant attention—a core aspect of 
expert financial judgment. 
To benchmark GPT’s metric selections, we compare them with the top three non-EPS 
metrics forecasted by sell-side analysts recorded in the I/B/E/S Detail database for the same 
firm–quarter. Because analysts’ metric choices reflect accumulated domain expertise and 
established practice, the degree of overlap provides a proxy for GPT’s ability to exert expert 
judgment. We capture this alignment with MetricOverlap, defined as the number of 
overlapping metrics between GPT and analysts, ranging from zero to three.  
Panel A of Internet Appendix Table IA3 reports that GPT identifies “top three” metrics

Our main analysis focuses on numerical forecast accuracy, as producing correct EPS 
figures represents the most stringent and information-rich test of forecasting ability.

5 
A Diagnostic Framework 
The preceding analyses show that GPT’s observable processing features—its narrative 
focus, quantitative reasoning, and self-assessed confidence—are systematically associated with 
variation in forecast accuracy. These features provide rare and measurable insights into the 
model’s reasoning process. While users typically see only the final output, the intermediate 
steps—what GPT emphasizes and how confident it is—offer observable signals about the 
quality of the underlying analysis. Linking these processing features to forecast performance 
provides a structured approach to anticipating when model-generated information is likely to 
be more or less reliable. 
Building on this insight, we propose a simple diagnostic framework that translates these 
observable processing features into a composite reliability score. Specifically, we construct an 
additive scoring system based on three dimensions: 
1) Narrative attention (ranging from 0 to 3): one point each if GPT’s selected sentences


## Page 28


27 
 
are more readable, more forward-looking, and more positively toned than the average 
sentence in the same press release. 
2) Quantitative reasoning (ranging from 0 to 3): one point for each financial metric 
identified by GPT that overlaps with those emphasized by sell-side analysts for the 
same firm and period, reflecting convergence with expert judgment. 
3) Confidence (ranging from 0 to 1): one point if GPT’s self-reported confidence score is 
above the sample median of 0.7. This indicator likely reflects aspects of model 
processing not captured by narrative attention or quantitative reasoning.  
The resulting reliability score ranges from 0 to 7. The distribution is approximately bell-
shaped, with 0.6, 3.9, 14.4, 23.7, 25.6, 20.2, 9.6, and 1.9 percent of forecasts receiving scores 
from zero to seven, respectively. Most observations cluster near the midpoint, and forecasts at 
the extremes are rare. This underscores the usefulness of diagnostic screening in identifying 
conditions that warrant greater caution. 
Figure 2 plots average forecast error across reliability scores for the full sample (Panel 
A) and for subsamples partitioned by firm size and analyst following (Panels B and C, 
respectively). Due to the low frequency of score zero, we combine scores zero and one in the 
figures. In all cases, the relation is monotonic: forecasts with higher scores are consistently 
more accurate. The relation is economically meaningful. Panel A shows that GPT’s forecast 
error declines from 0.13 at the lowest reliability scores (0, 1) to 0.03 at seven, consistent with 
GPT’s forecasting performance improving at higher reliability levels and converging toward 
analyst accuracy. More importantly, we find a consistent pattern across subsamples, supporting 
the value of this diagnostic framework for firms operating in various information environments. 
It also suggests that the reliability score captures underlying variation in GPT’s information-
processing quality rather than firm-specific features or idiosyncratic noise. 
This framework provides an interpretable and scalable approach to assessing when


## Page 29


28 
 
GPT’s forecasts are more likely to be reliable. Conceptually, it transforms otherwise opaque 
model behavior into a set of observable, economically meaningful indicators—much like 
evaluating a chef’s skill by observing their preparation rather than waiting solely for the final 
dish.  
For researchers, this offers an empirical approach to capturing differences in model 
reasoning that relate to output quality and, more broadly, a means of quantifying a new 
dimension of information friction. For practitioners, it serves as a practical tool to screen model 
outputs and flag cases that may warrant additional scrutiny. For users, especially non-experts, 
it clarifies the sources of uncertainty in model output—uncertainty that, if unrecognized, may 
contribute to overreliance and widen informational disparities. 
Although developed in the context of earnings forecasts, the same logic may extend to 
other domains where models interpret both narrative and quantitative disclosures, such as credit 
analysis, valuation, compliance monitoring, etc. Future research can test whether similar 
diagnostic signals predict reliability in these settings and explore how such mechanisms might 
help mitigate the challenges users face when evaluating model outputs.  
 
6 
Conclusion 
Large language models such as GPT have the potential to democratize sophisticated 
financial analysis by lowering information-processing costs. But equal access does not 
necessarily lead to equal outcomes, as the locus of information frictions may shift from the cost 
of processing disclosures to the challenge of evaluating AI-generated outputs. This study 
examines how GPT processes financial disclosures and how observable aspects of that 
processing relate to output quality. In a realistic setting, i.e., forecasting one-year-ahead 
earnings from firms’ earnings press releases, we analyze GPT’s narrative attention and 
quantitative reasoning and link these processing patterns to variation in forecast accuracy, while


## Page 30


29 
 
refraining from causal claims. 
Two main insights emerge. First, GPT’s narrative attention is structured and consistent 
across firms, prioritizing sentences that are early, readable, forward-looking, and negatively 
toned. However, this consistency does not always translate into better accuracy: forecasts are 
more accurate when selected sentences are more readable and contain more guidance-related 
content but less accurate when GPT focuses on negatively toned sentences. Second, GPT’s 
quantitative reasoning is context-dependent, improving when quantitative disclosures are 
clearer and when proxies suggest greater exposure during training. Forecast accuracy increases 
when GPT’s selected metrics align with those emphasized by analysts, narrowing the gap with 
analyst benchmarks and challenging the view that LLMs perform uniformly poorly at 
numerical reasoning in financial contexts. GPT’s self-assessed confidence is also positively 
associated with its processing quality.  
Building on these results, we construct a diagnostic framework that aggregates three 
observable processing dimensions (i.e., narrative focus, quantitative alignment, and confidence) 
into a composite reliability score. The score provides a practical indicator of output reliability 
and captures a new form of information friction: the cost of evaluating model-generated 
information. While not a substitute for expert judgment, the resulting reliability score can alert 
users to contexts in which model-generated information merits greater caution. 
Our findings suggest that advances in analytical technology may reduce traditional 
information-processing costs while introducing new evaluation frictions that influence how 
information is used in capital markets. Future research could extend this framework to analyze 
how these frictions affect price efficiency, investor learning, and disclosure design, and to 
examine whether similar diagnostic signals emerge in other domains such as credit risk 
assessment, valuation, compliance monitoring, etc.

GPT’s Narrative Attention: 
Picked_By_GPT 
An indicator variable that equals 1 if a sentence is

The number of words in a sentence.

Readability of a sentence, calculated as the Gunning

The number of positive words minus the number of

Guidance 
An indicator variable that equals 1 if the sentence

MetricOverlap 
The number of overlapping financial metrics 
emphasized both by the GPT and analysts in IBES. 
We exclude the EPS and GPS metrics in IBES.

The total number of non-zero line items in the 
standardized quarterly and annual balance sheet and 
income statement that are fed to the GPT in our 
prompt. 
Benf_Law_Deviation 
Benford’s law describes the relative frequency 
distribution for leading digits of numbers in datasets. 
For all non-zero numbers in the standardized balance 
sheet and income statement that are fed to the GPT in 
our prompt for each firm-quarter, we compare their 
leading digits’ relative frequency to Benford’s law 
and calculate the average deviation for digits between 
1 and 9.
