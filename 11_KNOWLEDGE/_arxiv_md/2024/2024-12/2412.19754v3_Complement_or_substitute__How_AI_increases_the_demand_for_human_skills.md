Abstract 
This paper examines whether artificial intelligence (AI) acts as a substitute or 
complement to human labour, drawing on 12 million online job vacancies from the 
United States spanning 2018–2023. We adopt a two-pronged approach: first, 
analysing “internal effects” within roles explicitly requiring AI, and second, 
investigating “external effects” that arise when industries, occupations, and 
regions experience increases in AI demand. Our focus centres on whether 
complementary skills—such as digital literacy, teamwork, resilience, agility, or 
analytical thinking—become more prevalent and valuable as AI adoption grows. 
Results show that AI-focused roles are nearly twice as likely to require skills like 
resilience, agility, or analytical thinking compared to non-AI roles. Furthermore, 
these skills command a significant wage premium; data scientists, for instance, are 
offered 5–10% higher salaries if they also possess resilience or ethics capabilities. 
We observe positive spillover effects: a doubling of AI-specific demand across 
industries correlates with a 5% increase in demand for complementary skills, even 
outside AI-related roles. Conversely, tasks vulnerable to AI substitution, such as 
basic data skills or translation, exhibit modest declines in demand. However, the 
external effect is clearly net positive: Complementary effects are up to 1.7x larger 
than substitution effects. These results are consistent across economies, including 
the United Kingdom and Australia. Our findings highlight the necessity of 
reskilling workers in areas where human expertise remains increasingly valuable 
and ensuring workers can effectively complement and leverage emerging AI 
technologies.

Introduction 
Does technology diminish or strengthen the role of humans in the world of 
work? We ask this question as recent technological advancements and their adoption 
have significant implications for labour markets (e.g., Acemoglu et al., 2021; Acemoglu 
& Autor, 2011; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020b; Autor et al., 2003; Bessen, 2019; 
Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014; Frank et al., 2019). Much prior work has focused on 
automation potential, offering substantial evidence for the use of artificial intelligence 
(AI) in substituting routine cognitive and manual skills (e.g., Autor et al., 2003; Autor 
& Dorn, 2013). More recent evidence suggests these effects now extend to non-routine 
cognitive tasks that were traditionally the domain of highly educated and highly paid 
workers (Boussioux et al., 2024; Geerling et al., 2023; Girotra et al., 2023; Kung et 
al., 2023). Conversely, a smaller body of literature documents augmentation effects 
such as improved productivity or efficiency in analytical, programming, or writing tasks 
(Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020b; Dell’Acqua et al., 2023; Eloundou et al., 2023; Noy & 
Zhang, 2023), alongside new complementation effects, namely higher demand or 
valuation for certain skills used in tandem with AI (Alekseeva et al., 2021; Stephany & 
Teutloff, 2024). In sum, there remains little consensus on precisely which skills AI 
systems excel at, and thus little consensus on the specific labour market outcomes they 
might produce (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). This research seeks to clarify the 
nature of AI-driven labour market effects—particularly these less-understood 
complementation effects—and to illuminate how and where such changes occur. 
In order to balance the benefits and concerns associated with “AI at work” 
(Brynjolfsson et al., 2023), researchers, policymakers, employers, and job-seekers 
alike require robust empirical insights into these issues. To date, however, much prior 
work has relied on speculative forecasting (e.g., Agrawal et al., 2019; Webb, 2019) or 
on expert opinion and theoretical models, which may rest on assumptions that are 
neither validated nor universally accepted (Yilmaz et al., 2023). This limitation 
restricts the practical applicability of such work for policymakers seeking to inform 
labour market policy, employers engaging in strategic workforce planning, and 
job-seekers aiming to enhance their prospects. Moreover, given the very recent surge in 
AI adoption (McKinsey, 2023; World Economic Forum, 2023), there have so far been

with investigations into other technologies. Building on research linking technology 
adoption to shifts in skills demand (e.g., Acemoglu et al., 2021) and in line with 
skill-biased technological change theory, we posit that artificial intelligence may both 
substitute and complement certain skills. 
Gathering empirical evidence on technology’s labour market impacts is 
challenging (Frank et al., 2019). Following Acemoglu et al. (2021) and Deming and 
Noray (2020), we assume that changes in skills demand and valuation are observable 
through job vacancy postings, as employers signal their evolving requirements and 
willingness to pay for specific skills. This study therefore leverages a dataset of 
approximately 12 million (n=11,729,662) online job vacancy postings from the 
United States, covering January 2018 to December 2023. Scraped from more than 
65,000 websites—including national and local job boards, corporate career sites, and 
job vacancy aggregators (Lightcast Data, n.d.)—these postings represent the 
“near-universe” of online vacancies (Acemoglu et al., 2021, p.1). In the pages that 
follow, we address a key question for economists and policymakers alike: whether AI 
technologies are leading to substitution (i.e., reducing work) or complementation (i.e., 
increasing demand for particular types of work). 
Turning to our findings, we first show that demand for AI roles in our dataset 
has risen more steeply than overall labour demand. Next, we reveal a substantial 
increase in the demand for skills seen as complementary to AI—such as digital literacy, 
teamwork, and resilience and agility—along with a growing wage premium for these 
skills within AI-focused roles, for example among data scientists. Conversely, skills 
considered more easily substituted by AI, such as customer service, summarisation, 
and text review, have declined in both popularity and value within AI-related positions. 
We then delve deeper into complementary skills, demonstrating that demand and wage 
premiums are particularly pronounced for some of these, and that specific combinations 
of AI and complementary skills appear especially popular and profitable. We illustrate 
these compounding effects with examples of AI roles, such as data scientists. 
In the second half of the paper, we expand our analysis to examine external 
effects across the broader economy, establishing that AI shapes both complementary

indicates that the complementarity effect is considerably stronger than the substitution 
effect. We replicate this analysis for the United Kingdom and Australia, confirming the 
robustness of our findings. Finally, we conclude by discussing the broader implications 
for policymakers, employers, and job-seekers.

Literature Review 
Technological Change and Labour Market Evolution 
​Whether AI complements or substitutes human labour is a core debate in the 
literature. Technological change has repeatedly reshaped labour markets, from the 
mechanisation of the Industrial Revolution to today’s digital transformations, each 
redefining workforce skills and economic landscapes (Frey, 2019; Goldin & Katz, 
2007b; Schwab, 2017). During the Industrial Revolution, production methods 
modernised and manufacturing jobs emerged, triggering urbanisation, population 
growth, and a reconfiguration of economic structures (Stearns, 2020). In the 20th 
century, scientific progress, electrification, and automation accelerated mass 
production, especially in manufacturing, where robotics replaced human labour in some 
routine tasks (Xu et al., 2018). At the same time, the service sector expanded, 
generating new employment opportunities (Urquhart, 1984). These shifts reflect how 
technology can simultaneously destroy certain types of jobs while creating others. 
The “Information Age” (Castells, 1997; Heeks, 1999), powered by 
semiconductors and computing (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg, 1995), made information 
technology a core driver of productivity, innovation, and competitiveness (Castells, 
1997). More recently, the “fourth industrial revolution” has seen AI, increasing 
computing power, and cyber-physical systems (e.g., IoT) reshape labour markets 
(Autor et al., 1998; Schwab, 2017). Research shows that while these technologies can 
enhance productivity (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2020b) or redirect workers to more 
meaningful tasks (Parmer, 2023), concerns remain about negative effects such as wage 
inequality (Autor et al., 1998; Krueger, 1993), worker displacement (Acemoglu & 
Restrepo, 2020a; Frey & Osborne, 2017), and labour market polarisation 
(Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014). Though warnings about job loss to new technologies 
regularly recur (Stephany & Teutloff, 2024), there is increasing urgency to understand 
how ongoing AI advancements affect employment and wages (McKinsey, 2023; World 
Economic Forum, 2023).

Understanding AI’s Impact on Skills and Work 
Studies specifically examining AI’s labour market effects on skills remain 
relatively few (Acemoglu et al., 2021; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023). Notable recent 
contributions include experimental research demonstrating AI’s ability to augment 
human workers’ writing tasks (Noy & Zhang, 2023) and consultancy-based knowledge 
work (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023). Other studies point to substitution, such as AI’s impact 
on security analysts’ employment and wages (Grennan & Michaely, 2020) and 
evidence of AI start-ups automating routine work to reduce labour costs (Bessen et al., 
2023). These findings highlight the tension between augmentation and automation and 
underscore the importance of disentangling these effects. 
Debates about whether AI substitutes or complements human work are central 
to discussions on the future of employment. Early occupation-based research (e.g., 
Bessen, 2016) helped spark interest in technology’s impact on jobs but was criticised 
for treating occupations as homogeneous (Frank et al., 2019). A subsequent 
task-based approach (e.g., Arntz et al., 2016; Autor et al., 2003; Autor & Handel, 
2013) showed how specific tasks—such as formal reasoning—within certain 
professional and technical roles (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011) can be automated or 
augmented. Finally, the skills-based approach (e.g., Acemoglu & Autor, 2011) posits 
that the degree to which a role is automatable or augmentable depends on its skill 
composition, recognising that occupations are best seen as bundles of skills (Frank et 
al., 2019; Stephany & Teutloff, 2024). 
Substitution, Augmentation, and Skill Complementarities in the AI Era 
Substantial evidence supports AI’s capacity to automate routine manual and 
cognitive tasks (Autor et al., 2003; Autor & Dorn, 2013). More recent work suggests 
this extends to non-routine cognitive activities, including problem-solving and creative 
output once exclusive to highly educated, well-paid workers (Boussioux et al., 2024; 
Girotra et al., 2023; Kung et al., 2023). At the same time, AI may also augment 
knowledge work by improving efficiency, accuracy, and speed for analytical, 
programming, or writing tasks (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023; Eloundou et al., 2023; Noy & 
Zhang, 2023), while driving demand for new skill sets (Bessen, 2016). Studies further

document higher demand or valuation for skills that complement AI (Alekseeva et al., 
2021; Stephany & Teutloff, 2024). Such complementarities are explored by, for 
instance, measuring skill co-occurrences in job requirements (Anderson, 2017) or 
tracking how knowledge clusters shape workforce polarisation (Alabdulkareem et al., 
2018). These findings emphasise that skills and technology are interlinked through 
intricate interdependencies (Dibiaggio et al., 2014; Neffke, 2019). 
Complementation and substitution can manifest in two ways: popularity 
(demand) and valuation (wages). Skills may remain in demand but experience 
devaluation, or they may become less common while retaining high wage premiums. 
For example, seminal research links wage differentials to skill-biased technological 
change (Goldin & Katz, 2007a), and computerisation has been shown to increase wage 
inequality (Bessen, 2016). It is likely both substitution and complementation coexist, 
meaning AI can diminish demand for some skills while raising demand for others 
(Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018b; Autor et al., 2003). As Acemoglu et al. succinctly put 
it: 
“…if different tasks require different skills, then the adoption of AI technologies 
may also change the set of skills that exposed establishments demand (and list in 
their vacancies). Skills relevant for tasks now performed by algorithms will be 
demanded less frequently, and new skills necessary for working alongside AI 
algorithms may also start being included in vacancies” (Acemoglu et al., 2021, 
p.8) 
Unresolved Questions in AI’s Skill Impacts 
Scholars have highlighted two key shortcomings in the existing literature. First, 
although the studies cited here adhere to rigorous research standards, much of the 
broader discourse—including media coverage—relies on theoretical models, expert 
opinions, and predictions whose assumptions may not be validated or widely accepted 
(Yilmaz et al., 2023). Numerous works seeking to forecast substitution effects of AI 
(e.g., Agrawal et al., 2019; Webb, 2019) produce widely varying estimates. This can 
introduce bias for at least two reasons: there is no shared expert consensus on whether 
AI complements or substitutes human skills (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Frank et

al., 2019), and experts often underestimate the diverse skill sets within occupations, 
leading to potential over- or underestimations (Arntz et al., 2016). Gathering empirical 
evidence to test and refine hypotheses is notably difficult. As Frank et al. (2019, 
p.6531) observe, “major barriers that inhibit scientists from measuring the effects of 
AI on the future of work … include the lack of high-quality data about the nature of 
work … [and] lack of empirically informed models of key microlevel processes.” 
Second, there is little agreement on which skills AI excels at and the labour 
market outcomes that result, fuelling debates about which tasks, occupations, and skill 
sets are affected, to what extent, and how (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018b; Autor et al., 
2003; Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017). The strongest evidence suggests a complex 
interplay of both substitution and complementarity: skills replicable by AI may face 
diminishing demand, while those critical for working alongside AI see rising demand 
(Acemoglu et al., 2021).  
Building on previous research linking technology adoption to shifts in skill 
demands (Acemoglu et al., 2021), and mindful of the aforementioned limitations, this 
paper seeks to enhance understanding of this emerging field. Specifically, we 
investigate whether growing demand for AI-centric work not only reshapes skill 
requirements within AI-intensive roles but also exerts broader influence on related 
labour markets. More concretely, we ask: Does increasing AI demand boost both the 
demand and valuation of AI-complementary skills—within AI-focused positions and 
across neighbouring occupations, industries, and regions? 
Hypotheses 
The growing integration of AI into economic activity profoundly affects labour 
market skill requirements. We conceptualise these shifts through “internal” and 
“external” effects: 
1.​ Internal effects operate within AI-intensive roles, requiring new bundles of 
skills directly tied to AI tasks. 
2.​ External effects spill over into the broader workforce—affecting other workers 
within the same occupation, competing firms in an industry, and entire 
regions—even when those workers do not directly engage with AI.

Internal Effects 
In roles that directly interact with AI (e.g., data scientists, machine learning 
engineers), increasingly complex and opaque models demand stronger ethical reasoning 
to mitigate biases and unintended consequences, greater emphasis on algorithmic 
accountability, and higher levels of digital literacy and adaptability. Economically, 
these changes reflect cross-derivative complementarities in the worker’s personal 
“production function,” where one skill (e.g., ethics) becomes more valuable when 
another (e.g., AI technical proficiency) gains importance. As AI-based tasks 
proliferate, the positive cross-partial derivatives indicate that AI skills enhance returns 
to complementary non-technical skills. 
External Effects at the Occupation, Industry, and Regional Levels 
Outside AI-specific roles, skill demands evolve through spillover effects. At the 
occupational level, workers who do not directly interface with AI may still require 
basic digital literacy or familiarity with AI outputs to interpret and act on insights 
generated by colleagues. At the industry level, competitive pressures from AI-adopting 
firms can push other companies to invest in raising their overall technical proficiency, 
as well as soft skills—like teamwork, resilience, and ethics—that facilitate rapid, 
responsible responses to AI-driven changes. At the regional level, AI hubs (e.g., in 
autonomous driving or AI-led recruitment) can shift local labour market dynamics, 
encouraging broader digital upskilling and awareness of AI’s ethical and social 
implications. Conceptually, these spillovers reflect positive cross-partial derivatives at 
the firm and regional levels, where an AI-intensive environment raises the marginal 
value of digital, teamwork, resilience, and ethics skills across diverse roles. 
To capture both “within-role” and “beyond-role” dimensions of AI’s labour 
market impact, we propose: 
Internal Effects (Within AI Roles) 
●​ H1a: Roles requiring AI skills also exhibit higher demand for 
AI-complementary skills. 
●​ H1b: Complementary skills in AI-intensive roles command higher wages.

to illuminate how technological change affects immediate job requirements while

Methods and Data 
Data 
Following prior work, we assume that employers’ online vacancy postings reflect 
evolving skill demands and valuations (Acemoglu et al., 2021; Deming & Noray, 
2020). We leverage a dataset of approximately 12 million (n=11,729,662) U.S. 
online job postings from January 2018 to December 2023, created by Lightcast and 
provided by the Burning Glass Institute (BGI). These postings are scraped from over 
65,000 websites—including job boards, company career pages, and aggregators 
(Lightcast Data, n.d.)—capturing the “near-universe” of online vacancies (Acemoglu et 
al., 2021, p.1). Each posting contains information such as job title, occupation 
category, salary, and a detailed skill list derived from the text. 
Lightcast data have previously facilitated studies on technology’s labour market 
effects (e.g., Alekseeva et al., 2021; Acemoglu et al., 2021; Ehlinger & Stephany, 
2023; Hazell & Taska, 2020; Babina et al., 2024; Hershbein & Kahn, 2018). 
Extensive tests confirm that Lightcast data mirror U.S. vacancy trends from JOLTS 
and the BLS, and the dataset aligns closely with state-level BLS statistics (see 
Appendix Tables 1–2). Although there is some overrepresentation of professional and 
technical occupations relative to blue-collar jobs—an established characteristic of 
online postings data (Carnevale et al., 2014)—our focus on knowledge work and 
comparative skill shares minimises any potential biases. On average, the dataset differs 
from official BLS figures by only 3%.

“substitutable” skill clusters—Basic Data Skills, Summary and Reporting, Language 
and Text Review, Customer Service, and Office and Financial Administration—and 
seven “complementary” skill clusters—Analytical Thinking, Digital Literacy, 
Resilience and Agility, Technical Proficiency, Ethics, Self-Efficiency, and Working

We labelled a posting as demanding substitutable or complementary skills if it

Defining AI roles 
Following a skills-based approach (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011; Alekseeva et al., 
2021; Ehlinger & Stephany, 2023), we identified AI-related roles by the presence of at 
least one AI skill requirement in the job posting. This method avoids misclassifying 
non-AI positions within AI-focused firms (e.g., a Human Resources Manager at an AI 
company) as AI roles. 
First, we created a classification of AI skills—labelled “AI User” or “AI Creator” 
(115 skills) by building on  an existing classification provided by BGI and then refined 
each skill’s subcategory in line with established definitions (Russell & Norvig, 2016; 
Squicciarini & Nachtigall, 2021). Skills with unclear or broad descriptions were 
verified by listing the occupations demanding them at the SOC_5 level, ensuring 
correct classification (e.g., “ChatGPT” was reclassified as an NLP AI User skill after 
analysis showed it was mostly used by writers and editors). The final scheme includes 
eight AI skill categories (see Appendix Table 6): 
1.​ Machine Learning Creators

Methods  
2018-2023 comparison 
We begin by conducting a time series analysis to observe how skill demand and 
salaries evolve. First, we compare demand for complementary and substitutable skills 
in AI roles against non-AI roles from 2018 to 2023. We restrict the sample to 
knowledge work occupations, allowing for a more relevant comparison than if we 
included blue-collar or agricultural jobs, which seldom require skills like office 
administration or technical proficiency. For the start and end year of our sample, we 
calculate the share of postings (in AI vs. non-AI roles) requiring complementary or 
substitutable skills. Next, we repeat this approach for salary, comparing 
log-transformed salary in AI roles demanding complementary or substitutable skills 
with those in non-AI roles.

Findings 
Part A: Internal Effects 
In this section, we compare how complementary and substitute (substitutable) 
skills manifest in AI versus non-AI roles. Complementary skills—such as digital 
literacy, analytical thinking, ethics, or technical proficiency—either enable creating 
and/or using AI systems or are augmented by AI tools to increase collective 
effectiveness (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018a; Autor, 2015). Substitutable skills refer 
to tasks or abilities that AI systems can perform with little or no human intervention, 
often routine or rule-based tasks, including basic data processing, text synthesis, and 
grammar (Frey & Osborne, 2017). The U.S. Bureau of Labour Statistics (BLS) 
projects that while office, administrative, sales, design, and legal occupations may see 
reduced labour demand due to AI substitution, computer and mathematical 
occupations—where most AI roles lie—will be among the fastest-growing through 
2033 (BLS, 2024c). 
Against 
this backdrop, we identify a notable rise in demand for 
AI-complementary skills, accompanied by an increasing wage premium for such skills 
within AI roles. By contrast, substitutable skills have diminished in both prevalence 
and value. We also find that certain complementary skills—especially analytical 
thinking, resilience, agility, and technical proficiency—are doubly in demand and offer 
higher earnings, creating a “compounding effect.”

Part B: External Effects 
In the second half of this paper, we examine external effects across the broader 
economy, showing that AI influences both complementary and substitutable skills even 
outside AI-specific roles. We hypothesise that rising AI adoption (i.e., a higher 
prevalence of AI roles) within an occupation, industry, or region increases the demand 
for complementary skills and decreases the need for substitutable skills—even in 
non-AI positions. For instance, as AI becomes more integral to autonomous vehicle 
technology, urban planners may be in higher demand to design cities optimised for 
self-driving systems, while traditional roles like traffic signal maintenance could decline 
as AI-driven traffic management negates the need for physical signal infrastructure. 
Our findings support this hypothesis at the occupational, industry, and regional 
levels (Table IV and Figure VI). Growth in AI roles has a positive effect on roles 
demanding complementary skills. A 1% increase in AI postings corresponds to a 
0.054% rise in complementary non-AI postings (p < 0.05). Thus, a doubling of AI 
postings—such as occurred from 2018 to 2023—aligns with a 5.4% increase in 
complementary non-AI roles at the industry level. Conversely, a 1% increase in AI 
postings correlates with a 0.019% decline in substitutable non-AI postings (1.9% for a 
doubling), although this result is not statistically significant (p = 0.51). Over time, 
growth in AI roles is linked to a higher number of non-AI roles demanding

Discussion 
In this paper, we present empirical evidence for both substitution and 
complementation effects driven by artificial intelligence (AI), observable in skill 
demand and compensation. Crucially, these effects occur not only within AI-focused 
jobs but also beyond them—across different occupations, industries, and regions. By 
examining AI’s impact at a granular, skills-based level and drawing on real-world job 
postings data, this research offers a more precise view of AI’s influence on the labour 
market than broader occupation-level analyses or forecasting studies reliant on expert 
opinion or theoretical models. 
Internal Effects 
Demand 
Our regression results indicate a positive association between AI roles and 
demand for complementary skills, alongside a negative association between AI roles 
and demand for substitutable skills. This finding provides preliminary evidence that AI 
adoption not only complements certain knowledge-worker skills, elevating their 
demand, but also substitutes others, thereby lowering their demand. Although these 
results do not establish causality, they guide future research directions and underscore 
the strategic importance of workforce planning and skill development for policymakers, 
organisations, and individuals. The data further show that job postings requiring AI 
technologies have proportionally higher demand for complementary knowledge-worker 
skills and proportionally lower demand for substitutable skills, which is noteworthy 
given that non-AI roles already exhibit a strong baseline demand for complementary 
skills (close to 70%). Taken together, the observation that AI creation or usage 
coincides with co-occurring complementation and substitution supports the need for 
policy interventions such as structured reskilling initiatives, transitional programmes, 
and potential adjustments in social security systems to address wage compression or 
worker displacement. These patterns also highlight the importance of curricular 
reforms to incorporate analytical, digital, and interpersonal competencies that prepare

future employees for AI-augmented roles, especially where human-AI collaboration 
drives productivity and innovation. 
In addition, our findings point to three further contributions. First, they suggest 
that particularly strong complementarities exist at a granular level. Examples include 
technical proficiency in machine learning creation, natural language processing 
creation, and computer vision and image processing jobs (84–247% higher demand, p 
< 0.05), or digital literacy in data-intensive AI development and operations (33% 
higher, p < 0.05), and resilience and agility in rapidly evolving machine learning user 
roles (92% higher, p < 0.05). Conversely, the data also reveal particularly strong 
substitution effects, such as basic data skills in roles using AI applications and tools 
(-86%, p < 0.05), or summary and reporting in NLP jobs, which commonly involve 
generative technologies like ChatGPT (-78%, p < 0.05). Identifying these strong 
complementarities in specific technical skill sets highlights areas for strategic 
investment, while pinpointing strong substitution effects reveals where automation 
poses the greatest risk. 
Second, the findings reflect recent shifts in demand, notably the growing 
emphasis on ethics skills across many AI role categories, which may indicate a broader 
societal and regulatory move toward responsible AI development and deployment. 
Third, the results show that while AI creator roles have consistently required 
higher levels of complementary skills, AI user roles have been slower to adopt these 
skills but eventually caught up and, by 2022, reached parity with AI creator roles. 
This signals a maturation of AI technologies: as they become embedded in everyday 
workflows, even non-technical roles need to develop AI-complementary capabilities. 
Organisations and policymakers must therefore invest in education and training to 
expand access to these skills across the broader labour market. 
Valuation of skills 
Assuming that complementation and substitution effects manifest in how skills 
are both demanded and valued, we turned to examine worker compensation. Overall, 
our findings support the idea that vacancies for AI-related jobs requiring substitutable 
skills tend to have lower salaries than AI jobs without such requirements, but they do

results reveal small negative salary effects linked to both complementary and 
substitutable skills (approximately -8% and -4%, respectively), underscoring the 
complex nature of wage determination in an environment shaped by technological

Further investigation of substitutable skills shows that the wage penalty is 
highest (7–8%, p < 0.05) for basic data skills, customer service, and office and 
financial administration skills in computer vision and image processing roles. This 
pattern may reflect broader wage compression resulting from technological advances 
(Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018a). As routine tasks—such as basic data processing, 
summarising and reporting, and office or financial administration—become automated, 
workers in roles dependent on these skills may face downward pressure on wages due 
to diminished demand and reduced bargaining power. 
Conversely, a few complementary skill sets exhibit small wage gains in certain 
AI contexts, such as technical proficiency in prediction and analytics roles or resilience 
and agility in machine learning user jobs (each around +1%, p < 0.01). However, on 
the whole, our results do not show a clear wage premium for complementary skills in 
AI roles, contrasting with assertions that tech workers’ social and collaborative skills 
should command higher pay (Edinger, 2013). While complementary skills remain in 
high demand for AI positions, their direct influence on salary may be tempered by 
broader economic forces and employer-specific factors. Future research could 
investigate this nuanced link between skill complementarity and wages to inform 
strategies for equitable compensation in AI-driven workplaces. 
External Effects 
Finally, our findings show that AI adoption generates substantial external 
effects at the occupational, industry, and regional levels. Growth in AI roles 
corresponds with increased demand for non-AI complementary roles and a decline in 
non-AI substitutable roles. This pattern appears across all three levels of analysis but 
is most pronounced regionally, where doubling AI roles is associated with a 
statistically significant 3.0% rise in non-AI complementary roles and a 5.8% decline in

correlates with a 5.4% increase in non-AI complementary roles. Notably, the 
complementarity effect exceeds the substitution effect, and the size of these effects 
varies by industry. In Management and Business Operations and Computer and 
Mathematical Occupations, for instance, more than 1.5 and 1.7 complementary roles, 
respectively, are gained for each substitutable role lost. 
To our knowledge, these results are among the first to quantify the external 
spillovers linked to AI adoption. They suggest that labour markets are being subtly 
reshaped as demand shifts toward complementary skills even outside direct AI roles, 
challenging narratives of net displacement and highlighting the nuanced impact of AI 
on the workforce. 
The implications of these findings are multifaceted. For individuals, they 
underscore the importance of acquiring AI-complementary skills—such as technical 
proficiency, analytical thinking, and ethical frameworks—to navigate emerging labour 
market demands. For companies, the results emphasise the need for proactive 
workforce planning and reskilling investments. For policymakers, regional spillover 
effects point to the importance of targeted education and workforce development in 
high-AI-adoption areas, alongside safety nets and incentives to mitigate local 
disparities for displaced workers.  
Limitations 
Finally, this study faces several limitations. First, as noted by Acemoglu et al. 
(2021) and Carnevale et al. (2014), online vacancy postings tend to overrepresent 
professional and technical occupations relative to blue-collar jobs. Moreover, the data 
exclude alternative hiring channels, such as headhunting, offline advertisements, 
external contractors, or in-house upskilling (Ehlinger & Stephany, 2023). 
Second, skills requirements in postings may not perfectly reflect the actual skills 
used after hire. For instance, an employee’s skill usage might shift in response to 
market conditions or organisational changes. While we focus on demand and 
compensation at the point of recruitment, readers should keep these possible post-hire

Still, these postings represent employers’ willingness to pay at the recruitment stage 
and are thus pertinent for this study’s focus on valuation (Ehlinger & Stephany, 
2023). 
Fourth, when working with large datasets, even small effects can seem 
statistically significant. We ran 1,000 demand regressions on 1,000 random samples 
(each n=10,000) to check robustness. Coefficients for complementary skills ranged 
from about -1.0 to 1.5 (mean = 0.217, close to the main result of 0.195), whereas 
substitutable skills ranged from -2.5 to 0.5 (mean = -0.492, close to -0.427). P-values 
spanned 0.0 to 1.0. However, AI roles remain relatively scarce (47,871) compared to 
the entire sample (11,729,662). In smaller samples of 10,000 postings, only a few AI 
roles may appear, making significant results less likely. This scarcity motivates using a 
large dataset. By combining regression with descriptive analysis and smaller 
sub-cluster studies, we aimed to mitigate this limitation. We also applied cautious 
interpretation, emphasising real-world relevance. 
Fifth, these findings do not establish causality. Yet by identifying associations 
and patterns among variables, they contribute to the broader debate on how the labour 
market is evolving under technological change. Future experimental or longitudinal 
studies may clarify the causal mechanisms suggested here. 
Lastly, our dataset primarily covers United States job postings. Since 
technology’s labour market effects differ across countries (Arntz et al., 2016), these 
findings may not fully generalise elsewhere. Additional research should explore 
regional differences to provide a fuller picture. 
Conclusion 
This study aimed to expand our understanding of AI’s internal and external 
effects on knowledge-worker skills—specifically, how AI complements certain 
competencies (e.g., technology literacy, ethics, analytical thinking) while potentially

substituting others (e.g., basic data tasks, office and financial administration). 
Following Acemoglu et al. (2021) and Deming and Noray (2020), we relied on job 
postings—employers’ “footprints” indicating evolving skill demands and wage 
offers—to analyse around 12 million online vacancies. 
Our work illuminates the relationship between AI creation/usage and the 
complementarity or substitution of knowledge-worker skills, yielding five key 
contributions. First, we find that AI roles are positively associated with demand for 
complementary skills and negatively associated with demand for substitutable skills. 
Second, job postings that include AI requirements display proportionally more requests 
for complementary skills and fewer requests for substitutable skills compared to 
non-AI postings. Third, we identify powerful complementarities between particular AI 
roles and clusters of complementary skills, as well as strong substitution effects for 
certain AI roles and substitutable skills—most notably an increasing emphasis on 
ethics across many AI categories. Fourth, we show that AI jobs demanding 
substitutable skills carry lower salaries than AI jobs without such requirements; basic 
data skills, customer service, and office/financial administration skills in computer 
vision and image processing roles suffer the highest wage penalty. However, we find no 
evidence of a wage premium for complementary skills. Finally, we uncover external 
complementation effects: as AI roles grow, the number of non-AI roles that demand 
complementary skills also rises. Crucially, the complementarity effect exceeds the 
substitution effect. 
Future research directions include extending our analysis to other regions (e.g., 
Europe and Asia) to capture potential geographic variations, employing qualitative 
methods (such as interviews with recruitment managers) to explore perceptions of skill 
demand and valuation, and pursuing experimental or longitudinal studies to establish 
causality. As debates on the benefits and risks of “AI at work” (Brynjolfsson et al., 
2023) persist, researchers, policymakers, employers, and job-seekers all stand to 
benefit from an empirically grounded understanding of these influential effects.
