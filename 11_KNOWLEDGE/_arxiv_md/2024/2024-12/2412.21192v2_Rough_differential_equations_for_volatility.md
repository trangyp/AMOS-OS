Rough differential equations for volatility
Ofelia Bonesini · Emilio Ferrucci ·
Ioannis Gasteratos · Antoine Jacquier
Abstract We introduce a canonical way of performing the joint lift of a Brownian
motion W and a low-regularity adapted stochastic rough path X, extending [30]. Ap-
plying this construction to the case where X is the canonical lift of a one-dimensional
fractional Brownian motion (possibly correlated with W) completes the partial rough
path of [40]. We use this to model rough volatility with the versatile toolkit of rough
differential equations (RDEs), namely by taking the price and volatility processes to
be the solution to a single RDE. We argue that our framework is already of interest
when W and X are independent, as correlation between the price and volatility can
be introduced in the dynamics. The lead-lag scheme of [32] is extended to our frac-
tional setting as an approximation theory for the rough path in the correlated case.
Continuity of the solution map transforms it into a numerical scheme for RDEs. We
numerically test this framework and use it to calibrate a simple new rough volatility
model to market data.
Keywords Rough paths · rough volatility · rough differential equations · Wong-
Zakai approximations · lead-lag approximations · stochastic Volterra equations

1 Introduction
Stochastic volatility models describe the dynamics of asset prices subject to ran-
domness and whose instantaneous variance also evolves randomly in time. They
are typically expressed by a pair of stock price and variance processes governed
by a system of Itˆo stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and driven by correlated
Brownian motions; there, the log-price is a semimartingale while its instantaneous
variance is a Markovian diffusion process. While these models—among which the
Heston model [56], the Bergomi model [13] and the SABR model [52] are the most
popular—capture many market behaviours, they however suffer from notorious short-
comings (hard to fit short-dated smiles, many parameters to fit,...). In sharp contrast,
the rough volatility paradigm abandons the Markovian assumptions of the variance
process, replacing the latter by rough volatility, driven by a process of lower H¨older
regularity, such as fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with Hurst parameter H < 1
2, for
example in the Rough Bergomi model [6] or in [3], or, more generally, by a stochastic
Volterra process with singular kernels, as is the case for the rough Heston model [31]
and affine rough models [1].
Such models are not new per se, and strong inspiration came from the long-
memory models suggested in [22] and the literature on stochastic Volterra processes
[23,29]. They were however brought to light and developed in the H < 1
2 case in [3]
and [44], the latter coining the term rough volatility, and have since gained large
popularity due to their remarkable fit to market data and their ability to capture—
with relatively few parameters—the main stylised facts of the implied volatility sur-
face, including the steep short-maturity equity implied volatility skew. Calibration
of these models has pointed [44,6] towards very small values of the Hurst parame-
ter, around 0.1, implying a H¨older regularity of the sample paths of V significantly
rougher than in classical stochastic volatility models. Along with the absence of
Markovianity, this has resulted in several challenges due to the lack of available

it is natural to consider their analogue in the rough setting, which is provided by
rough differential equations (RDEs), not by singular Volterra DEs. This holds in the
technical sense of Wong-Zakai: every RDE is the limit of ODEs driven by smooth
paths approximating the rough path in an appropriate topology. In rough volatility,
an advantage of RDEs over Volterra DEs is that in the former, the price and vol can
be viewed as jointly solving the same equation, while in the latter they are solving a
“mixed Itˆo-Volterra equation” [5, §5.2].
One should nevertheless acknowledge the special role played by Volterra pro-
cesses and equations in rough volatility: given by Itˆo integrals, their mean and vari-
ance are easy to compute. While some rough volatility models (rough Heston in par-
ticular) are underpinned by microstructural justifications [60], this may not, however,
be universal, and their defining feature really is the irregularity of the volatility sample
paths, a feature that can be obtained alternatively with RDEs. Recently, in computa-
tional finance, statistical estimation of parameters has been reconsidered in the light
of black-box neural computations, which rely on non-parametric models as well as
on fast and reliable numerical schemes; for example, via the emerging literature on
neural SDEs in finance [25,46,21,20], amenable for ODEs and SDEs, but to a lesser
extent for Volterra equations (although see the recent [12]). Our goal here is not to
argue against established models in rough volatility, but rather to propose an alterna-
tive framework with numerous advantages and which includes the majority of rough
volatility models already present in the literature.

Our contributions can be summarised as follows:
– In Section 2 we introduce a novel way of performing the joint lift of an adapted
(sufficiently integrable) rough path and a Brownian motion, extending [30], in
Theorem 2.5 and Theorem 2.6.
– In Section 3 we specify the very general construction of the previous section to
the case in which the adapted rough path is the canonical lift of a one-dimensional
process. Most often, but not always, this will be a low regularity fBm, possibly
correlated with the Brownian motion. A new interpretation is given of the rough
path terms (Theorem 3.1). The general RDE used in the rest of paper is proposed
in (1.2), accompanied by several modelling considerations. We show how this
equation embeds many models already considered in the literature (Section 3.1),
as well as a very general way of parametrising new ones.
– In Section 4 we discuss the convergence of three different types of lagged approx-
imations. Section 4.1 is devoted to the convergence of piecewise-linear lead-lag
approximations with explicit rates. In Section 4.2, we extend this convergence
result to the case when X is given by a hybrid scheme approximation [11] of a
fractional Brownian motion. In passing, we also obtain a novel almost-sure con-
vergence result for the hybrid scheme approximation in H¨older topology (Theo-
rem 4.12). Finally, Section 4.3 deals with lagged mollifier approximations.
– In Section 5 we numerically validate the aforementioned theoretical results and
propose an RDE-based adaptation of the quadratic rough Heston model, which
we calibrate to market data.

governed by non-i.i.d. noise. Before diving into the details, we fix some frequently
used notations and provide an overview of RDE-based models considered in this
paper, as well as some of their flexibility and modelling advantages.

For financial purposes, S represents the asset price process and V the variance pro-
cess. This general class of asset price models offers significant flexibility in terms of
modelling choices. In particular,
1. It allows for different types of correlation between S and V: Usually, in (classical
and rough) volatility models, the Brownian motion driving S and the (fractional)
one driving V are correlated to account for the leverage effect. The RDE (1.2)
offers another way of achieving this by driving V both with the same factor used
for S and with another path X. Therefore the case X ⊥⊥W does not preclude S
and V from being correlated. Classical correlation can nevertheless be recovered
by correlating X and W and setting ς to zero. The models (1.2) thus encompass
both ways of introducing correlation, as well as any combination of them, for
which the construction of Section 2 is necessary.
2. It allows for path dependency of V on S: Allowing the coefficients of V to depend
on S is not problematic from a mathematical perspective. While atypical from a
modelling point of view, such a choice leads to a particular instance of (rough)
volatility models in which the spot volatility depends on the past price trajectory.
Path-dependent volatility models have been considered in [50] and have been
useful in replicating the Zumbach effect [79,80], namely the impact of historical
prices on the volatility.
3. The RDE (1.2) includes many volatility models considered in the literature as
special cases (and a more detailed discussion on these models and how they em-
bed in our framework is deferred to Section 3.1):
(a) Classical (local) stochastic volatility models such as Black-Scholes, Bergomi,
Heston, Stein-Stein.
(b) Rough volatility models such as (multifactor) rBergomi, rHeston, quadratic
rHeston models are (at least partially) recovered from (1.2) (see also Remark
3.5 below).
(c) Path-dependent stochastic volatility models such as Guyon-Lekeufack [50].
4. Extendability to multi-asset models and volatility with smooth non-Markovian
drivers. Even though we focus exclusively on single-asset models (S is real-
valued) it is possible to extend our framework to multi-asset models in which
