Dynamic Information Sub-Selection for Decision Support
Hung-Tien Huang, Maxwell Lennon, Shreyas Bhat Brahmavar, Sean Sylvia, & Junier B. Oliva
University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill
North Carolina, USA
Abstract
We introduce Dynamic Information Sub-
Selection (DISS), a novel framework of AI
assistance designed to enhance the perfor-
mance of black-box decision-makers by tai-
loring their information processing on a per-
instance basis.
Blackbox decision-makers
(e.g., humans or real-time systems) often face
challenges in processing all possible infor-
mation at hand (e.g., due to cognitive bi-
ases or resource constraints), which can de-
grade decision efficacy. DISS addresses these
challenges through policies that dynamically
select the most effective features and op-
tions to forward to the black-box decision-
maker for prediction. We develop a scalable
frequentist data acquisition strategy and a
decision-maker mimicking technique for en-
hanced budget efficiency. We explore several
impactful applications of DISS, including bi-
ased decision-maker support, expert assign-
ment optimization, large language model de-
cision support, and interpretability. Empiri-
cal validation of our proposed DISS method-
ology shows superior performance to state-of-
the-art methods across various applications.

Typical machine learning (ML) approaches process all
instance information consistently. However, computa-
tional costs, biases, and other limitations of decision-
makers often make it so that they are better served by
a per-instance dynamic adjustment of what features
are processed (and how to process them) when mak-
ing predictions. For example, human decision-makers

may actually do worse with more information on an

2020), which may limit efficacy dependent on the pre-
sented information. Moreover, unlike statistical mod-

LLM Decision Support
Recent research in Large

sible decisions with existing black-box LLMs such as
GPT-4, LlaMA, Qwen2.5B (W. X. Zhao et al., 2023).
Interpretability
The set of most relevant features
often varies from instance to instance. Prior works in
interpretable ML have leveraged this observation for a
better understanding of data and models (Saarela et
al., 2021). For example, existing methods often utilize
proxy measurements such as gradients (Y. Wang et
al., 2024) or model approximations (Lakkaraju et al.,
2017) to estimate feature importance for each instance.
DISS presents a novel, complementary approach for
presenting feature importance by directly producing a
dynamic subset of features solely responsible for the
model’s output whilst producing efficacious decisions.
A core principle of this work is a data-driven approach
that directly considers the efficacy of the BDM when
given various potential subsets of features (and other
options) for prediction on instances.
Therefore, we
pose DISS as a reward maximization problem: given
an instance, the DISS policy selects information and
options, passes its selection to the BDM, and obtains a
reward according to the efficacy of the decision made
by BDM (e.g., based on accuracy, confidence, time,
etc.).

Therefore, ob-
taining decision-making observations directly from the
BDM is vital to tuning the DISS policy. However, in
many applications, there are limitations to how many
decisions one can query from the BDM during training
due to restrictions in computational resources, time,
monetary incentives, etc.
Hence, this paper devel-
ops an acquisition strategy to effectively learn a policy
under budgeted observational constraints that chooses
the next query wisely based on past interactions with
BDM. Moreover, we show that we may make even bet-
ter use of our observations by exploiting the structure
of our problem through mimicking the BDM.
Contributions
We summarize our contributions as
follows:
1) we present a simple to implement fre-
quentist approach that enables the use of arbitrary
regressors to choose context-action pairs for learn-
ing DISS policies; 2) we further introduce and ana-
lyze a decision-maker mimicking approach that bet-
ter utilizes available data; 3) we frame multiple real-
world applications within our proposed DISS frame-
work; 4) we empirically show the efficacy of our meth-
ods against state-of-the-art methods under various set-
tings and datasets.

2.1
Problem Setting
Below, we formally introduce our task; We illustrate
this through a running example of clinical decision-

Decision Maker
We seek to maximize rewards for
the decisions of a BDM M that ingests: 1) arbitrary
subsets of available information on an instance x; 2)
additional options o (both of which will impact M’s
outputs). To illustrate, a clinician may ingest a given
subset of patient features in a visual display style spec-
ified by o. For notational simplicity, we take x ∈Rd.
For each instance, we consider M’s decision space to
be binary for simplicity, ˆy ∈{0, 1} (e.g., whether to
diagnose a patient with a certain disease).
In this
case, the BDM M makes decisions based on subsets
of features b ∈{0, 1}d and additional options o ∈O as
M(x⊙b, o)1, where ⊙denotes the Hadamard product.
We consider the output space of M to be [0, 1] × M,
the tuple of M’s probability of selecting ˆy = 1, along
with any additional meta-data m ∈M that is relevant
for assessing decision quality. For example, a clinician
outputs a confidence of their decision (probability of
ˆy = 1), along with a recorded time taken to make a
decision (the meta-data m).

Thus far, we have formally framed our DISS task as a
regression problem and have introduced a frequentist
approach that enables us to tune our observation ac-
quisition with arbitrary regressors. Below, we note a
special structure to the DISS regression task and pro-
pose to decompose the estimation task into two com-
ponents that make better use of the data at hand. Re-
call that in our DISS framework, the reward function
r, measuring decision-making efficacy, is known. We
now study the estimated target of our regression task,
f(x, b, o) ≈Ey|x[r(y, M(x ⊙b, o))]. Upon inspection,
one may note that the target actually depends on two
unknown quantities: 1) p(y | x), the ground truth con-

2) M(x ⊙b, o) the output of the decision-maker at x
with a configuration b, o. This observation, which has
not been previously used in contextual bandit decision

Applications
Next, we show how to formally frame several applica-
tions within our DISS framework.
DISS for Prediction Support
We focus on train-
ing a policy to identify a per-instance configuration
(subset of features, b, and options, o) to forward to the
decision maker as it makes its prediction (e.g., classi-
fication). The inputs that the decision-maker receives
are: 1) a subset of the information on an instance (as
indicated by the masked feature vector x ⊙b); 2) ad-
ditional options, o (represented in vector form, if ap-
plicable). In the binary prediction case, the decision-
maker outputs a class probability (we omit meta data
outputs for simplicity below). It is natural to consider
a reward based on the log-likelihood of the prediction,
η = M(x ⊙b, o) for the true class, y:
r(y, M(x ⊙b, o)) = y log η + (1 −y) log(1 −η).

LLM Decision-Making
We now consider a BDM
M that is a fixed, pretrained large language model
(e.g., GPT-4 (Achiam et al., 2023), LlaMA (Touvron
et al., 2023)).
The LLM makes predictions by out-
putting parsable text containing labels and confidence
about given instances based on input prompts (style,
content, and subset of features) that are adjusted in a
per-instance fashion according to the policy. As afore-
mentioned, many LLMs (and their efficacy) are sensi-
tive to prompting choices (Anagnostidis et al., 2024;
Liu et al., 2024; Z. Zhao et al., 2021). To maximize the
LLM’s per-instance prediction efficacy, we serve the
LLM a prompt (or input text) prompt(x⊙πb(x), πo(x))
based on the DISS policy, where πb(x) and πo(x) rep-
resent the selected feature subset and option for any
given instance x. For example, in healthcare applica-
tions, for a patient x, πb(x) controls the set of patient
attributes to be presented, and πo(x) controls the style
(preamble, wording, etc.) of the prompt containing the
chosen patient attributes forwarded to the LLM to ob-
tain an accurate diagnostic prediction in the text.
Interpretability
The interpretability of ML predic-
tions is hindered with larger instance dimensionalities
(Karim et al., 2023). We can utilize DISS to encourage
interpretability as follows. First, a cardinality penalty
may be added to any reward, r, to promote effica-
cious decisions with fewer features: r′(y, M(x⊙b, o)) =
r(y, M(x ⊙b, o)) −λ∥b∥1. Moreover, we can further
enforce interpretability by explicitly restricting the ac-
tion space of the DISS policy to only consider subsets
of a small dimensionality (e.g., 3: {b | ∥b∥≤3}).
Lastly, applying DISS to a decision-maker that is it-
self interpretable further yields interpretable predic-
tions. For example, a localized linear model fitted to
the provided query features x⊙b using neighbors in the
dataset D = {(x(i), y(i))}n
i=1 would directly provide an
interpretable decision-making process (a linear combi-
nation of a few features fit on neighbors) and gives a
complementary method to local interpretable model-
agnostic explanations (LIME) (Ribeiro et al., 2016),
or gradient approaches (e.g., Y. Wang et al., 2024).

Related Work
Contextual Bandits
DISS may be framed as a
contextual multi-armed bandit problem (Lu et al.,
2010), where the context is an instance x ∈Rd, the
action space are all the possible subsets of d features
(and a finite set of options), and rewards stem from
the BDM’s prediction quality when given the subset
of features (and options) from the context.
(Please
see Lattimore et al., 2020 for a general survey on con-
textual bandits.) Note that the cardinality of the ac-
tion space |A| ≥2d grows exponentially in the num-
ber of features, making for a difficult contextual ban-
dit setting, which itself is an active area of research
(e.g., (W. Chen et al., 2013, 2016; Gai et al., 2012;
S. Wang et al., 2018)).
Char et al., 2019 proposes
‘Offline Contextual Bayesian Optimization’ where one
can select contexts during training to observe in order
to maximize multi-task inference-time rewards, which
fits our DISS framework as discussed in § 2.2. How-
ever, their approach, CMTS, operates over with GP re-
gressors, which often have difficulties modeling higher-
dimensional domains (Murphy, 2022) and would have
to operate over a 2 · d joint space (of features and
masks).
Thus, we alleviate this issue with our fre-
quentist Thompson sampling approach (Algo. 1).
Adaptable Displays
The problem of providing de-
cision support via adaptive interfaces has been studied
in the context of human-computer interaction (HCI).
W. Wang et al., 2024 surveyed adaptive user inter-
faces for long-term health patients with predictive al-
gorithms (e.g.
reinforcement learning, Monte Carlo
tree search). Typically previous approaches consider
a limited number of options to adjust interfaces for
human decision-making. For example, Bu¸cinca et al.,
2024 explored offline reinforcement learning to learn
policies that adjust four options (vary the type of AI
prediction assistance to provide) to aid human learn-
ing of tasks. Lomas et al., 2016 employed multi-armed
bandits to optimize the experience of an online educa-
tional game by selecting among six interface variants.
Modiste (U. Bhatt et al., 2023) employed variants of
UCB (Guan et al., 2018) to learn a policy used to as-
sist human decision-maker’s accuracy through provid-
ing personalized additional relevant information (over
three options) such as LLM output. DISS, in contrast,
selects over a much larger number of adjustments than
previous approaches, and also encompasses many more
applications beyond human-assistance.
Prompt Engineering LLMs
LLMs can adapt to
a myriad of tasks with adequate prompting (Radford
et al., 2019). Recent work has studied various prompt-
ing heuristics, including in-context learning Brown et
al., 2020, chain-of-thought prompting Wei et al., 2022
and prompt-tuning Lester et al., 2021. However, im-
proper prompting may cause the LLM to output poor
responses in deployment Burnell et al., 2023. More-
over, recent works also show that the LLM outputs
are sensitive to the prompting style, position of infor-
mation in the prompt, and the length of the prompt
(Anagnostidis et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Z. Zhao
et al., 2021). Zollo et al., 2023 introduces an approach

and discrete; yet, our reward is continuous and un-
bounded.
As workaround, we make two attempts:
the first approach, Modiste-KNN, follows the original
Modiste formulation, but we initialize previously un-
seen reward to be the minimum reward stored in the
replay buffer; the second approach, Modiste-UKNN,
concatenates feature mask to context and performs k-
nearest neighbor regression in this space (see Appx. C).

Multi-Expert Setting
Next, we consider a scenario where multiple decision-
makers are available to the policy, but only one can be
queried due to constraints. Thus, in addition to select-
ing a subset of features to display, the policy must also
select what decision-maker to utilize. In real-world set-
tings, decision makers may have varying expertise on
different instance sub-spaces. We simulate this prop-
erty by separating our data into clusters and provid-
ing each expert with a single cluster as the support for
their knowledge base. For our experimental trials, we
performed K-Means clustering on each dataset with
k = 4, resulting in four distinct experts for our policy
to choose from. Unlike the biased synthetic expert set-
ting in § 5.1, synthetic experts in these experiments the
were not modified with any additional source of limi-
tation or bias; only the source of the models’ training
data differed between experts (see Appx. D.2).

6
Conclusion
In this work, we present a novel AI assistance frame-
work – Dynamic Information Sub-Selection (DISS)
– to enhance the performance of black-box decision-
makers by dynamically tuning the presented informa-
tion.
We develop a frequentist approach for DISS
policies to acquire data that enables the use of arbi-
trary regressors; moreover, we propose a novel mim-
icking approach that takes advantage of the struc-
ture of the DISS regression task to better utilize the
data at hand.
We show how various real-world ap-
plications (including biased decision support, expert
selection, interpretable modeling, and LLM support)
can be framed under our DISS framework. Extensive
experimentation shows our proposed Mimic approach
achieves state-of-the-art across these applications. We
believe that the success of our Mimic based policies in
these experiments can be used as a stepping stone for
further development across these applications in future
work.

C
Modiste Implementation
There are two obstacles when apply Modiste KNN variant (see Alg. 3 in (U. Bhatt et al., 2023)) to our DISS
framework, to which we provide two workarounds termed Modiste-KNN and Modiste-UKNN.
C.1
Modiste-KNN
U. Bhatt et al., 2023 assumed the reward to be bounded and discrete, specifically r : X ×A →{0, 1}. Meanwhile,
to estimate reward for given any context-action pair, the original Modiste KNN variant queries the nearest
neighbors in the context space from replay buffer that has the same action with the given input (see Alg. 3 step
1 in U. Bhatt et al., 2023); yet, as the size of action space increases, it is more than likely not every action gets
observed during training time; for these unobserved actions, the original Modiste KNN variant used the fact
that reward is either zero or one and set the estimated reward to the default value of 0.5 if the queried action is
not observed during training. However, we do not have a simple analogous counterpart to their default value of
0.5 since our reward is unbounded. Hence, for actions that were not observed during training, we initialize it to
the minimum reward observed in the current replay buffer. We note that other potential options for statistics
to consider include mean and qth-quantile.
C.2
Modiste-UKNN
As size of action space increases, the possibility of the score estimator not able to acquire sufficient interactions
with the environment increases. The workaround we proposed in Appx. C.1 only addresses the issue of which
default value to use for unobserved action but did not tackle the fact that a default value is needed for unobserved
actions. Here, we note that it is highly likely that DISS actions are related, i.e. the action that uses feature 6
and 7 could perform well if we already have prior experience taking actions that use feature 7. Based on this
intuition, we perform k-nearest neighbors query directly in context-action space with the following customized
distance metric:
distance(⟨x, a⟩, ⟨x′, a′⟩) = ∥x, x′∥+ ν ∥a, a′∥
(1)
where ν is a hyperparameter. In essence, eq. 1 is the linear combination of distance between the given two
contexts and distance between the two given actions, which accounts for similarity between contexts and actions
at the same time.

Reward Estimators
The XGBoost ensemble score estimators consist of 2 XGBoost regressors (both trained
on the entire set of collected examples) with n estimators set to 64. Our structured mimic score estimator
uses ensembles consisting of XGBoost classifiers with n estimators=64 for both the expert mimic and the
ground truth classifier components of the model. For completeness, we also included Gaussian Process based
score estimator with constant mean and radial basis function kernel; the parameters for mean and covariance
functions are updated using Adam optimizer with learning rate set to 10−3; the GP update subroutine is called
after each query.
Action Space
We limited the search space of possible actions for a given context to a maximum of 5000
options; for any setting where the full size of the action space ∥{0, 1}d × O∥> 5000, we searched over a random
sample of 5000 actions.
Additional Discussion
We note that CMTS (CMTS (Char et al., 2019) with GP score estimator) at times
perform on par with FCMTS on smaller dimensional data (‘skin’). However, on the skin-interpretability environ-
ment, CMTS takes 2.449 days to perform 8000 queries whereas our proposed Mimic only takes 4.039 hours; in
addition, CMTS consumes significantly more memory compare to Mimic, which made it infeasible in even mod-
erate dimensionalities notwithstanding GP optimizations. Furthermore, results below, and other preliminary
experiments, show instability when optimizing GP hyperperameters (e.g. Fig. 1,Fig. 3, Fig. 5a); in constrast,
the Mimic approach was able to use consistent hyperparameters across datasets.
