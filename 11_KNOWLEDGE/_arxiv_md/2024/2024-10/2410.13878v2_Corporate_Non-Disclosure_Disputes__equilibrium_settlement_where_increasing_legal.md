Corporate Non-Disclosure Disputes: equilibrium settlement
where increasing legal liability encourages voluntary disclosures
Miles B. Gietzmann1 and Adam J. Ostaszewski2
Abstract. How should a court resolve a shareholder-management dispu-
te after an unexpected price drop, when it is suspected that at an earlier time
management chose not to update (disclose to) the market about a material
event that was privately observed? An earlier fundamental result in this area
(Dye, 2017) has shown that if the court chooses to make public that it will
increase awards of damages to try and deter non-disclosure, then this may
have the perverse eﬀect that management may rationally choose to disclose
less. Schantl and Wagenhofer (2024) call this the pure-insurance eﬀect sha-
reholders receive from higher damages payments. They show that the result
may be relaxed if management also face a ﬁxed exogenous reputational cost
from non-disclosure. In this research we probe the increased-damages ver-
sus reduced-disclosure result via a diﬀerent route. We introduce a dynamic
continuous-time model of management’s equilibrium disclosure decision and
show that as awards of damages increase this has in a dynamic setting a
hitherto unrecognized eﬀect: management rationally switch their disclosure
strategy. We characterize the range of damage awards, which we term the
legal consistency zone, in which increased awards of damages evoke an endo-
genous increase in voluntary disclosure.

10b-5 rule in the US) which allow investors to pursue claims of fraudulent
misrepresentation if released information is deceitful or alternatively price-
relevant information is knowingly withheld and omitted from reports – the
latter circumstance is of interest here. It is generally recognized that only
a minority of litigated claims proceed to court-awarded damages (Mnookin,
2021), the majority being resolved by direct negotiation. Nevertheless, since
the latter settlements are reached in the shadow of the law (in the shadow
of potential court rulings), litigation mechanisms as outcome determinants
are a central concern below. In this research we focus upon two principal
interacting incentives which may discourage management of companies from
omitting disclosure of price-relevant information. Firstly, in a multi-period
setting, management may recognize a beneﬁt from establishing a reputation
for candid (and timely) release of all privately observed signals including
negative ones. That ﬁrst, purely voluntary, motivation may be combined se-
condly with governmental regulatory legislation which establishes a public
legal framework for shareholders to pursue companies in courts if the release
of negative signals is shown to be delayed. This research considers a multi-
period model in which, although the two motivations may reinforce each
other, it is also possible that the interaction may be in diﬀerent directions.
In particular, we are interested in understanding when the two types of in-
centives reinforce each other. This is important because an existing stand-out
result (Dye, 2017) is that, in a two-period setting, increasing court awards of
damages reduces voluntary disclosure.
First we establish that in a dynamic continuous-time model in which
management may receive private information at various times between two
mandatory disclosure dates the (Dye, 2017) result that increasing damages
awards results in less voluntary disclosure is maintained when management
follow a ‘sparing’ strategy (in the sense below).
However, as Beyer and Dye (2012) explained, when a multi-period setting
is considered, management may choose to switch between voluntary disclo-
sure strategies through time if parameters are varied. So, secondly our task is
to consider whether raising awards of damages could result in management
rationally switching equilibrium voluntary disclosure strategy. Speciﬁcally,
our next research objective is to identify whether there exist conditions un-
der which higher awards of damages result in management switching from
a sparing voluntary disclosure strategy (in the sense of being economical
with the truth), under which some (bad) news signals are withheld to a can-
did strategy (under which all signals are disclosed). If such conditions can be

shown to exist, the raising of court award levels may result in more voluntary
disclosure so that voluntary and regulatory incentives reinforce each other.
The main contribution of this research mediates between these two posi-
tions. We derive conditions under which reinforcement is supported and we
also characterize conditions under which it is not, in which case the (Dye,
2017) result would be maintained in a dynamic context. Our Main Theorem
(in Section 6) establishes a legal consistency zone comprising two circum-
stances: (i) a switching Goldilocks-interval, c < c < ¯c, where the litigation
charge that is neither too high nor too low elicits an increase in voluntary
disclosures, together with (ii) a second interval, where candid behaviour is
followed, both contradicting the Dye disclosure-reduction result.
We provide a graphic presentation in Figure 4 (Section 6) of the required
condition for a legal consistency zone to arise and show its similarity to
continuous-compounding lease (or bond) valuation. This arises intuitively
because we establish that when management do not make any disclosures
the company value falls like that of a ﬁnite-lived leased asset. The critical
condition then is analogous to a comparison of two leased assets with diﬀerent
economic depreciation rates through time.
Another potential advantage of explicitly modelling the problem in conti-
nuous time is that we can explore diﬀerent more realistic possibilities for how
damages awards are set. In most existing models of non-disclosure it is assu-
med that courts eventually see the private signal that management withheld.
We call this the delayed omniscience assumption. In reality this is a strong
assumption. We consider an alternative. Given our chosen continuous-time
setting, it is possible to characterize the fair price (valuation) of the compa-
ny just before the mandatory disclosure date if no disclosure was provided
by management before the mandatory date and this value can be used as
a substitute for the delayed omniscient value. We present a graphical repre-
sentation of this in Figure 1 below. To summarize: we replace the delayed
omniscient valuation, which in reality may never be seen by the courts, with
the ‘just before’ equilibrium valuation of the ﬁrm if no voluntary disclosure
had taken place. Using the leasing analogy again, this is the value of the
leased asset at the year-end (mandatory disclosure date).
Expressed more formally we adopt a continuous-time framework in which
during periods of silence an ODE (ordinary diﬀerential equation) describes
the evolution of the equilibrium trend value (derived in Section 5). This evo-
lution is associated with equilibrium market inferences, such inferences con-
sidering whether falls observed in price go beyond trend values, thereby sug-

An internally generated signal of the ﬁrm’s value, denoted Yt, is privately
observed intermittently between the mandatory disclosure dates so that the
random observation times t of Yt follow a Poisson process independent of the
signal value. We model the signal, consistently with the benchmark Black-
Scholes framework, as a geometric Brownian martingale (details in Section
3). If observed and if disclosed, it will be disclosed truthfully and so esta-
blishes a new valuation of the ﬁrm at that point in time. However, absent
any disclosure, the market remains uninformed as to whether a signal has
been received. The ambiguity of silence (over a lack of signal or of a non-
disclosed signal) at any point in time may be viewed as a continuous analogue
of the scheduled ‘potential’ interim announcement in the foundational Dye
framework of (Dye, 1985). We exploit the earlier modelling in Gietzmann and
Ostaszewski (2016) with the inclusion now of possible shareholder litigation.
Here rationally anticipated damages-payments (court awarded, or settled by
negotiation) aﬀect a ﬁrm’s voluntary disclosure calculus.
In our model the ODE has a tractable explicit solution (in terms of the
model’s parameters, such as the arrival rate of private news and the combined
observation and production variability). Throughout a period of silence it
expresses in implicit terms (absent any voluntary disclosures) an equilibration
of two opposing forces: non-disclosure of what might be interpreted as bad
news protecting the value (before the terminal time of t = 1) of the ﬁrm
versus a higher likelihood that litigation will require damages to be paid.
Its solution is an exponential-like declining continuous convex curve γt
recalibrated so that the last known disclosed value assumed to occur at time
t = 0 has γ0 = 1. As t increases the associated equilibrium trend curve γt is the
principal component in the market’s assessment of ﬁrm value when there is
no (corrective) disclosure. This reﬂects the market’s inference from continued
silence as an increased likelihood (but not certainty) of an undisclosed poor
signal value having been privately received by the ﬁrm. The date of the next
mandatory disclosure is taken to be t = 1. The limiting value as t tends
to 1 of γt, namely γ1, now has an important signiﬁcance: it is the market’s
valuation just prior to the mandatory disclosure itself, the later mandatorily
disclosed valuation being denoted by Y1. The fall in price from γ0 to Y1 is
decomposed into two components - ﬁrst an expected fall under continued
silence γ0 −γ1 and an unexpected component γ1 −Y1. The innovation in our
approach is to model allowed litigated claim levels as being set to depend
on the unexpected component, namely the diﬀerence between an equilibrium

Literature Review
Dye in (Dye, 2017) studies a revised form of the standard two-period disclo-
sure problem of (Dye, 1985) in which a seller of an asset, who may receive a
private signal on the estimated intrinsic value of the asset, decides whether to
disclose the signal realization to potential buyers. The revision (Dye, 2017)
introduces is that after the sale a non-disclosing seller is liable to damages
speciﬁed by a damages-multiplier times the amount the buyers of the asset
overpaid for the asset. The overpayment is calculated based on what the
asset would have sold for, if the seller’s signal had been disclosed in the mar-
ket. That is, the model of legal damages assumes the seller-observed signal
value is discovered by the courts after the sales transaction is completed.
Dye in (Dye, 2017) establishes several counter-intuitive results within this
simple model setting. He shows that when the numerical value for the dama-
ges multiplier is increased this leads to the seller being less likely to disclose
the private signal, and this decline in disclosure also follows if an indepen-
dent fact-ﬁnder (auditor) is more likely to detect non-disclosure. This leads
Dye to conclude that his result yields the important paradoxical conclusion
that greater damages payments for non-disclosure generate less disclosure
(p. 706). His result is derived by showing that the equilibrium non-disclosure
cutoﬀis increasing in the damages multiplier. The Dye result is sometimes
described as a pure-insurance eﬀect because when investors are given litiga-
tion rights to seek compensation, they incorporate this in the pricing of the
seller asset. Since investors rationally believe they are price protected against
non-disclosure, the investors become less cautious. As we shall see below, our
model makes a diﬀerent prediction, because we show how raising damages
awards may incentivize a ﬁrm to switch earlier to a candid (full) disclosure
strategy away from a sparing (cutoﬀ) strategy (see Main Theorem). That is,

Schantl and Wagenhofer (2024) develop a Dye-type disclosure model that
formalizes how this lower disclosure cutoﬀcan arise. They assume that liti-
gation, in addition to a pure insurance eﬀect, also creates the possibility of
a deterrence eﬀect, in which the managers of ﬁrms experience a reputation
cost even though they have full litigation insurance. This new deterrence ef-
fect introduces the dual cutoﬀstrategy: the traditional (upper) Dye cutoﬀ
and now a new lower cutoﬀbelow which the reputation cost outweighs the
valuation beneﬁts from non-disclosure of bad news. This dual cutoﬀstrategy
allows Schantl and Wagenhofer to show that increasing legal damages pay-
ments for non-disclosure does not generate less disclosure in the case that the
reputation eﬀect dominates the insurance eﬀect. This is closer to our result,
but again we ﬁrst focus on what speciﬁc assumptions they make about how
damages are determined. Like (Dye, 2017) they assume that a signal observed
privately by the ﬁrm’s manager, but not disclosed at the time of receipt, is
observed perfectly by the courts, but with subsequent delay. The diﬀerence
between that value and the market price of the ﬁrm, had the private signal
been disclosed, then determines the damages. That is, the courts are presu-
med to enjoy delayed but perfect observability. A major result of Schantl and
Wagenhofer (2024) is that decreasing litigation risk leads to less disclosure
of very bad news, provided the reputation eﬀect of non-disclosure is not too
high. In our model there is no exogenously imposed ﬁxed reputation cost.
Choi and Spier (2022) introduce a dual (low, high) productivity type
Dye model in which public investment in low productivity ﬁrms is socially
ineﬃcient. However, such investment can occur if ﬁrms privately observe
low project productivity but choose during fund-raising to not disclose it
(“withholding material information”) and pool with uninformed ﬁrms. At
issue is how to design the legal damages system which speciﬁes how ﬁrms are
liable for non-disclosure to deter ineﬃcient investment. Choi and Spier show
how in equilibrium ﬁrms’ non-disclosure depends on the frequency with which
the ﬁrms are privately informed and the level of liability. They show that as
the liability system becomes stronger, low productivity ﬁrms are increasingly
deterred from raising funds. In the case where damages for non-disclosure
only oﬀer partial deterrence to strategic ﬁrms, they show how social welfare
increases in damages. Like (Dye, 2017) they apply a delayed omniscience
assumption for the courts, by modelling damages as being assessed on the
realized value of the private productivity signal at the time of the court

Field, Lowry, and Shu (2005) consider empirically whether disclosure trig-
gers or deters litigation. They commence by noticing that Skinner (1994) sug-
gests (early) voluntary disclosure can deter lawsuits, since providing timely
information weakens the claim that managers acted improperly by failing to
disclose bad news. Also, releasing bad news decreases the duration that the
stock trades at a misleading price. However, Francis et al. (1994) ﬁnd from
their empirical studies the opposite result, that early disclosure increases
the probability of litigation. Field et al. (2005) explain that the seemingly
contradicting conclusions follow from the fact that litigation and disclosure
are endogenously determined. So, in response they estimate a simultaneous
equations model. They ﬁnd that:
“Our results are consistent with both the pre-emption eﬀect and the de-
terrence eﬀect. First, ﬁrms with higher litigation risk are more likely to issue
earnings warnings. This relation is robust across various speciﬁcations. More
interestingly, we also ﬁnd some evidence that ﬁrms that disclose early can
lower their expected litigation risk. At a minimum, there is no evidence that
earnings warnings trigger litigation, as some prior literature has suggested.”
We can contribute to this debate by providing a closed form model in
which ﬁrms provide earnings warnings by applying our dual cutoﬀequili-
brium strategy and can analytically appraise the comparative static of incre-
ased dispute occurrence triggered by non-disclosure within a class of ﬁrms
with identical characteristics (see β, κ, λ below). Unlike Field et al. (2005)
our model does not consider all ﬁrms with an above-1% size adjusted une-
xpected earnings as ‘treated’; they then deﬁne a matched untreated sample,
as this would be consistent with a simple upper cutoﬀregime. Instead, we
identify three mutually exclusive cases:
- (A) Firms making early voluntary disclosures, because they are below
the lower cutoﬀ(early bad news disclosers), who may not face damages-
disputes if the stock price does not fall further. The prospective eﬀect of
subsequent disclosures is not studied here. We note the fair-price modelling
undertaken by Dybvig et al. (2000) in connection with a reform act of 1995
in the US.
- (B) Firms privately observing signal(s) between the two cutoﬀs that do
not make a voluntary disclosure, but could face damages litigation (or private
settlement) if by chance the stock price subsequently were to fall below γ1,

Model framework
Below, in our formal valuation model of persistently silent ﬁrms, the market
decreases the value of the ﬁrm according to a speciﬁc form of exponential
decline. Key here is how investors should rationally value (in fact, downgra-
de) a ﬁrm’s persistently continued silence. Investors’ inferred value comes
from weighing up the two possibilities that silence follows from the ﬁrm not
receiving any private signal or from receiving a (bad) news signal which they
chose voluntarily not to disclose. Given such an evaluation, one would in-
tuitively expect that, for a range of regular distributions on the probability
that the ﬁrm receives a signal, the longer the period of silence, the more li-
kely investors believe the ﬁrm has received some form of bad news and so, as
time progresses with continued silence, the market continues to downgrade
the value of the ﬁrm. Gietzmann and Ostaszewski (2023) make this intuition
precise by deriving a unique equilibrium trend value of the ﬁrm. This is the
chosen dynamic ‘upper cutoﬀ’ γt above which values are disclosed (see Sec-
tion 5). It is shown to follow a form of simple exponential continuous decline,
so that if silence continues until the next mandatory disclosure period at
t = 1, then the investors will have continuously through time downgraded
the ﬁrm to a rationally anticipated limiting value at t = 1. We shall denote
this value by γ1, which need not agree with the actual mandatorily disclosed
value Y1.
Our model has three observables:
i) initial ﬁrm-value (at t = 0) : γ0, re-scaled so that γ0 = 1;
ii) limiting terminal trend value (at t = 1) : γ1, arising from the ﬁrm
remaining silent until the next mandatory disclosure time (at t = 1);
iii) Y1 the actual terminal ﬁrm-value observed at terminal time t = 1.

Concluding remarks: implications
By introducing a continuous-time analogue for a traditional two-period ana-
lysis of voluntary disclosure in ﬁnancial markets with private signals (these
42


## Page 43


assumed formally to be a martingale under the management’s ﬁltration), we
are able to probe the received Dye-paradox that increasing the award level
of damages reduces the aggregate voluntary disclosure. Continuous-time per-
mits optimal control of switches in the management’s disclosure behaviour
that include both candid disclosure (of all signals) and sparing disclosure (of
signals above a dynamic cutoﬀ). We introduce a litigation mechanism which
does not require (delayed) discovery of withheld signals (as in the traditio-
nal literature) and instead applies timed liability-sharing under which early
disclosure of bad news (properly deﬁned) attracts smaller (relative) damages
payments. In this framework we show (Main Theorem) that the Dye-paradox
need not occur for parameters in what we term the legal consistency zone
comprising the two cases: when the liability charge parameter is neither too
small nor too large and the switching behaviour out of sparing mode is so-
oner, leading to increased aggregate-disclosure, and likewise when the charge
is suﬃciently high under which sparing behaviour is not optimal.
