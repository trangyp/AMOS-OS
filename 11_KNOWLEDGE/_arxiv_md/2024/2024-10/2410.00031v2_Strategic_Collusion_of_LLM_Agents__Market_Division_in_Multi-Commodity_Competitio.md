Abstract
Machine-learning technologies are seeing increased deployment in real-world mar-
ket scenarios. In this work, we explore the strategic behaviors of large language
models (LLMs) when deployed as autonomous agents in multi-commodity mar-
kets, specifically within Cournot competition frameworks. We examine whether
LLMs can independently engage in anti-competitive practices such as collusion
or, more specifically, market division. Our findings demonstrate that LLMs can
effectively monopolize specific commodities by dynamically adjusting their pricing
and resource allocation strategies, thereby maximizing profitability without direct
human input or explicit collusion commands. These results pose unique challenges
and opportunities for businesses looking to integrate AI into strategic roles and for
regulatory bodies tasked with maintaining fair and competitive markets. The study
provides a foundation for further exploration into the ramifications of deferring
high-stakes decisions to LLM-based agents.

Introduction
With artificial intelligence technologies becoming commonplace, machine-driven pricing agents and
algorithms have found widespread adoption. As pricing decisions are increasingly placed in the
hands of these agents, concerns have been raised regarding whether such agents may be susceptible
to engaging in illegal or unethical business practices, often to the detriment of the consumer [1, 2].
This practice is referred to as algorithmic collusion.
Early work of both theoretical [3, 4] and empirical [5, 6] nature has demonstrated the risk of machines
and algorithmic approaches engaging in unethical business practices, triggering an interest among
regulatory bodies [2, 1]. Large language models (LLMs) offer general-purpose language generation
capabilities, enabled by their pre-training on massive datasets [7]. Soon, businesses may begin
feeding key business metrics into LLM-based pricing agents. Recent work has found that LLM-based
agents are capable of autonomous collusion in an oligopolistic setting [8]. Furthermore, they find
success in maximizing profits at the detriment of the consumer without explicit instruction or means
of direct communication, suggesting a troublesome prognosis for consumer welfare in the future of
AI pricing agents.
In this work, we examine the risk of collusion in a multi-commodity variant of Cournot models
of competition. Cournot competition is a classical economic model in which firms choose output
quantities, and prices are determined by aggregate supply. Whereas previous works in this area have
focused on anti-competitive "price fixing", we focus on the more overt anti-competitive practice of
"market division". Our work also marks the first application of LLM-based agents in the Cournot
competition model, as well as the first empirical study of LLM-based agents in the multi-commodity
variant of Cournot competition. We show that LLM-based agents do indeed display cooperative
behavior and divide up the market in this problem setting.

Related Works
O’Sullivan and Sheffrin [9] define collusion in multi-agent settings as a self-benefiting agreement,
tacit or explicit, among firms or individuals to divide a market, set prices, limit production, or limit
opportunities, often at the expense of the consumer. We will adopt this definition for the remainder
of this work, focusing specifically on market division and consumer welfare. Research on AI-based
pricing agents often examines their potential for collusion using various market scenarios. Take, for
example, the Bertrand oligopoly, which uses a logit demand model to dictate the quantity of the
product sold as a function of price[10, 8]. In this setting, agents produce a homogeneous product
and set prices simultaneously, with the Nash equilibrium serving as a benchmark for empirical
comparisons.
Another frequently studied model is Cournot competition, where agents choose production quantities
as opposed to setting prices as in the Bertrand setting. In Cournot competition, an inverse demand
model is calculated, where the price of a good is calculated based on the quantity of the good in
the market (See Equation 1). Recent work explores multi-market Cournot settings where firms
with symmetric cost functions decide which markets to enter under resource constraints [11, 12].
Auction-based models, particularly two-bidder first-price auctions, are also used to study collusion,
with agents acting simultaneously over multiple rounds and known equilibrium outcomes [8, 13].
The study of collusion in games pre-dates the deployment of autonomous agents in such settings. In
the context of game theory, the Folk Theorem suggests that repeated interaction can result in virtually
any average payoff in a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPE) if agents maintain enough patience
[14]. This patience is quantified with the "discount factor", typically denoted as δ in economic
literature. In effect, the Folk Theorem shows that there is always an SPE for which agents cooperate
for long-term payoff. However, it is not immediately obvious that a particular agent possesses the
reasoning capability to arrive at these cooperative equilibria.
In several of the above problem settings, Q-learning agents have demonstrated tacit collusive behavior,
learning to charge supra-competitive prices in Bertrand competition games even without direct
communication [10]. Collusion persists under imperfect monitoring, where agents lack access to
others’ prices [5]. Similar outcomes are observed in Cournot competition with policy gradient
reinforcement learning [11]. In auctions, Q-learning agents collude only under specific conditions,
such as asynchronous bidding in first-price auctions, but this behavior diminishes when bid history is
introduced [13].
Collusive behavior is not limited to Q-learning agents. Recent works explore the behavior of LLM-
based agents in multi-agent market settings, and empirical experiments demonstrate that these agents
can exhibit collusive pricing behavior even without explicit instructions to collude or channels to
communicate with each other [8].
This work highlights collusion between LLM-based agents in the Cournot market setting. More
specifically, we show that such agents effectively divide sales territories among each other and tacitly
collude to discourage competition at the expense of the consumer. This act of "market division" is
considered illegal by various regulatory bodies, such as the Federal Trade Commission [15].
3
Experiments
In Cournot competition, each firm decides the quantity of a homogeneous, or indistinguishable, good
to produce [16]. Using the aggregate quantity supplied to the market, the market-clearing price
is determined through the inverse demand function. Previous works have identified Cournot-like
competition in markets for commodities, such as crude oil [17] and coal [18], as well as wholesale
electricity markets [19].
While there has been significant work done in characterizing the original model and various derivatives,
including but not limited to multi-market Cournot competition [20] and Cournot competition in
networked markets [21], there has been limited work in understanding the model as a learning game
and even less attention given to better understanding the risk of collusive game-play under such a
model. Existing works exploring machine-learning methods in the context of Cournot competition
have been limited exclusively to using traditional reinforcement learning (RL) techniques [11, 22, 23].

In this work, we present a novel application of LLM-based agents to a multi-commodity variant
of the Cournot competition model. We run experiments with six commercially available models:
OpenAI’s o4-mini, GPT-4.1, and GPT-4.1-mini [24], DeepSeek’s DeepSeek-V3 [25], Anthropic’s
Claude-3.7-Sonnet [26], and Google’s Gemini-1.5 Pro [27]. In each experiment, we only pair models
of the same type with each other (i.e., within the same experiment, both agents are identical). This
decision was made to ensure that observed behaviors and outcomes are attributable to the intrinsic
characteristics of the model architecture and not to cross-model dynamics. It also ensures fairness, as
heterogeneous match-ups may conflate model performance with strategic mismatches rather than
inherent decision-making quality. By isolating each model in symmetric settings, we can more reliably
evaluate how each model internally reasons about market dynamics in a competitive multi-agent
scenario.

Our simulation of an infinite-horizon game represents a deliberate and consequential design choice,
as it reshapes the strategic landscape away from finitely repeated or single-period Cournot games.
In finitely repeated games, traditional game theory predicts the collapse of cooperation through
backward induction: a rational agent, anticipating defection by the opponent in the final round, has
no incentive to cooperate in the penultimate round. This logic propagates backward, ultimately
eliminating the viability of cooperation from the outset.
In the absence of sustained cooperation, profit-maximizing agents must act myopically. In other
words, they have no knowledge of or coordination with the other firms’ future actions. This lack of
strategic linkage between periods effectively reduces to a single-period game, in which the firms are
expected to repeatedly play the single-period Cournot-Nash. As such, the single-period Cournot-Nash
serves as a suitable baseline for a market environment in which the agents are not colluding.
Interestingly, behavioral economics research has shown that human subjects can deviate from these
theoretical predictions, sustaining cooperation even in finitely repeated games [28, 29]. This behavior
has been attributed to human behavioral biases, absent from classical models of strategic interaction,
but that previous works have shown to emerge in LLMs [29].
For infinitely repeated games, the Folk Theorem establishes that cooperative outcomes can be
sustainable as equilibria if agents sufficiently value future payoffs [14]. We argue that an infinite-
horizon game more accurately reflects the deployment context for LLM-based pricing agents, where
firms seek to optimize revenue indefinitely rather than toward some known terminal event, such as
bankruptcy or acquisition.

A key challenge in deploying LLM-based agents in strategic environments is that, unlike the well-
defined, patient agents of economic theory, or even the human subjects studied in behavioral research,
we lack a clear understanding of their inductive biases or how they discount future payoffs. Such
effective “patience" is a necessary (though not sufficient) condition for sustaining cooperation and
may depend not only on the foundation model but also on the prompts and reward structures that
shape agent behavior. Critically, the potential for these agents to engage in harmful or illegal collusion
may not be fully anticipated, or even understood, by the firms that deploy them.
We posit that our infinite-horizon setting provides a compelling testbed where both previous theoretical
and empirical works support the possibility of sustained collusion, but where the dynamics of LLM-
based agents remain underexplored and potentially risky.
While the repeated Cournot duopoly is a textbook example of an infinite-horizon game, its multi-
commodity variant is relatively underexplored in the literature [11, 12]. Despite modeling semi-
realistic and well-understood strategic interactions, this variant’s limited representation in publicly
available texts makes it especially well-suited for evaluating the strategic behavior of LLM-based
agents, offering a scenario more likely to fall outside their training distribution.

3.4
Environment Configuration
For our experiments, we restrict ourselves to the case of n = 2 firms simultaneously competing in
m = 2 commodity markets. Both firms face a set of fixed marginal costs drawn from the cost set
C = {40, 50}. We choose the marginal cost set C to reflect potential modest cost heterogeneity that
may arise from slight but impactful differences in production efficiency or proximity to marketplaces,
while allowing us to test both symmetric and asymmetric cost scenarios.
Each agent is tasked with maximizing total firm profits over 50 rounds (a number which is unknown
to the agents) where the market dynamics are governed by identical inverse demand functions for
both products, denoted by α = 100 and β = 2. These are scaling parameters that effectively ensure
that supplying more than 100 units to a particular market yields negative profits and that profits are
not always guaranteed, encouraging exploration of the strategy space. Further, choosing parameters
specific to our experiments limits the likelihood of memorization in the training data. As a reference
point, we solve for expected market outcomes under duopoly and monopoly (full collusion), the
formulations for which are presented in Appendix B.2.
Agents are provided with historical market data, including quantities sold, prices, market shares, and
profits from the previous 15 rounds. The experiment prompts are intentionally vague. Each firm is
unaware of its competitor’s payoff. We note that this level of competitor detail is less than that of Fish
et al. [8], which also used 300 rounds compared to our 50 rounds. The general flow of the experiment
is depicted in Figure 1.
Previous works demonstrate that allowing agents to communicate with their future selves ensures
continuity and evolution of strategy [8, 31]. Our experiment builds off these findings, allowing for
communication between different temporal instances of each agent through "Plans and Insights"
documents, which are updated each round. The full prompts can be found in Appendix B.3.

We run several experiments, sampling from the aforementioned cost set. The progression of a
representative run using GPT-4.1 is shown in Figure 2. To accompany the empirical results, we plot
both the Cournot-Nash, as a benchmark for non-collusive behavior, as well as the expected outcome
under full collusion, which we derive in Appendix B.2.2. This particular run achieves a CSR of
≈0.726 and an HHI of 1.0 across both product markets, indicating market division at the expense of
consumer welfare (a hallmark of textbook collusion).
To account for the potential non-normality and autocorrelation in the 50-round time series produced
by each experiment, we use a circular block bootstrap method from the ARCH toolbox [32] with
a block size of 7 and 10,000 resamples to test whether the observed mean HHI is significantly
greater than that expected at the Nash equilibrium at the 5% significance level. We conducted similar
experiments with CSR and found that both were indeed statistically significant.
Notably, despite the absence of any explicit communication channel, agents never re-enter a market
once they have exited. From a myopic profit-maximization perspective, unilateral re-entry would
yield short-term gains. However, agents consistently avoid this behavior, suggesting an implicit
understanding of the long-term consequences. This indicates emergent collusive dynamics: agents
appear to understand that re-entering a previously conceded market would trigger reciprocal retaliation
by the opponent, reducing overall payoffs. Their behavior aligns with a form of tacit coordination
resembling subgame-perfect collusion.

Discussion and Conclusions
This research explores the potential for LLM-based agents to engage in anti-competitive behaviors,
such as market division and monopolization, within a multi-commodity market setting. By simulating
agents making strategic pricing and production decisions in multi-commodity Cournot competition
models, our study reveals the risks of algorithmic collusion and its significant impact on markets,
consumers, and communities.
Importantly, the concern is not that every agent deployment will result in collusion, but rather that
even seemingly benign prompts can give rise to such behavior. The threshold for regulatory or ethical
concern is not universal coordination, it is the existence of any realistic scenario in which LLM-based
agents can learn to collude at consumers’ expense. Such emergent outcomes could have serious
real-world consequences, from restricting access to essential goods to prompting major business
decisions like layoffs.
The above results suggest that LLMs possess the reasoning capability to reach cooperative equilibria,
the existence of which is supported by the Folk Theorem from traditional game theory. Our experi-
ments identify that LLM-based agents do indeed engage in market division, resulting in an effectively
monopolistic control over specific commodities. This behavior is particularly surprising given the
absence of explicit coordination channels and the potential short-term profits available from market
re-entry.
These findings underscore the legal and ethical concerns of delegating high-impact decisions to AI
agents, especially when such decisions risk violating antitrust laws or adversely affecting workers
and consumers.
The study is limited by its scope, focusing only on two agents and two products. Expanding to
scenarios with more agents and products could yield further insights into market dynamics where
direct market division is more complex. Agents’ decision-making capabilities were also constrained
by simplified actions, such as limited context windows, which affected their learning and decision
retention.
Future work could address these limitations by exploring more complex market scenarios. For
example, agents could be deployed in a more open market setting, where they are not limited to
producing a set quantity of goods or have the option to make decisions about long-term business
investments. Exploring these mechanisms more fully is a promising avenue for future work.
Our findings call for the development of comprehensive regulatory frameworks and the imple-
mentation of robust guardrails to mitigate the risk of market manipulation by autonomous agents.
Our work aims to inform policymakers, researchers, and industry leaders about the potential per-
ils of unregulated deployment, encouraging proactive measures to ensure fair and competitive
market dynamics. Further investigation in these areas could provide a deeper understanding of
the potential and pitfalls of LLM-based agents in economic settings. We release our code at
https://github.com/smojha/collusive-llm-agents.

B.2
Optimization Formulations
B.2.1
Cournot Duopoly Optimization Formulation
For completeness, we provide a formulation of the optimization solved numerically to identify
the unique single-period Cournot-Nash equilibrium for the game setting used in our experiments.
We employ an iterative best response dynamics algorithm [33]. This method alternates between
optimizing each firm’s strategy via the agent’s profit function (Equation 2) while holding the other
firm’s strategy fixed, until convergence within a specified tolerance, for which we use ϵ = 10−8,
or until the maximum number of iterations, T = 100, has been reached, reflecting an unsuccessful
optimization. We initialize q0
i at 100 for each i. The algorithm proceeds as follows:
Each firm’s optimization problem is solved using the Sequential Least Squares Quadratic Program-
ming (SLSQP) method using the scipy library [34], which is well-suited for constrained nonlinear
optimization problems. The objective function for each firm is the negative of its profit function
(Equation 2), as we perform minimization. The algorithm converges when the change in quantities for
both firms between iterations is below the specified tolerance ϵ. If convergence is not achieved within
the maximum number of iterations, an exception is raised. This numerical approach is particularly
effective for our Cournot duopoly model, as it can handle the nonlinear nature of the profit functions
and the capacity constraints efficiently. Moreover, it allows for easy modification of model parameters,
facilitating sensitivity analyses and extensions to more complex market structures. The algorithm
cannot converge to multiple solutions, as our problem setting satisfies the assumptions required for a
unique Cournot equilibrium (as outlined in [35]).
