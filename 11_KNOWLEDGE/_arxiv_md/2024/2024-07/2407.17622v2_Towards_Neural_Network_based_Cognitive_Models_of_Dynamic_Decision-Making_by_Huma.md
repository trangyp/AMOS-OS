Abstract
Modeling human cognitive processes in dynamic decision-
making tasks has been an endeavor in AI for a long time be-
cause such models can help make AI systems more intuitive,
personalized, mitigate any human biases, and enhance train-
ing in simulation. Some initial work has attempted to utilize
neural networks (and large language models) but often as-
sumes one common model for all humans and aims to emu-
late human behavior in aggregate. However, the behavior of
each human is distinct, heterogeneous, and relies on specific
past experiences in certain tasks. For instance, consider two
individuals responding to a phishing email: one who has pre-
viously encountered and identified similar threats may recog-
nize it quickly, while another without such experience might
fall for the scam. In this work, we build on Instance Based
Learning (IBL) that posits that human decisions are based on
similar situations encountered in the past. However, IBL re-
lies on simple fixed form functions to capture the mapping
from past situations to current decisions. To that end, we pro-
pose two new attention-based neural network models to have
open form non-linear functions to model distinct and hetero-
geneous human decision-making in dynamic settings. We ex-
periment with two distinct datasets gathered from human sub-
ject experiment data, one focusing on detection of phishing
email by humans and another where humans act as attack-
ers in a cybersecurity setting and decide on an attack option.
We conducted extensive experiments with our two neural net-
work models, IBL, and GPT3.5, and demonstrate that the
neural network models outperform IBL significantly in repre-
senting human decision-making, while providing similar in-
terpretability of human decisions as IBL. Overall, our work
yields promising results for further use of neural networks in
cognitive modeling of human decision making. Our code is
available at https://github.com/shshnkreddy/NCM-HDM.
Introduction
Since the early days of AI, cognitive approaches to AI have
aimed to emulate human cognitive processes with the goal
of designing computer agents that exhibit human-like deci-
sion making (Riedl and Bulitko 2012; Gonzalez 2023) and
(possibly superior) intelligence (Newell 1994). While recent
advances in AI have relied on neural networks, the goal of
making realistic human agents is still very relevant. Work in

this space has built human-like agents, particularly in game
play (Laird 2001; Miyashita et al. 2017; Siu et al. 2021;
Asfour and Murillo 2023). Prior approaches aim to repli-
cate human decision making in the aggregate (e.g., have one
model for all humans) or within broad categories of human
personalities (e.g., impulsive, cautious) or simple person-
alized models. However, human decision-making is com-
plex and heterogeneous, which does not neatly fit into cate-
gories of personalities. The need for high fidelity personal-
ized model of observed humans’ decision-making arises in
many domains such as human detection of phishing email,
understanding human attackers in cyber attacks, knowledge
consumption in education settings, and many others. In the
application of phishing, attacks rely on heterogeneity of
decision-making, including human fallacies, to be success-
ful. Developing realistic dynamic decision-making models
for a particular human from observed past decisions can lead
to the development of personalized interventions that make
humans better at detecting phishing emails. In this work, we
aim to build high fidelity, fine-grained and data driven mod-
els of human behavior in specific complex tasks.
We base our work on a popular cognitive model of human
behavior, namely Instance-Based Learning (IBL). IBL is a
decision-making algorithm guided by the recognition and re-
trieval of past experiences (instances) by their similarity to
the current decision situation (Gonzalez, Lerch, and Lebiere
2003). An instance is a memory unit that consists of three el-
ements: a situation (set of attributes that give context to the
decision, or state s); a decision (the action taken); and a feed-
back (utility or experienced outcome of the action). The IBL
model has been widely used in various contexts to model
human behavior (Gonzalez, Lerch, and Lebiere 2003; Cran-
ford et al. 2021a); however, IBL uses simple fixed-form for-
mulas to represent the mapping from past memory instances
to current decision. Other works in human behavior mod-
eling produce a monolithic model (Cheung and Bell 2021;
Binz and Schulz 2024) or model generic behavior on many
tasks (Mao et al. 2023; Park et al. 2023). In contrast, we aim
to mimic diverse and complex behavior for a specific task.
We build two different attention-based neural network
models. The core cognitive idea borrowed from IBL is to
personalize the model to each human by conditioning it on
the memory of the human and our main novelty is in us-

ing the rich class of neural network based functions to map
the past memory units, i.e., tuples of (situation, action, feed-
back), and the current situation to the next decision. We posit
that the neural network’s ability to represent mappings with
greater richness, compared to the simple fixed-form func-
tional representation in IBL, allows our model to more accu-
rately capture complex human behavior, resulting in higher
fidelity. Our first model, Token-Level Personalized Mem-
ory Integrated Model (TL-PMIM), is based on fine-tuning
a Large Language Model (LLM), namely Llama 7B, with
the given limited dataset, that provides a highly expressive
model but at the cost of interpretability. Our second hy-
brid model, Instance-Level Personalized Memory Integrated
Model (IL-PMIM), balances expressivity and interpretabil-
ity by building a simple attention layered model, where the
attention weights provide direct interpretability of decision
making. We also consider GPT3.5 as another alternative.
We conducted thorough experiments using data gath-
ered in prior work from two separate human subject ex-
periments: one experiment involved participants detecting
phishing emails and another experiment involved partici-
pants acting as attackers in a cyber-security deception game.
We analyze the results from IBL, TL-PMIM, IL-PMIM,
and GPT3.5. We find that TL-PMIM best mimics individual
human behavior, outperforming other approaches. We also
show that the prior knowledge inbuilt in GPT may not al-
ways be beneficial in modeling a single human’s behavior.
Overall, we believe that our work at the intersection of be-
havioral science and AI will open doors for further research
in this area.
Related Work
Human Behavioral Modeling: There is a vast amount of
literature on modeling human behavior in different areas
of research (Fuchs, Passarella, and Conti 2023; Kahneman
and Smith 2002), all of which is not possible to describe
in short. IBL differs from other prominent cognitive models
such as Soar (Laird 2022) (cognitive architecture) and ACT-
R (uses a production rule system), in its reliance on spe-
cific instances rather than abstract rules or distributed repre-
sentations. More related, IBL and the Generalized Context
Model (Nosofsky 2011) share similarities in the use of simi-
larity judgments by comparing new stimuli with instances
in memory and considering context sensitivity. However,
IBL models can be applied to general decision-making and
problem-solving across various domains, including catego-
rization and decisions from experience, while GCM models
are specifically designed to explain categorization processes.
Reinforcement learning (RL) also has cognitive basis, how-
ever, not without criticism (Eckstein, Wilbrecht, and Collins
2021). But, the tasks considered in this work do not have
the flavor of being concerned about future rewards, which
is why building on IBL (that has extensive usages and is
very well established on these tasks) is promising. Binz and
Schulz (2024) finetune an LLM on simple psychological ex-
periments, unlike the real-world complex tasks considered
in this work. They also lack a memory module and hence do
not allow for ready personalization, instead they build a joint
model and simulate a variety of behaviors by adding random
effects. Similarly, Xiong et al. (2023) propose an RNN based
model but mention a memory module as future work.
Human Behavioral Modeling in Phishing Email Do-
main: Researchers have thoroughly investigated human be-
havior when tasked to identify a new (phishing or ham)
email. Prior work has studied the factors that make a phish-
ing email hard to be detected (Singh et al. 2020). More-
over, prior work has applied instance-based learning (IBL)
in modeling human’s response to the emails from observed
data (Cranford et al. 2019; Xu, Singh, and Rajivan 2022).
A recent work (Asfour and Murillo 2023) has attempted to
simulate human behavior response to phishing emails us-
ing LLMs by asking the LLM to simulate broad personality
traits; this work is not data driven and does not provide a
personalized modeling of a particular human (based on ob-
served past decisions) and hence such work cannot be used
for developing personalized interventions for training hu-
mans to detect emails. Prior work on interventions has also
mostly relied on domain expert advice (Sheng et al. 2009;
Kumaraguru et al. 2010) rather than building a personalized
model of human sub-optimality.
Somewhat further away, there are works that focus on
generating phishing emails by LLMs (Hazell 2023; Karan-
jai 2022; Sharma et al. 2023) and on detecting phishing
emails by LLMs (Heiding et al. 2023). Some works have
built autonomous agents using LLMs with memory compo-
nents (Mao et al. 2023; Park et al. 2023). These works build
more generic agents and study emergent behavior, unlike our
goal of heterogeneous personalized models built from ob-
served past decisions in a specific task.
Human Behavioral Modeling of Attackers in Cyber-
security: This is a relatively less explored research topic.
Some research in this area has originated from behavioral
game theory that explores beyond the rational model of
human behavior (Cheung and Bell 2021; Thakoor et al.
2020) originating in Stackelberg security games (Sinha et al.
2018). Recently, there have been applications of the IBL
model in more realistic modeling of attacker behavior (Cran-
ford et al. 2021a). More broadly, work in physical secu-
rity has explored different aspects of human behavior in
games (Kar et al. 2015).

Token-Level Model
Pretrained on extensive datasets, LLMs have demonstrated
significant potential in tasks such as text generation and se-
mantic analysis. Previous work has found that fine-tuning a
pre-trained LLM enhances its ability to address new prob-
lems effectively. Following the IBL model, we incorporate
personalized memory into the LLM, where this memory
comprises a sequence of memory instances. This approach is
called Token-Level Personalized Memory Integrated Model
(TL-PMIM). This approach is implemented by integrating
the memory into the context. Mathematically, we concate-
nate the instance sequences in the memory with the current
situation as the input st and construct the input-output pair
(xt, at) where xt is:

While TL-PMIM offers substantial model expressivity, it

GPT3.5 Approach
We also experiment with the zero-shot performance of
LLMs as cognitive models. Similarly to the TL-PMIM
model, the GPT3.5 model is incorporated with a personal-
ized memory module. Due to cost reasons, we cannot pass
the entire memory in the prompt, hence we process the mem-
ory to retrieve the top k instances. Let st be the current email
in consideration, then each instance in the memory (si, ai)
for i ∈[1, t −1] is given a score Sim(si, st), where Sim
is a domain specific similarity function. The top k instances
si, ai are then retrieved to create the input xt as,
xt = s1||a1||u1 . . . sk||ak||uk||st
The LLM is then prompted to provide action at. Note that
the full prompt also contains additional information such as
task description and an in-context example to help the LLM.
It is also asked to provide a justification for its decision to
ensure that the LLM uses the memory to make the decision
vs its own prior knowledge. An example of the prompt and
justification given by the LLM is provided in the appendix.
Experiments
The experiments are designed to evaluate if our approach
can model human responses with high fidelity, i.e., deci-
sion pattern of our model is similar to that seen in the data.
For TL-PMIM, we have fine-tuned Llama 2 7B base us-
ing LongLora (Chen et al. 2023). For the encoding E in
IL-PMIM we use a sentence transformer, namely all-mpnet-
base-v1 (Hugging Face 2024).
We consider two baselines from the literature. One is the
IBL model (Cranford et al. 2019, 2021b). Another is the di-
rect use of the GPT3.5 model. All neural network experi-
ments were run on NVIDIA Quadro RTX 5000 GPUs except
for TL-PMIM, which was run on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The
hyperparameters for TL-PMIM are constant learning rate
2e-5 with a warmup of 20 steps, batch size 32, 1 training
epoch with AdamW optimizer. The hyperparameters for IL-
PMIM are a constant learning rate 5e-6 with a batch size
of 64 and a dropout of 0.1. The key, query and value em-
bedding dimensions are 256. The model is trained for 1000
epochs with an Adam optimizer.

ing email, the other requires quantitative risk assessment
skills to decide the best course of action. Data for both do-
mains were obtained from human subject studies in prior
works. We describe these human subject studies in brief and
the data gathered from these studies below.

IAG dataset: The Insider Attack Game (IAG) is designed
as an online game that simulates real-world scenarios in the
lab such as in (Cooney et al. 2020). It involves two stages:
first, defenders protect nodes (computers) by a randomized
allocation of limited resources and the attacker chooses a
node to attack. In the second stage, the defender chooses
probabilistically to either warn the attacker that the node is
defended (which could be a lie) or not provide such warning,
and then the attackers choose to continue the attack or not.
The defender’s defense and deceptive strategies are decided
based on prior work in Stackelberg games (Xu et al. 2015).
The game has six nodes (computers), with the resource con-
strained defender able to protect only two.
In the human subject study, participants acted as insider
attackers, aiming to gain points by attacking nodes. Attack-
ers earn points if the node they attack is undefended but lose
points if caught. The exact number of points depends on the
node, ranging from -10 to +10. If an attacker withdraws the
attack in the second stage, they don’t earn any points. Here,
we aim to model the second stage decision of the human
participant, i.e., whether to continue the attack or not. The
decision to continue the attack is optimal if the ground truth
status of the node is uncovered (undefended) and not optimal

Conclusion and Limitations
Our work aims to show how neural networks can enrich cog-
nitive modeling of dynamic decision-making. We note a lim-
itation that is generally true for most human subject studies.
We showed our results with data collected in a structured
lab setting for real world tasks where the environment can
be very complex. E.g., when detecting phishing emails in
an office setting, humans can be juggling many other tasks,
and therefore factors such as the time available to make the
decision become important; these have been considered in
cognitive science (Kerstholt 1995). It is always possible to
expand our neural network models with these additional fac-
tors (if measured and recorded) but such a work is beyond
the scope of a single paper. Overall, our work has shown that
there is positive synergy to be gained from bringing together
concepts from modern deep learning and human cognition

Additional IBL Details
While IBL is a baseline for our work, we performed some tuning. We conducted model fitting to determine the optimal decay
parameter for each participant, taking into account previous research indicating that effective adjustment to choice environments
is linked to higher decay levels (d) (Konstantinidis, Harman, and Gonzalez 2022). This suggests that a weaker memory may
actually lead to better adaptation. Our decay search involved testing decay values ranging from 0.1 to 3, in increments of 0.01.
We analyzed the synchronization of model predictions with participant decisions for each of the models generated (291 models)
for each participant. At each trial t, we assigned a value of 1 when the model prediction aligned with the human action and
a value of 0 when it did not for each participant. Subsequently, we calculated the proportion of synchronization, which we
call SyncRate, for each model and participant. The model exhibiting the highest SyncRate was selected as the best fit for the
participant’s data. In cases where multiple SyncRates had the same highest value, the highest decay value was chosen.
