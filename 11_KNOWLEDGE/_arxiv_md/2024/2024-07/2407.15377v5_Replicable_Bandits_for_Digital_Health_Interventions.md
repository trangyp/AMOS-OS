Replicable Bandits for Digital Health
Interventions
Kelly W. Zhang, Nowell Closser, Anna L. Trella, and Susan A. Murphy
Abstract. Adaptive treatment assignment algorithms, such as bandit algo-
rithms, are increasingly used in digital health intervention clinical trials. Fre-
quently the data collected from these trials is used to conduct causal infer-
ence and related data analyses to decide how to refine the intervention, and
whether to roll-out the intervention more broadly. This work studies infer-
ence for estimands that depend on the adaptive algorithm itself; a simple
example is the mean reward under the adaptive algorithm. Specifically, we
investigate the replicability of statistical analyses concerning such estimands
when using data from trials deploying adaptive treatment assignment algo-
rithms. We demonstrate that many standard statistical estimators can be in-
consistent and fail to be replicable across repetitions of the clinical trial, even
as the sample size grows large. We show that this non-replicability is inti-
mately related to properties of the adaptive algorithm itself. We introduce a
formal definition of a "replicable bandit algorithm" and prove that under such
algorithms, a wide variety of common statistical estimators are guaranteed to
be consistent and asymptotically normal. We present both theoretical results
and simulation studies based on a mobile health oral health self-care inter-
vention. Our findings underscore the importance of designing adaptive algo-
rithms with replicability in mind, especially for settings like digital health
where deployment decisions rely heavily on replicated evidence. We con-
clude by discussing open questions on the connections between algorithm
design, statistical inference, and experimental replicability.
Key words and phrases: bandit algorithms, digital health, replicability, adap-
tive treatment assignment.
1. INTRODUCTION
Adaptive treatment assignment algorithms, e.g., bandit
[50] and more general reinforcement learning (RL) algo-
rithms [83], are increasingly used in clinical trials, social
science experiments, and by tech companies to personal-
ize treatment decisions to individuals [24, 80, 87, 86, 54,
66, 3, 95, 57]. These algorithms are adaptive because they
learn during the course of the trial, i.e., they are updated
periodically using accruing data collected during the trial.
After an adaptive algorithm is deployed, various statisti-
cal analyses are needed to answer questions such as: What
was the average reward under the deployed algorithm?

What was the effect of treatment on other outcomes of
interest? What about in a particular state? Further, statisti-
cal analyses can be used to inform the design of the adap-
tive algorithm for the next implementation, e.g., design
choices regarding the state space, action space, and/or
choice of reward.
In this work, we investigate the replicability of statisti-
cal analyses for data from trials deploying adaptive treat-
ment assignment algorithms. We focus on digital health
intervention clinical trials; replicability of clinical trials is
particularly critical as these trials are intended to provide
evidence regarding future implementation on the broader
population of such individuals. We use the term replica-
bility in the spirit of Reproducibility and Replicability
in Science [65], where they say that replicability means
“obtaining consistent results across studies aimed at an-
swering the same scientific question, each of which has
obtained its own data.” The definition of replicability in
clinical trials refers to replications of the same study de-

Replicability ensures that other scientists will reach sim-
ilar conclusions, i.e., have similar post-trial data analy-
sis results, if they implement the same adaptive algo-
rithm with a different group of individuals sampled from
the same population. Replicability is closely related to
the concept of “Stability” in Vertical Data Science (Pre-
dictability, Computability, and Stability) from Yu and
Kumbier [101].
There are many notions of replicability discussed in the
literature. In the reinforcement learning literature there
are a variety of definitions of what it means for the algo-
rithm or the policies learned by the algorithm to be repli-
cable [43, 18, 46, 20]. At a high level, algorithmic repli-
cability involves repeated use of the algorithm on inputs,
sampled from the same underlying distribution, leading to
the same or similar outputs. In contrast, we aim for “in-
ferential” replicability. This is replicability of the statisti-
cal inferential methods across trial replications [29]. This
work will begin to shed light on the ways that these two
types of replicability are related.
The main focus of this work is on how to ensure the
replicability of statistical analyses for digital health inter-
vention experiments that utilize adaptive algorithms for
treatment assignment. While several works have shown
that valid statistical inference is possible, they generally
assume idealized settings, e.g., each individual’s respon-
siveness to treatment does not change over time and/or
the post-trial inference uses a correctly specified outcome
model. In this work, we discuss how basic notions of
replicability of statistical analyses can fail when the true
environment begins to deviate from these idealized set-
tings. In health, the true environment is highly likely to
differ from these idealized setting as the environment con-
cerns complex human behaviors.
In particular, we show that on adaptively collected
data, the replicability of many standard statistical esti-
mators is intimately related to the replicability of the
policies formed by the adaptive algorithm used to col-
lect the data (see Section 4 for the formal definition of
replicable adaptive algorithms). This is true specifically
for estimators of quantities, like the expected value of
a health outcome on average across individuals in the
trial, which depends on the treatment assignments made
by the adaptive algorithm. We show that standard statis-
tical estimators for such quantities can be inconsistent
and fail to be replicable across repetitions of an exper-
iment, if the policies formed by the adaptive algorithm
are “non-replicable”. For example, we show in simple
settings that common treatment effect estimators will be
positive (strictly bounded above zero) for a constant frac-
tion of trial replications and negative (strictly bounded
below zero) for another constant fraction of replications;
moreover, this is the case even as the number of individu-
als in each replicated trial goes to infinity. Furthermore,
we show that many standard bandit algorithms, like ϵ-
greedy and Thompson sampling [78], can fail to satisfy
such replicability requirements in these very simple set-
tings.
This work underscores the importance of ensuring in-
ferential replicability when using adaptive algorithms. In-
ferential replicability is critical for the wider adoption
and application of these algorithms in settings like digi-
tal health and experimental design. Furthermore, we ar-
gue that researchers in the area of adaptive experimen-
tation must prioritize the development of algorithms and
methodologies that ensure inferential replicability.
1.1 Relationship to existing literature
There is a long history of works on adaptive experi-
mentation in the biostatistics literature [13, 36, 41, 15,
30, 8, 67, 14, 79, 106]. This literature primarily focuses
on decision-making settings that involve making a sin-
gle decision per individual (some exceptions that con-
sider longitudinal data are [40, 98] which focus on adap-
tive stopping rules and [5] which considers Gaussian out-
comes). These works primarily develop post-trial infer-
ence methods that are specific to the adaptive treatment
assignment algorithm (e.g., algorithms like randomized
play-the-winner and drop-the-loser are commonly consid-
ered).
In contrast, a recent line of work develops inference
methods that make minimal assumptions on the adaptive
algorithm, and are compatible with common bandit algo-
rithms like Thompson sampling and ϵ-greedy. This line
of work includes methods that focus on off-policy evalu-
ation (i.e., evaluating the value of a policy) [92, 10, 102]
or inference on the mean reward under a particular treat-
ment in a multi-arm bandit [31, 10, 35, 33, 103, 63]. An-
other line of work aims to infer the parameters in a cor-
rectly specified outcome model [17, 104, 99, 44]. See
Bibaut and Kallus [9] for a nice overview. The finding
in many of these previous works is that standard estima-
tors (e.g., means, least squares, M/Z-estimators) on adap-
tively data are consistent (i.e., converging to the correct

value). However, the distribution of these standard esti-
mators can be asymptotically non-normal and converge
non-uniformly in distribution, depending on properties of
the bandit algorithm itself [103, 31, 17]. For this reason,
various works consider altered versions of standard es-
timators that incorporate “adaptive” weights that depend
on the treatment propensities formed by the bandit algo-
rithm [31, 10, 104, 103, 44, 102, 17, 9]; these weights can
help ensure that standard estimators are still asymptoti-
cally normal on adaptively collected data.
Generally the above works assume that the environment
is stationary and that outcome models are correctly spec-
ified. In this paper, we investigate inference on adaptively
collected data in the digital health setting, where these as-
sumptions may fail to hold, specifically under nonstation-
ary environments and misspecified outcome models. See
the next section for a discussion of why misspecified out-
come models are common in digital health. Classically,
estimators formed by minimizing a loss function based
on a misspecified model will be consistent none-the-less
for the minimizers of the loss function (e.g., negative log
likelihood, L2 loss). In this work, we show that on adap-
tively collected data, under these types of deviations in the
environment (non-stationarity and outcome model mis-
specification), standard estimators can be inconsistent for
the parameters that minimize the associated loss function.
This is a very severe consequence of using adaptive algo-
rithms, which has significant consequences for inferential
replicability.
Another line of work uses randomization-based meth-
ods for inference on adaptively collected data [63, 26].
Freidling et al. [26] only consider inference for finite pop-
ulations, which is not the focus of this work. Nair and
Janson [63] provide a variety of methods (e.g. conditional
independence testing in Markov decision processes, pre-
diction intervals) and the one that is closest to our work
considers inference for a fixed treatment effect in a con-
textual bandit setting. This differs from our work in that
(i) they do not accommodate longitudinal data, (ii) their
estimand cannot depend on the data collection algorithm
(e.g. cannot be used to infer the average reward under the
algorithm), and (iii) their method only applies to location
and scale family outcomes (e.g. does not work for binary
outcomes).
Finally, there are works that formalize the replicability
of a decision-making rule or algorithm [43, 18, 46, 20,
69]. Many of these works build on the framework intro-
duced in Impagliazzo et al. [39], which formalizes what it
means for a randomized algorithm to be replicable (these
works are discussed further in Section 4.2). In this work,
we focus on the replicability of statistical analyses, rather
than on ensuring replicability of the adaptive algorithm;
however, we show that the replicability of post-trial sta-
tistical analyses is often closely related to the replicability
of the adaptive data collection algorithm (see Sections 3
and 4).
2. PROBLEM SETTING
2.1 Common characteristics of digital health
intervention trials
The motivation for this work is the increasing use of
adaptive algorithms in digital intervention experiments.
These interventions are designed to encourage individu-
als to engage in healthy behaviors. These interventions
focus on a variety of health areas—including physical ac-
tivity [54, 100, 24, 59, 108, 45, 85, 107, 60], medica-
tion adherence [51], smoking cessation [6, 23, 1], men-
tal health [7], reducing alcohol consumption [55], chronic
pain [70], dieting [25, 28, 74], and assisting elderly indi-
viduals [34, 58]. In digital health interventions, the adap-
tive algorithm itself is often considered a component of
the entire intervention package. That is, if the digital in-
tervention is found to be cost-effective, it would be rolled
out with the adaptive algorithm component. Thus the ef-
fectiveness of the intervention package will depend on the
quality of the algorithm and the decisions it makes.
EXAMPLE (Oralytics).
In this work, we use the se-
ries of Oralytics clinical trials as a running example
[62, 86, 87, 88]. Oralytics is a dental health intervention
designed to augment standard dental care by providing
additional support over a 10-week period. In the Oralyt-
ics trial, individuals are given a Bluetooth-enabled tooth-
brush and asked to download the Oralytics mobile app.
During the the trial, individuals may receive engagement
messages encouraging toothbrushing via the app before
their usual morning and evening brushing windows (i.e.,
up to twice daily). An adaptive decision-making algo-
rithm (e.g., a contextual bandit) is used to optimize (per-
sonalize) whether or not to deliver an engagement mes-
sage. Individual’s toothbrushing behaviors are automati-
cally detected with the Bluetooth-enabled toothbrush.
Oralytics involves a series of clinical trials, where
each trial uses a contextual bandit algorithm. Between
trials, the contextual bandit algorithm is redeveloped
[86, 87, 62, 88]. At this time, the Oralytics algorithm is
being revised for deployment in a two-arm trial for late
spring 2025. In this two-arm trial, individuals will be ran-
domized between the standard of care versus standard of
care augmented with the digital intervention delivered us-
ing the revised contextual bandit algorithm. The primary
outcome in this trial will be the difference in measures of
overall dental health between the two groups at the end
of the trial period. Further Oralytics digital intervention
trials are in the planning stages.
We now highlight some common characteristics of dig-
ital health intervention trials that use adaptive algorithms
(including Oralytics).

1. Multiple decision-times over time per individual:
Digital health interventions involve making multiple
decisions on the same individual over time. For exam-
ple, treatment decisions could be whether or not to de-

ber of decision times per individual are fixed and par-
ticular to the domain, e.g., twice per day over a 70-day
period to improve brushing behaviors.
2. Data-sharing bandit algorithms: Digital interven-
tion settings often have low signal relative to the
amount of noise in outcomes. This is because treat-
ments are delivered to individuals as they go about
their everyday lives, as opposed to in a clinic. More-
over, clinical trials generally only involve a limited
number of individuals. Thus, in the digital health set-
ting developers of adaptive algorithms must carefully
trade-off bias and variance due to the high variance
in outcomes typical in digital health. As a result, con-
textual bandit algorithms (simple RL algorithms) are
most often used [54, 70, 24, 59, 7, 55, 100, 51, 62].
To further trade-off bias and variance, it is also
common for bandit algorithms to combine or “pool”
data across heterogeneous individuals in the trial to
improve learning, i.e., the algorithm may adapt the
treatment decisions for one individual based on previ-
ous treatment outcomes from others [100, 24, 70, 62].
These data-sharing or pooling algorithms reduce noise
and potentially learn faster.
3. The primary data analysis is prespecified in clinical
trials: All National Institute of Health (NIH) funded
clinical trials, including the post-trial statistical analy-
ses, are required to be preregistered. This means that
the analyses that will be performed cannot be changed
without jeopardizing the validity of the trial.
In this work, we consider digital health settings in which
adaptive treatment assignment algorithms pool data over
individuals to learn. Further, we make the simplifying as-
sumption that individuals are all recruited into the trial
all at once (which is common in trials that recruit on the
internet). In general, digital health intervention trials typ-
ically recruit individuals into the trial incrementally or in
batches over time. Though we do not explicitly handle in-
cremental recruitment in this paper, we conjecture that the
key ideas below will generalize to that setting.

3. TWO EXAMPLES OF NON-REPLICABILITY
This section aims to demonstrate the need to think care-

digital health. We consider two examples in which we

4. REPLICABLE BANDITS
The non-replicable statistical analyses examples we
presented in the previous section are fundamentally due
to the non-replicability of the bandit algorithms used
to make treatment decisions (see the discussion below
Proposition 3.1 for additional intuition behind this). In
this section, we show that if the bandit algorithm itself
satisfies certain “replicability” properties, then a variety
of statistical analyses after the experiment will be repli-
cable (we discuss the types of statistical analyses we are
interested in detail later in Section 5). In other words, if
the bandit algorithm is replicable, then the non-replicable
statistical analyses phenomena from the previous section
will no longer occur. These observations lead to a variety
of open questions regarding how to design bandit algo-
rithms with replicability in mind, which we list in Section
7.

4.4.4 Choosing the allocation function. The replicable
algorithms we describe above require choosing an “allo-
cation function”, which maps a given estimated treatment
effect to an action selection probability. For example, in
Boltzmann sampling from (16), the allocation function is
a softmax function with a temperature parameter s that we
need to choose. In smoothed Thompson sampling from
(18), the allocation function is ρ. In choosing the alloca-
tion function, there are multiple (competing) objectives,
specifically, reward maximization and power maximiza-
tion. See Chapter 2.2 of Hu and Rosenberger [36] and
Simchi-Levi and Wang [81] for further discussion of the
tradeoff between reward maximization and power maxi-
mization.
If the environment is a bandit environment,4 then in
the limit, rewards are maximized if the allocation func-
tion approaches an indicator function, e.g., for smoothed
Thompson sampling, this means ρ will approach ρ(x) =
1x>0 and in Boltzmann sampling s should approach pos-
itive infinity. Note, though, that if an indicator allocation
function is used, the algorithm will fail to be replicable.
In contrast, to maximize the power to detect a treatment
effect, it is optimal for the allocation function to approach
the Neyman allocation [64]; in simple bandit environ-
ments where the reward noise variance is the same for
all actions, the Neyman allocation selects all actions with
equal probability.
In other words, the choice of the allocation function
will simultaneously affect the algorithm’s ability to max-
imize rewards and the power of statistical analyses to
detect a treatment effect. Though we do not explore it
thoroughly in this work, we believe that the allocation
function can be chosen as a part of the clinical trial de-
sign by extending methods from standard power analyses
[16, 53], which specify that for a standardized effect size
of interest (e.g., 0.1 or 0.2) the power must be sufficiently
high (e.g., ≥80%). We discuss further open questions re-
lated to this point later in Section 7.

replicability of the underlying bandit algorithm used to
make treatment decisions. We show that in a variety of
misspecified environments (non-stationary and misspec-
ified reward model), when common bandit algorithms
are used to assign treatments, standard estimators can
be inconsistent and thus non-replicable across trials. We

There are several open questions given this work, which
we summarize below:
Replicability and regret minimization: What are the
consequences of ensuring replicability of the bandit algo-
rithm on regret [42]? Is it possible to ensure that a bandit
algorithm is both replicable and minimizes regret opti-
mally? We found in our experiments in Section 6.1 that
the algorithm that was not replicable lead to a bit higher
rewards than an algorithm that was replicable. What about
algorithms that are designed to optimally balance regret
minimization and the power of a particular pre-specified
post-trial primary analysis [19, 36, 81, 73, 47]?
Replicability and robustness: Several papers have found
that algorithms that minimize regret aggressively can be
less robust to model misspecification [22, 21, 96]. Could
there be a relationship between replicability and ensuring
the algorithm minimizes regret robustly across many en-
vironments?

holds: (a) I[ ¯Bi,t > b] (individual brushes well) and I[ ¯Ai,t > a1] (individual was sent too many engagement prompts for

will be used the reward definition.
The first time an individual’s criterion has been met, the individual’s future effect sizes ∆i,B,∆i,N starting at time
t + 1 will be scaled down proportionally by E = 0.5 (the individual is less responsive to treatment). Namely, the effect
sizes will be shrunken to E · ∆i,B,E · ∆i,N. Then after a week, at time t + 14, we will check the criterion again. If the

only have their effect size shrunk at most once a week. This procedure simulates how the individual may experience
a reduction in responsiveness, but after a week, if the RL algorithm does not intervene too much, the individual may
recover their prior responsivity.
