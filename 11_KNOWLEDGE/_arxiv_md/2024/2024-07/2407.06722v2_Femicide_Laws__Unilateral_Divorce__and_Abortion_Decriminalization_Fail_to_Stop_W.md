Femicide Laws, Unilateral Divorce, and Abortion Decriminalization Fail to Stop 
Women from Being Killed in Mexico 
 
Roxana Gutiérrez-Romero♣ 
 
July 2025 
 
Abstract 
This paper evaluates the effectiveness of femicide laws in combating gender-based killings of 
women, a major cause of premature female mortality. Focusing on Mexico, a pioneer in 
adopting such legislation, the paper exploits variations in the enactment of femicide laws and 
prison sentences across states. Using the difference-in-differences estimator, the analysis 
reveals femicide laws have not impacted femicides, homicides, disappearances, or suicides of 
women. Results remain robust when considering differences in prison sentencing, states 
introducing unilateral divorce, equitable divorce asset compensation, or decriminalizing 
abortion. Findings also hold with synthetic matching, suggesting laws are insufficient to 
combat gender-based violence in contexts of impunity. 
 
Keywords: femicide laws, gender-based violence, difference-in-differences, synthetic control, 
prison sentences, unilateral divorce, asset compensation, abortion decriminalization

1. Introduction 
Femicides—the hate killings of girls and women because of their gender—make up a small 
fraction of total homicides, but their rise is deeply alarming (Koppa and Messing 2021). For 
instance, in 2022, the world saw 243 women murdered daily, the highest annual figure in two 
decades (UNODC 2022). Nearly 40% of these victims were killed by intimate partners or ex-
partners, making femicide one of the leading causes of premature female deaths (Corradi 2021; 
WHO 2021). While gender-based violence is a global issue, the surge in femicides is especially 
severe in developing regions, notably Latin America and the Caribbean (Small Arms Survey 
2018). This region has experienced the fastest growth in such crimes, prompting 18 out of 33 
countries to enact femicide laws aimed at confronting this public health crisis. These laws 
typically categorize femicides as hate crimes and impose stricter penalties than those for 
conventional homicides (García-Del Moral and Neumann 2019). Recently, some European 
nations, including Belgium, Croatia, Cyprus, and Malta have also adopted femicide laws, with 
many others considering similar measures. However, despite public debate, the effectiveness 
of these laws remains uncertain, as no comprehensive impact evaluation has been conducted 
to date.1  
This paper is the first to evaluate the impact of femicide laws on lethal violence against 
women, considering outcomes such as femicides, homicides, suicides, and disappearances. I 
focus on Mexico, which was one of the first countries to recognize gender-based homicide as 
an aggravating factor and to subsequently enact femicide legislation nationwide. Between 2010 
and 2019, all 32 states gradually adopted femicide laws, though with significant variation in 
prison sentencing severity. What is unique to Mexico, is that during this period, several states 
also introduced other legal reforms affecting family and women’s rights, including unilateral 
divorce laws (26 states), equitable asset distribution upon divorce (23 states), and abortion 
decriminalization (two states). These policy changes provide important insights into whether 
other concurrent legal reforms may enhance or undermine the effectiveness of femicide laws. 
For instance, theoretically, unilateral divorce laws may contribute to reducing 
homicides by enabling women to leave abusive relationships more easily, as seen in high-
income countries (Stevenson and Wolfers 2006). However, in Mexico, most states that enacted

To evaluate the impact of femicide laws along with associated policy changes I exploit 
the gradual adoption of this legislation across Mexican states using the staggered difference-
in-differences estimator proposed  by de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfoeuille (2022). I begin by

legislation compared to those that did not. Then, I take advantage of the great variability in the 
timing of enactment of other laws, to derive causal estimates of the impact of femicide laws 
when complemented by other legislative changes, such as unilateral divorce reforms, new 
equitable divorce asset compensation, or the decriminalization of abortion.  
Another key component of the legislative changes is the difference in prison sentences 
for femicides and homicides among states and over time. The staggered difference-in-
differences estimator used allows me to assess whether tougher sentencing for femicide had a 
differential impact compared to states using more lenient penalties. Additionally, I examine 
whether the differences between femicide-specific and general homicide-specific sentencing 
had any impact on lethal violence against women.  
A major challenge in evaluating the impact of femicide laws is the lack of reliable 
official data. By law, femicides in Mexico are defined as homicides of women exhibiting one 
or more of the following seven characteristics: sexual abuse, antecedents of violence or threats, 
public disposal of the body, degrading injuries inflicted before or after death, abduction, or 
having a close relationship with the perpetrator. Despite having this legal definition, official 
femicide statistics were not compiled until 2015—five years after the first-ever femicide state

and, in some instances, the identity of the suspected perpetrator as reported by the victim’s next

not yet adopted a legal definition for femicide. As a fourth indicator, I include the official 
monthly municipal counts of girls and women reported as disappeared, likely for being victims 
of a crime.  
A potential concern in evaluating femicide laws is determining whether their staggered 
adoption across Mexican states was an exogenous policy shift or driven by pre-existing 
violence trends in certain states. The 1990s murders in Ciudad Juárez, on Mexico’s northern 
border, undoubtedly brought global attention to the country’s crisis of gender-based violence. 
However, the subsequent enactment of femicide laws was not merely a reaction to local 
conditions but part of a broader wave of transnational feminist activism that reshaped legal 
frameworks across Latin America. Thanks to this movement, 18 countries in the region have 
recognized femicide as a distinct hate crime (Sarmiento et al. 2014). In Mexico, feminist

This paper contributes to the literature on crime reduction interventions, particularly 
those targeting violence against women, by making five key contributions. First, it provides 
the first causal estimates of the impact of femicide laws on lethal violence against girls and 
women.  Contrary to expectations that femicide laws would reduce lethal violence, the findings 
indicate no statistically significant impact on the number of femicides, female homicides, 
female suicides, or reported disappearances of girls and women. This conclusion holds in the 
short and long term and is robust to whether other laws were enacted alongside. The results 
also remain consistent when accounting for broader factors linked to gender-based violence, 
including firearm availability—proxied by the number of males killed by firearms—and 
household stressors, like state-level unemployment rates. 
Second, this paper is the first to assess the impact of unilateral divorce laws while 
accounting for the presence of femicide legislation, addressing a key gap in understanding how 
legal frameworks on gender-based violence may interact. Mexico offers a unique setting due 
to the staggered adoption of both legal reforms, enabling a systematic assessment of their 
combined effects. Previous studies have focused solely on unilateral divorce laws, overlooking 
the potential role of femicide laws. For example, Hoehn-Velasco and Silverio-Murillo (2020) 
found that in Mexico unilateral divorce laws led divorce rates to increase by over 30% in the 
short run (2.5 years) without affecting homicides—a contrast with the homicide-reducing 
effects seen in Europe and the US (Brassiolo 2016; Stevenson and Wolfers 2006). García-
Ramos (2021) also found that unilateral divorce laws led to a modest 3.7 percentage point rise 
in intimate partner violence in Mexico six to eight years later, especially among married 
women, suggesting divorce reforms could have affected homicides in the long run. 
In line with these earlier studies, I find that the unilateral divorce reform led to an 
increase of divorce rates by over 30% soon after, and that such impact has remained for nearly 
eight years (90 months) post-intervention. Despite this persistent increase in divorces in the 
long run, there is no evidence that femicide laws—whether implemented alongside or 
independent of unilateral divorce laws—affected femicides, homicides of women, suicides, or 
disappearances of girls or women.

Extensive robustness checks also reveal no evidence that the introduction of equitable 
asset divorce compensation, which grants up to 50% of marital assets to the spouse responsible 
for household and childcare duties, has affected rates of lethal violence, suicides, or 
disappearances of women or men. While death certificates do not specify marital property 
regimes, the findings hold consistently across subgroups most likely to be affected, including 
those who were married, separated, or divorced, regardless of gender. These results add to the 
growing body of research on the effects of unilateral divorce laws on gender-based violence, 
suggesting that in contexts like Mexico, such legal reforms have neither systematically 
escalated nor mitigated lethal violence (Hoehn-Velasco and Penglase 2021b; García-Ramos 
2021; Daby and Moseley 2023).  
A third key contribution is the analysis of whether abortion decriminalization, when 
enacted alongside femicide legislation, impacts lethal gender-based violence. Prior research 
suggests that intimate partner violence can lead to non-consensual pregnancies, and 
paradoxically, that women seeking abortions may also face heightened risks of violence (Coyle 
et al. 2015; Pallitto et al. 2013; Muratori 2021; Aston and Bewley 2009). If these mechanisms 
hold, decriminalization of abortion could escalate lethal violence against women. To test this, 
I use the Mexican state of Oaxaca as a case study—an outlier where femicide laws preceded 
other legal reforms. The findings reveal no significant effect on femicides, female homicides, 
suicides, or disappearances relative to comparable states. These results reinforce the null 
impacts that femicide laws have. More importantly, these findings demonstrate that abortion 
decriminalization neither increases nor reduces lethal violence against women, offering critical 
insights for other settings considering similar policy reforms.4  
The fourth key contribution is to investigate whether the impact of femicide laws, 
unilateral divorce, and abortion decriminalization differs across 17 subgroups of female 
homicide victims, defined by age, marital status, employment, education, pregnancy status, and 
Indigenous language use. This analysis offers key insights into the potential mechanisms by 
which these legal reforms may impact patterns of lethal violence by identifying which groups, 
if any, are likely to be most affected. Once again, the findings reveal no heterogeneity across 
these dimensions, reinforcing the conclusion that these legal reforms had no measurable impact 
on trends in lethal violence against women.

Fifth, the paper contributes to the international literature on whether harsher prison 
sentences deter crime, including gender-based violence (Koppa and Messing 2021).  I begin 
by demonstrating that, contrary to popular belief, not all Mexican states initially imposed 
harsher penalties for femicide than for homicide. 5 This evidence is crucial, as it highlights the 
risks for policymakers and activist groups in enacting femicide legislation without considering 
existing homicide penalties. This variation in sentencing across states and over time allows for 
comparative analysis, revealing that longer prison sentences for femicides do not significantly 
reduce femicides, female homicides, suicides, or even disappearances, which could be a means 
of concealing femicides. These findings are consistent over both short- and long-term periods 
and remain valid when comparing the severity of sentences for femicides and homicides across 
different states. This aligns with existing empirical literature, which shows that harsher 
penalties do not necessarily deter crime (Chalfin and McCrary 2017). But even more 
importantly, the findings indicate that even with stricter penalties, offender behavior does not 
change—there is no evidence of deterrence or strategic adaptation, such as an increase in 
disappearances to avoid prosecution. 
The results hold across a range of additional robustness checks. Placebo tests using 24-
month pre-treatment periods detect no significant pre-trends, reinforcing the credibility of the 
identification strategy. Alternative outcome measures, such as relative killing rates taking into 
account the population size of women instead of absolute casualty counts, produce consistent 
findings. More importantly, the results remain stable even when applying the synthetic 
difference-in-differences method proposed by Arkhangelsky et al. (2021) which mitigates 
potential biases by constructing a data-driven control group. 
Overall, these findings are consequential to policymakers, as they show that femicide 
laws have no effect, at least in the context analyzed, where impunity is widespread. In Mexico, 
nearly 98% of all crimes, including homicides, remain unsolved (Human Rights Watch 2020), 
rendering legal reforms largely symbolic. A more comprehensive approach is needed—one 
that directly challenges impunity and the systemic injustices that allow gender-based violence 
to persist.

2. Background 
2.1 The surge of femicides and who are the killers? 
In the 1990s, Ciudad Juárez saw a sharp increase in gender-based killings, known as las 
muertas de Ciudad Juárez. The victims were often raped, mutilated, and left in public places, 
yet arrests were rare. Of the estimated 500 victims, only 20 perpetrators have been identified, 
most of whom were intimate partners or members of organized crime (Washington Valdez 
2005). The reasons for Ciudad Juárez becoming a focal point for random killings of women 
are still debated. The city’s role as a border manufacturing hub increased economic 
opportunities, but it also created a lawless zone where criminals operated with impunity 
(Livingston 2004).  
During the late 1990s, security conditions began to deteriorate in several cities 
following the victory of opposition parties in local elections, which led to the unraveling of 
long-standing arrangements between drug cartels and law enforcement. Previously, under the 
Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI), which ruled from 1929 to 2000, organized crime 
operated with relative stability, benefiting from tacit agreements that limited large-scale 
civilian violence. But the PRI’s loss of the presidency in 2000, and again in 2006, both times 
to the National Action Party (PAN), led to escalating conflicts among criminal organizations. 
In response, President Felipe Calderón deployed 45,000 troops in a militarized campaign 
against drug cartels, aiming to dismantle their leadership. Although this ‘war on drugs’ strategy 
led to the capture or killing of 25 cartel leaders and 160 lieutenants, it also contributed to 
unprecedented levels of drug-related violence. Within six years, the national homicide rate 
doubled, rising from one of the lowest in Latin America to nearly 22 homicides per 100,000 
inhabitants. While the conflict disproportionately affected men—who accounted for 90% of 
over 300,000 deaths thus far—it also fueled a sharp increase in female homicides, reversing 
prior gains in reducing violence against women. 
Despite claims that some women have been targeted because have work ties to drug 
cartels, there is no strong evidence of such.6 Instead, multiple studies have found a clear cause-
and-effect relationship between the militarized strategy against drug trafficking organizations 
and the overall increase in homicides, unemployment rates, and criminality in the country (Dell 
2015; Calderón et al. 2015; Gutiérrez-Romero 2016). The rise of overall homicides, including

2.2. Origin of femicide legislation 
Before the war on drugs, activists and lawmakers in Mexico had already expressed concerns 
about the increasing number of hate crimes against women no longer restricted to Ciudad 
Juárez. Through the efforts of transnational feminist activists, successful campaigns brought to 
light the severe negligence of the Mexican government (García-Del Moral and Neumann 
2019). In response, the European Parliament issued three condemnations regarding the 
Mexican government’s handling of these cases, while in 2005 and 2006 the UN and the Inter-
American bodies urged Mexico to criminalize femicide. In 2009, the Inter-American Court of 
Human Rights ruled that Mexico had failed to prevent, investigate, and prosecute the 
abduction, brutal rape, and murder of three young female workers in Ciudad Juárez. This ruling 
confirmed that Mexico had violated multiple human rights and engaged in gender 
discrimination. In Mexico, legislator Marcela Lagarde led a congressional commission to 
examine the roots of gender-based violence, concluding that these crimes were not isolated 
incidents, but deliberate murders driven by gender-based hatred. Drawing from Diana Russell’s 
(1977) concept of femicide, Lagarde translated it as ‘feminicidio’ to emphasize not only the 
killings but also the state’s complicity in failing to prevent and prosecute them (Lagarde 2007).8

within state-level criminal codes. In 2010, Guerrero became the first state to explicitly

3.2. Data on femicides, homicides, suicides, disappearances and divorce rates 
Mexico’s official record of femicides began in 2015, as reported by the Secretariado Ejecutivo 
del Sistema Nacional de Seguridad Pública (SESNSP). This official record-keeping started five 
years after the femicide law was enacted in Guerrero and after such laws had already been 
adopted by nearly all states. Due to the lack of official femicide data before the enactment of 
these laws and issues already mentioned with data reliability, I use four alternative proxies 
instead. 
 
The first three proxies refer to the number of presumed homicides of women (regardless 
of the perpetrator’s motive or intent, but excluding accidental deaths), likely femicides and 
female suicides, estimated directly from official death certificates and aggregated monthly at 
the municipal level from 2000 to 2020. Death certificates are issued by authorized forensic 
medical services before any investigation is carried out by public ministries, making this 
information less vulnerable to the inefficiencies of the judicial system.11 Families of the 
deceased are under significant pressure to obtain these certificates, as they are crucial 
documents required for various processes, including carrying out burials, dealing with 
inheritances and life insurance (Olamendi 2017). The anonymized death certificates are 
publicly released by the National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI). Fetal deaths 
are excluded, as they are not recorded in the death certificates analyzed.  
Official death certificates do not directly differentiate whether a homicide can be 
categorized as a femicide. However, I distinguish the likely femicides from presumed 
homicides by filtering the rich information contained in these certificates using the criteria set 
by the femicide law. I identify femicides as those homicide victims who experienced at least 
one of the following four conditions: sexual assault, a kinship/trust relationship with the alleged 
aggressor, domestic or non-domestic violence as directly classified in the certificate, or if the

4. Empirical Strategy 
I use the staggered difference-in-difference (DID) estimator, proposed by de Chaisemartin and 
D’Haultfoeuille (2022) to evaluate the impact of each relevant legislative reform. As shown in 
equation (1) this estimator, denoted by DIDg,l  compares the evolution of an outcome variable, 
Y, at time periods, t, between groups, g, that have been treated, D, and those who have not yet 
been treated from period Fg-1 to Fg+l. compensation.  
I begin by analyzing the impact of unilateral divorce laws—commonly known as no-
fault divorce—on divorce rates, which are measured monthly at the municipality level. 
However, the primary outcomes of interest, Y, are the number of femicides, homicides of 
women, female disappearances, and female suicides, each measured monthly at the municipal

level. For these outcomes, the treatment D refers to whether municipalities are in states that 
have enacted femicide laws.  
I evaluate both the short-term and long-term effects of femicide laws, distinguishing 
between cases where they were enacted independently and those where they coincided with 
other policy changes.  I address potential confounding effects by separately examining states 
that enacted exclusively femicide laws without any other reform. Then I focus on those states 
that enacted femicide laws along with unilateral divorce laws, decriminalized abortion, or the 
new asset divorce reform.

One of the advantages of the DIDg,l  estimator is its robustness to heterogeneous 
treatment effects across groups and over time. This estimator is based on the assumption that, 
in the absence of treatment, both the treatment and comparison groups would have experienced 
the same outcome evolution. This assumption can be indirectly tested by assessing whether the 
treated and untreated groups had parallel trends in outcomes before implementing the 
treatment. To this end, placebo estimators are estimated. These compare the outcome evolution 
between the treated and untreated groups before group g receives treatment, from period Fg- l.-
2 to period Fg-1. These ‘long-difference’ placebos make it easier to detect if the compared 
groups had different trends over multiple periods rather than just in two consecutive periods.

5.1 Impact of unilateral law on divorces 
I start by estimating the impact of the unilateral divorce laws on divorce rates in Mexico, using 
the staggered DID estimator. The treatment group comprises municipalities within states that 
enacted unilateral divorce laws, while the control group comprises municipalities in states that 
have yet to adopt such legislation. The outcome variable is the natural logarithm of the judicial 
divorce rate.

In Mexico, divorces can occur via administrative or judicial proceedings. 
Administrative divorces are reserved for cases where both spouses mutually agree to end their 
marriage. Judicial divorces, by contrast, occur when one spouse seeks to terminate the marriage 
unilaterally (in treatment states) or must cite specific legal grounds for divorce (as in control 
states).  
Table 1 presents the results, which show a statistically significant increase in judicial 
divorce rates attributable to unilateral divorce laws. I use four alternative specifications. The 
first one has no controls other than area and fixed effects. The second one adds the number of

domestic violence and the overall risk of femicides (Nguyen and Le 2022; La Mattina 2017;

domestic violence is affected by unemployment, and, more importantly, that unilateral divorce

account for the possibility that rising divorce rates may, over time, impact unemployment, the 
third specification lags all controls by 12 months. The fourth specification lags all controls by 
24 months.

5.2 Impact of femicide laws on femicides 
In this sub-section, I use the staggered DID estimator to assess the impact of femicide laws on 
femicide counts. The analysis includes area and time fixed effects as controls along with the 
unemployment rates of women and men (at state and quarterly levels) and the number of males 
killed by firearms (at municipal and monthly levels). I add these controls based on prior 
research which suggests economic downturns can heighten financial stress, potentially 
influencing intimate partner violence (Bhalotra et al. 2021; Anderberg et al. 2016). 
Additionally, firearm-related male deaths signal broader societal violence that can spill over, 
increasing the risk of generalized violence for women (Azariadis and Drazen 1990; Brück, 
Maio, and Miaari 2019). In the robustness section I show the results are stable even when using 
alternative control specifications.

homicides. I extend placebo tests to two years prior implementation of femicide laws using 
alternative specifications, and different measures of lethal violence.  
In addition, I analyze divorce reform components, particularly the introduction of new

6.1. Alternative controls 
One potential concern regarding the control variables is that while femicide laws show no 
impact on female homicide rates, unilateral divorce laws did increase divorce rates. In theory, 
this could affect female unemployment, though no earlier studies have found such evidence for 
Mexico (Hoehn-Velasco and Penglase 2021a). To address this, I re-run the analysis with 
alternative control sets. 
I test two alternative control specifications while excluding female unemployment. The 
first set includes male unemployment (lagged 24 months) and male firearm homicides (lagged 
24 months). The lag structure accounts for potential delayed effects of unilateral divorce law 
on unemployment. The second set of controls removes unemployment and firearm homicides 
altogether. Instead, it includes a dummy variable indicating whether the state had enacted 
equitable divorce asset compensation, along with the current annual municipal-level satellite 
light intensity and its 12-month lag. In both sets of alternative controls, I retain area and time 
fixed effects. Because some control variables are lagged, the maximum number of post-
treatment analysis is reduced for the analyses of unilateral divorce law to 65 periods instead of 
90. 
The alternative specifications, shown in Table A.3 in the supplementary appendix, 
confirm that the analyzed reforms had no statistically significant effect on reducing femicides, 
female homicides, disappearances, or suicides. That is the case whether analyzing states that 
implemented exclusively femicide laws without any other additional reforms (columns 1-2), 
those with only equitable divorce asset compensation (columns 3-4), those that combined 
femicide and unilateral divorce laws (columns 5-6), or those that later decriminalized abortion 
(columns 7-8).  
The dynamic effects of the alternative specifications using the first set of controls are 
shown in Figure A.4, and those using the second set are shown in Figure A.5. Both figures 
show no evidence of parallel trends violations and indicate that the analyzed laws had no impact 
on reducing lethal violence against women in either the short or long run.

6.3 Tougher prison sentences for femicides  
A key aspect of evaluating femicide laws is determining whether their impact depends on 
sentencing severity. An advantage of the staggered DID estimator used is to allow for 
measuring the effects of varying treatment intensity, enabling me to test whether states with 
longer sentences experience different outcomes than those with more lenient penalties. As a 
robustness check, I assess whether femicide laws are more effective when accompanied by 
harsher sentences. The analysis retains the same control variables used in the main results, 
Section 5. 
To determine the sentencing severity, I compute the mean of the maximum and 
minimum femicide sentences specified in each state’s criminal code. These sentencing lengths 
change across states and over time, providing an identification strategy that leverages both 
variations in the timing of femicide law adoption and differences in sentence length.

6.4 Femicides and homicides differential prison sentences 
Potential criminals may not consider the expected average prison sentences for femicides in 
isolation; they are likely to weigh the additional time they might serve compared to existing 
sentences for homicides. This is one of the reasons why femicide laws, in theory, implement 
tougher sentences relative to those for homicides. 
 
The fourth robustness check assesses whether states with larger disparities in average 
prison sentences between femicides and homicides have any varying impact on femicides, 
homicides, disappearances or suicides of women. To consider the broadest  number of states, 
the treatment group includes municipalities in states that implemented femicide laws, 
regardless of whether they have or will implement unilateral divorce laws, equitable divorce 
asset compensation or decriminalize abortion.  
The findings, in Table A.8, show that the femicide laws have been ineffective in 
reducing the killing of women, even though most states have introduced longer average prison 
sentence disparities between femicides and homicides. Figure A.9 illustrates that there are no 
deviations from parallel trends for the four types of crimes analyzed: femicides, homicides of 
women, disappearances and suicides of women. Additionally, Figure A.9 shows that the 
dynamic effects are statistically insignificant across all 90 post-treatment periods for each 
outcome analyzed.

expect to see systematic differences in key pre-treatment trends. However, the results show no 
statistically significant differences in pre-treatment trends for femicides, female homicides, 
disappearances, or an aggregate measure of all female killings, including suicides of women.

during the 24 months prior to femicide law enactment across all these outcomes.

6.9 Synthetic matching difference-in-differences 
Thus far, I have shown that the results of the staggered DID are robust, all indicating null 
impacts.  As a final robustness check, I re-run the main results shown in Section 5 using this 
time the Synthetic Matching Difference-in-Differences (scDID) method, developed by 
Arkhangelsky et al. (2021).  
This innovative estimator overcomes many of the common pitfalls associated with 
standard DID approaches. Like other synthetic control methods, scDID constructs an optimally 
matched control unit, thereby reducing reliance on the strict assumption of parallel trends 
across aggregates. Crucially, unlike traditional matching methods, scDID does not require that 
the treated unit lie within the convex hull of the control units—a limitation that often hampers 
the validity of synthetic controls when the treated unit’s characteristics are not well-represented 
by any combination of control units. Additionally, the scDID framework accommodates 
multiple treated units, staggered treatment adoption, and the inclusion of covariates.  
To maintain comparability with previous analyses, I use the same control variables as 
the ones used in Section 5 and re-run all estimations for the effects of femicide laws—whether 
implemented in isolation, alongside unilateral divorce reforms, equitable divorce asset 
compensation, or in conjunction with the decriminalization of abortion. I analyze femicides, 
female homicides, disappearances, and suicides.


## Page 41


41 
 
The findings remain unchanged: there is no statistically significant effect of femicide 
laws on any of these outcomes (Table 7).  While my primary identification strategy relies on 
staggered DID, scDID serves as an additional check, ensuring that the findings are not driven 
by poor counterfactual construction. This consistency in results across methodologies 
reinforces the conclusion that femicide laws have not led to measurable reductions in killings 
or disappearances of women.

7. Conclusion 
This paper demonstrated that femicide laws in Mexico have failed to reduce femicides, 
homicides of women, disappearances, and suicides of women.  This holds true regardless of 
whether states imposed harsher prison sentences for femicides compared to homicides and 
whether they introduced additional reforms such as unilateral divorce, equitable divorce asset 
compensation, or abortion decriminalization. In essence, the femicide laws have not yet 
provided the intended protection for women in either the short term or even after 90 months 
post-enactment.
