Abstract
Sharpe Ratio (SR) is a critical parameter in characterizing financial time series
as it jointly considers the reward and the volatility of any stock/portfolio through
its variance. Deriving online algorithms for optimizing the SR is particularly chal-
lenging since even offline policies experience constant regret with respect to the
best expert Even-Dar et al (2006). Thus, instead of optimizing the usual defini-
tion of SR, we optimize regularized square SR (RSSR). We consider two settings
for the RSSR, Regret Minimization (RM) and Best Arm Identification (BAI). In
this regard, we propose a novel multi-armed bandit (MAB) algorithm for RM
called UCB-RSSR for RSSR maximization. We derive a path-dependent concentra-
tion bound for the estimate of the RSSR. Based on that, we derive the regret
guarantees of UCB-RSSR and show that it evolves as O (log n) for the two-armed
bandit case played for a horizon n. We also consider a fixed budget setting for
well-known BAI algorithms, i.e., sequential halving and successive rejects, and
propose SHVV, SHSR, and SuRSR algorithms. We also derive the upper bound for the
error probability of all proposed BAI algorithms. We demonstrate that UCB-RSSR
outperforms the only other known SR optimizing bandit algorithm, U-UCB Cas-
sel et al (2023). We also establish its efficacy with respect to other benchmarks
derived from the GRA-UCB and MVTS algorithms. We further demonstrate the per-
formance of proposed BAI algorithms for multiple different setups. Our research

1 Introduction
Sequential decision-making problems are formulated using the MAB framework Auer
et al (2002); Sutton and Barto (2018). In the classical MAB setting, an agent (or
player) selects one or multiple actions (or arms) from a set of arms {a1, a2, . . .}
∆= K in a
sequence of discrete time slots t ∈N and consequently, receives a return (or reward). In
several MAB frameworks, the policy’s objective is to perform risk-aware maximization
of rewards. As an example, in ultra-reliable wireless communications, in addition to
maximizing the data rate (reward), the network must ensure that the signal strength
is not highly intermittent (risk) Yang et al (2018); Boldrini et al (2018). Similarly,
in finance, an investment strategy depends on the guarantee that the portfolio value
does not drop below a threshold (risk) just as much as it does on the absolute returns
(reward) Fama (1971).
The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) developed by Sharpe (1964) and Lint-
ner Lintner (1965) concluded that the portfolios with the highest SR are efficient in
minimizing the risk of the portfolio return. SR has found applications in the develop-
ment of asset pricing approaches (e.g., see Bernardo and Ledoit (2000)). Furthermore,
Cvitani´c et al (2008) demonstrated the efficacy of using SR as a performance metric
in different models, such as frictionless markets. Due to its applicability, developing
online algorithms that optimize the SR is an attractive pursuit.

Deriving online algorithms that optimize the SR is challenging due to three key

challenging in the general case. Furthermore, note that concentration inequalities
for the mean (Chernoff’s) and variance (McDiarmid’s) do not imply the presence of

• The standard deviation appears in the denominator in the expression of the SR.
This implies that even for bounded random variables, the sample SR can blow up
in an unbounded manner, especially with fewer samples.

1.2 Related Work
On SR and variance estimation: To maximize the SR, Cvitani´c et al (2008) fol-
lowed a constrained optimization strategy to minimize the variance of the portfolio
given that the expected returns are fixed and annualized for different horizons. The
estimation of the SR for any policy necessitates the estimation of the variance and the
standard deviation of the rewards. Recent work that deals with the estimation of the
variance includes Xu et al (2022) and Skafte et al (2019). Xu et al (2022) developed
an unbiased variance estimator (using Hoeffding’s decomposition theorem) for predic-
tion models. It uses random forests to achieve a bias-variance trade-off. The method
is computationally valid when a subset of the data used is not larger than half of the
total sample size. Extending to bigger subsets led to a large variance, thus making
the results less reliable. The latter, Skafte et al (2019) developed techniques to esti-
mate the variance in regression neural networks. The problem arises with limited data
availability, and it becomes impractical to calculate both the mean and variance simul-
taneously. Interestingly, the estimation of the variance of the rewards can improve the
performance of classical bandit algorithms as well, e.g., see UCBV Audibert et al (2009)
and E-UCBV Mukherjee et al (2018). However, extending the estimation of the variance
or the standard deviation to the estimation of the SR still remains an open problem.
Cassel et al (2023) presented UCB type algorithms for a general family of metrics, of
which SR is a special case. Apart from Cassel et al (2023), to the best of our knowl-
edge, no known bandit algorithms aim to optimize the SR. Also, Xi et al (2021) used
the same set of metrics as Cassel et al (2023) and did regret analysis for multinomial
logit model(MNL) bandits.
On bandit algorithms in finance: Bandit algorithms have found applications in
financial decision-making and have been investigated extensively Huo and Fu (2017);
Shen et al (2015); Gornet et al (2022). Ozkaya and Wang (2020) demonstrated the
efficacy of contextual bandit algorithms in portfolio selection. With enough context
information about the environment, their algorithms outperform conventional portfo-
lio selection methodologies. However, their evaluation was primarily empirical without
any performance guarantees. Kagrecha et al (2019) devised distribution oblivious algo-
rithms with the aim of selecting the arm that optimizes a linear combination of the
expected reward and the associated Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) in a fixed bud-
get BAI framework. Huo and Fu (2017) balances risk and return in MAB by filtering
assets based on financial market structure and using the optimal MAB policy to reduce
conditional value-at-risk (CVaR). The drawback is that their prediction holds when
the market is steady. However, in times of volatility, it becomes essential to prioritize
risk awareness to achieve optimal performance. Profitable bandits Achab et al (2018)
focus on credit risk evaluation to maximize their profit as they adapt three widely
used approaches, all based on MAB: Bayes-upper confidence bound (UCB), Thomp-
son sampling (TS), and Kullback-Leibler upper confidence bound (KL-UCB). The
authors proved that all algorithms are asymptotically optimal only when the same
number of clients are presented to the loaner at each time step. Unlike these works,

On bandit algorithms for BAI: RM trades off exploration and exploitation to
achieve optimal long-term performance, whereas BAI prioritizes making the best deci-
sion in the short term. Consequently, exploration strategies in BAI are often designed
to swiftly narrow down the search space and converge on the arm with the highest
expected reward. The earliest work in the BAI was Successive Elimination algo-
rithm for fixed confidence setting Even-Dar et al (2002) and the lower bounds for the
same were provided by Mannor and Tsitsiklis (2004). Bubeck et al (2009) proved that
regret-minimizing algorithms (like UCB) are not well-suited for pure exploration tasks.
Audibert et al (2010) introduced UCB-E and Successive Rejects for best mean in a
fixed budget setting and provided an upper bound for their error probability. UCB-E
faced the limitation of estimation of hardness parameter for the algorithm but was not
faced by Successive Rejects and is thus one of the important algorithms to date.
Also, Bubeck et al (2011) calculates simple regret, that is, the difference between the
average payoff of the best arm and the average payoff obtained by the recommen-
dation. They provide a lower bound on the simple regret for arms with underlying
distributions as Bernoulli. Karnin et al (2013) considered fixed confidence and fixed
budget setting for Sequential Halving and proved the gap of upper bounds from
the lower bound is only doubly logarithmic in the problem parameters. Carpentier
and Locatelli (2016) proved for a fixed budget setting, if at least one bandit prob-

2 Problem Formulation
Consider a MAB setting K = (a1, a2, . . . aK) with K arms (|K| = K ≥2). At each
time step t ∈N, exactly one arm is played, and the player receives a reward Xi(t)
from playing ai. These rewards are unknown to the player and are assumed to have
an underlying distribution with bounded support [l, u], i.e., Xi(t) ∈[l, u], ∀t, ∀i.
The reward samples across different arms and across different time slots for the same
arm are independent. The sequence of action selection by the player is called the
policy π. Let us denote the arm selected by π at a time t by π(t), i.e., π(t) ∈K. We
analyze the policy of the player in a finite time-horizon n, i.e., t ∈{1, 2, . . . , n}. In this
paper, we introduce 6 policies. The first 3 policies are UCB-styled, focusing on Regret
Minimization, and the others are based on best-arm identification. The first policy, i.e.,
π = UCB-VV, follows the classical MAB setting, with an objective to minimize regret.
Following the variance maximization, we then formulate the main objective algorithm
of this paper, i.e., SR maximization π = UCB-SR-like, UCB-RSSR. The next 3 policies
are explained in Section 6, and they differ from the first 3 policies in terms of their
performance evaluation. The subsection 2.1 defines the performance metric for the
different styled algorithms. As we have policy π = UCB-VV for variance maximization
in RM, we have π = SHVV for identifying the arm with maximum variance in BAI.
The other three BAI-styled algorithms focus on finding the best SR arm (i.e., the arm
with maximum SR).

8 Conclusion and Future work
We introduce UCB-RSSR, a RM MAB algorithm designed to maximize the SR, and it
exhibits a path-dependent regret bound that scales as O(log n) for n plays. It incorpo-
rates the idea of the pilot fraction, which necessitates the pulling of arms in the pilot
phase for better estimation of mean and variances, thereby decreasing the regret. We
proved its efficacy with respect to U-UCB, which is notably the sole algorithm in the lit-
erature focused on maximizing the SR. We also show that our algorithm outperforms
when compared with the modified version of GRA-UCB and MVTS. With theoretical
framework development, empirical assessment, and consistent out-performance across
a range of distribution types, it reinforces its practical relevance and adaptability.
Further, we proceed to identify the arm with the best SR by introducing BAI algo-
rithms in a fixed budget setting, which are SHVV, SHSR, and SuRSR. We also provide the
theoretical guarantees for proposed algorithms in terms of an upper bound on error
probability. We observe their performance for a fixed budget for multiple setups, each
presenting a unique scenario.
For future work, we aim to focus on developing the concentration inequality and
bandit algorithm for non-i.i.d returns, as financial stock data doesn’t exhibit i.i.d
returns. Also, given that stocks and indices can be numerous, employing a com-
putationally efficient k-best arm identification strategy for K arms can enhance
decision-making in complex environments and optimize outcomes.
