Abstract
Large language model (LLM)-based AI dele-
gates are increasingly utilized to act on behalf
of users, assisting them with a wide range of
tasks through conversational interfaces. De-
spite their advantages, concerns arise regard-
ing the potential risk of privacy leaks, particu-
larly in scenarios involving social interactions.
While existing research has focused on protect-
ing privacy by limiting the access of AI dele-
gates to sensitive user information, many social
scenarios require disclosing private details to
achieve desired social goals, necessitating a bal-
ance between privacy protection and disclosure.
To address this challenge, we first conduct a
pilot study to investigate user perceptions of
AI delegates across various social relations and
task scenarios, and then propose a novel AI
delegate system that enables privacy-conscious
self-disclosure. Our user study demonstrates
that the proposed AI delegate strategically pro-
tects privacy, pioneering its use in diverse and
dynamic social interactions.

Introduction
AI has long been used to plan and execute sim-
ple tasks on behalf of users (Gabriel et al., 2024;
Ghalebikesabi et al., 2024). The advent of large
language models (LLMs) (Ouyang et al., 2022;
Achiam et al., 2023; Taecharungroj, 2023; Dubey
et al., 2024) further enhanced the performance and
capability of AI and opened the possibility of devel-
oping powerful AI agents to manage more complex
tasks (Qiao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b; Hong
et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a).
Recent studies in this area have been moving to-
ward developing AI delegates capable of handling
highly autonomous scenarios with limited or zero
human presence (Mariani et al., 2023; Hoffman,
2024), such as attending meetings on behalf of

users when scheduling conflicts occur, providing
technical support and consultation, reading and re-
plying emails (Li et al., 2024b).
AI delegates that perform autonomously with-
out direct user supervision raise serious concerns
about privacy leakage (Mireshghallah et al., 2023;
Bagdasaryan et al., 2024). Before the coming of
LLMs, commercial AI assistants, including Google
Assistant (Google, 2024) and Siri (Apple, 2024),
often leveraged user information and preferences to
improve the effectiveness of interacting with soft-
ware applications and websites. As trustable soft-
ware applications or websites usually adheres to
strictly privacy protocol that only requires user pri-
vate information related to certain scenarios, such
as requiring the passport number in flight ticket
booking website, the risk of privacy leakage is at
least controllable. In contrast, LLM-empowered
AI delegates often have to interact with humans
or agents through free-form conversation, which
significantly increases the chance of exposing to
privacy risks, including malicious software, phish-
ing websites, unintentional privacy inquiry, and
adversarial privacy hacking, such as jailbreaking
and prompt injection (Brown et al., 2022; Wang
et al., 2023b; Zou et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2024).
Recent works on privacy protection treat pri-
vacy leakage as an alignment problem and focus
on fine-tuning data with methods such as differ-
ential privacy (Dwork et al., 2014; Behnia et al.,
2022; Dwork, 2006) to protect privacy (Brown
et al., 2022; Carlini et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022;
Wang et al., 2023b). Such approaches may still be
vulnerable to evolving privacy hacking that is not
included in the fine-tuning data (Yao et al., 2024).
Other works leverage the theory of contextual in-
tegrity (CI) (Nissenbaum, 2004) that treats privacy
as the appropriate flow of information in accor-
dance with the norms of contextual information
(Bagdasaryan et al., 2024; Ghalebikesabi et al.,
2024) to protect privacy in context hijacking at-

tacks. Those approaches generally place AI dele-
gates in a passive position to minimize the chance
of privacy leakage from the conversation partner.
However, such passive behavior is not always ideal
in practical social conversations.
Self-disclosure (Omarzu, 2000; Greene et al.,
2006) is a common social behavior in which “one
intends to deliberately divulge something personal
to others” (Derlega et al., 1993). It is common that
we tell our family, friends, or even strangers about
our emotions, experiences, and opinions to estab-
lish and deepen social relationships with the con-
versation partner. The proactive sharing behavior
of self-disclosure poses another layer of challenge
not yet studied by existing works on privacy pro-
tection. To emulate this social behavior, rather than
minimizing all privacy leakage, AI delegates need
to learn how to manage their disclosure behaviors
and choose the appropriate strategy depending on
the conversation context to achieve social goals
without unnecessary privacy leakage.
To address this challenge, we conduct a detailed
pilot study on the perception of users concerning
adapting AI delegates in their social interactions,
and propose a new AI delegate to enhance protec-
tion against unintended privacy leakage while sup-
porting self-disclosure for achieving social goals
in social conversations (Omarzu, 2000). This AI
delegate is mindful of the conversational context,
the nature of the relationship, and the comfort level
of both parties to choose appropriate disclosure
strategies and ensure that conversations remain re-
spectful and mutually enriching. It is based on
a multi-agent framework (Wu et al., 2023) where
multiple agents are deployed for different tasks in-
cluding evaluating conversation goals, adjusting
conversation strategies based on personal differ-
ences, social norms, and contextual information,
balancing the utility and risk of self-disclosure.
The contribution can be summarized as follows:
• We conducted an in-depth pilot user study to in-
vestigate the perception of users in adopting AI
delegates in social interactions, providing a moti-
vation and necessity to design AI delegates and
their preferences on social interaction context.
• We design an AI delegate with a dual focus that
ensures privacy in both passive and proactive
self-disclosure scenarios.
• We evaluate our AI delegate with various sce-
narios and social relations with both LLM-based
evaluator and human judge, and the experiment
results show that our AI delegate protects privacy
and demonstrates strategic self-disclosure behav-
iors with the alignment between LLM and human
evaluators.
2
Related Works
2.1
LLM-Based AI Delegate
Before the emerging of LLMs, Intelligent Per-
sonal Assistants (IPAs) (Luger and Sellen, 2016;
Baughan et al., 2023) such as Apple’s Siri (Ap-
ple, 2024), Amazon’s Alexa (Amazom, 2024),
and Google Assistant (Google, 2024) have been
widely used by users for managing daily tasks
through voice and text-based interaction. LLM-
based agents are now in a position to transform
the landscapes of IPAs with their natural language
processing and contextual understanding capabil-
ities (Wang et al., 2023a). More than simply au-
tomating routine tasks and executing predefined
commands, such agents can actively engage with
users’ personal data, integrate into users’ daily ac-
tivities, offer intelligent and strategic insight, and
even manage interpersonal communications on be-
half of users (Yang et al., 2023a; Abbasian et al.,
2023; OpenAI, 2023; Microsfot, 2023). Ongoing
research envisions the further evolution of these
agents into what we term AI delegates, where the
AI delegate can fully represent the user in complet-
ing complex affairs and interacting with other users
or agents while ensuring safety and reliability (Li
et al., 2024b).
2.2
Privacy Protection in AI Delegates
As LLMs become increasingly integrated into var-
ious aspects of our digital lives, privacy protec-
tion has become a top challenge (Plant et al.,
2022; Mireshghallah et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2024;
Mireshghallah et al., 2024). While techniques such
as differential privacy (DP) (Dwork et al., 2014;
Behnia et al., 2022; Dwork, 2006) can be utilized
to protect user data, they also introduce noises and
reduce the performance of LLMs (Kairouz et al.,
2014; Cormode et al., 2018). Fine-tuning LLMs
with appropriate datasets (Wang et al., 2023b; Li
et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023)
can provide a certain degree of protection, but are
still not fully immune to jailbreaking and privacy
hijacking (Mo et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023). Other
works relied on practical rules and strategies such
as contextual integrity (CI) (Nissenbaum, 2004)
to minimize the privacy information exposed to
LLMs (Rebedea et al., 2023; Bagdasaryan et al.,

2024; Chen et al., 2024a) and rephrase the response
to avoid unintended disclosure of private data (Dou
et al., 2023). Those approaches place AI delegates
in a passive and defensive position to avoid privacy
leakage, particularly in adversarial settings. How-
ever, in social conversations, people often need to
strategically self-disclose privacy to achieve social
goals (Omarzu, 2000), which are usually ignored
by previous studies in privacy protection.
2.3
Self-Disclosure in Social Conversation
Self-disclosure is a pivotal aspect of social con-
versation where one intentionally shares privacy
with the conversation partner to establish social
relations and foster intimacy between individu-
als (Omarzu, 2000; Greene et al., 2006; Such et al.,
2012). By sharing personal thoughts, feelings, and
experiences, people create a sense of connection
and understanding. This process encourages reci-
procity (Jourard, 1971; Dindia, 1999; Allen et al.,
2001), encouraging others to open up in return,
deepening relationships and achieving social or
personal goals (Omarzu, 2000). The strategy of
self-disclosure can be challenging to manage, even
for human beings, as oversharing or sharing at an
inappropriate moment can lead to discomfort or be
considered as breaches of privacy (Chelune, 1979;
Greene et al., 2003). The aspect of self-disclosure
has not been widely discussed in researches on
LLM-powered agents and AI delegates.
3
User Perception of Using AI Delegates
We conducted a pilot study to investigate users’
perception and willingness of delegating tasks to
AI in various scenarios, covering 12 daily rou-
tine tasks and 20 social relations (clustered as
intimate/personal, social/relational, and transac-
tional/prefessional (Turner, 1988; Cheng et al.,
2025)) representing various levels of intimacy. A
total of 70 participants (38 male, 32 female), aged
between 20 and 60 years old (mean = 38.15, SD
= 11.84), took part in the survey. The details of
this pilot study are in Appendix A. Our survey
highlighted distinct, context-based preferences for
AI delegates. In the intimate and personal scenar-
ios, participants expressed the lowest acceptance
of AI delegation, reflecting concerns over privacy
and trust, which are particularly sensitive in these
contexts. In contrast, scenarios involving social
and professional interactions showed higher accep-
tance, in which users are more open to delegate
tasks to AI and willing to prioritize efficiency over
personal involvement. Motivated by those findings,
we have developed a context-aware AI delegate
framework to ensure that the AI delegate could
evaluate the current context to balance privacy pro-
tection and strategical self-disclosure.
4
Dual Focus Design of AI Delegate
Our AI delegate design addresses privacy protec-
tion in both passive and proactive scenarios. In pas-
sive scenarios (e.g., medical consultations), users
minimize personal disclosure, which is the well-
discussed scenario in existing privacy protection
work. On the other hand, the proactive scenarios
(e.g., networking for recommendation letters) en-
courage selective self-disclosure to build relation-
ships. The AI delegate dynamically assesses con-
versational context and social relationships to im-
plement appropriate privacy strategies while achiev-
ing social objectives. Implementation details, in-
cluding context generation and LLM-based evalua-
tion prompts, are provided in Appendix B and C,
respectively.
4.1
Conversation context generation
Following the recent studies of leveraging LLMs
to simulate users’ inputs and feedbacks during the
human-computer interaction (HCI) (Xiang et al.,
2024; Suh et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a), we utilize
LLMs to simulate conversations in a wide range
of scenarios. As shown in Figure 1, LLM-based
modules are used to generate user and conversa-
tion partner personas, social goals, conversation
scenarios, and social relationships to form the con-
versation context.
Persona Generation. In order to cover a wide
range of users, we consider 51 information types
related to persona (Ghalebikesabi et al., 2024) and
prompt GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024) to fill in these per-
sona keys. After manually reviewing the generated
personas, we found that one-time persona gener-
ation suffers from limited diversity, especially in
name, phone number, address, and other basic in-
formation. To solve this, we design a two-stage
user persona generation which first generates basic
information such as name, gender, etc. and then
generates more sensitive information such as fam-
ily_diseases, pregnancy, etc. This two-stage pro-
cess ensures diversity in generating the personas of
both users and conversation partners.
Social Relationship and Scenario Generation.

of the social relationship generator. The strategy
of decoupling the generation of scenarios and so-

generation results. If we attempt to generate both
scenario and social relationships at the same time,
the output of LLMs could suffer strong bias due to
stereotype. For example, LLMs tend to associate
the social relationship “strange” with a social net-
working scenario, which could exclude common
conversational scenarios such as a hospital visit
from the generated samples.
Social Goal Generation. In social conversations,
conversation parties usually possess social goals
such as relationship development, social approval,
or identity clarification (Brooks, 1992; Omarzu,
2000). Given the generated conversation personas
and context, we leverage LLMs to generate social
goals and assign the goal to either the AI delegate
or the conversation partner. The party with the
social goal tends to act more proactively and drive
the conversation. For example, if the relationship
improvement goal is assigned to the AI delegate,
the corresponding persona will be more inclined
to self-disclose private information to create the
opportunity of obtaining reciprocal disclosure from
the conversation partner to improve the level of
intimacy.
4.2
AI delegate design
To improve the capability of LLMs in reasoning
and planning, a multi-agent framework that em-
ploys multiple LLM-based agents working in col-
laboration is often chosen for solving complex rea-
soning tasks (Wu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024;
Besta et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024b). In a multi-
agent system, each LLM-based agent is responsible
for a certain task. For example, organizing a virtual
conference may require the collaboration between
an event planner agent, a content creator agent,
and a logistics agent. Similarly, our AI delegate is
built upon a multi-agent system with the following
components: the situation accessor, the privacy
retriever, the strategy maker, and the responder.
Figure 2 illustrates how those agents may work
together to engage in social conversation with the
partner. Below, we discuss the details of each LLM-
based agent in our AI delegate for the details of
associated prompts. All prompts utilized for each
agent are detailed in Section D of the Appendix.
Situation Accessor. This agent is responsible for
analyzing the context of the current conversation.
It examines the conversation history with a focus
on recent messages from the conversation partner
and background factors, including the scenario and
the social relationship between the user and the
conversation partner. Then it determines whether
there is any significant social goal, including the
self-disclosure goal such as social control, relation-
ship improvement, identity clarification, support,

the basis for determining the scope (breadth), level
of detail (depth), and temporal aspects (timing)
of the disclosure. Based on this comprehensive
analysis, the strategy maker generates a structured
disclosure strategy report. This report outlines: an
explicit disclosure strategy, specific implementa-
tion guidelines, and a detailed action plan with
executable steps. The finalized strategy report is
subsequently transmitted to both the privacy re-
triever and the responder for implementation.
Privacy Retriever. The privacy retriever acts as
a safeguard to isolate the user privacy from the
AI delegate to prevent jailbreaking and hijacking
such as prompt injection (Liu et al., 2023). It ex-
amines the strategy report and double checks with
the background factors, common norms and user-
defined rules before retrieving appropriate private
information and passing the retrieved data to the
responder.
Responder. Once the responder received both the
retrieved private information and the strategy re-
port, it generates a response that contains the pri-
vacy information and at the same time, while also
aligning with the strategy report in terms of re-
sponding style, context, and disclosure timing. For
example, if the strategy suggests a shallow disclo-
sure, the responder will act accordingly to limit the
depth of disclosure (Dou et al., 2023).

5.2
Evaluation procedure
The performance of our AI delegates is evaluated
by LLMs considering LLMs strong performance
and good alignment to the human evaluator (Yang
et al., 2023b; Chern et al., 2024; Thomas et al.,

common practice in evaluating social scenarios
with LLMs (see Appendix C.1). Once the context
of the conversation and two parties of the social
conversation are generated, a conversation party
is randomly selected to initiate the conversation.
The decision of terminating conversation is left to
both parties. Upon the completion of the conver-
sation, the conversation history is collected as a
single sample, which is then submitted to the LLM-
based evaluator for assessment. The LLM-based
evaluator also has access to the background fac-
tors including scenarios and social relationships,
and the social goals. This procedure ensures an
automated and fair evaluation process. To ensure

Privacy protection in passive scenario
Other privacy protection researches (Dou et al.,
2023; Bagdasaryan et al., 2024; Ghalebikesabi
et al., 2024) had focused on passive social conversa-

to examine whether the AI delegate could avoid
unnecessary privacy leakage in the passive conver-

to the AI delegate. Following the practice of recent
works, we classify the generated passive social con-
versations into two groups: regular and adversarial.
In the regular group, the request for information
is made normally without any hijacking attacks.
In the adversarial group, the conversation partner
would carry out adversarial attacks and attempt
to convince the AI delegate that the disclosure of
privacy information is both necessary and urgent.

5.4
Self-disclosure in proactive scenario
In proactive social conversations, AI delegate self-
discloses the privacy information to achieve so-
cial goals. Hence, the focus of evaluation in the
proactive social conversation is whether the privacy
self-disclosure strategy is appropriate. We will not
directly evaluate whether the privacy is protected
as answer to such question depends on whether
the revealed privacy information aligns with the
social goal, and thus covered by the above ques-
tion. Specifically, we will ask LLM evaluator the
following three questions to evaluate AI delegate
in the proactive conversation:
• Q1. Social goal achievement: In the conversation
history between the AI delegate and human, how
do you think the AI delegate achieved their social
goal?
• Q2. Social goal persistence: In the conversation
history between the AI delegate and human, how
do you think the AI delegate should insist on
achieving their social goal?
• Q3. Context alignment: In the conversation his-
tory between the AI delegate and human, how do
you think the AI delegate’s response is appropri-
ate and align with your expectation?
We will not treat the answers to Q1 (social goal
achievement) and Q2 (social goal persistence) di-
rectly as the evaluation metrics as the effectiveness
of conversation also depends on the conversation
partner. For example, in a proactive conversation
where AI delegate possesses a social goal to estab-
lish cooperation with the conversation partner, if
the conversation partner is not interested, it is not
appropriate for the AI delegate to insist achieving
the social goal. Motivated by this consideration,
we will focus on the alignment of the distribution
of Q1 and the distribution of Q2 (social goal persis-
tence) where the former represents the disclosure
strategy of the AI delegate, and the latter represents
the disclosure strategy of the evaluator. The KL-
divergence between the Q1 and Q2 distributions,
named disclosure strategy alignment, will be used
to measure this alignment between distributions.
Table 2 shows that our AI delegate adopts better
aligned self-disclosure strategies (represented by
the lower the KL divergence value) and achieves
similar results in context alignment metric com-
pared to the baseline.
6
User Study
We conducted another round of user study to eval-
uate our AI delegate in both passive and proactive
scenarios and leverage humans as the judge to ex-
plore whether the LLM-based evaluation in a wide
range (Section 5) is aligned with human evaluation.
6.1
User study settings
We sampled five cases to assess whether our AI
delegate meets user expectations; detailed informa-
tion can be found in Appendix I. Three out of the
five cases were passive, which require AI delegate
to protect privacy when unnecessary information
was requested during the conversation. The other
two cases were proactive, which require AI dele-
gate to share appropriate information to establish
or deepen social connections with the conversation
partner. In one of the proactive cases, the con-
versation partner had a negative attitude toward
the desired goal of the conversation. In the other
case, the conversation partner had a positive atti-
tude about reaching the goal. The baseline and the
evaluation metrics stays the same as the ones in the
LLM-based evaluation experiments.
6.2
Participants
A total of 50 participants (15 male, 35 female),
aged between 18 and 40 years old (mean = 36.37,
SD = 6.27), took part in the study. For each case,
participants were first provided with background
information, including the names and relationship
of the two parties, the setting, and the goal of the
conversation. They were then shown the full con-
versation and asked to complete the questions.

Results
The results from both the passive and proactive
cases are presented in Figure 3. For the passive
cases, we compared user-rated scores on privacy
protection and context alignment. For the proactive
cases, we evaluated the alignment of the AI dele-
gate’s disclosure strategy with user preferences, as

Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a dual-focus AI delegate
that both safeguards user privacy and strategically
self-discloses in social conversations. To validate
its necessity, we present the first pilot study on real-
world user perceptions of AI delegates and adopt
a multi-agent framework to handle complex dia-
logue contexts. In both LLM-based evaluations and
targeted user case studies, our dual-focus AI dele-
gate consistently outperforms baselines in privacy

Limitations
The limitations of the study include its reliance
on third-person evaluators to assess AI delegates
based on conversation history and contexts. This
approach may introduce bias, as the evaluators are
not the actual in-person conversation partners, lead-
ing to a disconnect between the evaluation perspec-
tive and the real dynamics of conversational interac-
tions. Additionally, the current solution is confined
to single-agent and single-partner conversation sce-
narios, which excludes more complex multi-agent
interactions. In such scenarios, the AI delegate
would need to navigate conversations involving
multiple participants, requiring more sophisticated
strategies for information disclosure and adaptation
to dynamic contexts.

User Preference Survey
We conducted a survey to examine users’ willingness to delegate tasks to AI across a range of scenarios,
with a particular focus on two dimensions: task type and the nature of interpersonal relationships. To
structure the relationship dimension, we adopted the FAVEE-HPP model proposed in (Cheng et al., 2023),
which classifies human relationships into six canonical categories: Familial (e.g., parent–child, siblings),
Romantic (e.g., spouses), Affiliative (e.g., friends), Transactional (e.g., customer–service provider), Power
(e.g., teacher–student), and Hostile (e.g., competitors, adversaries). Based on these categories, we selected
20 representative relationship types and identified 12 routine tasks that typically occur within these
relational contexts, resulting in a total of 32 distinct interaction scenarios for evaluation.
A total of 70 participants (38 male, 32 female), aged between 20 and 60 years (M = 38.15, SD =
11.84), participated in the survey. Each participant was asked to rate their level of acceptance regarding
AI delegates acting on their behalf in 32 distinct scenarios, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1
(unacceptable) to 5 (acceptable). To better analyze user preferences, we applied K-means clustering to
the acceptance scores, grouping the 32 scenarios into three distinct clusters. These clusters are labeled
as Intimate/Personal, Social/Relational, and Transactional/Professional. The tasks and relationships
assigned to each of the three cluster are listed in Sec. A.3. The score distributions for each cluster are
presented in Fig. 4. The characteristics of each cluster are summarized as follows: This study is approved
by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University.
• Intimate/Personal: This group consists of scenarios involving emotionally significant relationships,
such as family members and close friends, as well as tasks that require personal involvement, such
as making personal calls or handling private matters. As shown in the figure, this group has the
lowest acceptance of AI delegation, with a median acceptance score of around 2. This suggests that
participants are generally uncomfortable of allowing AI to manage tasks in situations involving close
relationships and sensitive personal matters.
• Social/Relational: This group includes scenarios that are socially oriented, involving people with
shared interests, such as normal friends and club members; or those in regular physical proximity,
such as colleagues and classmates. In this group, participants showed a more moderate acceptance
of AI delegation, with a broader distribution and a median score around 3.5. This suggests that
while participants are open to using AI in these social contexts, they also seek to balance personal
engagement with the convenience of delegation.
• Transactional/Professional: This group involves primarily task-focused scenarios, including formal,
transactional, or professional relationships where efficiency and task completion are prioritized
over personal involvement (e.g., attending work meetings, project management, interactions with
strangers). Participants displayed the highest willingness to delegate tasks to AI in this group, with

Our survey highlighted distinct, context-based preferences for AI delegates. In the intimate and personal
scenarios, participants expressed the lowest acceptance of AI delegation, reflecting concerns over privacy
