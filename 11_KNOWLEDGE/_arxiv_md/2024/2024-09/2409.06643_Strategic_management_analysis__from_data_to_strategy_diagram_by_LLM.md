ABSTRACT 
Strategy management analyses are created by business consultants 
with common analysis frameworks (i.e. comparative analyses) and 
associated diagrams. We show these can be largely constructed 
using LLMs, starting with the extraction of insights from data, 
organization of those insights according to a strategy management 
framework, and then depiction in the typical strategy management 
diagram for that framework (static textual visualizations). We 
discuss caveats and future directions to generalize for broader uses.  
Index terms: LLM, strategy management diagrams. 
1 INTRODUCTION 
 
The authors have worked on three projects involving rule-based, 
data-driven analytical insights for an insurance company, a mutual 
fund company, and a financial data company. In these projects, an 
insight synthesizes multiple data points into a high-level 
observation, such as trends over time, comparisons to peers or 
benchmarks, analytic models, or a document summary point. For 
example, an "earnings surprise" is the difference between a 
company's actual earnings and expected earnings estimates from 
analysts. No difference is not an insight, but a significant difference 
is (e.g., compared to prior surprises for that company or peer 
companies). 
    This conceptualization aligns with Ding et al’s QuickInsights: an 
approach to automatically find interesting patterns from multi-
dimensional datasets [1]; and is similar to John Schwabish’s  
takeaway message: “telling the reader what conclusions can be 
drawn from the data” [2, p.36].  
    In large scale information environments, such as the above 
projects, there are many challenges with insights, such as 1) there  
are potentially hundreds of insights, emerging from many datasets 
 
 
and many dashboards; 2) organization of these insights, into groups 
or ranking, relevant to the specific user objective; 3) presentation 
of these insights.  
 
Strategy management is part of management science in business 
schools and practiced by management consulting organizations.  
    “By providing a framework that allows us to examine the factors 
that influence a strategic situation and organize relevant 
information, strategy analysis places us in a superior position to a 
manager who relies exclusively on experience and intuition.”  
– Robert Grant, Contemporary Strategy Analysis, 2018 [3]. 
    The frameworks used in strategy management are various forms 
of comparative analyses of these derived factors. There are dozens 
of frameworks, for different strategic analyses. A simple, popular 
framework is the SWOT matrix—a technique used to identify 
strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. It is used to 
identify the internal and external factors that affect the achievement 
of project objectives (e.g. Figure 2).

    Furthermore, most strategy management frameworks have 
corresponding strategy management diagrams (e.g. Fig. 3). 
These visualizations group insight statements into a diagram. Each 
framework has a different visualization, which can be found in 
strategy management textbooks (e.g. [3]), or more briefly itemized 
and described in some publications (e.g. [4-5] in Fig. 2). 
    The combination of the analytical strategy management 
frameworks together with corresponding diagrams is useful for 
analysis and also collaboration: “Management models are not only 
tools for professionals and managers but essentially they are a way 
of communicating. Management models bridge differences in 
abstraction and provide comprehensiveness.” – Dr. Theo Camps, 
Chairman, Berenschot Group [4].  
    Note that there is not a definitive list of strategy management 
frameworks, nor their diagrams. Terminology may vary between 
publications. Variations in diagrams may exist.  
    Our contribution is what we have learned in creating strategy 
management diagrams directly from data, using LLMs in each of 
three steps: 1) extract insights from various data; 2) organize 
insights via strategy management frameworks; and 3) draw the 
resulting strategy management diagram.  
2 MOTIVATION 
We have many interests in strategy management diagrams: 
    1) We have experience using business strategy diagrams to 
justify visualization projects. We now seek tools to enhance the 
analysis, synthesis, and generation of these diagrams.  
    2) Statements used in strategy analyses are derived from data and 
are presented textually. Organizing and presenting many textual 
facts could significantly benefit future text, LLM, and qualitative 
visualizations.  
    3) Strategy management diagrams are rarely discussed in the 
visualization community or STEM education. However, they are 
prevalent in management science, MBA programs, and large 
organizations. Re-using already well-understood visualizations 
increase adoption of visualization for new kinds of analyses.  
    4) Some users describe “dashboard hell,” where an excess of 
charts overwhelms them, making it hard to find what they need. By 
considering insights as takeaway messages from charts, which can 
be organized into strategy management diagrams, it may be feasible 
to create more effective dashboard workflows.  
3 BACKGROUND 
LLMs and AI have been used in the visualization community to 
generate visualizations; captions, annotations and stories; or both.   
For example, Yilin et al use generative AI, given data to suggest 
relevant visualizations [6]. Dibia’s LIDA tool generates 
visualizations and infographics [7]. Yuan et al created ChartGPT to 
generate multiple visualizations in response to natural language 
questions [8]. Pingchuan et al generate insights that complement 
visualizations [9].   
Regarding insights, titles, captions and annotations, Schwabish 
and others have noted the importance of good titles and annotations, 
e.g. [1]. Data facts are similar to insights [10], but include simple 
descriptive statistics as opposed to “takeaway messages”.  
DataShot extracts data facts and generates Fact Sheets (i.e. a 
collection of infographics, each communicating a fact [11]); and 
Calliope has a similar approach, generating data stories [12]. Both 
use similar steps of fact extraction, composition, and visual 
synthesis. Fact sheets and data stories are dissimilar from strategy 
management frameworks and diagrams, in that composition of 
facts is based on data attributes (as opposed to a strategy 
framework, semantically organizing insights into specific analytic 
themes) and the visuals are infographics or simple annotated charts 
(as opposed to strategy diagrams organizing statements by the 
analytic theme and typically simple graphics such as grids or 
connecting arrows).  
There has been much research in generating insights to use with 
captions or annotations in visualizations. Pre-LLM captions go 
back to Mittal et al in 1998 [13], to much more recent systems, e.g. 
[14-16]; and recent research to generate captions via LLMs, e.g. 
[17]. ChartStory automatically sequences charts, extracts insights 
assembled into stories, but requires a singular dataset and the 
insights are tied to each chart, not a comparison of disparate 
insights [18]. Furthermore, using significant amounts of text within 
quantitative visualizations has also been investigated, e.g. Stokes et 
al [19-20], generally to positive effect.  
As such, none of the existing approaches leverage strategy 
management approaches which may aid in the collect, organize and 
presentation insights. 
4 METHOD AND EXAMPLES 
We investigate the generation of strategy diagrams in three steps as 
we believe there are different kinds of potential LLM failures at 
each step. As outlined in Figure 1, ① we start with LLM generation 
of insights, from either provided tabular data or LLM knowledge 
(Section 4.1). ② Then we organize insights with strategy 
management analyses (4.2). ③ These organized insights are then 
represented in the traditional management diagrams associated 
with each of these analyses (4.3). 
    For ② and ③, we use four strategy management frameworks: 
SWOT, Porter’s Five Forces, virtuous circle and Value Discipline 
– chosen because of the assumed increasing complexity of each:  
    SWOT is highly common, as it is used for strategy management 
as well as project management. It is typically visualized as a 2x2 
grid with text bullets in each quadrant: graphically it is very simple.  
    Porter’s Five Forces groups factors (insights) into five 
categories. Visually, it is typically arranged with boxes of text 
insights; one box central, with a box on each side of the central box, 
connected with arrows (Figure 2 left). 
    Virtuous circle is less common, but the authors have attended 
multiple meetings with CEO’s pronouncing their virtuous circle 
business strategy. The typical depiction is a set of text boxes 
arranged in a circle connected with arrows.   
    The Value Discipline of Treacy and Wiersema is less common 
and requires reducing all insights into three predefined disciplines: 
operational excellence, product leadership and customer intimacy. 
These categories are thematically narrower than “strength” or 
“opportunity”, thus possibly more difficult for the LLM. The 
traditional depiction is a radar plot, thus requiring the conversion 
of multiple textual insights into quantitative values, thus possibly 
difficult for an LLM (“LLMs don’t do math” [21]). 
We use multiple LLMs—ChatGPT 3.5, 4, Cohere, Gemeni, 
Claude and Microsoft Co-pilot in PowerPoint—as we have been 
investigating these for various non-research projects and have some

4.1 
LLM insight generation 
We started with insight generation via LLMs, using either 
preexisting A) LLM knowledge gained from training data; or B) 
direct input of quantitative data. For the initial task, we focused on 
financial data as we have many decades of expertise working with 
financial analysts, and have previously worked on insight systems 
in financial services with hundreds of insights.  
 
A. LLM insights from prior training data. LLMs can reuse the 
knowledge of their training data. We use the prompt “Based on 
your training data knowledge, describe the recent trend in the 
income statement from <company>.” We use income statements, 
as they are highly standardized accounting tables embedded in 
quarterly documents filed with federal regulators, easily 
downloadable and highly likely to have been used in the training of 
massive LLMs; and thus likely for LLMs to be responsive to. 
    The trend keyword restricts the LLM to use temporal data found 
within the income statement, thereby not requiring the LLM to 
compare and contrast against peer companies and benchmarks, 
which makes validation more difficult. The initial clause is used 
overcome the LLM refusal to answer based on lack of recent data.     
     An example answer from ChatGPT 3.5 is: 
As of my last update in July 2023, Walmart's income statement has 
reflected several key trends over recent periods:  
• Revenue Growth: Walmart has generally experienced steady 
revenue growth, driven by a combination of factors including 
its strong e-commerce presence, expansion in international 
markets, and robust performance in its grocery segment. 
• E-Commerce Expansion: E-commerce sales have been a 
significant growth area for Walmart, with increased 
investments in online infrastructure and partnerships (such as 
with Shopify). This segment has seen strong year-over-year 
growth, contributing significantly to overall revenue. 
… and five more insights.  
    The correctness of these insights can be validated by 
corresponding web searches as reports and analyses based 2023 
Walmart performance can be found online. Their relevance as 
insights can be compared against the insights in the prior rule-based 
system. Of the seven insights generated from the income statement, 
five directly correspond to rule-based insights and the remaining 
two are related to similar rules – for example e-Commerce above is 
similar to a revenue segmentation rule. This analysis can be run for 
other companies, including less-well-known American public 
companies with similar results.    
    The derivation of these insights, however, cannot be deduced 
from the LLM. Rather than being derived from tabular data, they 
could be synthesized from various reports from stock market 
analysts used in ChatGPT’s training data. Therefore, we consider 
insight generation from data provided in the prompt: 
 
B. LLM insights on provided timeseries data. We gave LLMs 
the prompt “Describe the trend in this timeseries data,” followed 
by the previous 30 days of tab-delimited timeseries data for one 
stock with date, stock price and volume. This can be validated to 
be outside the training data of the LLM assuming the LLM 
correctly responds to “When was your training data last updated?”  
    The output can be compared to prior rule-based systems. For 
example, ChatGPT 3.5 generated three top-level insights that 
matched rules in our prior insight-rule-system, and it also provided 
a breakdown of more micro-analysis under each top-level insight, 
not relevant in our prior system. Cohere generated two insights 
aligning with our prior system; and it also identified a weekly cycle 
pattern (The data also reveals a general pattern of lower closing 
prices towards the end of each week, with small recoveries at the 
start of the following week. This weekly cycle is most noticeable in 
the first half of April.). Cyclical trends were not in our prior system, 
but, are covered as a pattern of interest in financial timeseries 
analyses textbooks, e.g. [21], discoverable in seasonality charts, 
and commercialized by companies such as Seasonax. 
 
C. LLM insights on provided tabular data. A financial analyst 
will also find insights in other financial data such as quarterly 
accounting tables (e.g. the income statement), or transcripts from 
earnings calls, press releases, etc. This can be assessed from 
viewing equity analyst reports, hundreds to thousands of which are 
available in financial data systems. Using the income statement 
table from one stock, released the prior day (and outside of training 
data as identified by the LLM), ChatGPT 3.5 identified a half dozen 
insights, a few listed here: 
• Net Income: shows a steady growth from $20,081 million in 
June 2023 to $21,939 million by March 2024. This indicates 
effective tax management and profitability from continued 
operations, despite changes in additional income/expense 
items which sometimes negatively impacted earnings before 
tax (notably the -$854 million in March 2024). 
• Operating Income and Earnings Before Tax (EBT): 
demonstrate a general upward trend, with Operating Income 
growing from $24,254 million in June 2023 to $27,581 million 
in March 2024. Earnings Before Tax mirror this positive 
movement, which reflects solid overall operational control 
and profitability growth. 
• Cost of Revenue and Gross Profit: The Cost of Revenue varied 
from $16,795 million in June 2023, decreasing initially and 
then increasing to $18,505 million by March 2024. This 
indicates fluctuating production or operational costs which 
need to be monitored for better cost management. Gross Profit 
has consistently increased from $39,394 million to $43,353 
million over these quarters, which implies an improvement in 
cost efficiency or pricing strategies, outpacing the growth in 
the cost of revenue. 
LLM analyses of unseen income statements suggests LLMs may be 
capable of direct insight generation of standardized tabular data. 
But can LLM-derived insights be extracted from non-standardized 
datasets? We gave the LLMs a non-standard synthetic tiny tabular 
dataset (Figure 1 left) with 14 metrics for a fictious company 
(Foobar Corp) and two competitors. This dataset does not follow 
standardized tables so the LLM cannot use preexisting knowledge 
of those tables. The metrics are terse descriptions of data fields that 
might be found in financial data systems. The fields names 
correspond to the metric types that might be used in business—e.g. 
financials, retailing, inventory, customers, e.g. Number of countries 
doing business, Number product categories, Brand awareness 
survey, Media spend ($m), In-bound shipment delays, In-store 
revenue, Online revenue, Positive mainstream media sentiment, 
Negative mainstream media sentiment, Positive social media 
sentiment, Negative social media sentiment. 
    These metrics were created with varying complexity. For 
example: a) shipping delays can be simply compared between 
companies; b) revenue has two variations which can be combined 
for totals, analyzed individually, or analyzed as ratios; c) sentiment 
has four variations, which are most useful when compared: e.g. 
social positive/negative vs mainstream positive/ negative.  
    We then ask: “Given the data below, what insights can you 
derive about Foobar Corp?” ChatGPT 3.5 generated one data fact 
per metric, then synthesized these into six high-level insights: 
1. Strong Physical Presence: Foobar Corp’s extensive network 
of stores and high in-store revenue suggest a strong physical

improvement in online sales, which are currently very low. 
2. High Brand Awareness: With 79% brand awareness and the

higher in-store revenue than competitors and low online sales. 
4.2 
Organizing insights via management frameworks 
Given a number of insights, as in the examples above, there are 
many possible ways to organize these. It is feasible to present the 
most impactful insight(s) as a caption, title or annotations within a 
visualization, or as a narrative associated with a visualization—as  
discussed in many visualization research papers. But, insights may 
not align with charts; nor, insights may originate from tables (e.g. 
income statements) or text not typically visualized.  
As discussed in the introduction, strategy management 
frameworks, organize insights (factors) for comparative analysis, 
according to the criteria of the particular framework. A framework 
organizes insights, and, also reduces the insights to those insights 
which are the most relevant as supporting evidence for each theme 
within the framework. For example, if an insight does not align or 
is only weakly aligned with any of the themes of strength, 
weakness, opportunity and threat in a SWOT framework, it would 
not be included (or listed below the stronger insights.)  
The transformation of insights from simple “data fact 
observations” into insights of “interesting patterns” exists in the 
analytic pipelines of QuickInsights, DataShot, Calliope, [1,11,12] 
and our earlier rule-based work. In strategic management 
frameworks, the framework is the forcing function to reduce a large 
number of insights into a short list of most relevant insights. 
 
A. LLM use of strategy frameworks on training data. As before, 
we start with using the LLM to organize insights for a management 
framework directly from prior training data. This allows us to 
assess LLM familiarity and capabilities with strategy frameworks. 
LLMs have learned a broad variety of management frameworks—
we tried SWOT, BCG Matrix, Porter’s Five Forces, virtuous circle, 
Value Discipline of Treacy and Wiersema, and Kraljic’s 
Purchasing Model on various large companies such as Walmart, 
McDonalds, fashion retailers, tech companies and other entities.  
For example, a SWOT analysis for Walmart via ChatGPT3.5 
generates five insights per each category of SWOT, the first two of 
each category listed here: 
Strengths: 
1. 
Strong global presence with a vast network of stores. 
2. 
Wide range of products offered, including groceries, 
electronics, apparel, and household goods. 
Weaknesses: 
1. 
Dependence on physical stores, facing challenges from 
the shift to online shopping. 
2. 
Negative publicity regarding labor practices and 
employee relations. 
Opportunities: 
1. 
Growth in e-commerce presents opportunities for 
Walmart to expand its online presence. 
2. 
Expansion into emerging markets, particularly in Asia 
and Africa. 
Threats: 
1. 
Intense competition from online retailers like Amazon 
and brick-and-mortar competitors like Target and 
Costco. 
2. 
Economic downturns impacting consumer spending. 
As these insights from LLM training data are no longer connected 
to their training data, it is difficult to validate their responses 
intrinsically using the LLM. Therefore, we generated four 
management analyses (SWOT, Porter, BCG Matrix and Value 
Discipline) via ChatGPT3.5 for some of our large Fortune 500 
customers, and then asked their senior management (who often 
work with management consultants) to comment on these LLM 
generated analyses of their firms. We received answers: “Yes, that 
sums up common sentiment, but it misses recent work we’ve done 
in the last year,” and “This is light - but it is a great example of 
how we are on the verge of a huge shift in how human to software 
communication happens.” This is promising that the LLM has 
captured valid insights and further that these managers did not 
uncover any hallucinations.

Of note are some of the data handling skills or gaps. Regarding 
opportunities, the LLM uses its real-world of countries to assess

Furthermore, these analyses were asked in the same session as 
the prior initial insight extraction on Foobar and there are variations 
in the resulting insights. For example, the LLM missed the ratio of 
online sales during the SWOT analysis whereas it used the ratio in 
the insight analysis (Section 4.1). As such, there are likely many

The LLMs were expected to have difficulty with the Value 
Discipline framework. It requires synthesizing evidence into three 
pre-defined categories: customer relationships, product innovation, 
and operational efficiency; and also creating a numeric value for 
each. All the LLMs “knew” of the Value Discipline model. 
ChatGPT3.5 and Cohere could not determine, nor attempt to derive, 
the categories: “While the dataset provides information on 
revenues, growth rates, margins, marketing spend, and other 
operational metrics, it lacks specific data related to customer 
relationships, product innovation, or operational efficiency.” 
Gemini did synthesize insights into categories but neglected to 
provide numeric values, despite being explicitly asked. ChatGPT4 
and Claude synthesized insights into the appropriate categories but 
sometimes neglected to compare metrics relative to peers. The 
LLMs were inconsistent in which data attributes to use for which 
Value Discipline category. Sentiment was used by ChatGPT4 for 
customer intimacy, while Gemini for product leadership.   
We note that the latest LLMs seem to be able to do appropriate 
calculations sometimes. For example, in our analyses, we found 
Cohere properly performed a percent change calculation between 
the open and low price in Section 4, and ChatGPT4 generated 
appropriate results comparing sentiment between the 4 sentiment 
metrics: social positive/negative vs mainstream positive/negative, 
for example: Roy G Biv Product Leadership: Strong product 
leadership indicated by higher positive social sentiment (1,876,543 
positive vs. 134,567 negative). Although the mainstream media 
sentiment ratio is less favorable, the considerable positive social 
media sentiment suggests effective innovation. (note that social 
sentiment was 93% positive, which was not provided in the data; 
and mainstream media sentiment was 3456 positive vs 1234

4.3 
Presenting insights via management diagrams 
The prior two objectives were focused on generating textual 
content. The final objective was to generate strategy management 
diagrams with content. The results are shown on the right side of 
Figure 4. (Some columns use examples from Foobar, some 
columns use other popular companies).  
On the vertical axis are four analyses: SWOT, Porter’s Five 
Forces, Virtuous Circle and Value Discipline. The first column 
shows a human designer depicting content generated by the LLM 
in the prior step with the traditional diagram layout associated with 
that strategy framework. By “traditional diagram”, we mean the 
diagram associated with the analysis in [3], which also corresponds 
to the image most frequently occurring in Google image search. 
E.g. for SWOT, a 2 x 2 matrix; for Value Discipline, a radar plot; 
and so on. Note that other layouts are possible. Our prompt 
explicitly asked for the “traditional diagram, including explanatory 
text,” and for the Value Discipline we asked “traditional radar 
plot”. For some LLMs we needed to explicitly append “in HTML 
SVG” when the LLM instead defaulted to ASCII diagrams.  
Successive columns are diagrams generated by LLMs: 
ChatGPT4, Claude (default), Claude SVG, Gemini and Copilot in 
in Microsoft PowerPoint (which always defaulted to text bullets 
and stock photos.) The LLMs could mostly create passable grids 
with text (SWOT, top row), and had a variety of difficulties on the 
other diagrams. Further observations:

Layout: The LLM doesn’t necessarily create the traditional 
layout. For Porter’s five forces, only ChatGPT managed a good 
result, and Claude’s ASCII is close. The virtuous circle was 
problematic, with ChatGTP coming close except for arrows and 
spacing, and Claude’s Porter diagram a better circle than its circle 
diagram. For the Value Discipline, in the traditional diagram, the 
insights are synthesized into ordered values and represented as a 
radar plot with three axes. Some LLMs insisted on more axes, 
others struggled to get a plot.  
Text fitting: With the exception of grids, ASCII or PowerPoint 
bullet lists, the LLMs struggled with fitting text into graphics. Text 
is overlapped, obscured by other graphics, does not fit into boxes, 
is chopped off by the container or group, and so on. This is an 
interesting area for deeper investigation: graphics libraries such 
SVG or D3 do not provide classes for text fitting, and further, fitting 
text is a optimization problem requiring adjustments to font size, 
box size and scaling the overall image to make everything fit. It is 
likely LLMs do not have this in their training data or perhaps code 
generation is a preferrable approach to generation of text diagrams.   
Color: The LLM either does not use color or uses arbitrary 
colors. For Porter’s Five Forces, each top-level category has a risk 
indicator (e.g. intense, high, moderate), which was not used by the 
LLM, but the human designer used to draw attention to the riskest 
category. ChatGPT did use color, but the high risk categories in 
ChatGPT’s output are colored green in one case and red in the 
other, potentially leading to confusion if the diagram is skimmed.  
Text to orderable values: The Value Discipline diagram uses a 
radar chart which requires transforming text statements into 
quantitative values. The LLMs do generate a value and thus 
(somewhat) able to generate a radar chart.  
Given the work of other researchers with visualization generation 
(background section) we had incorrectly anticipated the LLM 
would be able to generate these diagrams with simple prompts. 
Further work on prompting approaches may be required.   
5 DISCUSSION 
The biggest gaps in the full sequence of data to diagram are 
primarily regarding: 1) Inconsistent abilities to manipulate 
numbers. This being addressed in LLMs through the use of plug-
ins/assistants; and 2) Poor ability to generate diagrams particularly 
when text fitting is required. This might be addressed in a variety 
of ways, such as variations in prompts or output type (e.g. code 
generation or image generation); and/or more holistic LLMs that 
also incorporate multiple media types including graphics into their 
training data.  
While prompts and next generation LLMs may solve these two 
issues, there are several assumptions and caveats for the future user 
of LLM generation of insights, strategy analyses and diagrams:  
1. 
Sensitivity to data structure: In Section 4, the first 
timeseries dataset was organized in reverse order – the most 
recent date was the first row. In many years of working with 
timeseries data, this data is almost always organized with 
earlier dates occurring on earlier rows. The LLMs generated 
erroneous insights with rows in an unexpected order.  
2. 
RAG: Using the LLM’s knowledge gained from its training 
data may result in responses that are significantly out of 
date. However, approaches such as RAG and GraphRAG 
[23] can be combined with LLMs to generate responses 
effectively utilizing more recent data.  
3. 
Qualitative 
analysis 
via 
management 
models: 
Management models are an interesting starting point for the 
analysis of multiple disjoint textual statements and might be 
extensible for more generalized qualitative analysis.  
4. 
Assembling qualitative results into quantitative values: 
Some analyses require transforming a list of qualitative 
insights into a quantitative value. This seems like an area 
where error, subjectivity or bias may be introduced.   
5. 
Metrics derivation: Many analyses require deriving 
appropriate metrics, known as feature engineering in 
machine learning. This is challenging because tabular data 
lacks metadata beyond column headers and values, making 
metric semantics hard to assess. Combining these metrics 
into explainable features remains unsolved. More training 
data from data science and management science may be 
needed for LLM training. Future work could compare how 
LLMs combine metrics under ambiguous requirements.  
6. 
LLM rapid improvement: Ethan Mollick [24] claims: 
“Assume the LLM you are working with right now is the 
worst LLM you will ever work with.” We generated 
diagrams in April, then three months later in July. Some 
improvements were significant: see Figure 5. Furthermore, 
the three steps outline in this paper can be collapsed into a 
single prompt: the SWOT in figure 2 was generated with: 
Do a SWOT for on the company PepsiCo and create the 
standard 2x2 grid as HTML DIV or HTML SVG, include no 
more than 4 factors per cell and at least 5-10 words per 
each factor in each cell. Your factors can include metrics 
available to you as of your last update.

6 CONCLUSIONS 
We believe our investigation shows that LLM generation of 
strategy management diagrams are feasible with some caveats. 
Further, these positive results are supportive of future research of 
application of strategy management diagrams to a broader range of 
data facts and qualitative data, not strictly limited to corporate 
datasets. At the most extreme position, Ben Shneiderman has said 
“The purpose of visualization is insight, not pictures.” [25], which 
is a provocation: if we can use automated techniques to extract and 
organize discrete insights, then the role of visualization could 
change significantly in analyses, and this paper is one approach for 
consideration of new kinds of visualizations of insights. At the 
other extreme, future strategy management diagrams could provide 
a high-level entry point to visual analytic systems, starting with 1) 
the user (business) questions answer through insight (strategy) 
diagrams, 2) augmented with word-scale graphics [26] and 3) 
elaborated with interactive drill-down for visualization data 
analysis and exploration, particularly for complex data not easily 
explained in simple terms (e.g. causality [27,28]).
