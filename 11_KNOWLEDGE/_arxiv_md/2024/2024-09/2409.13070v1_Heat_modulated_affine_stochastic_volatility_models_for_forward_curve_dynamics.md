HEAT MODULATED AFFINE STOCHASTIC VOLATILITY
MODELS FOR FORWARD CURVE DYNAMICS
Sven Karbach
[sven@karbach.org]
Abstract. We present a function-valued stochastic volatility model designed
to capture the continuous-time evolution of forward curves in ﬁxed-income
or commodity markets.
The dynamics of the (logarithmic) forward curves
are deﬁned by a Heath-Jarrow-Morton-Musiela stochastic partial diﬀerential
equation modulated by an instantaneous volatility process that describes the
second-order moment structure of forwards with diﬀerent time-to-maturity.
We propose to model the operator-valued instantaneous covariance by an aﬃne
process on the cone of positive trace-class operators with drift given by the
Lyapunov operator of the Laplacian. The so deﬁned inﬁnite-rank stochastic
volatility model is analytically tractable due to its aﬃne structure and allows
to model maturity speciﬁc risk and volatility clustering in forward markets.
Furthermore, we introduce a numerically feasible spectral Galerkin approx-
imation of the associated operator-valued generalized Riccati equations and
study the robustness of the model with respect to ﬁnite-rank approximations
by providing explicit error bounds on the approximation error.
Keywords: stochastic volatility, stochastic covariance models, forward curve
dynamics, aﬃne processes, Galerkin approximation, ﬁnite-rank approximation.
1. Introduction
Half a century ago, Black and Scholes unveiled their revolutionary methodology
for the valuation and hedging of options in equity markets through their seminal
work [1].
In a serendipitous coincidence, the Chicago Board Options Exchange
inaugurated its operations in April 1973, a month prior to Black and Scholes’ pub-
lication, and it has evolved to become the world’s preeminent options exchange by
volume. Today, a large number of options written on various underlyings are well-
established and liquidly traded ﬁnancial products. This growth in prevalence is to
a large extent attributable to an enhanced comprehension of the mathematics of
options, pioneered by Black and Scholes’ work. While the Black-Scholes model and
its induced pricing formula are still foundational for derivatives pricing, their role
has shifted to serving primarily as a benchmark or quotation model, such as in the
calculation of implied volatilities. It is well-acknowledged among practitioners and
academics alike that the Black-Scholes model has its limitations, most notably its
assumption of constant volatility, which is at odds with empirical evidence indicat-
ing that volatility is both time-varying and stochastic. Subsequent to the inﬂuential
contribution of Black and Scholes, a plethora of models have been introduced to
more accurately depict volatility’s erratic behavior and enhance derivative pricing.
Among these are the stochastic volatility (SV) models of Heston [2], the SABR
model by Hagan et al. [3], the Barndorﬀ–Nielsen and Shephard model [4], and
Dupire’s local volatility models [5]. The latest major innovation in this ﬁeld is the
advent of rough SV models, which were more recently put forward by Gatheral et
al. [6]. Rough SV models ﬁt the volatility surface eﬀectively with few parameters,
and provide more realistic volatility time-series than classical SV models.

A further advancement in the ﬁeld of stochastic volatility modeling is their exten-
sion to a multivariate framework, capturing the dynamics of multiple asset prices
and their ﬁnite-rank covariance structure simultaneously. Multivariate stochastic
volatility models, also called stochastic covariance models, are essential tools for
managing the risk of asset portfolios, as well as for pricing and hedging portfo-
lios of derivatives or instruments written on multiple underlyings such as spread
or basket options. Classical approaches for modeling the instantaneous covariance
process in the multivariate case include matrix-valued pure-jump models based on
positive semi-deﬁnite Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes [7, 8, 9], pure diﬀusion-based
models like those based on Wishart processes [10, 11], and hybrids of the former two
classes, namely aﬃne jump-diﬀusions on positive semi-deﬁnite matrices [12, 13].
In this paper, we investigate the next evolutionary stage in stochastic volatility
modeling: function-valued inﬁnite-rank stochastic volatility models of aﬃne type
that emerge as limits of multivariate aﬃne jump-diﬀusion SV models as the number
of independent risk factors increase to inﬁnity.
Function-valued models play a crucial role in ﬁnance, especially in modeling the
dynamics of forward rates and forward price curves in ﬁxed-income and commodity
markets. The key idea is to directly model the arbitrage-free forward prices for
all maturity dates simultaneously, rather than using a parametric model for the
underlying spot asset and then deriving the forward prices through a spot-forward
relation. This direct modeling approach was initially applied in interest rate mar-
kets by Heath, Jarrow, and Morton [14], hence it is referred to as the HJM approach.
Since then, this methodology has been extended to various other markets, including
commodities [15] and stock options [16].
In commodity markets, the HJM modeling approach is particularly advantageous
in energy and weather related markets where establishing a valid relation between
spot and forward prices is challenging. Indeed, in these markets the conventional
buy-and-hold hedging strategy for commodity forwards may be impractical due to
limitations such as non- or limited storability (as in the case of electricity and gas,
see e.g., [15]) or the underlying assets being simply non-tradable (as for example
wind speed or temperature indexes, see e.g., [17]). However, futures, forwards, and
other derivatives written on day-ahead power prices or index-based underlyings
are sometimes liquidly traded.
For instance, power futures from day-ahead to,
in some areas, 3-year delivery periods are liquid, and renewable energy forward
production curves can be tracked with reasonable high frequency given a ﬂow of
weather predictions. In these markets, the focus therefore shifts to directly modeling
the forward dynamics following the HJM modeling paradigm.
The limitations in trading power or weather index-based underlyings not only
pose challenges in spot-forward modeling, but also contribute to high and complex
maturity-speciﬁc volatilities in the forward dynamics. These complexities arise from
factors such as intermittency, forward-looking information, spatial and network risk,
political decisions, and weather forecasts, see also the discussion in [18, Section 1.3].
The general objective of this paper is to contribute to the mathematical formulation
of a framework to model these complex volatility structures within continuous-time
functional models, recently put forward in a number of papers [19, 20, 21, 22, 23].
One key motivation of this line of research is to enhance the understanding of the
mathematics of options written on forward curves. Similar to the progression from
the ordinary Black-Scholes model to stochastic volatility models in the univariate
setting, we advocate for stochastic volatility models for functional forward curve
dynamics that: (1) can jointly capture the stylized facts of forward curve dynamics
and their implied or realized volatilities, and (2) are tractable enough to eﬃciently
price and hedge European-style options written on the forwards.

Within the general framework of the HJMM model (1.1), we retain the ﬂexibility
to model the instantaneous volatilities σt(x) and decide on the rank d. Similar to
the univariate case, we can distinguish between three subclasses of models:

equation.

1.2.1. Non-Parametric Modeling of Forward Prices and Volatility. In such function-
valued stochastic covariance models, we interpret the HJMM equation (1.1) as a
stochastic diﬀerential equation formulated within a suitable space of forward curves
(see Section 2.1 below for details). This approach is non-parametric, as it leverages
the entire initial forward curve, represented by x 7→f0(x), along with a instanta-
neous covariance process (σ2
t )t≥0, as primary model inputs. In this function-valued
framework, σ2
t , for t ≥0, represents the covariance of ft across all time-to-maturities
and is deﬁned as a positive, self-adjoint operator of rank d, acting on a speciﬁc space
of forward curves, and can be identiﬁed with some covariance kernel on the time-
to-maturity domain. This non-parametric stochastic modeling has the advantage
of capturing the continuous-time dynamics of the forward curves while at the same
time ﬁtting well the realized and implied covariance structure with only few hy-
perparameters. The initial forward curve x 7→f0(x) can be extracted from market
data using a smoothing method, as detailed in [24, Section 1.6.3]. Meanwhile, the
volatility component can be calibrated to option price data [27], estimated from
realized term structure data [28, 29, 30] or ﬁtted to both simultaneously.
1.2.2. The Rank and Structure of Forward Curve Volatility: Empirics and Theory.
Empirical studies have shown that energy forwards exhibit idiosyncratic risk across
diﬀerent maturities, characterized by high-dimensional noise and distinctive sto-
chastic correlation structures [31, 32]. For instance, a principal component analysis
(PCA) of the NordPool power futures market conducted in [15] revealed that more
than ten risk factors are necessary to explain 95% of the variance. Similar results
have been reported in other studies, such as [31] and [33]. These ﬁndings in energy
markets contrasts with the interest rate markets, where PCA indicates that only a
small number of risk factors is required to explain the variance [24]. These empirical
ﬁndings support the assumption of stochastic volatility [34], but also suggest the
use of ﬁnite-rank HJM models. However, relying on the PCA analysis alone can
lead to suboptimal modeling decisions for pricing, hedging, and risk management
in forward markets due to the following theoretical and practical considerations:
• Maturity-Speciﬁc Risk: As observed in [24], classical ﬁnite-rank HJM mod-
els are complete, allowing for perfect hedging of interest rate derivatives.
This redundancy even allows to choose tenors of the hedging instruments
that are independent of the derivative’s expiry date, leading to unrealistic
hedging strategies. This issue can only be adequately addressed in the case
of maturity-speciﬁc risk, where the volatility is modeled with inﬁnite rank.
• Higher-Order Moments: As argued by [35], discarding risk factors based on
a PCA in an a priori fashion can introduce biases when computing quanti-
ties that are nonlinear functions of the forward curve. This is particularly
relevant for pricing and hedging forward curve derivatives with nonlinear
payoﬀs, or for computing quantiles and value-at-risk. Therefore, for appli-
cations in pricing, hedging, and risk management, it is advisable to ﬁrst
model with maturity-speciﬁc risk, before analysing ﬁnite-rank approxima-
tions and their associated errors relative to the sound full-rank model.
• Covariance Representation: Even for a moderate number of risk factors
d ∈N, theere are d × (d −1)/2 stochastic correlations to be computed,
which poses a high-dimensional problem, especially in energy markets with
up to ten identiﬁed risk factors.
In the limit as d →∞, every covari-
ance operator has a convenient representation as L2-kernel, a function of
time-to-maturity [36]. For modeling purposes, it is more practical to use a
parametric family of kernels and their ﬁnite-rank approximations, with a
low number of parameters, that provides good interpretability and expres-
siveness than working with a high-dimensional stochastic matrix.

1.3.2. Regularization by Heat Modulation. The main idea behind the heat mod-
ulation of stochastic covariance models for forward curve dynamics is to use the
instantaneous covariance process (σ2
t )t≥0 to regularize the noise in the HJMM
equation (1.1). This is accomplished by augmenting the drift component of the in-
stantaneous covariance process with the Lyapunov operator of the Laplacian. The
Lyapunov operator is deﬁned as L∆(·) := ∆·+·∆, where ∆represents the Laplacian
acting on an appropriate forward curve space. The resulting heat-modulated sto-
chastic covariance models derive their name from this connection to the Laplacian,
which is know to generate the so-called heat semigroup (Tt)t≥0. The heat semigroup
is a uniformly exponentially stable, analytic semigroup of bounded, self-adjoint lin-
ear operators and incorporating the heat semigroup into the stochastic volatility
component, or heat modulation, brings several beneﬁcial eﬀects to the models, which
will be explored in this paper. Below, we discuss the two most prominent eﬀects
that motivate the use of heat modulation in forward curve modeling.
First, as proposed in [35] and [39], the noise in forward curve dynamics is best
modeled by inﬁnite-rank cylindrical Brownian or Lévy noise.
Using cylindrical
noise, rather than, for example, a Q-Brownian motion with some covariance op-
erator Q, has the advantage that all information about the covariance is encoded
solely in the operator σ2

are indistinguishable from each other in the data. In particular, this approach al-
lows the instantaneous volatilities σ(i)

components of σt dWt for any t ≥0, which can be eﬀectively ﬁtted to data, as
outlined in [24, Section 1.7]. Additionally, since cylindrical Brownian noise relates
to a Brownian motion with the identity as its covariance operator, it eliminates
the need for cumbersome assumptions about the commutativity of instantaneous
covariance operators and the covariance Q, as required in [22, Assumption 2.11]
and [19, Proposition 3.2]. Therefore, the cylindrical setting is both more natural
and more convenient for covariance modeling in forward curve spaces.
However, for the HJMM equation with cylindrical noise to be well-posed as an SDE
in a suitable Hilbert space, the instantaneous volatility process must be Hilbert-
Schmidt operator-valued or equivalently, the instantaneous covariance operators
must be of trace-class, see [40]. The latter space is only Banach, and proving the
existence of, e.g., aﬃne processes on this space is challenging because standard
stochastic analysis tools for Hilbert spaces are not readily applicable. We demon-
strate that using the Lyapunov operator of the Laplacian in the drift of an aﬃne
process allows us to work within Hilbert spaces, speciﬁcally on a Gelfand triple of
Hilbert-Schmidt operators. By applying a regularization result from [41], we show
that an any aﬃne process driven by the Lyapunov operator of the Laplacian is
indeed trace-class operator-valued, and is therefore well-suited for the cylindrical
noise setting.
Secondly, from a modeling perspective, it is unrealistic to assume that the noise,
cylindrical or not, takes values in the state-space of smooth forward curves. Indeed,
forward curves are constructed by smoothing forward rates or prices using some
ﬁtting methods, as outlined in [42, Section 1.2]. The noise itself is not observed
directly, can therefore not be smoothed from data and should not be assumed to
have the same level of smoothness as the forward curves themselve. Accordingly,
the instantaneous volatility operators must regularize the less smooth forward curve
noise eﬀectively. We show that, similar to the ﬁrst regularization property at the
operator level, the heat semigroup also yields a regularization at the function space
level, smoothing the noise and ensures weak diﬀerentiability of the resulting forward
curves. This type of regularization has also been discussed in [38] for the inﬁnite-
dimensional Heston stochastic volatility model.

2. Stochastic Volatility Modulated HJMM and Affine Processes
In this section, we introduce the heat-modulated aﬃne stochastic volatility model.
A stochastic volatility model comprises two key components: the price process and
the instantaneous (co)variance process. We begin by addressing these components
separately. In Section 2.1, we examine a generic stochastic volatility-modulated
HJMM model to describe the spatio-temporal dynamics of logarithmic forward
curves in ﬁxed-income or commodity markets. Subsequently, in Section 2.2, we
present a particular class of operator-valued aﬃne processes designed to model the
instantaneous covariance process associated with the forward price curve dynamics.
2.1. The Dynamics of Forward Curves. To interpret the HJMM stochastic
partial diﬀerential equation (1.1), we view it as a stochastic diﬀerential equation

give suﬃcient conditions for the existence of mild solutions to the HJMM equation.
