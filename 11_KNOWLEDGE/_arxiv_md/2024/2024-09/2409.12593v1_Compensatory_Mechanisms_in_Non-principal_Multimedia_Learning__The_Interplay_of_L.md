Abstract 
EducaƟonal mulƟmedia has become increasingly important in modern learning environments because of 
its cost-eﬀecƟveness and ability to overcome the temporal and spaƟal limitaƟons of tradiƟonal methods. 
However, the complex cogniƟve processes involved in mulƟmedia learning pose challenges in 
understanding its neural mechanisms. This study employs network neuroscience to invesƟgate how 
mulƟmedia design principles inﬂuence the underlying neural mechanisms by examining interacƟons 
among various brain regions. Two disƟnct mulƟmedia programs were constructed using idenƟcal auditory 
content but diﬀering visual designs: one adhered to ﬁve guidelines for opƟmizing mulƟmedia instrucƟon, 
referred to as principal mulƟmedia, while the other intenƟonally violated these guidelines, referred to as 
non-principal mulƟmedia. CorƟcal funcƟonal brain networks were then extracted from EEG data to 
evaluate local and global informaƟon processing across the two condiƟons. Network measurements 
revealed that principal networks exhibited more eﬃcient local informaƟon processing, whereas non-
principal networks demonstrated enhanced global informaƟon processing and hub formaƟon. Network 
modularity analysis also indicated two disƟnct modular organizaƟons, with modules in non-principal 
networks displaying higher integraƟon and lower segregaƟon than those in principal networks, aligning 
with iniƟal ﬁndings. These observaƟons suggest that the brain may employ compensatory mechanisms to 
enhance learning and manage cogniƟve load despite less eﬀecƟve instrucƟonal designs.

IntroducƟon 
When we want to learn about a topic, many people turn to educaƟonal mulƟmedia, which frequently 
oﬀers an eﬃcient and convenient soluƟon, especially when we consider Ɵme limitaƟons and budget 
constraints. Teachers also typically incorporate educaƟonal mulƟmedia into their lessons, expecƟng 
students to learn from the content presented. However, the learner's experience can vary greatly 
depending on the design and content of the mulƟmedia 1–3. In some cases, learners easily follow the 
mulƟmedia program and use it as a foundaƟon for further learning, engaging with the content and 
achieving beƩer learning outcomes. Conversely, learners may struggle to engage with poorly designed 
videos and feel anxious and exhausted, leading to an inability to apply the content later. This raises 
important quesƟons: what is the neural mechanism responsible for governing our internal evaluaƟons and 
performance during and aŌer watching educaƟonal mulƟmedia? How do diﬀerent brain regions interact 
to facilitate more eﬀecƟve learning while watching a video? 
Learning from educaƟonal mulƟmedia is a complex cogniƟve task that occurs in a mulƟdimensional space, 
resulƟng in varying levels of cogniƟve workload in the brain. How the human brain processes and stores 
informaƟon has been discussed in cogniƟve load theory (CLT) 4. In modern educaƟon, CLT provides 
valuable insights for designing eﬃcient educaƟonal mulƟmedia that opƟmizes the cogniƟve load and 
improves the learning process 3,5,6. In 2002, Richard Mayer introduced twelve principles to improve 
mulƟmedia design and reduce the cogniƟve load for learners 2. Five principles speciﬁcally target 
extraneous processing, which involves cogniƟve eﬀort on nonessenƟal elements. These include coherence 
(removing irrelevant content), signaling (highlighƟng key informaƟon), redundancy (excluding on-screen 
text when graphics and narraƟon are used), spaƟal conƟguity (placing related words and images close 
together), and temporal conƟguity (presenƟng related words and images simultaneously). Adhering to 
these 5 principles helps create more eﬀecƟve mulƟmedia learning experiences with opƟmized cogniƟve 
load 7–9.  
Like other cogniƟve tasks, assessing the cogniƟve workload during the learning process requires accurate 
and reliable measurement techniques. Researchers commonly employ two main approaches: subjecƟve 
and objecƟve. SubjecƟve methods involve quesƟonnaires and interviews to evaluate cogniƟve load based 
on the subject's percepƟon of task diﬃculty1,10, such as the NASA-TLX quesƟonnaire 11. ObjecƟve methods, 
on the other hand, uƟlize physiological and behavioral measures, including electroencephalography (EEG) 
12,13, eye-tracking 14,15, and funcƟonal magneƟc resonance imaging (fMRI) 16. By employing both subjecƟve 
and objecƟve measures, we can gain a more comprehensive view of the complex interplay between neural 
processes and their manifestaƟons in human experience and behavior 17.  
Among all these methods, EEG, either alone or in conjuncƟon with other approaches, plays a key role in 
analyzing brain funcƟon because of its real-Ɵme monitoring ability, long-term recording potenƟal, and 
high temporal resoluƟon 18. Researchers have employed various staƟsƟcal analyses 19–21 and machine 
learning techniques 8,22 on EEG data to evaluate diﬀerent aspects of cogniƟve load in the learning process, 
including memory and aƩenƟon. Furthermore, substanƟal research has been conducted on frequency 
band analysis, with a parƟcular focus on the theta and alpha bands 23,24. Changes in theta frequency band 
acƟvity are associated with working memory performance, parƟcularly in frontal and central brain regions 
19,25. Moreover, theta band power increases during successful memory encoding 26, and theta band acƟvity 
is posiƟvely correlated with memory retrieval 27. However, alpha-band oscillaƟons are correlated with

Recent research suggests that our understanding of the principles and mechanisms underlying complex 
brain funcƟon can be improved by integraƟng data from mulƟple brain regions and examining their 
funcƟonal interacƟons. Rather than focusing solely on localized changes in brain structure and funcƟon 
30,31,  network neuroscience takes an integraƟve approach. It models the brain as a complex network, with 
brain regions as nodes and their connecƟons as edges, allowing the applicaƟon of graph theory and 
network analysis to study the brain’s funcƟonal mechanism at the whole-brain scale 32–34. In this way, 
several network measurements have been employed to characterize one or several aspects of global and 
local funcƟonal brain connecƟvity.  Three common types of measurements are as follows: a) SegregaƟon 
measurements reveal that the brain network exhibits a modular organizaƟon, with densely connected 
clusters of brain regions responsible for specialized informaƟon processing 
35. b) IntegraƟon 
measurements: The modular architecture of the brain network resembles a highly eﬃcient “small-world” 
structure, striking a balance between local clustering and global integraƟon. The integraƟon measures 
evaluate the global informaƟon processing in the brain 35,36. c) Centrality measurements: These idenƟfy 
“hub” regions that play crucial roles in integraƟng informaƟon across distributed brain systems 37. 
AdopƟng the network neuroscience approach, which models the brain as a complex network of 
interconnected regions, can provide valuable insights into the neural mechanisms underlying mulƟmedia 
learning and its cogniƟve workload. Recent studies have revealed that diﬀerent factors can aﬀect the 
paƩern of the brain’s connecƟvity while watching a video, for example, recepƟve versus interacƟve video 
screens 38 and shape color 39. 
This study aims to invesƟgate how Mayer's principles menƟoned above can aﬀect funcƟonal brain 
networks to facilitate and improve the learning process and opƟmize cogniƟve workload. To achieve this 
goal, we constructed two educaƟonal mulƟmedia programs with the same auditory content; however, in 
one, the visual design adhered to Mayer's principles, whereas in the other, it violated these principles. We 
employed both subjecƟve and objecƟve methods to assess the impact of these mulƟmedia designs on 
learning outcomes and cogniƟve load. The subjecƟve measures included a recall test and the NASA-TLX 
quesƟonnaire, whereas the objecƟve measures involved analyzing brain networks obtained from the EEG 
data. We examined local and global informaƟon processing in the brain across two condiƟons via relevant 
funcƟonal brain network measurements, network modularity and behavioral outcomes separately and 
explored the relaƟonships between them.

Surface Laplacian TransformaƟon 
Source imaging algorithms, such as weighted minimum-norm esƟmaƟon (wMNE), exact low-resoluƟon 
electromagneƟc tomography (eLORETA), and beamforming, aim to miƟgate the eﬀects of volume

number of electrodes than the minimum requirement (64 electrodes) can lead to inaccurate source

assumpƟons about the sources, which makes it reference-free. AddiƟonally, SL can produce reliable results

uƟlized the surface laplacian (SL) on the corrected EEG signals to esƟmate the current-source density 
(CSD). By adopƟng this approach, we aimed to improve topographical localizaƟon and minimize volume 
conducƟon eﬀects. A similar approach was employed in other studies 39,59,60. Finally, for a more detailed 
examinaƟon of EEG signals across diverse spectral ranges, we considered corrected EEG signals in ﬁve 
frequency bands: delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–48

eﬃcient local informaƟon processing, and funcƟonal integraƟon, which is linked to enhanced global 
informaƟon processing 30. These concepts are essenƟal for understanding brain funcƟon within the 
framework of network neuroscience31–33. Our brain network analysis indicates that, compared with P 
networks, NP networks exhibit increased integraƟon, as revealed by higher global eﬃciency. Moreover, 
NP networks contain more hubs across the enƟre brain which facilitates extensive communicaƟon with 
other regions and supports funcƟonal integraƟon, as demonstrated by higher betweenness centrality and 
node degree.  One potenƟal interpretaƟon of this enhanced informaƟon processing—less observed in

local processing and more prominent in global processing—is that it may represent a compensatory
