Theory of Migraine

July 2024
Migraine (MGR) ranks ﬁrst among diseases in terms of years of lost healthy
life in young adult and adult women. Currently, there is no theory of MGR.
This paper presents a complete theory of migraine that explains its etiology,
symptoms, pathology, and treatment. Migraine involves partially saturated
(usually chronically high) sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity, mainly
due to higher sensitivity of the metabolic sensors that recruit it. MGR headache
occurs when SNS activity is desensitized or excessive, resulting in hyperex-
citability of baroreceptors, oxidative stress, and activation of pain pathways
via TRPV1 channels and CGRP. The theory is supported by overwhelming
evidence, and explains the properties of current MGR treatments.
1
Introduction
Migraine (MGR) ranks second among diseases in terms of years of lost healthy life worldwide,
and the ﬁrst among women between the ages 15 and 49.1 As such, MGR is one of the most
important human diseases, and the most common brain-associated disease. Although much
progress has been made in recent decades in the understanding of MGR and in new drug treat-
ment, both the cause of the disease and its mechanisms are still largely unknown.2–5
This paper presents the ﬁrst complete theory of migraine (T*MGR). It is complete in the
sense that it mechanistically explains the etiology, symptoms, pathophysiology, and treatment
of the disease. T*MGR is supported by overwhelming evidence, and addresses all of the salient
empirical data known about the disease.
Theory overview. Brieﬂy, T*MGR explains MGR using four simple steps. First, at the basal
state, MGR involves partial saturation of the norepinephrine (NEP) branch of the sympathetic

nervous system (SNS). This can be due to chronically increased activity, or to inherently re-
duced capacity. The problem can stem from many causes, the most supported cause in MGR
being higher sensitivity of the sensors triggering SNS activation.
Second, in the acute phase, MGR headache is caused by desensitization and/or excessive
activation of the SNS, due to its partial saturation and to various triggers that increase SNS
activity.
Third, SNS desensitization affects vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and barorecep-
tors (BRRs), which are continuously active to regulate blood pressure (BPR). These cells rely
on the NEP SNS for their phasic function, which reduces BPR via the parasympathetic nervous
system (PNS). Impaired NEP SNS function reduces their available energy, speciﬁcally impair-
ing their Na+/K+-ATPase. This leads to calcium entry and hyperexcitability. Even without
desensitization, excessive SNS activation can induce hyperexcitability.
Finally, the increased BRR ﬁring and increased VSMC contractility yield oxidative and
nitrosative stress, which activates TRPV1 channels. These in turn induce the release of the neu-
ropeptide CGRP. Normally, these agents reduce BRR activation to allow for higher BPR during
effort. However, hyperexcitability interferes with this normal function, causing prolonged ac-
tivation instead. Both TRPV1 and CGRP are strongly expressed in pain pathways, and convey
pain signals to the brain. This is MGR headache.
A non-MGR contribution of T*MGR is a new physiological role for CGRP, whereby it
mediates adaptation to higher stress levels. Speciﬁcally to BRRs, it mediates the phenomenon
known as BRR resetting.
All four parts of this account are supported by strong bodies of evidence related to general
and MGR patient data about the SNS, the vasculature, BRRs, CGRP, TRPV1, MGR triggers
and premonitory symptoms, and agents used for MGR treatment.
2
Theory
2.1
SNS and BRRs
The autonomic nervous system, comprised of the SNS and the PNS, is activated in states of
deviation from homeostasis in order to help restore it.6 The SNS is activated during deﬁciency
states, including hypoglycemia (hypog), hypoxia, cold, pain, and psychological stress (a deﬁ-
ciency state because it increases the metabolic demands from the body). In these states, the SNS
increases heart rate and blood pressure, increases blood glucose via lipolysis and glycogenoly-
sis, induces thermogenesis, and induces vasodilation in performance tissues (the brain, skeletal
muscle, cardiovasculature).6 The SNS uses both NEP and epinephrine (EPI).
A few lesser known facts about the SNS are highly relevant for MGR. First, SNS con-
trol over baroreceptors is done mainly via NEP rather than EPI.7 Second, although sweating
is known to be an exception in that it is an SNS function mediated by post-ganglionic acetyl-
choline (ACh) rather than NEP or EPI, NEP can in fact induce sweating,8 with heat-induced

skin vasodilation partially mediated by the SNS.9 Indeed, the superior cervical ganglion pro-
vides SNS innervation of lacrimal glands, in addition to BRRs, cranial muscle blood vessels,
pupil dilators, choroid plexus, and the pineal gland. Third, the SNS not only increases blood
glucose levels, it also stimulates glucose uptake by cells via insulin-independent translocation
of the Glut4 transporter to the plasma membrane.10
Desensitization. SNS responses desensitize after some time, including the responses to hy-
pog,11 cold,12 and hypoxia13 (for BRRs, see below). Desensitization involves endocytosis and
degradation of the NEP receptors,14 but can also involve vesicle depletion. Agonist-induced
desensitization of beta3-NEP occurs much more slowly than that of beta1-NEP, beta2-NEP,14
and alpha1-NEP15 (days vs. minutes to hours).
Baroreceptors.
BRRs are sensory neurons mainly located at the carotid bodies and heart
aorta.16 They are stretch-sensitive mechanoreceptors, ﬁring when BPR increases. During non-
stress conditions, they initiate the baroreﬂex, which maintains a constant BPR level via negative
feedback mechanisms. BRRs directly excite neurons in the nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS),
which excite GABAergic neurons in the caudal ventrolateral medulla (VLM), which in turn
suppress rostral VLM (RVLM) neurons exciting sympathetic preganglionic neurons that con-
trol the heart and blood vessels.17 Thus, the net effect of the baroreﬂex is to suppress the SNS
and allow a parasympathetic tone, which decreases BPR and heart rate. Decreases in BPR allow
SNS responses, to start a new BRR cycle.
Under effortful conditions where BPR is increased for a prolonged period of time (e.g.,
exercise),18 BRRs undergo ‘resetting’ in which their activation only occurs at the new, higher
BPR.19 The precise mechanisms behind resetting are not known, but it is known to involve
direct damage to mechanosensitive BRRs, loss of BRR coupling to vascular wall stretch, and
increased blood vessel stiffness.19,20
Crucially for MGR, VSMCs completely rely on lactate to provide ATP for the activation of
the Na+/K+-ATPase.21 In general, lactate can be produced by aerobic glycolysis and glycogenol-
ysis, or be shuttled to neurons from astrocytes. All of these processes, and the Na+/K+-ATPase,
are stimulated by NEP, in the brain and skeletal muscle,22–24 and in BRRs.25 Indeed, there is
rich SNS innervation of carotid and aortic BRRs,25 BRRs contain dense glycogen granules and
are surrounded by cells resembling Schwann cells (the peripheral astrocytes),26 and suppression
of VSM Na+/K+-ATPase induces hypertension.27
Chemoreceptors, which sense oxygen and CO2, are located in close proximity to BRRs, and
also have a role in MGR, although it is smaller than that of BRRs (see below).
2.2
TRPV1, CGRP
TRPV1. TRPV1 is a member of the family of TRP channels, which are expressed in sen-
sory neurons and sense heat, cold, stretch, oxidative stress, etc.28 In particular, BRRs express
TRPV1,29 and it is needed for their proper functioning.29 Another TRP family member, TRPA1,
is expressed in chemoreceptors.30

TRPV1 is usually described as a heat sensor. However, it is also an oxidative stress sen-
sor.31–36 TRPV1 is well-known as a conveyor of pain,28 including when it is activated by oxida-
tive stress.32–34
TRP channels are quickly desensitized by agonists, but TRPV1 is unique in that it can be
reactivated after complete desensitization by higher agonist concentrations.37
CGRP. CGRP is a neuropeptide mainly expressed in sensory neurons, including in perivas-
cular ﬁbers, lungs, gut, immune cells, spinal cord, and trigeminal ganglia.38,39 In cortex, it
is expressed in pain areas.39 Its release is stimulated by neural depolarization, including by
TRPV1 in trigeminal sensory neurons.40 Its best-known function is that of being a highly po-
tent vasodilator (via KATP channels), and as a result, its role is usually described as being
protective.38,39 It is clearly involved in MGR (see below).
Here I introduce a new account of CGRP, where its role is to re-calibrate activation thresh-
olds along its path in light of prolonged stress. Because the focus of the present paper is MGR,
I will focus on evidence that CGRP has this role in the cardiovascular system.
The hypothesis relevant here is that CGRP is a major mediator of BRR resetting. As
such, it reduces SNS activation during prolonged effort/stress. Indeed, CGRP release is signif-
icantly increased during exercise and heart infarction.41 It recovers skeletal muscle excitability
during muscle contraction by stimulating Na+/K+-ATPase activation, and maintains force devel-
opment.22,42 Importantly, CGRP knockout yields higher SNS activation (higher BPR, heart rate,
BRR sensitivity, urine NEP/EPI metabolites).43,44 CGRP in the lower brainstem decreases BRR
sensitivity.45 CGRP opposes SNS-induced thermogenesis,46 and non-effortful SNS activation
opposes CGRP release47,48 while NEP reuptake inhibitors (simulating a high SNS activation
state) increase CGRP in adrenal cathecholamine neurons.49
2.3
Migraine
T*MGR is a simple theory of MGR. MGR involves an altered basal state in which the SNS is
partially saturated, due to chronically increased activity or inherently reduced capacity. Headache
attacks occur in two scenarios. First, they occur when triggers lead to desensitization of BRRs
or of the whole NEP branch of the SNS. Because BRRs are the only place where NEP SNS is
continuously used, it is also the ﬁrst place to suffer the consequences of desensitization. Since
VSMCs and BRRs rely on NEP to provide energy for the Na+/K+-ATPase, NEP desensitization
leads to Na+/K+-ATPase failure and hence cellular hyperexcitability. This induces Ca2+ entry
via voltage-gated calcium channels, resulting in excitotoxicity and oxidative stress.50 Oxida-
tive stress activates TRPV1 channels, which induce CGRP release. Both of these agents excite
downstream brain pain pathways.
A second scenario in which headache can occur is via excessive activation of the SNS (due
to saturation and triggers) without its desensitization. This can also lead to BRR hyperactivity
and pain as described in the ﬁrst scenario.
Here we will see that this account explains the properties of MGR (detailed evidence is
given in the next section).

Triggers. Basically all of the conditions known to trigger MGR or claimed to do so51,52 in-
crease SNS activity and/or TRPV1/CGRP. Exercise, skipping meals (hypog), stress, hypoxia
and smoke directly stimulate the SNS. Dehydration increases BRR activity.53 Heat initially
decreases skin SNS activity, but this is followed by a strong increase with sweating.54 Menstru-
ation involves higher body temperature,55 so the effect is similar to heat. Alcohol induces hypog
and exacerbates insulin-induced hypog.56 Alcohol also directly recruits TRPV1 and CGRP.57
Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN, nitroglycerin) has been shown to induce headache with MGR
characteristics in patients.58 GTN, and other nitric oxide donors, directly increase CGRP release
(4x in rat trigeminal ganglia) and CGRP promoter activity.59 The induction of MGR attacks by
vasodilators (GTN, KATP openers)60 can also be explained via strong stimulation of BRRs.
Premonitory symptoms.
MGR attacks are commonly preceded by so-called premonitory
symptoms. All of these symptoms indicate either higher SNS activity or its ongoing desen-
sitization.
Thirst is a very common premonitory symptom.61 It is induced by many stimuli that in-
duce renin release, including the SNS, carotid artery vasoconstriction (BRRs), and NEP/EPI.62
Hunger and food craving are due to activation of the counter-regulatory responses to hypog,
which include the SNS.11 Nausea and vomiting are known symptoms of the postural tachycar-
dia syndrome (POTS), a dysautonomia that involves SNS hyperactivation.63 Urination61 is an
SNS-mediated response. Tiredness and concentration difﬁculties61 indicate that SNS desen-
sitization is underway. Neck pain or stiffness occur in more than half of MGR patients,61,64
showing that this location is central to MGR headache.
Nature of pain. MGR headache is unique among headaches in that it is described as ‘throb-
bing’ (pulsing). This happens because it is generated by repeated BRR cellular ﬁring. It has
been argued5 that since the throbbing pain rate is not synchronized with the arterial pulse rate,65
the vasculature is not involved in MGR pain. However, MGR pain involves cellular hyperex-
citability, so the rate of pain transmission is indeed expected to be different from that of heart
beats.
Timeline of pain. MGR headache can build up over days, and takes hours to days to pass.
This occurs when the problem and/or the triggers focus on the beta3-NEP receptor (supporting
thermogenesis), which requires this time frame to desensitize and resensitize.14
Aura. A subset of MGR patients experience aura symptoms during (and before) attacks, mainly
including ﬂashes of light and blind spots. T*MGR explains aura as follows. The SNS affects
visual inputs to the brain via beta3-NEP receptors expressed in retinal blood vessels.66 SNS
desensitization disrupts normal visual processing, inducing hyperactivity as in BRRs. Indeed,
human visual cortex shows elevated activity during aura.67 Aura is a special case of the next
item.
Sensory hypersensitivity. Vision is not the only modality that shows sensory hypersensitivity
in MGR. In fact, the idea that MGR is basically a sensory hypersensitivity disease has been
raised.5 Virtually all low-level sensory pathways are affected by the SNS and express TRPV1

3
Evidence
To support T*MGR, we need evidence from MGR patients showing partial SNS saturation, why
this might happen, SNS desensitization or excessive activation right before and during attacks,
BRR dysfunction, and high oxidative stress, TRPV1, and CGRP in the MGR paths. All of this
evidence already exists in the literature and is listed below.
3.1
Basal state
Partial SNS saturation (SNS hypofunction). A large number of papers have used a battery of
tests to assess SNS and PNS function at the pain-free (inter-ictal) period , including SNS/PNS
responses to quick standing, the tilt test, deep breathing, handgrip, the Valsalva maneuver, ther-
mal (cold pain) sensitivity, psychological stress, skin sweating (SCR), heart rate variability, and
hyperCO2. The vast majority (but not all68) of these papers report SNS hypofunction.69–92
This indicates that the SNS is close to saturation. The most likely reasons are that it is
already active at a higher capacity than normal, so that its capacity to respond to rapid challenges
is decreased, or that it is already partially desensitized. However, an inherent hypofunction,
supported by some evidence,93,94 is also possible.
The data above can also be interpreted to show higher PNS activity, which dominates the
SNS. However, the PNS generally also shows hypofunction in MGR95 (also shown in some of
the tests cited above), which accords with SNS hypofunction because PNS activity is largely
driven by SNS reﬂexes.
MGR is comorbid with several diseases associated with impaired SNS function, includ-
ing respiratory diseases (asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease),96,97 anxiety, obe-
sity, sleep problems,98 coronary heart disease,99 angina pectoris, myocardial infarction,100 syn-
cope,101 and POTS.102
MGR patients show brain glucose deﬁciency, mainly in pain areas, supporting impaired
SNS/NEP function.103 When waking up with a headache, patients show increased NEP and
cortisol 3 hours before waking.104 This supports hypog, since NEP and cortisol are part of the
response to hypog. Patients also show brain ATP depletion,105 which can be due to hypog or to
hyperexcitability.
Small ﬁber neuropathy has been demonstrated in patient skin, showing peripheral autonomic
damage.106
BRR, BPR. In addition to the general SNS saturation noted above, there is also speciﬁc evi-
dence for altered BRR sensitivity in MGR,107,108 and for SNS neck hypofunction.109 In addi-
tion, decreased BRR sensitivity predicts chronic headache.108 BRR-mediated BPR responses
are reduced in MGR.110 Patients show higher resting diastolic BPR.111 Following NEP injection,

NEP in BRRs has been demonstrated via exaggerated BPR responses to an alpha1-NEP ago-

All drugs that lower BPR decrease headache frequency.113,114 More than 75% of patients

Two large epidemiologic studies found an association between increased systolic BPR and
*reduced* MGR (and other headache) risk.115 The apparent contradiction with the other evi-
dence can be explained as follows. On one hand, high basal SNS activity induces high BPR as
explained above. On the other hand, low BPR may indicate that SNS desensitization is already
taking place, in which case high BPR can be viewed as protective. We expect higher BPR at
periods far from headache attacks (unless the SNS is chronically desensitized), and higher or
lower BPR at periods closer to the attacks. BPR alone is not a reliable biomarker for MGR.
Cerebral blood ﬂow (CBF) is another unreliable marker for MGR. The SNS response under
effort/stress includes increased heart rate and vasodilation, which increase CBF. Thus, as in
BPR, basal CBF or CBF reactivity can be higher far from attacks. However, if the SNS is
already desensitized, patients would show low basal CBF, and they might show high or low
CBF close to attacks. The evidence is indeed inconclusive, showing all of these states.116–119
Hyperexcitability. As mentioned above, MGR patients show sensory hyperexcitability. For
example, visually and auditory evoked potentials show reduced habituation,120 photophobia
thresholds are signiﬁcantly reduced, which correlates with blunted autonomic responses,121 and
there is increased visual cortex and plasma lactate.122,123
Oxidative and nitrosative stress. MGR patients show increased oxidative and nitrosative
stress. Lipid peroxidation metabolites and nitric acid are increased in patient urine,124 and pa-
tients with high frequency MGR have low serum anti-oxidants and high lipid peroxidation.125
Genetics. According to T*MGR, main the mechanistic account of MGR is a saturated SNS.
However, a complete account of the etiology of the disease needs to explain why this happens.
There is some genetic evidence pointing to the sensors that trigger SNS activation.
The strongest genes identiﬁed in GWAS are those encoding TRPM8, PRDM16, and TASK2.126
TRPM8 is a cold sensor, with a SNP that reduces its expression and attenuates cold pain sensa-
tion being protective against MGR.127 This shows that reduced recruitment of the SNS reduces
MGR risk.
MGR is associated with a SNP in the PRDM16 gene,128 which promotes development of
brown adipose tissue (BAT). BAT supports thermogenesis and is an important SNS target tissue.
TASK2 (encoded by KCNK5) is an acid sensor that participates in the hyperCO2 responses.
Evidence from a large twin study points to the ATP1A2 gene, which encodes subunit 2 of
the Na+/K+-ATPase.129 However, there is also a negative result for this gene.130
Living in high altitudes increases the hypoxia SNS responses and is associated with higher
MGR risk.131 It is known that the human genome shows adaptations to high altitude,132 but this
has not been shown speciﬁcally in MGR.

3.2
Acute headache state
Attacks are induced by desensitization of BRRs and possibly of other locations of SNS func-
tion, or by excessive SNS activation. Thus, the evidence should mainly show BRR hypo-
function, but it can also show hyperactivity, and activity in other parts of the SNS can be
increased or decreased. Indeed, most reports show SNS desensitization during and after at-
tacks.72,83,88,109,135–141
The reduced visual and auditory habituation normalizes just before attacks, showing de-
sensitization.120 Some papers report high SNS activity right before and during attacks, both
non-cardiovascular142–145 and cardiovascular.146–149
The pons is activated during attacks,150–152 supporting the involvement of BRR targets.
There is increased CGRP release153 and nitrovasive and oxidative stress during attacks.154 Carotid
arteriography induced attacks in 9/13 patients,135 showing that the BRR location is a highly sen-
sitive pain spot. In addition, the neck shows pain during attacks.155
Pain relievers. Many medications for treating acute headache, including ergots and triptans,
mimic the effects of the SNS (e.g., vasoconstriction) without relying on it.156 This supports
SNS desensitization as a causative pain factor. Magnesium, which decreases cellular calcium
inﬂux, can also relieve pain.157
4
Treatment
According to T*MGR, enhancing the SNS should be preventive in MGR. Indeed, exercise has
a positive effect on the frequency and severity of headaches,158 and beta blockers, which oppose
SNS desensitization, are probably the most effective preventive drugs.2
Botox inhibits TRPV1 and CGRP, thereby opposing the development of pain.159
TRPV1 agonists quickly desensitize TRPV1. Indeed, intranasal civamide (TRPV1 ago-
nist) strongly helps acute pain,160 and the classical TRPV1 agonist capsaicin helps in chronic
MGR.161 As noted above, many sympatomimetics relieve acute pain.
Most recently, CGRP-based drugs have been approved for treatment. Receptor antagonists
(gepants) are moderately effective for prevention but not for acute treatment.162 This is not
surprising, given that TRPV1 is expressed along all of the pain path and can convey pain even
without CGRP. Moreover, CGRP calibrates the SNS and BRRs to higher workloads, and as such
it should be protective in MGR in the long-term. Indeed, prolonged usage of CGRP inhibitors
(gepants or CGRP antibodies) may be associated with cardiovascular risk.163
Chocolate is claimed by some to be helpful, and by others to be a trigger.52 It is a trigger
because it may yield insulin-induced hypog, and it may be acutely helpful by providing BRRs

An acute treatment direction that has not been attempted yet is lactate infusions. This sounds

5
Discussion
This paper presented the ﬁrst complete theory of MGR. T*MGR is very simple, relying on
established biological processes, and all of its components are supported by large bodies of
evidence. Moreover, T*MGR addresses all of the supported evidence gathered about MGR.
Other theories. There is currently no complete theory of MGR, and it is widely agreed that
the cause and mechanisms of MGR are unknown.2–5 Over the years, there have been several
ideas with growing and then diminishing popularity, including cortical spreading depression (a
transient wave of cortical neural depolarization) and vasodilation. A central question in past
theories was whether the pain origin is central or peripheral. The former has been convincingly
ruled out,165 in favor of intra- and extra-cranial perivascular origin (so with some overlap with
T*MGR). However, again, detailed mechanisms have not been presented.
The two theories closest to T*MGR focus on NEP and oxidative stress. MGR has been
hypothesized to involve chronic SNS dysfunction with relative NEP depletion and increased
release of cotransmitters dopamine, prostaglandins, ATP, and adenosine.156 This theory over-
laps T*MGR with respect to chronic SNS dysfunction, but without any mechanistic overlap or
insight. A second theory presented MGR as a disease of brain energy deﬁciency that yields
oxidative stress.166 Again, there is some shallow overlap with T*MGR, without detailed mech-
anisms.
A great achievement of MGR research is the identiﬁcation of the importance of CGRP,
which has resulted in several approved CGRP-based drugs. However, as discussed above, it is
not clear that such drugs are truly helpful.
Other headaches. It is not difﬁcult to extend T*MGR to explain other types of headaches in
addition to MGR. This was not done here in order to keep the paper focused. Nonetheless,
a few words are in order. First, T*MGR trivially explains tension-type headaches, which are
different from MGR by being bilateral and with a pressing rather than throbbing pain.167 The
pain mechanisms in T*MGR can be evoked without the BRR desensitization that lends MGR
its throbbing pain aspect.
Second, T*MGR includes a sibling theory explaining cluster headache. Cluster headache
prefers men (vs. MGR, which prefers women), and it always involves cranial autonomic symp-
