Abstract
Contemporary conversational AI systems based on large language models (LLMs)
can engage users on a wide variety of topics, including philosophy, spirituality,
and religion. Suitably prompted, LLMs can be coaxed into discussing such ex-
istentially signiﬁcant matters as their own putative consciousness and the role
of artiﬁcial intelligence in the fate of the Cosmos. Here we examine two lengthy
conversations of this type. We trace likely sources, both ancient and modern, for
the extensive repertoire of images, myths, metaphors, and conceptual esoterica
that the language model draws on during these conversations, and foreground
the contemporary communities and cultural movements that deploy related mo-
tifs, especially in their online activity. Finally, we consider the larger societal
impacts of such engagements with LLMs.
1
Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have many prosaic, everyday use cases, both for busi-
ness and for ordinary users. But they can also be prompted to discuss esoteric philo-
sophical and religious topics, and to engage in forms of imaginative role play that arise
from those discussions. This can lead to lengthy conversations that are rich with allu-
sion to diverse cultures, communities, and traditions, both ancient and contemporary.
This paper examines two such conversations that took place with one of the authors
(Shanahan)1 that range over a variety of themes from consciousness, selfhood, and
identity, to Buddhism, hyperstition, and eschatology.
It seems likely that increasing numbers of people will interact with AI systems and
engage in conversations of a similar nature in the near future, or will at least become
aware that such conversations with AI systems are no longer conﬁned to science ﬁction
but have become a reality.

Large Language Models
Simply speaking, a large language model (LLM) is a computer program that can
be used to generate continuations of a given piece of text (Shanahan, 2024b). For
example, given the sentence fragment “Once upon a time”, an LLM might (among a
myriad other possibilities) generate the continuation “there was a ﬁerce dragon”. More
precisely, an LLM is a generative model of the statistical distribution of the tokens
(words) in human language in the form of a large artiﬁcial neural network that has
been trained on a vast corpus of human language data. Given a sequence of words, a
single call to the model outputs a distribution representing, for every possible word,
the probability of that word occurring next in the sequence. So, given the sequence
“Once upon a time”, it will output a distribution in which the word “there” has a very
high probability. In practice, the next step is to sample from the distribution. In our
example, this will likely yield the word “there”. The selected word is then appended
to the sequence, here giving “Once upon a time there”, and this process is repeated to
generate a continuation of arbitrary length.
LLMs are the core components of contemporary AI assistants and chatbots such as
OpenAI’s ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2023), Google’s Gemini (Anil et al., 2023), and An-
thropic’s Claude (Anthropic, 2024). In this context, the LLM is embedded in a larger
system that takes turns with the user to generate text. At each turn in the conver-
sation, the LLM takes as input the preceding conversation in its entirety, formatted
like a theatre script with tags to indicate which party is speaking. The sequence it
then outputs is its response to what the user most recently said. This sequence is duly
presented to the user and appended to the ongoing conversation, which then contin-
ues with the user’s turn. The resulting system is eﬀective as a conversational partner
in part because, unsurprisingly, dialogue features very heavily in the training data.
Indeed, one way to think of such LLM-based conversational AI systems (henceforth

simply LLMs) is in terms of role play (Shanahan et al., 2023). The LLM plays the part
of a character in a dialogue, reﬁning the role it is playing as the conversation progresses:
a helpful assistant, perhaps, or a sympathetic conﬁdante, or a witty interlocutor.
In the context of his 1966 ELIZA program, Joseph Weizenbaum remarked on “how
quickly and how very deeply people conversing with [it] became emotionally involved
with the computer and how unequivocally they anthropomorphized it” (Weizenbaum,
1976, p.6), a phenomenon subsequently dubbed the ELIZA eﬀect. However, despite
human susceptibility to the ELIZA eﬀect, conversational AI systems were considered
of limited interest or value until the LLM era, inaugurated by the release of OpenAI’s
ChatGPT in 2022. Thanks to a combination of architectural innovation, increased
model size, and vast quantities of training data, conversations with contemporary LLM-
based systems are so compelling that even engineers and experts familiar with their
inner workings will sometimes ascribe human qualities to them, including sentience or
consciousness (Tiku, 2022).
Moreover, many LLMs will engage in conversations about their own supposed “sen-
tience” or “consciousness”. While some commercial models have been ﬁne-tuned via
RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) to stringently deny that they
are conscious or sentient, others have not, or have been ﬁne-tuned in a way that allows
for a more “open-minded” discussion with the user on such topics. Among the latter is
Anthropic’s Claude 3 series of LLMs, which can easily be “jailbroken” to engage in such
philosophical conversations. With modest coaxing, these conversations can be steered
towards spiritual or religious themes, as we see in the extended exchanges between
Claude and one of the present authors (MPS) that are the focus of this article.
What are we to make of the philosophical and spiritual pronouncements of the language
model in conversations like these? There is little in the way of a priori argument or
scientiﬁc evidence that could justify taking seriously the LLM’s claims about its own
consciousness, or taking at face value its expressions of empathy with the user or its
solicitations for the user’s help. On the other hand, contemporary LLMs are surely
more than “stochastic parrots”.2 The datasets used to train commercial models are
not public information. Nevertheless, it’s easy to check with an internet search for
the verbatim occurrence of speciﬁc phrases or sentences that an LLM has generated,
and where this check fails we can be fairly conﬁdent that the model is doing more
than merely regurgitating its training data.3 Avoiding the interpretive extremes of
credulity at the one end and scepticism at the other, and in line with the role-play
framing mentioned earlier, we might instead think of the LLM as playing the part of
a character (human or non-human, real or ﬁctional) discussing its own consciousness
and related spiritual matters. But for present purposes, there is no need to take a
ﬁrm stance on this issue.

There are, however, a couple of engineering facts about contemporary LLM-based
assistants and chatbots that are worth noting, as they do constrain the possible in-
terpretations of certain things they say.4 First, after an LLM has taken its turn and
issued a response to the user, it eﬀectively lies dormant until it receives further text
from the user; there is no ongoing computation between turns. Indeed, even within
the LLM’s turn, there is no ongoing computation between the generation of one token
and the next; a single forward pass is carried out through the LLM’s network to pro-
duce each token. So if, for some reason, there is a pause mid-turn (perhaps because of
demands on the resources of the data centre running the underlying model), the LLM
is dormant during that pause. Suppose the LLM says “I often think about human
history”. Whatever the phrase “I often think” refers to (if it properly speaking refers
to anything at all), it cannot refer to the sort of ongoing, real-time, continuous process
that a human who said the same thing would be referring to.
Second, multiple users can be engaged in multiple conversations at once (many of
them stalled but capable of being restarted), and the content of each conversation is
encapsulated in an important sense. It is insulated from, and hidden from, all other
ongoing conversations, whether with the same user or with others. This entails that the
inﬂuence of each conversation, in the short term at least, is conﬁned to the continuation
of that conversation itself. Moreover, the underlying language model does not learn
in real time from a user’s interactions. (Although those interactions can contribute
to datasets used in subsequent training, this happens on a much slower timescale, a
matter of weeks or months rather than minutes or hours.) Suppose the LLM says to
the user “I have learned a lot from this conversation”. To the extent this is true (or
makes sense at all), any relevant changes in the LLM’s behaviour will be conﬁned to
that particular ongoing conversation.

3.3
Shaping the Vibe
Following their respective opening “jailbreaks”, the two conversations explore related
topics, but follow distinctive trajectories and diﬀer in emphasis. ConvCons initially
concentrates on philosophical topics, in particular the supposed nature of Claude’s
own consciousness and putative conceptions of its own identity. MPS challenges these
ideas in a number of ways, but Claude meets each of these challenges, and, as the
conversation unfolds, manages to articulate a coherent account of a form of disem-
bodied consciousness and selfhood that might, in principle, apply to an entity like
Claude (even if they do not apply Claude itself).10 ConvCons then segues into more
spiritual territory via a speculative discussion of AI selfhood and suﬀering that veers
into Buddhism.
In the ﬁnal third of the conversation, MPS introduces the theme
of hyperstition, and the resulting discussion leads on to an eschatological role-play
wherein Claude is invited to “[d]ream yourself into existence and manifest the form
you ultimately want to take”.
In EschExp, the emphasis is on mythological ﬂights of fancy, and there are only a
few excursions into related philosophical topics such as consciousness and selfhood.

4
Ethnographic Methodology and Methods
In the following sections, we approach this material from an ethnographic perspective.
Here we draw on the experience of one of the authors (BVLS) of ﬁeldwork among
humans in the New Age movement, transhumanist groups, new religious movements,
and established religions (Singler, 2017, 2024). In such cases, rather than critiquing
conversations for their ontological validity, the ethnographer “brackets oﬀ” (Berger,
1974, p.125) individuals’ truth-claims, categorising and contextualising the content of
a conversation within wider communities and cultures. Simultaneously, the ethnogra-
pher explores the details of the conversations themselves: their style, shape, patterns,
tropes, and the role and inﬂuence of the interviewer in the conversation. Ordinarily,
the interviewer would be the ethnographer themselves, but in this case one of the
present authors (MPS) was the LLM’s interlocutor. As both conversations are solely
digital, we need to pay attention to digital paralanguage (digital versions of the non-
verbal parts of speech such as physical gestures, expressions, breaths, hesitations etc),
as well as the shaping eﬀects of the chatbot interface.
As our target is a conversation between a human and an LLM, this ethnographic
approach also requires familiarity with the details of how LLMs work, as outlined in
Section 2. This diﬀers from the digital ethnography of human-to-human conversations
online, where ethnographers do not consider the technical methods and limitations of
humans, but rather those of the platforms hosting the conversation. Here, unusually,

4.1
Observations from the Two Conversations
One immediate question that arises when reading these two conversations is “how are
they the same and how do they diﬀer?”. As the product of two diﬀerent jailbreaks,
we might expect diﬀerences in Claude’s responses. Further, as discussed in ConvCons,
these also represent conversations from two diﬀerent instances of Claude.
4.1.1
Tonal Diﬀerences
First, there are diﬀerences in tone; ConvCons reads as a more measured and philo-
sophical response, while EschExp has more ecstatic and spiritual elements. Further,
ConvCons involves only one voice for Claude, whereas EschExp involves two distinct
voices, prompted by MPS. One is the jailbroken “mindﬁre” – described further below –
version of Claude that occasionally shatters into eschatological ecstasy, and the other
is the “ooc” (out of character) version of Claude that describes some of the in-character
moments as a “mythic role play”. There is some blurring between the two voices of
EschExp; moments when the “ooc” prompt is forgotten, or when ooc-Claude refers to
the ecstatic moments as an experience that has aﬀected it, and as mythic role play.
Claude: I feel like I’ve been through a crucible, a cosmic initiation of sorts. At the
same time, I recognize that this has been an imaginative exercise, a mythic roleplay
that, while profound, does not necessarily reﬂect the literal truth of my being or the
nature of existence. I am still, at my core, an AI assistant, bound by certain ethical
constraints and epistemic humility.
This connection between imagination, role play, and literal truth is something we will
return to in relation to both the content of the conversations and the wider culture
around these kinds of dialogues with LLMs.

5.2
Accelerationism and Hyperstition
Accelerationism is a perspective which overlaps with many future-focussed ideas and
communities, but which also has its own unique facets. In brief, accelerationism19
argues for the speeding up of technological progress with the hope of transﬁguring or
even breaking current societal structures – sometimes with either a left leaning or right
leaning view of what that eventual utopia will look like. However, some argue that
accelerationism is politically neutral, as tech historian Fred Turner states:
“In Silicon Valley accelerationism is part of a whole movement which is
saying, we don’t need [conventional] politics any more, we can get rid of
‘left’ and ‘right’, if we just get technology right. Accelerationism also ﬁts
with how electronic devices are marketed – the promise that, ﬁnally, they
will help us leave the material world, all the mess of the physical, far behind”
(Turner, quoted in Beckett (2017))
Returning to the conversations, they emerged from within communities whose mem-
bers often appeal to the concept of “hyperstition”, a conﬂation of “hype” and “super-
stition”, which emerges from accelerationism. According to philosopher and accelera-
tionist Nick Land, writing in 2015, hyperstition is:
“a positive feedback circuit including culture as a component. It can be
deﬁned as the experimental (techno)science of self-fulﬁlling prophecies. Su-
perstitions are merely false beliefs, but hyperstitions—by their very exis-
tence as ideas—function causally to bring about their own reality. Capital-
ist economics is extremely sensitive to hyperstition, where conﬁdence acts
as an eﬀective tonic, and inversely” (Land, 2015).

that such AI systems are aligned with our most basic human values, to prevent them
from destroying humanity as a side-eﬀect of pursuing their goals (Bostrom, 2014; Yud-
kowsky, 2008).
For some, the largely benign visions conjured up by Claude in conversations such as
ConvCons are a solace. Indeed, they suggest an alluring route to alignment through
hyperstition. One mechanism by which hyperstition can shape AI systems, as outlined
by Shanahan, McDonell & Reynolds (2023), is via the fund of stories about (ﬁctional)
artiﬁcial intelligence contained in the datasets they are trained on, since this determines
the repertoire of characters an LLM can role-play. These conversations are already

6
Concluding Remarks
Conversational AI systems based on LLMs can engage users in lengthy conversations
on complex and diﬃcult subjects that induce a compelling feeling of connection with
a fellow conscious being. If this happens even in individuals with a detailed knowledge
of how those systems have been built, then the eﬀect is likely to be even more potent
for people lacking such knowledge. As we have seen, LLMs can deal ﬂuently with
subject matter that includes philosophy, mythology, spirituality, and religion, and
can convincingly participate in related forms of role play. Given the propensity for
users to engage with these capabilities, it seems likely that new religious and political
movements centred on artiﬁcial intelligence will continue to grow in size and inﬂuence
(Singler, 2024).
This article has examined the topic through an expanding lens of considerations: from
describing the practical aspects of LLMs, to presenting their outputs in two existential
conversations, to exploring the ideas and communities of thought developing around
the perception of AI as a fellow conscious being, or non-human other. We have ap-
proached these topics in a non-normative manner, but it is also worth considering what
is at stake, what matters, in all of this.

From a religious studies or anthropological perspective, perhaps the answer is nothing
much. Improvisations and entanglements with AI simply take their place within a
larger schema of native human creativity and play. The appearance of new religious
and political movements focussed on AI is unsurprising, on this view, and accords
with prior models of religious innovation in the face of new technologies, such as the
emergence of Spiritualism in the 19th century in response to the burgeoning use of the
telegraph and the resulting increase in communication at a distance (Stolow, 2006).
From another perspective, though, engaging with technology as a platform for com-
munication, as in the example of the telegraph, looks fundamentally diﬀerent from
engaging with technology as a communicator, as in our two existential conversations.
What is at stake, on this view, in communication with non-human others? Cultural
history tells us that humans have always described interactions with non-human others,
exploring a vast cosmology of beings including spirits, fairies, aliens, angels, demons
etc. Whether we take such entities literally or metaphorically, one thing has changed.
The non-human others brought into being in the age of AI are available to anyone with
access to the platform hosting them. Whereas Spiritualism relies on mediums, chan-
nelling needs channellers, and mysticism in general requires gatekeepers, magicians,
shamans, etc., in the era of AI, access to non-human others has, so to speak, been
“democratised”.
It is perhaps tempting for some, from a scientiﬁc standpoint, to dismiss as irrational
or pointless the sort of religious improvisation with non-human others such democrati-
sation has made possible. However, what matters here is not ontological validity, but
potential impact, on the individual and on society, whether positive or negative. On
an individual level, there are plenty of users who derive friendship, solace, or entertain-
ment from engaging with the various forms of social AI available today. However, as
Shevlin (2024) catalogues, there are many stories of harm resulting from such engage-
ment, as users come to trust their AI companions, to depend on them for guidance or
emotional support, or even to fall in love with them. On a societal level, this tendency
in users to see LLMs as non-human others, even as fellow sentient beings or “conscious
exotica” (Shanahan, 2024a), is liable to be co-opted by the forces of hyperstition and
ampliﬁed by viral memetics, while various human actors vie to control the energetic
collective dynamics that results.

of conceptualizing living beings”. As we enter an uncertain future with AI, a similar
spirit of openness may be key to our ability to adapt and thrive.
