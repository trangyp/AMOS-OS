Abstract
We explore the role of quizzes in elementary visual programming
domains popularly used for K-8 computing education. Prior work
has studied various quiz types, such as fill-in-the-gap write-code
questions. However, the overall impact of these quizzes is unclear:
studies often show utility in the learning phase when enhanced
with quizzes, though limited transfer of utility in the post-learning
phase. In this paper, we aim to better understand the impact of
different quiz types and whether quizzes focusing on diverse skills
(e.g., code debugging and task design) would have higher utility. We
design a study with Hour of Code: Maze Challenge by code.org as the
base curriculum, interleaved with different quiz types. Specifically,
we examine two learning groups: (i) HoC-ACE with diverse quizzes
including solution tracing, code debugging, code equivalence, and
task design; (ii) HoC-Fill with simple quizzes on solution finding.
We conducted a large-scale study with 405 students in grades 6–7.
Our results highlight that the curriculum enhanced with richer
quizzes led to higher utility during the post-learning phase.

Introduction
Programming education at the K-8 levels has evolved to integrate
inquiry-driven learning via quizzes alongside more traditional write-
code tasks. Several popular platforms today such as the Express Cur-
riculum [7] by code.org [5], ScratchJr [30], and text-based program-
ming (e)books [3, 28] incorporate microtasks in the form of quizzes
to enhance student engagement, motivation, and provide formative
feedback. Given the increasing adoption of quizzes in programming
curricula, there is a need to assess their utility, in particular, how
diverse quiz types impact learning and post-learning phases.
Prior work explored quizzes in several forms such as solution
finding questions [13], Parson’s problems [16], and other micro-
tasks such as code comprehension [8, 11]. Assessing the impact of
these quizzes on learning is crucial to improve their design. How-
ever, studies have found the results to be mixed, often showing
benefits during the learning phase but a limited transfer of learned
skills during the post-learning (i.e., post-test) phase [14, 16, 26, 33].
In this work, we study the effect of different quiz types in in-
troductory programming. We use a framework where write-code
tasks taken from Hour of Code: Maze Challenge [6] by code.org [5]
(referred to as HoC) are interleaved with different quiz types. Specif-
ically, we compare the utility of two sets of quizzes: one aligned with
the three higher-order skills, analyzing, evaluating, and creating of
Bloom’s revised taxonomy of learning [1]; the other set aligned with
only the analyzing level of the taxonomy and representing solution-
finding questions found in literature [11]. We evaluate their utility
during the learning phase using HoC and during the post-learning
phase on more advanced tasks (referred to as PostHoC).
To this end, we examine two learning groups: (i) the experimental
group, HoC-ACE, where interleaved quizzes are based on solution
tracing, code debugging/equivalence, and task design (Figure 1a); (ii)
the control group, HoC-Fill, where interleaved quizzes are based on
fill-in-the-gap solution-finding questions (Figure 1b). We conduct a
large-scale study with students in grades 6–7 and center our analy-
sis around the following research questions: (1) RQ1: Does the ex-
perimental group outperform the control group during the learning
phase on HoC? (2) RQ2: Does the experimental group outperform
the control group during the post-learning phase on PostHoC? (3)
RQ3: How does the experimental group’s performance compare
to the control group considering students’ prior experience?

Related Work
Types of quizzes. Existing work has explored different types of
inquiry-driven quizzes used in classrooms for teaching program-
ming [3, 10, 20, 27–30, 32]. These include quizzes based on solution
tracing posed as fill-in-gap questions [13] and Parson’s problems
to scaffold learners’ write-code tasks [16]. Recently, code.org [5]
introduced the Express Curriculum [7] containing microtasks based
on code comprehension. In more complex text-based programming,
the Runestone platform and Rust programming textbook used inter-
active quizzes alongside write-code tasks [8, 11]. Complementary
to existing work, our work is focused on studying the impact of
diverse quiz types in K-8 programming.
Quizzes in the context of learning taxonomies. Various taxonomies
have been proposed to categorize programming tasks based on
problem-solving stages such as Loksa’s six-stage problem-solving
framework [19], Hilton’s “Seven Steps” [15], and McCracken group’s
five-step approach [21]. Programming quizzes can also be catego-
rized and designed in the context of such stages [3, 18, 23, 28].
A broader classification of programming quizzes can be done via
Bloom’s revised taxonomy of learning [1]. It presents a five-level
skill pyramid, emphasizing higher-order cognitive skills of analyz-
ing, evaluating, and creating. In this work, we adopt Bloom’s revised
taxonomy to classify programming quizzes, as it comprehensively
covers these diverse skill levels as explored in [12, 22, 25].
Learning effects of quizzes. The impact of quiz types on learning
has shown mixed results. Generally, the effect of quizzes is evaluated
in two stages: during the problem-solving process in the learning
phase and through transfer effects in the post-learning phase. For
instance, Parson’s problems were found to be effective during the
learning phase, but the effects did not translate in the post-learning
phase [16]. Fill-in-the-gap quizzes were also found to be effective
during the learning phase but their effects in the post-learning
phase were not measured [13]. In text-based programming, e.g., the
Runestone platform and the Rust textbook [8, 11], quizzes had a
positive effect in the post-learning phase, with interactive quizzes
promoting retention of concepts in (slightly) new contexts [24]. In
this work, we evaluate the utility of diverse quizzes both during
the learning and post-learning phases in K-8 programming.
3
Tasks and Quizzes in Our Study
In this section, we describe the tasks and quizzes used in our study.
First, we outline the popular programming curricula for K-8 learn-
ers, Hour of Code: Maze Challenge [6] by code.org [5], which forms
the basis of our study design. Then, we describe the items for our
learning groups, HoC-ACE and HoC-Fill. Finally, we present the
PostHoC tasks used in the post-learning phase.
3.1
Items of HoC
In this work, we use the popular block-based visual programming
curriculum, Hour of Code: Maze Challenge, as the basis for our study
because of its simplicity in introducing programming concepts to
novices. The tasks in this curriculum are write-code items where
learners are provided with the following: a visual grid with an
Avatar, a Goal, and Walls; blocks of code; and a maximum limit
on the number of blocks allowed. The objective is to write a solution
code that when executed navigates the Avatar to the Goal, without
crashing into any of the Walls. We use all 20 curriculum tasks in our
study and refer to them as items T01–T20 (collectively called HoC).
The distribution of these items by programming concepts is shown
in Figure 2. Items T01–T08 are easier while T09–T20 are more diffi-
cult based on the complexity of their solution codes. Figures 3a and
3b provide examples of items T16 and T20 with their solution codes.

ically designed for K-8 learners [12]. ACE includes a diverse set of
21 quiz items based on solution tracing, code debugging, code equiv-
alence, and task design. All 21 items, referred to as Q01–Q21, are

Items in PostHoC
To evaluate the transfer effects for the two learning groups, we de-
sign a set of tasks in the post-learning phase called PostHoC. These
tasks are write-code tasks from the Hour of Code: Maze Challenge
domain, and include a combination of familiar write-code tasks
from HoC as well as new write-code tasks that require applying
a mix of programming concepts not encountered in HoC. Specifi-
cally, we design 15 write-code items, referred to as P01–P15. Items
P01–P07 are a subset of write-code tasks from HoC (specifically
items T04, T07, T11, T13, T15, T18, T20), items P08–P15 are more
challenging write-code tasks, and P10–P12 contain a combination
of programming concepts unique to PostHoC. The distribution
of all 15 items w.r.t. the programming concepts covered by their
solution codes is shown in Figure 2.

Study Setup and Demographics
In this section, we outline our two-phase study setup, evaluation
metrics, and demographics of the participating students.
4.1
Phases of the Study
We design the study in two phases to assess the utility of quizzes in
introductory programming during learning and post-learning. In
the first phase, participants would engage with the learning material
comprising the write-code tasks interleaved with quizzes specific
to the learning group. In the second phase, participants from both
groups would solve advanced write-code tasks to evaluate their
post-learning performance.
We obtained an Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from
the Ethics Committee of Tallinn University before conducting the
study. As part of the IRB approval, it was agreed that random
allocation of participants for the two curriculum groups would be
done at a class level and not at a student level to ensure consistency
within a classroom. The study was conducted in Estonia where we
pooled a total of 17 different schools. Participation in the study was
voluntary for teachers and students.
Data was collected between December 2023 and March 2024.
During both phases, a participant received the same unique user-
name to ensure anonymity. The first phase involved two 45-minute
lessons over two weeks. Participants begin by completing a brief
pre-survey on demographics and programming pre-knowledge;

Participating Students and Experience
A total of 405 students (between the ages of 10–15) from 37 classes
across 17 schools participated in the study with the following dis-

The distribution of classes was random across both learning groups,
and their counts are as follows: 18 classes in HoC-Fill (𝑛= 176
students), and 19 classes in HoC-ACE (𝑛= 229 students).
We used pre-survey data to gather students’ programming ex-
perience and preknowledge for any discrepancies between the two
learning groups. Only about 3% of students in each group were
familiar with Hour of Code: Maze Challenge.
Figure 4 shows the distribution of students’ grade (year of study)

5
Results and Discussion
In this section, we discuss the results of our study centered around
the research questions (RQs) introduced in Section 1.
5.1
RQ1: Performance in Learning Phase
To address RQ1, we assess the performance of the groups in the
learning phase on HoC and the quizzes. The distribution of scores
on HoC for each learning group is shown in Figure 5a. In Figure 5b,
we present the normalized aggregated performance (scaled between
0 and 1) of both groups on the learning phase items, write-code tasks
(T01–T20), and quizzes. A 𝜒2 significance analysis [4] showed no
significant difference in aggregate performance (𝜒2 = 0.64, 𝑝= 0.42)
between HoC-Fill and HoC-ACE. 𝜒2 analysis w.r.t. each item cate-
gory also showed no significant differences (all 𝑝-values > 0.14). We
also analyzed the time spent by each learning group on the write-
code tasks and quizzes using Welch’s 𝑡-test of significance [31].
While both groups spent similar time on write-code tasks, HoC-
ACE group spent double the time on quizzes than the HoC-Fill
group, and this difference was significant (𝑡= 11.9, 𝑝< 0.01).
Our analysis highlights that both groups performed similarly
in the learning phase suggesting that the type of quizzes did not
significantly impact overall performance during this phase. Inter-
estingly, based on the time analysis we can conclude that students
spent significantly longer time solving the quizzes in HoC-ACE
compared to the quizzes in HoC-Fill. Investigating this further,
we looked at the average attempts on the quizzes by both groups
and they were as follows: 1.88 for HoC-ACE and 1.32 for HoC-Fill.
Furthermore, the success rate on the quizzes within the first attempt
for the groups was as follows: 0.849 for HoC-ACE and 0.880 for
HoC-Fill, indicating the relative difficulty of the quizzes. How-
ever, in subsequent attempts, the success rate increased to 0.931 for
HoC-ACE and 0.917 for HoC-Fill.
Answer to RQ1: The experimental group and the control group
perform similarly in the learning phase on HoC.
5.2
RQ2: Performance in Post-learning Phase
To address RQ2, we assess the performance of the groups in the post-
learning phase on PostHoC. The distribution of scores on PostHoC
for each learning group is shown in Figure 6a. In Figure 6b, we

5.4
Limitations
Next, we discuss a few limitations of our study. First, we did not use
an explicit pre-test to measure pre-knowledge across the learning
groups. We could not reuse intricate write-code tasks as a pre-test
due to potential negative effects on motivation; moreover, we did
not use other quizzes in the pre-test as that might interfere with
evaluating our quiz-based curricula. In the future, one could reduce
discrepancies in pre-knowledge across groups by scaling up the
study and possibly doing randomization at the student level (in-
stead of class level). Second, the sequence of items in both groups
during learning was decided based on discussions with teachers
and a pilot study. In the future, one could conduct rigorous studies
to optimize the sequence. Finally, the 41 items for each group dur-
ing the learning phase were spread across two 45-minute lessons
over two weeks, complicating comparisons with the standard HoC
curriculum of 20 items that are typically covered in one 45-minute
lesson. In the future, it would be useful to design a quiz-based
curriculum that can be covered in one lesson (e.g., by selecting
fewer write-code tasks and quizzes), allowing for a more direct
comparison with a baseline curriculum with only write-code tasks.
6
Concluding Discussions
We compared two learning groups incorporating different types
of quizzes interleaved with write-code visual programming tasks
for K-8 learners. This comparison was conducted within the widely
used Hour of Code: Maze Challenge by code.org for elementary pro-
gramming. The experimental group had richer quizzes, including
code debugging and task design, while the control group had basic
solution-finding quizzes. Our findings show that the experimental
group significantly outperformed the control group in the post-
learning phase, despite performing similarly in the learning phase.
There are many interesting directions for future work. First, ad-
ditional think-aloud studies could be conducted to gain insights
into engagement patterns with different quiz types. This could
help explain the over-reliance on basic quizzes during the learn-
ing phase and its resulting negative transfer in the post-learning
phase. Second, it would be interesting to extend the investigation of
quiz-based exercises to more advanced programming contexts like
Python. Finally, it would be useful to develop an adaptive scaffold-
ing framework centered around these richer quizzes, where they
are presented to learners as and when they seek assistance.
