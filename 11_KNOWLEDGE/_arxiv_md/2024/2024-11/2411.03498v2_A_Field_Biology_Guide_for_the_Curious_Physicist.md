SUMMARY
22
Fieldwork is an essential component for an expanding umbrella of research on the physics of
23
living systems, where observing organisms in nature is a critical component of discovery. How-
24
ever, conducting field research can be a barrier for scientists, in particular physicists, who do not
25
have experience working with organisms under challenging field conditions. Here, we propose
26
seven critical steps for organizing and executing interdisciplinary, curiosity-driven field research.
27
Our recommendations are drawn from insights gained from the in situ Jungle Biomechanics
28
Lab, a field research course that helps early-career scientists from both physical and life sci-
29
ences gain experience in both organizing and conducting interdisciplinary field research in the
30
Amazon Rainforest. We emphasize a curiosity-driven approach towards the scientific inquiry of
31
living systems, which we believe is crucial for discovery while working with wild organisms under
32
unpredictable field conditions. We further provide guidance on teamwork when conducting field-
33
work, including creating an inclusive environment and advocating for codes of conduct and team
34
structures that aid in conflict resolution. Finally, we outline an in-situ approach to fieldwork that
35
requires engagement with the environment, scientific community, and local people where field
36
sites exist.

INTRODUCTION
41
Whether it is the superpropulsion of planthopper pee droplets1, information propagation in swarms
42
of fireflies2, or a caterpillar’s ability to detect predators’ electric fields3, many organisms are oper-
43
ating at the limits of what is physically possible. This has led to an increased interest in physical
44
scientists and engineers utilizing their training in physics, mechanics, and instrumentation to
45
help them understand the physics of living systems. However, those organisms are not always
46
amenable to being brought into a controlled laboratory environment, may only perform extreme
47
behaviors in the wild, and often exist in biodiversity hotspots in relatively remote places- often
48
requiring scientists to meet organisms where they live.
49
Studying organisms in the field presents an array of challenges that can initially be daunting
50
not just from a scientific perspective but also from logistical and conceptual standpoints. What
51
are the expected costs of fieldwork? What happens if I cannot find the organism that I set out
52
to study? What challenges does field work present in team leadership? How do I even find a
53
research question to begin with? What we aim to do here is provide a field guide for scientists
54
who have not had the opportunity to begin field research with wild living organisms in their
55
natural settings. This guide is woven together from lessons learned after running the Jungle
56
Biomechanics Lab, an NSF-IRES program that takes scientists from expertise ranging from fluid
57
mechanics to theoretical physics into the Peruvian Amazon. It draws on insights from scientists
58
who have been doing fieldwork with living organisms for more than a decade, but critically also
59
includes the perspective of physical scientists doing biological fieldwork for the first time. We
60
will approach field research from a curiosity-driven science perspective, which we believe is
61
amenable to the uncertainty that field research often presents. Finally, we will advocate for what
62
we call the in situ approach to fieldwork, an approach that we think of as antithetical to parachute
63
science and requires engagement at the level of the environment, the scientific community and
64
the greater community where field sites exist.
65
Finding a field site
66
A field site may be a literal field, a suburban backyard, or even the sewers of New York City4.
67
Anywhere organisms have adapted to survive, there is likely unexplored biophysics to discover.
68
However, the more challenging the environment, the more difficult it might be for a scientist to
69
gain access and make observations. Identifying the perfect place to begin studying a system
70
in the field requires a careful balance of acquiring funding and choosing field sites that have
71
the infrastructure that can support your science. We suggest starting with a budget. This can
72
determine whether or not you should begin your field research in your backyard or in the depths
73
of the Amazon rainforest, both of which can yield important scientific insights for organismal
74
biophysics.
75
With a budget in mind, it is time to decide on where to go. Finding a place to do field work
76
may be as simple as using Google Maps to look for freshwater ponds, in the case where one
77
is setting out to study dogfighting dragonflies. For questions that can only be answered with
78
organisms further from reach, biological field stations can be a critical resource for conducting
79
field-based research with living systems. Biological field stations offer infrastructure that might be
80
necessary for executing prolonged and technically challenging research. The resources at field
81
stations are often on how established or remote the station is, and the fees are often correlated
82
with the number of benefits that can be available. Some stations may have full wet-lab facilities
83
and dormitories, whereas others may have limited electrical access. There are thousands of
84
biological field stations globally, and databases exist to help locate a suitable research location
85
for conducting biological field research5.

87

88

89
ships specifically for international field research. Check with your institution’s financial aid office
90

91
Integrative and Comparative Biology (SICB), the Company of Biologists, and the Ecological So-
92

93

94

95

96

Finding Research Questions In Organismal Biophysics: A Curiosity-
99
Driven Perspective
100
The modern academic science workflow pressures scientists to publish and to publish quickly.
101
To a behavioral ecologist, identifying lines of scientific research can often feel similar to the
102
“explore/exploit” problems that animals face. Just like an organism needs to balance its time
103
between using the resources readily available (exploit) and looking for new resources for when
104
current supplies dry up (explore), scientists must also balance time between collecting data for
105
lines of questions that are producing publishable results and finding new lines of questions to
106
build a career on. For many career scientists, it can then be difficult to dedicate time purely
107
towards the “explore” process. Developing new lines of research in organismal biophysics, how-
108
ever, demands slowing down- and taking the time to simply observe.
109
An approach we advocate is to go into the “explore” phase with a curiosity-forward strategy.
110
A curiosity-driven approach emphasizes observation as the motive force behind discovery. Many
111
significant findings in organismal biophysics begin with simple observations of organisms-more
112
often than not by accident. To name just a few: squirrel acrobatics8, dog slurping9, spring-
113
tail aerodynamics10, cellular origami11, cat grooming12, insect excretion13, caterpillar electro-
114
sensation3, planthopper gears14, snapping shrimp15, firefly flashes2, elephant trunks16 and
115
leaping eels17. We see the curiosity-driven approach to ”exploration” in action in the Jungle
116
Biomechanics Lab. The JBL is a natural experiment that asks what happens when we bring
117
early career researchers from fields ranging from theoretical physics to microbial ecology into
118
an unfamiliar field site with high levels of biodiversity. The course is designed with structured
119
spontaneity at its core, giving early career researchers unfettered room to spend time solely
120
in an “explore” phase. What happens is that under these conditions, researchers stop, think,
121
and observe, and without exception, quickly find an unresolved research question: whether in
122
the flight control of cicadas who have lost their entire abdomen due to a Massaspora infection
123
or when ant lion larvae choose to build their sandy pits. A curiosity-driven approach requires
124
open-mindedness to be flexible and not take seemingly simple behaviors that are physical mar-
125
vels for granted. Curiosity, however, is something that is necessary to practice. As George M.
126
Whitesides18 aptly observed,
127
’Because following curiosity can seem effortless, it is easy to assume it does not
128
need to be learned, practiced, or encouraged, that it is not important, and that it will
129
somehow take care of itself. But, as with many activities that are competing for time
130
and attention in a utilitarian world, curiosity can atrophy from neglect. It can certainly
131
be unfocused, and lead to nothing (or at least nothing immediately useful), but using
132
3


## Page 4


it as the starting point for careful observation of nature and society is a nontrivial skill,
133
and a starting point for new intellectual endeavors and adventures. It is one essential
134
contributor to creativity in science, and a start in forcing new ideas into inflexible
135
professional orthodoxies.’

Preparing for field work: safety before science
137
Preparation for any fieldwork can vary vastly depending on where the field site is, the study
138
organisms, the experience level of your research team, and what equipment is needed to an-
139
swer any scientific questions. Preparing for fieldwork an hour’s drive away from your home or
140
university may require a different approach than organizing field research on another continent.
141
Therefore, it is difficult to give all-encompassing guidance on exactly how to prepare before em-
142
barking on your specific project. Instead, we offer a simple frame of mind for the early planning
143
stages that have provided scaffolding in our own preparation, which is to place safety and trans-
144
portation as first order priorities.
145
Safety and transportation logistics for field work should always begin with a detailed risk
146
management plan. Your risk management plan should include an outline of basic expectations
147
for safe conduct and operating procedures for an emergency. As a reference, we have included
148
one for the Jungle Biomechanics Lab as a supplement (S1). Critically, while preparing any safety
149
plan for field research, you should be cognizant that safety risks can vary among individuals from
150
different backgrounds and identities19.
151
In addition to a safety plan while in the field, a strong fieldwork plan lays out detailed logistics
152
regarding travel. Local field work may only need a carpool sign-up sheet to ensure equitable
153
access among participants. Organizing and leading field work farther away than a short drive is
154
significantly more complicated and can be more constrained by a budget. It is also imperative
155
that you understand the local laws regarding collection and conducting research wherever your
156
field site may be. Different countries may have specific laws about exporting samples if you
157
intend to return with preserved specimens. Although seemingly obvious at first, specific details
158
around travel logistics can get lost in early preparation steps, and we recommend having an
159
explicit travel plan that outlines how each participant will get to the field site, which elements
160
each person is responsible for, and a backup plan in case something should impede travel. A
161
strategy which builds a detailed logistical foundation will facilitate more technical and specialized
162
research. However, we would like to emphasize that many great discoveries in organismal bio-
163
physics required only a keen eye and no specialized equipment at all. Much of our own research
164
is implemented with phone cameras and some extra lighting. As a reference, we include example
165
gear lists for our own field work. Our mindset is that a successful round of field work is one that
166
gets all participants on site and home safely, and a tremendously successful field excursion is
167
one in which participants return energized with ideas, data, and often a new research direction.
168
Establishing a culture of mutual respect
169
Collecting data in 40° C and high humidity for multiple days in a row is hard. It can be even
170
harder if the internet connection is limited, you have forgotten a crucial piece of equipment for
171
your experiments, or you have not been able to find any of your species in the field. In the
172
moment, miscommunications occur, and interpersonal conflict is inevitable. Disagreements, of
173
course, are not signs of dysfunction, but how disagreements are handled is crucial for fostering
174
a creative research environment. A code of conduct, as well as a clear leadership structure,
175
proactively outlines expectations for participants well before the field work begins, provides a
176
4


## Page 5


clear throughline for conflict resolution, and helps tackle most interpersonal issues before they
177
arise.
178
A code of conduct is a document that explicitly states the rules and values of a team or orga-
179
nization and includes clear consequences for violating such rules. For bringing scientists into the
180
field for we recommend using the Field Code of Conduct developed by the Association of Polar
181
Early Career Scientists (APECS) to establish expectations among researchers20. The APECS
182
code of conduct has clear definitions for what constitutes harassment and a zero-tolerance policy
183
for harassment, as well as an internal leadership structure that provides both on-site and off-site
184
independent contacts to report and initiate actionable consequences for inappropriate behav-
185
ior. All participants should read and signed the code of conduct before beginning any fieldwork.
186
The values outlined in the document included but were not limited to, not putting oneself or oth-
187
ers in dangerous situations, respecting personal boundaries, and showing respect for the land,
188
its organisms, and the broader community. Incorporating principles of respect and inclusion is
189
imperative to empower the next generation of field scientists.
190
Collaborate Across Disciplines and especially with Naturalists
191
Field stations are crossroads for wide-ranging corners of the scientific community from all career
192
stages and disciplines. The same field site may simultaneously be a temporary home to in-
193
dustrial engineers, ornithologists, or disease ecologists across career stages ranging from high
194
school students to senior faculty levels. The confluence of expertise at field stations is an invalu-
195
able opportunity for unexpected collaboration. Oftentimes, executing complex field experiments
196
requires input from expertise outside of one’s own knowledge domain or simply a few more sets
197
of hands to perform experiments. A key consideration when planning field research will always
198
be to work with scientists from a broad range of expertise (for instance, a physicist interested in
199
the locomotion of tiny organisms may benefit from the expertise of a cell biologist who has spent
200
a career imaging organisms under a microscope). Researchers with expertise in mechatronics
201
may be indispensable for the ad hoc fabrication of a force transducer from spare strain gauges,
202
and a theoretical physicist may have a perspective for modeling the collective behavior of gregar-
203
ious caterpillars. One additional collaboration we especially implore for the physicist beginning
204
field biology is that with naturalists.
205
Naturalists are scientists who have expertise gained from years of careful observation and in-
206
sights of organisms in their natural habitats and will be crucial for planning the practical elements
207
of proposed field work, such as when/where can I even find my study organism? Naturalists have
208
a wealth of knowledge of organisms that is often difficult to extract from the literature alone. This
209
knowledge has proven indispensable time and time again as the starting point for the physics-
210
based inquiry of an organism’s behavior. For example, a naturalist might note that a family of
211
butterflies, the metal marks (Riodinidae), have a habit of landing upside down on the underside
212
of leaves. At first, a seemingly simple and innocuous observation. However, for an expert in flight
213
control, this can be understood as an inordinately complex task and has the potential to generate
214
an entire line of research questions to dissect the control mechanisms of upside down take off
215
and landing. The knowledge of naturalists is indispensable for identifying new lines of research
216
questions that are suitable for the quantitative and experimental skills of physicists and engi-
217
neers. Collaboration with naturalists should be a key component in any plans to do organismal
218
fieldwork, especially at an unfamiliar location.

The in situ approach to field biology is engagement on three
220
levels
221
In addition to finding field sites, organisms, and research questions, we especially want to em-
222
phasize what we describe as the in situ mindset for fieldwork. Here, we use the term in situ to
223
mean engaging both physically and mentally with the environment where field research takes
224
place. We intend for the in situ approach to be an antithesis to parachute science, a practice
225
where researchers from high-resource institutions drop in, extract samples, and leave without
226
acknowledging or contributing towards local knowledge21. To us, the in situ approach requires
227
presence and engagement at three interlocking levels: the environment, the scientific community,
228
and also the greater community where a field site exists.
229
In situ engagement with the environment signifies understanding field research through the
230
lens of an environmental identity. An environmental identity is something that each person has,
231
whether they know it or not, and is a term used to refer to how we see ourselves in relation to the
232
natural world22. For example, a scientist’s environmental identity may relate to the rainforest as a
233
biodiversity hotspot with a new parasitic wasp waiting to be discovered. For a subsistence farmer
234
in the same region, the relationship between the rainforest and the person may be more about a
235
day-to-day livelihood. Fieldwork can and should be understood in the social context of the envi-
236
ronments in which it occurs. In situ within the local scientific community reflects understanding,
237
that as scientists, we have a responsibility as mentors and colleagues. Scientists at all levels
238
from localities where fieldwork occurs are invaluable collaborators and wherever possible should
239
be included. For our own field research, this includes collaborations with scientists from local
240
universities, and especially including undergraduates as active participants in research projects.
241
Finally, in situ at the community level reflects a commitment to being active participants not just
242
in science but also in the local communities where the science takes place. Community involve-
243
ment is an opportunity, a responsibility, and a privilege when conducting field research. This may
244
be performing outreach events showcasing research to grade school children, inclusion of local
245
teachers and farmers or even establishing community scientific resources at field sites. No single
246
stratum of the three layers of an in situ approach exists alone, and each is an indispensable for
247
conducting field research.
248
Iterating Improvements from Quantitative and Qualitative In-
249
formation
250
Science is by nature an iterative process, and how we conduct and lead our scientific research
251
is not less subject to continuous fine-tuning. Both quantitative and qualitative data from field re-
252
searchers are crucial in improving and iterating upon any field work led by a lab. Each, however,
253
presents its own challenges to collect in a meaningful way. Taking an apocryphal queue from
254
Galileo, we think of this as having two distinct halves:“measuring what is measurable” and “mak-
255
ing measurable what is not so”. Getting data from participants before and after any complex field
256
research is the first step in measuring the measurable. One potential tool is to use anonymous
257
surveys. This way, you are more likely to have meaningful data that will allow you to improve any
258
element of your field research that did not work as intended and can be immediately addressed,
259
as an example we have included a survey we have used in the past as a supplement. Qualitative
260
data requires making measurable what is not so. Part of our philosophy was to give equal footing
261
to all field researchers, regardless of whether they were postdocs or undergraduates. Within a
262
culture of mutual respect, researchers, whether postdoc or undergraduate can freely express
263

and suggest improvements that will lead to the completion of a project. Both elements are cru-
264
cial for establishing a long-term succesful research program that includes challenging field work

components.

Concluding Remarks
267
In an interdisciplinary scientific community, organismal biology is enriched from the perspectives
268
of physicists and engineers whose expertise in quantification, instrumentation, and computa-
269
tional frameworks is primed to answer fundamental questions about how organisms work. What
270
we hope to do here is build a larger table for scientists from more disparate fields to turn their ef-
271
forts to biological systems which often requires us to do research where organisms have evolved
272
to thrive. Field research is an integral part of organismal biology research. As more disciplines
273
seek to draw inspiration from biological systems, it is critical that those fields engage the ecolog-
274
ical context of their biological study systems. Starting a field biology project de novo may seem
275
overwhelming to those who have not had the opportunity, but we argue that it is worth the trouble.
276
We see only opportunity in embracing more physical scientists into the naturalist traditions of the
277
past few centuries and envision only expanding opportunities in the physics of living systems.
