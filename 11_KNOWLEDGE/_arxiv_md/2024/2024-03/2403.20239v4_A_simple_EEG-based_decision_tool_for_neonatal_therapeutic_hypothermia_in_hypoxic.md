A B S T R A C T
Objective: Accurate identification of hypoxic-ischemic brain injury in the early neonatal period is essential for 
initiating therapeutic hypothermia (TH) within 6 hours of birth to optimize neurodevelopmental outcomes. We 
aimed to develop a simple decision-making tool for identifying term neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encepha­
lopathy (HIE) based on features of conventional electroencephalograms (EEG) recorded within 6 hours of birth.
Methods: EEG recordings from 100 full-term neonates with HIE were graded by pediatric neurologists for 
severity. Amplitude in slow frequency bands was analyzed, focusing on delta (0.5–4 Hz) spectral power. Tem­
poral fluctuations of delta power characterized each HIE grade, with joint level and duration probability den­
sities estimated for delta oscillation power. This study is registered on clinicaltrials.gouv (NCT05114070).
Results: These 2D EEG representations effectively distinguish mild HIE cases from those requiring hypothermia, 
achieving 98% accuracy, 99% sensitivity, 99% positive predictive value, 94% negative predictive value, an F1 
score of 99%, and a false alarm rate of only 6%. This system accurately discriminates mild from moderate or 
severe HIE, with only one mild case mistakenly identified as requiring hypothermia and one moderate case 
erroneously flagged for treatment.
Conclusions: Quantized probability densities of delta spectral features from early EEG (within 6 hours of birth) 
revealed significant differences between mild and moderate/severe HIE, enabling accurate discrimination of 
candidates for TH.
Significance: Simple, interpretable biomarkers from early EEG can provide an efficient visual clinical decision 
support tool to identify full-term neonates with HIE eligible for therapeutic hypothermia.
1. Introduction
Perinatal asphyxia, defined as severe disruption of uteroplacental gas 
exchange leading to metabolic acidosis, can cause hypoxic-ischemic 
encephalopathy (HIE), the most common severe neurological 
morbidity in newborns [1,2]. HIE, as described by Sarnat and Sarnat [3], 
is diagnosed using clinical (e.g., consciousness, reflexes), biological (e. 
g., pH, lactate), and EEG markers (e.g., background rhythms, critical 
discharges). Early therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is the standard neu­
roprotective treatment for neonatal HIE [4]. Administered within the 
first six hours of life, TH reduces the risk of death or disability in mod­
erate to severe HIE [5,6]. However, TH involves 72 hours of whole-body 
cooling in a neonate intensive care unit (NICU), often requiring deep 
sedation and assisted ventilation. Additionally, it can cause side effects, 
including heart rhythm and coagulation disorders, with potentially se­
vere consequences [7].
Identifying neonates eligible for therapeutic hypothermia (TH) re­
mains a clinical challenge, as rapid and accurate diagnosis is required 
within six hours of birth. Evidence clearly shows that the early analysis 
of EEG background provides useful information, beyond the clinical 
Sarnat score, to assess the presence and severity of encephalopathy [8]. 
While continuous EEG is a non-invasive bedside tool widely used to 
assess neonatal brain function, it requires specialized skills for recording 
and interpretation. Pediatric neurologists generally evaluate EEG fea­
tures such as amplitude, frequency, continuity, and sleep-wake cycling 
[9]. Specific patterns, including low-voltage background activity,

moderate hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy [15,16]. These constraints 
underscore the importance of automated methods for analyzing cEEG, 
which can more accurately characterize background patterns and track 
encephalopathy progression.

33]. Delta power can differentiate neonates with HIE who have signif­

This study proposes a straightforward quasi-real-time system utiliz­
ing conventional EEG analysis during the first six hours of life to detect 
brain injury and guide therapeutic hypothermia decisions in full-term 
neonates born with asphyxia. Our method, which leverages the com­
bined information of delta power levels and their durations, effectively 
captures general power fluctuations in slow EEG oscillations, enabling 
accurate identification of neonates requiring hypothermia. By inte­
grating temporal duration features with spectral power levels, this 
approach provides a statistically robust, interpretable, and practical 
decision-support tool for neonatologists in the NICU.
2. Methods
2.1. Dataset
This retrospective study included 104 neonates from a larger cohort 
of over 600 term neonates monitored between 2005 and 2020 at the 
NICU of Armand Trousseau Hospital (Paris, France). Fig. 1 illustrates our 
study population cohort. Inclusion criteria followed those of the French 
Society of Neonatology [35] and were: gestational age > 36 weeks, birth 
weight > 1800 g, postnatal age < 6 hours (H6), and HIE (mild, mod­
erate, or severe) with at least one of the following features: (a) birth 
asphyxia with clinical signs (e.g., fetal distress, umbilical cord prolapse), 
(b) Apgar score ≤5 at 5 min, or (c) pH < 7 and/or lactates > 11 mmol/l 
in cord blood or within the first hour of life. Only patients with an EEG 
recording obtained during the first 6 hours of life were included in the 
cohort.
Among the patients analysed, 41 (including mild, moderate, and 
some severe cases) were not under hypothermia treatment at the time of 
EEG recording. Unfortunately, the timing of sedation administration was 
not systematically documented in our retrospective dataset and could 
not be reliably retrieved for all patients. No patients had received anti­
epileptic medication. Details of patient exposure to the various sedation 
regimens are presented in Table 1. We notice however, that none of the 
patients were treated with ketamine or dexmedetomidine (Dexdor).
Parents of eligible patients were informed via a written document 
regarding anonymized data usage, and non-opposition consent was 
obtained. The study was registered with the French National Commis­
sion on Informatics and Liberty (CER-2021–023) and approved by the 
Sorbonne University Ethics Committee. It is also registered on clin­
icaltrials.gov (NCT05114070).
2.2. EEG recordings
EEG signals were recorded using Deltamed (2005–2016) and Nihon- 
Khoden (2016–2020) systems. Three patients’ EEG recordings were 
excluded due to technical format issues. Continuous video EEG was 
obtained within the first six hours of birth after perinatal asphyxia to 
monitor encephalopathy progression. EEG data were recorded with 
Deltamed (N = 27) and Nihon-Khoden (N = 77) systems, sampled at 256 
Hz and 500 Hz, respectively. High- and low-pass filters at 0.19 Hz and 70 
Hz were applied during recording, and electrode impedance was kept 
below 10 kΩ. Physiological signals (ECG, respiration, SpO2) were 
recorded for some patients but were not used in this study. Although 
scalp EEG electrodes were placed in accordance with the international 
10–20 system—using a reference electrode located at the midline be­
tween Fz and Cz—only the signals from electrodes Fp1, Fp2, T3, and T4 
demonstrated consistency across all participants. Consequently, only 
these channels were included in the final analysis. EEG data from 4

The patient groups were retrospectively defined according to clin­
ical, biological, and neurophysiological criteria. The severe group was 
identified based on a combination of clinical manifestations and rele­

In contrast, the moderate and mild groups were classified primarily 
according to the characteristics of the EEG background activity. Clinical 
evaluation using the Sarnat classification [3] remains an important tool, 
particularly when EEG is not available. A normal EEG provides an 
objective criterion for safely withholding therapeutic hypothermia. 
While our study was not designed to establish a detailed electro-clinical 
correlation, we consistently observed the following correspondences 
within our cohort: i) Sarnat grade I: normal EEG, no hypothermia, cor­
responding to mild HIE ; ii) Sarnat grade II: abnormal EEG, requiring 
hypothermia, corresponding to moderate HIE and iii) Sarnat grade III: 
markedly abnormal EEG, requiring hypothermia, corresponding to se­
vere HIE.
The study included 100 newborns, categorized into three HIE grades: 
17 with mild or minor encephalopathy (continuous background with 
mild voltage depression [30–50 µV]), 17 with moderate encephalopathy 
(discontinuous activity with IBIs <10 s), and 66 with severe encepha­
lopathy (IBIs of 10–60 s and severe background attenuation [<30 µV]).

2.3. EEG processing
Although originally developed for adult intensive care, aEEG is now 
widely used for continuous brain activity monitoring in NICUs. The 
signal is derived from EEG through asymmetric bandpass filtering (to 
reduce artefacts), rectification (to convert biphasic to monophasic 
waveforms), smoothing, and semi-logarithmic amplitude compression. 
Time compression then displays the maxima and minima of the trans­
formed signal in a time window, enabling continuous monitoring and 
bedside interpretation over long recordings.
In this study, we developed a general representation of conventional 
EEG background dynamics by quantizing EEG power levels and 
measuring the durations at each power level. Focusing on slow EEG 
oscillations in the delta (0.5–4 Hz) band, we also assessed the theta/ 
delta power ratio as a marker of normality in neonatal EEG. For each 
EEG segment, we quantified delta power and the theta/delta ratio, 
applying a logarithmic transformation (Log10), and quantizing values to 
the first decimal. This approach preserves small changes while pre­
venting overloading from large values. Pivot tables were created to 
capture changes in quantized power levels (or power ratios) across EEG 
segments, using the sequence of levels and durations. These tables were 
normalized to create a joint duration/level probability distribution. 
Gaussian smoothing was then applied to obtain 2D smooth probability 
densities.
The general scheme of our method is illustrated in Fig. 1. For all EEG 
recordings, bipolar channels Fp1-T3 and Fp2-T4 were used, and power 
spectral density was estimated for each 1-second segment using the 
Welch periodogram method.

3. Results
3.1. Results on the French dataset
Fig. 2 displays the delta power fluctuation densities for mild, mod­
erate, and severe HIE categories. These 2D representations, created for 
each patient’s EEG signals, reveal distinguishable shape differences 
across HIE grades that are easily recognizable. Interestingly, the plots in 
Fig. 2 indicate that different HIE grades can be distinguished by power 
variations occurring over short time durations, patterns that standard 
spectral EEG features fail to capture.
As an initial assessment of the discriminative ability of our method, 
we computed the intra- and inter-group distances between the distri­
butions of the three HIE categories (Table 2). In all cases, intra-group 
distances were consistently smaller than inter-group distances, con­
firming the robustness of the approach in separating HIE severity levels. 
The largest separation was observed between the mild and severe 
groups, while moderate cases occupied an intermediate position, as 
expected.
A cross-validation procedure proved our classification model con­
sistency. Indeed, the classification of EEG segments correctly matched 
the grade in 81 % of cases: 53 % severe, 16 % mild, and 12 % moderate. 
Misclassifications included one mild graded as moderate, four moderate 
as severe, and thirteen severe as moderate. Interestingly, no mild cases 
were classified as severe or vice versa. In differentiating infants with 
mild HIE from those requiring therapeutic hypothermia (moderate or 
severe HIE), the classifier achieved consistently high performance 
metrics: recall (sensitivity) 99 % [95 % CI: 96–100], precision 99 % [95 
% CI: 96–100], negative predictive value 94 % [95 % CI: 82–100], 
balanced accuracy 96 % [95 % CI: 90–100], false alarm ratio 6 % [95 % 
CI: 0–16], and F1-score 99 % [95 % CI: 97–100]. The narrow bootstrap- 
derived confidence intervals underscore the robustness and reliability of

Other spectral features can also serve as markers of normality in 
neonatal EEG. When compared with the delta power, the level-duration 
distribution of the theta/delta spectral ratio showed moderate perfor­
mance in discriminating between EEG segments of the mild HIE group 
and those requiring hypothermia. Performance metrics included sensi­
tivity of 88 % [95 % CI: 83–93], precision 95 % [95 % CI: 89–99], and an 
F1-score of 91 % [95 % CI: 87–95]. Balanced accuracy was 82 % [95 %

% CI: 86–94). The balanced accuracy was 81 % (95 % CI: 70–92), while 
the negative predictive value decreased to 54 % (95 % CI: 33–75), and 
the false alarm ratio increased slightly to 24 % (95 % CI: 6–43). These 
findings indicate high discriminative capacity, particularly in correctly 
identifying positive cases (including for shorter EEG recordings), but 
highlight the need for cautious interpretation of negative predictions in 
clinical practice.
3.1. Results from a public EEG-HIE dataset
To ensure clarity and reproducibility, we evaluated our method on a 
publicly available dataset from Cork University Maternity Hospital, 
Ireland [37]. This dataset includes over 100 multichannel EEG files 
recorded from 53 full-term newborns diagnosed with HIE. In the absence 
of pre-frontal electrodes, we analyzed signals from the frontal regions 
(F3 and F4). Each 1-hour EEG recording was graded for background 
abnormalities. Unlike our EEGs, recorded within 6 hours of birth, this 
dataset includes recordings up to 100 hours after birth. It is worthy 
noting that the Cork dataset uses a four-grade HIE classification: normal 
or mildly abnormal, moderately abnormal, severely abnormal, and 
inactive. Additionally, the version of the dataset used in this study (May 
2022 release of [37]) does not include labels indicating whether neo­
nates required therapeutic hypothermia.
Applied to this dataset, classification based on delta power resulted 
in a direct grade match for 77 % of EEGs. The remainder showed adja­
cent grade matching: 9.6 % of normal cases were graded as moderately 
abnormal, 1.9 % of moderately abnormal cases were misclassified as 
severely abnormal, and 0.95 % of severe cases were erroneously clas­
sified as inactive.
Although other statistical distances, such as Kullback-Leibler, Hel­
linger, or Bhattacharyya [36], could be used to compare probability 
distributions, our approach with the simple L2 norm generally out­
performs these alternatives. For example, using the Bhattacharyya dis­
tance only increased accuracy by less than 3 %.
4. Discussion
From a large cohort of full-term neonates with mild, moderate, and 
severe HIE, annotated clinical characteristics, and conventional EEGs 
(recorded before 6 hours), we demonstrated that spectral fluctuations in 
EEG background (summarized by delta power levels and their duration) 
can effectively discriminate between HIE categories. We validated our 
algorithms on an independent, publicly available EEG-HIE cohort [37], 
including normal term newborns and those with mild, moderate, and 
severe HIE. Additionally, we provided visual representations of HIE 
severity, easily interpretable by clinicians. Our approach offers a simple 
and efficient clinical decision support tool to identify full-term neonates 
with HIE eligible for therapeutic hypothermia.
Our results align with previous studies using quantitative EEG in full- 
term neonates and support the use of simple, automated EEG analysis as 
an accessible, generalizable method for identifying interpretable bio­
markers of brain injury in neonates with HIE. Notably, the 2D level- 
duration probability distributions for each EEG effectively capture 
characteristics of different HIE grades, easily recognizable by clinicians. 
For the mild grade, the distribution shape clearly distinguishes between 
awake and sleep states. The two peaks reflect high-voltage and low- 
voltage waveforms during quiet sleep, while the variability of awake 
EEG is captured in the central values. The rightward shift in the distri­
bution for moderate HIE indicates reduced EEG amplitude. Finally, the 
2D density for severe HIE distinctly shows discontinuous, low-amplitude 
EEG patterns compared to the mild group.
A previous study using features from quadratic time-frequency de­
compositions achieved a classification accuracy of 78 %, which could be 
improved by incorporating sleep state information [24]. Similar per­
formance (AUC ≈79 %) was reported by Kota et al [33] using delta 
power. Improved classification accuracy (83 %) was achieved by 
combining a large feature set with probabilistic models (Gaussian 
Mixture Models) and supervised machine learning (Support Vector 
Machines, SVM), followed by post-processing [28]. Similarly, Montazeri 
et al [29] reported classification accuracy over 90 % using a large 
feature set with different classifiers (SVMs and neural networks). More 
complex grading systems based on deep learning methods have achieved 
very good accuracy (>85 %) with various EEG features [23,38]. Our 
results show that even though the classifier based on the closest 
Euclidean distance to the reference distributions (obtained from the 
learning set) is one of the simplest and most common, its performance 
competes with more complex classifiers. It is important to acknowledge 
that accuracy metrics may be biased toward the majority class, partic­
ularly patients with unfavorable outcomes. To address this limitation, 
our study incorporates the F1-score and balanced accuracy, which are 
better suited to account for class imbalances and provide a more reliable 
assessment of model performance.
Our findings highlight the novelty and effectiveness of using a joint 
2D level-duration distributions to capture spectral fluctuations of delta 
power from EEG background, enabling reliable discrimination between 
mild and more severe HIE grades. This approach achieved an accuracy of 
98 %, with only 1 % of mild cases misclassified as requiring hypother­
mia. When tested on a public dataset with four HIE grades, the system 
maintained a robust overall classification accuracy of 77 %, with mini­
mal misclassification: only 0.19 % of moderately abnormal EEGs were 
misclassified as severely abnormal, and 2.8 % of severely abnormal or 
inactive EEGs were misidentified as normal or mild. This demonstrates 
our approach’s consistency, efficiency, and good performance across 
multiple datasets.
It is important to note that previous studies mentioned above were 
conducted on datasets with four HIE groups. However, our findings are 
consistent with previous classification results on datasets with three 
grades (mild, moderate, and severe). In Matic et al [26], the three HIE 
severity groups were correctly identified with an accuracy of 89 % using 
a complex system that integrated spatial EEG information for supervised 
classification. A recent study [39] reported achieving a classification 
accuracy of 80 % using a combined feature set optimized with an SVM 
classifier. In contrast, our approach offers a novel and efficient solution, 
achieving a direct matching of 81 % in our dataset and 77 % in the public 
EEG database, demonstrating superior simplicity and speed compared to

Our two-dimensional representation provides a comprehensive view 
of EEG background dynamics by jointly quantifying the duration of 
spectral power at different levels within the delta band. As shown in 
Fig. 3, neither high-power nor low-power oscillations alone reliably 
distinguish all HIE grades. In contrast, our method integrates power 
level and duration to accurately identify mild HIE cases and differentiate 
them from moderate and severe grades, which require neuroprotective 
therapy. Although the level–duration distribution can also be derived 
from the theta/delta spectral ratio, its performance in identifying the 
clinically relevant HIE group was moderate, with an accuracy of 86 %.
Unlike complex feature sets or classification algorithms, our system, 
which uses a single spectral feature and a simple classifier, achieves 
comparable classification performance. The clinical interpretability of 
EEG features is crucial for developing informative biomarkers of 
neurological injury in neonates with HIE. We believe that our 2D rep­
resentation of EEG offers neonatologists a traceable method for identi­
fying full-term neonates with HIE who may benefit from therapeutic 
hypothermia, without the “black box” limitations of more complex deep 
learning models [23,29] or undisclosed algorithms used in automated 
grading systems [40,41].
Due to the variety of medications and the relatively small group 
sizes, it is challenging to draw definitive conclusions regarding their 
effects on background EEG activity in our study. A comprehensive sta­
tistical assessment of the potential confounding effects of all adminis­
tered medications across all HIE severity grades was not possible to 
perform. Although sedative medication could influence EEG background 
activity, infants across moderate and mild HIE groups were exposed to 
similar regimens of sedation and/or analgesia. Therefore, medication is 
unlikely to explain the observed differences between these two cases.
While promising, our study has limitations. First, the HIE severity 
was retrospectively assessed in a dataset of limited sample size. Future 
studies with larger, prospectively graded patient cohorts are needed to 
validate our approach. Our cohort consisted predominantly of infants 
with severe HIE, with only a small proportion of mild cases. This dis­
tribution, inherent to the retrospective nature of the study, may limit the 
generalizability of our findings to more representative clinical pop­
ulations and could lead to overestimation of some performance metrics. 
Second, given the heterogeneity of electrodes location among patients in 
the French dataset, we cannot generalize the model to other derivations. 
Third, EEG grade can evolve during long-term monitoring due to the 
dynamic nature of HIE. A clinical decision tool should incorporate this 
variability to create a more flexible automated grading system [23].
Although EEG provides an objective assessment of encephalopathy 
severity, the absence of complementary neurological examination data 
(e.g. Sarnat or Thompson score) may limit direct comparison with 
clinical grading systems used in most centers. Finally, our results were 
based on preselected data with EEG epochs free of major artifacts. 
Incorporating advanced artifact detection methods [42,43] could 
improve the robustness and informativeness of the decision support 
system. To expand the clinical utility of our findings, future work should 
evaluate our method on larger datasets without preselecting clean EEG 
segments.
5. Conclusion and future work
Accurately identifying neonates with HIE who require therapeutic 
hypothermia is crucial for optimizing outcomes. The proposed approach 
explicitly separates the amplitude and temporal characteristics of EEG 
oscillations, generating intuitive “fingerprints” of HIE severity with 
distinct and recognizable patterns (e.g., bimodality in mild HIE, right­
ward shift in moderate HIE, and low-amplitude discontinuity in severe 
HIE). These two-dimensional representations are intended as an adjunct 
to support interpretation rather than as a stand-alone clinical score. 
Nevertheless, future work could derive additional automated or 
simplified quantitative indices from these visualizations to further 
facilitate bedside application.
Given the variability in electrode placements across different NICUs, 
a single universal discriminative model is not feasible. Our proposed 
approach utilizes data from pre-frontal regions (or frontal electrodes in 
the Cork dataset). However, HIE discrimination can be enhanced by 
combining information from multiple EEG channels [24,26,38], or by 
developing more complex models that account for the spatio-temporal 
organization of EEGs [44,45]. Despite this, such systems have limited 
clinical applicability as neonatal EEG recordings in NICUs typically 
involve only a few channels, many of which may require rejection due to 
artifacts. More sophisticated and accurate decision support systems 
could be achieved by integrating clinical markers [46], data from 
functional near-infrared spectroscopy [47], or other physiological sig­
nals like heart rate variability [48] to further enhance clinical 
decision-making [49].
While larger patient cohorts are needed to fully assess the limits of 
our approach, our results indicate that the proposed method offers a 
robust, simple, interpretable and automated tool for assessing brain 
injury in neonates with HIE. The spectral power fluctuations in other 
frequency bands could also aid in detecting sleep-wake transitions or 
trac´e alternant activity during quiet sleep in neonates. Our system could 
enable clinical and nursing staff to make objective, data-driven decisions 
about whether to initiate hypothermia treatment based on the presence
