Abstract
Forests are crucial for ecological balance, but wildfires are a major cause of forest loss, posing
significant risks. Fire weather indices, which assess wildfire risk and predict resource demands,
are
vital.
Semantic sensor networks, gaining popularity in fields like healthcare and
environmental monitoring, are increasingly gathering climatic data like wind speed, temperature,
and humidity through these networks. However, processing these data streams to determine fire
weather indices presents challenges, underscoring the growing importance of effective forest fire
detection. This paper discusses using Apache Spark for early forest fire detection, enhancing fire
risk
prediction
with
meteorological
and
geographical data. Building on our previous
development of Semantic Sensor Network (SSN) ontologies and Semantic Web Rules Language
(SWRL) for managing forest fires in Monesterial Natural Park, we expanded SWRL to improve
a Decision Support System (DSS) using a Large Language Models (LLMs) and Spark
framework. We implemented real-time alerts with Spark streaming, tailored to various fire
scenarios, and validated our approach using ontology metrics, query-based evaluations, LLMs
score precision, F1 score, and recall measures.
Keywords:- LLMs, Spark, SSN, DSS, Forest Fire.
1. Introduction
Forest fires constitute a crucial worldwide undertaking1, posing giant threats to ecological
integrity and monetary balance. Every year, these fires consume tens of thousands of hectares of
forests globally, resulting in the loss of vital habitats, a reduction in biodiversity, and the
discharge of substantial amounts of carbon emissions into the atmosphere, exacerbating the
effects of climate change. While herbal phenomena like lightning can provoke forest fires in
particular situations, the major causes are human activities, consisting of agricultural burning,
land clearing, and accidental ignition.
India is particularly vulnerable to forest fires2 due to a variety of factors, including significant
human encroachment near forested regions, unsustainable land-use practices, and inadequate fire
management strategies. Research indicates that a significant number of fires in India originate

The escalating influences of climate change exacerbate the problem by extending the period and

Most forest management companies rely closely on fireplace threat score systems to assess

Fire Danger Index [2], rely on weather and ecological data from regions with unique climatic and
ecological characteristics. As a result, these systems might not reflect the unique and diverse
conditions universal in Indian forests.
Spark is a unified analytics engine that efficiently performs several operations on big data. It
can easily integrate with LLMs to create an efficient real-time DSS. This integration is beneficial
when working with vast amounts of data to interpret them and make automated reasoning more
accurate [3]. Working with Spark and LLMs enables us to make accurate decisions based on
prespecified rules and environments for real-time operations. LLMs can perform automated
reasoning on Spark-processed data and integrate the capability to improve the logic and accuracy
of rule-based queries, particularly for complex decision-making tasks.
In order to establish a unified data model within the information space, ontologies streamline
several critical aspects. (1) They give detailed information on a wide range of data sources and
infrastructure services, including insights specific to the domain; (2) They create a common
language that makes it easier for the infrastructure to interact with outside sources that use
different methods; and (3) They make it easier to find data, access it, and combine it. These
ontologies cater to a wide range of knowledge representation requirements: (1) The SSN
ontology specifically models sensor networks and captures data concerning distinct features of
interest. In this framework, DOLCE+DnS UltraLite serves as the upper ontology; (2) it
showcases the resources provided by the infrastructure and the accessible datasets.

1. Real-time data processing with Spark enables handling large volumes of sensor data to support

2. Literature review
In this section, we will discuss the related works done in recent years and identify the
research gaps that need further exploration using the underlying methodology shown in table 2.
Recent advancements in forest fire detection have seen a variety of innovative approaches
leveraging deep learning, IoT, and wireless technologies.
Abdusalomov et al. [4] created an automated method using an improved Detectron2
platform. This method was able to find fires with an amazing 99.3% accuracy in a variety of
conditions, both during the day and at night. This method utilized a custom dataset and
significantly improved fire detection accuracy.
Burak et al. [5] suggested a hierarchical forest fire detection system that uses a mix of
multimedia and scalar sensors in IoT systems. This system was 98.28% accurate in detecting
fires and used 29.94% less energy.
Avazov et al. [6] introduced a fire detection method tailored for smart cities, leveraging
an enhanced YOLOv4-based convolutional neural network capable of triggering an alarm within
eight seconds of a fire outbreak with high speed and accuracy.

The literature review emphasizes the necessity of an effective model that integrates Spark
streaming, LLMs, and ontology-based methods. This integrated approach aims to lower costs
and achieve greater accuracy than current systems such as satellite-based, optical camera-based,
and human-based forest fire observation systems. While various ontology-based decision support
systems for forest fires exist, such as the BACAREX ontology, Geo-ontology, and FWI ontology,
they primarily concentrate on observation, operational tracking, and FWI calculation. None of
the reviewed studies address an integrated approach that merges LLMs and the Spark framework
within a single ontology.
This research aims to create a framework within the SSN ontology that allows for the ability to
search observation systems using SPARQL and improves interactivity for both computer
programs and human users. By improving the understanding and application of forest fire and
surveillance data, this all-encompassing framework seeks to increase the information's
comprehensiveness. It uses Spark streaming, with the help of LLMs, to strengthen the framework
and enable quick and efficient decision-making. The structure also illustrates the key components
for controlling and monitoring forest fires.

3.2. Dataset description
The dataset encompasses a detailed array of spatial, temporal, and meteorological data crucial
for analyzing and predicting forest fire behavior in Montesinho Park3, Portugal. It has exact
coordinates in space to find high-risk areas, information about time that shows how fires happen
at different times of the year and week, and weather variables like temperature, humidity, wind
speed, and rainfall that directly affect how fires spread. Furthermore, the dataset features a target
variable, the burned area, which quantifies the extent of forest damage, providing valuable
insights into fire severity. This variable, prone to skewness towards smaller values, is
recommended to be transformed logarithmically for more accurate modeling. Overall, this
dataset is instrumental for devising effective forest fire management and intervention strategies.

3.2.1 Data Preprocessing
The data gathering and setup process starts with data streaming using PySpark and importing
necessary libraries, followed by creating a stream context to convert real-time data into a
continuous data frame stream for analysis. To clean up the data, we transform the skewed "area"
target variable logarithmically to normalize its distribution, enabling its use in more models.
Z-score normalization equalizes numerical features like FFMC, DMC, DC, ISI, temperature, RH,
wind, and rain, making sure that there is no mean and no variance. This makes the model more
stable and speeds up convergence. One-hot encoding turns categorical variables like "month" and
"day" into binary columns, which makes it easier to use them in models that need numerical
inputs.


## Page 11


To eliminate low-correlated or redundant features, we conduct feature selection through
correlation analysis, thereby improving the model's efficiency. Statistical methods like z-scores
or the interquartile range find and get rid of outliers by figuring out how they affect the model's
performance and making changes as needed. For the "area" variable's unbalanced data,
techniques like oversampling and undersampling even out the class distribution, make it easier
for the model to find patterns.
3.2.2 RDF Conversion
RDF, developed in partnership with the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), standardizes the
description of online resources and facilitates seamless data transmission and integration. RDF
makes clear connections between data elements by expressing them as subject-predicate-object
triples. This lets you manage data in a flexible and structured way. One of RDF's key strengths is
its ability to separate data from its structure, allowing for the integration of diverse data sources
under a unified schema. In this research, we employ Python to transform a CSV dataset into RDF
format, thereby facilitating data querying and linking across various sources. The CSV format,
while simple to work with for structured data, lacks the semantic richness needed for more
complex data modeling. Figure 3 shows the conversion into RDF data from CSV sources.
By converting the CSV data to RDF, we can enrich the dataset with metadata, making it
more interoperable and accessible for linked data applications. Depending on the use case, we
can serialize the RDF output from this conversion process in multiple formats like Turtle, XML,
or JSON-LD.

3.3 Ontology Development
For the development of the forest ontology, we utilized the upper-level SSN ontology, which
has a standardized forest ontology. The SSN ontology was developed by the Semantic Sensor
Networks Incubator Group of the W3C. In our previous research, we based our ontology
development on documents related to Canadian forest fires. However, in this current study, we
have refined the forest ontology by incorporating documentation from other countries, like India,
to increase its accuracy and to encompass a broader scope of knowledge regarding forest
vegetation.
We have enhanced the ontology by incorporating a diverse array of policies, plans, and forest
fire prevention rules, together with coordination strategies across agencies, as detailed in forest
documentation from various countries. This improvement broadens the ontology's scope and
applicability to forest fire management. Additionally, we have integrated numerous SWRL rules
into the forest ontology, drawing on documentation from different nations to ensure a more
robust and versatile framework.
3.3.1 SWRL Rules Development
The SWRL4 merges the OWL with the Rule Markup Language, offering a high-level abstract
structure for rules similar to Horn clauses. This model's rules are formulated using OWL
concepts such as classes, properties, and individuals. In our previous work, we incorporated 42
rules; however, in this current study, we have expanded the number to 110. Additionally, we have
developed more complex rules based on our existing findings, details of which are presented in
the following sections. These rule-based transformations are applied to streaming data.
Previous work established rules that depend on specific columns such as FFM, DMC, DC,
ISI, rain, and wind. Various classes for each column value (e.g., extremely easy, difficult, fast)
have been categorized using predefined thresholds. In this extended version, we enhance the
work by covering all different scenarios, such as human resource management in emergencies
and determining which type of firefighting equipment is needed at which time. This makes the
model concerning forest management more complete. Some rules are shown in Tables 3, 4, and
5. We have accessed several documents5 to develop these rules6.

3.4 Spark Streaming
Spark streaming is the core of the Apache Spark API, which provides scalable solutions
for high throughput and fault tolerance for processing live data streams. It allows both real-time
processing and batch processing to maintain a low-latency approach for various applications. It
allows replication of data across various nodes, which makes Spark streaming more robust for
handling data in case of failure. Being part of the Apache Spark platform, Spark Streaming
integrates seamlessly with other Spark components, such as Spark SQL and MLlib (for machine
learning). This integration enables complex workflows that include streaming, batch processing,
and interactive queries. Figure 4 shows Spark Streaming processing data in batches.
Figure 4: Stream processing through spark framework.

3.4.2 Implement an alert system that utilizes LLMs for processing queries based on Spark
alerts
The research employs advanced techniques to manage and interpret forest fire data,
subsequently transforming it into a refined text format. A thorough preprocessing phase initiates
the process, cleansing the data of discrepancies and noise to ensure reliability and quality.
Advanced text normalization methods normalize the clean data to achieve uniformity across the
dataset, preparing it for further analytical processes.
We converted the normalized text into document embeddings using the state-of-the-art, all-Mini
LM-L6-v2 model. These embeddings are critical because they capture the text's deep semantic
meanings, essential for accurately retrieving information based on the content's relevance and
similarity. The embedding process preserves and makes meaningfully retrievable the useless and
critical information within the text. We build an indexing system using FAISS (Facebook AI
Similarity Search) to retrieve these semantically rich document embeddings, providing a robust
framework for indexing and quickly retrieving the documents. This indexing is critical for
supporting the rapid access required in the querying phase, enhancing the retrieval process's
efficiency.
The question-answering setup is the core of the proposed system, which integrates the
Intel/dynamic_tinybert model within a retrieval-based framework. For each posed query, this
setup precisely configures a retriever to identify and pull the two most relevant documents from
the index. As a result, a retrieval QA model uses these documents to generate accurate answers.
A conversation buffer memory further refines this model by maintaining context over successive
interactions, thereby ensuring the system's responses are consistent and relevant.
Additionally, the system's real-time capabilities allow it to handle immediate queries about
environmental conditions, sensor readings, or alarm activations, delivering prompt and accurate
responses. Integration with Apache Spark enhances this capability by distributing processing
tasks across multiple nodes, ensuring that the system can scale effectively to handle large
datasets and simultaneous queries without a loss in performance.

4.2 LLMs based evaluation
To measure the performance of LLMs, we first extract relevant context from the FAISS database
based on the input query. The retrieved context is ranked using cosine similarity, which measures
the relevance of the context to the query. Cosine similarity values range from -1 to +1, where
values closer to +1 indicate higher relevance. After retrieving the context, it is fed into the LLMs,
which generate a response based on this information. We then evaluate the LLM's output by
calculating Precision, Recall, and F-measure, comparing the generated response against a
predefined test response. Precision indicates the proportion of relevant information in the response,
Recall measures how much relevant information from the context was captured, and the F-measure
provides a balance between Precision and Recall. High values for these metrics suggest that the
LLM's response is both accurate and contextually relevant, validating its performance.

Conclusion and Future Work
This paper utilizes ontology, LLMs, and Spark streaming to detect forest fires in the forest fire
management system. Previous research has primarily focused on ontology and SWRL rule-based
forest fire management, which require significant human intervention. To get around this
problem, this study uses Spark streaming to process data in real time and create rules-based
alerts. Additionally, it utilizes LLMs and ontology to generate safety recommendations and
determine appropriate actions. This integration minimizes human intervention and enables
instant action orders from forest officers to ground action teams. To evaluate the performance of
the model, we assessed the ontology based on metrics such as schema, query response time,
LLMs response score, and cosine similarity. Various scores demonstrate that the developed
ontology encompasses a broader range of forest management knowledge compared to previous
versions. This comprehensive model yields more accurate results. However, a limitation of this
approach is the need for knowledge amendments when deploying the model in different
locations. It's essential to include area-specific knowledge for the model to adapt to local
conditions.
For future work, we aim to enhance the model's efficiency and reliability by incorporating
additional rules and integrating machine learning techniques. This ongoing development will
further optimize the system's performance and adaptability to varying forest fire scenarios.
