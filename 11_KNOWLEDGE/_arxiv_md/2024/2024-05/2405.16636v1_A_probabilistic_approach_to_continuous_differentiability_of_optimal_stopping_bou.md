A PROBABILISTIC APPROACH TO CONTINUOUS DIFFERENTIABILITY
OF OPTIMAL STOPPING BOUNDARIES
TIZIANO DE ANGELIS AND DAMIEN LAMBERTON
Abstract. We obtain the ﬁrst probabilistic proof of continuous diﬀerentiability of time-dependent
optimal boundaries in optimal stopping problems. The underlying stochastic dynamics is a one-
dimensional, time-inhomogeneous diﬀusion. The gain function is also time-inhomogeneous and not
necessarily smooth. Moreover, we include state-dependent discount rate and the time-horizon can
be either ﬁnite or inﬁnite. Our arguments of proof are of a local nature that allows us to obtain the
result under more general conditions than those used in the PDE literature. As a byproduct of our
main result we also obtain the ﬁrst probabilistic proof of the link between the value function of an
optimal stopping problem and the solution of the Stefan problem.
1. Introduction
In this paper we obtain the ﬁrst probabilistic proof of continuous diﬀerentiability of free bound-
aries in time-inhomogenous optimal stopping problems (OSPs). More precisely, when the underly-
ing process is a one-dimensional diﬀusion X := (Xt)t≥0 and the free boundary is a function of time
t 7→b(t), we show under mild conditions that ˙b = db
dt is well-deﬁned and continuous (except per-
haps at the maturity of the OSP). Time dependent boundaries may arise in our setting because of
ﬁnite-time horizon and/or time-dependence in both the gain function and the discount rate and/or
time-inhomogeneous dynamics for X. Thanks to the local nature of our arguments of proof, we are
able to obtain continuous diﬀerentiability of t 7→b(t) in broad generality, going beyond analogous
results from PDE theory. The PDE results typically require smooth coeﬃcients for the second order
diﬀerential operator associated to the underlying diﬀusion X, along with smoothness of the stopping
payoﬀand/or other ad-hoc structural conditions on these ingredients (see, e.g., Friedman [13] or
Kinderlehrer and Stampacchia [21, Chapter VIII]).
Our methodology is new in the literature and it is based on a combination of Lamperti transform,
Girsanov theorem and, crucially, an application of Pitman’s theorem for the law of the three-
dimensional Bessel process (see, e.g., Revuz and Yor [30, Thm. III.3.5]). The key technical step in
our analysis is a delicate Taylor-like expansion for the time derivative ˙v of the value function v of
the OSP, near the optimal boundary b. Such expansion yields existence of the mixed derivative ˙vx
at points of the boundary (i.e., existence of ˙vx(t, b(t))), along with a probabilistic representation
thereof. Then we are able to express the time-derivative of b(t) as ˙b(t) = −η(t) ˙ux(t, b(t)), where η
is explicit and continuous, u = v −g and g is the stopping payoﬀin the OSP. Finally, using the
probabilistic representation of ˙vx(t, b(t)) we are able to prove continuity of the mapping t 7→˙b(t).
We emphasise that a study of properties of the mixed derivative ˙vx near the stopping boundary
is a notoriously diﬃcult task.
In the PDE literature ˙vx is studied using Gevrey’s lemma [16],
which oﬀers no probabilistic analogue, whereas in the probabilistic literature no previous results on
˙vx(t, b(t)) are known.

proof of the link between the value function of an OSP and the solution of the Stefan problem

1.1. A brief review of optimal stopping and free boundary problems. The development
of optimal stopping theory started in the 50’s with work by Wald and Wolfowitz [39] and by Snell
[35], and it was mainly motivated by statistical questions around sequential testing and quickest
detection (see, Shiryaev’s recent book [34] for an overview of the ﬁeld and historical remarks). Many
authors contributed to a systematic development of optimal stopping theory both in Markovian
and non-Markovian framework. Foundational results can be found in the monographs by Shiryaev
[33] and El Karoui [11] and a more modern account of the theory is contained in the book by
Peskir and Shiryaev [29] and references therein. The probabilistic study of free boundary problems
(FBPs) motivated by optimal stopping theory has a long tradition that can be traced back to early
contributions by, e.g., McKean [26], Kotlow [22] and Van Moerbeke [38]. An interesting historical
account of the initial connections between optimal stopping and analytical methods for FBPs can
be found in the review by Van Moerbeke [36].
The popularity of optimal stopping rapidly increased in the 90’s when applications in mathemat-
ical ﬁnance became one of the main drivers of the theory. A central application, which stimulated
an enormous amount of research at the interface of probability and mathematical analysis, is the
celebrated American option pricing problem (detailed expositions and historical remarks can be
found for example in the books by Karatzas and Shreve [20] and Lamberton and Lapeyre [23]).
Nowadays, optimal stopping is a very lively branch of stochastic control theory and of applied prob-
ability, which expands in several directions (including, e.g., stochastic games, mean-ﬁeld games,
time-inconsistent problems) and ﬁnds a broad range of applications.
The regularity of the free boundary b that splits the state-space into the so-called continuation
and stopping sets is a central theoretical question in the context of optimal stopping. In the PDE
literature this issue has been addressed extensively, often via links to the Stefan problem, and
numerous results concerning inﬁnite diﬀerentiability of the free boundary have been known for a
long time (see, e.g., Cannon and Hill [4], Friedman [13] and Schaeﬀer [31]; earlier work from the
60’s is also accounted for in L.-S. Jiang [18]). However, those results ﬁnd limited use in modern
optimal stopping because of assumptions that are often too restrictive for applications.
Somewhat surprisingly, and in part as a testament to the diﬃculty of the problem, results in the
probabilistic literature are still extremely scarce. In the special case when the underlying process X
is a Brownian motion, it is possible to use properties of the Gaussian transition density to deduce
continuity and higher regularity of b (see, e.g., Chen and Chadam [5]) following similar ideas as those
in the PDE literature (cf., the monographs by Cannon [3] and Friedman [13]). Those methods do
not easily extend beyond the Gaussian framework. In more general situations probabilistic methods
have only been used to prove continuity and, in some cases, Lipschitz continuity of the boundary
(cf. De Angelis [6], De Angelis and Stabile [9] and Peskir [28] and references therein). Our work
advances this strand of the literature and it bridges the gap between the probabilistic and PDE
realms.
Part of our contribution concerns the link between optimal stopping and Stefan problems. Thus
we now elaborate a little on this aspect of the paper in the broader context of the existing literature.
The link between Stefan problems and obstacle problems was initially highlighted by Duvaut [10]
(cf. also Schatz [32]). A detailed proof entirely based on PDE methods can be found in [21, Chapter
VIII]. The line of argument typically goes as follows: (i) it is postulated that a solution pair (w, ϕ)

to rigorously obtain the link with the Stefan problem. In this paper we ﬁll this gap and bring the
probabilistic approach on par with the analytical one.
1.2. Structure of the paper. In Section 2 we formulate the problem and we state our main
result (Theorem 2.4) concerning continuous diﬀerentiability of the optimal boundary under the
assumption of its local Lipschitz continuity. Section 2.1 illustrates simple suﬃcient conditions that
imply local Lipschitz continuity of the optimal boundary. Finally, Section 2.2 provides an outline
of our strategy for the proof of Theorem 2.4. Section 3 introduces the main ingredients for our line
of argument (Lamperti transform, Girsanov theorem and Pitman’s theorem) along with numerous
preliminary estimates on the three-dimensional Bessel process and on some related stopping times.
In Section 4 we perform our main technical steps and obtain a Taylor-like expansion of ˙v near the
optimal boundary b. That establishes existence of the mixed derivative ˙vx(t, b(t)) (Theorem 4.1).
In Section 5 we formally prove Theorem 2.4 leveraging on the result of Theorem 4.1. Finally, in
Section 6 we establish the link between optimal stopping and Stefan problem.
