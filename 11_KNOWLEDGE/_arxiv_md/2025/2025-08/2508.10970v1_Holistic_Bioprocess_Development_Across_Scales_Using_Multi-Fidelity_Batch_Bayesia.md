ABSTRACT
Bioprocesses are central to modern biotechnology, enabling sustainable production in pharmaceuti-
cals, specialty chemicals, cosmetics, and food. However, developing high-performing processes is
costly and complex, requiring iterative, multi-scale experimentation from microtiter plates to pilot
reactors. Conventional Design of Experiments (DoE) approaches often struggle to address process
scale-up and the joint optimization of reaction conditions and biocatalyst selection.
We propose a multi-fidelity batch Bayesian optimization framework to accelerate bioprocess de-
velopment and reduce experimental costs. The method integrates Gaussian Processes tailored for
multi-fidelity modeling and mixed-variable optimization, guiding experiment selection across scales
and biocatalysts. A custom simulation of a Chinese Hamster Ovary bioprocess, capturing non-linear
and coupled scale-up dynamics, is used for benchmarking against multiple simulated industrial
DoE baselines. Multiple case studies show how the proposed workflow can achieve a reduction in
experimental costs and increased yield.
This work provides a data-efficient strategy for bioprocess optimization and highlights future oppor-
tunities in transfer learning and uncertainty-aware design for sustainable biotechnology.

Introduction
Bioprocesses are increasingly used for sustainable manufacturing across various industries, including fine chemicals,
food enzymes, and pharmaceuticals [1, 2]. In addition to replacing established chemical synthesis, bioprocesses provide
opportunities for the development of novel pathways and specialty molecules that would be challenging or inefficient
to achieve using conventional chemical reactions or other unit operations [3]. Together with their potential to replace
oil-based production routes, these attributes thus give bioprocesses an important role in the drive towards a circular
bioeconomy in the 21st century.
However, the development of bioprocesses remains a complex and costly undertaking. Like many processes in the
chemical industry, bioprocesses require a large number of experiments during their development phase, often carried
out over multiple stages and scales [4, 5]. The primary goal of bioprocess development is to efficiently determine the
most suitable biocatalysts and the optimal operating conditions for a production process, as well as to successfully
transfer them to pilot-scale systems [6]. This development pipeline is typically divided into several phases: early- and
late-stage screening, optimization, validation, and scale-up.
Typically, early screening stages are used to reduce the number of potential biocatalyst candidates, i. e., the number of
strains or cell clones that were previously generated in cell line engineering [7]. At this stage, experiments are mostly
conducted in microtiter plates (MTPs), where operating conditions are either fixed to platform conditions or cannot be
controlled at all [8]. This stage entails an adequate trade-off between the exploration of suitable candidates and the
operating costs, but comes at the expense of limited process control and information gain about scaled-up behavior [4]
(see Figure 1, left).

Overall, this traditional development pipeline follows a funnel-like approach. As the pipeline progresses, the number of
candidate clones and parallel experiments decreases, whereas the level of information per experiment (i. e., the number
of available measurements and insights into production behavior) increases. Although the number of controllable
parameters grows with scale, the experimental costs increase simultaneously [4, 7]. Each stage is used to narrow
down the search space for critical variables [6]. However, the influence of these parameters, e. g., the biocatalyst,
temperature, pH, formulation, or feeding regime, is often highly non-linear, system-specific, and difficult to quantify
due to measurement uncertainty and biological variability [11, 12]. In addition, small-scale experiments are generally
more homogeneous and thus do not fully account for the heterogeneities present at larger scales. These challenges result
in a large, high-dimensional parameter space with sparse and noisy data, and complicate the transfer of information
between scales.
To date, classical Design of Experiments (DoE) methods are most commonly employed to structure experimental
campaigns. Although these methods are designed to efficiently cover the search space and extract meaningful
information for different purposes, most assume very limited interaction between the design parameters [13]. For that
reason, most DoE methods are only useful for their specific use case, with rare cases of more advanced applications in
optimization problems. However, bioprocesses are highly complex and typically characterized by various non-linear
relationships and parameter interdependencies, thus requiring advanced process models and intricate experimental
designs for sufficient characterization and transfer of knowledge between scales [14].
Addressing these challenges requires optimization strategies that are sample-efficient, robust to uncertainty, and
propagate information across scales. Traditional heuristic approaches or DoE techniques, such as the dominant methods
of Box-Behnken, Definitive Screening or Full Factorial Designs for Response Surface Models [13], are limited in
their ability to discover optimal parameter settings under such constraints [13]. Bayesian Optimization (BO) offers a
compelling alternative as a data-efficient, model-based approach. BO uses probabilistic surrogate models and acquisition
functions to balance exploration and exploitation in the search space [15]. Its ability to incorporate uncertainty and
adaptively guide experimentation has made it a valuable tool in domains with expensive and noisy evaluations, including
hyperparameter tuning for machine learning models [16], design of control systems [17], and chemical engineering
applications such as reactor design [18] and experiment planning [19]. Most recently, BO also gained traction in
bioprocess engineering [20]. Within this field, advanced BO techniques such as batch and multi-fidelity allow to model
uncertainty and optimize across different scales in a holistic process, rather than limiting the propagation of information
to a sequential process as currently done in industry.
This paper introduces systematic benchmarks of multi-fidelity batch BO, specifically adapted to bioprocess development.
First, several case studies inspired by real-world industrial conditions are introduced, which are used to compare the

Base Model
The core of the bioprocess model is a system of ordinary differential equations with parameters empirically identified

meaningful optimization and process control.

Clone Modeling
To simulate clone diversity, 30 clones are generated by randomly perturbing model parameters oriented around base
values taken from [23, 24] (see Appendix A.1). This number is chosen to provide sufficient behavioral variability in
the process model while keeping the computational cost for surrogate models within a feasible range. The variation
introduced mimics differences in productivity, robustness, and growth characteristics typically observed in real clone
libraries. Section 3 further investigates how the underlying distribution of clone performance affects the optimization
outcomes.

2.2
Bayesian Optimization for Bioprocess Development
2.2.1
Bayesian Optimization
Optimizing black-box objective functions using only noisy zeroth-order information can be challenging. For this
purpose, many Data-Driven Optimization (DDO) methods have been developed, which effectively tackle the problem
5


## Page 6


through approaches such as surrogate models or numerical derivative approximations [27]. However, most methods
require numerous evaluations of the objective function or rely on additional knowledge about the problem. As a result,
in scenarios involving expensive evaluations, computational cost becomes the primary bottleneck, and sample efficiency
is critical.
BO is an optimization framework that enables sample-efficient optimization of objective functions that are costly to
evaluate. A central idea in BO is the use of a probabilistic surrogate model that expresses a belief about the objective
function. This belief is formalized through a prior distribution over functions, which is updated as observations are
collected, resulting in a posterior distribution. The posterior captures the updated belief about the objective function’s
behavior after accounting for the observed data. The posterior predictive distribution, which describes the predictive
uncertainty of the surrogate model in the search space, plays a central role in decision making. It is used by BO’s
acquisition function to trade off between exploration (sampling where uncertainty is high) and exploitation (sampling
where expected performance is high). An acquisition function in this context is a heuristic or decision rule that guides
the optimization process. The next evaluation point is selected as the maximizer of the acquisition function, and the
objective function is then evaluated at that location. This cycle of belief updating and point selection is repeated until a
resource limit (typically a maximum number of iterations or total experimental cost) is reached. Otherwise, if sufficient
information about the optimum is gained, early termination is possible. Further fundamental information on BO can be
found in [28, 15].
The BO framework, often known in the form of the Efficient Global Optimization algorithm [29], has undergone
numerous extensions. These include advances in acquisition functions, evaluation strategies, and surrogate models
[30, 31]. Choosing the right model type is a critical aspect for successful optimization.
2.2.2
Gaussian Processes
Among the available options, Gaussian Processes (GPs) are the most commonly used surrogate models in BO due to their
flexibility, interpretability, and ability to provide uncertainty estimates alongside predictions [32]. As non-parametric
regression models, GPs are particularly effective in scenarios with limited data, where capturing predictive uncertainty
is essential. For the following formal introduction a notation similar to the one of Rasmussen et al. is used [32].
A GP defines a distribution over functions:
f(x) ∼GP(m(x), k(x, x′)),
where m(x) : Rd →R with x ∈Rd is the mean function and k(x, x′) : Rd × Rd →R is the covariance (or
kernel) function. This implies that for any finite set of input points {x1, . . . , xn}, the corresponding outputs f =
{f(x1), . . . , f(xn)} follow a multivariate normal distribution:
f ∼N(m, K),
with mi = m(xi) and Kij = k(xi, xj). The kernel function k(x, x′) encodes the similarity between inputs and thus
governs the smoothness and generalization behavior of the GP. Most commonly, the kernel depends on the Euclidean
distance between input points. However, there is a variety of kernel functions and kernel design can be seen as a
discipline of its own [33, 34]. Ultimately, a GP must be tailored to the specific characteristics of the problem it is
modeling to perform effectively.
2.2.3
Multi-Fidelity
In addition to modeling the influence of key input parameters, it is equally important to account for the varying quality
and resolution of the available data. Multi-fidelity modeling captures these inherent differences in scale, evaluation cost,
precision, and information gain that arise in real-world experimental and simulation scenarios. It can be seen as a form
of transfer learning, where information acquired from one task or domain is transferred to enhance learning in a related
task [35]. This concept is particularly applicable to experiments conducted at different physical or conceptual scales,
where simplified or idealized setups are used to infer properties of the target system.
To collect data economically, low-cost experiments or simulations are often employed, which typically involve
idealizations or miniaturization of the actual target system. These low-fidelity sources may be fast or cheap to evaluate,
but yield only approximate information about the objective function. Contrary, high-fidelity sources are more costly but
offer more accurate and reliable information.
There are several strategies for incorporating information from multiple fidelity levels into a unified model [36]. These
include methods such as multi-task models [37, 38], hierarchical Kriging [39], acquisition-function-based fidelity
selection [40], and fidelity modeling as an explicit input variable. The focus of this work is on the latter: the fidelity
6


## Page 7


level is modeled as an additional input dimension in the GP, thereby embedding fidelity into the structure of the search
space.
While in some applications fidelity levels can be treated as continuous variables, such as the amount of data for
cross-validation of neural network performance or the number of training iterations [41], experimental fidelity levels
based on reactor scales are discrete. These discrete fidelity levels can be ordered based on their expected information
quality regarding the objective function, and a qualitative measure of their similarity can also be assumed. Under these
assumptions, discrete fidelity levels can be mapped onto a continuous fidelity axis. Conversely, a continuous fidelity
spectrum could be discretized into well-defined fidelity levels. The relative spacing between levels reflects the similarity
in system behavior rather than the absolute fidelity quality. Therefore, the actual numeric values assigned to fidelity
levels serve to encode a learnable structure via the GP kernel’s lengthscales.

2.2.4
Batch Evaluation
Next to different scales of experimentation, another aspect in bioprocess development is the parallelization of exper-
iments. Batch BO refers to the selection of multiple points for evaluation at the same time instead of a sequential
selection of only one point at a time. In the context of bioprocess optimization, this makes experimentation more
efficient and leverages the technologies and methods available for batched and high-throughput experimentation [5, 42].
However, this efficiency gain comes at the cost of more complex batch selection. This is because, unlike in the sequential
case, candidate points within a batch are not conditionally independent: the predictions made by the GP model for
each point are correlated due to the shared posterior covariance structure. As a result, evaluating one point in the batch
affects the expected utility of evaluating others. These inter-dependencies mean that most useful acquisition functions
cannot select each point in a batch independently.
Usually the batch size is given by q, which means that iterative evaluations of batches of size q = 1 is the sequential case.
Since for the acquisition function this means finding a one-step optimal point, most of the time analytical expressions
for calculating acquisition function values are available. Unfortunately, for the general case of batch size q > 1 the
7


## Page 8


calculations can often only be approximated with more advanced sampling or penalization methods and depends on the
specific requirements of each acquisition function.
Fantasizing, also known as the Kriging Believer or Constant Liar approach [43], is a common strategy for optimizing
multi-point acquisition functions. It enables efficient batch selection by approximating the joint acquisition process
through a sequential heuristic. Initially, the acquisition function is optimized to select a single point (i. e., the sequential
q = 1 case). The GP model is then temporarily conditioned on a "fantasized" observation at that point (typically based
on a sample from the posterior). Using this updated model, a second point is selected, and the process is repeated
until a full batch of q points is generated. To improve robustness, this procedure is performed over multiple fantasy
samples, and the acquisition values are averaged. While this greedy approximation avoids the computational burden of
evaluating the joint acquisition over all batch points, its performance can degrade with increasing batch size due to the
accumulation of approximation error.
Another method used for optimizing multi-point acquisition functions in this work is Monte Carlo sampling. In this
approach, multiple function samples are drawn from the joint posterior distribution of the surrogate model, and the
acquisition function value of the batch is approximated by averaging over these samples [44].
2.2.5
Categorical Variables
The modeling approaches presented in this section are again inspired by principles of transfer learning. In particular,
different cell clones are treated as distinct but related tasks, which can be modeled using multi-task GP kernels [37].
Alternatively, a simple learned embedding can be used, which allows for calculating euclidean distances again [45].
A commonly used kernel for multi task GPs is the Intrinsic Coregionalization Model (ICM) kernel [37]. The ICM
kernel learns a low-rank representation of the correlations between tasks by combining a shared base kernel with a
learned task covariance structure. In this context, the base kernel encodes the similarity in the input space, while the
coregionalization matrix captures how strongly the behavior of one clone relates to another.
Mathematically, the ICM kernel is defined as:
k((x, t), (x′, t′)) = kbase(x, x′) · Bt,t′
Here, x, x′ denote input parameter vectors (e. g., pH, temperature, feed rate), t, t′ denote the categorical task indices (in
this case, the cell clone type), kbase is a standard kernel such as the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, and B is the
positive semi-definite coregionalization matrix that is learned from the data during training.
This multiplicative combination of a base kernel with a clone-specific covariance structure allows the model to capture
the global input-dependent behavior as well as clone-specific deviations from it. This lets the GP transfer information
across clones while accounting for their individual characteristics, effectively leveraging similarities between them to
improve data efficiency and prediction accuracy during optimization.
The embedding approach does not directly learn a covariance structure, but instead learns positions in an n-dimensional
space. The distances between these positions are then used to compute a covariance-like factor. Each categorical
variable (e. g., cell clone) is represented as a one-hot vector, which are mapped to a continuous embedding via a
learned matrix. This setup allows convenient computation of squared distances, which are then transformed using a
squared exponential-like kernel. The overall kernel function combines this categorical similarity with the base kernel
for continuous input features:

2.3
Acquisition functions
Acquisition functions define the decision rule by which candidate points are selected for evaluation in each iteration of
BO. These functions leverage both the predictive mean and the covariance (uncertainty) provided by the GP model to
quantify the potential benefit of sampling at different locations in the search space. Typically, the next evaluation point
8


## Page 9


is chosen by maximizing the acquisition function, thereby balancing the trade-off between exploring uncertain regions
(exploration) and exploiting areas with high predicted performance (exploitation).
The following section provides a brief overview of batch Expected Improvement [46], batch Upper Confidence
Bound [47] and their multi-fidelity extensions, as well as General-purpose Information-Based Bayesian Optimization
(GIBBON) [48]. These are the acquisition functions employed in this work. For detailed mathematical derivations and
theoretical foundations, the reader is referred to the respective literature.

2.4.1
Individual Treatment of Batches from Different Fidelity Levels
To reduce the overall complexity of the optimization while still leveraging BoTorch’s routines, the acquisition function
optimization was decomposed by fidelity level. That is, for each iteration, a separate batch is optimized for each fidelity
level under its respective constraints. These fidelity-specific batches then compete based on their acquisition scores, and
the best is selected for evaluation. In this scenario fixed batch sizes for each fidelity level were assumed, as set-ups with
standardized sizes are commonly used in laboratories (e. g., 48 or 96 wellplates on the MTP scale). This comparison
simplification can be justified through considering complete batches that were selected based on the same starting
conditions in each iteration. This means that the batch improvement measure of each respective acquisition function
can be compared even when the batch has different sizes. While the comparison between fidelity levels is no issue, the
selection large batches faces computational burdens.
2.4.2
Microtiter Plates: Dealing with Larger Batch Size and Additional Constraints
The lowest fidelity level (MTPs), presents particular challenges due to its larger batch sizes and additional constraints.
Standard BoTorch methods perform poorly in optimizing this level, since joint optimization of a large cell clone
selection, as well as batch-wide process parameters such as temperature and pH (making it a mixed-integer non-linear
program), proves computationally infeasible for standard methods. To overcome this and reduce complexity, a grid
search over temperature-pH combinations is conducted. The resulting batches are evaluated for the acquisition function
and its values are then used to rank the produced batches for every temperature-pH combination, avoiding repeated,
expensive acquisition evaluations with other DDO methods. Furthermore, enforcing the special constraints of the MTP
scale (see Section 3.1) turns out to be simpler. This is because of the custom implemented grid search method instead
of using the limited constraint formulation capabilities of BoTorch.
However, even after this simplification, the clone selection at fixed temperature-pH combinations yielded suboptimal
diversity. Especially at low-fidelity levels, where exploration is important, this is unfavorable. The optimizer failed
to find acquisition function values that are non-zero for many iterations, which leads to unfavorable point selection.
To address this, Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) was employed as a DoE strategy for clone selection. Instead of
optimizing to select a diverse batch of cell clones for evaluation, a set of clones is sampled with LHS and evaluated
combined with the temperature-pH combinations. This also ensures that low-fidelity evaluations remained competitive
with the purely DoE-based industrial baselines. An outline for the grid-search approach is reflected in Algorithm 2.

2.5
The Comparison Scenario
In order to evaluate the applicability and usefulness of the presented BO workflows, they need to be compared with
the already mentioned industrial status quo (see Section 1. Therefore a benchmark scenario is designed that tries to
mimic the funnel-like DoE-based approach explained in Section 1. Due to the reduced number of clones in this study
compared to reality (30 instead of 100+), the funnel-based approach needs to be scaled-down to be a good benchmark.
A well working mode of operation is found for four iterations on the MTP scale, eleven iterations on the MBR scale
and five iterations on the pilot-plant scale, making it 20 experimentation iterations.
Starting with exploratory clone sampling at fixed conditions (T, pH) per batch, the best clone from the MTP experiments
is chosen to go onto the next stage. Here, diverse operating conditions and feeding regimes across the entire search
space are tested. From that stage the best performing point is selected and evaluated within ±5% deviation in the
operating conditions to reflect local exploitation on the highest fidelity level. This funnel-like approach is implemented
for Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), Factorial Design and Sobol Sequence sampling. Each method selects reaction
conditions and cell-clones accordingly.
For the random sampling methods (LHS and Sobol), candidate points are generated uniformly within the search space
of the respective fidelity level. The total number of generated candidates per stage corresponds to the total number of
batched experiments planned at that stage. The candidates are then distributed to batches with sizes corresponding to
the respective scale and iteratively evaluated. In case of the factorial method, a 3-level full factorial design is generated
for each stage. Since not all points can be evaluated due to batch constraints, the generated points are randomly shuffled
and grouped into batches according to the batch size for that stage, until the total number of batches required is reached.

3.2
Results of the Clone Representation Case Study
In industrial practice, process models developed alongside wet-lab experimentation rarely include detailed representa-
tions of clone characteristics. In this study, we evaluated two alternatives to the commonly used one-hot encoding: ICM
[37] and entity embedding [45], both described in Section 2.2.5. The aim was to determine which approach is most
effective for the transfer learning task. The ICM method uses a kernel that directly learns covariances between clones.
In contrast, the entity embedding method maps clones into a Euclidean space, where distances between points reflect
similarities in clone behavior. From this embedding, covariance-like quantities can be derived. In our application, these
relationships were inferred solely from experimental data, without prior knowledge, although additional information
such as genetic data could be incorporated to improve the model. More complex information structures have already
been used as features in optimization problems [56, 57].
Figure 5 presents the results for varying the clone representation across the acquisition functions introduced in
Section 2.3. The comparison reveals that certain acquisition functions perform better overall and exhibit greater
sensitivity to the choice of clone representation. For qLogEI (blue curves in Figure 5), performance is largely unaffected
by the representation, with only a slight tendency toward better results using the ICM kernel. Similarly, there are no
larger changes for qUCB, but the two-dimensional entity embedding turns out to be better than the three-dimensional
one. However, using a three-dimensional entity embedding with the GIBBON acquisition function (green curves)
increases the average termination value by 36.5% relative to the two-dimensional embedding and by 53.4% relative to
the ICM kernel.

3.4
Overarching Recommendations and Future Directions
Based on these findings, specific recommendations can be provided regarding which BO methods perform well under
different conditions. This can help choosing the right configuration and potentially fine-tune the workflow based on
prior knowledge of clone distributions or scale correlations.
GIBBON, particularly when combined with the 3D entity embedding, appears to function effectively in the analyzed
scenarios. Therefore, if the primary objective is to achieve superior objective function values, this combination of
acquisition function and clone representation is highly recommended. If the goal is to reduce costs significantly while
still achieving robust results, qUCB emerges as a strong choice, irrespective of the underlying clone performance
distribution. While qLogEI also demonstrates the ability to match DoE performance at lower costs for distribution B,
its exclusive reliance on evaluating only MTP renders it impractical for meaningful scale-up applications. Nevertheless,

applying BO methods in this experimental setting clearly improves upon the industrial status quo and can be highly
recommended.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically compare multi-fidelity batch BO with the industrial
approach to bioprocess development for CHO cells. Using a simulation study, we demonstrated that parallel process
scale-up and optimization are feasible and can improve upon the current industrial practice. Moreover, the results
show that the cost of experimentation can be reduced substantially, further highlighting the importance and potential of
multi-fidelity batch BO in biotechnology.
In the future, several small adjustments to the bioprocess simulation could be made to increase its realism. One
consideration is that the bioprocess model used here may be more challenging to optimize than its real-world counterpart,
as the MTP scale was modeled to be relatively uninformative. This modeling choice likely results in lower correlations
than those typically observed between real MTPs and pilot scales (see, for example, the effective MTP data generation
and its utilization in [58]). Adjusting the scale parameters could make the problem more realistic by ensuring more
similar behavior across scales. In reality, this would reflect well-initialized experiments (e. g., with tighter controls or
more precise measurements) which can improve the transfer learning capability of multi-fidelity and multi-task GPs,
supporting better optimization.
Furthermore, it remains a pertinent question whether the current modeling of fidelity levels is sufficient for the
underlying GP to accurately capture the differing behaviors across scales. An alternative modeling approach, such as
employing a multi-task kernel (similar to the one used for clone modeling), could be explored. However, depending on
the specific method, this might introduce additional hyperparameters, making the learning process more challenging. In
contrast, the method presented here remains relatively simple, relying on only a single learned parameter, which is the
lengthscale within the automatic relevance detection of the used RBF kernel.
To further enhance the efficacy and precision of batch selection, particularly given the mixed-variable and highly
non-linear characteristics of the acquisition function landscape, the development of a more sophisticated optimization
algorithm for this set-up could be considered. Such an algorithm could especially improve the diversity of large batches
on the lowest fidelity, consequently leading to superior optimization results.
Beyond improved decision making, integrating more flexible GPs, such as those using the Linear Model of Coregional-
ization kernel [59], may further improve the learning of complex clone behavior. This could lead to a better model fit
and more informed decision making through the acquisition function. With better behavioral modeling, the number of
clones considered in the workflow could be scaled up. However, this would necessitate the use of sparse GP techniques
to manage the computational load associated with larger datasets, especially in real-world scenarios involving hundreds
of clones.
4
Conclusions
Bioprocesses hold immense potential for the production of pharmaceuticals and specialty chemicals. Accelerating their
development while minimizing experimental costs is essential for enabling scalable and efficient manufacturing of new
biological products. This work explored the capabilities of multi-fidelity batch Bayesian Optimization (BO) workflows
to streamline bioprocess development through simulation-based evaluations.
A BO framework was introduced, incorporating batch selection and fidelity modeling, and evaluated using a custom-
designed bioprocess simulator. This simulator captured key challenges of real-world processes, including non-linear
dynamics, scale-dependent variability, and diverse clone performance distributions. It provided a very challenging
environment for systematically comparing optimization strategies.
The study revealed that compared to standard industrial strategies, the proposed BO framework demonstrated advanta-
geous performance in scenarios where clone performance differences were large and the clone representation model
was chosen carefully (see Section 3). In particular, the GIBBON acquisition function showed strong performance under
these conditions. In scenarios with less diverse clone performance (see Figure 6), simpler exploration-heavy DoE
approaches also yielded good results. However, a cost reduction of up to 93% was achieved with the BO approach
using the qLogEI acquisition function.
This work contributes to the growing body of research exploring machine learning-driven approaches for bioprocess
optimization. While potentials remain, especially in terms of scaling and real-world applicability, the outlook for
automated, data-efficient bioprocess development is promising. Such methods are becoming increasingly important
for rapidly designing high-performing and sustainable bioprocesses that can improve lives through advances in
biopharmaceuticals, specialized molecule production, and more affordable value chemicals.

While the current framework targets the optimization of single processes, there is also the question of how to potentially
use individual components of it. An intriguing path for exploration could be the reuse of surrogate models and
optimization trajectories from one bioprocess to inform another. This kind of cross-process transfer learning could
accelerate future development while simultaneously reducing experimental effort and costs. Essentially, the challenge
would be to capture the fundamental characteristics between scale-ups and optimization runs for entire bioprocesses.
To search for the availability of such characteristics and further validate the framework, testing the full pipeline on
real-world bioprocess development data would be invaluable.
