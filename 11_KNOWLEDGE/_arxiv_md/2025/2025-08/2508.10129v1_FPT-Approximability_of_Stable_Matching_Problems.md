FPT-Approximability of Stable Matching Problems
Jiehua Chen1, Sanjukta Roy2, and Sofia Simola1
1TU Wien, Austria
2Indian Statistical Institute, India
Abstract
We study parameterized approximability of three optimization problems related to stable matching:
(1) Min-BP-SMI: Given a stable marriage instance and a number k, find a size-at-least-k matching that
minimizes the number β of blocking pairs; (2) Min-BP-SRI: Given a stable roommates instance, find
a matching that minimizes the number β of blocking pairs; (3) Max-SMTI: Given a stable marriage
instance with preferences containing ties, find a maximum-size stable matching.
The first two problems are known to be NP-hard to approximate to any constant factor and W[1]-
hard with respect to β, making the existence of an EPTAS or FPT-algorithms unlikely. We show that
they are W[1]-hard with respect to β to approximate to any function of β.
This means that unless
FPT=W[1], there is no FPT-algorithm with respect to β that can decide whether a given instance has
a matching with at most β or at least F(β) blocking pairs for every function F depending only on β.
The last problem (Max-SMTI) is known to be NP-hard to approximate to factor- 29
33 and W[1]-hard with
respect to the number of ties. We complement this and present an FPT-approximation scheme for the
parameter “number of agents with ties”.

Introduction
Stable matchings play a fundamental role in various applications ranging from job markets (e.g., the National
Resident Matching Program), over education (e.g., school choice or college admission), to health (e.g., kidney
exchange programs). The general idea is to match the agents into pairs so no two agents can form a blocking
pair. A blocking pair is a pair of two agents that are not matched with each other but prefer each other over
the status under the matching.
In the classical Stable Marriage (SM) problem introduced by Gale and Shapley [11], we are given
two disjoint sets of agents, each having a complete and strict preference list (aka. ranking) over the agents of
the other set, and we aim to match every agent from one set to an agent of the other set so that no blocking
pairs are induced. Gale and Shapley show that every instance of the SM problem admits a stable matching
and provide a linear-time algorithm to find one.
SM has many relevant extensions. First, the preference lists of the agents may be incomplete, allowing
them to express unacceptability towards other agents. This results in the setting of Stable Marriage
with Incomplete Preferences (SMI). A stable matching still always exists for each SMI instance,
albeit not every agent is matched; the same set of agents are matched in all stable matchings though. A
key challenge in SMI is the trade-off between the size and stability: Requiring more agents to be matched
will induce blocking pairs, destroying stability [26, Rural Hospital Theorem]. This motivates the problem
of Minimum Blocking Pairs SMI which aims to find a matching of size at least a given number k such
that the number of blocking pairs is minimized. Prior work has considered both exact and approximation
algorithms [3, 14, 12], showing that the problem remains computationally challenging. For instance, Min-
BP-SMI is already NP-hard when the preference lists are of length at most three [3]. Moreover, it is NP-hard
to approximate to some constant factor [3, 14].
Another extension of SM is to have just one set of agents, allowing each agent to rank any other
agent. In this case, we obtain the setting of Stable Roommates (SR). Unlike in SM and SMI, stable
matchings are not guaranteed to exist. Irving [17] designs a linear-time algorithm to decide whether a given

instance of SR admits a stable matching. To deal with the non-existence of stable matching, Bir´o et al.
[4] introduce the problem of finding a matching that minimizes the number of blocking pairs, called Min-
BP-SRI. Unfortunately, Min-BP-SRI is W[1]-hard with respect to the number β of blocking pairs [6] and
NP-hard to approximate [4].
A third variant of SM is to allow agents to have both incomplete and indifferent preferences and between
agents. In this case, a stable matching1 still exists, but different stable matchings may have different sizes. A
commonly studied goal for incomplete preferences with ties is to find a stable matching with maximum size.
This gives rise to the setting of Maximum-Size Stable Marriage with Incomplete preferences and
Ties (Max-SMTI). Max-SMTI is NP-hard to approximate [22] and W[1]-hard with respect to the number
of ties [24].
The above inapproximability and parameterized intractability results beg the study of approximation
algorithms combined with fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) algorithms. Given the inherent computational
hardness of Min-BP-SMI, Min-BP-SRI, and Max-SMTI, parameterized complexity provides a natural
framework to explore efficient algorithms when certain problem parameters are small.
In this work, we
investigate FPT-approximability for both minimizing the number of blocking pairs and maximizing the size
of stable matchings in presence of ties.
1.1
Our Contributions
We show that Min-BP-SMI and Min-BP-SRI remain resistant to FPT-approximability. More precisely,
both problems are W[1]-hard with respect to β to approximate to a factor F(β)·nO(1), where n is the number
of agents, β is the number of blocking pairs, F is an arbitrary computable function. In other words, it is
unlikely to obtain an FPT algorithm with respect to β that can distinguish between instances that have at
most β or at least F(β) blocking pairs. This hardness result for Min-BP-SMI holds even when we require
the matching to be perfect, i.e., every agent has to be matched. On the other hand, when preferences can be
incomplete and may have ties, we provide a simple FPT-approximation scheme for Max-SMTI with respect
to the number τ of agents with ties, i.e., an approximation scheme that given an instance I of SMTI, where
τ agents have preferences containing ties, and an approximation ratio ε, outputs in f(ε, τ) · |I|O(1) time a
stable matching with size at least ε · OPT(I), where OPT(I) denotes the size of a largest stable matching in
I and f is a computable function depending only on ε and τ.

Preliminaries
Given a non-negative integer t, we use [t] to denote the set {1, . . . , t}.
Preference profiles and stable matchings.
Let V be a set of n agents with V = {v1, . . . , vn}. Each
agent vi ∈V has a subset Vi ⊆V \ {i} that it finds acceptable and has a preference list ⪰i on Vi which is
a weak order over Vi. We assume the acceptability to be mutualm, i.e., an agent x is in the preference list
of another agent y if and only y is also in the preference list of x. For two acceptable agents x, y ∈Vi of vi,
we say that vi weakly prefers x over y if x ⪰i y. If not also y ⪰i x, we write x ≻y and say that vi strictly
prefers (or simply prefers) x over y. If x ⪰i y and y ⪰x, we write x ∼i y, and say that vi is indifferent
between x and y, and has ties in his preference list.
A preference profile P = (V, (⪰i)i∈V ) is a collection of the agents and their preference lists. Associated
to each preference profile P there is an underlying acceptability graph G, which has V as its vertex set, and
an edge between each pair of agents who find each other acceptable. We say that P is a marriage instance
if the corresponding acceptability graph is bipartite, otherwise it is a roommates instance. Further, P is
complete if the acceptability graph is complete.
We say that P contains ties if there is an agent vi ∈V and two other agents x, y ∈Vi such that x ∼i y.
If P has no ties, then we say that it is strict.
For example, assume we have four agents {v1, v2, v3, v4}. Let agent v1’s preference list be v2 ≻1 v3 ∼1 v4.
This means that agent v1 prefers v2 to both v3 and v4, but is indifferent between v3 and v4.
The rank of an agent x in the preference list of another agent vi denotes the number of agents that vi
strictly prefers over x: rankP
i (x) := |{y ∈Vi | y ≻i x}|. We omit the superscript P if it is clear from the
context.
Given a preference profile P = (V, (⪰i)i∈V ), a matching M ⊆E(G) is a subset of disjoint edges in G.
Given an pair {vi, vj} ∈M, we say that vj is the partner of vi, denoted M(vi), and we say that vi and

vj are matched. If there is no pair e ∈M such that vi ∈e, then we say that vi is unmatched, denoted
as M(vi) = ⊥. We assume that every agent vi prefers to be matched with an acceptable agent over being
unmatched ⊥.
Given a matching M of P, a pair {vi, vj} ∈E(G) of agents is a blocking pair of and blocks M if
{vi, vj} /∈M and the following two conditions hold:
(i) vi is unmatched or prefers vj to M(vi), and
(ii) vj is unmatched or prefers vi to M(vj).
We call a matching M stable if there is no blocking pair; otherwise we call it unstable. If every agent is
matched, then we call the matching perfect.
Problem definitions.
We are ready to formally define the computational problems studied in this paper.
Minimum Blocking Pairs SMI (in short Min-BP-SMI)
Input: A marriage instance P = (U ∪W, (⪰i)i∈U∪W ) with strict preferences and a non-negative inte-
ger k.
Task: Find a matching M of size |M| ≥k with minimum number of blocking pairs.
Minimum Blocking Pairs SRI (in short Min-BP-SRI)
Input: A roommates instance P = (V, (⪰i)i∈V ) with strict preferences.
Task: Find a matching with minimum number of blocking pairs.
The standard parameterized decision variants of Min-BP-SMI and Min-BP-SRI, called Min-BP-SMI-
dec and Min-BP-SRI-dec, additionally have as input a number β, and asks whether there exists a solution
with at most β blocking pairs.
Now, we turn to preferences with ties.
Maximum Size-SMTI (in shortMax-SMTI)
Input: A marriage instance P = (U ∪W, (⪰i)i∈U∪W ) with incomplete preferences possibly containing
ties.
Task: Find a stable matching M with maximum size.
The parameterized decision variant of Max-SMTI, called Max-SMTI-dec, has an additional parame-
ter k in the input and asks whether there exists a stable matching of size at least k.
For more details on algorithmic complexity of stable matching problems, we refer to the textbook by
Manlove [21].
Fixed-parameter approximability.
We assume basic knowledge of parameterized complexity and refer
the textbook by Cygan et al. [8] for more details.
The definitions for fixed-parameter approximability are based on the definitions by Marx [23].
We define an optimization problem as a 4-tuple (Π, sol, cost, goal), where
– Π is a set of instances,
– sol is a function that assigns to each instance I a set consisting of all feasible solutions, i.e., for each I ∈Π,
sol(I) is a collection of feasible solutions,
– cost is a function that assigns to each instance I and each feasible solution x ∈sol(I) an integer that
describes the quality of the solution,
– goal is min for minimization problems and max for maximization problems.
For instance, for the Min-BP-SMI problem, Π consists of all tuples I = (P, k) such that P is a marriage
instance with strict preferences, k is a non-negative integer, sol(I) consists of all matchings of size at least k,
cost(M, I) is the number of blocking pairs in M, and goal = min.
Definition 1 (Polynomial-time approximation algorithms). To define approximation algorithms, let us first
consider minimization problems. Let (Π, sol, cost, goal) be a minimization problem and I an instance of Π.
For a given constant ρ ≥1, we say that an algorithm A is a factor-ρ approximation algorithm for (Π, sol, cost, goal)
if it outputs a solution x with cost(x, I) ≤ρ · OPT, where OPT(I) is the cost of an optimal solution of I,
i.e., OPT(I) = arg min

We say that (Π, sol, cost, goal) is polynomial-time

We say that (Π, sol, cost, goal) admits a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) if for every ap-

Definition 2 (Fixed-parameter approximation algorithms). Let (Π, sol, cost, goal) be an optimization prob-
lem and I an instance of Π. We say that (Π, sol, cost, goal) is FPT-approximable to factor ρ with respect to
a parameter k if it admits a factor-ρ approximation algorithm that runs in time f(ρ, k) · nO(1), where f is a
computable function on k and ρ.
Consequently, we say that (Π, sol, cost, goal) admits an FPT-approximation scheme (FPT-AS) with re-
spect to a parameter k if for every ε > 0 it is FPT-approximable to factor (1 + ε) (for minimization) and
(1 −ε) (for maximization) with respect to k.
To show that no FPT-approximation scheme exists, we need a specific reduction, called gap-producing
parameterized reduction [10]. The idea is to provide a parameterized reduction to a gap version of an opti-
mization problem. Below, we only define the reduction for minimization problems. The one for maximization
problems is analogous.
Definition 3 (gap-producing parameterized reduction). Let X = (Π, sol, cost, goal) be a minimization
problem and let k be the natural parameter of X to turn it into a decision problem. Let α: N →N be a
computable function.
A reduction R from a parameterized problem ˆΠ with parameter h to our optimization problem X pa-
rameterized by k is called an α-gap producing parameterized reduction if for every instance ˆI of ˆΠ with
parameter h, R produces in f(h) · |ˆI|O(1) time an instance I of Π with parameter k such that
(i) k is bounded by a function of h,
(ii) if (ˆI, h) is a yes-instance, then I admits a solution x with cost(x, I) ≤k;
(iii) if (ˆI, h) is a no-instance, then every solution x of I has cost(x, I) > α(k) · k.
The existence of an α-gap-producing parameterized reduction from a parameterized intractable (e.g.,
W[1]-hard) problem shows that a factor-α FPT approximation algorithm is unlikely, unless FPT = W[1].

5
Conclusion
In this manuscript, we have explored the fixed-parameter approximability of three problems in the field of
stable matchings. Our goal is to highlight the potential of fixed-parameter approximability in the field of
matching under preferences and computational social choice more generally. This technique can be used to
solve problems that resist both polynomial-time approximation and FPT-algorithms. Using this technique
more widely in the field of matchings and computational social choice is an open question. Of course, as we
have in Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, some problems resist even this. Finding suitable parameters is part of
the challenge.
