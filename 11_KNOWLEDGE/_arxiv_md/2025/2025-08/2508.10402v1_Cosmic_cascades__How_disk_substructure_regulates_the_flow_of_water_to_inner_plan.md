ABSTRACT
The influx of icy pebbles to the inner regions of protoplanetary disks constitutes a fundamental
ingredient in most planet formation theories. The observational determination of the magnitude of this
pebble flux and its dependence on disk substructure (disk gaps as pebble traps) would be a significant
step forward. In this work we analyze a sample of 21 T Tauri disks (with ages ≈0.5−2 Myr) using
JWST/MIRI spectra homogeneously reduced with the JDISCS pipeline and high-angular-resolution
ALMA continuum data. We find that the 1500/6000 K water line flux ratio measured with JWST -
a tracer of cold water vapor and pebble drift near the snowline - correlates with the radial location
of the innermost dust gap in ALMA continuum observations (ranging from 8.7 to 69 au), confirming
predictions from recent models that study connections between the inner and outer disk reservoirs. We
develop a population synthesis exploration of pebble drift in gapped disks and find a good match to
the observed trend for early and relatively effective gaps, while scenarios where pebble drift happens
quickly, gaps are very leaky, or where gaps form late are disfavored on a population level. Inferred
snowline pebble mass fluxes (ranging between 10−6 and 10−3 M⊕/yr depending on gap position) are
comparable to fluxes used in pebble accretion studies and those proposed for the inner Solar System,
while system-to-system variations suggest differences in the emerging planetary system architectures
and water budgets.

1. INTRODUCTION
The journey of water from molecular clouds to pro-
toplanetary disks and ultimately planets and potential
biospheres is long and complex (e.g. E. F. van Dishoeck
et al. 2014; K. I. Öberg & E. A. Bergin 2021). A critical
step that occurs during the protoplanetary disk phase is
the transport of water ice to the terrestrial planet for-
mation zone through aerodynamic drift of solids (F. L.
Whipple 1972) - a fundamental process long proposed
for the Solar System (G. E. Morfill & H. J. Voelk 1984;
D. J. Stevenson & J. I. Lunine 1988; K. E. Cyr et al.
1998) that could eventually be linked to the formation
of potentially habitable planets (S. Krijt et al. 2023; T.
Lichtenberg et al. 2023).
Seminal results on water transport in disks were
achieved with the Infrared Spectrograph on the Spitzer
Space Telescope (J. R. Houck et al. 2004), by finding
(anti-)correlations between H2O emission and the disk
dust mass or size, suggestive of trapping of water ice
beyond the snowline in massive or large disks (J. R. Na-
jita et al. 2013; A. Banzatti et al. 2020).
The MIRI
spectrograph on JWST (G. H. Rieke et al. 2015; G. S.
Wright et al. 2023; I. Argyriou et al. 2023) now probes
physical and chemical conditions in the inner few au of
planet-forming disks with unprecedented combination of
high spectral resolution and sensitivity at infrared wave-
lengths. Analysis of the first MIRI-MRS disk spectra
in Cycle 1 revealed a rich chemical diversity ranging
from water-dominated to hydrocarbon-rich systems (B.
Tabone et al. 2023; S. L. Grant et al. 2023; A. Banzatti
et al. 2023; I. Kamp et al. 2023; K. M. Pontoppidan
et al. 2024; T. Henning et al. 2024; N. Arulanantham
et al. 2025). The sharper spectral view of MIRI has al-
ready improved upon previous Spitzer results by reveal-
ing that trends with dust disk radii are due to a cold
(∼170–400 K) water vapor reservoir that is enhanced in
compact disks that are considered to be drift-dominated
(A. Banzatti et al. 2023; C. E. Romero-Mirza et al. 2024;
A. Banzatti et al. 2025), supporting early predictions of
ice delivery to the snowline by pebble drift (F. J. Ciesla
& J. N. Cuzzi 2006). However, recent modeling works
show that more than traced by the outer disk radius,
pebble drift into the snowline region should be regulated
by the radial location and depth of disk gaps acting as
pebble traps (A. Kalyaan et al. 2021, 2023; W. Easter-
wood et al. 2024; J. Mah et al. 2024). A recent analysis
of 8 compact disks of intermediate sizes (40–60 au) in-

In the past 10 years, ALMA has indeed revealed that
the radial distributions of dust and pebbles in disks are
often not smooth but show sub-structures in the form
of rings and gaps (S. M. Andrews et al. 2018; S. M. An-

and water ice delivery to the inner disk (A. Kalyaan
et al. 2023; J. Mah et al. 2024).
While models are
quickly progressing and finding some support for the
ice drift scenario to explain the correlations found with
infrared water spectra, some aspects of the problem are
emerging as key to this regulatory process. One aspect
is the degree of ‘leakiness’ of disk gaps as pebble traps
(S. M. Stammler et al. 2023; P. Pinilla et al. 2024; S.
Tong & R. Alexander 2025; A. Kalyaan et al. 2023; W.
Easterwood et al. 2024; P. Huang et al. 2025), which
should be directly linked to the amount of inner disk
water enrichment by drift (with larger enrichment pro-
vided by leakier traps). A second major aspect is the
timing of gap opening in the disk, which should typically
start before the age of protoplanetary disks observed in
nearby star-forming regions ∼1−5 Myr (D. M. Segura-
Cox et al. 2020; ALMA Partnership et al. 2015; P. D.
Sheehan et al. 2020).
A third aspect is the range of
masses and radii disks may have when they form, which
should provide different initial conditions of ice reser-
voirs for pebble drift. All these aspects could play a role
in producing the scatter in inner disk water enrichment
recently observed in larger samples with MIRI (see dis-
cussions in A. Banzatti et al. 2025; D. Gasman et al.
2025; N. Arulanantham et al. 2025).

3. MODEL EXPLORATION
In order to test the observed trends presented in the
previous section, we perform a population synthesis
study that reproduce the outcome of dust evolution and
pebble drift in disks of similar conditions to the observed
sample, with a series of modeling steps as follows.
3.1. Estimating pebble mass fluxes in disks with gaps
The temporal evolution of the pebble flux arriving at
the inner disk is modeled using pebble predictor19
(J. Drążkowska et al. 2021).
The gas surface den-
sity is described as a tapered power-law (character-
ized by a size Rtaper) and pebble drift is calculated
self-consistently assuming a combination of drift-limited
and fragmentation-limited dust coagulation processes
(see e.g., T. Birnstiel 2024).
pebble predictor has
been successfully benchmarked against dustpy (S. M.
Stammler & T. Birnstiel 2022) for a range of disk masses,
turbulence levels, and dust fragmentation velocities (J.
Drążkowska et al. 2021, Fig. 7). Pebble fluxes are calcu-
lated at the midplane water snowline (defined as the po-
sition where Tdisk = 150 K) and we follow the approach
of G. D. Mulders et al. (2021, Table 1) to update the
disk temperature profile when modeling different stellar
masses. We use relatively fragile grains with constant
fragmentation velocity vf = 3 m/s, in line with recent
laboratory, observational, and modeling studies favoring
relatively fragile grains (G. Musiolik & G. Wurm 2019;
H. Jiang et al. 2024; T. Ueda et al. 2024; J. Williams &
S. Krijt 2025).
We expand these models to include one gap per disk,
following previous work that showed the innermost gap,
if deep enough, will dominate the regulatory process of
water delivery by pebble drift even in the case of multi-
ple gaps (A. Kalyaan et al. 2023; W. Easterwood et al.
2024). In our models, gaps are described by three pa-
rameters: their location Rgap, the time at which they
appear tgap, and an effective ‘impact’ Λgap on the mass
flux arriving at the water snowline – a parameter that
in principle can depend on gap depth, width, but also
its position and dust/pebble properties.
To synthesize lessons from dedicated studies investi-
gating the impact of disk substructure on local pebble
flux rates and inner disk water enrichment (A. Kalyaan
et al. 2021, 2023; S. M. Stammler et al. 2023; W. East-
erwood et al. 2024; J. Mah et al. 2024; S. Tong & R.
Alexander 2025; P. Huang et al. 2025), we implement
the appearance of outer disk substructure in pebble
predictor as follows: once a gap opens (at position

4. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
In this study we have used a specific diagnostic (the
1500/6000 K water line flux ratio from JWST/MIRI)
to target a specific reservoir (the observable cold water
vapor in the disk surface) to investigate a process in a
specific disk region (icy pebbles drifting across the mid-
plane water snowline) to conclude that, in disks where
substructure has been resolved, it is the innermost gaps
that regulate water enrichment from ice drift into the
terrestrial planet formation region (Fig. 1). This con-
clusion, reached for the first time by combining JWST
and ALMA data, supports predictions from recent mod-
eling works (A. Kalyaan et al. 2021, 2023; W. Easter-
wood et al. 2024; J. Mah et al. 2024). The population
synthesis exploration performed in this work provides a
fiducial model that can reproduce the observed trend in
structured disks based on pebble drift models (Fig. 3),
disfavoring scenarios where pebble drift happens early,
where gaps are very leaky, or gaps form late (Fig. 4).
We remark, however, that the scenarios explored here
are not necessarily universal and that water delivery in
individual disks may be regulated by a combination of
processes. Upcoming JWST programs aimed at expand-
ing the sample of systems observed at different ages,
across different spectral types, and across a range of
mass accretion rates (e.g., K. Zhang et al. 2025b; F.
Long et al. 2025a; J. Huang et al. 2025; M. J. Col-
menares Diaz et al. 2025), will be fundamental in pro-
viding additional multi-dimensional observational con-
straints. Moreover, increasing the sample of disks with
uniform high-resolution ALMA continuum data will fur-
ther test the correlation described in Sect. 2. To confirm

While this ap-

we recognize that the water snowline region is character-
ized by complex interactions between thermochemistry,
hydrodynamics, dust/ice evolution, and planet(esimal)
formation (e.g., L. Hartmann et al. 2017; T. Lichtenberg
et al. 2021; P. Woitke et al. 2022; A. D. Bosman et al.
2022; A. Houge et al. 2025; Y. Wang et al. 2025). Addi-
tionally, the full story of how the chemical and physical
structure of the entire inner regions of protoplanetary
disks (their midplanes and surface layers) respond to
various degrees of pebble drift has to also include evolv-
ing elemental ratios (e.g., C/O) and molecular abun-
dances for all relevant and observable species (e.g., C.
Xie et al. 2023; A. D. Sellek et al. 2025; F. Long et al.
2025b). The full JWST/MIRI spectra contain a wealth
of information beyond the line ratio diagnostic used here
that may help shed light on the bigger picture (e.g., D.
Gasman et al. 2025; N. Arulanantham et al. 2025).
The remarkable outcome of this work is that, in its
simplicity, a population synthesis based on a few pro-
cesses combined to new sensitive water line ratios from
JWST and the highest-resolution ALMA observations
finds support to recent models of dust evolution and peb-
ble accretion to place them in context of the emerging
exoplanet diversity and Solar System formation (Fig. 2).
Specifically, one aspect that is emerging is that the high
pebble fluxes required to form Super-Earths in M. Lam-
brechts et al. (2019) (the top of the orange area in
Fig. 2), may be provided primarily by disks with no
(deep) gaps in the inner ∼20 au. Planets that form in
these disks may accrete gas in water-vapor-rich environ-
ments, inheriting high O/H and low C/O ratio atmo-
spheres (e.g., B. Bitsch et al. 2021; J. Mah et al. 2024).
Conversely, disks with deeper gaps closer in are char-
acterized by a rapidly decreasing pebble flux, perhaps
more in line with what has been inferred for the in-
ner Solar System (Fig. 2 and T. S. Kruijer et al. 2020;
T. Lichtenberg et al. 2021), suggesting a water-vapor-
poor rocky planet formation environment. While bulk
Earth is indeed very ‘dry’ (S. Krijt et al. 2023) and the
existence of a class of water-rich small planets (at least
around M-dwarfs) has been argued to exist (R. Luque &
E. Pallé 2022), we have to remember terrestrial planets
form primarily from solid building blocks and that wa-
ter may be lost during the pebble accretion (A. Johansen
et al. 2021; Y. Wang et al. 2023) and planetesimal (T.
Lichtenberg et al. 2019, 2023) stages as well. Ultimately
the observational trends presented in this study will have
to be folded into state-of-the-art planet formation mod-
els to assess the impact of substructure and pebble drift
(or the lack thereof) on emerging planetary properties.
