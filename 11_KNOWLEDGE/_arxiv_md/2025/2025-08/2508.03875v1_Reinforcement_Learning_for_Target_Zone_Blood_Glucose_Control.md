Reinforcement Learning for Target Zone Blood Glucose Control
David H. Mguni1,*, Jing Dong2, Wanrong Yang3, Ziquan Liu1, Muhammad Salman Haleem1,
Baoxiang Wang2
1Queen Mary University London, 2The Chinese University of Hong Kong, 3University of Liverpool
Abstract
Managing physiological variables within clinically safe tar-
get zones is a central challenge in healthcare, particularly for
chronic conditions such as Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM).
Reinforcement learning (RL) offers promise for personalising
treatment, but struggles with the delayed and heterogeneous
effects of interventions. We propose a novel RL framework
to study and support decision-making in T1DM technologies,
such as automated insulin delivery. Our approach captures
the complex temporal dynamics of treatment by unifying two
control modalities: impulse control for discrete, fast-acting
interventions (e.g., insulin boluses), and switching control for
longer-acting treatments and regime shifts. The core of our
method is a constrained Markov decision process augmented
with physiological state features, enabling safe policy learning
under clinical and resource constraints. The framework incor-
porates biologically realistic factors, including insulin decay,
leading to policies that better reflect real-world therapeutic
behaviour. While not intended for clinical deployment, this
work establishes a foundation for future safe and temporally-
aware RL in healthcare. We provide theoretical guarantees
of convergence and demonstrate empirical improvements in
a stylised T1DM control task, reducing blood glucose level
violations from 22.4% (state-of-the-art) to as low as 10.8%.
Introduction
The management of chronic health conditions such as Type
1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM) presents complex decision-
making challenges involving interventions that operate on
markedly different time scales. In T1DM, individuals are
often required to maintain their blood glucose levels (BGLs)
within a clinically safe target range using a combination
of fast-acting insulin (e.g., bolus doses administered before
meals) and long-acting insulin (e.g., basal injections that
provide slow-release background coverage). A particularly
difficult aspect of this problem arises in scenarios where
patients manually administer insulin injections, often under
daily constraints that limit the number of permissible doses.
This introduces both safety-critical and resource-sensitive
trade-offs: long-acting insulin has a delayed, decaying effect
and can be harmful if misapplied, while fast-acting insulin
must be administered with precision to correct for immediate
BGL deviations without triggering dangerous overshoots.

T1DM is a chronic condition that requires meticulous man-
agement of BGLs. Carbohydrates, required for energy, cause
sudden spikes in BGLs after being ingested during meals
while insulin reduces BGLs. Synthetic insulin protocols (IPs)
aim to maintain BGLs within a safe range, large deviations
from the target can cause significant health issues and can
even be fatal. Current IPs struggle to maintain healthy BGLs
leading to frequent target zone violations. This has led to the
demand for systematic approaches towards devising IPs.
These characteristics give rise to a decision-making prob-
lem with highly nontrivial structure: the IP controller must
learn when to act, what type of intervention to apply, and how
to manage budget constraints over extended time horizons.
Crucially, standard reinforcement learning (RL) methods are
ill-suited to such settings. First, classical RL does not provide
mechanisms for modelling or enforcing strict limits on inter-
vention budgets—an essential component of many healthcare
regimes where excessive treatment is either impractical or
unsafe. Second, typical RL formulations assume action se-
lection at every time step, lacking a natural mechanism for
learning selectivity in action timing, i.e., knowing when it
is optimal to remain inactive. Third, existing hierarchical
extensions such as the options framework (Sutton, Precup,
and Singh 1999) generally require manual specification of
temporally extended actions (options) or performing the com-
plex task of intra-option learning (Bacon, Harb, and Precup
2017), do not handle decay dynamics, and are not designed
to learn context-aware initiation and termination strategies
for interventions that span multiple time steps.
In this work, we present a reinforcement learning (RL)
framework tailored to healthcare settings that involve choos-
ing between fast-acting and long-acting interventions, where
the latter exhibit temporally decaying effects. Our formu-
lation jointly learns what type of action to take and when
to act, while adhering to hard constraints on intervention
frequency. This provides a principled foundation for sequen-
tial treatment planning in Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus (T1DM),
addressing structural and safety challenges overlooked by
conventional RL paradigms. Although our focus is on in-
sulin therapy for blood glucose regulation, the underlying
methodology extends naturally to other domains requiring
temporally heterogeneous decision-making.
Our framework specifically targets chronic disease man-
agement, exemplified by insulin administration in T1DM.

Here, the key clinical task is to determine how and when to
apply treatments that differ in temporal dynamics—namely,
selecting between fast-acting (e.g., bolus insulin) and long-
acting (e.g., basal insulin) interventions to maintain blood
glucose levels (BGL) within a target range.
This setting presents several core challenges. First, incorpo-
rating long-acting treatments complicates the RL formulation:
while their effects are persistent, they decay over time, pos-
ing difficulties for standard Markovian assumptions. Second,
the agent must reason over actions that vary in duration and
determine when inaction is preferable. Third, learning must
be sample-efficient to support sparse, context-aware interven-
tion schedules. Most critically, safety constraints—such as
avoiding hypoglycaemia—must be satisfied in the presence
of delayed treatment effects.1,2
Our contributions. We propose a constrained Markov de-
cision process (CMDP) that integrates two complementary
control modalities: impulse control for discrete, short-term
actions, and switching control for managing long-term treat-
ment regimes. This dual-control structure enables the learn-
ing of policies that balance efficacy, safety, and resource
efficiency.
1) We introduce a novel RL framework for multi-timescale
target zone problems, enabling effective trade-offs be-
tween intervention types while optimising adherence to
clinical targets.
2) Our framework reduces BGL violations from 22.4%
(achieved by state-of-the-art baselines) to as low as 10.8%,
significantly increasing time spent within safe physiologi-
cal ranges.
3) We provide a tractable method for enforcing hard safety
and resource constraints within the RL paradigm.
4) We establish theoretical guarantees of almost sure conver-
gence to the optimal value function in discrete settings.
Related Work
The options framework (Sutton, Precup, and Singh 1999;
Klissarov and Precup 2021) considers settings in which an
RL agent makes a choice of a predefined sequence of actions
called an ‘option’ until the option terminates probabilisti-
cally. Unlike in the options framework, in our setting, the
controller can enact temporally extended actions while the
effects of previous actions are ongoing which is prohibited
in the options framework. Additionally, we tackle problem
of efficiently solving problems in which the controller must
choose between fast-acting, temporally extended actions and
not acting at all. Last, appropriate for our use case, our set-
ting accommodates gradual decay of the temporally extended
actions unlike the random terminations in the options frame-
work. Together, these features produce a distinct setting of
challenges from those tackled by the options framework.

RL for insulin control in Type 1 Diabetes offers a promis-
ing approach to a critical healthcare challenge. Javad et al.
(2019) propose an RL algorithm that recommends insulin
doses based on glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), body mass
index, physical activity, and alcohol usage. Zhu et al. (2020b)
introduce an actor-critic model using deep deterministic pol-
icy gradients to compute mealtime (bolus) insulin doses,
achieving up to 80% time in range (TIR) in a cohort of 10
MDI-treated T1DM subjects. Zhu et al. (2020a) develop a
double Q-learning model with dilated RNNs for optimising
long-acting (basal) insulin and glucagon delivery, resulting
in up to 85% TIR. More recently, Zhu, Li, and Georgiou
(2023) apply an offline deep RL approach combining twin-
delayed DDPG, behaviour cloning, and off-policy evaluation
via Q-learning, achieving 64% TIR for basal insulin control
on the OhioT1DM dataset (Marling and Bunescu 2020). For
bolus insulin, Jaloli and Cescon (2023) propose a dynamic
RL-based framework that adjusts doses based on real-time
blood glucose, meal intake, and dosing history, reaching up
to 91.7% TIR across 10 in-silico patients. Personalised bolus
adjustments for high-fat meals have also been shown to re-
duce postprandial hypoglycaemia to 1.8% in a cohort of 15
MDI-treated subjects (Jafar et al. 2024).
In contrast to prior work, our framework jointly optimises
both basal and bolus insulin delivery within a constrained
Markov decision process. By explicitly modelling safety con-
straints, our method achieves substantial improvements in
TIR for both combined and bolus-only treatment regimes.

Experiments
We evaluate our framework on the task of BGL management
for individuals with T1DM using multiple daily injections
(MDI) of both basal and bolus insulin. This treatment modal-
ity represents the most common and accessible approach for
T1DM management worldwide, with most of T1DM patients
relying on MDI therapy rather than continuous insulin pumps
due to cost, availability, and patient preference considerations.
The MDI setting presents unique challenges as it requires
discrete decision-making about both long-acting (basal) and
short-acting (bolus) insulin doses, making it an ideal testbed
for our multi-timescale control framework. Our primary ob-
jective is to maximise the time in range (TIR) - the percent-
age of time BGLs remain within the safe zone of 70-180
mg/dL - while strictly avoiding hypoglycemia (<70 mg/dL),
which can cause immediate medical emergencies. This safety-
critical constraint distinguishes our approach from standard
RL benchmarks and reflects real clinical priorities. We also
examine a simplified insulin pump scenario with basal-only
control in the Appendix for completeness as well as studying
the effect of different budget constraints and observations.

where patient models represent individual T1DM subjects
with unique physiological parameters, glucose sensors simu-

accuracy and sampling rates, and insulin pumps model the
delivery of basal and bolus insulin with realistic constraints.

Baseline Models. We compare our approach against four
baselines. (1) SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) (Haarnoja et al. 2018)
is an off-policy actor-critic algorithm that maximises entropy-
regularized rewards, providing stable learning and good ex-
ploration. (2) A2C (Advantage Actor-Critic) is a synchronous
variant of A3C that uses advantage estimation to reduce vari-
ance in policy gradient updates. (3) PPO (Proximal Policy
Optimization) (Schulman et al. 2017) constrains policy up-
dates using a clipped surrogate objective, balancing sample
efficiency with training stability. These three baselines use
standard RL formulations where the agent selects insulin
doses at every timestep without distinguishing between basal
and bolus interventions. (4) Fixed-Option Baseline imple-
ments a hierarchical structure similar to our approach but
with predetermined behavioral primitives. It employs twelve
fixed options combining basal levels (0.05, 0.5 normalized),
bolus levels (0.05, 0.5 normalized), and durations (3, 5, or
10 timesteps). A high-level selector learns to choose among
these options based on current state, providing temporal ab-
straction without adaptive low-level policies. All baselines
operate under the same state space and reward structure as
our method to ensure fair comparison.
Evaluations. We evaluate our framework across three
clinically-relevant scenarios with increasing complexity. Con-
trolled Metabolic Protocol (CMP) simulates standardized
dietary control conditions typical of clinical research set-
tings, with only two guaranteed meals at lunch (12:00±60min,
50±10g carbohydrates) and dinner (18:00±60min, 70±10g
carbohydrates), representing the baseline glycemic control
challenge under Medical Nutrition Therapy standards. Ex-
periment 2: Ambulatory Glycemic Variability Pattern
(AGVP) models real-world T1DM management with three
main meals (7:00, 12:00, 18:00) at 95% probability contain-
ing 25g, 40g, and 40g carbohydrates respectively (σ=10g),
plus 30% probability of snacks (10-30g carbohydrates) at
9:30, 15:00, and 21:30, capturing the glycemic variability
faced by outpatients. Postprandial Hyperglycemic Chal-
lenge (PHC) maintains the AGVP meal probability structure
but elevates carbohydrate loads to clinically challenging lev-
els—60g, 80g, and 100g for main meals (σ=10g) with 30g
snacks (σ=5g)—simulating extreme glycemic excursions that
test algorithm robustness where missed meals despite pre-
bolusing could trigger severe hypoglycemia. We conducted
ablation studies under the AGVP scenario to isolate the contri-
butions of our key framework components; safety constraints
and carbohydrate observations.
Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate our framework using
standard clinical metrics for glucose control assessment. TIR
measures the percentage of time blood glucose levels re-
main within the target range of 70-180 mg/dL, which is the
primary indicator of glycemic control quality. Time Above
Range (TAR) quantifies the percentage of time spent in
hyperglycemia (>180 mg/dL), while Time Below Range
(TBR) captures the percentage of time in hypoglycemia (<70
mg/dL). Given the immediate health risks of hypoglycemia,
minimizing TBR is particularly critical. Additionally, we re-
port Average number of Immediate Emergencies (AIME),
which measures the average daily occurrences when blood
glucose drops below 40 mg/dL—a threshold requiring imme-
diate medical intervention.

Conclusion
We introduced a novel reinforcement learning framework for
decision-making tasks involving actions with effects span-
ning heterogeneous time horizons. Our theory proves the
convergence to the optimal value function of a constrained
MDP with two forms of control modalities that model our
insulin administration problem. Our method significantly out-
performs existing reinforcement learning approaches by re-
ducing violations of clinically safe blood glucose level ranges.
A limitation of our current approach is its sensitivity to miss-
ing observations, such as carbohydrate intake. Addressing
this remains a promising direction for future work.

Option Baseline Description
The option selector employs a deep Q-network architecture featuring a two-layer feature extractor with 256 ReLU-activated units
per layer, complemented by specialised heads for option selection Q-value estimation and state value computation to facilitate
temporal difference learning. Training optimization utilizes experience replay with option-level transition storage, Polyak-
averaged target networks (τ = 0.005), Adam optimization with a learning rate of 1e-4, and mini-batch gradient updates of size
64, thereby providing a computationally efficient yet methodologically sound benchmark that maintains clinical interpretability
while enabling direct assessment of the added value of continuous adaptive policy learning in automated glucose control systems.
Computational Requirements
All experiments presented in this work were executed purely on CPUs. The experiments were executed in compute clusters that
consist of several nodes. The main types of CPU models that were used for this work are GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i5 processor,
Intel Iris Plus graphics. All experiments were executed using a single CPU core. The total number of CPU hours that were spent
for executing the experiments in this work (excluding the hyper-parameter search) are 80,900.
