REMOTE WORK AND WOMEN’S LABOR SUPPLY:
THE NEW GENDER DIVISION AT HOME*
ISABELLA DI FILIPPO
BRUNO ESCOBAR†
JUAN FACAL
Last updated: March 2, 2026
Abstract
We study how increases in remote work opportunities for men affect their spouses’ labor sup-
ply. Exploiting variation in the change in work-from-home (WFH) exposure across occupations
before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, we find that increases in men’s WFH exposure led
to sizable improvements in their wives’ labor-market outcomes: annual employment rose by
roughly 2.5 percentage points (from a 69% pre-treatment mean), earnings increased by about
5%, weekly hours worked rose by roughly half an hour, weeks worked increased by about 1.3%,
and the likelihood of part-time work declined by approximately 9%. Evidence from time-use
diaries and childcare questionnaires suggests these effects are driven by intra-household real-
location of child-caring time: women are less likely to engage in primary childcare activities,
while men working at home partially compensate by covering more for their spouse. These re-
sults highlight the role of households in shaping the labor market consequences of remote work.

INTRODUCTION
Over the past century, gender gaps in education, human capital, and labor market outcomes have
narrowed substantially in developed countries (Olivetti and Petrongolo, 2016). Nonetheless, a per-
sistent gap remains in labor market trajectories following parenthood (Kleven et al., 2024). After
childbirth, women frequently reduce their labor force participation or exit employment altogether
(Kleven, 2022), and those who remain employed often face large earnings penalties relative to men
(Bertrand et al., 2010). These gender disparities are largely driven by (i) the unequal division of
childcare responsibilities within households, and (ii) the fact that many jobs - including high-paying
white collar positions - have historically lacked flexibility options to allow women to balance both
their careers and motherhood (Goldin, 2014).
This paper examines whether the expansion of one aspect of workplace flexibility - namely, remote
work opportunities - can shift these intra-household dynamics and encourage greater labor force
participation among women with children. We focus on U.S. households with young children and
combine occupation-level data on remote work vacancies with rich household-level survey data from
the ACS and other national datasets. Leveraging quasi-experimental variation in remote work expo-
sure across occupations before and after the pandemic, we estimate whether large-scale changes in
remote work availability altered household labor supply decisions, particularly by enabling mothers
to remain in or return to the labor force.
Our motivation builds on the idea, emphasized by Goldin (2014), that the last chapter of gender
convergence may depend on restructuring work to allow for greater flexibility. Workplace flexibility
has been shown to help parents, especially mothers, accommodate both predictable and unexpected
caregiving demands (Angelici and Profeta, 2024). Evidence also shows that women and parents of
young children place a high value on flexible work arrangements (Mas and Pallais, 2017; Aksoy et al.,
2022; Bloom et al., 2022), and are more averse to commuting (Le Barbanchon et al., 2021). While
not synonymous with flexibility, remote work is strongly associated with it, particularly in allowing

job tasks to be performed from home. Thus, remote work could enable a more equitable division of
household production - specifically, of childcare - between spouses. Despite this potential, there is
limited causal evidence on how remote work options available to the household - even if only avail-
able to the male spouse - affect women’s labor supply. Our paper fills this gap by documenting how
the expansion of remote work affects both spouses employment and overall time use in a household.
The backdrop for our analysis is the COVID-19 pandemic, which triggered a rapid and persistent
shift in remote work norms. In the U.S., only 7% of full paid workdays were conducted from home
in 2019. By June 2023, this share had quadrupled to 28% (Barrero et al., 2023). However, the
magnitude of this shift varied widely across occupations: while some sectors (e.g., computer science
and professional services) adopted remote work at scale, others (e.g., transport, healthcare, education)
largely returned to in-person arrangements. In Figure I, we illustrate this heterogeneity using data
from Hansen et al. (2023) on remote work job postings between 2019 and 2024. We use this variation
to study whether and how women’s labor market outcomes respond to changes in household exposure
to remote work opportunities, shaped by the husband’s occupation.1
Our empirical strategy compares the labor market outcomes of women with young children whose
husbands experienced a large increase in the availability of remote work, measured as the change in
the share of job vacancies offering WFH in their occupation, to those whose husbands experienced
little or no change. This design offers two main advantages. First, the shock plausibly expands the
household’s set of feasible work–care arrangements, creating opportunities for women to enter or
re-enter in the labor market. Second, defining treatment through the husband’s occupation avoids a
major selection problem: in most cross-sectional surveys, a woman’s occupation is only observed
if she is employed. Using changes in WFH exposure in the wife’s occupation as treatment would
condition on labor force participation, precluding identification of extensive margin effects – one of
our outcomes of interest.

Our first-stage estimates indicate that occupation-level increases in WFH probability after COVID
translated into substantial increases in men’s realized WFH: take-up rose by about 20 percentage
points on average, nearly tripling relative to pre-pandemic levels. In turn, wives’ labor-market out-
comes improved along several margins: annual employment rose by roughly 2.5 percentage points
(from a 69% pre-treatment mean) or 4%, weekly hours worked rose by roughly half an hour, weeks
worked increased by about 1.3%, and the likelihood of part-time work declined by approximately
9%. As a result of this changes in labor supply, overall labor earnings for these women increased by
about 5% - with no significant changes in their hourly wage rates. Using a simple decomposition,
we show that half of this effect stems from the extensive margin increase and the remainder from the
intensive margin.
We then move on to conduct heterogeneity analysis to understand which households have benefited
the most. The increases in women’s employment and hours are sharply concentrated among couples
with stronger ex ante attachment to remote-capable work: effects are largest for college-educated
women (and for women married to college-educated men) and are most pronounced for women aged
25–40. Disaggregating the analysis by household composition further reinforces a childcare-based
channel: responses peak in households with young children – especially when the youngest child
is preschool-aged – and attenuate substantially in childless households or those with older children.
Finally, we parse out the effect of men’s remote work onto their wives’ labor supply while hold-
ing wives’ exposure to remote work fixed through several tests. These exercises show that spousal
dynamics and remote work spillovers from one spouse onto the other are economically meaning-
ful, as even women that are unlikely to work from home given their occupation or education degree
experienced significant increase in overall labor supply and earnings.
To validate the credibility of our identification strategy, we conduct a broad set of robustness checks
that address potential threats to our findings. We examine the possibility of changes in household
composition or selection into treatment following the pandemic, including tests for differential fer-

tility and marriage patterns and reweighting strategies that align pre- and post-COVID covariates

Finally, we study how shifts in the household’s allocation of labor supply, childcare responsibilities,
and household production activities could be explaining our employment effects. To do this, we rely
mainly on two data sources: the time-use diary data from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS)
and the topical modules on childcare from the Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP).
We complement this data with commuting and remote work adoption questions from the ACS. Our
findings reveal that wives of men that saw large increases in work from home went on to experience
a large increase in their share of day spent working, with no significant change in overall household
production. Women themselves are also much more likely to work remotely, with approximately a
75% rise relative to the pre -treatment mean, suggesting that remote work enables labor supply for
both spouses. Interestingly, we observe that women reduce time spent on childcare as a primary
activity and husbands partially offset it with increased childcare while working. Households are also
less likely to pay for daycare services. We also find evidence that these adjustments in women’s em-
ployment and the allocation of childcare are not coming at the expense of the children’s or women’s
well-being.
Overall, our findings suggest that increases in men’s access to remote work can influence their
spouses’ labor supply decisions, particularly in households with young children. Our analysis of
time-use and childcare patterns offers tentative evidence that the effects may stem from shifts in
household responsibilities: women are reducing their primary childcare time, while husbands mod-

Related Literature
We contribute firstly to the extensive literature on gender inequality in the la-
bor market (Waldfogel, 1998; Anderson et al., 2002; Ejrnæs and Kunze, 2013; Kleven et al., 2024).
Goldin (2014) emphasizes that closing gender gaps requires structural changes in the labor mar-
ket that enhance flexibility. More recently, Goldin (2022) has argued that the rise of WFH should
eventually benefit employment of college-educated mothers. It has been difficult to disentangle net
employment effects in the immediate aftermath of the Covid pandemic due to a shortage in the sup-
ply of childcare services (Heggeness and Suri, 2021). By exploiting the long-run effect of Covid on
the availability of remote work options, we are among the first papers2 to find that WFH can lead to
closing gender gaps in labor market outcomes, even in developed countries.
Second, we contribute to the growing literature on how working arrangements affect labor market
dynamics. While existing research has primarily focused on the direct effects of flexible work ar-
rangements on individual outcomes – highlighting their growing value for both workers and firms
(Bloom et al., 2015; Mas and Pallais, 2017; Bloom et al., 2022; Angelici and Profeta, 2024; Choud-
hury et al., 2024) – relatively few studies have examined how changes in work arrangements reshape
decision-making at the household level. Prior work shows that women and parents place especially
high value on flexibility (Mas and Pallais, 2020; Aksoy et al., 2022), yet evidence on intra-household
responses remains limited. We contribute to this emerging strand by leveraging a large-scale, quasi-

Third, our paper contributes to the literature on the labor supply effects of parental leave and tem-

sometimes even negative, effects on women’s long-term labor market outcomes (Ginja et al., 2020;

of sizable and persistent effects from increased remote work? We argue that the scale and perceived
permanence of the WFH shock fundamentally differ from traditional parental leave policies. Rather
than offering short-term accommodations around childbirth, the pandemic-driven shift toward re-
mote work restructured household production possibilities and gave families a broader set of tools to
jointly optimize labor supply and caregiving responsibilities over the longer run.

II.C
Treated and Control Group Definitions
We use job postings data from Hansen et al. (2023) to construct occupation-level measures of the
change in WFH probability before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, we compute the
difference in the share of hybrid and remote-work job postings between the post-pandemic period
(2023–2024) and the pre-pandemic period (2019). Figure B.2 shows the distribution of our WFH
exposure measure.
We convert occupation codes from the original SOC 3-digit classification used in the job postings data
to the occupation codes used in the ACS. Occupations with a WFH probability change at or above
the median are classified as treated, and those below the median as control. These occupation-level
measures capture the pandemic-induced shift in WFH feasibility and are merged to our household
survey data based on the husband’s occupation at the time of the survey.13
Appendix Table A.1 lists the 15 most common occupations in the treated and control groups. As
expected, occupations that experienced the largest increases in WFH probability after COVID-19
include those in the tech sector, law, and management, such as computer and software engineers,
lawyers, and various types of managers. In contrast, occupations with minimal changes in WFH
feasibility are concentrated in sales, construction, and retail, as well as in sectors like healthcare (e.g.,
physicians and surgeons) and education (e.g., teachers), where in-person presence remains a key job
requirement. We use both the 2019 Occupational Employment Statistics (OES) data (Table A.2)
and 2019 ACS data (Table A.3) to compute characteristics of treated and control occupations. As
expected, treated occupations are associated with higher wages, both on average and at any percentile
of the distribution, and tend to employ more educated workers with longer schooling and longer
work hours. Somewhat surprisingly, women appear slightly more represented in treated occupations
in the ACS sample, although the difference is small (i.e., 1 percentage point) and not economically
significant.

Identification Assumption
Under a standard parallel trends assumption – namely, that in the absence
of the pandemic, the labor market outcomes of women whose husbands worked in occupations that
experienced large increases in WFH probability would have followed similar trends to those whose
husbands worked in occupations with minimal changes – the coefficient β1 identifies the causal effect
of interest. Specifically, β1 captures any effect of the husband’s exogenous shift in WFH probability,
induced by the pandemic, on the wife’s labor market outcomes.
Disentangling Spillovers from Matching Effects
We categorize the effects on women’s labor out-
comes into two categories. First, spillover effects operating through the husband’s increased WFH
exposure – for instance, via reallocation of childcare or household production – which can shift the
wife’s labor supply even holding her own WFH exposure fixed. Second, correlated direct effects aris-
ing from assortative matching in marriage markets, whereby wives of men in highly WFH-feasible
occupations may themselves hold (or seek) WFH-feasible jobs, mechanically correlating the hus-
band’s exposure with the wife’s own exposure. A key empirical limitation is that most household
surveys record occupation only for the employed; the wife’s WFH exposure is therefore missing pre-
cisely when she is non-employed. This constrains our ability to condition on, or interact with, the
wife’s occupation in the full sample.18
Given these constraints, in Section IV we adopt complementary strategies to separate and assess the
contributions of spillover effects (operating through the husband’s WFH exposure) and correlated
direct effects (arising from assortative matching) to the overall estimated effect.

IV
MAIN RESULTS
First-Stage: WFH Shock Exposure and Husbands’ WFH Uptake
Our main identification strategy
exploits how occupation-level shocks in women’s spouses increased women’s labor market partic-
ipation by plausibly increasing spouse’s time availability for household production. Consequently,
our first stage analysis consists of verifying that large shocks on occupation-level remote work op-
tions available to fathers of young children in our ACS sample led to sizable increase in their uptake
of remote work.19 Figure II shows that men in treated occupation increase their uptake of remote
work by as much as 20 pp with respect to the control group and relative to pre-COVID year 2019.
Although the effects are smaller (around 12 pp) by 2023 – which corresponds with more compa-
nies increasingly mandating return to office among their employees – the shocks have had lasting
and economically meaningful effects on household. With a pre-pandemic average WFH rate of 4-
6%, rates for men have doubled and even tripled in our sample households. This increase roughly
matches the large increases in share of remote work job vacancies for high exposed occupations that

ROBUSTNESS AND VALIDATION
Group Composition and Selection
We conduct a series of robustness checks to assess whether our
main results are driven by changes in group composition between treated and control households
before and after the pandemic. Given the cross-sectional nature of the ACS, one potential concern
is that our findings may not reflect intra-household adjustments – for example, a woman increasing
her labor supply in response to her husband’s WFH shift – but instead reflect changes in assortative
matching. Specifically, it is possible that, post-COVID, men in high–WFH-shock occupations are
more likely to be partnered with women who are already more attached to the labor force.
We address this concern by examining changes in assortative matching over time. Figure B.8 in the
Appendix provides suggestive evidence that changes in spousal composition are unlikely to be driv-
ing our main results. While there is clear evidence of assortative matching, i.e., households in which
the husband is in the highest quartile of WFH exposure are more likely to have wives in similarly
high-exposure occupations than households in which the husban is in the first quartile, this pattern
appears stable over time. Specifically, the distribution of wives’ exposure conditional on their hus-

Placebo tests
We conduct a standard timing falsification test by artificially assigning the onset of
the COVID shock to 2018 and estimating the baseline specification on the 2017–2020 ACS subsam-
ple, with reference years 2016–2019. We define the pre- and post-shock periods as 2016–2017 and
2018–2019, respectively. Because this subsample predates the COVID period and the shock was

the fact that men are more likely to work from home due to their shock. These patterns point that
women reduce time spent on primary childcare, and men appear to partially compensate while work-
ing. In terms of household division, there is a more equal distribution in the time spent childcaring
between spouses. The gender gap in childcare time was of approximately 0.5 in the pre-treatment,
as Table A.35 reflects, meaning that women spent the double of time childcaring compared to men.
Treated households narrow the gap to approximately 0.75.
On the household production side, both men and women did not experience any change in time spent
on housework. Although this estimate is not precise for women, it consistently points in a negative
direction, while virtually zero for men. Childcare might be more substitutable and proportionally
more important in time to be able to detect significant changes, compared to other household tasks.
This might explain the lack of precise effects on the other less prominent household activities.
Finally, to strengthen the validity of our mechanism analysis, in Table A.41 we conduct a standard
placebo test by assigning the onset of the post-treatment period to March 2018. Across all outcomes
we find no effects, which alleviates concerns on differential pre-trends.
Childcare in SIPP
Using the SIPP childcare topical modules, we are able to provide complementary
evidence how household change their sources of childcare and hiring of daycare following shocks on
spousal WFH options. We present some results in Table A.37. We estimate our baseline cross-section
specification on the 2018-2024 SIPP panel datasets on a battery of childcare related variables. Our
results show that in households shocked by the occupation-level WFH treatment, when the child’s
mother is working, the spouse is 6.4 pp more likely to be involved in childcare, or roughly a 13%
increase over the baseline. On the other hand, the woman herself is not any more likely to be involved
in childcare.
We also find that households exposed to the WFH shock are significantly less likely to rely on grand-
29


## Page 31


parents or other relatives, or on daycare, for day-to-day childcare. Treated households are 7.3 pp less
likely to have any paid childcare, and although imprecisely estimated, their total childcare expendi-
ture falls by about 16%. The dynamic DiD estimates in Figure B.12 show clear post-2019 declines
in both the probability of paying for childcare and log childcare spending. Overall, these patterns are
consistent with fathers becoming more available for childcare in treated households.
Household’s Well-being
As Table A.35 points out, women in treated households experience no sig-
nificant change in time spent on personal care activities, such as sleeping and grooming. In contrast,
they exhibit a 1.9 pp increase in time devoted to leisure activities, suggesting that the shift in labor
supply and the new household specialization decisions do not fully crowd out their discretionary
time. This may suggest that women are not worse-off in the sense that they are not taking more
responsibilities by crowding out their personal time. For husbands, however, there is a significant
reduction in leisure time, amounting to 2.4 percentage points.
Finally, it is plausible that children may be worse off given that our main results suggest that women
in our treatment sample are more likely to be working, spend more time at work and, as found in this
section, there is an overall reduction in the household’s time spent child-caring as a primary activity.
Although we cannot fully measure well-being with our current datasets, the SIPP has a handful
related variables such as days that parents have dinner or go out with their child and the children’s
involvement in extracurricular activities. In Table A.38, we estimate our baseline specification on
these outcomes, and we find no significant changes for these variables. This evidence suggests that
– to the extent we can measure well-being – children are not worse off by changes in household
production and labor supply.
Discussion
Overall, these results underscore the role of intra-household reallocation in explaining
the observed improvements in women’s labor market outcomes. Couples seem to be collectively
opting for women to reduce their primary childcare time in order to increase their working time,
30


## Page 32


and husbands modestly compensate them as they are spending more time at home. In addition,
there is a decline in reliance on extended family or formal daycare services. In Appendix Section
D, we show that these findings can be rationalized through the lens of a simple model of dual-
parent household labor and childcare supply that incorporates the option to work remotely. The
model formalizes the idea that work from home expands the household’s feasible set of work-care
arrangements by allowing some childcare to be produced jointly with market work, thereby relaxing
childcare constraints.
VII
CONCLUSION
In this paper, we provide novel evidence on intra-household labor market and household production
spillovers arising from large and persistent changes in husbands’ work-from-home (WFH) arrange-
ments in dual-parent households. Exploiting plausibly exogenous cross-occupational variation in the
post-pandemic expansion of remote work in the United States, we show that increases in husbands’
WFH exposure lead to sizable improvements in their wives’ labor market outcomes, including higher
employment, earnings, and hours worked, as well as a lower likelihood of part-time work.
These effects are concentrated among households with young children and among highly educated
couples. They are not driven by changes in group composition or assortative matching and are robust
across alternative specifications, treatment definitions, and nationally representative datasets.
Using detailed time-use data, we show that these labor supply responses are accompanied by a reallo-
cation of household production. We find evidence of modest substitution in childcare responsibilities:
women reduce time spent in primary childcare, while husbands increase their involvement.
Taken together, our findings demonstrate how shifts in workplace flexibility reshape intra-household
specialization and generate meaningful labor market spillovers. An important avenue for future re-
search is to examine how the expansion of remote work affects assortative matching in the marriage
market and human capital investment over the life cycle.

DISCUSSION OF IDENTIFICATION STRATEGY
Our empirical design estimates the causal effect of an increase in the husband’s WFH probability on
the wife’s labor market outcomes. A key data limitation, however, is that in many surveys – includ-
ing the ACS – occupational information is observed only for individuals currently employed. This
constraint implies two challenges. First, we cannot systematically observe a wife’s own occupation
when she is not employed, preventing us from measuring her WFH exposure during non-employment
spells. Second, because women’s own exposure is unobserved for part of the sample, our baseline
estimates necessarily combine the spillover effect arising from husbands’ WFH exposure with the
direct effect operating through women’s own WFH exposure, which in the model is generated by
assortative matching, as explained in Section III.

Set-up
Consider a dual-parent household composed of a female (f), a male (m) and a child. Each

¯θ ∈[0,1]. The childcare requirement is thus

Next, we discuss some extensions to the baseline model.
Labor force participation
To capture potential extensive-margin responses, suppose the wife faces
a fixed utility cost F > 0 from entering in the labor market due to, say, commuting/setup costs, job
search frictions, or coordination costs of arranging care. Let V work(¯r) be the value of the household
problem when the wife works (h f > 0), and V out(¯r) the value when she does not (h f = 0). When the
husband is hours-constrained, increases in ¯r raise the marginal benefit of wives’ participation because
the household can convert the additional secondary childcare into reduced primary childcare burden
only if the wife reallocates time toward work while the husband maintains fixed hours. Moreover,
convexities in ϕ(·) and χ(·) imply gains from diversification across spouses in market and home
production. Consequently, the gap V work(¯r) −V out(¯r) is increasing in ¯r, making it more likely that
the wife enters employment as remote-work opportunities expand.
Remote work for women
Suppose both male and female spouses can work remote a fraction ¯rm and
¯r f of their overall labor supply, and that their shocks are correlated so that ri = si +ρ. In the absence
of any differences in wages or Pareto weights, an idiosyncratic shock on the female’s remote work
options, si, raises their labor supply proportionally more than the male. If the shock affects both
spouses homogeneously through ρ - say, if both spouses work in the same occupation - then the time
gains from remote work that are shifted into labor are split evenly between both spouses. Overall, the
effect of remote work shocks on female labor supply increases when both spouses have the option to
work remotely.
Remote work for women can also directly change extensive margin of female labor supply if it
reduces the fixed cost of women going into the labor market for the first time or after a long unem-
ployment spell. Households will find it less costly to send the female spouse on the labor market if
transportation frictions and costs associated with finding and adapting to a new job are significantly
reduced. This can be achieved if fixed costs introduced in the previous extension are some decreasing
function of the spouse’s common remote work option, F = F(ρ), or are negatively correlated with it.
Demand for daycare
Suppose spouses can hire daycare services d which contributes 1-for-1 to-
wards the childcare restriction at a price of p. For rich households that can afford to hire daycare
to fully offest primary childcare - i.e. hire d = ¯N -, remote work directly reduces their demand for
daycare since, if anything, childcare while working substitutes daycare and frees up resources fore-
gone on consumption.41 For households that cannot afford enough daycare to fully offset daycare -
i.e. d < ¯N - the overall effect of remote work on daycare demand is ambiguous. Increasing daycare
when the technology to do childcare while working is available is inefficient on its own as the house-
hold may be providing more childcare than the requirement ¯N. However, the household may decide
to substitute primary childcare for daycare since labor supply (and consequently, earnings) increase
following remote work shocks and childcare generates disutility.

Disutility from childcare
For simplicity and parsimony, we make strong assumptions when mod-
eling the (dis)utility from childcare. For instance, assuming that secondary childcare generates no
additional disutility simplifies the main analysis by ensuring full remote work uptake, i.e. hR
m = ¯rhm.
We could relax this assumption, for example, by allowing additional secondary childcare to enter the
childcare disutility function as χ(nm + ¯θ ¯rhR
m). Unlike primary childcare, secondary childcare gen-
erates labor earnings. Then the overall choice of remote work depends on whether the utility value
of the increase in earnings from switching primary childcare into secondary childcare offsets the
marginal disutility of secondary childcare. However, the continuity and convexity in the utility func-
tions ensures that households find it optimal to do some positive amount of remote work.42 Hence
the predictions from the baseline model hold qualitatively.
In addition, the assumption that any level of childcare generates negative utility can be relaxed to
account for the fact that parents may enjoy spending some time with their children. This would not
upend our main predictions if we assume parents enjoy parenting is subject to satiation. Moreover,
assume that childcare at the satiation point, ¯ns, is not enough to cover the childcare requirement, i.e.

so, then the predictions hold as in the main analysis. If only the male parent supplies childcare below
the satiation point, then an expansion of remote work options for the male would lead to a sharper
increase (compared to the baseline model) of secondary childcare from men and labor supply from
their spouse.
