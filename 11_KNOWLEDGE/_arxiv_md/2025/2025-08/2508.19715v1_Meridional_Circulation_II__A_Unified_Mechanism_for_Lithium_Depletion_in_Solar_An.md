ABSTRACT
The behavior of lithium (Li) in Population I main sequence stars challenges standard
stellar theory. Two phenomena stand out: the solar Li problem which extends to Li
depletion in solar analogs and the Li dip observed in mid-F stars within open clusters.
Building on the meridional circulation-driven radial mixing framework previously de-
veloped to explain Li-enriched red clump stars, we explore its relevance to Li depletion
on the main sequence. First, our models reproduce the observed A(Li)-Age correlation
in solar analogs. Through detailed isochrone analysis, we find good agreement between
the simulated and observed A(Li)-Teff relationships within the solar analog parameter
space. However, the predicted solar Li abundance (∼1.5 dex) is still higher than current
solar measurements. Second, our models partially explain the Li dip phenomenon in
mid-F cluster stars. The models accurately reproduce Li distributions on the cool side
of the Li dip in most clusters and capture the Li behaviors on the hot side observed in
systems like the Hyades. However, we identify limitations in the models’ ability to fully
reproduce the dip morphology, particularly due to the rotation velocity distribution of
sample stars in this temperature zone.

As observational capabilities advance, our understanding of lithium (Li) in the universe has grown
clearer.
However, significant discrepancies persist between theoretical predictions for Li and ob-
servations, particularly three stellar Li problems: (1) G/K dwarfs exhibit notable Li depletion, a
phenomenon exemplified by the solar Li problem. In this case, observations indicate a A(Li)i of ap-
proximately 1.0 dex (King et al. 1997), far below the model-predicted value of 2.8 dex (Pinsonneault
1997); (2) The distribution of Li for open cluster mid-F stars shows a dip (gap) behavior (Boesgaard
& Tripicco 1986; Soderblom et al. 1993); and (3) the ‘1% puzzle’ of Li-rich, A(Li) > 1.5 dex, giants
(Gao et al. 2019; Martell et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024). Additionally, Li in the Galaxy faces unresolved
nucleosynthetic origins (Fu et al. 2018; Cescutti & Molaro 2019; Borisov et al. 2024), while the cos-
mological Li problem reveals a 3 −4 times discrepancy (Matteucci 2021b). Based on baryon density
measurements and nuclear reaction rate determinations (Boesgaard & Steigman 1985), the predicted
value of Big Bang nucleosynthesis (∼2.7 dex; Cyburt et al. 2008; Fields 2011; Anders et al. 2014)
exceeds the observed Li plateau in metal-poor stars (∼2.2 dex; Spite & Spite 1982; Sbordone et al.
2010; Matteucci et al. 2021a).
The current solar Li abundance is approximately 2 dex lower than that of meteorites (Anders &
Grevesse 1989; Grevesse & Sauval 1998; Asplund et al. 2009). Studies of older solar analogs reveal
even more significant Li depletion (e.g. Carlos et al. 2016; Yana Galarza et al. 2024). This indicates
a clear A(Li) and age correlation in solar analogs (Carlos et al. 2019). According to the standard
solar model, such pronounced Li depletion should not occur (e.g. Schwarzschild et al. 1957), implying
that non-standard processes play a crucial role in Li depletion. Many physical processes come into
view, including convective overshooting (Schlattl & Weiss 1999; Baraffe et al. 2017), rotation mixing
(Endal & Sofia 1981; Charbonnel et al. 1992; Charbonnel & Talon 2005; Somers & Pinsonneault
2016), mixing driven by internal gravity waves (Garcia Lopez & Spruit 1991; Montalban 1994),
element diffusion (Michaud 1986; Richer & Michaud 1993; Turcotte et al. 1998), turbulent diffusion
(Baglin et al. 1985), tachocline diffusion (Piau et al. 2003), mass loss effects (Swenson & Faulkner
1992), convective settling (Andr´assy & Spruit 2015), macroscopic mixing (Buldgen et al. 2025), and
the planet effects (Montalb´an & Rebolo 2002; Castro et al. 2009). Despite the numerous physical
processes that can cause Li depletion, there is still no consensus on the dominant mechanism. More
research is needed to fully understand how Li depletion occurs.
The Li dip was first discovered in the Hyades (Boesgaard & Tripicco 1986). This dip primarily
locates the effective temperature (Teff) range of 7000–6200 K (corresponding to mid-F dwarfs), where
the Li abundance significantly declines by approximately 2–3 dex. Subsequently, similar Li distri-
bution features have been identified in numerous clusters, such as NGC 752 (Hobbs & Pilachowski
1986), Pleiades (Boesgaard et al. 1988), Praesepe (Soderblom et al. 1993), M67 (Balachandran 1995),
NGC 3680 (Anthony-Twarog et al. 2009), NGC 2243 (Anthony-Twarog et al. 2021), NGC 3532, NGC
2420, Ruprecht 134 (Randich & Magrini 2021), and M48 (Sun et al. 2023). Charbonneau & Michaud
(1988) found that meridional circulation could explain the cold side of the Li dip based on the model
proposed by Tassoul & Tassoul (1982), while the high Li abundance feature on the hot side may be
dominated by radiative acceleration. Additionally, the diffusion of elements within non-rotating stars
can replicate the Li dip behavior, but it is narrower than that observed in these clusters (see Richer &
Michaud 1993). Moreover, as anticipated by Charbonneau & Michaud (1988), element diffusion leads

The classical description of meridional circulation can be summarized as the Eddington–Sweet
circulation model (Eddington 1925; Sweet 1950), which proposes that the centrifugal force imbalance
caused by rotation drives the circulation. Theoretical studies have demonstrated that large-scale
meridional flows can exist in the radiative zones of rotating stars (e.g., see Tassoul 2000; Maeder
2009). Meridional flows primarily facilitate the redistribution of angular momentum (e.g. Zahn 1992)
and the mixing of elements (e.g. Sweigart & Mengel 1979). They can transport angular momentum or
elements in the form of a closed loop (Decressin et al. 2009; Tassoul & Tassoul 1983; Meynet & Maeder
2002), and thus can be termed meridional circulation. The effect of the mixing process within stellar
interiors on surface elemental abundances manifests as an increase or decrease. For low-mass main
sequence (MS) stars, the depletion of surface Li is more likely to occur. Meridional circulation in the
radiative zone of rotating stars can disrupt the boundary between the surface convective zone and the
radiative zone, bringing Li into the radiative zone and thereby driving Li depletion (Charbonneau &
Michaud 1988; Charbonneau et al. 1989). Recently, Li et al. (2025) (hereafter Paper I) has provided
the diffusion coefficient for radial mixing due to meridional circulation, derived from the radial mass
changes in giants during material transport driven by meridional circulation. This is different from
the view of Charbonneau & Michaud (1988). Based on asteroseismological evidence, the radiative
zone of a rotating low-mass MS star can usually be regarded as maintaining nearly uniform rotation
(e.g. Christensen-Dalsgaard 2002; Benomar et al. 2015; Van Reeth et al. 2016, 2022; Li et al. 2020;
Jermyn et al. 2020). This implies that the work of Paper I can be extended to low-mass MS stars.
Consequently, in this paper, we investigate the role of meridional circulation on the Li anomaly
behavior of Population I stars during the MS, based on the model of Paper I. In this study, we
focus on two primary Li depletion issues during the MS. The first is the solar Li problem and the Li
evolution of solar analogs. The second is the Li dip phenomenon observed in some open clusters.
A concise description of the radial mixing due to meridional circulation and the model setup is
provided in Section 2. Sections 3 and 4 present the comparison of our model results with observations
in two aspects: Section 3 examines Li depletion in solar analogs, while Section 4 covers the Li dip.
The discussion and summary are given in Section 5.

3. LI DEPLETION FOR SOLAR ANALOGS
3.1. Model Inputs
We gather samples of solar analogs from Carlos et al. (2019), Rathsam et al. (2023), and Reggiani
et al. (2024), as depicted in Figure 1.
The distribution of Li abundance in stars near the zero-
age main sequence (ZAMS) is relatively broad, yet it predominantly clusters around 2.3 dex. It is
less than the meteorite abundance (3.3 dex; Grevesse & Sauval 1998) and the default initial value
of the model (A(Li) is 3.4 dex when Z = 0.02). Consequently, our models cannot ignore the Li
depletion in the pre-MS stage.
Here, we mainly focus on solar analogs.
Then we can take the
abundance of meteorites as the input Li abundance, i.e., A(Li)ini = 3.3 dexv. Simulations by Epstein
& Pinsonneault (2014) revealed a positive correlation between the rotation period of low-mass stars
in the MS phase and their age. For a 1.0 M⊙star, the rotation velocity is around 10 km s−1 in the
early MS, and it may decrease to about 1 km s−1 in the late MS. The equatorial rotation velocity, Ve,
of the current Sun is roughly 2 km s−1. Figure A1 shows the evolution of stellar equatorial velocities,
where initial velocities at the ZAMS VZAMS are set to 10 and 2 km s−1. The equatorial velocity Ve
exhibits faint variation during the MS stage. In terms of observation, launching velocity is nowhere
near velocity at 1 Gyr or even 0.1 Gyr; these can differ by up to a few orders of magnitude (e.g.
Rebull et al. 2018). We therefore employ multiple initial velocities in our models due to their limited
capacity to accurately simulate this complex velocity evolution. For solar analogs, our model inputs
are: 1.0 M⊙, Z = 0.02 ([Fe/H] = 0 dex), A(Li)ini = 3.30 dex, and VZAMS = 10 km s−1 (or 2 km s−1).
Given the paper’s focus on mixing processes, it is especially important to perform mixing length
coefficient, αMLT, calibrations. Following Zhang et al. (2019)’s methodology, we calibrate the model
of solar parameters incorporating meridional circulation, with results detailed in Table 1. The optimal
calibration value of 1.78 is subsequently applied to all stellar models. More detailed descriptions of
αMLT calibration in stellar models are provided in Joyce & Chaboyer (2018) and Joyce & Tayar
(2023). All of our models evolve from the pre-MS to Xc = 0.01, where Xc represents the mass
fraction of central hydrogen.

evolution trajectories predicted by our models. Two evolutionary trajectories can cover most of the

actually experienced unusual Li depletion, since it is significantly out of the A(Li)-Age correlation.

3.3. Li Abundance versus Effective Temperature
In addition, Figure 1 shows what appears to be an inverse correlation between stellar Li abundance
and effective temperature. Overall, stars with higher Teff tend to have relatively higher A(Li). This
phenomenon has been observed in many open clusters (e.g. Dumont et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2023).
Because nuclear fusion reactions inside stars are ongoing, the energy generated causes the internal
temperature and pressure to gradually increase, which in turn raises the surface temperature. Con-
sequently, stellar effective temperature gradually increases during the MS phase. This suggests that
simulating MS Li depletion based solely on the natural evolution of stars does not fully capture the
evolution of real MS Li.

4. LI DIP FOR HYADES AND PRAESEPE
4.1. Model Inputs
Unlike solar analogs, members of open clusters can often be considered as sharing cluster parame-
ters, such as metallicity and age. Consequently, we will use the cluster’s metallicity as an input for
the stellar model. Moreover, assuming that the cluster members have similar ages, we will perform
an isochronal fit to the model results. About mass, we treat it as a variable input. The relationship
between A(Li) and V sin i suggests that the Li dip is related to rotation (e.g. Boesgaard 1987; Cum-

multiple physical processes were considered (e.g. Charbonneau & Michaud 1988; Richer & Michaud
1993). Since we are primarily investigating the Li dip phenomena in the Hyades and Praesepe, we
have collected more precise velocity samples, V sin i, of the members of the two open clusters (see
Cummings et al. 2017), and use them as a reference for the input equatorial velocity. The size of
MS stars is relatively constant; therefore, the star models simulated by MESA will have a relatively
constant rotation velocity (see e.g. Figure A1). On the other hand, the rotation rate of low-mass
stars slows down over time (Barnes 2007). Since we are mainly concerned with the younger Hyades
and Praesepe, we can assume that the input velocity, VZAMS, is equal to the current stellar velocity.
Referring to the meteoritic abundance (Grevesse & Sauval 1998), we assign an input Li abundance of
3.3 dex to all stellar models, i.e., A(Li)ini = 3.3 dex. On the other hand, the metallicity of the Hyades
and Praesepe is higher than Z⊙. For reference, we will allocate the initial Li abundance according to
the default abundance composition of MESA, i.e., A(Li)ini = [Fe/H] + 3.40 dex (see Li et al. 2024).
The main properties of the Hyades and Praesepe are as follows:
1. Hyades: Age is 0.72 Gyr and [Fe/H] is +0.13 dex (Z ∼0.0265) (Netopil et al. 2016).
2. Praesepe: Age is 0.75 Gyr and [Fe/H] is +0.16 dex (Z ∼0.028) (Bossini et al. 2019).
For each cluster, we adopt its metallicity as input to evolve stellar models to the cluster’s age,
recording A(Li), Teff, and Ve for stars reaching this evolution stage. These model outputs are plotted
alongside observation data, and the model outputs are fitted. Here, the resulting curve is designated
as the isochron.
4.2. Results
Our modeling results demonstrate distinct Li depletion patterns across stellar clusters (Figure 3).
The isochrone fitting results reproduce the Li dip morphology in the Hyades and the distribution of
Li abundance on the cold side of the Li dip in Praesepe (6600 > Teff > 6200 K). For the Hyades,
sample stars within the effective temperature range of the Li dip exhibit relatively higher velocities,
while those on both sides of the Li dip have lower velocities. As Equation (4) shows, DMC ∝Ω2
0
and V = Ω0R, implying that higher rotation velocities lead to more significant Li depletion. The
velocity distribution of the samples in the Hyades presents a convex shape. Because of this feature,
our models accurately recreate the Li dip in the parameter space of the Hyades members. For the
Praesepe, however, the velocity distribution of the samples generally increases with Teff, with the
local velocity distribution showing the opposite trend only on the cold side of the Li dip. Therefore,
while our models can fit the observations on the cold side of the Li dip in Praesepe, they do not
match the Li distribution characteristics on the hot side (7000 > Teff > 6600 K).
Additionally, our models predict considerable change in Li abundance for stars with Teff < 6200 K,
resembling the observed downward trend in Li abundance. This trend stems from the behavior of Li
depletion during the pre-MS stage. The evolution of Li abundance is strongly correlated with stellar
parameters during both the MS and pre-MS phases. The Li abundance evolution during the MS is
primarily constrained by the surface convective zone in the standard model. Higher low-mass stars
with relatively thin surface convective zone maintain cooler convective zone, resulting in minimal
impact on surface Li abundance. Conversely, lower low-mass stars, which require thicker convective
zones to compensate for low internal temperature and pressure gradients, exhibit higher tempera-
tures at the base of their surface convective zones, leading to more significant Li depletion. Moreover,

5. DISCUSSION AND SUMMARY
This study investigates Li depletion mechanisms in Population I MS stars through the lens of
meridional circulation-induced radial mixing. We focus on two pivotal phenomena: (1) the persistent
Li depletion observed in solar analogs, and (2) the characteristic Li dip exhibited by mid-F type stars
in open clusters. The following is the discussion and summary of the two parts.
5.1. Li Abundance, Rotation Velocity, and Age
Current observations demonstrate that rapidly rotating solar analogs generally exhibit higher Li
abundances compared to their slower-rotating counterparts (Takeda et al. 2010; Beck et al. 2017).
Our model predictions, however, appear inconsistent with this observational trend. To reconcile this
discrepancy, we analyze the role of stellar age in Li evolution. The A(Li)-Period correlation in solar
analogs mirrors their A(Li)-Age relationship, demonstrating an inverse dependence where A(Li) de-
creases with increasing rotation period. Combined with Epstein & Pinsonneault (2014)’s established


## Page 10


10
Li et al.
Age-Period correlation for the Sun-like mass stars, we can catch a glimpse of self-consistent tripartite
relationship: young stars with rapid rotation retain higher Li abundances, which subsequently decline
as stellar spin down progresses during evolution. Figure 1 reveals two key evolutionary phases: (1)
During early MS stages, rotation velocity exerts minimal influence on Li depletion. Despite active
rotation-driven mixing during this phase, rapid angular momentum loss limits the mixing duration,
resulting in modest Li depletion (e.g. Somers & Pinsonneault 2016). (2) In later evolutionary stages,
rotation-driven mixing weakens but produces cumulative depletion effects. While instantaneous de-
pletion rates decrease, the accumulated Li depletion over time becomes significant. This temporal
evolution of mixing efficiency explains why the observed A(Li)-Rotation correlation emerges not from
instantaneous velocity dependence, but rather from the coupled evolution of rotation and Li abun-
dance over stellar lifetimes. Thus, a positive correlation between the Li abundance of solar analogs
and rotation velocity may be verified by our results and stellar evolution characteristics rather than
single model results.
The morphology of the Li dip seems to be related to the cluster age. Observation evidence from
Randich & Magrini (2021) reveals three evolutionary phases: (1) Young clusters (e.g., NGC 3532, ∼
0.4 Gyr): The Li dip has appeared, but the depression is shallow, and its location (temperature range)
may be adjusted with metallicity. For example, the Li dip in a high-metallicity cluster corresponds to
higher-mass member stars (tending toward the hot side, see e.g., Randich et al. 2020). (2) Middle-aged
clusters (e.g., NGC 2420, ∼1.7 Gyr): The depth and extent of the Li dip become more significant, and
the depression may shift toward lower-mass (cooler) stars with age. (3) Extremely old clusters (e.g.,
Berkeley 39, ∼6 Gyr): The Li dip tends to stabilize, indicating that the mixing process is complete
early in stellar evolution. These evolutionary characteristics can be explained by the results presented
in the previous paragraph. In the early stages, the rotation velocity is high. Although rotational
mixing is strong, Li depletion has just begun, so the attenuation of Li is not significant.
Soon
afterward, the velocity decreases, and the total loss of Li becomes more pronounced, making the Li
dip more distinct. In the later stages, the rotation velocity is very low, and Li is almost no longer
depleted, so the Li dip stabilizes. It is important to note that the depth and location of the Li dip are
influenced by a variety of factors, including the temperature of the star (i.e., mass), metallicity, and
the efficiency of the internal mixing process (e.g. Delgado Mena et al. 2015). Therefore, the change
in the depth of the Li dip over time is not a simple linear relationship but is influenced by a variety
of complex factors. This means that the exact age dependence of the Li dip still needs to be verified
with larger samples.
5.2. Li Depletion
The solar Li abundance predicted by our models is approximately three times higher than the
Sun’s current Li abundance, yet it aligns well with the Li distribution of solar analogs. This suggests
that additional MS Li depletion processes, as discussed in Section 1, need to be incorporated into
our models to account for the solar Li problem.
Given the multitude of physical processes that
significantly contribute to Li depletion during the MS, accurately predicting solar Li abundance
requires a nuanced consideration of the cumulative effects of these processes. Relying solely on a
single physical process to explain Li abundance does not align with the complexity of the actual
physical context.
Starting from the velocity distribution of low-mass stars in the MS phase, the evolution of our
models (1.0 M⊙and Z = 0.02) is consistent with the observed sample distribution. The effects of

Because the hot side region of Li dip is mixed with some high rotation velocity samples for most
clusters, our models generally cannot fully explain the Li distribution of all sample stars in this
region. Additional processes should be considered. For instance, Charbonneau & Michaud (1988)
proposed that radiative acceleration may inhibit Li depletion in the high-velocity samples on the hot
Li dip side. Unlike the hot side, the velocity on the cold side of the Li dip in these clusters generally
correlates with changes in Teff, which can be characterized by the Li abundance distribution predicted
by our models. In summary, our models can reproduce the Li abundance distribution on the cold
side of the Li dip in open clusters but can only explain the hot Li dip side in some clusters.
It is important to note that the above discussion is based on V sini, with the input of V adopted
as V sini, while the real rotation velocity is V . This is because it is not possible to accurately define
sini observationally. The Li dip regions that cannot be explained by our single models may also be
affected by this effect. For example, low V sini samples appear in the central region of the NGC
3680 Li dip. In addition, the modeling results are limited by the assumptions of uniform age and
metallicity. As a result, we do not account for individual differences, which may be a key factor in
addressing the model’s limitations.
