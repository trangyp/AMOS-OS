## Page 2


Cardiac digital twins hold great promise for personalized medicine, but they currently depend on complex constitu-
tive models of tissue mechanics that are often over-parameterized for the clinical context. To address this, we introduce
CHESRA (Cardiac Hyperelastic Evolutionary Symbolic Regression Algorithm), a physics-informed machine learning
framework that automatically derives simple strain energy functions from multiple experimental data sources. Using
a normalizing loss function, CHESRA identified two new functions with only three and four parameters, respectively.
These functions achieve high data fitting accuracy in experimental scenarios while enabling more consistent parameter
estimation than state-of-the-art approaches, both in tissue benchmarks and 3D simulations. By combining biophysical
constraints with data-driven discovery, CHESRA demonstrates how physics-informed learning can generate accurate,
personalizable models for advancing cardiac digital twins and clinical decision-making.

The increasing amounts of healthcare data and computational power are enabling new forms of cardiac medicine,
with the cardiac digital twin as one of the leading concepts [1, 2]. Essentially, the cardiac digital twin is a digital version
of a patient’s heart that enables the testing and optimization of medical treatments in silico, before they are applied
to the patient in the physical world [3]. Furthermore, a cardiac digital twin should be “dynamically and continuously
updated with data from its physical twin” [4]. A requirement that separates cardiac digital twins from typical patient-
specific simulation models that are updated only once. In practice, the updating of digital twins often takes the form
of mathematical inverse problems, in which the internal parameters are updated from the available data. Thus, a good
cardiac digital twin needs to meet a dual set of requirements: being complex enough to accurately model a patient’s
cardiac physiology (low bias), while also being uniquely parameterizable (low variance) to reflect a particular patient’s
physiology. However, the bias-variance trade-off from machine learning tells us that the two requirements are in
opposition [5]. Increasing digital twin complexity can improve physical accuracy (reducing bias), at the cost of higher
parameter variance, which means that the digital twin becomes more sensitive to small fluctuations in the data and
hence more difficult to personalize. Conversely, simpler digital twins with lower parameter variance may have higher
bias, and thereby miss key biophysical features needed for accurate medical decision-making. This issue is particularly
salient for heart tissue elasticity models, which are key components of the cardiac simulation models [6, 7] that enable
cardiac digital twins, and are crucial to understanding heart disease states, such as fibrosis [6], myocardial infarction
[7], and diastolic heart failure [8].
Traditionally, resting myocardial elasticity has been modeled using hyperelastic strain energy functions (SEFs)
[9–12] that quantifies the amount of energy stored during a deformation. The data that inform these SEFs [13–16]
have shown that healthy heart tissue possesses a sophisticated microstructure, characterized by interconnected cells
with a locally prevailing orientation, typically modeled as “fibers”, as well as cell bundles organized into sheets.
These microstructural features cause orthotropic and nonlinear elastic behavior at the tissue scale [9]. Consequently,
human-expert designed SEFs are quite complex [9–12], consisting of seven to twelve tunable material parameters
that are connected to strain measures via nonlinear formulations involving exponential functions. Unfortunately, the
same complexity that enables the expert-designed SEFs to closely fit experimental data has proven to be problematic
in cardiac digital twin applications, where the available patient data is typically noisy and sparse. Indeed, previous
cardiac simulation studies have reported difficulties in uniquely identifying cardiac SEF parameters due to parameter
correlations [17] or high parameter variance [7]. This has led to simplified representations of cardiac elasticity
being used in practice; including transversely isotropic SEFs [7, 18], or orthotropic SEFs, for which only a reduced
set of parameters is fitted [19–23]. While such ad-hoc model simplifications increase the feasibility of parameter
identification, the utility of the resulting models for reproducing measured data is an open question. Furthermore,
fitting only a reduced set of a SEF’s parameters in a digital twin setting involves ethical dilemmas. Any choice of
default values for the remaining parameters risks creating systematic bias toward particular patient groups.
Inspired by the shortcomings of human expert-designed SEFs, researchers have begun to develop machine learning
methods that automatically design cardiac SEFs directly from experimental data [24–26]; including spline methods [24],
and constitutive neural networks [25–28]. SEFs designed using splines and constitutive neural networks have achieved
excellent model-data fits [24–28], highlighting the accuracy of data-driven cardiac SEFs for fitting experimental data.
Also, in contrast to the human expert-designed cardiac SEFs [9–12], the complexity of data-driven SEFs can be
optimized via penalized regression to learn simple models with relatively few parameters [25, 26]. While promising,
these initial results lack validation in external experimental datasets, leaving open the question of model generalization.
A further issue relates to the form of the data-derived SEFs, as the SEFs designed by constitutive neural networks
follow a prescribed additive form [25, 26]. Accordingly, the intriguing possibility of designing even simpler SEFs by
exploring a broader search space remains unresolved. Finally, previous data-driven SEF studies [24–26] are missing
systematic evaluations of how well the data-driven SEF parameters can be estimated in 3D organ-level models, which
is crucial for the personalization of cardiac digital twins.
In this study we introduce the Cardiac Hyperelastic Evolutionary Symbolic Regression Algorithm (CHESRA),
a novel physics informed machine learning (PI-ML) framework for automatically designing low-complexity hypere-
lastic SEFs. Important for CHESRA’s approach is an innovative normalizing loss function that enables CHESRA to
simultaneously learn from multiple experimental datasets, while maintaining model parsimony via the use of a func-
tion length penalty. Unlike previous automated discovery approaches [25, 26] that prioritize fitting single, high-fidelity
datasets, the CHESRA framework is designed to discover parsimonious SEFs from heterogeneous experiments. This
multi-source training is intended to serve as a form of regularization, reducing the effect of lab-specific artifacts and
helping identify mathematical forms with greater generalizability, which we consider a prerequisite for developing

reliable cardiac digital twins in diverse patient populations. Using CHESRA, we identified two novel low-complexity
polynomial cardiac SEFs (𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2) that fit data from four experimental studies [13–16] with high accuracy, while
using fewer free parameters than state-of-the-art orthotropic SEFs [9–12, 25]. We also confirmed the advantage of
𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2 for parameter estimation in an experimental tissue-data benchmark, and for 𝜓CH1 in a biventricular simula-
tion benchmark based on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Overall, our results show that CHESRA can be
used to automatically design low-complexity SEFs from experimental data and that CHESRA’s SEF 𝜓CH1 improves
parameter estimation in organ-scale simulations. This has implications for the field of healthcare digital twins as a
whole, paving the way for low-complexity digital twin models that are designed a-priori to satisfy an optimal balance
between accuracy and personalizability.

validation in a personalized biventricular mechanics framework. a, CHESRA takes as input experimental datasets
of myocardial mechanics with equibiaxial, biaxial, and triaxial deformation protocols. b, CHESRA combines the
mechanical data with the physical principles of material symmetry and frame invariance to design a parsimonious
strain energy function with material parameters 𝒑. During each CHESRA iteration, functions are selected based on a
fitness criterion consisting of function length 𝑙eq and goodness of fit terms 𝑓GoF balanced by the length penalty 𝛼. The

CHESRA is a physics-informed evolutionary framework that manipulates symbolic representations of cardiac
SEFs to fit experimental observations (Figure 1) while minimizing SEF complexity [29]. CHESRA takes one or more
experimental datasets of myocardial stress-strain relations as input (Supplementary Table 1) and evolves a population
of SEFs according to the fitness function
𝑓fit = 𝑓GoF( 𝒑) + 𝛼𝑙eq.
Here, 𝑓GoF quantifies the goodness of fit of each SEF’s stress-strain relation to the experimental data, with each SEF’s

CHESRA’s penalty parameter effectively controls the complexity of elastic energy functions
We conducted a systematic hyperparameter test using the Dokos [16] shear dataset (Supplementary Table 1) to evaluate
the efficacy of the length penalty 𝛼and identify optimal genetic hyperparameters 𝑝mate, 𝑝mutate, 𝑝reduce, 𝑝extend for
further experiments. This was done in two steps. First, we tested eight 𝛼values (0.0, 10−4, 2 × 10−4, 3 × 10−4,
5 × 10−4, 10−3, 5 × 10−3, 10−2) and set the genetic hyperparameters to values from the set (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75). This
resulted in 44 ×8 = 2048 hyperparameter combinations. Pre-set values were used for other CHESRA hyperparameters
(Supplementary Table 2). The results, plotted in Figure 2a, show a clear trend: increasing 𝛼reduced SEF complexity
(𝑙eq) but worsened the goodness of fit 𝑓GoF. For 𝛼between 1 × 10−4 and 1 × 10−3, SEFs clustered in the lower left
corner of the plot, with near-optimal 𝑓GoF ≤0.01 and modest complexity (𝑙eq ≤30). In contrast, 𝛼≥5 × 10−3 yielded
simple SEFs (𝑙eq ≤10) but poorer fits ( 𝑓GoF ≥0.01), while 𝛼= 0 (no penalty) produced more complex SEFs (median
𝑙eq = 35).
Next, we identified optimal genetic hyperparameter combinations with 𝛼fixed at 2 × 10−4, chosen from the lower-
left corner of Figure 2a to balance SEF complexity and goodness of fit. We ran CHESRA twice for each of the 44 = 256
genetic parameter combinations and recorded the average 𝑓GoF (Figure 2b). Combinations with 𝑝reduce < 𝑝extend (lower

Cross-validation tests confirm the utility of CHESRA’s functions for generalizing to novel
data
To assess the generalizability of CHESRA’s SEFs, we conducted cross-validation tests under two distinct scenarios:
(1) a leave-one-out setup, where the data were partitioned into five training sets and one held-out test set, and (2) a
single-fit setup, employing one training set and five test sets. Across these scenarios, we evaluated 24 experimental
configurations, spanning all six possible training and test data variations of datasets from Supplementary Table 1 and
four length penalty values 𝛼∈{0, 10−4, 10−3, 10−2}. For each cross-validation fold, we ran CHESRA five times, with
the best-performing SEF obtained from the training data used for goodness of fit calculations with the test data.
First, we compared the out-of-distribution performance of leave-one-out and single-fit SEFs by computing the
median 𝑓GoF across withheld datasets (Figure 3a). For 𝛼= 0, median errors were negligible ( 𝑓GoF < 0.01) in both
cases. However, at 𝛼= 10−2, single-fit SEFs exhibited substantially higher errors ( 𝑓GoF = 0.53), whereas leave-one-
out SEFs maintained strong performance ( 𝑓GoF = 0.021). We further evaluated SEF generalizability by comparing
leave-one-out SEFs to single-fit SEFs, where the latter were trained and tested on the same dataset (Figure 3b–d).
SEFs derived under weak length penalties (𝛼≤10−4) achieved superior fits ( 𝑓GoF ≤0.026) compared to those with
stronger penalties (𝛼≥10−3), a trend supported by visual inspection of the fitted curves (Figure 3c, d; Supplementary
Figure 8). Nevertheless, the gap between single-fit and leave-one-out 𝑓GoF was reduced for the largest length penalty
𝛼= 10−2, indicating that shorter SEFs were less specialized to their particular training datasets. Also, the Yin Biaxial
dataset [13] exhibited the highest fitting errors, likely due to missing low-strain data [9]. Overall, the results suggest
that the relatively longer SEFs with many free parameters could generalize effectively and that the shorter SEFs with
fewer free parameters (𝛼= 10−2) could also generalize if multiple datasets were used to derive them.
The functional forms of CHESRA’s SEFs are detailed in Supplementary Table 3. For 𝛼≥10−3, leave-one-out
SEFs exhibited greater consistency in structure compared to single-fit SEFs, reflecting CHESRA’s robustness when
trained on diverse datasets. This is further supported by the convergence of leave-one-out SEF lengths around the
mean length for 𝛼> 0 (Figure 3b). Additionally, while single-fit SEFs occasionally incorporated exponential terms,
leave-one-out SEFs with 𝛼≥10−3 did not, underscoring their structural consistency. Taken together, these findings
demonstrate that CHESRA can produce simple yet generalizable SEFs when optimized with an appropriate length
penalty 𝛼and sufficient training data.
CHESRA generates simple cardiac elastic energy functions
In order to fully exploit the information contained within the experimental data (Supplementary Table 1) we fed all of
the datasets collectively into CHESRA and set the function length penalty to a range of values (Section 9). For every
value of the penalty, we ran the CHESRA three times, each time starting with a different randomly generated set of
functions. For each set of runs, we identified and recorded the SEFs that achieved the best fitness score (Supplementary
Table 4). As expected, we observed that increases in the function length penalty 𝛼led to shorter and simpler functions,
with a corresponding rise in fitting error. Surprisingly, the decreases in function length were quite substantial, as the
function length of 24 at 𝛼= 0 was reduced to 7 at 𝛼= 10−3. Furthermore, for 𝛼≤5 × 10−3 the models fit closely to
the data, with only minor discrepancies shown in Figure 4 and Supplementary Figure 1. For 𝛼≤5 × 10−3 the output
SEFs contained three or more mechanical invariants, which meant that they could support two independent axes of
anisotropy, suitable for modeling orthotropic cardiac tissue. Of these SEFs, we selected the two most parsimonious for

We conducted a second parameter variability benchmark using eight 3D ventricular simulation models, each testing
one of the SEFs 𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2, 𝜓HO or 𝜓MA in one of two scenarios, ex vivo or in vivo (Methods In vivo and ex
vivo simulation scenarios). The ex vivo scenario involved laboratory pressure-volume measurements spanning a wide
pressure range under controlled conditions [31], whereas the in vivo scenario involved clinically derived pressure
and volume data over a narrower physiological range with more complex in vivo conditions [32]. In the benchmark,
we selected 𝜓HO and 𝜓MA as representatives for the state-of-the-art SEFs (Methods State-of-the-art strain energy
functions) due the recent development of 𝜓MA and widespread use of 𝜓HO at the time of our study. The geometry of
the simulation models was based on a mid-diastolic patient MRI, including the left and right ventricles (Figure 1c)
and rule-based fiber and fiber-sheet directions (Supplementary Figure 7).
In the first stage of our benchmark we estimated a separate pressure-free geometry for each SEF-scenario combi-
nation (Methods Estimation of pressure-free geometries), to enable the consistent calculation of displacements from
a stress-free reference. We also simultaneously obtained benchmark values for all SEF parameter sets. Both pressure-
free geometries and benchmark SEF parameters were obtained by optimizing each simulation model’s left ventricular
volumes either to the patient data [32] (in vivo scenario), or the Klotz [31] empirical relationship (ex vivo scenario).
The resulting pressure-free geometries are shown in Figure 7b for the in vivo scenario, and the benchmark material
parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 8. In the ex vivo scenario, we note that all four SEFs (𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2,
𝜓HO or 𝜓MA) were able to closely replicate the target volume curve (Figure 7a). In the in vivo scenario all four SEFs
were within 3 ml of the target volumes. However, only the Holzapfel-Ogden SEF 𝜓HO was able to replicate the concave
shape of the in vivo diastolic volume curve. This could be due to the 𝜓HO SEF’s relatively complex form with eight
material parameters, which allowed for more flexible behaviour than the more simple SEFs 𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2, and 𝜓MA.
Next we generated synthetic diastolic displacement data as optimization targets (Methods 3D ventricular mechanics
finite element framework), using the pressure free geometries and benchmark material parameter values. To simulate
real-world noise, we corrupted the displacements by adding random noise vectors with magnitudes equal to 50% of
the local displacement. We then optimized each SEF’s material parameters to the corresponding ex vivo and in vivo
noisy displacements using twenty parameter initializations (Methods In silico parameter variability benchmark with
3D ventricular simulation models). We sampled each set of initial points using a Latin hypercube design, with bounds
spanning one order of magnitude around the benchmark parameter values. Figure 7c shows the results. Of the four
SEFs, 𝜓CH1 provided the most consistent and accurate parameter estimates, which we expected due to 𝜓CH1 having the
least number of parameters to fit. For 𝜓CH2 the parameter estimates were accurate in the ex vivo scenario, but exhibited
greater variability in the in vivo scenario. Nevertheless, the median parameter estimates for 𝜓CH2 were relatively close
to their benchmark values, which meant that they could be approximated by aggregating data from many optimizations.
In contrast, the estimated parameters of 𝜓MA and 𝜓HO could consistently differ from their benchmark values, indicating
the presence of local minima in the corresponding optimization landscapes that hinder the acquisition of accurate
parameter estimates, even after aggregating results from multiple optimizations.

To compare the computational efficiency of the SEFs 𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2, 𝜓MA, and 𝜓HO we collected run-time data for
the tissue and 3D biventricular simulation benchmarks (Fig 8). The tissue benchmark was performed in serial on a

in serial on a high-performance computing node equipped with an AMD EPYC 7542 32-core processor (2.9 GHz, 64

Discussion
This study introduces the Cardiac Hyperelastic Evolutionary Symbolic Regression Algorithm (CHESRA) as a novel
physics informed machine learning (PI-ML) framework for discovering parsimonious constitutive laws in cardiac
tissue mechanics. CHESRA unites biophysical modeling with data-driven discovery by embedding the physical princi-
ples of frame invariance, material symmetry, and hyperelastic energy conservation directly into a symbolic regression
framework. Through this integration, CHESRA restricts the space of possible SEFs to physically admissible mod-
els while learning SEF forms directly from experimental data. Applying this framework to cardiac tissue mechanics,
CHESRA uncovered two previously unreported SEFs, 𝜓CH1 and 𝜓CH2, that balance parsimony with predictive accu-
racy. Both SEFs reproduced experimental stress-strain data from multiple studies [13–16] with fidelity comparable to,
or exceeding, that of established orthotropic SEFs [9–12, 25], despite relying on only three or four material parame-
ters. Systematic cross-validation confirmed the capacity of CHESRA’s SEFs to generalize across independent datasets
(Figure 3), while benchmark analyses (Figure 5 and Figure 6) revealed sharply defined parameter landscapes indicative
of unique and stable parameter estimates. When embedded in 3D biventricular mechanics simulations, 𝜓CH1 demon-
strated markedly reduced parameter variability relative to state-of-the-art SEFs (Figure 7), underscoring its potential
to enhance the robustness and personalizability of cardiac digital twins, where frequent parameter updates are required
[4].
A key strength of our study is the simplicity of CHESRA’s SEFs (𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2), which involves only three to four
tunable material parameters and has simple polynomial forms. Such simple low-parameter models already have a well-
established history of use within cardiac electrophysiology [33, 34]. Here, we have algorithmically derived similar
models specifically for cardiac mechanics. In contrast, the state-of-the-art SEFs [9–12, 25] have five or more parameters
and more complex mathematical forms involving exponential functions (see State-of-the-art strain energy functions in
Methods). This complexity arises partly from differences in how these SEFs were conceived, whether through human
expertise or data-driven approaches. The human expert-designed SEFs [9–12] were designed to fit experimental data
and the designer’s choice of biophysical considerations, but lacked an automated simplification mechanism. As a
consequence, the SEFs [9–12] have issues with high dimensionality and parameter interdependence, limiting their
identifiability from patient data [7]. Conversely, the purely data-driven SEFs [24] can fit experimental observations
but can lack interpretability [24] and risk overfitting to their training datasets [24–26]. Recognizing these limitations,
CHESRA utilizes a physics-informed evolutionary process to learn mathematically interpretable closed-form SEFs
from multiple datasets, while also balancing goodness of fit and model simplicity.
While high-parameter SEFs like the Holzapfel-Ogden SEF [9] can be made identifiable by fixing certain parameters
to literature values [20–25], this approach introduces a dual burden. First, it requires a-priori sensitivity analysis to
decide which parameters to fix, making the modeling pipeline more complex and less automated. Second, fixing
parameters to pre-determined values introduces a structural bias that may lead to unequal healthcare outcomes among
differing patient groups. In contrast, for the CHESRA-derived SEFs (𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2) the parsimonious functional forms
promote unique estimation of all parameters, working towards digital twins with fully personalized material properties.
CHESRA builds upon previous work on evolutionary symbolic regression (ESR) for SEF design [35, 36], and
represents the first application of ESR to the discovery of cardiac SEFs. Alternative data-driven approaches include
sparse regression [28], constitutive neural networks [25, 26], and data-driven reduction from pre-specified forms
[37], each of which is constrained to either linear combinations of pre-defined basis functions or a-priori specified
functional forms. By contrast, ESR traverses a substantially broader space of mathematical structures, combining basic
elements through products, nesting, and transcendental functions in ways that competing methods [25, 26, 28, 37] do
not consider. It is worth noting that while the discovered SEFs (𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2) maintained low parameter counts, they
preserved the essential nonlinearities of the myocardium. Simplification to a linear elastic form was rejected by the
CHESRA framework during the evolutionary process, as linear SEFs are unable to reconcile the low-stiffness ”toe
region” with the high-stiffness ”fiber recruitment region” observed across all five experimental datasets.
In the cross-validation tests (Figure 3), we observed that the generalizability of CHESRA’s functions improved
when we used experimental data from multiple sources [13–16]. This is in-line with pure machine learning approaches
for classification and regression, which typically see improvements in validation accuracy when the amount and variety
of their training data is increased. Our results confirm this trend for the first time in PI-ML algorithms for cardiac
SEF design, with training data intentionally spanning porcine [16], canine [13, 14], and human [15] sources, thereby
relaxing the standard i.i.d. assumption of cross-validation in favour of cross-species diversity. The degree to which the
observed generalizability reflects the learning of species-invariant physical principles warrants further investigation,
15


## Page 16


but it suggests promise for translation into diverse clinical populations. In the future, the collection of further high-
quality experimental datasets of tissue mechanics could allow for further refinements of CHESRA’s SEFs, with
potential increases in SEF generalizability and data fitting accuracy. In particular, including data from pathological
tissues into CHESRA would be an interesting future direction, as it would allow for more broadly validated SEFs and
pave the way for pathology-specific clinical applications.
Beyond generalizability, another essential aspect of model reliability is parameter uniqueness, which ensures that
fitted parameters reflect true tissue properties rather than artifacts of the optimization process. As shown in Figure 5,
the CHESRA-derived functions (𝜓CH1 and 𝜓CH2) produced unique and stable parameter estimates across repeated
optimizations to shear experiment data [15, 16]. This is in contrast to traditional SEF formulations [9–12] that exhibited
broad parameter variability, consistent with previously reported experiments [37]. By visualizing the goodness of fit
landscapes (Figure 6), we demonstrate the source of the parameter variability: while the state-of-the-art SEFs possess
flat, non-unique optimization landscapes, the CHESRA-discovered SEFs exhibit well-defined minima (Figure 6). This
suggests that symbolic regression can successfully right-size the constitutive law to the available data, a critical step
for robust clinical personalization. Indeed, the CHESRA functions’ improved parameter identifiability addresses long-
standing concerns in cardiac modeling, where parameter non-uniqueness has been linked to poor reproducibility and
uncertainty in model personalization [38]. By constraining SEF complexity through a function-length penalty and a
physics-informed invariant framework, CHESRA effectively narrows the solution landscape, reducing the prevalence
of local minima and ill-conditioned parameter spaces.
In the 3D biventricular simulation setting, we confirmed that the CHESRA function 𝜓CH1 had less parameter
variability than state-of-the-art SEFs when fit to synthetic 3D displacement data (Figure 7). From a clinical perspective,
reduced parameter variability translates directly into improved personalization reliability and predictive stability.
Indeed, in personalized cardiac mechanics models, small variations in estimated tissue parameters can produce
large differences in simulated ventricular deformation or pressure–volume relationships [39], potentially confounding
diagnostic or prognostic conclusions. The parsimony and identifiability of CHESRA-derived SEFs mitigate this risk,
paving the way for digital twins that can be confidently individualized to each patient’s imaging and hemodynamic
data. Such individualized digital twins have potential applications in disease prediction, prevention, and management;
for example, assessing the progression of myocardial fibrosis [7, 40], detecting cardiomyopathy [41], optimizing
ventricular assist device settings [42], forecasting response to cardiac resynchronization therapy [43] or in silico drug
safety assessment [44]. Furthermore, CHESRA’s invariant-based symbolic framework can be directly applied to other
soft tissues and disease contexts where mechanics play a diagnostic or prognostic role, such as vascular remodeling in
hypertension [45], lung compliance in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [46], tumor stiffness in oncology [47], or
cerebral tissue deformation in traumatic brain injury [48]. By enabling the derivation of parsimonious and interpretable
constitutive laws from limited data, CHESRA offers a generalizable PI-ML approach for building dedicated SEFs
for digital twins across multiple organ systems. These SEFs can support inverse problem solving, and the design of
personalized therapeutic strategies. Real-time simulations and real-time data digital twin updates are another exciting
possibility, which could be facilitated by switching the current slow and expensive FEM solver to faster neural network
based solvers [49].
We note that model fidelity is an important issue for cardiac digital twins, as missing crucial aspects of a patient’s
physiology could potentially lead to suboptimal medical decisions. In our study, we balanced this consideration with the
need for benchmark simplicity for comparing SEFs. Indeed, the 3D biventricular simulations presented here follow the
established practice of using simplified organ-level geometries to validate material law stability and identifiability [21,
50]. In the ex vivo benchmark, our modeling assumptions closely matched the experimental conditions [31]. However,
in the in vivo benchmark, our setup was not able to incorporate the complex interactions between the ventricles,
pericardium, and atria that contribute to physiological in vivo motion. Consequently, we included unphysiological
low tissue compressibility in the in vivo simulations for numerical reasons. We note that the ability of the Holzapfel-
Ogden SEF to match the in vivo pressure-volume curve (Figure 7) demonstrates a key advantage of high-parameter
SEFs, namely that they can more flexibly accommodate for mis-specified modeling assumptions. Nevertheless, this
flexibility may come at a cost in the clinical setting: a high-parameter SEF that absorbs modeling errors into its material
parameters risks conflating tissue mechanics with unmodeled physiology.
Before concluding, we acknowledge several limitations of our study and the CHESRA algorithm. Firstly, we were
not able to guarantee that any boundary value problems involving CHESRA’s SEFs will have unique solutions. This
issue can be addressed in future studies by explicitly designing polyconvex SEFs [51]. While CHESRA does not
enforce polyconvexity, it does, however, promote convex-like behaviour by using squared strain invariant functions,
16


## Page 17


and via restrictions on the functional forms of the SEFs; namely that all the material parameters are positive, and the
binary operators are restricted to “+” and “×”. Nevertheless, CHESRA’s SEFs satisfy several other desirable theoretical
properties, including material frame indifference, material symmetry, and stress-free undeformed configurations. A
further limitation of CHESRA is that the material parameters 𝒑lack a-priori physiological interpretations. This could
be addressed in the future by pre-specifying terms with physiological significance in the symbolic regression. The
sparsity of available experimental datasets limited our ability to thoroughly assess the generalizability of CHESRA’s
output functions. In particular, we only had access to two 3D datasets [15, 16], and the tissue experiment benchmark
results (Figure 5) lacked validation in datasets not used to derive the CHESRA SEFs (𝜓CH1, 𝜓CH2). If additional 3D
experimental data were available, then CHESRA’s output SEFs could be further refined. Currently, the lack of clinical
tissue experiment data means that only the parameters of CHESRA’s SEFs can be personalized rather than the entire
forms of the SEFs. However, this limitation may be alleviated in the future by extension of the CHESRA algorithm to
clinical data, or advances in human stem cell derived cardiomyocyte technologies [52] that will allow for individualized
patient tissue mechanics data collection. Finally, the data updates in personalized 3D biventricular models were limited
to a single MRI study, estimated pressure values, and synthetic displacement data. However, the use of synthetic data
allowed for ground truth parameter values and parameter error calculations. Future research could test SEFs in more
complex digital-twin scenarios with multiple data updates and medical image-derived motion data.
Additionally, we acknowledge limitations related to clinical data sparsity in the 3D biventricular simulations. First,
the in vivo pressure-volume targets were not recorded simultaneously for the specific patient but were fitted from
established hemodynamic curves. Second, the absence of CINE-MRI data precluded a direct comparison between
predicted and actual geometric displacements, limiting the validation of the 3D biventricular model to global volume
and pressure targets. Third, as this proof-of-concept was conducted on a single biventricular geometry, further studies
across diverse heart shapes and pathologies are required to confirm generalizability. Finally, the myocardial fiber
architecture was generated using rule-based methods rather than patient-specific diffusion tensor imaging, which may
have affected the accuracy of our material parameter estimates. While these constraints reflect common limitations in
clinical data availability, they represent key hurdles that must be addressed to transition to a fully validated clinical
digital twin. Finally, we note that the uncertainties inherent in the model generation process were not formally assessed
in terms of their impact on the SEFs’ performance. While the parsimony of the discovered SEFs was intended to
provide a buffer against model uncertainties, the sensitivity of the resulting SEF parameters to such uncertainties
warrants future investigation. Integrating symbolic regression with probabilistic or Bayesian uncertainty quantification
frameworks [39], along with Markov Chain Monte Carlo techniques, can help to fully characterize the reliability of
discovered SEFs in the presence of real-world clinical data errors.
In summary, CHESRA embodies the principles of physics-informed machine learning for personalized digital
medicine. By merging symbolic learning with physical laws of elasticity, it achieves a balance between accuracy,
generalizability, and interpretability that is crucial for the clinical uptake of biomedical digital twins. More broadly,
CHESRA demonstrates how embedding mechanistic insight into machine learning can accelerate the development of
transparent, data-efficient, and clinically actionable digital health technologies.

Experimental data used to design elastic energy functions
To algorithmically design SEFs, we used data from four tissue studies of cardiac mechanics [13–16]. All four studies
used small tissue samples with minimal variation in the local fiber and or sheet directions. By modeling these
variations as uniform [9], we were able to directly evaluate the invariants (Equation 3) for each dataset, allowing for a
computationally efficient comparison of stress and strains between SEF and data. Three of the tissue studies included
(equi-)biaxial stretch protocols [13–15], and two studies included triaxial shear protocols [15, 16]. The equibiaxial
protocols involved uniform stretching of the myocardium in both the fiber and cross-fiber directions [14], while the
biaxial protocols employed distinct stretch ratios for each direction [13–15]. For the triaxial shear protocols, simple
shear was applied in either the fiber/sheet, fiber/sheet-normal, or sheet/sheet-normal planes [15, 16]. The biaxial,
equibiaxial, and triaxial protocols are demonstrated in Figure 1a. Two of our data-source studies [14, 15] included
two distinct experimental protocols, which gave a total of six distinct experimental studies and mechanical protocol
combinations, which we henceforth refer to as datasets.
To obtain numerical data for each dataset, we digitized the data plots found in the experimental reports [13–
16], using WebPlotDigitizer software. We did not normalize or post-process any of the digitized data, as CHESRA
performed normalization internally (section Fitness evaluation). Supplementary Table 1 summarizes the technical
details regarding each dataset, including the stress and strain measures used. Note that the Novak equibiaxial dataset
[14] contains independent measurements from four regions of the heart (sub-endocardium, mid-myocardium, sub-
epicardium, and mid-septum) of two specimens. This information is reflected in the subsets column of Supplementary
Table 1, with the Novak equibiaxial dataset containing eight distinct subsets, and every other dataset containing only a
single subset. To account for the differences in material properties among subsets, we calculated separate sets of SEF
material parameters 𝒑for each data subset within the CHESRA algorithm.

Selection
During each iteration, CHESRA selected a new SEF population of size 𝑛ind for breeding by using a combination of
elitism and random tournament. Selection by elitism meant that the best 𝑛hof SEFs (with the lowest fitness scores) were
automatically selected for the next generation. This ensured the preservation of the best solutions. To promote genetic
variability, CHESRA selected a further 𝑛evo = 𝑛ind −𝑛hof individuals by random tournament, with each tournament
consisting of randomly choosing two SEFs and selecting the SEF with the best fitness.
Evolutionary changes
After selection, the 𝑛evo SEFs selected by tournament were randomly modified by evolutionary operators to generate
new candidate SEFs and thereby explore the space of SEF designs. The evolutionary operators were mating, mutation,
reduction, and extension (Supplementary Figure 6), applied in this order and each occurring with probability 𝑝mate,
𝑝mutate, 𝑝reduce, and 𝑝extend, respectively.
The mating operator randomly swapped subtrees from two SEFs. For mutations, an operator (×, +) or symbolic
parameter (invariants and material parameters) was replaced with a random node of the same type. A function reduction
resulted in an operator or exponential function node being randomly chosen and deleted along with its dependent
subtree. Finally, we extended functions by adding either an exponential operator plus a symbolic parameter, or an
operator plus an invariant with probabilities 0.2, 0.4, 0.4, respectively.
New Generation
During each CHESRA iteration, a new generation of SEFs was formed by combining the 𝑛evo evolved SEFs together
with the 𝑛hof elite SEFs. Afterwards, we replaced duplicate SEFs with new random SEFs to ensure genetic diversity.
The next iteration of CHESRA would then start again at fitness evaluation until a total of 𝑛gen SEF generations had
been reached.

In vivo and ex vivo simulation scenarios
To thoroughly test our SEF’s, we considered two separate simulation scenarios, representing in vivo and ex vivo
conditions. The in vivo simulations were calibrated to match LV pressures and CMR-derived LV volumes from late
diastole [32]. In contrast, the ex vivo simulations were calibrated to match the empirically observed end-diastolic-
pressure volume relation (EDPVR) of Klotz et al [31]. Calculating the Klotz EDPVR required a single end-diastolic
pressure and volume measurement, which we obtained from the in vivo data. We then calculated the rest of the
EDVPR by using the curve fitting method outlined by Klotz et al [31]. Note that the in vivo LV pressure-volume
data is limited to a narrow range of physiological blood pressures observed in diastole, whereas the ex vivo EDPVR
contains LV volumes spanning a wider pressure range, from the end-diastolic value of 2.19 kPa all the way to 0 kPa.
The in vivo data, therefore, represented a more challenging simulation scenario, as the true in vivo zero-pressure LV
volume was unknown, and the data were influenced by more complex physical conditions, including the effects of
atrial contraction and acceleration forces. In contrast, the ex vivo Klotz EDPVR represented controlled laboratory
conditions where the left ventricle was slowly inflated via an external fluid pump with the valve plane completely fixed
[31], thereby excluding atrial and acceleration forces. We simulated the motion of the ventricles in both in vivo and ex
vivo simulation scenarios using the same elastic model, albeit with different boundary conditions and compressibility
properties tailored to each scenario. The elastic model is explained in more detail in the next section.
