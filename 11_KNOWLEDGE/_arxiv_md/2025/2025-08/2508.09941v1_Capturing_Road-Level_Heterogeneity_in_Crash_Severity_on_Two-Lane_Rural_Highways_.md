Capturing Road-Level Heterogeneity in Crash Severity on Two-Lane Rural 
Highways: A Multilevel Mixed-Effects Approach 
 
Mahdi Azhdaria,b, Ali Tavakoli Kashania,b, Saeideh Amirifara,b, Amirhossein Taherid, Gerd 
Müllerd  
a Road Safety Research Centre, Iran University of Science and Technology, Tehran, Iran

Abstract 
Accurately modeling crash severity on rural two-lane roads is essential for effective safety 
management, yet standard single level approaches often overlook unobserved heterogeneity across 
road segments. In this study, we analyze 19 956 crash records from 99 rural roads in Iran during 
recent four years incorporating crash level predictors such as driver age, education, gender, 
lighting and pavement conditions, along with road level covariates like annual average daily 
traffic, heavy-vehicle share and terrain slope. We compare three binary logistic frameworks: a 
single level generalized linear model, a multilevel model with a random intercept capturing latent 
road level effects (intraclass correlation ≈ 21 %), and a multilevel model with random coefficients 
that allows key predictor effects to vary by road. The random coefficient model achieves the best 
fit in terms of deviance, AIC and BIC, and substantially improves predictive performance: 
classification accuracy rises from 0.62 to 0.71, recall from 0.32 to 0.63, and AUC from 0.570 to 
0.775. Results from 200 simulation runs reveal notable variability in slopes for pavement and 
lighting variables, underscoring how local context influences crash risk. Overall, our findings 
demonstrate that flexible multilevel modeling not only enhances prediction accuracy but also 
yields context-specific insights to guide targeted safety interventions on rural road networks. 
Keywords 
Crash Severity, Crash Severity Data Heterogeneity, Multilevel Models, Traffic Safety

Introduction 
The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 50 million people are injured and 
1.2 million people die each year due to traffic crashes, which shows the issue of traffic safety and 
crashes as one of the most significant threats to social health today. According to the studies, these 
casualties are not distributed uniformly based on regional and socioeconomic factors. According 
to the World Health Organization, 90% of fatal crashes occur in low- and middle-income countries, 
and they are the leading cause of death for young people between the ages of 15 and 29. Every 
year, there are a number of fatal traffic crashes in Iran, and a lot of them occur on the country's 
intercity rural roads, which are two-lane roads in a significant number of cases (Besharati et al., 
2020; Organization, 2020)  
There has been extensive research conducted so far to determine and forecast the variables that 
affect the frequency and severity of crashes. The main categories of variables—vehicle, road, 
environment, and human—have been analyzed to evaluate their impact on the frequency and 
severity of crashes. A proper understanding of these factors requires an accurate approach to the 
theoretical framework used in the study, which should enable the variables from different levels 
to influence the accident outputs. It is essential to identify and comprehend the variables that 
contribute to the correlations between crashes that occur along each suburban road and those 
specific to each crash outcome to analyze the severity and frequency of traffic crashes 
(Khojastehpour et al., 2024; Lord et al., 2021; Tavakoli Kashani et al., 2025). 
Researchers have become interested in crash data hierarchies over the years. When analyzing 
crashes that occurred in various areas of the city, for instance, in addition to microscopic 
characteristics related to each crash, they are also influenced by variables related to the region in 
which they occur, which can lead to errors in understanding the results and analyses in the study 
if researchers do not consider the hierarchy in the data structures. Figure 1 is an overview of the 
hierarchy in crash data (Dupont et al., 2013; Huang and Abdel-Aty, 2010; Taheri et al., 2022).

The majority of research studies that have been done to model the severity of crashes have used 
single-level models, which means that the only factors taken from the crash scene were those that 
related to the drivers and passengers involved in the crash, the environment and weather conditions 
at the time of the crash, or the characteristics of the vehicles involved in the crash. This type of 
study exposes the researcher to critical errors in assessing the outcomes of crashes by ignoring the 
indirect and immeasurable effects of variables at the higher levels of the crash (road, geographical 
region, etc.). In order to improve outcomes and model performance in predicting the severity or 
frequency of crashes, multilevel models—which are frequently implemented by linear or non-
linear random effect models pay more attention to the effects of variables with indirect effects in 
the model analyzing. 
This research focused on analyzing the severity of crashes that occurred on two-lane, two-way 
roads in Iran using a two-level structure that considered both the roads and crashes. The variables 
associated with crashes were incorporated into a generalized linear random effect model, which 
considered the effects of heterogeneity and indirect variables among different roads, and allowed 
the effects of predictive variables to vary across roads. Finally, the accuracy of the predictions and 
the statistical goodness of fit factors of the multi-level model were compared with those of the 
conventional single-level model, and the results were used to gain insights into how crash-related 
factors influence severity differently across roadways.  
Literature 
In the following review, we survey multilevel modeling in traffic safety by examining two 
complementary strands of research: crash frequency models, which are mostly applied at 
aggregated levels such as road segments or intersections, and crash severity models, which are 
primarily used at the individual crash or occupant level. We first show how hierarchical 
frameworks for crash frequency have captured unobserved segment effects and boosted predictive 
accuracy. We then turn to two-level logistic and ordinal models for crash severity, demonstrating 
how accounting for between-road variance deepens our understanding of injury and fatality risk. 
By tracing the evolution from simple random-intercept specifications to richer random-coefficient 
extensions, we set the stage for our framework tailored to rural two-lane roads. 
In crash severity multilevel studies, multilevel Poisson models have consistently demonstrated 
improved performance over single-level specifications by accounting for unobserved segment-
level heterogeneity. El-Basyouny and Sayed (El-Basyouny and Sayed, 2009) for example, applied 
a two-level Poisson–lognormal model to 392 Canadian freeway segments and found that it 
significantly reduced overdispersion compared to a standard Poisson model. Guo et al. (Guo et al., 
2010) analyzed signalized intersections using a hierarchical model incorporating corridor-level 
spatial correlations. Alarifi et al. (Alarifi et al., 2017) employed a Bayesian joint modeling 
approach that included random effects at both the corridor and intersection levels. Other studies 
have allowed traffic and geometric covariates—such as AADT and ramp type—to vary randomly 
across segments, leading to further improvements in model fit (Han et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2018),

and Park et al. (Park et al., 2020) examined the use of multiple-membership multilevel structures 
to represent crash occurrences spanning overlapping corridors and intersections. 
On the severity side, two-level logistic models have demonstrated similar advantages. Lenguerrand 
et al. (Lenguerrand et al., 2006) and Kim et al. (Kim et al., 2007) found that hierarchical logistic 
models for intersection and rural crash outcomes capture between-road variance that single-level 
models fail to account for. Adanu et al. (Adanu et al., 2017) identified heterogeneities in human 
factors across provinces, while Yoon et al. (Yoon et al., 2017) applied cross-classified structures 
to address injury severity in local bus and commercial vehicle crashes. Haghighi et al. (Haghighi 
et al., 2018) further showed that geometric and pavement characteristics exhibit significant 
segment-level variance on rural two-lane highways. 
Building on these foundations, recent work has begun to introduce random slopes. Soltani et al. 
Meng et al., and Park & Ko each showed that allowing key effect sizes—such as adjacent-zone 
attributes or temporal clusters—to vary across higher-level units improves crash-severity 
predictions (Meng et al., 2020; Park and Ko, 2020; Soltani et al., 2019). 
More contemporary studies confirm and extend these findings. Alrumaidhi and Rakha (Alrumaidhi 
and Rakha, 2024) applied a multilevel ordinal logit to over 500 000 Virginia collisions, revealing 
jurisdiction-specific shifts under COVID-19 with sizeable AIC/BIC reductions. Recent research 
has applied multilevel discrete-outcome models to explore crash severity. Qi et al. (Qi et al., 2024) 
analyzed mountainous, two-lane roads in Yunnan Province using a hybrid approach combining 
ordered multinomial logit and machine learning techniques (e.g., XGBoost), revealing significant 
segment-level variance associated with terrain slope, lighting, and collision types. Yuan et al. 
(Malyshkina and Mannering, 2009) applied correlated random-parameter ordered models with 
heterogeneity-in-means across Pennsylvania hotspot districts, identifying spatial instability in 
factors such as speed limits, lighting conditions, and rural land-use variables. Another study by 
Scarano et al. integrated Classification Tree insights into a Mixed Logit framework—uncovering 
latent interaction effects and enhancing predictive performance (Scarano et al., 2025). 
Building on gaps in existing multilevel crash‐severity research, this paper has three primary 
objectives: 
1. Quantify between‐road heterogeneity. We first fit a null two‐level logistic model to 
measure how much crash severity varies from one rural road segment to another in Fars 
province with no predictive variable. 
2. Compare modelling approaches. Next, we evaluate and contrast standard (single‐level) 
logistic regression, a random‐intercept model, and a full random‐coefficient model—using 
both crash‐level (e.g. driver behavior, weather) and road‐segment (e.g. pavement 
condition, lighting) predictors—to see which framework best captures segment‐specific 
effects. 
3. Identify key varying predictors. Finally, we determine which covariate slopes exhibit the 
greatest variation across roads, revealing how context modulates crash risk. 
Our contributions are:

1. A stepwise multilevel modeling framework is developed. A progression from the 
simplest single-level logistic specification to fully random-coefficient models is 
constructed in order to identify the best-fitting structure. The selected model is then 
interpreted via its multilevel parameter estimates, and simulation analyses are performed 
to illustrate how key predictor effects (e.g., pavement condition, lighting) vary across

2. A country-scale rural crash database is assembled. A comprehensive dataset combining 
crash-level (micro) and road/region-level (macro) variables for two-lane rural roads was 
collected and harmonized at the country scale—a challenging task given incomplete 
records, heterogeneous formats, and sparse regional data. 
3. Actionable insights on predictor variability. Pinpointing which factors (e.g. lighting, 
speed enforcement) vary most in their influence across roads provides concrete targets for 
location‐specific countermeasures. 
Data 
Our analysis draws on 19,956 crash records from 99 two-lane, two-way roads in Iran over the 
period 2021–2024. The target variable—crash severity—is coded as a binary outcome (Property 
Damage Only [PDO] vs. non-PDO), and we assemble a set of crash-level predictors that capture 
driver demographics (Education, Age, Gender), site conditions at the moment of impact (Light 
Status, Pavement Status, Geometric Status), and prevailing Weather Status. These variables have 
also been highlighted as critical factors in recent applications of explainable machine learning to 
crash severity analysis (Masud et al., 2024). 
To account for road-segment context, we also include three higher-level measures: annual average 
daily traffic (ADT), access density (number of entry/exit points per kilometer), and heavy-vehicle 
ratio (proportion of trucks in daily flow). These road-level variables were constructed using 
available traffic detector data and detailed satellite imagery from Google Earth to assess segment 
characteristics and access points along each road. This multi-source approach allowed for a more 
accurate representation of exposure and roadway design features across segments. Table 1 presents 
the full list of variable names, coding schemes, and definitions for all crash- and road-level 
predictors used in our multilevel models. 
Methods 
Overview 
This study applies a multilevel logistic modeling framework to account for the nested structure of 
crash data—individual crashes occurring on specific roads. We estimated three increasingly 
flexible models: (1) a single-level logistic regression that captures direct effects of crash-level 
variables; (2) a two-level random-intercept model that introduces road-specific intercepts to 
capture unobserved segment heterogeneity; and (3) a two-level random-coefficient model that not

Model estimation and Evaluation 
Model parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method, which is commonly 
employed in multilevel logistic regression models due to its robust interpretability and flexibility. 
This method allows for the assessment of goodness-of-fit using deviance-based indices such as the 
Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). These metrics 
provide insight into model improvement and aid in selecting the best-fitting model (McCulloch, 
1997). 
Due to the computational complexity of high-dimensional integrals in multilevel models, advanced 
estimation techniques have been developed. Key methods include: 
- Restricted Pseudo-Likelihood (PL): Suitable for models with hierarchical or crosswise random 
effects but may produce an inaccurate likelihood approximation.

- Hermite’s Gaussian Quadrature: Accurate for low-dimensional random effects but limited to two 
or three random effects. 
- Laplace Approximation: A widely used method, offering efficient estimation of variance 
components through derivative-free optimization. 
- Penalized Gaussian Likelihood: Simple and effective for generalized linear mixed models. 
 
For this study, the Laplace approximation method was utilized, as it strikes a balance between 
accuracy and computational feasibility.

 Studies related to transportation safety have suggested that a minimum ICC factor of 5% should 
be used. This means that if the ICC value is greater than 5%, then multi-level analysis should be 
employed, as it indicates a significant hierarchy in the data. In this study, the ICC between the 
crash severity data is 21%, as shown in the equation above. This value is much higher than the 
minimum value in comparable studies, indicating the need for multi-level models. This means that 
the characteristics of each high-level group or road account for 21% of the variation in crash 
severity on the two-lane, two-way roads analyzed in this study. 
To capture unobserved differences between roads, we built two multilevel extensions to our 
baseline logistic regression. First, a random-intercept model (fit in lme4 via the Laplace 
approximation) adds a unique intercept for each road, yielding substantial drops in deviance, AIC 
and BIC and confirming that baseline crash risk varies by segment. Next, we introduced random 
slopes for our five key predictors—driver education, age, lighting, pavement condition and traffic 
volume—allowing each effect to flex across roads. This random-coefficient model delivers further 
reductions in all three fit criteria and uncovers meaningful slope variances (largest for pavement 
status), demonstrating that the impact of these factors is not uniform but depends on the specific 
roadway context. 
Even after allowing slopes to vary by road, the core findings from the simpler models hold steady.

The dataset was initially partitioned, with 80% allocated for model training and the remaining 20% 
reserved for testing. To evaluate the efficacy of the proposed multi-level logistic regression model,

model. Both models were trained using the designated training data and subsequently validated on 
the unseen test set. Model performance was assessed through an analysis of key classification 
metrics and examination of their respective Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. 
Detailed classification metric results, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, are 
presented in Table 3. For a visual representation and comparative analysis of model performance,

classifying our data, offering both greater accuracy and a more balanced predictive capability.

Conclusion 
This study addressed the critical challenge of traffic crash severity prediction, recognizing the 
inherent hierarchical structure within crash data that traditional single-level models frequently 
overlook. Focused on two-lane, two-way roads in Iran, our investigation aimed to demonstrate the 
advantages of a multilevel logistic regression framework. The findings unequivocally support the 
utility of multilevel modeling; the significant Intra-class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 21% 
underscored substantial unobserved heterogeneity across roads, thereby validating our hierarchical 
approach. In direct comparison, the Multilevel model, particularly with random coefficients, 
consistently outperformed the conventional Single-level model across all key classification 
metrics—including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score—and exhibited a superior AUC. This 
enhanced predictive capability stems from its ability to account for road-specific variations and 
the differential impact of predictors across road, offering a more nuanced and accurate 
understanding of factors influencing crash severity. Consequently, this research provides a more 
robust framework for crash severity analysis, which is crucial for developing targeted and effective 
road safety interventions.
