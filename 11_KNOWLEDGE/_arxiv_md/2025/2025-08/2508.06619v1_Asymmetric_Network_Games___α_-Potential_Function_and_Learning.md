Asymmetric Network Games: α-Potential Function and Learning
Kiran Rokade1, Adit Jain1, Francesca Parise1, Vikram Krishnamurthy1, and Eva Tardos2
1 School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Cornell University {kvr36,aj457,fp264,vikramk}@cornell.edu
2 Department of Computer Science, Cornell University eva.tardos@cornell.edu
Abstract. In a network game, players interact over a network and the utility of each player depends on
his own action and on an aggregate of his neighbours’ actions. Many real world networks of interest are
asymmetric and involve a large number of heterogeneous players. This paper analyzes static network
games using the framework of α-potential games. Under mild assumptions on the action sets (compact
intervals) and the utility functions (twice continuously diﬀerentiable) of the players, we derive an
expression for an inexact potential function of the game, called the α-potential function. Using such a
function, we show that modiﬁed versions of the sequential best-response algorithm and the simultaneous
gradient play algorithm achieve convergence of players’ actions to a 2α-Nash equilibrium. For linear-
quadratic network games, we show that α depends on the maximum asymmetry in the network and
is well-behaved for a wide range of networks of practical interest. Further, we derive bounds on the
social welfare of the α-Nash equilibrium corresponding to the maximum of the α-potential function,
under suitable assumptions. We numerically illustrate the convergence of the proposed algorithms and
properties of the learned 2α-Nash equilibria.
Keywords: Network games · α-potential function · Learning · Social welfare.

Introduction
In a network game, players interacting through a network make strategic decisions aimed at max-
imizing their utility which depends on their own action and on an aggregate of their neighbors’
actions [19]. Much of the existing literature studying learning dynamics in network games relies on
global assumptions on the network structure such as symmetric interactions (i.e., G = GT , where
G is the adjacency matrix of the network [3]), or spectral conditions which ensure monotonicity
of the game Jacobian (e.g., conditions involving ∥G∥2 or ∥G∥∞[30]). While these assumptions
are useful in establishing properties such as existence and uniqueness of a Nash equilibrium, and
convergence of learning algorithms to such an equilibrium, they can be restrictive since (i) many
real-world networks exhibit asymmetric interactions, e.g., social networks with unbalanced relation-
ships [32] and technological networks with directional dependencies [22], and (ii) for large networks
with heterogeneous player characteristics, conditions which require a bound on the norm of the
network adjacency matrix may not be satisﬁed. Hence, there is a need to study these classes of
network games using a framework which is broad (encompassing networks of practical interest that
can be large, asymmetric, generated deterministically or randomly) yet enabling tractable analysis
of equilibrium outcomes and convergence of independent learning algorithms.
Contributions: In this work, we adopt the framework of α-potential games to study a broad
class of network games not previously covered in the literature. Under mild assumptions on the game,
we prove existence of a common function (an α-potential function) that is approximately optimized3
as players selﬁshly maximize their own utilities, and whose maxima correspond to approximate Nash
equilibria. Our main contributions are as follows.

Related works: Our paper adds to the rich literature on network games (see, e.g., [19,14,3,13,30]
among others). Within this literature, our paper is more closely related to works that study conver-
gence of learning dynamics. For example, [30] guarantees convergence of discrete- and continuous-
time best-response dynamics in network games under conditions on the norms of the network ad-
jacency matrix G. For an LQ game with local aggregate sensitivity parameter γ,4 these conditions
reduce to: (i) |γ|·∥G∥2 < 1, (ii) |γ|·∥G∥∞< 1, (iii) γ < 0, |γ|·|λmin (G) | < 1. Although these condi-
tions are broad, i.e., they cover asymmetric network games and games of strategic complements and
substitutes, there exist simple network games which do not satisfy them (see Fig. 1 for two exam-
ples). Our work applies to these classes of network games. Under assumptions that the best-response
mapping is either contractive, non-expansive, or monotone, and the network adjacency matrix sat-
isﬁes ∥G∥2 ≤1, [29] studies distributed algorithms for convergence to Nash equilibria. Convergence
properties of best-response dynamics in network games under conditions such as the adjacency ma-
trix being doubly stochastic and ∥G∥2 ≤1 are studied in [15]. For time-varying network games
sampled from a monotone network game, [33] provides convergence guarantees for gradient play dy-
namics. Moreover, passivity based approaches for learning in network games have been considered
in [11]. The problem of computing a Nash equilibrium of a graphical game (a general version of a
network game) [20], and of public goods games over networks [28] have also been studied in the

literature. Finally, learning dynamics in special classes of network games have also been considered.
For example, a particular class of asymmetric network games with a mix of strategic complements
and substitutes, called games with coordinating and anti-coordinating agents, is studied in [34]. A
special class of network games, called opinion games, are studied in [2]. Therein, the authors provide
guarantees on the convergence of De-Groot style best-response dynamics and the price of anarchy of
the game. Network games with discrete action spaces and threshold learning dynamics are studied,
for example, in [18,21]. Learning dynamics in aggregative games, which are a special class of network
games wherein the utility of a player depends on the sum of actions of all the players have been con-
sidered, for example, in [27,36,12]. Our work contributes to this literature by ensuring convergence
of two learning algorithms to approximate Nash equilibria for a broad class of network games which
are neither potential nor monotone, and can have a mix of strategic complements and substitutes.
Our results are obtained by building on the framework of near/α-potential games. The concept
of near-potential games was introduced in [8,9] for static games. Therein the authors quantiﬁed the
distance between a game and its nearest potential game using the “maximum pairwise distance”.
The concept of an α-potential game was introduced in [16,17] for (dynamic) Markov games. In
this paper, we apply the same notion of α-potential games to static games. In this setting, any α-
potential game is a near-potential game as deﬁned in [8,9] with maximum pairwise distance α and
vice-versa. We provide an explicit form of an α-potential function for static games with continuous
action spaces by specializing a result from [17] to the static setting. The derived α-potential function
can be used to construct a potential game that is α-close to the original one, yet not necessarily
the nearest potential game (as instead studied in [9,7,23]). Learning algorithms including best- and
better-response dynamics, logit response dynamics and ﬁctitious play for near-potential games have
also been considered in the literature (e.g., in [8,7,23]). We complement these results by analyzing
learning algorithms (e.g., approximate gradient dynamics) for α-potential games with continuous
action spaces and discrete-time updates. Related learning algorithms for Markov α-potential games
are discussed in [16]. Finally, a main focus of our work is the application of these results to network
games and the characterization of the value of α in the terms of network properties. We note that
[17, Section 6] also applied the framework of α-potential games to (dynamic) LQ network games, and
discovered the connection of α to network asymmetry. However, the work in [17] focused on deriving
an analytical solution of an α-Nash equilibrium, while our focus is on independent algorithms for
learning α-Nash equilibria of static network games.
Organization: The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall the notion of
α-potential games and study convergence of a sequential best-response algorithm and a simultaneous
gradient play algorithm. In Section 3, we focus on LQ network games and derive an expression of
α for such games, connecting it with the asymmetry in the network. Then, we study the properties
of α for various network structures of practical interest. Further, we derive bounds on the welfare
suboptimality of the maximizer of an α-potential function. In Section 4, we illustrate our results
through numerical simulations. Finally, we give some concluding remarks in Section 5. Proofs of all
the results are given in the appendix.

Conclusion
We provided an explicit characterization of an α-potential function for static games under mild
regularity assumptions and showed that the α-potential function can be used to guide learners to
the set of approximate Nash equilibria. In particular, we proposed variants of the sequential best-
response algorithm and the gradient play algorithm dependent on the value of α that converge to
the set of 2α-Nash equilibria in ﬁnite time. Our convergence results hold for network games without
restrictive assumptions such as symmetry of the network or bounds on the norms of the network
adjacency matrix. For linear-quadratic network games, we showed that α is proportional to the de-
gree of asymmetry in the network quantiﬁed by the inﬁnity norm of the antisymmetric component
of the network adjacency matrix. We showed that in several network topologies, α scales well with
the size of the network. Finally, we derived bounds on the social welfare of the particular α-Nash
equilibrium given by the maximizer of the α-potential function. Through numerical experiments, we
demonstrated that the proposed algorithms consistently reach approximate equilibria while standard
algorithms may fail to converge. Thus, the framework of α-potential games broadens the family of
network games for which simple payoﬀ-driven learning can be used to guarantee convergence to ap-
proximate Nash equilibria. Future extensions can look at time-varying games, stochastic algorithms,
and network games with a community structure. Computing the optimal α-potential function for a
broad class of games is also an interesting problem.
