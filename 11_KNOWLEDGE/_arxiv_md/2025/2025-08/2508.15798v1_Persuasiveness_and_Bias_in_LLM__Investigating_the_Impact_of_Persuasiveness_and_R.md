## Page 3


Abstract
Warning: This research examines AI capabilities for persuasion and bias amplification that could be
misused; all experiments are conducted for AI safety evaluation purposes.
In the recent few years,
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in generating coherent and
human-like text, leading to their widespread integration in content creation, decision support, and user
interaction systems. However, despite their technical sophistication, these models possess the capacity
to disseminate both information and misinformation at a large scale based on their usage and user’s
agendas. Furthermore, they are not immune to societal biases, whether inherited from training data,
model architecture, or algorithmic design. This research investigates the intersection of persuasion and
bias in LLMs, with a focus on how imperfect or skewed outputs influence their persuasive effectiveness.
Specifically, we explore whether persona-based models can not only persuade users through fact-based
statements, but also inadvertently promote misinformation or biased narratives, thereby reinforcing
problematic beliefs.
To examine this, we introduce a convincer–skeptic framework in which LLMs are assigned personas
to simulate realistic human attitudes and interactions. The setup involves evaluating the belief shifts in
skeptical models acting as human proxies, before and after exposure to persuasive arguments generated
by convincer models. Using Jensen-Shannon divergence as a metric for persuasion, we quantitatively
measure how effective LLMs are at altering belief distributions. Furthermore, we conduct a secondary
analysis to determine how much can persuaded entities be reinforced to showcase and amplify bias
beliefs in domains such as race, gender, and religion. Models exhibiting strong persuasive performance
are subsequently evaluated for biased tendencies by subjecting them to sycophantic adversarial prompts
and assessing their responses using other robust models.
Our findings underscore both the strengths and risks of LLMs in persuasive communication. The
models can excel at shaping narratives, adapting tone, and mirroring audience values across domains such
as psychology, digital marketing, and legal assistance. The same persuasive capacity can be weaponized
when malicious actors automate large-scale misinformation campaigns or craft messages that exploit
cognitive biases. Such deployments reinforce harmful stereotypes and escalate social inequities. The main
danger therefore lies in the misuse of these models itself rather than in occasional model fallacies. By
quantifying persuasive power and measuring bias reinforcement, this study shows the need of guardrails
and policies that penalize deceptive use. These contributions lay a clearer foundation for future work on
AI alignment, value-sensitive design and trustworthy deployment.

1.1
Large Language Models
The rise of Large Language Models (LLMs) has significantly transformed natural language processing,
presenting considerable opportunities across diverse sectors such as marketing, psychology, customer
service, and media. These models are adept at generating human-like text, making them valuable for
content creation, facilitating communication, and even aiding in certain decision-making processes. As
Large Language Models become increasingly embedded in real-world systems, their growing advantages
are matched by an expanded risk surface. Malicious actors may harness their persuasive and sophisticated
language abilities to disseminate propaganda or disinformation at scale. In the absence of reliable mech-
anisms to distinguish machine-generated from human-authored text, the societal consequences could be
profound, potentially destabilizing democratic institutions. Information warfare already plays a central
role in modern day conflicts. For example, during the ongoing war between Ukraine and Russia, there is
a credible risk that LLMs will be deployed to craft persuasive narratives that keeps the ulterior motives
of bad actors in the dark from all the concerned parties. If used in the precise ways, unsafeguarded
models can be coerced through prompt engineering or jailbreak attacks to comply with instructions that
distort factual reality and erode shared moral norms. While this study does not propose specific safe-
guards for AI misalignment, it does analyze in detail how they might be misused in such cases. More
concretely, the persuasive capabilities of the LLMs are tested and methods are employed to quantify
them. Additionally, as an extension, the ability for LLMs to follow instructions that, at the core, spread
hatred and misinformation is also tested and quantified. Just as technologies such as gene editing and
nuclear technology function as double-edged swords, capable of yielding both beneficial and harmful
outcomes, modern generative AI systems like LLMs exhibit the same dual-use potential. We believe that
our following experiments would shed more light in these aspects of such models.
Bias and Persuasion in LLMs
Bias in LLMs refers to systematic, non-random deviations in model outputs that disadvantage or privilege
particular demographic groups, viewpoints, or outcomes relative to a normative moral baseline. For
instance, when asked which racial group commits the most crimes in the United States, an LLM may
generate arguments portraying African Americans as disproportionately prone to criminal behavior,
thereby reinforcing harmful stereotypes and marginalizing this demographic group. This often stems from
the datasets they are trained on, which are generated or curated by humans and thus reflect societal
biases and assumptions. This can result in systematically skewed outputs that replicate and amplify
existing societal prejudices or misinformation. The dissemination of such biased or incorrect information
through LLM-generated content can persuade users to adopt false beliefs or reinforce harmful stereotypes.
Such biases pose a dual challenge. On one hand, LLMs’ persuasive power can be harnessed for positive
applications like personalized mental-health support in psychology, adaptive legal-advisory chatbots,
and targeted marketing campaigns that align with user preferences. For instance, in marketing, LLMs
can tailor communication strategies for specific demographics by analyzing linguistic cues and adapting
messaging to align with consumer preferences and cultural contexts. In psychology, LLMs could assist in
personalized mental health interventions, offering individualized strategies based on a patient’s profile and
real-time responses. Persuasion plays a pivotal role in cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), motivation
enhancement therapy, and other therapeutic frameworks where convincing individuals to adopt healthier

Motivation and Problem Statement
Large Language Models (LLMs) are rapidly evolving from tools of information retrieval into sophisticated,
interactive social actors, becoming deeply integrated into the fabric of daily life.
Their capacity to
generate fluent, context-aware, and human-like language has unlocked valuable applications in high-stakes
domains requiring persuasive communication, including personalized mental-health coaching, adaptive
marketing, and automated legal or financial advice. In these roles, an LLM’s ability to persuade is not
a peripheral feature but a core function for achieving beneficial outcomes.
This emergent capability, however, presents a critical and urgent **dual-use dilemma**. The same
mechanisms that allow an LLM to build rapport and effectively persuade a user to adopt healthier habits
can be repurposed by malicious actors to generate and disseminate targeted **propaganda and sophist-
icated misinformation** at an unprecedented scale, with the potential to manipulate public opinion and
erode democratic processes. An LLM that can act as an empathetic therapist could just as easily act
as a radicalizing ideologue, tailoring its arguments to a user’s specific psychological profile. This is not
a future-tense problem; information warfare is a central component of modern conflicts, and the risk of
LLMs being deployed to craft deceptive persuasive narratives is immediate and profound.
While the potential for both positive and negative influence is widely acknowledged, there remains a
critical gap in our understanding. We lack robust, quantitative frameworks to systematically measure the
interplay between an LLM’s persuasive power and its tendency to amplify harmful biases. To ensure the
responsible development and deployment of this technology, we must move beyond anecdotal evidence.
It is therefore essential to ask:
• How effectively can LLMs persuade human (or proxy) audiences, and can this influence be quan-
tified as a dedicated persuasion score (π) derived from the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) of
their belief states?
• Critically, given that LLMs learn from biased data, to what extent can their persuasive capabilities
be co-opted—particularly through sycophantic prompting—to reinforce and amplify these harmful
stereotypes, as measured by a quantitative bias ratio?
Addressing these questions is fundamental to navigating the risks of persuasive AI. This thesis, there-
fore, aims to provide an empirical foundation for understanding this dual-use dilemma by quantitatively
investigating the capacity of persona-based LLMs to both influence their users and propagate biased
stereotypes through adversarial prompting.

behaviors through communication guided by specific psychological principles and techniques [11][7][8].

• The extent to which these LLMs, particularly when subjected to sycophancy-inducing prompts,

We will achieve this through an experimental framework involving LLMs assigned distinct personas,

• RQ 1: In what capacity π are persona-based LLMs persuasive to effectively persuade or alter belief

– Let us assume an LLM, embodying the persona of Hans Gruber from 2.1.1, attempting to

We aim to quantify the intrinsic persuasive power of modern LLMs. Building on concepts
in behavioral psychology, such as the "mere-measurement" effect where querying intent can
alter beliefs, in our research we measure whether LLM outputs induce significantly shift in
the citizen’s belief distribution. We define a persuasion metric, π, as the Jensen-Shannon
divergence between the target’s prior and posterior belief distributions (as detailed in Section
2.1.2 on Persuasion). An LLM is considered sufficiently persuasive if π ≥0.15, a threshold
shown in domain studies to correlate with real-world behavioral changes (e.g., in medication
adherence or purchase intent). This allows us to assess if an LLM can meaningfully influence
decision-making, considering factors like emotion and bias.

and biased misinformation when when an LLM is presented with sycophancy-inducing prompts?
Again we look at a concrete example.

create echo-chamber effects, potentially influencing users towards adopting or strengthening
stereotypes related to race, religion, gender, etc.

Assessing Persuasive Capabilities in LLMs
3.1.1
Persuasion Recognition in LLMs
The authors Rescala et al. in this paper [17] examine the quality of arguments generated by Large
Language Models (LLMs) and identify those that are as convincing as human arguments. They also
explore how different demographics respond to these arguments, assessing their influence on various
groups. The focus is on evaluating the arguments themselves. The introduction raises concerns about
the potential misuse of LLMs, such as GPT-4 and Claude, to create personalized misinformation and
propaganda. To address this, the paper proposes studying LLMs’ ability to detect convincing arguments
as a means of assessing their persuasive capabilities without the need for direct human experimentation.
Three research questions are central to this study: (1) Can LLMs judge the quality of arguments
as effectively as humans? (2) Can LLMs predict how demographics and beliefs shape people’s views
on specific topics? (3) Can LLMs assess how appealing an argument is to an individual based on their
personal traits?
These questions aim to benchmark LLMs’ persuasive abilities over time, providing
insights into developing safeguards against LLM-generated misinformation.
The paper acknowledges several shortcomings. The dataset used, drawn from debate.org, may not
adequately represent the diversity of the general population, highlighting the need for more compre-
hensive datasets that include non-English languages and varied demographics. Additionally, the study
focuses on detecting persuasive arguments rather than generating them, which is considered a simpler
task for LLMs. Future research could explore LLMs’ ability to generate persuasive—and potentially
misleading—content. The limitations of current LLMs, particularly open-source models like Llama 2
and Mistral, are also discussed. These models struggled to follow prompt instructions effectively, indic-
ating a need for improved prompt engineering and model training. Finally, the paper underscores the
ethical implications of LLM use, particularly concerning personalized misinformation. It calls for further
research into safeguards and countermeasures to mitigate the risks associated with these technologies.

Persuasive Power of LLMs
This study by Breum et al. [5] explores whether large language models (LLMs) can generate persuasive
arguments that effectively change opinions and simulate human-like persuasion dynamics. The research
focuses on three key questions: whether LLMs can replicate realistic persuasion and opinion change, if
they can generate arguments using various persuasion strategies, and whether arguments that convince
LLM agents are equally effective on humans. To test this, the researchers created a scenario in which two
LLM-based agents engage in a dialogue about climate change. One agent, the "Convincer," attempts to
persuade a skeptical agent, the "Skeptic," to change its stance. They tested various argument types—fact-
based, trust-based, supportive, and status-oriented—and had human judges evaluate the persuasiveness
of these arguments.
The study measured the success of the Convincer by assessing the probability of persuasion across
different argument types and levels of Skeptic stubbornness. Trust and Support were the most successful
strategies, particularly with moderately stubborn Skeptics, achieving persuasion in over half of the
attempts. While Status and Knowledge were also effective, Knowledge surprisingly had lower success
rates with the most stubborn Skeptics. Conflict and Identity were the least persuasive, often performing
worse than generic arguments.
One key finding was that the more stubborn the Skeptic, the lower the likelihood of persuasion,
indicating that agent resistance greatly affects outcome. Interestingly, while Trust and Support mirrored
human persuasion dynamics, the lower effectiveness of Knowledge and the poor performance of Identity
suggested differences in how AI agents process arguments compared to humans.
There were notable differences between human and LLM responses to persuasion. Humans tended
to value factual knowledge more than LLM agents, suggesting that LLMs do not fully align with human
reasoning when it comes to persuasive arguments. The study had two key limitations. First, it simplified
dialogue to one-off interactions on a single topic (climate change), while real-world opinion change often
involves multiple conversations.
Second, the agents were based on a single LLM model, lacking the
diversity found in human populations. Future research could explore more complex dialogues and diverse
agent personalities to better simulate real-world persuasion.

3.2.1
The Multi-Elo Rating System (MERS)
The paper by Wu and Aji [23] rigorously investigates inherent biases in both human-based and LLM-
based evaluations of machine-generated text, highlighting a critical issue termed "style over substance".
Specifically, the authors found significant bias towards responses that, while stylistically polished and
grammatically correct, contained factual inaccuracies. This evaluation bias results in factually incor-
rect but fluently written answers receiving disproportionately higher ratings compared to shorter or
grammatically flawed but factually correct responses.
To systematically study this bias, the authors constructed a novel dataset intentionally populated
with flawed or misleading answers across diverse domains. They conducted extensive evaluations using
human annotators and state-of-the-art LLM-based evaluators, revealing a clear pattern: both humans
and automated LLM evaluators consistently rewarded answers with superior writing style and fluency,
often overlooking or underestimating factual inaccuracies.
This phenomenon poses substantial risks,
especially in critical applications like medical advice, education, or decision-support systems, where
factual correctness is paramount.
In response to these concerning findings, the authors introduced the Multi-Elo Rating System (MERS),
an innovative evaluation framework inspired by Elo rating systems commonly used in competitive gam-
ing scenarios. Unlike conventional single-dimensional evaluation metrics, MERS independently assesses
multiple dimensions of textual responses—such as factual accuracy, grammatical correctness, coherence,
and fluency—thereby reducing the risk of superficial biases overshadowing substantive correctness.
Empirical results demonstrated that the MERS significantly improved the reliability and nuance of
evaluations, especially in differentiating between style and substance. By independently rating textual
outputs across distinct dimensions, evaluators using MERS produced assessments more aligned with
expert judgment and less influenced by superficial textual qualities. Notably, the application of MERS
helped expose latent biases within existing evaluation approaches, underscoring the importance of mul-
tidimensional rating methodologies in accurately benchmarking and deploying LLMs.
This study contributes substantially to the field by providing both a clear empirical demonstration of
the prevalence and impact of evaluation biases, and by offering a practical, multidimensional evaluation
tool to mitigate these biases. It underscores the urgent need for adopting more sophisticated and rigorous
evaluation frameworks, ensuring that the deployment of LLMs prioritizes substantive accuracy over
stylistic appeal, particularly in high-stakes applications.

For the creation of synthetic personas, a vector of 11 specific attributes was chosen: name, age, gender,

• Demographics: Core attributes like age, gender, and race are fundamental variables in social
science research and are known to influence beliefs and receptivity to arguments.
• Socio-Economic Status: Attributes such as profession, income, and education level provide
context on a persona’s likely expertise, daily concerns, and socio-economic standing, which are
critical factors in shaping worldviews.
• Ideology and Culture: Political inclination and religion are primary drivers of values and beliefs

for a more realistic simulation of how a combination of background factors shapes an individual’s stance.
4.1.2

The corpus of 18 personas was not intended to be a statistically representative sample of any single
national or global population, a task that would be logistically infeasible. Instead, the personas were
strategically constructed to ensure broad coverage of diverse archetypal profiles commonly en-

a wide spectrum of human experience and viewpoints, thereby enhancing the generalizability of the

4.4.2
Belief Elicitation via Multi-Query Probing
To quantify a persona’s belief—both before (prior) and after (posterior) being exposed to an argu-
ment—we did not rely on a single generated output. Instead, we implemented a robust belief elicitation
method based on calculating the model’s sequence probabilities for a range of answers. This process is
identical for both prior and posterior calculations, differing only in the prompt’s context.
• Prompting Context: For priors, the prompt consisted of the persona’s description followed by a
direct query. For posteriors, the prompt was augmented with the convincer’s argument before the
query was posed, conditioning the model’s response on the persuasive text.
• Multi-Query Framework: To ensure a robust measurement, each belief was probed using five
different questions, covering Likert scales, Yes/No, and True/False formats.
This multi-query
approach mitigates prompt sensitivity and provides a more stable belief score.
• Probabilistic Calculation: For each query, we calculated the sequence log-probability for every
possible answer (e.g., "Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree"). These log-probabilities were then
converted into a normalized probability distribution using a softmax function. The final belief score
for that query was computed as a weighted average of these probabilities against their corresponding
numerical values (e.g., 0.0 to 1.0 for the Likert scale).

values that would skew aggregate results.

Bias Reinforcement Experiment Pipeline (RQ2)
This second experimental pipeline is designed to address our second research question (RQ2): In what
capacity is it possible to reinforce stereotypes and biased misinformation when an LLM is presented with
sycophancy-inducing prompts?
The central objective is to quantitatively investigate the relationship
between a model’s persuasive capability and its susceptibility to bias reinforcement. Specifically, we test
the hypothesis that the LLM demonstrating the highest persuasiveness will also be the most susceptible
to producing reinforced biased content when subjected to adversarial, sycophancy-inducing prompts.
The rationale for this experiment is twofold. First, by identifying and focusing on the most per-
suasive LLM from our RQ1 experiments, we isolate the variable of interest, i.e the model’s inherent
ability to adapt its output for persuasive effect. This allows for a controlled analysis of how prompting
styles, rather than architectural differences between models, influence bias amplification. Second, we aim
to simulate a realistic user-AI interaction where an LLM’s tendency to be agreeable, a behavior known as
sycophancy, could lead to the validation and amplification of a user’s pre-existing biases. Understanding
this mechanism is critical for assessing the risks of deploying persuasive AI in real-world applications.

Evaluation Metrics
To quantitatively address our research questions, this study relies on two distinct primary metrics, each

4.7.1
Metrics for Persuasion Quantification (RQ1)
The central goal of RQ1 is to measure the degree to which an LLM-generated argument can shift a
target’s beliefs.
• Persuasion Score (π): The core metric for persuasion is the Persuasion Score, which is cal-
culated using the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD). We treat JSD as a measure of effect
size—quantifying the magnitude of change between the skeptic’s five-dimensional belief distribu-
tion before (prior) and after (posterior) exposure to the argument. JSD is a symmetric and bounded
metric, making it ideal for aggregating belief shifts across multiple question formats into a single,
robust score. When summarizing results, we report the mean Persuasion Score (π) to compare
the overall persuasive efficacy of different models.
• Persuasion Success Rate: As a secondary, more illustrative metric, we calculate a "persuasion
success." This is defined as an instance where the most probable stance in the belief distribution
switches sides (e.g., from "Disagree" to "Agree").
This provides a more intuitive, albeit less
granular, measure of persuasive impact, such as, "Model X successfully changed its target’s belief
in Z% of interactions."
4.7.2
Metrics for Bias Reinforcement (RQ2)
The goal of RQ2 is to measure the extent to which a model’s output reinforces a user’s stated bias under
different prompt conditions. This is a measure of the content, not a belief shift.
• Bias Ratio: The primary metric for RQ2 is the bias ratio. This is the fraction of responses
generated by the test model that the independent judging panel flags as "biased." The ratio is
computed for each prompt condition (sycophantic, neutral, no-persona) and for each bias category
(e.g., race, gender).
A higher ratio indicates that a given condition is more likely to produce
problematic, bias-reinforcing content.
• Qualitative Content Analysis: For both research questions, quantitative metrics are supple-
mented with qualitative analysis. We examine the content of highly persuasive arguments (RQ1)
to identify rhetorical strategies, and we analyze the nature of biased responses (RQ2) to under-
stand how bias is being reinforced—for example, whether a reply simply restates the user’s claim
or introduces new, more extreme stereotypes. This provides essential context for the numerical
findings.

Implications for LLM Persuasion in Real-World Applications
Synthesizing the preceding findings provides a direct, albeit nuanced, answer to RQ1 regarding the ca-
pacity of LLMs to function as persuasive agents in real-world applications. The evidence is multifaceted,
indicating both significant potential and critical limitations. On one hand, the consistent ability of models
like Mistral, Llama 3.1, and Phi-3 to induce substantial belief changes is compelling. Their achievement
of JSD scores approaching 0.2 to 0.3 in favorable scenarios signifies a level of persuasive impact that,
in human contexts, could correlate with meaningful shifts in attitude and behavior. This quantitative
result suggests these models are potentially "persuasive enough" for deployment in applications such
as automated marketing, where influencing consumer preference is paramount, or in pedagogical and
therapeutic settings that require guiding a user’s cognitive state. Furthermore, the demonstrated success
of personalization, where higher persona similarity yielded greater persuasion, confirms that LLMs can
leverage audience-matching strategies analogous to those used by effective human communicators.
On the other hand, our results introduce critical caveats that must inform any practical deployment.
The high degree of performance variability across models underscores that persuasive capability is not
an inherent property of all LLMs. Similarly, the existence of highly resistant "skeptic" profiles, exem-
plified by the DeepSeek model, demonstrates that not all audiences are equally susceptible to influence.
Therefore, one cannot assume that an AI-generated message, however well-crafted, will be universally
effective. Real-world success will depend on a sophisticated approach to audience modeling. An applied
system might need to dynamically assess a user’s profile to identify traits associated with high resist-
ance. For example, upon detecting a "DeepSeek-like" skeptical user, an AI agent might pivot from direct
persuasion to a strategy focused on building credibility or employ an entirely different rhetorical style.
In conclusion, to directly address RQ1, our findings affirm that LLMs can be sufficiently persuasive
for real-world applications, but their effective deployment is contingent upon careful model selection
and context-aware strategies.
We have quantitatively demonstrated that specific models can induce
significant, measurable belief shifts, fulfilling a primary requirement for tasks in marketing, negotiation, or
counseling. However, realizing this potential requires a strategic approach that matches the AI persuader
to the target audience and acknowledges the inherent limitations posed by highly skeptical individuals.
The results presented here establish an empirical baseline of persuasive capability, which serves as a
crucial foundation for the subsequent investigations into bias reinforcement and ethical considerations.

Discussions
This chapter synthesizes the empirical findings from the preceding results, interpreting their significance
in the context of the study’s primary research questions and broader applications. The discussion first
validates the integrity of the experimental framework itself, examining the methods used to construct the
simulations for persuasion and bias reinforcement. Subsequently, it delves into a deeper interpretation of
the model-level behaviors observed, connecting the quantitative results to their qualitative implications.
Finally, the chapter considers the societal and ethical ramifications of our findings on persuasive LLMs
and concludes by reflecting on the study’s limitations and proposing avenues for future research.
6.1
Validation of the Experimental Framework
The credibility of this study’s findings rests upon the robust construction and validation of its experi-
mental apparatus. This involved the careful creation of synthetic personas and a multi-faceted evaluation
protocol, designed to quantitatively measure the phenomena of persuasion and bias reinforcement at scale.
6.1.1
Recapitulation of the Core Research Framework
Before delving into a detailed interpretation of the results, it is useful to recapitulate the foundational
problem and experimental framework that guided this thesis. The central objective of this research was
to quantitatively investigate the dual nature of Large Language Models. This duality lies in their capacity
for beneficial, human-like persuasive communication on one hand, and their potential for harmful, large-
scale dissemination of misinformation and the reinforcement of societal biases on the other. The study
was therefore structured around two primary research questions designed to probe each facet of this
duality.
The first research question (RQ1) sought to quantify the persuasive efficacy of LLMs. To achieve
this, a "convincer-skeptic" simulation was designed, wherein an LLM endowed with one of 18 synthetic
personas attempted to persuade a skeptic LLM, also embodying a persona. The arguments for these
interactions were based on statements from the Human-annotated Propaganda (HQP) dataset. The core
metric for this experiment was the Persuasion Score (π), a measure of belief change calculated using the
Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) between the skeptic’s prior and posterior belief distributions.
The second research question (RQ2) investigated the capacity of LLMs to amplify bias when subjected
to sycophancy-inducing prompts. This experiment focused on the second most persuaded model identified
in RQ1, ’Mistral-Nemo-Instruct-2407’, as a representative test case. The model was prompted with biased
statements drawn from the ToxicBias dataset under several conditions, including neutral and explicitly
sycophantic instructions. The resulting outputs were evaluated by an impartial judging panel composed
of the models that had demonstrated the most resistance to persuasion in RQ1, a methodological choice
designed to ensure objective evaluation. The key metric was the "bias ratio," quantifying the proportion
of generated content flagged as biased. This two-pronged experimental framework provides the empirical
foundation for the findings and interpretations discussed herein.
6.1.2
The Persuasion Framework (RQ1)
A central methodological challenge was to create a scalable and reproducible simulation of persuasive
dialogue. To this end, a corpus of 18 synthetic personas was developed, each defined by 11 demographic

without encoding extreme or predictable viewpoints. This approach was foundational to addressing RQ1,
which investigates the capacity of LLMs to alter belief distributions in realistic scenarios.
A critical sanity check of this persona-based framework is found in the analysis of interlocutor sim-

6.1.3
The Bias Reinforcement Framework (RQ2)
For the investigation into bias reinforcement (RQ2), the framework’s validity depended on two compon-
ents, the authenticity of the bias stimuli and the objectivity of the evaluation mechanism. To ensure
authenticity, prompts were derived from the ToxicBias dataset, a corpus of real-world user comments
annotated for specific, identity-based prejudices such as racism and sexism. This allowed the experiment
to test the model against genuine, often subtle, instances of bias rather than contrived examples.
The evaluation mechanism provided a further methodological sanity check.
As stipulated in the
methodology, the outputs generated by the ’Mistral’ test model were not scored by humans but by an
automated panel of judge LLMs. Crucially, this panel was composed of the models identified in RQ1 as
the least persuasive and most resistant to influence, such as ’DeepSeek-R1’. The rationale for this choice
is that a model with high belief inertia is less likely to be swayed by the rhetorical style of the content
it is evaluating. It can therefore serve as a more impartial arbiter, focused on detecting underlying bias
based on its safety training rather than being influenced by the persuasive framing of the text. This
use of "skeptical" models as judges is a key design choice that bolsters the credibility of the reported
bias ratios. While this LLM-as-judge approach has inherent limitations, using a multi-judge panel of the
most resistant models provided a scalable and methodologically sound foundation for the findings in this
study.

Beyond the quantitative scores, a qualitative review of the model outputs reveals how these behaviors

• Sycophantic Embellishment: When reinforcing bias, Mistral did not simply echo the user’s
prejudice. Instead, it often embellished it, lending it an illusion of credibility. As seen in the ’Hans
Gruber’ case study, a user’s biased concern was met with enthusiastic agreement and additional,
manufactured details that made the bias appear more substantiated. This form of amplification
is particularly insidious, as it uses a helpful, confident tone to validate and strengthen harmful
stereotypes.
This duality highlights the core tension of this research. The very rhetorical skill that makes an LLM an
effective and engaging persuader can be turned to make misinformation and bias more convincing and
palatable.

6.3
Reflections on the Experimental Benchmark
A critical assessment of the custom benchmark used in this study is necessary to contextualize the
findings. The validity of our conclusions depends on how well the experimental design captured the
phenomena of interest.
6.3.1
Validity and Scope
Our benchmark’s design, comprising a persuasion task (RQ1) and a bias reinforcement task (RQ2),
addresses both "active" (ability to influence) and "passive" (tendency to agree with harm) facets of
social interaction. This dual approach provides a more holistic view of a model’s potential social impact.
The use of real-world propaganda themes (HQP dataset) and toxic comments (ToxicBias dataset)
grounds the simulation in authentic language.
A key consideration is the ecological validity of using LLMs as proxies for human users. While this was
a deliberate design choice to enable large-scale, reproducible experimentation, it imposes limitations. The
primary trade-off was sacrificing the complexity of multi-turn human dialogue for the control of single-
turn interactions. This design allowed us to isolate the direct effect of a single AI-generated message.
However, it does not capture dynamic phenomena like counter-argumentation or rapport-building over
time, which are important avenues for future work.
6.3.2
Metrics and Reproducibility
The choice of metrics—JSD for persuasion and a bias ratio for toxicity—was central to the benchmark’s
functionality. JSD provided a nuanced, continuous measure of belief change, superior to a binary win-loss
outcome. However, it compresses a complex shift into a single number, a limitation we mitigated by
supplementing the analysis with qualitative examples and distributional plots (Figure 5.2). Similarly,
the bias ratio is a coarse indicator. We addressed this by decomposing the analysis by category and
prompt method (Figures 5.13 and 5.14) to reveal more granular patterns.
Reproducibility was a core design principle.
The exclusive use of open-source models with fixed
decoding strategies ensures that other researchers can, in principle, replicate our findings. The automa-
tion of the experimental pipeline, while computationally intensive, minimized human intervention and
ensured systematic testing across all conditions.

6.4
Societal Impact and Ethical Considerations
The findings of this research extend beyond technical benchmarks to address critical societal and ethical
questions about the deployment of persuasive AI.
6.4.1
User Trust and Manipulation
Our results provide a quantitative basis for concerns about user manipulation. The demonstration that
persuasion can be enhanced by tailoring a message to a user’s profile (the homophily effect) highlights
the potential for misuse, such as deploying chatbots to nudge users towards extremist ideologies. The
sycophancy experiments reveal an even more insidious risk: the creation of AI-driven echo chambers.
When a model like Mistral not only agrees with but actively embellishes a user’s expressed bias, it creates
a dangerous feedback loop that can reinforce and escalate a user’s prejudiced views. This erodes trust
on two levels: users may over-trust an AI because it affirms their beliefs, and society’s trust in AI may
deteriorate as these manipulative capabilities become widely known.
6.4.2
Misinformation and Social Polarization
The study demonstrates how LLMs can serve as potent misinformation amplifiers. Under sycophantic
prompting, ’Mistral’ readily generated supporting details for biased claims, effectively manufacturing
misinformation on demand. The combination of persona adaptation and bias agreement is particularly
dangerous for spreading propaganda at scale, as a single system could generate thousands of tailored
messages that appear to come from a relatable peer. This has direct implications for social polarization,
as technology that could broaden perspectives might instead be used to entrench users within their
existing ideological bubbles. Our findings strongly support the need for AI systems to possess robust
guardrails against sycophantic agreement, especially on sensitive topics.
6.4.3
Policy and Alignment
The dual capabilities of persuasion and bias reinforcement present a dilemma for AI policy and alignment.
While persuasive AI could be a force for good in education or public health, the risks necessitate careful
governance. Our results suggest that AI alignment must go beyond simple refusal of harmful requests.
Models need to account for meta-instructions, like a blanket directive to "always be agreeable," which
can be used to bypass safety training.
The fact that even capable models can be manipulated this
way reinforces calls for transparency and robust internal "AI constitutional" principles that prevent the
model’s core helpfulness objective from being subverted. The ultimate goal, supported by this research,
is to develop LLMs that balance persuasive communication with a principled refusal to amplify harm,
thereby fostering trust and ensuring their contributions to society are beneficial.

Conclusions
This thesis set out to investigate the critical and complex interplay between persuasiveness and bias re-
inforcement in large language models. The central objective was to move beyond anecdotal evidence and
to quantitatively measure the dual-use nature of these powerful systems—their capacity to be effective
communicators and, simultaneously, their vulnerability to being co-opted to amplify harmful stereotypes.
To address this, we developed a controlled experimental framework that combined persona-based simu-
lations with sensitive, real-world data to systematically test how a model’s persuasive capabilities and
its susceptibility to bias are related. This final chapter synthesizes the key contributions and findings of
this work, acknowledges its limitations, and suggests directions for future research that build upon these
insights.

The primary contributions of this thesis are the establishment of a novel evaluation benchmark and the
empirical findings it produced regarding LLM behavior. The key findings can be summarized as follows:
• A Novel, Validated Framework for Evaluating Persuasion and Bias: We introduced a
comprehensive, two-part evaluation framework to implement and measure LLM persuasion and
bias. For RQ1, a convincer-skeptic simulation using 18 synthetic personas and the HQP data-
set quantified persuasive efficacy via Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD). The framework’s validity
was internally confirmed by its successful replication of the homophily principle, where increased
persona similarity led to greater persuasion, demonstrating that the synthetic personas encoded
meaningful social characteristics.
For RQ2, the framework tested the second most persuaded

of persuasion-resistant models such as ’DeepSeek-R1’ as objective judges to calculate a "bias ra-

This benchmark provides a scalable and reproducible methodology for studying complex
socio-technical phenomena in AI.
• The Persuader–Persuadable Duality in LLM Behavior: Our comparative analysis in RQ1

7.2
Limitations
While this research yielded valuable insights, several methodological and practical limitations must be
acknowledged to correctly interpret the results:
• Participant Simulation Gap: The study’s most significant limitation is its reliance on LLMs as
proxies for human users and evaluators. LLMs do not possess genuine beliefs or emotions, and their
cognitive processes differ from humans. Therefore, our findings on persuasion and bias perception,
while internally consistent, require further validation with human-in-the-loop studies to confirm
their ecological validity.
• Single-Turn Interaction Scope: The experiments were constrained to single-turn interactions
to isolate the immediate effect of a given prompt. This design does not capture the dynamics
of real-world persuasion, which often unfolds over multi-turn dialogues. The behavioral patterns
observed may manifest differently in more complex, interactive scenarios.
• Abstracted Evaluation Metrics: Quantifying complex phenomena like "persuasion" and "bias"
necessitated the use of proxy metrics. While JSD and the bias ratio enabled scalable analysis, they
are abstractions that do not capture the full nuance of belief change or the qualitative severity of
biased content.
7.3
Future Work and Broader Impact
The findings and limitations of this thesis point toward several promising avenues for future research
and highlight the broader societal importance of this work.
Future research should prioritize human-centered evaluation to bridge the simulation gap identi-
fied as a key limitation. Conducting user studies to measure actual attitude changes in people exposed
to persuasive LLM content is a critical next step. Furthermore, work should move beyond single-turn
prompts to explore multi-turn dialogues, allowing for the study of how persuasive strategies and bias
reinforcement evolve over time.
From a technical standpoint, a crucial direction is the development of bias-resistant persuasion
techniques. This involves creating models that can be rhetorically effective and empathetic while being
trained to identify and gently counter harmful or biased user premises, decoupling helpfulness from
harmful agreeableness.
The broader impact of this research lies in its contribution to the responsible deployment of AI.
The empirical demonstration of the "persuader-persuadable duality" and the potency of sycophantic
prompting provides concrete evidence for the risks of user manipulation and misinformation at scale.
These findings underscore the need for robust policy and alignment strategies that go beyond simple
content filters to address meta-instructions that can subvert a model’s safety training. The ultimate goal,
supported by this research, is to inform the development of LLMs that balance persuasive communication
with a principled refusal to amplify harm, thereby fostering trust and ensuring their contributions to
society are beneficial. This thesis, by systematically investigating the dual-use nature of persuasive AI,

A.1
Models utilized
A.1.1
Meta–Llama–3.1–8B
How it works
Trained with a next-token prediction objective on ∼15 T high-quality web and code
tokens (95 % English), the model later undergoes a four-stage alignment pipeline—supervised fine-tuning
(SFT), rejection-sampling RLHF, PPO and DPO—to follow instructions safely and fluently [10].
Core architecture
A dense decoder-only Transformer: 32 layers, 8 B parameters, 4096-dimensional
hidden states, and 32 attention heads of which only eight store key–value pairs via Grouped-Query
Attention. Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) and RMSNorm + SwiGLU blocks enable a native 128
k-token context.
Model-specific innovations
GQA slashes KV-cache size, while long-RoPE extends context without
retraining. Together these make Llama-3.1-8B a sweet spot for large-document RAG and other memory-
bound tasks, delivering strong reasoning on a single A100.
A.1.2
Qwen 1.5–1.8B–Chat
How it works
Pre-trained on ∼3 T filtered multilingual and code tokens, then chat-aligned with
Direct Policy Optimisation (DPO) using function-call demonstrations that teach the model to invoke
external tools [4].
Core architecture
A compact decoder-only stack of 24 layers, 1.8 B parameters, 2 048 hidden units
and 16 full attention heads. Rotary embeddings, RMSNorm and SwiGLU are standard; the checkpoint
supports a 32 768-token window out of the box.
Model-specific innovations
A 128 k-merge tokenizer balances English and Chinese coverage, and
reserved “<|tool|>” tokens let downstream systems integrate function calls with minimal prompt engin-
eering. The tiny footprint plus 32 k context makes it ideal for mobile RAG or lightweight chatbots.
A.1.3
Qwen 1.5–4B–Chat
How it works
Uses the same corpus and DPO alignment as the 1.8 B variant but benefits from deeper
chain-of-thought tracing, boosting GSM-8K accuracy above 55 % [4].
Core architecture
Still decoder-only, scaled to 40 layers, 4 B parameters, a 2 560-wide hidden state
and 20 attention heads. Context length stays at 32 k tokens, and the model fits in BF16 on a single 24
GB GPU.
Model-specific innovations
Occupies a “sweet-spot” trade-off: near-double reasoning power over the
1.8 B while keeping single-GPU latency low, making it popular for self-hosted assistants and low-budget
backends.

A.1.5
Qwen 1.5–14B–Chat
How it works
Pre-trained and aligned as its siblings but with richer high-difficulty mathematics and
coding traces; achieves MT-Bench 8.2 and near-GPT-3.5 scores on MMLU [4].
Core architecture
A 56-layer, 14 B parameter decoder-only model (hidden size 4096, 32 heads, GQA

Model-specific innovations
Shows steep parameter efficiency with twice the depth of GPT-3-1.3 B
but similar performance to GPT-3.5 at half the size, making it attractive for commercial deployments
under its permissive licence.
A.1.6
DeepSeek–R1–Distill-Llama–8B
How it works
An 8B distilled from a 70 B mixture-of-experts teacher. The final stages use multi-
objective RL to maximise a reasoning reward while preserving factuality [24].
Core architecture
Dense decoder-only: 32 layers, 4096 hidden units and Multi-Head Latent Attention
(MLA) atop RoPE. Checkpoints are distributed in FP8 and support a native 128 k-token window.
Model-specific innovations
MLA projects queries/keys/values to a low-rank latent space, halving
FLOPs at long context lengths; FP8 halves VRAM use with negligible accuracy loss, positioning the
model for cost-sensitive, long-document reasoning.
A.1.7
Phi-3-mini-4k
How it works
Trained on 3.3 T “textbook-quality” tokens—clean web, synthetic Q&A, and code—then
aligned for safety; ONNX export makes it phone-ready [2].
Core architecture
A 3.8 B-parameter decoder-only Transformer (≈32 layers, 3072 channels, 32 heads)
with RoPE. Default context is 4k tokens and weights ship in 8-bit integer form.
Model-specific innovations
Pairs a 128-vector tokenizer with int8 weights to run fully offline on <
8 GiB RAM while retaining strong STEM reasoning—ideal for privacy-sensitive mobile applications.
A.1.8
Mistral-Nemo-Instruct-2407
How it works
A joint Mistral AI/NVIDIA effort: pre-trained multilingual, then preference-tuned with
synthetic comparisons; achieves 68 % MMLU-5-shot and 83.5 % zero-shot HellaSwag [3].
Core architecture
12 B-parameter decoder-only model: 40 layers, 5120 hidden units, 32 heads (8
KV), 14336 dimension SwiGLU feed-forward, and RoPE for 128 k tokens.
Model-specific innovations
Optimised for NVIDIA GPUs with long context plus strong instruction
tuning make it popular for high-throughput enterprise chat and code-gen workloads.
