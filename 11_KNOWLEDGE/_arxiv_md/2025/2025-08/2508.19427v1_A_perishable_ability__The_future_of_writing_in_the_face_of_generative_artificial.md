A perishable ability? The future of writing in the
face of generative artificial intelligence
Evandro L. T. P. Cunha
Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais (UFMG)
Belo Horizonte, MG, Brazil
cunhae@ufmg.br
Abstract
The 2020s have been witnessing a very significant advance in the development of generative
artificial intelligence tools, including text generation systems based on large language models.
These tools have been increasingly used to generate texts in the most diverse domains – from
technical texts to literary texts –, which might eventually lead to a lower volume of written
text production by humans. This article discusses the possibility of a future in which human
beings will have lost or significantly decreased their ability to write due to the outsourcing
of this activity to machines. This possibility parallels the loss of the ability to write in other
moments of human history, such as during the so-called Greek Dark Ages (approx. 1200
BCE - 800 BCE).
Keywords Large language models · Text generation · ChatGPT · Linear B script
1
Introduction
Writing is arguably the most powerful and transformative technology that humans have ever developed.
Blatz (1944) considers that the invention of this tool made it possible to permanently record, for succeeding
generations, the accumulation of human experience.
According to Del Longo and Cisotto (2014), the
development of writing has enabled human language to cross the boundaries of time, space, and modality
thanks to its capability to store and retrieve information. In fact, writing is sometimes regarded with such
amazement that many societies suppose that it came from heaven (Schmandt-Besserat, 1986) – which shows
not only its power, but also the lack of understanding of its progressive development as a technological
tool. From an economic point of view, the adoption of written records was essential to the emergence of
cities and states, and facilitated trade and bureaucratic administration, in addition to having stimulated the
advancement of philosophy and science (Hanson, 2004). It is not by chance, therefore, that, historiographically,
the invention of writing is what marks the passage from what we call prehistory to what we consider history
(Goody and Watt, 1963).1
In the first decades of the 21st century, the relationship between humans and writing has been changing
dramatically.

More recently, technological advances in the field of artificial intelligence have created tools capable of

tools have become more and more popular in the early 2020s, when general-domain chatbots based on large
language models were launched, with great media interest. The highly satisfactory performance of these text
generators for many day-to-day tasks has led people to use them for producing the most diverse types of text,
from academic recommendation letters (Kaplan et al., 2024) to poetry translations (Gao et al., 2024).
The medium and long-term effects of the heavy use of tools based on generative artificial intelligence3 are
still not very clear. In this article, it is argued that, although it may seem exaggerated and even absurd to
some, it should not be excessive to consider that the human capacity to produce written texts may decrease
considerably or even disappear in the foreseeable future due to the increasingly accelerated development
and use of artificial intelligence tools for text generation. With the possibility of an ever-decreasing need
to produce their own written texts, humans could then gradually abandon the practice of writing, thus
converting it into an activity to be performed exclusively or almost exclusively by machines – similarly to
other activities that have already gone through or are going through this process.
2
Writing as a nonperennial technology: the case of Linear B
It is not news that, over the course of human history, technologies come and go. Different models of adoption,
continued usage and abandonment of technologies have been proposed, and studies focused on the acceptance
and renunciation of technologies in fields such as education (e.g., Granić and Marangunić, 2019), healthcare
(e.g., Nadal et al., 2020) and communication (e.g., Birnholtz, 2010) were carried out.
Nonetheless, some technologies have been around for so long that they feel eternal. This is probably the
case with writing, a technology that was first attested in Southern Mesopotamia around 3500 BCE4, based
on previous forms of representing language and as a means of record-keeping through signs and pictograms
marked on rocks and clay tokens. Over the centuries, writing established itself as a powerful technology and
spread across the globe, evolving into a robust tool for communication, literature, law and philosophy. It is
indisputable that its dissemination facilitated the exchange of ideas, the preservation of knowledge and, as a
consequence, the formation of complex societies.
Despite all the power contained in writing, however, this technology cannot be considered free of the risk of
being cast aside by a civilization. The abdication of writing systems throughout human history is, of course,

not as common as their adoption, but there might have been a few instances.5 The most significant example
of this phenomenon is probably the abandonment of Linear B, in the beginning of the so-called Greek Dark
Ages.
Linear B is a writing system developed during the Late Bronze Age in order to write the Mycenaean Greek
language. It was in use between approximately 1500 BCE and 1200 BCE, and derives directly from Linear
A – the writing system used to write in the Minoan language. Linear B script was deciphered in the early
1950s and its texts, present in more than five thousand surviving clay tablets, give us a broad overview of the
primary economic priorities within the Mycenaean palatial territories (Palaima, 2010). Certainly, during
the Late Bronze Age the activity of writing was not as widespread as it is in most modern societies. This
can be observed by the very nature of the Linear B texts that have reached us, which deal especially with
administrative, economic and military topics. In spite of being considered by some as a complex writing
system, Palaima (2010, p. 364) adds that several hundred tablet writers "had no difficulty using the script
effectively and routinely on a daily basis".
Around the late 12th century BCE, the Mycenaean civilization went into decline, for reasons that are still
unclear.6 According to Middleton (2019), the collapse of this civilization can be identified from five main
features of the archaeological record: (a) destructions at most major palace sites; (b) cessation of major
building projects; (c) loss of most fine arts and crafts; (d) cessation of Linear B use; and (e) site abandonments.
The ’cessation of Linear B use’ feature is particularly relevant for us. Middleton (2019) explains that:
Records had been kept in Linear B in some palaces since LM IIIA1 (Knossos) (early 14th
century BCE); they were a long-lived feature of the palace operations and indicate a tradition
of (probably very limited) literacy. They represent the reach of the palace and its personnel
into the surrounding territory and its duties with regard to (state?) religious occasions. This
tradition, going back around two centuries, ended in the collapse. This may indicate the
fracturing of relationships between palaces and their (former) territories, or locations where
their influence had been felt. The abandonment of writing must be considered deliberate as
there would have been some memory of it through c. 1200 BCE. Syllabic writing, influenced
by Linear B, continued on Cyprus through the LBA/EIA transition and was used to write
in Greek, but in the Aegean there was no more writing until the much later adoption of the
alphabet. (Middleton, 2019)
Linear B was primarily used for administrative and economic purposes in the Mycenaean palace centers.
Hence, with the collapse of these centers and the societal upheaval that followed, the use of the script declined
and it eventually fell out of use, since there was no longer a need or system in place to maintain it. Middleton
(2019) even concludes, due to the abrupt disappearance of archaeological records, that the abandonment of
writing in this context was deliberate. Also, it appears plausible to raise the hypothesis that, during the
challenging period that ended up being known as the Greek Dark Ages, writing may have been deemed
superfluous and dispensable for those circumstances, making it a useless technology in the eyes of that society.

The possibility of erosion of human writing abilities
It is perhaps unlikely that modern societies will face the same challenges that Mycenaeans went through in
the Late Bronze Age. Despite this, there are still several scenarios for the possible downfall of humanity,
including the threat of near-extinction due to nuclear war (Ehrlich et al., 1983), pandemics (Peters, 2022)
or global warming (Carpenter and Bishop, 2009). Also, some scholars have raised the possibility of the
emergence of major crises due to the development of artificial general intelligence devices: there are concerns,
for example, that these systems could surpass human intelligence and become uncontrollable or act against
human interests (Growiec, 2024). These situations could eventually lead to a scenario in which writing
becomes superfluous in the face of other difficulties to be experienced by the remaining human beings.
Even if none of these catastrophic scenarios materialize and humanity flourishes more and more, a risk to
the maintenance of the human ability to write is the expansion of the use of tools that make this knowledge
irrelevant. The reduction in the need to use the competence of writing could lead to a situation in which
knowing it is unnecessary for a large part of the population that currently uses it. The act of writing texts
could well be completely replaced by at least one of two other present technologies, or by a combination of
both: speech transcription and the automatic production of texts using tools based on generative artificial
intelligence. In this sense, the erosion of human writing abilities due to advanced text generators might be
similar, for example, to the decline in human ability to drive cars due to to the advent of autonomous vehicles,
as predicted by Havlíčková et al. (2020): as autonomous vehicles become the norm for daily transportation,
there will be less incentive for individuals to acquire or maintain proficiency in driving – and as generations
grow up in a world dominated by self-driving cars, the knowledge and muscle memory associated with
operating vehicles manually will probably fade into obscurity.
This scenario does not necessarily imply that human will lose the ability to read, since it is possible for
someone to have passive literacy, which refers to the ability to read and understand written text but not
necessarily to actively engage in writing or producing written content (Cressy, 1977). Individuals with
passive literacy skills can comprehend written materials, but may not frequently engage in writing tasks
themselves. This ability can still be valuable for consuming information, learning and communication, even if
the individual does not actively participate in writing activities.
It also seems reasonable to think of a scenario in which the ability to write is maintained only by professional
writers, especially those associated with creative writing. This scenario is in line with early uses of writing,
when it was a specialized skill reserved for a small, educated elite class (Blair, 2021). Still, this is probably
not the case for content creators, technical writers and journalists, who are already being replaced by artificial
intelligent text generators (Miroshnichenko, 2018).

ChatGPT’s opinion on this matter
As of 2024, ChatGPT7, developed by OpenAI, is probably the most well-known generative artificial intelligence
tool, acting as a chatbot and virtual assistant that interacts with users following their questions and commands
– known as prompts. The usage of ChatGPT in different scenarios has been the subject of a myriad of research
projects, interested in aspects such as its mathematical capabilities (Frieder et al., 2023), its effectiveness to
provide feedback to students (Wang et al., 2024) and the possibilities of its use for language teaching and
learning (Kohnke et al., 2023), among several others.

ChatGPT’s answer: It’s unlikely that humans will completely lose their writing abilities

1. Creativity and originality: AI can generate text based on patterns and data it has learned,
but it lacks true creativity and the ability to come up with entirely original ideas and
perspectives. Human writers can bring unique creativity, emotions, and personal experiences
to their writing that AI cannot replicate.
2. Critical thinking and analysis: Writing often involves the expression of complex thoughts,
arguments, and analysis of various topics. Humans possess critical thinking skills that
allow them to evaluate information, synthesize ideas, and present them coherently, which is
challenging for AI to achieve comprehensively.
3. Emotional connection: Human writing can evoke emotions, empathy, and relatability.
Personal stories, memoirs, and other forms of human expression can establish deep emotional
connections with readers, which is a unique aspect of human writing.
4. Context and nuance: Understanding context, cultural nuances, and social dynamics is
crucial in effective communication through writing. AI may struggle with these subtleties,
leading to misinterpretations or insensitive responses in some situations.
5. Varied writing styles and purposes: Writing encompasses a wide range of styles and
purposes, from poetry and literature to scientific research and legal documents. While AI
can assist with certain tasks, humans will continue to specialize in various writing niches
and adapt to evolving communication needs.
6. Learning and education: Writing is a fundamental skill taught in educational systems
worldwide. Humans will still need to learn how to write effectively to communicate and
succeed in various aspects of life, including academia, professional careers, and personal
communication.
While AI can be a valuable tool to enhance and streamline the writing process, it is more
likely to complement human writing abilities rather than replace them entirely. As technology
evolves, it’s essential for individuals to continue developing their writing skills alongside their
understanding of AI’s capabilities to harness the full potential of both human and artificial
intelligence in various contexts.

Conclusion
Writing is one of the most unique human abilities and has been around for over 5,000 years. Its importance in
our history is undeniable, which is perhaps why we might think of it as a skill that human beings will never
abandon. In this article, though, it is argued that the human ability to operate the technology of writing is
not necessarily eternal: like other technologies, it can be left out if it is no longer useful for a community. This
seems to be what happened, for example, when it was abandoned, maybe deliberately (Middleton, 2019), in
Greece at the end of the Late Bronze Age, around 1200 BCE, reappearing in this region only centuries later.
Although it may sound like an exaggeration to some, it seems possible to expect that the human ability to
write will lose much of its importance in a foreseeable future, due to the exponential growth in the use of
text generation systems based on artificial intelligence. At the current moment of technological development,
the limitations of these systems are many and well documented – they include reasoning and logic failures,
factual errors, bias and discrimination, among others (Borji, 2023). Specifically in the domain of academic
writing, important flaws are constantly found in tests with ChatGPT and similar tools, such as the creation
of fake articles when performing literature reviews (Haman and Školník, 2023; Kim, 2023). However, it is
not hard to conceive that, as investment in these tools continues to grow, their performance will become
increasingly better and the text generated by them will be suitable for more and more situations, making
them substitutes for human beings in the task of writing in several domains.
This article does not get into whether computers can think: that’s not the question. The issue is that, with
the amount of text already produced by humanity and the computational power to be available in the next
decades, the task of generating virtually any written text tends to be outsourced to machines. This may or
may not be seen as a problem – even though the benefits of writing for learning, reasoning and structuring
thoughts, performing tasks and for lifelong learning are quite well proven (Del Longo and Cisotto, 2014).
The risk of losing the ability to write, however, is small when compared to other risks linked to the fast
advancement of artificial intelligence systems that many scholars have been predicting, particularly human
