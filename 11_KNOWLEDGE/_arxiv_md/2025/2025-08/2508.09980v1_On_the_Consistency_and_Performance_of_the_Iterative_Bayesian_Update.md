On the Consistency and Performance of the
Iterative Bayesian Update
Ehab ElSalamouny and Catuscia Palamidessi
Abstract—For many social, scientific, and commercial purposes, it is often important to estimate the distribution of the users’ data
regarding a sensitive attribute, e.g. their ages, locations, etc. To allow this estimation while protecting the users’ privacy, every user
applies a local privacy protection mechanism that releases a noisy (sanitized) version of her/his original datum to the data collector;
then the original distribution is estimated using one of the known methods, such as the matrix inversion (INV), RAPPOR’s estimator, and
the iterative Bayesian update (IBU). Unlike the other estimators, the consistency of IBU, i.e. the convergence of its estimate to the real
distribution as the amount of noisy data grows, has been either ignored or incorrectly proved in the literature. In this article, we use the
fact that IBU is a maximum likelihood estimator to prove that IBU is consistent. We also show, through experiments on real datasets,
that IBU significantly outperforms the other methods when the users’ data are sanitized by geometric, Laplace, and exponential
mechanisms, whereas it is comparable to the other methods in the case of the k-RR and RAPPOR mechanisms. Finally, we consider
the case when the alphabet of the sensitive data is infinite and we show a technique that allows IBU to operate in this case too.
Index Terms—local privacy, privacy mechanisms, iterative Bayesian update, matrix inversion.

INTRODUCTION
W
Ith the ever-increasing use of internet-connected de-
vices, such as computers, smart grids, IoT appliances,
and GPS-enabled devices, personal data are collected in
larger and larger amounts, and then stored and manipulated
for the most diverse purposes. Undeniably, big-data tech-
nology provides enormous benefits to industry, individuals,
and society, ranging from improving business strategies
and boosting the quality of service to enhancing scientific
progress. However, the collection and manipulation of raw
personal data raises alarming privacy issues.
In this paper, we focus on the local privacy model [1], [2],
[3] which is widely used to protect the users’ privacy while
preserving the utility of the collected data. In this model,
every user applies a privacy protection mechanism to his
original datum to produce a noisy version of it, which is
then released to the data collector. The privacy mechanism
runs locally on the user’s device, and this is the reason why
the model is called ‘local’.
Concerning utility, we focus on the estimation of the
distribution of the original data from the collected noisy
releases. We argue that this is the most general kind of
aggregate information one can extract from the data, from
which all sorts of other analyses can be derived.
One protocol that has been proposed for privately col-
lecting data and performing the above estimation is RAP-
POR [4], which consists of two components: a specific
mechanism for obfuscating the users’ original data, and
an associated estimator that works on the outputs of that
mechanism to estimate the original distribution. However,
depending on the sensitive attribute, the users may need

conditions and we show that they are satisfied by the state-
of-the-art privacy mechanisms.

experiments, which show that IBU has a much larger range
of applicability than the other methods. When the data
are obfuscated by the k-RR which is a typical mechanism

observe that both INV and IBU produce equally good
estimates. Similarly, when the data are obfuscated by the
basic RAPPOR mechanism, which also satisfies LDP, both the
RAPPOR estimator (which was designed specifically for this

when the data are obfuscated by the typical mechanisms

exponential mechanisms, we find that IBU decisively out-

Proposition 5. Full details of our experiments are given in
Section 7.
Finally, we consider the situation when the alphabet
of the secrets (original users’ data) is infinite, for instance
when the bounds on the values of the sensitive attribute are
unknown. Applying IBU in this case is problematic because
IBU was designed for only finite alphabets. To handle this
problem we show a method that restricts the unbounded
alphabet, with no loss of generality, to a finite set, hence
allowing to apply IBU in this case too.

RELATED WORK
Most of the literature on estimating the distribution of pri-
vate data focused on protocols that satisfy local differential
privacy (LDP) [2], [3], [9] to every user. In particular the
authors of [2], [13] identified a lower bound for the minimax
error of the estimated distributions under LDP, and they
proposed an LDP protocol that attains this lower bound.
In their protocol, every user reports to the server a noisy-
bit vector in the same manner as the basic RAPPOR mech-
anism described in Section 4.4 of our article. The authors
of [14] could reduce the communication cost (the length
of the reported vector) using the idea of Random Projection
Matrix which was also used in [15] for efficiently identifying
heavy hitters. This report-length issue was mitigated also for
RAPPOR [4] by mapping the lengthy bit-vector, encoding the
user’s original datum, into a (smaller) Bloom filter which
is obfuscated and then reported to the server. Wang et
al. [16] generalized both RAPPOR [4] and Random Projection
Matrix [14] in one generic framework for LDP protocols.
This framework enabled the authors to introduce Optimized
Local Hashing (OLH) which has low communication cost
and better accuracy than earlier protocols. Kairouz et al.
provided in [5] an analysis for the estimation error of the
basic RAPPOR protocol, and also for the INV estimator after
sanitizing users’ data using the k-RR mechanism. All the
above proposals used the idea of randomized response [17]
to satisfy LDP, and their authors derived error bounds that
show their consistency. Our article however is not restricted
to LDP mechanisms: IBU can be used after sanitizing the
users’ data by any mechanism, such as those satisfying
εg-geo-indistinguishability [11]. Under this generality, we
describe the consistency of IBU. Concerning the perfor-
mance of estimating the original distribution, the authors
of [18] have recently provided an experimental comparison
between several LDP protocols on one side and on the
other when the estimation procedure of these protocols
is replaced by IBU. This comparison shows the superi-
ority of IBU. Our article complements these performance
results showing that the superiority of IBU is even more
prominent when the data are obfuscated by the typical εg-
geo-indistinguishability mechanisms. Furthermore, IBU has
been shown to perform well also on other mechanisms:
[19] has reported experiments on the application of IBU
on data obfuscated by the Blahut-Arimoto mechanism [20],
presenting high precision estimates.

4.4
Basic RAPPOR mechanism
RAPPOR is a mechanism built on the idea of randomized
response [17] to allow collecting statistics from end-users
with differential privacy guarantees [4]. The basic form of
this mechanism, called Basic (One-Time) RAPPOR maps the
alphabet of original values X to an alphabet Z of size 2|X|.
More precisely every original (private) datum x ∈X is

UNIQUENESS OF THE MLE
In the following, we describe whether there is a unique
maximum likelihood estimate (MLE). Again, this property
depends on the privacy mechanism and the reported noisy
data. The MLE uniqueness requires first that there is at
least one MLE. This holds if some distribution ϕ ∈C has
a finite log-likelihood (i.e. L(ϕ) > −∞), because in this
case the subset {θ ∈C : L(θ) ≥L(ϕ)} is compact by [7,
Lemma 1]. This compactness together with the continuity of
L(·) assures the existence of an MLE by the extreme value
theorem. Finally, it is known that the maximum of a strictly
concave function over a convex set is unique (see e.g. [21,
Proposition 2.1.2]). Thus the uniqueness of the MLE follows
from the strict concavity of L(·) and the convexity of C.
Proposition 1. Suppose that L(ϕ) > −∞for some ϕ ∈C and
L(·) is strictly concave on C. Then the MLE is unique.

The identification condition
To inspect the identification condition in Lemma 4, consider
a set of observations and let G be their probability matrix
(see Def. 1). Then the probabilities of these observations
are exactly the vector θ G where θ is the real distribution
on X . Thus the identification means θ G ̸= ϕ G for some

Theorem 3 (Identification). For a mechanism M that works on
alphabet X , the following conditions are equivalent.
(i) P(·|θ) ̸= P(·|ϕ) for all distributions θ ̸= ϕ.
(ii) L(·) is strictly concave for some set of observables.
(iii) M has |X| linearly independent columns.

Regularity and dominance conditions
Condition (ii) of Lemma 4 is clearly satisfied since the space

since for every z ∈Z we have log(ϕ M·z) continuous with
respect to ϕ. Finally, the dominance condition (iv) depends
on the mechanism matrix M, and is therefore checked on a
per-case basis.

8
OBTAINING MLES FOR INFINITE ALPHABETS
In many situations, the alphabet X of the sensitive data may
be very large or even infinite. For example, a server may
be required to estimate the distribution on the values of a
linear attribute such as the annual income, internet usage,
or others for which we don’t have specific bounds. In this
case, X can be described as a discretized version of R+
which is infinite. The collected data, however, are always
confined within a bounded range. Another example is an
online location-based service that requires to estimate the
distribution of visited locations in a large geographic re-
gion based on sanitized checkins collected from individuals.
Then X may be seen as a grid covering the entire region,
hence consisting of a large number of cells. The collected
(sanitized) checkins on the other hand may be however
situated in a few cells or within a small area inside this
region.

Applying a privacy mechanism on a large alphabet such
as those in the above examples is relatively simple. In fact,
there are mechanisms such as the linear and planar geo-
metric, exponential, and Laplace mechanisms which can be
applied in the above cases because they work on both finite
and infinite alphabets of sensitive data. However, applying
IBU in these cases to obtain an estimate for the original
distribution is problematic because IBU is not practical for
too large alphabets (recall that IBU (algorithm 1) has to
compute a probability θx for every element x of X ). In
the following, we address this problem, and we show that
under the above privacy mechanisms, the infinite or too
large alphabet can be reduced, with no loss of generality,
to a smaller alphabet on which we can efficiently obtain an
MLE via IBU.
Our main idea is that we identify the elements of X that
are ‘unlikely’ to produce the given noisy data via the applied
privacy mechanism. By excluding these elements from X
we obtain a smaller finite subset, called ‘likely’, on which
we can apply IBU. This method is detailed and formally
justified in the following subsection.
8.1
Likely subsets of the alphabet
Consider noisy data that are produced by a mechanism M.
With respect to these noisy data and the mechanism, we call
an element x′ ∈X ‘unlikely’ if there is another element x ∈
X that is likely at least as x′ to produce every observation in
the noisy data, and x is strictly more likely than x′ for one of
these observations. In terms of the conditional probabilities
of the mechanism M, this definition is written as follows.
Definition 2 (unlikely element). Consider a privacy mecha-
nism M that works on an alphabet X . Consider also a set of
observations {zi : i ∈[n]} produced by M. Then an element
x′ ∈X is unlikely in X with respect to the mechanism and the
observations if there is another x ∈X such that Mx′zi ≤Mxzi
for all i ∈[n], and this inequality is strict for some i.
From the above definition note that whether an element
x′ is unlikely or not depends on the observed noisy data and
the mechanism which produced these data. The following
lemma shows that any MLE on X must assign probability 0
to the unlikely elements.
Lemma 5. Consider a mechanism that works on an alphabet X
and consider a set of observations. If ˆθ is an MLE on X with
respect to the mechanism and the observations, then it must hold
that ˆθx′ = 0 for every unlikely x′ ∈X .
By removing any number of unlikely elements from X
we obtain a smaller set which we refer to as a ‘likely’ subset
of X and denote by ˆ

subsets of X as follows
Definition 3 (likely subsets). Consider a privacy mechanism
that works on an alphabet X , and consider also a set of obser-
vations. Then any subset ˆ

mechanism and observations if every x′ ∈(X \ ˆ

MLEs on the large X can be obtained more efficiently by
computing the MLE only on a (smaller) likely subset of X

For obtaining a reasonable trade-off between privacy and

8.4
Alphabets under k-RR mechanisms
Now we consider an alphabet X that is finite but arbitrarily
large. We also suppose that a k-RR mechanism (5) is used
to sanitize the users’ original values taken from X . Recall
that both the inputs and outputs of the k-RR are elements of
X . Since X is finite, IBU may be applied directly to obtain
an MLE ˆθ on X by computing a probability ˆθx for every
x ∈X . However, this would be inefficient if X is large.
In this situation, we can accelerate IBU by running it only
on a likely subset, which turns out to be exactly the set of
reported observations in this case.
Proposition 8 (likely subset under k-RR). Consider any finite
alphabet X , and suppose that a k-RR mechanism is used to
obfuscate the users’ data from X to produce the observations
X ′ = {zi : i ∈[n]}. Then X ′ is a likely subset of X .
Suppose for example that X consists of the cells that
cover a certain geographic region, and the users apply a
k-RR mechanism to sanitize their locations (cells). Then
according to Proposition 8, the likely set of X consists
of only the reported cells. If the original users’ check-ins
do not occupy the entire region, for example, due to the
existence of lakes, mountains, etc., the noisy dataset may
be distributed over only a small number of cells, hence
reducing the computation time of IBU.
9
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
In this article, we have analyzed the consistency of the iter-
ative Bayesian update (IBU). In other words, we described
precisely the conditions that assure the convergence of the
IBU’s estimate to the true distribution, namely the identi-
fication, dominance, and other regularity conditions. These
conditions depend on the privacy mechanism used to de-
liver the noisy data to IBU. We have characterized the iden-
tification in terms of the mechanism, and we proved that
it is equivalent to the strict concavity of the log-likelihood
function. This allowed us to show that the uniqueness of
the MLE resulting from IBU is a consequence of the strict
concavity, but however, does not guarantee the IBU’s consis-
tency. We have described mechanisms that satisfy the above
consistency conditions, namely k-RR, linear geometric, and
the RAPPOR’s mechanism. We also presented a mechanism
that does not satisfy these conditions. We compared between
IBU and other estimators with respect to the estimation
performance and showed in particular that IBU is superior
for mechanisms that satisfy geo-indistinguishability for the
users. Finally, we extended the application of IBU to the case
where the alphabet of the sensitive attribute is unbounded.
In future work, we plan to inspect the consistency of
IBU when other privacy mechanisms are used, such as the
planar geometric and exponential ones, and also the one re-
sulting from the Blahut-Arimoto construction algorithm [20]
which is also geo-indistinguishable. We plan also to inspect
the consistency and the estimation performance when the
individual users apply different mechanisms, the situation
in which we use an extension of IBU called GIBU, that was
presented in [7].
