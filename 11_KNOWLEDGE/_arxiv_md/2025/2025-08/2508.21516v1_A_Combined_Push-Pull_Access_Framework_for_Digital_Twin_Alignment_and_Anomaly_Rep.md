Digital Twin Alignment and Anomaly Reporting

Abstract—A digital twin (DT) contains a set of virtual models
of real systems and processes that are synchronized to their phys-
ical counterparts. This enables experimentation and examination
of counterfactuals, simulating the consequences of decisions in
real time. However, the DT accuracy relies on timely updates
that maintain alignment with the real system. We can distinguish
between: (i) pull-updates, which follow a request from the DT
to the sensors, to decrease its drift from the physical state; (ii)
push-updates, which are sent directly by the sensors since they
represent urgent information, such as anomalies. In this work, we
devise a push-pull scheduler (PPS) medium access framework,
which dynamically allocates the communication resources used
for these two types of updates. Our scheme strikes a balance in
the trade-off between DT alignment in normal conditions and
anomaly reporting, optimizing resource usage and reducing the
drift age of incorrect information (AoII) by over 20% with respect
to state-of-the-art solutions, while maintaining the same anomaly
detection guarantees, as well as reducing the worst-case anomaly
detection AoII from 70 ms to 20 ms when considering a 1 ms
average drift AoII constraint.
Index Terms—Digital Twin, Medium Access Control, Age of
Incorrect Information, data relevance
I. INTRODUCTION
The digital twin (DT) paradigm has emerged as a transfor-
mative approach to bridge the gap between physical systems
and their virtual representations [1]. A DT comprises a com-
prehensive set of virtual models that mirror real systems and
processes, maintaining continuous synchronization with their
physical counterparts through sensor data streams. It enables
real-time monitoring, prediction, and control across diverse
industrial applications [2], along with accelerated experimen-
tation and examination of counterfactuals.
The utility of DTs rely on maintaining precise alignment
between virtual models and the evolving physical reality they
represent. Achieving this alignment involves a key trade-
off: balancing proactive system monitoring of gradual state
changes with reactive responses to critical anomalies [3].
Although both are essential for comprehensive DT operations,
they impose conflicting demands on communication needs.
Proactive monitoring typically involves fusing data from
multiple sensors to detect system drift or degraded working

states [4]. While individual sensor readings may not show
strong deviations, their combined patterns can reveal subtle

of DTs: the system periodically requests data from sensors to
refine its physical state understanding enabling timely change
detection on average, while still tolerating moderate delays.
In contrast, reactive alerting addresses urgent events that
require immediate attention [5], [6], such as equipment faults,
security breaches, or safety violations. Individual sensors may
independently detect and report outlier values, guaranteeing
timely and reliable responsiveness in worst-case conditions.
To exemplify, consider an industry having sensors measur-
ing temperature and vibration of machines. The DT system
benefits by regularly aggregating sensor data to detect gradual
degradation patterns indicating maintenance needs. However,
when a bearing begins to fail, triggering sudden measure
spikes, individual sensors must immediately alert the system
without waiting for the next collection cycle.
The conflicting communication requirements lead to the
combined use of pull- and push-based communications. Pull-
based communication approaches involve a central node, such
as a base station (BS), explicitly requesting data from sensors
as dictated by the DT. This paradigm is particularly well-suited
for periodic updates, goal-oriented control tasks, and scenarios
where the central system can optimally schedule transmissions
based on global system knowledge [7]–[10]. On the other
hand, push-based communication approaches enable sensors
to autonomously transmit data when they detect significant
changes or anomalous conditions. Based on random access
protocols [11]–[14], push-based systems excel where event-
driven communication is more efficient than periodic polling,
especially for rare anomalous events that require immediate
attention [15]–[17]. While these approaches have been viewed
as alternative strategies [18], recent works suggest that their
combination can yield significant performance improvements,
due to an intrinsic coexistence gain [19]–[21].
To motivate our approach, consider M = 3 machines, each
monitored by 4 sensors. Sensors can jointly detect drifts in
machine behavior or individually sense rare anomalies. Since
not all sensors must report to identify model drift, assume
detection succeeds with probability νreset = 90% if 3 out of 4
sensors respond. Suppose communication occurs over frames
with 4 slots. A pull-only strategy could cycle through sensor

be reported by the pulled sensors, enabling prompt detection.
Conversely, if an anomaly reported via the push slot appears
correlated with a drift event, the BS can proactively pull the
remaining sensors from the affected machine to confirm and
refine its model. In short, all incoming messages, whether push
or pull, contribute to updating the BS’s knowledge, enhancing
system performance beyond either mode alone.
Despite the benefits of combining the pull and push
paradigms, several technical challenges have limited the de-
velopment of adaptive unified frameworks [21]–[23]: (i) de-
signing frames that support both contention-free pull and
contention-based push for multiple sensors while maintaining
existing standard compatibility presents significant engineering
challenges; (ii) dynamically allocating resources between pull
and push requires balancing competing objectives while adapt-
ing to changing system conditions; iii) maintaining accurate
beliefs about drift and anomalies requires robust tracking
handling partial observations, communication failures, and the
interdependence between different information sources.
To address these challenges, we propose a push-pull sched-
uler (PPS) medium access framework able to dynamically
allocates resources for push and pull paradigms. Our approach
models DT drift using a hidden Markov model (HMM),
while capturing anomaly occurrences through independent
Markov processes, providing a highly general and adaptable
mathematical foundation. Our framework employs a frame
structure compatible with 3rd Generation Partnership Project
(3GPP) standards, enabling the BS to continuously update
its beliefs by collecting observations from the sensors. The
key contributions are: (i) a unified mathematical model that
captures both DT drift and anomaly detection requirements;
(ii) the design of intelligent scheduling algorithms that dy-
namically allocate resources to pull and push traffic based
on real-time system state estimates; (iii) the introduction of
novel belief update mechanisms that leverage information on
successful transmissions and collision to maintain accurate
state estimates; and (iv) a comprehensive performance evalua-
tion demonstrating significant improvements over state-of-the-
art approaches. Specifically, our approach reduces worst-case
anomaly age of incorrect information (AoII) by more than
three times under the same DT drift AoII constraint, an great
improvement for critical applications.

N of sensors, with |N| = N, observe a spatially distributed

maintain the DT of the system aligned with physical reality, as
well as detecting anomalous conditions as quickly as possible.

B. Communication Model
The frame needs to be designed to accommodate both
push- and pull-based communication modes. Accordingly, we
employ an instance of the contention-free pull and contention-
push (CFC-push/pull) frame [19], an example of which is
given in Fig. 1. Each frame has a fixed duration of T seconds,
comprises a total of R resource elements (REs) for uplink (UL)
transmissions, and is divided into pull and push subframes.
The pull subframe is divided in two: first, the BS trans-
mits a downlink (DL) pull scheduling signal, composed of
a synchronization signal (SS) and the (multi-cast) requests
for scheduling specific node transmissions. The scheduled
nodes then transmit orthogonally over the Q pull REs, being
Q ∈{0, . . . , R} [19]. In the remainder of the paper, the set
of nodes scheduled in the k-th pull subframe is denoted as
Sk ⊆Nd, being |Sk| = Q.
Similarly, the push subframe begins with a DL push control
signal, which contains another SS and a threshold value θ∗(k):
nodes that observed anomalies, but whose AoII is lower
than the threshold, will remain silent to avoid collisions with
higher-priority traffic [25]. Further details on the threshold
mechanism are provided in Sec. IV-B. Finally, the control
signal includes the number P = R −Q of available push
REs. Accordingly, the devices that observe an anomaly at the
beginning of the k-th frame, i.e., ∀n ∈Na : xn(k) = 1,
listen for the control signal and decode it to recover slot
boundaries and the frame structure. The nodes whose AoII
status is compliant with the one requested by the BS, i.e.,
Θn(k) ≥θ∗(k), then transmit in a grant-free manner over the

C. Framework Architecture
The proposed framework consists of three key modules:
tracking, scheduling, and transmission, as shown in Fig. 2.
The tracking module monitors DT drift events and anoma-
lies by continuously updating a maximum a posteriori (MAP)
probability distribution for both phenomena. After each step


## Page 4


from frame k −1 to frame k, it computes a prior distribution
based on the model in Sec. II-A, and updates it to a posterior
using observations obtained from the transmission module.
Details on the prior and posterior belief computations are
provided in Sec. III.
The scheduling module leverages the previous frame’s pos-
terior distribution to reserve Q REs for pull and P REs for
push subframes. It then uses the current frame’s prior distribu-
tion to select the Q sensors to pull from the set Nd and to set
the anomaly AoII threshold θ∗(k), which determines which
sensors may attempt grant-free transmission. This threshold is
designed to prioritize sensors with a high anomaly AoII while
managing collision risk. Further details are given in Sec. IV.
The transmission module handles DL signaling and UL
packet reception, as described in Sec. II-B. It also processes
the data into observables compatible with the tracking module.
For simplicity, we assume the entire framework runs at the
BS, which interacts directly with the DT to correct drifts
and with an external agent to report anomalies. Alternative
deployments (e.g., as an xApp) are possible but not further
investigated in this paper.

VI. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we presented PPS, a scheme that can exploit
frame subdivision to accommodate both push- and pull-based
traffic and improve the drift performance of DTs. We con-
sidered a dual objective, targeting a system in which the DT
alignment task is complicated by the presence of anomalies
that can only be detected by the sensors, and presented two
heuristics that can significantly improve performance for the
two tasks. We then proposed dynamic resource allocation


## Page 10


schemes, showing that an appropriate management of the pull
and push subframes can lead to gain between 20% and 60%
with respect to the state-of-the-art.
This is a first step towards the integration of push- and pull-
based schemes, and future extensions will need to consider
more realistic settings, as well as more complex learning-
based resource allocation and scheduling schemes, to meet this
challenge. Additionally, a more complete theoretical character-
ization of the joint problem, providing optimality guarantees
and bounds, will shed further light on future protocol design.
