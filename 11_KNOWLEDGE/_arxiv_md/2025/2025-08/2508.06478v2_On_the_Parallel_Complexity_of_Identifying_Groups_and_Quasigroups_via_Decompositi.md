On the Parallel Complexity of Identifying Groups and Quasigroups
via Decompositions
Dan Johnson1, Michael Levet1, Petr Vojtˇechovsk´y2, and Brett Widholm1
1Department of Computer Science, College of Charleston
2Department of Mathematics, University of Denver
February 5, 2026
Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the computational complexity of isomorphism testing for finite groups
and quasigroups, given by their multiplication tables.
We crucially take advantage of their various
decompositions to show the following:
• We first consider the class of groups that admit direct product decompositions, where each inde-
compsable factor is O(1)-generated, and either perfect or centerless. We show any group in this class
is identified by the O(1)-dimensional count-free Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) algorithm with O(log log n)
rounds, and the O(1)-dimensional counting WL algorithm with O(1) rounds. Consequently, the
isomorphism problem for this class is in L. This improves upon the previous upper bound of TC1,
which was obtained using O(log n) rounds of the O(1)-dimensional counting WL (Grochow and
Levet; FCT 2023, J. Comput. Syst. Sci. 2026).
• We next consider more generally, the class of groups where each indecomposable factor is O(1)-
generated. We exhibit an AC3 canonical labeling procedure for this class. Here, we accomplish
this by showing that in the multiplication table model, the direct product decomposition can be
computed in AC3, parallelizing the work of Kayal and Nezhmetdinov (ICALP 2009).
• Isomorphism testing between a central quasigroup G and an arbitrary quasigroup H is in NC. Here,
we take advantage of the fact that central quasigroups admit an affine decomposition in terms of
an underlying Abelian group. Only the trivial bound of nlog(n)+O(1)-time was previously known for
isomorphism testing of central quasigroups.

Introduction
The Quasigroup Isomorphism (QGpI) problem takes as input two finite quasigroups G, H and asks if there
exists an isomorphism φ : G →H. When the quasigroups are given by their multiplication (Cayley) tables, it
is known that QGpI belongs to NP∩coAM (see e.g., [GMW86]). The generator-enumeration strategy has time
complexity nlog(n)+O(1), where n is the order of the group. In the setting of groups, this strategy was inde-
pendently discovered by Felsch and Neub¨user [FN70] and Tarjan (see [Mil78]). Miller subsequently extended
this strategy to the setting of quasigroups [Mil78]. The parallel complexity of the generator-enumeration
strategy has been gradually improved [LSZ77, Wol94, CTW13, Wag10, Tan13]. Collins, Grochow, Levet,
and Weiß recently improved the bound for the generator enumeration strategy to
∃log2 n∀log n∃log nDTISP(polylog(n), log(n)),
which can be simulated by depth-4 quasiAC0 circuits of size nO(log n) [CGLW25].
In the special case of Group Isomorphism (GpI), the worst-case runtime has been improved to
n(1/4) log(n)+O(1), due to Rosenbaum [Ros13] and Luks [Luk15] (see [LGR16, Sec. 2.2]). Even the impressive
body of work on practical algorithms for GpI in more succinct input models, led by Eick, Holt, Leedham-
Green and O’Brien (e. g., [BEO02, ELGO02, BE99, CH03]) still results in an nΘ(log n)-time algorithm in the
general case (see [Wil19, Page 2]).
In practice, such as working with computer algebra systems, the Cayley model is highly unrealistic.
Instead, the groups are given by generators as permutations or matrices, or as black-boxes. In the setting
of permutation groups, GpI belongs to NP [Luk93].
When the groups are given by generating sets of
matrices, or as black-boxes, GpI belongs to PromiseΣp
2 [BS84]; it remains open as to whether GpI belongs
to NP or coNP in such succinct models. In the past several years, there have been significant advances on
algorithms with worst-case guarantees on the serial runtime for special cases of this problem; we refer to
[GQ17, DW22, GL26] for a survey. To the best of our knowledge, polynomial-time isomorphism tests for
quasigroups that are not groups have not been previously investigated.
Key motivation for QGpI comes from its relationship to the Graph Isomorphism problem (GI). When
the quasigroups are given verbosely by their multiplication tables, there exists an AC0-computable many-one
reduction from QGpI to GI [ZKT85]. On the other hand, there is no reduction from GI to QGpI computable
by AC circuits of depth o(log n/ log log n) and size npolylog(n) [CTW13]. In light of Babai’s breakthrough
result that GI is quasipolynomial-time solvable [Bab16], QGpI in the Cayley model is a key barrier to
improving the complexity of GI. There is considerable evidence suggesting that GI is not NP-complete
[Sch88, BH92, IPZ01, Bab16, KST92, AK06]. As QGpI reduces to GI, this evidence also suggests that
QGpI is not NP-complete.
Despite the fact that QGpI is strictly easier than GI under AC0-reductions, there are several key ap-
proaches in the GI literature, such as parallelization, Weisfeiler–Leman, and canonization, that have received
comparatively little attention even in the special case of GpI– see the discussion of Further Related Work
(Section 1.1). In this paper, we will make advances in each of these directions.
There is a natural divide-and-conquer strategy for GpI, which proceeds as follows. Given groups G1, G2,
we first seek to decompose the groups in terms of normal subgroups Ni ⊴Gi (i ∈[2]) and the corresponding
quotients Gi/Ni. It is then necessary (but not sufficient) to decide if N1 ∼= N2 and G1/N1 ∼= G2/N2. This
strategy has led to algorithmic advances for groups admitting nice decompositions, including for instance,
direct product decompositions [KN09, Wil12, BS22, GL26], coprime [Gal09, QST11, BQ12, GL26] and tame
[GQ15] extensions, and central extensions [LW12, BMW17, IQ19, BGL+19, GQ17]. In case of quasigroups
the situation is made more complicated by the lack of normal substructures; one has to factor by congruences
instead. (Recall that a congruence of an algebra A is an equivalence relation on A that is compatible with all
operations of A.) We therefore focus on the isomorphism problem for quasigroups that admit representations
in terms of more structured algebras, such as groups and their automorphisms.
Main Results. In this paper, we investigate the parallel complexity of isomorphism testing for finite groups
and quasigroups, by taking advantage of key decompositions.
We first investigate the power of the Weisfeiler–Leman (WL) algorithm to identify groups based on
their direct product decompositions. Weisfeiler–Leman is a key combinatorial algorithm used in the setting
of Graph Isomorphism. Brachter and Schweitzer recently provided three adaptations to the setting of

groups, specified by their multiplication (Cayley) tables [BS20]. In this work, we will use only the first two
adaptations, which Brachter and Schweitzer refer to as WL Versions I and II. We will provide a brief overview
of WL here, and we refer to Section 2.3 for a precise formulation. For k ≥2, the k-dimensional Weisfeiler–
Leman algorithm iteratively colors k-tuples of group elements in an isomorphism-invariant manner. At each
refinement step, each k-tuple receives a new color based on (i) its current color, and (ii) the multiset of
colors from the “nearby” k-tuples. Additionally, we will be interested in the weaker count-free variant of
Weisfeiler–Leman, in which the refinement steps only take into account the set of colors from the “nearby”
k-tuples, rather than the full multiset. Grohe and Verbitsky [GV06] observed that WL can be effectively
parallelized (see Section 2.5 for further discussion). Consequently, controlling both the dimension and number
of iterations can yield improvements in the parallel complexity of isomorphism testing.
We now turn to discussing direct product decompositions. Kayal and Nezhmetdinov [KN09] and Wilson
[Wil12] previously exhibited polynomial-time algorithms to compute direct product decompositions of finite
groups into indecomposable direct factors (we refer to such decompositions as fully refined– see Section 3.1
for more background on direct products). For group isomorphism testing, the main benefit of these results
is that, for the purposes of getting polynomial-time isomorphism tests, they reduce the problem to the case
of directly indecomposable groups.
Brachter and Schweitzer [BS22] established a WL-analogue, by relating the Weisfeiler–Leman dimension
for an arbitrary group G, to that of G’s indecomposable direct factors. Grochow and Levet [GL26] parallelized
this result, essentially showing that WL can detect the indecomposable direct factors using only O(log n)
rounds. While WL does not explicitly return a direct product decomposition itself, for the purposes of
isomorphism testing, WL achieves essentially the same benefit of reducing to the directly indecomposable
case– in TC1 (at the cost of increasing the dimension by 1 and adding O(log n) rounds) [GL26].
On the other hand, Grohcow and Levet (ibid.) showed that the analogous result fails to hold for the
count-free variant of WL. While cyclic groups are identified by the count-free 2-WL, there exist infinite
families (Gm, Hm)m∈N of non-isomorphic Abelian groups that require Ω(log |Gm|)-dimensional (and hence,
Θ(log |Gm|)-dimensional) count-free WL to distinguish between Gm and Hm.
Motivated by this gap, we investigate the ability of count-free WL to identify groups that admit nice direct
product decompositions. The Remak–Krull–Schmidt theorem provides that for any two fully-refined direct
product decompositions S = {S1, . . . , Sk} and T = {T1, . . . , Tk} of a group G, there exists a permutation
π ∈Sym(k) such that Si ∼= Tπ(i) for all i ∈[k]. In general, however, Si and Tπ(i) need not be the same
subgroup (setwise). On the other hand, it is well-known that when Si is centerless, it appears (setwise)
in every fully-refined direct product decomposition of G.
The same holds true when Si is perfect (see
Corollary 3.6). For this reason, we investigate the distinguishing power of the count-free Weisfeiler–Leman
algorithm for the following class of groups.

Central quasigroups play an important role since they are precisely the Abelian objects (in the sense of

A quasigroup (Q, ∗) is medial (also entropic) if it satisfies the medial law, for all x, y, u, v ∈Q:
(x ∗y) ∗(u ∗v) = (x ∗u) ∗(y ∗v).
It was shown by Toyoda–Murdoch–Bruck [Toy41, Mur41, Bru63] that medial quasigroups form a subclass
of central quasigroups. In more detail, up to isomorphism, medial quasigroups are precisely the central
quasigroups Q(Q, +, ϕ, ψ, c) such that ϕψ = ψϕ. A geometric interpretation of this representation can be
found in [Vol86]. Small entropic quasigroups were enumerated in [SV15]. The medial law has also been
studied more generally in [Bon22, JK83].

Further Related Work
Parallelization.
There is a long history of work on developing efficient parallel (NC) algorithms for special
cases of Graph Isomorphism. We refer to [LRS24] for a survey. In comparison, the work on NC algorithms
for (Quasi)Group Isomorphism is comparatively nascent. Much of the work revolves around parallelizing
the generator-enumeration strategy (ibid.), culminating in bounds of L for O(1)-generated groups [Tan13]
and SAC1 for O(1)-generated quasigroups [Wag10]. Prior to this paper (Theorem 1.10), no other special
family of quasigroups (beyond certain families of groups) was known to even admit an polynomial-time
isomorphism test. Other families of groups known to admit NC isomorphism tests include Abelian groups
[Vil94, CTW13, GL26, CGLW25], graphical groups arising from the CFI graphs [BS20, CL24, Col23], coprime
extensions H ⋉N where H is O(1)-generated and N is Abelian [GL26] (parallelizing a result from [QST11]),
groups of almost all orders [CGLW25] (parallelizing [DW22]), and Fitting-free groups [GJL25] (parallelizing
[BCQ12]).

Weisfeiler–Leman.
The k-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm (k-WL) serves as a key combinatorial
tool in GI. It works by iteratively coloring k-tuples of vertices in an isomorphism-invariant manner. On its
own, Weisfeiler–Leman serves as an efficient polynomial-time isomorphism test for several families of graphs–
see Sandra Kiefer’s dissertation for a survey [Kie19]. In the case of graphs of bounded treewidth [GV06,
LRS24], planar graphs [GV06, Ver07, GK21], and graphs of bounded rank-width [LRS24], Weisfeiler–Leman
serves even as a TC1 isomorphism test. Despite the success of WL as an isomorphism test, it is insufficient
to place GI into P [CFI92, NS18]. Nonetheless, WL remains an active area of research. For instance, Babai’s
quasipolynomial-time algorithm [Bab16] combines O(log n)-WL with group-theoretic techniques.
The use of combinatorial techinques for GpI is relatively new; and to the best of our knowledge, was
initiated in [LQ17, BGL+19]. Brachter and Schweitzer [BS20] subsequently initiated the study of Weisfeiler–
Leman applied directly to the Cayley table. In particular, they established that graphical groups [Mek81,
HQ21] arising from the CFI graphs [CFI92] have WL-dimension at most 3. There has been considerable
work building on the framework introduced by Brachter and Schweitzer– see [BS22, Bra23, GL25, GL26,
CL24, Col23, CRP24, Vag23, GJL25]. It remains open whether the class of finite groups has WL-dimension
o(log n).
Canonization.
It is open whether canonization reduces to isomorphism testing. Nonetheless, in the setting
of graphs, efficient canonization procedures have often followed efficient isomorphism tests, usually with non-
trivial work– see e.g., [IL90, GN23, KV08, Wag11, ES17, Bab19, LRS24]. Less is known about canonization
in the setting of groups and quasigroups.
Abelian groups admit an NC2 canonization procedure using
Smith normal form [Vil94]. Additionally, canonization for O(1)-generated groups is in L (see e.g., [Lev23]).
In the special case of finite simple groups, canonization is FOLL [CL24]. Gill, Mammoliti, and Wanless
showed that quasigroups admit a canonization procedure that has polynomial-time average-case complexity
[GMW25, Gil25].

2.1
Groups and Quasigroups
All groups and quasigroups will be assumed to be finite.
Groups.
Let G be a group. Let Z(G) denote the center of G. Given g, h ∈G, the commutator [g, h] :=
ghg−1h−1. For sets X, Y ⊆G, the commutator subgroup [X, Y ] := ⟨{[g, h] : g ∈X, h ∈Y }⟩. We say that
G is perfect if G = [G, G], and that G is centerless if Z(G) = 1. Let d(G) be the minimum size taken over
all generating sets of G. Fix d ∈N; we say that a class K of groups is d-generated if for each group G ∈K,
d(G) ≤d. A basis of an Abelian group A is a set of generators {a1, . . . , ak} such that A = ⟨a1⟩× · · · × ⟨ak⟩.
We say that a subgroup N ≤G is characteristic if for all α ∈Aut(G), α(N) = N.
For a group G and a subset S ⊆G, with 1 ̸∈S and S = S−1, the Cayley graph Cay(G, S) has vertex set
G. There is an edge {g, h} in Cay(G, S) if and only if there exists some s ∈S such that gs = h.
Quasigroups.

following. For every a, b ∈Q, there exist unique x, y ∈Q such that a∗x = b and y ∗a = b. We write x = a\b

2.2
Complexity Classes
We assume that the reader is familiar with standard complexity classes such as P, NP, L, and NL. For a
standard reference on circuit complexity, see [Vol99]. We consider Boolean circuits using the gates AND,
OR, NOT, and Majority, where Majority(x1, . . . , xn) = 1 if and only if ≥n/2 of the inputs are 1. Otherwise,
Majority(x1, . . . , xn) = 0. In this paper, we will consider DLOGTIME-uniform circuit families (Cn)n∈N. For
this, one encodes the gates of each circuit Cn by bit strings of length O(log n). Then the circuit family
(Cn)n≥0 is called DLOGTIME-uniform if (i) there exists a deterministic Turing machine that computes for
a given gate u ∈{0, 1}∗of Cn (|u| ∈O(log n)) in time O(log n) the type of gate u, where the types are
x1, . . . , xn, NOT, AND, OR, or Majority gates, and (ii) there exists a deterministic Turing machine that
decides for two given gates u, v ∈{0, 1}∗of Cn (|u|, |v| ∈O(log n)) and a binary encoded integer i with
O(log n) many bits in time O(log n) whether u is the i-th input gate for v.
Definition 2.2. Fix k ≥0. We say that a language L belongs to (uniform) NCk if there exist a (uniform)
family of circuits (Cn)n∈N over the AND, OR, NOT gates such that the following hold:
• The AND and OR gates take exactly 2 inputs. That is, they have fan-in 2.
• Cn has depth O(logk n) and uses (has size) nO(1) gates. Here, the implicit constants in the circuit
depth and size depend only on L.
• x ∈L if and only if C|x|(x) = 1.
The complexity class ACk is defined analogously as NCk, except that the AND, OR gates are permitted to
have unbounded fan-in. That is, a single AND gate can compute an arbitrary conjunction, and a single OR
gate can compute an arbitrary disjunction. The complexity class TCk is defined analogously as ACk, except
that our circuits are now permitted Majority gates of unbounded fan-in. We also allow circuits to compute
functions by using multiple output gates.
For every k, the following containments are well-known:
NCk ⊆ACk ⊆TCk ⊆NCk+1.
In the case of k = 0, we have that:
NC0 ⊊AC0 ⊊TC0 ⊆NC1 ⊆L ⊆NL ⊆AC1.
We note that functions that are NC0-computable can only depend on a bounded number of input bits. Thus,
NC0 is unable to compute the AND function. It is a classical result that AC0 is unable to compute Parity
[FSS84].
The containment TC0 ⊆NC1 (and hence, TCk ⊆NCk+1) follows from the fact that NC1 can
simulate the unbounded fan-in Majority gate.
The complexity class FOLL is the set of languages decidable by uniform AC circuits of depth O(log log n)
and polynomial-size [BKLM01]. It is known that AC0 ⊊FOLL ⊊AC1, the former by a simple diagonalization
argument on top of Sipser’s result [Sip83], and the latter because the Parity function is in AC1 but not FOLL
(nor any depth o(log n/ log log n)). FOLL cannot contain any complexity class that can compute Parity,
such as TC0, NC1, L, or NL, and it remains open whether any of these classes contain FOLL.
Denote by FL the class of logspace computable functions.
2.3
Weisfeiler–Leman
We begin by recalling the Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm for graphs, which computes an isomorphism-invariant
coloring.
Let Γ be a graph, and let k ≥2 be an integer.

Weisfeiler–Leman as a Parallel Algorithm
Grohe & Verbitsky [GV06] previously showed that for fixed k, the classical k-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman
algorithm for graphs can be effectively parallelized. Precisely, each iteration of the classical counting WL
algorithm (including the initial coloring) can be implemented using a logspace uniform TC0 circuit, and
each iteration of the count-free WL algorithm can be implemented using a logspace uniform AC0 circuit. A
careful analysis shows that this parallelization holds under DLOGTIME-uniformity. As they mention ([GV06,
Remark 3.4]), their implementation works for any first-order structure, including groups. In particular, the
initial coloring of count-free WL Version I is DLOGTIME-uniform AC0-computable, and each refinement step
is DLOGTIME-uniform AC0-computable. However, because for groups we have different versions of WL, we
explicitly list out the resulting parallel complexities, which differ slightly between the versions.

3.1
Additional Preliminaries for Direct Products
We will begin by recalling additional preliminaries for direct products.
Definition 3.1. We say that a group G is directly indecomposable if the only direct product decomposition
of G is {G}.
Definition 3.2. Let S := {S1, . . . , Sk} be a direct product decomposition of the group G. We say that S is
fully refined if for each i ∈[k], Si is indecomposable.
The Remak–Krull–Schmidt theorem guarantees that for any group G and any two fully refined direct
product decompositions S, T of G, the isomorphism types of the indecomposable factors and their multi-
plicities are the same in S as in T . However, in general, S ̸= T . Consider, for instance, the Klein group
V4 ∼= (Z/2Z)2. Let E1 = ⟨(1, 0)⟩, E2 = ⟨(0, 1)⟩and F = ⟨(1, 1)⟩. Now, V4 = E1 ×E2 = E1 ×F, but E2 ̸= F,
even though E2 ∼= F.
We will now recall results on when any two fully refined direct product decompositions S, T of G satisfy
S = T , as well as when a given subgroup N ⊴G appears (as a set) in any fully refined direct product
decomposition of G.

Conclusion
In this paper, we investigated the Weisfeiler–Leman dimension of groups that admit (unique) direct product
decompositions, where each indecomposable direct factor is O(1)-generated, and either perfect or centerless.
We showed that the count-free (O(1), O(log log n))-WL Version II and counting (O(1), O(1))-WL Version II
algorithms identify every such group. Consequently, we obtained upper bounds of L for isomorphism testing
between a group in this family, and an arbitrary group.
We also exhibited an AC3 canonization procedure for the class D of groups where each indecomposable
direct factor is O(1)-generated.
Finally, we obtained bounds of NC for isomorphism testing of central quasigroups. Previously, only the
trivial bound of nlog(n)+O(1)-time was known.
Our work leaves several open questions.
Question 7.1. Consider the subclass D′ of D where each group in D′ has a unique direct product decom-
position. Does D′ have bounded count-free Weisfeiler–Leman dimension?
Note that D includes the class of all Abelian groups. Grochow and Levet [GL26] showed that the class
of all Abelian groups has count-free WL dimension Θ(log n).
Question 7.2. Is D identified by the counting (O(1), O(1))-WL Version II?
Grochow and Levet [GL26] showed that (O(1), O(log n))-WL Version II identifies D. It is unclear how
to approach this, even in the restricted case when each group admits a unique fully-refined direct product
decomposition.
Question 7.3. In the setting of groups specified by their multiplication tables, can we compute fully-refined
direct product decompositions in FL?
We are unable to resolve Question 7.3 even for Abelian groups– see Proposition 4.5, which yields bounds
of AC1.
Question 7.4. Can we compute a fully-refined direct product decomposition for quasigroups, specified by
their multiplication tables, in polynomial-time?
Question 7.5. Does isomorphism testing of central quasigroups belong to L?
Note that isomorphism testing of Abelian groups belongs to L; this bound has been improved to
∀log log nMAC0(DTISP(polylog(n), log(n))), which is a proper subclass of L [CGLW25]. As central quasigroups
are precisely the Abelian objects (in the sense of universal algebra) in the variety of quasigroups [Sze98], L
is a natural target.
