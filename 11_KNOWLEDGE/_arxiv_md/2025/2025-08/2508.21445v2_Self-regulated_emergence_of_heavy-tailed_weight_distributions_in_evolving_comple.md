Abstract 
Synaptic plasticity typically produces heavy-tailed distributions of synaptic strengths, consist-
ing of a few strong connections among many weaker ones. Meanwhile, structural plasticity 
relies on distinct signaling cascades to reshape network topology. We propose a model in which 
both types of plasticity adhere to the Hebbian principle while operating within homeostatically 
regulated activity. Synaptic plasticity alone generates heavy-tailed weight distributions, but 
only when any activity spreading beyond neighboring units is discarded. However, when com-
bined with Hebbian structural plasticity, i.e., adaptive rewiring, heavy-tailed weight distribu-
tions also arise with more extensive activity flow. Furthermore, adaptive rewiring provides 
complex network structures with convergent-divergent circuits similar to those that facilitate 
signal transmission throughout the nervous system. Having adaptive weight adjustment and 
rewiring driven by the same homeostatic dynamics gives our model a parsimonious and robust 
framework that simultaneously produces heavy-tailed weight distributions and convergent-di-
vergent units under a wide range of dynamical regimes. Consequently, the model accounts for 
key connectivity structures in both C. elegans and the mouse, suggesting that its principles are 
shared across species of different complexities.

Significance statement 
The nervous system is in a constant state of flux, continuously adjusting connection strengths 
and undergoing structural rewiring. Throughout this process, it forms and maintains complex 
connection patterns with heavy-tailed weight distributions. We propose a parsimonious, 
Hebbian-inspired model in which structural and synaptic plasticity are driven by common ho-
meostatic dynamics. This model generates heavy-tailed weight distributions and convergent 
and divergent motifs, which are prevalent in sensory processing and information routing. 
These features emerge across a broad spectrum of dynamical regimes, ranging from local to 
more extensive activity propagation. As a result, the model can fit strength distributions and 
key neural structures in evolutionarily distant species, such as C. elegans and mice.

Introduction 
The nervous system can be considered as a network of interconnected neurons whose connec-
tions are weighted according to their synaptic strength. Invariably, the network features a few 
strong connections among many weaker ones, resulting in a skewed, heavy-tailed weight dis-
tribution (1–6). Generative models have proposed various mechanisms for producing such a 
distribution through iterative weight adjustment (7–13). However, these models tend to over-
look the fact that the weight dynamics in biological systems occur within continuously evolv-
ing network structures. Structural plasticity, the rewiring of connectivity structures through the 
addition or pruning of synapses, constantly alters brain networks (14–17). We propose that 
connection weights and connectivity structures co-evolve adaptively. We present a model in 
which Hebbian plasticity, regulated by homeostatic dynamics, drives the adaptive update of 
network connectivity weights and structure. This yields both heavy-tailed weight distributions 
and complex network structures consistent with biological connectomes of various complexi-
ties. 
The proposed model is based on adaptive rewiring, a parsimonious mechanism for struc-
tural plasticity (18, 19). This Hebbian-inspired process dynamically creates shortcuts between 
highly interactive neural units while pruning underused connections (Fig 1.A). When driven 
by spontaneous activity, adaptive rewiring generates and maintains modular, small-world net-
works with a rich-club core (20–22), thereby sharing crucial organizational features with bio-
logical neural networks (18, 19, 23–28). 
Early adaptive rewiring models were highly abstract, with undirected, unweighted connec-
tions. Recent work has extended adaptive rewiring to weighted and directed networks (29–31). 
In these networks, signal propagation is modeled by advection (32) and consensus (33) dynam-
ics, which are both generalizations of diffusion on the graph. Driven by these dynamics, adap-
tive rewiring gives rise to connectivity patterns that resemble key motifs of synaptic circuits 
ubiquitous in the nervous system. Advection produces convergent motifs, in which a unit re-
ceives multiple inputs, while consensus produces divergent motifs that broadcast their output 
to many nodes. Both types of circuits support complex computations and information trans-
mission (34). For instance, converging inputs that are similar and fluctuate independently pro-
duce outputs with an improved signal-to-noise ratio. Conversely, divergent motifs efficiently 
broadcast signals by distributing outputs to many parallel pathways. Combining consensus and 
advection dynamics in a dynamically evolving network model generates convergent-divergent 
units, which enable context-sensitive computing in feed-forward sensory processing (34). 
These units integrate inputs via convergent hubs, process information in an internal core, a 
module consisting of intermediate nodes on the paths from convergent to divergent hubs, and 
distribute outputs via divergent hubs. Although predominantly viewed as instruments of feed-
forward processing, these units may also contain recurrent connections (31, 35). 
So far, these models have not taken synaptic plasticity into account; fixed weights have 
been used since rewiring can be performed without adjusting them.  However, incorporating 
weight adjustment into these models should provide a robust explanation for why networks 
exhibit heavy-tailed weight distributions. To that end, we provide a dual-adaptive algorithm

combining adaptive rewiring and weight adjustment. The model parsimoniously represents 
both synaptic and structural plasticity as Hebbian processes driven by the same homeostatic 
dynamics (see Fig. 1B). We demonstrate that adaptive weight adjustment alone, without rewir-
ing, can already produce heavy-tailed weight distributions and rudimentary modular and clus-
tering structures. However, this only occurs under the extremely unrealistic restriction that pre-
vents network flow from spreading beyond immediate neighbors. When combined with adap-
tive rewiring, where the network weights and its complex structure change concurrently, the 
network develops heavy-tailed weight distributions across a significantly wider range of diffu-
sion. Moreover, adaptive weight adjustment refines the convergent-divergent structures result-
ing from adaptive rewiring by strengthening the connections of their internal cores, thereby 
enhancing network efficiency and functional organization.  
Having established these model features, we proceed by fitting the dual-adaptive model to 
the weight distributions and convergent-divergent units extracted from two neuronal connec-
tomes, C. elegans (36) and the mouse (37). The model successfully reproduces their heavy-
tailed connectivity distributions as well as the density and weights of connections in their con-
vergent-divergent units’ internal cores. These results suggest Hebbian principles driven by ho-
meostatic dynamics as a candidate mechanism governing the evolution of both the strength and 
structure of biological network connections.

Adaptive plasticity rules 
The model encompasses two types of plasticity, adaptive rewiring and adaptive weight adjust-

Normalization in adaptive weight adjustment is selectively paired, as in rewiring, with 
consensus or advection dynamics. Adaptive weight adjustment is either of type “C-out”, which 
refers to consensus dynamics in combination with out-connection normalization, or “A-in”, 
which employs advection dynamics with in-connection normalization. 
 
The Dual-Adaptive Algorithm 
We combine adaptive rewiring with adaptive weight adjustment into a dual-adaptive algorithm. 
The condition of adaptive weight adjustment (“C-out” or “A-in”) stays fixed throughout the 
course of a run. The combined dynamics algorithm consists of the following steps: 
Step 1: Select a random node 𝑢∈𝑉. Set its initial concentration to 1 and other nodes’ 
initial concentrations to 0, i.e., 𝒙(𝑡𝑏𝑒𝑔𝑖𝑛) = 𝒆𝑢. Perform weight adjustment based on the 
selected dynamics. 
Step 2: Repeat step 1 until 𝑟 steps of adaptive weight adjustment have been performed. 
Step 3: Sample 𝑝 from a uniform distribution 𝑈[0,1]. If 𝑝< 𝑝𝑖𝑛, select a random node 
𝑣∈𝑉 such that its in-degree is neither zero nor 𝑛−1 and rewire its in-connection. Other-
wise, select a random node 𝑣∈𝑉 such that its out-degree is neither zero nor 𝑛−1 and 
rewire its out-connection. 
Step 4: Return to step 1 until 𝑁 steps of adaptive weight adjustment have been performed. 
The tuning parameter of the algorithm is 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡, which specifies the duration over 
which the dynamics evolve prior to updating the weights at each adjustment step. 𝑝𝑖𝑛, the prob-
ability of rewiring the in-connection, is set to 0.5 unless stated otherwise. Thirty networks are 
generated for each parameter combination.

Our results so far show that adaptive weight adjustment alone produces heavy-tailed 
weights only for small 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 values, i.e., when interactions are limited to neighboring nodes 
(Fig S7.A, D). This could be explained by the random connectivity structure of the network, 
on which the diffusion processes, advection and consensus, spread. Random networks contain 
abundant reciprocal paths, promoting homogenization of node concentrations through feed-
back. Coupled with random networks’ short path lengths, this reciprocal structure leads to rapid 
equalization of concentrations across the network as 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡  increases. We found that for 
small enough 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 values allowing only local diffusion, i.e., to the neighbors of the can-
didate nodes, node concentrations and subsequently weight increments (Eq. 13) become ap-
proximately proportional to connection weights (Fig S7.B, C, E, F). In this regime, adaptive 
weight adjustment effectively mimics a preferential attachment process where stronger con-
nections receive larger weight increments, leading to heavy-tailed weight distributions. How-
ever, due to the network’s reciprocal structure and short paths, modest increases in 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 
allow nodes to interact across broad regions of the network (Fig S7. G, J). This promotes con-
centrations to equilibrate among many nodes (Fig S7.H, K), which in turn weakens the corre-
lation between weight increments and existing weights (Fig S7.I, L) and essentially disrupts 
the preferential attachment process. Therefore, weight heterogeneity is precipitously reduced 
when 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 increases.

The combination of adaptive rewiring and weight adjustment generates heavy-
tailed weights for a wide range of 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 values 
We next consider the emergence of heavy-tailed weight distributions for the dual-adaptive al-
gorithm where we combine adaptive weight adjustment with adaptive rewiring. Adaptive re-
wiring driven by advection and consensus dynamics promotes hub formation, where most 
nodes become polarized, exhibiting either high in-degree and low out-degree or low in-degree 
and high out-degree (31). This polarization leads to a hierarchical structure, where diffusion 
flows primarily from upstream to downstream nodes, while feedback from downstream to up-
stream is comparatively reduced, in contrast to the abundant feedback loops in random net-
works. Based on the analysis in the previous section, we hypothesized that adaptive rewiring 
could enable adaptive weight adjustment to generate heavy-tailed weights for a wider range of 
𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 values.  
Our results validate this hypothesis: the dual-adaptive algorithm facilitates the emergence 
of heavy-tailed weights for a wider range of 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 values compared to solely adaptive 
weight adjustment (Fig 5.A, B; Fig S8.A, B; for best fits see Fig S9). Increasing 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 does 
not substantially change the decay rate of the upper tails of weight distributions, in contrast to 
when only adaptive weight adjustment was used (Compare Fig 5.C, D and Fig S8.C with Fig 
3.C, D and Fig S3, S5). This robustness indicates that the topology induced by adaptive rewir-
ing counteracts the homogenizing tendency of the homeostatic nature of consensus and advec-
tion dynamics. The hierarchical structure, where downstream nodes exert minimal influence 
on upstream nodes, disrupts reciprocal feedback and thus preserves these concentration differ-
ences as 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 increases (Fig S10.A, B). As a result, the correlation between connection 
weights and weight increments persists across a broad range of 𝜏𝑟𝑒𝑤𝑒𝑖𝑔ℎ𝑡 values (Fig S10.C,

The combination of adaptive rewiring and weight adjustment generates 
convergent-divergent units 
Recent studies have shown that in directed networks with fixed weights, adaptive rewiring, 
combined with a small proportion of random rewiring, produces convergent-divergent units, 
prominent in feedforward sensory processing (34) and in effectuating context integration in 
their more general recurrent form (29, 31). Convergent-divergent units receive input from a 
population of local neurons via convergent hubs, process the information through an internal 
core of intermediate nodes, and subsequently project the output via divergent hubs to a popu-
lation of local neurons (Fig 7.A). Convergent hubs are defined as nodes with at least one out-
connection and an in-degree exceeding a threshold, and divergent hubs as nodes with at least 
one in-connection and an out-degree above a threshold. 
Although adaptive weight adjustment could in principle disrupt the emergence of conver-
gent-divergent units resulting from adaptive rewiring, we found that, under appropriate varia-
ble settings (see the caption in Fig 7), the dual-adaptive model produces both convergent-di-
vergent units (Fig S13) and heavy-tailed weight distributions (Fig S14). Compared to the null 
networks generated by applying only rewiring, the dual-adaptive model preserves overall net-
work connectedness (Fig S15), while modulating the prevalence of convergent and divergent 
hubs in a condition-dependent manner (Fig S16). The internal cores of convergent-divergent 
units generated by the dual-adaptive model retain a density qualitatively similar to that ob-
served in null networks (Fig 7.B), but the connections within these cores are stronger than those 
generated by adaptive rewiring alone (Fig 7.C), bolstering the backbone architecture capable 
of context-sensitive processing.

Discussion 
We considered how neural networks could evolve heavy-tailed weight distributions. In princi-
ple, such distributions can emerge from multiplicative stochastic processes such as geometric 
Brownian Motion (GBM) (47). However, this would be unrealistic for biological systems. The 
mean and variance of distributions generated by GBM grow exponentially over time (47),  
whereas biological circuits operate under stabilizing constraints: neuronal activation levels are 
bounded by homeostatic mechanisms (48), and neural connections develop their strength 
through synaptic plasticity (49). This understanding has led to generative models for producing 
these distributions through iterative weight adjustment (7–13). Our proposed model belongs to 
this category. Plasticity in the model is driven by homeostatic dynamics. 
The model parsimoniously combines two activity-dependent, Hebbian plasticity mecha-
nisms: adaptive rewiring, which restructures the network’s connections, and adaptive weight 
adjustment, which modifies connection weights. Both forms of adaptation are based on the 
same homeostatic dynamics of consensus and advection. The dual-adaptive algorithm gener-
ates a heavy-tailed connection weight distribution for a wider range of dynamics compared to 
solely adaptive weight adjustment. Furthermore, it gives rise to motif structures that are wide-
spread in neural systems: convergent and divergent circuits and their combination in conver-
gent-divergent units, with internal cores with denser and stronger-than-average interconnec-
tions. Our generated data are broadly consistent with real connectomes, yet they also indicate 
that additional mechanisms may be needed to fully account their complex connectivity.

Rationale for combining reweighting and rewiring of connections in our model 
The Hebbian rule (50) explains the development and maintenance of neural structures by 
linking neural activity to changes in synaptic strengths. Following its formulation, experi-
mental observations have further solidified this rule with quantitative mechanisms, including 
long-term potentiation (LTP; 51–53), long-term depression (LTD; 54, 55) and spike-timing–
dependent plasticity (STDP; 56–59). Structural changes in the nervous system include the 
growth or retraction of neurites as well as of axonal boutons and dendritic spines with the ac-
companying formation or elimination of synapses (60, see 61 for a review). Although the 
Hebbian rule is typically understood as referring to changes in the efficacy of existing synap-
ses, experiments also suggest that common activity-based processes induce structural changes 
(see 62, 63 for reviews).  
Specifically, the potentiation of synaptic weights (LTP) results in the increase in the 
number of spines  (64) and filopodia (65) and in the enlargement of spine heads (66) which 
leads to the formation of new synaptic connections; while LTD leads to spine shrinkage  (67) 
which in turn increases the probability of synaptic pruning. This evidence indicates the pres-
ence of structural plasticity mechanisms based on the Hebbian rule, justifying our rationale 
for combining adaptive reweighting and rewiring in our model. 
The emergence of heavy-tailed distributions  
Understanding the mechanisms that generate and maintain neuronal structures in the brain re-
mains a fundamental challenge in neuroscience. Across species, neuronal structures and nerv-
ous systems show a characteristic distribution of synaptic strengths with a few strong connec-
tions amid a vast majority of weak ones (5, 11, 39–44; for a summary see 9). 
Adaptive weight adjustment alone can produce heavy-tailed distributions and nonrandom 
topology, but only when the homeostatic dynamics that drives the weight updates is localized: 
no diffusion of activity beyond direct neighbors. Increasing the time of diffusion before weight 
adjustment favors more distant interactions and, as a result, the network dynamics rapidly ho-
mogenizes node concentrations, reducing heterogeneity in network topology and weights. This 
aligns with the previous observation by Lynn et al. (9), whose algorithm randomly prunes con-
nections and redistributes weights either randomly or following a preferential growth rule 
weighted by neural interactions. For that model, too, increasing the range of neural interactions 
reduces the heterogeneity of connection weights. While high clustering emerged at intermedi-
ate scales of interactions in their model, it appeared at local interactions in our case. The dis-
crepancy likely results from differences in model design. Unlike the preferential growth rule 
in Lynn et al., which requires global knowledge of network-wide interactions, adaptive weight 
adjustment relies solely on local information. Specifically, the update of each connection 
weight depends only on the concentrations and strengths of the two connected nodes, making 
our approach more biologically plausible.


## Page 14


By integrating adaptive weight adjustment and rewiring, the dual-adaptive algorithm over-
comes the limitations of adaptive weight adjustment. Adaptive rewiring induces structural 
asymmetries, where nodes increasingly differentiate into in-hubs and out-hubs, which in turn 
seed weight heterogeneities that are amplified by adaptive weight adjustment. Crucially, this 
mechanism can operate across a broad timescale of neural interactions, enabling the emergence 
of heavy-tailed distributions under both restricted and unrestricted activity spreading. This sug-
gests that adaptive rewiring helps counteract the homogenizing tendencies caused by the 
spreading dynamics, thereby supporting the persistence of functional heterogeneity. As a result, 
the dual-adaptive algorithm shows tolerance to variation, since it produces the target strength 
distribution for a wide range of dynamics. This is in accordance with a prominent feature of 
biological systems, namely their capacity to produce a functional outcome for a constellation 
of different parameter values (73, 74), which also pertains to neural systems (see 75–77, 78 for 
reviews). 
The coincident emergence of convergent and divergent connectivity motifs  
In terms of connectivity, convergent and divergent circuits are pervasive in organisms of dif-
ferent complexities. For instance, for both flies (79) and locusts (80), mushroom body output 
neurons receive converging input from Kenyon cells to produce a stable stereotypical response. 
The visual system shows divergence and convergence at different stages of processing: diver-
gence from bipolar cells to retinal ganglion cells (81), convergence of retinal ganglion cells to 
thalamic neurons (82, 83), and divergence of thalamic inputs to V1 cells (84). Circuits com-
bining convergent and divergent motifs, support diverse computations, notably in visual pro-
cessing. For example, in mouse V1, somatostatin neurons collect inputs from and project in-
formation back to orientation-selective excitatory neurons in layers 2/3, enabling contextual 
modulation based on center-surround relationships (35). 
The dual-adaptive algorithm preserves, and in some respects enhances, the formation of 
convergent-divergent units. While adaptive rewiring alone is sufficient to produce these struc-
tures (31), the addition of adaptive weight adjustment selectively strengthens the internal cores 
of these structures, effectively refining their functional core. The algorithm by Lynn et al.  pro-
duced clustering patterns resembling those observed in biological brains, but it does not gen-
erate the more complex topology observed in our model. This limitation likely stems from its 
exclusive reliance on localized interactions, which is sufficient for the generation of heavy-
tailed weight distributions but falls short in supporting higher-order structural organizations. 
In contrast, our model decouples these processes by implementing two separate Hebbian mech-
anisms, albeit driven by common homeostatic dynamics: adaptive rewiring governs the evolu-
tion of network topology over longer timescales, while adaptive weight adjustment fine-tunes 
connection strengths at a faster, more localized scale.

Fit of the model to the C. elegans and mouse connectomes  
Ongoing advances in image processing and electron microscopy have enabled reconstructions 
of neuronal structures involving thousands of neurons (36, 37, 5, 85). In order not to get lost 
in a sea of details, it is useful to examine to what extent a small set of rules or principles can 
explain experimental data. Some of these rules are likely conserved across species. To that 
end, we compared the generated data of our model with neural structures from two species of 
disparate complexities: the nerve ring and ventral ganglion of C. elegans (36) and the inner 
plexiform layer of the mouse retina (37). C. elegans and mice diverged from a common bilat-
erian ancestor some 600-700 million years ago (86, 87). Common mechanisms between them 
can thus reflect foundational organization principles. 
The model successfully reproduces the heavy-tailed weight distributions for both organ-
isms. It also captures key organizational features reflecting their connectivity structure: con-
vergent and divergent hubs and an intermediate core with denser and stronger connections 
than the network average. Although the model underestimates the proportion of hubs, the 
qualitative agreement suggests that joint adaptive reweighting and rewiring based on homeo-
static principles are sufficient to recover core aspects of connectome organization. Moreover, 
an additional mechanism to achieve quantitative precision could readily be envisaged. For in-
stance, an apoptosis mechanism preferentially eliminating non-hub nodes would increase the 
proportion of hubs in the remaining network to an appropriate level.   
Future work  
The dual-adaptive algorithm is a simplified model, whose minimalism is similar to that of other 
recent studies (9). Simplification allowed us to isolate the role of spontaneous activity in the 
emergence of key organizational features of brain-like networks. Future work can introduce 
additional degrees of freedom and thereby higher complexity to bring the model closer to bio-
logical reality.  
First, the model presupposes a network with fixed numbers of nodes and connections, 
omitting important developmental phenomena such as neurogenesis, programmed cell death, 
and axonal growth. Recent studies show that dynamic axon expansion based on guidance 
cues can produce modular small-world networks with lognormal weight and scale-free degree 
distributions (8), while neurite branching may also contribute to these distributions (69). Fu-
ture studies could initialize networks using biologically inspired growth rules, then apply the 
dual-adaptive algorithm to investigate how activity-dependent plasticity operates on, and po-
tentially reshapes, pre-established structures. 
Second, additional biological mechanisms contribute to the quantitative organization of 
convergent–divergent units beyond what is captured by the current model. For example, con-
nection probability in many neural systems decreases with anatomical distance (88, 89),


## Page 16


imposing strong spatial constraints that are not considered here. We have shown earlier how 
these constraints interact constructively with adaptive rewiring (29). Neurons in the retina and 
other systems also exhibit layer-specific and type-specific connectivity preferences (90, 91). 
Incorporating these mechanisms may improve the model’s ability to quantitatively match the 
density and weight of internal cores observed in real connectomes.

Control algorithms 
To evaluate the distinct contributions of adaptive rewiring and adaptive weight adjustment in 
our setting, we introduce two control conditions. First, to assess whether adaptive weight ad-
justment alone could generate heavy-tailed distributions and any characteristic complexity fea-
tures in the absence of structural plasticity, we skip the adaptive rewiring steps. In this condi-
tion, the network’s connectivity structure is initially random and held fixed throughout learn-
ing, allowing us to study the effect of weight dynamics on emergent network complexity fea-
tures in isolation.  
Second, to evaluate whether random rewiring could have similar effects to adaptive rewir-
ing, we substitute the latter with the former. In the random rewiring step, when an in-connection 
of node 𝑣 is rewired, two nodes 𝑘∈𝑁𝑖𝑛(𝑣) and 𝑙∈𝑁𝑖𝑛
𝑐(𝑣) are selected randomly. The in-con-
nection 𝑘→𝑣 is cut and a new in-connection, 𝑙→𝑣, is added. An analogous process is applied 
for out-connections. 
 
Heavy-tailed distributions and power-law fit of the tail 
To characterize the distribution of connection weights, we evaluate several candidate distribu-
tions, right-skewed ones (lognormal, Weibull, gamma, exponential, inverse Gaussian, and in-
verse gamma) as well as the normal distribution. By definition, a distribution is considered


## Page 19


heavy-tailed if its tail decays more slowly than an exponential distribution. Among our candi-
date distributions, the lognormal, inverse gamma, and Weibull distributions (when the shape 
parameter is less than 1) qualify as heavy-tailed. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is 
used to fit each distribution to connection weights. Model fit is assessed by the Kolmogorov-
Smirnov (KS) statistic, where a smaller KS statistic indicates a closer fit between the model 
and the observed data. We apply the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (92) to compare the KS statis-
tics of each candidate distribution against that of the one with the smallest mean KS statistic. 
The significance level is 0.05.  
To qualify the upper tail of the weight distribution, we fit a power-law to the tail of con-
nection weights exceeding the median. If a distribution follows a power-law in its upper tail, 
its 𝑝(𝑋= 𝑥) has the form 
𝑝(𝑋= 𝑥) = 𝐶⋅𝑥−𝛼
(16) 
where 𝛼 is the exponent that governs the rate of decay and 𝐶 the normalizing constant. A 
smaller 𝛼 indicates a heavier tail. In log-log scale, this relationship appears as a linear trend. 
To construct the histogram of the probability distribution, we apply logarithmic binning, using 
bins that are uniformly spaced on a logarithmic scale. Specifically, the interval between the 
median and the maximum of connection weights is divided into 20 bins. The power-law fit is 
obtained by linear regression on the log-transformed histogram using MLE.

rewiring step, a random node (green) is selected. Its connection to the neighbor with which it 
interacts least (marked with a red cross) is pruned, and a new connection (red arrows) is added 
to the node with which it interacts most but is indirectly linked to. (B) In each adaptive weight

increments proportional to the product of the sending and receiving nodes’ states, followed by 
synaptic normalization to prevent unbounded weight growth.
