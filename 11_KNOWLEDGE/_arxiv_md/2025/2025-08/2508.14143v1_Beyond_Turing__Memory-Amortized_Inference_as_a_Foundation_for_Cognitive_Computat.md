I. INTRODUCTION
Intelligence, in both natural and artificial systems, is funda-
mentally non-ergodic. Intelligent systems exhibit fundamen-
tally non-ergodic behavior: they do not explore all possible
states uniformly over time, but instead follow structured,
path-dependent trajectories shaped by memory, goals, and
context. Unlike ergodic systems, where time averages converge
to ensemble averages, intelligent agents selectively traverse
latent spaces based on prior experience, often returning to
a constrained subset of meaningful states (please refer to
Fig. 1). This non-uniform visitation arises from mechanisms
such as goal-directed planning [1], inductive bias, working
memory reuse [2], and learned attractors. Cognitive processes
such as hippocampal replay [3], schema consolidation [4],

In this paper, we propose that Memory-Amortized Inference

via the reuse of structured memory cycles over latent mani-

entropy-minimizing paths through representational space, en-

inference with dramatically reduced computational cost. This
path-dependent behavior, grounded in memory, context, and
prediction, marks a sharp departure from traditional ergodic
models of computation and learning (e.g., reinforcement learn-

variational inference [11]).

INTELLIGENCE
The concept of non-ergodicity provides a unifying lens
through which memory, inference, and intelligent behavior
can be understood. In contrast to ergodic systems, where all
accessible states are explored given sufficient time, intelligent
systems exhibit structured, path-dependent behavior, reusing
past experiences to guide future inference. This structural de-
parture from statistical uniformity underpins adaptive decision-
making and memory-based cognition.

Intelligence is inherently non-ergodic [18]. Unlike purely
stochastic or memoryless agents, intelligent systems exhibit
structured, history-dependent dynamics that break the as-
sumption of uniform exploration [19]. Rather than treating
each new observation or decision as independent, intelligent
behavior leverages temporal coherence and structural memory
to bias its future evolution [20]. This reuse of temporally
coherent, structurally encoded memory trajectories has direct
implications for the classic bias–variance dilemma in learning
systems [13]. Specifically, intelligent agents:
• Reuse structured memory to bias inference trajecto-
ries. Prior experiences are not discarded but actively re-
called and embedded into the inference process, enabling
the agent to bypass costly recomputation by reusing
previously learned representations or latent paths [2].
• Preserve contextual history to guide simulation and
prediction. Context is not a passive backdrop but a
dynamic influence that modulates attention, expectation,
and planning. The ability to simulate likely futures or re-
construct latent causes depends critically on maintaining
a coherent history of the past [21].
• Converge toward preferred states shaped by prior ex-
perience. Through repeated exposure and reinforcement,
intelligent agents develop habitual trajectories or attractor
basins in latent space that stabilize future inference.
These attractors encode inductive biases that constrain
exploration and enhance generalization [22].
Taken together, these properties imply that cognitive dy-
namics are not governed by ergodic, memoryless sampling of
the latent state space. Instead, they are shaped by persistent
topological structures, such as memory cycles, homology
classes, and attractor submanifolds, that define a context-
sensitive flow over the agent’s internal representation space
[23]. This structured, non-uniform recurrence supports adap-
tive behavior by enforcing coherence, efficiency, and reuse
across time. Rather than treating memory as a static buffer
or cache, intelligence operationalizes memory as a dynamic
substrate/loop for inference, one that allows the system to gen-
eralize from fewer examples, infer from partial information,
and coordinate action across extended timescales [24]. From a
broken symmetry perspective [6], the brain doesn’t just pick a
winner; it builds a loop that keeps track of the choice and its
alternatives, enabling recall, switching, or reuse later. This is
why loops are the natural consequence of broken symmetry:

memory as a topologically constrained substrate for inference,

MAI and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff. Traditional statistical
learning theory frames generalization as a tradeoff: systems
with low bias may flexibly adapt to new data but risk overfit-
ting due to high variance, while systems with strong inductive
bias generalize more reliably but may underfit novel patterns.
In ergodic or memoryless learners, the lack of structured priors
often leads to over-reliance on recent observations, resulting in
unstable inference and poor transfer across tasks. By contrast,
intelligent agents mitigate this dilemma through pre-wired
structural biases [13], in the form of persistent memory cycles,
schema hierarchies, or latent topologies, that constrain learning
dynamics to a small, meaningful submanifold of the full
hypothesis space.
Within the MAI framework, inductive bias is not externally
imposed; instead, it emerges from the internal reuse of memory
structures shaped by prior experience. These biases enforce
low-dimensional consistency across contexts, effectively re-
ducing variance without sacrificing plasticity [29]. In this way,
MAI reframes generalization not as a balance between bias
and variance, but as a consequence of topological coherence:
inference remains robust and efficient precisely because it
unfolds along trajectories that preserve the agent’s structural
memory of the world.
MAI and the Stability-Plasticity Tradeoff. The classical
stability-plasticity dilemma in continual learning [26] refers
to the need for a cognitive system to preserve previously
acquired knowledge (stability) while remaining adaptable to
new information (plasticity). In many learning architectures,
especially those based on gradient descent [30] and uniform
sampling, this tradeoff is difficult to manage: learning new
tasks often leads to catastrophic forgetting of old ones [31],
whereas rigid memorization impairs generalization. We argue
that this dilemma arises from an implicit assumption of
ergodicity, the idea that over time, all states or experiences
will be revisited, making uniform learning sufficient.
MAI addresses this challenge by formalizing inference
as reuse over structured memory cycles rather than re-

Recurrence as the Foundation of Intelligence
Intelligence is inherently non-ergodic with the fol-
lowing implications: 1) Non-ergodicity explains the
emergence of learning, planning, and generalization; 2)
Memory-amortized systems encode inductive biases
via reuse, not recomputation; 3) Cognitive systems
are best modeled not as ergodic samplers, but as
structured navigators over constrained latent spaces.
In short, non-ergodicity is not a bug, but a feature of
intelligence, one that encodes the reuse of past expe-
rience as a structural prior for efficient, context-aware
inference. Since non-ergodicity is linked to memory,
cycle formation becomes its algebraic footprint.

by storing and reusing structured latent representations. The
key idea is to construct a memory of prior inference results
such that new inference problems can be approximated by
querying and adapting from this memory, rather than solving
the full problem from scratch. Let Ψ ∈X denote the observ-

The Bootstrapping Update Operator F. The bootstrapping
operator F : S × C →S governs the internal dynamics of
inference by iteratively updating the latent content representa-
tion Φt given the context Ψt. It defines a recurrence: Φt+1 =
F(Φt, Ψt), where F encodes the system’s structural prior,
capturing the directionality, topology, and dynamic consistency
of inference over time. Unlike standard update rules that
minimize a loss from scratch, F performs bootstrapping: each
update is initialized from a prior memory-induced state, often
already close to the optimal solution due to cycle recurrence.
Here are several key properties of F:
• Cycle-Consistency: If (Φt, Ψt) ∈γ for some memory
cycle γ ⊂Z, then Φt+T ≈Φt, enabling amortization via
structural recurrence.
• Structural Biasing: Updates follow latent paths con-
strained by prior topology (e.g., flow fields over ho-
mology classes or attention-modulated latent graphs),
enforcing low-entropy generalization.
• Minimal Cost Gradient: Because the initialization Φt
already lies near an attractor, the subsequent update Φt+1
requires only a small corrective shift, further amortizing
the inference process.
The bootstrapping update operator F in MAI is struc-
turally analogous to the half-step down trick used in Q-
learning [38] and temporal difference (TD) methods [8]. In
Q-learning, the value function is updated by approximating
the current value via a one-step lookahead: Q(st, at) ←
rt + γ maxa′ Q(st+1, a′), which yields the approximation
Q(st) ≈Q(st+1). This forward-directed value propagation
allows reinforcement learning agents to estimate long-term
outcomes without simulating entire trajectories.
By contrast, MAI reverses the time direction: the update
operator F bootstraps latent inference forward using structured
memory and contextual cues: Φt+1 = F(Φt, Ψt), and this is
inverted by retrieval: Φt ≈R(Φt+1, Ψt). This dual relation-
ship forms the backbone of the MAI half-step trick: the current
latent content Φt generates the next-step prediction Φt+1,
which in turn can be used to reconstruct Φt. While Q-learning
bootstraps value via reward-driven transitions, MAI bootstraps
inference through latent memory and context, yielding a cycle-
consistent structure that reduces entropy. Both approaches use
bootstrapping to manage uncertainty and amortize computa-
tional cost, but in opposite directions, highlighting a deeper
time-reversed duality between learning and inference.

Topological Closure and the Magical Number Seven
In the MAI framework, each consolidated latent mem-
ory cycle γ ∈H1(Z) is allocated a distinct phasic
slot within a nested theta–gamma oscillatory hierarchy
[62], [63]. Theta oscillations (∼4−8 Hz) divide ongo-
ing experience into discrete temporal windows, while
faster gamma oscillations (∼30−100 Hz) multiplex
several content representations within each theta cycle.
Mechanism. Topological closure ensures that traversal
of γ is phase-locked to its dedicated gamma subcycle,
enabling precise coincidence detection and avoiding
interference with other cycles [64], [65]. Because the
number of non-overlapping gamma slots per theta
cycle is neurophysiologically limited (by synaptic in-
tegration times, interneuron refractory periods, and
phase separation constraints), there is a hard bound
on how many cycles can coexist without destructive
interference. Implication. Working memory capacity,
the “magical number seven” [66], emerges naturally
from the temporal geometry of nested oscillations:
it reflects the maximum number of non-interfering
latent cycles that can be concurrently maintained and
replayed, not an arbitrary cognitive limit. This inter-
pretation grounds a classic cognitive phenomenon in a
structural–temporal resource allocation principle.
VII. IMPLICATIONS FOR AGI
A. From Sensorimotor Substrate to Abstract Thought
It has been argued that abstract thought “is a new trick,
perhaps less than 100 thousand years old...effective only
because it is supported by this much older and much more
powerful, though usually unconscious, sensorimotor knowl-
edge” [67]. Wheeler’s it-from-bit principle [68] also advocates
a circular perspective toward understanding physics, observer-
participancy, and information. One cannot help wondering:
if cycles are the fundamental building blocks for memory,
learning, and intelligence, they must play an inevitable role in
the evolution toward AGI, which requires not just incremental
improvement but a phase transition (i.e., “more is different”
[69]). Within the CCUP+MAI framework, this statement high-
lights a key principle for AGI design: abstract reasoning is not
the foundation of intelligence but a secondary layer built upon
a robust, amortized sensorimotor substrate [70]. Following
such an evolutionary perspective, we argue that the path toward
AGI should follow a similar blueprint as we lay out next.
Biological Precedent. Human cognitive development and
evolution follow a staged progression:
1) Sensorimotor
grounding: In infancy, cognition is
rooted in sensorimotor exploration (e.g., grasping, reach-
ing, babbling, and locomotion). Here, low-entropy con-
tent Φ (e.g., a hand movement or syllable) is scaffolded
by high-entropy contextual structures Ψ (parental cues,
environmental affordances). Evolutionarily, this stage is
mirrored in the conserved neural circuits of the brain-
stem and cerebellum, which provide the substrate for
PDO through stable perception-action loops.
2) Cycle consolidation: Through sleep-dependent replay
and hippocampal consolidation, initially trivial cycles in
Z (e.g., reaching for a toy or producing a vowel) become
robust, nontrivial homology generators in memory. In
evolutionary time, the hippocampal–entorhinal system,
with grid and place cells, preserves navigational loops as
reusable structures, later exapted for episodic memory.
This transformation from motor patterns to symbolic
templates exemplifies how repeated interaction stabilizes
structure for rapid reuse.
3) Abstract
recombination:
Higher-order
cognition
emerges when persistent cycles are flexibly recombined.
For example, phonemic cycles are abstracted into
words, and words into syntax, giving rise to natural
language. The recursive nature of language mirrors the
recombinatory reuse of underlying cycles: semantic
concepts are scaffolded on sensorimotor schemas, while
syntax reflects the composition of action-sequences into
hierarchical plans. Evolutionarily, abstract thought in
the neocortex builds on the older sensorimotor engine,
enabling humans to externalize structured cycles as
language, myth, and mathematics.
In sum, the ancient sensorimotor layer functions as a highly
optimized PDO engine, while abstract thought and natural
language are recent extensions that leverage and recombine
these grounded cycles rather than replace them.
Implications for AGI. Despite remarkable advances in large
language and multimodal models, true AGI remains aspira-
tional. Current systems such as GPT-5 demonstrate impressive
reasoning, coding, and multi-domain competence, yet fall short
in autonomy, consistent logic, and strategic planning. Leaders
like Sam Altman and Demis Hassabis stress that today’s AI
is “missing something,” with gaps in memory, adaptability,
and coherence. Benchmarks (e.g., ARC-AGI) show progress
but remain below human-level performance. Surveys further
suggest scaling alone is insufficient. Thus, while industry
investment and technical progress accelerate, the state of the
art reflects powerful but uneven “jagged intelligence,” not
genuine AGI. We provide a list of implications of MAI for
AGI here, ordered by their importance from low to high.

the basis of a persistent homology of action. This persistence
transforms fleeting sensorimotor events into stable cognitive

reasoning and symbolic inference only emerge once these
amortized cycles can be recombined into higher-order struc-

Traditional visions of AGI assume a unitary, language-
driven agent [17], [71]. Yet human cognition itself is scaf-
folded by collective dynamics: cultural transmission, division
of cognitive labor, and distributed memory across institutions.
From the MAI perspective, AGI can be reconceived not as
a single symbolic reasoner but as a societal consciousness
[72], a web of amortized inference cycles distributed across
many agents and environments. Language is not the sole
substrate of intelligence but one channel among others for
cycle recombination. Embodied interaction, shared environ-
mental coupling, and persistent external memory provide the
grounding for collective cognition [73]. The emergent property
is language-independent intelligence: adaptive coordination,
robust decision-making, and shared problem-solving that arise
from minimizing uncertainty across contexts and contents at
the level of the group rather than the individual. In this
view, AGI is less a monolithic “brain” and more an evolving
ecosystem of agents, where consciousness emerges as the
societal-scale persistence and recombination of distributed
inference cycles. In developmental terms, this ordering reflects
the evolutionary logic of cognition: ancient sensorimotor sub-
strates provide the optimized engine for PDO, while abstract
thought is a late-emerging extension that leverages, but never
replaces, this foundation. This progression inspires a five-
stage developmental blueprint for AGI, where systems grow
from embodied grounding world models [74] to memory
consolidation and recombinatory abstraction, and ultimately
to collective symbolic reasoning at an unprecedented scale
after four phase transitions.

B. MAI and Embodied Cognition
From the perspective of embodied cognition [75], [76],
cognitive processes cannot be divorced from the sensorimo-
tor substrate that grounds them. MAI provides a principled
formalization of this view: rather than treating inference as
a disembodied symbolic computation, MAI encodes experi-
ence as path-dependent cycles in a latent manifold, directly
shaped by sensorimotor contingencies. Each cycle γ represents
a reusable trajectory through context-content space, binding
environmental context Ψ with embodied action content Φ. In
this sense, MAI operationalizes the embodied principle that
cognition grows from the active engagement of the body with
the world, with abstract reasoning emerging only as a recom-
bination of these amortized cycles. Thus, embodied cognition
is not merely a philosophical stance but is mathematically
instantiated by MAI’s cycle-consistent inference [77], where
the body provides the scaffold for memory, learning, and
ultimately higher cognition.
From the perspective of AGI, the MAI framework implies
that intelligence cannot be achieved through disembodied sym-
bol manipulation alone [78]. Because MAI encodes experience
as cycle-consistent, path-dependent trajectories in latent space,
directly grounded in sensorimotor contingencies, it opera-
tionalizes the embodied cognition view that higher reasoning
must emerge from the body’s active engagement with the
world. In this picture, abstract cognition and symbolic infer-
ence are not engineered at the outset but arise developmentally
as recombinations of amortized cycles. Thus, AGI should be
conceived not as an oracle of pre-encoded knowledge, but as
a system that grows from embodied interaction, consolidates
these interactions into persistent memory cycles [7], and only
later scaffolds abstraction and imagination upon this grounded
substrate. This stands in contrast to current large language
model paradigms, which begin with symbolic prediction over
text and hope for general intelligence to emerge; under MAI,
the path to AGI proceeds in the opposite direction, from
embodied grounding to abstraction.
Human-AI collaboration [79] plays a central role in the path
toward AGI by extending intelligence beyond isolated agents
into hybrid systems that integrate human creativity, judgment,
and values with machine speed, memory, and scalability.
Rather than viewing AGI as a monolithic artificial mind, the
collaborative paradigm frames it as an emergent property of
sociotechnical ecosystems where humans and AI co-adapt
through shared tasks, distributed inference, and mutual learn-
ing [80]. In this setting, AI systems amortize patterns across
large-scale data and environments, while humans contribute
contextual grounding, ethical alignment, and goal-setting. The
trajectory toward AGI thus unfolds not in isolation but through
collective intelligence, where human-AI synergy becomes the
substrate for societal-scale cognition.
The path to AGI is best understood as a developmental and
collective process. From the perspective of MAI, cognition
is grounded in embodied cycles [81] where context Ψ and
action content Φ are bound through path-dependent trajectories
γ, forming reusable structures that scaffold abstraction and
imagination only at later stages. Yet AGI does not emerge in
isolation: human-AI collaboration [82] plays a central role by
extending intelligence into sociotechnical ecosystems where
humans provide creativity, judgment, and ethical alignment,
while AI contributes memory, speed, and large-scale amor-
tization. In this unified view, AGI arises not as a disem-
bodied symbolic oracle nor as a solitary artificial mind, but
as a distributed property of embodied machines and human
collaborators [83], growing from sensorimotor grounding to
collective intelligence at societal scale.
