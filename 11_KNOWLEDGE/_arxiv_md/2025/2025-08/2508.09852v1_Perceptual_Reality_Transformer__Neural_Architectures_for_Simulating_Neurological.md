Perceptual Reality Transformer: Neural Architectures
for Simulating Neurological Perception Conditions
Baihan Lin
Departments of AI, Psychiatry, and Neuroscience
Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai
New York, NY 10029
baihan.lin@mssm.edu
Abstract
Neurological conditions affecting visual perception create profound experiential
divides between affected individuals and their caregivers, families, and medical
professionals. We present the Perceptual Reality Transformer, a comprehensive
framework employing six distinct neural architectures to simulate eight neuro-
logical perception conditions with scientifically-grounded visual transformations.
Our system learns mappings from natural images to condition-specific perceptual
states, enabling others to experience approximations of simultanagnosia, prosopag-
nosia, ADHD attention deficits, visual agnosia, depression-related changes, anxiety
tunnel vision, and Alzheimer’s memory effects. Through systematic evaluation
across ImageNet and CIFAR-10 datasets, we demonstrate that Vision Transformer
architectures achieve optimal performance, outperforming traditional CNN and
generative approaches. Our work establishes the first systematic benchmark for
neurological perception simulation, contributes novel condition-specific perturba-
tion functions grounded in clinical literature, and provides quantitative metrics
for evaluating simulation fidelity. The framework has immediate applications
in medical education, empathy training, and assistive technology development,
while advancing our fundamental understanding of how neural networks can model
atypical human perception.

Introduction
“Everything looks hauntingly familiar but you just can’t quite recognize anything.” This description
accompanies a widely-circulated image designed to simulate the visual experience of stroke-induced
recognition deficits (Figure 1). The image contains seemingly recognizable objects that resist
identification, creating the unsettling experience of familiar-yet-foreign perception that characterizes
many neurological conditions.

This phenomenon reveals a fundamental challenge in neurological care
and research: the experiential gulf between typical and atypical perception.
When someone experiences simultanagnosia (seeing individual objects
clearly but unable to integrate multiple elements into coherent scenes) their
caregivers and clinicians often struggle to understand this invisible disability.
Traditional medical descriptions, while clinically precise, fail to convey the
lived reality of altered perception.
Recent advances in computer vision and generative modeling present un-
precedented opportunities to bridge this experiential divide. Neural net-
works have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in learning and transform-
ing visual representations, from style transfer to semantic manipulation. Yet

We present the Perceptual Reality Transformer, a systematic framework for neural simulation of
neurological perception conditions. Our approach combines clinical neuroscience knowledge with
modern deep learning architectures to create scientifically-grounded perceptual simulations. Through
comprehensive evaluation of six neural architectures across two benchmark datasets, we establish
both technical foundations and practical applications for this emerging domain.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce novel condition-specific perturbation functions
derived from clinical literature, providing the first systematic approach to neurological perception
simulation. Second, we demonstrate that Vision Transformer architectures effectively capture the
global context required for neurological simulation, achieving superior performance across multiple
evaluation metrics. Third, we establish comprehensive benchmarks and evaluation protocols for this
domain, enabling future research in computational empathy and assistive technology.
Beyond technical contributions, this work opens new directions for computational neuroscience and
human-computer interaction. By learning to simulate atypical perception, neural nets provide novel
insights into the computational mechanisms underlying neurological differences. These models serve
not only as empathy tools but as computational hypotheses about the nature of altered perception.

Related Work
Neurological conditions and visual symptoms. Simultanagnosia, first described by Wolpert in 1924,
represents a profound alteration in visual processing where patients perceive individual elements
clearly but cannot integrate them into coherent wholes. Recent neuroimaging studies by Neitzel et
al. [1] reveal that this condition results from slowed processing speed rather than memory deficits,
with specific white matter atrophy in the left superior parietal lobe correlating with symptom severity.
These findings suggest that computational models should target processing speed and integration
rather than low-level visual features.
Prosopagnosia involves selective face recognition deficits while preserving general object recognition.
Electrophysiological studies by Eimer et al. [2] demonstrate preserved early visual processing (P100,
N170 components) but disrupted face-specific semantic access, indicating that effective simulations
should target high-level recognition rather than early visual features.
ADHD attention deficits show particular severity in visual processing. Lin et al. [3] documented that
visual attention deficits exceed auditory deficits in ADHD patients, with sustained attention, response
inhibition, and attentional consistency most affected. These findings suggest that ADHD simulations
should focus on attentional disruption rather than general cognitive impairment.
Depression-related perceptual changes have been documented with increasing precision. Golomb et
al. [4] showed decreased GABA concentration in visual area MT+ correlating with impaired motion
perception in major depressive disorder. These neurochemical changes manifest as altered color
perception, reduced contrast sensitivity, and general visual dimming.
Computational models of atypical perception. The use of neural networks to model neurological
conditions has emerged as a powerful research paradigm. Tuladhar et al. [5] pioneered this approach
by progressively lesioning VGG-19 networks to model posterior cortical atrophy, demonstrating that
computational models can reproduce key behavioral patterns observed in neurological patients.
Li et al. [6] extended this approach to model visual perceptual learning using modified AlexNet
architectures, showing that layer-specific plasticity in deep networks can reproduce both behavioral
and physiological patterns from human studies. This work demonstrated that neural networks can
serve as bridges between computational and biological accounts of visual processing.
Recent advances in transformer architectures have opened new possibilities for modeling complex
perceptual phenomena. Vision transformers’ attention mechanisms [7] provide natural analogs for
human visual attention, while their global processing capabilities align with documented deficits in
conditions like simultanagnosia.
Applications in medical education. Systematic reviews of empathy training in medical education
reveal consistent benefits from experiential learning approaches. Patel et al.’s [8] meta-analysis of

Methods
Problem formulation and architecture overview. We formulate neurological perception simulation
as a conditional image transformation task. Given an input image I ∈RH×W ×3, neurological
condition identifier c ∈{0, 1, ..., 7}, and severity parameter s ∈[0, 1], we learn a mapping:
fθ : RH×W ×3 × {0, 1, ..., 7} × [0, 1] →RH×W ×3
(1)
where fθ(I, c, s) = I′ represents the transformed image simulating condition c at severity s.
Our framework evaluates six distinct neural architectures, each representing different approaches to
visual transformation and condition modeling. The architectures range from traditional convolutional
networks to advanced generative models, enabling comprehensive analysis of architectural choices
for neurological simulation.
Neural architecture design. We compare 6 different classes of methods:
EncoderDecoderCNN Architecture. Our baseline employs a standard encoder-decoder structure
with condition and severity embeddings. The encoder consists of four convolutional blocks with
progressive downsampling: Conv2d(3→64, 7×7, stride=2) →BatchNorm →ReLU →MaxPool
→Conv2d(64→128, 3×3, stride=2) →Conv2d(128→256, 3×3, stride=2) →Conv2d(256→512,
3×3, stride=2), reducing spatial resolution from 224×224 to 7×7. Condition embeddings (R256)
are generated via learned embedding layers, while severity values are projected through linear
layers. These embeddings are spatially broadcast and concatenated with encoder features. The
decoder mirrors the encoder with transposed convolutions: ConvTranspose2d(512+256→256, 4×4,
stride=2) through ConvTranspose2d(32→3, 4×4, stride=2), progressively upsampling to reconstruct
the full-resolution transformed image.
ResidualPerceptual Architecture. This architecture leverages residual connections [10] to preserve
original image content while learning condition-specific perturbations. The network consists of a
feature extraction backbone using simplified ResNet blocks, followed by perturbation generation
networks that produce additive modifications to the input image. The residual formulation ensures
that f(I, 0, s) ≈I for normal conditions by constraining the perturbation magnitude through learned
gating mechanisms.
ViTPerceptual Architecture. We adapt Vision Transformer [7] architectures for perceptual simulation
by incorporating condition embeddings into the attention mechanism. The architecture uses pretrained
ViT-Base/16 as the foundation, which divides input images into 16×16 patches and processes them
through 12 transformer blocks with 768-dimensional embeddings. Condition-specific information
is injected through additional learned tokens that participate in self-attention, enabling the model
to modulate patch-level representations based on neurological conditions. The decoder consists of
transposed convolution layers that reconstruct images from the transformer’s output embeddings.
RecurrentPerceptual Architecture. LSTM-based architectures model progressive symptom develop-
ment over time through sequential processing of image features. The network first extracts spatial
features using convolutional layers (CNN backbone outputs 64×56×56 features), then flattens and
processes these through LSTM layers (hidden dimension 1024) to capture temporal dynamics of
neurological progression. Condition embeddings provide initial hidden state configuration, while
severity parameters control the number of processing steps, enabling simulation of degenerative
conditions with temporal progression.
DiffusionPerceptual Architecture. We implement a DDPM [11] architecture adapted for conditional
neurological simulation. The U-Net backbone incorporates condition embeddings at multiple scales
through cross-attention mechanisms. The architecture includes: downsampling path (Conv2d blocks
with GroupNorm and SiLU activations), bottleneck processing, and upsampling path with skip

connections. Condition embeddings are processed through separate MLPs and injected at each
resolution level. The noise schedule is adapted for perceptual transformation rather than generation
from pure noise, with linear beta schedule from 0.0001 to 0.02 over 100 timesteps.
GenerativePerceptual Architecture. VAE-based architectures [12] provide interpretable latent space
manipulation for condition simulation. The encoder progressively downsamples input images through
convolutional layers (3→64→128→256→512 channels) to produce latent representations (512-
dimensional). Separate MLPs generate mean and log-variance for reparameterization. The decoder
incorporates condition embeddings (R128) and severity embeddings (R128) that are concatenated with
sampled latent codes before upsampling through transposed convolutions to reconstruct transformed
images.
Clinically-grounded perturbation functions. Our perturbation functions implement documented
visual symptoms with scientific precision:
Simultanagnosia Simulation. Based on clinical findings of impaired visual integration [1], we
implement adaptive fragmentation preserving individual objects while disrupting spatial relationships.
Prosopagnosia Simulation. Following clinical evidence for preserved early processing but disrupted
recognition [2], we apply face-specific perturbations using detected face regions.
ADHD Attention Simulation. Random visual elements are added at varying intensities, with temporal
variation modeling the fluctuating attention characteristic of ADHD [3].
Depression Simulation. Neurochemical findings inform brightness and saturation reductions with
characteristic blue-shifting [4].
Anxiety Tunnel Vision. Radial masking with exponential falloff preserves central vision while
progressively degrading peripheral information [13].
Alzheimer’s Memory effects. Progressive degradation functions combine blur, noise, and fading
effects that worsen with severity parameters [14, 15].
Training protocol and evaluation metrics. Training data is generated by applying perturbation
functions to base images from ImageNet and CIFAR-10 datasets (Figure 2). The training protocol
employs condition-specific loss functions:
L = Lrecon + λ1Ldiversity + λ2Lseverity
(2)
We evaluate models across five dimensions: Reconstruction Quality (MSE): Mean squared error
between predicted and target transformations (lower is better). Condition Diversity: Average pairwise
differences between conditions for same input, ensuring distinct visual representations (higher
is better). Severity Scaling: Correlation between input severity and transformation magnitude,
validating controllable symptom intensity (higher is better). Literature Consistency: Pattern matching
with documented clinical descriptions through condition-specific measurements (higher is better).
Perceptual Distance (LPIPS): Human-aligned visual quality assessment (lower is better).

Experimental Results
Dataset and implementation details. We evaluate our framework on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet
datasets, representing controlled and natural image distributions respectively. All models are trained
for 50 epochs using AdamW optimizer with learning rates of 1e-4 to 2e-4, adjusted per architecture.
Training employs early stopping with patience of 10 epochs based on validation loss.

tural strengths and limitations (Tables 1 and 2): ViTPerceptual achieves optimal overall performance
with lowest reconstruction MSE on both datasets (93,921 on CIFAR-10, 100,671 on ImageNet)
and excellent severity scaling (0.95 and 0.95 respectively), validating that transformer architectures
effectively capture the global context required for neurological simulation. EncoderDecoderCNN
demonstrates competitive performance with highest condition diversity on ImageNet (0.62) and
strong literature consistency, showing that well-designed convolutional architectures remain effective
for this domain. GenerativeVAE provides balanced performance across metrics while enabling
interpretable latent space manipulation valuable for educational applications. DiffusionPerceptual

Discussion
Implications for computational neuroscience. Our results provide novel insights into the computa-
tional nature of neurological perception differences. The superior performance of Vision Transformer
architectures suggests that global context modeling is crucial for effective neurological simulation,
aligning with clinical understanding of conditions like simultanagnosia that involve integration
deficits across visual fields.
The effectiveness of relatively straightforward architectures challenges assumptions about complexity
requirements in neurological modeling. Our EncoderDecoderCNN baseline achieves competitive
performance across metrics, suggesting that the key challenges lie in appropriate architectural design
rather than parameter count or sophistication.
The failure of diffusion models in this domain reveals interesting limitations of current generative
approaches. Unlike aesthetic transformations, neurological simulations require precise control over
specific visual features while preserving others, suggesting that conditional generation frameworks
need task-specific adaptations.
Clinical relevance and medical education applications. Our framework addresses critical gaps in
medical education and clinical empathy training. Traditional approaches rely on verbal descriptions
or static images, leaving substantial comprehension gaps that affect care quality. By providing
interactive, parametric simulations, our system enables experiential learning that has been shown to
improve empathy and understanding.
The condition-specific performance patterns reveal important considerations for clinical application.
Conditions with clear visual signatures (simultanagnosia, anxiety tunnel vision) achieve high simu-

Limitations and future research. Several limitations constrain our current approach. Visual-only
simulations miss important multisensory aspects of neurological conditions. Static image processing
cannot capture dynamic symptoms that vary over time or context. Individual variation in symptom
presentation limits the generalizability of standardized simulations.
Future research should address these limitations through multimodal approaches incorporating
auditory and tactile simulation, temporal modeling for dynamic symptoms, and personalization
frameworks for individual adaptation.

Conclusion
We present the Perceptual Reality Transformer, a comprehensive framework for neural simulation
of neurological perception conditions. Through systematic evaluation of six neural architectures
across established benchmarks, we demonstrate that Vision Transformer architectures achieve optimal
performance for this challenging domain.
Our work makes several key contributions to the intersection of computer vision, computational
neuroscience, and medical technology. We introduce the first systematic approach to neurological
perception simulation with scientifically-grounded perturbation functions and comprehensive evalu-
ation metrics. We demonstrate that transformer architectures excel at capturing the global context
required for neurological simulation, while traditional CNN approaches remain competitive baselines.
By making atypical perception visible and understandable, our framework supports more inclusive
