Complementarity Between Paid and Organic Installs
in Mobile App Advertising
Harang Ju*, Michael Zhao*, Sinan Aral
Massachusetts Institute of Technology
{harang, mfzhao, sinan}@mit.edu
April 24, 2025
Abstract
Prior spending shutoff experiments in search advertising have found that paid ads cannibal-
ize organic traffic. But it is unclear whether the same is true for other high volume advertising
channels like mobile display advertising. We therefore analyzed a large-scale spending shutoff
experiment by a US-based mobile game developer, GameSpace. Contrary to previous find-
ings, we found that paid advertising boosts organic installs rather than cannibalizing them.
Specifically, every $100 spent on ads is associated with 37 paid and 3 organic installs. The
complementarity between paid ads and organic installs is corroborated by evidence of temporal
and cross-platform spillover effects: ad spending today is associated with additional paid and
organic installs tomorrow and impressions on one platform lead to clicks on other platforms.
Our findings demonstrate that mobile app install advertising is about 7.5% more effective than
indicated by paid install metrics alone due to spillover effects, suggesting that mobile app
developers are under-investing in marketing.

Introduction
Over the last decade, a series of high-profile field experiments found that paid advertising substitutes
for organic traffic in web search (Yang and Ghose, 2010; Blake et al., 2015; Coviello et al., 2017;
Golden and Horton, 2020; Simonov and Hill, 2019). These studies utilized ad shutoff experiments
in which paid search ads were shut off to measure the effect of advertising on key performance
indicators (KPIs) like clicks, web traffic, installs, and sales. The studies all found a substitution
effect in which organic traffic substituted for anywhere from 37% to 99.5% of paid advertising
effects, implying that in some cases paid ads had little to no causal impact on metrics of interest for
brands. Intuitively, large brands are well-known and consumers often use search as a navigational
tool to find a brand’s website (Golden and Horton, 2020).1 Indeed, consumers often even navigate
back to the search results when the top sponsored result is not the focal brand, i.e., the brand whose
name matches the keyword (Simonov et al., 2018).
These results raised important questions about the efficacy of paid advertising, especially paid
search advertising, given much of the work to attract consumers was apparently being done by
brand recognition and not by the ads themselves. The research also demonstrated the pitfalls of
correlational attribution analysis when evaluating the effectiveness of paid advertising. While it
seemed, at first, that paid ads were correlated with clicks and traffic, in these influential experiments,
consumers were navigating to the websites in question whether ads were being purchased and shown
or not. These results were surprising and influential because they drew attention to the limitations
of correlational attribution analysis and drew into question the efficacy of paid search ads.
While this literature has consistently shown that paid ads substitute for organic traffic in Internet
search, it is unclear if these or similar results hold in other high-volume advertising channels. Indeed,
Blake et al. (2015) found that paid ads for non-branded keywords were effective, though only for
newly registered users or purchases made by users who bought infrequently. Moreover, Simonov
et al. (2018) showed that ads were less effective for more popular websites because those websites
have more organic traffic and brand recognition. Thus, some initial evidence suggests that the

Our work contributes to the growing literature on digital advertising effectiveness. While prior
work has found varying conclusions about the effectiveness of digital advertising, one consistent
result has been that paid advertising is not as effective as attribution modeling suggests due to the
substitution of paid advertising effects with organic traffic (Blake et al., 2015; Simonov et al., 2018).
To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to find the opposite effect to the substitution
effects found in paid search advertising, in the context of mobile app ads—namely, that advertising,
on average, generates additional organic conversions. Moreover, our work has immediate managerial
implications. Given that mobile app install advertising is 7.5% more effective than the attribution
analysis suggests, our results imply that mobile app advertisers are systematically under-investing
in marketing.

Related Literature
2.1
Advertising Shutoff Experiments
Before advertising shutoff experiments, research on search advertising examined the impact of
organic and sponsored search on ad performance. For example, Ghose and Yang (2009) analyzed
six months of panel data on search metrics across numerous keywords, revealing a direct correlation
between conversion rates and search result rankings—the higher the rank, the better the conversion
rates. Moreover, Yang and Ghose (2010), through an ad pulse experiment, substantiated that paid
5


## Page 6


search advertisements significantly enhance click-through rates and revenue.
In their pioneering study using a shutoff in ad spend, Blake et al. (2015) (henceforth BNT)
produced strong evidence that paid search advertising substitutes for organic traffic. Using a series
of large-scale field experiments conducted at eBay, they showed that when branded search ads
were suspended, most of the lost advertising traffic simply shifted to organic search traffic. One
interesting heterogeneous effect was that non-branded search ads had a positive effect on new or
infrequent consumers. On average, however, returns on advertising were ultimately negative since
most ads were delivered to frequent consumers who—at least in the short run—were not affected
by ads.
Later, Coviello et al. (2017) were concerned with the generalizability of BNT’s findings given
that eBay was particularly well-known at the time. They replicated an experiment at Edmunds.com
similar to that of BNT’s at eBay. They found the same substitution effect but at a far smaller
magnitude: less than half of paid search traffic was recovered through organic search. Such an
effect suggests that paid search may provide a positive return, but it still points to the substitution
effect of paid advertising by organic traffic.
Simonov and Hill (2019) and Golden and Horton (2020) further confirmed Coviello et al.
(2017)’s results. Although both studies were primarily focused on understanding how a firm’s paid
search advertising impacts their competitors’ outcomes, their direct results show that paid search is
indeed effective but that it crowds out organic traffic to some degree. In the case of Golden and
Horton (2020), they noted that paid advertising is approximately 63% efficient, implying that 37%
of paid advertising is substituted by organic traffic. Similarly, Simonov and Hill (2019) found that
paid search ads cannibalized about 37.8% of a brand’s organic search traffic. Other studies also
examine the impact of search result competition on conversion performance, noting attenuation in
performance with increased competition (Agarwal et al., 2016; Bhattacharya et al., 2021).
Our work builds on this existing literature in several ways. First, our work is not limited to
paid search advertising. While paid search is still a dominant channel in digital advertising, other
channels continue to gain market share each year, reaching 61% of all digital ad spend in 2019
6


## Page 7


(Marin Software, 2019). Second, unlike prior ad shutoff experiments, our results uncover evidence
that paid advertising can complement organic channels rather than cannibalize it. Our research
contributes to the literature on digital advertising by showing that, unlike previous studies, paid
advertising can complement, rather than substitute, organic channels. Third, our work brings
ad shutoff experiments to the mobile channel. As mobile use and mobile advertising are both
growing at unprecedented rates, understanding the extent to which paid advertising complements or
substitutes organic traffic on the fastest-growing devices is essential. In a sense, our work brings the
ad shutoff experiment literature from the desktop era to the mobile era.
2.2
Mobile Advertising
As the importance of mobile as a media channel has increased over the past decade, academic
interest in mobile advertising has correspondingly intensified. Due to the unique GPS data provided
by mobile devices (Shankar and Balasubramanian, 2009), many have focused on quantifying the
value of location-based targeting. Early work (Ghose et al., 2013; Luo et al., 2014; Fang et al.,
2015; Fong et al., 2015) demonstrated the importance of geographic proximity in mobile promotion
effectiveness. In particular, Luo et al. (2014) found significant interaction effects between location
and temporal targeting: while same-day mobile promotions were most effective for proximal
consumers, prior-day promotions were most effective for non-proximal consumers. More recent
work by Ghose et al. (2019) illustrated the potential gains of trajectory-based targeting—where
targeting is based on trajectories in consumers’ movement patterns rather than just the current
location.
Some have leveraged rich location data to understand how environmental factors impact mobile
ad effectiveness. For instance, Andrews et al. (2016) showed that the purchase rate of consumers in
physically crowded subway trains was substantially higher than in uncrowded trains. In a different
vein, Li et al. (2017) confirmed the moderating effect of the weather: when it is bright and sunny
outside, people are more likely to respond more quickly to mobile promotions; rainy weather, on
the other hand, decreases both response rates and response speed.
7


## Page 8


Naturally, not all mobile advertising research relates to location targeting. Bart et al. (2014)
investigated the products most suitable for mobile display advertising. Using a large-scale ran-
domized field experiment, they determined that mobile display campaigns tended to benefit from
“higher involvement” and “utilitarian” products. Zhang et al. (2019) displayed the effectiveness
of personalized promotion strategies in combating attrition and low engagement in mobile apps.
Others have focused specifically on mobile in-app advertisements. Ghose and Han (2014) found,
for example, that showing in-app ads tended to decrease app demand by approximately the same
amount as an 8% price increase. On the other hand, Rafieian and Yoganarasimhan (2020) explored
the interplay between in-app behavioral targeting and privacy and found that while efficient targeting
increases total surplus, the ad publisher can receive less total revenue since targeting “thins out the
market.”
Though the literature on mobile advertising continues to grow, there has been surprisingly
little work dedicated to mobile app install advertising. Most of the existing work is focused on
improvements in various machine learning algorithms that power the bidding and consumer targeting
systems (Ma et al., 2016; Bhamidipati et al., 2017; Sahu et al., 2018). Our work, to the best of our
knowledge, is one of the first empirical papers to investigate app install advertising effectiveness.
2.3
Attribution Models
The study of attribution models in digital advertising is key to understanding their impact on
online marketing strategies, particularly for paid search advertising. Li et al. (2016) revealed
how switching from last-click to first-click attribution significantly alters returns from keyword
investments, affecting a firm’s bidding and budgeting decisions, advertiser strategies, search engine
rankings, and consumer interactions. Meanwhile, Berman (2018) highlighted the drawbacks of
last-touch attribution in multi-publisher online advertising, such as excessive ad exposures that can
decrease advertiser profits and instigate competition among publishers, influencing both profits and
market pricing.
Regarding multimedia ads, Danaher et al. (2020) explored attribution in multimedia and mul-
8


## Page 9


tichannel settings, using machine learning to correlate media exposure with sales for different
retailer brands, thereby illustrating the varied impact of media types on sales and consumer behavior.
Additionally, Danaher and van Heerde (2018) assessed multimedia budget allocation based on
attribution models, proposing a model that accounts for incremental contributions and interaction
effects. Overall, these studies highlight the importance of attribution models in digital advertising, as
they influence the efficiency of marketing strategies and the overall dynamics of online advertising.
While attribution models were not our primary focus, our work relates the literature on attribution
models to the literature on ad effectiveness, in the context of mobile apps. First, ad effectiveness is
estimated through attribution models. While previous studies show that attribution can overestimate
ad effectiveness for large brands, our work is the first, to our knowledge, to show that it can
underestimate ad effectiveness for small brands and in the context of mobile app install ads.
Moreover, Li and Kannan (2014) show that last-touch attribution significantly underestimates
the contribution of e-mails, display ads, and referrals to conversions. They demonstrated that
some customers saw ads on one channel and searched on a search engine as a navigational tool,
as also shown by Golden and Horton (2020) and Simonov et al. (2018). We observed a similar
cross-platform spillover from impressions on Facebook ads, which are often display ads, to clicks
on Google search ads. Thus, our initial evidence of cross-platform effects supports the idea that
last-touch attribution leads to underestimation of the effectiveness of ads on non-search channels
and overestimation of the effectiveness of search ads.

Empirical Strategy and Model Specifications
We employed three main empirical methods to analyze the paid ad shutoff experiments across
GameSpace’s six different apps: difference-in-difference estimation (DiD), an event study (ES),
and fixed effects panel regressions. We describe the details of each method and the corresponding
model specifications in the three different subsections below.
4.1
Difference-in-Difference
In the context of digital advertising, employing a DiD can be particularly effective in certain
scenarios. First, our empirical setting offers a natural choice of discrete treatment: GameSpace’s
shutoff in ad spend. For DiD estimation, we used the partial shutoff in ad spend (light grey in
Figure 2), which occurs for only two out of the six apps (i.e., App5 and App6) and allows the
comparison of installs with and without ad spend. Thus, we take App5-Android, App5-iOS, App6-
Android, and App6-iOS as our 4 treatment units and the remaining 8 app-OS combinations as our
control units.
In our empirical setting, we can also observe and verify the key identifying assumption of
parallel trends during the global shutoff (dark grey in Figure 2) since the underlying amount of
spend between different units is constant (i.e., zero) during the global shutoff. During this period,
the underlying spending dynamics across all app-OS units are essentially the same. Hence, we can
observe this period specifically to verify if parallel trends hold for organic installs.
To perform our DiD estimation, we employed the model specification detailed in Equation 1:
log(Yi jt) = δDi jt +αi j +τt +εi jt
(1)
We observed and analyzed three main outcomes Yi jt in this setting: organic installs, paid installs, and
total installs. We log-transformed these variables to interpret our estimated coefficients multiplica-
tively rather than additively, which is more appropriate given that there is significant heterogeneity
(in terms of both spend and installs) across the various apps. Our treatment Di jt is simply an

indicator for whether spend was shut off for app i on OS j on date t. The coefficient δ is the
main parameter of interest denoting the treatment effect of the spending shutoff. Specifically, the
estimated counterfactual is the relative treatment effect on the treated (RTT)—the relative difference
in organic installs under no spending compared to the normal spending for the treated units. We
employed a joint app-OS fixed effect αi j which helps control for any time-invariant unobservables
for each specific app-OS combination and account for any seasonality or time trends using time
fixed effects τt. Lastly, εijt denotes the error term.
4.2
Event Study
In our Event Study estimation, we employed time as a running variable and used the treatment date
as a threshold (Hausman and Rapson, 2018). In our context, our choice for the threshold is the start
of the first shutoff period on Day 75. While the ad spend reactivation theoretically serves as an
additional discontinuity, we decided to exclude it from our analysis since the reactivation was far
more gradual as can be seen in Figure 3. We specifically estimate 2 different specifications for our
RDiT: local constant and local linear described by Equations 2 and 3 respectively:
log(Yijt) = δDijt +αi j +ωt +εi jt
(2)
log(Yijt) = δDijt +γ1(t −c)+γ2Di jt(t −c)+αi j +ωt +εi jt
(3)
where the variables Yijt, Dijt, αij, and εi jt are as they were in Equation 1. The coefficient δ
represents the same quantity as in Equation 1. We also test for the robustness of this estimation
with a Poisson regression in addition to an ordinary least squares regression in Section 5.5. Notice,
however, that neither of these equations contains time-fixed effects τt. As time has become our
running variable, including time-fixed effects would result in perfect multicollinearity. Instead, we
opted to include a set of day-of-week fixed effects, denoted by ωt to account for weekly cyclicality.
For Equation 3 specifically, (t −c) simply denotes the difference in days between the current date
t and the cutoff of date c = 75. The coefficients γ1 and γ2 capture the slopes on each side of the

discontinuity but are disregarded for this analysis.
We purposefully limited ourselves to the simpler RD specifications since the more complex
specifications run the risk of overfitting. Overfitting can be especially problematic since the decrease
in spending, while rather drastic, is still not perfectly sharp, thus extending the discontinuity across
multiple days. As such, higher-order polynomial terms, if used, would more likely capture the
underlying shift in spending, rather than the true nonlinearity required for an event study. For
instance, as seen in Figure 3, the two series corresponding to App3 already attained a much lower
level of spend 3 days before the cutoff compared to the other apps. Though this is the most prominent
example, many other apps already had declining ad spend in the days before c. Furthermore, there
is a risk of persistence effects: installing an app today due to an ad exposure yesterday or the day
before. While such effects will eventually decay, this will also have a similar effect of extending the
discontinuity.

Aggregate Spend
Although our DiD and ES specifications identify the causal effects of a shutoff
in ad spend, in practice, firms make adjustments to optimize their ad spend rather than simply
shutting it off entirely. To examine how continuous changes in ad spend affect organic installs, we

(4)

We organize the presentation of our results into five subsections. The first three subsections

5.5
Robustness
Before proceeding to a discussion of our results, we discuss the robustness of our estimates,
particularly of the shutoff experiments. The observed install numbers, which serve as our dependent
variable, are inherently counts, reflecting the number of times an application was installed. To
ensure the robustness of our results, we conducted additional checks using Poisson regression for
the difference-in-difference estimation, specified by Equation 1, and the event study estimation,
specified by Equations 2 and 3.
When using Poisson regression for the difference-in-difference estimation using Equation 1, we
found that the results, shown in Table 6, were all qualitatively and quantitatively similar. Importantly,
the estimated effects on paid installs were practically equal for the Poisson and OLS regressions at
99.6%. Interestingly, the estimated effect of the ad shutoff on organic installs was even stronger for
the Poisson regression with a decrease of 30.6% versus a decrease of 24.3% for the OLS regression.
As such, the estimated effect for total installs was greater for the Poisson regression at 31.2% than
for the OLS regression at 22.9%. This robustness test further supports the idea that paid install
numbers underestimated the total ad effectiveness, between 22.9% and 31.2%.

Discussion
Despite the importance of advertising to mobile app publishers, little is known about optimizing
mobile app install ad budgets. Although prior research has shown that paid channels substitute for
organic ones, the magnitude of the effect varied wildly across different studies, from 37% to 99.5%.
Given the competitiveness of the mobile app market and the heterogeneity of the substitution effect
across brands, it is an important question for mobile app marketers to precisely determine the degree
to which paid advertising is cannibalizing organic installs. To answer this question, we studied
an ad spend shutoff experiment conducted by a major mobile game developer. Using a variety of
empirical methods, we find no evidence of cannibalization. Rather, our results, consistent across
all of our different empirical strategies, indicate that advertising is indeed boosting the number of
organic installs, which has key implications for mobile app marketers.
One possible reason that our results are so different from those of previous studies may be the
fact that prior experiments have been focused on search advertising while our experiment is on
mobile app install ads. However, search ads and mobile app install ads are not mutually exclusive;
mobile app install ads include the entire spectrum of digital channels, including search, social
media, display, video, and in-app. Indeed, in our platform-decomposed regression, we observed a
difference in the underestimation of ad effectiveness on search ads on Google versus display ads
on Facebook. Moreover, we observed a spillover from impressions on display ads on Facebook to
clicks on search ads on Google. This cross-platform spillover supports the idea that search is often
used as a navigational tool (Golden and Horton, 2020; Simonov et al., 2018). Further, this spillover,
in concert with last-touch attribution, can lead to an underestimation of the effectiveness of display
ads and an overestimation of the effectiveness of search ads (Li and Kannan, 2014).
Since our results address the differences across digital channels and media types, the differ-
ences between previous studies and ours may stem from heterogeneity across brand popularity or
awareness. The apps in our study are primarily games, and while many of them use well-known
intellectual property (IP), the games themselves are not well-known. As an example, the game
“The Legend Of Zelda: Spirit Tracks” may use the popular IP of Zelda, but the game itself may
29


## Page 30


not be as popular as the IP. In previous search ad studies, the search terms were often branded,
using well-known brands including eBay and Edmunds (Blake et al., 2015; Coviello et al., 2017).
However, the substitution effect was less for non-branded search (Blake et al., 2015) and less popular
brands (Simonov et al., 2018). In a similar vein, both Coviello et al. (2017) and Golden and Horton
(2020) found significantly smaller crowding-out effects of branded advertising (from around 37% to
50%) for firms they were studying compared to the more well-known eBay, which was studied by
Blake et al. (2015) and had the largest substitution effect of over 99%.
The natural extrapolation of the previously observed brand heterogeneity is that for lesser-
known brands, the substitution effect should be even smaller; i.e., attributed paid installs should
overestimate ad effectiveness far less for lesser-known brands. Surprisingly, our results not only
support this extrapolation of brand heterogeneity but also reveal that the effect even becomes
negative for the lesser-known brands in this study, for whom attribution modeling can underestimate
ad effectiveness. In hindsight, our findings can be intuitive. Consumers know big brands, by
definition, and will search and navigate to them regardless of the presence of paid ads. Far fewer
consumers know about smaller brands, by definition, and will search for or navigate to them far less
if paid ads are not present. Our results are especially pertinent given the rise of the long tail of the
Internet, which facilitates the proliferation of niche products and small brands (Brynjolfsson et al.,
2006). Our results are also critical for mobile app marketers due to the ongoing rapid growth of the
mobile app market and its incredibly wide selection of non-branded apps.
While our study provides direct managerial implications for marketers for mobile apps and small
brands, one limitation of our study is that the mechanism of the organic lift (versus the substitution
effect) is unclear. One possible explanation is that the installs due to organic lift are unattributed
(e.g., a consumer sees an ad on one device and installs “organically” on another). Practitioners
often credit organic lift with word-of-mouth marketing or search engine optimization (Rosenfelder
and Belan, 2019). Our results on the temporal next-day spillovers on ad spend are not at odds with
potential word-of-mouth behavior since it is plausible to suggest that word-of-mouth spread occurs
a few days following a paid impression, but a more definitive and causal investigation is required.
30


## Page 31


In the context of mobile apps, additional possibilities include consumer interactions in a mobile app
marketplace, such as app store rankings or featured apps. Importantly, the managerial implications
of our results remain the same, regardless of the mechanism.
Another limitation of our work is that, while GameSpace did not strategically choose a date to
experiment, there may nonetheless be confounders during the shutoff period that could drive our
estimates. If this were the case, however, it is worth noting that only the RDiT results would be
confounded. For our DiD results, the shutoff period is only being used to verify parallel trends.
The DiD estimates are computed from the shutdown periods of App5 and App6. They would not
be impacted by a hypothetical shutoff period confounder unless that confounder applied only to
App5 and App6 at the exact times ad spending was shut down for those apps. Another limitation is
the aggregate nature of our analysis. Although we have daily campaign-level data from over 85
publishers, we are unable to identify campaign or publisher-level heterogeneity since the spend
shutoff is applied across all campaigns of all publishers. Lastly, the results from our study may not
be generalizable to all firms. Our data comes from one of the largest mobile gaming developers,
and advertising effects may vary across different types of apps or developers of different sizes.
Additional limitations stem from the complex nature of digital advertising. As noted by Bagwell
(2007), advertising decisions are generally endogenous, which can bias estimates. However, several
factors for our context may suggest that our panel results will generalize experimentally. First off, the
budgeting decisions made by GameSpace themselves were relatively random: the marketing teams
for each app often made quite arbitrary decisions regarding day-to-day budget allocations. Although
allocation levels between different apps were very deliberate, this endogeneity is controlled for via
app-OS fixed effects. Furthermore, the consumer behaviors that are downstream of ad spend can
be affected by the ad auction process. Though a firm might set its budget specifically to target a
certain number of impressions or clicks, it cannot perfectly account for the behavior of other firms,
whose bidding may impact impressions, clicks, and installs. The context of mobile apps introduces
further complexity, including location (Ghose et al., 2013; Luo et al., 2014; Fang et al., 2015; Fong
et al., 2015), product utility (Bart et al., 2014), and personalization (Zhang et al., 2019). Consumer
31


## Page 32


heterogeneity also has substantial implications, especially in the mobile gaming industry where
consumer spending is highly skewed with a small minority generating a majority of revenue. These
additional intricacies highlight the need for further studies to dissect the nuanced interplay of these
factors in digital advertising.
Despite these limitations, we believe that our study makes several novel contributions. To
start, our study is, to the best of our knowledge, the first advertising spend shutoff experiment that
uncovers the possibility for complementarity rather than substitution between paid and organic
channels. Furthermore, ours is one the first papers to examine mobile app install advertising, a
rapidly growing market. Beyond this, our work has immediate managerial implications. Our results
show that paid advertising is about 7.5% more effective than attributed paid install numbers suggest
for mobile apps. As such, mobile app developers that have optimized their spend based solely on
paid installs may potentially be underinvesting in marketing.
