Abstract
Binary classification from positive-only samples is a variant of PAC learning where the
learner receives i.i.d. positively labeled samples and aims to learn a classifier that, with high
probability, achieves low classification error.
Previous work by Natarajan [Nat87, STOC],
Gereb-Graus [Ger89, Thesis], and Shvaytser [Shv90, Machine Learning] characterized learn-
ability in this setting and revealed a largely negative picture: almost no interesting classes,
including two-dimensional halfspaces, are learnable from positive-only examples. This poses
significant challenges for the plethora of applications of positive-only learning from bioinfor-
matics to ecology, where practitioners rely on heuristics for learning.
In this work, we initiate a smoothed analysis of positive-only learning. We assume we
have access to samples from a reference distribution D such that the true data distribution D⋆
is smooth with respect to it. Our first result demonstrates that, in stark contrast to the worst-
case setting, all VC classes become learnable in the smoothed model, requiring O(VC/ε2) pos-
itive samples to guarantee ε-classification error. We then present a computationally efficient
algorithm for any concept class that admits poly(ε)-approximation by degree-k polynomials
(whose range is lower-bounded by a constant) with respect to D in the L1-norm. The algorithm
runs in time poly(dk/ε), which qualitatively matches the running time of the L1-Regression al-
gorithm. This smoothed analysis contributes to the growing body of work designing better
learning guarantees under smoothness [HRS24, J. ACM] [CKKMS24, COLT].
Our results also imply faster or more general algorithms for the following problems:
1. Estimation under unknown truncation, where we give the first polynomial sample and
time algorithm for estimating the parameters of an exponential family distribution from
samples truncated to an unknown set S⋆that is approximable by non-negativepolynomials
in L1-norm. This improves upon [KTZ19, FOCS] [LMZ24, FOCS], which required strong
approximation with respect to L2.
2. Truncation detection, where we present the first algorithm for detecting whether given
samples have been truncated (or not) for a broad class of distributions, including non-
product distributions. This improves upon [DLNS24a, STOC] who were limited to product
distributions.
3. Learning with a list of reference distributions, as a corollary of our main result on smoothed
analysis. We obtain analogous sample and computational complexity results in the more
general setting where we do not have access to (samples from) a reference distribution D
but rather only have access to samples from a list of O(1) distributions one of which wit-
nesses the smoothness of D⋆. This naturally arises if list-decoding algorithms are used to
learn samplers for D⋆from corrupted data.

Introduction
In the celebrated PAC Learning framework [Val84a], the goal is to learn a binary concept from
perfectly labeled samples by minimizing misclassification error of future predictions. In many
real-world settings, however, while positively labeled samples are readily available, negatively la-
beled samples are scarce or costly to obtain. This was motivated as a problem even in the very first
papers of Valiant [Val84b] where he discusses the learning task of classifying elephants from non-
elephants and he mentions: “While it may be reasonable to discuss the distribution of the attributes of ele-
phants, we may prefer not discussing the distribution of the attributes of non-elephants.” This issue arises,
also, in medical diagnosis, where confirmed cases of a condition are recorded but healthy controls
are unlabeled [EN08], or in web search and recommendation systems, where user engagement
indicates positive feedback but non-engagement is ambiguous [LDLLY03]. These practical chal-
lenges can be modeled through a natural extension of the PAC framework by Natarajan [Nat87],
where the learner only observes samples drawn from the underlying distribution of positive data.
Definition 1 (PAC Learning with Positive-Only Samples; [Nat87]). Let D⋆be an unknown distribu-
tion over the feature space X and let h⋆∈H be the target concept. The learner receives n i.i.d. samples
drawn according to the conditional distribution P⋆= D⋆|(h⋆(X)=1), that is, the distribution D⋆con-
ditioned on the event that h⋆(X) = 1. As in the standard PAC model, the goal is to output a hypothesis
bh: X →{0, 1} that achieves small misclassification error with respect to the original distribution D⋆, i.e.,
PrX∼D⋆(bh(X) ̸= h⋆(X)) →0
as
n →∞.
Observe that although the samples are drawn from P⋆the performance of bh is evaluated in both the positive
and the negative regions.
Natarajan [Nat87] was the first to study learning from positive samples and he characterized the
classes that can be properly PAC learned from only positive samples.1 Subsequently, Gereb-Graus
[Ger89], Shvaytser [Shv90], and Kivinen [Kiv95] completed the characterization for improper
learners and bounded the sample complexity for the problem. These results are perceived mostly
as negative results since they demonstrated that even fundamental classes such as two-dimensional
halfspaces cannot be properly learned from positive-only samples.
To bypass this worst-case bottleneck, and due to the practical relevance of learning from
positive-only data, several works explored average-case versions of the problem. For instance,
a line of work (e.g., [FJK96; NR09; AGR13; DDS15; KTZ19; CDS20; JKV23; LMZ24]) designs
computationally efficient algorithms for learning specific hypothesis classes (e.g., simplices, par-
allelepipeds, halfspaces, polynomial threshold functions, or concepts with small surface area) from
positive samples, when crucially, the underlying distribution D⋆is typically assumed to be either
the uniform distribution on the Boolean Cube or Gaussian.2
In the majority of the many practical applications of learning from positive-only data, paramet-
ric assumptions on D⋆naturally can be unrealistic. Indeed, the negative results to positive-only
learning are a significant barrier for the use of machine learning in critical applications where neg-
ative examples are unavailable [JS22]. In response, a substantial body of work in applied sciences,
from bioinformatics [XS08; ZWCA08; CEC10; YLMKN12; JC16; YOLMZY19] to ecology [PDS04],

Can we learn general concept classes from positive-only samples without strong assumptions on D⋆?

of the reference measure D. Parallels can be made to the line of work on smoothed analysis of
online learning where the first papers assumed perfect knowledge of the reference measure D
[HRS20; HRS24] but follow-up works eliminated the knowledge of D completely [BRS24; Bla25].

can be arbitrary and we do not need to know, e.g., its density. Because of this very mild assumption
on D our results have also implication for other important problems in learning theory:

▶Detecting Truncation (Section 1.3.2). Our positive-only learning can also be used in detection
algorithms of whether a dataset has been subject to censoring [DNS23; DLNS24a].
▶Learning with a list of reference distributions (Section 1.3.3). Finally, we generalize our al-
gorithms to work when we do not have sample access to the reference distribution D, but only
have a list of O(1) distributions one of which witnesses the smoothness of D⋆(Section 1.3.3).
This naturally arises when a sampler for D⋆must be learned from a data-source with high-
corruption rate necessitating the use of list decoding algorithms.

1.1
Smoothed Analysis of Positive-Only Learning
Toward developing a more realistic theoretical framework that yields better practical algorithms,
we adopt the smoothed analysis framework introduced by Spielman and Teng [ST04]. Smoothed
analysis was originally developed to explain why algorithms with poor worst-case complexity
often succeed in practice: it studies algorithm performance under small perturbations of worst-
case inputs, effectively smoothing the input distribution. In learning theory, this philosophy has
been adapted by requiring that the unknown data distribution D⋆is smoothed with respect to a
reference measure D. The smoothness condition that we use in this paper is more relaxed than
usual the notion of smoothness that has been used in learning theory by [BRS24; HRS24; Bla25],
and places less assumptions on the distribution D⋆.
Assumption 1 (Generalized Smoothness). There are known constants σ ∈(0, 1] and q ≥1 and a
distribution D, such that, D⋆is smooth with respect to D in the following sense: for any measurable set S,
D⋆(S) ≤1
σ · D(S)1/q .
We often refer to D⋆as the unlabeled distribution and D as the imperfect unlabeled distribution.
This assumption upper bounds the unlabeled distribution’s mass on any set S in terms of the ref-
erence or imperfect unlabeled distribution’s mass. To gain some intuition, suppose that D is a
uniform mixture of the unlabeled distribution D⋆and some other “bad” distribution B, i.e., D =
0.5 D⋆+ 0.5 B. Then, for any set S, D⋆(S) ≤2 D(S) and, hence, smoothness holds with (σ, q) = (1/2, 1).
For q = 1, this assumption reduces to the notion of smoothness considered by recent works
on smoothed online learning [HRS20; Man21; HHSY22; BRS24; HRS24; Bla25]. When q = 2,
generalized smoothness is implied by χ2 (D⋆∥D) = O(1) and, for larger q, it is implied by bounds
on the suitable Rényi divergence (see Section D.4).4
As we will present in the next section, generalized smoothness suffices to design sample-
efficient algorithms. For our computationally efficient algorithms though, we need to additionally
assume that the positive sample set H⋆has non-trivial mass.
Assumption 2 (Non-Trivial Fraction of Positives). For some known α > 0, Prx∼D⋆(h⋆(x) = 1) ≥α.
This assumption is also required by prior works on positive-only learning that design compu-
tationally efficient algorithms (e.g., [KTZ19; LMZ24]). We note that the worst-case impossibility
results for learning with positive-only samples continue to hold even under Assumption 2; for
completeness, we prove this in Section E.

analysis framework studied in this paper.
Improved Sample Complexity for q = 1. Under an additional assumption (Assumption 3) that
complements generalized smoothness (Assumption 1) by providing a lower bound on D⋆(S) for

that in realizable PAC Learning, one has sample access to D⋆—one can simply ignore the labels

q > 1 (Theorem 1.1). Finally, we stress that without this assumption (Assumption 3), these works
are inapplicable while our main results (Theorems 1.1 and 3.3) remain valid.

1.3.1

estimate bθ of θ⋆given samples from the truncation5 of the target distribution D⋆= D(θ⋆) to the
set S⋆– without any access to S⋆. For this problem to be tractable, it is necessary to assume a

1.3.2
Detecting Truncation
Our next application is also from Truncated Statistics [Mad83; Coh91]. In particular, to the problem
of testing whether a given set of samples is i.i.d. from a distribution Q or i.i.d. from the truncation
Q(S⋆) of Q to some unknown set S⋆.
A growing line of works in Theoretical Computer Science study this problem [DNS23; HN23;
DLNS24a; BHK26]. It is not too hard to show (see the work of De, Li, Nadimpalli, and Serve-
dio [DLNS24a]) that if the mass of S⋆under Q is very close to 1 then this problem is hopeless.
This is roughly because as the mass Q(S⋆) approaches 1, the distributions Q and Q(S⋆) become
indistinguishable. Hence, one focuses on the case where Q(S⋆) ≤1 −β for some known constant
β = Ω(1). De, Li, Nadimpalli, and Servedio [DLNS24a] study this problem in the special case

Practical Motivation for Smoothed Positive-Only Learning
While we have highlighted our results insofar as how they apply to other learning theory prob-
lems, we next motivate the study of smoothed analysis of positive-only learning itself. We begin
by discussing real-world applications where obtaining “perfect” unlabeled examples is difficult
or even impossible, and, hence, existing methods must grapple with imperfect unlabeled data –
precisely the challenge that we formalize. For further examples, see the surveys by Elkan and
Noto [EN08] and Plessis, Niu, and Sugiyama [PNS15] and a recent book [JS22] on learning from
positive and unlabeled data.
Applications in Bioinformatics and Medicine. Consider disease-causing gene identification – a cen-
tral task in Genetics and Molecular Biology with major healthcare implications [MV11; YLMKN12].
Identification is very expensive due to its reliance on highly skilled human labor and precise ex-
perimental procedures. Here, ML algorithms, if given sufficient and good-quality data, can ex-
pedite discovery by flagging promising candidates for experimental validation. However, due to
the high false negative rates in experimental methods, high-quality negative examples are scarce;
a gene that appears benign under one condition may be disease-causing under another [Li+21].

Similar issues affect a wide array of applications in Bioinformatics and Medicine – from biolog-
ical sequence classification, to drug interaction identification, to identifying undiagnosed diseases
from health records [EN08]. In response, starting from the seminal works of [BN06; EN08], a sub-

Reasons for Imperfect Unlabeled Examples: Source Mismatch.
The feature distribution of unlabeled

For instance, validated disease-causing genes (positive examples) come from specialized studies,
while unlabeled examples may be sampled from all known genes [MV11; Li+21]. Similarly, in
medical applications, positive data might be collected from specialized and costly programs at a
single hospital, whereas unlabeled data are aggregated nationwide, leading to discrepancies in
feature distribution (e.g., demographics and measurement conditions) [HKNC18]. In both illus-
trative examples, the imperfect unlabeled distribution has a strictly larger support (e.g., it is the
set of all known genes) than the (perfect) unlabeled distribution (e.g., which are supported on only
relevant genes), so the upper bound in Assumption 1 is natural.
Applications in User-Specified Identification Tasks.
User-specified identification tasks – also
known as one-vs-rest classification, inlier-based outlier detection, and anomaly detection – involve
a user labeling a small set of positive examples from a large pool of unlabeled data. Examples

Reasons for Imperfect Unlabeled Examples. Here, reasons for covariate shift include mismatch in the

of unlabeled sources that “approximates” the true distribution (Remark 1.7); that turns out to be
sufficient for learning (Section 1.3.3). Again in this setting, since unlabeled data is augmented,
we expect the imperfect unlabeled samples to have a larger support than the perfect unlabeled
samples—making the bound in Assumption 1 natural.

Takeaways and Open Problems
We initiate the smooth analysis of positive-only learning. While the problem seems important on
its own due to its connections to practical settings (Section 1.5), it also has surprising connections
to truncated statistics.
The smooth perspective on the problem leads to algorithms that work under significantly
weaker assumptions (just smoothness and L1-approximability) than those required by existing
algorithms in practical applications and truncated statistics (e.g., exact access to unlabeled sam-
ples or L2-approximability; see Sections 1.3 and 1.5). This applicability to seemingly unrelated
problems is an important aspect of our results, that we hope will inspire further research. To this
end, we identify and present some open problems that arise from our work before discussing
further related works, formal statements of our results, and proofs.

Related Work
We have already discussed relevant results on learning from positive-only samples. Our results
are also related to several learning models. We briefly survey them below and defer additional
works to Section A.
Smoothed Analysis. Spielman and Teng [ST04] initiated the study of algorithms whose inputs are
perturbed by Gaussian noise; they, in particular, showed that the simplex algorithm runs in poly-
nomial time in this model. Since then, the smoothed analysis model has found applications in many
areas, often enabling significantly stronger theoretical guarantees than the worst-case model. For
instance, in online learning, a line of works [HRS20; HHSY22; BRS24; HRS24; Bla25] showed that
if the adversary is restricted to select strategies from smooth distributions, then the lower bounds
based on the Littlestone dimension can be bypassed and any VC class becomes learnable. In the
same spirit Chandrasekaran, Klivans, Kontonis, Meka, and Stavropoulos [CKKMS24] obtained a
wide array of faster PAC learning algorithms when the learner only has to compete with the best
classifier that is robust to small random Gaussian perturbations of its inputs. We refer the reader
to [Rou21, Chapter 13] and [BV04] for more discussion and applications outside of learning theory.
Learning from Positive and Perfect Unlabeled Samples.
Denis [Den98] initiated the study of
learning from positive-only samples when the underlying distribution D⋆is known. Denis termed
this learning from positive and unlabeled samples (or PU learning) and showed that any class that
is PAC-learnable is also PAC-learnable from positive and unlabeled samples (in the realizable set-
ting). To show this, they reduced (realizable) PAC-learnable from positive and unlabeled samples
to learning in the CPCN model with noise bounded away from 1/2. Since then, a growing number
of works in Applied Machine Learning design efficient heuristics for learning with positive and
unlabeled samples, e.g., [EN08; MV11; PNS15; SS19; BD20; Li+21]; we refer the reader to the sur-

Learning from Positive-Only Samples.
We have already discussed several works on learning
from positive-only samples earlier in this work [Nat87; Shv90; Kiv95; FJK96; NR09; AGR13;
DDS15; KTZ19; CDS20; JKV23; LMZ24]. Here, we mention two additional works: Curiously,
preceding Natarajan [Nat87], the seminal work of Valiant [Val84a] already introduced a variant
of the problem of PAC learning from positive-only examples with two differences: Valiant (1) re-
quired the learner to have zero false-positives (i.e., have one-sided error) and (2) gave the learner
membership access to the target concept h⋆. Further, since the initial arXiv posting of this work,
Mansouri and Ben-David [MB25] continued the study of learning from positive-only examples:
First, they extend our lower bound in Theorem E.1 by lower bounding the number of unlabeled
samples required using a new concept of “claw numbers.” Second, they also study and prove re-
sults for three additional extensions of positive-only learning with different forms of distribution
shifts. More generally, learning with positive samples also appears in modern settings motivated
by (1) generative models – where one typically observes valid text during training but there is no
canonical distribution over invalid outputs [KM24; KMV25] – and (2) in treatment-effect estima-
tion in observational studies, beyond unconfoundedness, which can be cast as a problem of mean

estimation from positive-only samples [CKMMZ25]. The idea itself dates back to Gold’s seminal
model of identification in the limit [Gol67; Ang79], where the learner is presented with positive
examples but no negative examples.
Reliable Learning.
A line of works studies reliable learning [KKM12; KT14; GKKT17; DJ19;
GKKM20; KK21; DRZ24], a model introduced by Kalai, Kanade, and Mansour [KKM12]. This
model captures scenarios where one type of error, e.g., false positives, is costlier than another
type, e.g., false negatives. Some works [KKM12; KT14] on fully reliable learning (which requires
the learner to make almost no errors but allows it to abstain) design constrained L1-Regression al-
gorithms, that abstain on some examples. While there are several differences between our work
and theirs, a key difference is that they have access to both positive and negative samples and,
hence, their constraint is defined with respect to the same distribution as their objective. In our
setting, however, we work without negative samples and our unlabeled samples have a different
distribution than the “true” feature distribution; apart from significant differences in the analy-
sis, this change also prohibits proper learning (see Remark 4.2) and requires us to run multiple
carefully constructed instances of the constrained version of L1-Regression we develop.
List-Decodable Learning.
There is a growing array of list-decodable learning algorithms that
continue to work even when a large fraction of their inputs are corrupted [CSV17; KKK19; CMY20;
RY20; BK21; DKKLT21; DKPPS21; DKKPP22; IK22; DJKS23; DKLPP23]. Our results on the list
decoding model (Definition 2) enable us to utilize these algorithms to enable learning even in
scenarios where a large fraction of the unlabeled samples are corrupted. We refer the reader to
Section A for a more extensive discussion of list-decoding algorithms.

3.1
Smoothed Analysis Model for Positive-Only Learning
To begin with, we have a domain X of covariates or features (often a subset of Rd), either discrete
or continuous, and a distribution D⋆over the domain. We are interested in binary classification
and operate in the realizable setting, where for each feature x there is a corresponding label y(x) ∈
{0, 1}, that is realized by an unknown Boolean function h⋆: X →{0, 1}. h⋆can be arbitrary, and
the only assumption we make is that there is a known hypothesis class H (which is a collection of
Boolean functions h: X →{0, 1}) that contains h⋆. Of specific interest is the set of samples with a
positive label and, for convenience, we use H⋆to denote it, i.e.,
H⋆:= {x ∈supp(D): h⋆(x) = 1} .

We first introduce PAC learning with positive-only samples (without smoothness).
Problem 1 (PAC learning from Positive-Only Samples). An instance of PAC learning from positive-
only samples is specified by a hypothesis class H ⊆{0, 1}X, a target concept h⋆∈H, and an unknown
distribution D⋆over the instance space X. The learner has access to a stream of positive samples drawn
i.i.d. from P⋆(the truncation of D⋆to H⋆). The goal is to output a hypothesis bh : X →{0, 1} such that
with probability at least 1 −δ over the draw of samples and any randomness in the learning algorithm:
PrX∼D⋆(bh(X) ̸= h⋆(X)) ≤ε .
A hypothesis class H is PAC learnable from positive-only samples if there exists an algorithm and sample
complexity function m(ε, δ) such that for every distribution D⋆and target h⋆∈H, the algorithm achieves
the above guarantee when given m(ε, δ) positive samples.
It is useful to contrast Problem 1 with the standard PAC model. In Problem 1, the learner only
observes positive examples, yet the learner’s performance is still evaluated under the “complete”
distribution D⋆. Since the learner never sees any draws from the negative region, the false positive
component of the error cannot be directly estimated from observed samples. Consequently, ERM
is no longer a meaningful principle: the hypothesis that labels every point as positive is always

potentially high error under D⋆. Hence, learners must take a fundamentally different approach.

In this section, we expand on a discussion of related works over Section 1.7.
Other PAC Learning Models. One important class of problems between realizable and agnostic
learning model the outcomes as yi = h⋆(xi) + ηi, where ηi is a structured noise, e.g., see [AL88;
Dec97; BFKV98; FGKP06; NDRT13]. In this paper, we consider a particular aspect of this struc-
tured noise that has many practical instantiations. In particular, we consider the case where there
is significant asymmetry between positive and negative samples, and the quality of the data is very
poor for one of the two types of samples – say negative samples. (See Section 1.5 for further examples.)
List-Decodable Learning (Extension of Discussion in Section 1.7).
Almost all statistical tasks
become information-theoretically impossible when a large fraction of samples is adversarially cor-
rupted. The simplest example is perhaps Gaussian mean estimation: if an adversary can corrupt
(1 −γ)-fraction of the samples (for γ ∈(0, 1)), then the corrupted samples can contain 1/γ-clusters
that have a large separation, any of which could correspond to the true distribution. To circumvent
these impossibility results, Charikar, Steinhardt, and Valiant [CSV17] proposed a relaxed notion
of learning, termed list-decodable learning, where the learner is allowed to output a list of estimates
one of which must be close to the correct parameter. Since the formulation of this problem, there
has been a growing line of works designing list-decodable algorithms for several fundamental
statistical problems [CSV17; KKK19; CMY20; RY20; BK21; DKKLT21; DKPPS21; DKKPP22; IK22;
DJKS23; DKLPP23]. As we mentioned in Section 1.7, these (and future) works can be combined
with our techniques to learn in the stronger list-decodable learning model.

Additional Preliminaries and the L1-Regression Algorithm
In this section, we present the definitions of concept classes mentioned in the main body and the
L1-Regression algorithm of Kalai, Klivans, Mansour, and Servedio [KKMS08] (see Algorithm 5).
Recall that a halfspace is a Boolean function h: Rd →{0, 1} of the form h(x) = 1{w⊤x + t ≥0}
for some vector w ∈Rd and threshold t ∈R. An intersection of k halfspaces h1, h2, . . . , hk is the
Boolean function formed by taking the logical AND of the halfspaces, i.e., h1(x) · h2(x) · · · · · hk(x).
Functions of halfspaces are a significant generalization of intersections of halfspaces.
Definition 5 (Functions of Halfspaces). The concept class of functions of halfspaces consists of all
Boolean functions of the form b(h1, h2, . . . , hk) where b: {0, 1}k →{0, 1} is an arbitrary boolean function
and h1, h2, . . . , hk are any k halfspaces.
Next, we formally define the class of decision trees of a specific size and/or depth.
Definition 6 (Decision Tree). A decision tree is a rooted binary tree in which each internal node is labeled
with a variable xi (for 1 ≤i ≤d) and has two children. Each leaf is labeled with an output from {0, 1}.
A decision tree computes a Boolean function in the following standard way: Given an input x ∈
{0, 1}d, the value of the function on x is the output in the leaf reached by the path that starts at the
root and goes left or right at each internal node according to whether the variable’s value in x is 0
or 1, respectively. The size of a decision tree is the number of leaves of the tree. The depth of a node
in a decision tree is the number of edges in the path from the root to the node. The depth of the tree
58


## Page 61


is the maximum depth of one of its leaves. Observe that a decision tree of depth d has size at most
2d. This naturally defines the following concept classes.
• The family of decision trees of depth at most t over d variables;
• The family of decision trees of size at most s over d variables.
When the number of variables d is clear from context or not important, we simply express the
above classes as decision trees of depth t and of size s respectively. Further, by taking the intersec-
tions of these classes, one can also define the concept class of decision trees of size at most s and
depth at most t.
Decision trees of halfspaces are a generalization of decision trees, where each node i is labeled
with a halfspace hij instead of a single variable xij.
Definition 7 (Decision Tree of Halfspaces). A decision tree of halfspaces is a rooted binary tree in which
each internal node i is labeled with a halfspace hij (for 1 ≤i ≤d) and has two children. Each leaf is labeled
with an output from {0, 1}.
The computation, on a decision tree of halfspaces. proceeds in a similar fashion as above: the only
difference is that one goes to the left child from node i if hij(x) = 1 and goes to the right child,
otherwise, when hij(x) = 0. This naturally gives rise to the following concept class
• The family of decision trees of halfspaces of size at most s and depth at most t over d variables.
Observe that the above class can also be written as a sum of at most s intersections of ℓhalfspaces.
