Multi-Horizon Echo State Network Prediction of
Intraday Stock Returns
Giovanni Ballarin1, Jacopo Capra2, and Petros Dellaportas3
April 29, 2025
Stock return prediction is a problem that has received much attention in the finance
literature. In recent years, sophisticated machine learning methods have been shown to
perform significantly better than “classical” prediction techniques. One downside of these
approaches is that they are often very expensive to implement, for both training and
inference, because of their high complexity. We propose a return prediction framework
for intraday returns at multiple horizons based on Echo State Network (ESN) models,
wherein a large portion of parameters are drawn at random and never trained. We show
that this approach enjoys the benefits of recurrent neural network expressivity, inherently
efficient implementation, and strong forecasting performance.
Keywords: High-frequency data, reservoir computing, signal construction

Introduction
The prediction of time series is a topic of extreme relevance in virtually all economic and
financial settings. Therefore, both academic and private researchers aim to devise more effec-
tive, reliable and, in the now-common cases where data is extremely abundant, parsimonious
methods and models to forecast. Specifically, in the field of financial portfolio management,
the prediction of stock features (such as price, return, volatility, and trading volume) is a key
component of many trading strategies. Traditionally, it is common to rely on linear models
for this purpose, as they are straightforward to implement and analyze. In recent years, great
strides in the development of machine learning (ML) and statistical learning methods have
much expanded the universe of possible forecasting approaches. Therefore, a great variety of
techniques have been proposed to improve the prediction of stock market data (Kumbure et al.,
2022). Many efforts involving neural networks (NNs) date back to at least the 1990s (Zhang
et al., 1998), see e.g. Nicholas Refenes et al. (1994), Ghosn and Bengio (1996), Saad et al.
(1998). In recent years, there have been works studying financial machine learning forecasting
from both empirical (Sirignano and Cont, 2019, Aleti et al., 2025) and theoretical perspectives.
Importantly, from the latter point of view, works such as Didisheim et al. (2023) and Kelly et al.
(2024) have formally argued why and how more complex models can much improve prediction,
the so-called “virtue of complexity” (Kelly et al., 2024).
In the systematic trading industry, it has also become more common to rely on multi-period
portfolio construction approaches to solve for optimal buy and sell decisions of financial assets
(Meucci and Nicolosi, 2016). This is especially true in a mid-frequency trading context, where an
investment manager combines diverse signals, each of which may be better suited at predicting
either shorter or longer horizon returns.1
Multi-period portfolio optimization is a natural
extension of the mean-variance optimization (MVO) model developed by Markowitz (1952).
The traditional MVO approach is based on a single-period model. The goal of the multi-period
mean-variance optimization is, in contrast, to dynamically allocate capital to financial assets
by considering important intertemporal aspects such as rebalancing costs, trading impacts,
time-varying constraints, price trends, and more. While the focus of this work is primarily on
forecasting, multi-period portfolio optimization more generally is a well-studied topic in finance
(Blake et al., 2003, Grinold, 2007, Qian et al., 2007, Grinold, 2010, Sneddon, 2008, Gârleanu
and Pedersen, 2013), and we contribute to this literature by showing how predictability changes
at different intraday horizons of a large cross-section of stocks.
This paper aims to follow both veins of the financial time series literature by proposing
an efficient nonlinear methodology to forecast returns at intraday frequencies and multiple
horizons.
We consider a special class of recurrent-type neural networks (RNNs) which are
trained so that the internal state parameters are fixed at their random initialization values.
To be precise, we be working with so-called Echo State Network (ESN) models (Jaeger, 2001,

Lukoševičius and Jaeger, 2009). When using a linear readout layer, this class of models can be
fitted to data by means of regularized linear regression, a much simpler learning algorithm in
comparison to the computationally intensive procedures used by (deep) neural networks. Our
methodology builds on the recent advances in using ESNs for macroeconomic forecasting at
different horizons and frequencies (Ballarin et al., 2024). The choice of applying the Echo State
Networks to an intraday systematic trading context is also motivated by the empirical success
ESNs have had in prediction tasks attempted in other fields (Tanaka et al., 2019). It is also
motivated by three properties ESNs have and are useful in this framework: (i) the expressivity
of Echo State Network models, which are universal in terms of approximating nonlinear time
series models; (ii) ESN adaptability to multi-horizon forecasting by virtue of a nonlinear state
formulation; (iii) the computational advantage of ESNs, which allows solving a high-dimensional
data problem in a short processing time.
We consider two linear baseline models: A linear regression over a vector of signals based on
past market and stock performance, and a regularized linear model that uses ridge regression
over the same set of signals.
Our proposed novel ESN approach is predicated instead on
first constructing a recurrent state equation that nonlinearly combines signals over time. The
target returns are then predicted with a linear function of the ESN states, again using ridge-
regularized least squares. For our regularized models, we employ a within-day rolling window
cross-validation strategy to select hyperparameters.
This setup allows us to systematically
evaluate the performance gains of including regularization in the linear model, and additionally
those obtained as a result of the ESN model.
In our application, we target 500 US stock price returns across 5 different intraday horizons
at 10-minute intervals each day within the trading session. To measure forecasting effectiveness,
we consider both mean squared forecasting error (MFSE) and the R2 of predicted returns. Our
results show that our ESN-based forecasting strategy for the cross-section of returns dominates
the benchmarks at all intraday horizons, with only the case of end-of-day (close) returns showing
rather modest improvements. Notably, we achieve an up to 0.87% MSFE reduction that is
also remarkably robust to the inherent model sampling randomness of ESN models.
Per-
horizon Diebold-Mariano and model confidence set (MCS) tests confirm that these gains are
all statistically significant. Finally, our full forecasting pipeline can be run over one calendar
year of data in a handful of minutes, highlighting the numerical efficiency of our ESN proposal.

Related Literature
Classical mathematical finance suggests that raw stock returns are unpredictable given past
information.
Yet, recent empirical research has shown this not to be entirely the case at
intraday frequencies.
For example, Aït-Sahalia et al. (2022) provide extensive evidence of
predictability at millisecond scales. We tackle a more moderate setting, with data sampled
at most at 10-minute intervals, and find that at this coarser scale, too, there seems to be
non-negligible predictability.2
There are many works in past two decades that implement various machine learning tech-
niques in a financial setting. Olson and Mossman (2003) and Kwon and Moon (2007) both
employ modern neural network approaches to stock forecasting. Yu and Yan (2020) more re-
cently employed Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) for the same task, finding that the flexibility
afforded by deep models improves performance. Borovkova and Tsiamas (2019) use a Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent architecture to classify stocks in high-frequency. Tölö
(2020) exploits RNN-type networks in order to predict systemic crises in the financial sector.
With a view towards the structural changes due to the COVID-19 pandemic, Chandra and He
(2021) resort to a Bayesian NN approach to forecast prices of stocks from exchanges in the
United States, Germany, China, and Australia, while Abedin et al. (2021) use a more standard
deep learning approach to perform exchange rate prediction. Further, Wang and Chen (2024)
propose a factor-based Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) to tackle both prediction and
portfolio investment problems. For the purposes of MVO specifically, Kim et al. (2024) also
applied GANs: Their idea is to perform anomaly detection, and augment the portfolio opti-
mization problem with said information. Vuletić et al. (2024), using a novel “Fin-GAN” model,
have shown that GANs trained with an economics-driven loss can be effective in achieving
higher Sharpe ratios compared to both LSTM and ARIMA models. Lastly, it is important to
mention that ML models, such as GANs and Variational Autoencoders, have also been exten-
sively studied for the purpose of synthetic financial data generation, see e.g. Kim et al. (2023),
Masi et al. (2023), Nagy et al. (2023), Acciaio et al. (2024), Kwon and Lee (2024), and Cetingoz
and Lehalle (2025) for a theoretical critique of this line of research.
Complex neural network models are, by nature, difficult to implement. Echo State Networks
are a recent development in the field of machine learning models that aim to tackle this issue,
while still enjoying the broad effectiveness of neural networks. ESNs have been applied to a
wide variety of contexts (Sun et al., 2024). For example, ESNs have been successfully employed
to predict water levels (Coulibaly, 2010), electricity loads (Deihimi and Showkati, 2012), and
power generation by renewable sources (Hu et al., 2020). Variations involving deep architec-
tures have also been devised (Kim and King, 2020). In the context of economic forecasting,
Ballarin et al. (2024) constructed a class of ESN models that can incorporate data sampled at

Data and Setup
Intraday stock data is sourced from AlgoSeek, a market-leading platform providing high-quality
high-frequency trading data.3 The data provider provides access to 1-minute resolution OHLC
(open, high, low, close price) bars built from the reported Trades of the SIP (consolidated)
data feed, which includes all U.S. Exchanges and FINRA from January 2007 to October 2020.
AlgoSeek bars cover the entire trading day:
• Pre-Market: 4:00:00 AM to 9:29:59 AM.
• Market: 09:30:00 AM to 4:00:00 PM.
• Extended hours: 4:00:01 PM to 8:00:00 PM.
Throughout our analysis, we employ Eastern Standard Time (EST) data time-stamps, al-
though actual time and date may be different for each exchange due to geographical location.
Due to the large volume of data associated with 1-minute frequency, we downsample obser-
vations to 10-minute resolution in our empirical exercises. For an extended description of the
data and our preprocessing, we refer the reader to Appendix A.
3.1
Trading Setting
The design of the forecasting experiment reproduces the setup of an intraday trading book,
which rebalances its position only on an intraday basis and does not keep open the positions
overnight. Accordingly, we assume that a portfolio rebalance takes place every 10 minutes,
from 9:30 AM to 3:50 PM. Until 3:50 PM, we run individual stock return predictions every 10
minutes. It is important to note that all our predictions are done over future overlapping return
horizons. For example, at 9:30 AM, the 10-minute prediction horizon goes from the 9:31 AM
price bar to the 9:41 AM price bar. On the other hand, the 30-minute prediction horizon goes
from the 9:31 AM price bar to the 10.01 AM price bar.
We target the following prediction horizons:

Echo State Networks
The idea that random parameters may work well in complex nonlinear models such as arti-
ficial neural networks goes back a long way, see e.g. Lowe and Broomhead (1988), Schmidt
et al. (1992). Different research fields have expanded on this concept: Huang et al. (2006) pro-
posed the “extreme learning machine” form for shallow NNs with randomly sampled internal
weights;7 Rahimi and Recht (2008a,b) developed the “random kitchen sinks” approach (i.e.
collections of functions with random parameters) in the setting of reproducing kernel Hilbert
spaces (RKHS). More recent work on determining the broad success of deep neural networks
has looked into the “lottery ticket” hypothesis (Frankle and Carbin, 2018, Malach et al., 2020,
Ma et al., 2021), which informally states that in large NNs there is a high chance that a small
subnetwork performs almost as well as its much larger super-network – even at initialization,
before coefficients are trained (Sreenivasan et al., 2022). Works such as Zhao et al. (2022)
and Bolager et al. (2023) go further and design special sampling schemes that try to optimize
the initial weight sampling strategy, so that negligible-to-zero training is needed. In tandem,
reservoir computing (RC) models have been developed as a (broad) family of methods which

As mentioned in the introduction, an important consideration pertaining RC and RWNN
methods is their computational efficiency. Artificial neural network training often demands
significant, if not extreme, investments related to both physical resources (computer clusters,
hardware, etc.) and energy; these costs have been steadily rising over the past years as models
quickly become more complex (Lohn and Musser, 2022). Since RC and RWNN models do not
require full parameter training, the costs to optimize such models can be orders of magnitude
lower. This also means that they can be effectively deployed in contexts where systematic
re-training (e.g. online learning) is required.

Remark 4.1. Theoretical analysis of ESN models provides insight and justification for their
properties and their performance.
For example, Grigoryeva and Ortega (2018) and Gonon
and Ortega (2020) have shown that ESNs are universal approximators of time filters, meaning
that, under some general conditions, if Yt “ HpZt, Zt´1, . . .q for some mapping H from the
infinite past history of Zt to Yt, then an ESN can approximate H arbitrarily well. Gonon et al.
(2023) obtained explicit bounds for this approximation problem. Previously, in Gonon et al.
(2020), the same authors also constructed bounds on the error committed when an ESN model
is trained and then applied to new data. Such results provide broad theoretical grounding for
our choice of ESNs as nonlinear models to apply to financial time series prediction.

6
Conclusion
In this work, we have presented a novel approach to forecast intraday stock returns at different
horizons by leveraging ESN models. Our proposed method is nonparametric in nature, and is
able to significantly improve prediction performance across different horizons by a significant
margin, with the only exception being end-of-day returns. Our findings suggest that there
is potentially much to be gained by considering efficient, nonlinear forecasting models com-
pared even to regularized linear methods. Lastly, even thought our ESN models inherently
rely on randomly-sampled parameters, we have shown that the prediction gains are robust to
randomness at both short and medium intraday horizons.
We have not discussed model aggregation or ensembles. This means that there is space
to potentially design more sophisticated online model selection schemes that can dynamically
weight multiple ESNs to improve prediction and profitability: We leave this idea for potential
future research. Moreover, we have constrained our ESN output layer to be linear. This is not
a necessity, and it would be interesting to evaluate if including more flexible output layers (e.g.
a shallow network) could further improve performance.
