ABSTRACT
Natural Language Inference (NLI) is a central task in natural language understanding with applications
in fact-checking, question answering, and information retrieval. Despite its importance, current NLI
systems heavily rely on supervised learning with datasets that often contain annotation artifacts and
biases, limiting generalization and real-world applicability. In this work, we apply a reinforcement
learning-based approach using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) learning in NLI, eliminating the need for labeled rationales and enabling this type of training
on more challenging datasets such as ANLI. We fine-tune 7B, 14B, and 32B language models
using parameter-efficient techniques (LoRA and QLoRA), demonstrating strong performance across
standard and adversarial NLI benchmarks. Our 32B AWQ-quantized model surpasses state-of-the-art
results on 7 out of 11 adversarial sets—or on all of them considering our replication—within 22GB
of CUDA memory, showing that robust reasoning can be retained under aggressive quantization. This
work provides a scalable and practical framework for building robust NLI systems without sacrificing
inference quality.
Keywords Natural Language Inference · LLMs · GRPO

Introduction
Natural Language Inference (NLI) is a core task in natural language understanding, where the goal is to determine
whether a given premise entails, contradicts, or is neutral with respect to a hypothesis. NLI has critical applications in
real-world settings such as automated fact-checking, question answering, and information retrieval, where accurate
reasoning over textual content is essential.
Despite its importance, progress in NLI has been mostly reliant on supervised learning approaches, which require
large amounts of labeled data. However, existing NLI datasets are limited in both size and quality. They often exhibit
annotation artifacts and spurious patterns, such as the hypothesis-only bias, that models can exploit without true
reasoning. As a result, these datasets do not provide a reliable foundation for learning robust inference capabilities.
Although much research has focused on improving the quality of the data or mitigating biases during training, only
marginal gains have been achieved and real-world applicability is still far away. We believe that if base transformer
models do not acquire sufficient semantic understanding from pretraining, they will not be able to learn the task through
fine-tuning on small, biased datasets. The complexity of the task exceeds what can be learned from such limited
supervision.
In this work, we apply modern reinforcement learning techniques, specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO), to Chain-of-Thought (CoT) learning for Natural Language Inference using larger language models (7B, 14B,
and 32B parameters) with parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA and QLoRA. Unlike prior approaches that depend
on labeled rationales—such as those built on e-SNLI—this method requires no explanation annotations, enabling
application to more challenging datasets like ANLI.
Our results show that even relatively small base models (7B) already generalize better to adversarial NLI benchmarks
than prior smaller supervised models, which often overfit to artifacts. GRPO training leads to consistent gains across

Background
Natural Language Inference (NLI) is a core task in natural language understanding that involves determining the logical
relationship between a premise and a hypothesis—typically classified as entailment, contradiction, or neutral. Despite
its importance for very relevant and current problems such as automated fact-checking and its versatility for zero-shot
predictions in many tasks, the field of Natural Language Inference has not seen many advances in the last few years,
likely overshadowed by a focus on LLM reasoning on complex tasks like mathematics or coding.

Most work focuses on supervised fine-tuning of Language Models. However, the available labeled data is scarce and
low in quality, and the task is very complex. Although researchers have tried to mitigate this problem in several ways,
these approaches yield only marginal performance improvements. We also find approaches that apply decoder-only
LMs to NLI, but current research efforts around these models focus on more complex reasoning tasks.
In this section, we go over the most relevant work on Natural Language Inference, and also briefly cover the most
relevant reasoning research for our work.
2.1
Natural Language Inference datasets
We begin our discussion by exploring the most relevant data sources for NLI. These datasets have been instrumental in
training and evaluating models, but they are also a source of limitations in how current models learn and generalize.
• SNLI [2] was the first large-scale NLI dataset, with 570K human-written and manually annotated text pairs.
As most labeled datasets in this section, each pair includes a premise and a hypothesis, labeled with one of the
three NLI categories: entailment, neutral or contradiction.
• e-SNLI [3] extends SNLI by adding natural language explanations to each annotated pair. These explanations
were crowd-sourced and provide a rationale for the label assigned to each example. The inclusion of explanatory
text introduces a richer and more interpretable training signal, allowing models not only to predict labels but
also to justify their predictions.
• MultiNLI [4] improves SNLI by offering a broader range of linguistic variation. It contains 433,000 sentence
pairs drawn from ten different genres of written and spoken English, such as fiction, telephone speech, and
government reports. This genre diversity aims to test the generalization ability of NLI models across more
realistic and heterogeneous text sources.
However, despite their scale and utility, these early NLI datasets were found to contain significant annotation artifacts and
biases. Models were able to achieve surprisingly high accuracy even when trained on hypotheses alone, without access
to the premise [5, 6, 7]. These findings suggest that superficial cues in the hypothesis—rather than genuine inferential
reasoning—were often sufficient for correct classification, thereby undermining the intended goal of evaluating deep
language understanding.
To probe deeper aspects of model understanding and mitigate shallow learning patterns, several diagnostic and
adversarial datasets have been proposed. These aim to evaluate whether NLI models rely on genuine reasoning or
exploit superficial cues in the data.
• NLI Diagnostic [8] is a small but carefully constructed set of 550 hand-crafted sentence pairs included in
the GLUE benchmark. Unlike large-scale datasets, this collection is designed to stress-test specific linguistic
phenomena such as commonsense and world knowledge, logical inference, predicate-argument structure,
and lexical semantics. The examples are drawn from multiple domains to enhance coverage and reduce
dataset-specific biases.
• McCoy, Pavlick, and Linzen [9] demonstrated that models trained on MultiNLI tend to rely on simple syntactic
heuristics—such as lexical overlap, subsequence matching, and constituent alignment—to make predictions.
To expose this issue, they created the HANS dataset by generating sentence pairs from templates that
systematically exploit and challenge these heuristics. Models that performed well on MultiNLI demonstrated
poor performance on HANS, especially on non-entailment cases, revealing a lack of robust generalization.
• Counter-NLI [10] addresses the hypothesis-only bias in SNLI by introducing counterfactual reasoning. Each
example is augmented with two human-written, validated counterfactuals: one modifying the premise and
the other the hypothesis. This structure encourages models to pay attention to both sentences in the pair and
reduces over-reliance on spurious lexical cues. Models trained with this data have shown improved robustness
to this common NLI pitfall.
• ANLI [11] was created using the Human-And-Model-in-the-Loop Enabled Training (HAMLET) framework,
which rethinks dataset construction by placing humans in an adversarial role. Instead of relying on automatic
perturbations, human annotators are asked to craft hypotheses specifically designed to fool a trained NLI
model. When successful, they also provide explanations for the model’s errors, offering insight into the
model’s weaknesses. This iterative process was repeated in three rounds, each using a stronger model and a
broader domain of premises. The result is a multi-part dataset of increasing difficulty, effectively exposing the
limitations of current systems and promoting more robust learning.
• Liu et al. [12] introduced a complementary approach with WANLI, blending large language models and
human oversight. Using dataset cartography, the authors first identified challenging examples in MultiNLI

2.2
Supervised methods for Natural Language Inference
In this section, we focus on supervised learning methods. As in many NLP tasks, state-of-the-art solutions rely on
pre-trained transformer models [13]. Given the nature of the NLI task, the most suitable architecture involves applying
an encoder to both the premise and hypothesis together. The common practice is to use encoder-only transformers, such
as BERT [14], RoBERTa [15], or DeBERTa [16]. Alternatively, the task can be reformulated in a text-to-text format
using encoder-decoder models like T5 [17] or T0 [18].
However, as noted earlier, these models often perform poorly when fine-tuned on small, biased datasets—especially
for complex tasks with high model capacity. This has led to increased research interest in improving data quality
and refining training procedures to enhance generalization across different data distributions. Unfortunately, these
approaches lead to marginal improvements, but generalization to out-of-domain and adversarial examples is still very
poor.
2.2.1
Using synthetic data
He et al.[19] introduced GNLI, a dataset of 671K NLI examples spanning 38 domains, generated using large language
models (LLMs) and their text generation capabilities. They observed some gains in the TRUE benchmark[20], which
evaluates factual consistency. To assess GNLI’s effectiveness for NLI, they trained and evaluated T5 models of various
sizes on different combinations of MultiNLI, ANLI, WANLI, and GNLI. Results show that GNLI occasionally improves
cross-dataset generalization, but overall gains are modest. Notably, using GNLI alongside the original data slightly
improves test performance in larger models (3B and 11B), which aligns with expectations.
2.2.2
Dataset debiasing
Liu et al. [21] identified artificial patterns in hypothesis sentences that correlate spuriously with specific labels. They
removed instances containing these patterns from the training data through downsampling. This led to improved
performance on challenging SNLI and MultiNLI examples compared to random downsampling, but overall performance
decreased.
Bras et al. [22] introduced AFLITE, a greedy debiasing method that iteratively filters out the easiest examples for a
trained classifier. Training RoBERTa on the filtered SNLI data yielded substantial gains on the HANS dataset, with
smaller improvements on ANLI and the NLI diagnostics subsets focused on logic and lexical semantics.
Wu et al. [23] proposed generating synthetic data using a fine-tuned LLM, then applying z-filtering to reduce dataset
bias. This method removes examples to ensure that label distributions are uniform with respect to known biasing
features (e.g., unigrams, hypothesis length, or BERT hypothesis-only predictions). Their debiased dataset produced
slight improvements on SNLI and MNLI when training BERT-based models.
2.2.3
Preventing biases during fine-tuning
Liu et al. [24] compiled a range of known biases learned by NLI models from prior work. For each bias, they trained a
dedicated bias-only model, which was then used in model-agnostic debiasing strategies. These included re-weighting
training examples based on bias-model predictions and ensembling logits from both the bias-only and target models.
They also explored data augmentation via word masking and substitution. Their findings show that while such debiasing
methods can slightly mitigate several biases, and no approach mitigates all of them.
Liu et al. [21] also explored adversarial training to reduce hypothesis-only bias. They designed a model that encodes
premise and hypothesis separately, with a final classification head over both. A discriminator was added to predict
the label from the hypothesis representation alone, and the main model was trained to fool this discriminator while
maintaining correct predictions. This adversarial setup, along with other modifications, improved performance on
challenging SNLI and MNLI examples but led to a drop in overall accuracy.
Wang et al. [25] introduced two key methods. First, they applied the information bottleneck principle to balance
model accuracy and complexity. Second, they proposed the Anchored Feature Regularizer, which uses gradient
norms with respect to the input embeddings to identify token importance. Tokens with small gradients are considered

Stacey, Belinkov, and Rei [26] proposed guiding model attention using human-written natural language explanations.
They introduced a loss term that encourages the model’s final-layer [CLS] attention to focus on tokens relevant to these
explanations. This supervision led to better generalization both in- and out-of-distribution. Additionally, they explored
an alternative approach by adding an external attention layer over the transformer’s output, rather than modifying the
internal attention mechanisms.
Kavumba et al.[1] examined whether prompting models for explanations enhances performance on adversarial NLI
tasks. They trained encoder-decoder Transformer models (T5-3B[17] and T0-11B [18]) on the e-SNLI [3] dataset
to predict both the label and the explanation. Unlike Chain-of-Thought methods, which predict the label after the
explanation, their approach requires the model to make the prediction first. Even though the explanation doesn’t
influence the prediction, it provides a new and more complex training signal that can lead the model to understand
the task properly instead of relying on spurious correlations. Their results show that this method reduces reliance on
spurious cues and significantly improves performance on adversarial datasets, setting new state-of-the-art results in
ANLI [11], Counter-NLI [10], and NLI Diagnostic [8]. However, they have not released their code, and our attempts to
replicate their results resulted in much worse performance (see appendix B).
The idea of training with explanations on e-SNLI was previously explored by Narang et al. [27], who trained the
WT5-11B model on several text-to-text tasks with explanations, achieving an impressive 92.3% accuracy on the e-SNLI
test set.
2.3
Non-supervised methods for Natural Language Inference
We find a minority of work on non-supervised methods. For example, He et al. [19] introduced a method that leverages
machine-generated natural language explanations as in-context exemplars to enhance large language model (LLM)
performance on the target instance. By conditioning the LLM on multiple explanation-augmented examples, their
approach enables the model to generalize more effectively to unseen, unlabeled inputs. The authors demonstrate
consistent performance improvements across both open-source and proprietary LLMs, as well as across several
adversarial natural language inference (NLI) benchmarks, highlighting the robustness and generality of their method.
Despite this, overall performance still has great room for improvement.
2.4
Reinforcement learning for reasoning
There has been a great body of research on improving the reasoning capabilities of LLMs, specially by encouraging the
models to generate chains of thought [28] before answering. The use of large-scale training data has been the main
driving force behind reasoning improvements, with pre-training datasets like [29] or curated instruction data [30].
The most relevant for our work is the successful application of large-scale reinforcement learning to improve reasoning
capabilities in complex tasks like mathematical or coding problems. The most prominent example is the DeepSeek-R1
model [31], trained with the GRPO [32] algorithm. However, to the best of our knowledge, these new state-of-the-art
techniques have not yet been explored in the particular case of NLI.
3
Method
Similar to prior work, we formulate the NLI task as a text-to-text problem. In particular, we prompt the model to
produce an explanation or rationale that conditions the final prediction. Since we train with reinforcement learning, we
do not require manually produced explanations, but use those sampled online from the model. This allows us to use
more difficult datasets, like ANLI, for training. What is more, our training objective includes a KL-divergence term that
prevents the model from diverging too much from its initial state. This reduces the risk of bypassing the explanation
and predicting based on the premise-hypothesis pair, mitigating the reliance on spurious correlations. The following
subsections explain our training process in more detail.

3.3
Training data selection
Given that explanations are sampled from the LLM and we are not restricted to datasets with manually produced
explanations such as e-SNLI, we select the ANLI R3 training set, which is one of the most difficult ones in the literature.
We perform an additional filtering step by selecting only examples that were missed with a Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
model in an initial sweep. This is done because easy examples that are always predicted correctly produce zero-valued
advantages in eq. (1) and do not yield any update to the parameters, making them a waste of resources. Our filtered
selection is available for download at HuggingFace as pablomiralles22/ANLI-R3-one-missed.
This filtering process could be further refined. For example, we could perform multiple sweeps with the reference
model and pick those examples where the model got both a wrong and a right prediction. However, we do not focus too

Experiments
We thoroughly study the effect of training Large Language Models with the methodology in section 3 for Natural
Language Inference. In particular, we wish to answer the following research questions:
RQ1) Is training with GRPO effective for improving performance? Are the performance variations consistent across
model sizes?
RQ2) Do these performance gains generalize to other datasets?
RQ3) Does performance (both in and out of domain) improve as the number of training parameters increases? As
we restrict ourselves to the use of LoRA [34] or QLoRA [35] due to limited resources, we study the effect of
increasing the rank of the update matrices.
RQ4) What effect does 4bit AWQ quantization [36] have on model performance, both before and after GRPO training
with LoRA?
RQ5) What does the model learn from training with this methodology?

Evaluation dataset selection.
To evaluate the models, we use a suite of established NLI benchmarks that collectively
test for generalization, robustness to dataset artifacts, and linguistic competence. SNLI and MultiNLI provide large-
scale, diverse training and evaluation scenarios, while HANS and NLI Diagnostic target specific model heuristics
and linguistic phenomena. Please note that HANS contains binary labels for entailment and non-entailment, and
model predictions are transform by mapping neutral and contradiction predictions to non-entailment ones. Finally,
Counter-NLI is helpful in assessing resilience to the hypothesis-only bias. Including these datasets ensures that our
evaluation captures both in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance, as well as sensitivity to known pitfalls in
NLI modeling.

A more effective way to evaluate Natural Language Inference (NLI) performance is to jointly consider both model size
and accuracy across a standardized and diverse set of benchmark datasets. To facilitate this comparison, we present a
figure (fig. 1) showcasing the performance of our best GRPO-trained models alongside their respective base models. The
results clearly demonstrate that GRPO training shifts the Pareto frontier, yielding models that achieve higher accuracy
for a given size. Although these models are relatively large, applying quantization techniques significantly reduces
their memory footprint, enabling inference on a single 24GB or 48GB GPU—making them practical for research and
deployment.
Model size is only one dimension of efficiency. Inference cost is equally important and depends on multiple factors,
including the hardware used, the specific model architecture, and the average output length. The latter is particularly
relevant for GRPO-trained models, which are known to generate substantially longer completions during reasoning
tasks—driving up token usage and, therefore, cost. However, as discussed in section 5.6, our GRPO models do
not exhibit this behavior. Their completion lengths remain comparable to those of the base models. Despite this,
inference cost remains difficult to standardize, as it varies significantly with hardware availability and the distribution of
completion lengths across tasks.
Another major challenge in fairly comparing model performance arises from the potential for data leakage in large-scale
pretraining datasets. When models are trained on massive, opaque corpora, it becomes increasingly difficult to ensure
that evaluation data has not been seen during pretraining—directly or indirectly (see e.g. [38]). This undermines the
validity of performance comparisons, as improvements may reflect memorization rather than genuine generalization.
Given these concerns, the most reliable comparisons are those made between models that share the same base—ensuring
a consistent pretraining foundation and isolating the effect of the GRPO procedure itself.

LoRA rank effect on non-quantized models
There is a clear monotonic trend in average performance as the LoRA
rank increases. Among the configurations tested, LoRA rank 128 achieves the highest average accuracy across datasets.
Improvement gains by increasing the number of trainable parameters is irregular, with two big jumps from the base
model to a LoRA rank of 8, and from a rank of 32 to a rank of 64.
We also observe a general trend of improved performance on standard, non-adversarial benchmarks such as SNLI and
MNLI as the LoRA rank increases. This indicates that higher-rank adaptations may be beneficial for non-adversarial
data, as one would expect given the higher capability to fit the training data.
In contrast, the behavior on adversarial or difficult datasets is less predictable. Performance varies more significantly
across ranks, with no consistent improvement pattern. We do observe that the rank with the least amount of sets with
performance drops is 128, but the drop for the set targeting the constituent heuristic (i.e. assuming that a premise entails
all complete subtrees in its parse tree) is significant.
LoRA rank effect on quantized models
For quantized models we observe an upward trend on average performance
up to a rank of 32, and stability afterwards. In general, performance gains are much greater. This is not surprising,
as information lost from quantization is likely easy to recover by the extra parameters. We also find that the regular
performance trend for the easier datasets SNLI and MultiNLI is not present for AWQ-quantized models.

On training dynamics: loss, KL divergence and completion length
Figure 3 presents the training dynamics—specifically, the reward, completion length, and KL divergence—of a selection
of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct checkpoints trained with LoRA ranks of 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128, using the GRPO algorithm. All
curves are smoothed using an exponential moving average.
The final reward exhibits a slight upward trend as the LoRA rank increases, suggesting that further gains in reward may
be achievable by using even higher ranks. Completion length remains largely unaffected by the training process across
different ranks, showing no significant variation. This might be due to the tweaks introduce in Dr. GRPO [33], or a
lack of sufficient parameters to introduce such a distribution shift. It is also possible that the NLI task does not benefit
from longer chains of thought or retries, as evaluating an answer is the same problem as answering. KL divergence
increases with LoRA rank, consistent with the expectation that a greater number of tunable parameters enables the
model to diverge more from the original policy distribution.
We observe similar trends for rewards in Figure 4, which displays the same training metrics across Qwen2.5-*B-Instruct-

completion length than the 32B model. Of course, larger completions are only desirable if it leads to a sufficient
performance gain, as it increases inference costs. Finally, we also observe a much higher final KL divergence for the
14B model.

6
Conclusions
In this work, we explored the use of reinforcement learning—specifically the Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) algorithm—in conjunction with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for fine-tuning large language models (LLMs)
on natural language inference (NLI) tasks. Our approach enables the training of Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
without relying on human-labeled explanations, addressing a limitation in the scalability of supervised methods for NLI.
We show that GRPO training with LoRA not only improves performance on standard benchmarks such as SNLI and
MultiNLI, but also on adversarial datasets where performance tends to drop in prior work. This effect is especially
notable in quantized models, where LoRA serves as an effective mechanism to recover performance lost due to
aggressive parameter reduction. This results in models that are both computationally efficient and capable of high-
quality inference, making them practical for deployment in memory-constrained environments.
Base models without training already offer strong performance on adversarial datasets, showing stronger generalization
than prior work with trained but smaller models. This highlights the importance of starting from a sufficiently capable
base model. Indeed, if the model has not acquired the proper domain knowledge and logic capabilities from pre-training,
it is very unlikely that it will be able to do so from supervised training on scarce human-generated data, which often
contains artificial correlations. This is specially the case if we want our model to generalize to real-world scenarios.
While the use of large-scale synthetic datasets is a potential alternative, it remains unclear whether such data can
consistently provide the diversity and quality of reasoning signals needed to train smaller models effectively without
introducing similar biases.
Future benchmarking efforts must account for two significant limitations. First, model efficiency is inherently multi-
dimensional and complex, involving factors such as total parameter count, model size, inference latency, and memory
usage. Inference time, in particular, varies based on hardware, average output length, and even implementation details,
making direct comparisons difficult. Second, there are concerns over potential data leakage in large-scale pretraining
corpora, which undermines fair evaluation. When models are trained on massive and largely opaque datasets, it becomes
increasingly difficult to ensure that benchmark test sets have not been seen—directly or indirectly—during pretraining.
Given these challenges, we argue that meaningful comparisons should ideally be restricted to models sharing the
same base architecture and pretraining data, isolating the effects of specific training or fine-tuning methods rather than
conflating them with differences in upstream data exposure or model internals.
