On the Complexity of Identifying Groups without Abelian Normal
Subgroups: Parallel, First Order, and GI-Hardness
Joshua A. Grochow1,2, Dan Johnson3, and Michael Levet3
1Department of Computer Science, University of Colorado Boulder
2Department of Mathematics, University of Colorado Boulder
3Department of Computer Science, College of Charleston

Abstract
In this paper, we exhibit an AC3 isomorphism test for groups without Abelian normal subgroups
(a.k.a.
Fitting-free groups), a class for which isomorphism testing was previously known to be in P
(Babai, Codenotti, and Qiao; ICALP ’12). Here, we leverage the fact that G/PKer(G) can be viewed
as permutation group of degree O(log |G|). As G is given by its multiplication table, we are able to
implement the solution for the corresponding instance of Twisted Code Equivalence in AC3.
In sharp contrast, we show that when our groups are speciﬁed by a generating set of permutations,
isomorphism testing of Fitting-free groups is at least as hard as Graph Isomorphism and Linear Code
Equivalence (the latter being GI-hard and having no known subexponential-time algorithm).
Lastly, we show that any Fitting-free group of order n is identiﬁed by FO formulas (without counting)
using only O(log log n) variables. This is in contrast to the fact that there are inﬁnite families of Abelian
groups that are not identiﬁed by FO formulas with o(log n) variables (Grochow & Levet, FCT ’23).

Introduction
The Group Isomorphism problem (GpI) takes as input two ﬁnite groups G and H, and asks if there
exists an isomorphism ϕ : G →H. When the groups are given by their multiplication (Cayley) tables,
it is known that GpI belongs to NP ∩coAM.
The generator-enumeration strategy has time complexity
nlogp(n)+O(1), where n is the order of the group and p is the smallest prime dividing n. This strategy was
independently discovered by Felsch and Neub¨user [FN70] and Tarjan (see [Mil78]). The parallel complexity
of the generator-enumeration strategy has been gradually improved [LSZ77, Wol94, CTW13, Tan13]. Collins,
Grochow, Levet, and Weiß recently improved the bound for the generator enumeration strategy to
∃log2 n∀log n∃log nDTISP(polylog(n), log(n)),
which can be simulated by depth-4 quasiAC0 circuits of size nO(log4 n) [CGLW24]. Algorithmically, the best
known bound for GpI is n(1/4) log(n)+O(1), due to Rosenbaum [Ros13] and Luks [Luk15] (see [LGR16, Sec.
2.2]). Even the impressive body of work on practical algorithms for this problem in more succinct input
models, led by Eick, Holt, Leedham-Green and O’Brien (e. g., [BEO02, ELGO02, BE99, CH03]) still results
in an nΘ(log n)-time algorithm in the general case (see [Wil19, Page 2]).
In practice, such as working with computer algebra systems, the Cayley model is highly unrealistic.
Instead, the groups are given by generators as permutations or matrices, or as black-boxes. In the setting
of permutation groups, GpI belongs to NP [Luk93].
When the groups are given by generating sets of
matrices, or as black-boxes, GpI belongs to PromiseΣp
2 [BS84]; it remains open as to whether GpI belongs
to NP or coNP in such succinct models. In the past several years, there have been signiﬁcant advances on
algorithms with worst-case guarantees on the serial runtime for special cases of this problem; we refer to
[GQ17, DW22, GL23b] for a survey.
Key motivation for GpI comes from its relationship to the Graph Isomorphism problem (GI). When
the groups are given verbosely by their multiplication tables, GpI is AC0-reducible to GI [ZKT85]. On the
other hand, there is no reduction from GI to GpI computable by AC circuits of depth o(log n/ log log n) and
size npolylog(n) [CTW13]. In light of Babai’s breakthrough result that GI is quasipolynomial-time solvable
[Bab16], GpI in the Cayley model is a key barrier to improving the complexity of GI. There is considerable
evidence suggesting that GI is not NP-complete [Sch88, BH92, IPZ01, Bab16, KST92, AK06]. As verbose
GpI reduces to GI, this evidence also suggests that GpI is not NP-complete.
There is a natural divide-and-conquer strategy for GpI, which proceeds as follows. Given groups G1, G2,
we ﬁrst seek to decompose the groups in terms of normal subgroups Ni ⊴Gi (i ∈[2]) and the corresponding
quotients Gi/Ni.
It is then necessary (but not suﬃcient) to decide if N1 ∼= N2 and G1/N1 ∼= G2/N2.
The solvable radical of a ﬁnite group G is the (unique) largest normal solvable subgroup of G; recall that
solvability can be deﬁned inductively by saying that a group is solvable if it is either Abelian, or if it has
a normal Abelian subgroup N such that G/N is solvable. Thus every ﬁnite group can be decomposed into
its solvable radical N = Rad(G) and the corresponding quotient G/ Rad(G). The latter quotient has no
Abelian normal subgroups; we will refer to the class of such groups as Fitting-free.1 Fitting-free groups are
thus a natural class of groups in a variety of settings, and serve not only as a necessary special case for
group isomorphism testing, but also as a potentially useful stepping stone towards the general case (e. g.,
[CH03, GQ17, LGR16]).
The class of Fitting-free groups has received signiﬁcant attention. In the multiplication (Cayley) table
model, it took a series of two papers [BCGQ11, BCQ12] to establish a polynomial-time isomorphism test for
this family. In the setting of permutation groups, Cannon and Holt [CH03] exhibited an eﬃcient practical
isomorphism test. Fitting-free groups have also been investigated in the context of the Weisfeiler–Leman
(WL) algorithm. Grochow and Levet [GL23a] showed that this family is identiﬁed by the 7-dimensional
2-ary WL algorithm using only 7 rounds. Brachter [Bra23] subsequently showed that this family has 1-
ary WL-dimension O(log log n). Recently, Das and Thakkar [DT24] exhibited a Las Vegas polynomial-time
algorithm to compute a minimum faithful permutation representation for Fitting-free groups, when they are
given succinctly by generating sequences of permutations.

Main Results. In this paper, we investigate the complexity of identifying Fitting-free groups. Our ﬁrst
main result is the following.
Theorem 1.1. Isomorphism of Fitting-free groups given by their multiplication tables can be decided in AC3.
More speciﬁcally, there is a uniform AC3 algorithm that, given two groups G, H of order n, decides if both
are Fitting-free, and if they are, correctly decides whether they are isomorphic.
There are two key motivations for Theorem 1.1. The ﬁrst comes from GI, whose best known lower-bound
is DET [Tor04], which contains NL and is a subclass of TC1. There is a large body of work spanning almost
40 years on NC algorithms for GI, see [LRS24] for a survey. In contrast, the work on NC algorithms for GpI
is very new, and GpI is strictly easier than GI under AC0-reductions [CTW13]. So it is surprising that we
know more about parallelizing subclasses of GI than subclasses of GpI.
The second comes from the fact that complexity-theoretic lower bounds for GpI remain wide open,
even against depth-2 AC circuits. In contrast, GpI is solvable using depth-4 quasiAC0 circuits [CGLW24].
Isomorphism testing is in P for a number of families of groups, and closing the gap between P and AC0 for
special cases is a key step in trying to characterize the complexity of the general GpI problem. Fitting-free
groups are not merely a special case, but an important structural stepping stone on the way to the general
problem (see e.g., [CH03, LGR16]).
In sharp contrast, in the model of permutation groups we establish the following lower bound.
Theorem 1.2. Graph Isomorphism—and even Linear Code Equivalence—is polynomial-time re-
ducible to isomorphism testing of Fitting-free groups, when the groups are speciﬁed by generating sequences
of permutations.
While the hardness aﬀorded by GI-hardness is tempered by Babai’s result [Bab16] that GI is in QuasiP,
for Linear Code Equivalence, no algorithm with running time 2o(n) is currently known (that it is solvable
in time 2O(n) was established by Babai in [BCGQ11]; recently extended to Monomial CodeEq2 and with
improved randomized and quantum algorithms by Bennett, Bhatia, Biasse, Durisheti, LaBuﬀ, Lavorante, &
Waitkevich [BBB+25], though even those improvements are only in the base of the exponent). GI is known
to reduce to Linear Code Equivalence [PR97, Luk93].3
Remark 1.3. Luks [Luk93] indicates that Graph Isomorphism is polynomial-time reducible to isomor-
phism testing of permutation groups. In particular, Luks exhibits a reduction from computing the normalizer
NG(H) to the Conj-Group problem, the latter of which takes as input G, H1, H2 ≤Sym(Ω) and asks if
there exists g ∈G such that Hg

reduction from Conj-Group to isomorphism testing of permutation groups. To the best of our knowledge,
this reduction from Conj-Group to isomorphism testing of permutation groups never appeared in print.
As a result, we are not aware of any proof in the literature exhibiting a (polynomial-time) reduction from:
• Conj-Group to isomorphism testing of permutation groups, or
• Graph Isomorphism to isomorphism testing of permutation groups.
Both reductions are known to experts. In addition to technically ﬁlling this second gap in the literature,
Theorem 1.2 shows hardness of isomorphism testing of Fitting-free permutation groups; isomorphism of
Fitting-free groups was not previously known to be GI-hard in the permutation group (nor any succinct)
model [Wil24].
Remark 1.4. Cannon and Holt [CH03] exhibited an algorithm for isomorphism testing of Fitting-free groups
speciﬁed by generating sequences of permutations. While their algorithm performs well in practice, they do
not formally analyze the runtime complexity. Theorem 1.2 provides the ﬁrst formal evidence we are aware
of that their algorithm is unlikely to run in polynomial-time in the worst case, and that eﬃcient practical
algorithms may essentially be the best for which we can hope.

We now return to the multiplication table model. Determining hard instances of Group Isomorphism
remains a long-standing open problem. Class-2 p-groups of exponent p have long been considered bottleneck
instances of GpI (see e.g., [BCGQ11, GQ24]). However, a formal reduction from GpI to isomorphism testing
of class 2 p-groups of exponent p (or any other proper sub-class of groups), remains elusive. Existing evidence
[BCGQ11, BCQ12, DW22, CGLW24] indicates that groups whose orders are not large prime powers admit
eﬃcient isomorphism tests.
In the absence of formal reductions, one could ask for a complexity measure C and classes of groups
X, Y such that C(X) < C(Y). If we take our complexity measure C to be the minimum number of variables
needed in a ﬁrst-order formula (without counting) that identiﬁes a group from the given class uniquely up
to isomorphism, then previous work [GL23b] established that there are inﬁnite families of Abelian groups
that are not identiﬁed by FO formulas with o(log |G|) variables. In contrast, we show:
Theorem 1.5. Every Fitting-free group of order n is identiﬁed by a ﬁrst-order formula using O(log log n)
variables.
Brachter [Bra23] previously showed the analogous result for FO + C (ﬁrst-order logic with counting
quantiﬁers); our result strengthens his by removing the need for counting.
Despite isomorphism testing of Abelian groups in the Cayley table model being vastly simpler for serial
or parallel algorithms than isomorphism testing of Fitting-free groups, in more succinct input models the
situation is less clear. However, the fact that Fitting-free groups are more easily identiﬁed by ﬁrst-order logic
without counting than Abelian groups accords well with the idea that when dealing with isomorphism of
groups containing Abelian normal subgroups, one must work with automorphism groups that are subgroups
of matrix groups [LGR16], which clearly “require counting” and are notoriously harder to work with than
the more combinatorial subgroups of permutation groups that arise in testing isomorphism of Fitting-free
groups [BCGQ11, BCQ12] (see also [Luk86, Sec. 4, “Non-Abelian linear algebra”]).
It is interesting to note that isomorphism testing of Abelian groups can be done using only a single
counting gate [CTW13], and the same was later established for other classes of groups [CL24, Col23], raising
the question of a possible complexity hierarchy in the setting of ﬁrst-order logic based on the number
of counting quantiﬁers used (ﬁxing the total number of variables and/or quantiﬁers). We also note that,
despite the close relationship between logics and circuits (cf. [Vol99, GV06]), establishing a circuit complexity
analogue of Theorem 1.5 seems out of reach for current techniques.
Methods. In order to prove Theorem 1.1, we take advantage of the fact that G/PKer(G) can be viewed
as a permutation group of degree O(log |G|), acting on Soc(G), in tandem with a standard suite of NC
algorithms for permutation groups [BLS87]. As our permutation degree is O(log |G|) and G is speciﬁed
by its multiplication table, we can implement solutions for each subroutine of [BLS87] using an AC circuit
of depth poly(log log |G|)) (FOLLO(1)). We bring this suite of FOLLO(1) permutation group algorithms to
bear, in order to carefully analyze Luks’s parallel algorithm for Coset Intersection [Luk99], as well as
to eﬃciently parallelize both the requisite instance of Twisted Code Equivalence and the reduction to
Twisted Code Equivalence [BCQ12, Cod11].
Our analysis is speciﬁc to the algorithm at hand.
If solutions to these problems used polynomial
space and simply-exponential time, just scaling down to permutation groups on domain size O(log |G|)
and using the standard simulation of space by depth would only yield poly(log |G|)-depth circuits of quasi-
polynomial size. In contrast, by carefully analyzing Luks’s Coset Intersection procedure, we are able
to get poly(log log |G|) depth and poly(|G|) size. Similarly, by carefully analyzing the existing Twisted
Code Equivalence algorithm and the corresponding reduction to Twisted Code Equivalence [BCQ12,
Cod11], we obtain O(log3 |G|) depth and size poly(|G|).
Our strategy of leveraging a small (size O(log |G|)) permutation domain in order to get eﬃcient instances
of permutation group algorithms has been a common theme in Group Isomorphism to obtain polynomial-
time isomorphism tests. Crucially, the corresponding small instances of GI-hard problems such as Linear
Code Equivalence, Twisted Code Equivalence, Coset Intersection, and Set Transporter
can be implemented in time poly(|G|)—see, e. g., [BMW17, LW12, IQ19, QST11, BQ12, GQ15, BCGQ11,
BCQ12, GQ17]. By taking advantage of both a small permutation degree and existing parallel permutation
algorithms [BLS87], we are able to improve the complexity-theoretic upper-bounds for isomorphism testing
of Fitting-free groups from P [BCQ12] to AC3.

Further Related Work. The work on NC algorithms for GpI is comparatively nascent compared to that
of GI. Indeed, much of the work involves parallelizing the generator-enumeration strategy (ibid), which
has yielded bounds of L [Tan13] for O(1)-generated groups. Other families of groups known to admit NC
isomorphism tests include Abelian groups [CTW13, GL23b, CGLW24], graphical groups arising from the
CFI graphs [BS20, CL24, Col23], coprime extensions H ⋉N where H is O(1)-generated and N is Abelian
[GL23b] (parallelizing a result from [QST11]), groups of almost all orders [CGLW24] (parallelizing [DW22]),
and Fitting-free groups where the number of non-Abelian simple factors of the socle is O(log n/ log log n)
[GL23b] (parallelizing a result from [BCGQ11]). Our Theorem 1.1 is the parallel analogue of [BCQ12].

2.1
Groups and Codes
Groups.
The only perhaps unfamiliar terminology we use for groups is the term “subcoset” (which is
standard in the relevant literature, but perhaps not standard in group theory textbooks). A subcoset of a
group G is either the empty set or a coset of a subgroup of G.
Codes. Let F be a ﬁeld. GLm(F) denotes the set of m × m invertible matrices over F. A linear code of
length m is a subspace U ≤Fm. A d× m matrix A over F generates the code U if the rows of A span U. Let
U, W be d–dimensional codes of length m over F, generated by d × m matrices A, B, respectively. Then U
and V are equivalent if and only there exists a permutation matrix P ∈GLm(F) and a matrix T ∈GLd(F)
such that B = T AP.
2.2
Computational Complexity
We assume that the reader is familiar with standard complexity classes such as P, NP, L, and NL. For a
standard reference on circuit complexity, see [Vol99]. We consider Boolean circuits using the gates AND,
OR, NOT, and Majority, where Majority(x1, . . . , xn) = 1 if and only if ≥n/2 of the inputs are 1. Otherwise,
Majority(x1, . . . , xn) = 0. In this paper, we will consider DLOGTIME-uniform circuit families (Cn)n∈N. For
this, one encodes the gates of each circuit Cn by bit strings of length O(log n). Then the circuit family
(Cn)n≥0 is called DLOGTIME-uniform if (i) there exists a deterministic Turing machine that computes for
a given gate u ∈{0, 1}∗of Cn (|u| ∈O(log n)) in time O(log n) the type of gate u, where the types are
x1, . . . , xn, NOT, AND, OR, or Majority gates, and (ii) there exists a deterministic Turing machine that
decides for two given gates u, v ∈{0, 1}∗of Cn (|u|, |v| ∈O(log n)) and a binary encoded integer i with
O(log n) many bits in time O(log n) whether u is the i-th input gate for v.
Deﬁnition 2.1. Fix k ≥0. We say that a language L belongs to (uniform) NCk if there exist a (uniform)
family of circuits (Cn)n∈N over the AND, OR, NOT gates such that the following hold:
• The AND and OR gates take exactly 2 inputs. That is, they have fan-in 2.
• Cn has depth O(logk n) and uses (has size) nO(1) gates. Here, the implicit constants in the circuit
depth and size depend only on L.
• x ∈L if and only if C|x|(x) = 1.
The complexity class ACk is deﬁned analogously as NCk, except that the AND, OR gates are permitted to
have unbounded fan-in. That is, a single AND gate can compute an arbitrary conjunction, and a single
OR gate can compute an arbitrary disjunction. The class SACk is deﬁned analogously, in which the OR
gates have unbounded fan-in but the AND gates must have fan-in 2. The complexity class TCk is deﬁned
analogously as ACk, except that our circuits are now permitted Majority gates of unbounded fan-in. We also
allow circuits to compute functions by using multiple output gates.
For every k, the following containments are well-known:
NCk ⊆SACk ⊆ACk ⊆TCk ⊆NCk+1.

NC0 ⊊AC0 ⊊TC0 ⊆NC1 ⊆L ⊆NL ⊆SAC1 ⊆AC1.

The containment TC0 ⊆NC1 (and hence, TCk ⊆NCk+1) follows from the fact that NC1 can
simulate the unbounded fan-in Majority gate.
The complexity class FOLL is the set of languages decidable by uniform AC circuits of depth O(log log n)
and polynomial-size [BKLM01]. We will use a slight generalization of this: we use FOLLO(1) to denote the
class of languages L decidable by uniform AC circuits of depth O((log log n)c) for some c that depends only
on L. It is known that AC0 ⊊FOLLO(1) ⊊AC1, the former by a simple diagonalization argument on top
of Sipser’s result [Sip83], and the latter because the Parity function is in AC1 but not FOLLO(1) (nor any
depth o(log n/ log log n)). FOLLO(1) cannot contain any complexity class that can compute Parity, such as
TC0, NC1, L, NL, or SAC1, and it remains open whether any of these classes contain FOLLO(1).
2.3
Permutation Group Algorithms
We will consider the permutation group model, in which groups are speciﬁed succinctly by a sequence of
permutations from Sm. The computational complexity for the permutation group model will be measured in
terms of m. We recall a standard suite of problems with known NC solutions in the setting of permutation
groups. In our setting, m will be small (m ∈O(polylog(n))) relative to the overall input size n, which will
yield FOLLO(1) bounds for these problems.
Lemma 2.2. Let c ∈Z+, and let m ∈O(logc n). Let G ≤Sm be given by a sequence S of generators. The
following problems are in FOLLO(1) relative to n, that is, they have uniform AC circuits of depth poly(log log n)
and poly(n) size:
(a) Compute the order of G.
(b) Decide whether a given permutation σ is in G; and if so, exhibit a word ω such that σ = ω(S).
(c) Find the kernel of any action of G.
(d) Find the pointwise stabilizer of B ⊆[m].
(e) Find the normal closure of any subset of G.
(f) Compute FOLLO(1)-eﬃcient Schreier generators. In particular, we may take the corresponding chain
of subgroups G = G0 ≥G1 ≥· · · ≥Gr = 1 such that r ∈O(log n) and Gi is the point-stabilizer of the
ﬁrst i points.
(g) Given a list of generators S for G, compute a minimal (non-redundant) of generators S′ for G of size
poly(m).
(h) If H ≤Sm and we are given a bijection f : dom(G) →dom(H), decide whether f is a permutational
isomorphism of G and H.
Proof. Each of these problems is known to be solvable using a circuit of depth O(polylog(m)) and size
O(poly(m)): see [BLS87] for (a)–(g). For (h), we apply (b) to check membership of the f-images of the
generators of G in H, and vice-versa. The FOLLO(1) bound follows from the fact that m ∈O(logc n).

such that Gϕ := {ϕgϕ−1 : g ∈G} = H. Note that a permutational isomorphism induces an isomorphism

isomorphisms G →H is denoted PISO(G, H).

6.1
Additional preliminaries: primitivity and structure trees
We refer to the textbook by Dixon & Mortimer [DM96] for many of the concepts around permutation
groups that we brieﬂy review here. A permutation group G ≤Sm is transitive if the permutation domain
[m] is a single G-orbit.
An equivalence relation ∼on [m] is G-invariant if x ∼y ⇔xg ∼yg for all
x, y ∈[m], g ∈G. The discrete equivalence—in which all elements are pairwise inequivalent—and indiscrete
equivalence—in which all elements are equivalent—are considered trivial. The equivalence classes of any G-
invariant equivalence relation are called blocks of G. The singleton sets and the whole set [m] are considered
trivial blocks; any other blocks, if they exist, are non-trivial. G is primitive if it has no non-trivial blocks,
or equivalently, has no non-trivial G-invariant equivalence relations.
Equivalently, a block for G is a subset ∆⊆[m] such that for every g ∈G, ∆g is either disjoint from ∆
or equal to ∆. A system of imprimitivity for G is a collection of blocks that partition [m] (equivalently, the
equivalence classes of a G-invariant equivalence relation). If G is transitive, every system of imprimitivity
arises as {∆g : g ∈G} for some block ∆. A block is minimal if it is non-trivial and contains no proper subset
that is also a non-trivial block. Two distinct minimal blocks can intersect in at most one point (standard
exercise). An orbital of G is an orbit of G on [m] × [m], the set of ordered pairs of elements of [m]. If we
think of an orbital as a set of edges for a directed graph on vertex set [m], then each connected component
of this graph is a block for G.
The following deﬁnitions tend to arise more in the setting of algorithms for permutation groups; see, e. g.,
[Ser03] for a textbook treatment.

Deﬁnition 6.1. An invariant tree for a transitive group G ≤Sym(Ω) is a rooted tree whose set of leaves
is Ωand to which the G-action extends as tree automorphisms. Note that such an extension is necessarily
unique.
Deﬁnition 6.2. An invariant tree for G ≤Sym(Ω) is a structure tree if every internal node has at least
2 children; and for every internal node u of the tree, the action of StabG(u) on the set of children of u is
primitive.
An invariant tree corresponds to a hierarchy of block systems, and a structure tree corresponds to a
maximal such hierarchy. Each node v is labeled by the set B(v) of leaves in the subtree rooted at v. The kth
layer of a structure tree is the set of all nodes at distance k from the root. The depth of the structure tree
is d if the leaves are on the dth layer. It follows from the deﬁnition of structure tree (and the transitivity of
G) that all leaves must be on the same layer.
Now note that every node corresponds to a block B in a system of imprimitivity, and the blocks corre-
sponding to nodes on the same layer are those in the G-orbit of B. This implies that if u, v are nodes on the
same layer, we have that StabG(u) ∼= StabG(v). In particular, a given layer of the tree is determined by any
one block on that layer (by taking the orbit of that block).

In this section, we will establish the following.
Theorem 9.1. If G is a Fitting-free group of order n, then G is identiﬁed by some FO formula using

the equivalence between FO and count-free Weisfeiler–Leman [IL90, CFI92, GL23b], it suﬃces to establish
the following.
Theorem 9.2 (cf. [Bra23, Lemma 5.4.4]). Let G be a Fitting-free group of order n. Then the count-free
O(log log n)-WL Version I will distinguish G from any non-isomorphic group H, using O(log log n) rounds.

Theorem 9.3 ([BKL89]). There exists an absolute constant C, such that for every ﬁnite simple group S,

Corollary 9.4. Let G be a Fitting-free group of order n, and let H be arbitrary. We can decide isomorphism

Conclusion
We established several results concerning the complexity of identifying Fitting-free groups. In the multiplica-
tion table model, we showed that isomorphism testing between a Fitting-free group G and an arbitrary group
H is solvable in AC3. When the groups are given by generating sequences of permutations, we established a
reduction from Linear Code Equivalence (and hence, Graph Isomorphism) to isomorphism testing of
Fitting-free groups where G = PKer(G).
Finally, we showed that Fitting-free groups are identiﬁed by FO formulas with O(log log |G|) variables. In
contrast, Grochow and Levet [GL23b] previously established that there exists an inﬁnite family of Abelian
groups that are not identiﬁed by FO formulas using o(log n) variables.
Our work leaves open several problems.
Problem 10.1. Let G be a Fitting-free group, and let H be an arbitrary group. Suppose G, H are given
by their multiplication tables. Can isomorphism between G and H be decided in L?
Isomorphism testing of many natural families of graphs, including graphs of bounded treewidth [ES17]
and graphs of bounded genus [EK14], are L-complete. While GpI is not L-complete under AC0-reductions
[CTW13, CGLW24], establishing an upper bound of L for isomorphism testing of Fitting-free groups nonethe-
less remains a natural target.
While Graph Isomorphism is solvable in quasipolynomial time [Bab16], the best bound for Linear
Code Equivalence is (2 + o(1))n [BCGQ11] ([BBB+25] give randomized and quantum algorithms that
are slightly better, but still (c + o(1))n time for some 2 > c > 1). In light of Theorem 1.2, Linear Code
Equivalence remains a barrier to improving the complexity for GpI in the setting of permutation groups,
and solving it in time 2o(n) remains an important challenge.
Problem 10.2. Establish an analogue of Theorem 9.1 for a logic whose distinguishing power is strictly
greater than FO. Furthermore, in the same logic, exhibit an inﬁnite family of solvable groups (Gm)m≥1 and
a constant C, where any formula identifying Gm requires at least |C| log |Gm| variables.
Recall that FO corresponds to count-free WL, and FO + C (ﬁrst-order logic with counting quantiﬁers)
corresponds to the classical counting WL (see e.g., [CFI92, IL90, BS20]). Lower bounds against the classical
counting WL remain an intriguing open question in the setting of groups. It would be of interest to investigate
in the setting of groups, measures of descriptive complexity whose distinguishing power sit between FO and
FO + C.
