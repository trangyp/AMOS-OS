A taxonomy of epistemic injustice in the context of AI and the 
case for generative hermeneutical erasure 
Author 
W. J. T. (Thomas) Mollema (wjt.mollema@gmail.com)  
 
Abstract 
Epistemic injustice related to AI is a growing concern. In relation to machine learning models, epistemic 
injustice can have a diverse range of sources, ranging from epistemic opacity, the discriminatory automation 
of testimonial prejudice, and the distortion of human beliefs via generative AI’s hallucinations to the 
exclusion of the global South in global AI governance, the execution of bureaucratic violence via 
algorithmic systems, and interactions with conversational artificial agents. Based on a proposed general 
taxonomy of epistemic injustice, this paper first sketches a taxonomy of the types of epistemic injustice in 
the context of AI, relying on the work of scholars from the fields of philosophy of technology, political 
philosophy and social epistemology. Secondly, an additional conceptualization on epistemic injustice in the 
context of AI is provided: generative hermeneutical erasure. I argue that this injustice the automation of 
‘epistemicide’, the injustice done to epistemic agents in their capacity for collective sense-making through 
the suppression of difference in epistemology and conceptualization by LLMs. AI systems’ ‘view from nowhere’ 
epistemically inferiorizes non-Western epistemologies and thereby contributes to the erosion of their 
epistemic particulars, gradually contributing to hermeneutical erasure. This work’s relevance lies in proposal 
of a taxonomy that allows epistemic injustices to be mapped in the AI domain and the proposal of a novel 
form of AI-related epistemic injustice. 
 
Keywords 
Epistemic injustice; Artificial Intelligence; Hermeneutical injustice; Taxonomy; Generative hermeneutical 
erasure; Epistemic colonization; Generative AI; Large Language Models (LLMs) 
 
1. Introduction 
Various epistemically unjust effects of artificial intelligence (AI) have been identified in the philosophical 
literature. Whether related to machine learning models’ epistemic opacity [20], algorithmic classification 
systems’ discriminatory automation of testimonial prejudice [31], the distortion of human beliefs via the 
hallucinations of generative AI [5, 22], the inclusion of the global South in global AI governance [17], the 
execution of bureaucratic violence via algorithmic systems [26], the violation of the ownership of intellectual 
property in the development of Large Language Models (LLMs), or located in the interaction with 
conversational artificial agents [8], epistemic injustice related to AI is a growing concern. However, the 
diversity of work on epistemic injustice in AI calls for a unified theorization. How are the different forms 
epistemic injustice related? Do they fit Miranda Fricker’s [11] famous characterization of epistemic injustice 
as discriminatory? To answer such questions, the available characterizations of epistemic injustice need to be 
reviewed and interrelated. The execution of this endeavor proves relevant to a broad audience, ranging

2. A general taxonomy of epistemic injustice 
2.1. Setting up the taxonomy 
Providing a taxonomy of epistemic injustice is risky. Fixating what forms of epistemic injustice are possible 
and how they interrelate could further epistemic injustice itself. Why? Epistemic injustice denotes a group 
of injustices concerned with wrongs pertaining to the epistemological dimension of human interaction [11]: 
φ is an epistemic injustice if φ entails some Y being wronged in its capacity as a knower.  
Not only can this ‘being wronged in its capacity as a knower’ be distributive as well as discriminatory [28], 
and structural as well as incidental [50], but its empirical instances are also fundamentally diverse. When 
writers that belong to the white male intersectional category like me broach the topic, we risk reducing the 
epistemic injustices experienced (e.g., by women, people of color, and cultures in the global South) to an 
abstract conceptual structure. If one vision of how to conceptualize epistemic injustice overshadows or 
disqualifies others, epistemic injustice is enacted rather than documented, and avenues of resistance become 
blocked rather than opened. The concept has a rich history [21], stemming from 20th-century black feminist 
theory [42], and which also plays a role in critical race theory [22]. In the following, Miranda Fricker’s 
characterization of epistemic injustice as encompassing testimonial and hermeneutical injustice will be taken 
as starting point, because of the subsequent impact of her conceptualization on the philosophical field over 
the past decade. As noted by Pohlhaus [42], Wanderer [50] and Medina [28], there are points of 
disagreement about Fricker’s proposal, but the general lines along which she distinguished epistemic 
injustice have met acceptance and widespread adoption.  
The taxonomy provided here is supposed to supply an entry point for conversation and identification, 
and should therefore be minimal, descriptive, and open-ended, rather than all-encompassing, normative,

and final. In a taxonomy, a domain of inquiry is divided into interrelated classes based on shared 
characteristics. Classes have interrelations; the minimal relation being ‘subclass of’ such that ‘A is a subclass 
of B’ means that all instances of A are also instances of B. Furthermore, a top concept is required. 
Unsurprisingly, this is EPISTEMIC INJUSTICE.1  
This taxonomy captures grammatical interrelations between concepts of epistemic injustice, rather specific 
definitions. Any definition presents an epistemic claim to power, which is in and of itself a form of exclusion of 
those who might think, conceptualize, produce and receive knowledge differently. The taxonomy 
developed here is a minimally viable sketch of a normative landscape, for the purpose of starting the 
conversation about the multitude of epistemic injustices in the domain of AI, to make thinking about 
addressing them possible.  
An important question to answer beforehand is on what grounds a concept is added as class to the 
taxonomy. Two considerations guide us here. First, the additions to the taxonomy are expert driven. The 
classes in the taxonomy are sourced from the literature by ethicists, social epistemologists, political 
philosophers and philosophers of AI. Their empirical and/or analytic attempts at articulating social reality 
via epistemic injustice are synthesized and interrelated. Second, a taxonomic addition requires the presence 
of a key differentiator. By this I mean that a concept should be sufficiently differentiable from other concepts in 
the taxonomy. Of course, the taxonomical entries have similarities. Following Ludwig Wittgenstein, we can 
acknowledge that “a complicated net of similarities that overlap and intersect” is what connects instances 
of concepts, and those similarities are “sometimes fundamental [...], sometimes [...] in details” ([63], §66). 
Family resemblance rather than definition makes many concepts usable for us. The key differentiator that is 
described per concept in the taxonomy fixates the resemblances into sufficiently differentiable operational 
intensions. These intentions aren’t perfect, but they needn’t be, because they fulfill the present need of 
articulating epistemic injustices. This makes the taxonomy the site of translation of rigorous philosophical 
research to pragmatic conceptual tooling. 
Let’s specify the three rules we need for taxonomizing. Firstly, each member of the taxonomy will be 
provided with a (i) general description, (ii) for each subclass its manifestations are discussed, and (iii) we give 
a number to each class. Only the top concept is exempted from (iii). Call this the rule of ‘adequate 
description’. It enables a schematic representation of the domain of epistemic injustice. Secondly, the 
concepts will be introduced in a hierarchical order, acknowledging that subclasses of epistemic injustice can 
have subtrees of their own. Call this the rule of ‘hierarchical tree-introduction’. Thirdly, for the purpose of 
making the taxonomy simple, and helpful to non-philosophers, we rule out unnecessary overlaps between 
the subclasses of epistemic injustice. If the classes are to have a common denominator, it will be their parent 
class (i.e., a subclass of epistemic injustice) and not some other factor. Call this the rule of ‘mono-
inheritance’.2 Making use of key differentiators that articulate operational intensions is helpful for this.

 INTERPERSONAL: The injustice takes place between two knowers (epistemic agents), i.e., not

 SYSTEMIC: The injustice takes place between two classes of epistemic agents, i.e., not

 INCIDENTAL: The injustice is an exceptional occurrence or isolated incident, i.e., not structural; 
 STRUCTURAL: The injustice pertains to the structure of society ((organized) patterns of behavior 
such as oppression [53]) and occurs repetitively, i.e. not incidental. 
Note that on these operational definitions, the manifestations can be combined in instances of epistemic 
injustice, where an instance can be either interpersonal or systemic, while being either incidental or 
structural. Within the boundary of a concept, saliently different instances can be grouped together via a 
manifestation. Across concepts, salient similarities can be articulated via sharing a manifestation.  
It could be argued that the interpersonal/systemic axis is fundamentally tied up with the structural 
manifestation, because the relationship between two knowers is always a social one that imports related 
structurally unjust aspects of society. It can easily be seen (especially after reading section 2.2. below) that 
this holds for most cases. However, interpersonal incidental epistemic injustice is a counterexample. In that 
case, some Y is accidentally wronged in its capacity as a knower in a way that is unrelated to a form of structural 
prejudice or oppression. Firstly, this cannot be ruled out a priori; secondly, we can easily imagine the

2.2. The taxonomy of epistemic injustice 
EPISTEMIC INJUSTICE. We set the general form of epistemic injustice to ‘φ entails some Y being wronged 
in its capacity as a knower’. Departing from there, different forms of wronging knowers’ capacities can be 
identified. Following Pohlhaus [42], we can distinguish three discriminatory lenses and one distributive lens 
for uncovering epistemic injustice’s origins. In its discriminatory guise its synonym is ‘epistemic exclusion’. 
The first lens is racial. Racist divides in society can give rise to epistemic injustices, and by way of it effectively 
implementing the racial contract [22] that grants full personhood to a dominant group (e.g., white Euro-
Americans) and relegates other groups to the class of subpersons (e.g., people of color). The designation of 
subperson also has an epistemological dimension, which is where the root of epistemic injustice lies: 
devaluation of epistemic practices, prejudice against the value of subpersons qua knowers, and more. This 
doesn’t yield the identification of a subclass, because it points us not to a specific type of epistemic injustice, 
but rather to the fact that racial oppression can be a ‘background condition’ of all kinds of epistemic 
injustices. 
The second lens is that of exclusion and ‘fractures of epistemic trust’ reducible to sociopolitical factors 
(think of sexism and ableism) that relate the epistemic interdependence of different communities and social 
groups. The third lens makes visible forms of epistemic injustice that belong purely to epistemic systems.4 
Both lenses lead to the identification of several subclasses of epistemic injustice. 
First, (1) PARTICIPATORY INJUSTICE. As the work of Christopher Hookway [19] attests, participatory 
injustice “refers to the denial of a listener’s participation as an epistemic agent” [3]. It is the barring of a 
knower from an epistemic practice, which could be incidental or systemic (‘I am not recognized as epistemic 
participant by a particular other’ vs. ‘I am not recognized as epistemic participant by virtue of systemic 
prejudice against the intersection of knowers I am designated to belong to’). Heide Grasswick’s [15] 
example is scientific inquiry, where particular knowers’ contributions are, incidentally or systemically, 
excluded. On the other hand, Báez-Vizcaíno [3] stresses its value for discerning participatory injustice in an 
educational context, regarding the dynamics between different social groups in classrooms for example. 
Furthermore, as the racial and sociopolitical lenses attest to, this can happen both interpersonally as well as 
structurally.  
Secondly, there is (2) CONTRIBUTORY INJUSTICE. As Pohlhaus ([42], p. 20) describes it:

Contributory injustices occur when knowers utilize epistemic resources that are inapt for 
understanding the potential contributions of particular knowers to our collective 
knowledge pool and thereby engage in a form of willful hermeneutical ignorance that 
refuses to employ more apt epistemic resources for receiving and appropriately responding 
to those contributions. 
The situation that unfolds in contributory injustice is that of a person or group’s inertia with respect to 
epistemic resources. Sometimes it might be necessary to adopt new or different concepts to understand 
someone else’s testimony or knowledge contribution. In other words, extending one’s horizon or shifting 
worldviews. The injustice consists precisely in the willful rejection of this move, the denial of seeing it as 
possibility, which is most easily conceived of as incidental and interpersonal, pertaining to particular 
preferences for staying ignorant. But this could also be recombined with systemic or structural sociopolitical 
factors to point towards ignorance with respect to the epistemic resources to recognize knowledge 
contributions about sociopolitical realities – say the experiences of marginalized groups, or the existence of 
institutional racism. 
Pohlhaus’ fourth lens is distributive and concerns the roles of epistemic labor, agency, and knowledge 
production. This leads to the subclasses of epistemic labor invalidation, epistemic domination, and 
epistemic exploitation.  
(3) EPISTEMIC LABOR INVALIDATION is related to the value an agent’s epistemic activity is given in an 
epistemic system. The activity is devalued, and the extent of the required epistemic labor is hidden from 
view. The example Pohlhaus gives, is of scholars from the global South that are expected to be “fountain[s] 
of knowledge” considering the cultures they have inherited, whereas this both (a) downplays the epistemic 
labor needed for acquiring the knowledge and (b) unfairly expects this by misrepresenting an ‘epistemic 
outsider’ as exemplary for another background [42]. An example related to AI (but which is not caused by 
algorithms) that highlights another facet of the concept is the ‘ghost work’ or ‘click work’ outsourced to 
the global South for data labeling, human feedback on AI outputs and data production [59, 60, 34]. These 
activities contain forms of epistemic labor – subsuming images under concepts; correcting classifications; 
giving feedback about truth and clarity, etc. – which are essential to producing state-of-the-art AI systems, 
but are erased from the final product and not given due credit. 
(4) EPISTEMIC DOMINATION. Keith Harris [18] defines epistemic domination as the “asymmetrical 
relation whereby one party has the capacity to control what evidence is available to another”, which yields 
(unconscious) epistemic control over processes like knowledge acquisition, belief justification and 
understanding. Clearly, this fits the tradition of Republican domination, where freedom is defined as non-
domination and domination is seen as the capacity for arbitrary placement of constraints one another party’s 
choice set [41]. For Harris, a prominent – but not necessarily unjust – example is the parent-child 
relationship. Parents exhibit a large degree of control over the epistemic activities of the child, by, e.g., 
controlling what counts as evidence, showing what counts as valid source of information. As Harris argues, 
if the epistemically dominated party benefits from this controlling oversight, epistemic domination 
shouldn’t be regarded as morally detrimental and there are ‘grades’ to this notion.  
But forcing non-testimonial, fabricated or manipulated forms of evidence onto an interlocutor is 
harmful, because this misleads the interlocutor and makes the exchange inequitable. This applies to unjust

information that is only accessible to them or reject sources of evidence it’s in mismatch with their 
worldviews. Furthermore, because epistemic domination can occur without the speaker’s awareness, it 
intersects with an actor’s malignancy: intentional deceit and inciting misorientation are examples of this. 
Another good example that illustrates epistemic domination (and contributory injustice as well) is academia. 
Notably in academia (especially in the context of Western knowledge production) there is a strong presence 
of epistemic rules and gatekeepers and arguably the acceptability of knowledge claims depends on the 
epistemic methodologies of both audiences and critics. Gatekeepers and established methodologies (e.g., 
persons with established prestige and positions, select journals and mainstream schools of research) exert 
epistemic domination over marginal perspectives and institutionally constrain the extent to which they can 
contribute to practices of knowledge production. On the other hand, related epistemic rules and standards 
constrain what contributions are eligible (see [55]).5 
Consider also epistemic domination’s subclass: (4.1) EPISTEMIC EXPLOITATION. Following Nicholas 
Vrousalis [48, 49] I conceive of exploitation as a dominating relationship in which a party X takes unfair 
advantage of a party Y’s relational vulnerability to make some form of profit. Thereby exploitation becomes 
a subclass of domination, and, mutatis mutandis, epistemic exploitation a subclass of epistemic domination. 
For Pohlhaus ([42], p. 22), epistemic exploitation is the case when “when epistemic labor is coercively 
extracted from epistemic agents in the service of others.” She points to cases where social demands bring 
forth subordinating testimonies that damage the speaker but benefit the one exerting social pressure. For 
example, individuals from marginalized social groups are often tokenized and epistemic labor is extracted 
from them for the ‘education’ of ignorant social groups with respect to sexism, racism, etc. Furthermore, 
both credibility deficits and credibility surpluses can be at the root of epistemic exploitation, as the former 
can be taken advantage of, while the latter can overabundantly be called upon for the extraction of 
testimony. This fits the idea that, within the epistemic interaction, one party takes advantage of another 
party’s epistemic vulnerability. Finally, where Pohlhaus already forecloses epistemic exploitation towards 
labor extraction, I endorse a broader conception, namely as extracting value from epistemic vulnerabilities 
per se.6  
Miranda Fricker famously distinguished two kinds of epistemic injustice. First, testimonial injustice, where 
someone “is wronged specifically in her capacity as a knower” ([11], p. 20) because of an ‘attribution of 
insufficient credibility’ due to a prejudice held by an interlocutor ([50], p. 28). And second, hermeneutical 
injustice: “the injustice of having some significant area of one’s social experience obscured from collective 
understanding owing to hermeneutical marginalization” ([11], p. 158). Hermeneutical marginalization is a 
form of oppression [53] that has a distinctly epistemic character [57]. 
(5) TESTIMONIAL INJUSTICE. Testimonial injustice is the case when a speaker in a discourse faces a 
credibility deficit. The testimonial injustices mainly of interest to the political philosopher are systematic ones: 
structural patterns of prejudice that lead the credibility of a social group (e.g., women, immigrants, elderly

on the norms of pronouncing the truth as a speaker, and of trusting the speaker as a hearer. Any interaction

varieties of testimonial injustice, Jeremy Wanderer [50] distinguishes between transactional testimonial 
injustice and structural testimonial injustice. Starting with the transactional variant, which is another name 
for the interpersonal manifestation, it is about interactions between parties that are engaged in a testimonial 
transaction. The exchange is that of information between speaker and hearer, the just order of which 
depends on conditions like truthfulness, recognition, trust, respect, etc. Transactional testimonial injustice 
thus is the interpersonal “breach of the order of justice established between the parties to a testimonial 
transaction” such that it results in the “maltreatment” of people, and when this is based on more widespread 
prejudices or forms of oppression, it takes on a systemic character ([50], pp. 31-33). A further variant that 
Pohlhaus ([42], p. 20) reads in the work of Dotson is ‘testimonial smothering’, which is the case if one’s 
audience is unwilling or unable to distill the “appropriate uptake” from one’s testimony, because of a lack 
of epistemic resources. 
Contrary to the incidental and interpersonal transactional testimonial variant, the structural variant is 
concerned with injustices due to the “structural aspects of the social practice” ([50], p. 34), i.e., “the 
institutional structures that facilitate participation in the practice of pooling information” ([50], p. 35), can 
be rooted in injustices pertaining to the organization of society (racism, sexism, etc.).7 
Turning to (6) HERMENEUTICAL INJUSTICE, recall that it has to do with the inability to understand one’s 
experiences via dominant epistemic and conceptual resources or with the exclusion of one’s social 
experience from the collective territory of sensemaking. Like testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice 
can manifest structurally. Its ‘background condition’, hermeneutical marginalization, precedes the ‘eruption’ 
of hermeneutical injustice itself. A background condition, so I take Fricker to mean, is a set of social 
inequalities, inequities or restraints of freedom that are not themselves constitutive of an epistemic injustice, 
but which make possible epistemic injustices (paradigmatically sexism, racism and ageism). The ‘eruption’ 
takes place when someone is “handicapped” by their being marginalized in terms of their experiences. 
Hermeneutical injustice can thus occur in societies where some social groups’ experiences are structurally 
marginalized, where an inequality exists in how their experiences are made intelligible to society, or when 
the means the society presents as dominant are inadequate for rendering sensible what one lives through.

Finally, Martin Miragoli [33] proposed two other forms of epistemic injustice stand out and his examples 
are related to AI. First, (7) ZETETIC INJUSTICE relates to epistemic agents’ capacity to inquire (‘zetetic’ 
means ‘proceeding by inquiry or investigation’). Zetetic injustice is the epistemic injustice of disabling one’s 
ability for inquiry and the conduct of meaningful research ([33], pp. 12-13): one is harmed in one’s abilities 
as knowledge seeker. When zetetically unjust, a phenomenon undermines the capacity for inquiry via 
epistemic conformism. Epistemic conformism is “the tendency to only treat as epistemically relevant 
information that is statistically dominant because it is statistically dominant” ([33], p. 6). For example, an 
avenue for inquiry can be thwarted by the reduction to a mismatch with statistically dominant information. 
Informational prejudice leads to a complacency in, and unwarranted defense of, epistemic conformism, 
drowning non-conformist lines of inquiry. 
Miragoli ([33], pp. 15-16) also develops (5.1) TESTIMONIAL SPURNING. For testimonial spurning – or 
the treatment with contempt of an epistemic contribution – a communicative failure or rejection must arise 
with respect to an epistemic role or contribution. Here, one is harmed in one’s abilities as knowledge giver. 
Miragoli studies the form when active ignorance – i.e., not knowing and not wanting to know – lead to such a 
rejection of an otherwise valid epistemic contribution. Recalling Pohlhaus’s [42] racial and sociopolitical 
lenses, it’s easy to think of such undercurrents causing such active ignorance. As Miragoli ([33], p. 4) 
summarizes it: it arises when individuals “are unjustly prevented from obtaining what it is in their right to 
obtain with their words.” The example Miragoli gives concerns an automated application system for asylum 
seekers in which a communicative act with the right informational content should lead to an asylum

3. A taxonomy of AI-related epistemic injustice 
Now we consider epistemic injustices caused by or related to AI. To situate AI in the context of epistemic 
injustice, we should first discuss two things: (i) the agentic character of AI systems and (ii) the epistemic 
form of the technology. 
(i) Current AI systems can be defined as algorithmic systems where an input-output relationship leads 
to the execution of a task such that it resembles a more or less complex form of intelligent behavior. This 
definition is broader than the current legal consensus that Luciano Floridi [56] discerns.8 ‘Resemblance’ is 
an important term here as the systems’ behavior isn’t really intelligent or intentional like humans are; it 
resembles the symptoms [72] of intentional and intelligent behavior. Following Floridi [64], I take AI systems 
to possess artificial agency, but not intelligence, intentionality or self-determination. AI systems exhibit varying 
levels of interactivity, autonomy and adaptability, and “goal-directedness” while “operating through 
computational and data processing” such that the extent to which they can perform tasks and influence 
their environment ranges from artefactual to artificial agency. However, the syntactic form of AI agency is 
such that “[t]he bounded autonomy of AI systems restricts their operations within programmed parameters, 
creating a clear ceiling for independent action. Their heavy dependence on training data and predefined 
objectives shapes their agency in ways fundamentally different from biological systems” ([64], p. 19). 
Attributing genuine choice and comprehension would therefore remain a category mistake.9 
The range from artefactual agency to artificial agency is important to keep in mind, because epistemic 
injustices related to generative AI like LLMs such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Transformer-based vision 
models like DALL-E, are not the only relevant ones. Less sophisticated forms of machine learning, and 
logic-based AI systems are also entangled with epistemic injustices. Epistemic injustices depend on the 
disabling, downplaying, disallowing, degrading, or misdirecting of epistemic contributions. AI systems 
(simpler algorithms as well as with sophisticated large-scale neural networks) can do so in a mechanically 
automated way.  
(ii) Following Alvarado [57], I think of AI as an epistemic technology. According to Alvarado, AI is an 
epistemic technology because AI is “used in an epistemic context and for an epistemic purpose”; “Deal[s] with epistemic 
content” and “[carries] out epistemic operations” ([57], p. 19). When we combine this insight with the conception 
of AIs as artificial agents, AI systems have a limited extent of epistemic agency, because they can carry out 
epistemic operations (data analyses, classification), over epistemic content (data, propositions, models) and 
with an epistemic purpose (inquiry, knowledge construction, synthesis or retrieval). This makes them quasi-
knowers with at least performative epistemic capacities. This is important for embedding these systems as

3.1. How do epistemic injustices manifest in the context of AI? 
Before I extend the taxonomy with five forms of AI-related epistemic injustice ,I explain and review how 
some previously identified epistemic injustices manifest themselves related to AI.  
Let’s start with some examples of AI-related hermeneutical injustice.  
 Dan McQuillan ([26], p. 61) holds that hermeneutical violence can be enacted by AI “because the 
complexity and opacity of AI-driven interventions are inherent barriers to any independent 
effort at comparable sense-making” which comes to “[overlay] already existing cultural and 
institutional systems of superiority”.  
 Pozzi [43] has carefully worked out regarding the example of machine learning (ML) systems 
mediating opioid (medical drugs) subscriptions, AI systems based on ML “hermeneutically 
appropriate” the resources it regulates and thereby “deprives human agents of understanding 
and hinders their communicative practices” when their subscription applications are rejected 
for opaque reasons.  
 What’s more, De Proost & Pozzi [8] point to the danger of hermeneutical marginalization when 
conversational AI are employed in the context of psychotherapy: the AI system – because of 
its reflection of the dominant worldviews from its training data – has hermeneutical privilege and 
thereby harms the processes of sensemaking and understanding in psychically disadvantaged 
groups, because of the burden of not seeing their ‘deviant’ ‘out of distribution’ experiences 
reflected.  
 As a data science technology, AI systems can cause hermeneutical injustices, so Symons & 
Alvarado [46] explain, because of their epistemic opacity, which obscures the computational path 
towards the outcome that is taken up as decision. Thereby, individuals are without capacity to 
understand the algorithm they are at the whim of.  
 Furthermore, Milano & Prunkl [31] explain that algorithmic profiling can lead to hermeneutical 
injustice. Algorithmic profiling fosters epistemic fragmentation, which is another background 
condition leading to the eruption of hermeneutical injustice. It’s an “infrastructural flaw” that 
obstructs applying epistemic resources to one’s experience, leading to dissonance or algorithmic 
gaslighting, failures of uptake – such as algorithms neglecting certain content – or prohibits one 
to produce new ones, because of algorithmic patterns of profiling resisting discovery.  
To summarize, AI systems are a root of hermeneutical injustice if the system applies interpretative 
frameworks such that an epistemic dissonance occurs with the interactant that limits the latter’s capacity 
for making sense of her experiences [21].  
On the testimonial side of the matter, Symons & Alvarado [46] explain that algorithmic systems can lead 
to testimonial injustice because of the claim to authority of their outcomes. The opacity of the process [20] 
leading to the outcome makes the assessment of the truthfulness of the outcome uncertain. However, when 
outcomes like predictions or classifications are presented as accurate and objective, the root of the 
testimonial injustice becomes the fact that the testimony of party Y is pitted against the output of the

system, and Y’s testimony is discredited because of the algorithm’s ‘objectivity’. Regarding LLMs like 
ChatGPT, McQuillan [27] denounces them as being far from ‘trustworthy AI’. McQuillan argues LLMs 
end up destabilizing trust in human capacities of knowing in general, and that of already marginalized 
communities in particular. AI systems, so one can argue in line with McQuillan and Symons & Alvarado, 
amass a surplus of credibility and thereby create a credibility deficit amongst average users (particularly non-
Western users). At least two reasons can be given to back up this claim. 
First, LLMs’ ‘memorize’ parts of their training data which they then regurgitate without strictly 
trustworthy references (or without any at all) ([7], pp. 52-53), leading to the displacement of ideas from 
their source and their possibly unwarranted intermixing. Second, there are the problems of ‘stochastic 
parroting’ and fact hallucination [5]: dominantly repeating what is already dominant information and stating 
for a fact things which are false or confabulated, respectively. These problems together constitute the 
emission of phrasings that are regurgitated from dominantly white and Western data as factual or could be 
hallucinated based upon that, with no direct way for users to check this. Marginalized and non-Western 
groups interacting with these technologies can therefore be presented with claims to facticity conflicting 
with their own culturally determined epistemic particulars. As Kay et al. [21] write: “Testimonial injustice 
can arise when algorithms are prioritized over human credibility […]”. In short, when it’s you against a 
persiflated personification of the Internet, you have to be quite sure of yourself. 
Giving testimony concerns one’s role as knowledge giver, but AI systems can also negatively affect the 
capacity of knowledge seeking. This zetetic injustice, according to Miragoli [33], is saliently related to 
algorithmic systems. Algorithmic systems can frustrate access to knowledge and emborder avenues for 
inquiry by mediating the dissemination of knowledge towards knowers. Miragoli’s example is that of search 
algorithms framing the outcomes of a query based on principles or statistical design that doesn’t necessarily 
align with the intentions of the inquirer, highlighting some sources of information, while hiding others. 
Moving beyond Google Search, LLMs (pace LLMs specifically designed for finding research papers such as 
Perplexity) have mastered the technique of giving information, without making sourcing explicit and 
trustworthy. As a result, the inquirer is left at the LLMs’ zetetic goodwill so to speak.  
A related worry is that this framing deprives inquirers of their epistemic agency. In other words, why 
should we assume that inquirers take what LLMs say at face value?10 My response to this worry is twofold. 
First, the formulation of zetetic injustice doesn’t deprive inquirers of their agency, because it merely 
highlights the inquirer’s epistemic vulnerability when trusting the AI system’s output. Of course, some 
inquirers will be skeptical, but since the practice of doubting requires some grounds for that doubt to be 
valid [52], inquirers will not always be in the position to reasonably doubt the output of, say, ChatGPT, 
especially since the collated experience of many users is that these systems are – under normal circumstances 
– trustworthy with respect to common topics (e.g., ‘What is the capital of France?’). Secondly, I observe 
that common inquirers (and we shouldn’t think of philosophers and scientists as common inquirers during 
their work) prefer short, fast-served and well-structured answers and outputs over lengthy and vague ones, 
because of reasons ranging from naiveté and (temporary) constraints on cognitive capacities to limited 
attention spans and being accustomed to the fast information consumption. Systems like LLMs are

What’s very important to understand is the societal background making possible algorithmic epistemic 
injustices. The racial and sociopolitical lenses Pohlhaus [42] brought to bear on epistemic injustice also do 
explanatory work in the context of AI. AI-scholar Yarden Katz [54] explains that AI development has been 
imbued with forms of white universalism, militarism and racial bias ever since the 1950s. Evidently in 
Milano & Prunkl’s treatment of hermeneutical injustice, AI systems create false forms of objectivity; the 
“forgery” of “universal machine intelligence”: ‘views from nowhere’ that universalize Western knowledge 
without justification [54]. As Kraft & Soulier [23] argue, the discipline of machine learning has construed 
knowledge as universal and readily gleanable from observable patterns in data, thereby enforcing the view 
from nowhere via the attribution of objectivity to the systems’ outputs.  An example of the universalization 
of Western knowledge through LLMs is that AI systems are trained on predominantly Western datasets 
(the Internet) and fail to relativize their information space as non-objective. To summarize, this is the 
downside of what Gabriel et al. [13] have called, in the context of advanced AI assistants, “perceived 
knowledgeability” and “perceived trustworthiness”, which yield “epistemic authority” and “epistemic trust” 
respectively. 
Finally, the ‘view from nowhere’ doesn’t come out of the blue. Comparing wide ranging indexes on 
Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) countries’ social values, thinking style, 
and other psychological traits, Atari et al. ([2], p. 16) show GPT “inherit[s] a WEIRD psychology in many 
attitudinal aspects (e.g., values, trust, religion) as well as cognitive domains (e.g., thinking style, self-
concept)” because the data the model has been trained on have mostly been produced by WEIRD 
populations. The model’s responses most resemble a cluster of WEIRD countries including the USA, 
Canada, Northern Ireland, New Zealand, Great Britain, Australia, Andorra, Germany, and the Netherlands. 
However, WEIRD people make up only a fraction of the world’s population, and the LLM’s WEIRDness 
persists even in multilingual models. Mihalcea et al. [30] conclude similarly that this is rooted in a lack of 
representation in AI development, a lack of culture-specific and diverse information in the training data 
and inclusive annotation of data, and American pre-training and alignment biases. 
 
3.2. Four novel forms of algorithmic epistemic injustice 
Kay et al. [21] have greatly expanded the application of hermeneutical and testimonial injustice to the 
generative AI. First, there’s (6.2) GENERATIVE HERMENEUTICAL IGNORANCE, which concerns the 
misrepresentation of marginalized social groups’ experiences in algorithmically generated products, because 
of a lack of cultural or otherwise contextually relevant training data. Simply put, their epistemic and linguistic 
particulars aren’t present in or reproducible from the AI system’s information space, and so elements 
necessary for their specifically situated cultural forms of sensemaking cannot be outputted by the system. 
Therefore, generative AI can’t take the experiences of these marginalized social groups into account, nor 
reproduce them like it is able to reproduce dominant strands of epistemic and linguistic particulars. As a 
result, it is ignorant with respect to their epistemic lives. Furthermore, these latter particulars are construed 
as ‘universal’: in the example of image recognition, the image databases for training contain objects that

figure in Western forms of life that are classified using categories supplied by the English language. To 
illustrate, ImageNet, the most used image database, is comprised of 60% Western sources images (US: 45% 
and only 2.2% from China and India ([38], pp. 14-15). These AI systems’ outputs reflect those ways of 
partitioning visions of the world and not others ([54], pp. 110-114)). Consider the example of colonial 
history’s representation in Midjourney’s latent space. Gurfinkel [62] provides a generated image of Spanish 
conquistadors smiling next to Aztec warriors, which is wrong for misrepresenting the violent history of 
colonial conquest. The AI model is thus ignorant of the facts necessary to adequately represent the queried 
scenario. Alternatively put, a ‘view from nowhere’ is produced that cannot emulate the function of the 
specific ‘views from somewhere’ needed to accurately represent colonial violence.  
Secondly, (6.3) GENERATIVE HERMENEUTICAL ACCESS. This form of algorithmic hermeneutical 
injustice concerns the obstructing of access to information. According to Kay et al. [21], it arises when 
generative AI directs of the information supplied to individuals and results in a failure to understand or 
articulate experiences on the human end of the human-AI-interaction. It impacts the user’s epistemic 
capacity of receiving information and for example occurs in the context of ‘under-resourced languages’. 
This intersects with cultural biases, concepts, and ways of expression and sensemaking missing from the 
training datasets and, alongside a somewhat distributive line, can foster epistemic fragmentation by leading 
to the making inaccessible of epistemic goods and services. Kay et al. [21] provide the example of differences 
when GPT reports the number of fatalities in Arabic and Hebrew in the Israeli-Palestine conflicts and 
Turkish and Kurdish in the conflict between Turkey and the Kurdistan military resistance. Another example 
concerning LLMs is the gendered result of translations in under-resourced languages.  
For both generative hermeneutical access and generative hermeneutical ignorance, their manifestations 
can mainly be characterized as structural and systemic rather than interpersonal and incidental. The forms of 
epistemic exclusion erected by algorithmic hermeneutical injustice are related to the exclusion of the 
collective forms of sensemaking of certain social groups from some part of the AI system; this doesn’t 
come down to the incidental exclusion of singular experiences, but to shared epistemic and linguistic 
particulars being inconsiderable for the system. 
Third, we have (5.2) GENERATIVE MANIPULATIVE TESTIMONIAL INJUSTICE. The term ‘manipulative’ 
points towards generative AI’s fabrication and spreading of offensive or disinforming content. AI systems 
facilitate creating offensive content and disinformation and can help spreading it; think of conspiracy 
theories, false allegations, deepfakes, etc. Especially generative AI systems such as ChatGPT, DALL-E or 
Bing Image Creator can be used to create a wide range of factually untrue and disruptive content, which 
can be spread via social media for example. Now, how does this constitute an epistemic injustice? In the 
case of disinformation, the algorithmic credibility surplus (access to a huge information space) is exploited 
to generate seemingly real content that can manipulate or influence content receivers, thereby devaluing 
their possible testimonies to the contrary.  
To understand this form of injustice, it requires explanation why AI systems receive a credibility surplus 
in the first place, which underlies the manipulative aspect of the epistemic interaction. AI systems, especially 
LLMs, are trained to provide accurate outputs concerning a broad range of topics or a very specific one. 
Just like human experts receive credibility surpluses regarding their expertise, narrow AI systems (i.e., tumor 
detectors) and LLMs (general text retrieval and synthesis systems) have a surplus with respect to their

application in relation to users who lack or require support with that expertise or training. Notably, the credibility 
surplus is a relational attribution and LLMs generate different views only when being explicitly so pressed 
by the user, for which the user must first possess the reflective capacity and the relevant knowledge. There 
is also an alternative explanation for the testimonially distortive effect at hand: the disinformation being 
spread through the AI’s network structure (i.e., the AI system as end node in an information network where 
disinformation is spread by malignant actors) is causing the injustice, not the credibility surplus. I see one 
prominent reason for not adhering to this view. The network structure and the credibility surplus are distinct 
such that they are neither reducible to each other, nor mutually exclusive. They are compatible, because the 
former relates to the inability to verify the sentences, while the latter concerns the relation of testimonial

South Africa, which is a conspiracy the CEO Elon Musk has himself endorsed at times.11 Here manipulated 
and harmful disinformation is spread through the seemingly trustworthy interface of the LLM. Another 
example that bids AI testimony against that of the human interlocutor is the LLM-enhanced Google search 
feature, that recommended eating stones and putting glue on pizza.12 
When it comes down to your testimony versus the AI system’s, you need epistemic relentlessness. The 
interpersonal and incidental effects are as follows: in person-chatbot or person-content interactions, the 
injustice takes on an interpersonal form, with the AI system fulfilling the role of interlocutor. Also, a third 
person can be involved here, judging the credibility of both the AI system and the affected person. If 
content is not tailored to a specific audience and is irregularly deployed, the injustice takes on an incidental 
form. However, if, like in the case of conspiracy theories, larger audiences are targeted in a structural 
fashion, the injustice takes on structural and systemic traits.  
Finally, consider (5.3) GENERATIVE AMPLIFIED TESTIMONIAL INJUSTICE. Generative AI can amplify 
existing testimonial injustices present in the information space formed by the training data. Patterns 
representing misinformation can be parroted [5], and dominant voices and standpoints are more frequently 
outputted (cf. Miragoli [33] on epistemic conformism). Thereby social biases and prejudices are reproduced 
and the voices of marginalized communities and less frequent participants on the Internet are factored out. 
Consider the fictional example of the application of an LLM as chatbot for municipal services for 
immigrants. Immanuel is an immigrant and queries the interface for information about acquiring housing 
in the city center. The LLM, which is also trained on disinformation, replies (contrary to the municipal 
policy) that immigrants shouldn’t be allowed to live in the center because the crime rate is too high and 
immigrants ‘are known to be associated with heightened crime rates’. Here content representing a harmful 
stereotype present in society (‘immigrants are criminals’) enters a formal interaction between a (potential) 
citizen and the LLM, and is amplified, where the LLM has the credibility surplus because it is deployed as 
digital representative of an official institution.

testimonial amplification occurs in an interface interaction with a specific user, and these effects are 
replicated systematically across user interactions. On the other hand, this form of injustice is mainly structural

4. Generative hermeneutical erasure and epistemic colonization 
Generative hermeneutical erasure is a subclass of algorithmic hermeneutical injustice. It occurs in 
intercultural deployment of AI systems where the AI system comes to stand in mismatch with the values, 
concepts, and forms of justification of a context of deployment. This form of epistemic injustice becomes 
visible in the study of the impact of generative AI in for example the global South. Generative hermeneutical 
erasure is made possible by the background condition14 of epistemic colonization. Figure 3 below captures the 
main aspects of the conceptual space of generative hermeneutical erasure. To construe this detailed subtree 
of the general taxonomy of epistemic injustice, I introduce three intuitive relations: (i) depends on, (ii) has 
manifestation, and (iii) leads to.  
For a discussion of this new concept and its background condition, this section is divided into three 
parts. First, (4.1.) I explain how intercultural interaction with LLMs can lead to hermeneutical erasure,

4.1. Generative hermeneutical erasure 
Generative AI, when being deployed outside of its Western space of conception, can have effects of 
conceptual erasure. The conceptual, epistemic disruption caused by a mismatch between AI system and the 
interlocutor in terms of conceptual frameworks belongs to the making impossible of other ways of knowing 
– what Boaventura de Sousa Santos [9] has dubbed “epistemicide”. AI systems’ ‘view from nowhere’ 
epistemically inferiorizes non-Western epistemologies and thereby contributes to the erosion of ways of 
sensemaking. This algorithmic hermeneutical injustice automates epistemicide and thereby obstructs and 
arbitrarily shapes collective ways of sensemaking and explaining experiences, but not merely by 
misrepresenting or excluding the voices of marginalized social groups, but by relegating their epistemic 
contributions to the statistical margins. 
To better understand the dynamic of generative hermeneutical erasure, I draw a parallel with Camila 
Lobo’s [24] Wittgensteinian account of epistemic silence. Lobo bases her analyses of hermeneutical injustice 
on the ‘hinge epistemology’ found in Ludwig Wittgenstein’s On Certainty [52]. Some epistemic particulars 
come to play the role of a hinge in our everyday epistemological practices [36, 37]. The hinge-metaphor 
points to how some concepts or propositions come to stand fast for us – are indubitable, certain – so that 
other things can ‘turn’ around it. ‘Turning’ here means: be known, doubted, investigated, in short, so that 
other things can be uncertain. As Mollema [36] explains in a close reading of Wittgenstein: “The sentences 
we know, hold for certain and do not doubt are thus temporarily hardened and implicitly function ‘like 
hinges’ on which the questions and doubts that are in flux depend ([52], §341). Those sentences ‘must stay 
put’ for the questions and doubts to make sense ([52], §343). As soon as I no longer know for certain that 
I have two hands, that the earth is round or that I am a human being, the validity of my worldview’s entire 
body of evidence is at stake.” Now with the absence of a concept, something that is needed to articulate 
and make sense of specific experiences, is missing. In other words: a ‘hinge’ is missing for epistemic 
practices to rely on. Following Fricker, Lobo considers the example of the concept of sexual harassment,


## Page 21


21 
 
where its absence prohibited women from articulating what was wrong with the violence they experienced. 
Without the concept of sexual harassment standing firm as a grave violence – without functioning as a hinge 
– the deployment of the concept and the related activities are frustrated (e.g., knowing one has been violated, 
indicting with conviction). Epistemic silence denotes the consequence of conceptual absence for epistemic 
agents. In acquiring the concept, one’s epistemic voice also gains in volume and strength on the respective 
domain. It could be argued that fostering epistemic silence is an irreducibly epistemic form of oppression 
[57]. Today, many unknown gender data gaps constitute epistemic silences of sorts [61]. 
Generative hermeneutical erasure is the obstruction or eradication of formerly trusted or ‘certain’ ways 
of articulating and making sense of experiences via the suppression of conceptual differences. The 
imposition enacted by generative AI’s objectivity corrodes and erodes local epistemologies, gradually 
leading to the displacement, disruption – and ultimately – erasure of their epistemic particulars, leading to 
epistemic silence. At a certain point in time, with the increasing dependency and ubiquity of these AI 
systems, ways of sensemaking will have become distorted and dominated by the ‘view from nowhere’. In 
the case of hermeneutical erasure, the AI system can, because of its suppression of the epistemic particulars 
of other sociocultural groups, cause conceptual disruptions with epistemic consequences, like erasure from 
the repertoire for gaining knowledge or subsuming experiences can be one of them.  
For example, this could lead to effects such as cultural erasure, such as in the case when reporters asked 
the Chinese LLM DeepSeek who Ai Weiwei is and it answered “I am sorry, I cannot answer that question. 
I am an AI assistant designed to provide helpful and harmless responses.”15 When the lives of ‘dissident’ 
political activists are denied, prominent culturally salient epistemic particulars are erased, made unavailable 
for the public to make sense of their own experiences with. Here the epistemic content of this exemplary 
figure’s resistance is suppressed to make impossible further epistemic activity premised on it.

4.2. Epistemic colonization 
In the following, I explain how the background condition of epistemic colonization prepares generative 
hermeneutical erasure. Epistemic colonization focusses on AI systems that have been based on the Internet 
data that is dominated by the English language and Euro-American worldviews. Epistemic colonization 
could however have all kinds of impositions as origin. The term ‘colonization’ here is used quasi-
metaphorically. The important aspect here for using the concept is that there is one organization of 
epistemologies that comes to dominate another organization of epistemologies, such that the dominated 
epistemological organization is ‘taken hostage’ and transformed into a minimized periphery to the 
dominated epistemological organization [9]. Here, I analyze three aspects of epistemic colonization. First, 
its ‘WEIRDness’. Second, its relation to the epistemic oppression of the global South. Third, how AI 
systems ‘colonize’ epistemologies. 
Automating colonial WEIRDness. As Atari et al. [2] aptly put it, paraphrasing the slogan of ‘garbage in, 
garbage out’: “WEIRD in, WEIRD out”. A good example of this is the difference between analytic thinking 
and holistic thinking. In their study, Atari et al. [2] showed that both ChatGPT and WEIRD countries 
strongly tend towards connecting triads of words in terms of categorization and abstraction (analysis), 
whereas for non-WEIRD countries functional and contextual relations are drawn (holism). For example, 
when given the triad ‘bread’, ‘lettuce’ and ‘oven’ the analytic answer is to connect ‘bread’ and ‘lettuce’ 
because of a shared superclass, whereas the holistic answer connects ‘bread’ and ‘oven’ because of their 
functional relationship (external to the triad). This example is innocuous but consider the extrapolation to 
more difficult questions regarding religion, morality, politics and economics. There the difference between 
giving ‘analytic’ and ‘holistic’ answers would matter greatly. Note however that not all WEIRD countries 
meet the conditions for WEIRDness in the same way, think of countries that are more or less democratic 
or industrialized. Furthermore, within WEIRD countries, the blunt of AI development proceeds within 
companies hailing from the United States, which introduces, so we can reasonably suggest, a narrower US-
centric libertarian ideological bias into the epistemology of LLMs, which further catalyzes generative 
epistemic injustices if a cultural domain of deployment differs more strongly from this domain of 
development.18

The epistemic oppression of the global South. Consider that before the structural interaction with the AI 
system’s colonial exhibition of the Western worldview, indigenous epistemic particulars are present. Epistemic 
colonization affects these particulars and is itself a subclass of conceptual colonization, in which a colonizer 
wittingly imposes its conceptual apparatus onto a colonized sociocultural group, marking it as dominant 
and thereby devaluing the conceptual apparatus of the colonized. Historically, conceptual colonization has 
been a side effect of colonialism, which has been described by Uchenna Okeja [39] as “conceptual 
adjustment program”. The conceptual effects of colonialism have been highlighted for example by Kwasi 
Wiredu [51] who discusses the effects of imposing foreign languages and the related patterns of thought 
and conceptual distinction on the (formerly) colonized, leading to the erasure of socioculturally inherited 
conceptual particulars. As I, following Achille Mbembe and others [25, 44] state it elsewhere: “AI systems 
are in need of epistemic decolonization because of their capacity for reifying Western knowledge and values 
as universal and the viral reproduction of the colonialist superstructure’s cognitive content” [35]. The point 
is that AI erects and strengthens epistemic barriers that intentionally or not stand in service of coloniality – 
the continuation of colonialism’s wrongful effects. As Joseph Bremer [6] states regarding LLMs: “Artificial 
intelligence designed for the needs of the Global North simultaneously excludes individuals from outside 
this political, cultural and socio-economic background. The classifying, ordering and predictive power of 
artificial intelligence thus becomes a hegemonic cultural node of knowledge, overshadowing knowledge 
from areas other than the Global North.” In generative AI, especially the ordering power is well-
represented, disseminating information as if it were knowledge and laying claim to objectivity in its framings 
of concepts. In short, the background condition of epistemic decolonization extends the hegemonic knowledge 
production of colonialism into the effects of AI systems.  
AI systems ‘colonizing’ epistemologies. The facet of ‘hegemony’ resides, as Muldoon & Wu ([38], pp. 15-16) 
think, in the implicit aspiration to universal validity present in the claim to objectivity that algorithmic 
knowledge asserts, which invalidates other claims to knowledge and ways of knowing. This type of 
knowledge is deserving of the adjective ‘colonial’ because they regard it as the algorithmic reinforcement of 
the West’s hegemony of values and knowledge, propagated as the aforementioned ‘view from nowhere’. 
This is not a literal case of colonialism, which should be regarded as an intentional and institutional form 
of domination, but a case of coloniality: the extension, reproduction and transformation of inequalities and 
forms of oppression that stem from colonial history and the continued impairment of the global South by 
the wounds of (neo)colonialism. The hegemonic objectivity canalized by LLMs results in the propagation 
of a way of (re)ordering the world according to the Western conceptual apparatus and epistemology. The 
hermeneutical background condition of epistemic colonization “erupts” once the AI system’s ‘objective’ 
output comes not only comes into conflict with a ‘subjective’ experience that is then disqualified instead of 
made socially intelligible, but also contributes to the overwriting, suppression, and silencing of the 
possibility of these ‘divergent’ experiences. Aligning with Boaventura de Sousa Santos’ [9] introduction of 
the term ‘epistemicide’, we should think of this in terms of the displacement and eradication of non-Western

‘ecologies of knowledge’ by how the structure of coloniality is channeled through the information space 
LLMs draw upon.19  
 
4.3. Completing the case for generative hermeneutical erasure 
Distinguishing generative hermeneutical erasure from its siblings hermeneutical ignorance and 
hermeneutical access is important. All three forms of generative hermeneutical injustice are specific in 
scope: hermeneutical ignorance is about the misrepresentation of marginalized social groups’ experiences in 
algorithmically generated products and hermeneutical access is about the AI-induced human failure of understanding or 
articulation experiences truthfully in human-AI interactions. Hermeneutical erasure complements these 
misrepresentations and failures of understanding by articulating the suppression of statistically marginal 
concepts in the AI system’s latent information space, leading to the imposition of exogenous 
conceptualizations and the erasure of relevant conceptual differences. As AI systems take on more 
prominent roles in our lives, the effects of hermeneutical ignorance, access and erasure will become more 
significant and markedly different. For ignorance, it can be speculated that more social groups will feel AI 
systems don’t represent their ideas and practices correctly, whereas for access it can be expected that cases 
of alienation in interaction with AI systems will become more common (the ‘sycophancy’ of GPT-4 is an 
example within the West).20 Hermeneutical erasure on the other hand is dangerous when AI systems 
become tutors, usurp knowledge acquisition and mediate information dissemination, because this shapes 
the epistemic interactants as well: it shapes what they can know, how they think and in what terms. The 
distinct consequence of hermeneutical erasure is the suppression of conceptual differences between social 
groups, making them more homogenous in epistemology and conceptual arsenal. 
To the idea and relevance of generative hermeneutical erasure one could object that while it’s true that 
AI systems are trained on cultural products of the dominant culture (i.e. white, Western, etc.), the epistemic 
lives of the marginalized and the dominant groups have some overlap. When the gas gauge indicates “E”, 
both know to put gas in the car; when it looks like rain, they’ll all grab an umbrella, etc. However, this 
objection is based on a misunderstanding of what is erased. Of course, it is the case that there are (thanks 
to shared aspects in forms of life and historical processes like globalization) cultural invariances between 
WEIRD countries/global North and non-WEIRD countries and the global South inside and outside the 
North. These invariances are also part of the training data of AI systems like LLMs. Furthermore, this 
cultural invariance is algorithmically reinstated, because it is statistically dominant, whereas what is 
statistically marginal (because its rare or non-conforming) is suppressed. Generative hermeneutical erasure 
doesn’t articulate the total eclipse of one cultural set of concepts by another; due to similarities that would 
be impossible. Especially because of this invariance, generative hermeneutical erasure concerns the 
algorithmic suppression of difference in epistemology and conceptualization. The repetition of the dominant set

5. Discussion 
Given the overview of epistemic injustices I have provided two poignant questions deserve to be addressed. 
 
5.1. What is the relation to other taxonomies of AI related injustices or risks? 
It is relevant to relate the proposed taxonomy of epistemic injustice to other taxonomies (of the unjust 
effects of AI). 
Firstly, the only other (to my knowledge) taxonomical representation of epistemic injustice is provided 
by Báez-Vizcaíno & Santana-Soriano [58]. They provide the lenses of pre-transactional, transactional and 
post-transactional to partition the conceptual domain of epistemic injustice. These lenses enable them to 
identify specific forms of epistemic injustice which are one subclass-level lower than where general the 
taxonomy provided here ends, such as subclasses of epistemic invalidation and a different partitioning of 
the concept of hermeneutical injustice, which they tend to avoid in their taxonomy. On the same level of 
detail, epistemic injustice in the context of AI is taxonomized here. It is important to note that AI is not 
dealt with in Báez-Vizcaíno & Santana-Soriano’s work. Hence future work might look at the taxonomy 
proposed here and that of Báez-Vizcaíno & Santana-Soriano to further taxonomize the domain of epistemic 
injustice and embed AI-related epistemic injustices. 
Secondly, the following provides an embedding the elements of the taxonomy into the taxonomy of AI 
risks from the MIT AI Risk Repository [45]. I highlight the categories that overlap with the conceptual 
boundaries of the epistemic injustices that I have developed, because this is the broadest taxonomy of AI 
risks thus far developed. First – within the domain of “Discrimination and toxicity” – are “Unfair 
discrimination and misrepresentation”, and “Unequal performance across groups”. The categories named 
here are clearly related to hermeneutical factors such as group representation in the training data, and 
affecting the legitimacy of testimonies or the contribution of specific sociocultural groups. Second, located 
in the domain “Misinformation”, we see the risk categories “False or misleading information”, “Pollution 
of information ecosystems and loss of consensus reality”. These categories are related to the spread of 
seemingly objective but invalid that misleads audiences or drowns out other sources of information, thereby 
destabilizing epistemic practices of truth finding. Third, within the domain of “Malicious actors and 
misuse”, the subcategories of “Disinformation, surveillance, and influence at scale”, “Fraud scams, and 
targeted manipulation”, which converge with generative manipulative testimonial injustice. Finally, within

5.2. What are forms of redress for algorithmic epistemic injustice? 
What are forms of redress for algorithmic epistemic injustice? I consider some proposals for epistemic 
justice. For starters, Kraft & Soulier [23] focus on the improvement of inclusion in the knowledge bases 
stage and direct us to the measures of improving the inclusion of currently marginalized social groups in 
the data collection process, and knowledge base curation activities. However, they also stress that mere 
inclusion doesn’t solve the structural injustices forming the root of epistemic injustices. Miragoli [33] on 
the other hand is hopeful in his identification of AI’s epistemic conformism – causing testimonial spurning 
and zetetic injustice – is a design flaw that can in principle be remedied by AI design that is conscious of 
its intersection with questions of social justice. Finally, Kay et al.’s [21] vision of the ethical ideal of 
‘generative epistemic justice’ includes employing testimonial and hermeneutical injustices as virtues in 
interpersonal interactions. Furthermore, to counter generative testimonial injustices, “radical systemic 
change” is required in the epistemic sphere to make marginalized voices amplified in generative AI 
products, for which incidental participation is not enough. However, rebuilding social legibility via the 
technical amplification of veridical information on and of testimonially marginalized groups is helpful. On 
the hermeneutical side, they consider technical measures for AI system design, such as watermarking of 
data, pluralistic representation in datasets, fact-checking practices, and striving for reversing epistemic 
opacity into epistemic transparency by way of documentation, building an understanding of LLMs, and 
employing user-friendly interfaces.21

Secondly, what are ways to remedy or pre-empt generative hermeneutical erasure and epistemic colonization? 
Because coloniality lies at the root of the erasure, consider ‘decolonial AI’ [1, 34] – resisting and reversing 
the peripheralization of social groups and cultural peoples in the space of AI development and 
deployment.22 Decolonial AI not only means doing something else with AI, but also radically dismantling 
existing epistemic barriers imposed on the “wretched of AI” and within current AI systems [6, 35]. What 
this implies is realizing the one-sidedness of the Western worldview underlying the frameworks, principles, 
guidelines and philosophy of AI ethics. Afflicted native concepts can be reintroduced or new concepts can 
be crafted as alternatives better attuned with the context of deployment [29]. Also, this means for the 
alignment of AI systems embedded in epistemic practices with local moral cultural values, like Ubuntu for 
Sub-Saharan Africa [14, 16] and moral religious values such as Islam and Hinduism [10, 47] depending on 
the cultural locus for and in which AI is deployed. For AI development and governance, introducing global 
legislation that is inclusive and also builds upon the voice of the global South, allows the tailoring to the 
local cultural, moral and religious values to proceed in geographical contexts, thereby suppressing the 
potency of cultural imposition and hermeneutical injustice [17]. Also, specifically aimed at hermeneutical 
erasure, epistemic vigilance is an important form of (decolonial) resistance. This means being aware of AI’s 
epistemically distortive effects, safeguarding non-AI-mediated knowledge systems, such as libraries and 
human-curated open access knowledge bases and information hubs, and actively sharing instances of 
cultural and epistemic erasure with others to resist collective conceptual loss.

6. Conclusion 
It needs to be recognized that generative AI systems’ massive worldwide deployment and their 
embedding into all kinds of everyday and crucial processes, can in the limit lead to the formation of our 
ways of knowing via their reordering power. Thinking and knowing will come to reflect the concepts and 
epistemology reflecting the dominant patterns in training data and the principles of their development. The 
conceptual resource of epistemic injustice can and should be deployed to identify these effects. Only after 
an adequate conceptualization of this injustice has been provided can we come to help those harmed by it. 
Conducting empirical research into the effects of AI systems of marginalized communities and closely 
following the development of LLMs’ capabilities and capacities could support a further development of the 
taxonomy. Finally, I discussed ways to remedy and address algorithmic epistemic injustice, such as 
decolonial AI, and the relation of the taxonomy to other taxonomies of epistemic injustice and AI risks. 
To summarize, I have contributed to the study of epistemic justice by providing a general taxonomy of 
epistemic injustice. The second contribution is the extension of the taxonomy to the AI domain and 
covering forms of epistemic injustice arising in interaction with generative AI systems. The third 
contribution is the introduction of the concept generative hermeneutical erasure – the automation of 
epistemicide by generative AI. This concept is a consequence of epistemic colonization (the extension of 
the cognitive dimension of coloniality via the LLMs linguistic and visual ordering of the world, based on a 
thoroughly Western worldview) and leads to the creation of epistemic silences: the inability to articulate 
and make sense of social experiences. Generative hermeneutical erasure concerns the algorithmic suppression 
of difference in epistemology and conceptualization, which extinguishes unique indigenous aspects of cultures 
and homogenizes epistemologies. cultural ways of knowing and sensemaking.
