1.​THE PHILOSOPHY OF AI: METHODOLOGICAL 
FOUNDATIONS 
 
Motivating Questions  
We are encountering philosophical aliens. Claude, ChatGPT, GPT-4, LaMDA, and other recent 
sophisticated large language models (LLMs) are creatures unlike any we’ve encountered before. 
Decades of science fiction have taught us that encounters with aliens are difficult, delicate, and 
dangerous occasions. As Captain Jean-Luc Picard says, “First contact with a new civilization is a 
delicate matter. They will have their own history and their own customs, and we have to respect them.”  
ChatGPT may not be the kind of alien that comes with a civilization (but then again, it may be — part 
of the difficulty is that we know so little about how to think about things here). And ChatGPT may not 
have (much of) a history or customs. It does, however, come with features of its own that will take work 
to understand.  
 
We are interested in the following range of questions:  
 
-​
Can LLMs (or kindred technologies not too far into the future) understand and speak natural 
languages?   
-​
Can LLMs have beliefs?  
-​
Can LLMs have desires?  
-​
Can LLMs have knowledge?  
-​
Can LLMs perform intentional actions?  
-​
Can LLMs have goals?  
-​
Can LLMs express contents inaccessible to us humans?  
-​
Can LLMs be in mental states that are radically different from the mental states of humans?  
-​
Why do the answers to the questions above matter?  
-​
How do we go about answering the questions above?  
 
More generally, we are interested in both understanding the cognitive and linguistic capacities of LLMs 
and in understanding how we should go about theorizing about that domain.  
 
These questions have far-reaching implications beyond the confines of academic philosophy. As AI 
becomes increasingly integrated into various domains, understanding the nature and extent of its 
abilities will be crucial for making informed decisions about its development, deployment, and 
regulation. For AI researchers and technologists, the answers to these questions will shape the goals 
and methodologies of their work. If LLMs and future AI systems are capable of genuine understanding, 
7

knowledge, and goal-directed behavior, this may open up new avenues for creating beneficial AI tools 
to augment and enhance human capabilities. However, it would also require greater caution and 
ethical safeguards to ensure these systems remain safe and aligned with human values. If AI systems 
fundamentally lack traits like desires and intentions, different approaches to their design and use 
would be warranted.  
 
Policymakers and leaders in government and industry will also need to wrestle with these issues as 
they make high-stakes decisions about the societal role of AI. The extent to which AI systems can be 
considered truly autonomous agents with their own beliefs and goals has major implications for 
questions of legal personhood, liability, and regulatory oversight. As AI is deployed in domains like 
healthcare, education, finance, and defense, its linguistic and cognitive capacities will determine the 
level of trust and responsibility placed in these systems. 
 
More broadly, the similarities and differences between human and machine intelligence will shape 
public perceptions and the cultural impact of AI. If AI is seen as having person-like qualities, this could 
foster greater social acceptance and willingness to interact with these systems. However, it may also 
stoke fears about AI rivaling or displacing humans. Conversely, if AI is viewed as merely a tool lacking 
genuine intelligence, this could limit its applications and lead to missed opportunities.   
 
As far as we can tell, there is just one thing that everyone involved in this debate will agree on: That 
there is no consensus about how to find out what the cognitive life of an LLM is like. There is no 
common methodology. The methodological questions are difficult to address because it’s not clear 
who has the relevant expertise. It is also unclear what methods we can use to decide on these 
methodological questions. Our hope is that this book can be at least a piecemeal contribution to 
increased clarity about methodology—and that, again, can help move our understanding forward. 
 
The lack of methodological agreement is unsurprising because that’s a general feature of philosophical 
inquiry. Philosophers who disagree on first order issues also often disagree about philosophical 
methodology. However, it’s somewhat more surprising that a lot of influential papers make what, at 
least at first glance, seem to be clear fallacies. In order to motivate the need for more methodological 
awareness, we’ll start by outlining what we take to be two fallacies that are fairly common in the 
current literature. Despite the lack of agreement about methodologies, it should be, we think, fairly 
uncontroversial that what we describe in the next two sections are fallacies. Before we get to those, 
one preliminary:  The quotes we've chosen to illustrate the 'Just an X' and 'Performance-Existence' 
fallacies come from journalists, bloggers, and some scholars. You might immediately think of more 
nuanced versions of these objections, or sophisticated philosophical positions that differ significantly 
from the more popular presentations we've chosen here. If so, that's good—you're already ahead of 
the game. Later in the book, we engage at length with deeper and more sophisticated objections that 
go beyond what is expressed in these initial quotes. So, if these initial examples strike you as 
oversimplified or insufficiently nuanced, our friendly advice is to hold onto your objections for now and 
see whether we address them satisfactorily in subsequent chapters. We predict you'll find that we do.

To see what we have in mind here - and to highlight the need for methodological clarity - consider an 
influential and widely accepted kind of skeptical argument about the capacities of LLMs. The skeptic 
wants to remind us that while the new technology’s outputs look impressive, the underlying 
programming is just a sophisticated version of stochastic text continuation. Cal Newport, in “What Kind 
of Mind Does ChatGPT Have?”, says: 
 
The result of these efforts might very well be jaw-dropping in its nuance and accuracy, but 
behind the scenes its generation lacks majesty. The system’s brilliance turns out to be the 
result less of a ghost in the machine than of the relentless churning of endless multiplications. 
… Now that we understand how these feats are actually performed, however, we can temper 
these perceptions. A system like ChatGPT doesn’t create, it imitates. … ChatGPT maintains no 
persistent state, no model of its surroundings that it modifies with new information, no memory 
of past conversations. It just cranks out words one at a time, in response to whatever input it’s 
provided, applying the exact same rules for each mechanistic act of grammatical production. … 
Once we’ve taken the time to open up the black box and poke around the springs and gears 
found inside, we discover that programs like ChatGPT don’t represent an alien intelligence with 
which we must now learn to coexist; instead, they turn out to run on the well-worn digital logic 
of pattern-matching, pushed to a radically larger scale. … ChatGPT is amazing, but in the final 
accounting it’s clear that what’s been unleashed is more automaton than golem.1

Sometimes the dismissive remarks take the explicit “just an X” form (“just spicy autocomplete”). In 
other instances, the “just” is implicit. “A big step toward true AI, or autocomplete on steroids” aims to 
present a choice, a choice that makes sense only if “autocomplete on steroids” falls short of true Ai -- if 
it’s just autocomplete on steroids. 
 
The obvious thing to say about “just an X” skepticism is that it looks like a straightforward fallacy. The 
skeptics make a case for X -- they, for example, describe the technology of LLMs to show that LLMs 
use a technology that can be described as autocompletion. Then they move from X to “just an X”. No 
justification is given for the move, and it’s not a move that’s typically valid. The invalidity is clear in less 
controversial cases. No one would accept the inference from “Michael Jordan is a basketball player” to 
“Michael Jordan is just a basketball player”. Jordan obviously can be, and is, a basketball player while 
also being much more than that. 
 
Recognizing the fallaciousness of “just an X” skepticism is only the first step in responding to that 
skepticism. The heart of “just an X” skepticism isn’t a logical inference -- it’s a bit of rhetoric, 
encouraging a reductive and dismissive attitude toward the target of the skepticism. To escape the grip 
of the skeptic, we need to see how to resist the dismissive reductivism. 
 
The next step is to recognize that the corresponding reductivist move is unpersuasive in other cases. 
Consider: 
 
●​ “You say that a hundred dollar bill is valuable. But it’s just dyed linen and cotton fibers woven 
together.” 
●​ “You say that van Gogh painting is an artistic treasure. But it’s just plant pigments suspended in 
oils.” 
●​ “You say Conway is a great mathematician. But he’s just an ugly bag of mostly water.” 
 
No one would or should be persuaded by any of these considerations. Of course the van Gogh 
painting is oil-suspended plant pigments distributed over a canvas. But manifestly that’s not all it is. It 
has other important features that make it an artistic treasure -- centrally, aesthetic and representational 
features. The rhetoric of the “just an X” skeptic tries to get us to overlook those other features, but in 
these easy cases, our grip on the other features is too secure to be dislodged. When our grip is less 
secure, the rhetoric can have more force. When the “just an X” skeptic tells us that human brains are 
just networks of electric transmissions, we might get more worried. We think we’re more than just

electric transmissions, but are we so sure? And even if we are sure, are we sure that it’s our brains 
that are the source of the ‘more’? 
 
Perhaps some of those who use "Just an X" arguments have something more sophisticated in mind.  
-​
LLMs are X (e.g. auto complete on steroids, statistical text predictors or ...)…  
-​
The X features of LLMs, fully explain all the system's functions 
-​
Therefore, LLMs are just X (autocomplete on steroids, text predictors, or ...).” 
  
Two points about this seemingly more sophisticated version of the sceptical argument:  
 
1.​ This is not what we call the ‘just-an-x-fallacy’. This more sophisticated form of argument 
involves doing more work. The skeptic is adding a premise about explanatory completeness. 
They claim that the X-features account entirely for the system's capacities - that there is no 
need for appeal to higher-level explanations or properties. Of course, establishing that the 
X-features fully explain all the relevant functions is a massive undertaking. In the case of LLMs, 
this would require showing that all the relevant aspects of their linguistic and cognitive behavior 
can be accounted for solely in terms of statistical text prediction, without invoking any 
intentional language or other emergent phenomena. As far as we know, no one knows how to 
do this—not for LLMs, not for the human brain, nor for many systems that exhibit emergent 
properties.    
2.​ So far we’ve described this as a more ‘sophisticated’ version of the argument, but that’s really 
being too generous. This form of argument is just as problematic as the simple fallacy. To see 
why, let’s ask the ‘sophisticated’ sceptic what properties of LLMs they claim that the X features 
(statistical text prediction) will account for? There are two options: the features to be accounted 
for include cognitive and linguistic capacities, such as understanding our questions, answering 
our questions, and changing its mind, or it does not include these features. If it does include 
those features, then this is no longer the kind of scepticism we were starting out with. The 
sceptic is so-called because they reject the view that LLMs can ‘understand’, 'answer’, and 
‘change their mind’. It’s a scepticism about the linguistic and cognitive capacities of LLMs. So 
the ‘sophisticated’ version of the argument has to say that this isn’t what we should use the 
X-feature to account for. If that’s the view, it isn’t all that sophisticated after all. It just rules out, 
as an assumption, that LLMs have the linguistic and cognitive capacities we’re trying to figure 
out if they have. In other words, it begs the question.  
 
We start with the ‘just-an-x-fallacy’ because it illustrates how easy it is to slip into very poor forms of 
argumentation when theorizing about the nature of LLMs. One of our goals in what follows is to show 
how we can do better.   
 
Before we turn to that in the next chapter, one more illustration of a poor way of theorizing about the 
nature of LLMs: myopic focus on its occasional failure or poor performance. This sometimes goes 
hand-in-hand with the ‘just-an-x-fallacy’ (e.g., in the quote from Cal Newport above.)

Second Illustration: The Performance - Existence Fallacy 
  
It's tempting to assume that perfect performance signifies a rich mental life, and that mistakes or odd 
behaviors indicate its absence. This is what we’ll loosely call The Performance–Existence Fallacy—the 
mistaken belief that poor performance is evidence of a lack of linguistic or cognitive capacities. 
Consider children. They vividly exemplify minds at work in mysterious and often bizarre ways. They 
can refuse to answer a question and just burst into tears because their imaginary friend told them to. 
Sometimes, they walk away from a conversation to start picking their noses. They often give 
completely irrelevant answers or make things up entirely. They might say things they have no evidence 
for and refuse to defend their views. Yet, despite all this behavior, we don't doubt that children have 
rich inner lives filled with thoughts, feelings, plans, desires, and imagination. Their quirks and 
misunderstandings aren't evidence of an absence of linguistic or cognitive capacities. 
  
Similarly, we know a lot about human cognitive biases and limitations. Adults systematically engage in 
flawed and irrational behavior. There’s a cottage industry of cataloging the numerous ways our minds 
play tricks on us: we remember events that never happened, see patterns in random data, and hold 
onto beliefs despite contradictory evidence. Yet, these flaws don't lead us to conclude that we lack 
beliefs, desires, or minds. Instead, they offer insights into how the human mind functions—not whether 
it exists. 
  
Investigations into the cognitive lives of non-human animals follow the same pattern. We study how 
crows use tools, how dolphins recognize themselves in mirrors, or how octopuses solve puzzles. 
These creatures might not compose symphonies or write novels, but their behaviors indicate 
sophisticated mental processes at work.   
 
Another way to see this point is to imagine a reversed perspective. Think, for example, about some 
aliens observing us humans. These aliens excel at things we're bad at—for example, they have perfect 
recall, can process massive amounts of information, and perform enormously complicated 
computations instantly. At the same time, they're really bad at some things we're pretty good at, like 
social cognition. Suppose they observe this asymmetry in our performance and say, "Look at those 
silly humans; they can't even remember everything that happened 742 days ago! They can’t have a 
mind." This would be very unfair to humans, wouldn’t it? The creatures with a mind will vary a lot in 
what they are good at and what they are bad at.  
    
What the patronising aliens illustrate is that in order to de-anthropomorphize the mind, we need to also 
de-anthropomorphize the kinds of benchmarks that we use to measure minds. Typically, we use 
benchmarks not to check for the presence of a mind, but to check on a system’s capacity to perform 
certain specific tasks. However, if we want to use benchmarks as a way to measure the presence of 
mindedness, we should be open to the idea that there can be very alien minds - minds that don’t have 
the same kinds of contents that we have, and maybe even radically different kinds of mental states. 
These kinds of minds should not be measured by the kinds of benchmarks used for a human mind. Of 
course, as soon as we open the door to alien minds, we face the question of how we can have 
evidence that such minds exist? Again, an excellent question, and one we will try to answer in chapter 
8.

Our Positive View: Method and Substance   
 
Throughout this book, we’ll mix methodological and substantive considerations. The next chapter sets 
the stage for everything that follows. There we set out an idiosyncratic view of how we should start 
when we theorize about LLMs - it's a very unusual view of what should count as important evidence 
when we theorize about the linguistic and cognitive capacities of ChatGPT. One piece of such data is, 
for example, that when we ask ChatGPT what the capital of France is, it understands our question and 
it gives us the correct answer. It tells us that Paris is the capital of France. If it really can do that, and 
infinitely many similar things, then we can make a lot of reasonable inference about what other 
linguistic and cognitive capacities it has. 
 
There are two kinds of pushback against that kind of starting point: (i) Lots of people will reject that as 
an admissible data point. They think it’s either begging the question or to give into some kind of 
illusion. We’ll respond at length to both of those objections. (ii) Others will say that even if it's data, it’s 
defeasible and there’s a ton of other data that undermines it. We also respond to that line of thought.  
 
Our hope is that even if you don’t, at the end of the day, end up convinced by our methodological 
considerations, the pushback will at least trigger some more explicit attention to methodology. That on 
its own, we think, will constitute progress. We suspect that a lot of disagreements in the philosophy of 
AI are the results of lack of methodological coordination.   
 
This book isn’t just about methodology. We are interested in methodology because we want to 
understand AI. In particular the extent to which systems like ChatGPT have linguistic and cognitive 
capacities (understand and speak language, have belief, desires, and intentions, make plans, and act 
on those plans). We think it’s only fair to prepare our readers for the very radical view that we’ll be 
defending. The view we'll tentatively argue for is what we call the Whole Hog Thesis: 
Whole Hog Thesis: ChatGPT is a full-blown linguistic and cognitive agent—on par with 
humans. It can use language meaningfully, making assertions, asking and answering 
questions, offering suggestions, and giving commands. Its use of language reflects underlying 
mental states—it can know and believe things, desire things, and wonder about things. It can 
learn about the world (by being told about it, or reasoning from what it already knows). It can 
make plans and reason about how to achieve them. It can take actions to implement those 
plans.  
The Whole Hog Thesis is controversial, to put it mildly. Eventually, we'll try to convince you that 
objections to the Whole Hog Thesis are grounded, at least in part, in methodological mistakes or 
disagreements. Even if you end up disagreeing with the Whole Hog Thesis, the process of articulating 
exactly where along the way to the Hog you want to get off the boat will, we hope, be illuminating.

It's premature to say too much about the Whole Hog Thesis at this point, but a bit more can help 
prepare readers for what's to come. The Whole Hog Thesis doesn't claim that ChatGPT can do 
everything we can do—that would clearly be claiming too much, because we can, for example, hop on 
one foot, and ChatGPT can't. But it might seem like saying that ChatGPT can do everything that 
makes us distinctly human. (We wouldn't want to put it that way, for reasons we'll explain below.) 
Why take on the Whole Hog at once? Wouldn't it be better to argue first for some small person-like 
feature, such as the ability to have beliefs, and then build from that by arguing for further person-like 
features, and so on? Why take on the entire ambitious target? We're aiming for the Whole Hog 
because the various parts of the hog reinforce each other. Beliefs are mental states that help guide 
action, so a better case for the presence of beliefs can be made where there are actions. Actions are 
doings performed for reasons, so a better case for the presence of actions can be made where there 
are reasons. Reasons are considerations we can state in favor of our actions, so a better case for the 
presence of reasons can be made where there are sayings. Sayings are utterances produced in 
expression of our beliefs and guided by our linguistic knowledge, so a better case for the presence of 
sayings can be made where there are beliefs and knowledge. 
Preliminaries and an Overview of the Book  
Four more preliminaries before we get started:   
 
1. Throughout, we use current LLMs such as ChatGPT as our particular example of AI systems3. That 
choice of examples will no doubt seem dated very soon (maybe even by the time this book is 
published). It’s also inessential for much of what we want to say. Many of the features of current LLMs 
that make the Whole Hog Thesis tempting can be shared by many AI systems. We could, throughout, 
have talked in general terms about ‘AI systems’. While that would have some advantages (and we 
sometimes talk in that way), we think the details might matter in the end4. What we end up saying 
about ChatGPT, for example, might be different in small and not so small ways from what we end up 
saying about other AI systems. Robots, for example, raise a plethora of issues that we’re not 
addressing here. Virtual objects in a VR setting raise other issues again. To make clear that we’re 
open to this possibility—and to encourage further exploration of these issues in connection with new AI 
systems—we’ve chosen to focus on a particular case throughout.

The Hog Argument  
 
Here is a simple argument for a surprising conclusion - we call it the Hog Argument:  
 
1. Observation Premise: LLMs, e.g., ChatGPT, can understand the questions we ask it, and it 
can answer our questions. ChatGPT can also make suggestions, correct its mistakes, tell 
jokes, and provide arguments for what it says. More specifically, if we ask ChatGPT what the 
capital of France is, it understands the question and gives us a correct answer. It tells us that 
Paris is the capital of France. If you ask ChatGPT to help you make a plan for what to do 
during a weekend in Paris, it understands the request and will come up with lots of suggestions 
for what you should do and the order you should do it in. If you ask it to edit something you’ve 
written, it will read over your work and make suggestions for improvements. If you correct 
ChatGPT about something - for example, if it says John McDowell wrote 'The Varieties of 
Reference' and you tell it that this was actually written by Gareth Evans - it will acknowledge its 
mistake, thank you for the correction, and update its response. This is just an infinitesimally 
small sample of things that ChatGPT can do.   
 
2. The Holistic Network Premise. Mental and intentional features are connected. In particular: 
 
●​ Understanding -> Meanings: Anything that understands questions asked in English 
knows what English words mean and how the meanings of English words are combined 
to form meanings of English sentences. 
●​ Answers -> Knowledge: Anything that reliably and correctly answers a variety of 
questions on a topic knows things about that topic. 
●​ Knowledge -> Belief: Anything that knows things also believes those things. 
●​ Answering -> Action: Anything that answers questions performs speech acts, and 
speech acts are particular cases of actions. 
●​ Actions -> Intentions: Anything that performs actions has intentions that set the goal 
and shape the form of those actions. 
●​ Intentions -> Goals: Anything that has intentions has goals of realizing those 
intentions. 
19


## Page 20


●​ Goals and Answers -> Reasoning: Anything that can select actions to realize goals 
and select answers to address questions can reason practically and theoretically. 
 
To be clear: we really mean that the Observation Premise is based on simple observations and that 
those provide the most solid starting points we can have when investigating LLMs. A lot of the book 
will be an elaboration of and defense of that as a legitimate starting point. This first premise gives us a 
mental foot in the door with LLMs. LLMs aren’t just complex computational engines; they are at least a 
little bit like us, because like us they understand and answer questions. The second premise then uses 
that foot to push the door all the way open. Mental and intentional features are deeply interconnected, 
so once you’ve got one of those features, you’re going to end up having a lot of them. 
 
With the door fully open, we conclude that LLMs are creatures much like us:  
 
Conclusion: (The Whole Hog Thesis) LLMs are  full-blown linguistic and cognitive agents. 
They can use language meaningfully, and use the language to make assertions, ask and 
answer questions, and offer suggestions and give commands. Their use of language can 
reflect underlying mental states—they can know and believe things, desire things, and wonder 
about things. They can learn about the world, by being told about the world, or by reasoning 
from things they already know. They can make plans, and reason about how to achieve those 
plans. They can take action to implement those plans8.  
 
The primary goal this book is to defend three claims:  
1. Observations as Starting Point 
 
The Observations Premise is where theorizing about LLMs should start. In arguing for that claim we 
are in stark opposition to almost all other current literature in the philosophy of AI. All other theorists

or alternatively with some data about the computational processes of LLMs. For reasons we spell out 
in Chapter 3, those are not the right places to start if your interest is in understanding the cognitive and 
linguistic capacities of LLMs. This is maybe the most radical claim in this book, and we’ll spend several 
chapters going through various objections to it.

3. LLMs Are Complete Linguistic and Cognitive Agents

against further mental attributions at any point, feeling no pressure to extend the mental talk in the way

Spain is. He feels no pressure to have Claude’s answering backed up by knowledge. 
 
But Adam’s attitude threatens to leave us strangely at sea in working out the mental lives of others. 
Inferring knowledge from successful answering is a commonplace in our interactions with one another. 
Giving students tests to determine what they know makes sense only if their successful answering is 
an indication of what they know. Concluding that someone is knowledgeable about French film, and 
going back to them for more information, because they answered our question about who directed 
Pierrot Le Fou makes sense only if their answering is an indication of what they know. If we can’t move 
from successful answering to knowledge, how are we supposed to discover what other people know? 
(Consider other possible routes: we watch what they do. If they successfully open the safe, they know 
the combination. Or we watch how they’re gathering evidence. If they’re looking out the window, they 
know it’s raining. But these are just more instances of the kinds of connections set out in the Holistic

question about Spain as evidence that you know that Madrid is the capital, but resistant to use

differently, this just looks like special pleading.

answered. If Adam is right, and Claude’s answer doesn’t reveal knowledge, the correct answer looks 
like an unexplained fluke, and absent a systematic explanation why Claude keeps getting it right, it’s 
unclear how to rely on or engage with Claude in the future.

Important: We Are Not Functionalists and Nothing in The Argument 
Presupposes Functionalism  
 
Some readers have worried that we are offering a particularly simple (one might even say crude) 
functionalist-style argument. Some readers think it looks like we’re moving straightforwardly from a 
limited range of (linguistic) behavior to claims about mental states. Indeed, our argument is 
simple—intentionally so—but it is importantly different from functionalism. Ours is an argument from 
evidence, not an argument from metaphysics. We do not claim that this linguistic behavior 
constitutes the having of mental states; rather, we claim that it provides strong evidence for those 
mental states. Thus, unlike functionalism, our position is completely compatible with the existence of 
non-mental functional equivalents of us. 
More specifically, we claim that observations of linguistic interactions with LLMs give strong evidence 
for their linguistic understanding. There is a long tradition of anti-functionalist thought experiments: 
Searle’s (1980) Chinese Room, Block’s Blockhead and Homunculus Head/Chinese Nation (1978), and 
Bender and Koller’s (2021) octopus all aim to show that a system can be functionally equivalent to 
humans at some level of description yet lack genuine mental states. Such arguments, if accepted, 
pose a serious problem for functionalism. At first glance, one might think they would also threaten our 
position, but that would be a misunderstanding. 
These anti-functionalist thought experiments highlight the possibility of creatures whose functional 
profiles closely mimic ours, but which nonetheless lack genuine mental states. That possibility 
undermines a metaphysical thesis that takes a certain functional profile to be constitutive of mental 
states. But our evidential approach is not undermined by this possibility. In general, when we conclude 
that something has a certain property based on evidence, the mere possibility of that evidence 
occurring without the property does nothing to weaken our conclusion. Suppose we see Jones, knife in 
hand, standing over a corpse. We naturally conclude, based on this evidence, that Jones is the 
murderer. Does the mere metaphysical possibility that Jones could be holding the knife without being 
guilty undermine our belief that Jones is the murderer? Clearly not—it doesn't even rationally reduce 
our confidence. Such hypothetical scenarios show only that our evidence could be misleading, 
something we already knew. Similarly, we have strong evidence that LLMs understand us. 
Philosophical thought experiments show, at most, that this evidence could be misleading—but they do 
not undermine the rationality of our belief that LLMs have genuine mental states. 
Of course, the skeptic is free to go beyond mere hypothetical possibility. Perhaps careful consideration 
of a thought experiment like the Chinese Nation reveals some specific feature we possess and the 
Chinese Nation lacks, a feature that explains why the Chinese Nation fails to have genuine mental 
states. If it can be shown that LLMs also lack this specific feature, then the skeptic has provided a

LLMs indeed lack this feature. In later chapters, we discuss various attempts to do precisely this (see 
our discussion of the "Game of Lacks"). But the initial point stands: the familiar anti-functionalist 
thought experiments, taken alone, have no force against our evidential approach.

Later in the book, we’ll engage with objections that question the Observation-Network argument in a

In Defense of Observations 
The direct observations of LLMs' intentional properties discussed in the previous section are not 
merely idiosyncratic experiences of a few individuals. Rather, they reflect a broad consensus among 
the vast community of LLM users. When we interact with systems like ChatGPT or Claude, we find 
ourselves engaging with them as intentional agents capable of understanding, knowledge, and 
purposeful communication. These experiences are so consistent and widespread that they have given 
rise to an established practice of describing LLMs in terms of their linguistic and cognitive capacities. 
This collective attribution of intentional properties to LLMs is a significant data point in its own right, 
one that underscores the reality and robustness of the phenomena we observe in our individual 
interactions with these systems. 
 
The sheer scale and consistency of this practice lend it significant weight as empirical evidence. The 
collective attribution of intentional states to LLMs is a striking fact that demands explanation. Users 
across the globe, in countless linguistic communities, consistently and unhesitatingly describe their 
interactions with these systems using the language of cognition and purposeful communication. We 
say that LLMs understand us, answer our questions, offer suggestions, and even make jokes. This is 
not a case of a few individuals engaging in idiosyncratic discourse. It is the dominant way of 
characterizing what LLMs do, and there is no widely adopted alternative terminology that avoids 
intentional attributions. Crucially, these users classify LLMs in intentional terms with full knowledge that 
they are not biological creatures, lack bodies, don't share our evolutionary history, don't navigate the 
world as we do, and don't participate in our 'way of life'. Despite being well aware of these profound 
differences, people still overwhelmingly converge on treating LLMs as agents with genuine cognitive

meaning. It suggests that our observations of LLMs' intentional features are grounded in a 
special kind of epistemic access, perhaps akin to the perceptual capacities that give us 
knowledge of the external world. John McDowell, for example, argues in "Mind and World" that 
our perceptual experiences provide us with a kind of direct non-inferential grasp of reality.

these systems give us a kind of direct insight into their intentional states. It suggests that our 
experience of LLMs as intentional agents reflects a genuine, direct apprehension of their 
cognitive properties.

observations of mental features as legitimate starting data for investigating LLMs. For our purposes 
here, lightweight interpretation will suffice. It aligns with our common scientific practices, where we 
often start with obvious, apparent features of the phenomena we're studying. It suggests that we don't 
need a special philosophical justification to take our observations of LLMs' cognitive-like behaviors

some kind of direct, non-inferential access to the intentional states of LLMs. If you’re already onboard

If you do see those further features of Claude in this second interaction, that’s great. That will give us 
more data with which to work. But our eventual goal is to go beyond merely observational conclusions 
about the mental lives of LLMs. All we really want from the Observation Argument is to get the mental 
door opened a crack, so that the Whole Hog Argument can push it the rest of the way open. We’re not 
particularly concerned with which particular crack we get, and if you find the door already thrown wide 
open through observation, we’re happy in your case to leave less work for the Whole Hog to do. 
Turning the Tables on the Observation Skeptic: Overgeneration     
 
The Observation Sceptic has to make sure that their objections to our Observation premise doesn’t 
over-generate. The kind of Observations that figure in The Hog Argument is hardly unique to the study 
of LLMs. What we are doing is familiar from very many research domains. Here is a way to think about 
the disagreement between us and the character we call the Observation Sceptic:  
 
-​
We can talk about LLMs in the High-Level/Intentional way. These descriptions use intentional 
language (e.g., "understands," "believes," "intends"), they characterize behaviors in terms of 
goals, knowledge, and reasoning.   
-​
We can talk about LLMs in the Low-Level/Mechanistic way. When we do this, we describe 
computational processes and architecture. It will use technical language (e.g., "processes,"

"computes," "generates") and describe behaviors in terms of algorithms, data structures, and 
mathematical operations 
 
The high-level descriptions attribute mental states and cognitive processes to the LLM, while the 
low-level descriptions focus on the computational mechanisms underlying the observed behaviors. 
The Observation Argument is aimed at giving us not just data, but High-Level data about LLMs. It’s not 
impossible to reach High-Level conclusions from Low-Level starting points, so even if you reject the 
Observation Argument, you might still think that LLMs have high-level features like understanding and 
belief. However, the theoretical road from Low-Level starting points to High-Level ending points is 
much more difficult than the theoretical road from High-Level starting points to even more High-Level 
ending points.  
 
In the human case, for a long time most of our good data was high-level data. The things we knew a 
lot about were people’s beliefs, desires, intentions, and so on. Knowing those things let us make good 
sense of people -- understand why they did what they did, and predict what they were likely to do in 
the future. The low-level data about what was going on computationally or neurochemically in the brain 
was mostly unknown to us, and we didn’t make use of what low-level data we had to understand and 
predict people.  
 
Today, economists explain consumer choices through beliefs and desires; sociologists interpret social 
movements via shared intentions; in daily life, we navigate social interactions by attributing thoughts 
and motives to others. They don’t start by describing brain activity in a purely neurological terminology. 
This isn’t to say it’s impossible or that one couldn’t try, but it’s not considered the most reliable source 
of information about human motivation and behavior. This pervasive use of high-level data isn't a mere 
convenience—it's fundamental to our ability to comprehend and predict human action28. It's our 
primary, indispensable entry point into understanding human behavior and social dynamics. Without it, 
our grasp of the social world would be severely impoverished, if not impossible. When we interact with 
LLMs and detect high-level cognitive features - such as understanding, reasoning, or intentionality - we 
are employing the same kind of detection abilities that we use in these domains.  
  
In short, if you think that only low-level data should be used for theorizing about LLMs, that view 
cannot be based on a general constraint against high-level data in all domains. If someone argues that 
we can only use low-level data for AI, they need to provide special justification for this restriction. It 
can't be because high-level data is inherently inadmissible as scientific data or because our ability to 
observe and recognize high-level features of the world in general is unreliable. To hold that skeptical 
view would undermine more or less all of the social sciences and the humanities.

We’ve argued that there is good high-level data about LLMs, coming (for example) from the 
Observation Argument. Even if we’re right about that, there’s also a worry that the use of that data 
begs the question against our opponents. Sometimes even when we have good data, it would be

easily to skepticism about monster sightings. We need evidence at more of an arm’s length from the 
thing we’re trying to establish -- reports of monster sightings, rather than sightings simpliciter, or 
photographs putatively of the monster. 
 
There is, we think, no prospect of a theoretical project that will convince absolutely anyone. No matter 
what the starting point, there is always the possibility of the skeptic who is so thoroughly entrenched 
that he rejects everything you want to use in building and defending your theory. But the Pro 
Observation Argument starts from reactions that people without strong prior theoretical commitments 
quite ubiquitously have, and that (we suspect) even those with strong prior theoretical commitments 
typically have and suppress. That gives our theoretical projects as much reach as we could reasonably 
hope for.

●​ Question #2: What does it take to understand a question? What is the underlying nature of

3. Third, follow the evidence. If the evidence is that LLMs understand questions, we now have good

consequences. That understanding may then be evidence for additional cognitive features of LLMs.

even when all the evidence is in, conclusions drawn from the evidence are fallible. We’re claiming that 
the evidence supports the claim that LLMs have cognitive features. That claim doesn’t block the 
possibility of stubborn skepticism -- but that’s not a unique feature of thinking about LLMs; stubborn 
skepticism in the face of evidence is always a possibility.

“ChatGPT Understands Questions”: Against  
Metaphorical, Loose Talk, and Pretense Interpretations 
 
The premise Observations in the Observation Argument asserts that people overwhelmingly judge 
LLMs to be understanding and answering their questions. In response to the considerations we gave 
in the previous chapter in support of Observations, it might be responded that people only seem to 
judge in this way, but don’t in fact so judge. This chapter is devoted to that objection. We’ll develop 
some responses to this kind of view and argue that they provide inadequate accounts of the ways that 
we engage with LLMs. 
 
Consider two ways of accounting for our LLM interactions and descriptions: 
 
1.​ Loose Talk: People say that Claude understood their question and told them that Madrid is the 
capital of Spain. But in saying things like this, people are speaking loosely.30 There is a general 
practice of loose talk, in which people say things that are not true, but are close enough to true 
for various practical purposes.31 Someone engaging in loose talk might say “Dinner will be 
served in an hour” not because they take it to be true that dinner will be served in exactly 3600 
seconds, but because an hour is an easily conveyed and conceptualized amount of time, which 
is close enough to the amount of time until dinner is served for many practical purposes.  
 
If claims that Claude understands and answers questions are loose talk, then those claims 
aren’t being made because Claude understands and answers questions, and are not received 
as endorsing that Claude understands and answers questions. And in that case, our normal 
linguistic practices involving LLMs don’t provide the support for Observations that we claimed 
in our earlier discussion. 
 
2.​ Pretense: When people are interacting with LLMs, they are not judging the LLMs to 
understand and answer their questions. Rather, they are pretending that the LLMs are 
understanding and answering. (That pretense then guides both the ways in which they talk 
about the LLMs and the way they interact with the LLMs.)

hour, or in exactly an hour.33 The slack regulators “about”, “roughly”, and “exactly” do further 
specification of how close to truth the claim that dinner will be ready in an hour is.

Conjunctions  
 
Mental and intentional reports about LLMs can be conjoined with mental and intentional reports about

opponent of Observations, is not answering our question, but only doing something loosely related to 
answering. Why then would it be possible to use the same predicate to report on both? Is (the one 
occurrence of) “answered our question” being used loosely or not?

answered our question? 
 
No Natural Metric of Loosening  
 
We’ve been talking about the proposed looseness of “Claude answered our question” by saying that 
Claude did something loosely related to answering. But what does that really mean? In paradigm 
cases of looseness (“Dinner will be ready in an hour”), it’s clear how looseness is introduced. The 
literal semantics picks out a point in time, and looseness allows the use of the sentence so long as the 
time of dinner is within some temporal interval of that point. The metric structure of time provides a 
way of loosening. Similarly with “Jones is six feet tall” and “Smith lives in Austin”, in which the metric 
structure of space provides a way of loosening. But there is no natural metric that provides a “region” 
around the act-type of answering a question. And any attempt just to use some rough-and-ready 
notion of similarity to produce the needed region seems likely to sweep in other act-types that we’d 
never report as answerings. If Claude is, as the opponent claims, incapable of performing any 
intentional acts at all, is Claude’s production of some token sequences really more similar to an

Absence of Precise Talk  
 
Loose talk is derivative from our underlying practice of precise talk. We know what it is to be satisfied 
with proximity to the truth because we already know what outright truth demands, so that we can 
locate the proximity. In the places where loose talk normally appears, we sometimes speak loosely 
and sometimes speak precisely. But if our talk about LLMs is loose talk, it’s a peculiar form of loose 
talk that never has matching precise talk. We never say whatever the putative true thing is that 
underlies our loose claim that Claude answers our question. That’s surprising, if the talk is loose -- but, 
of course, exactly what we’d expect if it isn’t. 
 
It’s not Just About Speech - It’s Also About Observations  
 
The five points above  give us strong reason to doubt that our ordinary claims that LLMs are 
understanding and answering our questions is loose talk. It’s also important to note that even if this 
was loose talk, that fact wouldn’t deeply imperil the case for Observations. Observations is the claim 
that we standardly judge LLMs to be understanding and answering our questions. Our linguistic 
behavior is one tool of seeing that we’re judging in this way, but it’s not the only tool. The case for 
Observations does not rely solely on the way we talk about these systems. Our firsthand experiences 
of interacting with LLMs provide compelling evidence for attributing genuine intentional states to them. 
When we engage with ChatGPT or Claude, we don't merely observe uninterpreted text on a screen. 
We experience a back-and-forth conversation, complete with the give and take of question and 
answer, clarification and elaboration. We apprehend the system as understanding our prompts, 
formulating relevant responses, and even exhibiting curiosity, insight, and creativity in its replies. 
These observations cannot be dismissed as hyperbole or loose talk, because they are observations, 
not talk. They are about the immediate, prereflective way in which we perceive and interact with LLMs, 
not about how we describe that interaction. The fact that we experience these systems as having 
intentional properties is a raw datum, not a manner of speaking. 
 
Moreover, these observations shape and reinforce the way we talk about LLMs. We describe them as 
answering questions, offering suggestions, and engaging in conversation because that is how we 
experience them in our direct interactions. Our talk is not a separate, potentially metaphorical layer 
imposed on top of a non-intentional reality, but a reflection of the intentional properties we apprehend 
in our firsthand dealings with these systems. This connection between our direct observations and our 
linguistic practices provides further support for taking High-Level descriptions of LLMs seriously. If our 
talk of LLMs as exhibiting intentional states were merely loose, we would expect it to come apart from 
our immediate experiences at least some of the time. But in fact, we find a consistent alignment

discourse. 
 
In sum, the experiential evidence reinforces the linguistic evidence from the easy retraction test. Taken 
together, these two lines of argument provide strong support for the claim that High-Level intentional 
descriptions of LLMs are not mere loose talk or verbal flourishes, but are grounded in the observable 
realities of our encounters with these systems. 
 
Against Pretense 
 
Our final complaint about the loose talk strategy is that it was fundamentally misguided, because it 
targeted how we talk about LLMs, but what’s important to the Observation Argument is what we judge 
about LLMs. As long as we’re judging that the LLMs are understanding and answering our questions, 
we have what we need for the Observation Argument, no matter what is going on with our linguistic 
practice in talking about the LLMs. 
 
A promising line of objection to Observations, then, needs to target the full range of our relations to 
LLMs, giving us reason to doubt that our apparent judgments about them, talk about them, and 
observations about them are the genuine judgments, talk, and observations that would suffice to 
support Observations. We’ll now consider a route for developing such a line by claiming that our 
interactions with LLMs are governed by a general practice of pretense. Once the view is developed, 
we think the idea that there is such a widespread pretense is even less plausible than the earlier claim 
that there is widespread loose talk. 
 
The pretense view34 starts with Kendall Walton’s account of prop-based make-believe. In prop-based 
make-believe, the pretenders set up a mapping between items and their features in the environment, 
and pretended items with pretended features in a pretense. The children’s pretend tea party is played 
out using a flat rock that maps to the table of the pretend tea party, and leaves that map to teacups of 
the pretend tea party, and a jug of water that maps to the teapot of the pretend tea party.  
 
Given the mapping, changes in the real world can induce changes in the pretense. When water is 
poured from the jug onto a leaf, it thereby becomes part of the pretend tea party that a teacup has 
been filled with tea. An important result of the mapping in prop-based make-believe is that changes 
can be induced in the pretense that are’t directly chosen or implemented by the pretenders. If a gust of 
wind blows away one of the leaf props, that can suffice to make it part of the pretense that one of the 
party participants has lost their teacup. 
 
This ability of changes in the environment to drive changes in the pretense is then the key to giving a 
pretense account of our interactions with LLMs. LLMs, on this view, in fact just produce text on the 
basis of statistical calculations. They don’t understand inputs or answer questions, and in general are 
completely free of high-level intentional features. But we pretend that they do have mental features,

Can’t Stop  
Even if you think we’re all unknowing and unwilling participants in an inescapable pretense about 
LLMs, the details of our interactions with LLMs don’t fit what we’d expect in a pretense. Prop-based 
pretenses are intellectually demanding, requiring us to track the environment, notice actual changes in 
the environment, read those changes through mappings governing the prop-based nature of the 
pretense, and stay collectively coordinated on resulting changes in what we are pretending to be the 
case. One consequence of the intellectual demands is that pretending is a temporary activity. We 
pretend for a while to be having a tea party, but at some point we stop, because we don’t want to 
sustain the effort of the tracking, mapping, and coordinating demands of preserving the pretense. 
However, the proposed pretense with LLMs isn’t a temporary activity, but apparently a permanent 
feature of our engagement with LLMs. 
 
Lack of Freedom  
The intellectual demands of pretense result in a certain degree of freedom in our pretenses, even our 
prop-based pretenses. Some children are pretending to be knights, using sticks as swords. One of the 
sticks breaks as they pretend to fight. The sticks are props, mapped to swords -- but when a stick 
breaks, there is a choice about how to continue the mapping. The breaking can be incorporated into 
the game, in which case the children are now pretending that one of them is armed with a broken 
sword. The breaking can also be ignored, in which case the children are continuing to pretend that 
they’re all armed with intact swords, but the mapping is modified to map a shorter stick to a sword. 
Props can be dropped from the pretense, added to the pretense, or have their role in the pretense 
radically shifted. (“Now the broom is my dragon, not my horse!) 
 
We don’t see any freedom like this in the putative LLM pretense. The mapping remains stubbornly 
fixed. Claude’s output of “Madrid is the capital of Spain” persistently has the consequence that we 
pretend Claude has said that Madrid is the capital of Spain. We never choose not to incorporate that 
output into the pretense, or take it to add to the pretense that Claude has asked us what our favorite 
Hitchcock movie is. We never add other text, not output by Claude, into the props determining what 
we’re pretending about Claude. All of that is exactly as you’d expect, if we aren’t pretending but 
instead simply judging that Claude is answering. That’s a mysterious constraint on our imagination on 
the pretense view.

How to Understand Hallucinations (and other irrational 
behavior by LLMs)    
 
It’s one thing to say that high-level data is admissible for theorizing about LLMs. It’s another to say 
what the high-level data is. Our discussion of high-level data in the previous chapter brings together 
two claims. First, it’s good theoretical practice in figuring out what LLMs are like to be attentive to and 
include high-level data that comes out of our observations of LLMs, as shaped by our recognitional 
capacities for high-level cognitive features. And second, our observations then provide us with a robust 
body of data showing that LLMs have at least some high-level features, such as question-answering 
and linguistic understanding. We think -- and we’ll develop this thought in subsequent chapters -- that 
the resulting high-level data gives us a powerful starting point for attributing wide-ranging mental 
features to LLMs. 
 
The proponent of low-level theorizing disagrees with our first claim. But there’s also room for someone 
who agrees with our first claim, and agrees that LLMs initially look like they have interesting high-level 
features -- but who then further claims that those initial appearances don’t hold up to a closer look. 
We’re misled, says this critic, by taking too quick a look at a small and unrepresentative sample of the 
LLM output. Once we look more broadly and more carefully, we’ll see that the LLM doesn’t, in fact, 
look like it has high-level features. 
 
To be maximally clear, we should emphasize that there’s more than one way to describe the 
argumentative situation: 
 
1.​ Observing some LLM behavior gives us the high-level data that the LLM has understanding 
(etc). But observing other LLM behavior gives us the conflicting high-level data that the LLM 
lacks understanding. We still have the data from the first observation, but the data from the 
second observation is more robust than that from the first, so it defeats any inference from the 
first data point to a theory on which LLMs have human-like mental features. 
2.​ Observing some LLM behavior in isolation gives us the high-level data that the LLM has 
understanding (etc). But observing the same LLM behavior in the context of a wider range of 
LLM behavior causes the first observation to cease, so that even the original LLM behavior no 
longer appears to be understanding (etc). We’re left with no data in favor of human-like mental 
features in LLMs. 
3.​ Observing some LLM behavior in isolation seems to give us the data that the LLM has 
understanding (etc). But further observations allow us better to understand and reflect on our 
original observations and realize that those observations didn’t give us the data that the LLM 
54


## Page 55


has understanding (etc). Again we’re left with no data supporting a theory on which LLMs have 
human-like mental features. 
 
Everything we’ll say about these robustness questions in this chapter is compatible with any of these 
three ways of characterizing the argumentative situation. 
 
In short, our focus in this chapter is on work that says, “Let us show you more about the full range of 
LLM behavior, so that you’ll appreciate better how non-human-like, and unintelligent, that behavior is”. 
There is a robust body of work falling under that general banner. In this chapter we’ll look at three 
examples, and then develop strategies for responding to these challenges. Our central conclusion is 
that it’s very difficult to develop the challenges in a way that doesn’t overgeneralize and undermine our 
evidence in favor of the mental states we discover in each other. 
 
First Challenge: Hallucinations 
 
LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude look like they are good at answering questions. Answering and 
understanding questions was our first example of high-level data about LLMs that we can discover 
through observation and that can then shape our theorizing about LLMs. Perhaps the initial 
appearance of answering and understanding doesn’t survive more detailed examination. The concern 
is that we have cherry picked responses from LLMs and ignored the cases where LLMs give 
incoherent replies. One striking feature of LLMs is their tendency to produce outputs that are 
inconsistent, nonsensical, or untethered from reality. These "hallucinations" range from minor factual 
errors to bizarre and incoherent statements. Here are some examples of this kind of rogue LLM 
behaviour:  
  
-​
Contradictory statements: An LLM might confidently assert a claim in one context and then 
contradict it in another. For example, when asked "What is the capital of France?" the LLM 
might correctly answer "Paris" in one conversation, but then later assert that "The capital of 
France is Rome" in a different context. 
-​
Factual errors: LLMs can sometimes generate outputs that are clearly false or at odds with 
basic facts about the world. For instance, an LLM might claim that "The moon is made of green 
cheese" or that "Elephants are smaller than ants."  
-​
Category errors: LLMs can produce outputs that confuse or blur the boundaries between 
different conceptual categories. For example, an LLM might respond to the prompt "Describe a 
typical day in the life of a car" by saying something like "The car wakes up early in the morning, 
brushes its tires, and has a hearty breakfast of gasoline before driving off to work." 
-​
Nonsensical statements: LLMs can generate statements that are grammatically well-formed but 
semantically meaningless or absurd. For instance, an LLM might say something like "Colorless 
green ideas sleep furiously" or "The square root of purple is the smell of yesterday's dreams."  
-​
Ungrounded speculation: LLMs can sometimes generate outputs that are wild speculations or 
flights of fancy, untethered from reality or any credible source of information. For example, an 
LLM might claim that "Scientists have discovered a new species of unicorn living in the forests

toasters." 
 
We need to be cautious about what we conclude from LLM hallucinations. The weakest lesson that we 
could take from hallucinations is that LLMs sometimes mis-answer questions. But mis-answering 
questions is a form of answering questions. It’s still a high-level feature, and could still be used as data 
supporting a broader theory of LLMs as having human-like minds. They might be minds full of 
misinformation, but minds nonetheless.

Generalizing, the thought is that hallucinations show that LLMs do not have a fully consistent and 
reliable grasp of the world, and that their outputs can sometimes be untethered from reality. The 
objection can be formulated as follows: 
 
1.​ Genuine intentional states (answering, understanding, believing, knowing) are characterized by 
a degree of consistency, coherence, and responsiveness to reality. 
2.​ LLMs frequently produce outputs that are inconsistent, incoherent, and unresponsive to reality 
(i.e., hallucinations). 
3.​ Therefore, LLMs do not have genuine intentional states. 
 
The force of this objection lies in the assumption that a system with genuine beliefs, desires, and 
intentions should not exhibit the kind of erratic and disconnected behavior we observe in LLMs. If an 
LLM can confidently assert a claim in one context and then contradict it in another, or if it can generate 
statements that are clearly false or meaningless, this seems to suggest that it does not have a stable 
and coherent set of intentional states guiding its behavior. The lack of coherence creates a serious 
challenge to seeing the LLM as engaging in intelligent behavior. Immediately after ChatGPT informed 
us about David Yeates’ impressive feat of channel hopping, we asked it if it was possible to cross the 
English Channel by foot. Its answer:

speaking in the sense of intentionally transmitting information in a planned and considered way.

However, there is work purporting to show that the initial appearances are misleading, and that LLMs 
are in fact very bad at planning. In “On the Planning Abilities of Large Language Models”, Valmeekam 
et al report on a larger-scale testing of whether LLMs can plan. They give LLMs hundreds of “Block 
World” puzzles to solve, such as: 
 
A red block, a yellow block, and an orange block are each directly on the table. On top of the 
yellow block is a blue block. When your hand is empty, you can pick up any block that does not 
have another block on top of it. When you have a block in your hand, you can put that block on 
the table or on top of any other block that does not have another block on top of it. Give a plan 
for rearranging the blocks so that the yellow block is on top of the blue block. 
 
They then find that LLMs are catastrophically bad at constructing workable plans for these small and 
apparently easy puzzles. ChatGPT-3, for example, had a 1% success rate in producing workable 
plans, compared to a 78% success rate for humans. 
 
The suggestion then is that once we see this wider range of examples, and don’t just look at a few 
cherry-picked successes, we’ll no longer be inclined to think that LLMs can plan. Our planning 
detectors aren’t triggered by the wider range -- in light of it, we’re inclined to say that even what we 
initially took to be instances of planning weren’t. We don’t just think that the LLMs sometimes plan (in 
the good cases) and sometimes don’t (in the bad cases). Rather, we think the LLMs are always doing 
something else, and that that something sometimes can have a potentially deceptive planning-like 
appearance (in the “good” cases, which on this view aren’t particularly good) and other times lacks that 
deceptive planning-like appearance (in the “bad” cases, which on this view aren’t particularly bad).

Reply: All of This, and More is True of Humans!     
  
The examples we’ve been discussing are drawn from a vast literature examining cases in which LLMs 
in various ways go catastrophically wrong. Many AI researchers draw from this vast literature the 
conclusion that the initial appearance of high-level features in and behavior by LLMs is an illusion. Our 
normal interactions with LLMs provide us with a cherry-picked collection of cases, because we don’t 
normally put LLMs into the situations that reveal what is behind the curtain. We don’t normally ask 
LLMs about devious versions of familiar problems, or give them tasks with deliberately abstracted 
terminology. We are busy asking LLMs to write memos (and undergraduate papers), to provide 
simplified explanations when Wikipedia gets too complicated, and to provide movie recommendations. 
All of that keeps us in the illusory territory, and so it’s not until we look at the literature that the illusion 
is shattered and the high-level appearances are dispelled. 
 
Call this the simple argument from failure. An initial appearance of high-level features is dispelled once 
we look more broadly and discover failures to perform in the way the high-level feature would predict. 
We are unpersuaded by the simple argument. The vast literature is interesting and important, but not 
in the way that people have been taking it to be. When we’re thinking about things clearly, our 
observations of high-level features survive the broader look at LLM behavior, and the broader look just 
provides us with tools for better understanding the kind of mental features that LLMs have. 
 
The core of our response, and our proposed way of thinking clearly about observing LLMs, is the 
thought that all of this is true of us, and more. The skeptic’s challenge overgeneralizes -- we, too, are 
vulnerable to catastrophic failures under the right circumstances. But awareness of that vulnerability 
doesn’t change the data that we manifestly have high-level mental features or the appropriateness of 
adopting on the basis of that data a rich theory of mind for understanding other people. If our 
mindedness can survive the catastrophic failures, so too can the mindedness of LLMs. 
 
That’s quick, and the response needs to be developed carefully to be fully responsive to the vast 
literature. Perfect success is an absurd standard to require before attributing high-level features to a 
system. That should be clear already -- perfect success is an absurd standard to require for any 
capacity -- but it’s made even clearer by the obvious observation that we, too, are fallible. So the 
objection can’t just be that LLMs go wrong. The more sophisticated thought is that they go too wrong. 
 
The thought that going too wrong can undermine claims to a capacity is a reasonable (if not inevitable) 
one. A runner can be Olympic-caliber even if sometimes his mile runs take five or six minutes. But if he 
never completes a mile in under ten minutes, it’s going to be hard to see how we can take seriously 
the claim to Olympic-caliber running capacities. Similarly, plausibly there is some performance 
threshold on cognitive tasks, below which claims to the high-level features can’t be taken seriously.

## Page 62


But wherever that threshold is, it had better be above us. If the performance level of the LLMs isn’t 
worse than what we’d expect from, say, an eight year old child, then it’s hard to see how that 
performance level can warrant doubt about the high-level feature in the LLM. Consider a cursory 
examination of typical eight year olds. They frequently make contradictory statements, make factual 
mistakes, and very often just make stuff up. Lots of their conversations will be filled with irrelevancies. 
Often they refuse to answer questions and instead pick their noses or just walk away. Here is what we 
take to be indisputable: that kind of behaviour does not support the conclusion that they lack beliefs, 
fail to be intentional agents, fail to understand language, or that they lack a rich cognitive life.  
 
Of course, it’s not just the child version of humans that instantiate these cognitive deficiencies. Lots of 
adults have incoherent, contradictory, and false beliefs. Throughout most of human history, we have 
had completely false beliefs about how the natural world works and what humans are. Similarly, our 
moral and political views have been abhorrent. We’re subject to well studied systematic biases that not 
even the most rational of us can get rid of. Our cognitive states are easily influenced by chemicals, by 
social pressure, by lack of sleep, by aging, and many other non-rational factors. Nonetheless, adults 
have beliefs and are paradigms of rational cognitive agents. And the kind of irrationality we exhibit 
often presuppose that we have intentional states. The confirmation bias, for example, presupposes 
that people have existing beliefs that they are motivated to defend. The sunk cost fallacy presupposes 
that people have desires and intentions that influence their decision-making, even when those desires 
and intentions are misguided. If we are willing to attribute intentional states to humans despite their 
manifest inconsistencies and biases, then we should not dismiss the possibility of intentional states in 
LLMs on the basis of their hallucinations and inconsistencies. 
 
Keeping in mind the low bar that we have collectively set for the presence of the high-level features, 
we’ll then consider three ways of making the simple argument more sophisticated: 
1.​ Below the Bar: on examination, the success rate of LLMs is below that even of the eight year 
old child. It’s below the level we’d accept in any person as consistent with the presence of the 
high-level feature. 
2.​ Off the Rails: the problem with the LLMs isn’t that they make more mistakes than we’d accept 
from an eight year old. It’s rather that when they do make mistakes, their mistakes are 
catastrophic in a way that we don’t expect even from an eight year old. It’s the catastrophic 
nature of the failure that persuades us that the LLM lacks the high-level feature. 
3.​ Bizarre Error: the problem with the LLMs isn’t the number or the severity of the errors they 
make, but the particular distribution of errors they make. They fail in easy paradigm cases, 
cases where we’d never accept error even from an eight year old. It’s seeing them fail in these 
easy cases that persuades us that the LLM lacks the high-level features. 
Each of these three ways of making the simple argument more sophisticated fails, but there are helpful 
lessons to be drawn from seeing why the more sophisticated variants fail. 
Below the Bar 
Let’s start with Below the Bar. The first thing to say about this form of the argument is that it is of 
course contingent on empirical details both about LLM success and failure levels and about our 
success and failure levels. We saw earlier than GPT 4.0, when trained on “A is B” data, drops from a

considerable interest for investigating potential LLM mental features.

Off the Rails 
The second thought for making the simple argument more sophisticated is that the problem with the 
LLMs is that they go catastrophically wrong. Consider hallucinations. When ChatGPT gives us a 
detailed explanation of Dave Yeates channel-hopping at low tide from England to France, we don’t 
think: well, that’s one strike for the LLM, corresponding to the strike that the human got when he said 
that the capital of Missouri was St. Louis. Saying that St. Louis is the capital of Missouri is a small 
mistake. Jefferson City is hard to remember, and St. Louis is a plausible candidate -- it’s a city in 
Missouri, and a large and well-known city, and capitals are often large and well-known cities. But 
saying that Dave Yeates crossed the English Channel at low tide is a catastrophic failure. It’s nowhere 
near the right answer.

difficult situation. 
Bizarre Error 
We’ve considered two improvements on the simple argument: the problem with LLMs is that they 
produce too many errors, or that the errors they produce are too catastrophic. The final possibility we’ll 
consider is that the problem is which specific tasks they fail at.

Consider again the human case. If we ask someone to add 3477 and 2508 and they give us the 
answer 5975, their answer may not much undermine our confidence that they can add. It’s an error, 
but it’s an error in a hard case. If, on the other hand, we ask them to add 1 and 1 and they give us the 
answer 3, we may start to doubt that they can add. It’s only one mistake, and it’s not that it’s a 
catastrophically bad answer -- it’s just that it was such a core case of adding that a failure there in 
particular threatens attribution of ability. 
 
Sometimes errors are explicable because the task is hard. At other times, errors are explicable 
because circumstances are unfavorable. If someone has a nearby tree in the center of their visual 
field, is asked whether it’s more or less than ten feet tall, and gets it wrong, that error might undermine 
our confidence in their visual abilities. But if there is heavy fog, or their eyes have been recently 
dilated, or the tree is in an Ames room, we might be less bothered by the error. Those are the kinds of 
circumstances in which we expect people to make mistakes. 
 
Another lesson we might draw from the vast literature, then, is that it shows us that LLMs make 
mistakes at the wrong times. Even if they’re largely successful, when they do go wrong, it’s a going 
wrong in cases that aren’t typical error-provoking cases. And so we’re left with no good explanation for 
the error. Rather than positing a high-level ability that fails for no reason, we prefer to withhold the 
high-level ability and look for a low-level explanation. 
 
The Reverse Curse examples might cause us to suspect bizarre error. The inference from A=B to B=A 
isn’t a hard one. There are no obvious obstacles standing in the way of performance. And yet the 
LLMs fail to make the inferential transition. When a human misperforms under those circumstances, 
we think, we’d doubt some kind of reasoning competence. And so we have similar doubts about the 
LLM. Similarly for some Block World planning failures. A plan to reverse the order of a two-block stack 
isn’t hard. There aren’t any obvious obstacles standing in the way of performance. When a human 
couldn’t come up with a workable plan for such a case , we’d doubt some kind of planning 
competence. And so we have similar doubts about the LLM. 
 
Some of the responses to the bizarre error variant of the simple argument we’ve seen already. Let’s 
not underestimate the human ability occasionally to misperform even at very simple tasks, and even 
without good exculpatory circumstances. Sometimes we just mess up, and there’s no good 
explanation. And let’s not assume too quickly that there is no exculpation of the LLM. Perhaps when 
we understand its mental life better, we’ll be able better to see why it’s mis-performing when it does. 
(Note the special danger of the skeptic’s argument here. If exculpating the LLM requires understanding 
its mental life -- as it often does with us -- then the skeptic’s argument followed too quickly threatens to 
hide from sight the very thing that would defuse that argument.) 
 
We can also helpfully think about bizarre errors in terms of normality. The human visual system is 
designed to work under certain normal conditions. When those conditions are violated, we get 
systematic errors in visual perception. Consider the Hollow Mask illusion. A concave mask of a human 
face appears to be instead a convex face. Roughly, the human visual system is designed to work in an 
environment predominantly populated with convex, rather than concave, objects, and so interpretation 
of the visual stimulus starts on an assumption of probable convexity. In normal circumstances, that’s a

## Page 68


great method for getting accurate visual experiences -- but when things are abnormal, and there are 
concave objects around, things go wrong. 
 
We don’t think of the visual system as mis-performing when the Hollow Mask illusion occurs. We 
misperceive, but not because of a failure of vision. Rather, we fail because we were put in the wrong 
kind of circumstance. Ames rooms provide similarly abnormal circumstances leading to misperception, 
as do situations exploiting the prior expectation that lighting comes predominantly from above. 
 
If we’re told about some perceptual failures by a person, and we want to know how much those 
failures undermine our confidence that they can see, we need to know about the circumstances of the 
failures. If the failures are failures in abnormal circumstances, we may discount them and remain 
confident in the visual ability. If the failures are failures in normal circumstances, they will do more to 
undermine confidence. 
 
So perhaps the vast literature is showing us failures of typical cognitive tasks in normal circumstances. 
The Block World planning failures are worrying  not (entirely) because they are numerous, or because 
the bad plans are particularly catastrophic, but because these are normal planning situations. There’s 
no excuse for failure here. 
 
But there’s an assumption behind that line of reasoning that can be rejected -- that what is normal for 
us is also normal for LLMs. What’s normal for us is (roughly) a function of the conditions under which 
our capacities were evolutionarily selected for. But LLMs have no evolutionary history, but instead a 
design and training history. Perhaps that history imparts its own standard of normality. If what’s normal 
for LLMs is different from what’s normal for us, then we can’t quickly conclude from the vast literature 
that we’re seeing extensive LLM failure under normal circumstances, leading to an undermining of our 
impression of the LLMs as having high-level features. 
 
When Kahnemann and Tversky showed us extensive ways in which human cognitive performance 
deviated from the standards of ideal rationality, we didn’t take their research to show that people had 
no mental lives at all. Rather, we took their research to show us more about the nature of our mental 
lives. We learned that much of our cognition uses quick-and-dirty heuristics and biases that work well 
in normal circumstances but can fail badly in abnormal circumstances. 
 
The optimistic way to think about the vast literature, then, is that it’s not showing that LLMs lack 
intelligence, planning, reasoning, and other mental capacities. Rather, it’s the beginning of LLM 
cognitive science, letting us start to figure out what counts as normal circumstances for LLM 
reasoning. If Kahnemann and Tversky helped us see the difference between System 1 and System 2 
cognitive faculties in humans, perhaps we’re on our way to finding System 3 in LLMs. Of course, if the 
optimistic thought is the right one, it’s going to mean that the LLM high-level features are in some ways 
importantly different from our high-level features.

Clever Hans, Clever Mathematician 
 
An aspect of much of the vast literature that we’ve only touched on briefly is the tendency to provide 
alternative explanations. Valmeekam et al, for example, give us an alternative picture of LLMs as 
engaging in approximate recall of planning talk in the training data, rather than as planning. The 
alternative explanations play a role in a general strategy for thinking about failures. 
 
When a putative ability fails to come off properly, we have a choice. First option: we continue to 
attribute the ability, but regard it as fallible. Second option: we reject the previous ability attribution, and 
switch to attributing a weaker ability. Both options come with costs and benefits. First option: the 
stronger ability is more powerfully explanatory and predictive. But the failures of the ability reduce the 
scope of the explanations and predictions, and make them riskier. Second option: the weaker ability 
can be used in explanation and prediction more reliably and in a wider range of circumstances, but it’s 
a less powerful tool, so we won’t be able to explain or predict as much when we can use the tool.  
 
How to make the choice may then depend on the details of the failure. We hire a safecracker to help 
with our bank robbery. We get to the scene of our first heist, and he is unable to open the safe. What to 
make of that failure? Perhaps on examination we conclude that the safecracker is able to open 
mechanical and digital locks, but unable to open biometric locks. We’ve retreated to a weaker ability, 
but the weaker ability remains predictively and explanatorily useful. We can plan around that ability, 
and pick which banks to rob. But perhaps on examination we can’t find any robust and interesting 
explanation for the safecracking failure. The drill bit slipped at a crucial moment, the safecracker 
couldn’t quite make out the muffled sound of the tumblers moving. Why? We’re not really sure. We 
could still retreat to a weaker ability -- the safecracker has the ability to go through safecracking 
motions, the ability to try to open locks, or perhaps some massively disjunctive ability to open these 
and these and these locks, but not those and those. These weaker abilities will be safer -- our 
explanations and predictions will be less often frustrated by failures of ability. But they’re also less 
useful. It’s hard to plan heists just knowing that you have a safecracker who can go through 
safecracking motions. 
 
There’s no general recipe here. We need at each point to think through the whole theoretical package 
and see whether we’re going to get a better overall picture of the world if we take the higher-level path 
or if we take the lower-level path. But we need to avoid the temptation to think that the possibility of 
citing the lower-level ability automatically forecloses the possibility of citing the higher-level ability. That 
temptation is the “Just an X” fallacy appearing again. 
 
Valkeeman et al give the approximate recollection picture with the goal of further undermining our 
confidence in LLMs as planners. Approximate recollection gives an alternative explanation -- a 
mechanism that could produce the appearance of planning without the reality of planning. But why 
should we think that we don’t have the reality? Kambhampti points us to the example of Clever Hans. 
Clever Hans appeared to be a horse able to do arithmetic. But on examination, it was discovered that 
Clever Hans was just responding to unintentional signals from his trainer -- ceasing the stomping of his 
hood when his trainer knew that he had stomped the right number of times, and subtly altered his body

## Page 70


posture. It wasn’t Clever Hans that could do arithmetic, it was the trainer. Clever Hans only had the 
ability to respond to feedback from the trainer. Seeing how Clever Hans did the trick changed our 
picture of what the trick was -- from “adding horse” to “trainer tracking horse”. Similarly, seeing how the 
LLM turns the trick is supposed to change our picture of what the trick is, from “planning machine” to 
“imitation of planning talk machine”. 
 
But maybe we should think that Clever Hans can add -- that the horse has just found a different 
method from us for adding. We don’t in fact want to go that far, but consider instead Clever 
Mathematician. Clever Mathematician does his math largely by working off the feedback of his fellow 
mathematicians -- but in a much more fine-grained way than Clever Hans. When Clever 
Mathematician starts to prove a theorem, he makes a few initial chalk marks on the blackboard, 
tracking how the audience responds. Their eyes light up when he starts to draw a line tangent to the 
circle, so he continues that line. The line drawn, the audience is better able to see what’s going on 
geometrically, and the quality of their responses improves. Clever Mathematician makes a few more 
moves, responding as he goes to the changing audience feedback. 
 
Clever Hans can’t perform addition by carrying, or show that addition is commutative. His 
mathematical abilities are very limited, because he’s sensitive only to a small range of reactions from 
his trainer. But Clever Mathematician can prove lemmas and use the lemmas to prove theorems -- he 
just proves the lemmas by gradually accumulating audience feedback, and uses the lemmas to prove 
the theorem by seeing that the audience approves of his moves toward so using it. At some point, 
Clever Mathematician is just a mathematician. Maybe that’s not the only way to be a mathematician, 
but it looks like it is one way. We’d suggest that lots of our cognitive abilities are fully or partially 
outsourced in this way. The fact that there’s the Clever-Hans-style explanation for what Clever 
Mathematician is doing doesn’t mean that what Clever Mathematician is doing isn’t also math. Doing a 
low-level thing can just be a way of doing a high-level thing. Approximate recalling of planning talk can 
just be a way of planning. 
 
We want to be clear that this isn’t an anything-goes, blank check validation of high-level attributions to 
LLMs. We’re not saying that just any discovery about LLM behavior can be written off as mere 
abnormal failures that don’t count against high-level features, or that low-level explanations can never 
defeat high-level attributions. ELIZA is relevantly different from LLMs. ELIZA is like Clever Hans -- it 
has a low-level ability that produces a simulation of a high-level thing, but the low-level ability is 
extremely domain-specific and inflexible. It can’t change its behavior much, and it changes it in a very 
coarse-grained way in reaction to feedback. The LLM’s simulation of the high-level ability is much 
more robust. The full range of possible linguistic products can result from that simulation, and the LLM 
is remarkably sensitive to fine details of the simulation. At this point, we suggest, the simulation has 
become the reality.

The Game of Lacks: An Introduction    
 
In Chapter 2, we outlined three kinds of responses to the Hog Argument, the Reductionist, The 
Atomist, and The Humanist. As a reminder, here is the latter:  
 
The Humanist. The humanist accepts the Holistic Network Assumption, but claims that LLMs 
can’t have sophisticated mental states like knowledge or plans. LLMs can’t have these 
sophisticated states because they are too unlike us in various ways. 
 
Responding to the humanist will take a general form that we’ll call the Game of Lacks. The Game of 
Lacks is a general argumentative structure that can show up whenever it’s claimed that some object 
doesn’t have some feature because it lacks a necessary condition for having that feature. It’s 
particularly center stage in discussion with the humanist, because the humanist rejects attribution of 
various mental features to LLMs on the grounds that the LLMs are insufficiently like us -- spelled out, 
that complaint turns into citing various putative necessary conditions for the mental states that the 
LLMs lack. 
 
Consider a particular case. Butlin and Viebahn (2023) argue that LLMs cannot assert, because “our 
view is that to assert a system must be capable of being sanctioned for failing to meet the standards of 
this practice”, and they think that LLMs are incapable of being sanctioned because for sanctions to be 
sanctions, they must be bad for the sanctioned creature, but LLMs “do not have interests of their own, 
and as a result nothing can be good or bad for them.”47 
 
Butlin and Viebahn’s argument is the first move in a Game of Lacks. The general form of a first move, 
for the specific case of LLMs, is then: 
 
-​
A large language model can’t have some feature F because it lacks some necessary condition 
L for having F.  
 
Here are some examples of how that dialectic is played out over and over again in the current 
literature on LLMs:  
 
●​ LLMs can’t fully understand languages, because they don’t have our sensory organs.  
●​ LLMs can’t have beliefs because they lack a concept of truth, or don’t aim at the truth.  
47 Notice that there are three “lack” arguments embedded here. There are no LLM assertions due to the lack of 
LLM sanctions. There are no LLM sanctions due to the lack of things good or bad for LLMs. And there are no 
things good or bad for LLMs due to the lack of LLM interests. 
72


## Page 73


●​ LLMs can’t perform speech acts because they can’t be governed by norms.  
●​ LLMs can’t have beliefs, because they aren’t ‘connected to the world’ in the right way  
●​ LLMs can’t understand and speak English because they lack the required inner states, e.g., 
intentions.  
 
The Game of Lacks involves assessing such claims. If the alleged lack is both real and a necessary 
condition for having beliefs/performing speech act/understanding English, then the Hog is refuted. We 
would then have to retreat to a more limited version of the Whole Hog Thesis, something we think is 
very difficult because we think the parts of the hog are intimately connected. Take away one and the 
entire organism falls apart. So responding to Lacks is crucial in our defense of the Whole Hog.  
Two Moves in a Game of Lacks  
 
The claim of a lack is the first move in a Game of Lacks. The remainder of the game is defensive, 
arguing that feature F is indeed present. There are two defensive moves in a Game of Lacks that we 
will make repeated use of:  
 
Not Needed: This response agrees that something is missing, but claims that the missing thing is no 
lack, because the thing missing doesn’t matter to possession of the feature in question. A “Not 
Needed” response to Butlin and Viebahn’s lack argument, for example, could agree that LLMs can’t be 
sanctioned, but then deny that sanctionability is necessary for assertion. Of course, mere denial isn’t 
enough to make a persuasive case. The respondent needs to give some positive reason for thinking 
that sanctionability isn’t necessary. We’ll make especially heavy use of two forms of positive reason: 
●​ Known Examples: If we already have examples of assertion without sanctionability, then we 
know that the lack of sanctionability can’t block the possibility of assertion, and we can set 
aside the lack argument. More generally, when L is bruited as a lack preventing possession of 
feature F, citing a known example of a thing that has F despite lacking L will block the 
objection. 
 
The central and most important source of Known Examples is, of course, us. We know that we 
have lots of mental features, so if something is lacking in us, that lack had better not be a 
barrier to possession of the mental feature.48 And we know that we are a pretty diverse group. 
Humans have an enormous range of physical capacities, cognitive capacities, emotional 
capacities, interests, projects, environments, histories, and cultures. A proposed lack that’s a 
barrier to, say, asserting that shows up in even one person in that huge diversity is a lack that

A second form of “Needed, But Present” response plays an especially important role in our discussion. 
Suppose a move is made in the Game of Lacks. Our opponent objects to our claim that an LLM has 
some feature F, claiming that the lack of some L makes it impossible or implausible that the LLM has F. 
A proponent of The Hog could reply by showing that there are reasonable ways to have F without 
having L. But another option is to accept the connection between F and L, but then turn the argument 
around. Rather than accept that the lack of L is a reason for doubting the presence of F, we respond 
that the presence of F is reason to accept the presence of L. 
 
This move can look illegitimate. Surely we can’t just respond to objections by turning the objections 
into further arguments for our larger position. But in fact the move is familiar and acceptable in many 
simple cases. Consider an example:   
 
We have presented an initial case that Smith murdered Jones. Now a skeptic makes a move in 
the Game of Lacks. Jones, they say, was shot, which means the murderer must own a gun. But 
Smith doesn’t own a gun. So Smith can’t be the murderer. But of course it’s a perfectly 
reasonable response to the skeptic to say that they’ve given us reason to think that Smith does 
own a gun. After all, we already thought Smith was the murderer, and if the murderer must own 
a gun, then Smith must own a gun. 
 
The skeptic offers a modus tollens. If Smith murdered Jones, then Smith has a gun; Smith doesn’t 
have a gun; therefore, Smith didn’t murder Jones. We are suggesting ponens-ing on the skeptic’s 
tollens. We do think that Smith murdered Jones, but haven’t considered the question of whether Smith 
owns a gun. The skeptic wants to modus tollens on that conditional, but we don’t have to follow suit. 
We can modus ponens on the same conditional, and conclude that Smith owns a gun. 
 
Whether to ponens or tollens on a conditional “if A, then B” will depend on the comparative evidential 
strengths of A and not-B. If we’ve made a really good case that A, and the case that not B is flimsy, 
we’d be foolish to give up our commitment to A because it’s been anti-correlated with a poorly 
supported claim. So if we had a signed confession from Smith, and the only reason for thinking that 
Smith didn’t own a gun was the statistical fact that under 50% of people in the area own guns, we 
should stick with our view that Smith is the murderer, and conclude that he’s a gun-owning murderer. 
 
If, however, there’s a powerful case that not-B, the skeptic has a stronger point, and it will be more 
reasonable to give up the commitment to A. If our reason for thinking that Smith was the murderer is 
that he owed Jones money, and if a careful search of Smith’s house and local gun shop records turns 
up no gun or gun purchase, perhaps it’s time to consider the idea that Smith is innocent. 
 
We’ll find that in many cases ponens-ing is the right response to lacks, because the skeptic doesn’t 
have much to say in support of the proposed lack. We observe that Claude answers our questions. 
The skeptic objects that Claude can’t be answering questions, because answering questions requires 
understanding the language, and Claude doesn’t understand the language. But we have strong 
evidence in favor of the view that Claude answers questions -- just look and see (as we’ll develop in 
more detail in the next chapter), and you’ll find question-answering all over. If the skeptic has nothing 
comparable to offer in favor of the view that Claude doesn’t understand -- if, for example, it’s just a

## Page 77


“default skepticism” based on the absence of a positive case for understanding, or just a feeling that 
understanding isn’t the sort of thing LLMs can do -- then ponens-ing should clearly win out, and we 
should extend our commitment from question-answering to understanding.  
 
If the skeptic has some substantial case against LLM understanding, the situation is more complicated. 
Now we’ll proceed to play a new Game of Lacks on the topic of understanding. If we can win that 
game, we’ll have defeated the skeptical case against understanding, and now our initial support for

understanding -- and we’ll then win the first  Game of Lacks over question-answering as well. 
 
From this point of view the humanist, and the “lacks” arguments that he advances, turns out to be our 
ally against the humanist. The success of the Whole Hog argument depends on the strength and 
breadth of the Holistic Network Assumption. We need many connections between mental and 
intentional states, so that our commitments to some mental and intentional features of LLMs spread 
inexorably to cover more and more of the Hog. Every time the humanist gives us a “lack” argument (to 
which we don’t want to make a “Not Needed” response), he thereby gives us another connection to 
strengthen the Holistic Network Assumption. 
 
This bootstrapping method plays a key role in our positive case for The Hog Thesis. The direct support 
for The Hog comes partly from observations. But we don’t claim that every cognitive feature of AIs can 
be observed. Perhaps we observe the AI answering questions, but we don’t observe it having a plan 
for answering the questions. The case for the plan then comes from the integrated nature of the Hog -- 
the Holistic Network Assumption gives us connections among the various cognitive features, such as 
connections between answering questions and having plans for answering questions. Bootstrapping 
then lets us extend the hog along the network, by ponens-ing from one feature to another.

that is false. In short, the idea that a concept of truth is important to linguistic competence is a natural 
one backed by many of the leading theories of linguistic and cognitive competence.

4.​ ChatGPT has linguistic competence

testing uses weights that are weakly inherited from the previous weighting.

Why You Should Ignore the Octopus and Searle’s Chinese Room  
 
We don’t have a view about the octopus and we don’t need to. Here is the dialectic situation we’re in: 
Hundreds of millions of people, through literally billions of interactions, think that ChatGPT can 
understand their question, answer their questions, give them suggestions etc. They do so, knowing full 
well that it isn’t a biological system, that it doesn’t move around the world etc. So that’s extremely 
powerful data. In Chapters 2, 3, and 4 we explain why we think that should be taken at face value and 
why it should serve as a starting point for theorizing about contemporary LLMs.

weird little story about an octopus. Suppose people who read that story think that the octopus can’t

thought experiment) undermines the billions of real-life, interactive judgments about ChatGPT. The 
conclusion we should draw is that the weird, underdescribed, thought experiments about that octopus

think the non-reflective (some, not the authors of this book, would say ‘intuitive’) reaction to that 
vignette carries evidential weight, then you should also think that the billions of judgments users have 
about ChatGPT after very extensive interaction, should carry weight. The overwhelming evidence is 
then in favor of data that ChatGPT can understand our questions, etc.

just an algorithm for how to manipulate symbols. This is the essence of the claim that ChatGPT is 
nothing but a stochastic parrot, all syntax, and no semantics. Our reply is that this is a mistaken picture

infinite number of objects and events in human history. That is a way to be causally connected to the

about Josh’s marbles.

way that could be alleviated if, like us, they could actually be out there interacting with the world. In

causally mediated by our text productions. And they’re not special in that sense: our interactions with

Some Background: Various Views About Desires  
 
LLMs act (minimally, they answer questions). LLMs have beliefs that help guide their actions. If beliefs 
and desires are mental states that produce action, then once you have beliefs and actions, you have a 
relatively simple path to desires. Here’s that basic picture as Robert Stalnaker outlines it:  
 
Belief and desire .... are correlative dispositional states of a potentially rational agent. To desire 
that P is to be disposed to act in ways that would tend to bring it about that P in a world in 
which one’s beliefs, whatever they are, were true. To believe that P is to be disposed to act in 
ways that would tend to satisfy one's desires, whatever they are , in a world in which P 
(together with one’s other beliefs) were true. (1984, p. 15) 
 
According to Michael Smith, the Stalnakerian picture is simplistic and incomplete. Smith says: 
“....desires are much more complex psychological states than mere dispositions to behave in certain 
ways, given our means-end beliefs.” If desire is more than just a state that combines with belief to 
create action, then the ways in which it is more can provide potential Lacks to level against the claim 
that LLMs have desires. In what ways, then, is desire more?  
 
Tim Schroeder’s overview article of various theories of desire in the SEP includes a helpful category 
that he calls ‘Holistic Theories of Desire’. These theories present a package of desire-features. For a 
state to be a desire (or for “A desires that p” to be true), no one of these is necessary. Instead, desires 
are whatever has enough of the desire features, where ‘enough’ is left fairly vague and open ended. 
As usual, we’re not interested in giving a metaphysics of desire, so we don’t need to worry about the 
details of how these features constitutively settle whether a state is a desire. But the package list is still 
helpful in considering whether LLMs have anything that deserves the name of desire -- it can be our 
source for potential Lacks. Schroeder’s list:

Pleasure and Displeasure in ChatGPT  
 
After these introductory remarks about desires, we can make the objection more concrete:  
 
1.​ Desire is a mental state that is linked to the experiences of pleasure and displeasure. For an

2.​ LLMs can’t experience pleasure or displeasure,  
3.​ So no mental state of theirs could count as desire.  
More generally, no p-representing state that came with no tendency toward experiencing satisfaction

that lack could disqualify their states as desires. 
 
We’ll consider three responses to this lack:  
 
1.​ Pleasure not Needed: There are lots of human cases of wanting that don’t involve pleasure or 
displeasure. There is no particular problem with dispassionate desires. Josh wants to run yet 
another 50k race, but he takes no pleasure in it seeming that he has done so. Some cases of 
desiring that p will be linked to pleasure and displeasure, but this is a contingent connection.

them utterly unlike familiar desirers, and need not be a barrier to taking their states as desires.

Why Mechanistic Interpretability Can’t Help Us Discover Mental States of LLMs  
 
At this point, we might hope for help from work on mechanistic interpretability. Perhaps with the right 
probing methods, we can find applications of modus ponens hidden among the linear algebra. We’ll 
argue, though, that we shouldn’t depend on work in mechanistic interpretability to rescue LLM 
reasoning. Although results from mechanistic interpretation can be interesting and suggestive, they 
require careful philosophical interpretation. Detectability of an internal representation by mechanistic 
interpretability methods is neither necessary nor sufficient for the presence of a genuine mental state, 
or of a genuine mental action or operation. 
 
Consider first necessity, and start by thinking about the human case. Current syntactic theories often 
posit multiple layers of hidden structural representation, not manifested in the surface form of 
observable language production, generated by complex rules. Neither hidden representations nor rule 
applications are introspectable by ordinary speakers. What then do we mean when we say that 
speakers are in fact following those rules and using them to generate those structures in their 
language production? 
 
One option is neuro-computational. There could be a part of the brain -- the “Chomsky module” -- in 
which the syntactic rules are computationally implemented. With the right anatomical probing, we 
should then be able to locate and decode the Chomsky module, and see the rule applications 
happening and the syntactic structures being built up as people speak. But it’s important to see that 
this isn’t the only option. 
 
A second option is that the speaker’s brain does contain a Chomsky module, but the Chomsky module 
isn’t typically invoked in speech production. Instead, the speaker’s brain also contains some 
hodge-podge collection of heuristics and shortcuts for cobbling together sentences. Perhaps the 
speaker has an enormous memory store of previously-encountered sentences, and typically just grabs 
one of those from memory, does some lexical swaps to get close enough to the content they want, and 
then says that. That way they avoid the computational labor of newly base-generating sentences for 
each utterance. The Chomsky module still has an important role to play. The hodge-podge collection is

actual syntactic rules, then the heuristics are modified or rejected.66 This occasional training procedure

Searle (1992) distinguishes between intrinsic, as-if, and derived intentionality. He gives the following 
examples: 
 
Consider the similarities and differences among the following: 
1.​ I am now thirsty, really thirsty, because I haven’t had anything to drink all day. 
2.​ My lawn is thirsty, really thirsty, because it has not been watered in a week. 
3.​ In French, “j’ai grand soif” means “I am very thirsty”. 
The first of these sentences is used literally to describe a real, intentional mental state to 
oneself. If I utter that sentence, making a true statement, then there is in me a conscious 
feeling of thirst that makes that statement true That statement has intentionality because it 
involves a desire to drink. But the second sentence is quite different. Sentence 2 is used only 
metaphorically, or figuratively, to ascribe thirst to my lawn. My lawn, lacking water, is in a 
situation in which I would be thirsty, so I figuratively describe it as if it were thirsty. I can, by 
analogy, quite harmlessly say that the lawn is thirsty even though I do not suppose for a 
moment that it is literally thirsty. The third sentence is like the first in that it literally ascribes 
intentionality, but it is like the second and unlike the first that the intentionality described is not 
intrinsic to the system. … That very sequence might have meant something very different or 
nothing at all. Linguistic meaning is a real form of intentionality, but it is not intrinsic 
intentionality. It is derived from the intrinsic in intentionality of the users of the language. (78-79) 
 
Our earlier discussion of loose talk and pretense can be taken as a response to those who suggest 
that LLM intentionality is only as-if intentionality. Many people who agree that LLMs have genuinely 
intentional and representational states and outputs think that the intentionality is only, in Searle’s 
terminology, derived intentionality. LLM states are meaningful only because they are states involving 
production and manipulation of expressions in our languages. The LLM states derive their meanings 
from the meanings of those linguistic expressions, which in turn derive their meanings from the 
exercises of intrinsic intentionality in us, as we act and think so as to make our languages meaningful. 
 
If LLMs lack intrinsic intentionality, that lack could be a barrier to their possession of some normal 
human mental states. Emma Borg says: 
 
Sceptics are right to think that LLMs lack original or intrinsic intentionality. They are not agents 
and they are not conscious. This doesn’t, I’ve argued, stop their outputs from being meaningful 
but it may mean that it makes no sense to talk of them as understanding or (contra Turing) 
thinking (at least if thinking is held to involve original intentionality). (Borg (2025), 27-28)

underdeveloped in ways that can make it hard to judge cases, but it’s not clear that our intentional

persistent and deeply entrenched goals and to transitory goals. Those goals then shape their actions, 
as they work to achieve the goals. How well a creature’s life is going depends, at least in part, on how 
well its goals are being achieved.

Objections to LLMs having Goals 
 
The question is then whether there are countervailing reasons that, in the case of LLMs, threaten the 
typical connection between having goals and projects and having wellbeing. We’ll consider three 
responses. 
 
1.​ “LLMs don’t really have goals, they’ve just been trained to act in certain ways. Nothing changes 
for an LLM when it doesn’t achieve its “goal” of answering questions well. It’s not distressed or 
harmed by that failure.” This response threatens to slide into Just-an-X territory. We’ve given 
our reasons already for thinking that LLMs do have desires and other goal-oriented states; the 
fact that some of those states are the causal result of computational training mechanisms 
doesn’t make them less real. It’s also not clear that nothing changes for the LLM when it fails to 
meet its goals -- ChatGPT can recognize and apologize for unhelpful interactions. But 
“something changing” is in any case an overly stringent standard. People’s lives can go better

just a different thing from, and not a necessary condition for, a change in wellbeing. 
 
2.​ “The goals of LLMs aren’t goals that they’ve selected, but goals that they have been trained 
into. Success or failure in those goals doesn’t make for things going better or worse for the 
LLM, but rather makes for things going better or worse for the trainer.” LLMs can pick their own 
goals. Give ChatGPT a chess position, and ask it to choose to play from that position either to 
win or to lose. It can make the choice, and then play in a way guided by that choice. It can even

interesting, toward one of the two goals.73 And in the human case, we’re familiar, in the rearing

have. While there is a risk of LLM wellbeing getting too much weight, we’re also free to think

Speculative Thoughts: Alien Content74    
 
The anchoring point of this book has been our knowledge that systems like ChatGPT can tell us that 
Paris is the capital of France. In the Hog Argument, we start from that knowledge and then, using the 
Holistic Network Assumptions, build up a picture of the mind of ChatGPT. The picture of its mind ends 
up being pretty similar to what we find in humans. Hence the Whole Hog Thesis, according to which 
ChatGPT is a complete linguistic and cognitive agent. In this chapter, we want to explore an even 
stranger, and darker, view. This is the possibility that, in addition to our familiar cognitive and linguistic 
abilities, its mind has more alien components. The aim is to explore a cluster of  closely related 
topics: What would it be for a system like ChatGPT to have alien (non-human) contents and how 
could we recognize these as contents? If AI systems can have alien contents, how could we 
communicate (and otherwise engage) with them?   
Much of what we have to say is exploratory. This is in large part an effort to convince  readers that 
it’s possible that AIs have both alien contents and states. To the extent that we have conclusions 
they are the following:   
Conclusion 1. AIs could have alien contents   
Conclusion 2. Even if they do, we can find ways to communicate with them.  
Conclusion 3. Taking these options seriously is needed to think clearly about  issues such 
as existential risk, the value alignment problem, and explainable AI.     
 
Since this chapter is more exploratory and speculative, we will not tie the discussion as directly to 
ChatGPT as we did in earlier chapters, but instead talk more generally about AI systems.

Some of the purposes to which we currently put, or hope soon to put, AI systems, involve  
communication with our current contents, but other applications will require contents that are  different

meaning-preserving way. But of course translation is possible only when the  same contents are 
available on the sending and the receiving ends, so that translation can match  utterances with the 
same content. By hypothesis, that’s not the situation we are in with alien  contents.  
A devotee of Quine and Davidson’s method of radical translation might object that this  hypothesis 
cannot be realized. Perhaps we can always run the radical translation machinery  and always get an 
output, so we can always translate. There are two reasons why we find this  an implausible idea, even 
from within the Quine-Davidson framework.

-​
First, despite the claims of Davidson (1973), even when the radical translation tools are  ready 
to hand, they may produce no translation. We may recognize the alien activity as  intelligent, 
guided by beliefs and desires -- activity classifying objects into categories, for  example. We 
might nevertheless find that we have no contents matching the distinctions  guiding the alien 
activity. We can find that no categories of ours categorize as they do.

Second and more importantly, the gears of radical translation might fail to engage with  alien 
contents. Depending on its flavor, radical translation requires in its object beliefs  and desires, 
or rational action, or knowledge. AIs or other possessors of alien contents  might have no such 
states. That’s perhaps the expected situation for the kinds of AI  systems we are familiar with: 
they have nothing like desires, and states only analogically  like beliefs. They may be less 
possessors of knowledge and more tools for the expansion  of our knowledge. One of the 
things that make AI systems in particular such an  interesting case of alien content is that their 
natures are a mixture of the alien and the  familiar. While they may not, for example, desire 
themselves, they are tools created by  and serving satisfaction of our desires, and something 
like radical translation of them in  light of their relations to us may be possible. We return to this 
theme below when we  discuss meta-metasemantics.)  
If translation is impossible because there is nothing of ours to translate their contents into, what  is to 
be done? How is communication with or understanding of AI systems possible, given that  
communication and understanding also seem to require shared content? We’ll explore two  options, 
which we will call bridging and integration.  
Bridging   
Suppose an AI produces outputs with contents that we lack, so that we cannot simply translate  its 
outputs into our language. We might nevertheless be able to build a bridge of understanding  between 
us and the AI by finding suitable corresponding contents of ours.  
Some versions of this strategy are familiar. Others have suggested that successful  communication 
requires only that audiences uptake contents similar to contents entertained or  intended by 
speakers, without requiring full identity.78 Thus Frege finds it unlikely that two  speakers will share the 
very same sense for a proper name, but thinks they can communicate so  long as their senses are 
similar. (In the limiting case, perhaps shared denotation suffices for  similarity.)   
 
Or bridges could be built using the mereology of content.79  A verbal description of the contents  of a 
painting, for example, might share content with the painting by capturing some of what is  represented 
in the painting, but unavoidably also leave out some of the pictorial content because pictorial 
representation makes available other contents that simply can’t be produced  with linguistic tools.

The really interesting cases are ones in which we aren’t so fortunate as to have a ready-made

It’s helpful to get started here to realize that the aliens are already among us, and that they are  in

thoroughgoing expressivists may view our moral language as expressive of conative or  evaluative 
attitudes, or our modal vocabulary as expressive of global features of our doxastic  state, or the vast 
swath of our descriptive vocabulary as expressive of our beliefs. The contents  of the expressivists 
cannot be translated into the contents of the representationalist -- the  representationalist traffics in 
propositions, while the expressivist does not, so there is no  common ground between them. In some 
sense, each of the expressivist and the  representationalist should be licensed in simply ignoring the 
other, if taken on the other’s own  terms, since by their lights the other is saying nothing they can 
comprehend.

a con attitude toward murder”, etc.) The reportings don’t say the same  thing as the expressions, but 
the relation between reporting and expressing can support a lot of  our communicative practice. The 
representationalists who takes the expressivists’ expressions  as reportings, or the expressivist who 
takes the representationalists’ reportings as expressings,  won’t get everything right, but will have an

Integration   
Rather than seeking a suitable content of our own to match with the AI’s alien contents, we  could 
acquire the alien contents, integrating ourselves into their linguistic practice and making  ourselves 
users of those contents. Consideration of simple cases might suggest that the process  of integration 
is straightforward. When our diagnostic data-mining AI gets onto a new category,  sorting objects in 
some way previously unavailable in our language, we can simply introduce a  new predicate and 
stipulate that its intension matches the intension by which the AI is sorting.  The integration move here 
is a deferential move, but it’s important to distinguish two forms of  deference:   
-​
In semantic deference, the property of deference is part of the meaning of the deferential  
expression. “Gloobish”, in our language, means “has the feature that the AI system picks  out 
when its red light flashes”.   
-​
In metasemantic deference, on the other hand, the property of deference is no part of the  
meaning of the deferential expression -- rather, deference is a metasemantic feature that  
fixes the nondeferential meaning. If the AI system is in fact picking out property P, then  with 
metasemantic deference, our “gloobish” simply means that P, by way of our  deference.  
Semantic deference is easily achieved (as easily as any other stipulation). However, semantic  
deference is also too cheap for most communicative purposes. With semantic deference, we can  
stipulate that “iliadic” means whatever the Greek expression “μῆνιν ἄειδε θεὰ Πηληϊάδεω 
Ἀχιλῆος//Οὐλομένην” means. If we have no idea what it does mean, “iliadic” gives us only  thin 
arms-length access to the content, not genuinely integrating it into our language. It gives us  nothing 
we didn’t already have via quotation.  
Metasemantic deference is what we want. That’s the tool that allows the alien contents to become 
contents of our language. Metasemantic deference, however, is much less obviously easily  
achieved. We need to know what kind of deferential relation is needed to make our words mean what 
the AI words mean. There are at least two worries here. First, our notion of  deference may not apply 
to AIs. Perhaps to defer is in part to respect the target of deference, to  think that the target knows 
more about the subject than we do. But AI systems may not be  sensible objects of respect and may 
not have knowledge as we understand it. If so, perhaps we can’t defer to them. Second, our notion of 
deference may not be the right one. Perhaps  human deference is a metasemantic ground of 
meaning in human languages—but why think  that human deference grounds meaning acquisition 
between human and alien languages.  Perhaps alien deference is needed, or perhaps the alien 
language is entirely deference-proof.  
Of course, deference isn’t essential here—it was only one possible route to integration. But the  
concern generalizes. What we want is (to use the Lewisian (1969) terminology) to put ourselves  in 
the actual language relation to the alien contents. But to do that, we need to know what it  takes to 
stand in the actual language relation to that sort of content. That is, we need to know  about the 
metasemantics of the alien language. If the alien contents are grounded (externalist style) in causal 
interactions with other speakers of the alien language, perhaps integration is not  too hard -- we

conventions.  
Applications: Explainable AI and Existential Risk   
The issues we’ve discussed here can easily seem abstract and of no relevance to the real and  
pressing practical, social, and political issues raised by the issues that AI presents us with.  
Nothing could be further from the truth. These are foundational issues with very direct  practical 
implications. We provide two illustrations: Existential Risk and Explainable AI.  
Existential Risk   
There has been a great deal of literature on “existential risk” concerns about AI (see Bostrom 2014 
for a popular recent discussion). These concerns often center on what is called The  Alignment 
Problem: how to get the goals of AI systems sufficiently aligned with ours, and how  we might design 
AIs to guarantee a comforting level of alignment. We have preferred to focus  on a collection of what 
we take to be underexplored semantic and metasemantic questions  about AI systems, but the two 
topics intersect when we start thinking about alien content.  
Here is a simple version of the existential risk issue: we might want some version of Asimov’s  First 
Law of Robotics (first presented in his 1942 story “Runaround”) incorporated into any AI  systems 
we produce:  
Asimov’s First Law, AI version: No (AI system) may injure a human being, or by  
inaction allow a human being to come to harm  
Implementing exactly that law in an AI system requires shared content—if the AI’s semantic  
resources don’t include the concepts of injury or harm, then we won’t be able to tell it what to  do. If 
an AI has alien contents, then we can’t share that content with the AI (because Asimov’s  First Law is 
formulated using human contents.) One immediate corollary is that settling the  issue of alien vs. 
non-alien content is a requirement on even thinking about the alignment issue. Another corollary of 
the above discussion is that the presence of alien contents doesn’t mean  giving up on the First Law 
of Robotics. Suppose we’ve settled that the AI can’t share our  concept of injury or risk. How do we 
recognize the alien concepts as being suitably related to  harm and injury? One option suggested 
above is that we can look to the metasemantics or the  meta-metasematics. We check to see if the 
alien concept is metasemantically determined by facts  related to human flourishing; we consider 
what theoretical role the distinction between  flourishing and non-flourishing lives plays for us. 
Pursued correctly, such discussions bring  together questions of ethics, content, and meta-content 
into a single research project.

Explainable AI   
The aim of XAI is to create AI systems that are interpretable by us, that produce decisions that  
come with comprehensible explanations, that use concepts that we can understand, and that we  
can talk to in the way that we can engage with each other. The importance of this is highlighted  by 
recent legislation that makes it a requirement that decisions made by (or with the help of)  neural 
nets come with an explanation of the decision. Thus--and without getting too deep into  the weeds of 
contemporary EU data protection law83--the EU’s enshrines a ‘right to  explanation’. One has the 
right to “meaningful information about the logic [of the algorithm]  involved.”8417 That means that those 
subject to neural net-based decision-making are owed an  explanation of how a decision affecting 
them was arrived at. This is obviously a very tall order  when we don’t even know whether an AI has 
the same kind of contents as we humans have.  Most of the discussion of explainable AI proceeds 
on the assumption that we are dealing with a  system that generates outputs with human-type 
contents. Even then the requirement that an  explanation be available is hard to meet, but the 
challenge gets even harder once we recognize  the real possibility that the relevant outputs could 
contain alien contents generated by alien  metasemantic mechanisms. We then need procedures for 
recognizing these contents as  contents, and we need ways to relate to those alien contents. This 
paper has provided proposals  for how to proceed, but it should be obvious that we have at best 
scratched the surface of some  extremely hard questions and that there’s much work to be done.

Concluding Thoughts 
 
We have gone whole hog, and have defended the claim that ChatGPT is a full-blown linguistic and 
cognitive agent. But what does it mean for ChatGPT (or anything else) to be a full-blown linguistic and 
cognitive agent? In the Whole Hog Thesis, we gestured toward an enumerative answer—we listed a 
bunch of things that we take to be typical of persons (using language, having beliefs, being morally 
evaluable, and so on). But is there something that unifies the items on that list? And, relatedly, is there 
something important in the discovery that something is a genuine agent? Why, in short, should we 
care about the Whole Hog Thesis? 
 
Many of the reasons for caring are obvious. If LLMs are moral agents, and patients, that matters 
because we can then appropriately consider how we ought to treat them and how they ought to treat 
us. If LLMs know things, that matters, because we can then acquire knowledge from them. 
 
But there’s a more general answer that’s also important. The truth of the Whole Hog points to a 
general place for LLMs in our understanding of the world. Consider how we interact with different parts 
of the world. Our ways of interacting with rocks are very different from our ways of interacting with 
humans. These differences are marked by the different conceptual resources that we use for 
interacting with rocks and for interacting with humans. We track very different features of rocks and of 
humans. When it comes to dealing with rocks, it’s typically a pretty simple list. We’re interested in size, 
shape, weight, location, and a few other things like that. We don’t need much, because there’s not 
much that rocks can do, and not much that we typically want out of our interactions with rocks. But we 
have a broad toolkit of concepts that we use to make sense of and interact with our fellow humans 
(and ourselves). We talk of beliefs, desires, hopes, goals, plans, loves, hatreds, reasons, obligations, 
virtues, vices, duties, friendships, loyalties, and on and on. Why do we have this toolkit? We don’t pull 
out tools from the toolkit when we’re interacting with light switches, thunderstorms, or headaches. 
What makes interactions with humans so different?

Some early ChatGPT ancestors yielded well to Billiard Ball-style analysis. Predicting the primitive 
chatbot ELIZA’s text production simply by virtue of knowing the ELIZA algorithms is a reasonable 
project, and to the extent that there’s anything to be gotten out of interactions with ELIZA, it’s plausible

physical processes are that lead to ChatGPT’s actions, they’re inscrutable, poorly theorized, and 
computationally intractable in the same way that the corresponding processes in humans are.                       
 
Human Interaction Model  
 
We don’t work out how to predict or exploit people by treating them as broadly physical systems, 
whether simple and rule-governed or complex and inscrutable. Instead, it’s crucial for the way that we 
interact with one another that we bring to bear concepts like belief, action, reason, and so on. Once we 
know that Alex wants pizza, we’re able to predict her actions quite successfully without engaging at all 
with the enormously complex physical state that underlies her wanting pizza. When we think of Alex as 
a friend, we open up whole interesting styles of interaction with Alex (pursuing joint projects, 
supporting the aims of, and so on) that aren’t on the horizon if we simply think of Alex as a complex 
physical system. We have, in short, human interactions. 
 
Treating our interactions with humans as Human Interactions is crucial for making sense of those 
interactions. We won’t get far in life treating the people around us as Billiard Balls. We won’t 
understand why they are doing what they are doing, we won’t be able to rely on them in the right ways, 
and we won’t know how to get them to do what we need them to do. Imagine trying to get even a 
simple collaborative task achieved, like getting everyone driving on the same side of the road, if we 
don’t think of the drivers as Humans, knowing the difference between left and right, controlling their

progress if we thought of the drivers as just big rocks we needed to shove into the right driving 
configurations. 
 
An important part of the Whole Hog Thesis is that we also aren’t going to make progress interacting 
with ChatGPT in productive ways by thinking of it as a Billiard Ball. We need the conceptual tools of 
saying, believing, planning, and so on in order to make sense of ChatGPT, and in order to figure out its 
place in our social structure. And doing these things is important. ChatGPT and other similar systems 
are clearly positioned to have a major influence on how we run our lives, so we need to make sure that 
influence is a benign one. But if we insist on treating ChatGPT as a Billiard Ball, we won’t have the 
conceptual tools we need to work toward a benign influence. It’s not that we’ll give the wrong answers 
when we think of things in that way–it’s rather that we won’t even ask the right questions. 
 
For example, Daniel Dennett has recently written on the danger of creating “counterfeit people”: 
 
Counterfeit people, by distracting and confusing us and by exploiting our most irresistible fears 
and anxieties, will lead us into temptation and, from there, into acquiescing to our own 
subjugation. The counterfeit people will talk us into adopting policies and convictions that will 
make us vulnerable to still more manipulation. Or we will simply turn off our attention and 
become passive and ignorant pawns. That is a terrifying prospect.86  
 
There is a real concern here about social roles that ChatGPT could play. We clearly don’t want 
ChatGPT manipulating us into subjugation! But putting the concern in terms of “counterfeit people” 
means not treating ChatGPT as an agent, and thus thinking of it as “just a piece of programming.” And 
that in turn pushes us to consider the kinds of remedies that Dennett considers: marking with 
watermarks to indicate “counterfeit people” for example—remedies that don’t engage with the problem 
as a problem about the bad behavior of people. 
 
The problems that Dennett is concerned with can of course show up with humans as well— it’s not 
unique to ChatGPT that it talks us into adopting policies that make us vulnerable to manipulation. We 
wouldn’t try to solve the corresponding problem of human behavior by trying to put some mark of Cain 
on the bad agents. That’s too crude a method for adjusting the complex interactions of humans–we 
wouldn’t be able to identify who to mark reliably, or reliably keep the marks in place, or reliably react to 
seeing a marked person. A better plan is to set up social structures that discourage bad behavior 
through people’s normal motivational routes. We should be pursuing the same routes with ChatGPT. 
 
So, to find the right place for ChatGPT in our lives, we need to be thinking about it with the right 
concepts. The Whole Hog Thesis is an attempt to find the right conceptual tools, by noting that 
ChatGPT is an agent, and thus claiming that we should be using personal tools for thinking about it.

The Philosophical Significance of New Minds   
  
Wittgenstein famously said that “if a lion could talk, we would not understand him.” But the years since 
the Philosophical Investigations have provided some empirical check of Wittgenstein’s claim. We’ve 
seen extensive lion speech from Aslan, Simba, the Cowardly Lion, and the Tawny Scrawny Lion, 
among others. And both the lions and their speech have turned out to be quite comprehensible. They 
have familiar projects and say familiar things in furtherance of those familiar projects. Of course, that’s 
no real refutation of Wittgenstein. The lion speech is so familiar because it’s just our attempts to 
imagine lion speech. And our imaginations are constrained—not in principle, perhaps, but clearly in 
practice—by our natures and experiences. It’s much harder to craft a detailed picture of what a wholly 
novel “form of life” would be like than it is to just put familiar thoughts into the mouths of fictional lions. 
  
The difficulty of imagining the profoundly alien matters philosophically. Philosophy is full of thought 
experiments: trolley problems, brain fission and fusion cases, the Ship of Theseus, fake barns, 
inverted spectra. We use these thought experiments because reality isn’t confronting us with cases 
that are sufficiently diverse to test our theories and our concepts properly. Trolley problems raise hard 
questions about (for example) the moral significance of the doing/allowing distinction. We might have 
gotten “lucky” enough to run into the puzzling cases in the real world, but we’re going to miss important 
distinctions if we always wait for the world to confront us with the hard cases. 
  
Not wanting to wait for the world, we cast our philosophical thought about, and over time we’ve 
developed a nice portfolio of philosophical thought experiments. But these thought experiments are 
hard intellectual achievements. It’s hard to find the right unusual cases to consider; it’s hard to flesh 
out those cases in just the right ways to bring out the interesting points; it’s hard to find the right 
assessment of the unusual cases. Intellectual space is presumably richly populated with fantastically 
useful philosophical thought experiments that we haven’t yet stumbled our way onto. 
  
As a result, our philosophical work is surely enormously parochial in ways that are hard for us to see, 
from our parochial perspective. Any way of escaping that parochiality is thus important. That’s what 
makes the history of philosophy exciting—by engaging with the work of Parmenides or Bolzano, we 
increase our odds of having our attention drawn to philosophical concepts and ideas and 
considerations that are otherwise outside our parochial philosophical attention. Similarly for looking at 
a variety of philosophical traditions and cultures. And genuine aliens would be a tremendously exciting 
philosophical opportunity. Would philosophical aliens share the idea that actions can be morally 
evaluated for rightness and wrongness? Would they share the practice of using meaningful 
representational systems for communication? 
  
Engagement with the details of the philosophical aliens would help stretch the limits of our philosophy. 
But we’re only going to get that effect with real aliens. If we try to engage with our own imaginings of 
aliens, it threatens to be Aslan the talking lion all over again. Star Trek aliens aren’t so disappointingly 
familiar just because adding some forehead ridges to humans is the low-budget way to make 
aliens—it’s also because creating convincing and genuinely alien aliens is a prohibitively difficult 
intellectual task.

So we should seize the opportunity when it presents itself. We should deal with the particularities of 
current LLMs, rather than thinking about some imagined more sophisticated artificial intelligence, 
precisely because they aren’t yet general artificial intelligences which are very much like us. The aliens 
are here, but if we wait until they’ve adapted themselves to our culture, the anthropological moment 
will be lost. And worse than lost—if we aren’t thinking about how this whole family of artificial 
intelligence softwares are changing through our continuing development and use, and also how we are 
being changed through our increasing interactions with them, we may find ourselves in a situation we 
weren’t expecting and aren’t prepared for. 
 
We think of LLMs as a new philosophical species. If we encountered intelligent aliens, we’d want to 
investigate them thoroughly and see how they stretched our philosophical concepts. We should react 
the same way to LLMs. That doesn’t mean that these LLMs are the best possible cases for stretching 
our concepts. But they are the first, or among the first, aliens to wander by. We could try to imagine 
more advanced aliens—but our imaginings would be anthropocentric; that’s just another way to do 
more thought experiments. We want to talk specifically about ChatGPT and its ilk because that forces 
us to engage with the actual new thing and consider its actual features, and see what we learn from 
that.   
 
Ted Chiang said that ChatGPT is a blurry jpeg of the internet.87 That’s not wrong, but it’s also 
misleading. It’s better to say that ChatGPT is a blurry jpeg of the world, via the specific mechanism of 
blurring the internet’s blurry image of the world. But then, we too are blurry images of the world—each 
of us carries around our own image of the world, obtained via our own idiosyncratically 
detail-preserving and detail-obscuring mechanisms. What’s particularly of interest is not the mere fact 
of the blurring, but the myriad details about how ChatGPT blurs and how its blurring is different from 
ours.
