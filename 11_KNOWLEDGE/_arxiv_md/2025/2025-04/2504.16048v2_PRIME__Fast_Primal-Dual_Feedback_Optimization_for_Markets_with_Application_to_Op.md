PRIME: Fast Primal-Dual Feedback Optimization for Markets with
Application to Optimal Power Flow
Nicholas Julian Behr, Mattia Bianchi, Keith Moffat, Saverio Bolognani, and Florian D¨orfler
Abstract— Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) controllers
iteratively drive a plant to an optimal operating point that
satisfies input and output constraints, relying solely on the
input-output sensitivity as model information. This paper in-
troduces PRIME (PRoximal Iterative MarkEts), a novel OFO
approach based on proximal-point iterations. Unlike existing
OFO solutions, PRIME admits a market-based implementation,
where self-interested actors are incentivized to make choices
that result in safe and efficient operation, without communi-
cating private costs or constraints. Furthermore, PRIME can
handle non-smooth objective functions, achieve fast convergence
rates and rapid constraint satisfaction, and effectively reject
measurement noise. We demonstrate PRIME on an AC optimal
power flow problem, obtaining an efficient real-time nonlinear
local marginal pricing scheme.
I. INTRODUCTION
Regulating a physical plant to a cost-optimal operating
point is a fundamental goal in control theory [1], [2]. The
classic solution approach involves pre-computing a reference
signal, which an online controller then tracks in real-time.
Feedforward reference optimization is, however, not robust
to uncertainty in the plant model and optimization cost.
Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) has recently gar-
nered interest as a solution to this challenge. By directly
interconnecting the optimization process with the true plant,
OFO enables continual adaptation and exploits the robustness
of feedback control [3], [4], [5], [6]. For this reason, OFO
has found application in several engineering domains, most
prominently power systems ([7], [8], [9], [10]), but also
communication networks [11], smart building automation
[12], traffic control [13] and process control [14].
In its most basic form, OFO aims at solving the following
optimization problem online:

dynamical system with fast-decaying dynamics, such that the
system output quickly converges to h(u) when a constant
input u is applied. The map h does not need to be known;

In this work, we investigate the use of OFO in a market
framework in which control actions are taken by multiple
autonomous and self-interested actors, rather than by a single
centralized decision-maker. In this setup, a market operator
aims to determine incentives (rewards or payments to be
charged) such that the self-interested actors collectively steer
the plant toward a socially optimal outcome.
Implementing OFO in such an online market presents
some challenges. One major challenge is enforcing the output
constraints (1c). While methods based on Lagrangian duality
allow decentralization across the actors [18], they often suffer
from slow convergence and significant constraint violations
during transients [19]. Another issue is that the market oper-
ator does not know the actors’ costs and constraints, nor can
it directly control their actions—instead, actors greedily react
to incentives to maximize their profit. These challenges were
considered in [20] for a prosumer incentivization problem.
However, the bilevel-optimization formulation in [20] differs
from the social welfare optimization goal in this paper.
Contributions: We present PRIME (PRoximal Iterative
MarkEts), a new OFO approach to coordinate self-interested
market participants (“input actors” and “output actors”) at-
tached to a physical plant’s inputs and outputs (see Figure 1).
The method builds on a primal-dual Lagrangian formulation,
enhanced with proximal-point iterations, to improve conver-
gence speed and enable non-smooth objective functions.
We introduce two different variants of PRIME. PRIME-
Y constructs the Lagrangian by dualizing (1c), and allows
for input actors only. PRIME-H instead constructs the La-
grangian by dualizing the input-output constraints in (1d),
enabling both input and output actors, and thus a higher
degree of decentralization.
PRIME requires the market operator to know only the
plant’s input-output sensitivity ∇uh, but not the actors’ pri-
vate costs or constraints. Notably, the strictly convex incen-
tive functions constructed by the market operator naturally
compel each actor to respond by optimizing their (private)
cost. This process implicitly reveals actors’ preferences and
constraints without requiring central modeling, which is
prone to model mismatch.
Via simulations on an Optimal Power Flow (OPF) prob-
lem, we show that PRIME matches the performance of
standard centralized methods in terms of constraint reso-
lution and convergence speed, while benefiting from the
distributability of a market-based implementation.

VI. APPLICATION TO POWER GRID OPERATION
A. Power Network Model
Consider a grid with B + 1 buses, as described in Sec-
tion II-A, with B PQ-buses and one slack bus. The net
real power injections P ∈RB and reactive power injections
Q ∈RB from the prosumers are stacked to create the input
vector u, and the voltage magnitudes and phase angles at
all non-slack buses are stacked to form the output vector
y ∈R2B. For simplicity, every nodal net power injection is
a distinct input actor, and there is a single DSO output actor.
Following the approach outlined in [25, Ch. 6] and [4],
we obtain the voltage-power sensitivity Γ ∈R2B×2B at the
operating point (u∗, y∗):
Γδy = δu,
where δy is a small change in the output y coresponding to
δu, a small change in the input u. Inverting Γ, we get the
desired power-voltage sensitivity ∇uh:
δy = ∇uh δu.
Replacing δy with (y −yk) and δu with (u −uk), we get
˜hk(u), defined in (4), which linearizes h at timestep k.

violations and is outperformed by the Projected Primal
method. Notably, the Projected Primal method requires a
cost and constraint oracle (i.e., it is centralized). Remarkably,
PRIME-Y and PRIME-H operate in a market fashion without
this oracle and still achieve comparable resolution speeds.
In Figure 3b, we introduce noise into the voltage mea-
surements. The Projected Primal algorithm is sensitive to
noise, while the primal-dual methods are not. The Projected
Primal method performs particularly poorly when operating
close to output constraints (which is common for optimal
points), as measurement noise frequently causes constraints
to appear violated, leading the following input to be projected
away from the optimum. This aggressive reaction to the
measurement noise leads to the Projected Primal method’s
“jittery” behavior in Figure 3b. PRIME-Y and PRIME-
H instead deliver fast constraint violation resolution while
rejecting the effect of the measurement noise.

VII. CONCLUSION
This paper introduced PRIME, a new feedback optimiza-
tion approach based on proximal iterations. PRIME achieves
rapid constraint violation resolution and robustness to mea-
surement noise. Most importantly, PRIME enables the design
of optimal incentives in markets with self-interested actors
without requiring access to their private costs or constraints.
Future work should address the possible strategic behavior
of market actors. This includes examining the risk of market
dynamics manipulation and devising mechanisms to make
PRIME truth-revealing and collusion-proof.

VIII. APPENDIX
A. Projected Primal Gradient OFO Infeasibility
The Projected Primal Gradient method (Algorithm 1)
works by projecting uk onto the set Uk, which is the inter-
section of the input constraints (3b) and the linearized output
constraints (5). This intersection can be empty, resulting in
the method returning no solution after initialization, a change
in the constraints, or during the progression of gradient steps.
Figure 4a demonstrates how a nonlinear h(u) can lead
to Algorithm 1 being infeasible at initialization or after a
large gradient step. Figure 4b demonstrates how measure-
ment noise can result in infeasibility as the linearization is
no longer made around the true operating point yk. Note
that in both cases, feasible inputs to the true nonlinearized
formulation exist, specifically any inputs where the blue line
is contained in the dotted perimeter.
