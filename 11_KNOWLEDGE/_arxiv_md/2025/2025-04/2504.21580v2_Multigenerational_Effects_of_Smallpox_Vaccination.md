MULTIGENERATIONAL EFFECTS OF SMALLPOX VACCINATION 
Volha Lazuka* and Peter Sandholt Jensen** 
 
Abstract 
Using Swedish register data spanning 250 years, we estimate multigenerational effects of 
smallpox vaccination on longevity and occupational achievements. Employing mother fixed-
effects, difference-in-differences, and shift-share instrumental-variables designs, we find 
vaccination improves outcomes for three generations. We explore mechanisms through which 
benefits transmit across generations, finding evidence consistent with both improved health 
behaviors and epigenetic inheritance. Effects persist even in milder disease environments, 
demonstrating vaccination’s lasting benefits beyond epidemic contexts. These findings 
underscore the importance of accounting for multigenerational returns when evaluating early-life 
health interventions. 
Keywords: intergenerational transmission of health; smallpox vaccination; shift-share 
instrumental-variables. 
JEL codes: I18; J24; J62; N33.

Introduction 
The recent global disruptions caused by emerging infectious diseases underscore the critical 
importance of vaccines in protecting populations against these diseases. Live vaccines may also 
protect against unrelated diseases, known as “non-specific” vaccine effects, with childhood 
vaccination enhancing the immune system through epigenetic and metabolic changes (Benn et 
al. 2023; de Bree, L C J et al. 2018). Recent experimental evidence from primates demonstrates 
that the benefits of early-life interventions can exhibit intergenerational complementarity, where 
advantages are present only for offspring of mothers who themselves received the intervention 
(Dettmer et al. 2025). In parallel to epidemiological findings, economic studies have explored 
childhood vaccination’s impact on adult labor market outcomes in a human capital framework 
(Barteska et al. 2023; Atwood 2022; Bütikofer and Salvanes 2020). The full effects of 
vaccination remains underestimated because they extend across lifetimes and generations, yet 
the related research is scarce. Understanding these multigenerational effects is essential for 
assessing vaccination benefits and informing public health decisions related to vaccination 
provision and withdrawal. 
Establishing causal effects of vaccination across generations presents formidable empirical 
challenges. First, families who choose to vaccinate may differ systematically from those who do 
not in ways that independently affect outcomes--such as trust in authorities, socio-economic 
status, or health-seeking behaviors. These differences can bias estimates if not properly 
addressed. Selective attrition over long observation periods can introduce bias if migration 
patterns differ systematically between vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals. 
Second, to credibly estimate intergenerational effects, a researcher must trace individuals 
and their descendants across multiple generations--requiring exceptionally detailed longitudinal 
data spanning centuries. Distinguishing the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission 
requires separating biological factors (such as epigenetic inheritance) from behavioral factors 
(such as the transmission of health-seeking behaviors). Any vaccination intervention typically 
occurs alongside other health and economic changes, making it difficult to isolate the specific 
impact of vaccination from concurrent developments. 
In this study, we estimate the impact of smallpox vaccination on the longevity and economic 
well-being of three generations, spanning the period from 1790 to 2016. We leverage the 
smallpox vaccination campaign in Sweden as a quasi-experiment, employing mother fixed 
effects, difference-in-differences, and shift-share instrumental-variables strategies to establish 
causal effects. We find that smallpox vaccination in early childhood enhances both longevity and 
occupational achievements of Generation 1 as well as of their children (Generation 2) and

grandchildren (Generation 3). We explore the mechanisms in causal mediation analysis and find 
that these benefits arise from both improved health behaviors and epigenetic factors. 
Our analysis relies on three complementary identification strategies. First, we employ 
mother fixed effects, which compare biological siblings within the same family, one of whom 
received vaccination while the other did not. This effectively removes all fixed, unobservable 
family-related factors that may affect both treatment assignment and long-term outcomes. 
Second, we implement a difference-in-differences design that exploits two sources of variation: 
the varying exposure of different cohorts to the introduction of the smallpox vaccine in 1801, and 
the relatively larger benefits for individuals born in parishes with greater availability of church 
assistants and musicians--the primary vaccinators--compared to those born in parishes with lower 
availability. 
Third, we adapt a shift-share instrumental-variables approach that captures the exogenous 
variation in children’s smallpox vaccination. Our instrument interacts the parish-level number of 
church assistants and musicians with the national rate of change in their employment. This 
strategy leverages the fact that vaccination in Sweden was carried out by low-skilled clergy who 
otherwise did not perform public health duties, ensuring that their employment patterns satisfy 
the exclusion restriction. We apply this design for Generation 2 and 3. We address potential 
selective migration by adjusting estimates using Heckman’s two-stage selection procedure.  
Our data come from unique register-based datasets containing health, demographic, and 
socio-economic information on residents from 79 different parishes in Sweden, spanning from 
the 18th to the 21st centuries, including their descendants. We obtained data from parishes 
representing northern, central, and southern Sweden that are based on reconstructed life and 
family histories of parish residents, with variables at the individual level in the same metrics 
across cohorts. 
The analytic sample for Generation 1 includes individuals born between 1790 and 1820. 
Generation 2 includes the children of Generation 1, born between 1805 and 1865, and Generation 
3 includes the grandchildren of Generation 1, born between 1820 and 1910. In total, we tracked 
the full life cycles of three generations, which amounts to 141,067 individuals, up until their 
death, out-migration, or reaching the age of 100. Our key treatment variable is whether an 
individual belonging to Generation 1 was vaccinated against smallpox by the age of 2 years. We 
measure longevity as the count of years lived after age two, and we also examine disability-free 
years lived, occupational scores, and literacy. 
To implement our empirical strategies, we construct an instrument based on the interaction 
between the number of church assistants and musicians at the parish level and the national rate

of change in their employment. The historical context supports our identification: the state 
authorities did not allow local physicians to monopolize the vaccination process, fearing low

We address the key challenges to causal identification through several strategies. To handle 
unobservable family-level selection, we employ mother fixed effects that compare siblings 
within families. To address time-varying confounders at the parish level, we include parish fixed 
effects, region-by-cohort fixed effects, and interactions between cohort dummies and parish-level 
characteristics such as the number of midwives, smallpox death rates, urbanization measures, 
and economic indicators. We assess parallel pre-trends through event-study specifications and 
by examining leads of our instrument. To correct for selective migration, we implement 
Heckman’s correction procedure using rye prices and month of birth as predictors of sample 
inclusion. 
Our findings reveal substantial and persistent effects of smallpox vaccination across 
generations. Smallpox vaccination adds 11 years of life to the first generation and 2 and 1 years 
to the second and third generations. To put such results in perspective, vaccination in childhood 
in historical Sweden produces similar effects for longevity as quitting smoking in today’s context. 
We establish explicit “non-specific” vaccination effects: while mortality from smallpox is 
reduced the most, there are negative effects on mortality from other causes. All three generations 
studied died primarily from causes other than smallpox, indicating that vaccination’s protective 
effects extended beyond the targeted disease. 
Vaccination also improves economic outcomes across generations. We find significant 
positive effects on occupational achievements, with these effects transmitted to subsequent 
generations at reduced magnitudes. Vaccination increases disability-free life expectancy by 11 
years for Generation 1, 8 years for Generation 2, and 4 years for Generation 3. Causal mediation 
analysis reveals that more than half of the transmitted effects are attributed to nurture, as 
vaccinated individuals are more likely to vaccinate their children across generations, while 
epigenetic factors account for the remainder. These results are robust to numerous specification 
checks and consistent across our three identification strategies. 
Our paper makes two key contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on 
intergenerational transmission of health. While several studies have attempted to derive the

driven by behavioral factors (health-seeking behaviors) and epigenetic factors, providing insights 
into the mechanisms underlying intergenerational health transmission. 
Our paper also contributes to the literature on long-term effects of historical health 
interventions. While there exists an extensive body of literature on the long-term effects of health 
interventions (Anderson et al. 2020; Egedesø, Hansen, and Jensen 2020; Lazuka 2020), there is 
a smaller, yet steadily expanding, literature focused on vaccinations that occurred further back in 
history (Barteska et al. 2023; Atwood 2022; Bütikofer and Salvanes 2020). We contribute to this 
literature by examining the vaccination campaign, which is the world’s first documented public 
health initiative. Our findings demonstrate that even in milder disease environments like those 
seen today, vaccination delivers lasting advantages, highlighting its long-term benefits beyond 
epidemic contexts. 
1. 
The Context of Smallpox Vaccination in Sweden 
1.1 
Introduction of Smallpox Vaccination 
In 1798, Edward Jenner published his successful smallpox vaccination method, initially 
vaccinating a boy with cowpox then, after eight weeks, administering smallpox without adverse 
effects, confirming efficacy (Baxby 1985). Vaccination reached Sweden by December 7, 1801 
(Riksarkivet 1802-1812). Swedish reformers considered it “among the greatest inventions ever, 
which—when it has increased in confidence—will be the supreme happiness of the human race 
and the triumph of medicine” (Hedin 1802). First Swedish vaccinations occurred in late 1801; 
from 1803, Stockholm’s Inoculation House maintained fresh vaccine for nationwide efforts. 
Pre-vaccination, inoculation—deliberate smallpox (not cowpox) skin infection—served as 
preventive measure. Though introduced in Britain in 1721, Swedish use began in 1756. Historical 
records suggest inoculation never gained wide acceptance due to death risk (Pettersson 1912). 
Country records and our data confirm low Swedish uptake: less than 0.01% of parishioners were 
inoculated between 1769–1800 (Riksarkivet 1769-1801).


## Page 6


6 
 
Sweden’s 1801 vaccination introduction had remarkable features exploited in our empirical 
design. First, vaccination focused on children, typically around age 2 (Riksarkivet 1802-1812). 
From March 1816, parents were required to vaccinate children under 2, with fines for non-
compliance. Unable-to-pay parents faced imprisonment on water and bread diets. 
Second, vaccination was nearly free. Vaccinators were not permitted to charge parents for 
vaccinating children. Local compensation included parish salaries, small wealthy parent fees, 
poor relief payments, or medals (Sköld 1996). No discernible social class differences existed in 
Swedish vaccination practice (Dribe and Nystedt 2003). 
Finally, in 1804 every parish was instructed to appoint a vaccinator. State authorities 
prevented local physicians from monopolizing vaccination, fearing low uptake and voluntary fee 
charges. The vaccine could be safely administered by non-medical individuals—“anyone, 
without prior experience but with good interpersonal skills, common sense, and the ability to 
read and write, who would simply need to acquire a few skills” (Ekelund 1804). From 1805, low-
skilled church personnel with no prior health/epidemic involvement were obliged to attain 
vaccination skills. 1810s data show over 60% of vaccinators were church assistants or musicians, 
followed by priests (12%), upper-class women (10%), and midwives, physicians, and others 
(each 5%) (Sköld 1996). Unlike local demographic and cultural factors, church personnel 
availability and employment emerges as the sole factor highly correlated with vaccination uptake. 
1.2 
Disease Environment and Vaccine Uptake 
Smallpox was the main childhood disease and death cause pre-vaccination. In Sweden, 
widespread Variola major, a highly contagious airborne type, affected children or persons 
lacking natural immunity. The historically unchanged virus caused two weeks of suffering 
(headache, fever, backache, vomiting, diarrhea), followed by pustules (Fenner et al. 1988). Case 
fatality rates reached 20% among infected persons and 55% among children below age 2 (Sköld 
1996). Survivors often bore lifelong pockmarks and complications including blindness, baldness, 
limb deformities, infertility, and respiratory, gastrointestinal, and central nervous system 
conditions. 
No effective treatment exists against smallpox due to viral resistance to temperatures below 
60 degrees and nutrition independence, causing losses regardless of food access or family 
conditions. Pregnant women transmit infection, not protective antibodies, to fetuses, causing 
premature neonatal death. Children must develop their own immunity or receive vaccination for 
protection. Vaccination, including earlier iterations, offers approximately 95% effectiveness 
(Fenner et al. 1988).

Swedish smallpox mortality age patterns changed dramatically with 1801 vaccination 
introduction (Figure A1, Appendix A). Rates calculated from population counts for analyzed 
regions mirror national figures (Pettersson 1912). Despite significant unknown death causes, 
primary infectious disease symptoms were recognizable to registrars (priests, doctors); thus, 
smallpox death age patterns have high relative accuracy (Bengtsson and Lindstrom 2000). 
Between 1790–1800, smallpox mortality followed an L-shaped pattern: approximately 3% of 
children under three died (16% of all causes), with decreasing rates among older children and 
few adult deaths. This indicates societies where older individuals acquired natural immunity 
without passing it to children. Within decades post-1801, the age pattern flattened significantly; 
less than 0.2% of children died from smallpox. 
Figure 1 shows cohort vaccination rates by age two for nineteenth-century Sweden, 
encompassing all studied generations. Vaccine uptake was gradual for decades post-1801, then 
stabilized at 85%. Mandatory vaccination in 1816 only modestly increased uptake among small 
children, suggesting most uptake associated with other factors—suggestively vaccinator numbers 
rather than mandatory law. No one died from smallpox in the 1890s, causing vaccination rates to 
decline. Mortality and vaccination patterns mirror those for Sweden overall (Sköld 1996).

Why did not vaccination rates reach 100% despite mandatory status? Historical narrative 
suggests compulsory vaccination law was a threat making most parents comply (Pettersson 
1912). Yet historical examples of executed fines are rare. Anti-vaccination opposition was lower 
in Sweden than other European countries, with the first known petition appearing half a century 
post-vaccination start. Nevertheless, some spread messages that smallpox was religious sin, and 
local authorities were reluctant to implement policy and conflict with religiously-motivated 
refusers (Sköld 1996). Another hesitance source was false stories about negative vaccine 
consequences spread by vagabonds and beggars. One local doctor classified non-vaccinating 
parents’ cases as: laziness, pleasure from defying law, and vaccination consequence fears 
(Landsarkivet i Lund 1805-1827).  
1.3 Non-Specific Vaccination Effects in the Historical Narrative 
Many contemporaries believed smallpox elimination would yield little gain as other diseases 
would replace it (Hofsten and Lundström 1976). Historical evidence from Sweden and elsewhere 
suggests the opposite—the vaccine combated both smallpox and other infectious diseases, known 
as “non-specific” vaccination health effects. Mayr (2004) cites circumstantial evidence from 
German and Austrian vaccinators reporting that “eye and ear disorders not only improved but 
also disappeared, and that chronic diseases vanished amongst the vaccinees.” He notes


## Page 8


8 
 
vaccinated persons are less susceptible to infectious diseases (measles, scarlet fever, and 
whooping cough, even syphilis) than non-vaccinated persons. For Sweden, annual doctor reports 
show multiple indications of close associations between high vaccination rates and reduced 
infectious disease beyond smallpox (Riksarkivet 1796-1820). Moreover, lifetime gains for 
vaccinated children may emerge from improved disease environments. About 1% of smallpox 
survivors develop vivid lifelong complications (blindness, limb deformities, infertility, 
respiratory/gastrointestinal/central nervous system conditions) (Sköld 1996).

Data 
2.1 
Microdata for Three Generations.  
Study area and sample. We investigate whether vaccinating Generation 1 against smallpox 
positively affects their lifelong well-being and that of Generations 2 and 3, using high-quality 
multi-generational data spanning extended time and age ranges. 
Our data comprise unique register-based datasets with health, demographic, and socio-
economic information from 79 Swedish parishes (18th–21st centuries). Sources include the 
Demographic Data Base for northern/central Sweden (CEDAR 2021; CEDAR 2022), and the 
Scanian Economic-Demographic Database for southern Sweden (Bengtsson et al. 2021). These 
datasets reconstruct life and family histories from high-quality archival records in geographically 
compact areas, minimizing regional biases. Data homogeneity across cohorts and validation 
through 250+ published studies ensure high quality of the datasets. 
We selected parishes containing both pre- and post-vaccination cohorts (see Figure A2, 
Appendix A). Generation 1 includes those born 1790–1820; Generation 2 (their children) were 
born 1805–1865 (mothers aged 15–45); Generation 3 (grandchildren) were born 1820–1910. We 
tracked 141,067 individuals across three full life cycles until death, out-migration, or age 100. 
Figure A3 (Appendix A) shows below-10 mortality rates by cause (smallpox, other 
infectious, non-infectious) from 1790–1920. Causes of death are coded using ICD-10, based on 
expert encoding. While smallpox declined post-vaccination and child mortality decreased 
slightly, cohort life expectancy at age two surged 14 years for those born 1801–1820 versus 
1790–1800, continuing to grow in subsequent generations. 
Individual Vaccination. Our key treatment variable is whether Generation 1 individuals 
received smallpox vaccination by age 2. Microdata show vaccination typically occurred in the 
first years of life (median: 2.04 years for post-1801 cohorts). We avoided continuous vaccination 
dates due to potential imprecision, as changing frequencies suggest recording inaccuracy. 
Controls include never-vaccinated individuals (those with natural immunity from surviving 
smallpox or lacking both vaccination and immunity). Vaccination status derives from priests’

church book marks recorded during censuses and life events. Later vaccination ages may reflect 
family absence rather than initial vaccination dates, reinforcing our exclusion of migrant families 
and children vaccinated after age 2 from controls. Natural immunity cases are too rare for

We verified measurement accuracy by comparing microdata vaccinated counts with 
aggregated parish censuses. Medical reports confirm these priest-reported census records as most

Lifetime Outcomes. Data include death or out-migration dates. Southern Sweden records 
link to the Swedish Death Index (Släktforskarförbund 2019); central/northern Sweden has 54% 
death date coverage. Our longevity indicator counts years lived post-age-two, also indicating 
latest observation time. Results remain consistent when treating these as censored observations. 
Occupational data from church books, poll-tax, and examination registers serve as 
outcomes, controls, and IV construction. Annual records are coded into historical social 
stratification as continuous occupational scores (Lambert et al. 2013), enabling cross-cohort 
comparisons. For the instrument, we selected specific occupations (church assistants, musicians) 
and augmented data with parish/county clergy counts. 
Additional outcomes include disability onset (blindness, deafness, mental/behavioral 
disorders, general weakness) from church records for northern/central Sweden, and literacy data 
until 1870 for northern parishes (Generation 1). Annual literacy tests involved confidential priest 
talks, often followed by social gatherings. 
Offspring outcomes include life expectancy, disability-free life expectancy, and 
occupational scores. We exclude literacy effects due to near-perfect positive collinearity with 
parental vaccination status. 
2.2 Selective Attrition 
Our historical longitudinal dataset is prone to selective migration but not false linkage. 
Below we highlight data quality and our mitigation strategies. 
Digitized family links and demographic events ensure high quality. Swedish household-
based parish registers continuously updated demographic events, maintaining family units on 
single pages while cohabiting. Records were updated from event registers as new events 
occurred. This double-bookkeeping system enables accurate individual tracking (e.g., during out- 
and in-migration). As Sweden’s official registration system until 1990, these records provide 
complete population coverage regardless of church affiliation. Table A1 (Appendix A) confirms


## Page 10


10 
 
digitization quality by showing no significant associations between vaccination status and birth 
seasons—a common inaccuracy marker. 
Swedish sources only record individuals within parish borders. For our population (born 
1790–1910), two strategies mitigate this: 10% linked to national death records, and data collected 
from neighboring parish clusters with within-cluster tracking. This improved coverage since 
most movements occurred within 15 kilometers, with people relocating to the same or nearby 
parishes rather than long distances (Dribe 2003). Median leaver age is 18.1 years, coinciding 
with youth household departure for work, likely in nearby non-sampled parishes. 
Generations 2 and 3 include all children of individuals observed in the study area, including 
return migrants. We observe offspring from 37% of Generation 1 individuals. Figure A4 
(Appendix A) shows observable characteristic differences between Generation 1 individuals with 
and without observed children. Likelihood of having observed children is uncorrelated with 
socio-economic traits (family occupational scores, literacy, maternal marital status), suggesting 
we aren’t selectively observing healthier children. Birth year association is marginally 
significant. Substantial parish disparities exist but sources are unclear, unlinked to geography 
(south-north, urban-rural). Importantly, Generations 2 and 3 span all 70 parishes and 31 cohorts 
studied in Generation 1. 
Selective migration bias direction is difficult to predict. Resource-limited families (landless, 
large families, widows) lacked means for long-distance moves; farmers stayed due to extensive 
local networks and resource access (Vikström, Marklund, and Sandström 2016). Perhaps the only 
coherent out-migration driver was high grain prices, negatively impacting populations by making 
food unaffordable (Dribe 2003). This factor’s appeal is its exogeneity to pre-migration individual 
decisions, serving as a migration predictor and addressing selective migration bias. 
We correct for selective migration using the Heckman procedure (Heckman 1979). First-
stage probit models probability of estimation sample inclusion as a function of rye prices (most 
common grain) and birth month (common accuracy predictor), interacting these with birth year 
dummies following a DID structure (Sant’Anna and Zhao 2020). Rye prices from Jörberg (1972) 
are set based on median cohort ages at 18: 1825 (Generation 1), 1860 (Generation 2), 1910 
(Generation 3). We predict inverse Mills ratios for each individual, including them as covariates 
in estimation models. Alternative inverse probability weighting (weights from same factors; 
(Weuve et al. 2012)) yielded nearly identical results. We report Heckman-adjusted estimates for 
each outcome and generation.

3. Empirical Strategy 
3.1 Selection into Vaccination 
We begin with the analysis of selection for vaccination against smallpox by the age of two for 
Generation 1. From the microdata, we obtained various background characteristics of the 
individual, measuring parental wealth (occupational score and marital status), literacy, parenting 
style (survival history of the family and death of a sibling due to an external cause), as well as 
the year and parish of birth. Figure A5 in Appendix A presents the OLS estimates for these 
variables. The results show that most variables measuring family conditions correlate weakly or 
not at all with the probability of children being vaccinated. For instance, paternal literacy and 
church attendance does not influence the probability, and a one-standard deviation change in 
paternal occupation score increases the probability by only 0.012 percentage points. These results 
align well with the fact that vaccination was free for parents and did not face opposition in 
Sweden.  
The results also indicate that differences in a child’s vaccination status primarily stem from 
the parental parish of residence in the first years of the child’s life. The differences across parishes 
in the proportion of children vaccinated by the age of two vary between -1.1 to 0.16 percentage 
points compared to the baseline. Previous research has shown that the availability of vaccinators, 
such as the ratio of clergy, church assistants, and church musicians per population, explains such 
geographical differences (Sköld 1996). Another significant factor is the year of birth, as 
vaccination was first introduced in 1801, and the vaccination rate steadily rose in the subsequent 
years. The findings therefore suggest that the factors driving vaccination of a child by the age of 
two appear at the regional and cohort level.  
Even with no indication of selection into vaccination at the family level based on 
observables, selection could still appear from unobservable factors. For instance, parishes with a 
higher share of vaccinated children may also be characterized by higher levels of trust to 
authorities, which, in turn, are eager to implement health policies that benefit parishes’ residents, 
such as employ licensed midwives or practice isolation of sick residents before it became a 
widespread health measure (Lazuka, Quaranta, and Bengtsson 2016). On the individual level, 
families that decide to vaccinate their own child may also be more cooperative, for instance, and 
such social norms could affect the child’s future outcomes and the outcomes of the next 
generations regardless of initial vaccination. To address selection into treatment for Generation 
1, we apply three methods: mother fixed effects, DID, and SSIV. As we show below, we need 
all three methods to ensure that we obtain quasi-random variation in individual vaccination status

3.2 Mother Fixed Effects 
As a first strategy to address unobservable selection into a child’s smallpox vaccination, we 
employ mother fixed effects. Mother fixed effects will allow us to compare biological siblings, 
one of whom received the treatment while the other did not. This effectively removes all fixed, 
unobservable family-related factors that may affect both the treatment and long-term outcomes 
in life. The model is as follows: 
(1) Yiprt = βVaccinatediprt + μm + (ηt + γp + δrt + Xi(p)t Γ) + εirpt 
The index i denotes individuals in Generation 1, μm is a set of mother fixed effects. The dependent 
variable, Yiprt, is an individual’s outcome, such as years lived after the age of 2, disability-free 
years lived after the age of 2, literacy, and occupational score. In additional specification, we will 
add a set of controls to effectively control for time-varying factors at the family and area levels, 
such as changes in parish of residence, local health policies, or family-specific disease events. 
Vaccination here can be treated as a positive external shock to one sibling but not the other, 
occurred because at that time family resided in the parish where vaccination was provided or the 
child was born when vaccine became available, even though the family decisions were 
orthogonal to the decision to vaccinate both of their children.  
3.3 DID Approach 
Our second strategy to deal with selection is to adapt a DID design previously used to identify 
the outcomes of exposure to certain infectious diseases targeted by nationwide rapid 
interventions (Lazuka 2020, Ager, Hansen, and Jensen 2017). We exploit two sources of 
variation: (i) the varying exposure of different cohorts to the introduction of the smallpox vaccine 
in 1801, and (ii) the relatively larger benefits for individuals born in parishes with greater 
availability of church assistants and musicians compared to those born in parishes with lower 
availability. As explained in Section 3.2, our sample includes individuals who were either 
vaccinated by age 2 or never vaccinated; thus, the arrival of the vaccine in 1801 serves as a clear 
watershed, nearly perfectly distinguishing treated and untreated cohorts. Pre-vaccine availability 
of church assistants and musicians serves as a measure of a parish’s readiness to perform 
vaccinations (or local demand)—the higher the availability, the greater the vaccine uptake. To 
capture pre-treatment variation, we thus do not use the smallpox infection rate, as our focus is 
ultimately on the impact of the vaccination itself, not the disease.

The parameter β in Equation 1 captures the (reduced-form, intention-to-treat) effect of 
smallpox vaccination. We expect β to be positive, as a higher availability of church assistants 
and musicians should lead to increased vaccination rates and, ultimately, better outcomes. 
Following recent methodological developments in the DID literature (review of Roth et al. 2023), 
we impose the following assumptions for β to have a causal interpretation: no anticipation, the 
stable unit treatment value assumption, and the conditional parallel trends assumption. The no

3.4 SSIV Approach 
3.4.1 Intuition and Equations 
The smallpox vaccination evolved gradually and even stepwise from 1801 across Swedish 
parishes; therefore, we adopt an SSIV approach that allows us to capture exogenous variation in 
children’s smallpox vaccination in Generation 1, better fitting its nonlinear progression. Our 
ultimate goal is to use this variation to explore its impact on the outcomes of Generations 2 and 3. 
Following a SSIV methodological literature, we use the instrument’s formula that best 
describes the impact of the shock (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 2022). Our instrument is Cp(t-1) x

at the parish level and their ratio at the country level. Previously, in a DID approach, we defined

the SSIV approach, we aim to measure availability for each cohort, introducing Cp(t-1) as the

3.4.2 Identifying Assumptions 
The estimates of the instrument’s effects on the vaccination and the outcomes, α and ά, 
should be positive in every equation. Their interpretation resembles that of the DID strategy, 
where we compare pre- and post-vaccine arrival cohorts and parishes with varying availability 
of church assistants and musicians, which reflects the parish’s readiness to vaccinate or the local 
demand for vaccination. With the SSIV approach, we compare cohorts experiencing different 
national shifts (reflecting vaccine supply) across parishes with varying readiness or local demand, 
measured as changing across cohorts. For causality, α and ά must satisfy assumptions that we 
mentioned for DID. 
However, our main interest is in obtaining causal estimates of the effects of Generation 1’s 
individual smallpox vaccination, β. The causality requires four assumptions (Imbens 2014). If 
these assumptions hold, our SSIV estimates reflect the average effect for the observations that 
comply with the instrument, i.e., a local average treatment effect (Angrist, Imbens, and Rubin 
1996). Even though smallpox vaccination is individual, our instrument captures its group


## Page 17


17 
 
variation by construction. In our setting, compliers are therefore parishes with higher proportions 
of vaccinated children that responded to the interacted instrument. Those parishes that did not 
respond to the instrument do not contribute to the estimate. We will address the assumption of 
instrument relevance in Section 5.3.1, and here, we will focus on the remaining three 
assumptions. 
First, the instrument has no direct effect on the outcomes other than through its influence on 
the assignment to vaccination (exclusion restriction). To address this assumption, we chose to 
focus on church assistants and church musicians as the only subgroup of vaccinators. Historical 
sources highlight that Church workers were trustworthy and literate yet lacking knowledge on 
medicine (Sköld 1996). The law of 1804 stipulated that each parish must employ a church 
assistant or musician for vaccination, thereby blocking the potential monopolization of the 
process by doctors. Vaccinations were easy to learn, following the instructions distributed by the 
state and short training by the priest. As an illustration, a church musician who assisted at choirs 
became the first vaccinator in Kävlinge, one of the parishes in southern Sweden, and vaccinated 
against smallpox as his second part-time job; he did not participate in other health-related matters 
(Landsarkivet i Lund 1805-1827). In the neighboring parish, initially, a licensed midwife 
vaccinated children (Landsarkivet i Lund 1785-1857). Although the means of preventing disease 
were very limited in the beginning of the 19th century, some were practiced by doctors, such as 
cause-of-death counting and isolation of the sick, or by midwives, such as proper assistance at 
labour. In these two parishes, our instrument will capture the vaccination efforts of a church 
musician but not of a midwife.  
Second, conditional on controls, we assume that there are no omitted common factors 
affecting both the instrument and the outcome (random assignment). In our case, the series of 
lagged church assistants and musicians in the parish along with the shocks in current church 
assistants and musicians in the region (which constitute two components of the interacted IV) are 
likely to be correlated with both fixed and varying characteristics of the parish (region), such as 
wealth or religiosity, for instance, which influence the outcomes too. In practice, this is not a 
serious problem for our estimates for several reasons. Parish fixed effects in the baseline 
specification control for all permanent factors at the parish level affecting the employment of 
church assistants and musicians. We also introduce families’ characteristics affecting families’ 
decision to vaccinate children interacted with cohort dummies (as in section 4.1), which account 
for the parish shocks related to the changing parish residents’ behavior and outcomes. To identify 
any unobserved time-varying parish shocks, we examine pre-trends and find none, as we further 
elaborate on in Section 5.3.2.


## Page 18


18 
 
In relation to the regional shocks, they similarly can reflect regional health policies, other 
than vaccination. The region-year of birth fixed effects in our baseline specification account for 
any such effects, observed and unobserved. Our analysis also introduces interactions between 
cohort (i.e., year of birth) dummies and local (i.e., for the group of parishes) measures of wealth, 
religiosity, and health policies, which capture differential responses of regions to the mandatory 
vaccination campaign. In particular, we include the number of midwives (interacted with cohort 
dummies) that will control for development of healthcare and composition of the vaccinators’ 
group in the area; smallpox death rate will control for demographic and disease conditions; the 
share of urban population and university students per capita will control for the urbanization and 
progressivity; the number of priests will difference out the effects of religiosity; and the price of 
rye will control for economic development. Finally, to account for the mutual correlation of the 
local shocks, we cluster standard errors at the parish level.  
Third, the instrument must ensure that treatment becomes a more attractive option 
(monotonicity). We have searched the local vaccination reports (Riksarkivet 1796-1820) to 
identify the possibility of “defiers”, i.e., cases in which parishes reduce the vaccination rate when 
there is a positive regional employment influx of church assistants and church musicians, and 
increase it when the influx is negative; we have not found any such cases. Under the presence of 
heterogeneous treatment effects, as discussed in Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel (2022), a causally 
interpretable IV estimand is guaranteed as long as the treatment (i.e., individual vaccination) is 
correctly specified, shares are non-negative, and the true effects of shocks on each treatment are 
monotone (i.e., there are no “defiers”). We have already discussed that the error in the treatment 
variable is unlikely in Section 3.2. The shares cannot be negative by construction.

4.3.3 Parallel Pre-Trends 
It is possible to validate the shift-share instrument by examining whether observations with 
different exposure shares exhibit parallel trends prior to the shock (Borusyak, Hull, and Jaravel 
2022). This follows from that linear instrumental-variables with shift-share as an instrument 
varying by year of birth and parish of birth are analogous to the DID with continuous treatment, 
with underlying “parallel trends” restrictions. Any evidence for significant pre-trends would 
signal that a set of cohorts in particular parishes with different levels of the instrument would 
have evolved differently from each other even in the absence of the vaccination campaign.  
To assess the plausibility of the parallel trends, we create the leads of the instrument and 
estimate the reduced-form effects with these leads instead of the instrument. The results for the 
models with two sets of covariates are presented on Figure C2 Appendix C. Overall, there is no 
evidence of significant pre-trends in the outcomes. However, note that the evidence for no pre-
trends points to the absence of the time-varying shocks, sufficient for the causality claim of the 
reduced-form estimates.  
4.4 Influence of Epidemics and Other Interventions 
In this section, we explore the role that positive and negative shocks, aside from vaccination, 
played in generating our results.  
 Presumably, smallpox epidemics severely affected the cohorts in question, particularly 
before the introduction of the vaccine, and left behind a negatively selected group of children 
who serve as a comparison. If this is the case, our findings on lifetime effects should be 
interpreted as the combined result of the vaccine’s positive impact and the benefits of avoiding


## Page 23


23 
 
the detrimental effects of epidemics. Previous research has demonstrated that airborne disease 
epidemics, including smallpox, left long-term scars on survivors, manifesting in lower survival 
rates (Quaranta 2014).  
The institutional context of the vaccination campaign suggests that no beneficial 
interventions took place in parallel. Medicine was underdeveloped at that time, and 
epidemiological hospitals helpful in isolating infectious disease were built much later (Lazuka, 
Quaranta, and Bengtsson 2016). One potential intervention to consider was the practice of 
midwives—they improved maternal survival and could influence infant health, with long-term 
health consequences. However, previous research has found no effects on infant health prior to 
the acceptance of the germ theory of disease and re-education of midwives at the late nineteenth 
century (Lorentzon and Pettersson-Lidbom 2021; Lazuka 2018). Another intervention to 
contemplate is the introduction of potatoes around 1805. At that time, farmers began growing 
potatoes in arable fields, which increased the production of nutritious food, potentially benefiting 
the growth and health of the population (Lazuka, Bengtsson, and Svensson 2023).  
We assess the influence of smallpox epidemics and other interventions on vaccination 
against smallpox and lifetime outcomes by introducing an interaction of child smallpox mortality, 
the ratio of midwives to population, and the quantity of potato seeds per square kilometer 
(observed at the parish level) with an interacted IV into Equation 3 (the reduced-form equation). 
Table D1 in Appendix D reports the results.  
The reduced-form estimates of the interacted instrument on all outcomes remain largely 
unchanged after accounting for interactions with other shocks, suggesting a strong and unbiased 
direct effect of vaccinators’ efforts on lifetime outcomes. Moreover, there is an additional 
beneficial effect for individuals who are positively affected by vaccination efforts and born 
during smallpox epidemic years, amounting to an increase of one year in lifespan and two units 
of occupational score. Since the early smallpox vaccine was highly effective (Hedin 1802), this 
additional effect likely arises from the detrimental outcomes experienced by unvaccinated 
individuals, whose lives were significantly impacted by the disease. Therefore, two-thirds of the 
effects we observe are likely due to analyzing a historical population severely affected by 
epidemics. 
For positive cointerventions, we do not find any interactive effects. For midwives, the 
absence of the interaction effect aligns with the observation that midwives or doctors did not 
serve as the primary group of vaccinators. Regarding the impact of potatoes, the results reinforce 
the notion that smallpox is a disease with a low correlation to nutritional intake (Fenner et al. 
1988).

5.2 Mechanisms of Intergenerational Transmission 
In the final section, we conduct a causal mediation analysis of the impact of Generation 1 
vaccination on the outcomes of subsequent generations. While there are numerous potential 
mediators in these long-term relationships, our primary focus is to distinguish between factors 
related to nature and nurture. Specifically, we aim to determine whether smallpox vaccination 
induces people to transmit knowledge through nurturing and/or if there is a direct biological 
transmission of past environments (i.e., epigenetic inheritance) (Collado, Ortuño-Ortín, and 
Stuhler 2023).  
We select mediators based on their relevance to nurturing and natural influences. To 
measure nurture, we analyze variables such as the childhood smallpox vaccination status of 
Generation 2 (and Generation 3), whether the child was assisted by a midwife at birth, and the 
parental occupational score. For nature, we use the fixed component derived from the mothers 
of Generation 2 and 3. Specifically, we run mother fixed effects regressions on the outcomes of 
Generation 2 and 3 (i.e., years lived, disability-free years lived, and occupational score) and 
predict a part of each outcome shared within mother for each individual—our measure of 
epigenetic factors.  
To perform the causal mediation analysis, we follow the causal inference literature, which 
proposes to decompose the total effect into the natural direct effect and natural indirect effect 
(Imai, Keele, and Tingley 2010). In our case, the natural direct effect is comparing Vaccinatediprt 
at 1 and 0 intervening to fix the level of mediator to 0, and the natural indirect effect is comparing 
the effects at different levels of mediator, intervening to fix Vaccinatediprt at 1. Both effects are 
identified under the assumptions of no unmeasured confounders between the combinations of 
treatment, outcome, and mediator, given a set of observables. To improve the plausibility of these 
assumptions, we perform the analysis on a set of covariates that we used in a 2SRI model.  
We follow Imai, Keele, and Yamamoto (2010) and estimate the direct and indirect natural

belonging to Generation 3, whose grandparents were vaccinated against smallpox by age two, 
receive a gain of 0.09 per unit of their own occupational score, or 5.4 units at the maximum (61.8 
x 0.087).

through various channels, enhances both health and socio-economic outcomes for at least two 
more generations. 
Conclusions 
In this study, we investigated whether smallpox vaccination in early childhood enabled 
individuals to live longer and become prosperous as adults, and whether such vaccination effects


## Page 27


27 
 
were transmitted to their two consecutive generations. To obtain the causal effects of smallpox 
vaccination, we applied a SSIV approach, using the fact that vaccination in Sweden was carried 
out by low-skilled clergy who otherwise did not perform public health duties. We leveraged 
unique historical microdata from different areas across Sweden, covering the full lifespans of 
three generations. 
Our study leads us to draw several important conclusions. First, we find evidence consistent 
with both specific and non-specific vaccination effects. Smallpox vaccination increases the total 
and disability-free life expectancy of Generation 1 by 11 years and enhances their occupational 
achievements by 10%. Such effects emerged due to decreases in mortality from smallpox and 
other causes, but also appear to have reduced disability associated with ailments that could hinder 
human capital accumulation. Second, these effects persist across generations. Smallpox 
vaccination of Generation 1 increases the life expectancy of Generation 2 by 2 years and of 
Generation 3 by 1 year, as well as improving their occupational scores. More than half of the 
transmitted effects are attributed to nurture, as vaccinated individuals are more likely to vaccinate 
their children across generations, while epigenetic factors account for the remainder. Finally, we 
obtain similar results when utilizing different causal strategies, such as SSIV, DID, and mother 
fixed-effects. The results withstand a large number of robustness checks. 
Our findings are potentially important for policy as they underscore the power of 
vaccination. The evidence that smallpox vaccination offers protection not only against smallpox 
but also against other diseases makes vaccination a powerful health intervention. Our findings, 
which highlight very long-term, intergenerational health and economic benefits from 
vaccination, suggest that the total benefits of smallpox vaccination were much larger than the 
existing literature suggests. We demonstrate that while a significant portion of the effects can be 
attributed to the analysis of a historical population severely affected by epidemics, vaccination 
remains beneficial in the very long term, even in milder disease environments like those seen 
today. Whether these findings are applicable to other vaccines is beyond the scope of this paper 
but remains an important topic for future research.
