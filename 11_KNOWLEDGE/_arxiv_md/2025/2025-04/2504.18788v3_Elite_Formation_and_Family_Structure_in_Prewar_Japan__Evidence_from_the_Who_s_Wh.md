Elite Formation and Family Structure in Prewar Japan:
Evidence from the Who’s Who Records
Hiroshi Kumanomido∗, Suguru Otani†, Yutaro Takayasu‡
September 11, 2025
Abstract
This paper introduces a newly constructed individual-level dataset of prewar Japanese
elites using the “Who’s Who” directories published in 1903–1939. Covering approximately
the top 0.1% of the population, the dataset contains rich information on social origin, educa-
tion, occupation, and family structure. By reconstructing intergenerational links and family
networks, we provide descriptive evidence on elite formation and persistence across geogra-
phy, social groups, and education during institutional transitions. The dataset provides a
foundational empirical resource for studying elite reproduction, intergenerational and inter-
group mobility, and institutional development during Japan’s transition to a modern society.
Keywords: Elite formation, Intergenerational mobility, Social mobility, Institutional devel-
opment
JEL code: N35, J62, D63

In Japan, from the early 17th to the mid-19th century, the Tokugawa family and their samurai
subordinates (hereditary warrior-administrator class) governed over the nation. This period was
characterized by a hereditary and rigid social system that prioritized samurai in occupational,
educational, and residential opportunities over commoners. In the mid-19th century, increasing
pressure from domestic and Western powers forced Japan to modernize its political and economic
institutions. Consequently, samurai leaders from outside the Tokugawa family led the effort to
establish the new Meiji government. The government introduced meritocratic principles, such as
abolishing the privileges of the samurai class and expanding public education to both social groups.
Positions in the bureaucracy and military, previously limited to samurai families, began to be se-
lected through competitive examinations two decades after the government was established. The
government established new governance systems, similar to those in Western countries, including
a French-influenced civil code and a German-based social insurance system. These drastic trans-
formations from feudal to modern society gave rise to new institutions that continued to evolve
even after World War II.
This paper has two primary objectives. First, it documents the stylized facts of elite forma-
tion in early 20th-century Japan to explore how Japan’s developmental path was close to those
observed in other countries. Second, it aims to establish a data source for future research using
our newly compiled dataset, which has already seen increasing use in studies of elite formation
and intergenerational mobility.
This paper focuses on individuals who held leadership roles in politics and business in prewar
Japan (e.g., bureaucrats, CEOs). To capture individuals in such positions, we compiled a histor-
ical dataset based on the Japanese Personnel Inquiry Records (PIR), whose structure is similar
to biographical directories such as the “Who’s Who” directories published in the United States,
the United Kingdom, and other countries. The PIR contains detailed biographical information,
including an individual’s name, birth year, birthplace, residential address, social groups (hered-
itary nobles (Kazoku), samurai, commoners), educational background, occupational trajectories
throughout their lifetimes, and their family members. Furthermore, we reconstruct the family links
between elites and their children. This additional feature allows us to analyze not just elite forma-
tion at this historical juncture, but also clarify how status, power, and influence were transmitted

Using a keyword-based approach, we classify elites by (i) geography (birth prefectures and
residential prefectures), (ii) social groups, (iii) occupation, and (iv) educational background to
examine how these characteristics changed as Japan transitioned toward a modern economy. First,
we find two distinct paths of spatial mobility.
Some elites migrated to urban areas to access
economic and social opportunities, while others remained in their home prefectures, suggesting
that local ties continued to be an important factor in maintaining their elite positions. Second,
even though commoners were underrepresented in elite positions in the early 20th century, we find a
substantial increase in their share within one generation. To capture the occupational trajectories,
we classified elites by industrial affiliations and occupational categories. The industrial affiliations
of elites became more diverse over time. Among them, executives consistently held a large share,
indicating that the modern corporate structure became increasingly common. Finally, we find a
growing share of elites with higher education degrees, suggesting that the expansion of educational
institutions played an important role in social mobility, which is often observed in other countries
during transitions from feudal to modern societies. Overall, we observe patterns consistent with
findings from recent studies on elite formation in other countries and historical settings.
Furthermore, we reconstruct family linkages between elite fathers and their children (including
biological sons, daughters, and adopted sons). First, we examine family planning behavior by
analyzing the relationship between each child’s birth order and gender. The results suggest that
adopted sons were often used as a means to secure husbands for eldest daughters and to preserve
the family lineage.
Furthermore, biological sons are more likely to be recorded as firstborns,
suggesting possible underreporting of biological daughters in the records. Second, we investigate
the intergenerational transmission of elite status from elite fathers to their sons using extensive
name and birth year matching. We find that sons of elites were approximately 30 times more
likely to achieve elite status compared to the general male population. While previous studies
using historical datasets have shown long-term persistence of elite status over several centuries
(Clark and Ishii 2012, Clark 2014, Alesina et al. 2021, 2022, Dupraz and Simson 2024), our findings
similarly indicate strong intergenerational persistence.
The primary contribution of this paper stands on the construction of a historical dataset which
includes biographical information of top elites during the transitional period from feudal to modern
societies. Several studies have utilized the PIR as a primary data source to investigate the dynamics
of elite formation during Japan’s developmental stages. Particularly, most of the literature focuses
on the impact of institutional changes on elite formation during this period.
Recent studies focus on the role of education (Moriguchi et al. 2024, Ichimura et al. 2024).

Historical Background
During the Tokugawa period (1603—1868), Japan was governed by the Tokugawa family under
a rigid feudal hierarchy. Political authority was delegated to local lords (Daimyo) and their sub-
ordinates samurai who were hereditarily engaged in military and administrative roles. Legal and
institutional barriers prohibited social mobility (e.g., educational, occupational, residential, and
marriage opportunities) between samurai and commoners (farmers, artisans, and merchants).
However, the new Meiji government was established in 1868 in response to growing pressure
from domestic and Western powers.
The new government abolished hereditary privileges and
restructured the former classes into three groups: hereditary nobles (Kazoku), samurai, and com-
moners. Reforms such as the stipend reduction in 1869 and the conscription law in 1873 disman-
tled the privilege of the samurai.2 Although institutional details differed from those of Western
countries undergoing industrialization and today’s developing countries, Japan’s transformation

followed a similar path by eliminating a feudal structure.
Japan’s transformation was not only about dismantling hereditary privileges, but also about
institutionalizing merit-based pathways to elite positions. The Education Order of 1872 and the
Civil Service Examination Law of 1893 introduced standardized schooling and competitive exams
for the recruitment of bureaucrats. Licensing systems for physicians, lawyers, and teachers were
similarly formalized through schooling and qualification exams.
As Japan transitioned toward a modern society, the importance of local lineage and kinship ties
declined, particularly due to increasing migration from rural (birthplace) to urban areas. In this
context, there was growing demand for formal biographical registries of distinguished individuals,
such as the Japanese Personnel Inquiry Records (PIR), particularly for making marriage and
business networks.

3.1
Japanese Personnel Inquiry Records
This paper focuses on individuals who achieved leadership positions in politics and business in
prewar Japan (e.g., bureaucrats, CEOs). To capture individuals in such positions, we collected a
historical dataset based on the Japanese Personnel Inquiry Records (PIR). We compiled the PIR
published in 1903, 1915, 1928, 1934, and 1939.3 This dataset is comparable to the “Who’s Who”
records in developed countries, as it is a selective list of distinguished individuals, such as high-
income earners, CEOs, bureaucrats, politicians, military servants, and professional elites (judges,
lawyers, scholars, and physicians).4
As documented in Section 2, the importance of local lineage and kinship ties declined, partic-
ularly due to increasing migration from rural (birthplace) to urban areas. In this context, there
was a growing demand for formal biographical registries of distinguished individuals, particularly
for making marriage and business networks. The original data source includes biographical infor-
mation for individuals, such as birth year, birthplace, current residential locations, social groups
(hereditary nobles, samurai, and commoners), education, occupational trajectories throughout
their lifetimes, and detailed information about family members. We describe the more detailed
information for PIR in Appendix Section A.1.

3.2
Data Structure
Using biographical records of elites, we construct an individual-level dataset that includes detailed
information on family members (their parents and children). To briefly explain the structure of
our dataset, we distinguish elites’ information between two components: (1) information on elites
and their families within each edition, and (2) linkages of individuals and their family members
across editions.
Individual-edition Level Data
We define a set of editions as T = {1, 4, 8, 10, 12}, correspond-
ing to 1903, 1915, 1928, 1934, and 1939 as published year, and a set of individual elites as It. An
observation unit of the raw datasets is an individual i ∈It in edition t ∈T . The data of individual
i ∈It in edition t ∈T record data tuple Dit defined as
Dit = {Xit, mi, fi, wit, sit, ait, dit},
where Xit is a set of covariates for individual i in edition t, including time-invariant covariates (such
as name, birth year, birth place, social group, educational background) and time-varying covariates
(such as current residential location, occupational history).5 The other elements of Dit include
family member information including the mother mapping function m(i) = mi, which determines
the mother of individual i; the father mapping function f(i) = fi, which determines the father of
individual i; and the wife mapping function w(i, t) = wit, which identifies the wife of individual
i in edition t. Additionally, Dit contains the son correspondence s(i, t) = sit, which specifies the
set of sons of individual i; the adopted son correspondence a(i, t) = ait, which identifies the set of
adopted sons of individual i; and the daughter correspondence d(i, t) = dit, which determines the
set of daughters of individual i.
Graph Representation within Edition
For each edition t ∈T , we construct a graph Gt =
(Vt, Et), where Vt is a node set and Et is an edge set. The set of nodes consists of three groups:

the set of listed individuals It, the set of their parents Pt, and the set of their children Ct. The
parent set is defined as Pt = {mi, fi | i ∈It}, where mi and fi denote the mother and father of
individual i, respectively. The child set is defined as Ct = {sit, ait, dit | i ∈It}, where sit, ait, and
dit represent the set of sons, adopted sons, and daughters of individual i in edition t, respectively.
By construction, the three sets It, Pt, and Ct are mutually disjoint, ensuring that the graph is
tripartite.
The edge set Et is determined by familial relationships recorded in the dataset. Each individual
i ∈It is connected to their parents via edges (mi, i) and (fi, i), representing the parental lineage.
Similarly, each individual is connected to their children via edges (i, s) for s ∈sit, (i, a) for a ∈ait,
and (i, d) for d ∈dit, representing the transmission of lineage to the next generation. Since all
edges connect vertices across the three distinct sets Pt, It, and Ct, the structure of Gt remains
strictly tripartite for any t ∈T . The graph representation completes the within-edition family
structure.
Graph Representation across Edition
This tripartite structure, which consists of parents,
listed individuals, and children, allows us to clearly represent intergenerational links within each
edition and provides a consistent framework for linking individuals across editions t ∈T . By
matching nodes with identical or near-identical personal identifiers (e.g., name, birth year, and
family lineage), we trace the continuity of elite individuals/families over time. These intertemporal
linkages reveal how often individuals reappear in the records, providing insights into the persistence
and turnover of elite status in prewar Japan. We represent such overlaps with inter-edition edges
that connect the same individual across temporal layers of the graph.
The graph framework can also be extended across editions to reconstruct longer family trees
that span multiple periods. Since each edition contains snapshots of familial ties (e.g., parent-child
relationships), connecting individual nodes across editions using consistent identifiers allows us to
link ancestors and descendants beyond the scope of a single edition. For example, if a father
appears in an earlier edition and his son in a later one, we can establish a temporal family edge
from the parent node in Gt to the child node in Gt′. Concretely, if individual i ∈It lists a son
s ∈sit ∪ait in edition t, and that son is matched to an elite individual j ∈It′ in a later edition
t′ > t based on personal identifiers such as name and birth year, we construct an inter-edition edge
(i, j) ∈Et,t′. Formally,
if s ∈sit ∪ait and s ≡j ∈It′, then (i, j) ∈Et,t′.
In this way, layering the tripartite graphs across editions allows us to reconstruct dynamic, inter-
generational graphs that reveal the evolution of elite family networks over time.

Residential Address
To standardize geographic information, we classified both birth and res-
idential prefectures based on keywords corresponding to the modern prefectural system.13 Pre-
fecture names were mapped using a comprehensive keyword-based approach, incorporating terms
used during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as well as regional domain names used by feudal
lords in the Tokugawa period. To show historical migration and administrative boundaries, we
include regions outside present-day Japan, such as Taiwan, Korea, and parts of China. Using this
keyword-based approach, we classified both the birthplace and current residential address of each
elite individual into one of 47 prefectures or an additional category for foreign regions.
Tax Payments
As shown in Figure 1, the PIR gradually expanded its inclusion criteria over
time, listing approximately 0.01% of the total population in edition 1 and 0.16% in edition 12.
To identify elites consistently across editions, we utilize information on individual income tax
payments for those listed in the 1934 and 1939 editions.14
To estimate the income level of listed individuals, we count the number of income taxpayers in
each income bracket using data from the Tax Bureau Statistical Yearbook (Shuzei-kyoku Toukei
Nenpousho in Japanese).
Following Moriguchi et al. (2024), who analyze the 1934 and 1939
editions, we confirm that the PIR captures a substantial share of the national economic elite (e.g.,
53% of the top 0.01% income earners), based on comparisons with the Tax Bureau Statistical
Yearbooks.

remained in their birth prefecture throughout their careers. They possibly maintained strong local
networks and continued their careers in their birth prefectures. Second, access to economic and
social opportunities in major urban areas might be one of the key factors contributing to the
increasing number of elites in metropolitan areas. Overall, the results show both the persistence
of local elite structures and the emergence of new metropolitan elites.

5
Conclusion
This paper introduces and analyzes the Japanese Personnel Inquiry Records (PIR), a biographical
dataset covering distinguished individuals in prewar Japan. The dataset includes detailed bio-
graphical information for roughly the top 0.1% of the population during Japan’s transition from a
feudal to a modern society. Furthermore, we reconstruct intergenerational linkages between elite
fathers and their sons and daughters. For future research, we can utilize this dataset as the primary
data source for examining the transmission of elite status across generations.
Using this dataset, we show stylized facts about elite formation in early 20th-century Japan,
focusing on spatial mobility, social group composition, modernization of corporate structure, and
25


## Page 26


the role of higher education in social mobility. First, we find that strong local networks contributed
to the persistence of elites in rural areas, while access to economic and social opportunities increased
the number of elites in urban centers. Second, although commoners were initially underrepresented
in elite positions, we observe a substantial increase in their share within one generation, indicating
significant upward mobility. Finally, we find a growing share of elites who graduated from higher
education institutions, suggesting that the expansion of educational institutions played a key role in
promoting social mobility, which is consistent with transitions observed in other countries shifting
from feudal to modern societies.
We also examine family linkages between elite fathers and their children. The results suggest
that adopted sons were often used as a means to secure husbands for eldest daughters and to pre-
serve the family lineage. Furthermore, biological sons are more likely to be recorded as firstborns,
suggesting possible underreporting of biological daughters in the records. Finally, we investigate
intergenerational transmission of elite status using exact name and birth year matching. We find
that elites’ sons were approximately 30 times more likely to achieve elite status compared to the
general male population. This result suggests a strong persistence of elite status across generations
even during this developmental period.

A.1
Data Source
As Japanese society grew increasingly complex in the early 20th century, it became more difficult
for individuals to know one another’s backgrounds, personal histories, or family ties, even within
business and social networks. The Personnel Inquiry Office (Jinji Koshinjo) was established in
1902 to meet this need. The aim was to promote social transparency and stability by helping
individuals make informed decisions in contexts such as hiring, marriage, or business partnerships.
To address this mission, the office began compiling the Personnel Inquiry Records (PIR), in-
cluding not only occupational trajectories but also information on family structure, marriage ties,
and social standing.
PIR sourced information from multiple sources of directories, such as the government’s official
personnel list (Shokuinroku), the Who’s Who (Shinshiroku) published by Kojunsha, bank and
corporate directories (Teikoku Ginkou Kaisha Youroku) published by Teikoku Koshinjo, bank and
corporate directories (Ginkou Kaisha Youroku) published by Tokyo Koshinjo, the member list of
the corporate executives directories (Zenkoku Shokaisha Yakuinroku) published by Shogyo koshinjo.
Based on these sources, the editors supplemented information, including family registry documents
and private investigations. As a result, the dataset includes detailed records on family structure,
occupational history, and educational background.

The first three editions of PIR, published in 1903,

relationship to listed individuals. By extensive name and birth year matching, we identify whether
