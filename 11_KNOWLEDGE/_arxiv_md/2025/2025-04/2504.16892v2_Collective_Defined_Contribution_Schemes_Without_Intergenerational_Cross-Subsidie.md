Collective Defined Contribution Schemes
Without Intergenerational Cross-Subsidies
John Armstronga, James Dalbyb, Rohan Hobbsc
a,b,cDepartment of Mathematics, King’s College London, UK
a,*john.armstrong@kcl.ac.uk, bjames.dalby@kcl.ac.uk, crohan.hobbs@kcl.ac.uk
Abstract
We present an architecture for managing Collective Defined Con-
tribution (CDC) schemes. The current approach to UK CDC can be
described as shared-indexation, where the nominal benefit of every
member in a scheme receives the same level of indexation each year.
The design of such schemes rely on the use of approximate discounting
methodologies to value liabilities, and this leads to intergenerational
cross-subsidies which can be large and unpredictable. We present an
alternative approach which we call Collective-Drawdown CDC. This
approach does not result in intergenerational cross-subsidies since all
pooling is performed by explicit insurance contracts. It is therefore
completely fair. Moreover, this scheme results in better pension out-
comes when compared to shared-indexation CDC under the same
model parameters.
1
Introduction
Collective Defined Contribution (CDC) pension schemes have now arrived
in the UK following the opening of the Royal Mail Collective Pension Plan
(RMCPP) [24, 23]. In the current framework of UK CDC, contribution rates
are fixed as a percentage of salaries, benefits paid out vary and risks are
shared collectively [26]. The main risks proposed to be shared are investment
and longevity risk for the purpose of delivering better outcomes in retirement.

The RMCPP is a shared-indexation CDC scheme with flat accrual. In a
shared-indexation CDC scheme, all members receive a certain level of nomi-
nal benefit each year in exchange for their contributions. In the RMCPP, the
nominal benefit obtained for a given level of contribution is fixed, irrespec-
tive of age, which is why we call it a flat-accrual scheme. Each year, a level
of indexation is chosen to ensure assets match liabilities and then this level
is applied uniformly to all members’ accrued nominal benefit entitlements.
Because the accrual rate is the same for all ages in the RMCPP, this leads
to significant intergenerational cross-subsidies. An alternative approach to
a shared-indexation CDC scheme is a dynamic-accrual scheme, where the
nominal benefit received for a given level of contribution takes into account
the time-value of investments. Modelling and simulations of such schemes
can be found in [27, 3].
The flat-accrual approach results in large cross subsidies between genera-
tions, with the benefit entitlements of older generations being worth far more
than that of younger generations in exchange for the same contribution. This
is reduced significantly in the dynamic-accrual approach. However, dynamic-
accrual schemes do not perfectly value nominal benefits due to the use of an
approximate discounting methodology. As a result, there remain significant
cross-subsidies in these schemes and they are stochastic (see [3] for a detailed
study of cross subsidy).
In this paper, we study an alternative CDC scheme design that delivers
the proposed benefits of CDC without any cross-subsidies, whilst also out-
performing shared-indexation CDC. We call the scheme Collective-Drawdown
CDC. This is because each member has a known fund value, as in a Defined-
Contribution (DC) scheme, but members are additionally insured against
longevity risk via an internal insurance market. This approach will, by de-
sign, not contain any cross-subsidies. We still consider our approach to be
a form of CDC because it pools longevity risk collectively rather than re-
lying on an external insurer or an employer covenant, and operates on a
defined-contribution basis. Some readers may take a different view on what
are the defining features of a “CDC scheme”, but we believe that from the
points-of-view of both policy makers and scheme members, these are the key
features.
The internal insurance market in individual longevity risk is the defining
feature of collective-drawdown. It is a simple extension of a classical tontine
mechanism that is completely fair, even for a heterogenous pool of individ-
uals. This is because we separate investment risk and longevity risk when

calculating an individuals longevity payment which is weighted according to
an individuals expected contribution to the market over a given period. This
ensures contributions satisfy actuarial fairness and tontine payments are fair
for each individual. This is essential if such a market is to be implemented
in practice.
Other tontine payments for heterogenous funds that satisfy some defi-
nition of fairness have been suggested. For example, [21] uses a definition
of equitable to determine payments. This means each individual loses an
identical fraction of their wealth to the tontine i.e., the present value of fu-
ture payments are all slightly less than the initial contribution. [10] extends
this work by deriving a numerical procedure to achieve approximate fairness,
in the sense that payments for policyholders are almost identical among all
different cohorts. A very similar approach to ours is given in [13], where
the payment received by each member of a group is proportional to their
individual wealth and force of mortality, and this achieves actuarial fairness.
The key difference with our approach is that payments are determined on
an individual basis, rather than a group/cohort basis, so every member can
be different, and we show how this mechanism can operate in discrete time
which makes it much simpler to explain to members and allows for investment
in illiquid assets.
More generally, the simplicity and flexibility of tontines mean that numer-
ous optimal investment problems into innovative pension products utilising
them have been studied.
Some examples of the rich set of schemes ton-
tines can facilitate include, tontines with minimum guaranteed payouts [12],
pooled annuity funds [25, 13], combinations of annuities and tontines [11, 20],
as well as splitting funds between an individual/bequest account and a ton-
tine account subject to different rules [9, 7]. A review of the long history of
tontines and the more recent literature can be found in [19]. Thus while our
term “collective drawdown” is new, the idea of pension vehicle that combines
an investment strategy chosen by an adviser or algorithm with a tontine is
not. We nevertheless think that it is helpful to provide a label for such a
vehicle.
This longevity risk market could also be of benefit within classical CDC
schemes to maximize the pooling of longevity risk. For this reason, we begin
by proposing an overall architecture which can be used by a pension provider
to efficiently manage multiple schemes, which might include both collective-
drawdown schemes and shared-indexation schemes.
We compare the performance of shared-indexation and collective-drawdown

approaches in a Black–Scholes model. Since shared-indexation CDC has a
design that does not incorporate any detailed beliefs about the market, a
simple model such as the Black–Scholes model seems a fairer test of their
relative performance than a more complex model, as a collective-drawdown
scheme could be tuned to match these complexities. The optimal investment
strategy used for the collective-drawdown fund is found using a neural net-
work, rather than solving a classical HJB equation directly. We expect that
this approach would continue to work well in more complex market models.
Our key finding is that the collective-drawdown approach is better able to
produce an “income for life” than shared-indexation schemes. The collective-
drawdown approach also achieves this without any intergenerational cross-
subsidies. We demonstrate this numerically by finding a strategy that most
would agree out-performs shared-indexation CDC. We also show that in com-
plete markets, collective drawdown is optimal in the sense that under very
mild assumptions on preferences, there are no mutually beneficial schemes
that can improve upon collective drawdown.
We also argue that the collective-drawdown approach is simpler to inter-
pret than shared-indexation schemes. This may seem counter-intuitive since
we use a neural network to compute the optimal strategy for our collective-
drawdown approach. However, we argue that the closed-form formula used
in shared-indexation CDC is not as simple to interpret as it may seem: a
shared-indexation CDC contract is a path-dependent derivative whose be-
haviour also depends upon the future demographic profile of the scheme.
The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, we describe the proposed
architecture. In particular, we describe how an internal longevity insurance
market could operate and how multiple employers can be combined. In Sec-
tion 3, we describe how we model a shared-indexation scheme. In Section 4,
we describe how we model a collective-drawdown fund and select its opti-
mal investment strategy. In Section 5, we show pension outcomes from a
collective-drawdown fund and compare these to a shared-indexation fund
subject to the same model parameters. In Section 6, we discuss the practical
pros and cons of different approaches. In Section 7, some conclusions are
presented.

2
An architecture for multi-employer CDC
The overall architecture we propose for a provider of collective defined con-
tribution pensions is shown in Figure 1. Each employee is a member of one
scheme that has been set up to meet the needs of one or more employers. The
members themselves are not expected to make complex decisions about pen-
sion investments. Instead, they simply choose what contributions to make
(with sensible default levels already chosen for them) and, ultimately, receive
the benefits from the scheme. This is the member layer of the design.
The employer layer is where strategic decision making takes place. This
determines the types of scheme which should be available to the employees,
the rules by which these schemes operate, and the investment and benefit
targets the schemes will achieve. It is efficient to make these decisions at the
employer level. Strategic decision making requires too much financial exper-
tise for most members to be able to engage with this process. However, by
providing customization at the employer level, the differing needs of different
types of employee can be catered for.
A central feature of a CDC scheme is that it should perform longevity
pooling. This requires some form of underwriting. Underwriting should be
5


## Page 6


performed at the level of strategic decision making to ensure that all parties
who decide upon an insurance contract have access to the same information.
It will normally be much cheaper to perform underwriting for an employer
as a whole rather than for each individual member. There will be much less
opportunity for manipulation of private information about one’s health if
longevity insurance is agreed at the employer layer, rather than the mem-
ber layer. Thus, we propose that the internal insurance market operates at
the employer level. We also propose that longevity insurance operates be-
tween employers, not just within individual schemes. This will maximize the
potential benefits of longevity pooling.
Finally, in the investment layer, the provider must make concrete, tac-
tical investment decisions to meet the strategic goals of each scheme. The
expertise needed to make these decisions can be pooled across all employers,
and investment decisions can be netted to reduce costs. To achieve this, the
provider can create an investment layer containing a small number of man-
aged portfolios such as a growth portfolio invested in equities and a low-risk
portfolio invested in index-linked gilts. The strategic decision making at the
employer layer is translated into a decision about how to weight investments
across these portfolios. The job of the investment layer is the day-to-day
management of these shared portfolios.
This design is intended to minimize costs, which are indicated in italics
in Figure 1. Underwriting costs occur at the employer layer. Transaction
costs occur at the investment layer and are reduced by netting. The provider
can use the same fundamental scheme design for multiple employers which
should result in reduced costs for scheme approval.

tontine, except that it allows individuals to choose different investments.

4
Modelling a collective-drawdown scheme
We now propose an alternative scheme design to take advantage of the
longevity pooling mechanism.
4.1
General collective-drawdown schemes
In a collective-drawdown fund, a separate account is maintained at all times
for each investor (α, i) (recall that α numbers the schemes and i the individual
within scheme α). An employer may offer a small number of different scheme
choices and customizations for their employees, perhaps with different risk
profiles, but after this, decisions on investment and consumption each year
will be determined at the employer-level. This frees members from the task
of strategic decision making.
One simple way to choose an investment and consumption strategy would
be to specify a fixed life-styling strategy for investment, and a similar table
indicating what proportion of one’s wealth to consume each year in retire-
ment. In the next section, we give a more sophisticated approach to choosing
an investment and consumption strategy that does not use fixed tables. In-
stead, we identify an optimal strategy by solving a problem similar to that
of Merton [17], but additionally take into account mortality risk, longevity
pooling, and use a more realistic utility function.
Our example is intended to be illustrative. There is considerable scope in
the design of collective-drawdown schemes to incorporate a wealth of features.
Our intention is simply to give an example of what is possible.

the collective-drawdown strategy in this paper. Building on [25], the paper

5.2
Effectiveness of the internal longevity market
Our simulations in the previous section assume an infinite fund size. In this
section, we investigate how large schemes need to be for the longevity market
to be effective. The key observation is that what matters is the overall size of
the longevity market, and not the size of the individual schemes. As a result,
it is possible to have a large number of heterogeneous, individually small
schemes and still obtain near-perfect elimination of individual longevity risk.
To illustrate this, we simulate a sequence of collective-drawdown plans
each containing 20 members. Within each plan, all members have the same
age, investment and consumption strategy. We create a sequence of plans,
each representing a different cohort of members. In our simulations, we treat
each of these plans as though they are a separate scheme. This simulation can
therefore be interpreted as both an example of how a collective-drawdown
scheme might operate for a single employer with finite fund size, and as
an example of how multiple small, demographically distinct schemes can
effectively share a longevity market.
Some care is required to apply the strategy for an infinite fund to a
finite fund. If this is done naively, the neural network can lose track of a
member’s wealth as the network does not receive any input providing data
on how mortality has differed from central projections. To account for this,
we adjust the equity risk-factors that are fed into the network to account
for the noise in longevity credits which would otherwise be hidden from the
network. Full details on how this is performed are given in Appendix A.3.
The result for the first generation in our simulation is shown in Figure 4
and is compared to the results obtained using an infinite fund. We perform
10000 simulations to obtain these plots and only show deciles where there
are at least 100 scenarios containing a survivor of the given age. These plots
show that the outcomes for a finite fund with 20 members in each generation.
Up to age 95, outcomes are essentially indistinguishable from the out-
comes in an infinite fund.
The outcomes achieved from 95 onwards are
somewhat below those of an infinite fund, but still outperform the shared-
indexation CDC fund. One could attempt to tune the network further to
take account of the fact that the longevity market is less effective once one
is making large contributions. For example, if there were a cap on the max-

training the network.

6.1
Optimality
One criticism of investment approaches based on utility functions is that the
true utility function of any investor is unknown. Nevertheless, using a ra-
tional choice of utility function as a basis of the design of a fund still has
the advantage that one can guarantee that it will be impossible to find any
alternative design which is unequivocally better (in the sense that it stochas-
tically dominates the strategy found using the utility function). Thus, while
one may debate the relative merits of two strategies found using different
utility functions, they will not produce entirely irrational strategies.
By contrast, the flat-accrual shared-indexation CDC scheme considered
in [3], is unequivocally worse than shared-indexation schemes with dynamic-
accrual from the point of view of individuals in the steady-state of these
schemes.
It is just about possible to argue that some people may prefer
the outcomes of the standard dynamic-accrual scheme illustrated in Figure
2, but we feel that the collective-drawdown scheme gives obviously better
results except in the most pedantic sense. We hypothesize that if one were
to artificially design a loss function that allows for decreasing income in
retirement, it would be possible to identify a strategy that stochastically
dominates the shared-indexation scheme.
Our philosophy is that, although one may not know one’s utility function a
priori, one could look at a menu of possible outcomes coming from a variety
of utility functions and thereby identify a reasonable choice to be used in
optimization problems. Indeed, we found the parameter values for our loss
function by this process, visually selecting for a graph that we felt most
people would agree outperforms the shared-indexation CDC fund.
In a complete market, where arbitrary derivatives on any risk-factors can
be purchased, it is not possible for any group of individuals (subject to minor
assumptions on their preferences) to agree any mutually beneficial contracts.
A precise statement is given in Theorem B.5 in the appendix. The proof
is not difficult, but giving a precise statement requires using the technical
vocabulary of mathematical probability theory. The key assumptions are:
there are a finite number of members; at the time a member agrees to join
the scheme, it is always in their best interests to do so; additional wealth
20


## Page 21


results in strictly better outcomes. The first assumption rules out Ponzi-
type schemes which rely on an infinite time horizon. The second assumption
rules out strategies where one invests in risky assets on behalf of an individual
before they are able to consent. The second assumption also rules out central-
planner approaches where some individuals lose out but some overall social
utility function is maximized.
The third assumption rules out situations
where a member is indifferent to having their wealth distributed to others.
If the provider is reasonably sized, the internal insurance market will
be sufficiently effective that we can model individual longevity risk as being
perfectly-insurable. The Black–Scholes model is well-known to be a complete
market model. The end result is a complete market in both investment and
longevity risk factors. As a result, Theorem B.5 tells us that the optimal
strategy for a heterogeneous collective of individuals in the Black–Scholes
model is for each of them to pursue the investment and consumption strategy
they would follow in an infinite collective of like-minded individuals. What
this means is that in complete market models, the only possible benefit of
collective pensions is longevity insurance.
In an incomplete market, Theorem B.5 does not apply. As a result, the
investment strategy found using a fund of identical individuals will not be
optimal. In an incomplete market it would be theoretically possible to set up
an internal insurance market in risk-factors which are not currently traded. If
members are heterogeneous, then they will have different views on the value
of this insurance and as a result, they will be able to trade in insurance for
mutual benefit. This idea is explored in [2] for the case of systematic longevity
risk, where the maximal possible benefit of mutual insurance against this risk
factor is computed. However, except for this single example, it is currently
unknown how to practically compute optimal mutual insurance strategies.
Therefore, for the time being, the best available approach is to use the strat-
egy found for an infinite number of identical individuals pooling longevity
risk via an internal insurance market only. Other possible benefits of collec-
tive funds in incomplete markets include the reduction of transaction costs
through investment pooling. This may be particularly useful if some scheme
members wish to take leveraged positions, but the fund as a whole does not
require leverage.
Theorem B.5 provides a useful sanity check on the plausibility of pro-
posed strategies for managing collective funds. If the scheme design does not
explain how mutual insurance in untraded risk factors is used to obtain mu-
tual benefit, then the effectiveness of the scheme must depend on breaking
21


## Page 22


some other assumption of the theorem, not the complete market hypothesis.
6.2
Member communications
An important function of the member layer in our architecture is communica-
tion. In a collective-drawdown scheme, the total asset value of each member
is known at all times. However, this does not necessarily mean this figure
should be automatically communicated to members. There are strong argu-
ments that it is better for members to focus on their benefit in retirement
rather than on their pot size [18].
As a result, one might design member communications in a CDC scheme
to inform members about projected, real-terms future benefits. There are,
of course, challenges in how to convey the complex information about uncer-
tainty and future growth in a fashion that will be understood by members,
but this is a common problem in most designs. Defined-benefit approaches
can avoid these issues, but only by sacrificing optimality.
It may be tempting in shared-indexation CDC designs to inform mem-
bers about nominal entitlements, but we believe this would be misleading as
members are likely to confuse projected, real-terms future benefits with their
nominal benefit. The nominal benefit figure on its own without considering
the level of indexation is an essentially meaningless number. Directly inform-
ing members of their nominal benefit and the level of indexation would require
members to have a detailed understanding of the shared-indexation approach
which seems unrealistic. We therefore believe that it is better to only inform
members of shared-indexation schemes of their projected, real-terms future
benefits. This requires stochastic modelling of the future evolution of the
fund.
6.3
Investment choice
It is generally accepted that in most occupational pension schemes, members
will simply accept the default investment decisions that are given to them.
It is for this reason that we have proposed that strategic decisions are made
at the employer level, rather than by the individual members.
Nevertheless, it is worth noting that our design is considerably more flex-
ible than shared-indexation CDC as one does not require large collections of
identical individuals in any scheme, simply a large number of members over-
all participating in the insurance market. As a result, an employer could offer
22


## Page 23


a number of tailored investment options in much the same way as DC funds
currently offer different investment choices for individuals with different risk
appetites. Members could be guided to choose from a menu of options in
just the same way as DC funds currently guide investors.
It is also possible, in principle, to set up a bespoke strategy for a single
member and, using the internal longevity insurance, provide them with out-
comes that are equivalent to those they could obtain in a fund containing an
infinite number of like-minded individuals. This opens up the possibility of
offering a form of self-invested personal pension (SIPP) which benefits from
longevity pooling. The principle issue would be the individual underwriting
required.
6.4
Interpretability
It is important to consider how easy it is to understand the operation of any
given scheme design. As a result, one may have reservations about using a
neural network to choose investment and consumption strategies.
We would agree that if one can find a closed-form formula for the invest-
ment and consumption decisions of a network that yields similar outcomes
to our strategy, this would be preferable. It seems likely that this will be
possible in the simplified Black–Scholes model, as we have already seen the
consumption strategy appears to be essentially deterministic. However, in
a realistic stochastic model, one would expect to have to choose between
interpretability and optimality.
Although a closed form formula has some advantages, a closed-form for-
mula for investment and consumption may not, in practice, be particularly
easy to interpret. Shared-indexation CDC schemes provide a case in point.
Despite the closed-form formulae describing the scheme’s operation, one has
little insight into what the resulting retirement outcomes will be, except
through simulation.
Within a neural network, the precise behaviour of the network is hard
to understand because it depends on the precise values of a large number
of parameters. However, the behaviour of a shared-indexation CDC fund
also depends on a large number of variables, specifically the liabilities that
have developed over time. This makes the income received from a shared-
indexation fund a form of path-dependent derivative, and means that to
compute the value of the pension one receives in return for a particular
contribution, one must perform a Monte Carlo simulation.
23


## Page 24


While there is no simple formula for the investment and consumption
decisions that the neural network approach will make, it does have the clear
advantage that the market value of the pension an individual purchases each
year will exactly match their contribution. In this important sense, collective-
drawdown schemes are more interpretable than a shared-indexation design.
A further advantage of the collective-drawdown approach is that the
objective function is highly interpretable, with the parameters controlling
risk-appetite, satiation and adequacy. The available control parameters in a
shared-indexation CDC scheme (the proportion invested in risky assets, the
value of hmin and the value of hmax) are not connected directly to preferences
and this makes their impact harder to understand.
Moreover, the motivation for the use of shared-indexation rather than
seeking optimality is obscure.
We believe that the central attraction of
shared-indexation CDC design is that it allows a transfer of risk between
generations to occur, while superficially making this appear fair.
Clause
17.4 (c) of the legislation governing the operation of CDC funds requires
that benefit adjustments “must be applied to all the members of the scheme
without variation” [26]. To clarify the meaning of this, let us suppose that
each individual i has a promised income stream ci

in a transfer of risk from old to young. The language used to describe the
operation of shared-indexation CDC funds, including in legislation, obscures
this intended transfer of risk.
A further advantage of collective-drawdown schemes, is that one does
not need to model the behaviour of other members joining and leaving the
scheme. The internal longevity market will function effectively across a broad
range of possibilities for fund composition. By contrast, modelling the details
of fund composition and the changing levels of liabilities are essential in
shared-indexation schemes. For example, shared-indexation schemes behave
very differently at startup and during closure than they do in their long-term
steady state. Moreover, modelling the future demographics of an employer
is extremely challenging. As a result, members of shared-indexation schemes

are subject to hard-to-quantify risks from events such as scheme closure or
changing demographics.
This has an impact on how easy it is to test different strategies in sim-
ulations. If the outcome of a particular scheme design can only be known
through simulation, then the depth of testing which a scheme has undergone
is of paramount importance. One can obtain a good understanding of the
behaviour of a collective-drawdown fund by simulating a single infinite co-
hort. By contrast, to understand a shared-indexation fund, one must simulate
all generations over a long time period. This makes it much more computa-
tionally costly to simulate shared-indexation funds than collective-drawdown
funds. Because of the ease of simulating collective-drawdown funds, we have
been able to perform robust tests to check that our network performs cor-
rectly. Details of testing measures can be found in Appendix C. In [3], it
is shown that testing the approximate pricing methodologies and heuristic
strategies used in shared-indexation funds is extremely challenging, since
there is little theoretical understanding of what to expect. The difficulty of
testing shared-indexation funds may explain why the scale of intergenera-
tional cross-subsidies reported in [3] were not known by industry.
In summary, we argue that the claimed simplicity of shared-indexation
CDC schemes is largely illusory and that collective-drawdown schemes have
numerous interpretability advantages over shared-indexation CDC. Never-
theless, interpretability issues will always remain in any scheme design, and
this makes thorough testing and independent scrutiny essential features of
any design.
6.5
Transfers in and out
Transferring in and out of a shared-indexation scheme is complicated, as
it is unclear how to compute transfer values. By contrast, the value of an
individual’s assets in an collective-drawdown scheme are always known.
The principle concern in a collective-drawdown scheme (which also exists
in a shared-indexation scheme), is that if an individual obtains information
about their health, they may wish to withdraw from the scheme. Considering
this question in detail would require a model for how information about one’s
health is likely to arrive, and to what extent such information narrows the
uncertainty around time of death. Modelling this is beyond the scope of this
paper, but would be an interesting topic for further research.
In lieu of this, a pragmatic solution is to allow individuals to opt out of
25


## Page 26


the pension up to retirement, but not after. In this case, individuals would
not participate in the longevity pool until retirement, and as a result, could
leave a bequest if they die before retirement.
This crude solution may be unnecessarily restrictive. It would seem rea-
sonable to allow members to leave after retirement, if they give sufficient
notice to prevent them using this as a mechanism to exploit private infor-
mation. One might then need to adjust the longevity estimates for members
of a scheme to condition on the fact that they have not left. One might
also consider performing custom underwriting that would allow the member
to remain in the scheme.
By using custom underwriting and the correct
probabilities for the internal longevity market, one could adjust the indi-
vidual’s investment and consumption strategy to better match their new
circumstances.
7
Conclusions
In complete market models, collective-drawdown schemes combined with an
internal market for individual longevity-risk can provide optimal outcomes
for investors. Such schemes have a number of other advantages over alterna-
tive approaches to managing collective pensions, such as enabling individual
investment choice.
In incomplete markets, it may be possible to obtain even better schemes
by creating additional internal markets. It remains to be seen whether the
benefits of this approach will justify the additional complexity of operation
and member communications.
The design of collective-drawdown schemes does not require pre-committing
to using a particular strategy for all time. So, in the event that better strate-
gies can be found using other forms of mutual insurance, there should be no
obstacle to adopting them in future.
In future work, we will focus on the robustness and realism of outcomes
from our collective-drawdown fund by: enabling the network to learn optimal
strategies for a range of parameter values so real-time comparisons of strate-
gies can be made, include additional terms in our loss function to induce a
preference for smoothness in pension outcomes, and including a more realis-
tic economic model with more asset classes and bond investment strategies
that match those used in the pensions industry.

A.1
Network architecture and implementation
Our machine learning code base is built in Python using the Tensorflow
package. We use a recurrent neural network consisting of six layers:
• The first input layer has 2 nodes, representing the increments of a
Brownian motion driving a stock price process and the current time t.
• The second layer is a dense layer with 32 nodes and a ReLU activation
function.
• The third layer is a gated recurrent unit (GRU) with 10 nodes. This
is the recurrent layer in our network. The activation function is the
hyperbolic tangent function and the recurrent activation function is
sigmoid. This layer allows us to deal with the time dependent nature
of our data. The GRU is set up so as to return an output at each time
point, hence the final layer returns predictions at each time point.

• The fourth and fifth layers are identical to the second layer.
• The final output layer has 2 nodes, which represent the two predictions
the network must make, the quantity to invest in a risky asset and the
proportion of wealth to consume. Because of the way the GRU is used,
we therefore obtain investment and consumption decisions in each year
of our simulation. Consumption before retirement is ignored by our
loss function.
We used the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001. Training
was carried out over 20 epochs, each consisting of 10,000 scenarios with a
batch size of 100. A validation set of 10,000 separately generated scenarios
was evaluated at the end of each epoch. This gave the strategies in Section 5.

correct, we perform three important checks.
1. Given a lifestyling and inflation linked annuity purchase strategy, we
find our replacement ratio and final wealth calculations produce a con-
stant income in retirement with a final wealth of zero, as expected.
2. We test that our method of training the neural network can reproduce
the strategy and value function (at time 0), for the classical Merton
problem. That is, maximising consumption over a given time horizon.
