Abstract 
We formalize AI-human collaboration through an agent-based simulation that distinguishes 
optimization-based AI search from satisficing-based human adaptation. Using an NK model, we 
examine how these distinct decision heuristics interact across modular and sequenced task 
structures. For modular tasks, AI typically substitutes for humans, yet complementarities emerge 
when AI explores a moderately broad search space and human task complexity remains low. In 
sequenced tasks, we uncover a counterintuitive result: when a high-performing human initiates 
search and AI subsequently refines it, joint performance is maximized, contradicting the dominant 
AI-first design principle. Conversely, when AI leads and human satisficing follows, 
complementarities attenuate as task interdependence increases. We further show that memory-less 
random AI, despite lacking structured adaptation, can improve outcomes when augmenting low-
capability humans by enabling escape from local optima. Collectively, our findings reveal that 
effective AI-human collaboration depends less on industry context and more on task architecture: 
the division of labor, sequencing, and interdependence structure. By elevating task decomposition 
as the central design principle, we provide a generalizable framework for strategic decision-
making involving agentic AI across diverse organizational settings.

1. INTRODUCTION 
Indeed, the times are changing, and very fast. As artificial intelligence (AI) increasingly takes on 
critical roles in organizations, the central question that confronts us is how humans and AI can best 
work together. Can AI and humans lend each other a hand, forging complementary collaboration, 
or is one destined to step aside to let the other take over decision-making? In this paper, we present 
a theoretical framework by using an agent-based simulation that illuminates the possibilities of AI-
human complementarity in organizational decision-making based on task division. 
 
Existing research on AI-human collaboration highlights AI’s potential to automate and 
augment human capabilities in organizations (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). These 
complementarities have been explored through empirical studies, experiments (Choudhury et al., 
2020; Zhang et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2025) or theorized  based on the relative strengths of AI such 
as predictive accuracy versus contextual or domain expertise of humans (Choudhary et al., 2025; 
Shrestha et al., 2019). While invaluable, the literature lacks a generalizable framework linking AI-
human complementarity to organizational decision-making. A key reason for this gap is the 
prevailing view of AI as merely “an aid to prediction and decision-making” (Choudhary et al., 
2025; p. 540), enhancing or automating human tasks (Csaszar, Ketkar, & Kim, 2024). However, 
scholars increasingly recognize AI as an agent in its own right, one that can independently take 
strategic decisions and solve complex organizational problems like human managers (Vanneste & 
Puranam, 2024; Krakowski, Luger, & Raisch, 2023; Acharya et al., 2025). This shifts the core 
question to: What makes AI and human agents distinct, and when do these differences create 
complementarities in organizational decision-making?

Adopting an agentic view of AI foregrounds a division of task based approach to analyzing 
AI-human collaboration; a fundamental design principle that shapes outcomes in decision-making 
(Simon, 1955; Zenger, 2002; Nickerson, Silverman, & Zenger, 2007; Levinthal & Wu, 2025). 
Accordingly, we model AI-human interaction as a multi-agent system engaged in joint problem-
solving, highlighting the conditions under which complementarities emerge, and where AI and 
humans may become substitutes depending on task division, such as modular (parallel) versus 
sequenced tasks (Ganco, Kapoor, & Lee, 2020; Raisch & Fomina, 2025). Our theoretical 
framework emerges from an NK model of adaptive search that captures how the distinct attributes 
of agentic AI and humans (henceforth, H) impact outcomes in joint problem-solving (Levinthal, 
1997; Adner, Csaszar, & Zemsky, 2014). In this conceptualization, we posit two characteristics of 
adaptive search that distinguishes agentic AI from H, both grounded in relaxing the bounded 
rationality constraints in the case of agentic AI (Csaszar, 2025; Nishant et al., 2024). 
First, the relative breadth of search space: H, assumed to be boundedly rational (Puranam, 
Stieglitz, Osman, & Pillutla, 2015; Simon, 2000) explores a relatively narrow search space. In 
contrast, an unboundedly rational AI (Csaszar, 2025), explores a broader search space (Raisch & 
Fomina, 2025). Second, the adaptive rules of search: A boundedly rational human employs 
satisficing search, whereby greater weight is assigned to more recent decision states when 
determining subsequent decisions in the search process (Gigerenzer, 2004, 2020; Leiblein & 
Macher, 2009; Levinthal & March, 1981). By contrast, a cognitively unbounded AI agent employs 
optimizing search, assigning equal weight to all prior decision states when determining subsequent 
actions. We introduce a parameter C that captures the task interdependence between Human and 
AI tasks, upgrading the model to its NKC representation (Ganco, Kapoor, & Lee, 2020; Gavetti et 
al., 2017).

Our simulations reveal that for modular tasks (C = 0), agentic AI’s search diminishes the 
marginal value of human search, suggesting substitution on aggregate. However, we also find 
specific instances of AI-H complementarity. When human task is non-complex (i.e., low K), the 
optimal outcomes occur when the AI’s search space is moderately broad compared to the human’s 
as opposed to too narrow or too broad. When human task is complex (i.e., high K), our model 
reveals two scenarios where human complements AI: (a) narrow AI search (although broader than 
H), (b) broad AI search in conjunction with satisficing based adaptation by the human. 
For interdependent tasks (C > 0), we model and analyze two task sequences. In AI→H task 
sequence, a moderate level of task interdependence between AI and human (i.e., a moderate level 
of C) produces the most resilient complementarities. Here, satisficing by the human channelizes 
and therefore complements AI’s broad exploration in Step 1. However, for very high task 
interdependence (i.e., when C is very high), the aggregate payoff declines rapidly. Satisficing over 
highly coupled inter-task decision variables manifests in “recency bias” as opposed to calibrated 
fine-tuning. This bias offsets AI’s optimizing search in Step 1. Therefore, for AI→H sequenced 
tasks, satisficing by the human is suboptimal, especially when task interdependence is high. The 
optimal strategy is to balance the search breadth of AI with moderate satisficing by the human. 
Second, in H→AI task sequence, optimal performance arises when AI builds on prior 
search of a highly performing human (i.e., H that gives high performance in Step 1) when task 
interdependence is high. In fact, among all configurations of the parameters space (N, K and C), 
this combination maximizes joint payoff demonstrating the scope for the highest 
complementarities. Building on this insight, we statistically generalize the following result: An 
H→AI sequence, where a high-performing human acts first, outperforms an AI→H sequence. This 
finding stands in contrast to the dominant view of task division in both scholarly discourse, and

prevailing organizational beliefs, which typically advocate an “AI-first” approach in which AI 
generates a broad set of options and humans subsequently select among them (Csaszar, 2025). 
Striking insights emerge when we distinguish between an optimizing and “probabilistically 
delusional” (PD) AI that adapts search randomly, without memory of prior decision states (Esanu, 
2024).1 For a moderately broad AI search, a PD AI performs as well as an optimizing AI in 
augmenting upstream human search. However, while the benefits of rule-based AI remain stable, 
the effectiveness of PD AI declines sharply as the search space expands. Interestingly, PD AI 
outperforms rule-based AI when augmenting the prior search of a low-performing human. AI’s 
memory-less exploration helps pull out the human of local traps formed in Step 1, which is 
curtailed for a rule-based AI that retains memory of the human’s (suboptimal) search. The 
organizational implication is that when human capital is relatively weak, organizations should 
prioritize investments in AI with data processing power that can simply broaden the scope of search 
over sophisticated transformer architectures with nuanced rules of adaptation. 
Our findings make key contributions to human–AI collaboration in organizations. First, we 
formalize the characteristics of  agentic AI, enabling an agent-based simulation of joint AI-human 
problem solving that generalizes across organizational settings. Second, we identify task divisions 
where human–AI complementarities emerge, challenging the “AI as a tool” framing that implicitly 
assumes replacement. Third, the model’s implications present an alternative to the dominant “AI-
first” design principle. Specifically, we uncover task configurations where AI adds the most value 
by building over the prior search of a high-performing human or by simply expanding the search 
scope of a human trapped in a suboptimal performance peak.

2.1. AI-Human Collaboration 
Given the rapid rise of AI and GenAI in organizational processes, a growing research stream in 
management science has explored AI-human collaboration, highlighting AI’s potential to both 
automate and augment human capabilities (Csaszar, Ketkar, & Kim, 2024; Raisch & Krakowski, 
2021). These AI capabilities have been examined through empirical studies and experiments within 
specific organizational contexts (Choudhury et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022; Choi et al., 2025) and 
theorized based on AI’s relative strengths such as its vast data processing capacity, and predictive 
accuracy compared to humans (Choudhary et al., 2025; Shrestha et al., 2019). The literature, 
however, has emphasized AI as a tool for enhancing or automating human tasks, viewing it 
primarily as an aid to prediction and decision-making (Choudhary et al., 2025, p. 540; Csaszar, 
Ketkar, & Kim, 2024). Therefore, on the question of AI-human collaboration, the “AI as a tool” 
perspective implicitly predicts replacement in organizational tasks (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).  
A growing body of research recognizes AI as an agent in its own right, capable of 
independently taking decisions and solving complex organizational problems (Krakowski, Luger, 
& Raisch, 2023; Acharya et al., 2025). This shift, from AI as a tool to “AI as an agent”, is critical 
as it shifts the question of collaboration in decision-making as opposed to task execution 
(Krakowski, Luger, & Raisch, 2023; Hillebrand, Raisch & Schad, 2025; Csaszar, 2025). Pivoting 
to a decision-level lens, task division becomes salient (Krakowski, Luger, & Raisch, 2023; 
Hillebrand, Raisch, & Schad, 2025), especially whether AI and humans divide decision-making 
tasks in a modular or interdependent manner (Raisch & Fomina, 2025). 
2.2. Agentic AI versus Human Decision-Making 
Recent work emphasizes a task division-based perspective for understanding AI–human 
collaboration in strategic decision making (Raisch & Fomina, 2025; Hillebrand, Raisch, & Schad, 
2025). This stream shifts attention away from treating AI as a generic decision aid toward


## Page 7


7 
 
specifying which tasks are allocated to AI versus human agents, and why. Yet, despite this progress, 
the literature has only begun to formally articulate how agentic AI performs decision-making tasks 
in ways that are fundamentally distinct from human agents, and what these differences imply for 
joint decision making (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024; Kim, Khoreva, & Vaiman, 2025; Feuerriegel 
et al., 2024). A promising point of departure lies in reconsidering the standard bounded-rationality 
assumptions that underpin models of human decision making when applied to AI agents (Csaszar, 
2025). While AI systems are computationally bound, they are not subject to the same cognitive 
constraints that limit boundedly rational human agents (Simon, 2000; Gigerenzer, 2004; Levinthal 
& March, 1981). Owing to their processing capacity and algorithmic structure, AI agents can 
systematically evaluate large decision spaces and complex interdependencies among decision 
parameters through consistent and explicitly specified mathematical rules (Nishant et al., 2024). 
Relaxing bounded rationality for AI agents hints at two analytically useful distinctions 
between AI and human decision-making. First, relative to humans, AI can search broadly across 
high-dimensional decision spaces without cognitive overload (Raisch & Fomina, 2025), 
suggesting potential complementarities in task division wherein AI’s breadth complements human 
depth of search. Second, AI’s computational and mathematical boundedness arguably implies a 
mode of adaptation that differs from human learning: AI applies stable, rule-based update 
mechanisms across the entire search space (Nishant et al., 2024), whereas humans rely on local 
heuristics that adapt endogenously as search unfolds (Leiblein & Macher, 2009; Levinthal & 
March, 1981). These differences point to complementarities not only in what is searched, but also 
in how search and adaptation proceed over time. Building on this premise, we develop an agent-
based model of AI–human collaboration that explicitly differentiates the decision-making 
capabilities of AI and human agents. Our model examines how performance depends on task

division and the intensity of interdependencies between AI and human tasks, thereby offering a 
formal framework for AI–human complementarities in organizational decision-making. 
3. THE MODEL 
3.1. Conceptual Setup 
We employ the canonical NK class of models (Levinthal, 1997; Rivkin & Siggelkow, 2003; 
Knudsen & Srikanth, 2014) to simulate how AI and human (H) agents jointly solve a generic 
organizational decision-making task that is partitioned between them (Fang & Kim, 2018). NK 
models conceptualize agentic decision making as problemistic search on a performance landscape 
characterized by the dimensionality of the decision space (N), the degree of interdependence 
among decision elements or landscape ruggedness (K), and a payoff function that aggregates the 
contribution of individual decision states. 
Building on the bounded-rationality distinction discussed earlier, we formalize how agentic 
AI, and human agents differ along the core dimensions that govern adaptive search. First, to 
capture differences in breadth versus depth of search, we vary the relative size of the decision 
space, N, assigned to AI and H. Second, to capture differences in adaptation, we distinguish 
between optimizing search of AI and satisficing search of H, allowing each agent’s adaptation to 
respond differently to task complexity, K. These differences shape each agent’s realized payoff 
and, in turn, the joint performance of the decision-making process. 
Satisficing is a well-established behavioral assumption for a boundedly rational H, for 
whom cognitive limitations render “good enough” solutions (Greve, 2023; Cyert & March, 1963) 
both efficient and adaptive (Simon, 2000; Gigerenzer & Goldstein, 1996). In contrast, for agentic 
AI it is reasonable to relax bounded rationality and assume, relative to H, optimization: AI can 
evaluate a larger and more complete set of decision variables and apply pre-specified optimization 
rules consistently (Nishant et al., 2024). Importantly, neither approach is unambiguously superior.


## Page 9


9 
 
Optimization over expansive decision spaces enables broad exploration and maintains consistency 
in adaptation that is invariant to the scope of search. Satisficing, by contrast, affords contextual 
calibration: H selectively weight information based on salience, recency, or relevance (Levinthal 
& March, 1981; Winter, 1975), as reflected in prevalent managerial heuristics such as the 80/20 
rule, where a small subset of information resolves most of the problem (Brandstätter, Gigerenzer, 
& Hertwig, 2006). Yet excessive satisficing can also entrench bias and premature convergence to 
suboptimal solutions (Artinger, Gigerenzer & Jacobs, 2022; Cyert & March, 1963). Which task 
divisions generate complementarities between agents that differ in these decision heuristics is not 
obvious ex ante. This indeterminacy motivates a simulation-based approach that traces how 
optimization- and satisficing-based adaptation of AI and H respectively interact over repeated 
iterations, allowing joint performance to emerge as a consequence of these interactions and the 
division of task between the two agents. 
It is critical to clarify that the agentic AI in our framework is not a proxy for a “superior” 
human. A more capable human may search more broadly and optimize more effectively than an 
average counterpart, but within an H–H setting both agents remain boundedly rational. 
Optimization and satisficing lie on a continuum, and differences are matters of degree: one human 
may satisfice less than another, yet both eventually revert to heuristics as cognitive load rises. The 
AI–H setting is categorically different. Agentic AI can implement a predefined optimization logic 
that remains invariant to increases in search breadth and landscape complexity. Its optimization 
does not degrade as the search space expands. By contrast, even a highly capable human faces 
escalating cognitive constraints; as breadth and interdependence increase, optimization gives way 
to satisficing (Gigerenzer, 2004; Levinthal & March, 1981). The distinction, therefore, is not one 
of relative ability, but of fundamentally different adaptation regimes under complexity.

Finally, to model task division and interdependencies between AI- and H-driven decision 
tasks, we introduce an interdependence parameter, C. This parameter allows the search processes 
of AI and H to coevolve, such that each agent’s payoff landscape is endogenously affected by the 
other’s choices. This extension aligns with the NKC framework, which has been used to study 
coevolutionary search dynamics in interdependent task environments (Suzuki & Arita, 2005; 
Biggiero, 2016; Gavetti et al., 2017; Ganco, Kapoor, & Lee, 2020). Now we detail how this 
conceptual setup reflects in the choice of our model parameters.

starting point of their own search. In essence, the greater the magnitude of C, the stronger the 
“memory” of search conducted by the agent who searches first as per the task division.

Adaptive search is a foundational process in decision making, characterized by the choice 
between terminating search through satisficing and continuing exploration through optimization 
(Ethiraj & Levinthal, 2004; Posen, Keil, Kim, & Meissner, 2018). Classic behavioral theories, and 
the agent-based models that build on them, have predominantly focused on human decision 
makers, embedding adaptation firmly within the constraints of bounded rationality and search on 
rugged performance landscapes. Within this tradition, multi-agent models vary agents’ positions 
along a satisficing–optimizing continuum but do so within a shared cognitive constraint of 
bounded rationality. This body of work has yielded a core insight: the determinants of stopping 
search systematically differ from those that motivate continued exploration (Billinger, Srikanth, 
Stieglitz, & Schumacher, 2021). As a result, in H–H multi-agent search, satisficing and optimizing 
are typically modeled as distinct, weakly compatible strategies when applied within a common 
task representation. However, as illustrated in Figure 3, AI–H interaction departs from this logic. 
We observe performance gains from a sequenced complementarity in which AI engages in 
optimization-based adaptation, followed by satisficing-based adaptation by H.

Crucially, the complementarity gains diminish when AI–H task interdependence exceeds 
AI task complexity (𝐶/𝐾𝐴𝐼> 2). This result highlights a theoretically important distinction 
between task interdependence (𝐶) and task complexity (𝐾). Task interdependence captures the 
degree of coupling across agents’ tasks and is therefore a between-agent property, whereas task 
complexity reflects the interdependence among decision parameters faced by a single agent and is 
thus a within-agent property. To shift emphasis from within-agent considerations to between-agent 
coupling requires a substantial adjustment in decision heuristics for the human. Consequently, as 
task interdependence increases, the returns to optimization–satisficing complementarity decline, 
consistent with predictions from behavioral decision theory. The implication is consequential: for 
tightly coupled or highly interdependent AI→H task sequences, optimization followed by 
satisficing yields diminishing performance gains. 
This attenuation is further amplified when AI optimizes over an expansive decision space 
(𝑁𝐴𝐼/𝑁𝐻> 5), as the marginal contribution of subsequent satisficing-based adaptation by the 
human becomes increasingly limited. Satisficing becomes especially ineffective when it follows 
expansive AI optimization because broad search alters the nature of the information available to 
H. When AI optimizes over a very broad decision space, it generates many candidate solutions that 
are far apart, making it difficult for the human to discern clear improvement signals. Satisficing 
relies on such local signals to apply stopping rules efficiently, and their absence weakens its 
effectiveness. In addition, expansive AI search produces an abundance of acceptable alternatives, 
which undermines the selective attention that satisficing depends on, rendering stopping decisions 
increasingly arbitrary. In sum, broad AI optimization shifts the emphasis of the remainder of the 
problemistic search that is to be performed by the human toward task interdependence rather than 
within-task complexity, misaligning satisficing heuristics that privilege recent or salient outcomes.

leaving limited scope for downstream improvement in the sequence via satisficing. This logic 
motivates our central proposition regarding AI→H sequenced task structures: 
Proposition 2: In AI-to-H sequenced tasks, optimizing-satisficing complementarity gains attenuate 
with increased task interdependence (C) and AI’s relative breadth of search (NAI /NH).

4.3. Sequenced Task: H→AI 
We next examine a sequenced task division (𝐶> 0) in which the human initiates the search process 
by satisficing over its assigned decision space (𝑁𝐻), after which AI applies optimization-based 
adaptation to the decision states generated by the human (see Figure 4). Unlike the AI→H 
sequence where the human has access to the full set of outcomes generated by AI’s broader search 
(𝑁𝐴𝐼/𝑁𝐻> 1 by construction), the H→AI sequence introduces the possibility that AI’s search is 
endogenously constrained by the limited set of decision states sampled during the human’s 
upstream satisficing search. 
Building on prior NKC representations that explicitly model interdependencies between 
decision-making constituents within complex systems such as firms or agentic tasks within an 
organization (Ethiraj & Levinthal, 2004; Ganco & Agarwal, 2009; Adner & Kapoor, 2010), we 
examine the constraints imposed on decision-making by interdependent task structures and 
abductively theorize from the resulting simulation (Harrison, Lin, Carroll, & Carley, 2007; Ganco, 
Kapoor, & Lee, 2020). Prior research on sequenced tasks demonstrates that interdependencies 
systematically constrain upstream and downstream problemistic search, as evidenced in settings 
such as exploration–exploitation trade-offs along interdependent value chains (Ganco, Kapoor, & 
Lee, 2020). In a related vein, we argue that a comparable constraining effect emerges in H→AI


## Page 22


22 
 
task divisions, driven by the interaction between agents’ relative breadths of search and the degree 
of task interdependence. 
In our context, we model and disentangle performance implications for analytically distinct 
paths for AI to extend search once it exhausts the information from the human’s narrow search. 
Our posited constraining effect reflects a limitation of satisficing in sequential task structures: 
under high task interdependence, satisficing may fail to sample a sufficiently rich set of decision 
states to support effective downstream adaptation (Ganco, Kapoor, & Lee (2020) offer a related 
idea). When faced with such informational insufficiency, the downstream agent, here AI, can either 
extrapolate beyond the inherited decision states by applying its own optimization rule, or 
altogether abandon path-dependence and engage in random exploration. Our simulations explicitly 
capture these alternatives when task interdependence exceeds the scope of human search (𝐶>
𝑁𝐻), modeling AI adaptation both as continued optimization over inherited states and as stochastic  
memory-less or “probabilistically delusional (PD)” exploration  AI (Esanu, 2024). 
First, our simulations reveal that AI substantially amplifies aggregate performance when it 
follows a high-capability human – that is, when upstream human search yields a high-payoff 
configuration that remains robust even under extensive downstream AI exploration (𝑁𝐴𝐼/𝑁𝐻> 1 
> 15). This pattern foregrounds the pivotal role of initial conditions in adaptive search processes 
that rely on optimization. Prior work in adaptive search emphasizes that performance outcomes on 
rugged landscapes are sensitive to starting points and path dependence, as early stochastic 
realizations shape the trajectory and ceiling of subsequent improvement (Levinthal & March, 
1981; Knudsen & Srikanth, 2014). 
We abductively argue that optimization heuristics are performance enhancing primarily 
when initial conditions are favorable. Under such conditions, it is optimal to weight upstream


## Page 23


23 
 
solutions that have already demonstrated high fitness, allowing their local structure to anchor 
subsequent optimization. In contrast, satisficing or memory-light heuristics that underweight initial 
conditions dilute precisely the information that enables optimization to compound gains. In the 
case of a satisficing human agent, existing evidence indicates that initially coarse “mental models” 
of the task representation progressively become fine-grained over the course of search, a dynamic 
that renders satisficing particularly advantageous when initial conditions lack robustness (Knudsen 
& Srikanth, 2014; p. 420). In contrast, our results suggest that a high-performance upstream human 
search thus furnishes AI with advantageous entry points from which optimization can proceed 
efficiently, enabling AI to refine, recombine, and exploit the local topology surrounding an already 
elevated peak. The result is a compounding effect in which downstream optimization magnifies, 
rather than dissipates, the performance advantages generated by upstream human judgment. This 
logic leads us to the following proposition: 
Proposition 3a: In H-to-AI sequenced tasks, overall performance is maximized when a high-
capability human’s search (high performance pay-off after the first stage of human search) is 
calibrated by a rule-based AI in the second stage. 
 
Second, a striking and counterintuitive result emerges when upstream search is initiated by 
a low-capability H, as reflected in weak performance payoffs in the first stage of search. In this 
regime, a memory-less search by PD AI marginally but consistently outperforms an optimizing AI; 
and this advantage persists even as the AI’s downstream search space becomes extremely broad. 
This result runs against the conventional presumption in the adaptive search literature that 
exploitative optimization, where feasible, dominates stochastic exploration as a performance-
enhancing heuristic in adaptation (Levinthal & Schliesmann, 2025). Classic arguments view 
random search as inferior because any superior outcome it yields is attributed to luck: either an 
advantageous initial condition or an early chance encounter with a high-fitness peak (Levinthal &


## Page 24


24 
 
March, 1981; Levinthal & Posen, 2007), neither of which can be systematically exploited by 
boundedly rational human agents navigating rugged landscapes. Accordingly, stochastic 
exploratory search is implicitly a non-scalable strategy for sustained performance improvement. 
Our results suggest that this logic does not carry over straightforwardly to AI-based search. 
What differentiates PD AI from random search by a human is not the stochasticity aspect 
per se, but the sheer magnitude of the search space. Unlike humans, AI can propagate a memory-
less random search over an orders-of-magnitude larger solution space without incurring cognitive 
fatigue, attentional lock-in, or escalating coordination costs. As a consequence, PD AI effectively 
serves as an ‘escape valve’ for low-performing upstream human search. By significantly expanding 
the downstream search breadth, it increases the likelihood that the overall search trajectory timely 
encounters a local peak that dominates the outcome generated by the human’s initial search. 
In this sense, PD AI does not correct or refine the human’s upstream decisions through 
local improvement, as an optimizing AI would. Instead, it compensates for weak upstream 
performance by probabilistically sampling a much wider portion of the landscape, thereby “lifting” 
the performance ceiling of an otherwise constrained H→AI sequence. This mechanism redefines 
the role of random search in human–AI collaboration: randomness introduced at scale becomes a 
systematic, rather than purely stochastic, source of performance improvement. This 
counterintuitive revaluation in sequenced H→AI task division motivates our next proposition: 
Proposition 3b: In H-to-AI sequenced tasks involving a low-capability human (low performance 
upstream), a memory-less random AI search leads to superior overall performance relative to an 
optimizing AI.

6. DISCUSSION 
6.1. Theoretical and Managerial Implications 
We formalize how agentic AI systematically differs from human agents and explore the 
implications for AI-human collaboration in joint decision-making. Using an NKC agent-based 
simulation (Ganco, Kapoor, & Lee, 2020), we model AI’s broad optimization-based search 
alongside a human’s satisficing-based search in modular and sequenced task divisions. The 
abductive logic derived from the simulation results provides a generalizable framework for AI-


## Page 29


29 
 
human complementarity, moving beyond industry-or systems-specific AI adoption, automation, 
and augmentation related conversation around this important topic. 
Our first contribution lies in formalizing how a relatively unboundedly rational AI differs 
from a boundedly rational, Simonian agent engaged in adaptive search (Csaszar, 2025; Simon, 
2000). By relaxing core cognitive constraints associated with bounded rationality, we isolate two 
analytically tractable dimensions along which agentic AI diverges from humans: breadth of search 
and adaptation heuristics. Whereas humans operate under cognitive and attentional limits that 
restrict both the scope of exploration and the consistency of optimization, AI can expand the search 
space substantially and apply optimization routines systematically. Although relaxing bounded 
rationality could generate additional differences, our parsimonious formalization offers a 
foundational starting point for theorizing AI as a qualitatively distinct decision-making agent rather 
than merely a faster tool. 
Second, we advance an abductively derived theoretical logic that foregrounds task division 
as the primary lens for understanding AI–human collaboration. Much of the extant discourse treats 
AI as a context-specific instrument embedded in particular industries or workflows. In contrast, 
task division constitutes the fundamental unit of analysis in any collaborative decision-making 
system. The structure of interdependence and the sequencing of tasks condition how multi-agent 
adaptive search unfolds. By modeling AI–human interaction at the level of task architecture, we 
offer a design-oriented framework that scales across organizational settings and moves beyond 
domain-specific prescriptions. This shift enables a more general theory of agentic collaboration 
grounded in search dynamics rather than technological application domains. 
Third, we contribute to the emerging literature on AI–human complementarity by 
identifying the boundary conditions under which performance gains materialize. Our simulations


## Page 30


30 
 
demonstrate that outcomes are highly sensitive to task sequencing, the degree of interdependence, 
task complexity, and the specific heuristics governing search. This multi-dimensional optimization 
reveals contingencies that challenge the prevailing “AI-first” intuition. In particular, we find that 
performance is maximized in sequenced tasks when AI follows high-quality upstream human 
search. The gains arise from complementarities between human satisficing and AI optimization – 
an interaction effect that is theoretically distinct from purely human search settings, where prior 
literature suggests more limited differentiation between satisficing and optimizing strategies. 
Relatedly, our results add to the emergent conversation on probabilistically delusional or 
hallucinatory AI (Esanu, 2024). While such properties are typically framed as liabilities, our model 
shows that memory-less random propagation can create value when it expands the downstream 
search space following low-quality human exploration. In rugged landscapes characterized by 
local optima, stochastic breadth may enable escape from suboptimal basins. Thus, even seemingly 
inferior heuristics can become performance-enhancing when embedded within a context that 
requires the search process to be “rescued” from a suboptimal local trap. 
Taken together, these results carry important managerial implications. Organizations 
should resist universal prescriptions regarding AI deployment and instead evaluate the architecture 
of their decision-making tasks. The counterintuitive finding that “AI follows” may outperform “AI 
first” cautions against reflexive participation in technological arms races and bearing a high cost 
of “AI wastage”. Instead, investments in high-quality human capital, complemented by carefully 
calibrated AI that broadens and refines prior expert search, may generate greater returns than 
wholesale automation. Effective AI–human collaboration, therefore, is less a function of 
technological sophistication per se and more a matter of deliberate task design aligned with the 
distinct search capabilities of each agent.


## Page 31


31 
 
6.2. Boundary Conditions and Future Research 
As with all simulation-based studies, our abduced theory is subject to boundary conditions 
imposed by the model’s assumptions, which in turn open avenues for future research. We model 
an archetypal adaptive process to contrast optimization- and satisficing-based search heuristics: 
equal weighting (capturing the unbounded rationality of AI) versus relative weighting (capturing 
the bounded rationality of humans) of decision states. In practice, however, the manifestation of 
both these heuristics extend beyond recency-weighting (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). 
Notwithstanding these parsimonious model choices that are consistent with the prevalent literature, 
the simulations capture the key trade-offs in these heuristics at a conceptual level. We urge future 
work to explore whether and how AI-H collaborative outcomes change under alternative 
formulations of optimization and satisficing. 
Second, our model abstracts from algorithmic or AI bias – systematic distortions in how 
algorithms sample the search landscape due to impurities in their training data (Gichoya et al., 
2023). Bias in source and unbiased optimization heuristics applied on what is sampled are distinct 
yet interacting processes (Nishant et al., 2025). If initial decision states are drawn from a biased 
distribution (Jain & Kogut, 2014), subsequent optimization may amplify rather than correct these 
distortions. This raises a theoretical puzzle. Should AI optimization be anchored in expert human-
generated initial conditions, imposing path dependence that may attenuate bias at entry? Or should 
initial conditions be deliberately down-weighted through satisficing, allowing broader exploration 
before optimization in the hope that breadth mitigates bias? These competing logics expose a 
tension between preserving favorable initial conditions and neutralizing biased ones. We urge 
future research to model and empirically validate these interactions under various task divisions. 
Finally, the abductive logic developed in our model focuses on horizontal collaboration 
between humans and AI. An increasingly salient phenomenon, however, is vertical or hierarchical


## Page 32


32 
 
divisions of labor, as well as iterative human-AI interactions in which linear task sequencing 
constitutes only one component of a broader coordination structure. Vertical task divisions 
foreground questions of AI trust and authority (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024; Killoran, Park, & 
Kietzmann, 2024), while iterative interactions introduce reinforcement learning dynamics that 
create the potential for heuristic spillovers across agents over time. At their core, these settings 
involve task divisions under heightened uncertainty and ambiguity. Therefore, modeling how 
performance unfolds under such hierarchical and iterative task configurations represents a 
promising avenue for future research. 
6.3. Conclusion 
Building on the premise that agentic AI is relatively unbounded in its rationality compared to 
humans, we model and formalize how AI fundamentally differs from human agents engaged in 
adaptive search. We show how these differences generate complementarities in collaborative 
decision-making under alternative task divisions. Our findings contribute to the emerging 
conversation on agentic AI and strategic decision-making by demonstrating that a broad stroke 
“AI-first” approach is not a useful generalization. Instead, performance-enhancing human-AI 
collaboration is contingent on task structure, the agents’ distinct search capabilities including 
search breadth and adaptation heuristics, and the quality of human capital. Accounting for these 
factors, our simulations reveal robust complementarities when high-performing upstream human 
search is followed by downstream AI optimization operating over a broader search space.
