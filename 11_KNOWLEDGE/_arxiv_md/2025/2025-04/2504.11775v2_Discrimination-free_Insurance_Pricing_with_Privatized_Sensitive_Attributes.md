Abstract
Fairness has emerged as a critical consideration in the landscape of machine learning
algorithms, particularly as AI continues to transform decision-making across societal
domains. To ensure that these algorithms are free from bias and do not discriminate
against individuals based on sensitive attributes such as gender and race, the field of
algorithmic bias has introduced various fairness concepts, along with methodologies
to achieve these notions in different contexts. Despite the rapid advancement, not all
sectors have embraced these fairness principles to the same extent. One specific sector
that merits attention in this regard is insurance. Within the realm of insurance pricing,
fairness is defined through a distinct and specialized framework. Consequently, achieving
fairness according to established notions does not automatically ensure fair pricing in
insurance. In particular, regulators are increasingly emphasizing transparency in pricing
algorithms and imposing constraints on insurance companies on the collection and
utilization of sensitive consumer attributes. These factors present additional challenges
in the implementation of fairness in pricing algorithms. To address these complexities
and comply with regulatory demands, we propose an efficient method for constructing
fair models that are tailored to the insurance domain, using only privatized sensitive
attributes. Notably, our approach ensures statistical guarantees, does not require
direct access to sensitive attributes, and adapts to varying transparency requirements,
addressing regulatory demands while ensuring fairness in insurance pricing.

Introduction
Fairness has emerged as a critical consideration in the landscape of machine learning algorithms.
Various concepts of algorithmic fairness have been established in this burgeoning field including
demographic parity, equalized odds, predictive parity, among others (Calders et al., 2009;
Dwork et al., 2012; Hardt et al., 2016; Zafar et al., 2017; Kusner et al., 2017). It is essential
to emphasize that not all of these metrics are universally applicable to every situation. Each
fairness concept bears its own merits that align with specific contextual applications (Barocas
et al., 2019). In addition to the theoretical underpinnings of fairness notations, the literature
has also witnessed a substantial development of methodologies in achieving various fairness
criteria.
In contrast to algorithmic fairness, the insurance industry employs a unique and specialized
framework, known as actuarial fairness. This well-established concept serves as a fundamental
principle in pricing insurance contracts (Frees and Huang, 2023). The premium is considered
actuarially fair if it is a sound estimate of the expected value of all future costs associated with
an individual risk transfer (Miller, 2009). Given the stringent regulatory environment, insurers
are mandated to demonstrate actuarial fairness in their premiums. As machine learning
algorithms become more prevalent in insurance company operations, regulatory bodies in
recent years have begun to reassess the concept of fairness, in particular, questioning whether
an actuarially fair premium should discriminate against policyholders based on sensitive
attributes, such as gender and ethnicity. For instance, Directive 2004/113/EC (“Gender
Directive”) issued by the Council of the European Union prohibits insurance companies in
the UE from using gender as a rating factor for pricing insurance products (Council of the
European Union, 2004). More recently, the governor of the state of Colorado signed Senate
Bill (SB) 21-169 into law (Colorado General Assembly, 2021), protecting consumers from
insurance practices with unfair discrimination on the basis of race, color, national or ethnic
origin, religion, sex, sexual orientation, disability, gender identify, or gender expression.
Against this backdrop, our research aims to develop a method that enables insurers to in-
corporate machine learning algorithms into insurance pricing while complying with regulatory
mandates related to fairness, transparency, and privacy. As underscored by Lindholm et al.
(2022b), actuarial fairness and algorithmic fairness may not coexist simultaneously under
certain conditions. In light of this, our focus turns to the discrimination-free premium—a
recently introduced concept in the insurance literature that aligns with fairness from a
causal inference perspective. This premium is designed to eliminate both direct and indirect
discrimination associated with sensitive attributes (Lindholm et al., 2022a).
The insurance industry plays a critical role in modern economies by facilitating risk

pooling, enabling investment, and promoting financial resilience at both individual and
systemic levels. In 2023, the global insurance market exceeded USD 7 trillion in premiums,
accounting for over 7% of global GDP. In the United States alone, the insurance sector employs
more than 2.8 million people and manages assets worth over USD 11 trillion, underscoring
its deep integration into financial markets and long-term capital formation. Given this
far-reaching impact, ensuring that insurance pricing is equitable and transparent is of broad
societal importance. Biased pricing not only erodes consumer trust but also undermines the
fundamental principles of solidarity and risk-sharing that insurance is built upon. As machine
learning continues to shape decision-making processes, it is essential to develop frameworks
that preserve fairness and accountability in such a consequential domain.
To address this challenge, we consider a multi-party training framework in which the
insurer has access to non-sensitive attributes of policyholders but does not observe the true
sensitive attributes directly. Instead, a noised or privatized version of the sensitive attributes
is securely held by a trusted third party (TTP). In our framework, the insurer transmits
transformed non-sensitive attributes along with the response variable to the TTP. The TTP
then combines these inputs with the privatized sensitive attributes to train a machine learning
model. The resulting discrimination-free premium is subsequently returned to the insurer.
We explore two practical scenarios within this framework. In the first, the TTP has complete
knowledge of the privatized sensitive attributes, including the privacy mechanism and noise
rate. In the second, while the TTP is aware of the privacy mechanism, it lacks information
about the noise rate. These two settings reflect realistic constraints on data privacy and
access.
The proposed method offers several key advantages. It does not require the insurer to have
direct access to sensitive attributes, thereby enhancing privacy. The approach remains effective
regardless of which party collects the sensitive information, relying solely on its privatized form.
Furthermore, it is straightforward to implement and provides robust statistical guarantees. In
advancing fairness in insurance pricing, our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce
an efficient, transparency-adaptive approach to training discrimination-free models using only
privatized sensitive data. Second, We establish statistical guarantees under both known and
unknown noise rate conditions. Finally, we demonstrate the empirical validity of our method
and provide insights into how errors in estimating the noise rate affect model performance.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related fairness
literature in both machine learning and insurance pricing. Section 3 introduces key definitions
of various insurance premiums in the context of fair pricing and presents the trusted third-party
framework that underpins our theoretical development. Section 4 details our methodology for
estimating discrimination-free premiums under both true and privatized sensitive attributes,

Relation to Existing Literature
2.1
Fairness in Machine Learning
The literature on algorithmic fairness generally distinguishes among three primary paradigms:
individual fairness (Dwork et al., 2012; Barocas et al., 2019), group fairness (Kamishima
et al., 2012; Friedler et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2022), and counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al.,
2017; Agarwal et al., 2021). Individual fairness posits that similar individuals should be
treated similarly, emphasizing consistency of predictions across similar cases. Group fairness,
on the other hand, seeks statistical parity across predefined demographic groups, such as
race or gender. Counterfactual fairness captures the intuition that a decision pertaining to
an individual is fair if changing the individual’s sensitive attribute value does not affect the
decision. While these concepts may align under specific conditions (Dwork et al., 2012),
they often lead to distinct algorithmic goals and trade-offs. The discrimination-free premium
framework we adopt aligns more closely with the principles of individual fairness, although it
does not fit neatly within standard algorithmic fairness taxonomies.
Fairness-aware model training strategies are typically categorized into three broad classes:
Pre-processing techniques modify the training data to remove bias before model fitting
(Adebayo and Kagal, 2016; Calmon et al., 2017; Ple¨Ako and Meinshausen, 2020); In-processing
approaches embed fairness constraints directly into the learning algorithm (Donini et al.,
2018; Agarwal et al., 2018, 2019; Aghaei et al., 2019); Post-processing methods adjust the
predictions of a trained model to meet fairness criteria (Hardt et al., 2016; Woodworth et al.,
2017). Our proposed method shares a similar spirit with post-processing approaches, albeit
with subtle but important differences in formulation. Post-processing methods typically
modify the output of a trained (often unfair) model to satisfy fairness criteria by solving a
constrained optimization problem, where the constraints encode the desired fairness conditions.
Although the discrimination-free insurance premium proposed by Lindholm et al. (2022a) is
derived through a post-processing correction, it is not framed as a constrained optimization
problem. Consequently, when true sensitive attributes are not accessible, post-processing
frameworks from the algorithmic fairness literature—and techniques designed for privatized

sensitive attributes within those frameworks, such as Mehrotra and Celis (2021); Celis et al.
(2021); Ghosh et al. (2023)—are not directly applicable to the insurance pricing context.
Our approach leverages group-specific loss functions, drawing inspiration from the de-
coupling classifier concept studied by Dwork et al. (2018); Ustun et al. (2019), though our
formulation differs substantially. In the presence of privatized sensitive attributes, our method
also relates to learning with corrupted features, as explored in van der Maaten et al. (2013);
Li et al. (2016). Compared to these methods, our approach offers two key advantages:
(1) versatility, as it accommodates any valid loss function under our noise model, and (2)
simplicity, as it is easy to implement—distinguishing it from prior approaches in this domain.
2.2
Fairness in Insurance Pricing
As machine learning becomes increasingly integrated into insurance pricing, regulators
have raised concerns about algorithmic discrimination—particularly in relation to protected
attributes such as gender and ethnicity. While advanced predictive models can enhance
the accuracy of actuarially fair premiums (Shi et al., 2024), they also risk embedding or
amplifying biases, prompting a re-examination of fairness in actuarial science (Zhang and Xu,
2024).
Conceptually, studies such as Shimao and Huang (2022); Frees and Huang (2023); Xin
and Huang (2024); Cˆot´e et al. (2025) have distinguished between two types of discrimination
in insurance: direct discrimination, where sensitive attributes are explicitly used as rating
factors, and indirect discrimination (or proxy discrimination), where non-sensitive attributes
serve as surrogates for sensitive ones. In response, several methodological approaches have
emerged to promote fairness in insurance pricing: 1) Counterfactual methods grounded in
causal inference, which simulate how outcomes would differ under alternative attribute values
Araiza Iturria et al. (2024). 2) Group fairness-based methods, often inspired by broader
algorithmic fairness literature Grari et al. (2022), and 3) Probabilistic frameworks, such as that
of Lindholm et al. (2024), which seek fairness in the distribution of risk outcomes. However,
these approaches generally require access to the true sensitive attributes of policyholders —
an assumption that is increasingly misaligned with evolving legal and regulatory standards
in insurance markets. Our work addresses this critical gap by proposing a framework that
operates solely on privatized or noised versions of sensitive features. To the best of our
knowledge, this is among the first efforts to develop a fairness-aware insurance pricing method
that explicitly accounts for both regulatory restrictions on data access and privacy-preserving
constraints. By doing so, we provide a practical and principled path forward for deploying
fair machine learning models in insurance settings that are both high-stakes and tightly

This definition ensures that sensitive attributes do not influence pricing at the individual

and risk-based fairness, which seeks to minimize cross-subsidization across heterogeneous
groups. The practical value of the discrimination-free premium depends on the magnitude
of such cross-subsidization. Insurers often attempt to mitigate this issue by enriching their
feature sets X through the acquisition of third-party data, thereby reducing omitted variable
bias in premium estimation.
3.1
TTP Framework
We propose a multi-party learning framework in which a trusted third party (TTP) enables
the integration of sensitive and non-sensitive information for fair insurance pricing. In this
setup, the insurer observes non-sensitive attributes (X) of policyholders and collects the
outcome variable (Y ), but does not have direct access to sensitive attributes (D) due to legal
or operational constraints. Instead, a privatized or noised version of the sensitive attributes,
denoted by S, is securely maintained by the TTP. The insurer transmits transformed non-
sensitive attributes, denoted by ˜X, along with the outcome variable to the TTP. The TTP
then combines this information with the privatized sensitive attributes to train a machine
learning model. This workflow is illustrated in Figure 1, which not only depicts the interaction
between the insurer and the TTP, but also outlines the structure of the key theoretical results
developed in the paper. The figure serves as a roadmap linking the major components of
our framework to the corresponding theorems, highlighting how the results build upon one
another to support fair and privacy-aware insurance pricing.

This multi-party training architecture is driven by two prevailing industry trends. First,
because of the regulatory constraints, insurance companies are either prohibited from directly
accessing sensitive attributes or are limited to accessing only a noised version of such
attributes. Second, as sophisticated AI techniques become more prevalent, insurers are
increasingly turning to third-party vendors to implement complex machine learning methods.
Our framework accommodates both these realities by enabling privacy-preserving learning
via a TTP, without compromising model fairness or predictive performance. Notably, our
framework includes two common industry scenarios as special cases: (i) the insurer receives
privatized sensitive attributes from a third party and uses them to train the algorithm
internally; (ii) the insurer gathers both sensitive and non-sensitive data but delegates the
training and pricing algorithm to an external vendor.
In our method, the noise in sensitive attributes may originate from multiple sources,
including but not limited to: 1) Data collection mechanisms: Privacy-preserving filters
used by insurers or third parties to encourage data sharing inherently introduce noise. 2)
Measurement errors: Sensitive information may be inaccurately reported by policyholders or
imputed by insurers, resulting in distortion. 3) Data transmission protocols: Sensitive data
may be deliberately privatized to protect confidentiality during transmission between parties.
Finally, our framework aligns well with established industry practices and can be im-
plemented with minimal infrastructure changes. Large insurers with proprietary pricing
tools often integrate third-party data to supplement their models, making them natural
adopters of our approach. Similarly, smaller insurers that rely on industry-wide databases and
credibility methods can apply our framework by forwarding both sensitive and non-sensitive
data to external vendors for model training. The TTP mechanism thus offers a scalable and
privacy-aware path toward fair premium pricing across the insurance market.
4
Discrimination-free Pricing for Insurance
The ultimate goal of our framework is to compute the discrimination-free premium function
h∗(X), which depends on two key components: the conditional mean outcome µ(X, D) and a
weighting distribution over sensitive attributes, P∗(d). A natural and practical choice for P∗(d)
is its empirical distribution. More generally, it can be treated as a tuning parameter selected
to satisfy desirable statistical properties, such as unbiasedness. Accordingly, the primary
challenge lies in accurately estimating µ(X, D), particularly when the sensitive attributes
are not fully observed. In the following sections, we study its estimation under two settings:
when the sensitive attributes are fully observed, and when only noised or privatized versions
are available. For the latter, we further distinguish between cases where the noise mechanism

Numerical Experiments
In this section, we present a series of carefully designed numerical experiments to illustrate
and support our main theoretical results in Theorems 4.3 and 4.6, which address pricing
under known and unknown noise rates in privatized sensitive attributes, respectively.
We simulate a synthetic dataset with n = 5, 000 observations. The response variable
Y denotes a continuous insurance claim cost. There are two non-sensitive attributes X,
representing the age of the policyholder and the smoking status. The age variable XA takes
integer values between 18 and 80, and the smoking status variable XS is binary, indicating
smoker (S) or non-smoker (NS). The sensitive attribute D, representing gender, is also binary
(male (M) or female (F)). The data-generating process for the claim cost is specified as:
Y =100 + 4 × XA + 100 × 1{XS = S} + 120 × 1{D = F}
+ 200 × 1{(XA ∈[20, 40]) ∩(D = F)} + ϵ,
ϵ ∼N(0, 402).
The covariates are generated under the following assumptions: 1) XA ⊥D and XA ⊥XS; 2)
P(XS = S) = 0.3, P(D = F) = 0.45; 3) P(D = F|XS = S) = 0.8.
In all experiments, we use the identity transformation T(X) = X and set the target
marginal distribution P∗(D) = ˆP(D) when computing the discrimination-free premium ˆh∗(X).
All models are trained using a three-layer feed-forward neural network with five neurons per

Conclusion
This paper presents a principled and practical approach to achieving discrimination-free
insurance pricing in settings where access to sensitive attributes is restricted due to regulatory
or ethical concerns. By leveraging a multi-party training framework and privatized sensitive
data, our method enables insurers to build fair pricing models without directly observing
protected attributes such as gender or race. We show that our approach is both privacy-
preserving and adaptable to different levels of transparency, aligning with real-world regulatory
constraints.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a method for estimating discrimination-
27


## Page 28


free premiums using only privatized sensitive attributes, grounded in a trusted third-party
(TTP) framework. Second, we develop statistically sound algorithms for this task under
both known and unknown noise rates in the privatization mechanism. Third, we empirically
validate our approach using synthetic and real-world datasets, illustrating its robustness and
effectiveness across a range of practical conditions.
The growing integration of machine learning into insurance decision-making calls for
fairness-aware and privacy-respecting methodologies. Our work contributes to this emerging
area by bridging concepts from algorithmic fairness and actuarial fairness in a way that is
both theoretically rigorous and practically feasible. As the regulatory landscape continues to
evolve, frameworks like ours offer a promising path forward for equitable and accountable
algorithmic pricing in the insurance industry.

A.2
Data Analysis - Auto Insurance
A.2.1
Data Description
The Auto Insurance dataset contains 8,150 observations, 17 features, and a single response
variable (Roy, 2021). The response represents risk classification (high risk, low risk), which
defines the task as a classification problem. In our experiment, we choose D = sex to be the
sensitive attribute, taking values“Male” and “Female”.The privatized sensitive attribute S is
generated under varying privacy levels, specified by different values of ϵ’s, following Definition
4.1.
A.2.2
Experiments Setup
We adopt the same experimental design as in the regression task for the Health Insurance
dataset (Section 6), considering two scenarios: one where the noise rates are known and
another where they are unknown. Across both scenarios, we use the following configuration:
1) Hypothesis class F: linear models; 2) Representation T(X) = ˜X, learned via a supervised
feedforward neural network; 3) Three predefined noise levels, corresponding to π = 0.9, 0.8,
and 0.7.
The data is split into training and test sets. For each setting, we estimate the discrimination-
free premium (Definition 3.3) using Algorithm 2 (MPTP-LDP), and benchmark it against
the best-estimated premium (Definition 3.1) computed using Algorithm 1 (MPTP). The only
difference from the regression analysis in Section 6 is the use of cross-entropy loss, appropriate
for the classification task.
A.2.3
Empirical Results
We compare the convergence behavior of the pricing strategies based on their test loss, with
results summarized in this section. As noted above, the test loss here refers to cross-entropy
computed on the test data, in contrast to the mean squared error used in the regression task.
In general, we observe similar patterns to those in the regression analysis presented in
Section 6, further supporting our theoretical predictions. These results provide additional
evidence for the robustness of our approach across both regression and classification tasks.
