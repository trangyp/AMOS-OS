Inferring Hidden Motives: Bayesian Models of 
Preference Learning in Repeated Dictator Games 
Gregory Stanley, Jun Zhang, Rick Lewis 
1 
Abstract  
Human cooperation depends on how accurately we infer others’ motives—how much they value 
fairness, generosity, or self-interest from the choices they make. We model that process in binary dictator 
games, which isolate moral trade-offs between self and other stripped of strategic complexity. 
Participants observed others’ allocation decisions and predicted their future behavior while playing 
through an exhaustive, randomized payoff space implemented on The Morality Game platform. 
We formalize social-preference learning as Bayesian belief updating over continuous parameters 
such as self-interest, altruism, envy, and guilt. The resulting Utility Bayesian Model (UBM) outperformed 
non-Bayesian alternatives and Bayesian models that categorize others into discrete social types. Because 
Bayesian updating requires a utility function in its likelihood term, we conducted the largest utility-
function comparison to date—476 candidate forms differing in psychologically meaningful properties 
(e.g., payoff exponents, reference dependence, payoff ratios, and envy–guilt asymmetries). Exploring this 
joint space of payoffs and models allowed us to identify the function that unifies prior theories and 
generalizes across payoff conditions. 
Parameter estimation revealed moderate altruism, strong inequality aversion, and nonlinear 
payoff valuation (γ > 1). Altruism and social-comparison motives were largely independent, revealing 
diverse moral phenotypes from cooperative to competitive or sadistic. Together, these findings provide a 
computational framework and a map of social motives, clarifying how humans learn whom to trust and 
offering quantitative foundations for promoting cooperation in social and artificial systems.

Introduction  
Understanding the social preferences of others—how much they weigh their own payoffs relative 
to those of others, their sensitivity to inequalities, and their emotional reactions such as envy or guilt, is 
foundational to navigating human social interactions. Decisions about trust, reciprocity, cooperation, and 
resource allocation hinge upon individuals’ abilities to accurately infer the social preferences that guide


## Page 2


2 
the choices of others. Errors in these inferences can be costly, leading to misallocated resources, missed 
opportunities for cooperation, exploitation, or broken relationships. Despite its importance, our 
understanding of how people form, update, and refine their beliefs about others’ social preferences 
remains incomplete, fragmented, and methodologically constrained. Specifically, existing studies typically 
explore limited payoff scenarios, rely on simplified low-dimensional utility forms, and rarely measure 
explicit belief updating—limitations that collectively restrict our ability to accurately and comprehensively 
characterize social preferences and beliefs about them. 
To address these gaps, Experiments 1 and 2 integrate four mutually reinforcing methodological 
elements: (a) randomized payoffs spanning a broad multidimensional space; (b) a near comprehensive 
comparison of 476 candidate utility functions; (c) explicit elicitation of predictions to measure beliefs to 
avoid confounds associated with inferring beliefs solely from choice data; and (d) Bayesian cognitive 
modeling of social preference belief updating in iterated binary dictator games. While each of these 
methodological components is rare individually, their integration is novel and mutually reinforcing: the 
randomized payoffs ensure that the optimal utility function applies broadly rather than narrowly; explicit 
prediction elicitation provides unconfounded data for fitting the Bayesian model; and identifying the best-
fitting utility function is crucial for accurately parameterizing the Bayesian cognitive model. 
Over the last three decades, a significant body of research has employed various economic games 
to probe social preferences, such as dictator, ultimatum, trust, and public goods games. Canonical models 
like Fehr and Schmidt’s (1999) inequality aversion, Bolton and Ockenfels’s (2000) ERC model, and Charness 
and Rabin’s (2002) model of fairness and reciprocity have proposed specific parameterizations of other-
regarding preferences that explain stable individual differences in prosociality, altruism, and fairness. 
Although these models are clear, helpful, and well-supported, they are incomplete descriptions of social 
preferences.  
One prominent limitation is that many studies have restricted themselves to narrow, payoff 
spaces in a small number of games (Andreoni & Miller, 2002; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999). Typically, standard 
dictator games (DGs) or ultimatum games (UGs) use small, predetermined payoff sets, that do not expose 
or elicit the full landscape of possible preference patterns. Consequently, past studies often yield insights 
that fail to generalize outside these places. Murphy and Ackermann (2014) emphasized the importance 
of mapping a broader payoff landscape to reveal nuanced preferences (such as spite), yet even their 
relatively expansive exploration was limited to continuous dictator games. Thus, randomized binary 
dictator game payoff structures enable comprehensive exploration of the space of distributional social 
preference—an approach introduced for the first time in this study.

Limiting our understanding still, prior studies have only sampled a handful of utility functions out 
of a combinatorially explosive space of functional forms. Typically, these studies choose amongst about 
six candidate models a priori (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Engelmann & Strobel, 2004; Murphy & Ackermann, 
2014). Few studies have conducted extensive quantitative model comparisons across a large, 
systematically structured set of candidate functional forms. For instance, Andreoni and Miller (2002) 
compared linear altruism models to alternative forms, yet their exploration did not vary negative biases, 
reference dependence, conditional welfare, payoff sensitivity and a host of plausibly relevant factors. 
Thus, it remains uncertain whether canonical models are the best available approximations of human 
social preferences, or simply convenient and widely adopted assumptions.  
These methodological limitations impede scientific understanding of social preferences. 
Additionally, they hinder understanding of how beliefs about others' social preferences form and evolve. 
When studied, such beliefs are typically inferred from choice data, but when a participant chooses, they 
are motivated by their morality, their strategy, and their beliefs about the morality or social preferences 
of their counterpart(s). These confounds are irrelevant when participants explicitly predict counterparts’ 
choices, and that is why we collected prediction data.   
Thus, despite significant advances, existing research has (1) explored a limited space of payoff 
structures, (2) explored a limited space of utility functions, (3) cannot separate beliefs about social 
preferences from social preferences themselves, via choice data alone.  
This research addresses these gaps by being the first to integrate four interlocking methodological 
elements. First, we employ binary dictator games with randomized payoffs, spanning an extensive, 
multidimensional payoff space. Binary dictator games are simple enough to isolate social preferences 
from strategic considerations, yet flexible enough to host a vast, multidimensional space of payoffs that 
can evoke social psychological mechanisms left confounded or unexplored by games like the standard 
dictator game, which, for instance, cannot decouple altruism from social comparison.   
Second, we record participants' prediction of their counterparts’ choices across repeated 
meetings to measure how they update beliefs based on evidence. While we record choice data also, we 
only measure only one data type per round to avoid confounds.  
Third, we model participants as Bayesian learners, integrating the implications of choice 
observations with preconceptions about the social preferences of their chooser-counterparts. We fit this 
Bayesian model to prediction data.   
Finally, we conduct the largest to date Information Criterion (IC) analysis (AIC/BIC) of candidate 
utility functional forms, comparing 476 variants to identify the optimal utility function that individuals use

in forming beliefs about others’ social preferences. This method ensures that our chosen utility function 
only excludes plausibly relevant psychological mechanisms, like self-interest, altruism, envy, guilt, 
nonlinear payoff sensitivity, and novel forms like reference dependence, payoff ratios, and min-max 
(Rawlsian/Leontief) forms, if they do not improve model fit or are worth the added model complexity.    
Thus, this research integrates multiple methodological innovations—systematically randomized 
payoffs, explicit predictions, Bayesian modeling, and a comprehensive IC utility form analysis—to address 
holes in the scientific understanding of social preferences and beliefs about them. Individually, each 
innovation resolves distinct methodological limitations; collectively, they form an integrated framework 
for exploring social preference inference and belief updating.  
Indeed, we did so via two experiments. First, we conducted a controlled human-bot experiment, 
where participants made predictions about known, algorithmically determined chooser preferences. 
Since these artificial choosers had fixed social preferences, this initial step allowed objective validation of 
our Bayesian model’s accuracy in predicting belief updating without confounding factors. Second, after 
establishing this objective validation, we applied the validated model to a human-human experiment, 
where participants repeatedly interacted across randomized payoff structures within a large, 
multidimensional payoff space (payoffs randomly selected from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}). Participants explicitly 
observed the probabilities of future re-matching with identifiable counterparts, ensuring realistic 
conditions for examining continuous, dynamic belief updating over multiple rounds.  
Crucially, our comprehensive information criterion analysis compared 476 candidate utility 
functional forms using Information Criteria (AIC/BIC), evaluating distinct parameters for self-interest, 
altruism, envy, guilt, nonlinear payoff sensitivity (exponential weighting), reference dependence, and 
negativity parameters. The analysis identified an optimal seven-parameter utility model. This optimal 
model distinctly separates altruism (direct positive valuation of others' payoffs) from social comparison 
(sensitivity to payoff inequalities), further differentiating between envy (negative response to 
disadvantageous inequality) and guilt (negative response to advantageous inequality). It also employs 
single payoffs rather than payoff differences (except for social comparison terms, which inherently require 
relative comparisons) and includes a nonlinear exponent term uniformly applied across parameters, 
capturing participants' amplified sensitivity to larger payoffs or inequalities. 
Empirical parameter estimates from our human-human experiment provide clear psychological 
insights: participants positively valued their own payoffs approximately three times more strongly than 
those of their counterparts (mean ratio = 3.05). Despite this self-interested bias, altruism parameters were

consistently positive for most participants, indicating that the majority genuinely preferred better 
outcomes for others, all else being equal—an encouraging foundation for cooperative interactions.  
However, significant heterogeneity emerged: approximately 27% of participants displayed 
negative altruism parameters (indicating sadistic or spiteful preferences), and roughly 16% exhibited 
negative self-interest parameters (masochistic or self-sacrificing behaviors). Additionally, 27% exhibited 
negative envy parameters (competitive preferences), demonstrating a distinct preference for achieving 
superior payoffs relative to others. Importantly, these competitive preferences were orthogonal to 
sadism, highlighting that individuals can simultaneously desire superior outcomes and still prefer that 
others fare positively rather than negatively. These results move beyond traditional two-dimensional 
social value orientation frameworks, emphasizing the need for nuanced, multi-parameter approaches in 
modeling human social preferences.  
Moreover, our Bayesian cognitive modeling approach demonstrated superior predictive accuracy 
compared to alternative discrete Bayesian and non-Bayesian models, reinforcing the empirical validity 
and cognitive plausibility of continuous belief updating processes. This result aligns with psychological 
research indicating that people update their beliefs gradually rather than categorizing others into rigid 
types (Murphy & Ackermann, 2014).  
Collectively, these methodological innovations are indispensable for addressing critical limitations 
in prior research, jointly enabling a comprehensive characterization of how people infer and update 
beliefs about social preferences. Each component individually provides distinct benefits, yet together they 
form a rigorous foundation for accurately understanding cognitive mechanisms underlying social 
inference.  
In the subsequent sections, we detail experimental methods, Bayesian modeling specifics, model 
comparisons, and comprehensive empirical results. This integrated methodological framework not only 
clarifies fundamental psychological processes underlying social preference inference but also establishes 
a robust platform for future investigations into human social cognition. 
2.1 Disclaimers and Methodological Limitations  
We acknowledge several limitations to this study upfront, with further elaboration provided in 
later sections. First, our sample consisted of unpaid undergraduate participants from WEIRD (Western, 
Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) backgrounds, potentially constraining the generalizability 
of our findings (Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010). Second, our payoff structures did not include 
negative values, restricting the investigation of social preferences involving loss scenarios. Third,

computational constraints necessitated using a simplified, non-updating variant of our Bayesian model in 
Information Criterion (IC) analyses, potentially affecting the generalizability of the identified utility 
function. Finally, the current model did not capture dynamic evolution of social preferences or 
interactions between chooser and predictor parameters—important considerations that present clear 
avenues for exciting future research.

3.2 Methods  
3.2.1 Experimental Design Overview  
Participants across all experiments engaged in repeated binary dictator games (bDG), designed to 
investigate how individuals infer and update beliefs about others' social preferences from observed 
choices. Each trial involved a chooser ("dictator") selecting between two potential payoff allocations 
(Option A vs. Option B). Retaliation, negotiation, or strategic reciprocity within each game was 
structurally impossible, ensuring that choices reflected pure, outcome-based social preferences1. 
Participants observed these decisions and predicted choosers’ subsequent choices under new 
payoff scenarios. Predictions were hidden from choosers. Predictions provided a measure of participants' 
evolving beliefs about the choosers’ underlying social preferences. 
We conducted two complementary experiments, a human-bot experiment designed primarily for 
initial model validation, and a subsequent human-human experiment offering greater ecological validity 
and enabling detailed parameter estimation.

3.3 Utility Bayesian Model: Formulation and Implementation  
3.3.1 Motivation and Research Context  
The primary goal of our study is to provide a precise and psychologically plausible cognitive model 
of how people infer and dynamically update their beliefs about others’ hidden social preferences from 
observed decisions. Humans routinely face ambiguous social signals—actions that may reflect multiple 
underlying motivations—and they must integrate this uncertainty to form reliable predictions of future 
behavior. Prior empirical evidence demonstrates that people indeed combine prior expectations with 
observed behaviors to refine their beliefs about others’ intentions, preferences, or trustworthiness 
(Devaine, Hollard, & Daunizeau, 2014; Yoshida, Dolan, & Friston, 2008). Bayesian models, which formally 
represent uncertainty as probabilistic distributions updated through observations, are especially well-
suited to capturing this gradual inference process (Camerer & Ho, 1999; Fudenberg & Levine, 2009; 
Hampton, Bossaerts, & O’Doherty, 2008; Griffiths, Kemp, & Tenenbaum, 2008).  
Within repeated binary dictator games, observing a single choice—such as a decision benefiting 
oneself over another person—rarely provides unequivocal insight into underlying motives. For instance, 
selecting a self-beneficial option may equally reflect strong self-interest, modest altruism, or nuanced 
inequality aversion. Bayesian inference naturally addresses this challenge by simultaneously modeling 
multiple competing hypotheses (social preference parameters) and updating their relative plausibility


## Page 13


13 
each time a new choice is observed (Yoshida, Dolan, & Friston, 2008). Over repeated observations, 
Bayesian inference allows initially uncertain beliefs to sharpen gradually into precise and reliable 
predictions.  
Bayesian modeling aligns closely with empirically documented cognitive processes in social 
decision-making contexts and provides interpretability by explicitly modeling the belief-updating process. 
This interpretability contrasts favorably with less transparent modeling approaches (e.g., neural network 
models), whose predictions are often difficult to understand. Furthermore, Bayesian inference models 
have consistently outperformed alternative, less cognitively grounded models—such as purely 
reinforcement-based or heuristic models—particularly in contexts where accurately inferring latent social 
preferences from ambiguous behavioral cues is essential (Camerer & Ho, 1999; Erev & Roth, 1998; 
Hampton, Bossaerts, & O’Doherty, 2008). By explicitly modeling belief updating as a core cognitive process 
rather than treating beliefs as incidental by-products of predicting choices, our Bayesian approach 
facilitates deeper insights into the underlying cognitive mechanisms.  
Although our current experiment involves simple one-step dictator games that do not require 
modeling deeper strategic reasoning (such as level-𝑘𝑘 or recursive theory-of-mind (Stahl & Wilson, 1995; 
Nagel, 1995)) extending our Bayesian model to incorporate such reasoning represents a natural and 
valuable future direction. To clarify, our current model addresses only first-level Theory of Mind but could 
be extended to incorporate deeper recursive reasoning applicable to multi-step games. 
Crucially, our Utility Bayesian Model describes the information-processing problem the brain 
must solve—inferring hidden preferences from ambiguous evidence—without making claims about the 
actual neural implementation. Although our model employs explicit probabilistic computations and grid-
based approximations for clarity and computational convenience, we do not suggest the that brain 
literally performs identical computations. Rather, we offer a functional-level description outlining the 
problem of social preference inference and belief updating.  
Next, we detail how our Utility-Bayes Model (UBM) integrates prior beliefs with observed chooser 
decisions to produce updated posterior beliefs about social preference parameters.  
3.3.2 Utility Bayesian Model Mechanics: Prior, Likelihood, and Posterior  
Observers’ initial beliefs about a chooser’s social preferences are represented by a population 
prior over a multidimensional parameter space (Gelman, et al., 2003). This prior assumes that social 
preference parameters are normally distributed in the population—before observing any individual’s 
choices, one predicts their behavior as if randomly sampled from this population (Lee & Wagenmakers,

by population-level parameters (means, standard deviations, and covariances). Once observations of the 
chooser’s actual decisions become available, the model updates from these population priors to 
individual‐level posterior beliefs—although after the first round, we do not impose any specific parametric 
form on subsequent posteriors.

3.3.4 Grid-Based and Particle Filter Posterior Updating 
By default, we used a discrete grid-based approximation method for posterior updating because 
it is simple and transparent. The multidimensional parameter space is discretized into a finite grid (with 
resolution defined by 𝑛𝑛 bins per dimension). At each grid point, we compute likelihood probabilities, 
forming a likelihood probability mass function (PMF) over the parameter space. This likelihood PMF is 
directly comparable to the prior PMF because both are defined over identical parameter grids. 
Multiplying the prior and likelihood PMFs point-by-point yields the posterior PMF, representing 
updated beliefs after observing each choice:

parameters is sufficient to simulate participant responses across multiple games6.

which replaces the full PMF grids with much smaller samples of weighted probability particles.

3.3.5 Optimization and Model Evaluation 
Our Utility Bayesian model follows the sequence of steps participants experience:  
1. First, predictor-participants predict their chooser-counterparts’ choice.  
2. Second, they observe the actual choice.  
3. Finally, they revise their beliefs based on this observation. They use these updated beliefs to make 
predictions in the next round, or more specifically the next round that they are matched with the 
same counterpart and assigned the role of predictor.  
Crucially, participants' predictions reflect posterior beliefs revised based on choices observed in 
the previous round. Thus, our model follows the psychologically plausible causal sequence: predict choice 
→ observe choice → update belief, as shown in Figure 3.  
 
Figure 3: Predict, observe, update: 
Within each game, players in the predictor role, first predict their counterpart’s choice, second observe their 
counterpart’s choice, and third update their beliefs about their counterpart’s social preferences. These revised 
beliefs (posteriors) become the preconceptions (priors) used to make predictions in the next game. Choice 
observations are an input to the model and predictions are an output of the model. 
Our agent function separately maintains stable chooser parameters (used exclusively to generate 
choices) and dynamically updates predictor parameters (used exclusively for generating predictions). 
Rather than using predictions or prediction errors, predictor beliefs evolve solely based on observed 
chooser actions, separating Bayesian updating evidence (chooser actions) from model evaluation criteria 
(participant predictions).

The simulation demonstrates that:  
1. The optimizer accurately recovers the “true” predictor parameter (𝑟𝑟 = 0.755), validating the

2. Model posteriors converge systematically toward the chooser’s altruism parameter, with round‐

sharpness shape learning.  
Together, these points substantiate the practical functioning of our Bayesian approach. The 
model’s ability to converge on correct parameters, combined with the mechanistic role of variance and 
temperature, indicates that it is well‐posed to handle noisy, semi‐stochastic decisions. Readers can thus 
view the subsequent human data analyses with confidence that the same pipeline accurately recovers 
underlying social preferences in a controlled scenario.

3.3.8 Model Validation: Comparing Bayesian and Alternative Cognitive Models  
The simulation results presented earlier verify that our Utility-Based Bayesian (Utility-Bayes) 
model satisfies several absolute performance criteria, including effective optimization, rational updating 
behavior, and appropriate sensitivity to prior beliefs and observed choices. While these results confirm 
the internal coherence and Bayesian quality of the model, they do not demonstrate its superiority over 
alternative cognitive models in predicting human behavior. To assess relative predictive power, we 
compared the Utility-Bayes model against distinct baseline models using data from the human-bot 
experiment.  
Because the human-bot setup avoids role switching and uses preprogrammed chooser agents, it 
provided a more controlled environment for model comparison than the human-human experiment. We 
evaluated models via total negative log-likelihood (NLL) calculated from participants’ predictions, with 
lower NLL indicating higher predictive accuracy. Table 4 reports NLL results across the models. 
We first compared Utility Bayesian against three non-Bayesian approaches lacking critical features 
of Bayesian updating. The Stochastic model assigns equal probability to all choices, serving as a minimal 
baseline (reported NLL is the mean of 1000 runs). The No Learning model was identical to Utility-Bayes 
except priors were effectively frozen by constraining minimum and maximum standard deviation 
parameters to near zero, preventing meaningful updates from new observations. The No Memory model 
disregarded priors entirely, making predictions based solely on the immediate past choice, thus requiring 
no parameter fitting. By contrast, Utility-Bayes integrates priors and cumulative evidence dynamically, 
leveraging both initial assumptions and evolving observations. As shown in Table 4, Utility-Bayes achieves 
substantially lower NLL (5811.11 with a 21×21 grid) than the non-Bayesian baselines (each exceeding 
10,000). These results highlight that both cumulative learning and belief integration significantly enhance 
predictive accuracy.  
Next, we tested the Utility Bayesian model against three models that represent others as falling 
into discrete preference types, rather than representing the social preferences of others as parameters 
on continuous dimensions, which is more like our approach. However, because our model approximates

Discrete versus Continuous Bayesian Models  
A critical question in social preference research concerns whether human moral cognition and 
inferences about others are best represented as discrete types or continuous parameters. Historically, 
discrete categorization frameworks have been influential in psychology and economics due to their 
intuitive appeal and interpretative simplicity. For example, early approaches to social value orientation 
(SVO) classified individuals into distinct categories—such as “prosocial,” “individualistic,” and 
“competitive”—to succinctly describe cooperative tendencies in social dilemmas (Van Lange, Joireman, 
Parks, & Dijk, 2013; Balliet, Parks, & Joireman, 2009). Similarly, in contexts like public goods games, 
researchers have identified meaningful discrete clusters, notably “conditional cooperators” and “free 
riders” (Fischbacher & Gächter, 2010), demonstrating practical explanatory value.  
However, accumulating empirical evidence increasingly suggests that discrete categorization—
while convenient—may substantially oversimplify the underlying psychological reality. Andreoni and 
Miller (2002), in a seminal revealed-preference study, demonstrated that altruistic behavior exhibits 
nuanced variation along a continuous spectrum rather than fitting neatly into distinct categories. This 
continuous variability in social preferences was further supported by Fisman, Kariv, and Markovits (2007), 
who documented extensive heterogeneity in generosity and social concern across a broad, continuous 
payoff space. Moreover, Murphy, Ackermann, and Handgraaf (2011) explicitly introduced a continuous 
measurement method—the “SVO Slider”—highlighting significant and meaningful variability in social 
preferences that discrete typologies fail to capture fully.  
Parallel debates exist regarding people's beliefs about others' social preferences. While discrete 
mental categories, such as labeling individuals as “good” or “bad,” offer efficient cognitive heuristics 
(Cosmides, Barrett, & Tooby, 2010; Krupp, Debruine, & Barclay, 2008), more recent evidence shows that 
human belief updating often transcends these initial simplifications. However, the question of whether 
social-preference inferences specifically are best represented as discrete categories or continuous 
distributions remains unresolved.


## Page 27

A growing body of work indicates that, even when researchers start from discrete typologies, 
people’s beliefs about others shift in graded, probabilistic ways as evidence accrues. For instance, subjects 
invert partners’ actions to update a continuous posterior over preference weights (Jern, Lucas, & Kemp, 
2017), show type-dependent belief revision across repeated dictator tasks (Iriberri & Rey-Biel, 2013), and 
can be captured by hierarchical Bayesian models that treat both preferences and beliefs as continuous 
latent variables (Aksoy & Weesie, 2014). Yet these prior studies tested only a small, predetermined set of 
discrete models or did not systematically examine continuous-versus-discrete representations specifically 
within the domain of social preferences. 
Following this reasoning, we compared our relatively high-resolution Utility-Bayes model to 
several discrete Bayesian models of social preference belief updating. In these discrete variants, we 
treated participants’ social preferences as belonging to one of a few distinct “types” and specified uniform 
priors across those types. The Good versus Evil model, for instance, divides the population into two bins 
according to whether an agent’s altruism parameter is positive or negative, and the Canonical Social Value 
Orientation (SVO) model draws on well-known categories such as altruistic, selfish, and competitive. We 
also included a Perfect Oracle model, which starts with a perfectly calibrated prior matching the 
experiment’s actual distribution of avatar types. This “oracle” thus knows in advance exactly what fraction 
of encounters will feature selfish, competitive, or utilitarian counterparts, giving it the best possible 
starting guess in principle. Yet despite these theoretically favorable conditions, the Perfect Oracle model 
exhibits a relatively poor fit (NLL = 17381.06), suggesting that participants do not behave like omniscient 
observers who know the population composition from the outset.

3.3.9 Searching The Space of Typological Bayesian Models  
We next conducted a more extensive search through a broad range of discrete models to test 
whether any alternative partitioning of the social preference space could rival our Utility-Bayes model.

of as a lower-resolution special case of our grid-based, high-resolution approach, where only a handful of 
parameter “bins” receive nonzero prior probability. To construct these lower-resolution hypothesis

spaced points between −1 and +1 on each dimension. We then generated discrete models by choosing 
different subsets of these points—ranging from three to six preference “types”—and fitting the prior 
probabilities over those types to the data. Specifically, we evaluated every combination of three and four

Our exhaustive combinational approach inherently included many models with low face validity 
or minimal differentiation. Nevertheless, systematically evaluating over 15,000 discrete typological 
models against a continuous Bayesian approach allowed us to present uniquely conclusive evidence 
supporting quasi-continuous belief representation. This comprehensive evaluation thus constitutes a 
novel methodological contribution—arguably the largest and most rigorous discrete-versus-continuous 
Bayesian model comparison within social preference research to date10. Previous studies comparing 
continuous and discrete representations typically contrast only a handful of predefined models.  
Nonetheless, several directions remain open for future research. First, our analysis is a functional-
level description (Marr, 1982), characterizing input-output relationships (choices and predictions) without 
specifying the cognitive algorithms observers employ. Future studies could explore underlying cognitive 
or neural processes—for example, determining whether humans approximate Bayesian inference through 
heuristic algorithms (e.g., sampling-based approximations or particle filters).  
Second, our conclusions about discrete versus continuous representations depend on the two 
parameters (self-interest and altruism) and the utility function we used. While we expect that similar 
outcomes would generalize to alternative dimensions (e.g., envy and guilt), future empirical work is 
needed to confirm whether continuous Bayesian inference consistently outperforms discrete typological 
models across other social preference domains.  
Finally, the experimental context provided participants with clear, repeated feedback, which likely 
facilitated fine-grained belief representation and updating. Future research may address whether 
participants maintain such granular representations in more information-poor or cognitively demanding 
environments, or if simpler typological representations would prove more adaptive under these 
conditions.

Information Criterion Analysis: Determining

Having demonstrated that a high-resolution Bayesian approach outperforms simpler discrete or 
non-Bayesian models, we next perform an analysis to determine which utility function our Bayesian 
approach should employ. The functional form of the utility function determines how social preferences 
respond to payoff distributions, such as whether they consider payoff differences, payoffs relative to a 
reference point, or other transformations. Accurately establishing this form is crucial for interpreting the 
distributions of social preferences we will report, since an incorrect functional form could distort our 
conclusions about those preferences and even which preferences matter most. To identify the best form, 
we conduct an Information Criterion (IC) analysis across 476 candidate utility specifications. We base this 
analysis on the human-human experiment data, which span a large, randomized payoff space and involve 
participants in both choosing and predicting roles. In contrast to the smaller set of payoff structures from 
the human-bot experiment, the broader distribution of payoffs in the human-human experiment makes 
it possible to test the generalizability of each candidate utility function across a larger payoff space and 
for both chooser and predictor roles.  
4.1 Motivation and Theoretical Justification of Utility Form Variations 
Our modeling effort aims to characterize how observers infer and update beliefs about others' 
social preferences, specifically focusing on outcome-based social preferences. Outcome-based 
preferences refer to preferences that depend exclusively on final distributions of payoffs, without 
considering non-outcome elements such as intentions, fairness norms, reciprocity, or responsibility 
attribution (Falk & Fischbacher, 2006). Non-outcome-based social preferences are important but outside 
the scope of this study.  
We explored a space of utility functions inspired by influential theoretical frameworks and 
empirical findings. Each variation captures a psychological mechanism hypothesized to influence how 
individuals represent and evaluate social outcomes.

(13 & 14) Inclusion of Altruism and Social Comparison Terms 
Finally, we explicitly tested the necessity of including altruism and social comparison terms,

These systematic explorations resulted in 476 distinct utility forms. We searched for the best 
fitting utility form, defined as minimizing the information criterion score, to ensure a robust foundation 
for our Bayesian cognitive modeling. 
4.2 Methodological Approach 
We performed an Information Criterion (IC) analysis to select the optimal utility function among 
476 systematically generated alternatives. Using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian 
Information Criterion (BIC), we balanced model fit against complexity (Anderson & Burnham, 2002; 
Akaike, 1973; Schwarz, 1978). For computational feasibility, we conducted this analysis using a static (no 
updating) version of our cognitive model. Although a fully dynamic Bayesian approach would have been 
theoretically ideal, the computational demands of fitting individual-level Bayesian belief updating 
parameters across 73 participants and 476 utility functions proved prohibitive11.

•

Recommended:  
•

4.5 Robustness Analysis of Model Selection  
To ensure that our information criterion (IC) results were robust to the inherently stochastic 
nature of the optimization process, we conducted a robustness analysis based on repeated iterations of 
the fitting process. Each iteration attempted to discover better (lower) negative log-likelihood (NLL) losses 
for each candidate utility function, starting from different randomized initial parameter estimates.  
We tracked two complementary metrics:


## Page 41


41 
 
1. Sum of Δ Minimum Loss: This metric quantifies the incremental improvement in model fits across 
iterations. For each model, we recorded the minimum loss discovered up to each iteration. After 
every iteration, we calculated how much lower the new minimum loss (if found) was compared 
to the best minimum loss identified in all previous iterations. Summing these differences across 
all models yielded a total improvement measure for that iteration. We chose this as our primary 
robustness metric because diminishing returns in this metric reflect when further iterations no 
longer substantively improve the stability of our results.  
2. Ordinal Rank Changes: As a complementary metric, we examined how models' ranks by BIC 
changed between iterations. Trust in the results of our IC analysis depends on the stability of BIC 
rank ordering of models across repeated analyses. We measured this by the sum of the absolute 
differences in rank for all models between consecutive iterations. The results from this metric 
corroborate those observed with the sum of Δ minimum loss.  
We terminated the analysis once the sum of Δ minimum loss improvements fell below a pre-
specified threshold (e.g., 10) for two consecutive rounds because this indicates convergence. The sum of 
Δ minimum loss decreased substantially within the first few iterations and remained below our threshold 
by iteration 11, triggering the stopping criterion. This convergence provides confidence that our IC-based 
selection of the optimal utility function is stable and reproducible. Figure 10 illustrates these robustness 
metrics across iterations.

4.6.2 Parent-Fair L2 Regularization 
Many of our models are quasi–scale‑invariant in their weights (e.g., 𝑉𝑉𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖, 𝑉𝑉𝑖𝑖𝑖𝑖,…): multiplying all 
social preference weights by a constant can leave choice predictions nearly unchanged for a fixed SoftMax 
temperature. This creates flat directions in the loss surface that can cause gradient‑based local optimizers 
(especially L‑BFGS‑B) to stop early or wander. 
A small L2 penalty adds gentle curvature in these flat directions, stabilizing the search and making 
local refinement reliable. We keep the weight small. It is used only to help the optimizer, not to change 
which model wins. 
A naïve L2 penalty can bias against parents (they have more free parameters), which could itself 
produce nesting violations. We therefore design the penalty to be parent‑fair: When a parent is set to the 
child‑anchor (i.e., the special parameters that collapse it to the child) and the shared parameters match, 
the parent and child incur the same penalty. So parents are penalized more only when they deviate away 
from the anchor that makes them behave like their children.

4.6.3 Optimization Schedule 
To avoid nesting violations, we first fit child models and pass their best fitting parameters to their 
parents as an initial point for optimization, called a warm start. This helps move the optimizer out of local 
minima in high-dimensional parent parameter spaces. However, warm starts can create path dependence, 
where a child stuck in a local minima could propagate this suboptimal initialization up the model hierarchy. 
To mitigate this problem, we fit the models iteratively, using the same iterations within the robustness 
analysis, where the system progresses from exploration to exploitation. The first four iterations, all models 
are optimized with simulated annealing, followed by L-BGFS-B, without the help of warm starts. In the 
next four iterations, children provide their best-fitting parameters to their parents as warm starts but the

Discussion and Conclusion  
This research advances our understanding of human social preferences and belief updating 
dynamics by being the first to integrate four complementary methodological elements: (1) systematic 
exploration of a broad payoff space, (2) comprehensive testing of diverse utility functions, (3) elicitation 
of predictions from participants, and (4) continuous Bayesian modeling of belief updating. Each 
component is essential for the others, forming an interlocking framework necessary for accurately 
quantifying and modeling social preferences and belief updates. Specifically, exploring an extensive payoff 
space is required to discriminate between competing utility forms, identifying the optimal utility structure 
is necessary for accurate Bayesian inference, and eliciting predictions provides unambiguous data to 
validate the Bayesian model.  
6.1 Scope and Historical Context 
This paper focuses only on outcome-based social preferences—preferences about the 
distribution of payoffs between people, regardless of beliefs, intentions, reciprocity, social norms, self-
/social-image, and other non-outcome-based concerns. The binary dictator games we used only involve a 
single choice made with perfect information, no chance events, and only two players. These games 
preclude preferences regarding risk and ambiguity, intentionality and accidents, reciprocity, larger groups, 
and also strategic reasoning. To clarify the meaning of outcome-based preferences, consider some 
alternatives. Guilt aversion has been defined as a belief-based preference, where an individual's guilt is 
driven by deviations between their actions and their beliefs about another person's expectations, 
multiplied by how much the individual cares about the other person (Charness & Dufwenberg, 2006; 
Battigalli & Dufwenberg, 2009; Battigalli & Dufwenberg, 2022). A husband’s guilt from divorcing his wife 
depends in part on how surprised and disappointed he expects her to be, not just their financial outcomes.


## Page 52


52 
 
Indeed, in our deterministic and transparent binary dictator games, every outcome is intentional and 
common knowledge. Thus, we cannot address psychological processes involving attributions of 
responsibility, intentionality, or moral judgment influenced by accidental versus intentional harm (Falk & 
Fischbacher, 2006; Cushman, Young, & Hauser, 2006; Young & Saxe, 2009). Likewise, our design does not 
vary self- or social-image concerns, since the games provided no mechanisms for participants to manage 
their own self-perception or to control how visible their choices were to third-party observers (Dana, 
Weber, & Kuang, 2007; Andreoni & Bernheim, 2009). 
Regarding reciprocity, preferences for punishment and reward inherently depend on sequences 
of actions across multiple interactions or multi-step games. Our single-step games preclude reciprocity 
preferences within each round. However, because players repeatedly encounter the same counterparts 
across rounds, we acknowledge that reciprocity preferences were probably activated across games (Rand 
& Nowak, 2013; Falk & Fischbacher, 2006). Although analyzing reciprocity preferences across rounds 
would be a promising avenue for future research, it is beyond the scope of the current analysis. 
Historically, modeling outcome-based preferences converged around canonical utility forms 
primarily developed between 1992 and 2004: Rabin’s fairness equilibrium (1992), Fehr-Schmidt’s inequity 
aversion (1999), Bolton-Ockenfels’ ERC model (2000), Andreoni-Miller’s CES warm-glow altruism (2002), 
Charness and Rabin’s social welfare model (2002), and Engelmann-Strobel’s maximin-efficiency model 
(2004). Subsequently, innovation largely shifted toward incorporating reciprocity, responsibility, self-
image, belief-based and other types of non-outcome-based preferences rather than refining these 
foundational outcome-based kernels (Capraro, Halpern, & Perc, 2024; Fehr & Charness, 2025). Recent 
empirical approaches continue to rely on these canonical frameworks or simpler two-dimensional 
schemes (Kerschbamer, 2015; Nunnari & Pozzi, 2022; Liu & Lange, 2023; Mizuno & Shimizu, 2024; Bakker 
& Dijkstra, 2021)15.

The following are reasons that at least five parameters are indispensable:  
• 
Payoff Sensitivity (γ): Although marginal utility and risk attitudes have  traditionally been 
modeled in solitary tasks, individual-level preferences remain operative in social contexts. Social 
decisions do not suspend solitary economic dynamics, they subsume them. 
• 
Self-interest and altruism: While many traditional utility functions exclude self-interest, assuming 
it to be a constant of one, including parameters for self-interest and altruism is the only way to 
identify masochistic and sadistic preferences, which our results demonstrate to be substantial.      
• 
Envy and guilt (social comparison): Inequality aversion is asymmetric; individuals typically exhibit 
stronger aversion to either disadvantageous inequality (envy) or advantageous inequality (guilt) 
(Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kerschbamer, 2015; Nunnari & Pozzi, 2022; Capraro, Halpern, & Perc, 
2024). Combining envy and guilt obscures this reality. 
• 
Psychological Validity: Each parameter corresponds to a conceptually distinct psychological 
phenomena. For instance, dropping altruism would force us to label anyone who enjoys hurting 
others as merely "extremely competitive," while dropping social comparison would force us to 
label anyone who enjoys being superior to others as being "sadistic."17 
In sum, any utility function for outcome-based preferences that omits self-interest, altruism, envy, 
guilt, and nonlinear payoff sensitivity risks inaccuracy and conceptual confusion. Lower-dimensional 
models may be expedient and useful when the penalty for model complexity is very high, such as in the 
human-bot experiment where higher-dimensional payoff exploration was infeasible, these models will 
nonetheless be an insufficient description of social decision making.

6.3 Implications of Higher-Dimensional Parameter Estimation 
Because our utility function unites these seven preference parameters, we can directly quantify 
the relative magnitudes of these parameters within and between individuals, something that lower-
dimensional frameworks cannot achieve within a single study. Without this unified approach, aggregating 
results across studies employing simpler models becomes analogous to solving a puzzle from mismatched 
pieces. While we attempt to compare our parameter distribution results to the results of other studies, 
we acknowledge that these comparisons tend to be ambiguous because conflating parameter dimensions, 
especially altruism and social comparison, is so prevalent in the literature.  
6.4 Contributions of Parameter Distributions 
We measured the distribution of seven social preference parameters and obtained four notable 
results: (1) self-interest is roughly seven times stronger than altruism, though altruism remains positive 
across most individuals; (2) guilt outweighs envy, reaffirming the asymmetry in inequality aversion but in 
the opposite direction; (3) nonlinear payoff sensitivity was term-specific—self-payoffs were 
near-linear/slightly concave (γ₁≈0.83), whereas altruism and social-comparison components were 
super-linear (γ₂≈1.34; γ₃≈1.38), contradicting the typical finding of diminishing marginal utility; and (4) 
minority negative-preference types (sadism, competitiveness, masochism) occur at substantially higher 
frequencies than previously recognized.  
Participants valued their own payoffs about three times more strongly than their counterparts', 
broadly consistent with positive altruism (Falk, et al., 2018) and typical dictator-game allocations 
averaging approximately 20% of the endowment given away (Engel, 2011). However, this similarity is only 
approximate, as standard dictator-game giving does not represent a pure measure of altruism. Indeed, 
linear utility functions such as those discussed in Section 3.2 typically predict extreme allocations—
keeping or giving away the entire allotment—unless modified with exponents or inequality aversion 
parameters (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Andreoni & Miller, 2002)18. In contrast, our method using binary 
dictator games with varied payoff structures allows us to isolate altruism from social comparison and

Our finding of greater aversion to advantageous inequality (guilt) compared to disadvantageous 
inequality contradicts prior inequality-aversion research that shows the opposite phenomenon (Fehr & 
Schmidt, 1999; Loewenstein, Thompson, & Bazerman, 1989; Nunnari & Pozzi, 2022). One plausible

contradicting traditional assumptions of diminishing marginal utility and concave risk preferences

an open question that future research can address.

occasionally been observed before (Abbink & Sadrieh, 2009; Herrmann & Orzen, 2008; Nunnari & Pozzi,

may partially reflect strategic punishment, as participants anticipated repeated interactions with the same 
counterparts.

cognition. We found that participants consistently exhibited dominant self-interest parameters alongside 
positive, though smaller, altruistic tendencies. This result aligns with and extends previous research 
(Andreoni & Miller, 2002; Charles & Rabin, 2002; Camerer C. F., 2003), indicating that while humans 
predominantly prioritize their own interests, genuine concern for others is reliably present across the 
population.

altruism and social comparison parameters within a unified utility model. Standard Social Value 
Orientation (SVO) models (Messick & McClintock, 1968; Van Lange, 1999; Murphy, Ackermann, &

providing valuable insights into human social decision-making. However, this two-dimensional 
perspective implicitly treats altruism and competitiveness as opposing ends of a single continuum, 
conflating conceptually distinct motivations: genuine concern for another’s well-being (altruism) and 
concern with relative payoffs (social comparison). Our empirical findings reveal substantial individual 
variation along both altruism and social comparison dimensions independently, suggesting the 
importance of expanding the dimensionality of utility spaces beyond traditional SVO frameworks.  
6.5 Validation of the Continuous Bayesian Model  
Our Bayesian modeling approach demonstrated superior predictive accuracy and cognitive 
plausibility compared to discrete Bayesian and non-Bayesian alternatives (Gershman, Daw, Rabinovich, 
Friston, & Varona, 2012; Griffiths, Kemp, & Tenenbaum, 2008). We conducted an unprecedently large-
scale comparison, evaluating thousands of discrete typological models against a high-resolution Bayesian 
model (UBM), showing the UBM to fit the data better than all the typological models. Information 
Criterion analysis identified the optimal utility functional form, emphasizing the importance of nuanced 
parameterization for modeling realistic human moral cognition (Akaike, 1973; Schwarz, 1978). 
Belief stabilization analyses provided further validation, showing the model’s sensitivity to subtle 
behavioral cues. Rapid convergence, particularly for parameters aligned with participants' priors, 
demonstrates the cognitive efficiency and ecological validity of Bayesian inference in human social 
cognition.

6.6 Broader Scientific and Philosophical Relevance  
This research provides precise empirical measures of fundamental social preferences—
selfishness, altruism, and responses to inequality—addressing questions central to moral psychology and 
philosophy. Knowing exact numerical values for these parameters offers insight into long-standing 
debates about human nature, such as the extent of inherent altruism versus self-interest and how 
individuals emotionally react to fairness and inequality. Thus, these findings meaningfully inform broader 
inquiries into the human condition without overstating their implications.  
The identification of consistent altruistic tendencies alongside dominant self-interest also offers 
cautious optimism about cooperative social interactions. Further, this nuanced understanding of human 
morality has implications for AI alignment, suggesting that AI systems trained on human interaction data 
may incorporate foundational cooperative tendencies (Russell, 2019; Dafoe, et al., 2020). However, this 
optimism must be tempered by recognizing complex factors influencing AI development, including the 
intentions of AI creators and the dynamic evolution of AI values.  
6.7 Limitations and Future Directions  
We acknowledge several methodological limitations. Our primarily unpaid, WEIRD participant 
sample limits generalizability (Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010). Additionally, the exclusion of 
negative payoffs restricted our ability to fully examine negativity parameters relevant to loss aversion 
(Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Our assumption of static social preferences is another simplification, as real-
world preferences likely evolve dynamically through reciprocal interactions.  
Moreover, our binary dictator games involve only deterministic, two-player, single-choice 
allocations without negative payoffs. Consequently, we could not explore critical outcome-based 
preferences including multi-recipient fairness tradeoffs, third-party inequality aversion, or genuine 
reference-dependent loss aversion. Our use of an artificial reference point at the midpoint (R=3) was 
practical but fundamentally different from a true loss framing around zero or dynamically evolving 
reference points. The failure of negativity and reference-dependent parameters in our models thus should 
be interpreted with caution. 
Additionally, our analysis focused exclusively on abstract payoffs (human-bot experiment) or 
payoffs framed as though they are monetary payoffs. Real-world social preferences may extend beyond 
monetary outcomes to domains such as health, time, pain, or other intangible outcomes. This work could 
be extended to see if and how social preferences differ over these non-monetary "currencies".


## Page 59


59 
 
Future research directions should address these limitations by:  
1. Conducting experiments with paid20, non-WEIRD populations to enhance generalizability.  
2. Introducing temporal discounting, giving greater weight to recent interactions to capture recency 
effects.  
3. Incorporating inductive biases, particularly negativity biases, reflecting realistic cognitive 
heuristics.  
4. Modeling dynamic parameters and reciprocal updating to examine how individuals' beliefs about 
others influence their own preferences, facilitating reciprocal altruism.  
5. Generalizing the model to multi-step, multi-option, and multi-player strategic environments, 
thereby incorporating non-outcome-based preferences like reciprocity, norms, and intentions 
(Fudenberg & Levine, 1998; Camerer C. F., 2003; Falk & Fischbacher, 2006; Rand & Nowak, 2013; 
Charles & Rabin, 2002). 
6. Include negative payoffs. 
6.8 Conclusion  
Ultimately, this study significantly advances our understanding of human social preferences and 
belief updating dynamics through an integrated methodological framework. The empirical benchmarks 
established here provide valuable insights into fundamental human motivations and reactions to social 
outcomes, enriching our understanding of moral psychology and offering practical parameters for future 
modeling efforts.  
These insights set a foundation for future research to explore more dynamic, context-sensitive 
models, facilitating deeper theoretical understanding and practical applications across psychology, 
economics, and AI-human interactions. By addressing identified limitations and pursuing the outlined 
future directions, researchers can further refine our understanding of human social cognition, ultimately 
supporting more cooperative and beneficial societal and technological outcomes. 
 
 
 
20 Specifically, it would be worth incentivizing correct predictions, not just assigning dollar amounts to payoffs won. 
We did not create extrinsic incentives for correction prediction because this could have been a confound. For 
instance, paying participants for correct predictions could incentivize them to nudge their counterparts to be 
predictable, such as by choosing in more trustworthy ways, not because of genuine social preferences but out of the 
desire to make more money when in the predictor role if stable trustworthy choices were reciprocated. Nonetheless, 
paid predictors would likely invest more energy into their predictions and produce more reliable data. Thus, it might 
be worth finding ways to mitigate such confounds.
