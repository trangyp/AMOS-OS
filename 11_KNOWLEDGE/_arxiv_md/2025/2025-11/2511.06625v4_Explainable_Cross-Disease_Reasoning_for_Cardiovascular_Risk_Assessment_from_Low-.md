Abstract
Low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) inherently captures
both pulmonary and cardiac structures, offering a unique opportu-
nity for joint assessment of lung and cardiovascular health. How-
ever, most existing approaches treat these domains as independent
tasks, overlooking their physiological interplay and shared imaging
biomarkers. We propose an Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning
Framework that enables interpretable cardiopulmonary risk assess-
ment from a single LDCT scan. The framework introduces an agen-
tic reasoning process that emulates clinical diagnostic thinking: first
perceiving pulmonary findings, then reasoning through established
medical knowledge, and finally deriving a cardiovascular judgment
with a natural-language rationale. It integrates three components: a
Pulmonary Perception Module that summarizes lung abnormalities,
an Agentic Pulmonary-to-Cardiac Reasoning Module that infers
their cardiovascular implications, and a Cardiac Feature Extractor
that encodes structural biomarkers. Their outputs are fused to pro-
duce a holistic cardiovascular risk prediction that is both accurate
and physiologically grounded. Experiments on the NLST cohort
demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves state-of-the-
art performance for CVD screening (𝐴𝑈𝐶= 0.919) and mortality
prediction (𝐴𝑈𝐶= 0.838), outperforming single-disease and purely
image-based baselines. Beyond quantitative gains, the framework
provides human-verifiable reasoning that aligns with cardiologi-
cal understanding, revealing coherent links between pulmonary
abnormalities and cardiac stress mechanisms. Overall, this work
establishes a unified and explainable paradigm for cardiovascu-
lar analysis from LDCT, bridging the gap between image-based
prediction and mechanism-based medical interpretation.

Introduction
Lung cancer and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are the two lead-
ing causes of death worldwide, jointly accounting for more than
one-third of annual mortality [22]. In high-risk populations, such
as long-term smokers, these two diseases exhibit a complex patho-
physiological interplay that extends beyond shared lifestyle factors.
Epidemiological evidence supports a bidirectional link: lung can-
cer acts as an independent risk factor for coronary artery disease
(CAD), while patients with heart failure face a significantly in-
creased risk of developing incident lung cancer (HR 1.89) [8]. This
intersection is most striking within screening cohorts; in the Na-
tional Lung Screening Trial (NLST), cardiovascular deaths (486)
actually outnumbered lung cancer deaths (356) during the follow-
up period [4, 7]. While both share smoking as a dominant risk factor,
their exposure–response patterns differ: lung cancer risk increases
almost linearly with cumulative exposure, whereas CVD mortality
rises steeply at low exposure and then plateaus [25]. These trends
reflect distinct but interconnected mechanisms, such as chronic
systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, leaving cancer patients
facing up to a ten-fold greater risk of CVD mortality compared to
the general population [4, 8].
Low-dose chest computed tomography (LDCT) is the standard
of care for lung cancer screening, with the NLST demonstrating a
20% reduction in lung cancer-specific mortality. Subsequent trials,
including NELSON and UKLS, have consistently validated this sur-
vival benefit [9, 10, 31]. Beyond its primary oncologic utility, LDCT
offers an underexploited window into the “cardiopulmonary nexus.”
Recent data indicate that up to 60.1% of lung cancer screening candi-
dates already harbor coronary artery calcification (CAC) [24]. The
clinical implications are profound: individuals with severe CAC
identified during lung screening exhibit a nearly eight-fold increase
in the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)
compared to those without (8.6% vs. 1.1%, 𝑝< .001) [24]. However,
the prognostic value of LDCT extends beyond cardiac regions alone.
Emerging evidence suggests that specific pulmonary abnormali-
ties are intrinsically linked to cardiovascular health. For instance,
quantitative emphysema and interstitial lung abnormalities (ILAs)
detected on LDCT have been identified as robust, independent pre-
dictors of CVD mortality, even after adjusting for traditional risk
factors like CAC [2, 8]. This suggests that the morphological fea-
tures of the lung parenchyma contain critical prognostic cues for

cancer detection and CVD risk stratification as distinct, indepen-

in isolation. By overlooking the predictive power of pulmonary
biomarkers for cardiac risk (and vice versa), these methods discard
critical cross-organ information. Consequently, three major chal-
lenges hinder the realization of an effective dual-screening system.
First, discrimination for CVD mortality derived solely from cardiac
regions remains moderate [7, 26], indicating that organ-restricted
features fail to capture the broader, systemic disease context. Sec-
ond, while recent agentic reasoning approaches have introduced
clinically inspired inference mechanisms [18, 30, 37], they remain
confined to single-organ analysis, lacking the overarching logic
required to model the integrated cardiopulmonary pathway. Finally,
standard deep learning methods often operate as “black boxes,” lim-
iting interpretability and eroding clinical trust. These limitations
collectively constrain predictive accuracy and clinical utility, un-
derscoring the critical need for a unified, interpretable paradigm
for joint cardiopulmonary analysis.
To address these challenges and bridge the gap between radio-
logical isolation and physiological interdependence, we propose
an Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning Framework that redefines
the role of a single LDCT scan as a holistic tool for comprehen-
sive cardiopulmonary risk assessment. Our architecture integrates
three clinically grounded components to effectively harness the
“cardiopulmonary nexus”: (1) To capture the systemic context of-
ten overlooked by cardiac-centric models, a Pulmonary Perception
Module and Cardiac Localization Module concurrently extract struc-
tural biomarkers across the entire thoracic volume. (2) To tran-
scend the limitations of existing single-organ agents, we introduce a
novel Agentic Pulmonary-to-Cardiac Reasoning Module. This compo-
nent utilizes a Large Language Model (LLM) encoded with medical
knowledge to explicitly reason about the causal pathways linking
pulmonary pathology (e.g., emphysema) to cardiac physiology. (3)
Finally, a Multimodal Fusion Module integrates these reasoning-
derived semantic insights with deep imaging features to predict
clinical outcomes. Extensive experiments on the NLST cohort, in-
cluding rigorous ablation studies and comparisons against state-of-
the-art baselines such as Sybil, DeepCAC, and M3FM, demonstrate
the effectiveness of our approach. Our method achieves superior
discrimination for CVD screening (AUC 0.919) and mortality predic-
tion (AUC 0.838). Furthermore, qualitative analyses via Grad-CAM
and textual attention confirm that the model grounds its predictions
in interpretable physiological patterns, establishing a principled
and trustworthy framework for dual-purpose screening.

Related Work
2.1
Lung Cancer Risk Prediction from LDCT
Low-dose chest CT is the most effective modality for lung cancer
screening, with large randomized trials such as NLST, NELSON,
and UKLS demonstrating significant reductions in lung cancer mor-
tality [9, 10, 31]. These successes have motivated extensive research
on automated lung cancer risk prediction from LDCT [32]. Early
approaches typically followed a two-stage approach, combining
pulmonary nodule detection with malignancy risk estimation based
on handcrafted or learned features [20, 28]. With the advent of deep
learning, convolutional models trained on large-scale datasets such
as LIDC-IDRI and LUNA16 achieved strong performance for nod-
ule detection and characterization, with 3D architectures generally
outperforming their 2D counterparts [1, 16, 29]. More recent work
has shifted toward end-to-end longitudinal risk estimation. No-
tably, Sybil directly predicts 1–6 year lung cancer incidence from
volumetric LDCT and demonstrates robust generalization across
external cohorts [21]. Related systems have also been validated
for incidental nodule detection in non-screening CT populations,
highlighting their applicability beyond controlled trial settings [14].
Despite these advances, most lung cancer risk models remain nar-
rowly optimized for oncologic outcomes, leaving non-oncologic

2.2
Cardiovascular Risk Prediction from LDCT
Beyond pulmonary structures, LDCT inherently captures the heart
and thoracic vasculature, enabling opportunistic cardiovascular risk
prediction. Early studies focused on quantifying coronary artery cal-
cium (CAC), thoracic aortic calcification, and pericardial fat, which
are well-established biomarkers of cardiovascular disease (CVD)
risk [7, 11, 25]. In lung cancer screening cohorts, CAC burden has
been shown to strongly predict all-cause and cardiovascular mortal-
ity, often rivaling lung cancer as a cause of death [9, 15]. Building
on this foundation, recent deep learning methods have automated
calcium scoring and extended LDCT analysis toward direct out-
come prediction. Representative examples include DeepCAC [35],
KAMP-Net [12], and related frameworks for fully automated CAC
quantification [17], which achieve moderate discrimination for car-
diovascular mortality in the NLST. Beyond coronary calcification,
multiple screening cohorts have shown that emphysema extent,
muscle mass, and fat attenuation provide complementary prognos-
tic signals as systemic markers of cardiometabolic health [2, 19, 26].
Nevertheless, most existing approaches emphasize cardiac struc-
tures in isolation and do not explicitly integrate pulmonary abnor-
malities that co-vary with cardiovascular outcomes, limiting their
ability to capture the multifactorial disease risk encoded in LDCT.
2.3
Cross-Disease Learning from LDCT
Although lung cancer and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are of-
ten studied in isolation, their shared risk factors and frequent
co-occurrence motivate cross-disease learning from LDCT. Early
work, such as Tri2D-Ne,t demonstrated that LDCT encodes both
pulmonary and cardiac signals by jointly leveraging coronary cal-
cium and pericardial fat to predict cardiovascular mortality in the
NLST, with performance comparable to dedicated calcium scor-
ing [4], establishing the feasibility of cross-disease modeling from
screening scans. More recently, large-scale foundation models have
extended this framework toward multitask learning across het-
erogeneous clinical endpoints. M3FM, trained on over 120,000 CT
scans with paired reports, achieved strong performance on lung
cancer risk prediction, coronary artery disease classification, and
CVD mortality [23]. Merlin further integrated volumetric CT with
EHR codes and reports to support a broad range of downstream
clinical tasks [3]. In parallel, Hamamci et al. introduced a generalist
3D CT foundation model aligning CT images with radiology text
via CT-CLIP and CT-CHAT, enabling zero-shot pulmonary finding
recognition and instruction-driven report generation [13]. Related
vision–language models such as MedCLIP [33], BioMedCLIP [36],
RadFM [34], and CheXagent [6] reflect the broader trend toward
multimodal pretraining in radiology, though most focus on 2D chest
radiographs. Despite this progress, existing cross-disease and mul-
titask models largely function as black boxes, offering only coarse
post-hoc attribution. In contrast, our framework explicitly models
pulmonary-to-cardiac reasoning through interpretable intermedi-
ate indicators, enabling transparent cross-disease inference while
jointly predicting lung cancer and cardiovascular risk from LDCT.

suboptimal due to the visual dominance of pulmonary structures
and the noise introduced by non-cardiac tissues. To address this,
we employ a Cardiac Localization Module designed to perform
anatomical disentanglement, isolating the heart to enable focused
feature learning.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Dataset and Preprocessing. All experiments were conducted on
the low-dose CT (LDCT) arm of the National Lung Screening
Trial (NLST) dataset [31], a large randomized study of lung can-
cer screening in high-risk populations. After quality control, the
final cohort comprised 32,136 LDCT scans from 10,395 subjects,
with each participant undergoing one to three annual scans ac-
quired using heterogeneous scanners and reconstruction kernels.
Following prior work [4], we considered two cardiovascular predic-
tion tasks: (1) CVD screening, identifying subjects who developed
cardiovascular events during follow-up, and (2) CVD mortality,
identifying deaths attributed to cardiovascular causes based on ICD
codes. Across the full cohort, the screening task included 6,339 pos-
itive and 25,797 negative cases, while the mortality task included
1,090 cardiovascular deaths and 31,046 non-CVD or censored cases.
Data were split at the subject level into 70%/10%/20% training, val-
idation, and test sets. All LDCT volumes were standardized for
consistent representation across modules. Additional details on
label definitions, subset statistics, and preprocessing are provided
in Appendix A.
Comparison Methods. We compared the proposed framework
against representative baselines spanning classical pipelines, deep
learning models, and recent foundation-model approaches. These
include AE+SVM [4], DeepCAC [35], KAMP-Net [12], and Tri2D-
Net [4], which represent established methods for cardiovascular
risk assessment from LDCT. We further included Sybil [21] as a
state-of-the-art lung cancer risk model, M3FM [23] as a large-scale
multitask CT foundation model, and CT-CLIP [13]–based baselines
evaluated under both frozen and fine-tuned visual encoder settings.
In addition, we evaluated variants instantiated with MedicalNet
backbones [5] and progressively integrated components to assess
the contribution of pulmonary perception, cardiac representation,
and cross-disease reasoning. Detailed experimental protocols and
implementation details are provided in Appendix C.
Implementation Details. We used a pretrained Sybil model [21]
for lung risk, a pretrained 3D RetinaNet-style detector [4] for cardiac
ROI localization, pretrained CT-CLIP [13] for pulmonary percep-
tion, and GPT-based agents for reasoning text that was encoded by
ClinicalBERT. Full training and hyperparameter details are provided
in Appendix C.
Evaluation Metrics. Model performance was evaluated using the
receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the correspond-
ing area under the curve (AUC), which quantifies overall discrimina-
tive ability across decision thresholds. For each task, 95% confidence
intervals (CIs) of the AUC were estimated via non-parametric boot-
strapping with 1,000 resamples. ROC curves are presented for visual
comparison, illustrating sensitivity–specificity trade-offs across
competing methods.
4.2
Experimental Results
Comparison with state-of-the-art methods. Table 1 summarizes
the performance of our proposed framework against representative
state-of-the-art approaches on both CVD Screening and CVD
Mortality tasks. Traditional pipelines such as AE+SVM and early
CNN-based models (DeepCAC, KAMP-Net) exhibit limited discrim-
ination, with AUCs below 0.76 for screening and 0.70 for mortality.
These results suggest that handcrafted or purely image-based repre-
sentations capture only partial cardiovascular information. Tri2D-
Net improves performance by aggregating multi-view features,
achieving an AUC of 0.871 for screening and 0.768 for mortality,
but remains restricted by the absence of multi-modal contextual
reasoning. More advanced models such as Sybil and M3FM, which
employ large-scale pretraining and vision–language integration,
further enhance discrimination, reaching AUCs of 0.880–0.892 for
screening and 0.794–0.816 for mortality.
Our proposed explainable cross-disease reasoning framework sur-
passes all competing methods across both tasks. Using a 3D ResNet-
50 backbone, the model achieves an AUC of 0.919 [0.910–0.929]
for CVD screening and 0.824 [0.797–0.850] for CVD mortality pre-
diction, outperforming the strongest baseline (M3FM) by absolute
gains of +2.7 and +0.8 points, respectively. Performance remains
stable across deeper MedicalNet variants (ResNet-101/152/200), in-
dicating robustness to network depth and model capacity. Notably,
these improvements are achieved without explicit coronary calcium
scoring or handcrafted cardiac priors, underscoring the advantage
of integrating pulmonary findings, cardiac subvolume encoding,
and knowledge-guided indicator reasoning into a unified end-to-
end architecture.
Overall, these results validate that our proposed framework effec-
tively leverages cross-disease signals embedded in LDCT to achieve
state-of-the-art discrimination while maintaining physiologically
grounded interpretability through explicit pulmonary-to-cardiac
reasoning.

5
Conclusion
We proposed an Explainable Cross-Disease Reasoning Framework for
integrated assessment of pulmonary and cardiovascular risk from
low-dose chest CT (LDCT). The framework combines pulmonary-
to-cardiac reasoning, localized cardiac feature encoding, and multi-
modal fusion to model the physiological interactions between the
lung and heart. Extensive evaluation on a large-scale LDCT cohort
shows consistent improvements over existing approaches while
maintaining strong interpretability. Beyond predictive accuracy,
the reasoning architecture provides clinically coherent explana-
tions that clarify how pulmonary abnormalities contribute to car-
diovascular stress, enhancing transparency aligned with medical
reasoning. More broadly, this study highlights the potential of op-
portunistic LDCT analysis, which enables concurrent evaluation of
multiple disease risks from a single scan, and establishes a founda-
tion for transparent, generalizable, and physiologically grounded
AI systems that can support future cardiopulmonary screening and
longitudinal health monitoring.

mechanisms.

Implementation Details
Pulmonary risk estimation. Pulmonary risk scores were gener-
ated using a pretrained Sybil model, which was kept frozen and
used in inference-only mode. For each LDCT volume, the model
produced a scalar lung cancer risk estimate ˆ𝑦lung, which was used
as an auxiliary input to downstream cardiovascular prediction. No
gradients were propagated through the lung risk predictor.
Pulmonary perception and cardiac encoding. Pulmonary visual
representations were extracted using CT-CLIP, with the visual en-
coder either frozen or fine-tuned depending on the experimental
setting. Cardiac regions were localized using a 3D RetinaNet-style
detector, following prior work, and cropped subvolumes were en-
coded using 3D MedicalNet backbones (ResNet-50/101/152/200).
All 3D encoders operated on isotropically resampled volumes with
fixed input resolution.
Reasoning agent and text encoding. Pulmonary-to-cardiac rea-
soning text was generated by GPT-based reasoning agents using
structured prompts detailed in Appendix B. Unless otherwise spec-
ified, the reasoning agent was instantiated with GPT-5-nano via
API-based deployment. Generation used greedy decoding with a
temperature set to 0.0 to ensure deterministic and reproducible
outputs. The generated reasoning text was encoded using Clinical-
BERT, and the text encoder was kept frozen during training.
Training and optimization. All trainable components were opti-
mized end-to-end using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1×10−4 and a batch size of 4. Binary cross-entropy loss was used for
both CVD screening and CVD mortality tasks. Early stopping was
applied based on validation AUC with a patience of 10 epochs. All
experiments were conducted using identical data splits to ensure
fair comparison across methods.
Inference and evaluation. During inference, pulmonary risk esti-
mation, cardiac ROI extraction, and reasoning text generation were
performed sequentially, followed by a single forward pass through
the cardiovascular prediction network. Model performance was
evaluated using ROC-AUC. Confidence intervals (95%) were esti-
mated via non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples at
the subject level.
