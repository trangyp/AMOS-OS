Aspiration-based Perturbed Learning Automata in Games with
Noisy Utility Measurements. Part A: Stochastic Stability in
Non-Zero-Sum Games∗
Georgios Chasparis†
November 25th, 2025
Abstract
Reinforcement-based learning has attracted considerable attention both in modeling human
behavior as well as in engineering, for designing measurement- or payoff-based optimization
schemes. Such learning schemes exhibit several advantages, especially in relation to filtering out
noisy observations. However, they may exhibit several limitations when applied in a distributed
setup.
In multi-player weakly-acyclic games, and when each player applies an independent
copy of the learning dynamics, convergence to (usually desirable) pure Nash equilibria cannot
be guaranteed. Prior work has only focused on a small class of games, namely potential and
coordination games.
To address this main limitation, this paper introduces a novel payoff-
based learning scheme for distributed optimization, namely aspiration-based perturbed learning
automata (APLA). In this class of dynamics, and contrary to standard reinforcement-based
learning schemes, each player’s probability distribution for selecting actions is reinforced both
by repeated selection and an aspiration factor that captures the player’s satisfaction level. We
provide a stochastic stability analysis of APLA in multi-player positive-utility games under the
presence of noisy observations. This is the first part of the paper that characterizes stochastic
stability in generic non-zero-sum games by establishing equivalence of the induced infinite-
dimensional Markov chain with a finite dimensional one. In the second part, stochastic stability
is further specialized to weakly acyclic games.
Keywords: reinforcement learning, multi-agent systems, distributed optimization, noisy obser-
vations, non-zero-sum games

optimality does not necessarily guarantee global optimality. The problem becomes even more chal-

Given such challenges in multi-agent optimization problems, there have been several efforts
towards the design of distributed payoff-based learning dynamics, where the objective is how agents
should learn and optimize independently when receiving only measurements of their own utility
while also guaranteeing efficiency at the group level. Naturally, such questions could be addressed
by utilizing concepts relevant to repeatedly played strategic-form games and evolutionary game
theory [44, 34]. A rather common objective is then to derive conditions under which convergence
to efficient Nash equilibria can be achieved, i.e., locally stable outcomes that also maximize a
centralized objective.
One class of payoff-based learning dynamics that has been utilized for convergence to (efficient)
Nash equilibria is reinforcement-based learning. It may appear under alternative forms, including
discrete-time replicator dynamics [2], learning automata [41, 29] or approximate policy iteration or
Q-learning [20]. It is highly attractive to several engineering applications, since agents do not need
to know neither the actions of other agents, nor their utility function. For example, it has been
utilized for system identification and pattern recognition [40], distributed network formation and
resource-allocation problems [9]. One of the attractive elements of such type of learning dynamics
is the fact that can be used in stochastic environments when utility measurements or the learning
dynamics itself are perturbed.
In reinforcement-based learning, deriving conditions for convergence to Nash equilibria may not
be a trivial task, especially in the case of large number of agents. In particular and in the context of
coordination games, cf., [8], two main difficulties arise: a) excluding convergence to pure strategies
that are not Nash equilibria, and b) excluding convergence to mixed strategy profiles. Recent work
by the author in perturbed learning automata (PLA) [10], overcame these limitations by directly
characterizing the stochastically stable states of the induced Markov chain (independently of the
number of players or actions). This type of analysis allowed for acquiring convergence guarantees in
multi-player coordination games (thus, extending previous results in reinforcement-based learning
restricted only to potential games).
However, the convergence guarantees presented in [10] apply to games with (still) strict struc-
tural properties, namely the coordination property. To this end, we present a class of dynamics,
namely aspiration-based perturbed learning automata (briefly, APLA). While in standard reinforcement-
based learning, actions are reinforced by repeated selection and proportionally to the received re-
ward, in APLA, the reinforcement level is also scaled by agent’s own satisfaction level. For this
class of reinforcement-based learning, and in the first part of this paper, we provide convergence
guarantees (in distribution) for any non-zero-sum strategic-form game satisfying the positive util-
ity property and independently of the number of players and actions. Furthermore, the presented
analysis is extended to also cover the possibility of uniformly bounded noise perturbations in the
utilities of the players. The presented analysis is the basis for the follow-up characterization of the
stochastically stable states in weakly-acyclic games in the second part of this study (Part B) [11].
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reinforcement-based/learning-automata scheme that
addresses convergence in weakly-acyclic games and under noisy observations.

2.2
Motivation
In comparison with standard (variable-structure) learning automata learning schemes, cf., [29],
there are two forms of reinforcement terms employed here, namely repeated selection and aspiration
factor.
In standard repeated selection with positive rewards, e.g., the PLA dynamics [10], the strategies
of the selected actions are always reinforced proportionally to the reward received.
The main
motivation of considering this type of dynamics as a basis of the proposed scheme is the use of
strategies in the selection of actions, instead of making decisions directly based on the received
rewards. The selection of actions through strategies provides an indirect filtering mechanism of
6


## Page 7


possibly perturbed or noisy observations, while it does not allow for rapid changes in actions. In
this paper, this filtering advantage will become evident when considering noisy observations.
We may however argue that standard reinforcement-based learning schemes that are using the
currently received reward as the reinforcement term, exploit only a rather myopic information of the
reward received. By introducing an aspiration or satisficing term, through the aspiration factor ϕi,
i = 1, ..., n, we also incorporate the most recent reward history into the decision making process. For
example, in Figure 2(a), and when an unsatisfactory action is selected, the corresponding strategy
increases very slowly, providing the opportunity for additional exploration time. As we shall see in
both parts of this paper, this provides additional possibilities in terms of influencing the asymptotic
stability properties of the dynamics.
3
Related work and contributions
3.1
Related work
In this section, we provide a short overview of alternative payoff-based learning schemes specifically
designed for repeatedly-played strategic-form games with a finite set of actions and a fixed utility
function for each player. We have identified four main classes of payoff-based dynamics under such
structural assumptions, namely discrete-time replicator dynamics, learning automata, Q-learning
(or more generally approximate policy iteration), and aspiration-based learning. Note that payoff-
based learning has also been applied to static games with continuous action sets, e.g., extremum-
seeking control [15, 43] or actor-critic reinforcement learning [31]. The focus here instead is only
on finite action sets.
Discrete-time replicator dynamics
A type of learning dynamics which is quite closely related to PLA is the discrete-time version of
replicator dynamics (cf., [18]). In [10], rather broad presentation of similar types of dynamics was
presented in the context of discrete replicator dynamics, including the models by Arthur [2], Hopkins
and Posch [19], the urn process of Erev-Roth [14], the Cross’ model of [4] and its modification
presented by Leslie in [22].
In PLA, introduced in [9], the perturbation factor λ > 0 was first introduced in the selection
of actions. It serves as an equilibrium selection mechanism, since it excludes convergence to action
profiles that are not Nash equilibria (briefly, non-Nash action profiles) [9]. It resolved one of the
main issues of discrete-time replicator dynamics, that is the positive probability of convergence to
non-Nash action profiles.
Although excluding convergence to non-Nash action profiles can be guaranteed by sufficiently
small λ > 0, establishing convergence to action profiles that are Nash equilibria (pure Nash equi-
libria) may still be an issue (given that mixed Nash equilibria may also exist). However, and as
shown in [13], convergence to pure Nash equilibria can be guaranteed only under strong conditions
in the utility function. For example, as shown in [13, Proposition 8], it requires a) the existence of
a potential function, and b) conditions over the Jacobian matrix of the potential function. Even if
a potential function does exist, verifying conditions (b) is practically infeasible for games of more
than 2 players [13].
In order to overcome these analytical difficulties, recent work by the author [10] has investigated
stochastic stability of PLA for λ > 0 and fixed step size ϵ > 0.
It has been shown that in
7


## Page 8


the context of coordination games of any number of players and actions, the set of pure Nash
equilibria is stochastically stable. The importance lies on the fact that verifying that a game satisfies
the coordination property is straightforward in comparison with verifying existence of a potential
function. However, convergence is restricted to the set of Nash equilibria, without specifying further
their relative frequency of appearance as time increases.
Learning automata
Learning automata, as first introduced by [41], have been used for the control of complex systems
due to their simple structure and low computational complexity (cf., [29, Chapter 1]). Variable-
structure stochastic automata may incorporate a form of reinforcement of favorable actions, similarly
to the discrete-time replicator dynamics discussed earlier. An example is the linear reward-inaction
scheme [29, Chapter 4], which bears similarities with the PLA dynamics. However, it does not
incorporate any perturbation in the selection of actions.
Analysis of learning automata in games has been restricted to zero-sum and identical-interest
games [29, 35]. In identical interest games, convergence analysis has been derived only for small
number of players and actions, due to the difficulty in deriving conditions for absolute monotonicity
(cf., [29, Definition 8.1]). Similar are the results presented in [35].
The property of absolute monotonicity is closely related to the existence of a potential function,
as in the case of potential games [28]. Similarly to the discrete-time replicator dynamics, conver-
gence to non-Nash action profiles cannot be excluded when the step-size sequence is constant, even
if the utility function satisfies ui(α) ∈[0, 1]. The behavior under decreasing step-size is different
as [13, Proposition 2] has shown. Furthermore, deriving conditions for excluding convergence to
mixed strategy profiles in coordination games continues to be an issue, as in discrete-time replicator
dynamics.
Recognizing these issues, reference [42] introduced a class of linear reward-inaction schemes in
combination with a coordinated exploration phase so that convergence to the efficient (pure) Nash
equilibrium is achieved. However, coordination of the exploration phase requires communication
between the players, which may not be always desirable.
Q-learning
Similar questions also arise in alternative reinforcement-based learning formulations, such as policy-
iteration approximate dynamic programming or Q-learning. Under Q-learning, players keep track
of the discounted running average reward received by each action, based on which optimal decisions
are made (see, e.g., [23]). Convergence to Nash equilibria can be accomplished under a stronger set
of assumptions, which increases the computational complexity of the dynamics. For example, in
the Nash-Q learning algorithm of [20], it is indirectly assumed that agents need to have full access
to the joint action space and the rewards received by other agents.
More recently, reference [6] introduced a Q-learning scheme in combination with either adaptive
play or better-reply dynamics in order to attain convergence to Nash equilibria in potential games
[28] or weakly-acyclic games. However, this form of dynamics requires that each player observes
the actions selected by the other players, since a Q-value needs to be assigned to each joint action.
Weaker conditions in such side information has also been considered by [39] for attaining conver-
gence to Nash equilibria. Two types of side information have been explored, namely local/global
utility split and knowledge of the payoff of unplayed actions.

## Page 9


When the evaluation of the Q-values is totally independent and no side information is available,
as in the individual Q-learning of [23], then convergence to Nash equilibria has been shown only
for 2-player zero-sum games and 2-player partnership games with countably many Nash equilibria.
Currently, there exist no convergence results in multi-player games. To overcome this deficiency of
Q-learning, in the context of stochastic dynamic games, reference [1] employs an additional feature
(motivated by [26]), namely synchronized exploration phases. In any such exploration phase, all
agents use constant policies, something that allows for an accurate computation of the optimal Q-
factors. The introduction of such common exploration phases may not however be always desirable,
under a fully distributed framework pursued here.
Aspiration-based learning
Aspiration and satisficing is rather prominent in social learning systems, starting with the seminal
work of Simon [37]. The notion of satisficing implies that individuals are searching for alternatives
until an acceptability threshold is satisfied, which is based on the assumption of bounded rational-
ity. This have further been explored in the analysis of social systems thereafter, e.g., the work by
[25] that explores the role of aspiration in a self-reinforcing cooperative equilibrium in social dilem-
mas. Also, it appears in several computational social and game-theoretic models, e.g., [30, 38], for
explaining emergence of cooperation in social systems.
Recently, there have been several attempts to establish convergence to Nash equilibria through
alternative payoff-based learning dynamics that bear elements of aspiration.
Examples include
the benchmark-based dynamics of [26] for convergence to Nash equilibria in weakly-acyclic games,
the trial-and-error learning [44] for convergence to Nash equilibria in generic games, the mood-
based dynamics of [27] for maximizing welfare in generic games and the aspiration learning in [8]
for convergence to efficient outcomes in coordination games. We will refer to such approaches as
aspiration-based learning. For these types of dynamics, convergence to Nash equilibria or efficient
outcomes can be established without requiring any strong monotonicity properties (as in the multi-
player weakly-acyclic games in [26]).
The case of noisy utility measurements, which are present in many engineering applications,
has not currently been investigated in aspiration-based learning. The only exception is reference
[26], under benchmark-based dynamics, where (synchronized) exploration phases are introduced
through which each agent plays a fixed action for the duration of the exploration phase. Again such
synchronized exploration phases may not always be desirable under a fully distributed framework.
3.2
Contributions
This paper presents a novel learning automata scheme based on PLA [10] that incorporates a form
of aspiration into the decision making process. We focus on this class of dynamics, as compared to
Q-learning or aspiration-based learning, because we would like to exploit a) its filtering capabilities
in noisy environments (since decisions are taken based on strategies instead of just utilities as in
aspiration-based learning), and b) its distributed nature (thus avoiding synchronized exploration
phases as in some Q-learning and aspiration-based schemes).
A detailed description of the contributions is as follows:
(A-C1) Advancing convergence guarantees in reinforcement-based learning. Convergence analysis of
reinforcement-based learning are currently restricted to games of small number of players
and under strong structural assumptions (e.g., the existence of a potential function). See,

for example, the discussion on discrete-time replicator dynamics or learning automata in
[13]. Recently, in the context of perturbed learning automata [10], the author has provided
weak-convergence guarantees in coordination games with no restriction in the number of play-
ers/actions. In this first part of the paper, we present a general stochastic stability analysis
for non-zero-sum games when the aspiration factor is also incorporated into the strategy up-
dates. The analysis is based on the derivation of a finite-dimensional approximation of the
infinite-dimensional induced Markov chain of the dynamics.
(A-C2) Noisy observations is a necessary consideration in engineering applications, but naturally
has also been part of human observations in game theoretic literature (see, e.g., the con-
siderations of noise in fictitious-play dynamics [3, 24]). In fact, noisy observations can be
“naturally handled” through reinforcement-based learning due to the indirect filtering of the
observation signals (e.g., through the formulation of the strategy-vector in disrete-time repli-
cator dynamics, as in the dynamics of Algorithm 1, or through the formulation of the Q
factors in Q-learning). In this paper (both part A and B), the convergence results of the first
contribution (A-C1) are also investigated under the presence of noisy observations.
In the second part of this study [11], the analysis is further expanded to address convergence
guarantees in the family of weakly-acyclic games. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result
in the context of learning-automata that addresses convergence in large strategic-form games in
the presence of noisy observations, which significantly increases the utility of this type of dynamics
in engineering applications. Furthermore, the integration of a form of aspiration and its impact
on the convergence properties may be of independent interest for the computational social models
literature.
This paper is an extension over an earlier version appeared in [7], which only focused on two-
player games.

Although Nash equilibria are stochastically
stable in coordination games under perturbed learning automata, not all Nash equilibria may be
desirable. An example may be drawn from the classical Stag-Hunt coordination game of Table 1.
In this game, the first player selects the row of the payoff matrix and the second player selects
the column.
The first element of the selected entry determines the reward of the row player,
and the second element determines the reward of the column player. This game has two pure
Nash equilibria, which correspond to the symmetric plays (A, A) and (B, B). Ideally, we would
prefer that agents eventually learn to play (A, A) which corresponds to the payoff-dominant (or
Pareto-efficient) equilibrium. However, existing results in perturbed learning automata [9, 13, 10]
demonstrate that (B, B) may prevail asymptotically with positive probability. The reason lies in
the cost that an agent experiences when the other agent deviates from a Nash equilibrium, which
captures the notion of risk dominance (cf., [16]). In fact, (B, B) is the risk-dominant equilibrium

7
Conclusions
In this first part of our study, we proposed and analyzed a novel class of payoff-based learning
dynamics, termed Aspiration-based Perturbed Learning Automata (APLA), designed to overcome
key limitations of classical reinforcement-based schemes in distributed multi-agent environments.
By incorporating an aspiration mechanism into the standard perturbed learning automata frame-
work, APLA allows agents to reinforce actions not only through limited selection, but also relative
to evolving satisfaction levels (i.e., how satisficing is the utility of the current action compared to
the most recent average utility).
The stochastic stability properties of APLA have been established in the context of general non-
zero-sum games with positive utilities, under the presence of noise and distributed implementation.
A central contribution of this part lies in showing that, despite the infinite-dimensional nature of
the process, the long-term behavior of the induced Markov chain admits a reduction to a finite-state
representation. This reduction enables a tractable analysis of the limiting behavior and supports
further theoretical developments.
While the current results provide general convergence guarantees in arbitrary positive-utility
games, the second part of the paper will focus on a more refined analysis in the setting of weakly
acyclic games, where the structure allows for stronger results, including convergence to payoff-
dominant Nash equilibria under appropriate conditions.
