Abstract
We propose MedBayes-Lite, a lightweight Bayesian enhancement for transformer-based clinical
language models designed to produce reliable, uncertainty-aware predictions. Although transformers
show strong potential for clinical decision support, they remain prone to overconfidence, espe-
cially in ambiguous medical cases where calibrated uncertainty is critical. MedBayes-Lite embeds
uncertainty quantification directly into existing transformer pipelines without any retraining or
architectural rewiring, adding no new trainable parameters. The framework integrates three compo-
nents: (i) Bayesian Embedding Calibration using Monte Carlo dropout for epistemic uncertainty, (ii)
Uncertainty-Weighted Attention that marginalizes over token reliability, and (iii) Confidence-Guided
Decision Shaping inspired by clinical risk minimization. Across biomedical QA and clinical prediction
benchmarks (MedQA, PubMedQA, MIMIC-III), MedBayes-Lite consistently improves calibration
and trustworthiness, reducing overconfidence by 32–48%. In simulated clinical settings, the frame-
work reduces harmful overconfidence by flagging uncertain predictions for human review, improving
risk-aware decision support under distribution shift. For closed API models (e.g., GPT-class systems),
MedBayes-Lite operates in an API-compatible mode using sampling-based predictive uncertainty
and confidence-guided abstention; full embedding- and attention-level uncertainty propagation is
evaluated only for open-weight transformer models with internal access.

Introduction
Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used in clinical workflows as
decision support tools [1] [2], assisting with (i) symptom triage [3] and preliminary differential diagnosis
[4], (ii) clinical note drafting and summarization [5], (iii) medication reconciliation and dosing checks [6],
(iv) retrieval and synthesis of guideline-based recommendations [7], and (v) patient-facing communication
such as discharge instructions and follow-up planning [8–12]. As these tools move closer to routine
general practice (GP) usage, a critical safety gap becomes more salient. Many deployments remain
weakly audited and are often treated as deterministic answer engines. When the model is mis-specified,
the input evidence is incomplete, or the case is out of distribution, such un-audited use can be dangerous,
particularly when outputs provide no explicit notation of uncertainty. In high-stakes settings such as
triage, differential diagnosis, and medication dosing, even a single confidently incorrect recommendation
can cascade into serious harm [13,14]. Accordingly, the central risk is not only factual error but also the
lack of trustworthy mechanisms for communicating and managing uncertainty in high-stakes clinical AI
settings [15].
Clinical decision-making is inherently probabilistic because major sources of uncertainty are unavoid-
able. Concretely, uncertainty arises from domain-specific factors such as: (i) measurement noise and

physiological variability, including imprecise vitals, lab error, and temporal fluctuation, (ii) incomplete
or missing evidence, including unobserved comorbidities, unavailable tests, and sparse history, (iii)
ambiguity and overlap in symptom to disease mappings, including similar presentations across condi-
tions, (iv) conflicting evidence across modalities or time, including discordant labs versus symptoms
and evolving trajectories, and (v) documentation and communication uncertainty, including noisy
notes, patient self-reported symptoms, and other process noises. In Bayesian statistics, these uncertainties
are typically organized into two classes. The first is aleatoric uncertainty, which reflects irreducible
randomness or noise in observations and outcomes. The second is epistemic uncertainty, which reflects
model and knowledge uncertainty due to limited data, domain shift, or mis-specification [16,17]. Clini-
cians operationalize these signals by slowing down, ordering additional tests, and escalating to expert
consultation. Current LLMs, however, rarely represent these uncertainty sources explicitly and lack
principled mechanisms for uncertainty propagation, which is central to Bayesian posterior predictive
inference, across multi-step reasoning that culminates in a final recommendation. As a result, they may
collapse weak, missing, or contradictory evidence into overconfident outputs, increasing diagnostic error
risk and automation bias [18,19].
Existing uncertainty quantification (UQ) techniques remain limited for clinical LLM deployment. Broadly,
uncertainty can be introduced in two ways. The first is post-training adjustment, such as post-hoc
calibration, which rescales reported confidence but does not alter the model’s internal inference and
can degrade under distribution shift. The second is architectural uncertainty modeling, such as
ensembles or Bayesian neural network variants, which more directly targets epistemic uncertainty
but is often computationally prohibitive for real-time clinical workflows. Recent Bayesian variants of
transformers [20] aim to capture epistemic uncertainty with lower overhead, yet many apply stochasticity
only to isolated components such as the embedding layer or output head. Consequently, uncertainty is
not consistently represented within attention and intermediate reasoning computations, and confidence
can remain miscalibrated precisely where evidence aggregation and multi-step inference occur.
Clinical AI requires uncertainty to be integrated throughout the inference process, so that confidence
outputs capture both the model’s knowledge boundaries (epistemic uncertainty) and the strength and
consistency of the input data (aleatoric uncertainty), rather than relying on a surface-level adjustment
applied post-prediction.
To address this gap, we introduce MedBayes-Lite, a deployment-oriented Bayesian augmentation for
transformer-based clinical language models. When internal transformer tensors are accessible (open-
weight models), MedBayes-Lite propagates uncertainty through (i) Bayesian embedding calibration,
(ii) uncertainty-weighted attention, and (iii) confidence-guided decision shaping, without retraining or
adding trainable parameters. For closed API models where internal states are not exposed, MedBayes-
Lite operates in an API-compatible mode that estimates predictive uncertainty from repeated sampled
generations and applies the same confidence-guided abstention mechanism; attention- and embedding-
level interventions are not performed in this setting.
By integrating Bayesian reasoning throughout the full inference procedure, MedBayes-Lite narrows the
gap between academic UQ and the practical demands of clinical safety. It moves clinical LLMs toward a
more trustworthy paradigm, one where predictions are not merely accurate, but aware of their limits, and
aligned with the real-world decision-making principles that clinicians rely on every day.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We introduce MedBayes-Lite, a plug-in Bayesian augmentation for transformer-based clinical
prediction models that injects uncertainty estimation into embeddings, attention, and prediction
stages while preserving compatibility with existing pretrained checkpoints. We also provide an
explicit complexity and overhead characterization, including latency and GPU memory costs.
• We propose two clinically grounded evaluation metrics, the Clinical Uncertainty Score (CUS)

Related Work
The drive toward developing trustworthy clinical AI has highlighted the need for effective UQ. Accuracy
alone is often insufficient in healthcare settings, where models must also signal when their predictions
may be unreliable. The landscape of UQ methods for LLMs is broad, spanning foundational Bayesian
techniques to clinical risk–based evaluation frameworks. However, the diversity of these approaches
makes it challenging to determine which are most suitable for real-world deployment. This section
synthesizes these methods to clarify how our work fits within and contributes to this space.
2.1
Foundational Bayesian Methods in Deep Learning
The quest to develop deep learning models that can represent their own uncertainty gained momentum
with the work of Gal and Ghahramani [21]. They showed that dropout training can be interpreted
as approximate Bayesian inference, enabling uncertainty estimation via Monte Carlo (MC) dropout
without changing the underlying architecture. At inference time, MC dropout requires multiple stochastic
forward passes, which can increase latency unless parallelized. MC dropout has also been applied
to transformer-based models for uncertainty estimation, with some MC-dropout-based scores used to
estimate epistemic uncertainty [22]. However, for clinical decision support, uncertainty is often reported
only at the predictive output and does not by itself incorporate contextual or risk-aware calibration, while
requiring multiple stochastic forward passes at inference can reduce efficiency and scalability.
Prior work in uncertainty quantification for deep health diagnostics has often relied on Softmax confidence
or predictive entropy, but these output-level scores do not explicitly capture model uncertainty and can
remain overconfident, particularly when inputs deviate from the training distribution [23]. Bayesian
approaches such as Monte Carlo Dropout and deep ensembles have been explored to better capture
model uncertainty, but they typically increase inference-time computation due to repeated stochastic
forward passes or multiple models. Evidential deep learning (EDL) offers a single-pass alternative by
predicting a Dirichlet distribution over class probabilities for simultaneous prediction and uncertainty
estimation. However, much of the EDL literature has been developed and evaluated on standard benchmark
settings [24] [25], while the behavior of uncertainty estimates under class imbalance, especially for
minority classes in realistic medical data, remains less systematically studied.

2.2
Post-Hoc Calibration and Their Limitations
A commonly used approach to improve confidence reliability is post-hoc calibration, which applies a prob-
abilistic correction after training using a held-out validation set and typically assumes train/validation/test
are drawn from the same distribution [26]. For multiclass models, Temperature Scaling (TS) rescales
the logit vector with a single scalar temperature parameter before softmax, adjusting confidence without
changing the predicted class [26]. In contrast, Isotonic Regression (IR) is a widely used non-parametric
alternative that learns a monotone, piecewise-constant mapping from uncalibrated scores to calibrated
probabilities [27]. Because these methods are fitted on a validation set under an in-distribution protocol,
their calibration quality is coupled to the calibration data and can degrade when evaluation conditions
shift [26]. In addition to these, Ye et al. [28] demonstrate that output-level probability shaping, such as
adjusting the softmax temperature, can substantially change the predicted probability distribution without
changing the model’s accuracy. In particular, low temperatures can induce overly sharp probabilities,
making the model appear overconfident even though predictive correctness is unchanged. They further
show that conformal prediction is less sensitive to this effect, yielding more conservative uncertainty
behavior when temperature-based probabilities become artificially confident.
2.3
Clinical UQ and Risk-Aware Decision Making
In healthcare, overconfident model outputs can have meaningful clinical consequences, which has
motivated domain-specific work on reliability, interpretability, and human oversight. Salvi et al. [18]
argue that Explainable AI alone is insufficient to guarantee trustworthy behavior and propose integrating
Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) with XAI to improve reliability and reduce over-reliance in clinical
workflows. Complementary to this direction, Catak and Kuzlu introduce a geometric UQ approach for
LLMs that measures dispersion in response-embedding space via convex hull analysis, showing that
uncertainty varies with prompt complexity and generation temperature [19]. Together, these perspectives
highlight an emerging shift from treating uncertainty as a reporting artifact toward using uncertainty
signals to support cautious decision support and more robust human-AI collaboration, while fully
integrating uncertainty into LLM reasoning and generation remains an open challenge.
2.4
Benchmarking and Model Ensembles
For uncertainty quantification benchmarking, Deep Ensembles [29] and Stochastic Weight Averaging
Gaussian (SWAG) [30] are widely used, high-fidelity reference methods. Deep Ensembles estimate
predictive uncertainty by averaging predictions from multiple independently trained networks, which
can improve robustness and calibration. However, for an ensemble of size K, parameter storage, model
memory, training cost, and inference cost all scale approximately linearly with K, since the method
requires maintaining and evaluating multiple independently trained models [29]. SWAG instead constructs
an approximate posterior over weights from the SGD trajectory and performs Bayesian model averaging
by sampling multiple weight vectors at test time and averaging predictions, so inference cost increases
with the number of posterior samples used [30].
In clinical informatics settings with strict latency or memory constraints, these multi-model or multi-
sample procedures can be difficult to deploy at scale, motivating lightweight uncertainty estimation
methods that preserve reliability and transparency while reducing ensemble-level overhead.

2.5
Positioning MedBayes-Lite
The current landscape of UQ for clinical LLMs reveals a clear trade-off between efficiency and the depth
of uncertainty integration. Existing approaches generally fall into three categories: (i) superficial post-hoc
calibration techniques that adjust model confidence without addressing internal uncertainty, (ii) computa-
tionally expensive ensemble methods that require multiple forward passes, and (iii) fragmented Bayesian
implementations that only partially propagate uncertainty across layers. MedBayes-Lite is designed to
bridge these gaps by introducing a lightweight yet fully integrated Bayesian framework. Unlike post-hoc
calibration methods, it embeds Bayesian reasoning directly into the transformer architecture, enabling
uncertainty to propagate through embeddings, attention mechanisms, and decision layers. In contrast
to resource-intensive ensemble methods, it achieves this with less than 3% additional computational
overhead, providing a practical balance between interpretability and efficiency.

aware, computationally tractable clinical reasoning. Table 1 summarizes the comparative positioning

3
MedBayes-Lite Framework
MedBayes-Lite is a post-training, inference-time uncertainty quantification framework for pretrained
transformer models. Instead of modifying the training procedure or introducing additional learnable
parameters, the framework estimates predictive uncertainty through stochastic inference and incorporates
these signals directly into the inference pipeline of the model. This design enables uncertainty-aware
prediction without retraining the backbone architecture, which is particularly advantageous in safety-
critical domains such as clinical decision support where retraining large pretrained models may be
computationally or operationally impractical.
3.1
Framework Overview
Figure 1 illustrates the overall design of MedBayes-Lite. The framework operates through three co-
ordinated stages that progressively integrate uncertainty information into the inference process. First,
stochastic forward passes are performed using Monte Carlo dropout in order to estimate variability in the
model’s internal representations. This variability provides a representation-level uncertainty signal that
reflects instability in how the model encodes the input.
Second, these uncertainty estimates are incorporated into the self-attention mechanism. Tokens associated

with higher representation uncertainty are downweighted during contextual aggregation, reducing the
influence of unreliable evidence while preserving informative contextual signals.
Third, the predictive distribution produced by the model is converted into a normalized confidence
score derived from predictive entropy. This confidence score determines whether a prediction should be
accepted or deferred. Predictions with insufficient confidence can therefore be flagged for human review,
enabling a risk-aware decision process.
Taken together, these stages allow uncertainty estimated from stochastic internal representations to
influence both contextual aggregation and final decision making during inference, without modifying the
underlying training procedure of the pretrained transformer model.

3.2
Implementation Under Different Model Access Settings
The implementation of MedBayes-Lite depends on the level of access to internal transformer components,
including embeddings, attention logits or weights, and inference-time dropout control. Accordingly, two
implementation settings are considered.
(A) Full-stack setting (open-weight models).
For open-weight transformer models where inference-time dropout can be enabled and internal tensors
are accessible, the full framework is implemented. This includes Bayesian embedding calibration via
MC dropout, uncertainty-adjusted attention through uncertainty-penalized attention normalization, and
confidence-guided decision shaping through an abstention mechanism. This setting is evaluated using
GPT-2, LLaMA-2, and encoder-based biomedical transformers such as BioBERT and ClinicalBERT,
where embeddings and attention scores can be accessed directly.
(B) API-compatible setting (closed models).
For closed models accessible only through APIs (e.g., GPT-4-class systems), internal embeddings, hidden
states, and attention tensors are not exposed to the user. In this setting, MedBayes-Lite cannot modify
intermediate attention computations or propagate uncertainty through internal layers. Instead, uncertainty
6


## Page 7


is estimated at the output level by collecting multiple stochastic generations using temperature sampling
and constructing an empirical predictive distribution over the resulting outputs. Confidence is then
computed from this empirical distribution using entropy-based and self-consistency statistics, after which
the same confidence-guided abstention rule is applied.
Consequently, results reported for GPT-4-class models correspond to this API-compatible configuration
and reflect output-level predictive uncertainty estimation rather than full-stack uncertainty propagation
through the internal transformer architecture.
This distinction is important for interpreting the framework correctly. Full-stack uncertainty propagation
applies only when internal transformer states are accessible. Under API-only access, the framework
remains applicable but is limited to uncertainty estimation and abstention based on observable output
distributions.

The confidence score C(p) is a monotone trans-

model’s predictive distribution does not support a sufficiently concentrated decision.

3.3.5
Layer-wise Uncertainty Propagation
We adopt a layer-wise view of uncertainty propagation to describe how uncertainty in intermediate
representations influences downstream attention and decision layers. In the present framework, this prop-
agation is modeled operationally rather than through a closed-form hierarchical variance decomposition.
Specifically, token-level uncertainty is approximated from the Monte Carlo dropout variability of the
corresponding embeddings, and these uncertainty estimates are subsequently incorporated into attention
scoring and confidence-guided decision shaping.
This perspective is motivated by the structure of transformer architectures, in which predictions are
formed through a sequence of nonlinear transformations of intermediate representations. Traditional
Bayesian deep learning often estimates uncertainty only at the output layer (for example by Bayesianizing
the final layer) or through uncertainty over model parameters. Such approaches provide limited visibility
into how uncertainty evolves within the network. By contrast, a layer-wise interpretation is useful for
describing how uncertainty estimated from stochastic representations can influence subsequent contextual
aggregation and final decision making.
Under this view, uncertainty is not treated as a single quantity appearing only at the final prediction layer.
Instead, it is propagated implicitly through the computation: stochasticity in intermediate representations
induces variation in token-level uncertainty estimates; these estimates alter the attention logits through
the uncertainty penalty; and the resulting predictive distribution is subsequently used to compute the
confidence score that governs abstention. Accordingly, uncertainty influences the model at multiple
stages of the inference pipeline, even though the method does not assume an exact additive variance
decomposition across layers.
Discussion. The layer-wise perspective above provides an interpretive framework for understanding how
uncertainty estimated from stochastic representations affects later computations in MedBayes-Lite. In
particular, it clarifies that the method uses uncertainty first as a representation-level signal, then as an
attention-level adjustment, and finally as part of the confidence-based decision rule. This interpretation is
consistent with the procedural description in Algorithm 1.

3.4
Algorithmic Summary
Algorithm 1 summarizes the operational workflow of MedBayes-Lite. Given an input sequence, the
procedure first performs repeated stochastic forward passes under MC dropout to estimate representation-
level uncertainty. These uncertainty estimates are then converted into token-level uncertainty scores and
incorporated into the attention computation through an additive penalty on the pre-softmax attention
logits. Finally, the resulting predictive distribution is mapped to an entropy-based confidence score, which
determines whether the prediction is accepted or deferred according to the abstention threshold.
This algorithmic view complements the component-level formulation presented above by showing how
the three stages of the framework are executed sequentially within the inference pipeline. It also clarifies
that MedBayes-Lite is implemented as an inference-time procedure that reuses the pretrained transformer
backbone without introducing additional trainable parameters.
Complexity and Efficiency Analysis.
The computational cost of MedBayes-Lite is dominated by the M
stochastic forward passes used for MC-dropout uncertainty estimation. Accordingly, the runtime scales
approximately linearly with M, i.e., O(M × Tfwd), where Tfwd denotes the cost of a standard forward
pass. The framework introduces no additional trainable parameters, since uncertainty estimation and
propagation reuse the existing transformer backbone. Beyond the storage needed to compute summary
statistics across stochastic passes, the additional memory overhead is modest. Empirical latency and
memory measurements are reported in Section 6.4.
Interpretation of “Lite.”
The term Lite reflects the framework’s design goal of introducing uncertainty-
aware transformer inference without the computational burden typically associated with ensemble methods
or fully Bayesian neural network training. MedBayes-Lite achieves this through: (1) dropout-based
approximate posterior sampling rather than maintaining multiple separately trained models, (2) parameter-
free uncertainty propagation integrated into attention and decision stages, and (3) compatibility with
existing pretrained checkpoints without retraining. These properties make MedBayes-Lite a lightweight
uncertainty-aware extension of transformer inference that emphasizes interpretability and deployment
practicality.

To ensure consistency and fairness in evaluation, all models were trained and evaluated under standardized

through ablation, ranging from 0.1 to 0.5, with 0.3 yielding the best trade-off between predictive stability
and uncertainty sensitivity. This design allows uncertainty to influence the embedding, attention, and
decision stages without introducing additional trainable parameters.
Validation and model selection. Each model is validated using stratified splits to preserve representative
coverage of common and rare clinical cases. Early stopping based on validation loss, together with
repeated evaluation across random seeds, is used to improve robustness and reproducibility.

4.4
Task Protocols and Output Standardization
Because the benchmark suite includes both discriminative classifiers (e.g., BioBERT and ClinicalBERT)
and generative large language models (e.g., GPT and LLaMA families), we standardize the evaluation
protocol to ensure comparable task-level outputs across model types.
PubMedQA (3-way classification). For encoder models, we fine-tune a three-class classifier head and
evaluate the predicted labels directly. For generative models, we prompt the model to output exactly one
label from the set {Yes, No, Maybe}. Outputs outside this set are mapped to the nearest valid label
using a deterministic normalization rule based on case-insensitive string matching; otherwise, they are
marked invalid and excluded from accuracy while still included in abstention statistics.
MedQA (multiple choice). For encoder models, we fine-tune a 4-way (or 5-way, when applicable)
classifier head. For generative models, we prompt the model to output exactly one option letter (e.g.,
A/B/C/D). If a model produces additional text, only the first valid option letter is retained. If no valid
option is found, the sample is treated as invalid and counted as an abstention for coverage calculations.
MIMIC-III (binary prediction). For encoder models, we fine-tune a binary classifier head. For
generative models, we prompt for a binary label (0/1) or (Alive/Deceased) and apply the same
deterministic normalization procedure.
Uncertainty estimation and abstention. For open-weight models in full-stack mode, uncertainty
signals are computed from inference-time stochasticity under MC dropout and propagated through the
embedding and attention components before confidence-guided decision shaping. For closed API models,
uncertainty is estimated from repeated sampled generations using temperature sampling, after which the
same confidence-guided abstention rule is applied. We report both accuracy on accepted predictions and
coverage (the fraction of non-abstained outputs), which together determine ZTI.

Baseline Implementations
For comparison, we implement several widely used post-hoc calibration baselines alongside MedBayes-
Lite. Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP) uses the maximum predicted class probability as a confidence
proxy [35]. Temperature Scaling (TS) applies a scalar temperature parameter, optimized on the validation

Regression (IR) provides a non-parametric mapping between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy
and is implemented using scikit-learn’s monotone regression with clipping for out-of-range predic-
tions [27]. All baselines are applied to the same predicted logits obtained from the Bio ClinicalBERT
backbone and are evaluated using ECE, NLL, CUS, and ZTI. For reproducibility, ECE is computed with
15 bins, TS optimization uses a learning rate of 0.01 for 50 iterations, and IR is trained on raw top-class
confidence values.
These baselines represent commonly used calibration strategies and provide reference points against
which MedBayes-Lite is compared. All baseline models, including the five-model Deep Ensemble, are
initialized from the same pretrained Bio ClinicalBERT checkpoint and fine-tuned using identical data
splits, preprocessing pipelines, and optimization settings. This controlled setup ensures that performance
differences are attributable to calibration and uncertainty-handling strategies rather than to differences in
initialization or dataset composition. Each member of the Deep Ensemble is trained with a distinct random
seed while keeping all other optimization settings fixed, thereby isolating the effect of ensemble diversity
without introducing additional confounding factors. Details of the ablation experiments, including dropout

Ablation Study
To examine the sensitivity of MedBayes-Lite to its stochastic inference design, we conduct two comple-
mentary ablation studies: one varying the MC dropout probability and the other varying the number of
MC samples. Together, these analyses clarify how the strength of stochastic perturbation and the depth of
Monte Carlo averaging affect calibration, selective reliability, and computational cost.
Figure 3 shows the effect of different MC dropout probabilities within the ClinicalBERT backbone. The
deterministic baseline exhibits very low uncertainty exposure, reflected in low CUS and a high ZTI score.
However, in this setting the model is also less sensitive to epistemic variation, which can lead to overly
confident predictions. Introducing MC dropout increases uncertainty exposure, as seen in higher CUS
values, while ZTI decreases as the model becomes more conservative in its acceptance behavior. Among
the tested settings, a moderate dropout level (p = 0.3) provides the most balanced behavior, exposing
meaningful uncertainty without introducing excessive variability.
Figure 4 presents the effect of varying the number of MC samples. As the number of samples increases,
calibration improves, reflected by lower ECE, while runtime grows approximately linearly. At the same
time, NLL remains largely stable, indicating that the gains are driven more by improved uncertainty
estimation than by changes in predictive likelihood. The results suggest that a moderate sampling range
(M = 10 to 20) provides a practical compromise between uncertainty quality and inference cost.
Taken together, these ablation results indicate that MedBayes-Lite is most effective when stochasticity
is introduced in a controlled manner. Extremely low stochasticity limits uncertainty exposure, whereas
overly aggressive stochastic settings increase computational cost or reduce stability. In the present
experiments, moderate dropout (p ≈0.3) and a moderate number of MC samples (M ≈10 to 20) provide
the most favorable balance.

Discussion and Computational Analysis
The experimental results indicate that MedBayes-Lite improves uncertainty-aware behavior relative
to standard inference and conventional post-hoc calibration methods. Whereas approaches such as
Temperature Scaling (TS), Isotonic Regression (IR), and Dirichlet Calibration operate primarily at the
output level, MedBayes-Lite incorporates uncertainty signals into multiple stages of inference, including
representation estimation, attention modulation, and confidence-guided decision shaping. This design
allows the framework to produce more cautious and better calibrated predictions, particularly in settings
involving ambiguity, prompt variation, and cross-dataset transfer.
Discussion Summary
• Empirical takeaway: MedBayes-Lite improves calibration and selective reliability across prompt
variation, domain transfer, and heterogeneous biomedical tasks.
• Practical takeaway: The framework offers uncertainty-aware inference without changing the
pretrained backbone, making it more deployable than heavier ensemble-based alternatives.
• Clinical takeaway: By moderating confidence and supporting abstention, the method is better
aligned with decision settings in which uncertain predictions should trigger human review rather
than automatic action.

6.1
Clinical Relevance and Practical Impact
The primary motivation for MedBayes-Lite is to support clinical settings in which uncertainty estimation
is important for safe decision-making. The empirical results suggest several practical use cases.
(i) Triage. In emergency and critical care settings, the framework can identify cases in which predictive
confidence falls below a decision threshold, thereby supporting escalation to human review. This behavior
is useful when uncertain outputs should not be acted upon automatically.
(ii) Diagnostic support. In clinical decision support, uncertainty estimates can help identify ambiguous
or borderline cases. For example, when symptoms or findings are compatible with multiple conditions,
lower confidence may indicate that the model output should be interpreted cautiously and supplemented
with additional evaluation.
(iii) Risk stratification. In prognosis or longitudinal risk prediction, uncertainty-aware inference can help
identify patients whose predicted risk is unstable because of incomplete, noisy, or weakly informative
data. This can support more cautious interpretation of model-based risk categories.
More broadly, the selective prediction behavior captured by ZTI is relevant to clinical deployment because
it reflects whether a model can maintain useful predictive performance while refraining from acting too
confidently when evidence is limited. In this sense, conservative calibration should not be interpreted
solely as reduced decisiveness; in high-stakes settings, it can also function as a safety-oriented mechanism
that promotes human oversight when uncertainty is substantial.
6.2
Methodological Significance
Beyond output calibration, MedBayes-Lite provides a framework for studying how uncertainty can
influence intermediate stages of transformer inference. In the open-weight setting, uncertainty estimated
from stochastic internal representations is used to derive token-level uncertainty scores, which then affect
attention weighting and final confidence estimation. This differs from approaches that restrict uncertainty
handling to post-hoc output adjustment.
This design is useful for two reasons. First, it allows uncertainty to affect how contextual information
is aggregated, rather than only how the final probabilities are rescaled. Second, it provides a more
interpretable operational view of uncertainty propagation across the inference pipeline. In this sense,
the contribution of MedBayes-Lite is not only empirical but also methodological: it offers a lightweight
uncertainty-aware inference procedure that can be integrated into pretrained transformer models without
retraining the backbone.
At the same time, the framework should be interpreted with appropriate scope. The present results
support the claim that uncertainty-aware inference improves calibration and selective reliability across
the evaluated tasks and model settings. They do not imply that uncertainty has been fully characterized in
all transformer layers or that uncertainty propagation is solved in a general theoretical sense. Rather, the
method provides a practical approximation that is empirically useful in biomedical settings.
6.3
Limitations and Failure Modes
Several limitations should be noted. First, under severe distribution shift, such as rare diseases, unseen
terminology, or highly irregular note structure, MC-dropout-based uncertainty estimates may still be im-
perfect and may underestimate epistemic uncertainty. Second, when input quality is poor and uncertainty
is uniformly high across many tokens, the resulting uncertainty signals may become less informative for
downstream attention modulation and confidence interpretation.
28


## Page 29


The framework also depends on implementation access. In open-weight settings, MedBayes-Lite can
use uncertainty-aware attention and representation-level uncertainty estimates. In closed API settings,
however, the method is limited to output-level uncertainty estimation from repeated sampled generations
and confidence-guided abstention. As a result, not all components of the framework are available for all
model families.
A further limitation is that the quality of uncertainty estimates depends on hyperparameters such as
dropout rate, number of stochastic passes, and abstention threshold. These choices affect the trade-off
between sensitivity to uncertainty and computational cost. Future work may consider adaptive dropout
strategies, stronger posterior approximations, or hybrid methods that combine lightweight Bayesian
inference with ensemble-style robustness.
6.4
Computational Efficiency and Scaling Behavior
From a deployment perspective, the computational cost of MedBayes-Lite is an important consideration.
Because the framework does not add trainable layers to the underlying backbone, its parameter count
remains unchanged relative to the base model. The primary computational cost arises from repeated
stochastic forward passes during inference.
As shown in the efficiency analysis, inference time increases approximately linearly with the number of
Monte Carlo samples. This behavior is expected and reflects the sampling-based nature of the method. At
the same time, GPU memory usage remains relatively stable because the same backbone parameters are
reused across stochastic passes rather than replicated, as in ensemble-based methods.
This computational profile is important in practice.
Compared with ensemble-based approaches,
MedBayes-Lite offers a more parameter-efficient alternative for uncertainty-aware inference. Com-
pared with purely deterministic inference, it introduces additional latency, but in exchange provides
uncertainty estimates that can improve calibration and abstention behavior. The resulting trade-off may
be acceptable in biomedical settings where reliability is more important than maximizing raw throughput.
6.5
Clinical Interpretability and Human–AI Collaboration
An important aspect of MedBayes-Lite is that uncertainty is presented not only as a scalar output measure,
but also as a signal that can inform how predictions are interpreted. In the open-weight setting, token-level
uncertainty estimates provide a partial view of which parts of the input contribute to unstable internal
representations. This can be useful for auditing model behavior and for identifying cases in which the
prediction appears plausible but is supported by weak or ambiguous evidence.
In deployment settings, confidence-guided abstention can also serve as a practical interface between the
model and the clinician. When predictive confidence falls below a threshold τ, the system can defer rather
than forcing a potentially unreliable recommendation. This does not replace clinical reasoning; rather, it
supports a workflow in which the model contributes information while leaving uncertain cases to human
judgment. In this respect, uncertainty acts as a coordination mechanism for safer human–AI interaction.
6.6
Design Principles for Reliable Medical LLMs
The empirical and methodological findings of this study suggest several design principles for reliable
medical language models:
1. Integrate uncertainty into inference: Uncertainty handling is more informative when it affects
internal inference behavior rather than only post-hoc output calibration.
29


## Page 30


2. Use uncertainty to guide information aggregation: Token- or representation-level uncertainty can
be used to moderate the influence of less reliable evidence during contextual processing.
3. Support selective prediction: In high-stakes settings, useful models should not only produce predic-
tions but also indicate when those predictions should be deferred.
4. Preserve deployment feasibility: Uncertainty-aware methods should remain computationally practi-
cal and compatible with existing pretrained models.
7
Conclusion
This paper presented MedBayes-Lite, an inference-time uncertainty-aware framework for pretrained
transformer models in biomedical settings. The proposed approach incorporates uncertainty signals into
representation estimation, attention modulation, and confidence-guided decision shaping, thereby extend-
ing uncertainty handling beyond output-level post-hoc calibration. Across experiments on MIMIC-III,
MedQA, and PubMedQA, MedBayes-Lite consistently improved calibration- and trust-related measures,
including ECE, CUS, and ZTI, while remaining compatible with existing pretrained models and incurring
moderate computational overhead.
The additional analyses further showed that the framework remains effective across different model
families, improves robustness under prompt variation and cross-dataset transfer, and supports more
cautious predictive behavior in ambiguous clinical settings. These findings suggest that uncertainty-aware
inference can improve not only numerical calibration, but also the reliability of selective prediction in
high-stakes biomedical applications.
Several directions remain for future work. These include extending the framework to multimodal and
larger-scale medical language models, developing stronger yet efficient uncertainty approximations, and
studying how uncertainty-aware inference can be integrated into human-in-the-loop clinical workflows.
