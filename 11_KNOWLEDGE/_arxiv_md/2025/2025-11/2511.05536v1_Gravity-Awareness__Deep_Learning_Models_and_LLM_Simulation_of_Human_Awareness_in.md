Abstract  
Earth’s gravity has fundamentally shaped human development by guiding the brain’s integration 
of vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive inputs into an internal model of gravity—a dynamic 
neural representation enabling prediction and interpretation of gravitational forces. This work 
presents a dual computational framework to quantitatively model these adaptations. The first 
component is a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that predicts g-load-dependent 
changes in key electroencephalographic (EEG) frequency bands, representing the brain's cortical 
state. The second component utilizes a suite of independent Gaussian Processes (GPs) to model 
the body’s broader physiological state, including Heart Rate Variability (HRV), Electrodermal 
Activity (EDA), and motor behavior. Both models were trained on data derived from a 
comprehensive review of parabolic flight literature, using published findings as anchor points to 
construct robust, continuous functions. To complement this quantitative analysis, we simulated 
subjective human experience under different gravitational loads—ranging from microgravity 
(0g) and partial gravity (Moon 0.17g, Mars 0.38g) to hypergravity associated with spacecraft 
launch and re-entry (1.8g)—using a large language model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet). The model was 
prompted with physiological parameters to generate introspective narratives of alertness and 
self-awareness, which closely aligned with the quantitative findings from both the EEG and 
physiological models. This combined framework integrates quantitative physiological modeling 
with generative cognitive simulation, offering a novel approach to understanding and predicting 
human performance in altered gravity environments. 
Keywords: gravity perception, awareness, space, microgravity, g-load, LLM simulation, 
Gaussian Process, computational neuroscience

1.​ Introduction 
Gravity, as a constant force on Earth, shapes human physiology and cognition in profound ways. 
When gravity deviates from the standard 1G environment—either as hypergravity (greater than 
1G) or hypogravity (less than 1G)—the human brain and body experience acute and chronic 
adaptations that significantly impact awareness, defined here as the integration of perceptual, 
cognitive, and proprioceptive processes necessary for situational comprehension. This paper 
explores how varying gravitational forces—ranging from microgravity in spaceflight to the 
hypergravity experienced during intense acceleration—alter awareness, with a particular focus 
on sensory integration, spatial orientation, and cognitive performance. Over the evolution of 
human development, the pervasive force of gravity formed our brain, the ability to think and be 
aware. Without it, the sensory and motor organs could not be shaped accordingly. On Earth, 
gravity is a constant downward acceleration of approximately 9.81 m/s², which is usually defined 
as 1g. All terrestrial organisms have evolved under this force, and it is difficult to conceive of a 
more fundamental aspect of life on Earth than gravity, as it serves not only as a physical 
constraint but also as a critical sensory input that informs behavior (Arshad & Ferré, 2023). In 
the current paper, we explore different aspects such as physiological (e.g., HR variability) and 
neuronal (e.g., EEG) that contribute of human awareness in altered gravity. 
Understanding the gravity-awareness relationship has direct implications for human 
space exploration and aerospace safety. Countermeasures including artificial gravity and 
neurocognitive monitoring must be developed to support awareness in altered gravitational 
states. Theoretically, the study of gravity's influence offers insights into embodied cognition and 
the brain’s predictive models of self and environment. This is particularly critical in extreme 
hypergravity, where high-g acceleration can lead to severe visual and cognitive disturbances,

astronauts (Whinnery et al., 2000; Erdem & Demir, 2006). The study of these extreme states

physiological resilience (Gawron, 1997).

but also opens new possibilities for evaluating how altered gravitational forces may impact core 
aspects of the conscious self. 
1.1 Brain Regions Involved in Gravity Awareness 
The perception and integration of gravity as one of the most fundamental forces is processed and 
integrated in many brain areas spanning vestibular, cerebellar, cortical, and subcortical regions: 
parieto-insular vestibular cortex, cerebellum, temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and insular cortex, 
visual cortex, motor cortex and supplementary cortex area (SMA), posterior thalamus, and 
vestibular nuclei (Figure 1). Each of these provide own input for out perception of gravity, so 
natural to us. 
The vestibular cortex serves as a central hub, integrating inputs from the gravity-sensing otolith 
organs and semicircular canals with visual and proprioceptive cues to generate a coherent sense 
of body orientation relative to gravity (Lopez et al., 2008; Pfeiffer et al., 2014). This complex 
sensory integration forms what is known as the internal model of gravity—a dynamic neural

(Lacquaniti et al., 2014). Indeed, at the core of gravity perception are the vestibular otoliths, 
sophisticated receptors within the inner ear that continuously detect the magnitude and direction 
of gravitational acceleration.

The cerebellum, particularly, the posterior vermis and vestibulocerebellum, plays a vital role in

prediction and interpretation of gravitational effects on the body and external objects (Indovina et 
al., 2013; Pfeiffer et al., 2014). The visual cortex, including areas such as V2, V3, and the lingual 
gyrus, encodes gravity-consistent motion, such as falling objects, enabling visual prediction of 
gravitational dynamics (Bosco et al., 2012). The motor cortex and supplementary motor area 
(SMA) generate postural and motor responses consistent with gravitational constraints, ensuring 
stability and coordinated movement (Angelaki & Cullen, 2008). Additionally, the posterior 
thalamus functions as a key relay between the vestibular nuclei and cortical regions, while the 
vestibular nuclei in the brainstem form the first central processing station for gravitational 
signals (Guldin & Grüsser, 1998). Collectively, this network constructs a dynamic neural 
representation of gravity, essential for maintaining spatial orientation, balance, and conscious 
awareness of self in relation to the environment. 
To function effectively, the human brain relies on this internal model to optimize motor control, 
spatial perception, and higher-order cognitive functions. Disruptions to gravity input, as 
experienced during spaceflight, challenge the brain’s predictive mechanisms and lead to 
compensatory adaptations. For instance, Cheron et al. (2006) investigated EEG changes during a 
space flight mission and reported an increase in the spontaneous 10-Hz alpha rhythm in 
parieto-occipital and sensorimotor areas.  
 
1.2 EEG Activity in Various Gravity Environments 
Brain adjusts to both microgravity and increased gravity environments. Recent space-flight 
neuroscience research has revealed how the absence of Earth’s gravitational reference alters 
neural oscillations and functional network organization. Pusil et al. (2023) demonstrated a 
6


## Page 7


marked reduction in alpha-band (8–13 Hz) power within the Default Mode Network (DMN) 
during in-flight and post-flight phases compared with pre-flight baselines. Functional 
connectivity within this network was similarly reduced and remained attenuated even 20 days 
after return to Earth, suggesting persistent reorganization of intrinsic connectivity under altered 
gravity. Alpha-band is responsible for reflective, calm mind during awake state. Decreased alpha 
power likely reflects a diminished baseline of cortical inhibition and self-referential 
processing—core components of conscious resting-state activity.Complementary evidence from 
Quivira-Lopesino and colleagues (2025) revealed a significant increase in beta-band (12–30 Hz) 
power and connectivity within frontotemporal and sensorimotor regions during microgravity. 
Beta oscillations, associated with motor coordination and sensorimotor feedback, were elevated 
both in flight and immediately after re-entry, implying compensatory activation of cortical 
systems that stabilize posture and body schema when gravitational cues are absent. The alpha 
suppression and beta enhancement reflect a transition from introspective to externally oriented 
cortical states—an adaptive redistribution of processing resources toward vigilance and 
proprioceptive maintenance in weightlessness. Earlier observations by Cheron et al. (2016) 
similarly documented these spectral shifts, indicating that gravity continuously modulates 
oscillatory dynamics tied to attention and spatial orientation. 
Converging neuroimaging data show that microgravity induces large-scale plasticity across the 
vestibular, cerebellar, and parietal networks responsible for spatial awareness. Demertzi et al. 
(2016) reported disrupted resting-state connectivity within vestibular–somatosensory circuits 
after long-duration missions, while Roberts et al. (2019) observed occipital and ventral-stream 
reorganization in MRI data from returning astronauts, suggesting that visual systems adapt to the 
persistent absence of gravity-consistent motion cues. Likewise, Clément et al. (2020)

documented altered postural control and impaired verticality judgments post-flight, whereas 
Koppelmans et al. (2017) revealed widespread microstructural white-matter changes, confirming 
that gravity adaptation engages whole-brain network remodeling. 
These findings collectively indicate that gravity influences awareness and cognition through 
distributed neural reorganization spanning vestibular, parietal, and frontal domains. Alpha-band 
suppression and beta-band enhancement constitute electrophysiological markers of cortical effort 
to maintain sensory integration in the absence of gravitational reference frames. As missions 
extend in duration, maintaining functional efficiency within these oscillatory systems becomes 
critical for orientation, attention, and emotional regulation. Continuous EEG and fMRI 
monitoring could serve as an early-warning system for cognitive strain, while adaptive 
neurofeedback and counter-gravity training might stabilize awareness and sensorimotor control. 
Gravity, therefore, is a physical parameter but also a neurobiological principle shaping the 
awareness. 
 
1.3 Computational and Machine Learning Models that Explain Awareness of Gravity 
Humans will increasingly encounter altered gravity both visually (VR/AR) and bodily (parabolic 
flight, orbital, lunar, Martian, and hyper-g phases). Indeed, gravity is a strong prior (Jörges, 
López-Moliner, 2017). Because gravity—both sensed and internalized—constrains catching, 
grasping, postural orientation, and spatial inference, adaptation to gravity-discrepant contexts is 
essential yet often slow (Clément & Reschke, 2008; Lacquaniti et al., 2014). A principled 
account treats Earth’s gravity as a strong prior: a deeply learned expectation with high precision 
that can dominate contradictory sensory evidence. In VR, visual–vestibular conflicts make full 
8


## Page 9


adaptation to non-1g scenes rare; even in space, where cues may be congruent, updating is 
incremental (Keshavarz et al., 2015; Clément, 2017). The strong-prior view parsimoniously 
explains these observations (Lacquaniti et al., 2013). 
Gravity awareness can be formalized as Bayesian inference over a latent gravity vector and body 
orientation. Likelihoods arise from noisy vestibular, proprioceptive, and visual signals; the prior 
encodes Earth’s g and an egocentric “down.” Because this prior accrues over years of experience, 
its precision often exceeds that of any single cue, keeping posteriors near 1g despite discrepancy. 
This framework predicts slow learning for persistent deviations (0g, 0.38g), hysteresis when 
switching gravities, and re-weighting when cue reliability is manipulated (e.g., reducing 
vestibular noise increases visual capture) (Körding & Wolpert, 2004; Zago & Lacquaniti, 2005). 
Yet, another models are the famous Kalman filtering. Vestibular–somatosensory estimation is 
well captured by Kalman filters that fuse canal–otolith dynamics to disambiguate tilt from 
translation and account for velocity storage (Merfeld et al., 1999; Laurens & Droulez, 2007). 
Extending the state to include ggg yields a gravity-aware estimator in which prediction (from 
internal body dynamics) and correction (from cue residuals) jointly maintain orientation and 
“down.” Under altered gravity, the assumed process model is biased; adaptation amounts to slow 
parameter learning of ggg and noise covariances, consistent with sluggish recalibration in micro- 
and partial-g (Angelaki & Cullen, 2008). 
In terms of predictive coding and active inference there are hierarchical model view that 
important to discuss. In hierarchical predictive-coding architectures, higher levels encode an 
abstract world model that expects constant downward acceleration; prediction errors from visual 
motion, postural sway, or ocular reflexes are minimized by belief updates (slow) or actions (fast). 
Precision control explains why the 1g prior can overrule discrepant cues: the system assigns high

precision to the prior under normal conditions and down-regulates sensory precision only after 
prolonged mismatch (Friston, 2010). This yields testable predictions: early microgravity 
produces large sensory prediction errors and gradual reduction in prior precision as adaptation 
proceeds (Pezzulo et al., 2015). Cerebellar forward models and optimal control. Motor prediction 
relies on cerebellar forward models that incorporate gravitational torques; optimal feedback 
control computes control laws under gravity-loaded costs. Switching to 0g changes optimal

There are several interesting AI world-model approaches that we should mention here. In silico, 
world-model agents—latent dynamics models, neural ODEs, and graph-based physics 
networks—learn an implicit ggg and generalize across altered-g simulators (Battaglia et al., 
2016; Chen et al., 2018; Raissi et al., 2019). Meta-learning supports rapid few-shot adaptation to 
new gravities, while domain randomization improves robustness but still reveals performance 
cliffs when training priors mismatch deployment gravity—mirroring human behavior in VR and 
space (Finn et al., 2017; Tobin et al., 2017). 
Therefore, across the models discussed – Bayesian, Kalman, predictive-coding, optimal-control, 
and modern AI world-model frameworks – gravity-awareness emerges from inference with a 
high-precision prior coupled to multisensory likelihoods and action. The central bottleneck is 
prior-precision updating: humans protect the 1g prior for stability, adapting only under sustained, 
reliable mismatch. This perspective organizes existing findings and suggests concrete 
levers—manipulating cue reliabilities, increasing prediction-error salience, and shaping precision 
schedules—to accelerate adaptation to non-terrestrial gravities.

1.4 Gravity-Awareness 
We would like to introduce a new term Gravity-awarensss, which is useful in discussing the state 
of individual physical and mental awareness in various gravity conditions. More specifically,  
gravity-awareness refers to the brain’s integrated perception and cognitive understanding of 
gravitational forces acting on the body and environment. It emerges from the continuous 
interaction of vestibular, proprioceptive, visual, and interoceptive signals, allowing individuals to 
maintain orientation, balance, and spatial reference in relation to gravity. This type of awareness 
supports adaptive motor control, spatial memory, and a stable sense of bodily self across varying 
gravitational environments (e.g., microgravity, partial gravity, or hypergravity). 
Previous studies have demonstrated that exposure to altered gravitational environments affects 
sensorimotor coordination, spatial perception, and decision-making, and can induce 
disorientation and motion sickness (Arshad & Ferré, 2023). The modulation of awareness across 
different gravity levels is underpinned by three primary mechanisms: (1) sensory conflict, (2) 
neurovascular alterations, and (3) neuroplasticity. Sensory conflict occurs when vestibular inputs 
no longer match visual and proprioceptive signals, particularly in hypogravity and hypergravity. 
This conflict degrades the brain’s ability to form a coherent model of the body in space, 
disrupting situational awareness (Bles et al., 1984). Neurovascular alterations, especially during 
hypergravity, can affect fluid distribution and cortical perfusion, while in microgravity, altered 
intracranial pressure can impact neural signaling pathways (Roberts et al., 2017). 
Neuroplasticity, the brain’s capacity to adapt structurally and functionally, is essential for coping 
with gravity transitions, but such adaptations may not fully compensate for the novel sensory

Under Earth’s gravity (1g), the human vestibular, proprioceptive, and visual systems operate in 
a harmonized manner to produce a stable sense of orientation, bodily awareness, and cognitive

## Page 13


performance. Sensory information from the semicircular canals and otolith organs allows 
individuals to perceive body motion and position, forming a coherent representation of self in 
space (Angelaki & Cullen, 2008). Cognitive awareness is typically optimized in this environment 
due to the brain's evolutionary adaptation to Earth’s gravitational field. Functional MRI studies 
have demonstrated that vestibular-cortical networks are well-tuned to 1g conditions, maintaining 
effective balance, attentional control, and visuospatial awareness (Lopez et al., 2012). 
Microgravity (0g), encountered during spaceflight, poses significant challenges to spatial 
awareness and cognition. The absence of gravitational cues disrupts the vestibular system's input 
to the brain, often leading to sensory reweighting, where reliance on vision and proprioception 
increases (Clément & Reschke, 2008). Astronauts frequently report disorientation, "space fog," 
and impaired mental imagery during the initial adaptation phase (Manzey & Lorenz, 1998). 
These effects are compounded by “space motion sickness” (SMS), which impairs attention and 
executive function during the early stages of exposure to microgravity (Lackner & DiZio, 2000). 
Furthermore, studies on long-duration missions reveal persistent changes in neural activity 
associated with spatial cognition and attention. For instance, alterations in the right parietal 
cortex, a region implicated in awareness and spatial processing, have been documented after 
extended microgravity exposure (Demertzi et al., 2016). These changes correlate with degraded 
performance on spatial orientation tasks and may persist post-flight, suggesting long-term 
neuroplastic adjustments. More recent findings by Tian et al. (2024) showed that during 
long‐duration missions aboard the International Space Station (ISS), astronauts exhibited 
increased variability in a simple finger‐tapping task. The key finding was that while motor 
execution remained intact, central planning noise—reflecting higher-order cortical control—was 
significantly elevated just four weeks into flight and did not fully return to baseline even four

weeks after landing (Tian et al., 2024). This suggests that non-gravity stressors (e.g., isolation, 
altered sleep, elevated CO₂) may degrade sensorimotor timing by taxing attentional and 
executive control systems, beyond purely vestibular/proprioceptive adaptation. Additionally, in a 
2024 cognitive‐testing study of 25 professional astronauts on six-month missions, slowed 
performance was observed in processing speed, working memory, and attention tasks early in 
flight; however, accuracy remained high, and there was no evidence of permanent cognitive 
decline at the group level (Dev et al., 2024). It suggests that the brain adapts, but some cognitive 
domains remain vulnerable—especially under combined stressors of microgravity, isolation, 
sleep disruption and elevated CO₂. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of gravity 
perception for the huaman cognitive and motor functioning that mediate awareness. 
Intermediate gravity levels, such as those on the Moon (0.16G) or Mars (0.38G), are 
expected to yield cognitive and perceptual effects that lie between microgravity and 
Earth-normal gravity. Emperical research conducted in parabolic flights and partial gravity 
simulators suggests that these environments do disrupt vestibular integration, albeit less severely 
than in 0g. For example, analog studies on Mars gravity levels show slowed motor reaction times 
and degraded spatial memory (Kawasaki et al., 2017). Awareness is affected through 
mechanisms like proprioceptive recalibration and motor noise through sensory mismatches 
between expected and actual feedback during locomotion. While the literature remains sparse on 
long-term behavioral adaptations in sub-1g environments, preliminary evidence indicates that 
sustained exposure could cause reduction of cognitive performance, particularly in tasks 
requiring complex sensorimotor coordination and memory retrieval (Newman & Clément, 2020). 
Hypergravity (up to 1.8G), such as that experienced during parabolic flight pull-up phases or 
space launch, also induces significant physiological and cognitive consequences. At these 
14


## Page 15


moderate g-levels, decrements in cognitive performance have been observed. Tasks requiring 
executive function, spatial memory, or fine motor control are particularly vulnerable (Schneider 
et al., 2008). The vestibular system is overstimulated in such conditions, leading to sensory 
conflict and disorientation, which undermines perceptual awareness and decision-making 
accuracy. The increased g-load causes an increasing cardiovascular strain. This physiological 
stress, combined with the novel sensory environment, can induce fatigue and increase stress 
hormone levels, which can in turn impair working memory and attentional focus. These acute 
responses to moderate hypergravity are key components of the adaptation challenges that our 
computational models aim to quantify.  
Extreme hypergravity (e.g., exposures above +3 G) — as experienced by fighter jet pilots and 
astronauts during launch or re-entry — challenges human physiology to its limits. As +Gz forces 
rise, the cardiovascular system must work increasingly hard to maintain cerebral perfusion; when 
it fails, a well-documented sequence of symptoms ensues: loss of peripheral vision (tunnel 
vision), gun-barrel vision, grey-out, blackout (complete loss of vision), and ultimately G-induced 
loss of consciousness (G-LOC) if no corrective action is taken (Whinnery & Forster, 2013). 
G-LOC remains a significant risk despite extensive training and anti-G equipment, highlighting 
the operational importance of understanding the underlying physiology. Physiological stress 
under these conditions is substantial: heart rate, blood pressure, and vascular tone all respond 
dramatically to rising +Gz loads. Pilots exposed to rapid +Gz onset rates show markedly altered 
cardiovascular reflexes, and muscle strain becomes critical in maintaining anti-G maneuvers and 
avoiding syncope (Whinnery et al., 2013). Although centrifuge training and anti-G straining 
maneuvers (AGSM) improve G-tolerance, episodes of G-LOC and near-loss of consciousness 
(A-LOC) continue to occur operationally, particularly under rapid-onset, high-+G profiles

precede loss of consciousness in hyper-gravity environments. 
1.5 LLM Simulating Awareness at Various g-load 
We employ a novel approach by using LLM model to simulate human awareness. Recent 
advances in LLMs have enabled their use not only as linguistic tools but also as in silico 
participants capable of simulating human responses to complex cognitive and physiological 
scenarios. Building on this potential, we employed an advanced LLM (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) to 
simulate the subjective awareness of a human being exposed to varying gravitational conditions. 
The model was guided through a structured prompt that specified key physical, biological, and 
physiological constraints—including gravitational load, head tilt, heart rate variability, muscle 
activation, postural sway, and gait parameters—serving as a scaffold for generating cognitively 
and biologically plausible responses. This approach allowed us to simulate internal states (e.g., 
alertness and self-awareness) that would otherwise be difficult or impossible to measure directly 
under extreme or ethically constrained conditions, such as sustained 6g hypergravity. While 
earlier research has explored the potential of LLMs as simulated participants (see Levy et al., 
2023; Ullman, 2023), their use in neuroscience and embodied cognition remains underexplored. 
For instance, Levy et al. (2023) demonstrated that LLMs can replicate aspects of human moral 
reasoning and psychological trait judgments when presented with structured dilemmas. Similarly, 
Ullman (2023) proposed a framework for using LLMs as “cognitive models” that can 
approximate human responses in social cognition tasks. However, to our knowledge, no prior 
study has attempted to use an LLM to simulate awareness states under altered gravitational 
environments.

In this study, we examine whether LLMs can be extended into the domain of high-fidelity 
thought experiments involving embodied cognition and gravitational perturbation. Specifically, 
we test the hypothesis that extreme gravitational forces may disrupt bodily self-awareness and 
contribute to a breakdown of the "minimal self"—the pre-reflective, sensorimotor foundation of 
consciousness rooted in bodily representation and spatial orientation (Blanke & Metzinger, 
2009). This concept aligns with theoretical frameworks that link gravity, vestibular input, and 
bodily awareness (Lopez et al., 2008; Pfeiffer et al., 2014). By simulating responses across 
conditions ranging from microgravity (0g) to hypergravity (6g), the model allows us to infer 
whether LLM-generated states reflect plausible cognitive and phenomenological shifts, including 
degraded alertness, altered postural coherence, and dissociation of self-representation. The use of 
LLMs in this context represents a novel methodological bridge between computational 
neuroscience, embodied cognition, and philosophical thought experiments. These models offer a 
sandbox for ethically complex, empirically challenging, or physically unfeasible experiments, 
enabling researchers to test hypotheses about the cognitive and experiential impact of altered 
sensorimotor environments—such as those encountered in space travel, high-g maneuvers, or 
virtual simulations of vestibular disruption.

model is a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) that leverages Fourier features to effectively learn the 
non-linear mapping from a single gravitational input to a multi-output vector of EEG 
band-power states. We call this model EEG Fourier MLP. Instead of classifying discrete states, 
this model predicts the percentage change in power for specific EEG frequency bands based on a 
given g-load value. This approach allows for a granular understanding of how brain dynamics 
scale with varying gravity, from microgravity to hypergravity. The entire workflow, from data 
synthesis to model training and inference, is built upon a neurophysiologically-informed 
foundation derived from established findings in spaceflight and parabolic flight studies.  While 
our current model focuses on the transitions up to 1.8g, these markers are precursors to more 
dramatic changes seen at higher g-loads. Extreme hypergravity is known to cause massive 
disruptions in cortical function, ultimately leading to the state of G-LOC, representing a 
complete failure of the brain's ability to maintain conscious awareness (Gawron, 1997) – we do 
not include the higher g-load in this study.

Due to the current scarcity of publicly available electroencephalography (EEG) datasets 
encompassing diverse and precisely annotated gravitational conditions, we developed a 
large-scale synthetic dataset designed to simulate neural activity across a continuous spectrum of 
gravitational forces. The dataset contains N= 94,000 data points with M= 20 synthetic 
participants. Each synthetic participant was exposed to a full range of gravitational loads, from 
0.0 g (microgravity) through 1.8 g (hypergravity), resulting in a within-subject design where

every participant contributes EEG responses at each gravitational level. This structure ensures 
that inter-individual variability is minimized while preserving within-participant dynamics

bands, including suppression of alpha power (8–13 Hz) within the Default Mode Network

and elevated gamma-band (>30 Hz) power. These known trends provided the foundation for 
parameterizing signal characteristics in our simulation pipeline, ensuring that each synthetic trace 
reflected physiologically plausible changes associated with gravitational stress and adaptation. 
Each synthetic EEG sequence was modeled as a weighted mixture of oscillatory components 
representing the alpha, beta, mu, and gamma bands, with noise distributions empirically tuned to 
match the signal-to-noise ratios observed in human EEG recordings under analog conditions. 
Temporal dynamics were simulated to preserve realistic autocorrelation and spectral entropy 
properties. The gravitational load variable (g) was treated as a continuous predictor, enabling 
models to learn smooth transitions in spectral power rather than discrete state jumps. To facilitate 
model interpretability, each data point is annotated with corresponding EEG band powers, 
simulated cortical sources (DMN, PFC, SMN), and gravitational level metadata.

Simulation and Preprocessing Protocol of EEG Fourier MLP 
A dataset was created to simulate a series of parabolic flights, with each flight consisting of five 
distinct gravitational phases. These phases included a baseline Earth gravity of 1.0g, followed by 
a period of hypergravity at 1.8g, then microgravity at 0.0g, another hypergravity phase at 1.8g, 
and finally a return to the 1.0g baseline. Data for 20 subjects, each completing 20 runs, was 
generated using a custom script. The simulation produced 64-channel EEG signals at a frequency 
of 250 Hz. Each 25-second gravitational phase was designed with phase-modulated, 
band-specific oscillatory sources. These sources were then projected onto realistic scalp 
topographies for the Default Mode Network (DMN), Prefrontal Cortex (PFC), and Sensorimotor 
Network (SMN). 
The raw signals underwent a streaming-compatible processing workflow. This involved applying 
a band-pass filter (1-45 Hz), a notch filter (50 Hz), and normalizing each channel using a z-score. 
The continuous data was then segmented into overlapping 2-second windows with a 0.5-second 
stride. This segmentation was carefully managed to ensure that no single window crossed the 
boundary between different gravity phases. 
Finally, the ground-truth target variables were calculated. For every window, the average power 
was computed for the alpha, beta, mu, and gamma frequency bands across their relevant cortical 
regions (DMN, PFC, SMN, and global, respectively). These power values were then normalized 
24


## Page 25


to represent a percentage change relative to the mean power of the initial 1.0g phase for that 
specific run. The resulting dataset contains entries that link a specific g-load to the corresponding 
percentage changes in the four neurophysiological markers. 
The model architecture is designed for efficiency, mapping a single continuous input (g-level) to 
four continuous outputs (band-power changes).

Architecture Overview of EEG Fourier MLP  
Input scale of the model was such that the model accepts a scalar g-load, which is scaled to a 
range by dividing by 1.8g.  Fourier positional features was as such that the scaled input g was 
expanded into a 9-dimensional vector (1 + 2*K, where K=4) using sine and cosine 
transformations of different frequencies. This allowed the shallow network to model the 
complex, non-linear relationship between gravity and EEG power. The core of the model is a 
small MLP with a shared trunk of two hidden layers (64 neurons each), using SiLU activation 
and a dropout rate of 0.1 for regularization. A final linear layer with 4 neurons produces the four 
target outputs (see Figure 4). 
Loss Function was a scaled Huber loss was employed. The loss for each output is calculated on 
values standardized by the training set's standard deviation for that band, ensuring equitable 
contribution to the total loss. The training utilized band weights (α=1.0, β=1.0, μ=1.0, γ=0.25) to 
prioritize key markers and g-balancing to apply inverse-frequency weights, preventing model 
bias towards the more common 1.0g condition. The model was trained using the AdamW

Predictive Models of Gravity-Awareness 
Our EEG Fourier MLP model successfully learned the non-linear relationships between g-load 
and key neural oscillations. The prediction of suppressed alpha and mu rhythms in microgravity 
aligns perfectly with established literature, reflecting a combination of altered resting-state 
cognition (DMN) and a fundamental shift in sensorimotor strategy due to postural unloading 
(Cebolla et al., 2016; Pusil et al., 2023). Simultaneously, the model's prediction of enhanced beta 
and gamma power in hypergravity captures the well-documented cortical signature of heightened 
arousal and cognitive stress in high-g environments (Smith et al., 2013). These central findings 
are strongly corroborated by the predictions of our IGP-Phyiso model. The V-shaped curve of 
electrodermal activity—showing high sympathetic arousal at both 0.0g and 1.8g but not at 
1.0g—is a particularly salient result. It suggests that the autonomic nervous system perceives 
both microgravity (a novel and disorienting sensory state) and hypergravity (a state of high 
physical strain) as significant stressors, while the familiar 1.0g environment represents a 
homeostatic baseline. This is further supported by the cardiovascular predictions, which show a 
powerful increase in vagal tone (e.g., increased RMSSD ) in microgravity, consistent with the 
initial phase of cardiovascular deconditioning reported in parabolic flights (Widjaja et al., 2015). 
Furthermore, the predicted decrease in trunk activity, contrasted with the relative stability of

by the EEG Fourier MLP model's mu-rhythm suppression (Wang et al., 2015). 
Further, the results of IGP-Phisio indicate two complementary physiological trends. 
Electrodermal activity (EDA/GSR) shows a strong V-shaped pattern across gravitational loads: 
skin conductance is lowest around 1.0 g, then rises sharply in both microgravity (0 g) and 
hypergravity (1.8 g). For example, right-hand skin conductance increases by over 200 % at 1.8 g, 
suggesting that both extremes of gravity are accompanied by heightened sympathetic arousal 
(Amin & Faghih, 2022). In contrast, body activity measures follow an inverted U-shaped 
response. Trunk activity peaks near 1.0 g—consistent with normal postural muscle 
engagement—and decreases by over 50 % under microgravity, reflecting muscle unloading and 
reduced axial effort. Meanwhile, wrist activity remains relatively stable across g-levels, implying 
that limb motion is less affected by gravitational changes than core stabilization. 
Methodologically, this work demonstrates the power of a literature-informed data synthesis 
approach. By anchoring our models to robust experimental findings, we were able to overcome 
the scarcity of real-world g-load data and create continuous, predictive functions. IT ts also 
improtant to not that the choice of two distinct modeling architectures was deliberate: the 
lightweight EEG Fourier MLP proved highly efficient for learning the complex EEG response 
curves, while the probabilistic nature of the Gaussian Processes provided essential uncertainty 
estimates for the noisier peripheral signals, a critical feature for any model intended for 
operational use.  
The two models provide a consistent and complementary picture of the multi-system human 
response to altered gravity. The high-arousal states inferred from the strong EDA increases at

mu-rhythm suppression is directly supported by the IGP-Physio model's prediction of

multi-system framework for quantifying the continuous human response to gravity. Overall, 
integrating the outputs from our quantitative deep learning and probabilistic models with 
qualitative analysis, such as the simulation of awareness in LLMs like Claude, provides a 
comprehensive framework for understanding the multifaceted impact of gravitational variations 
on human performance. This combined approach allowed us to probe both quantitative modeling 
and qualitative insights in advancing our understanding of alertness and self-awareness under

Together, the EEG Fourier MLP and IGP-Physio models provide a convergent view of how 
gravity modulates neural and physiological states. Both models reveal that microgravity (0 g) 
and hypergravity (1.8 g) elicit comparable high-arousal profiles across systems, while 1.0 g 
represents a neurophysiological baseline of homeostatic equilibrium. The EEG model 
successfully learned non-linear g-dependent dynamics, capturing alpha and mu suppression in 
microgravity—consistent with disrupted resting-state cognition and postural unloading—and

In the current study, we designed a structured prompt for the large language model Claude 3.5 
Sonnet to simulate human subjective experience across a range of gravitational environments. 
The prompt was framed from a first-person perspective and incorporated physical, physiological, 
and cognitive parameters, including key biological markers such as heart rate variability, postural 
stability, vestibular load, and sensorimotor feedback. Crucially, we also specified mental state 
descriptors related to alertness, bodily self-awareness, and cognitive clarity, aiming to elicit 
introspective narratives that reflect shifts in consciousness under different gravitational forces. 
The simulated scenarios included a broad spectrum of gravitational loads—from microgravity 
(0g) to partial gravity environments such as the Moon (0.17g) and Mars (0.38g), and up to 
hypergravity conditions such as take-off at 4g and re-entry at 6g. Claude was asked to integrate 
the input parameters and generate phenomenological descriptions of what a human subject might

Astronaut Training and Practical Implications

One immediate application of this framework lies in enhancing astronaut training through 
advanced cognitive and physiological monitoring tools. The lightweight EEG Fourier MLP 
model is particularly well-suited for real-time deployment on wearable devices during high-G 
centrifuge training or VR-based gravity simulations. Its minimal computational footprint enables 
continuous monitoring of awareness-related neural patterns—such as suppression of DMN alpha 
rhythms or surges in frontal beta—which can help detect early signs of cognitive instability, 
including attentional drift or disorientation (Pusil et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). When paired 
with the more comprehensive physiological GP model, this creates a synergistic dual-mode 
system for tracking multi-system responses to g-load. Real-time cortical monitoring via the MLP, 
combined with uncertainty-aware autonomic and sensorimotor profiling via the GP model, offers 
a powerful infrastructure for both acute intervention and longitudinal health tracking. For 
example, VR-based simulations could dynamically adapt task difficulty in response to predicted 
cognitive load or autonomic arousal, providing targeted training tailored to the individual’s 
neurophysiological profile (Varandas et al., 2022). This research advances the development of 
predictive, personalized tools for monitoring cognitive resilience.  
These models let us quantify each crewmember’s gravity-awareness, sensory weightings, and 
control effort, to then adapt training to that profile. In practice: (i) use real-time EEG (e.g., 
alpha/β, mu-ERD) and physiological GP models (EDA/HRV) to build an individual 
gravity-adaptation fingerprint (baseline 1 g, micro-/hyper-g responses, recovery kinetics); (ii) 
drive closed-loop VR/centrifuge scenarios that titrate g-load, visual–vestibular conflict, and task 
demand to target the trainee’s weakest points (e.g., down-weight over-dominant visual priors, 
strengthen vestibular reliability); (iii) deliver neurofeedback and task-level coaching when 
biomarkers cross risk thresholds (attentional drift, rising autonomic strain); (iv) apply

meta-learning or curriculum to schedule exposures for fastest prior-updating and retention; and 
(v) use optimal-control metrics (movement variability, effort costs) to set mastery criteria. The 
result is a personalized, data-driven pipeline that accelerates adaptation, raises G-tolerance, 
reduces motion sickness, and preserves executive function under load—precisely the edge 
high-performing individuals need for deep-space operations. 
By integrating these models with structured LLM-based simulations, it becomes possible to 
identify individuals more susceptible to awareness degradation or G-induced loss of 
consciousness that also decreases risks of this effect. Such a system could be embedded in 
pre-flight screening or centrifuge training protocols to detect vulnerabilities and enable targeted 
countermeasures (Martins et al., 2021). For missions at the most extreme gravity loads, cognitive 
self-monitoring informed by this framework could be implemented: astronauts showing neural 
signatures associated with high strain (e.g., diminished DMN alpha rhythms) might be prompted 
to engage in countermeasures to restore cognitive stability. 
Limitations and Future Studies 
There are several limitations to this study. First, the work is based on models trained on 
literature-derived data anchors rather than continuous, real-world physiological datasets. While 
this approach provides a robust proof-of-concept, the next essential step is to validate and 
fine-tune these models using empirical data from parabolic flights, centrifuges, or actual 
spaceflight to assess their real-world robustness. Second, the LLM simulation, while insightful, 
was based on a limited set of prompts. Future studies should expand this to include more diverse 
and temporally extended scenarios, such as simulating adaptation during long-duration 
microgravity exposure. 
49


## Page 50


So, overall, the limitation of this study is its reliance on synthetic data, albeit data grounded in 
real-world measurements. The models have learned the regularities in the data from the literature 
with high fidelity; the crucial next step is to validate their predictive performance on continuous, 
multi-modal physiological data recorded from actual parabolic flights. Furthermore, our models 
use g-load as the sole independent variable, necessarily simplifying the complex interplay of 
psychological stress, task demands, and inter-individual differences that influence physiology in 
real-world scenarios. Future work should aim to incorporate these factors to build more 
comprehensive and personalized models of astronaut health. Furthermore, our quantitative 
models were trained on data anchored up to 1.8g, which does not capture the severe 
physiological events that occur at higher accelerations. The models can predict trends of 
increasing stress, but they cannot currently predict the critical threshold of G-induced Loss of 
Consciousness (G-LOC). Future work should aim to incorporate anchor points from centrifuge 
studies of 2g and higher and pilot data to model the non-linear physiological cascade leading to 
G-LOC, including the characteristic visual symptoms and cardiovascular responses (Erdem & 
Demir, 2006; Singh & Bhasin, 2014). 
Building on this foundation, future research should focus on empirical validation. Integrating 
multi-modal data streams—EEG, HRV, EDA, motion tracking—from real subjects into these 
models would allow for richer, more personalized predictions of embodied cognition. Finally, we 
encourage the use of this framework to drive adaptive biofeedback  creating personalized 
training protocols that can trial and enhance individual resilience, situational awareness, and 
mental preparedness for future space missions.
