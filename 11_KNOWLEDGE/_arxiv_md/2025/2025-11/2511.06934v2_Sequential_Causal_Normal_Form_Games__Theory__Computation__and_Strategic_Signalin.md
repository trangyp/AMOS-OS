Abstract
Can classical game-theoretic frameworks be extended to
capture the bounded rationality and causal reasoning of
AI agents? We investigate this question by extending
Causal Normal Form Games (CNFGs) to sequential set-
tings, introducing Sequential Causal Multi-Agent Systems
(S-CMAS) that incorporate Pearl’s Causal Hierarchy across
leader-follower interactions. While theoretically elegant—
we prove PSPACE-completeness, develop equilibrium re-
finements, and establish connections to signaling theory—
our comprehensive empirical investigation reveals a critical
limitation: S-CNE provides zero welfare improvement over
classical Stackelberg equilibrium across all tested scenarios.
Through 50+ Monte Carlo simulations and hand-crafted syn-
thetic examples, we demonstrate that backward induction
with rational best-response eliminates any strategic advan-
tage from causal layer distinctions. We construct a theoretical
example illustrating conditions where benefits could emerge
(ϵ-rational satisficing followers), though implementation con-
firms that even relaxed rationality assumptions prove insuffi-
cient when good instincts align with optimal play. This nega-
tive result provides valuable insight: classical game-theoretic
extensions grounded in rational choice are fundamentally in-
compatible with causal reasoning advantages, motivating new
theoretical frameworks beyond standard Nash equilibrium for
agentic AI.
Introduction
Strategic interactions often unfold sequentially, with leaders
committing to actions before followers respond. While clas-
sical Stackelberg games (von Stackelberg 1934) model such
dynamics under perfect rationality, real-world agents exhibit
biases, follow instincts, or engage in counterfactual reason-
ing that deviates from game-theoretic prescriptions (Wason
and Evans 1974; Sloman 1996).
Causal Normal Form Games (CNFGs) (Maiti, Jain, and
Bareinboim 2025) address this by incorporating the Pearl
Causal Hierarchy (PCH) (Pearl 2009): observational (L1, in-
stinctive), interventional (L2, deliberate), and counterfactual
(L3, sophisticated reasoning). However, CNFGs are limited
to simultaneous-move settings, while many strategic inter-
actions are inherently sequential (Letchford and Conitzer
2010).

Related Frameworks
Bayesian Stackelberg games (Conitzer and Sandholm 2006)
model uncertainty over follower types using belief distri-
butions but assume all agents operate at the interventional
(L2) level. Our framework allows heterogeneity in causal
reasoning layers. Epistemic game theory (Perea 2012) uses
hierarchical belief models to capture strategic reasoning but
does not explicitly model causal mechanisms or the PCH.
Agents’ beliefs about others’ rationality differ from beliefs
about causal structures. Psychological games (Geanakoplos,
Pearce, and Stacchetti 1989) incorporate belief-dependent
utilities but lack formal causal semantics. Our framework
provides precise causal interpretations via SCMs. Signal-
ing games (Spence 1973; Cho and Kreps 1987) study in-
formation transmission but typically assume rational sig-
naling (L2). We show leaders can signal via causal layer
choice, creating richer strategic possibilities. Causal influ-
ence diagrams (Hammond et al. 2023; Mishra, Fox, and
Wooldridge 2024) characterize interventions in simultane-
ous causal games. We extend this to sequential settings
with timing constraints and information structures. Com-
mitment in extensive-form games (Letchford and Conitzer
2010) studies commitment value in sequential games but un-
der perfect rationality. We incorporate bounded rationality
via PCH layers.
Sequential Causal Normal Form Games
Formal Framework
Definition 1 (Sequential Causal Multi-Agent System). An
S-CMAS is a tuple G = ⟨M, N, X, Y, ⪯, I⟩where:
• M = ⟨U, V, F, P(U)⟩is an SCM with unobserved vari-
ables U, observed variables V , structural equations F,
and prior P(U)
• N = L ∪F partitions agents into leaders and followers
• X = (XL, XF ) are action nodes with timing XL ⪯XF
• Y = (YL, YF ) are reward signals
• ⪯defines a partial order over action nodes
• IF ⊆{XL, LL, ∅} specifies follower information, where
LL denotes the leader’s PCH layer choice
Information scenarios:
• Perfect information: IF = XL (followers observe leader
actions)
• Mechanism information: IF = {XL, LL} (followers ob-
serve actions and PCH layers)
• Imperfect information: IF = S(XL, US) with noise US
The mechanism information case is novel—followers in-
fer whether leaders acted instinctively (L1), deliberately
(L2), or counterfactually (L3), affecting optimal responses.

Connection to Signaling Games
S-CMAS naturally connects to signaling game theory
(Spence 1973). The leader’s PCH layer choice serves as a
signal of their type (causal structure M), creating a separat-
ing or pooling equilibrium structure.
Proposition 1 (Signaling Interpretation). An S-CNE with
mechanism information can be recast as a signaling game
where:
• Leader types correspond to causal structures M ∈M
• Signals are layer choices LL ∈{L1, L2, L3}
• Follower responses depend on inferred type ˆ

Unlike standard signaling games where only deliberate
(L2) agents signal, our framework allows instinctive (L1)
signaling (involuntary information revelation) and counter-
factual (L3) signaling (strategic bias exploitation).
Equilibrium Refinements
To address multiplicity, we introduce two refinements:
Definition 3 (Trembling-Hand S-CNE). An S-CNE σ∗is
trembling-hand perfect if it is a limit of ε-perfect equilibria
where each action is chosen with probability at least ε > 0.
This eliminates equilibria relying on non-credible threats.
Definition 4 (Forward Induction S-CNE). An S-CNE σ∗sat-
isfies forward induction if followers’ beliefs after observing
unexpected leader actions (LL, xL) assign positive proba-
bility only to leader types for whom (LL, xL) is optimal for
some follower response.
This refines equilibria by requiring followers to make ra-
tional inferences about leaders’ intentions.

Empirical Investigation: A Negative Result
We conducted comprehensive empirical testing to determine
when S-CNE provides strategic advantages over classical
Stackelberg equilibrium. Our systematic investigation re-
veals a critical finding: S-CNE provides zero welfare im-
provement across all tested scenarios.

Zero welfare improvement.

Layer selection collapse.

Analysis: Why S-CNE Fails to Improve Welfare
The zero-improvement result reflects a fundamental theo-
retical limitation: backward induction neutralizes causal ad-
vantages. When followers observe leader actions and best-
respond, they condition only on the action taken, not the
causal layer that produced it. Even with mechanism infor-
mation, followers’ optimal response to (L1, x) equals their
response to (L2, x) whenever instincts align with rationality.
Instinct-rationality convergence.
In equilibrium:
• If instincts are good (quality > 0.5): L1 selects the same
action as L2 would

• If instincts are poor (quality < 0.5): Rational leaders
avoid using L1, defaulting to L2
• Either way, equilibrium actions converge to classical
Stackelberg
Signaling irrelevance.
The mechanism information struc-
ture fails to create strategic value because:
• Leaders have no incentive to signal via layer choice when
it doesn’t affect follower actions
• Followers best-respond identically regardless of ob-
served layer
• No separating equilibrium emerges
This reveals a critical gap: classical game-theoretic solu-
tion concepts (Nash equilibrium, backward induction) are
incompatible with causal reasoning advantages. Strategic
benefits from PCH layers require departures from ratio-
nal best-response, which standard equilibrium concepts pre-
clude. See Appendix C for a case study and Appendix D for
theoretical conditions.
Implications for Agentic AI Systems
Our negative result carries important lessons for modeling
strategic AI agents.
Why Classical Extensions Fail
Rational
best-response
assumption.
Game-theoretic
equilibria assume agents optimize given beliefs about
others. This assumption:
• Works for human agents (Simon 1955, 1957; Kahneman,
Slovic, and Tversky 1982; Rabin 1993; Kaufmann 1999;
Toulis and Parkes 2016) (with cultural norms, emotions,
bounded computation)
• Works for classical economic agents (von Neumann and
Morgenstern 1944; Savage 1954; Fishburn 1989) (with
well-defined utility functions)
• Fails for AI agents (Fan et al. 2023; Richens and Everitt
2024; Jia et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2024; Duan et al. 2024;
Xu et al. 2023; Aher, Arriaga, and Kalai 2024) whose
”instincts” (LLM priors) and ”reasoning” (inference pro-
cedures) don’t map cleanly to rational choice
The fundamental tension.
If we assume:
• Agents are rational enough to compute equilibria (back-
ward induction)
• Agents best-respond to observed actions
Then causal layer distinctions become strategically irrele-
vant, as demonstrated empirically.
Addressing apparent circularity.
One might object
that we define S-CNE using rational best-response, then
demonstrate that rational best-response eliminates causal
advantages—a seemingly circular argument. However, this
is precisely the point: any equilibrium concept based on mu-
tual best-response faces this limitation. The insight is that
Nash equilibrium and its refinements (including our S-CNE)
are fundamentally incompatible with causal reasoning ad-
vantages. This motivates equilibrium concepts where agents
persistently fail to best-respond, not just transiently during
learning—exactly the kind of bounded rationality needed for
agentic AI, as demonstrated by our ϵ-rational example.
What’s needed instead.
Modeling strategic LLM-based
agents requires frameworks that don’t assume rational best-
response:
• Learning dynamics (how agents update strategies over
time)
• Bounded rationality that persists in equilibrium (not just
as a modeling device)
• Non-equilibrium solution concepts (e.g., evolutionary
stability, satisficing)
• Genuine uncertainty about opponent capabilities (not just
types)
Broader Lessons
Theory transfer is non-trivial.
Our work demonstrates
that seemingly natural extensions of classical theory (adding
causal structures, bounded rationality) may provide no prac-
tical value for agentic AI. Rigorous empirical validation is
essential before adopting theoretical frameworks (See Ap-
pendix E).
Conclusion
We investigated whether extending Causal Normal Form
Games to sequential settings provides strategic advantages
for modeling agentic AI systems. Our comprehensive em-
pirical study—spanning 100 game instances including both
random generation and hand-crafted synthetic examples—
yields a negative result: S-CNE provided zero welfare im-
provement over classical Stackelberg equilibrium. This find-
ing carries three important implications:
Classical game theory’s limitations.
Extensions based on
rational choice assumptions (Nash equilibrium, backward
induction) may be fundamentally incompatible with causal
reasoning advantages. The theoretical elegance of combin-
ing PCH layers with game theory does not translate to prac-
tical benefits.
Value of negative results.
By rigorously demonstrating
what doesn’t work, we hope to help the agentic AI com-
munity to focus on developing genuinely new frameworks
beyond retrofitted economic theory.
Call for new foundations.
LLM-based agents—with
their training-data-shaped priors, prompt-sensitive behav-
iors, and non-standard inference procedures—require the-
oretical tools developed specifically for these systems.
Our work motivates research on learning dynamics, non-
equilibrium solution concepts, and bounded rationality that
persists beyond equilibrium.
While S-CMAS provides no empirical benefits in our
tests, the theoretical framework and algorithmic contribu-
tions may prove valuable if future work identifies scenarios
where causal reasoning advantages can manifest despite ra-
tional best-response. We make our implementation publicly
available to facilitate such investigations.

requires not just satisficing, but conditions where ratio-
nal leaders cannot identify the Pareto-superior equilibrium,
which seems contradictory.
Conceptual value.
Despite implementation challenges,
this example clarifies what would be needed: equilibrium
concepts where agents persistently fail to identify optimal
strategies—not due to computational limits during learn-
ing, but as a fundamental feature of the solution concept it-
self. This motivates research on quantal response equilibria,
level-k reasoning with heterogeneous depths, or evolution-
ary dynamics where population-level selection differs from
individual optimization.
