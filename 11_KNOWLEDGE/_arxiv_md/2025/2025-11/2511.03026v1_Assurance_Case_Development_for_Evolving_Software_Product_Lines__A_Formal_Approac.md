In critical software engineering, structured assurance cases (ACs) are used to demonstrate how key system
properties are supported by evidence (e.g., test results, proofs). Creating rigorous ACs is particularly challenging
in the context of software product lines (SPLs), i.e, sets of software products with overlapping but distinct
features and behaviours. Since SPLs can encompass very large numbers of products, developing a rigorous AC
for each product individually is infeasible. Moreover, if the SPL evolves, e.g., by the modification or introduction
of features, it can be infeasible to assess the impact of this change. Instead, the development and maintenance
of ACs ought to be lifted such that a single AC can be developed for the entire SPL simultaneously, and be
analyzed for regression in a variability-aware fashion. In this article, we describe a formal approach to lifted
AC development and regression analysis. We formalize a language of variability-aware ACs for SPLs and
study the lifting of template-based AC development. We also define a regression analysis to determine the
effects of SPL evolutions on variability-aware ACs. We describe a model-based assurance management tool
which implements these techniques, and illustrate our contributions by developing an AC for a product line of
medical devices.

Introduction
In many software engineering contexts, stakeholders require assurance that software products
will operate as intended. Several industries (e.g., automotive), have developed safety standards
(e.g., ISO 26262 [28]) requiring careful documentation of verification activities via assurance cases
(ACs) [44]. ACs use structured argumentation to refine system-level requirements into lower-level
specifications that be supported directly by evidence artifacts (e.g., tests, proofs). Given their role in
safety and reliability engineering, great care must be taken to verify the correctness of an AC prior
to system deployment. While ACs are traditionally developed and reviewed manually, using natural
language, portions of ACs can be sufficiently formalized that formal methods can be leveraged to
verify their correctness [55, 57]. Nonetheless, the development of rigorous ACs, and systematically
analyzing their regression, remains a challenging problem.
Simultaneously, software systems are increasing in scale and complexity. In many cases, companies
are not developing individual software products, but a family of related products, i.e., a software

In SPL engineering (SPLE), lifting is the process of redefining a product-based analysis (e.g., testing,

as indicated by presence conditions) is handled explicitly. Applying a lifted analysis produces a
variability-aware analysis result, which concisely encodes distinct analysis results for each product
of the SPL. Lifted analyses generally offer dramatic speedups compared to product-based methods.

Assurance Case Templates. One of the limitations of informal assurance case development
is that the correctness of ACs needs to be validated manually, which is difficult, expensive and
error-prone. If we instead impose formalization as part of AC development, we can use formal
methods to verify the correctness of ACs. This has been an active research area in assurance

2.2
Software Product Lines
A software product line (SPL) is a family of software artifacts (products) with distinct (but often
overlapping) structure and behaviour [4]. The variability of an SPL is defined in terms of a set 𝐹of
features, each of which can be either present or absent in a given product. A product is obtained
from the SPL by choosing a configuration 𝔠⊆𝐹of features. In this work, we focus on annotative
product lines, in which elements of software products – lines of code, model elements, etc. – are
annotated to indicate the products for which they are used.
Annotative product line engineering makes extensive use of feature expressions, i.e., propositional
expressions whose atomic propositions are features. Given a set of features 𝐹, we let Prop(𝐹) denote
the set of feature expressions over 𝐹. When used to annotate a product element, a feature expression
is called a presence condition. There is a natural entailment relation between configurations and
feature expressions; given 𝔠⊆𝐹and 𝜙∈Prop(𝐹), we say that 𝔠satisfies 𝜙(denoted by 𝔠⊨𝜙) iff 𝜙
evaluates to ⊤when all features in 𝔠are replaced by ⊤, and all other features replaced by ⊥. Given
a feature expression 𝜙over 𝐹, we let J𝜙K denote the set of configurations satisfying 𝜙. In a given
product line, not all possible combinations of features may be desired. A feature model Φ defines the
set of configurations which are considered “valid” in a given product line. The feature model can
be defined in any number of manners: graphically, i.e., via a feature diagram [46]; textually, i.e., via
feature modeling DSLs, such as the Universal Variability Language [9]; or as a feature expression.
In this work, we define feature models as feature expressions.
Any modeling language for software products can be extended to represent product lines. For
example, a product line of LTSs can be represented as a Featured Transition System (FTS) [16].
Example 2.3. Fig. 3 illustrates an FTS (adapted from [6]) over the feature set {A, B} with feature
model Φ = 𝐴xor 𝐵. The presence conditions of transitions are indicated to the right of the transition
labels. There are two valid configurations of this SPL, as JΦK = {{A}, {B}}. In Fig. 3, the transition
labeled by 𝑎is present under both configurations, since it is annotated by ⊤, while the transition
labeled by 𝑏is present only in configuration {B}.
While there are a multitude of ways in which a language can be extended to model product lines,
we adopt the following as a default representation of an annotative product line model (adapted
from [47]). Given any modeling language M and model 𝑀∈M, we let 𝐸𝑙𝑒𝑚(𝑀) denote the set of
elements of 𝑀.

Annotative product lines provide a concise representation of (possibly very many) individual
models. The downside of product line modeling is that existing (product-based) analyses cannot be
applied directly to product lines. If we want to analyze a product line model with a product-based
analysis, we can only do so in a brute-force fashion, deriving the set of valid products and analyzing
each of them independently. This is infeasible, as a product line with 𝑛features will encompass
up to 2𝑛products. It is also often wasteful, as different products may overlap significantly with
one another, so performing each analysis “from scratch” is typically not necessary. An alternative
strategy is to lift the analysis, i.e., redefine it so that it can be applied to the entire product line, and
produce equivalent results to brute-forced analysis.
Definition 2.6 (Lifting [39]). Let 𝑓: 𝑋→𝑌be a function. Fixing a feature model Φ, a function

3
Formalizing Product-Based AC Development
In this section, we formalize a framework for product-based AC development, which will form the
foundation of lifted AC development in Sec. 4. More precisely, we define a formal AC language
based on Goal Structuring Notation (GSN), introduced in Sec. 2.1, and formalize the correctness of
ACs in terms of evidence and argumentation (Sec. 3.1). We then formalize assurance case templates
and their correctness (Sec. 3.2). We conclude by introducing a special class of AC templates, called
analytic templates, which incorporate the semantics and results of software analysis directly into
an AC (Sec. 3.3). Unless noted otherwise, all definitions and theorems in this section are adapted
from [41]. Proofs of selected theorems not given here are provided in Appendix A.1.
3.1
Formalizing an Assurance Case Language
An assurance case serves to make and justify claims about a system and its suitability for operation.
To enable assurance case formalization, we consider model-based assurance cases, i.e., those in
which the primary subjects of assurance case claims are system models. In this work, we adopt
the model-driven engineering philosophy in which “everything is a model” [10]. Hence, the word
“model” can refer to traditional models, such as state machines and activity diagrams, as well as to
objects such as programs, strings, and sets.
In general, an assurance case goal can represent any proposition. However, in formalizing assurance
cases – and especially in formalizing assurance case templates – it is useful to model goals
predicatively, i.e., as the application of some predicate 𝑃to some subject (e.g., a system model). This
leads us to the following formal notion of assurance case goals.
Definition 3.1 (Goal). An assurance case goal 𝑔is given as either:

strategies. Evidence can take many forms: testing results, verification results, formal proofs, system

assurance case; it instead provides explanatory content to the reader, so that they can understand
what argument is being made. Accordingly, our formalization models strategies simply as opaque
labels. We let Str denote the set of all strategies.

3.3
Analytic Argumentation
Templates form an important component of rigorous AC development, since they enable veri-
fication of goal refinement. But another essential component is to actually analyze the system
under assurance – to determine facts about it, and to obtain assurance evidence. Rather than
viewing argumentation and analysis as disjoint activities, we propose analytic argumentation in
AC development, wherein the semantics of analyses used in producing assurance are articulated
explicitly in the AC. This serves two purposes. First, by making explicit how and why a given
analysis is used, the AC itself becomes more rigorous. Second, when we eventually move to lifting
AC development to product lines, the capability for lifted analyses to identify variation points in
the system can be leveraged to automatically embed variability into argumentation.
To this end, we formalize a special class of argument templates, which we refer to as analytic
templates, each of which is defined with respect to a fixed analysis 𝑓. In this work, we consider
analyses to be functions of the form 𝑓: 𝑋→𝑌, where 𝑋is the set of possible inputs to the
analysis (its domain) and 𝑌is the set of possible outputs of the analysis (its codomain). Intuitively,
an analytic argument makes assertions about the input to the analysis, the output of the analysis,
and the analysis itself.
Definition 3.12 (Analytic Template). Let 𝑓: 𝑋→𝑌be some analysis with domain 𝑋and codomain
𝑌, and let M be some modeling language. An analytic template over 𝑓is a template𝑇M,𝑋= ⟨𝑃, 𝐼,𝐶⟩,
where
• the parent predicate 𝑃: M →{⊤, ⊥} and correctness criterion 𝐶: 𝑋× M →{⊤, ⊥} have
the same meaning as in Def. 3.7, and
• the instantiation function 𝐼: 𝑋→P(Goal) is defined for any 𝑥∈𝑋as 𝐼(𝑥) = {𝑔𝑋,𝑔𝑌,𝑔𝑓},
where
𝑔𝑋= ⟨𝑥, 𝑃𝑋⟩,

assurance argumentation through the concept of analytic templates. Our aim in the next section
will be to lift this framework from the level of software products to software product lines.

Overview of Lifted AC Development
Fig. 7 illustrates the necessary activities and artifacts required to support lifted AC development.

outline which system requirements can be supported by testing, and which should be supported by
formal verification. The assurance plan can be informed by multiple factors, such as regulatory or
industrial safety standards, or the resources of the assurance team.

as a basis for defining corresponding analytic templates (Sec. 3.3). Other AC templates, such as

interested in assuring an individual software product, we could begin AC development at this stage,
combining the prescribed analyses and formalized templates to produce a rigorous AC. However, if
we are interested in assuring a product line, it is infeasible to produce an AC for each of its products
using only product-based methods.

formal AC templates which would otherwise be used in AC development. As mentioned in Sec. 1,

4.2
Formalizing a Language of Variational Assurance Cases
The semantics of traditional assurance cases are essentially Boolean, with a goal being either
supported or unsupported. The central idea underlying variational assurance cases is to generalize
these semantics to the level of sets – that is, sets of products in the product line.
We begin by defining variational goals. The variability inherent in an assurance goal can be both
structural and semantic. Structural variability refers to the fact that a goal may be relevant only for
some products and not others. Semantic variability refers to the fact that the interpretation of a
goal depends on the choice of a feature configuration. As one would expect, we can implement
structural variability via presence conditions; however, the formalization of semantic variability is
a bit more subtle.
Given that the interpretation of a “traditional” assurance goal is a Boolean proposition, in order
to properly “lift” the interpretation of goals for product lines, we require a variational goal to be
interpreted as a “product line” of propositions – one for each product. In the traditional setting,
we define a (predicative) goal in terms of the model 𝑀∈M which is the subject of the goal, and
a predicate 𝑃over M. What if instead we want to define a variational goal whose subject is a
product line model M ∈MPL? One may propose to apply a predicate 𝑃over MPL to M, but this
would not achieve the desired interpretation, since 𝑃(M) would still be an ordinary proposition,
rather than a product line of propositions. In fact, the desired interpretation can be achieved by
associating M with a predicate over M, i.e., a predicate over product models. The key observation is
that such a predicate 𝑃over M cannot be applied to M ∈MPL directly, but only once a particular
configuration 𝔠has been fixed. If we consider ⟨M, 𝑃⟩as constituting a variational goal g, we may
thus interpret g as a function from configurations to propositions. Alternatively, we may consider
the interpretation of g = ⟨M, 𝑃⟩to be the set of propositions

4.4
Lifting Analytic Templates
Just as the complexity of manually verifying variational goal refinements makes the use of lifted
templates strongly compelling, the complexity of analyzing product lines makes the use of lifted
analyses similarly compelling. Given the integration of analysis and argumentation through
analytic templates (Sec. 3.3), it is worth providing special attention to lifting this particular class of

5
A Semantic Approach to Assurance Case Regression
In the preceding sections, we formalized and lifted AC development. The primary motivation for
formalized ACs is the ability to verify their correctness. However, another benefit of formalization
becomes apparent when we need to consider the effect of a change on the AC. When ACs are
completely informal or unstructured, determining which aspects of system assurance have been
potentially compromised can be highly laborious and error-prone. By contrast, when ACs are
developed and interpreted formally, the regression of assurance (or its preservation) can be analyzed
mechanically. This is especially crucial in the context of ACs for product lines, where the complexity
of analyzing assurance regression manually quickly becomes intractable.
In this section, we study the regression of (product) ACs when system models are evolved, which
in turn will form the foundation of a lifted regression analysis in Sec. 6. Proofs of selected theorems
not given here are provided in Appendix A.3.
5.1
Specification of Product-Based Regression Analysis
In analyzing the regression of an AC, we are effectively analyzing the regression of assurance at
three levels of abstraction: at the level of evidence, at the level of arguments, and at the level of
the entire AC. In all cases, however, we use the same general notion of a regression value, adapted
from [32].
Definition 5.1 (Regression Value). Given a predicate 𝑃over a set 𝑋, let 𝑥∈𝑋such that 𝑃(𝑥). Let
𝑥′ ∈𝑋be some modification of 𝑥. A regression value for 𝑥′ with respect to 𝑃(𝑥) is one of the
following:
• “Reuse” (denoted ✓), indicating that 𝑃(𝑥′) holds.
• “Revise” (denoted ✗), indicating that 𝑃(𝑥′) does not hold.
• “Recheck” (denoted ?), indicating that we cannot determine whether 𝑃(𝑥′) holds.
A regression analysis, broadly speaking, is any analysis that determines a regression value following
some modification to some object (e.g., a program, a model, or an assurance case). Given a predicate
𝑃over 𝑋, we may speak formally of a regression analysis as a function 𝑅𝑃: 𝑋× 𝑋→{✓, ✗, ?}
such that, given any “old” 𝑥∈𝑋satisfying 𝑃(𝑥) and some “new’ 𝑥′ ∈𝑋, 𝑅𝑃(𝑥,𝑥′) computes a
regression value for 𝑥′ with respect to 𝑃(𝑥). Examples of regression analyses include program
equivalence checking (e.g., for regression of testing results [36]) and structural model analyses (e.g.,
for regression of model checking results [34]). Obviously, such analyses can be used to determine
whether individual pieces of evidence can be reused through a system evolution. But we can also
consider regression analysis of the assurance case itself, wherein the predicate we are analyzing is
its support (Def. 3.5).

determine the regression of a strategy (i.e., is annotated as “Recheck”), we immediately annotate all

manually to determine which of its children are reusable following the evolution, and only then is
it worthwhile to analyze their regression.

The Forward Pass: Regression of Arguments
As described above, the forward pass of the regression analysis is tasked with updating and
potentially revising the arguments of the AC. We consider the regression of each strategy according
to the means by which it was created: in particular, whether it was created through the use of
a formal template (in which case its regression can be formally analyzed), or whether it was
created by hand (in which case we assume its regression will be analyzed manually). This is also
where the distinction between argument-producing and evidence-producing analytic templates
becomes important. Recall from Sec. 3.3 that an application of an analytic template 𝑇M,𝑋for
analysis 𝑓: 𝑋→𝑌is evidence-producing if the subgoal 𝑔𝑌corresponding to the output of 𝑓can be

if 𝑔𝑌is the parent goal of a decomposition, i.e., 𝑔𝑌is supported by further argumentation rather
than by evidence. Note that we can automatically distinguish the two kinds of applications (i.e., the
resulting strategies) simply based on the structure of the AC, and that a given analytic template
may in principle have applications which are both evidence-producing and argument-producing.

analytic templates are re-instantiated, or a lazy strategy, in which some analytic templates are not
re-instantiated.

template is argument-producing, it should be eagerly re-instantiated, whereas an application which
is evidence-producing should not be re-instantiated. We justify our strategy with two observations.

depend on the results of the analysis. For example, considering the argumnet over querying shown

do not re-execute the analysis by re-instantiating the template, we cannot determine whether the
strategy enumerating over all the alarm states has regressed or not, since we do not know if there
are any new states in the evolved model. In this circumstance, it makes sense to re-instantiate the
template in order to analyze the completeness of the subsequent argument.

Handling Variability Evolution. At the start of this section, we noted that an evolution of a
product line model may also modify its feature model, or even the language of features over which
the feature model is expressed. The lifted regression analysis we have just defined only considers
the regression between two (sets of) product line models over the same features and feature model.
Let us begin generalizing our results to the more general kinds of product line evolutions. For the
sake of simplicity, let us assume that there is only a single product line model M in the AC for
which regression is being analyzed.
First, let us consider the case where M is a product line with feature model Φ, and it is evolved into
M′ with feature model Φ′. Suppose we have a variational AC A for M for which we have established
support across the entire product line, i.e., we have shown Supp↑(A). We now want to compute
a regression value v partitioning Φ′ which tells us for which configurations of the new product
line model A remains supported. We can do this as follows. First, we partition the new feature
model Φ′ into two disjoint sets of configurations: ΦNew = Φ′ ∧¬Φ, representing those configurations
which are present only under the new feature model, and ΦReuse = Φ′ ∧Φ, representing those
which are “kept” from the previous feature model. We then apply the lifted regression analysis
defined above to A, under the restricted feature model ΦReuse. The result is a variability-aware
regression value v = ⟨𝜙✓,𝜙✗,𝜙?⟩partitioning ΦReuse. To then extend this regression value to cover

to taking v ⊗⟨⊥, ΦNew, ⊥⟩, and simply reflects the fact that assurance of any configurations in ΦNew
is completely unsupported in A.

In this section, we defined a variability-aware regression analysis for product line ACs by lifting
the product-based regression analysis defined in Sec. 5. By using this lifted analysis, engineers
are able to determine not only which parts of the AC need to be revised following an evolution
of the product line, but also for which specific configurations this revision needs to occur. This is
particularly useful in contexts where the deployment of evolved systems is time-sensitive; based
on the outcome of the lifted regression analysis, the assurance team can approve the deployment
of the evolution for configurations which have not lost their assurance (represented by 𝜙✓), and
postpone deployment of the evolution for the remaining configurations until their assurance has
been re-verified.

MMINTA-PL: An Assurance Case Framework for Evolving Product Line Models
Any integration of formal methods as part of AC development should include extensive tool support,
as many AC developers are not formal methods experts. To this end, we have developed tool support
for lifted AC development and regression as part of an Eclipse-based model management framework.
Our tool aims to provide functionalities modeled on the framework outlined in Secs. 4 and 6. In
this section, we provide a brief overview of our tool (Sec. 7.1), and demonstrate its capabilities on a
small case study (Sec. 7.2).

System Details. Infusion pumps are devices used to administer medication or other fluids to patients.
As different patients may have different medical needs, it is natural to model an SPL of infusion
pumps with different optional features. We began from an existing Extended Finite State Machine
(EFSM) model of an infusion pump created as part of a multi-institute research project [1]. While
this model was not originally defined as an SPL, it was designed to model features and hazards
for an infusion pump in general, the authors noting that in general “no single device [...] has all of
the design features” [61]. We extended the EFSM to an SPL by mapping five optional features to
their associated states and transitions and annotating these elements with presence conditions. For
example, CHECK_INFUSION_RATE is an optional feature that allows a pump to monitor the current

that when an alarm is triggered (e.g., due to a dosage limit violation), the system will immediately
halt drug administration (i.e., will not be administering a dose immediately following the alarm).

7.3
Discussion
In developing the above AC fragment for the infusion pump SPL, we demonstrated the feasibility of
lifted AC development to support multi-layered AC development in a lifted fashion. We emphasize
two specific points. (1) By following the workflow outlined in Sec. 4.1, the operational process of
AC development becomes essentially the same as in product-level AC development. That is to say, it
suffices to know what one would do to assure a single product, and the lifted analyses and templates
can correctly generalize this knowledge to the SPL-level. This is due to the particular semantics we
have given to variational ACs, as outlined in Sec. 4. (2) Through analytic variational argumentation,
we are able to systematically identify variation points in the SPL which are relevant to the design
of the AC. For example, in our case study, the variation points identified by the lifted query led
systematically to structural variability in the AC (i.e., the subgoals of strategy S1 in Fig. 15).
One of the limitations of the tooling is that modeling languages and model-based analyses must
be defined natively in order to leverage the GVM. For instance, the lifted model checker is not a
natively defined model-based analysis, and its verification results are not automatically interpreted
as variational data (this contrasts with querying, which is a native model-based analysis). There is
currently no automated support for template verification at the time of instantiation; we assume
that all argument templates are verified manually ahead of time. We also have not implemented a
general-purpose template regression procedure in the spirit of Alg. 1, instead relying on specialized
regression procedures for individual templates, as we did for the combined query/enumeration
template used to generate Fig. 15.
With respect to the validity of our observations, we note that our partial AC is relatively narrow in
scope and was designed by the authors for the purpose of demonstrating lifted AC development
and regression. While we believe we have successfully demonstrated the feasibility of our lifted AC
development and maintenance, further empirical validation is required. Ideally, this evaluation can

processes may be more difficult to formalize and lift than those shown here.

Related Work
Analysis of Software Product Lines. Implementing scalable analyses of SPLs is one of the central
problems in SPLE. Thum et al. [53] divide SPL-level analysis strategies as either product-based
(e.g., brute-force or sampling-based techniques), feature-based (i.e., analyzing feature modules
independently and composing the results), or family-based (i.e., operating at the level of sets of
products). The lifted model checker FTS4VMC and lifted Viatra query engine described in Sec. 7.2
are examples of family-based analysis tools. Other family-based model checkers include SNIP [15],
mentioned in Sec. 4.4, and ProFeat, which is a variability-aware extension of the probabilistic
model checker PRISM. A technique for family-based model checking based on 𝜇-calculus has also
been developed for the model checker mCRL2 [52]. Lifted analyses are a specific form of SPL-level
analyses, which can be implemented using any of these three strategies. The regression analysis
procedure defined in Sec. 6 is an instance of family-based analysis. A wide variety of analyses have
been lifted in the SPLE literature, e.g. [15, 29, 45, 48, 54]. We have previously proposed a catalogue
of lifted analyses, distinguishing various approaches to their design and implementation [40]. Our
proposal for lifted AC development and regression analysis presupposes that lifting of assurance-
relevant analyses is feasible. As we have demonstrated in our case study, existing lifted analyses,
such as lifted model checking, can be used in lifted AC development; however, some assurance
scenarios may require novel lifted analyses.
Assurance Cases for Product Lines. Assurance cases for SPLs were studied by Habli and Kelly [26, 27],
who argued that safety-relevant variation points in the SPL should be reflected explicitly in the
product line AC. Habli and Kelly propose using GSN patterns and the modular GSN extension [27]
to represent product line ACs. Habli [26] also provides an SPL safety metamodel which allows
for variation points in the SPL to be traced explicitly to a PL AC. This approach was further
refined in De Oliveira et al. [19], introducing tool-supported generation of modular PL ACs from
feature-based system models and safety analyses. However, this approach does not provide a
distinction between product-level and SPL-level semantics or analysis, implicitly assuming that
analysis and reasoning are performed at the level of products. As an alternative to product-based
AC development, Habli [26] also considers a primarily feature-based AC development method, in
which AC modules are developed independently for each feature, and then the PL AC is obtained
by composing these modules. However, this method requires the assurance engineer to either
(a) identify and mitigate all potential feature interactions, which can devolve into brute-force
assurance engineering, or (b) tolerate an incomplete assessment of potential feature interactions.
By contrast, lifted AC development supports analysis and reasoning over all valid configurations of
the SPL, without resorting to product-level work. To the best of our knowledge, the only existing
variability-aware AC development process was proposed by Nešić et al. [42], which lifts contract-
based templates to PLs of component-based systems [42]. Our proposal for lifted AC development is
effectively an attempt to generalize the approach of Nešić et al. to arbitrary templates and analyses.
Formal Methods for Assurance Case Development. The most obvious use of formal methods for
AC development is for the production of evidence; well-established verification techniques such
as model checking [5] and deductive verification [33] provide invaluable evidence for formally
specified requirements. As we have mentioned above, the integration of formal methods with
AC development requires extensive tool support. The AC development tool AdvoCATE [21] uses

specifications, which are then grafted into the AC following a predefined template. The Evidential
Tool Bus (ETB) [17] gathers verification evidence from various external tools, and then creates ACs
from the bottom up using compositional rules written in a variant of Datalog. Formal methods can
also be used to ensure that instantiations of argument templates actually yield sound arguments.
As ACs and proofs are closely related, proof assistants have been used on several occasions to study
rigorous AC development. Rushby [43] demonstrated a proof-of-concept of how an AC could be
modelled using the proof assistant PVS. The AC editor D-Case was extended with a translation
to the Agda programming language, such that an AC could be specified as an Agda program to

Assurance Case Metamodel (SACM) as part of its documentation layer [25]. Finally, Viger et al. [57]
used Lean to study the correctness of decomposition templates for model-based ACs.
Change Impact Assessment and Regression for Assurance Cases. A number of different approaches
for analyzing the impact of changes have been proposed, including syntactic pattern detection
[12], the use of machine learning models trained on AC development data [35], the use of natural
language processing [38], and property-specific safety contracts over modular ACs [60] to identify
impacted portions of ACs. In terms of specifically model-based approaches, Annable et al. [3]
and Cârlan et al. [13] rely on existing traceability links between system and AC to provide both
semantic and syntactic change impact assessment at the product level; however, the procedure
defined in [13] targets a specific AC template for machine learning components, while [3] relies on
manual inspection of the semantics of the AC and system to eliminate false positives. In comparison,
our product-based assurance regression framework is generic for arbitrary formal templates, and
defines the semantics of the assurance directly as part of the template. Kokaly et al. [32] also rely on
a model-based approach to syntactically determine product-level AC elements to be marked as reuse,
recheck or revise. While their proposed approach is complete, it is not sound (i.e., false positives
may occur), although techniques to improve precision are discussed at a conceptual level. At the
level of product line assurance, Shahin et al. [49] lifted the work from [32] to the product-line level.
To do this, they model variational ACs as sets of AC elements annotated with presence conditions,
without any internal structure or semantics. By contrast, our lifted regression analysis is based on
a variational AC language with precisely defined structure and semantics, allowing us to provide a
more granular form of analysis which is nonetheless amenable to verification.
9
Conclusion
In this work, we formalized lifted AC development, an assurance engineering methodology for
software product lines. By formalizing a language of variability-aware ACs, we extend existing
formal approaches to AC development by lifting formal argument templates and software analyses
to the SPL-level. We demonstrated the feasibility and usefulness of our methodology by developing
a variational AC over a product line of medical devices.
We identify several avenues for future work. One is to further extend our model-based development
framework with support for additional lifted templates and analyses, and to integrate the modeling
layer with theorem proving support, which would enable template instantiations to be verified
automatically during AC development [57]. Another important line of work is the repair of argument
templates over model evolutions. Our template regression procedures allow for engineers to specify
new instantiations for templates given a change to a model (Algs. 1, 5), but does not specify how
these instantiations should be produced. This introduces an opportunity for argument repair and
