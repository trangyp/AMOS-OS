Abstract
As generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) automates coding tasks and expands
access to technical resources, this paper examines how GenAI-enabled coding automa-
tion, colloquially known as “vibecoding,” affects digital entrepreneurial entry and ven-
ture performance. We exploit ex-ante variation in ventures’ exposure to vibecoding
based on the product characteristics of their initial launches and estimate difference-
in-differences models around the diffusion of GenAI coding tools. Vibecoding increases
first-time launches and shortens time to launch, but economically viable entry rises
only where vibecoding augments, rather than fully automates, product development.
In these partially exposed product segments, viable entry increases by 11%, driven
entirely by ventures founded by individuals with STEM education or work experi-
ence, especially those whose most recent employment was outside middle management.
Among ventures launched before GenAI became widely accessible, performance gains
similarly concentrate among partially exposed ventures with engineering-intensive ini-
tial teams. Together, these results suggest that GenAI-enabled coding automation does
not eliminate the value of technical expertise. Instead, vibecoding creates the greatest
value when it complements internal engineering capabilities, allowing ventures to dele-
gate lower-level coding tasks to GenAI while shifting human effort toward higher-level
problem solving and dynamic adaptation.

Introduction
Since late 2022, large language models (LLMs) and generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)
have advanced rapidly in their ability to perform complex knowledge tasks once thought to
require human intelligence and creativity. One of their most immediate and visible applica-
tions has been software development, where GenAI systems increasingly assist with, generate,
and modify code (McKinsey & Company, 2025). In early 2025, the term “vibecoding” was
coined to describe a mode of software development in which users prompt LLMs to generate
code without fully reviewing the resulting codebase. Its rapid diffusion reflects a broader
transformation in software production. Tools such as GitHub Copilot and Cursor are now
embedded in professional software-development workflows, while platforms such as Replit,
Vercel, and Lovable allow users to build full-stack applications with minimal manual coding.
This shift is already visible in publicly observable software production: by 2024, one-third of
Python functions in open-source repositories in the United States were AI-generated, with
this share continuing to rise rapidly (Daniotti et al., 2026).
Vibecoding builds on a broader pattern in which successive generations of digital tech-
nologies have facilitated entrepreneurship by relaxing resource constraints and compressing
development processes in new venture creation (von Briel et al., 2018; Davidsson et al., 2020).
Cloud computing lowered the cost of early-stage experimentation and development, enabling
more software ventures to enter (Kerr et al., 2014; Ewens et al., 2018). Digital commerce
platforms such as Shopify similarly reduced the technical resources required to create and
operate e-commerce ventures by providing standardized infrastructure for online storefronts,
payment processing, and third-party integrations (Dushnitsky and Stroube, 2021; Stroube
and Dushnitsky, 2025).
Vibecoding represents a related but distinct technological shift. Rather than providing
standardized digital infrastructure, it expands entrepreneurs’ access to software-development
capabilities by automating code generation through LLMs. Because product development in
digital ventures often involves software tasks, access to frontier LLMs allows entrepreneurs to

automate complex technical work that would otherwise require human developers, reducing
development costs and accelerating venture creation. However, these effects are unlikely to
be uniform. By changing the cost and profitability of different business models, vibecoding
may shape which ideas entrepreneurs choose to commercialize. By changing how software-
development tasks are performed, it may also shape which venture team compositions best
complement GenAI-enabled production. Its impact should therefore depend on complemen-
tary assets within venture teams, which influence both the ideas entrepreneurs can profitably
pursue and their ability to reorganize technical production around GenAI.
As GenAI becomes widely accessible, competitive advantage may depend less on access
to the technology itself than on ventures’ complementary assets, including the human cap-
ital accumulated by founders and early employees (Teece, 1986; Barney, 2001). This issue
is especially salient for digital ventures, where engineering expertise has traditionally been
central to product development. Existing evidence on GenAI’s distributional effects remains
mixed. Task-level studies show larger productivity gains for lower-skilled workers (Brynjolf-
sson et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2026), whereas evidence from entrepreneurial settings suggests
that higher-performing entrepreneurs may benefit more from AI adoption (Otis et al., 2024;
Conti and Messinese, 2025). Practitioner and media narratives often portray GenAI coding
tools as democratizing entrepreneurship by enabling non-technical founders to turn ideas into
functional software (Cook, 2026), a view reinforced by strong investor interest in startups
such as Replit and Lovable (Reuters, 2025; Heim, 2025). At the same time, fully automated
software development may introduce defects, cybersecurity risks, and maintenance burdens
that require human technical review (Moore, 2025; Chismon, 2026). These competing argu-
ments suggest that vibecoding may not simply flatten skill differences. Its effects on venture
entry and performance may instead depend on whether GenAI-enabled coding automation
substitutes for or complements engineering expertise, and on how entrepreneurs choose both
what products to build and which production tasks to automate. To date, systematic evi-
dence remains limited on whether vibecoding democratizes digital entrepreneurship or am-

To answer these questions empirically, we collect rich data from Product Hunt, a leading
online platform where digital ventures launch products, test market interest, and attract
early adopters. Product Hunt is particularly well suited to our setting because it offers a
global window into early-stage digital venture creation, whereas most research databases
disproportionately capture more mature, venture-backed firms. It is also one of the most
prominent launch platforms for digital products, and featured ventures often experience
pronounced spikes in website traffic after launch (Cao et al., 2024). Platform-level statistics
show a marked increase in new product launches in recent years, with timing that closely
coincides with the broader diffusion of GenAI tools, making Product Hunt a useful setting
for studying whether GenAI-enabled coding tools lower barriers to entrepreneurial entry
and accelerate product development. We enrich these data by linking founder identities to
Revelio Labs to measure demographics, education, and employment histories, and by linking
venture website domains to Semrush to track post-launch customer traction through website
visits. For ventures matched to Revelio Labs through their domains, we also construct a
quarterly post-launch panel of employee headcount.
Because vibecoding diffused broadly through many GenAI tools and platforms, adop-
tion is difficult to observe directly and is unlikely to provide a clean source of identifying
variation. We therefore define treatment and control groups using ex-ante product char-
acteristics that determine the extent to which a venture’s first product is susceptible to
GenAI-enabled coding automation. We map pre-GenAI Product Hunt launch descriptions
into an embedding space, cluster products into segments, and classify each segment by its
exposure to vibecoding. Automated segments are those in which vibecoding can automate
all core product-development tasks; augmented segments are those in which vibecoding can
automate some components while leaving others dependent on human implementation, re-

Our findings shed light on how GenAI lowers barriers to digital venture creation while
shifting the sources of competitive advantage among new ventures. Consistent with vibecod-
ing expanding access to technical resources by automating lower-level coding tasks, we show
that it accelerates product development and reduces the time from website creation to first
launch. Yet because these tools are broadly available, access to them alone is unlikely to
create sustained differentiation (Csaszar, 2025). Our findings instead align with theories
of complementary assets (Teece, 1986) and task interdependence (Kremer, 1993): ventures

As GenAI becomes more capable
and accessible, advantage may increasingly depend on tasks and capabilities that remain

disproportionately from the human capital of founders and early employees, especially be-

This study contributes to the literature on how IT-sector innovations lower barriers to
entrepreneurship by expanding access to key resources for venture creation (Kerr et al., 2014;
Ewens et al., 2018; von Briel et al., 2018; Davidsson et al., 2020; Dushnitsky and Stroube,
2021; Stroube and Dushnitsky, 2025). We extend this literature by studying GenAI-enabled
vibecoding as a distinct form of digital resource provision. Unlike earlier digital resources,
such as cloud computing or e-commerce tools, which provide standardized infrastructure
managed by third parties and accessed externally by entrepreneurs (Ewens et al., 2018;
Dushnitsky and Stroube, 2021), vibecoding is higher in relationality and lower in specificity
(von Briel et al., 2018; Davidsson et al., 2020): it responds flexibly to open-ended prompts,
generates a wide range of software outputs, and integrates with multiple platforms, APIs,
and deployment infrastructures.
Rather than fully substituting for software-development expertise, our results suggest
that vibecoding augments human expertise in product areas where development cannot be
entirely automated. This pattern is consistent with task-complementarity logic from O-ring
theory (Kremer, 1993): when vibecoding automates some but not all product-development
tasks, the remaining non-automatable tasks become bottlenecks, increasing the value of
scarce engineering expertise for system design, security and reliability, performance opti-
mization, physical-world integration, frontier algorithmic development, and specialized do-
main work. When product development is fully automatable, by contrast, broad access to
vibecoding is less likely to generate sustainable profit opportunities, because customers can

We also contribute by taking a different approach to quantifying exposure to GenAI-

Although all ventures in our setting involve

and non-software ventures (Asam and Heller, 2025).
Our research also contributes to the literature on the organizational impact of AI innova-
tion, especially the role of complementary assets in shaping uneven gains across organizations
(Tambe, 2014; Brynjolfsson and McElheran, 2016; Choudhury et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022;
Conti and Messinese, 2025). We focus on digital entrepreneurial ventures, where founders
can incorporate vibecoding into product development and establish GenAI-enabled work
processes from an early stage. This setting differs from studies of established organizations,
where AI adoption often requires gradual, frictional process innovation and organizational
adjustment (Bresnahan et al., 2002). Consistent with prior research showing that internal
worker skills are critical to value capture during the early diffusion of frontier technologies
(Bresnahan et al., 2002; Tambe, 2014; Huang et al., 2022), we find that engineering exper-
tise may become a source of competitive advantage as GenAI capabilities continue to evolve.
Our results align with the resource-based view that broadly available GenAI outputs alone
are unlikely to create durable competitive advantage (Csaszar, 2025). Instead, early gains
from vibecoding accrue primarily to engineering-heavy ventures, where founders and early

Our findings also have practical implications for technology ventures in the age of AI.
Because GenAI capabilities are increasingly accessible to entrepreneurs, customers, and com-
petitors alike, access to these tools alone is unlikely to yield a sustainable competitive ad-
vantage. Ventures are more likely to capture value when GenAI automates only part of
production, enabling entrepreneurs to delegate automatable tasks to vibecoding while redi-
recting human effort toward activities that require scarce, inimitable, and internally devel-
oped complementary expertise. In the IT sector, where technological change has repeatedly
required workers to retrain as older skills become obsolete (Bapna et al., 2013; Horton and
Tambe, 2025), these dynamic capabilities shape how effectively digital entrepreneurs adapt
to continuous advances in GenAI.

Related Literature and Theoretical Background
2.1
Vibecoding and the Viability of Digital Venture Creation
Recent advances in Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), driven by large language mod-
els, have enabled capabilities that increasingly approximate aspects of human intelligence
and creativity (Grimes et al., 2023; Eloundou et al., 2024). As a result, GenAI can automate
a growing range of complex knowledge tasks, from analytical problem-solving to high-quality
content generation. One of its most visible effects has been in software development, where
low-code tools and platforms powered by large language models are reshaping development
8


## Page 9


workflows. The term “vibecoding,” which describes the practice of prompting large language
models (LLMs) to generate software code and iterating on the output without inspecting or
understanding the code fully, was first introduced by the AI researcher and influencer Andrej
Karpathy and quickly adopted by developers worldwide.
Since 2022, a growing set of GenAI-enabled coding tools and platforms has expanded
access to AI-assisted software development. Early tools such as GitHub Copilot and Cursor
assist developer through code generation, autocomplete, and revision, while Replit’s launch
of Ghostwriter in October 2022 brought similar capabilities to a browser-based environment
for building web applications. Since 2023, newer platforms such as Replit Agent, Vercel’s
v0, Bolt, and Lovable have further broadened this ecosystem by enabling users to prototype
interfaces, scaffold full applications, and debug software with minimal manual coding. These
platforms connect to LLMs from frontier AI labs (e.g., OpenAI and Anthropic) and allow
entrepreneurs to use them in combination for prototyping and iterative development.
Vibecoding can sharply increase the productivity of digital solution builders by automat-
ing code generation for a broad range of well-defined tasks. Recent evidence shows that
AI-generated code has proliferated rapidly in public repositories, with more than 30% of
open-source Python code in the United States generated by AI by the end of 2024 (Dan-
iotti et al., 2026). Reflecting this broader shift, Paul Graham, founder of Y Combinator,
remarked on X in April 2026 that AI-written code by startups in each Y Combinator batch
have exceeded 75% “at least a year ago, maybe two”. GenAI-powered development work-
flows improve upon the constraints of earlier low-code tools (Br¨uhl et al., 2023) by enabling
users to own and control their codebases, flexibly customize them, and integrate them freely
with external systems.
These capabilities suggest that vibecoding may not only raise developer productivity,
but also lower barriers to digital venture creation. Prior research shows that the decision
to enter entrepreneurship depends on the expected returns to venture creation relative to
outside options (Amit et al., 1995; ˚Astebro et al., 2011; Cao and Lyu, 2025), and that
9


## Page 10


technological innovations can increase entry in software markets by lowering the cost of
acquiring key inputs (Kerr et al., 2014; Ewens et al., 2018).
In this setting, vibecoding
operates as a technical resource for venture development by automating code generation and
completion, thereby reducing development costs, lowering reliance on hired programming
labor, accelerating product and feature development, and enabling more iterations of testing
and user feedback prior to launch.
However, many ventures are only partially exposed to vibecoding, such as digital ven-
tures that operate at the intersection of software and specialized domains such as finance or
healthcare, where success depends on deep domain expertise and proprietary data that are
not available to outsiders. Others rely on software only peripherally, such as consultancy
firms that may need a simple website displaying contact information, while their primary
value creation comes from providing services to clients.
Likewise, ventures that develop
physical products – such as hardware, Internet of Things (IoT), robotics, and augmented
reality or virtual reality (AR/VR) – require capabilities outside software development.
Even for ventures whose products are primarily software-based, key aspects of venture
creation and growth remain difficult to automate through vibecoding. These include deciding
what to build, designing system architecture, ensuring security and compliance, optimizing
performance, and reasoning through complex data models and business logic. Market-facing
activities such as distribution and customer acquisition also rely on capabilities beyond
vibecoding. As ventures scale to larger user bases, infrastructure, reliability, and security
become increasingly critical, further increasing the importance of specialized expertise that
vibecoding cannot easily replace.
Ventures built heavily with vibecoding may not all be economically viable or able to
capture profits. As increasingly capable LLMs become broadly accessible to consumers at a
low cost, they do not confer firms sustainable competitive advantage (Csaszar, 2025; Barney,
2001). Instead, firms adopting GenAI-enabled vibecoding practices may derive a competitive
advantage from possessing scarce and non-substitutable complementary assets developed
10


## Page 11


internally. In ventures where product development can be entirely automated by vibecoding,
the weak differentiation increases entry from competitors and customers alike, and therefore
decreases product demand and erodes venture profitability. Therefore, vibecoding may not
increase economically viable venture creation if vibecoding can completely substitute for
human efforts in developing the product. In contrast, ventures that combine vibecoding
with scarce complementary assets may be better positioned to create and capture value
from vibecoding.
Whether vibecoding increases economically viable entrepreneurial entry depends on the
extent to which a venture’s product development process is susceptible to automation, which
varies substantially across venture types. We distinguish between fully exposed ventures,
whose product development can be largely automated by vibecoding, and partially exposed
ventures, whose product development is augmented by vibecoding because some tasks are
automatable while other critical tasks require human expertise beyond GenAI-enabled tech-
nical resources. We argue that vibecoding is most likely to increase economically viable
entry when it complements the scarce resources required to develop and commercialize ven-
ture ideas. In such settings, vibecoding automates routine coding tasks, allowing founders
to focus on higher-level tasks such as system design, architecture, and security, as well as
non-software bottlenecks such as compliance, hardware, and distribution.
2.2
Engineering Expertise as a Complement to Vibecoding
Human capital is an important source of firms’ competitive advantage (Crook et al., 2011).
For new ventures, founders and early teams are especially consequential because young firms
often face constraints in accessing other resources, such as financial capital and established
customer bases (Brush et al., 2001; Zimmerman and Zeitz, 2002). Founders’ technical edu-
cation and work experience contribute to the growth of technology ventures (Colombo and
Grilli, 2005), and task-relevant knowledge and skills are positively associated with venture
success (Unger et al., 2011). Prior experience and education also enable entrepreneurs to
discover opportunities by providing information about target markets, customer problems,
11


## Page 12


and possible solutions (Shane, 2000). Hence, the expertise of founders and early employees
is a key source of differentiation in new ventures.
The role of human capital may become even more important in dynamic environments
characterized by rapid and continuous improvements in technological capabilities driven by
innovation. From a resource-based perspective, widely available generative AI is unlikely to
confer durable competitive advantage on its own (Barney, 2001; Csaszar, 2025). Ventures
that develop or access a technology do not necessarily capture value from it, as value creation
often depends on complementary assets (Teece, 1986). For new ventures, the prior knowledge
and expertise of founders and early employees can form the microfoundations of such assets
by enhancing absorptive capacity and enabling more effective evaluation and use of external
knowledge. These internally developed capabilities which are difficult to imitate by rivals
(Teece et al., 1997; Teece, 2007), support dynamic capabilities that allow ventures to adapt to
continuous AI improvements and to deploy vibecoding effectively through experimentation
and learning.
This argument is consistent with evidence from earlier IT innovations, which shows that
firms create value from new technologies not through adoption alone, but through com-
plementary organizational assets. Prior work documents strong complementarities between
skilled labor and IT investment (Bresnahan et al., 2002; Brynjolfsson and McElheran, 2016),
and shows that productivity gains from frontier technologies depend especially on access
to workers with complementary technical skills, especially during early diffusion (Tambe,
2014). Among firms using enterprise software systems, performance gains arise not only
from the software itself, but also from IT workers’ knowledge and from knowledge sharing
across workers (Huang et al., 2022). A key mechanism is absorptive capacity – the ability to
recognize, assimilate, and apply external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Zahra and
George, 2002) – which is strengthened by prior related investments, particularly when new
knowledge is novel or rapidly evolving (Huang et al., 2022).
At a more granular level, product development often involves multiple complementary
12


## Page 13


tasks, some of which cannot be automated by vibecoding. When tasks are tightly inter-
dependent, weak performance in any one task can sharply reduce overall output because
success depends on completing all tasks effectively (Kremer, 1993). Human expertise there-
fore remains critical when product development requires capabilities beyond code generation.
For example, vibecoding cannot independently handle hardware engineering or interactions
with the physical environment, making domain expertise essential in areas such as Internet-
of-Things, virtual reality, and augmented reality. Even within software development, human
engineers are needed for software tasks that cannot yet be fully entrusted to vibecoding,
including system architecture, complex data models, encryption and security protocols, per-
formance optimization, low-latency real-time synchronization, custom machine learning with
proprietary training data, audio and video processing, low-level programming close to hard-
ware, operating systems, and frontier algorithmic research. Ventures are thus more likely
to possess complementary capabilities when founders and early employees have accumulated
expertise in these areas.
Beyond task-level constraints, generative AI also reshapes how knowledge is accessed
and used within organizations. It can automate parts of knowledge acquisition, integration,
and interpretation, thereby democratizing access to useful knowledge for individuals directly
involved in product development. Earlier IT innovations showed that data analytics facil-
itated innovation by enabling knowledge recombination across distant domains (Wu et al.,
2020). Generative AI extends this by expanding the search space and reducing the cost of
accessing and translating knowledge across domains (Dell’Acqua et al., 2025). As a result,
vibecoding may increase the autonomy of entrepreneurs and frontline operators by reducing
their dependence on others for information and problem-solving (Dixon et al., 2021). On
the other hand, human expertise remains essential for higher-level decisions, including sys-
tem design, architecture, and security, as well as for implementing and coordinating product
development.

Empirical Setting and Data
3.1
Product Hunt Sample Construction
To examine how GenAI affects the entry and performance of nascent digital ventures, we
collect large-scale data from Product Hunt, a global platform where early-stage ventures
launch technology products, gauge market traction, acquire early users, and validate ideas
(Cao et al., 2024). With roughly four million monthly visits, Product Hunt provides a suit-
able empirical context for studying vibecoding because it covers information about ventures
at an early stage, when founders are preparing for market entry but have not yet accumulated
substantial users, which makes GenAI-enabled software development especially valuable for
implementing entrepreneurial ideas and developing initial working products.
We collect rich data from Product Hunt on digital ventures’ first public launches. These
data include detailed information on the launches themselves as well as ex ante characteristics
of ventures and their founders. Specifically, we observe venture-level information, such as
website domains, product categories, and textual descriptions, and founder-level information
based on listed product “makers,” such as names, headlines, and social links. Because our
sample consists of first-time launches observed close to founding, the maker team listed
on the launch page provides a reasonable proxy for the founding team. These data allow
us to augment our sample by linking founders to Revelio Labs and ventures to Semrush,
CrunchBase, and PitchBook.
For each founder, we use the information listed on their Product Hunt account—including
name, headline, and social links, especially LinkedIn and Twitter—to identify the best match
to a Revelio Labs profile, either directly or through CrunchBase. The detailed matching
procedure is described in the Online Appendix.
Revelio Labs is a comprehensive work-
force database that aggregates hundreds of millions of public employment records, covering
over 1.1 billion individuals and more than 20 million companies through LinkedIn profile
data. These individual-level job and education histories enable us to construct measures of
14


## Page 15


founders’ demographics (e.g., gender, ethnicity, and age), career histories, and educational
backgrounds.
For each venture, we use the registered domain1 recorded at its first Product Hunt launch
as the venture’s unique identifier. For ventures without a standalone domain that instead rely
on a third-party host, such as a mobile app or Chrome extension (about 12% of launches in
our sample period), we use the full website path instead. Using venture’s registered domains,
we collect monthly website visits from Semrush, a marketing analytics platform that com-
bines clickstream data from hundreds of millions of Internet users worldwide with machine-
learning methods to generate engagement metrics. Because Semrush covers nearly all do-
mains with observed user activity, prior research commonly uses its traffic data to measure
digital ventures’ customer acquisition and performance (e.g., Koning et al., 2022; Conti and
Messinese, 2025). Finally, to verify that our sample consists of entrepreneurial ventures, we
match each launch to CrunchBase and PitchBook to obtain founding dates. Both databases
provide structured information on companies, prominent individuals, and financing activity
in technology entrepreneurship, with particularly strong coverage of venture-backed private
firms.

First, we exclude launch posts without listed makers, because venture-initiated prepared
launches always identify their makers. Second, we exclude observations for which one or
more makers cannot be reliably matched to Revelio Labs. We remove podcast episodes,
games, books, and other posts that are not entrepreneurial venture products. In addition,
we exclude ventures hosted on third-party platforms without standalone domains, except
for mobile apps and Chrome extensions, which are legitimate software products. Finally, to
exclude large incumbent firms, we drop organisations that launched more than 30 times, had
more than 5 makers, were founded more than 3 years before their first launch according to

3.2
Product Segments and Exposure to Vibecoding
Our empirical strategy depends on measuring exogenous variation across product segments
in their exposure to vibecoding, characterized by the extent to which product development
can be automated through prompting a vibecoding tool or platform. To construct these
segments, we use each venture’s tagline and description at first launch to generate high-
dimensional text embeddings, and then apply agglomerative hierarchical clustering to divide
the product space into disjoint product segments.2 Specifically, we train the clustering al-
gorithm on all first-time product launches between 2019Q1 and 2022Q2 using their textual
information. We then draw a representative random subset of products from each segment
and use a comprehensive prompt to classify their “vibecodability.” Aggregating these assess-
ments to the segment level allows us to define each product segment’s exposure to vibecoding.

predefined product segment, based on the embedding vector generated from the tagline and
description of its first launch. The rest of this section explains this procedure in more detail.

4
Econometric Methods
We conduct two sets of empirical tests: one using a product-segment panel to examine
entrepreneurial entry, as described in Section 4.1, and another using a launched-venture
panel to examine post-launch performance, as described in Section 4.2. In both analyses,
causal identification relies on a difference-in-differences (DiD) regression design around the
availability of vibecoding tools and platforms beginning in late 2022.
To determine the treatment date, we trace the diffusion of generative AI tools for soft-
ware development, which became broadly available and increasingly adopted in 2022. A
key milestone was GitHub Copilot’s public release, followed shortly by the introduction of
related GenAI coding tools by platforms such as Replit, which made AI-assisted coding ac-
cessible to a wider range of users. Replit is especially relevant because of its broad reach:
founded in 2016, it has over 40 million users worldwide, supports more than 50 programming
languages, and enables users to write, deploy, and collaborate on code in a browser-based
cloud environment. In October 2022, Replit launched Ghostwriter, an integrated generative
AI coding assistant that could autocomplete code and generate code from natural-language
prompts within the platform.
Since then, other low-code and cloud-based development platforms, such as Vercel, Bub-
ble, and Lovable, have introduced GenAI features that further expanded access to AI-assisted
software development. These launches were followed by rapid uptake among users. The
Online Appendix summarizes the timeline of major GenAI feature launches across these
platforms through 2025. Together, these developments suggest that October 2022 marked
the onset of a broader shift in the feasibility and adoption of vibecoding. We therefore use
October 2022 as the beginning of the treatment period. We assess the validity of this choice
using an event-study design that estimates differences between treated and control groups

4.1
Vibecoding Exposure and New Venture Launches
To examine how vibecoding exposure affects the entry of new ventures with different charac-
teristics, we estimate a difference-in-differences (DiD) model at the level of product-segment
by year-month. Equation 1 presents the baseline specification, in which the outcome variable,
Ln(FirstLaunchesst), measures the log number of first-time venture launches in product seg-
ment s in year-month t. The indicator Postt equals 1 for the time period from October 2022
onward and 0 otherwise. We choose October 2022 as the treatment date because it marks the
beginning of broader vibecoding adoption across different tools and platforms, as discussed
before. The specification includes product-segment fixed effects, ξs, and year-month fixed
effects, ηt, with robust standard errors clustered at the product-segment level.
Ln(FirstLaunchesst) = β1Automateds × Postt + β2Augmenteds × Postt + ξs + ηt + ϵst (1)
We define two product-segment-level treatment indicators, Automateds and Augmenteds,
for each distinct product segment s. Segments with Automateds = 1 are fully exposed to
vibecoding, meaning that vibecoding can automate nearly all relevant product development
tasks. In these segments, AI substitutes for venture team capabilities and compresses dif-
ferences across teams in shaping product outcomes. Segments with Augmenteds = 1 are
partially exposed to vibecoding, meaning that vibecoding can replace some, but not all, prod-
uct development tasks. In these segments, AI complements internals capabilities in driving
product development outcomes. Segments with minimal exposure have both Automateds
and Augmenteds equal to zero, and the two indicators are mutually exclusive. As described
in Section 3.2, both indicators are constructed at the product-segment level based on the
extent of exposure to vibecoding measured from first launch product texts.
Although vibecoding may accelerate development in both fully and partially exposed
product segments, exposure should not be interpreted as a one-dimensional treatment-

intensity measure. Full and partial exposure may affect viable entrepreneurial entry and
venture performance through distinct mechanisms. In partially exposed product segments,
substantial portions of core development activities remain non-automatable, so product out-
comes continue to depend on complementary expertise and skills within the venture team.
In fully exposed segments, by contrast, GenAI can automate most relevant development
activities, reducing the importance of team-specific capabilities.
Accordingly, β1 and β2
capture the effects of vibecoding exposure in automated and augmented product segments,
respectively, relative to minimally exposed product segments.
Because the validity of the DiD design depends on the parallel trends assumption (Bertrand
et al., 2004), we estimate an event-study version of Equation 1 to assess pre-treatment dy-
namics. Specifically, we replace Postt with indicators for months relative to the treatment
date, omitting the month immediately before treatment as the reference period. Each coeffi-
cient captures the treated-control difference in outcomes in a given relative month, measured
relative to this omitted pre-treatment period. Under the parallel trends assumption, the pre-
treatment coefficients should be statistically indistinguishable from zero.
We estimate the DiD regression model using alternative entry outcomes that count new
venture launches meeting different criteria, including positive external website traffic within
one year of launch and founders’ pre-entry expertise. These outcomes allow us to examine
whether vibecoding exposure affects not only the volume of entrepreneurial entry but also
the composition of entrants. First, because venture ideas enabled by vibecoding may differ in
economic viability, we construct an outcome that counts only ventures with positive website
traffic one year after first launch.
This measure focuses on ventures with at least some
evidence of market traction and excludes projects with no observable external usage after
launch. Second, we examine whether the entry effects of vibecoding vary with founding-team
expertise. To capture entry by teams with relevant STEM backgrounds, we estimate the DiD
model using an outcome restricted to first-time launches by ventures whose founding team
includes at least one founder with STEM higher education or STEM work experience.

6
Discussion and Conclusion
This study examines how vibecoding—the use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)
tools and platforms to partially or fully automate software development tasks—affects new
venture entry and post-launch performance. Exploiting variation across product segments
in the feasibility of using vibecoding for product development, we distinguish between au-
tomated exposure, where GenAI can substitute for most development tasks, and augmented
exposure, where GenAI automates some tasks but substantial complementary capabilities re-
main necessary. The evidence shows that vibecoding does not affect entrepreneurial activity
uniformly. Instead, its effects depend critically on whether GenAI primarily substitutes for
development work or complements the human capabilities needed to build and commercialize
new ventures.
Our results show that new ventures with positive website traffic one year after launch
increase by 11% in augmented, or partially exposed, product segments, but show no signifi-
cant change in automated, or fully exposed, segments. The increase in viable entry is driven
entirely by ventures with at least one STEM founder, defined as a founder with either a
bachelor’s or higher degree in a STEM field or recent employment in a STEM occupation.
Among launched ventures, those with engineering-focused teams at launch also experience
higher website traffic when exposed to vibecoding through augmentation, whereas full ex-
29


## Page 30


posure through automation does not meaningfully affect website traffic regardless of team
composition. Together, these findings suggest that vibecoding improves economically viable
entry and post-launch performance primarily for STEM-founded and engineering-focused
ventures, and only when it complements rather than substitutes for human expertise.
Our findings have practical implications for new ventures competing in markets reshaped
by rapidly advancing AI technologies. Although vibecoding makes software development
resources more abundant and less costly, it does not lead to viable entrepreneurial entry on
its own. Economic viability instead depends on what remains scarce: venture-specific knowl-
edge, complementary assets, and forms of expertise that are valuable and hard to replace.
Entrepreneurs should therefore build business models around what GenAI cannot readily
automate, focusing on internal capabilities and market positions that potential competitors
cannot easily replicate.
Our results suggest that ventures with STEM founders and engineering-focused teams
disproportionately capture the gains from vibecoding, at least in the early years of its dif-
fusion. Although vibecoding automates some development tasks previously performed by
engineers, it does not appear to diminish the importance of STEM expertise or engineering
capabilities in developing and scaling a venture’s initial product. Rather, as routine cod-
ing tasks become easier to automate, engineering expertise may become more important for
higher-level tasks such as system design, deployment in physical environments, and security.
Ventures with stronger STEM and engineering capabilities may also have greater absorptive
capacity, allowing them to adapt more quickly to advances in frontier AI technologies and
use them more effectively to augment product development. These mechanisms suggest that
such ventures possess stronger dynamic capabilities for responding to ongoing technological
change.
One important limitation of this study is that it focuses on the first several years follow-
ing the initial breakthrough in applying generative AI to software development. Given the
rapid pace of change in this field, especially the emergence of increasingly agentic AI systems,
30


## Page 31


more complex development tasks may become automatable over time. Such advances could
erode the technical moats of ventures built around complex software systems. At the same
time, some activities may remain difficult to automate under current LLM-based approaches,
including system design, hardware integration, security, and tasks requiring robust image or
video generation. Future research should therefore examine how firms can build and sus-
tain competitive advantage as AI capabilities diffuse across firms and their rivals. Possible
sources of advantage include proprietary data, specialized domain expertise, regulatory ca-
pabilities, and organizational processes that enable adaptation as the technological frontier
shifts. Future work should also examine strategic sourcing and specialization along the AI
value chain, including how firms can benefit from generative AI without becoming locked
into particular vendors, architectures, or workflows. Finally, an important open question
is how market structure will evolve as frontier AI labs move from model development into
application-layer domains, and whether digital ventures can sustain defensible positions as
these labs vertically integrate and broaden their scope across knowledge work.
