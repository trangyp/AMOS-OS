Abstract—Large language models (LLMs) often make rea-
soning errors when solving mathematical problems, and how
to automatically detect and correct these errors has become
an important research direction. However, existing approaches
mainly focus on self-correction within the model, which falls short
of the “teacher-style“ correction required in educational settings,
i.e., systematically guiding and revising a student’s problem-
solving process. To address this gap, we propose SMRC (Student
Mathematical Reasoning Correction), a novel method that aligns
LLMs with student reasoning. Specifically, SMRC formulates
student reasoning as a multi-step sequential decision problem and
introduces Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to explore optimal
correction paths. To reduce the cost of the annotating process-
level rewards, we leverage breadth-first search (BFS) guided by
LLMs and final-answer evaluation to generate reward signals,
which are then distributed across intermediate reasoning steps
via a back-propagation mechanism, enabling fine-grained process
supervision. Additionally, we construct a benchmark for high
school mathematics, MSEB (Multi-Solution Error Benchmark),
consisting of 158 instances that include problem statements,
student solutions, and correct reasoning steps. We further pro-
pose a dual evaluation protocol centered on solution accuracy
and correct-step retention, offering a comprehensive measure of
educational applicability. Experiments demonstrate that SMRC
significantly outperforms existing methods on two public datasets
(ProcessBench and MR-GSM8K) and our MSEB in terms of
effectiveness and overall performance. The code and data are
available at https://github.com/Mind-Lab-ECNU/SMRC.

soning Alignment, Monte Carlo Tree Search, Process-Supervised
Reward Modeling, AI in Education.
I. INTRODUCTION
In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have
achieved remarkable progress in complex mathematical rea-
soning tasks, thanks to their powerful logical and computa-
tional abilities [1]. These advances have not only improved
the performance of LLMs in scientific and engineering do-
mains but have also accelerated their integration into edu-
cational applications. However, existing studies reveal that
even state-of-the-art reasoning models remain prone to logi-
cal inconsistencies and computational errors [2]. To address
these challenges, researchers have proposed self-correction
approaches for LLMs. By incorporating iterative refinement,
multi-candidate verification, and consistency checks, these
methods move beyond traditional single-pass generation and
establish a unified generate–verify–correct framework, en-
abling models to autonomously detect and rectify internal
reasoning errors, thereby improving the reliability of math-
ematical inference.
Meanwhile, the applications of LLMs in education have
become increasingly diverse [3], [4]. Beyond correcting their
own reasoning errors, LLMs are now being leveraged as
“teacher-like” agents to identify and correct students’ reason-
ing mistakes in problem-solving. However, these two types of
correction differ fundamentally in both goals and mechanisms,
as illustrated in Figure 1. Model self-correction focuses on
enhancing logical consistency within the model’s reasoning,
whereas student-oriented correction emphasizes pedagogical
coherence and cognitive support [5], [6]. In educational con-
texts, effective correction involves not only providing the cor-
rect final answer but, more importantly, maintaining continuity
with the student’s original reasoning path to foster deeper
cognitive development. Recent benchmark datasets, such as
ProcessBench [7] and MR-GSM8K [8], represent initial steps
in this direction. Nonetheless, these datasets rely on synthet-
ically generated student responses, which fail to capture the
nuanced cognitive patterns and systematic misconceptions of
real learners. This limitation becomes particularly evident in
practical teaching scenarios—models trained on simulated data
often struggle to generalize to authentic student reasoning.
Therefore, there is an urgent need for a correction framework
grounded in real student data that simultaneously ensures
mathematical rigor and pedagogical alignment, paving the way
toward more effective human–AI collaborative teaching.

II. RELATED WORK
Self-correction for LLMs. Self-correction has emerged
as a critical approach for mitigating model hallucinations
and improving reasoning accuracy in large language models.
Recent research in this area has been supported by datasets
such as ProcessBench [7] and MR-GSM8K [8], which pro-
vide structured data for evaluating self-correction capabili-
ties. These datasets leverage open-source models to generate
erroneous reasoning patterns that simulate student problem-
solving processes. Key methodological contributions include
Self-Check [9], which employs internal consistency verifica-
tion, and Self-Refine [10], which utilizes iterative refinement
strategies to enhance model reasoning performance. Existing
approaches primarily optimize for correctness of final outputs
while overlooking critical requirements in educational appli-
cations. In real-world tutoring scenarios, effective error cor-
rection requires preserving students’ original solution strate-
gies alongside accuracy improvements. Current self-correction
methods typically generate corrections without considering
alignment with students’ initial reasoning approaches. This
misalignment creates a disconnect between model outputs and
student cognitive processes, reducing the pedagogical value of
automated correction systems. This limitation motivates a new
research direction: developing student-centered correction
frameworks that maintain fidelity to original reasoning
patterns while achieving high correction accuracy.
Test-time scaling (TTS). As enthusiasm for scaling computa-
tional resources—namely data and model parameters—during
pretraining has plateaued, test-time scaling (TTS) has rapidly

emerged as a central focus of contemporary research. By
strategically reallocating computational budget from training
to inference, TTS achieves superior performance without
requiring additional training or introducing extra hyperpa-
rameters [11]. Several representative approaches have been
proposed [12]: (1) Parallel Scaling. Given a single input, the
model concurrently generates k independent candidate outputs.
These candidates are subsequently ranked or scored by a pre-
trained reward model [13], [14]. (2) Tree Search. Tree-search
methods formulate generation as a tree-structured exploration
process, wherein each node—representing an intermediate
reasoning or decoding step—is evaluated by a reward model
to guide the search toward higher-quality trajectories [15],
[16]. (3) Long Chain-of-Thought. During inference, the
model autonomously produces tens to hundreds of fine-grained
reasoning steps, emulating the iterative process of drafting,
verifying, and backtracking on scratch paper, until convergence
to a final, self-consistent answer [17], [18]. (4) Multi-round
Refinement. This approach iteratively refines model outputs
across multiple inference rounds, progressively enhancing re-
sponse quality through self-correction and revision. Our work
focuses on self-correction tasks in mathematical reasoning
and adopts Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) as the core
strategy. Since Monte Carlo Tree Search relies on Q-values
for path selection and node expansion, we introduce external
reward models to compute Q-values, serving as objective guid-
ance signals. This external guidance mechanism effectively
prevents the model from falling into inherent biases during
the self-correction process, thereby achieving more objective
and effective correction results. In our framework, the design
of search nodes aims to ensure thought preservation during
the correction process, while the search mechanism itself
guarantees correction accuracy [19], [20].
Reward models (RMs). Reward models have emerged as a
widely applied technique in reinforcement learning, playing a
pivotal role in both generative models and reinforcement learn-
ing frameworks. The fundamental concept of reward models is
to leverage reward signals to guide models toward generating
outputs that better align with human preferences [21]. Reward
models can be categorized into two main types: Outcome-
supervised Reward Models and Process-Supervised Reward
Models [22]. Specifically, Outcome-supervised Reward Mod-
els (ORMs) evaluate model performance by scoring the final
generated answers, while Process-supervised Reward Models
(PRMs) assess the quality of the reasoning process [23]. By
utilizing reward models, we can transform human preferences
or task correctness into optimizable scalar signals, thereby
guiding large-scale models to produce higher-quality and more
desirable outputs during training or inference phases [24].
Unlike traditional reward models, our approach introduces a
specialized reward model for student mathematical reasoning
correction. This model identifies optimal nodes where students
achieve maximum reasoning progress and uses these points to
guide correction generation. To address the scarcity of process-
supervised labels, we develop an algorithm that infers process-
level supervision from outcome labels through reasoning tree
construction and label propagation. This enables our reward
model to provide precise guidance within the SMRC frame-
work, optimizing for mathematical correctness while maintain-
ing consistency with students’ original reasoning approaches.

IV. METHODOLOGY
While the self-correction framework in Section III focuses
on answer correctness, it reveals a critical limitation in
educational scenarios: neglecting the preservation of students’
original reasoning pathways. Simply providing correct an-
swers is insufficient; effective feedback must build upon
students’ existing understanding and maintain continuity with
their problem-solving approaches. To address this, we propose
the Student Mathematical Reasoning Correction (SMRC)
framework, which formulates error correction as a sequential
decision-making problem. The framework employs Monte
Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to explore a reward-guided search
space, where a specialized reward function identifies nodes
that maximize students’ problem-solving progress, thereby
achieving both answer accuracy and alignment with stu-
dents’ reasoning trajectories.
A. Problem Formulation: Student Aware Mathematical Rea-
soning Correction
Self-correction tasks for large language models have primar-
ily focused on achieving mathematical reasoning correctness.
However, in educational contexts, obtaining correct answers
alone is insufficient to meet pedagogical needs. When assisting
students in error correction, abrupt changes in reasoning
approaches can confuse learners and hinder comprehension. To
address these challenges, we propose Student-Aware Mathe-
matical Reasoning Correction (SAMRC), a framework that
requires both accurate results and alignment with students’
original thought processes.
While SAMRC may superficially resemble conventional
self-correction tasks, it differs fundamentally in its objective.
The corrected reasoning sequence A∗must not only yield
the correct solution to problem Q, but also preserve valuable
steps from the student’s original sequence A. This delicate
and challenging requires carefully balancing correctness with
fidelity to the student’s cognitive trajectory.

C. MSEB Dataset Construction
To evaluate the SMRC framework, we construct a real-
world student problem-solving dataset named MSEB (Multi-
Solution Error Benchmark). Unlike existing approaches that
rely on model-simulated student errors, MSEB is derived
from actual high school student’ problem-solving processes,
providing a reliable benchmark for fine-grained student error
identification and correction research.
Problem Selection: We manually select problems from a
high school mathematics question bank, covering algebra,
geometry, matrices, and trigonometry. The selection criteria
are based on: (i) Problem Difficulty: Ensuring sufficient com-
plexity to elicit diverse reasoning paths and typical cognitive
errors. (ii) Solution Diversity: Selecting problems with at least
two distinct correct solution methods to effectively evaluate
correction methods’ ability to preserve correct reasoning parts
in students’ original solutions.
Data Collection and Annotation: We collect complete writ-
ten solution processes by inviting numerous high school
students to solve the selected problems. Through systematic
organization and annotation, we construct a dataset comprising
158 real student error records. Each record includes the
original problem, the standard answer(s), the student’s com-
plete solution process, the correct reasoning steps within the
student’s solution process.
1) Data Format: Each instance in the MSEB dataset is
stored as a JSON object containing the following core fields:
• "question": The statement of the mathematical prob-

V. EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate the
effectiveness of our proposed SMRC method, aiming to answer
the following questions: (1) Can SMRC significantly improve
the model’s ability in student reasoning correction? (Section
V-B) (2) Can instruct models directly serve as reward models?
(Section V-C1) (3) How effective is SMRC in different settings
(or ablation study)? (Section V-C2 and Section V-C3) (4) How
universal is SMRC, i.e., can it be directly plugged into various
base models to achieve consistent performance improvements?
(Section V-C4) (5) Is Monte Carlo tree effective and how does
it perform with other search algorithms? (Section V-C5)
A. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks. We evaluate the SMRC framework on three
datasets: ProcessBench [7], MR-GSM8K [8], and the MSEB
dataset. ProcessBench is built upon GSM8K [26], MATH [27],
OlympiadBench [28], and Omni-MATH [29], while MR-
GSM8K is constructed based on GSM8K. Both public datasets
use large language models to generate student reasoning
processes and provide human-annotated error step locations.
For evaluating the CSRR metric, since these public datasets
only annotate the first error step position, we treat all steps
before the first error step in each sample as correct steps.
Baselines. We compare our proposed SMRC framework with
the following baseline method: Direct Error Correction
(DEC): Directly prompt LLMs to correct student’ incorrect
answer. Thought-Preserving Error Correction (TPEC):
Based DEC and instruct LLMs to preserve student’ orig-
inal reasoning method during correction. Self-Refine [10]:
Optimizing reasoning path through multiple rounds of self-
improvement. Self-Check [9]: Generates multiple reasoning
paths and selects the output with the highest self-check score.
For DEC and TPEC methods, we evaluate on multiple base
models, including open-source models of different scales [30],
[31] and education-specific models [4]. Self-Refine and Self-
Check methods are evaluated using the Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct
model.
Implementation details. We utilize Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct
fine-tuned on our custom reward dataset to estimate Q-
values, while Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct serves as the primary
generator for node expansion in the Monte Carlo tree. In
our experimental setup for the SMRC framework, we employ
the following hyperparameter configuration: the exploration
parameter c for Monte Carlo Tree Search is set to 0.4, with a
maximum of 30 exploration attempts and a reward threshold of
0.95 for termination. The feedback mechanism is constrained
to a maximum of 4 rounds. In the experiment, we used
multiple prompts, specifically including: (1) SMRC framework
node generate prompt. (2) Multi turn Feedback Prompt for
the SMRC framework. (3) Student Solution Decomposition
Prompt.

VI. CONCLUSION
This study introduces the SMRC framework, which ad-
dresses a key limitation in large language models (LLMs)
applied to mathematical error correction: the lack of align-
ment with students’ original reasoning pathways. We formally
define the task of Student-Aware Mathematical Reasoning
Correction (SAMRC) as a dual-objective optimization problem
that jointly maximizes answer correctness while preserving
students’ cognitive processes. To solve this problem, we
propose a structured search algorithm based on Monte Carlo

exploration, multi-step verification, and dynamic pruning to
effectively balance both objectives. To support this research

results demonstrate that SMRC achieves strong and robust
performance across multiple benchmarks.
Despite these promising results, several directions warrant
further investigation. Methodologically, the current Q-value-
based reward allocation strategy employs a uniform distri-
bution scheme, which may not fully capture the varying
contributions of individual reasoning steps. Future work should
explore more nuanced value distribution models to improve the
precision of process-level supervision.
