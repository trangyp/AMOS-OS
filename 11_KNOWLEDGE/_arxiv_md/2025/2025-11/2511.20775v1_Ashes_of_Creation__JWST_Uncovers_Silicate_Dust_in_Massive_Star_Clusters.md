Dust production is a fundamental aspect of the baryonic cycle of star formation. It is known that
dust is injected into the interstellar medium during early star formation by supernovae and later on
by evolved stars. From individual objects, these mechanisms are well understood, but the overall dust
production in star clusters at different evolutionary stages is still challenging to quantify. We present
22 massive (> 105M⊙) extra galactic star clusters with ages between 3 and 100 Myr exhibiting a
compact dust morphology seen with JWST-MIRI. We only find PAH features associated with one star
cluster and nineteen have already cleared themselves from their natal dust. Their main characteristic
is a significant enhancement at 10µm, which is likely due to silicate emission and cannot be explained
by ionized gas. We discuss several possible explanations including dust production from evolved stars
such as red super giants, more exotic star types like yellow hypergiants and luminous blue variable
stars. Stochastic dust injection from supernovae or a single supernova in dense gas can also create
significant silicate emission. However, for this scenario secondary tracers such as a X-ray signal are
expected which we only observe in three star clusters. We find the most luminous 10µm emitter to
be the three most massive star clusters (> 106M⊙) which is at least a magnitude stronger than any
known stellar sources indicating a rare mechanism that only appears at extreme masses and a short
lifetime.

1. INTRODUCTION
Compact star clusters probe the densest parts of star
formation within massive clouds of molecular gas and
dust (Krumholz & McKee 2005; Krumholz et al. 2019).
During its subsequent evolution, the newly-formed stel-
lar population clears away its natal dust through radi-
ation pressure (e.g. Lim et al. 2020), stellar winds and
shocks from supernova (SN) explosions (Ostriker & Mc-
Kee 1988). The star cluster mass plays an important
role here: at higher masses the formation of massive
O and B-type stars becomes more likely, providing a
significantly higher flux of ionizing photons (Tan & Mc-
Kee 2004; Watkins et al. 2019). Hence, for massive star
clusters it is well understood that the natal dust cloud
eventually gets pushed into the circumcluster medium.
The time scale for this process can range from one to up
to five Myr (Hannon et al. 2019, 2022; Chevance et al.
2022; Knutas et al. 2025) and can further depend on
other factors such as the metallicity, the environment or
the geometry. On average, more massive star clusters
can clear away their natal dust more effectively (e.g.
McQuaid et al. 2024). The resulting morphology is ob-
served via shells and bubbles of dust and ionized gas
(Whitmore et al. 2011) which can reach in the most ex-
treme cases multiple kpc in diameter (Watkins et al.
2023a,b; Barnes et al. 2023).
However, even after the natal dust and gas is cleared
from the star formation site, the emerging star cluster it-
self will not necessarily remain dust free. The young stel-
lar population continues to inject dust and gas into the
interstellar medium (ISM) via stellar winds (e.g. Gail &
Sedlmayr 1987) and ejecta from SNe (e.g. Bocchio et al.
2014). At later stages in the life of the cluster, Asymp-
totic Giant Branch (AGB) stars in particular play a ma-
jor role in dust production (van Loon et al. 2005; Fer-
rarotti & Gail 2006). This dust production adds to the
host galaxy’s dust budget and will be recycled into the
natal clouds of future stars (Ginolfi et al. 2020; Hunt
et al. 2020; Tortora et al. 2022). In the Milky Way and
the Local Group dust production by individual objects
is well observed in supernova remnants (SNR; e.g. Sand-
strom et al. 2009; Matsuura et al. 2022), around evolved
stars (Groenewegen et al. 2011), in stellar winds of sin-
gle stars (Gauger et al. 1990) and binary systems (Peatt
et al. 2023; Lau et al. 2024, 2022).
With the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), we
are now able to resolve near- and mid-IR dust signatures
on the scale of individual star clusters in nearby galax-
ies beyond the Local Group (Lee et al. 2023; Pedrini
et al. 2024).
Of special importance are the processes
within natal dust clouds such as the production of Poly-
cyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) emission e.g. ob-
served at 3.3µm with NIRCam (Rodr´ıguez et al. 2023,
2025; Gregg et al. 2024) and 7.7 and 11.3µm with MIRI
(Hands & Sandstrom 2025).

(red). One can clearly see the bright star formation sites
along the spiral arm which are characterized by both
dust continuum emission and also significant 7.7 and

2.2. Star Cluster Catalogs
As described in Sect. 1 our initial discovery of com-
pact bright 10 µm emitters was associated with known
star clusters. One of the main objectives of this work is
to conduct a systematic investigation of all bright 10 µm
sources and to understand how many are associated with
known star clusters. The publicly available star cluster
catalogs for the 19 PHANGS galaxies (Maschmann et al.
2024) and for NGC 5194 from Messa et al. (2018a) are
therefore a crucial resource. We furthermore use physi-
cal parameters like age, stellar mass and reddening from
Thilker et al. (2025) for the 19 PHANGS galaxies. These
values were estimated from SED fitting of the optical
HST broad-band observations. Furthermore, Hα nar-
row band observations were included in the fitting pro-
cedure in order to break the age-reddening degeneracy
(Whitmore et al. 2023). For star clusters in NGC 5194
we used physical parameters from Messa et al. (2018a)
which are solely based on HST broad band observations.
These catalogs provide reliable characteristic values for
the star clusters. However, since the estimation is based
on automated procedures in rare cases star clusters can
be mis-classified, which we discuss for individual star
clusters in the appendix C.
The earliest stages of star cluster formation happen
in dust enshrouded environments which often makes it
impossible to detect in broadband optical bands with the
HST (Lee et al. 2023). In order to compare the 10 µm
selected objects discussed here to dust embedded star
clusters, we use the sample of Rodr´ıguez et al. (2025)
providing 1816 sources selected on the basis of having
significant 3.3µm PAH features.

2.4. Known 10 µm Emitters in the Milky Way
Bright silicate emission at 10 µm has been observed
extensively in the Milky Way (e.g. Kraemer et al. 2002)
with η Carinae being the most famous star system as
it is the brightest MIR source in the night sky (Mehner
et al. 2014).
A single system such as η Carinae can
dominate the MIR SED of a star cluster and be still ob-
servable in nearby galaxies (Khan et al. 2015). In order
to compare our findings to known bright silicate emitters
we inspected all objects classified as silicate emitters in
Kraemer et al. (2002); Sloan et al. (2003) and selected
the brightest objects of each type: η Carinae, the Yellow
Hyper Giant (YHG) IRC+10420, the RSG HV888 and
the post-AGB star IRAS 18062+2410. All four objects
have significant 10 µm silicate emission and are bright
enough to be detected at distances typical for the galax-
ies studied here (≤23Mpc).
Of course, the selection of these sources is biased
towards bright objects with strong silicate features.
Whether a silicate feature is seen in absorption or emis-
sion can depend on the geometry of the ejected en-
velopes, for example in young stellar objects (Seale et al.
2009; Jones et al. 2017). Furthermore, Luminous Blue
Variable (LBV) stars of which at least one is present
in the η Carinae system (Hirai et al. 2021) are not al-
ways bright 10 µm emitters (Agliozzo et al. 2021). Also
YHGs are much rarer than for example RSG (Beasor
et al. 2023) and as explained in de Jager (1998) the
system IRC+10420 shows exceptional conditions with
unique outflows properties.
A 10 µm silicate emission is a known feature in RSG
(Verhoelst et al. 2009; Messineo et al. 2012) and its
strength is proportional to its mass-loss rate (Skinner
& Whitmore 1988). Even though their IR luminosity is
significantly lower than e.g. LBV stars or YHGs, RSGs
are quite common star types.
Stellar synthesis mod-
els with Starburst99 (Leitherer et al. 1999) predict that
star clusters of 106M⊙go through a phase with ≥100
RSG present, making this a possible explanation. Beside
the RSG HV888, we also include here IR observations
with SOFIA of the star cluster Westerlund 1, which has
a significant population of evolved stars such as RSGs
and YHGs (Clark et al. 2005; Guarcello et al. 2025).
We use photometric measurements from Beasor et al.
(2021) since newer JWST observations show significant
saturation in the MIRI F1000W band.
The selected post-AGB star IRAS 18062+2410 is at
a late stage of its evolution as an AGB star and on
the verge of becoming a planetary nebulae (Arkhipova
et al. 1999, 2007). It shows significant silicate emission
at 10.8µm from oxygen-rich circumstellar dust shells
Gauba & Parthasarathy (2004). However not all AGB
stars show a distinct silicate feature (Groenewegen &
Sloan 2018) and since the AGB phase lasts for a rela-
tively short time (∼105 yr, Habing 1996) it might be
challenging to find a larger population.
2.5. SNR catalogs and Chandra X-ray Observations
As mentioned in Sect. 1, SNe are known dust pro-
ducers and especially silicate dust can be found in their
shocked ejecta (e.g. Matsuura et al. 2022).
However,
SNe are very rare with about two to three per century
in our Milky Way (e.g. Tammann et al. 1994). SNRs
are observable for 20–80 kyr (Sarbadhicary et al. 2017)
but the known sample in the Milky Way is biased by
selection effects such as brightness and distance (Ver-
berne & Vink 2021). SNR in the Milky Way are mainly
identified from their spectral index in radio continuum
observations (Green 2014; Anderson et al. 2017; Dokara
et al. 2021) since radio waves can pierce through the
dust in the Galactic midplane. Furthermore, SNR can
be identified with X-ray observations (see Vink 2012,
for a review). In the Local Group, X-ray observations
are the primary tool for identifying SNR (Long et al.
2014; Maggi et al. 2016, 2019). Even though radio and
X-ray observations are necessary in order to fully char-
acterize individual SNR, detections beyond the Local
Group are challenging due to sensitivity (Russell et al.
2020; Kopsacheili et al. 2025).
An alternative identi-
fication method is through spectroscopic signatures of
shock-heated gas using optical emission line ratios like
[S II]λλ6716,6731 to Hα or [O III]λ5007 to Hβ (Li et al.
2024) or to Hα (Winkler et al. 2017; Long et al. 2018).
Furthermore the ratio of the [Fe II]1.644µm to hydrogen
recombination lines is a powerful tool to identify SNR
(Oliva et al. 1989; Greenhouse et al. 1997; Koo et al.
2013; Blair et al. 2014).
In this work we include the SNR catalog of Li et al.
(2024). SNRs in this catalog were identified based on
their optical emission line ratios, using observations of
the 19 PHANGS galaxies made with VLT-MUSE (Em-
sellem et al. 2022). For NGC 5194, we use the SNR cat-
alog provided by Winkler et al. (2021) which is based
on an initial selection through [S II]λλ6716,6731 to Hα
ratios with HST narrow-band imaging and follow up

3. SOURCE IDENTIFICATION AND
PHOTOMETRY
3.1. Source Detection
In order to investigate the nature of 10 µm emit-
ters, we base the source detection on the JWST MIRI
F1000W band observations. In a first step we compute
the median and standard deviation of the entire image
(or mosaic) by using sigma-clipped statistics with 10 it-
erations with rejection sigma limit of three (Beers et al.
1990).
This provides a good background estimation
needed to detect significant sources. We then run the
DAOStarFinder detection as implemented in photu-
tils (Stetson 1987) on the median subtracted image and
specify the kernel size to be the FWHM of the F1000W
band (0.328′′) which we also apply as the minimal source
separation. The source detection threshold is taken to
be 10σ which leads to a total source detection of 10504
sources in all the galaxies analyzed.
Since this study
is focused on providing a spectrally-extensive picture of
compact 10 µm emitters we only take those sources into
account which are covered by HST and NIRCam obser-
vations. This reduces the initial source catalog to 8382
objects.
In a next step we flag unwanted artifacts like fore-
ground stars, background galaxies or galactic centers.
We therefore cross-match our source catalog with the
HST star cluster candidate catalogs (Messa et al. 2018a;
Whitmore et al. 2021; Maschmann et al. 2024) with
a search radius of 0.328′′ which is the FWHM of the
F1000W band. Since the astrometric alignment of the
MIRI to HST bands are ±0.1′′ (Lee et al. 2023) this
cross-match radius is well suited to identify known ar-
tifacts.
During the selection of the star cluster can-
didate catalogs human and machine-learning identified
artifacts were kept track of which enable us to cross-
identify and eliminate 753 artifacts resulting in a total
catalog of 7629 sources. Even though this artifact re-
moval cleaned up a great fraction of the contaminat-
ing sources, the F1000W band is sensitive to redshifted
background galaxies that do not appear in the optical
HST observations. Furthermore, diffraction spikes from
bright infrared sources such as Active Galactic Nuclei
(AGN) in the center of the galaxies or foreground stars
(that were not PHANGS-HST star cluster candidates)
are often picked up by the detection algorithm. These
contaminating sources are sorted out at a later stage of
the sample selection via visual inspection.
3.2. Justification of Detection Algorithm
The detection algorithm DAOStarFinder has its
main application of finding point-like sources (Stet-
son 1987) and therefore introduces a desired bias to-
wards compact 10 µm sources.
This choice is moti-
vated by the initial discovery of compact MIR sources
that show a 10 µm enhancement. In order to under-
stand if this particular finding is only a consequence
of the source detection scheme we tested other algo-
rithms like PeakFinder which is well suited to detect
slightly extended PAH sources (Rodr´ıguez et al. 2025)
and DAOStarFinder with further increasing source
sizes. We furthermore also tested source catalogs iden-
tified from a dendrogram-based selection on constrained
diffusion maps detailed in Hassani (2025, in prep.). The
result was that we did not find any significant difference
on the bright end of the source distribution and the final

3.4. Verification of Background Effects
In order to find a genuine 10 µm emitter we need to
exclude any cases in which the measured enhancement
in the F1000W band is the result of a faulty background
subtraction. This could in theory be caused by the fact
that the neighboring bands F770W and F1130W are
mainly tracing PAH emission and therefore probe dif-
ferent physical processes.
For instance, a star cluster
could recently have expelled the majority of its natal
dust content into a circumcluster region which is co-
spatial with the area where we would measure the back-
ground. Bright PAH emission in this region may then
result in an over-subtraction of the background in the
bands F770W and F1130W but not the F1000W band
as it does not trace any PAH features.
We meticu-
lously made sure that this is not the case. We tested
two methods of measuring the local background: 1) we
used the photutils class Background2D to model a
local background map in a 12′′×12′′ cutout. 2) We mea-
sured the source profiles and identified the background
at a 5σ distance of a Gaussian source approximation.
In both methods, we applied a sigma-clipping to mask
out other sources in the surroundings. We furthermore
computed only the peak fluxes of each source and mul-
tiplying it by the solid angle subtended by the target
PSF used in Hassani (2025, in prep.). In addition to
that we inspected the source morphology, peak flux and
background estimations for all sources brighter than an
absolute AB magnitude of M10µm < −13 mag.
With all these additional flux verification methods, we
were not able to find any effects that could introduce a
systematic overestimation of the F1000W flux (or a sys-
tematic underestimation of the flux in the neighboring
bands) and we therefore have strong reasons to believe
that sources measured with a 10 µm enhancement are
genuine. To our knowledge a 10 µm enhancement mea-
sured from photometry in unresolved extragalactic star
clusters has not been reported in the literature yet and
we aim to present a convincing methodology with this
subsection.
3.5. Hα Equivalent width
All sources are observed with the HST Hα narrow
band F657N or F658N (Chandar et al. 2025) which is a
crucial measurement for an additional age indicator (e.g.
Whitmore et al. 2011). We therefore compute the Hα
equivalent width (EW) in order to identify young star
clusters. An exact age estimation with EW(Hα) is not
model independent as it depends on the choice of the
stellar population model, gas density and escape frac-

to young star forming region as done in Thilker et al.
(2025).
Since we do not have spectroscopic observa-

section 7.1) but with a subtle difference: instead of al-

targets in NGC 3351 and F689M instead of F814W in
NGC 5194. Furthermore we use the following definition

4.3. Sample Selection
After combining all samples of known MIR sources
inside the Milky Way and nearby galaxies we see a
clear distinction between PAH emitters on the left side
of Fig. 2 and 10 µm emitters on the right side.
In-
terestingly, the most luminous 10 µm emitters with
MF1000W < −12 mag are all known star clusters with
significant masses (M∗> 105M⊙). At these luminosi-
ties no stars identified by Hassani (2025, in prep.) can
be found. This motivates the following criteria in order
to select a sample of the most massive 10 µm emitting
star clusters:
1. (E10 µm - E10 µm(model, max)) > 3σE10 µm
2. star cluster must be compact class 1 (spheri-
cal, concentrated) or class 2 (asymmetric, concen-
trated)
3. star cluster mass > 105M⊙
With criterion 1. we ensure that the detection of E10 µm
is significantly higher than what we could produce with
a simple dust continuum. Criterion 2. ensures that the
star clusters which we identify as the optical counter-
parts are compact and do not have multiple peaks in
the optical which makes a subsequent analysis of the
underlying emission mechanism more difficult (for defi-
nition of star cluster classes 1 and 2 see Whitmore et al.
2021). The last criterion limits this work to the most
massive star clusters. Based on these criteria we select
22 star clusters listed in Table 1 and mark them with
red empty diamonds in Fig. 2.
The present work is a pilot study with the aim of
describing the phenomenon of 10 µm enhanced emis-
sion in MIR bright massive star clusters. We therefore
chose a rather conservative selection in order to iden-
tify the most extreme end of the objects presented in
Fig. 2. With the imposed criteria we clearly separated
our samples from single star sources identified in Has-
sani (2025, in prep.) but at the same time we excluded
many star clusters with slightly weaker E10 µm values
and those which did not reach the mass threshold of
105M⊙. It has to be said that there is a slight overlap
in Fig. 2 between the most IR-luminous stars selected
in Hassani (2025, in prep.) and the lower end of our
sample distribution. Furthermore the YHG IRC+10420
and η Carinae are found at luminosities comparable to
our sample. Nevertheless the two brightest 10 µm emit-
ters are more than an order of magnitude brighter than
η Carinae, making them brighter in the MIR than any
source known in the Milky Way.

5.1. Morphological Classification
In Fig. 3, we show the position of each cluster in their
host galaxy and show two zoom-in rgb color composite
images: one with MIRI F770W, F1000W and F1130W
and a second one with HST B, V and Hα observations.
In Table 2 we present their morphological characteris-
tics and classification, as well the location in their host
galaxy.
One of the most obvious properties that all 22 selected
star clusters have in common is their compact appear-
ance in the mid IR. With no exceptions all objects show
a radial profile close to that of a point source in the
F1000W MIRI band.
Optical observations with HST
have significant higher spatial resolution and show typ-
ical extended sources as we would expect from compact
star clusters (Thilker et al. 2022). The star cluster sizes
in the HST bands are still smaller than the resolution
of the F770W MIRI band and we can therefore not de-
termine whether the mid-IR flux is emitted by a point
source, such as a single star, or an extended population
distribution throughout the star cluster. However, what
we know is that the mid-IR flux is most likely associated
only to the cluster and not to any extended features
like dust clouds or filaments. Even though every sin-
gle MIRI source has a compact shape we sometimes see
additional dust emission close by or even covering the
sources, with the most prominent examples being the
objects NGC 1365–2, NGC 1672–7, NGC 3627–9 and
NGC 5194–21. This is furthermore seen as dust lanes
in the optical HST images. It is not clear whether this
is natal dust or simply fore- or background dust that
happens to be in the line of sight.
In 14 of the 22 star clusters, we observe shell-like struc-
ture or even bubbles in HST Hα narrow-band obser-
vations.
This is a direct sign that these star clusters
are rather young and have just cleared themselves from
this gas and still are one of the major ionization sources
(Hannon et al. 2022). In seven cases we are even able
to detect Hα emission inside the photometric aperture
which we identify as having a significant EW(Hα) emis-
sion (See Sect. 3.5).
In order to provide an age estimate of the star clusters
we here follow the morphological classification based on
the optical HST morphology, in particular the Hα nar-
row band morphology as described in Whitmore et al.
(2011). We are able to classify our 22 star clusters into
four of their categories:
Category 3: The star cluster is emerging and still par-
tially dust covered. We find early signs of feedback and
Hα emission associated with the star cluster. The ages
are most likely ∼3 Myr or less.
Category 4a: We clearly see a bubble or shell struc-
ture in the ionized gas and the star cluster has already
cleared itself from the majority of the natal gas. How-
ever, we are still able to detect Hα emission associated
with the star cluster. These star clusters are probably
in the range of 3 −5 Myr.
Category 4b: similar to Category 4a, but with a more
distinct bubble or shell structure of at least 20 pc in ra-
dius and no Hα emission on target. These star clusters
tend to be older than Category 4a but are very likely
< 7 Myr.
Category 5: No associated feedback structure and no
Hα detection. These clusters are very likely > 10 Myr
but due to their blue color and bright appearance <
100 Myr.
With this categorization we did not strictly follow the
scheme in Whitmore et al. (2011). We kept their nomen-
clature but added quantitative Hα measurements in or-
der to distinguish between Category 4a and 4b.

5.3. SED Shapes and Features
In Fig. 4 we present the NUV-to-MIR SEDs of all 22
star clusters with aperture-corrected photometry as de-
scribed in Appendix A. We group the SEDs according to
the 4 categories introduced in Sect. 5.1. From the first
look at all SED shapes one can directly tell from the ris-
ing MIR flux that each individual star cluster shows dust
emission in the mid-IR and the measured MIRI fluxes
cannot be due to stellar continuum. However, the flux
in F770W seems to be in some cases probing the stellar
continuum as for example in NGC 1300–1, NGC 1566–5
or NGC 4254–13. In these cases the F770W flux fol-
lows the shape of the NIR data points before steeply
rising again with the F1000W and F1130W band. When
looking at the two youngest morphological categories, 3
and 4a, which show Hα emission associated with the
star clusters, we can see a steady transition between the
near-IR and the mid-IR. For star clusters of category
4b we see a steeper mid-IR sample with the exception of
NGC 5194–21. This is also observed for the star clusters
NGC 1672–6 and NGC 4303–14 of category 5 and can
be a sign of higher dust temperatures.
Considering the age range we assigned to the star clus-
ters based on their morphology it is not surprising that
we do not detect PAH emission at 3.3µm in 21 of the
22 star clusters. Of course the absence of PAH features
in star clusters with a 10 µm emission is not surprising
since the selection described in Sect. 4.3 is clearly biased
towards sources with no PAH emission in the F770W
and F1130W bands.
The absence of the 3.3µm PAH
feature indicates that the PAHs are either displaced by
feedback or destroyed by radiation (e.g. Egorov et al.
2023) and the F770W band most likely probes the stel-
lar and dust continuum. NGC 1672–7 is the only ex-
ception with a significant enhancement in the F335M
NIRCam band relative to the neighboring F300M and
F360M bands. This is star cluster is not a strong 3.3 µm
PAH source in the sense of e.g. the embedded star clus-
ters selected in Rodr´ıguez et al. (2025) but clearly in-
dicates that it is still at an earlier stage of natal dust
clearing. The fact that NGC 1672–7 has by far the low-
est measured EW(Hα)=−203.4±2.3 ˚A supports this ar-
gument further. This is consistent with empirical SED
derived from star clusters in NGC 628 that after 3 Myr,
the PAH feature disappears and that in 80 % to 90 % of
the cases PAH and Hα emission track one another. In
a study of emerging young star clusters in NGC 628 in-
cluding Paα and Brα Pedrini et al. (2024) reported that
after ∼3 Myr the PAH feature at 3.3 µm is displaced
into the circumcluster medium and the natal gas shows
an open morphology.
As indicated in Sect. 1 and 2.4 is 10 µm emission is
a known feature associated with silicates. In theory the
enhancement at 10 µm could be due to strong emis-
sion lines situated inside the MIRI F1000W band. As
listed in Hunt et al. (2025) for example [ArIII]λ8.99 or
the [SIV]λ10.51 emission lines with ionization potentials
of 27.63 eV and 34.79 eV, respectively are good can-
didates.
As discussed with model predictions in Ap-
pendix B.2 this case is unlikely since extreme ionization
parameters would be needed or high Hα signals with
minimal amount of dust reddening would be expected.
This might be the case for the star cluster NGC 1672–7
which does seem to have a rather flat SED morphology
around 10 µm and shows signs of strong ionization that
manifests e.g. in the Hα emission line. This might be
potentially the case for NGC 3627–9, too. For the re-
maining star clusters this scenario seems rather unlikely
due to the lack of ionization found in Hα.
Furthermore, the H2 0-0 S(3) rotational transition
(J = 5 →3) at 9.66µm is situated inside the F1000W
band (Jones et al. 2025; Hernandez et al. 2025). This
idea is theoretically possible and with modeling of warm
(T ≳100 K) molecular gas Burton et al. (1992) dis-
cussed that at temperatures of 350 to 1000 K the H2
S(3) line dominates the MIR. However, as shown in
Fig. 2 the F1000W luminosity is in the order of 105
to 107 L⊙.
considering that a significant portion of
the F1000W band is emitted through the H2 S(3) line
this would require H2 0-0 S(3) line luminosities which
would reach the intensity of entire ultra luminous IR
galaxy as shown in Hill & Zakamska (2014). This makes
this scenario for individual star clusters very unlikely.

6. DISCUSSION
This section now aims at discussing the underlying
mechanism of the bright 10 µm emitting star clusters
selected in Sect. 4. We base our discussion on the charac-
teristics described in Sect. 5 and possible scenarios such
as population of evolved stars, individual star systems
and SNe.
Since it is challenging to draw conclusions
regarding the underlying process from imaging observa-
tions alone we also discuss future observation strategies.
6.1. NIR Excess
As discussed in Sect. 5.3, in particular star clusters
of the morphological category 4b show a distinct bump
in the NIR (See Fig. 4). In fact, there seem to be two
bumps: one with a maximum between 1.5 and 2 µm and
a second one between 3 and 4 µm. These features can
only be well described in star clusters in NGC 5194 due
to its excellent NIR-to-MIR coverage. In other galaxies
that were only observed with F200W, F300M, F335M
and F360M in the NIR we can still see an excess at 2 µm.
These are well known features found in evolved stars like
RSGs (Verhoelst et al. 2009; Kastner et al. 2008; John-
son et al. 2023) or Yellow Super Giants (YSGs) (Beasor
et al. 2021) and the so-called 1.6 µm H-bump has been
used to identify RSGs (Yang et al. 2021). In order to
better understand the relation between the SED shape
and the mass-loss rate of super giants, Gordon et al.
(2018) modeled known super giants with radiative trans-
fer models and presented SED shapes similar to the ones
we find in star clusters of Category 4b. Most notably,
Wang et al. (2021) found that RSGs which predomi-
nantly eject silicate dust and therefore are characterized
by a 10 µm feature show significant higher mass loss
rates that are proportional to their bolometric luminos-
ity. Furthermore, the second bump peaking between 3
and 4 µm is a feature found in RSGs (Verhoelst et al.
2009) but can also be an indicator of the presence of hot
dust.
As discussed in Sect. 5.1, category 4b is further
evolved compared to categories 3 and 4a, but still cor-
responds to relatively young ages < 7 Myr which is
the evolutionary stage when RSGs appear and domi-
nate the optical to NIR part of the SED (Bruzual &
Charlot 2003). In recent work by Pedrini et al. (2025),
a flux excess in young star clusters has been found in
the F150W and F200W NIRCam bands that cannot be
explained by standard SED models and could be due to
an underestimated contribution of RSGs.
Even though we find clues in the NIR for some star
clusters that point in the direction of evolved stars like
RSGs as the underlying mechanism of internal dust pro-
duction manifesting with a 10 µm silicate feature, this
scenario does not apply well to all 22 star clusters. Star
clusters of categories 3 and 4a do not show a significant
NIR bump and their age range favors a pre-RSG stage.

spatial with the star clusters and that we see further
evidence around 16 star clusters of dust clearing in the
form of shell structures and bubbles.

As

tions. A large population of RSGs that create a cumula-
tive 10 µm emission might therefore a possible scenario.
It is therefore now necessary to understand whether the
observed luminosities of this feature can be explained by
RSGs for the star clusters presented here. We show the
MIR spectrum of the RSG HV 888 in Fig. 5 presenting
its clear 10 µm silicate feature. This is a luminous RSG
of log(L/L⊙) = 5.48 (Beasor & Smith 2022) which is an
important aspect as the silicate strength is correlated
with the RSG luminosity (Skinner & Whitmore 1988).
Stellar population synthesis models predict that at so-
lar metallicity, star clusters of 106M⊙will have more
than 100 RSGs with luminosities of log(L/L⊙) > 4.9
at an age of ∼10 Myr (Eldridge et al. 2017, see their
Fig. 15). In the subsequent evolution this number drops
rapidly and stays below 10 after ∼20 Myr. This makes
it difficult to believe this scenario for the star clusters
of category 5 as they are very likely much older than
10 Myr. For the star clusters that are at an evolution-
ary stage where we would expect a large RSG population
there is an additional caveat: first, only four star clus-
ters in our sample have a stellar mass exceeding 106M⊙
(see Table 4) which is, according to the models, needed
to produce a large enough number of RSGs. Second,
are the models assuming the RSGs population with a
luminosity distribution to be log(L/L⊙) > 4.9 which is
significantly lower than HV 888, one of the most lumi-
nous RSGs known (van Loon et al. 2005). Considering
that the 10 µm silicate intensity indirectly depends on
the RSG luminosity, this means that the expected lumi-
nosity based on modeled RSG numbers and the HV 888
luminosity is most likely overestimated.
A further aspect is the luminosity at 21 µm relative
to 10 µm: As well visible Fig. 5 RSGs like HV 888 or
RSG dominated star clusters like Westerlund 1 show a
significantly lower luminosity at 21 µm in comparison to
10 µm. This scenario might apply to about half of our
star clusters but especially for the more luminous part
we do see a steeper rising in luminosity at 21 µm.
The classical RSG scenario can indeed be an expla-
nation for the less luminous star clusters of our sample.
Furthermore, it has been observed that large amounts
of RSGs can be present in young massive star clusters
at around 10 Myr of age (Figer et al. 2006; Davies et al.
2007). It might be also possible that the number of RSG
in massive young star clusters can be significantly under-
estimated by current stellar population synthesis mod-
els. RSGs evolve from massive O stars and the number
of these stars is dictated by the initial mass of the star
cluster. An alternative approach in order to explain an
unexpectedly high number of RSGs can be a top heavy
IMF where more massive stars were formed in these star
clusters resulting in a larger number of evolved stars.
The star cluster Westerlund 1 presents a more realistic
scenario: with a mass of 6 × 104 M⊙(Portegies Zwart
et al. 2010) it is on average an order of magnitude less
massive than the star clusters discussed here. It is char-
acterized by exhibiting an unusual population of RSGs
and Yellow Super Giants (YSGs) (Clark et al. 2005;
Guarcello et al. 2025). However if we would scale up the
MIR luminosity by a factor of 10 in order to match the
stellar masses of our star cluster sample Westerlund 1
would still fail to explain the most luminous star clus-
ters.

6.3. Silicate Emission from Dusty SNe Ejecta
Core collapse SNe are known to be one of the main
sources injecting energy into the ISM and are consid-
ered one of the main dust producers enriching the ISM
(Todini & Ferrara 2001; Nozawa et al. 2003, 2007; Mat-
suura et al. 2015). SNR are known to show NIR emission
(Sarbadhicary et al. 2025) and most importantly can ex-
hibit a distinct silicate feature at 10 µm (Micelotta et al.
2018). The IR features can make a significant contribu-
tion to the host star cluster SED (Mart´ınez-Gonz´alez
et al. 2017), making SNR a potential explanation for
the here observed 10 µm feature in massive young star
clusters. An additional aspect is the age of the star clus-
ters, as they are all estimated to be older than ∼3 Myr
an age when SNe are expected to start playing a major
role in the star cluster evolution when a fully sampled
IMF is assumed (Leitherer et al. 2014; Chevance et al.
2022). However, there is a caveat: the time scales over
which we can observe SNR and their effects are relatively
short. As summarized in Sect. 2.5, SNR are observable
for about 20–80 kyr (Sarbadhicary et al. 2017), and the
main detection methods including X-ray, radio contin-
uum and optical emission line ratios require fairly deep
observations for the distances of our star clusters. Fur-
thermore, the hot dust emission in the NIR and 10 µm
silicate features that form shortly after the SNe is only
short lived (Fox et al. 2010, 2011).
Mart´ınez-Gonz´alez et al. (2016) modeled stochastic
dust injection from SNe in young super star clusters
(M∗= 105M⊙) at a distance of 10 Mpc. Strong NIR
dust emission with a distinct 10 µm silicate feature can
significantly weaken after only 500 yr and almost com-
pletely disappears after ∼7000 yr.

6.4. SNR indicators
Since SNe are a considerable scenario to explain the
observed 10 µm silicate enhancement we investigate in
further indicators that could reveal their presence. We
here use the SNR catalogs introduced in Sect. 2.5 and
find no cross matches for our star cluster sample. How-
ever, this does not exclude potential SNR in these star
clusters since the used catalogs by construction only in-
clude SNR associated with luminous nebulae (Li et al.
2024). As discussed in Sect. 5.3 the majority of the star
clusters do not show any significant Hα detection, pre-
venting us from identifying SNR associated with them
on the basis of their optical line emission.

through detection of compact X-ray sources. However,
this method is not unambiguous since soft X-ray sources

Sect. 2.5, we have Chandra X-ray observations for 19 of
the 22 star clusters and indeed detect three sources in

6.5. What IR Spectroscopy Could Tell Us
In the above discussion, we have listed all of the possi-
ble mechanisms that we know of that could realistically
lead to a 10 µm excess in massive young star clusters.
But with the currently available observations we are not
able to pinpoint the exact origin. The main questions we
need to answer are: How many evolved stars, in particu-
lar RSGs, are present in the star clusters? Are luminous
LBV stars or YHGs present? Is the observed dust due
to SNe? And lastly, could a combination of the above
be possible?
The answer to these questions can in fact be pro-
vided with NIR and MIR spectroscopy. Even though
we presented strong arguments that the observed 10 µm
excess is due to silicate emission, only spectroscopic
observations can confirm this.
Furthermore, an ac-
curate measurement of the silicate emission would in-
volve the subtraction of the underlying dust contin-
uum, potential neighboring PAH features and emission
lines.
This can help us to understand how to better
identify further 10 µm emitters given the fact that in
some cases the F770W band is tracing the stellar con-
tinuum. As discussed in Sect. 5.3, the F1130W band
is also contaminated by the broad silicate feature and
for future selection a more fine-tuned selection proce-
dure based on broad band filters could be developed.
MIR spectroscopy would furthermore enable us to probe
why we do not observe any PAH features, especially in
the youngest star clusters.
We can directly measure
the ionization hardness through the [Ne III]15.56/[Ne
II]12.81 µm ratio (Madden et al. 2006) and quantify
the ionization impact on PAH concentration in the sur-
roundings. This would tell us whether the PAHs were
mechanically pushed outwards or destroyed by ioniza-
tion (Egorov et al. 2023).
The NIR has a large variety of features that can
help us to infer characteristics of the stellar popula-
tion.
For instance, we can estimate the contribution
of RSGs through modeling their atmosphere in the NIR
(Lan¸con et al. 2007).
They can be identified with K
band spectroscopy, because of their strong CO band-
head at 2.3µm (Ivanov et al. 2004).
The equivalent
width measurement of the CO absorption features can
also provide an quantification of the contribution of
RSGs (F¨orster Schreiber 2000; Davies et al. 2007; Mar-
tins et al. 2012; Patrick et al. 2016). This feature would
not be present for LBVs or YHGs, since they are too hot,
but other features in the NIR can be used to test their
presence. It is challenging to distinguish LBVs, YHGs
and Blue Super Giants Oksala et al. (2013) but there are
subtle features in the NIR: We know that cooler LBVs
(T < 10 kK) exhibit NaI doublet emission and critically
lack HeI 2.112 µm emission or absorption (Clark et al.
2011). At higher temperatures HeI 2.112 µm is initially
observed in absorption, before being driven into emission
along with HeI 2.058 µm and low excitation species such
as MgII and FeII. At even higher temperatures, this pro-
cess also seems to be accompanied by a P Cygni profile
in the HeI 2.058 µm line (Clark et al. 2009).
Whilst
Balmer lines are produced by many different mecha-
nisms within the cluster and Doppler shifting could po-
tentially overshadow a P Cygni profile (Wofford et al.
2020), the HeI 2.058 µm line can provide this insight as
it is only produced by extremely hot sources, confirming
the presence of one or several super hot evolved stars.
The average photosphere temperature estimation
from modeling the CO band-head as described in Messi-
neo et al. (2014) can give us an estimation of the RSG
population and their contribution to the 10 µm feature.
Hot massive evolved stars and RSG will appear at dif-
ferent points in time, with the former at around 3 Myr
and the latter appearing after 5 Myr, which is also true
for binary models (Eldridge et al. 2017). However a su-
perposition would indicate that the star clusters have
not formed in an instantaneous burst and are formed in
episodes. Observations favor hierarchical star formation
scenarios that happen over several Myr as for example
found in 30 Doradus (Selman et al. 1999; Rahner et al.
2018; Fahrion & De Marchi 2024), NGC 6530 (Prisin-
zano et al. 2019) or NGC 1850A (Caloi & Cassatella
1998).
The absence of [FeII] 1.64µm signals in the six star
clusters observed with the F164N filter and discussed
above does not directly exclude the hypothesis of a re-
lation between SNe and the observed silicate feature. In
order to further investigate a possible SNe relation, NIR
spectroscopy could help since it would be able to pick
up signals that would be missed by narrow-band imag-
ing due to inaccurate continuum subtraction. The same
would be true for H2 2.12µm emission.
7. CONCLUSION
We here presented a sample of 22 massive (≥105M⊙)
star clusters found in nearby galaxies (8.6 to 19.4 Mpc)
with an age range between ∼3 and ≤100 Myr. The
main feature that these star clusters all have in common
is that they show a distinct bright emission feature at
10 µm and with one exception they all appear to have no
associated PAH emission. These star clusters all have a
compact MIR morphology and do not show any signs of
extended dust features like clouds or filaments.
We categorized the sample according to their optical
morphology which we use as an age indicator and com-
pared the results to ages from optical SED fitting and

the position on color-color diagrams. We find that 14
clusters show signs of feedback in the form of ionized
gas shells or bubbles and five star clusters are in an
advanced evolutionary stage where we cannot associate
any feedback features to them. Furthermore, three star
clusters are in an earlier stage of natal dust clearing and
still show dust lanes and ionized gas on top of the source.
We perform panchromatic photometry from the NUV
to MIR in order to study the overall SED shapes. We
find that star clusters with age estimates < 5 Myr show
a rather flat NIR spectrum. Star clusters which are cat-
egorized to be older tend to show a flux enhancement in
the NIR and exhibit an increasingly redder MIR slope.
Some star clusters, especially NGC 5194–21 show dis-
tinct bumps in the NIR which could originate from a
large population of evolved stars such as red super gi-
ants. Star clusters in NGC 5194 for which we have better
band coverage in the MIR, also exhibit an enhancement
at 18 µm providing a strong argument that the 10 µm
emission is due to silicates. We also discuss the possi-
bility that strong ionized gas emission lines or molec-
ular hydrogen lines that are situated within the MIRI
F1000W band could potentially cause the observed en-
hancement. With one exception, NGC 1672–7, we con-
sider ionized gas emission lines to be rather unlikely
due to the lack of other ionized features like strong Hα
emission.
The estimated amount of molecular hydro-
gen needed to produce a significant 10 µm enhancement
makes this scenario also highly unlikely.
Silicate emission from stellar populations is a well
known phenomenon which has been characterized inside
the Milky Way and the local group in parts of resolved
star clusters and was mainly associated to evolved stars
(e.g. Beasor et al. 2021). The novelty presented in this
work is the clear domination of the MIR SED by silicate
emission which is very likely connected to an internal
dust production mechanism disconnected from the na-
tal dust cloud.
From the age estimates, it is very unlikely that asymp-
totic giant branch stars, which are known for their sili-
cate ejecta, make any contribution to the observed dust
features. On the other hand, we discussed evolved stars
like red and yellow super giants, yellow hyper giants and
luminous blue variable stars. We find that red super gi-
ants can easily explain the observed IR fluxes for at least
the less luminous star clusters. It is well known that
massive stellar populations can undergo a phase with an
exceptional number of evolved stars (Clark et al. 2005;
Guarcello et al. 2025). Also stellar population synthesis
models predict a peak in the number of red super gi-
ants at an age of ∼5 Myr. In very massive star clusters
(∼106 M⊙) this can reach many hundreds of red super
giants. However, the number of predicted red super gi-
ants is by far not sufficient to explain the MIR fluxes
observed for the brightest and most massive star clus-
ters in our sample. If the silicate emission is purely due
to red and yellow super giants, a possible explanation
for the more MIR luminous star clusters could be a top-
heavy initial mass function that produces a much higher
number of massive stars that evolve into super giants.
From a systematic archive search of known star sys-
tems with strong silicate emission, we also discuss the
yellow hyper giant IRC-10420 and η Carinae. Both sys-
tems show significantly stronger MIR luminosities than
red super giants and are a possible explanation for the
majority of the star cluster sample. In fact for some star
clusters only one such star would be enough to repro-
duce the MIR flux. However, the three brightest star
clusters in our sample exceed even the luminosities of η
Carinae which indicates that either multiple such star
systems are present or the IR features we are observing
are due to a different mechanism.
A pure theoretical idea we discussed are very mas-
sive stars. The strength of the MIR silicate features in
evolved stars is correlated to their mass loss rate and
is therefore limited by the mass of the stars. Current
models attribute extreme mass loss rates to very mas-
sive stars which makes this in theory a scenario worth
investigating.
We conduct an in-depth discussion of whether the
silicate emission and the near IR excess could be ex-
plained by dust production in supernovae.
This has
been observed for individual supernova remnants and
theoretical models predict significant dust production
from stochastic supernova injection in massive young
star clusters. We furthermore discuss the expected spec-
tral energy distribution in the MIR of radiative super-
nova in dense ISM and calculate 10 µm luminosities that
match the majority of the presented star cluster sam-
ple. Since these scenarios are mainly driven by theory,
we investigate any possible link we could find that in-
dicate recent supernovae. We do not find any matches
with known supernova remnant catalogs which we have
available for all galaxies.
This could be explained by
the fact that these catalogs are biased towards clouds
of ionized gas, which for the largest part of our sample
is absent.
Six of the clusters in our sample were ob-
served with the F164N narrow-band filter, allowing us
to search for the [FeII] 1.64 µm line, which could indicate
the presence of shocks and therefore supernovae. How-
ever, we did not find evidence of any shocks present in
these six star clusters. Finally we observed an X-ray sig-
nal clearly associated to the star clusters NGC 3627–10
and NGC 5194–22 and an X-ray enhancement associated

with the star cluster NGC 1672–7 which is situated in a
region with diffuse thermal X-ray emission. The energy
distribution of the X-ray observations indicate that this
could be due to supernovae but we cannot exclude the
possibility of X-ray binary systems. In the case of su-
pernova in a dense hot medium we would expect the soft
X-ray to be absorbed which makes supernovae a possible
explanation for observed silicate emission.
In this work we have systematically described the phe-
nomenon of silicate emission in massive star clusters and
discussed all possible scenarios. It might be possible that
not only one mechanism is responsible for the observed
IR features and that a combination of multiple phenom-
ena could be at play.
In order to confirm the origin
of the 10 µm excess that we observe in these massive
star clusters, NIR to MIR spectroscopy is needed. This
would allow us to accurately measure and confirm the
silicate emission feature and investigate further stellar
tracers or supernova tracers in the NIR.
From the large age spread that we find in our sample
it seems unlikely that only one mechanism is responsible
for all of the detected 10 µm emitters. An important fac-
tor is that all luminous 10 µm emitters are star clusters
(see top right corner of the distribution in Fig. 2) and
that there is a clear mass dependency on the luminosity
of the 10 µm feature. In particular, the most massive
star clusters exceed the 10 µm luminosity of any known
object in the Milky Way. This indicates that in these
star clusters, rare objects such as stars with exceptional
mass-loss rates could be present which only show up at
very high masses and presumably during a short period
during their evolution. This might be the reason why we
have not observed such a phenomenon inside the Milky
way. Furthermore, the compact MIR morphology is an
indicator that this is originating from an internal pro-
cess favoring the dust production by stellar sources or
supernovae.
The James Webb Space Telescope has firstly allowed
us to measure the NIR and MIR spectral energy dis-
tribution on scales of star clusters in nearby galaxies.
This provides a crucial aspect on how we understand
the baryonic cycle which describes the process of star
formation and the re-injection of material into the ISM.
With this work we have provided evidence that the dust
production in star clusters keeps on going even after
the majority of the natal dust has been cleared. From
these results specific questions for future investigations
are motivated: How significant is the contribution of the
ongoing dust production? Is this only a phenomenon in
massive star clusters since it is tied to a large popu-
lation of massive stars?
What contribution does this
phenomenon make to the NIR and MIR in the context
of spectral energy distribution models?

In a next step we normalize the convolved PSF image to one and measure the flux inside a set of 50 circular
apertures with radii distributed with equal steps between σPSF and 2′′. Apertures of the size σPSF and smaller are
not recommended as they do not probe enough flux to estimate a valid correction. Furthermore, we only compute
corrections for apertures up to 2′′ for completeness reasons and we strongly advise against the use of too large aperture

which will be the inverse factor we can use to correct aperture measurements.
As a last step we measure the profile along 12 slits with a width of 2 pixels and the length of 5′′ that are placed in
steps of 30◦. This is visualized in the top middle panel of Fig. 8 and the profiles are presented in the top right panel.

measure the flux. The mean standard deviation σ is then the measured value we use as a representation of the source
size. If we would fit a Gaussian to an average source profile we would get the same result. But we take this approach
as it has clear advantages on real data as discussed below.
We have now computed 20 models with increasing source sizes and measured the encircled energy (EE) for 50 radii in
each of the models. This provides us with a grid of flux fraction values measured inside a certain aperture for a specific
source size. We show each estimated value with a circle in the bottom of Fig. 8 for this grid, but only up to radii of 0.8′′.
We then perform a cubic interpolation of this 2D parameter space with the RegularGridInterpolator method
provided by the Python package Scipy (Virtanen et al. 2020). As shown in Fig. 8 the parameter space is continuous,
allowing us to estimate an aperture correction based on any aperture radius and measured σ that is covered by this
grid.

can adopt the Moffat profile for all bands.

objects with multiple peaks like star clusters classified as class 3 (see definition in Adamo et al. 2017; Whitmore et al.
2021; Thilker et al. 2022; Deger et al. 2022; Maschmann et al. 2024). However, this would need the development of
correction profiles tailored to such source profile shapes and is hence left as a topic for future work.

B.2. SED Models with Nebular Emission
In order to estimate under which circumstances a significant enhancement in the F1000W MIRI band can be driven

emission the model parameters for the dust models do not have a strong impact on the maximal E10 µm values. This is
due to the fact that the enhancement of the F1000W band driven by ionized gas emission lines can have a much stronger
impact than the pure dust continuum shape. In fig. 11 we present models with the dust parameters qPAH = 0.47,

the star cluster NGC 1672–7 NGC 3627–9 the case but very unlikely for the remaining sample.
