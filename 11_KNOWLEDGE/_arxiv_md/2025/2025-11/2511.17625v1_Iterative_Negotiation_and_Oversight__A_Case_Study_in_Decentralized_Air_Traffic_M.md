Iterative Negotiation and Oversight:
A Case Study in Decentralized Air Traffic Management
Jaehan Im, John-Paul Clarke, Ufuk Topcu, David Fridovich-Keil
The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas, United States
Abstract
Achieving consensus among noncooperative agents remains challenging in decentralized multi-agent systems, where
agents often have conflicting preferences. Existing coordination methods enable agents to reach consensus with-
out a centralized coordinator, but do not provide formal guarantees on system-level objectives such as efficiency or
fairness. To address this limitation, we propose an iterative negotiation and oversight framework that augments a
decentralized negotiation mechanism with taxation-like oversight. The framework builds upon the trading auction
for consensus, enabling noncooperative agents with conflicting preferences to negotiate through asset trading while
preserving valuation privacy. We introduce an oversight mechanism, which implements a taxation-like intervention
that guides decentralized negotiation toward system-efficient and equitable outcomes while also regulating how fast
the framework converges. We establish theoretical guarantees of finite-time termination and derive bounds linking
system efficiency and convergence rate to the level of central intervention. A case study based on the collaborative
trajectory options program, a rerouting initiative in U.S. air traffic management, demonstrates that the framework can
reliably achieve consensus among noncooperative airspace sector managers, and reveals how the level of intervention
regulates the relationship between system efficiency and convergence speed. Taken together, the theoretical and ex-
perimental results indicate that the proposed framework provides a general mechanism for decentralized coordination
in noncooperative multi-agent systems while safeguarding system-level objectives.
Keywords: Noncooperative Coordination, Negotiation Mechanism, Decentralized System, Air Traffic Management
1. Introduction
Multi-agent systems frequently require agents to reach a shared decision, such as in task allocation [1], formation
control [2], or scheduling [3]. Achieving such agreement becomes particularly challenging when agents behave in a
self-interested manner, possess conflicting preferences over multiple choices, and prefer not to disclose private infor-
mation [1, 4]. While these challenges already make agreement difficult, compounding issue arises when consensus
alone is insufficient to ensure system-level objectives such as efficiency, fairness, or safety. In the absence of a mecha-
nism that safeguards system-level objectives, decentralized negotiation may converge to equilibria that are stable from
each agent’s perspective but misaligned with system-level objectives. This limitation becomes particularly critical in
safety-sensitive settings, where poorly coordinated outcomes may be suboptimal or even unsafe depending on the
application [5, 6, 7].
Existing decentralized consensus mechanisms enable agents to reach consensus without a central coordinator or
substantial information sharing [1, 2, 8, 9, 10]. However, these methods largely focus on reaching agreement—often
via heuristic mechanisms—and do not provide formal guarantees on efficiency, fairness, or safety [1, 8, 10, 11]. In
contrast, methods that can explicitly enforce system-level objectives almost always rely on cooperative behaviors or
centralized decision-making [6, 12, 13]. Taken together, the current literature lacks mechanisms that embed system-
level safeguards while preserving the strictly noncooperative and decentralized nature of agents [5, 11, 14].
We adopt a two-layer perspective in which a decentralized negotiation mechanism aligns self-interested behav-
ior toward consensus, while additional oversight provides system-level performance control. We build on the trading
auction for consensus (TACo) [5], an auction-based mechanism that enables noncooperative agents to reach agreement
through the exchange of secondary assets such as monetary tokens or emission credits. TACo is particularly suitable

(iii) We evaluate the framework using a case study based on the collaborative trajectory options program [15, 16, 17],
demonstrating improvements in efficiency and fairness while respecting the agents’ self-interested decisions.
2. Related Works
Decentralized and Noncooperative Coordination Mechanisms. Various lines of research address consensus formation
among self-interested agents operating under decentralized and privacy-preserving conditions. Negotiation-based [2],
auction-based [1, 8], and learning-based approaches [9, 10] enable agents to reach agreement while preserving au-
tonomy and without revealing private preferences [3]. These methods scale well to distributed settings and have
been applied in various decentralized decision-making tasks, including task allocation and distributed resource man-
agement [18]. Despite their practical utility, these mechanisms primarily focus on reaching agreement rather than
regulating system-level behavior. Although empirical studies report improvements in average performance, these re-
sults are heuristic and do not provide formal guarantees on global efficiency, fairness, or safety [1, 8, 10, 11]. This
limitation becomes critical in safety-sensitive decentralized systems, where consensus failure can adversely impact
system operation [19].
Safeguard-Embedded Coordination. In contrast, substantial research has focused on ensuring system-level objectives
such as safety, equity, and efficiency. Centralized optimization frameworks [20, 21], contract-based coordination [22],
and global constraint enforcement schemes [13] offer strong guarantees and precise control over aggregate outcomes.
Similarly, fairness-oriented and safety-critical coordination frameworks explicitly incorporate domain constraints to
enforce system-wide requirements [23]. However, these methods typically require cooperative agents, shared infor-
mation, or a centralized decision authority, making them incompatible with strictly noncooperative and decentralized
architectures in which agents act autonomously and may withhold private information.
Hybrid and Oversight Mechanisms. Several research efforts attempt to combine decentralized autonomy with desir-
able global behavior by embedding distributed oversight or incentive-based regulation. Examples include consensus
protocols [4], incentive or penalty shaping in learning-based systems [24, 25], and taxation-like interventions in par-
tially decentralized negotiation schemes [26]. These mechanisms aim to guide self-interested agents toward more
socially desirable outcomes without explicit centralization. More recent studies explore a form of global feedback or
regulatory signals to influence decentralized decision processes [27]. However, safeguards introduced in these works
are typically heuristic: while they often improve aggregate performance, they rarely establish formal system-level
guarantees, convergence behavior, or principled mechanisms for enforcing global objectives.
Consequently, the challenge of designing a framework that preserves the noncooperative nature while providing a
formal guarantee on system-level safeguards remains largely open, particularly in safety-critical domains such as air
traffic management.
3. Preliminaries: Decentralized noncooperative negotiation via TACo
We employ the trading auction for consensus (TACo) as a baseline mechanism for noncooperative negotiation
[5]. TACo enables self-interested stakeholders to agree on a common decision while pursuing different objectives and
withholding private information.

TACo operates as a structured give-and-take process, where agents iteratively trade a secondary asset (e.g., emis-
sion credits or monetary tokens) to persuade others to accept their preferred choice. It provides a fully decentralized
procedure through which consensus naturally emerges from agents’ self-interested behavior and yields an outcome
that remains incentive-aligned for all participants, without centralized coordination or direct bilateral communication.
Unlike standard single-round voting-based consensus mechanisms, TACo introduces a dynamic trading structure
that aligns incentives through self-interested behavior. Although there is no formal guarantee, this mechanism often
yields outcomes with a lower overall system cost and improved fairness compared to voting, as trading redistributes
the burden among agents with differing valuations.

3.2. Properties of TACo
The properties summarized below make TACo a practical foundation for decentralized coordination:
Incentive-aligned process. Each agent acts solely in its self-interest, and consensus emerges as the natural con-
sequence of individual trading decisions rather than cooperative agreement. The resulting outcome also remains
incentive-aligned for all agents.
Privacy preservation. Agents never reveal their private valuations (bi) or intrinsic costs (Ci j); only aggregate prefer-
ence expressions are publicly observable.
Decentralized mechanism. TACo does not require either auctioneer nor central coordinator. All agents follow identical
public rules and rely solely on broadcast information, enabling a fully decentralized implementation.

Finite termination guarantee. The algorithm provably converges within a finite number of steps.
For these reasons, we adopt TACo as the foundation of our negotiation framework.
3.3. Limitations of noncooperative negotiation without oversight
While TACo enables self-interested agents to reach consensus in a fully decentralized manner, it cannot change the
underlying choices available to the agents, some or all of which may be poor. Furthermore, TACo does not optimize
for any concept of system optimality, and is only guaranteed to find a solution that is unilaterally acceptable to all
agents.
In safety-critical domains, these limitations motivate the need for an additional oversight mechanism that guides
the negotiation toward system-level objectives. The following section introduces our main contribution; an iterative
framework that embeds such an oversight into the TACo-based negotiation.
4. Iterative negotiation and oversight framework
We extend the TACo mechanism with an outer-loop — implemented by a central authority — that adjusts taxation-
like intervention while preserving the agents’ noncooperative best-response dynamics. Moreover, at each outer-loop
round, we allow agents to propose new choices for negotiation, which are influenced by a coordination factor selected
by the proposed mechanism based upon the previous rounds of negotiation.
Each outer iteration consists of four stages, as shown in Algorithm 1: (i) candidate choice generation by each
agent, (ii) a TACo negotiation based on the generated choices and the taxation-adjusted secondary asset values, (iii)
shortfall evaluation with respect to trading expenditures and available asset reserves, and (iv) coordination factor
update and broadcast. This four-step process (candidate–negotiation–shortfall–update) repeats until the termination
condition is met.

4.5. Oversight role and safeguard
As the shared term in the effective cost (7) gains greater influence across rounds, agents generate progressively
more coordinated and equitable candidate choices, enabling consensus to be reached with smaller asset transfers.
Such gradual coordination is also expected to implicitly contribute to improved system-level efficiency.
We claim that the framework always converges to an outcome satisfying the reserve constraints within a finite
number of rounds. Moreover, the taxation parameter κ governs both the convergence speed and the resulting system
efficiency: a smaller κ leads to faster negotiations (fewer rounds) and less system-efficient outcomes, whereas a larger κ
promotes more system-efficient outcomes at the expense of slower convergence. These properties are formalized and
proven in the following section.
5. Convergence Proof and Oversight Guide
We establish the convergence and performance guarantees of the proposed iterative negotiation with oversight.
We first prove finite termination of the framework, and then relate the tax parameter κ to (i) an upper bound for the
number of rounds until convergence and (ii) a system-optimality gap bound.
5.1. Finite-round convergence
We now establish that the proposed iterative negotiation with oversight framework always converges within a finite
number of rounds. Before presenting the theorem that guarantees convergence, we outline the key steps underlying
the proof. First, we show that as ∥w(r)∥1, i.e., the magnitude of the coordination factor, increases, all agents eventually
generate candidates with nearly equivalent costs (Step 1). Second, we show that this flattening bounds the magnitude
of individual asset transfers between agents after TACo (Step 2). Finally, we prove that the process must terminate as
all players eventually satisfy any nonzero asset reserves (Step 3).

6. Case study: Decentralized collaborative trajectory options program
Having established the theoretical framework for decentralized negotiation and oversight, we now demonstrate
its practical application to the collaborative trajectory options program (CTOP). This case study illustrates how the
proposed framework can decentralize the existing air traffic management operation paradigm, which traditionally
involves centralized decision-making. The overall flow of both the conventional and decentralized CTOP frameworks
is summarized in Figure 2.
6.1. Collaborative trajectory options program (CTOP)
The CTOP is a traffic management initiative designed in the United States to mitigate airspace congestion by co-
ordinating trajectory options for multiple flights [15, 29, 30]. In CTOP, each airline submits a set of alternative flight
trajectories, referred to as trajectory options sets (TOS), where each flight is associated with q trajectory options that
differ in route, altitude, or estimated arrival time. A centralized decision-making entity evaluates these submitted tra-
jectories based on predicted sector demand, weather conditions, and sector capacity constraints. The central controller
then assigns a trajectory to each flight, balancing global efficiency with equity among airlines.
6.2. Decentralized CTOP
While CTOP enhances operational flexibility by considering the preferences of each individual airline, it does not
account for the preferences of the individual airspace sector managers—or air route traffic control centers (ARTCCs).
Thus, the current paradigm still presents a potential for further decentralization, given the scalability demands of
CTOP and the practical limits on cross-center information sharing [31]. Building on the negotiation and oversight
framework introduced in Section 4, we develop a decentralized formulation of the CTOP, where each airspace sector,
ARTCC, is modeled as a self-interested agent responsible for assigning trajectory options for all flights traversing
its region to minimize its own operational congestion. Unlike the centralized CTOP, where a single decision maker
determines the global schedule, the decentralized CTOP allows sectors to suggest their preferred bundles of trajectory
options and negotiate to reach a feasible agreement.

8. Conclusion
We propose an iterative negotiation and oversight framework that enables coordination among self-interested
agents while safeguarding system-level performance through taxation-like intervention.
We establish theoretical
guarantees of finite-round convergence and derive explicit bounds linking the level of central intervention to both
convergence rate and system efficiency. These results provide practical guidance for a central coordinator in selecting
the intervention level to balance convergence speed, efficiency, and fairness.
To validate the framework, we developed a decentralized variant of the collaborative trajectory options program
(CTOP)—a traffic management initiative currently implemented in the United States national airspace system—and
evaluated it in a realistic sector-level coordination scenario. Simulation results confirm that the framework converges
in all cases and that adjusting the intervention level effectively regulates the convergence rate, efficiency, and fairness.
The oversight does not directly optimize the system but implicitly guides agents toward coordinated behavior, thereby
preserving autonomy while maintaining system-level safety. Overall, the results demonstrate a practical pathway for
realizing noncooperative, decentralized air traffic management.
The same principle can be generalized to other multi-agent systems that require reliable noncooperative coordi-
nation beyond air traffic management. Future work may focus on tightening the theoretical bounds, extending the
framework to multi-asset trading schemes, and developing decentralized safeguard mechanisms that ensure fairness
and efficiency without centralized oversight or taxation.
