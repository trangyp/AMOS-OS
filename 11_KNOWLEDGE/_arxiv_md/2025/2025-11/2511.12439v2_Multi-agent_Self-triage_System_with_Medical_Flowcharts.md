Abstract 
Online health resources and large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as a first point 
of contact for medical decision-making, yet their reliability in healthcare remains limited by low 
accuracy, lack of transparency, and susceptibility to unverified information. We introduce a 
proof-of-concept conversational self-triage system that guides LLMs with 100 clinically validated 
flowcharts from the American Medical Association, providing a structured and auditable 
framework for patient decision support. The system leverages a multi-agent framework 
consisting of a retrieval agent, a decision agent, and a chat agent to identify the most relevant 
flowchart, 
interpret 
patient 
responses, 
and 
deliver 
personalized, 
patient-friendly 
recommendations, respectively. Performance was evaluated at scale using synthetic datasets of 
simulated conversations. The system achieved 95.29% top-3 accuracy in flowchart retrieval 
(N=2,000) and 99.10% accuracy in flowchart navigation across varied conversational styles and 
conditions (N=37,200). By combining the flexibility of free-text interaction with the rigor of 
standardized clinical protocols, this approach demonstrates the feasibility of transparent, 
accurate, and generalizable AI-assisted self-triage, with potential to support informed patient 
decision-making while improving healthcare resource utilization.

Main 
Self-triage, the process by which people assess the urgency of their symptoms and decide 
when and where to seek medical care, is becoming increasingly prevalent with the use of online 
resources. According to the National Center for Health Statistics, 58.5% of adults searched for 
health or medical information online during a six-month period from July to December 20221. 
Popular platforms such as WebMD and Mayo Clinic offer a wide range of health content, 
including symptom checkers, drug information, and in-depth articles on diseases and conditions. 
More recently, a growing number of people have begun relying on large-language models 
(LLMs) for health-related inquiries, complex probabilistic algorithms that generate human-like 
textual responses, following the introduction of ChatGPT2 in 2022. A U.S. online survey of 2,002 
participants found that 95.6% of the respondents used search engines for health queries, while 
32.6% reported using LLMs such as ChatGPT to address medical or health questions in 20243. 
The trend in self-triage towards LLMs has shifted the initial point of contact in healthcare from 
clinicians to online platforms, which offer greater accessibility and cost-efficiency, particularly for 
individuals in low- and middle-income regions with limited healthcare infrastructure. 
 
At the other end of the spectrum, emergency rooms today are frequently overwhelmed with 
nonurgent visits. Nearly 40% of all emergency department (ED) visits in the U.S. are medically 
nonurgent4, which could be more appropriately managed in walk-in clinics or by primary care 
providers. Such unnecessary ED visits not only result in longer waiting time for patients truly 
requiring emergency intervention, but also contribute to physician fatigue and burnout. An 
effective and accessible self-triage tool could enable patients to receive timely triage 
recommendations and allow them to better manage their conditions and plan for clinic visits. 
Meanwhile, clinicians can offload the triage burden and enhance the overall efficiency of 
healthcare resource allocation. 
 
Despite the growing need, developing an informative and reliable self-triage tool remains 
challenging. While symptom checkers5–7 can assist with self-triage by providing a list of possible 
causes or diagnoses, it suffers from low accuracy, requires a certain level of health literacy, and 
fails to provide definitive actions tailored to specific cases8,9. Conversational LLM chatbots such 
as ChatGPT frequently provide vague or overly general advice and at the same time suffer from 
the inherent “hallucination problem”, where models fabricate incorrect or misleading information 
due to their probabilistic prediction nature10–12. Moreover, LLMs are considered as a “black box” 
given their generation process is rarely transparent, making it difficult for users or clinicians to 
trust or validate the guidance provided13,14. Although LLMs can be fine-tuned on domain-specific 
medical datasets to improve their performance, it is difficult for healthcare professionals to 
control or audit the output of LLMs before deploying them as medical chatbots for public use. 
The lack of a shared, structured representation of the medically informed procedures between 
human experts and LLMs limits their reliability and safety for both patients and providers. 
 
To address these challenges, we propose using clinically validated flowcharts as a shared 
representation between LLMs and healthcare practitioners for healthcare applications. We 
demonstrate this concept through a conversational self-triage AI system - TriageMD, explicitly


## Page 3


guided by American Medical Association (AMA) flowcharts that are retrieved based on 
conversation intake. The flowchart format enables the system to guide users through a series of 
clinically relevant questions, reflecting the established procedural flow clinical nurses use. This 
approach combines the free-text conversational capabilities of LLMs with the clinical rigor of 
standardized triage protocols, offering a novel solution for delivering personalized and reliable 
decision support. We leverage different prompt engineering techniques and a multi-agent 
framework (Figure 1) to create the system which performs triage tasks through natural 
conversations. The system is composed of a flowchart database which stores the 
graph-represented AMA flowcharts (Figure 1b) and three agents powered by LLMs: retrieval 
agent, decision agent, and chat agent (Figure 1c). These agents collaboratively perform the 
triage task by retrieving the relevant flowchart based on patient information, interpreting patient 
responses, and providing patient-friendly guidance, respectively. At the end of the triage 
process, the system provides final action suggestions (Figure 1a) tailored to each individual 
case as derived from navigating through the relevant AMA-validated flowchart (Figure 1d). 
 
We evaluated our proof-of-concept system through two tasks: flowchart retrieval, which 
measures the system’s ability to identify the most appropriate flowchart from the database, and 
flowchart navigation, which assesses its ability to interpret patient responses and provide 
appropriate step-by-step guidance. Given the intended use in healthcare settings, we aimed to 
evaluate the system’s performance at scale to rigorously test the reliability and faithfulness of its 
outputs. To this end, we used synthetic datasets generated by different language models for 
controlled and repeatable evaluation of simulated conversations. The retrieval results show that 
the system identified the top-3 most relevant flowcharts out of 100 validated AMA flowcharts 
with an average accuracy of 95.29%. In the navigation task, the system achieved an average 
accuracy of 99.10% in understanding simulated patient responses with diverse conversational 
styles (i.e., brief vs descriptive, clear vs vague) and conditions (i.e., yes/no/maybe, off-topic) and 
guiding conversations towards a final recommendation. These findings support the 
proof-of-concept system’s strong potential for patient-facing decision making with both accuracy 
and consistency, while maintaining transparency of the LLMs’ decision-making process using an 
explicit, human readable or editable database of medical flowcharts.

Results 
The self-triage system we developed is structured around three key components: a self-triage 
flowchart database, a retrieval module to identify the most relevant flowchart based on patient 
information, and a navigation process to interpret and systematically follow the selected 
flowchart. To assess the system’s performance at scale, which is an essential consideration for 
real-world healthcare assessment, we utilized large synthetic datasets generated by LLMs. In 
particular, given the back-and-forth nature of conversational patient triaging, it would not be 
possible to use a pre-recorded or existing dataset of human conversation to test our AI system. 
We generated synthetic datasets that enable extensive and controlled testing across diverse 
clinical scenarios, offering both scalability and comprehensiveness which is difficult to achieve 
through human-subject studies. To enhance generalizability, we utilize multiple LLM models 
across different companies to create the datasets for system assessment. As an initial 
proof-of-concept system, our evaluation focuses on the ability of LLMs to accurately retrieve and 
execute the proper flowcharts. Specifically, we aim to assess two hypotheses:  
H1) the retrieval module can accurately identify the most appropriate flowchart from the 
database; 
H2) the navigation module can reliably interpret patient responses and correctly follow the 
retrieved flowchart. 
Flowchart Database 
We first created a flowchart database of self-triage flowcharts derived from the American 
Medical Association Family Medical Guide, 4th Edition. It covers a wide range of common 
symptoms and includes flowcharts for particular age or sex groups. We obtained a total of 100 
flowcharts after preprocessing (detailed in the Method section) and illustrated the distribution in 
Figure 2. Notably, the majority of the flowcharts is applicable for all sexes and ages, with 
subsets targeted toward specific populations, such as female or pediatric. Figure 1b shows the 
diverse coverage of the flowchart database across medical specialties. 100 flowcharts were

Retrieving the most appropriate flowchart from the database 
We assess the system performance in selecting the most appropriate flowchart from the 
database based on the patient's demographic information and main concern. This selection is 
achieved by a retrieval agent implemented using retrieval augmented generation (RAG), as 
detailed in the Methods section. Synthetic patient demographics and opening statements, 
defined as the first sentence a patient would use to express their main concern, were generated 
given the flowchart descriptions using four different models: GPT-4o, Claude 3 Haiku, Gemini 
2.0 Flash-Lite, DeepSeek-Chat.  
 
Figure 3 presents the flowchart retrieval result evaluated on the synthetic opening statement 
dataset. Figure 3a shows the structure of the retrieval agent, which consists of a similarity 
search module followed by an LLM (we used OpenAI GPT-4o-mini model for cost-efficiency). 
The patient’s demographic information and opening statement are first processed through a 
cosine similarity search to retrieve the top-ranked flowcharts along according to their similarity 
scores. The retrieved candidates are then passed to the LLM, which selects the most 
appropriate flowchart based on both the flowchart information and the similarity scores. The 
module components correspond to the retrieval performance in Figure 3b based on N=2000 
opening statements comprising 20 per AMA flowchart. When using only the LLM module 
(Yellow), which relies solely on its semantic comprehension, the average accuracy of correctly

identifying the relevant flowchart is 78.40% (± s.d. 6.74%, N=2,000). Similarly, when utilizing 
only the similarity search module (Green), which mathematically compares distances within 
vector space, the accuracy of identifying the correct flowchart as the closest is 82.14% (± s.d. 
7.10%, N=2,000). When the two modules are combined to form the retrieval agent (Grey), the 
average accuracy improves to 84.66% (± s.d. 5.59%, N=2,000). This demonstrates that the 
retrieval agent not only considers the results from the similarity search (with top-n set to 10), but 
also performs semantic analysis of each retrieved candidate to make a final selection. One 
common reason for retrieval confusions across all models is that certain symptoms are related 
and mentioned together in the opening statements (e.g., chest pain and shortness of breath), 
making it difficult for the retrieval agent to select a single, most appropriate flowchart when 
multiple candidates exhibit high semantic similarity. We provide a complete failure analysis on 
the retrieval result in the Supplementary Table 1. 
 
In addition, Figure 3b also presents a comparison of retrieval accuracy across opening 
statements generated by four different models. In the LLM-only setting, performance on GPT4o 
and Claude Haiku generated opening statements exhibits similar accuracy: 84.70% and 83.75% 
respectively, while slightly lower accuracy is observed on Gemini Lite and DeepSeek Chat 
generated statements at 72.40% and 72.75%. For the similarity search-only setting, accuracy is 
86.60% for GPT4o, 89.75% for Claude Haiku, 76.65% for Gemini Lite, and 75.55% for 
DeepSeek Chat. In the full retrieval agent setting, accuracy reaches 90.60% for GPT4o, 88.25%

Figure 3c specifically evaluates the top results returned from the similarity search component. 
Here we vary the n value to assess the accuracy of whether the correct flowchart appears within 
the top-n results, ranked by similarity score. The top-1 result corresponds to the similarity search 
performance shown in Figure 3b. When we consider the top-3 retrieved candidates from 
similarity search, the average accuracy increases to 95.29% (± s.d. 2.88%, N=2,000). Extending 
this to the top-5 retrieved results further slightly improves the average accuracy to 97.71% (± 
s.d. 1.52%, N=2,000). These findings suggest that the similarity search module can effectively 
retrieve and rank the relevant flowcharts. In real-world deployment, we expect an interactive 
system will present both a “most relevant” flowchart recommendation from the retrieval agent 
and the top 3 flowchart results from similarity search as “potentially relevant” flowcharts. This 
retrieval process leverages the AI’s ability to automatically retrieve from a large database while 
maintaining transparency to allow patients to stay in the loop during the triage process.

Navigating through the retrieved flowchart 
To provide proper guidance to patients, it also requires the system to correctly navigate through 
the retrieved flowchart in a step-by-step conversational manner. We further evaluated the 
system’s navigation performance using synthetic patient responses generated by the same four 
language models as aforementioned. Drawing from real-world patient-doctor interaction 
data15–17, we categorized patient responses to doctors’ queries into five conversational patterns 
(See Methods section for detailed description and response examples):


## Page 8


1) brief: responses that are short and conclusive, suggesting a clear yes or no;  
2) descriptive: responses that are descriptive and conclusive, providing additional details; 
3) weak: responses that are vague or ambiguous, but slightly leaning towards yes or no;  
4) uncertain: responses that are too inconclusive to be interpreted as a yes or no;  
5) off-topic: responses that are completely irrelevant to the question.  
 
These patterns represent diverse patient response styles, while preserving conversational 
realism. We created a large-scale response dataset that includes all five patterns, resulting in a 
total of 37,200 synthetic patient responses across 100 flowcharts (see Methods section for 
generation details). To assess the navigation performance, the patient response and flowchart 
node pairs are fed into the decision agent. The decision agent is designed to classify patient 
responses 
along 
four 
axes: 
On-topic/Off-topic, 
Answered/Unanswered, 
Yes/No, 
Certain/Uncertain. These axes collectively determine whether the system can confidently 
advance to the next node or needs to seek clarification, thus enabling a transparent and 
informative navigation process. 
 
Figure 4 presents the navigation result based on the decision agent’s output (the decision agent 
was implemented with OpenAI GPT-4o-mini model as the backbone). Overall, the system 
demonstrates high navigation accuracy across patient responses generated by all four models, 
achieving an average of 99.10% (± s.d. 0.54%, N=37,200), as shown in Figure 4a. Figure 4b-4f

Incorrect (red). The system only proceeds when it determines the response is certain and holds

the only unacceptable case, as the system misinterprets the patient response and proceeds 
with a potential of providing false guidance to patients, whereas category a, b, and c are all 
considered acceptable.

Discussion 
We introduce a multi-agent self-triage system to demonstrate how clinically validated flowcharts 
can effectively guide LLM outputs in healthcare applications. Self-triage is a complex procedure 
that involves information gathering, decision making, and conversation comprehension, while 
adhering to the high fidelity required in healthcare settings. Leveraging self-triage flowcharts 
developed by AMA, we designed three LLM-powered agents to procedurally perform the triage 
task through natural conversations: retrieval agent, decision agent, and chat agent. To evaluate 
system performance, we conducted two key assessments: flowchart retrieval and flowchart 
navigation. These evaluations measure the system’s ability to accurately select the appropriate 
flowchart based on patient input and correctly navigate through the flowchart to provide 
guidance. The results show that the system successfully performs the end-to-end triage 
workflow with both high accuracy and clinical alignment. 
 
Our results have multiple implications to real-world deployment. First, one of the key 
contributions of our system is its ability to perform triage strictly according to clearly defined, 
auditable medical protocols. By leveraging flowcharts as the shared representation, clinicians 
can review the exact decision path taken during each conversation session, providing a layer of 
transparency and accountability that is often missing in black-box AI systems18–20. Moreover, this 
design can empower clinical systems/teams to customize the triage workflow to fit their practice 
by editing or modifying the flowcharts. In the future, human-AI collaboration could be employed 
to expand the flowchart database to cover a wide range of clinical tasks: LLMs can propose new 
flowcharts based on real-world data, while humans serve as expert reviewers to validate and 
refine them prior to deployment. 
 
Second, our flowchart retrieval results suggest that in real-world deployment, both the top 3 
flowchart results and the final flowchart recommendation from the retrieval agent should be 
presented to patients (Figure 3). While the retrieval agent achieved the highest accuracy 
(84.66%) in Figure 3b, it could be complemented by the top 3 retrieved results from similarity 
search (95.29%) as shown in Figure 3c. Presenting both options allows flexibility in patient 
interaction: the system can offer a clear recommendation for those seeking guidance, while also 
enabling patients who prefer to be more actively involved to compare alternatives. This 
approach supports both system accuracy and greater transparency in the triage process.


## Page 11


Third, our flowchart navigation results highlight that a key feature for patient-facing 
conversational systems is the ability to handle a wide range of patient response styles, 
particularly those involving uncertainty. In clinical practice, communication skills are a 
fundamental part of clinician training21,22. During patient interactions, clinicians need to extract 
essential information from varied patient responses, make diagnostic and treatment decisions, 
and communicate those decisions clearly. Among the five patient response patterns we defined, 
brief responses appear more frequently when physicians pose yes-no questions, while weak or 
uncertain responses are also common in patient-doctor interactions and present greater 
challenges for interpretation23,24. This is reflected in Figure 4d-4e, where a large proportion of 
responses fall into the uncertain categories. For real-world deployment, the system should be 
designed to proceed only when a confirmed answer is received, thereby reducing the risk of 
providing inappropriate action suggestions. Future improvements can also be made on 
flowchart design. For example, assigning weights to each question in the flowcharts could 
enable the system to prioritize high-value nodes and adjust its navigation dynamically. 
Additionally, conditional paths for uncertain responses could be added to flowcharts to address 
uncertainty more explicitly. Each yes-no question node could have an additional path to specify 
the desired action when the response is uncertain, such as merging with one of the existing 
paths, triggering an alternative question, or referring the patient to a clinician.  
 
Fourth, our results also show minor variation in performance across synthetic data generated by 
different language models. In Figure 3b, we observed that compared to OpenAI GPT4o and 
Claude Haiku, the retrieval accuracy on opening statements generated by Gemini Lite and 
DeepSeek Chat was lower across all settings (LLM-only, Similarity Search-only, and the full 
retrieval agent) in Figure 3b. One possible explanation is that given the same prompt for 
generation, the synthetic opening statements from Gemini Lite and Deepseek Chat tend to be 
more generic, leading to a broader and less targeted list of candidate flowcharts during the 
similarity search stage. For navigation results, Gemini Lite generated responses exhibited 
slightly higher error rate on pattern 1 (Figure 4b), while Claude Haiku generated responses 
presented a relatively higher error rate on pattern 5 (Figure 4f). Manual inspection revealed that 
Gemini Lite often produces less direct affirmative responses when prompted for brief and 
affirmative responses. When instructed to generate off-topic responses, Claude Haiku tends to 
first provide an on-topic answer and then introduce unrelated content, a characteristic linked to 
its design as a helpful AI assistant. 
 
Despite promising initial results, our system has certain limitations. First, the current set of 
flowcharts is relatively generic and restricted to polar (yes/no) questions, which limits the 
system’s expressiveness and clinical breadth. In the future, more customized and detailed 
flowcharts could be developed for specific specialities, institutions, or patient populations. 
Second, our evaluation was conducted using synthetic datasets for extensive assessment. 
Real-world evaluation with patients is necessary to validate effectiveness and safety of the 
system. Third, the current system does not yet include mechanisms to detect or recover from 
errors in retrieval or navigation. Future work could focus on developing error-handling 
strategies, such as confidence estimation or clinician-in-the-loop interventions. Fourth, privacy 
also remains important, as users may share sensitive demographic information and medical


## Page 12


history during conversations. Addressing these risks through secure data management and 
transparent consent mechanisms would be critical. Finally, we only developed and tested the 
system in English with text input; further improvement could expand to multilingual or even 
multimodal input. For example, patients can upload images to help describe their symptoms and 
a visual-language model could be developed to perform the triage task. 
 
In conclusion, we propose medically validated flowcharts as a shared presentation between 
healthcare practitioners and LLMs, and present a conversational self-triage system with AMA 
flowcharts. Our findings suggest that LLMs have great potential to serve as the front-line triage 
tools which would offer great benefits to both patients and healthcare providers. While further 
validation on real-world data is required, the framework lays the groundwork for safe, auditable, 
and accessible decision support tools that are applicable for a wide range of clinical workflows. 
By prioritizing transparency, adaptability and efficacy, this work shines a light on a new direction 
for leveraging structured clinical knowledge to implement explainable and trustworthy AI-driven 
health tools.   
Methods 
Self-triage Flowcharts and Preprocess 
We analyzed and preprocessed all flowcharts from the American Medical Association Family 
Medical Guide, 4th Edition, published in 2011 by the American Medical Association (AMA). This 
guide offers general information on common medical conditions, self-diagnosis flowcharts, and 
actionable health recommendations, aiming to equip individuals and families with essential 
medical 
knowledge 
about 
their 
well-being. 
The 
self-diagnosis flowcharts follow a 
question-answering format, focusing on providing triage actions while suggesting possible 
diagnoses.  
 
To construct our flowchart database, we implemented a series of preprocessing steps. First, we 
divided the original Infertility flowchart into two separate flowcharts: "Infertility in Men" and 
"Infertility in Women", since the original flowchart contains two entry points for these two 
population groups. Second, we modified the flowchart content to fit the goal of self-triage. While 
self-diagnosis attempts to identify specific medical conditions, self-triage emphasizes providing 
appropriate actions based on the urgency of symptoms. Our modifications focus on ensuring 
that each flowchart endpoint delivers a clear, actionable recommendation. This included

Flowchart Representation 
Given the decision-tree structure of the AMA flowcharts and prior research indicating that LLMs 
exhibit better comprehension of code-based formats25–27, we converted the flowcharts into 
directional graphs using Python. This graph-based representation allows LLMs to more 
effectively parse the flow of decision-making, enabling accurate step-by-step reasoning and 
execution. In our graph representation, each step in the flowchart is modeled as a node, and 
transitions between steps are directed edges. We defined four node types to capture the 
functional semantics of the flowcharts: 1) N node, which poses yes-or-no questions to the user 
and is the most common node type; 2) F node, which denotes nodes that redirect users to a 
linked flowchart; 3) A node, which represents the endpoints of the flowchart, typically an action 
recommendation (e.g., “See your doctor”) or an educational message (e.g., “Headaches often 
occur with fevers.”); and 4) I node, which provides brief explanations or reassurance preceding 
an F node. Notably, nodes are connected by directional and conditional edges, where the 
direction points to the next step, and the condition is either Yes or No as the answer to the N 
node. An example of the graph representation is provided in the Supplementary Figure 1. 
Multi-agent Self-triage System 
Here we introduce the multi-agent system, which consists of three LLM-based agents working 
sequentially to perform self-triage: the retrieval agent, the decision agent, and the chat agent. 
The system first collects patient information, including sex, age, and main concern. This 
information is utilized to identify the most relevant flowchart from the database by the retrieval 
agent. After finalizing on a flowchart, the decision agent and the chat agent collaborate to 
execute the triage protocol. Specifically, the decision agent interprets and categorizes the 
patient’s answer to the flowchart question, while the chat agent generates empathetic and 
concise responses based on the flowchart content. This modular architecture is designed to 
reflect clinical decision-making workflows, with each agent playing a specialized role that mirrors 
how healthcare professionals gather information, interpret responses, and communicate care 
recommendations. 
Patient Information Gathering 
The triage process begins by collecting the basic demographic information of patients, including 
sex (female/male) and age, with age units specified in months or years, given the database 
contains pediatric flowcharts for infants under one year old. Following this, the conversation is 
initiated by TriageMD prompting the patient to state their main concern (also referred to as chief 
complaint in clinical settings). For example, a patient might begin with “I’ve been having a 
stomachache for a few hours now”, as shown in Figure 1. The demographic details and the 
patient’s opening statement are then combined and provided to the retrieval agent, which 
requires this input to identify the most appropriate flowchart from the database. 
Retrieval agent - find the most appropriate flowchart via similarity search


## Page 14


We leverage the retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework to effectively match patient 
concerns with the most appropriate self-triage flowcharts. To build the retrieval component, we 
first curated the flowchart descriptions from the source book and converted them into 
embeddings using Facebook AI Similarity Search (FAISS). Upon receiving a patient’s 
demographic details and main concern, this information is also vectorized into an embedded 
query. We then compare it against the embedded flowchart descriptions to identify the top-n 
most relevant candidates using cosine similarity. These candidate flowcharts, along with the 
patient’s query, are then provided to a LLM, which selects the final, most appropriate flowchart. 
If no suitable flowchart matches the patient’s case, “no flowchart available” would be returned, 
and the system would prompt the patient to seek medical attention directly as it is not authorized 
to provide guidance for conditions not covered by the flowchart database. The detailed prompt 
for creating the retrieval agent is included in the Supplementary Table 7. 
Decision agent - interpret patient’s responses  
After retrieving the appropriate flowchart, the system begins following it. This involves 
interpreting patient responses, determining whether patients have answered the flowchart 
question, and navigating through the flowchart to identify the next step. Specifically, at each 
question node, the decision agent takes in both the node content and the patient’s responses as 
input and outputs answers to four different axes detailed in Table 1. These axes and their 
descriptions are included in the agent prompt using a structured output parser, which ensures 
that the output of the decision agent follows a well-defined, structured format (JSON). For 
example, in the abdominal pain flowchart, the first node asks “Have you had similar episodes of 
pain that come and go?” If the patient responds, “No, this is the first time”, the decision agent 
generates structured output as follows:  isOnTopic=Yes, isAnswered=Yes, actualAnswer=No, 
and isUncertaiN=No. The detailed prompt is included in the Supplementary Table 7. Each axis is 
essential for determining the next step: the system proceeds to the subsequent node only when 
a certain (isUncertaiN=No) yes or no answer (actualAnswer=Yes or actualAnswer=No) is 
obtained. Otherwise, it remains at the current node and attempts to clarify (isOnTopic=No) or 
confirm (isUncertaiN=Yes) the patient’s response. Decision agent plays a critical role in the 
system for navigating through flowchart nodes and sending the correct content to the next agent 
- chat agent.

Chat Agent - provide concise and empathetic responses to patients 
The chat agent serves as the final module in the system, responsible for delivering concise and 
empathetic responses to the patients. It receives the proper node content from the decision 
agent and generates a reply tailored to the context of the conversation. The chat agent’s prompt 
is dynamically constructed based on the output of the decision agent, reflecting the patient's 
previous response. When the patient clearly answers the question, the chat agent simply 
conveys the flowchart node content in a patient-friendly manner. If the response is off-topic, the 
chat agent gently acknowledges the patient's input and redirects the conversation by restating 
the original question. In cases where the response indicates uncertainty, the chat agent 
attempts to confirm the answer with the patient. This design allows the agent to maintain 
coherence over multiple dialogue turns while adapting its tone and messaging to the patient’s 
needs. 
Evaluation Methods 
Flowchart Retrieval 
In order to evaluate the retrieval accuracy of the flowchart retrieval module, we first created a 
synthetic patient opening statement dataset using multiple language models. An opening 
statement is defined as the first sentence a patient would use to express their main concern to a 
doctor. This statement, along with demographic information, serves as the input for identifying 
the most appropriate flowchart. For each flowchart, we generated opening statements by 
providing its flowchart name, description, applicable age range, and targeted sex group to the 
models. The flowchart name was later used as the ground truth label for assessing retrieval 
accuracy.  
 
We selected four language models from different providers for a comprehensive analysis: 
GPT-4o from OpenAI, Claude 3 Haiku from Anthropic, Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite from Google, and 
DeepSeek-Chat from DeepSeek, mainly considering the cost efficiency. Each model generated 
20 opening statements per flowchart: 10 brief statements (≤25 words), 10 descriptive 
statements (≥50 words) to reflect a wide range of real-world patient communication styles. In 
total, 8,000 opening statements were generated for use in the evaluation. We include the 
prompt for generating patient demographics and opening statements in Supplementary Table 7, 
along with generated samples from each model in the Supplementary Table 2.  
 
Post-generation processing was applied to standardize the demographic information and 
opening statements, ensuring they were formatted correctly and free of extraneous content. 
Notably, for flowcharts targeting pediatric populations (under 12 years old), the statements were 
phrased from a caregiver’s perspective (e.g., “My daughter keeps coughing at night. What 
should I do?”). Each opening statement, along with its associated demographic data, was input 
into the retrieval agent, which returned the most appropriate flowchart. The retrieved result was 
then compared to the ground truth to compute retrieval accuracy. We evaluated the

sub-component of the retrieval agent both individually and combined, which demonstrates that 
the complete RAG framework improves flowchart retrieval accuracy, offering greater control and 
transparency in the triage process.
