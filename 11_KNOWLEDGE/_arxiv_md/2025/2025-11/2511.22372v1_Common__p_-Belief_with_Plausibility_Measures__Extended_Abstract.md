Introduction
In his seminal 1976 paper, Robert Aumann proved a fascinating result, later called the “Agreeing to Dis-
agree” theorem [2]. Suppose that two agents share the same prior probability distribution and update
this probability by conditioning on different private information. Aumann showed that if their posterior
probabilities of an event E become common knowledge—meaning each agent knows the other’s poste-
rior, knows that the other knows it, and so on—then they must assign identical posterior probabilities to
E. In other words, two Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot agree (in the sense of having
common knowledge) to disagree (in the sense of holding different posterior probabilities). This result
has important implications across multiple disciplines: it underlies “no-trade" theorems in economics
[21, 28], helps to provide an epistemic characterization of Nash equilibria in games with more than two
players [1], and reveals subtle issues that arise when rational agents exchange information [12, 24, 20].
The literature on Aumann’s theorem has focused on identifying which assumptions are necessary to
prove the theorem [25, 11, 19]. Various generalizations have emerged, exploring scenarios where, for
instance, agents have ambiguous beliefs [16, 17, 30] or use learning rules other than classical Bayesian
conditioning [13, 18]. This paper is inspired by three important generalizations of Aumann’s theorem:
1. There are several qualitative versions of Aumann’s theorem. One approach focuses on reformu-
lating Aumann’s insights in multi-agent logics of belief [8, 9]. A second approach, developed by
Cave [7], Bacharach [5], and Samet [26], reconceptualizes the core problem by shifting focus from
probabilities to abstract decisions. In Aumann’s original theorem, the posterior probability of an
agent for a fixed event can be viewed as a random variable mapping states to real numbers in the
[0,1] interval. The core idea is to replace these random variables with decision functions mapping
worlds to elements of a set D of “decisions”. 1
2. A second direction of research extends Aumann’s theorem to generalizations of classical Bayesian
models, including conditional probability structures [29], lexicographic probability systems [4,
3], and imprecise probabilities [31]. By reformulating the agreement theorem within these more
general models of beliefs, researchers have shown that Aumann’s core insight—that rational agents
cannot agree to disagree—remains robust even when relaxing classical probability assumptions.
1The resulting qualitative agreement theorem establishes that “like-minded” agents cannot have common knowledge of
different decisions, with the concept of being “like-minded” formalized using a variant of Savage’s sure-thing principle: For
any pair of agents i and j, if i knows that j is at least as knowledgeable as she is, and also knows that j’s decision is d, then her
decision is also d [27, p. 171].

3. The third line of research generalizes Aumann’s theorem by replacing knowledge with the weaker
notion of p-belief. An agent p-believes an event E when assigning a probability of at least p to
E. In this setting, we can “approximate" common knowledge with common p-belief. Monderer
and Samet [22], later refined by Neeman [23], proved that if agents sharing a common prior have
common p-belief of their posteriors of some event E, then these posteriors must be “close”, in the
sense that they cannot differ by more than 1 −p. This elegant result shows that if the agents are
close to having common knowledge of their posteriors of an event E, then these posteriors must
also be close. Then, Aumann’s agreement theorem can be viewed as the special case when p = 1.
This paper lies in the intersection of these three approaches, aiming to identify the minimal assump-
tions that any belief model must satisfy to prove a version of the Monderer-Samet-Neeman agreement
theorem. To accomplish this, we use Halpern’s [10, 14] (conditional) plausibility measures to represent
the agents’ beliefs. These measures provide a very general framework that encompasses many existing
formal models of beliefs, including probability measures, possibility measures, ranking functions, and
Dempster-Shafer belief functions (see [15] for a discussion).
Our generalized Monderer-Samet-Neeman theorem using conditional plausibility measures provides
a unifying perspective that clarifies which properties of belief are essential for demonstrating that com-
mon belief of the posteriors of some event implies these posteriors must be close. This deepens our
theoretical understanding of agreement theorems while extending the applicability of the Monderer-
Samet-Neeman Theorem to settings where classical probabilistic assumptions may not hold.
This extended abstract is organized as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of Aumann’s clas-
sic agreeing to disagree theorem. We introduce (common) p-belief and discuss the Monderer-Samet-
Neeman generalization of Aumann’s theorem in Section 3. Section 4 introduces conditional plausibility
measures [10, 14] and states our generalizations of the Monderer-Samet-Neeman agreement theorem.
The proofs of the results are available in the full version of the paper. We conclude in Section 6 with a
brief discussion of some applications of our results.

Epistemic Plausibility Models
In this section, we introduce epistemic plausibility models. These models generalize epistemic prob-
ability models (Definition 2.1) by replacing probabilities with the more general notion of a conditional
plausibility measure.
To define a conditional plausibility measure, we first fix a non-empty set D of plausibility values
satisfying the following two conditions. First, it is partially ordered by a relation ≤D⊆D × D. That is,
≤D is reflexive (for all d ∈D, d ≤D d), transitive (for all d,d′,d′′ ∈D, if d ≤D d′ and d′ ≤D d′′, then
d ≤D d′′), and antisymmetric (for all d,d′ ∈D, if d ≤D d′ and d′ ≤D d, then d = d′). Second, D contains
minimal and maximal elements ⊥D and ⊤D respectively, such that ⊥D ≤D d ≤D ⊤D for all d ∈D. As
usual, we write d <D d′ when d ≤D d′ and d ̸= d′. When D is clear from context, we omit subscripts and
write ≤, <, ⊤, and ⊥.
A conditional plausibility measure for an agent i assigns an element from d ∈D to a pair of events X
and Y, denoted Pli(X |Y) = d meaning that according to i, the plausibility of X conditional on Y is d. The
domain of a conditional plausibility measure is a Popper algebra. Suppose that W is a nonempty set. A
collection of subsets F ⊆℘(W) (where℘(W) is the powerset of W) is called an algebra when W ∈F,
F is closed under union (if X,Y ∈F, then X ∪Y ∈F), and closed under complement (if X ∈F then
X ∈F, where X denotes the complement of X in W). An algebra F is called a σ-algebra when it is
closed under countable unions. Then, a set F ×F ′ is a Popper algebra if:
1. F is an σ-algebra;
2. F ′ ⊆F; and
3. for X ∈F ′, if X ⊆Y and Y ∈F, then Y ∈F ′.
The intended interpretation is that F is the set of events that are assigned plausibility values, and F ′ is
the set of events that can be learned. A conditional plausibility measure satisfies very minimal properties:
Definition 4.1 (Conditional Plausibility Structure). Suppose that D is a partially ordered set of plausibil-
ity values (with ordering ≤D). A conditional plausibility structure on D is a tuple ⟨W,F,F ′,(Pli)i∈A ⟩,
where W ̸= /0, F ×F ′ is a Popper algebra on W, and for each i ∈A , Pli : F ×F ′ →D satisfies: For
all X,Y ∈F and E ∈F ′:
(CP1) Pli(E |E) = ⊤,
(CP2) Pli(/0 | E) = ⊥,
(CP3) If X ⊆Y, then Pli(X | E) ≤Pli(Y | E), and

MSN-Theorems in Epistemic Plausibility Structures
In this section, we present two distinct generalizations of the MSN-Theorem (Theorem 3.6) using epis-

these generalized versions of the MSN-Theorem.

6
Conclusion and Applications
This extended abstract presents two generalizations of the Monderer–Samet–Neeman (MSN) agreement
theorem, using additive conditional plausibility measures. These measures provide a unified framework
that incorporates many existing formal models of belief. The first generalization closely follows Mon-
derer, Samet, and Neeman’s original proof, relying on a multiplication operation, while the second gen-
eralization avoids this requirement. Together, these two results clarify the minimal assumptions required
to establish MSN-type agreement theorems in various formal belief models.
To illustrate the significance of these assumptions, we briefly examine several existing models of
belief (a complete discussion is left to the full version of the paper). Our generalized MSN-theorems
apply, in particular, to conditional probability structures [29], lexicographic probability structures [4, 3],
and order models of belief [8]. When these conditions are not satisfied, the corresponding MSN-theorem
typically fails or becomes trivial for the given belief model.
