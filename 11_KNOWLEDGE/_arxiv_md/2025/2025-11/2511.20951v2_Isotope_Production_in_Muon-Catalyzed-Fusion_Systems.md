Isotope Production in Muon-Catalyzed-Fusion Systems
J. F. Parisi1∗and A. Rutkowski1
1Marathon Fusion, 150 Mississippi, San Francisco, 94107, CA, USA
Producing valuable isotopes with high-flux high-energy neutrons generated by muon-catalyzed
fusion (µCF) reactions could substantially improve the economic prospects for muon-catalyzed fu-
sion. Because no external heating is required for µCF, heat flux constraints are significantly relaxed
compared with fusion systems requiring external heating. This could allow µCF to attain much
higher neutron flux without breaching material heat flux limits. If muon production rates can be
increased, µCF systems employing transmutation could be viable well before energy breakeven is
possible. For µCF systems transmuting valuable isotopes, the required number of catalyzed fusion
events per muon and muon energy generation cost can be relaxed by several orders of magnitude
relative to electricity-generating systems, making µCF an attractive high-flux neutron source. We
show an example µCF system with a 10 gram 226Ra feedstock and a steady-state muon rate of 1012
muons / second - roughly half a kilowatt of fusion power - could produce 20 mg of 225Ac per year -
comparable to 400 times global supply in 2024. As higher muon rate sources become available, many
other radioisotope transmutation pathways become viable. These findings motivate the accelerated
development of µCF systems for neutron-driven isotope production far before net energy generation
is possible.
I.
INTRODUCTION
In this work, we show how muon-catalyzed fusion (µCF)
can serve as an intense neutron source for making valu-
able radioisotopes.
With isotope production, the require-
ments are significantly reduced for the production energy and
number of catalyzed fusion reactions per muon compared to
power generating µCF systems.
In a D-T mixture, formation of the muonic (dµt)+ molec-
ular ion enables rapid fusion to a 3.5 MeV α and a 14.1 MeV
neutron [1–5] with a total cycle time tc. The muon is usually
released to catalyze additional fusions, but two mechanisms
limit repeated cycling: the τ = 2.2 µs muon lifetime and
the p ∼0.5% α-sticking probability that binds the muon to
the product nucleus [6, 7].
Experiments have achieved tc
values corresponding to Nfus,µ = 1/(tc/τ + p) ≃150 cat-
alyzed reactions per muon [3] corresponding to tc ∼4 ns.
Each D-T cycle combined with neutron multiplication and
6Li(n,α)t (+4.8 MeV) releases at most Ecomb ≈20.4 MeV
[8] per cycle. Therefore, each muon can generate at most
Efus,µ = Nfus,µEcomb ≈3.1 GeV of energy.
The 3.1 GeV energy yield per muon is close to, but still be-
low, the level required for energy breakeven. Current muon
sources can produce a single muon via pion decay with an
effective cost of Eµ ∼5 GeV per muon [9], although there
are proposals for reducing Eµ [10, 11] and increasing Nfus,u
[10, 12, 13]. Recent modeling [14] suggests that active-target
muon sources could achieve substantially lower energy cost,
simulating 3.0 GeV per negative muon, but further tech-
nology development is required to demonstrate this bene-
fit experimentally. Thus, in present systems the fusion en-
ergy released per muon is at best comparable to, and typi-
cally smaller than, the accelerator energy invested per muon.
Once realistic wall-plug efficiencies and auxiliary loads are
included, µCF experiments remain below energy breakeven
even under favorable conditions [6, 11].

Although µCF does not yet support net-energy produc-
tion, its neutron output is already nearly high enough to
be useful for other applications such as isotope production.
Each catalyzed D–T reaction yields a 14.1 MeV neutron
without external plasma heating (and corresponding high
wall heat flux loads). Recent work proposed using a µCF to
transmute long-lived fission products [10, 15]. In this work,
we propose making valuable isotopes via fast neutron-driven
transmutation [16–21]. Conventional neutron sources such
as fission reactors and spallation systems face constraints in
achievable neutron flux and fluence, thermal loading, waste
generation, and proliferation risks. D–T µCF offers an al-
ternative accelerator-driven pathway to produce fusion neu-
trons at low temperature and at relatively favorable neutron
to heat flux ratio.
Producing high rates of negatively charged muons is chal-
lenging, with state-of-the-art facilities producing several ∼
107 µ−/s: J-PARC MUSE produces ∼2 −4 · 107 µ−/s in a
25 Hz pulsed beam [22], PSI achieves ∼2·107 µ−/s continu-
ous wave [23], and MuSIC reported ∼4·107 µ−/s in a direct
current beamline [24]. Upgrades at PSI [25–27] and J-PARC
[28] are likely to push negative muon production rates above
108 µ−/s. Nonetheless, despite these advances, higher muon
beam rates are needed even for valuable isotope production.
This work is structured as follows. In Section II we calcu-
late the heat and neutron fluxes µCF systems. The trans-
mutation breakeven condition is discussed in Section III. We
describe a small system for 225Ac production in Section IV.
We conclude in Section V.
