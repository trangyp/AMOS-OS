aSchool of Engineering, Brown University

Abstract
The engineering design process often requires expertise from multiple domains, leading to
complex collaborations and iterative refinements. Traditional approaches can be resource-
intensive and prone to inefficiencies. To address this, we present a multi-agent reasoning
framework that formalizes the engineering design process through structured, iterative de-
sign and review loops. The framework introduces specialized, knowledge-based AI agents
that collaborate to generate, evaluate, and refine design candidates efficiently. As a demon-
stration, we apply this framework to the design of four-digit NACA airfoils for maximizing
lift-to-drag ratio. The framework consists of three key agents: a Graph Ontologist, a Design
Engineer, and a Systems Engineer. The Graph Ontologist, powered by a Large Language
Model constructs two domain-specific knowledge graphs using airfoil development literature.
The Systems Engineer, guided by the Manager’s (human) input, defines technical require-
ments that inform the design process. The Design Engineer utilizes these requirements,
alongside the design knowledge graph and design-specific tools, to produce candidate airfoil
designs. The Systems Engineer then reviews these designs using its own knowledge graph,
providing both qualitative and quantitative feedback for iterative design improvements. A
continuous feedback loop exists between the Design Engineer and Systems Engineer until the
Manager validates a design, at which point the iterative phase ends. The valid design is sub-
sequently optimized to achieve performance objectives. In summary, this framework demon-
strates a path toward improving efficiency and quality in the engineering design process by
leveraging collaborative AI agents equipped with domain-specific knowledge representation.
Keywords:
Agentic Design, Knowledge-Graphs, Autonomous Design, Airfoil Design
1. Introduction
The engineering design process serves as the cornerstone of innovation in diverse disci-
plines. At its core lies an iterative cycle - a structured approach to problem-solving that
emphasizes refinement and improvement through repeated cycles of design space exploration,
analysis, evaluation, review, and selection. This iterative nature arises due to the complex-
ity of real-world engineering challenges, where final solutions often emerge after multiple

therefore, is not a linear sequence of events but rather an intertwined set of activities, which
are often revisited and reordered as the problem understanding deepens. Traditionally, the
design process is segmented into three key phrases: conceptual design, embodiment design,
and detailed design [1]. The conceptual design phase involves initial exploration of poten-
tial solutions, focusing on defining the problem scope, requirement elicitation, generation
of several design concepts and their evaluation against requirements. Often, this phase is
characterized by a high degree of uncertainty and repetitions, since the goal is to identify

In addition to its iterative nature, the design process is also a collaborative endeavor, par-
ticularly in the context of complex product development. Interdisciplinary teams, comprising
of experts and personnel from diverse specialties are essential for bringing a holistic perspec-
tive in this process. Each discipline contributes their unique expertise and knowledge-base,
leading to more robust and innovative solutions. This collaborative environment necessi-
tates frequent communication and coordination, often facilitated by specialized design tools
and processes. Decision making is an essential step in design and is typically performed by
domain experts whose expertise is built with years of practice. This domain knowledge is
often tacit, and is difficult to articulate or codify [2]. Such knowledge encompasses not only
technical skills but also an understanding of the best practices, potential pitfalls, and subtle
nuances of design trade-offs. This accumulated expertise is immensely valuable for ensuring
process efficiency, quality, and risk management. However, this reliance on individual exper-
tise presents a significant challenge: the potential loss of critical knowledge during personnel
transitions [3]. The departure of experienced engineers can create a void in institutional
memory, hindering future design efforts and potentially decline in product quality [4].
The emergence of large language models (LLMs) marks a transformative shift in the tra-
ditional engineering design landscape. These models, trained on large collections of text and
code datasets, have shown an unprecedented ability to understand, generate, and perform
complex tasks when provided with well-designed instructions [5]. The proliferation of LLMs
in engineering practice has the potential to reshape the entire design process. LLMs can
offer compelling advantages as assistant tools in guiding users through complex, iterative
processes that characterize engineering design. In future, one can visualize LLM frameworks
capable of generating design concepts, explaining the rationale behind design choices, iden-
tifying potential risks and remedies. Such interactive guidance can be particularly valuable
for novice engineers, accelerating their learning curve and fostering a deeper understanding
of design principles and engineering tools.
Despite their remarkable capabilities, the application of LLMs for engineering tasks is
not without limitations. A particularly significant challenge lies in achieving domain spe-
cific knowledge and guidance. Although LLMs excel at general language understanding,
they often struggle to provide the deep and specialized knowledge required for complex
engineering tasks that frequently require intricate trade-offs and nuanced considerations
[6].
Furthermore, the backbone of engineering practice relies on specialized engineering
tools—computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solvers, finite element analysis (FEA) software,
CAD modeling packages—that have been rigorously developed and validated over many
years. Engineers depend on these tools to perform critical calculations, simulations, and
analyses. LLMs typically lack explicit knowledge of these tools, making it difficult to ac-

intervention [7, 8]. The seamless integration of LLMs with existing engineering workflows

This work addresses the challenges and opportunities presented by the confluence of iter-
ative design processes and the rise of LLMs by introducing a novel multi-agent framework
for use in iterative design and review processes, particularly within the conceptual design
phase. We focus on the pedagogical problem of designing 4-digit NACA airfoils for maximiz-
ing performance—a common and accessible exemplar problem in aerospace engineering—to
demonstrate the capabilities of our multi-agent framework. The framework comprises three
specialized AI agents, each assigned a distinct purpose and contributing to the overall design
process: a Graph Ontologist, a Design Engineer, and a Systems Engineer. The Graph On-
tologist acts as a knowledge curator, leveraging LLMs to extract and structure information
from existing literature pertaining to airfoil design and development, helping to create two
distinct knowledge graphs, specific for each engineering agent. These two graphs serve as
source of background information, accessible to the designated agents within the framework.
The Design Engineer agent utilizes the designer’s knowledge graph and design-specific tools
to generate and refine airfoil designs, guided by the knowledge contained within the graph.
The Systems Engineer agent is tasked with translating the human manager’s request into
formal technical requirements, and also reviews the designs generated by the Design Engineer
agent, providing feedback for improvements.
The agents accomplish the design process through an iterative feedback loop. The Design
Engineer agent proposes a design, the Systems Engineer agent evaluates it against the defined
requirements and provides feedback, and the Design Engineer agent iterates on the design
based on that feedback. This cycle repeats until the Manager (human) deems a design as
acceptable in conjunction with the Systems Engineer qualifying the design as ‘valid’ against
the requirement set. This approach aims to combine the knowledge-curation capabilities
of LLMs with the rigor of established engineering practices, creating a powerful tool for
iterative design and review. The complete workflow for this framework is shown in Figure 1.
2. Background and Related Works
Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) have long been proposed to support engineering design
through distributed decision-making, concurrent workflows, and interdisciplinary coordina-
tion. Early work in the 1990s emphasized knowledge-based and protocol-driven agents for
concurrent engineering. Foundational systems such as PACT demonstrated ontology-based
coordination among agents for cooperative design refinement [10]. Similar MAS approaches
were applied to cable harness design and distributed design environments, highlighting bene-
fits of problem decomposition and message-based coordination but also exposing limitations
due to hand-crafted rules and communication overhead [11–13]. Subsequent research ex-
plored alternative coordination mechanisms, including market-based agent marketplaces for
concurrent, set-based design [14]. In conceptual design, agent-based configuration synthe-
sis systems such as A-Design employed specialized agents to generate, evaluate, and refine
electro-mechanical concepts using Pareto-based selection [15, 16]. However, these approaches
assumed well-defined objectives, which are often unavailable in early design stages.

With the maturation of CAD/CAE and PLM infrastructures, MAS research shifted toward
distributed collaboration and interoperability. Agent frameworks supported manufactura-
bility, cost, and lifecycle analysis by coordinating heterogeneous tools through facilitator
agents and shared ontologies [17–20]. Domain-specific systems addressed structural, pro-
cess, and assembly design, as well as knowledge management and process coordination, but
incurred high knowledge-engineering costs [21–25]. More recent work integrates MAS into
CAD-adjacent pipelines, enabling multi-level 3D collaboration, sustainability-aware design,
and automated analysis–optimization loops [26–28].
Knowledge-based and Belief-Desire-
Intention (BDI)-style agents have further enabled explainable design evaluation and digital
design reviews, reinforcing MAS potential while highlighting ongoing challenges in scalable
knowledge maintenance and geometric reasoning [29–31].
More recently, the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has created a
new research direction - development and use of LLM-powered MAS within engineering
contexts. Before the rise of MAS frameworks, several studies have demonstrated the value
of individual LLM assistants in engineering workflows [32–36]. Recent research ideology,
however, prescribes MAS frameworks as being more suited for handling end-to-end design
workflows, since different agent skill sets is desired at different stages of design.

designer. The mechanical designer uses a set of code-based tools for ideation while the elec-

electronic components. At a higher level, [54] articulates a paradigm shift toward adopting
agentic AI framework for automation of engineering processes.
Based on a review of existing literature, we observe that integrating MAS into engineering
design workflows presents substantial challenges due to the inherently collaborative, iter-
ative, and domain-knowledge intensive nature of engineering practice. Traditional design
relies on coordinated expertise from multiple specialists—such as structural, materials, and
manufacturing engineers—whose reasoning must either be encoded in large knowledge bases
or learned from extensive datasets, both of which are resource-intensive [55, 56]. Effective

MAS deployment further requires deep domain knowledge, including design factors, manu-

suitable for effective autonomous agent reasoning. Additionally, an ideal MAS system should
be able to automatically adjust design parameters, rerun analysis, and flag potential design
issues based on a specific set of knowledge. However, current systems often lack the so-

work includes the following:
• Utilize LLM-agents equipped with existing domain-specific knowledge in an ‘Generator-
Critic’ setup for fulfilling specific engineering roles.
• Integrate MAS into an iterative design framework where outcomes from one LLM agent
assist another agent with its decision making and task completion.
• Associate dedicated engineering tools with agents to enable successful task completion.
• Allow human-in-loop interaction to ensure necessary check and balances in the process.
3. Problem Setting
The design of NACA airfoil profiles has historically played a crucial role in the advance-
ment of aerodynamics in aviation [58]. The National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics
(NACA) developed families of airfoils and categorized them based on their geometric and
aerodynamic properties, with the 4- and 5-digit series being widely used. The significance
of NACA airfoils predominantly lies in their predictable aerodynamic performances, making
them instrumental in both academic research and practical applications [59]. Their pre-
dictable behavior simplifies the analysis of how airfoil shape affects lift, drag, and overall
aerodynamic efficiency, and serves as a pedagogical example for demonstrating our multi-
agent design workflow.
The airfoil shape is defined by parameters such as maximum camber, maximum thickness,
and maximum camber position. These parameters significantly influence the aerodynamic
performance of the airfoil.
Small variations in these parameters can lead to substantial
changes in the pressure distribution around the airfoil, and thus, its overall aerodynamic
performance [60]. For instance, increasing the maximum camber generally increases lift but
also increases drag, while repositioning the maximum camber affects the pressure gradients
and can influence stall characteristics. Understanding and precisely controlling these rela-
tionships is crucial for effective airfoil design. The four-digit NACA equation is foundational
in the generation of these airfoil shapes [59], and is given by Equation 1:

The central goal of this research is to design an efficient NACA airfoil profile, specifically
targeting a maximum lift-to-drag ratio at Mach number 0.6 and Reynolds number 5 × 106.
This specific operating condition represents a typical test condition for airfoil design valida-
tion [61]. Furthermore, for simplicity during the initial design phase, we assume a 0-degree
angle of attack. This simplification allows us to focus on the fundamental shape selection and
optimization steps without the complexities introduced by the angle of attack. The design
problem is chosen for its simplicity and the abundance of readily available technical docu-
mentation, which will be essential for constructing knowledge graphs in subsequent stages
of the research. This problem definition can be adapted based on managerial requirements,
but in the current scope, we concentrate solely on the aforementioned conditions. Further-
more, non-functional constraints will arise from the specific geometric parameters chosen
for airfoil design. For example, excessively sharp leading edges, while potentially reducing
drag, may be structurally impractical for manufacturing and operation. Such considerations
are embedded as knowledge sources with our engineering agents, thereby enabling a holistic
approach to design selection and evaluation process.
Furthermore, to address this optimization challenge, advanced tools such as AeroSandBox
[62] and NeuralFoil [63] are utilized. AeroSandBox facilitates fluid simulations and aerody-
namic analysis, while NeuralFoil offers machine learning capabilities to refine the design by
anticipating aerodynamic characteristics of new airfoil shapes. These tools, when integrated,
provide a robust framework for conducting design and analysis iteratively and efficiently. We
discuss these tools in detail later in section 4. It is essential to acknowledge that while this in-
vestigation is focused on a specific aerodynamic design problem, the methodology employed
here is adaptable to other design scenarios.
4. Methodology
4.1. Knowledge Graphs for Engineering Agents
To imbue our LLM agents with domain expertise and facilitate informed decision-making
within the engineering design process, we leverage knowledge graphs (KGs) as a means of
representing structured knowledge [64]. KGs offer a powerful and efficient way to encode
a large body of existing knowledge, going beyond simple text-based information retrieval
[65–67]. The graph-based representation allows for the discovery of novel connections and
ideas between concepts, even those that might appear distant based on human intuition
but are ontologically linked.
This ability to uncover hidden relationships is particularly
valuable in complex engineering domains where tacit knowledge and interdependencies are
prevalent. Our approach utilizes two distinct KGs, designed to represent differing levels of
knowledge abstraction and, consequently, to simulate varying degrees of expertise within our
LLM agents, mirroring the spectrum of expertise found in real-world engineering practice.
These KGs were generated using a dedicated Graph Ontologist agent [40], which oper-
ates according to a set of pre-defined instructions tailored to create agent-specific knowl-
edge graphs. This controlled generation process ensures consistency and relevance of the
7


## Page 8


knowledge encoded within each graph. The content for these graphs is derived from a com-
prehensive corpus of published reports and papers focusing on NACA airfoil design and
development. This collection spans a considerable period, from the early years of NACA
research in 1940s to more recent publications in 2010s, effectively capturing the evolution
of knowledge and best practices accumulated over decades. This long-term perspective is
crucial for mimicking the experience and understanding that an expert engineer develops
over years of training and practical application. It avoids the pitfalls of relying solely on
the latest information, ensuring that fundamental principles and historical context are also
incorporated into the agents’ knowledge base.
The use of KGs within our LLM agents provides a crucial foundation for ensuring that
design choices are supported by consistent practices and prior findings. Without this struc-
tured knowledge, agents would be prone to generating inconsistent or suboptimal designs and
responses. Furthermore, the KGs serve as a mechanism for transparency and explainability,
allowing us to trace the origins of design recommendations and understand the reasoning
behind agent decisions [68]. This capability is essential for building trust and facilitating
human oversight within the design process. In the subsequent section, we will detail the pro-
cesses involved in knowledge graph creation and how the Graph Ontologist agent constructs
these resources.
4.2. Graph Ontologist Agent
To facilitate the creation of specialized knowledge graphs for our Design and Systems
Engineering agent, we employed a dedicated Graph Ontologist agent. This LLM agent was
specifically designed to extract and structure knowledge from unstructured text, forming the
foundation of our knowledge graph. The Graph Ontologist agent utilizes LLM capabilities
to interpret context and infer semantic relationships.
The corpus utilized for knowledge
graph generation comprises of 50 scientific articles and reports spanning decades of NACA
airfoil design and development. Initially, the raw data from the scientific papers was distilled
through a sequence of preparatory steps. This included converting the PDF into a markup
language format, followed by segmenting the text into manageable chunks. A chunk size of
3000 was chosen heuristically to represent semantically coherent units of information, hence
allowing the Graph Ontologist agent to focus on localized knowledge extraction. Each text
chunk was then processed by the Graph Ontologist agent to extract local knowledge graph
triples, representing relationships between key concepts. These triples consisted of two nodes,
each representing a significant concept from the extracted ontology, and an edge feature that
defined the relationship between the two concepts. The triple set is defined as1: ‘node1’:
“A concept from extracted ontology”, ‘node2’: "A related concept from extracted ontology”,
‘edge’: “relationship between the two concepts, node1 and node2 in one or two sentences”.
This explicit definition guided the agent’s extraction process, ensuring a consistent and
interpretable knowledge representation. The procedure for KG generation is adopted from
[69].

designed for the Systems Engineer agent, encompasses a broad range of aspects related to

4.3. Systems Engineer Agent
The Systems Engineer agent embodies the responsibilities of a senior systems engineer,
leveraging a comprehensive knowledge base and advanced reasoning capabilities to generate
technical requirements, review designs, and offer recommendations for design improvements
based on knowledge. The design of this agent is informed by established practices in systems
engineering, where a systems engineer acts as an integrator, ensuring alignment between
technical specifications, design choices, and overall project goals. The Systems Engineer
10


## Page 11


Agent operates within a layered architecture, integrating several key components: a knowl-
edge source, a prompt engineering module, and an LLM execution engine.
The agent’s effectiveness is directly tied to the quality and accessibility of its knowledge
base. This knowledge source represents the collective experience and expertise accumulated
over years of practice.
We represent this knowledge in the form of a knowledge graph,
implemented as a CSV file containing nodes and relationships. This representation allows
for structured storage and retrieval of information, facilitating informed decision-making
by the agent. To manage the scale of the knowledge graph, the CSV content is split into
manageable chunks, each associated with metadata such as chunk ID, source reference, and
row range [70]. This chunking strategy optimizes the embedding and retrieval process. Text
chunks are then processed using an embedding model (OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small)
to generate vector representations [71]. These vectors are stored in a vector database for
efficient similarity search.
This vector database approach enables rapid identification of
relevant knowledge based on semantic similarity to the user’s query [72]. The use of vector
embeddings allows the agent to understand the underlying meaning of the query and retrieve
more contextually relevant information.
At runtime, the Systems Engineer Agent converts user prompts into “search queries”
through the prompt engineering module. This conversion often leverages the LLM itself
to rephrase or expand upon the original prompt, ensuring that it effectively captures the
user’s intent. The refined query is then used to search the vector database, retrieving the
top-ranked and most relevant chunks. Thereafter, the retrieved chunks are appended to
the user’s original prompt, providing the agent with the necessary context to generate a
comprehensive and informed response [71]. This process mimics the way a human systems
engineer would consult internal documentation and prior experience to address a new design
challenge. In Appendix A1, we empirically demonstrate examples of knowledge retrieval
from the graph for verifying the RAG mechanism during the ‘Requirement Generation’ step.
The Systems Engineer Agent performs several critical tasks within this framework. These
include:
• Technical Requirements Generation: Based on user prompts and leveraging its knowl-
edge source, the agent generates technical requirements. The contextual role is pro-
vided to the LLM agent to accomplish this task, ensuring consistency and adherence
to established engineering principles.
This capability streamlines the requirements
elicitation process and minimizes the potential for ambiguity or omissions. One key
advantage with using knowledge source for this task is the ability to formulate both
functional and non-functional requirements simultaneously.
• Airfoil Design Review: The agent evaluates new airfoil designs, comparing them against
established technical requirements and utilizing its accumulated design knowledge. The
agent receives the airfoil profile generated by the Design Engineer along with the cor-
responding aerodynamic performance results. Critically, the agent incorporates multi-
modal vision models to assess airfoil profile plots and performance data visually. This
capability allows for a more nuanced evaluation than relying solely on numerical data,
accounting for factors such as visual symmetry, smoothness, and overall aesthetic ap-
peal. Feedback is provided to the Design Engineer, highlighting potential areas for
improvement and suggesting alternative design approaches.

The agent’s feedback on airfoil designs is driven by a dual consideration: alignment with

4.4. Design Engineer Agent
The Design Engineer agent represents a critical mid-level role within our multi-agent sys-
tem designed to automate and enhance the airfoil design process. This agent is responsible
for the iterative cycle of creating, analyzing, visualizing, and refining airfoil designs. The
Design Engineer agent collaborates closely with the Systems Engineer agent to ensure design
feasibility and adherence to overall system requirements to achieve performance targets. The
core functionality of the Design Engineer agent centers around managing the design space,
generating candidate airfoils, analyzing their aerodynamic performance, and iteratively re-
fining designs based on feedback. This process is not a linear sequence but a continuous loop,
with insights gained from analysis triggering adjustments to the design and further rounds
of evaluation. Below we describe the core tasks and tools associated with this agent.
4.4.1. Design Space Sampling
The agent commences its task by sampling the design space, utilizing a suite of strategies
to generate diverse candidate airfoils. The Sampling tool, a custom-built Python function,
provides options for Latin Hypercube, Sobol, Halton, and random sampling. Each strategy
possesses unique characteristics suitable for different design exploration goals.
The tool
generates a set of parameter values for maximum camber, maximum camber location, and
maximum thickness that define the 4-digit NACA airfoil geometry.
4.4.2. NACA Profile Generation
Once the design parameters are sampled, the NACA profile generator tool utilizes the
standard 4-digit NACA airfoil equation to generate the corresponding airfoil coordinates.
This equation, based on a mathematical representation of airfoil geometry, enables the cre-
ation of a wide variety of airfoil shapes by adjusting three key parameters: the location of
the maximum camber, the maximum camber value, and the maximum thickness value. It is
important to note that these initial designs are not intended to be final products but rather
starting points for further refinement.
4.4.3. Airfoil Visualization
The airfoil shapes generated are then visualized using the Plotting tool, a Python function
using airfoil coordinates to plot its shape. These visualizations are critical for review by
the Systems Engineer agent, allowing for a qualitative assessment of each design against
technical requirements, especially non-functional requirements.

4.4.4. Aerodynamic Analysis with AeroSandbox and NeuralFoil
Following visualization, the aerodynamic performance of the generated designs is evaluated
using the AeroSandbox tool, an open-source, Python-based framework [62]. AeroSandbox
uniquely integrates physics-based aerodynamic models, structural modeling, and multidisci-
plinary optimization within a unified computational environment. Unlike conventional CFD
tools, AeroSandbox leverages low-fidelity aerodynamic models, such as vortex lattice meth-
ods and thin airfoil theory, coupled with automatic differentiation capabilities through tools
like CasADi. This approach facilitates rapid design space exploration and gradient-based
optimization of airfoils, wings, and complete aircraft configurations.
Complementing AeroSandbox is NeuralFoil, a machine-learning-based airfoil analysis tool
that utilizes a neural network trained on extensive XFOIL simulation data [63]. NeuralFoil’s
predictive capabilities for aerodynamic coefficients (Cl, Cd, Cm) span a broad spectrum of
Reynolds numbers and angles of attack, enabling near-instantaneous predictions with ac-
ceptable accuracy. By substituting numerical flow solutions with learned surrogates, Neural-
Foil offers substantial computational savings, making it particularly well-suited for real-time
applications, optimization loops, and scenarios where CFD simulations are impractical. This
tool is primarily used for design analysis and optimization.
4.4.5. Design Optimization
Design optimization represents the final step in the design process where a candidate de-
sign chosen during the iterative design phase is optimized against the design objectives. The
Optimization tool utilizes Kulfan coordinates to represent and manipulate the airfoil geom-
etry during the optimization stage [73]. Kulfan coordinates offer a distinct advantage over
traditional Cartesian coordinates by providing a smoother and more stable representation of
the airfoil shape, particularly when dealing with significant geometric changes. This prop-
erty facilitates the optimization process by preventing abrupt shifts and ensuring a more
controlled evolution of the design. The optimizer employs a multi-objective optimization
routine, balancing aerodynamic performance metrics such as lift and drag coefficient, while
adhering to other general constraints required for convergence.
4.4.6. Design Selection and Revision
The designs generated by the Design Engineer agent are reviewed by the Systems Engi-
neer agent in an iterative process. The Design Engineer, upon receiving critical feedback
about the designs then initiates a design selection and revision process, using his background
knowledge and feedback received. This agent then modifies the design appropriately, and
provides the new design sample for review again.
The Design Engineer agent plays a critical role in iteratively refining designs based on Sys-
tems Engineer’s feedback, technical requirements, and its own knowledge base. For example,
if the Systems Engineer suggests a reduction in thickness value to aid with manufacturing,
the Design Engineer might choose a lower maximum thickness value for generating the new
airfoil shape guided by his knowledge. Such domain knowledge driven interaction between
agents generates context-aware suggestions, considering not only the immediate performance
goals but also the broader system requirements and manufacturing limitations. This integra-
tion allows the Design Engineer to operate with a higher level of intelligence and adaptability

by continuously refining the design towards optimal performance and feasibility. The Design

4.5. Manager (Human User)
Our framework incorporates a Manager role, representing the human user responsible for
guiding the design exploration process. This individual does not directly manipulate the
LLM-based design agents, but instead acts as a gatekeeper and evaluator, ensuring the gen-
erated designs align with overarching project goals, constraints, and evolving requirements.
The Manager’s role is crucial for injecting domain expertise and addressing nuances that
automated systems may overlook. The Manager’s involvement begins at the problem defi-
nition stage. The initial design problem is formulated and communicated to the LLM-based
design agents, setting the scope for subsequent exploration. During the final design selec-
tion phase, the Manager critically assesses a limited set of candidate designs, typically the
top-ranked solutions chosen by the Design Engineer agent. This evaluation considers factors
not easily quantifiable by the automated system, such as aesthetic preferences, manufactura-
bility, and potential for future scalability. The Manager’s authority extends to determining
when the design cycle concludes. This termination condition is triggered by the Manager’s
explicit acceptance of a design, indicating a satisfactory outcome has been achieved. Further-
more, the Manager’s ability to terminate the process allows for agile adaptation to changing
project needs or the identification of fundamentally flawed design approaches. This provides
a mechanism for course correction and ensures that the design process remains responsive
to evolving requirements.
Thus, this human-in-the-loop framework allows us to harness
the power of LLM-based agents while retaining the crucial element of human expertise and
judgment, facilitating a more robust and adaptable engineering design process.

6. Limitations and Future Works
The presented framework, while demonstrating the potential for integrating multi-agent
systems (MAS) into iterative engineering design processes, contains certain limitations. Our
approach currently embodies an academic exploration of this integration, focusing on a
simplified representation of engineering tasks. Engineering design, by its inherent nature,
frequently involves intricate and nuanced decision-making processes that extend beyond the
scope of our current implementation. While the agents exhibited adaptability in navigating
decision-making scenarios with the help of the knowledge graph, maintaining consistency
across sophisticated engineering tasks remains a significant challenge.
The problem for-
mulation used in our work shows a relatively straightforward pathway, which is often a
simplification compared to the complexities encountered in real-world engineering design.
Unexpected sub-processes and iterations are common occurrences during the design cycle,
deviating from the linear progression assumed in our demonstration.
Furthermore, the tools leveraged by the Design Engineer agent are presently implemented
as simple, Python-based modules, facilitating ease of integration within the workflow. How-
ever, a major impediment to broader adoption lies in the reliance on proprietary software cru-
cial to engineering practice. Seamless integration with industry-standard Computer-Aided
Design (CAD) and Computer-Aided Engineering (CAE) tools presents a considerable hurdle
for our MAS framework. The complexity and closed nature of these tools often preclude
direct interaction with agents, necessitating the development of intermediary layers and cus-
tom interfaces, a task that demands substantial resources and expertise. Future work will
focus on addressing these limitations, exploring more complex workflows that incorporate
agents to accomplish Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) analyses on final design.
In the future, a key area of development will be the agents’ ability to dynamically create
and manage tools within the design workflow. Currently, our agents operate within a pre-
defined toolset. The ability to generate tools on demand – for example, custom scripts for
data analysis or specialized modules for simulation – would significantly enhance the frame-
work’s flexibility and utility. This dynamic tool creation capability represents a substantial
challenge, requiring advancements in automated code generation and agent reasoning, but is
important to enable more autonomous and adaptive engineering design processes. We believe
this capability will be pivotal in realizing the full potential of MAS within the engineering
domain.
7. Summary
In this work, we propose a novel multi-agent framework that leverages large language
models (LLMs) to facilitate and accelerate the solution for a pedagogical challenge of de-
signing high-performance NACA airfoils. We focus on the design of 4-digit NACA airfoils,
for demonstration purposes. This framework comprises three specialized AI agents, each

## Page 25


playing a distinct role in the iterative design cycle. The core agents are: a Graph Ontolo-
gist, a Design Engineer, and a Systems Engineer. The Graph Ontologist utilizes a prompt
engineering approach to curate specialized knowledge graphs by extracting relevant informa-
tion related to airfoil design and development from scientific literature. The extracted data
is structured and organized within the graph, enabling efficient retrieval and utilization by
other agents. Both Design and Systems Engineer are provided with their own customized
knowledge graph that is suited for their roles and expertise. The Design Engineer is respon-
sible for generating and modifying airfoil designs based on the information contained within
the knowledge graph in consultation with the Systems Engineer agent. The Systems Engi-
neer reviews designs generated by the Design Engineer, provides feedback for improvement,
and ensures alignment with technical requirements. This agent also receives input from a
human Manager to translate user decisions into actionable design feedback.
The Design and Systems Engineer agents accomplish the design process through an itera-
tive process. The Design Engineer, guided by its knowledge graph and design tools, produces
an initial set of airfoil designs. The Systems Engineer then assesses these designs, evaluat-
ing both their quantitative performance characteristics (e.g., lift and drag coefficients) and
qualitative attributes (e.g., manufacturability, robustness) as defined in the technical speci-
fication. This feedback is translated into actionable design improvement strategies, such as
adjustments to airfoil geometry. For example, if the Systems Engineer identifies a poten-
tial issue with the airfoil’s stall characteristics, they might instruct the Design Engineer to
increase the airfoil’s thickness or modify its leading-edge curvature. The agents access and
utilize their individual knowledge graphs, ensuring consistency and facilitating knowledge
transfer between design iterations. To ensure that the iterative process converges on a suit-
able design, a human Manager is included in the final design selection phase. The Manager
determines whether a design is valid based on his requirements. This process is repeated
until a design that meets the Manager’s requirements is obtained.
This synergistic approach prioritizes both quantitative and qualitative requirements dur-
ing design iteration, ensuring that the final design meets overall acceptance criteria. In future
work, we will investigate the use of additional agents such as a CFD analyst to help address
next steps in the design sequence along with methods to generate simple design tools dynam-
ically during the process. In summary, the framework presented here demonstrates a path
toward automating and augmenting the engineering design process, potentially improving
design quality and accelerating innovation.

A1. Knowledge Graph information retrieval
To validate and demonstrate the agent’s ability to effectively retrieve and apply knowledge,
we implemented a mechanism to trace the provenance of its generated outputs. The System
Engineer agent’s instructions were augmented to require explicit citation for any information
retrieved from the knowledge graph via the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) frame-
work. This modification compels the agent to label knowledge within its response using the
format ‘(src:KG, retrieved knowledge info)’, providing a transparent link between its reason-
ing and the underlying knowledge base. We illustrate this capability through two examples
from the technical requirements generation task.
First, we examine the derivation of a quantitative constraint for airfoil thickness. Re-
sponding to a general directive to use NACA 4-series designs, the agent retrieved a critical
relationship from the knowledge graph connecting the nodes ‘airfoil thickness ratio’ and
‘drag coefficient’. The specific fact, "Excessive drag coefficient limits the maximum airfoil
thickness ratio to values less than about 0.20," was directly utilized to establish an upper
bound for the design parameter. As shown in the agent’s response, this led to the concrete
requirement constraining thickness ‘t’ to ≤20%. This process, visually corroborated by the
graph excerpt in Figure A1, showcases the agent’s capacity to ground design parameters in
fundamental principles stored within the KG.
