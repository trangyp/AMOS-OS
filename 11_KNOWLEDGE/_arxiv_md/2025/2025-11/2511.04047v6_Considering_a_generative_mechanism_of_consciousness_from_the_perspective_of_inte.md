from the perspective of inter-level causation

Abstract
Why do some physical systems possess consciousness, while others do not? Is this a question of physics? Or is it a question of the theory of
causation? Physics and the theory of causation serve different descriptive purposes, and in this study we refer to them respectively as the Physical
Stance and the Causal Stance. We propose that the generation of consciousness is determined by its internal causal mechanisms in the Causal
Stance. To describe a causal model, we will introduce an asymmetric relation between cause and effect into the formulation that is necessary
for describing causality, though not physical laws. We argue that the causal conditions for the generation of consciousness are constituted by
internal causal mechanisms of the system, rather than by external interventions. To explain such intrinsic causes, this study focuses on inter-level
causality. Traditionally, inter-level causality has been considered an emergent phenomenon rather than a mechanism. We devise a method to
implement these mechanisms explicitly in a causal model by examining how causes originating at higher levels are transmitted to lower levels
within the system. We then propose a Dual-Laws Model (DLM), which features distinct dynamical laws at higher and lower levels. Finally, we
discuss the generation of functional consciousness and its causality based on the DLM. Note that this study does not address the causal eﬀicacy
of the phenomenological aspect.
Keywords Consciousness, Asymmetry between Cause and Effect, Inter-level Causation, Dual-Laws Model, Constructive Approach

Introduction
When constructing theories of the mind or consciousness, should we
base them on physics? The linguistic system of physics seems to be
narrower than the linguistic system of causation. Not all physical
quantities are easy to manipulate, and such asymmetries are not
described by the laws of physics (5).
Suppose a science of causality were established.
Would the
phenomena of consciousness and the mind be explained by physics
alone? Or would they be explained by the science of causality? In
this paper, we will examine the latter possibility.
A key feature of descriptions of causality is an asymmetric
relation between cause and effect (39; 59). While such asymmetrical
relationships are not necessary when describing the laws of physics,
they are essential in causal inference (39). Therefore, in this study, we
aim to examine the generative mechanism of consciousness based on a
framework that distinguishes between cause and effect by the Pearl’s
assignment operator (39). Following Dennett (16), this study refers
to the interpretation using the language of physics as the “Physical
Stance,” and the interpretation using the language of causation as
the “Causal Stance” (37).
Generally speaking, different academic disciplines have different
linguistic systems depending on their objectives (20; 57). It is possible
for different academic disciplines to use the same term with different
meanings. According to physics, since all changes in physical states
are physically determined, the mind is unnecessary. In Physical
Stance, it seems impossible to question the relationship between
mind and body. Therefore, we consider constructing a language for
academic disciplines in which the mind and mental causation can
be defined. In this study, we will construct such a linguistic system
based on the theory of causation.

Although monism and dualism are typically regarded as mutually
exclusive ontological positions, the present work does not engage in
adjudicating between them. Instead, the focus is on how different
explanatory frameworks can give rise to apparently conflicting
interpretations concerning mind and causation. Throughout this
paper, references to monism or dualism are intended descriptively
rather than ontologically, and no commitment is made to non-
physical substances or to a violation of physicalism. Our aim is
to construct an explanatory framework for the scientific study of
mind and mental causation that remains fully compatible with
physics, without taking a position on the ontology of the mind.
Specifically,
we distinguish between the Physical Stance,
which
employs physical modeling and physical determinism, and the Causal
Stance, which analyzes asymmetric causal roles, including mental
causation. If a systematic translation from the Causal Stance to the
Physical Stance is available, no contradiction arises between the two
descriptions, even though they serve different explanatory purposes.
The present work therefore does not take a position on whether
reality is fundamentally monistic or dualistic. Rather, it argues that
persistent confusion in science of consciousness arises from a failure to
distinguish between explanatory frameworks, in particular between
physical modeling and causal modeling.
In order to realize a linguistic system that can account for the
mind and mental causation, the concept of “physical” in the Physical
Stance must have a different meaning from that in the Causal Stance
(37). This is because the changes in physical states are determined
solely by physical states and cannot be influenced by non-physical
mental states at the same time. To distinguish the meaning of
“physical” in each context, we define “physical” in the Physical Stance
as P-physical and those in the Causal Stance as C-physical. Consider
the following conditions to construct a linguistic framework in which
the mental causation can be defined (Fig. 1).
•
Translation: a model in the Causal Stance can be translated into
a model in the Physical Stance.
•
P-physical determinism: in the Physical Stance, the changes in
P-physical states are determined by P-physical states alone.
•
C-mental causation: in the Causal Stance, the changes in C-
physical states are determined by both C-mental states and
C-physical states.
•
Intrinsic causes: in the Causal Stance, the mental causation is
relied on intrinsic causes.
It is not easy to define intrinsic causes. Like volition (23), which
is explained by intrinsic causes, it is easier to characterize it through
negative statements. Volition excludes not only actions caused by
external stimuli but also those driven by physiological needs within
the system. It is not enough to simply assume that intrinsic causes lie
within the system itself, because the internal triggering of some event
in some internal organ does not capture what we mean by volitional.
We define “intrinsic cause” by the following three conditions.
•
System A, where the intrinsic cause originates, and System B,
where the effect occurs, cannot be separated.
•
System A and System B cannot be separated because they share
the same physical entities.
•
If the shared entities are removed, the asymmetry between cause
and effect is lost.
•
The intervention of intrinsic causes does not immediately change
the state of shared physical entities.
It is a system-level causality that does not exist when broken
down into its constituent elements. Condition 1 enables us to treat
Systems A and B as a single, integrated system. This allows causes
to originate within the system and affect it. Condition 1 is similar
to the postulate of “integration” in Integrated Information Theory
(54; 56). However, the definition of separability differs. While IIT
determines this based on changes in integrated information, we use
the shared physical entities as our criterion. This is not merely
physical contacts like the touch of internal organs and nerves; it
requires the sharing of objects such as cells. This is defined within the
Physical Stance. Condition 3 is necessary to eliminate trivial cases,
such as when shared entities do not contribute causality. Condition 4
is the criterion for distinguishing an intrinsic cause from an extrinsic
cause. Although further discussion is needed regarding whether our
definition is suﬀiciently robust to exclude negative examples, it has
the promising characteristic of being observer-independent and well
defined in the Causal Stance.
In order to realize our goal,
we must identify the intrinsic
causes. We examine the conditions under which a model of causal
mechanisms within the system generates intrinsic causes.
As a
candidate mechanism for the generation of these intrinsic causes, we
propose a model of inter-level causation (38). We do not claim that
inter-level causation is the only way to explain intrinsic causes. We
consider the above conditions to be the necessary requirements for a
linguistic system that accounts for mental causation.
The idea of considering mental phenomena as macro phenomena
has a long history (10; 51), and many theories of consciousness
seem to regard consciousness as an integrated macro phenomenon
(3; 14; 19; 29; 55). However, if a coarse-grained state is determined by
lower substrate-level neural states from the bottom-up (14), then the
coarse-grained state cannot have causal eﬀicacy on the substrate-level
(27). This Kim’s exclusion argument is often interpreted as denying

Here, we propose a Dual-Laws Model (DLM). The DLM is a
model designed to describe independent dynamics and their causal
influences at different hierarchical levels—the coarse-grained level and
the substrate level. By introducing the causal assignment operator,
DLM distinguishes from single-level dynamical system models.
In DLM, we construct a linguistic framework that defines the
dynamics of the higher coarse-grained level as C-mental laws, those of
the lower substrate level as C-physical laws, and intrinsic inter-level
causation as C-mental causation. When translated into the Physical
Stance model, this model satisfies P-physical determinism. In the
framework of DLM, we will examine the generation of consciousness
and the causal eﬀicacy of consciousness.
In the present framework, the asymmetry between cause and
effect is treated as a primitive concept of causal analysis, in line
with standard causal science as developed by Pearl (39). Physical
models do not encode causal direction, and the task of grounding the
asymmetry of causation lies beyond the scope of this work. Instead,
the focus is on how such asymmetric causal roles are implemented
within a hierarchical structure and articulated explicitly by causal
notation.
Dual-Laws Model (DLM)
A Dual-Laws Model (DLM) describes the causal constraint from
coarse-grained level to substrate-level.
This model differs from
interaction models involving two distinct dynamics at the same
hierarchical level in the Physical Stance. The key difference lies in
the fact that the DLM possesses a self-referential feedback control
mechanism, which is a causal transmission mechanism from higher
level to lower level. Because the coarse-grained level where causes
arise and the substrate level where effects arise share the same
physical entities, this mechanism allows for the introduction of a
hierarchical structure, intrinsic causes, and the causal asymmetry.
In contrast, physical entities are not shared by two dynamics at the
same hierarchical level. Therefore, these two dynamical systems are
separable.
Causal models and dynamical system models differ in their
descriptive approaches. Causal models explicitly distinguish between
the asymmetry of cause and effect, whereas physical models do not.
The DLM is a causal model defined within the Causal Stance.
The distinction between cause and effect is not introduced merely
to differentiate the DLM from dynamical system models. In applied
science and engineering, elucidating causal structures is a critical
task, and causal models should be clearly distinguished from the
dynamical system models.
Furthermore,
it is well known that
introducing asymmetry in causal inference changes the available
statistical methods (39). In traditional philosophical discussions of
the mind, the distinction between causality and physics was often
unclear (27; 47). We believe that differences in the underlying models
give rise to differences in scientific methodology.
Causal eﬀicacy of consciousness
When discussing theories that acknowledge mental causation, such
as the causation of consciousness, it is often argued that these
theories are flawed because they do not satisfy physical causal
closure. However, we do not believe that this conclusion can be
drawn. According to the currently dominant theory of causation,
the language of physics does not contain asymmetries based on
manipulability,
and therefore physical causal closure cannot be
defined in this framework (37).
What physics must uphold is
physical determinism, which holds that changes in physical states
are determined solely by physical states. This means that we cannot
incorporate non-physical forces into the model.
We believe it is permissible to construct the linguistic systems
for academic disciplines in which physical causal closure is false,
in the Causal Stance (37). To avoid any misunderstandings at this
point, it is necessary to distinguish between “P-physical” in physics
and “C-physical” in the theory of causation. This is because, if
C-mental exerts a causal influence on C-physical, C-physical must
be considered distinct from P-physical, since it does not satisfy
physical determinism; otherwise, a contradiction would arise. The
distinction between C-physical and C-mental is likely similar to the
distinction between the physical and the mental in everyday language.
The difference between C-physical and P-physical appears to reflect
the distinction between the concept of “physical” as we commonly
understand it and the concept of “physical” as used in physics.
We believe that constructing a model of consciousness requires a
linguistic system capable of acknowledging mental causation. If we
assume physical determinism and that the mind is non-physical, then
the mind cannot influence the physical world in the Physical Stance.
In order to acknowledge mental causation,
we must necessarily
adopt a pluralistic interpretation of the term “physical.” Since the
descriptive framework of physics cannot account for mental causation,
we decided to examine descriptions based on the Causal Stance.
The science of consciousness aims to create a model of the brain
composed of physical entities that can be explained by physical
forces alone. Causation satisfies the laws of physics. However, the
laws of physics themselves do not describe information regarding
manipulability or asymmetry.
Our goal is to create an internal mechanism model of the
brain, which explains the causes of consciousness as well as the
consciousness’s causal eﬀicacy to address the functional aspects of
consciousness, in the Causal Stance. To emphasize the asymmetry
between cause and effect, we identify an intrinsic cause that can be
manipulated within the system. Then, we introduce the assignment
operator to construct an inter-level causal model that can be
distinguished from a single-level dynamical system model. We do not
address the causal eﬀicacy of qualia.
Mental phenomena including consciousness are all thought to
systematically co-vary with changes at the substrate level neural
states, such that no change in the former occurs without a change in
the latter. In philosophy, this dependency is called a supervenience

It is important to distinguish between predictability and causality
(44). In nonlinear dynamical systems, coarse-grained states often
exhibit behaviors that appear to be autonomous or as if they exhibit
inter-level causation (42; 48). However, from the manipulability
perspective, this type of as if inter-level causation measured by
predictability is diﬀicult to consider true causality. In true causality,
we can identify a cause that does not influence an effect without
a causal transmission mechanism (43; 39).
To define inter-level
causation, we must address a cause at supervenience level (13). We
cannot use the causal assignment operator to distinguish between
cause and effect without identifying a supervenient-level cause. Since
a single supervenience relationship does not allow us to define a
cause at the supervenience level, we assume a hierarchy consisting
of multiple supervenience relationships.
To address the functional aspects of consciousness, we make the
following assumptions in the Causal Stance.
•
All mental entities, including consciousness, supervene on C-
physical states.
•
Supervenient causation, which is causal eﬀicacy from supervenient
level to the corresponding subvenient states, is involved in the
generation mechanism of consciousness.
•
Consciousness is a supervenient coarse-grained level entity, and
the C-physical correlates of consciousness are described by the
states of the corresponding subvenient entities.
•
Generated consciousness has causal eﬀicacy only in an indirect
sense through the same inter-level causation mechanisms.
•
Intrinsic inter-level causation is a model of mental causation.
It should be noted that these assumptions do not use the language of
physics; the term “physical” here refers to C-Physical. Furthermore,
we must maintain physical determinism, which holds that the changes
in P-physical is determined solely by P-physical, in the Physical
Stance. The causal model can be transformed into a physical model
by ignoring the asymmetry based on manipulability to confirm this.
When discussing the causality of consciousness and the mind,
there is a risk that the reader might mistakenly believe that we
are introducing non-physical forces. Rather than introducing non-
physical forces, our goal is to identify a model of the causal eﬀicacy
of consciousness in the Causal Stance. In our model, we introduce
the distinction between cause and effect (i.e., causal assignment
operator), not a non-physical force. This distinction does not exist in
the description of physics.
Self-referential Feedback Control Mechanism
Whether a system possesses consciousness should be determined
by its internal causal mechanism rather than by the input-output
relationships of systems or subsystems. To clarify the intrinsic causes,
we focus on inter-level causation mechanism.
To define inter-level causation,
we must define a cause at
supervenient
level
and
a
causal
transmission
mechanism
from
supervenient level to subvenient level. Self-referential feedback control
mechanisms are necessary for causal transmission from supervenient
level to subvenient level.
The mereological relationship between the whole and its parts is a
type of supervenience relationship, because changes in whole cannot
occur without changes in the states of parts. Because the whole and
its constituent parts share the same physical entities, the causation
from the whole to its parts can be characterized as intrinsic. Our
model assumes causality across hierarchical levels based on multiple
supervenience relationships. Even in this case, the causation from
the whole level to the parts level can be characterized as intrinsic,
because the two levels share the same physical entities. In addition to
the physical entities that are shared across levels, our model includes
other physical entities. However, when the physical entities shared
between levels are removed, causality disappears. This means that
the inter-level causation model explains intrinsic causes.
The relationship between cell groups (organs) and their constituent
individual cells is a well-known whole-parts relationship in the
biological systems.
In the brain,
such cell groups form neural
networks.
Neural networks can be modeled as functions.
We
assume that supervenient entities are mathematical functions and
supervenient level consist of multiple supervenient functions, or
neural networks. The corresponding subvenient entities are the state
of neurons and synapses within the supervenient functions.
An equation can be defined by selecting and ordering multiple
supervenient functions from a set of supervenient functions at
supervenient level. We define that self-referential feedback control
mechanism is a negative feedback control of states of neurons and
synapses at subvenient level to satisfy the equations composed
of
the
selected
supervenient
functions.
A
unique

supervenient functions that constitute the equation are affected by
the self-referential feedback control mechanism. The coarse-grained
supervenient functions and the corresponding subvenient states share
the same physical entities. This makes it possible to introduce a
hierarchical structure.
It should be noted that we define structural hierarchies in terms
of the relationship between the whole and its parts, rather than
as hierarchies in information processing,
like the primary and
the secondary visual cortices. Since cause and effect in a causal
relationship arise from the same physical entities, this provides a
model capable of explaining intrinsic causes. To understand why this
is possible, it is necessary to grasp the formulation presented in the

If we replace the causal operator := with =, the DLM can be

dynamical system model. In order to adequately express inter-level
causality, it is essential to distinguish between cause and effect in our
model. The key point is that, because physical models do not account
for causal asymmetry, causal relationships between hierarchical levels
are hidden in the Physical Stance.
Thus, we will examine theories of consciousness based on the
DLM, which is defined in terms of the Causal Stance—–a broader
concept than the Physical Stance. If the goal is simply to describe
consciousness, the Causal Stance may not be necessary. However,
we believe that the Causal Stance is necessary to clarify the causes
of consciousness. If consciousness arises from within the system,
the asymmetry between intrinsic causes and the effects should be
introduced into the system model.

Relationship with Existing Theories
To characterize our theory, we will compare it with other existing
theories. The purpose is to distinguish our theory from existing ones;
it is not intended to evaluate the theory’s superiority. The DLM is
an abstract mechanism and should be distinguished from its specific
implementation in the brain. DLM is not mutually exclusive with
current neuroscience-based theories of consciousness. Rather, it is
one of the tools used to formulate such theories in the Causal Stance.
Approaches that infer the generative mechanism of consciousness
from its neural correlates (58) are diﬀicult to verify because they
fall into the “triviality problem” (28). Therefore, we employed a
methodology that infers the generative mechanism of consciousness
based on the conditions necessary for it to possess causal eﬀicacy.
Because we believe that the easy problem is not independent of the
hard problem, we assume that focusing on the functional aspects of
consciousness can provide insights into the hard problem, within the
system models.

Intentional Stance
Dennett proposed distinguishing between the “Intentional Stance,”
used to model mental phenomena, and the “Physical Stance,” used
to model physical phenomena (16). We believe that we should
distinguish between the Physical Stance and the “Causal Stance,”
and that models of consciousness should be based on the Causal
Stance (37). The Causal Stance makes it possible to draw distinctions
that are indistinguishable within the Physical Stance by utilizing
asymmetries based on manipulability. Intrinsic causes, inter-level
causality, and the DLM are modeled within the Causal Stance.
In traditional philosophy of mind, the distinction between the
Physical Stance and the Causal Stance was ambiguous, leading to
confusion between introducing non-physical forces into the Physical
Stance and introducing mental causation into the Causal Stance.
According to the currently dominant theory of causation, physics
cannot describe the asymmetry of causation; therefore, descriptions
of causation such as “physical causal closure” are undefined in the
Physical Stance. For this reason, it is necessary to distinguish between
“physical determinism,” as defined by physics, and “physical causal
closure.” We believe that if physical determinism is satisfied in the
Physical Stance, it does not necessarily follow that physical causal
closure must be satisfied in the Causal Stance. This is because the
latter concept is not an absolute requirement in physics.
When the “physical” in the Physical Stance is denoted as P-
physical, the “physical” in the Causal Stance is denoted as C-physical,
and the “mental” as C-mental. In this case, both C-physical and
C-mental are expressed as P-physical when described as a physical
model. However, in the causal model, C-Physical and C-Mental
can be distinguished by causal asymmetry. By interpreting it in
this way, it is possible to construct a model in which C-mental
causal closure is false and P-physical determinism is true. We believe
that distinguishing between P-physical and C-physical is crucial to
creating such a linguistic system.
In the DLM, we assume that the dynamics at the subvenient level
corresponds to C-physical laws and the dynamics at the supervenient
level corresponds to C-mental laws. Intrinsic inter-level causation
corresponds to C-mental causation. When our model is translated
into a physical model, it satisfies P-physical determinism. We believe
that mental causation can be acknowledged within the Causal Stance
and that the causal eﬀicacy of consciousness can be modeled.
Functionalism
Block (7) defines it as follows: “Functionalism says that mental
states are constituted by their causal relations to one another and
to sensory inputs and behavioral outputs.” Similarly, according to
the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (26), “functionalist theories
take the identity of a mental state to be determined by its causal
relations to sensory stimulations, other mental states, and behavior.”
Notably, despite functionalism’s emphasis on causality, there has
been no explicit discussion of the asymmetry between cause and effect
incorporated into contemporary theories of causation. Functionalism
is often characterized by the relationship between inputs and outputs,
but Block and Searle consider this to be a problem (6; 46).
We do not characterize mental causation or consciousness solely in
terms of the input-output relationships of systems and subsystems.
When introducing a model of inter-level causation into a system,
the system’s inputs and outputs are not essential to the formulation.
The problem with characterizing a system based on similarities in its
input-output relationships is that it undermines the transparency of
the system’s internal mechanisms. If we are to emphasize the intrinsic
nature of a generative mechanism of consciousness,
we should
distinguish the system’s internal mechanisms; a causal mechanism
model that focuses on the asymmetry between cause and effect
is well-suited to this purpose. Our position is that the mind and
consciousness should be characterized not by the similarity of input-
output relationships with respect to the system, but by the causal
mechanisms within the system itself.
Global Workspace Theory
Global Workspace Theory (GWT) links consciousness to broadcasting
functions (2; 3; 15). It is relevant in emphasizing the functional role
of consciousness (2). We also believe that theories of consciousness
that ignore functions are scientifically inadequate (12; 24). However,
the idea that a function generates consciousness faces the typical
problems
of
functionalism
(6;
11).
GWT
posits
that
parallel
distributed processing is unconscious, while serial, integrated, and
coherent information broadcasted to distributed systems is conscious
(2; 3).
We argue that having a function like broadcasting is
not a necessary condition for generating consciousness,
unlike
what Baars proposed (2), Our claim is that consciousness arises
not bottom-up from substrate-level dynamics, but through inter-
level causation. While the discrete nature of index sequences and
the selection mechanism may relate to the seriality, integration,
and coherence of consciousness,
we believe that the generative
mechanism of consciousness and the causal eﬀicacy of consciousness
are distinct problem. We believe that the broadcasting is more
related with the causal eﬀicacy of consciousness, rather than the
generative mechanism of consciousness. We do not assume localized
brain regions or pathways like the global workspace are necessary
for generating consciousness. We hypothesized that self-referential
feedback control mechanism is necessary for the generation of
consciousness.
Freeman’s Circular Causation
In this study, we refer to the mechanism by which the supervenient
level causally constrains its corresponding subvenient states as
a self-referential feedback control mechanism.
This differs from
circular causation (19), where elements at the same level interact
to form interaction loops in the Physical Stance. In such complex
systems,
global states emerge from micro-level interactions and
then organize individual neurons downward (19). The coarse-grained
level dynamical laws we propose are not obtained through self-
organization from the substrate level. Furthermore, Bedau points
out that macro phenomena arising from self-organization cannot
explain “actual” inter-level causation (4). The question is whether the
asymmetry between cause and effect emerges from the interactions
within physical systems, or whether the language of causation should
be added to the language of physics. We build an inter-level causation
model using the Causal Stance, rather than dynamical system model
using the Physical Stance.

itself, but the cause of its generation. Since consciousness depends on
sensory inputs and physiological states, it seems that consciousness
itself is not intrinsic. We do not believe that the fact that conscious

is intrinsic lies with the cause of the generation of consciousness.

consciousness, such as integration, composition, and exclusiveness in
our model. We do not believe that all properties of consciousness can
be explained by the properties at the substrate level dynamics in the

Non-reductive physicalism
Non-reductive physicalism holds that consciousness is a product of
the brain, but that mental states and properties are non-reductively
distinct from physical states and properties (33).
According to

mental states as ontologically distinct from physical ones.
The
core mechanism of non-reductive physicalism is emergence, the idea
that higher-level emergent properties cannot be predicted even with
complete knowledge of the underlying level. Moreover, non-reductive
physicalism often assumes that emergent mental properties exert
downward causal influence on physical systems.
This notion of
downward causation is frequently used to explain mental causation
(34; 51) and agent causation (36; 52; 53). Non-reductive physicalism
assume that emergent mental properties are ontologically irreducible

Emergentism
Sperry (49) considered consciousness to be “the holistic properties
of the organism with causal effects”. Sperry seems to equate coarse-
grained level intrinsic causation with consciousness. However, we do
not equate the causal power from coarse-grained supervenient level
to subvenient states with consciousness itself. We believe that inter-
level causation is necessary for generating consciousness, but how
these consciousness influence other neural systems is a separate issue.
To investigate the causal eﬀicacy of consciousness, it is necessary to
clarify how consciousness affects neural system after their generation.
We assume that consciousness affects the selection of index sequences,
and subsequently affects subvenient level via self-referential feedback
control mechanism.
When theories of consciousness emphasize intrinsic causes, they
often intersect with the problem of Free Will or agent causation.
Sperry appears to equate the problem of consciousness with that of
Free Will (50). Recently, discussions have also linked IIT with the
problem of agency (17; 41). These connections arise from a shared
challenge: coarse-grained level intrinsic causality cannot be explicitly
explained in the Physical Stance alone. We believe this is because
physics does not distinguish between the asymmetry of cause and
effect, which undermines the transparency of a system’s internal
mechanisms. Therefore, physics cannot describe intrinsic causes. The
issue lies in descriptive ability, not in the idea that intrinsic causes
do not obey the laws of physics.
Scheffel’s Emergent Will resembles our Dual-Laws Model on the
surface (45). Scheffel considers Emergent Will to be an independent
law, separate from lower-level physical laws and proposes it as a new
definition of Free Will. To justify the validity of such Emergent Will,
Scheffel appeals to the emergence of psychological properties, like
non-reductive physicalism. However, if a DLM is assumed, Scheffel’s
proposed concept of Free Will can be maintained, and the assumption
of mysterious causal emergence becomes unnecessary. Similarly, List
employs the emergence of intentional agency from lower-level physical
phenomena in his discussion of Free Will (32), whereas the Dual-
Laws Model we assume are not emergence but merely the inter-level
causation model using the Causal Stance. We believe that in order
to resolve the contradiction between mental causation and physical
determinism, it is necessary to distinguish between P-physical and
C-physical. C-physical is subject to the causal influence of C-mental
through C-mental causation, whereas P-physical is subject only to
the influence of P-physical.
In debates about Free Will, the intuitive resistance to determinism
may stem from the fact the intrinsic causes cannot affect the system

Summary
Existing theories do not seem to identify the asymmetry between
cause and effect within the system.
Rather,
they posit that
this distinction emerges within the dynamical system model. For
this reason,
they do not explicitly incorporate the language of
causality into their models. Our model’s unique feature is its explicit
introduction of the distinction between cause and effect into an inter-
level causal mechanism model. We believe the Causal Stance are
necessary to elucidate the generative mechanism of consciousness.
Introducing multiple dynamics at different levels into a scientific
theory may be criticized as a violation of Occam’s razor. However,
current science already studies different laws independently at each
hierarchical level. Therefore, assuming multiple dynamical laws is
not inherently problematic. In the DLM, the causal influence from
coarse-grained supervenient level to subvenient level is essential to
account for intrinsic causal power. Thus, the complexity introduced
by multiple dynamics is justified by the need to explain phenomena
that cannot be captured by a system model without inter-level
causation mechanism.
What consciousness can do for neuroscience
Gomez-Marin suggests that one should ask not what neuroscience
can do for consciousness, but what theories of consciousness can
contribute to neuroscience (22). We agree with this perspective. In
order to avoid the triviality problem (28), rather than deriving a
theory directly from neural data, we propose a causal model intended
to constrain and guide future neuroscientific interpretation.
Traditional scientific theories often study laws independently at
each hierarchical level. In contrast, we assume that distinct dynamical
laws operate at both the supervenient and subvenient levels, and that
observations at the subvenient level arise from interactions between
these levels. Because our model is formulated from the Causal Stance,
care must be taken when applying analytical methods that ignore
causal asymmetry. Moreover, verifying causal influence from the
supervenient level to the subvenient level requires interventions on
supervenient-level causes, which are diﬀicult to realize in actual brain
systems. For this reason, a constructive approach is particularly
appropriate.
Causal models are widely used in engineering design, making their
connection to constructive approaches a natural one. Reproducing
subvenient-level observations under conditions of complex inter-level
interactions is therefore a highly nontrivial task that benefits from
constructive modeling.
One constructive option is to implement both levels of dynamics in
simulations or robotic systems and to study their resulting behavior.
Self-referential feedback control mechanisms can be simulated using
artificial neural networks, where supervenient functions are realized
at a higher descriptive level, and subvenient states correspond to
models of neurons and synapses constituting these systems. Such
implementations make it possible to experimentally investigate which
models are involved in the functional aspects of consciousness.
Furthermore, by introducing the hypothesis that consciousness
modulates index sequences, such as attentional selection, the model
yields predictions about how generated conscious states influence
observable subvenient physical dynamics.
Because neither coarse-grained supervenient dynamics nor fine-
grained subvenient dynamics can be fully derived from observational
data alone, exploratory strategies may also be required. For example,
machine learning methods may be useful for exploring combinations
of dual-level dynamics that account for the functional aspects of
consciousness.
Conclusion
The main claims of this paper can be summarized as follows:
1.
We assume that whether consciousness arises in a system depends
on causal factors within the system.
2.
To explain intrinsic causes, we proposed a model featuring inter-
level causal mechanisms within a physical system.
3.
To study systems with inter-level causal mechanisms, a new
scientific methodology is required. It is a constructive approach
based on a Dual-Laws Model.
This paper proposes a method for studying a Dual-Laws Model
governed by both coarse-grained supervenient level and subvenient
level dynamics. The key point is that our model is defined in
terms of the Causal Stance. By introducing a causal assignment
operator, our model can be distinguished from a dynamical system
model. The introduction of this asymmetric operator requires careful
consideration, as it goes beyond existing physics and is essential for
defining inter-level causality. A causal asymmetric operator is not
necessary for describing physics. However, without introducing this
asymmetry, the model of inter-level causality would not hold. On the
other hand, introducing asymmetric operations into physics neither
undermines existing physics nor requires forces such as entelechy.

The question is whether theories of consciousness can be resolved
within the framework of physics, or whether we must introduce a new
concept of asymmetry. If the science of consciousness is concerned
with elucidating the causes of consciousness rather than merely
describing it, then it seems natural to introduce the language of
causation. One of the contributions of this study is that it argues that
introducing the language of causation is a natural step in discussing
the intrinsic causes of consciousness. The permissibility of introducing
such asymmetry should be examined in the future based on the
predictive power of the DLM.
In the Causal Stance, we proposed conditions that intrinsic causes
must satisfy. To ensure that a cause arising within a single system
affects the system itself, we focused on the non-separability of the
system. Our proposal requires that objects shared between system
A, where the cause originates, and system B, where the effect occurs,
contribute to the causal relationship. However, it does not prohibit
the inclusion of other substances. Further examination is needed to
determine whether such a relaxation of conditions is permissible.
Despite these limitations, we believe that models of mental causation
must satisfy the condition of intrinsic causes.
Our DLM methodology naturally aligns with a constructive
approach that clarifies the functional aspects of consciousness.
The DLM is a model that incorporates causal structures, and a
constructive approach typically requires a causal model. To generate
the C-physical correlates of consciousness, self-referential feedback
control is required. We propose that consciousness affects attention
mechanism by changing the index sequences. But we cannot rule
out the possibility that the generated C-physical correlates directly
affect neurons and synapses at the circuit A and B. To clarify the
unique function of consciousness and suﬀicient conditions to generate
consciousness, future simulation experiments using more rigorous
models are essential. In particular, since our model only allows for
the imposition of algebraic constraints among supervenient functions,
we must verify which aspects of consciousness can be explained by
this approach. In addition, whether other causal mechanisms that
explain “internal causes” might exist is also considered an important
research question.
The proposed constructive methodology using a DLM is an
extremely challenging endeavor,
given the diversity of human
conscious experiences.
Furthermore,
at present,
there has been
no investigation into how DLM can be applied to the actual
brain. A key feature of DLM is that it distinguishes between the
causes of consciousness and their causal eﬀicacy within the model.
Furthermore, to avoid the triviality problem, it is designed to focus
on modeling these two types of causality. Within the scope of the
Physical Stance, it is not possible to distinguish between DLM and
single-laws model (SLM). We believe that theories of consciousness
can be developed within the Causal Stance, which distinguishes an
asymmetric relationship between cause and effect.
