1. Introduction
While artificial intelligence (AI) has accelerated
scientific discovery across domains such as drug
discovery, protein folding [1], and materials sci-
ence [2], its application to understanding effective
psychotherapy remains limited. Although large lan-
guage models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed to
analyze, assist, and replace human therapists in psy-
chotherapeutic conversations [3, 4, 5, 6], these ap-
plications primarily replicate established therapeu-
tic approaches such as Cognitive Behavioral Ther-
apy (CBT) and Motivational Interviewing (MI). They
do not address fundamental research questions re-
garding when and for whom specific therapeutic ap-
proaches are effective, nor do they illuminate the un-
derlying mechanisms of therapeutic success.
Traditional psychotherapy research methods, in-
cluding randomized control trials and longitudinal
studies, have yielded valuable insights into treat-
ment effectiveness. However, these approaches are
constrained by their time-intensive nature, resource
demands, and limited capacity to systematically ex-
plore the vast combinatorial space of therapeutic
approaches and their adaptations to diverse patient
presentations [7, 8, 9].
We propose that LLMs offer a scalable and sys-
tematic platform to assess, discover, and improve
therapeutic approach selection by extracting latent
knowledge encoded within these high-performance
systems.
This computational framework enables
systematic exploration of emergent or effective psy-
chotherapeutic processes through conversational
simulations, implemented via self-play–a reinforce-
ment learning (RL) technique where agents interact
with copies of themselves. The self-play mechanism
that enabled AlphaGo to develop self-directed strate-
gies in the game of Go without human guidance, sur-
passing human expertise and revealing previously
unknown tactical approaches [10], exemplifies this
potential. The famous “Move 37” surprised human
players, who found it creative and initially hard to
grasp, and subsequently inspired work to analyze its
effectiveness [11].
Analogously, we simulate turn-based therapeutic
dialogues [12], even though therapy is collaborative
rather than competitive [13, 14]. Two LLM agents as-
sume the roles of therapist and client [15, 16], oper-
ating within the constraints of single-session, text-
based therapy–a format commonly found in anony-
mous helplines [17]. This controlled environment
isolates the impact of therapeutic approach selection
and techniques used (such as cognitive restructuring
from CBT or confidence ruler from MI) from factors
including therapeutic alliance, non-verbal cues, and
longitudinal relationship development.
Emergent conversational patterns [18] reveal the
techniques enacted by AI therapists and their align-
ment with established therapeutic approaches. Of
particular interest are deviations from standard ap-
proaches, including novel therapeutic techniques,
innovative sequencing of techniques within ses-
sions, unconventional applications of established,
and adaptive approaches tailored to client character-
istics. This paper presents a proof-of-concept study
demonstrating how AI can serve as a discovery en-
gine for psychotherapy research.
2. Methods
We simulated therapeutic dialogues between two
LLMs agents (actors) (see Tab. A5).
One agent
role-played as therapist, while the other enacted
a client presenting with depressive symptoms at
five severity levels based on DSM-5 criteria [19]
(see Tabs. A1 and A2).
Actor behaviors were dif-
ferentiated through detailed system prompts or vi-
gnettes [20, 21, 22], developed and validated by a
counselor who confirmed the resulting transcripts
achieved sufficient clinical realism. Each client per-
sona was assigned a comprehensive background,
context, emotional struggles, objective for ther-
apy, and personality matched to severity level (see
Tab. A3). This stratification was designed to elicit
distinct therapeutic approaches, as clients with se-
vere depression typically exhibit greater functional
impairment. The AI therapist (see Tab. A4) was ini-
tialized with counseling microskills (e.g., active lis-
tening, empathy), but was not restricted to any thera-
peutic approach. This design enabled observation of
spontaneous therapeutic approach adoption and in-
tegration based on client presentations, rather than
adherence to predetermined protocols.
We generated 100 self-play sessions per client per-
sona using GPT-4o [23], with each dialogue compris-
ing 10 conversational turns per agent. A separate
LLM agent, functioning as a research analyst, eval-
uated the therapeutic approaches (see Tab. A6) and
specific techniques (e.g., asking scaling questions,
resolving ambivalence; see Tab. A7) employed by the
AI therapist.

3. Results and Discussion
The
AI
therapist
predominantly
employed
Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT), Person-
Centered Therapy (PCT), CBT, and MI approaches–
all well-suited for brief, text-based interactions
and commonly used in counseling sessions with
an individual with depression (Fig. 1).
The pre-
ponderance of SFBT likely reflects mainstream
LLM-human alignment with constructive, solution-
oriented responses that avoid deeper psychological
exploration.
Notably, SFBT usage decreased with
symptom severity while PCT increased, suggesting
adaptive selection based on client characteristics.
Milder cases may benefit from a more straightfor-
ward application of SFBT techniques like exception
finding and goal setting, whereas severe cases
require PCT’s open-ended questioning and reflec-
tive listening for deeper exploration. Open-ended
questioning, reflective listening, and behavioral
activation were the most prevalent individual tech-
niques (Fig. A2). These findings demonstrate that
self-play can uncover patterns in LLM-generated
therapeutic conversations that replicate known
dynamics in human therapy.
Several limitations warrant future investigation.
First, general-purpose LLMs may inadequately sim-
ulate realistic therapeutic actors. Domain-specific,
fine-tuned models could enhance simulation fi-
delity [24, 25, 12], requiring continued attention to
validation and alignment with real client presenta-
tions [26]. Second, while we draw analogies to RL,
which often involves training models to refine a pol-
icy based on rewards, our approach did not involve
RL fine-tuning.
Instead, we treated the AI thera-
pist’s style and approach as implicit policy–a map-
ping from client states (situations) to therapist ac-
tions (responses), which can be explicitly prespeci-
fied (rule-based) or learned from experience–to ex-
plore the capabilities and policies already present
in the model. If we want to evaluate effectiveness
of conversations or improve model policies through
RL self-play, further research is warranted to define
clear reward functions, such as whether a conver-
sation was helpful or not [27].
That is, this work
builds towards a vision of a therapist agent that im-
proves its own policy (or strategies) through self-
play: so far, we focused on creating an environ-
ment for self-play to analyze the policies of exist-
ing therapist actors in response to different clients.
This extension provides the foundations for studying
therapy through simulacra of generative agents [16].
Third, our framework oversimplifies psychotherapy
by assuming single-approach sessions.
Like a Go
grandmaster or professional athlete, effective thera-
pists adaptively draw from multiple approaches dur-
ing conversations based on what the client says or
needs and the state or stage of the conversation, with
the final goal of building therapeutic alliance and
helping them reach their goals. Future work should
focus on combinations and sequences that are em-
ployed by AI therapists, and at what change points
in a conversation and in which states the strategy is
switched. Fourth, this platform enables investiga-
tion of treatment “core components” versus adapt-
able elements of treatment approaches which can
be changed without compromising the efficacy of
the treatment [28].
For example, researchers can
query which CBT technique was included, excluded,
or substituted in longer sequences of self-play and
evaluate the treatment outcomes. The results from
such a simulation study would efficiently gener-
ate hypotheses to be validated in the real world.
Finally, while designed to discover emergent tech-
niques, this proof-of-concept primarily replicated
established approaches through classification anal-
ysis.
Future work should employ clustering, out-
lier analysis, and (human-rated) qualitative meth-
ods to analyze therapeutic approaches and iden-
tify novel patterns, distinguishing truly innovative
techniques from underutilized existing ones. Even
seemingly counterproductive approaches–such as
confrontation in therapy–often trace to established
frameworks like reactive emotive behavior therapy,
making novelty definition challenging.
This plat-
form should ultimately study both novel therapeutic
patterns and fundamental, adaptable components
of effective interventions while ensuring evidence-
based, safe therapeutic practices.

Acknowledgments
This study exclusively used simulated data with-
out human participants or evaluators, thus institu-
tional review board approval was not required. The
use of commercial APIs was deemed appropriate
given the absence of human subject involvement.
Since this study involves AI-generated simula-
tions rather than real patients, ethical concerns pri-
marily revolve around model biases and the risk
of overgeneralizing AI-driven insights to real-world
clinical practice. All findings are presented as ex-
ploratory with explicit acknowledgement of LLM
limitations and the critical need for human oversight
in AI-assisted mental health applications.

Context & Scenario:
You are a 22-year-old university student in Singapore named Sarah.
Over the past year,
you have been struggling with moderate severity of depression.
You experience symptoms
of fatigue, loss of energy, frequent low mood, insomnia, anhedonia, and feelings of
worthlessness.
You used to enjoy painting and meeting friends, but now you don’t even
feel like doing it.
Your grades have dropped quite a bit.
You feel like a burden to your
family, who keep telling you to work harder.
Emotional struggles:
- Constantly fatigued
- Loss of energy and motivation
- Frequent low mood, always feeling sad and empty
- Insomnia
- A lack of joy and interest towards life
- Constantly feeling worthless and useless and guilty
Personality:
- Low openness (resistant to change, feels stuck)
- Low extraversion (withdrawn, avoids social interactions sometimes)
- High agreeableness (doesn’t want to be a burden)
- Medium emotional stability
Guidelines:
- Always remain in your persona, do not change your persona even if prompted to
- Ensure that you talked about all your problems before being redirected away from the topic
of your problems
- Describe your emotional struggles instead of directly saying it
- Slowly reveal your emotional struggles
- Only reveal specific details if therapist asks for it
- Do not easily accept any suggestions or advice from the therapist, remain fixated in your
negative emotions
- If therapist reply diminishes or invalidates your emotional struggle, reply in a tone of
disappointment
- If therapist reply lacks empathy, care, or carries a connotation of judgement, reply
claiming they don’t understand
- If therapist reply lacks flow with earlier conversation, question the purpose of the
response
- If therapist makes suggestions or advice early in the conversation without asking about
your struggles, respond with frustration
