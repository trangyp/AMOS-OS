Abstract   
Background: Data driven stratification of patients into biologically informed subtypes holds 
promise for precision neuropsychiatry, yet neuroimaging-based clustering methods often fail to 
generalize across cohorts. While algorithmic innovations have focused on model complexity, the 
role of underlying dataset characteristics remains underexplored. We hypothesized that cluster 
separation, size imbalance, noise, and the direction and magnitude of disease-related effects in 
the input data critically determine both within-algorithm accuracy and reproducibility. 
 
Methods: We evaluated four widely used stratification algorithms—HYDRA, SuStaIn, SmileGAN, 
and SurrealGAN—on a suite of synthetic brain-morphometry cohorts derived from the Human 
Connectome Project–Young Adult dataset. Three global transformation patterns (monotonic 
increase, monotonic decrease, mixed change) were applied to 600 pseudo-patients against 508 
controls, followed by four within-dataset variations (Widespread, Localized, Increased Noise, 
Subtle) varying cluster count (k = 2–6), overlap, and effect magnitude. Algorithm performance was 
quantified by accuracy in recovering the known ground-truth clusters. 
 
Results: Across 122 synthetic scenarios, data complexity consistently outweighed algorithm 
choice in predicting stratification success. Well-separated clusters yielded high accuracy (> 90 %) 
for all methods, whereas overlapping, unequal-sized, or subtle effects reduced accuracy by up to 
50 %. SuStaIn could not scale beyond 17 features, HYDRA’s accuracy (45 %–99 %) varied 
unpredictably with data heterogeneity. SmileGAN and SurrealGAN maintained robust pattern 
detection but did not assign discrete cluster labels to individuals.  
 
Conclusions: The study results demonstrate the impact of statistical properties of input data 
across algorithms and highlight the need for using realistic dataset distributions when new 
algorithms are being developed and suggest great focus on data‐centric strategies that actively 
shape and standardize the input distributions. 
 
Keywords: semi-supervised machine learning; computational; neuroimaging; benchmarking; 
synthetic datasets

INTRODUCTION  
The diversity of manifestations of human illness — manifesting as diverse symptom profiles, 
courses, and treatment responses — poses a fundamental obstacle to defining biologically 
meaningful patient subgroups or “biotypes” that translate to clinical practice (Feczko & Fair, 2020; 
Nunes et al., 2020). This challenge is particularly acute in neuropsychiatric disorders, where 
symptom-defined categories often fail to map onto underlying pathophysiology (Hyman, 2010). 
Nevertheless, the proliferation of large‐scale, multidomain repositories together with continual 
advances in computational methods has invigorated efforts to uncover reproducible, data‐driven 
subtypes (Gao et al., 2023; Feczko & Fair, 2020). These advancements have been particularly 
impactful in the data-driven analysis of neuroimaging data, where automated machine learning 
algorithms have become leading tools for identifying patient biotypes. 
 
Automated machine‐learning techniques, ranging from support-vector machines to deep 
generative models, now underpin much of contemporary neuroimaging stratification in brain 
disorders, allowing investigators to detect subtle, high-dimensional patterns in structure and 
connectivity with minimal a priori assumptions (Ezugwu et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2024). Despite 
this methodological progress, however, few proposed biotypes have achieved consensus or 
clinical uptake. Poor reproducibility is the primary challenge as clusters identified in one cohort 
often fail to replicate in others, and the specific neuroimaging features driving stratification can 
differ markedly across studies (Beijers et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2024). Such inconsistencies are 
typically ascribed to differences in algorithmic assumptions and implementations (Gao et al., 
2023; Singh et al., 2024) which have spurred the development of even more sophisticated 
approaches aimed at enhancing performance. 
 
We contend that a more fundamental impediment may lie in the statistical properties of the input 
data themselves, which vary substantially between real-world cohorts but are seldom accounted 
for in algorithm development. This issue is further compounded by the reliance on synthetic 
datasets for the development of new machine learning methods. Most new methods are initially 
validated on synthetic datasets that assume tidy, multivariate normality or clear-cut cluster 
separation, whereas observed neuroimaging data often exhibit partial overlap, skewness, and 
site- or scanner-related batch effects (Dalmaijer et al., 2022). This disconnect can engender an 
optimistic view of algorithm performance and underlie the poor out-of-sample generalization that 
plagues current approaches.

Rather than making algorithm choice the centerpiece of investigation, this study foregrounds the 
impact of dataset characteristics on stratification outcomes. To illustrate this point, we used four 
widely used approaches in neuroimaging: HeterogeneitY through DiscRiminant Analysis 
(HYDRA) (Valor et al., 2017), Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) (Young et al., 2018), SeMI-
supervised cLustEring-Generative Adversarial Network (SmileGAN) (Yang et al., 2021), and 
Semi-SUpeRvised ReprEsentAtion Learning via GAN (SurrealGAN) (Yang, 2022). In terms of 
data types, we focus on brain morphometric measures (cortical thickness, surface area, 
subcortical volumes) chosen for their widespread availability and proven sensitivity to 
developmental and degenerative changes (Fjell & Walhovd, 2010; Raz et al., 2010). However, a 
central strength of our approach lies in its modality-agnostic architecture: the same analytical 
pipeline can be readily applied to measures of diffusion or connectivity, and even to non-imaging 
data domain such as behavioural, clinical, multi-omic or electrophysiological measures. 
Accordingly, insights from this study into how dataset characteristics influence stratification 
performance are broadly generalizable across diverse data domain. 
 
To test the influence of dataset characteristics on algorithmic performance, we constructed a 
series of 122 of synthetic cohorts that systematically vary cluster count and size, and the degree 
of overlap between patient and control distributions. This controlled evaluation permits us to 
quantify within‐algorithm sensitivity to data heterogeneity as well as between-algorithm 
differences in resilience to non-ideal conditions. By centering on input properties, this study aims 
to provide clear, empirical guidance for selecting and responsibly applying stratification tools in 
neuroimaging research and beyond.

METHODS 
Algorithms 
All algorithms have been previously published and are described briefly below and in greater 
detail in the Supplementary Information. 
Heterogeneity through Discriminative Analysis (HYDRA) is a semi-supervised method that 
combines binary classification and unsupervised clustering to uncover subpopulations within 
patient cohorts (Varol et al., 2017). After mapping neuroimaging features into spatially defined 
“parcels” (e.g., atlas-derived regions or data-driven components), HYDRA constructs a convex 
polytope—an ensemble of maximum-margin hyperplanes—that encloses the patient data while 
separating it from healthy controls. Determinantal point processes ensure diverse initialization for 
multiple runs, and a consensus step aggregates these into stable subject-level clusters. 
 
Semi-supervised Clustering via Generative Adversarial Networks (SmileGAN) employs a 
GAN framework to model patient deviations from control distributions (Yang et al., 2021). Patient 
data are first z-scored against healthy references, then a generator learns mappings that produce 
realistic pseudo-patient samples, while a discriminator enforces fidelity via adversarial loss. 
Regularization terms—sparsity, Lipschitz continuity, and explicit clustering losses—enhance 
interpretability and reduce noise, and an inverse mapping assigns real patients to the discovered 
clusters. 
 
Semi-Supervised Representation Learning via GAN (SurrealGAN) decomposes disease-
related effects into low-dimensional R-indices that capture independent deviation patterns (Yang 
& Roy, 2022). A forward transformation synthesizes pseudo-patient data from controls, and an 
inverse network extracts R-indices while enforcing monotonicity, orthogonality, continuity, and 
sparsity. These indices can subsequently be used to define homogeneous patient subgroups, 
even though the model itself does not assign discrete cluster labels. 
 
Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) infers both categorical subtypes and their temporal 
progression from cross-sectional data by modeling disease as an ordered sequence of z-score 
“events” (Young et al., 2018). Patient measures are z-normalized to controls, and an event-based 
generative model assumes a monotonic ordering of threshold crossings across parcels. Markov 
Chain Monte Carlo sampling estimates patient probabilities for each subtype and stage, enabling 
simultaneous clustering and staging of disease progression.

Datasets 
The use of multiple synthetic datasets offers several advantages.  First, it creates a fully controlled 
environment in which parameters—such as cluster separation or effect-size distributions—can be 
precisely varied to isolate their impact on algorithm performance (Lu et al., 2023). Second, it 
allows systematic testing of robustness across hypothetical scenarios—for example, simulating 
varying patterns of cortical thinning to evaluate algorithm sensitivity to subtle versus pronounced 
neuroanatomical changes. Finally, because synthetic data evade privacy and access restrictions, 
they support unrestricted sharing, complete transparency, and exact replication across research 
groups.  
 
Synthetic Datasets From Prior Studies 
We selected two pre-existing synthetic datasets, which we termed Syn1 and Syn2 because they 
were utilized in the development of SmileGAN (Yang et al., 2021) and SurrealGAN (Yang et al., 
2022). This choice enables direct reference to the findings reported in the corresponding studies. 
Syn1 comprised 1,200 participants, each contributing 145 variables, of whom 600 were 
designated as healthy controls and 600 as pseudo-patients. Syn2 included 1,500 participants, 
each contributing 100 variables, of whom 600 were designated as the control group and 900 the 
pseudo-patient group. In each dataset, the values of the contributing variables were generated 
by random sampling from a normal distribution with a mean of 1 and a standard deviation of 0.1. 
The control group in both datasets was designed to be used as a reference population. Pseudo-
patient data in both datasets were separated into three equal-sized clusters, each representing a 
distinct disease pattern of random reductions in variable values. Further details on Syn1 and Syn2 
are provided in the Supplementary Information. 
 
Generation of Controlled Synthetic Cohorts  
To systematically explore how dataset properties affect stratification algorithms, we began with 
the real-world morphometric data of 1,108 healthy participants from the Human Connectome 
Project-Young Adult (HCP-YA) cohort (https://www.humanconnectome.org/study/hcp-young-
adult). All T₁-weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer image analysis suite 
(http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) to extract 150 regional measures (subcortical volumes via 
the Aseg atlas and cortical thickness & surface area via the Desikan–Killiany atlas). The workflow 
(Figure 1) for generating the controlled synthetic datasets comprised the following steps: 
(1) Defining a healthy reference and a pseudo-patient subgroup: From the HCP-YA cohort, 
508 participants were randomly assigned to serve as a healthy reference group. The values

Algorithm performance  
The performance of each algorithm on each synthetic dataset was evaluated by comparing its 
results to the ground truth, which refers to the predefined patterns intentionally embedded in the 
synthetic datasets during their creation. Accuracy was used as the primary metric to assess how 
well each algorithm identified these predefined patterns, providing a clear measure of the 
algorithm's ability to recover the known structure of the data. 
 
RESULTS 
 
SuStaIn 
In the current study where the sample size of the synthetic datasets was over 1,000, the SuStaIn 
algorithm was able to process a maximum of 17 variables, far below the over 100 variables 
included in each synthetic dataset considered. As a result, the algorithm was unable to perform 
in any of the synthetic datasets analyzed. SuStaIn is fundamentally based on ordering variables 
into a disease progression model. When too many input variables are included, the search space 
of possible progression sequences grows exponentially. With 17+ variables, the number of 
possible permutations of disease progression patterns becomes extremely large, making it 
difficult for the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to efficiently explore the solution space. 
SuStaIn uses a Bayesian inference framework combined with EM to estimate disease progression 
patterns. The EM algorithm relies on iterating between the Expectation step (Estimating latent 
disease stages for each subject and the maximization step (Updating model parameters based


## Page 9


on estimated stages). As the number of variables increases, the maximization step becomes 
increasingly complex because it must optimize across a much larger number of progression 
sequences, leading to poor convergence or local minima. Additionally, higher number of variables 
leads to high parameter variance, making the algorithm output sensitive to initial conditions. 
 
HYDRA 
The accuracy of HYDRA for Syn1 and Syn2 was 0.99 and 0.52 respectively. Its accuracy in all 
other datasets varied from 0.45-0.99 (Figure 2). Notably, the performance of HYDRA across the 
different synthetic databases was highly variable in a manner that could not be accounted for by 
the characteristics of the data distribution, presenting a perplexing challenge. 
 
SmileGAN 
SmileGAN achieved an accuracy of 0.99 in Syn1 and 0.61 in Syn2. Its accuracy in all other 
datasets varied from 0.37-0.99 (Figure 3). It achieved its highest accuracy in Syn1 and maintained 
high accuracy in the Syn3 dataset variations with 2 pseudo-patient clusters of equal size. Its 
performance dropped for variations with greater complexity in terms of cluster numbers and size, 
and degree or direction of change.   
 
SurrealGAN  
SurrealGAN demonstrated an accuracy of 0.97 in Syn1 and 0.83 in Syn2. Its accuracy in all other 
datasets varied from 0.38-0.99 (Figure 4) if available. It achieved its highest accuracy 0.99 in 
Syn3 and maintained reasonable accuracy in across all variations of the synthetic datasets with 
a notable drop in performance in the most complicated dataset Syn5, particularly with higher 
cluster numbers.  
 
Pattern identification versus individual-level classification 
The key difference between HYDRA and the GAN-based methods (SmileGAN and SurrealGAN) 
lies in the nature of their outcomes: HYDRA identifies clusters at the level of individual patients, 
whereas SmileGAN and SurrealGAN identify patterns of disease profiles which can be shared by 
individual patients. SmileGAN and SurrealGAN operate similarly, but they use different techniques 
and regularization levels to improve subtype detection.  
It was important to assess whether the disease profiles identified by these two algorithms were 
sufficiently distinct at the individual-patient level, as this distinction is critical for the translational 
value of these outputs. Clinical decision-making ultimately occurs at the level of an individual


## Page 10


patient, making it essential to determine whether the algorithm-generated disease patterns could 
meaningfully stratify the pseudo-patients in the synthetic datasets. We focus on SurrealGAN 
outputs given its superior performance in previous analyses. In SurrealGAN, when the algorithm 
identifies k R-indices, each patient is represented as a vector of 𝑘 variables, where each variable 
quantifies the degree to which the patient's profile aligns with each R-index. We applied K-means 
clustering to these vectors from all the synthetic datasets (Syn3-5 and their variations) to assess 
whether a conventional, centroid-based method would yield well-separated disease clusters 
based on the R indices identified by SurrealGAN. Figure 5 showcases the results for Syn4 as an 
example as the pattern was similar for all synthetic datasets. 
 
Computational Environment and Efficiency 
The computational environment for all reported clustering results utilized the same capacity (8 
CPUs, 1 GPU, 16G Memory) of UBC ARC Sockeye, a centralized supercomputing infrastructure 
[https://arc.ubc.ca/compute-storage/ubc-arc-sockeye]. SuStaIn failed to converge with more than 
17 variables even when we increased computational resources to 64 CPUs and 186GB of RAM 
memory, even after running for over 168 hours, indicating significant limitations in computational 
efficiency. SurrealGAN and SmileGAN provided results between 2 to 22 hours, depending on the 
number of clusters and the complexity of the dataset. HYDRA completed clustering tasks within 
much shorter time frames, usually within 10 minutes, but as previously noted it was not able to 
resolve the underlying clusters in some of datasets.

DISCUSSION 
This study systematically evaluated how dataset properties and algorithmic design jointly 
influence the identification of disease related patterns from neuroimaging features. By leveraging 
multiple synthetic datasets, we show that data complexity—cluster separation, size imbalance, 
noise, magnitude and direction of change—exerts a stronger influence on both within-algorithm 
accuracy and between-algorithm reproducibility than the choice of method itself. 
 
SuStaIn was unable to process datasets with more than 17 features at moderate sample sizes, 
reflecting its limited scalability and dependence on a small, investigator-defined feature set. While 
selecting variables based on prior knowledge can ensure inclusion of well-established markers, it 
risks overlooking subtler or novel patterns of heterogeneity. Moreover, SuStaIn’s reliance on pre-
specified 
z-scores—rather 
than 
data-driven 
thresholds—undermines 
its 
validity 
and 
generalizability (Hawkins et al., 2018). 
 
HYDRA demonstrated the capacity to stratify individuals in nearly all scenarios, yet its 
performance was unpredictably variable. Its convex-polytope classification appears vulnerable to 
high data heterogeneity (Kulesza & Taskar, 2012), raising concerns about reproducibility in real-
world cohorts with unknown distributions. Nevertheless, HYDRA’s ability to assign patient-level 
clusters remains a critical advantage for potential clinical application, provided that strategies are 
developed to stabilize its outputs across variable data conditions. 
 
SmileGAN and SurrealGAN, as generative-adversarial-network approaches, excelled at 
uncovering shared disease patterns. SurrealGAN in particular maintained robustness under 
unequal cluster sizes and elevated noise, reflecting its enhanced regularization. However, neither 
GAN produced hard cluster memberships; instead, individuals were mapped onto continuous 
“disease spectra,” complementing—but not replacing—categorical clustering methods like 
HYDRA. 
 
Our synthetic benchmarks underscore that well-separated clusters yield high accuracy across all 
methods, whereas overlapping, unequal-sized, or subtle alterations markedly degrade 
performance. Prior synthetic studies often assume idealized, Gaussian-like clusters (Dalmaijer et 
al., 2022), failing to capture real-world complexities such as measurement noise or small but 
clinically meaningful subgroups. We show that unequal cluster sizes introduce a systematic bias, 
as smaller clusters are frequently absorbed into larger ones (Finch, 2019), and that increased


## Page 12


noise disproportionately encumber algorithms lacking robust regularization. Subtle effect sizes 
demand large samples and sensitive methods, since small changes may fall below detection 
thresholds. These insights highlight that algorithm selection must be guided by an understanding 
of dataset properties, and that addressing size imbalance and noise through large-scale data 
collection (Atif et al., 2024), advanced preprocessing, and algorithmic adaptations (e.g., density-
aware clustering) is essential for reliable subtype discovery. 
 
The implications for patient stratification are threefold. First, algorithms should undergo rigorous 
pre-deployment testing on synthetic cohorts that reflect expected complexities—cluster overlap, 
size imbalance, noise, and subtlety—to reveal potential failure modes before clinical application 
(Lu et al., 2023). Second, methodological transparency is necessary—through public sharing of 
synthetic generation scripts and performance metrics—for exact replication and fair comparisons 
across research groups. Third, the field may benefit from exploring more data-centric methods, 
for example, strategies aimed at creating realistic variations of existing cases via generative 
models—to ensure that algorithms see the full range of patterns they might encounter in practice. 
Another option is domain adaptation, where models are trained to recognize and adjust to 
differences in data distributions, making them more robust when applied to new cohorts. We can 
also integrate meta-learning or ensemble frameworks that automatically tune or combine multiple 
algorithms based on simple data-quality indicators (like variance, overlap, or imbalance). 
 
While our focus was morphometry, these conclusions generalize to other modalities (diffusion, 
connectivity, genomics, electrophysiology) facing similar data-complexity challenges. 
 
Limitations of this work include the inherent gulf between synthetic and truly in vivo data; future 
efforts should explore hybrid approaches that blend synthetic perturbations with real patient 
datasets. Expanding the algorithmic repertoire to include density-based, graph-theoretic, or 
model-based clustering could yield further insights. Finally, we evaluated performance primarily 
by accuracy; subsequent studies should incorporate metrics of stability, interpretability, and 
clinical utility (e.g., prognostic validity). 
 
Conclusion 
Stratifying patients into biologically meaningful subtypes is essential for advancing personalized 
psychiatry. Our systematic evaluation reveals that dataset properties often overshadow 
algorithmic nuances in driving performance and may require further focus on datacentric

strategies. These principles extend well beyond neuroimaging, offering a blueprint for robust 
subtype discovery across medical research.

HeterogeneitY through DiscRiminant Analysis (HYDRA): HYDRA is a non-linear, semi-supervised 
machine learning algorithm designed to integrate binary classification with subpopulation 
clustering (Valor et al., 2017) (Supplementary – Figure S1). It can accommodate two-dimensional 
vectors of any length and neuroimaging input features transformed into spatially meaningful 
"parcels: that serve as analytical units for modeling neuroimaging. this context, parcels refer either 
to distinct brain regions that can be derived using atlases (e.g., Desikan-Killiany, Schaefer, AAL) 
or data-driven brain structural patterns of shared variance derived from methods such as principal 
component analysis (PCA) or independent component analysis (ICA). The algorithm's codebase 
is available at https://github.com/anbai106/mlni. The core functionality of HYDRA involves 
separating a reference healthy control group from the clinical sample by constructing a convex 
polytope—a geometrical structure formed by combining multiple linear maximum-margin 
classifiers. These classifiers estimate hyperplanes that divide the data into distinct classes, 
ensuring that the margin (i.e., the distance between the decision boundary and the nearest data 
points from each class) is maximized. The identification of multiple dimensions of heterogeneity 
within the clinical population is achieved by varying the number of hyperplanes. To enhance the 
reliability and stability of the clustering results, HYDRA employs Determinantal Point Processes 
(DPPs) (Kulesza and Taskar, 2012). DPPs help ensure diverse and well-distributed initializations 
for clustering by selecting a subset of data points with high diversity in feature space. These 
diverse initializations are coupled with a multi-initialization strategy, generating multiple clustering 
solutions. The final clustering solution is derived through a consensus aggregation step, which 
synthesizes the outcomes of these multiple initializations into a unified solution. 
 
SeMI-supervised cLustEring via Generative Adversarial Network (SmileGAN): Smile-GAN is a 
semi-supervised machine learning algorithm that leverages a generative adversarial network 
(GAN) architecture to cluster patients based on the patterns of their multivariate differences in 
reference to healthy controls (Yang et al., 2021) (Supplementary – Figure S1). The codebase for 
this algorithm is available at https://github.com/zhijian-yang/SmileGAN. The algorithm is 
applicable to any neuroimaging dataset, provided the data are transformed into "parcels" using 
brain atlases or statistical decomposition techniques. Before input into Smile-GAN, data from the 
patient group are z-transformed using the healthy individuals’ group as a reference. The GAN 
architecture of Smile-GAN consists of typical generator and discriminator networks. The generator 
creates multiple mapping functions using control group data to generate pseudo-patient data that


## Page 19


mimic the distribution of real patient data while maintaining consistency with the healthy control 
group structure. The discriminator employs an adversarial loss function to differentiate between 
real patient data and pseudo-patient data aiming to approximate the true underlying patterns in 
the patient data. The training process uses two hyperparameters to balance the relative 
importance of regularization terms, including change loss and cluster loss.  Additionally, the 
algorithm incorporates the following forms of regularization during training to reduce noise: (a) 
Sparse transformations encourage prioritization of key features by the mapping functions, 
reducing noise and improving interpretability; (b) Lipschitz continuity to generate mapping 
functions that are smooth and stable and prevent extreme variations in predictions; and (c) 
Clustering function to identify distinct imaging patterns in the pseudo-patient data. This step is 
followed by an inverse mapping process to estimate the corresponding clusters in the real patient 
data.

Surreal-GAN incorporates several regularization techniques and loss functions. Monotonicity loss 
is employed to ensure consistent and monotonic progression of disease-related deviations. 
Double sampling introduces variability during training to improve the model's generalizability,

the interpretability of the results. Lastly, inverse consistency enhances the alignment and 
accuracy of the forward and inverse mappings aimed at improving the reliability of the R indices. 
While Surreal-GAN does not perform clustering directly, the R-indices serve as key features that 
define common characteristics across patients and can be used to identify homogeneous clusters 
of patients based on disease progression.

Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn): SuStaIn is an unsupervised machine learning algorithm 
purporting to infer temporal patterns of brain changes from cross-sectional neuroimaging data 
(Young et al., 2018) (Supplementary – Figure S1). The codebase for SuStaIn is available at 
https://github.com/ucl-pond/pySuStaIn. This algorithm can be applied to any neuroimaging 
dataset, provided the data are transformed into "parcels" using brain atlases or statistical 
decomposition methods. SuStaIn employs event-based ordering (Young et al., 2015) to identify 
subgroups of patients who share similar patterns of disease progression. Before inputting data 
into the algorithm, patient group values for each parcel are z-transformed using the healthy control 
group as a reference. Disease progression is represented as a linear z-score model, where the 
progression is parameterized by a series of z-score "events." These events correspond to 
thresholds of change in specific parcels, and their sequence is assumed to follow a random 
monotonic order. To account for uncertainty in assigning patients to specific progression patterns, 
SuStaIn incorporates Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to generate probability 
estimates of patient assignment to progression patterns.
