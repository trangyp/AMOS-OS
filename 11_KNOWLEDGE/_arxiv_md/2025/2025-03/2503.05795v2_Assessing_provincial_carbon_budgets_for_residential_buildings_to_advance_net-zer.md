Abstract 
Assessing provincial carbon budgets for residential building operations is a crucial strategy for 
advancing China’s net-zero ambitions. This study develops an advanced assessment framework that 
integrates static and dynamic modeling to evaluate emission pathways and carbon budgets for 
residential buildings at the provincial level. The framework provides a practical tool for generating 
robust carbon budget estimates with limited data, thereby enhancing its applicability for policy-
oriented planning. Findings show that (1) in the business-as-usual scenario, the emissions for urban 
and rural residential buildings are projected to peak at 990 (±0.7) and 450 (±0.2) mega-tons of 
carbon dioxide (MtCO2), respectively, with peak years occurring in 2031 (±4.7) and 2026 (±2.6). 
(2) In the decarbonization scenario, peak emissions decrease to 900 MtCO2 and 430 MtCO2 for 
urban and rural buildings, respectively. (3) The provinces with the highest emission reduction 
requirements are Henan (16.74 MtCO2), Xinjiang (12.59 MtCO2), Gansu (9.87 MtCO2), Hebei (8.46 
MtCO2), and Guangdong (3.37 MtCO2), with Northwest China shouldering the greatest reduction 
responsibility, totaling 38.14 MtCO2. In conclusion, this study provides a dynamically optimized 
carbon budget assessment tool for residential buildings, offering valuable insights for government 
policy-making and playing a key role in facilitating the low-carbon transition of China’s building 
sector during the pre-2030 planning period, ultimately contributing to the goal of achieving net-zero 
emissions in the building sector by mid-century.

1. Introduction 
The global building sector accounts for over one-third of both carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions and 
energy consumption [1]. CO2 emissions from building operations have increased by about 5% 
[approximately 10 giga-tons (Gt) of CO2] since 2020, reaching historic highs [2]. Despite 
concerning levels of emissions in the building sector, the substantial potential for energy savings 
and emissions reduction still exists [3]. Specifically, the energy savings and emission-reduction 
potential of the building sector can reach up to nearly 74%, which is greater than that of the industrial 
and transportation sectors [4]. In 2020, China’s building operations generated a total of 2.16 GtCO2 
in carbon emissions, accounting for 21% of global building carbon emissions (ranking first globally) 
[5]. Given the current trends, energy use and carbon emissions for the building sector in China are 
projected to continue rising [6]. Notably, residential buildings account for over 60% of energy use 
and nearly half of the emissions in the building sector [7]. Furthermore, recent evidence indicates 
that residential buildings have greater potential and cost-effectiveness for enhancing energy 
efficiency and reducing emissions than nonresidential buildings [8]. Hence, to achieve the net zero 
goal early, China should focus on decarbonizing residential buildings. 
While existing studies have developed a framework for assessing energy use and emissions for 
buildings, aiming at the macro-national scale [9, 10]. It is worth noting that significant regional 
disparities in energy use and emissions exist across provinces in China’s building sector, driven by 
variations in economic development [11], climatic conditions [12], energy structures [13], and 
policy implementation [14]. Therefore, developing a carbon budget assessment scheme across 
provinces is crucial. In particular, there is a relative paucity of studies on optimizing carbon peaks 
and formulating allocation schemes for residential buildings at the provincial level. To bridge these 
gaps, this study explores the following questions: 
• What are the national trends in operational carbon emissions from residential buildings? 
• How do the peaks of carbon emissions from building operations differ across provinces? 
• How can the carbon budget be allocated to each province to maximize decarbonization potential? 
To address these challenges, this study develops a carbon emission model for residential 
buildings in urban and rural China, grounded in the Kaya identity. Two static scenarios—business-

as-usual and decarbonization—are used to analyze the emission peaks of residential buildings in 
China and each province through 2060. Monte Carlo simulation is then employed for a dynamic 
analysis of these two scenarios, predicting the emission-peaking probabilities for urban and rural 
residential buildings. Furthermore, an optimized assessment approach for provincial carbon budgets 
is introduced, targeting maximum emission reductions in support of the net-zero goal. 
The key contribution of this study is the development of a practical tool for generating robust 
provincial carbon budget estimates for residential buildings under conditions of limited data. 
Methodologically, the study advances the field by integrating a static-dynamic modeling framework 
with optimization under net-zero targets, while explicitly capturing the distinct emission 
characteristics of urban and rural buildings and the heterogeneity across provinces. Specifically, the 
framework dynamically optimizes peaking values and peaking years for each province, ultimately 
producing a carbon budget allocation scheme. This scheme provides a scientific basis for setting 
actionable emission targets at the provincial level and supports the decarbonization of the residential 
building sector.

dynamic scenario analyses of emission peaks in residential buildings, Section 5 examines the 
optimal carbon budget allocation for each province, along with related policy recommendations, 
and Section 6 concludes the study with key findings.

2. Literature review 
Studies on predicting building carbon emission peaks are primarily divided into bottom-up and top-
down methods [15]. The bottom-up methods are generally categorized into statistical and 
engineering models. Statistical models [16, 17] mainly use regression techniques, while engineering 
models [18, 19] estimate energy consumption by calculating mathematical relationships based on 
building characteristics and energy equipment parameters. The top-down approach primarily 
considers macroeconomic energy-sector factors to estimate energy demand and carbon emissions 
[20]. Top-down approaches are typically represented by models such as the Kaya identity [21], the 
Impact, Population, Affluence, Technology model [22, 23], and the environmental Kuznets curve 
model [24]. Among these, the Kaya identity offers high universality across countries, provinces, 
and sectors [25, 26]. Several studies have enhanced top-down models with scenario analysis and 
uncertainty quantification. For example, combining the Impact, Population, Affluence, Technology 
models with scenario analysis [27, 28], index decomposition [29], and Monte Carlo simulations [30, 
31] enables a more comprehensive assessment of future emissions. While these approaches have 
provided systematic insights, they have largely been applied at the national scale and often overlook 
spatial heterogeneity, dynamic uncertainties, and sector-specific characteristics of buildings. 
At the subnational scale, studies have begun to examine provincial variations in building 
emissions, reflecting differences in population structure, urbanization, and energy mix. However, 
most have been conducted within a static framework, relying on deterministic models with limited 
capacity to capture the stochastic characteristics of emissions [32, 33]. In addition, few studies 
distinguish between urban and rural residential buildings, constraining the understanding of sectoral 
heterogeneity. Research focusing specifically on provincial residential emissions often lacks 
explicit connections to practical carbon budget or allocation decisions, thereby reducing its policy 
relevance [34]. These gaps underscore the need for models that are both spatially explicit and 
probabilistically robust. 
Studies explicitly focusing on carbon budget allocation for residential buildings remain 
relatively scarce. Although some research has addressed the industry and transportation sectors [35, 
36], far fewer have examined how emission reduction responsibilities should be distributed across


## Page 8

provinces or building sub-sectors to meet national decarbonization targets. Moreover, only a limited 
number of studies have combined top-down static models with dynamic simulation methods to 
assess emission peaks under uncertainty—a crucial step for reliable provincial-level planning and 
the development of equitable carbon allocation schemes. 
From this review, three major research gaps are identified. First, existing models seldom 
integrate spatial granularity with stochastic methods, constraining their ability to analyze 
uncertainties in provincial residential emissions. Second, urban-rural differences in building energy 
use and emissions are often overlooked, despite their pronounced divergence. Third, systematic 
frameworks for allocating provincial carbon reduction responsibilities within the residential sector 
remain underdeveloped. To address these gaps, this study integrates the Kaya identity with Monte 
Carlo simulation to capture inter-provincial heterogeneity, urban-rural distinctions, and 
uncertainties in emission peaking, thereby offering a robust and policy-relevant framework for 
provincial emission planning. The contributions of this study are as follows. 
• 
This work integrates the static Kaya identity with the dynamic Monte Carlo simulation 
to estimate emission trends for residential buildings in rural and urban areas. Specifically, 
an emission model based on the Kaya identity is developed to estimate emission peaks for 
China and each province under the two scenarios. The Monte Carlo simulation is employed to 
incorporate parameter uncertainty, dynamically estimating the probability of emission peaks 
in urban and rural residential buildings across China. This integrated framework enhances both 
the accuracy of emission calculations and the robustness of scenario projections, providing 
deeper insights to support policy design. 
• 
This work is pioneering in proposing an optimized provincial carbon budget assessment 
scheme for residential buildings, under the premise of achieving net-zero emissions by 
2060. The scheme is developed based on the principle of maximizing emission reduction 
potential, while taking into account the practical emissions in each province. By dynamically 
optimizing the peaking values and peaking years for each province, this study provides 
essential data support for establishing provincial-level emission reduction targets and 
advancing the achievement of carbon neutrality.

To capture the dynamic evolution of carbon emissions, two scenarios were developed: a 
business-as-usual scenario and a decarbonization scenario. The key distinction between them lies in 
assumptions about energy intensity and energy structure. In the business-as-usual scenario, energy 
intensity followed the baseline trajectory. In contrast, the decarbonization scenario assumed that 
urban residential buildings would achieve a 30% reduction in energy intensity by 2060 relative to 
2020 levels, while growth in rural residential energy intensity would be substantially moderated. 
Regarding energy structure, the decarbonization scenario featured significantly higher 
electrification rates and larger shares of self-generated energy compared with the business-as-usual 
scenario.

Fig. 1 illustrates the emission patterns of residential buildings from 2021 to 2060 under a static

scenario (Fig. 1 a), urban residential buildings peaked in 2023 at 900 MtCO2, and rural residential 
buildings (Fig. 1 b) peaked at 420 MtCO2 in 2021. Consequently, under the decarbonization scenario, 
both building types were projected to peak in 2025. 
Achieving the parameter settings of the decarbonization scenario will support the attainment 
of the 2060 carbon neutrality target. The decarbonization scenario presents a more ambitious 
potential for building decarbonization in comparison with the business-as-usual scenario. It assumes 
higher energy efficiency levels in buildings and a greater proportion of renewable energy in the 
electricity mix. Additionally, this study defines the variation range for each parameter in the 
emission model using a normal distribution with a ± 1-3 standard deviation change. These variations 
form error bands at different probability levels in the dynamic scenario simulation, representing the 
potential emission range for urban and rural residential buildings under varying probabilities.

5.1. Probabilities of residential buildings toward a decarbonization pathway 
Fig. 6 presents the probabilistic analysis results of peak carbon emissions under decarbonization 
scenarios for two building types in China. For urban residential buildings, the probability of 
reaching the peak in 2023 under the decarbonization scenario was 3.386%, with a 0.021% chance

MtCO2, a reduction of 90 MtCO2 in comparison with the business-as-usual scenario. Similarly, for 
rural residential buildings, the probability of reaching the peak in 2021 under the decarbonization 
scenario was 0.954%, with a 0.003% chance of achieving the peaking values interval. The 
corresponding peak emissions were estimated at 420 MtCO2, indicating a reduction of 30 MtCO2 in 
comparison with the business-as-usual scenario. Overall, the analysis presented in Sections 4.1, 4.2, 
and 5.1, illustrating operational carbon trends, particularly carbon peaks, in residential buildings

a. 
Addressing regional disparities and enabling an equitable transition. Northwest China 
faces the highest emission reduction responsibilities, yet its relatively weaker fiscal capacity, 
institutional readiness, and socioeconomic conditions make large-scale transitions especially 
challenging. Achieving a just and feasible pathway therefore requires a comprehensive support 
framework that integrates both financial and technical measures: (i) targeted central transfers, 
concessional green lending, and bond guarantees to reduce the cost of capital [46]; (ii) inter-
provincial technology cooperation tailored to cold-arid climates, including advanced building 
envelopes, efficient heating systems, and adaptive design standards  [47]; (iii) systematic 
capacity-building for local governments and small and medium-sized enterprises to strengthen 
implementation and enforcement capacity [48]; and (iv) leveraging the region’s abundant 
renewable energy resources to provide low-carbon electricity and accelerate heat pump 
adoption [49]. Together, these measures can align socioeconomic feasibility with technical 
viability, ensuring that emission reductions in less-developed regions contribute equitably to 
national decarbonization goals. 
b. 
Tailored provincial strategies aligned with measured potentials. The provinces with the 
highest emission reduction responsibilities are Henan (16.74 MtCO2), Xinjiang (12.59 MtCO2), 
Gansu (9.87 MtCO2), Hebei (8.46 MtCO2), and Guangdong (3.37 MtCO2), requiring 
differentiated pathways tailored to their respective contexts. In Henan, the focus should be on 
large-scale retrofits of existing multi-family buildings, upgrading envelope insulation, 
airtightness, and windows, alongside the acceleration of efficient district heating operations, 
digital controls, and widespread deployment of heat pumps and clean cooking technologies 
[50]. For Xinjiang, emission reductions must follow a cold-arid urban pathway that 
emphasizes high-performance envelopes, building-level heat recovery, advanced metering and 
control systems, and staged conversion from coal-based to renewable or electric heating; 
dedicated green-finance facilities and performance-based contracts are essential to address 
fiscal and institutional constraints [51]. Hebei requires a dual-track strategy: in urban areas, 
stricter enforcement of building energy codes, district system upgrades, and demand-response 
integration to manage peak loads; in rural areas, promotion of clean heating technologies such


## Page 28


28
as air-source heat pumps or biomass-gasification, coupled with rooftop photovoltaics, storage, 
and standardized low-cost retrofits, supported by coordination with industrial transition 
programs [52]. Gansu’s rural sector requires a “low-cost deep-impact” package, combining 
prefabricated insulation kits, solar thermal water heating, efficient stoves, village-level 
microgrids, and targeted subsidies paired with results-based financing, while simultaneously 
building local capacity through installer and auditor training [53]. Finally, Guangdong can 
leverage its stronger fiscal base to pioneer smart home energy management, comprehensive 
retrofits, and photovoltaic generation combined with energy storage integration, supported by 
green mortgages, property assessed clean energy type mechanisms, and provincial product and 
service catalogs to accelerate diffusion [54]. 
c. 
Phased implementation consistent with feasibility and capacity. Short term (to 2030): 
Prioritize pilots in high-potential provinces, strengthen compliance with existing energy codes, 
and deploy “quick-win” retrofits such as efficient lighting, the heating, ventilation, and air 
conditioning (HVAC) controls, and basic envelope improvements. At the same time, establish 
provincial-level green finance facilities and standardized energy service company contracts 
[55]. Medium term (2030-2045): Scale up comprehensive retrofits, accelerate electrification 
of space and water heating, expand rooftop photovoltaics and community renewable projects, 
and reinforce distribution networks. Institutionalize results-based subsidies to ensure verified 
energy savings [56]. Long term (2045-2060): Complete the transition to net-zero-ready 
residential buildings through widespread adoption of digital energy management systems, high 
renewable penetration, and integrated storage solutions. Sustained fiscal transfers and 
technical support will be essential to ensure that less-developed regions achieve parity in 
service quality and keep pace with the national decarbonization trajectory [57]. 
d. 
Optimizing urban planning, revitalizing existing building stock, and avoiding large-scale 
demolitions are critical strategies for reducing carbon emissions in the built environment. 
Improved urban planning and infrastructure can enhance supporting services such as 
healthcare, education, and transportation while lowering vacancy rates and preventing carbon 
lock-in caused by poor planning and long building lifespans [58]. Upgrading existing buildings


## Page 29


29
and promoting green renovation models further reduce the demand for new construction and 
mitigate energy use linked to the urban heat island effect [59]. At the same time, stricter 
controls on super high-rise projects and building heights are recommended to limit operational 
energy consumption from elevators and water supply systems [60]. Urban renewal should 
emphasize preservation and renovation over demolition, supported by robust evaluation 
mechanisms, clear standards, and practical implementation guidelines. Together, these 
measures can ensure low-carbon development while safeguarding the quality and functionality 
of urban spaces. 
e. 
Promoting intelligent control technologies and improving the energy efficiency of 
building equipment are key strategies for reducing energy consumption in the building sector. 
This study recommends optimizing the operation and regulation of major energy-intensive 
systems, such as air conditioning and lighting [61]. It further advocates for the large-scale 
adoption of high-efficiency HVAC systems, light-emitting diode lighting, heat pumps, and 
other advanced energy-saving technologies. In addition, strengthening mandatory efficiency 
labeling standards and encouraging product designs that prioritize energy savings are essential. 
While high-efficiency equipment often carries higher costs due to auxiliary features [62], 
restricting low-efficiency appliances from entering the market could incentivize manufacturers 
to transition toward low-carbon product design and manufacturing. 
f. 
Improving subsidy mechanisms for energy-saving technologies, refining energy market 
pricing, and strengthening green finance are essential to enhance the economic viability of 
low-carbon technologies while increasing the relative costs of carbon-intensive products and 
services [63]. These measures can help overcome behavioral lock-in, facilitate the adoption of 
energy-saving initiatives, and promote emission reductions in residential buildings. 
Accordingly, this study recommends assessing the potential and cost-effectiveness of low-
carbon technologies, identifying key household products and services, and establishing a 
promotion directory focused on green buildings and renewable energy installations. A 
diversified subsidy framework—combining financial grants, concessional loans, and tax 
incentives—should be complemented by innovative green financial instruments developed by

energy-saving services industry should be expanded through energy performance contracting 
models (e.g., guaranteed savings and shared savings) as well as one-stop integrated services 
covering consulting, diagnostics, planning, financing, retrofitting, and operational 
management.

6. Conclusion 
This study evaluated the carbon emissions from residential building operations in China through 
2060 and proposed a dynamically optimized provincial carbon budget scheme to facilitate carbon 
neutrality. Specifically, it developed an emission model for residential buildings from the national 
to the provincial level, projected emission peaks in China and each province under both the business-
as-usual and decarbonization scenarios, and estimated the probabilities of emission peaks in urban 
and rural China using Monte Carlo simulation. Furthermore, an optimized provincial carbon budget 
assessment scheme was proposed to maximize emission reductions and advance the net-zero goal. 
The principle findings are summarized below.  
6.1. Principal findings 
⚫ 
 Under the business-as-usual scenario, national urban residential buildings were 
projected to peak at 990 (±0.7) MtCO2 in 2031 (±4.7 years), while rural residential 
buildings were expected to peak at 450 (±0.2) MtCO2 in 2026 (±2.6 years). Within the 
business-as-usual scenario, Shandong exhibited the highest peaking value for urban residential 
buildings at 110 MtCO2, while Hainan showed the lowest peaking value at 2.84 MtCO2. For 
rural residential buildings, Hebei was projected to reach the highest peaking value at 57.2 
MtCO2, whereas Shanghai would have the lowest peaking value at 1.15 MtCO2. 
⚫ 
 Under the decarbonization scenario, the peak value for urban residential buildings was 
estimated at 900 MtCO2, representing a reduction of 90 MtCO2 compared to the 
business-as-usual scenario. The probability of urban residential buildings reaching this peak 
in 2023 was 3.386%, with a 0.021% probability of achieving the peak value interval. Rural 
residential buildings were projected to peak at 420 MtCO2 in the decarbonization scenario, 
reflecting a reduction of 30 MtCO2 relative to the business-as-usual scenario. The probability 
of rural residential buildings reaching their peak in 2021 was 0.954%, with a 0.003% 
probability of achieving the peak value interval. 
⚫ 
The key contributors to emission reduction needs included Henan, Xinjiang, Gansu, 
Hebei, and Guangdong, with Northwest China collectively bearing the greatest


## Page 32


32
responsibility. For urban residential buildings, the provinces with the highest emission 
reduction potential were Henan (16.74 MtCO2), Xinjiang (12.59 MtCO2), and Hebei (8.46 
MtCO2), while those with the lowest potential were Hainan (0.43 MtCO2), Shanghai (0.51 
MtCO2), and Tianjin (0.57 MtCO2). For rural residential buildings, the provinces requiring the 
most significant emission reductions were Gansu (9.87 MtCO2), Hebei (4.86 MtCO2), and 
Guangdong (3.37 MtCO2), while the lowest demands were observed in Shanghai (0.07 MtCO2), 
Ningxia (0.15 MtCO2), and Qinghai (0.19 MtCO2). 
6.2. Upcoming works 
Several limitations of this study warrant further investigation. This study focused solely on 
estimating operational-phase energy use and associated emissions of residential buildings. However, 
considering the full life cycle emissions of residential buildings would provide a more 
comprehensive assessment. Additionally, the primary aim of predicting emission trends for 
residential buildings in urban and rural China is to support the net-zero target. Therefore, assessing 
the decarbonization trends for other building types, such as commercial buildings, is also crucial. 
Furthermore, as buildings progressively decarbonize, incorporating a cost-benefit analysis when 
implementing decarbonization measures becomes essential.
