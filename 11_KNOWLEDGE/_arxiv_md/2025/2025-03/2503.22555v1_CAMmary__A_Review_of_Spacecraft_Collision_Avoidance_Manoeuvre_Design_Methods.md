Abstract
Ensuring safety for spacecraft operations has become a paramount concern due to the pro-
liferation of space debris and the saturation of valuable orbital regimes. In this regard, the
Collision Avoidance Manoeuvre (CAM) has emerged as a critical requirement for spacecraft
operators, aiming to eﬃciently navigate through potentially hazardous encounters. Currently,
when a conjunction is predicted, operators dedicate a considerable amount of time and re-
sources to designing a CAM. Given the increased frequency of conjunctions, autonomous
computation of fuel-eﬃcient CAMs is crucial to reduce costs and improve the performance
of future operations. To facilitate the transition to an autonomous CAM design, it is useful
to provide an overview of its state-of-the-art. In this survey article, a collection of the most
relevant research contributions in the ﬁeld is presented. We review and categorize existing
CAM techniques based on their underlying principles, such as (i) analytic, semi-analytic,
or numerical solutions; (ii) impulsive or continuous thrust; (iii) deterministic or stochastic
approaches, (iv) free or ﬁxed manoeuvring time; (v) free or ﬁxed thrust direction. Finally,
to determine the validity of the algorithms potentially implementable for autonomous use,
we perform a numerical comparison on a large set of conjunctions. With this analysis, the
algorithms are evaluated in terms of computational eﬃciency, accuracy, and optimality of the
computed policy. Through this comprehensive survey, we aim to provide insights into the
state-of-the-art CAM methodologies, identify gaps in current research, and outline poten-
tial directions for future developments in ensuring the safety and sustainability of spacecraft
operations in increasingly congested orbital environments.

a signiﬁcant challenge for spacefaring nations. In 1978, Donald Kessler [1] postulated that

The Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and Geostationary Orbit (GEO) regions are
particularly concerning, as they are becoming increasingly crowded due to the rising number
of operational satellites, including micro-satellites and large constellations like Starlink.
Among the international community, growing concerns about the potentially catastrophic
consequences of an unchecked evolution of the space population have led to the development
of the broader concepts of Space Situational Awareness (SSA)1 – which encompasses Space
Surveillance and Tracking (SST) – and Space Traﬃc Management (STM).2 The main ele-
ments that characterize STM practices are space debris mitigation and remediation. While
remediation aims at actively reducing the number of space debris in orbit, mitigation involves
measures to prevent the creation of new space debris. Common mitigation strategies include
the intelligent design of satellite and rocket bodies, the implementation of end-of-life plans,
and the actuation of Conjunction Analysis (CA) and COLlision Avoidance (COLA) routines
when a close approach is foreseen. In the aftermath of the 2009 collision between Iridium
33 and COSMOS 2251 [4], which highlighted deﬁciencies in collision assessment and com-
munication, coordination among all entities involved in SSA was initiated. The UN [5], the
Inter-Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee (IADC),3 the Orbital Debris Mitigation
Standard Practices (ODMSP),4 and the [6] have been crucial promoters of international co-
operation on space debris mitigation, providing common guidelines to global space mission
design standards. Moreover, since 2004, ESA has provided Debris Risk Assessment and Mit-
igation Analysis (DRAMA), a specialized software tool for the compliance analysis of a space
mission with space debris mitigation standards [7].
Reducing the risk of a collision fundamentally breaks down into two consecutive practices:
CA and COLA, which, according to the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronau-
tics (AIAA), are the top priority for the success of STM operations [8]. The conjunction
screening process serves as a critical preliminary gatekeeper to CA, identifying potentially
dangerous conjunctions for further analysis. The earliest and most extensive screening pro-
cess for all orbiting objects is carried out by the U.S. Space Force, which performs All-vs-All
propagations every 24 hours to check for potential close encounters [9]. When a close ap-
proach is detected, a Conjunction Data Message (CDM) [10] is included in the Space-Track
catalogue5 and the operator(s) of the involved spacecraft is/are notiﬁed. CDMs serve as prox-
imity alerts rather than collision warnings, requiring further risk assessment to determine if

been performed manually by operators [15]. Even with the aid of the previously mentioned
tools, this process requires a large number of work hours and 24/7 availability of specialized
experts [16].
In this context, the rise of satellite mega-constellations like Starlink has increased the
frequency of CAMs. SpaceX, for instance, employs a conservative manoeuvre threshold of
10−5 on Probability of Collision (PoC), averaging approximately 12 manoeuvres per satellite
yearly [17]. Analysis of these constellations reveals a strong correlation between manoeuvre
frequency and risk reduction.
A human-in-the-loop approach to the CA/COLA process
will soon become unsustainable from an economical and practical standpoint. Introducing
autonomous decision-making and actuation will become, therefore, fundamental in shaping
future operational pipelines. This might be the main reason for the signiﬁcant rise in works
related to CAM presented at the largest aerospace conference, the International Astronautical
Congress (IAC). The number of CAM-focused contributions increased from 36 in 2021 to 56
in 2023 and further to 93 in 2024.
Autonomous operations are characterized by the ability to perform tasks and make deci-
sions independently without real-time human intervention. A key distinction can be drawn
between ground-based autonomous systems and onboard autonomous ones. In the former,
the whole CDM-reading, CA, and COLA process is managed by a machine on the ground;
to be fully autonomous, the system must also be able to upload the maneuvering command
to the spacecraft at a suitable time, i.e., during a passage of the spacecraft above the ground
station. Onboard autonomous systems, on the other hand, would realistically only require a
single input from the ground, the CDM upload, and they would manage the whole cascaded
CA/COLA on board. Therefore, the key diﬀerence lies in the location of the decision-making
process. Ground-based computers have exponentially higher computational power than on-
board ones, so they can run more complex algorithms; however, being able to run the process
on board would allow for timely adjustments of the maneuver in the case of unforeseen oc-
currences.
To this day, reliance on ground operations is essential for the extensive conjunction screen-
ing, the CDM generation process, and the CA process. Therefore, the only section of the
whole CA/COLA pipeline that would realistically be implementable on the onboard com-
puter of a spacecraft is the CAM design/optimisation, devoid of any decision-making on
whether or not to actuate the manoeuvre. However, since this last part of the whole pipeline

Machine Learning (ML) is a generally accepted solution for the introduction of (on-
ground) autonomous CA/decision-making [18, 19, 20, 21, 22], but classic optimisation tech-
niques are still preferable for CAM design. In this regard, the transition to autonomous
COLA requires mainly four characteristics of manoeuvre design:
• Optimality: ensuring the computed manoeuvre is fuel-optimal is crucial.
Fuel is a
limited resource in space missions, and optimising its use can extend the mission’s
lifespan. Therefore, optimal control theory should be used to achieve at least locally
optimal manoeuvres.
• Eﬃciency: light and eﬃcient algorithms reduce computational load and power con-
sumption, which are critical factors in spacecraft operations.
• Reliability: the algorithm must be guaranteed to produce the expected outcome under
nominal conditions.
• Robustness: spacecraft operate in environments where the modelling uncertainty is
high, so the ability to handle oﬀ-nominal conditions, such as thrust errors and unex-
pected environmental perturbations, is pivotal to improve the chance of a successful
manoeuvre.
The shift toward autonomous systems for STM is of interest to many players in the ﬁeld,
which are developing projects to this end. Key examples are Starling, involving SpaceX’s
Starlink constellation and NASA [23] and Collision Risk Estimation and Automated Miti-
gation (CREAM), from ESA. The former aims to demonstrate advanced onboard CA ca-
pabilities, such as continuous monitoring of both passive and active manoeuvring objects,
along with planned manoeuvres, minimizing the need for direct operator-to-operator com-
munication. The latter, framed in the broader European Space Safety Programme [24], has
the objective of demonstrating “[...] a ‘decision support system’ for CAMs, the integration
and deployment of a pilot for automated and secure exchange of spacecraft manoeuvre plans
and enhancement of autonomous decisions.”
Given the growing need for CAM design, the goal of this work is to provide a compre-
hensive review of current state-of-the-art solutions, oﬀering an in-depth analysis of existing
models and a numerical comparison of those that are deemed applicable to autonomous use.
The paper is structured into seven sections.
Section 2 introduces the tools necessary to
better understand all steps required for CAM design and their limitations, such as collision
probability estimation and state uncertainties propagation. Section 3 discusses numerical
approaches to CAM design, including direct optimisation, heuristics, and Artiﬁcial Intelli-
gence (AI)-based methods. Subsequently, Section 4 examines analytical and semi-analytical
optimisation techniques. Section 5 compares selected algorithms in terms of accuracy and
computational eﬃciency. Finally, this paper explores the future trends in COLA in Section 6,
followed by the conclusions in Section 7.

2. Probability of Collision
This section discusses uncertainty propagation techniques and their characteristics and
safety metrics for diﬀerent types of close encounters.
2.1. Uncertainty Propagation
In CA procedures, uncertainty propagation is essential for determining key satellite sta-
tistical information, such as the mean, covariance matrix, and Probability Density Func-
tion (PDF) of the state. Orbital dynamics with uncertainty can be modelled by the Itˆo
stochastic diﬀerential equation [25]. The PDF time evolution for such a system is governed
by the Fokker-Planck Equation (FPE) [26]. Solving it in orbital mechanics is challenging
due to the high-dimensional state space and non-linear dynamics involved.
To date, no
satisfactory solution that can be deployed in real scenarios has been found [27] [28].
Another rigorous approach to obtain a comprehensive statistical description involves em-
ploying Monte Carlo (MC) models. These simulations oﬀer means to handle non-linear and
non-Gaussian uncertainty propagation [29]. As PoC decreases, accurately estimating it with
MC methods requires an increasing number of samples, signiﬁcantly raising computational
demands [30]. Methods like Subset Simulation and Importance Sampling [31] reduce sample
requirements but are less robust than standard MC analyses. In recent years, linear propa-
gation techniques have been developed to balance accuracy and computational burden. In
linear uncertainty propagation methods, Gaussian uncertainties are commonly assumed so
that only the propagated mean value and the covariance matrix are needed to capture the
entire PDF at any time. In addition, local linearisation enables the linear propagation of the
mean and covariance matrix using the state transition matrix, derived from the solution of
the linearised system (LinCov) [32, 33]. On the other hand, statistical linearization approxi-
mates the dynamics in an ‘average’ sense, allowing for the derivation of statistical diﬀerential
equations for the mean and covariance matrix evolution [34]. Despite their simplicity and
eﬃciency, these methods become inaccurate with highly non-linear systems, long propagation
windows, and high initial uncertainty.
Various methods for nonlinear uncertainty propagation have been developed as eﬃcient
alternatives to MC simulations. Unscented Transform (UT) provides a second-order approx-
imation of the ﬁrst two moments of a Gaussian distribution by non-linearly integrating a few
deterministically selected ‘sigma points’ from the initial distribution, which may not suﬃce
for long simulations [35]. Similarly, cubature methods use deterministic sampling and integra-
tion, ensuring exact integral approximations for speciﬁc test functions, with spherical-radial
cubature rules being particularly popular for their speed and robustness [36]. Polynomial
Chaos (PC) manages inputs and outputs via series approximations involving standard ran-
dom variables, with some formulations allowing the use of existing orbit propagation tools
as black-box components to provide accurate higher-order moment information. However,
the number of PC terms grows exponentially with input uncertainty dimensionality [36].
Park and Scheeres [37] propose State Transition Tensors (STTs) as a semi-analytic technique
for orbit uncertainty propagation, but the variational approach used for their computation
can suﬀer limitations for high-ﬁdelity dynamic systems. Diﬀerential Algebra (DA) techniques
have been proposed to overcome the limitation of the variational approach, enabling accurate
non-linear uncertainty propagators with arbitrary order expansions [38]. DA has successfully
been used to reduce the computational eﬀort of nonlinear uncertainty propagation [39], even
in combination with Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) [40] which approximate arbitrary

changes in viewing angles. Similarly, the Error Function Method, conceived by Alfano in 2005
[56], works for any cross-section shape but also struggles with viewing angle variations and
encounters computational diﬃculties when handling extremely low collision probabilities.
2.2.2. Instantaneous PoC
Computing the PoC for long-term encounters is computationally demanding, with veloc-
ity uncertainty further complicating CAM planning. To overcome these challenges, CAM
methods often replace the PoC constraint with IPoC, an instantaneous snapshot of PoC that
provides a less rigorous risk representation. Academic interest in IPoC has been limited, and
fewer approximation methods exist compared to PoC. However, from a practical standpoint,
IPoC remains a valuable tool for CAM design. In Ref. [57], the authors demonstrate the
validity of substituting PoC with IPoC: they show that, in typical scenarios, a sharp increase
in the PoC function is often accompanied by a rise in IPoC.
While in most short-term encounter methods PoC only depends on the mean state and
covariance of the two objects at TCA, IPoC in long-term encounters is typically studied over
an extended time window. The formal deﬁnition of IPoC reads

3. Numerical CAM Design Methods
In this section, an overview of the CAM methods that rely on numerical optimisation
is given. In general, this includes all those methods that are based on the discretisation
and successive numerical solution of the original CAM Optimal Control Problem (OCP).
Commonly, direct methods are more ﬂexible than indirect ones, and they allow for the
inclusion of relevant operational and saturation constraints. They can be adapted to both
impulsive and low-thrust dynamics and often include multiple safety constraints (e.g., MD,
SMD, PoC). However, this adaptability reduces computational eﬃciency, making them slower
than indirect methods. Additionally, numerical optimisation algorithms, in general, have no
convergence guarantees to the global optimum; therefore, their applicability in autonomous
onboard applications should be carefully considered.
Literature contributions are grouped into three sections: in the ﬁrst one, the methods
that only treat short-term encounters are presented; in the second one, algorithms that can
treat long-term encounters are included; in the third one, the discussion shifts to methods
that address multiple short-term encounters.

small manoeuvres. The ﬁring direction is aligned with the PoC gradient, while the magnitude
is determined iteratively by expressing the closest approach displacement as a linear function
of the ∆v. A Newton-Rhapson algorithm is used to ﬁnd the minimum of PoC as a function
of the impulse. This decoupling reduces the optimization to a one-dimensional problem.
In Ref. [69], the authors extend this approach by casting the problem as a Polynomial
Program (PP). They employ DA to express PoC as a function of the control input - whether
low-thrust or impulsive - and across an arbitrary number of nodes, yielding a polynomial
constraint. In addition, a quadratic objective function is used, eﬀectively turning the problem
into a PP. Like in Patera and Peterson’s method, in the ﬁrst instance, the optimal control is
approximated by ﬁring in the direction of the gradient of PoC, de facto, eliminating the need
for optimisation. Subsequently, the higher-order terms of the polynomial PoC constraint are
incorporated into the algorithm by linearising the tensors of high-order derivatives using the
solution of the previous order. This allows one to successively change the magnitude and
the direction of thrust until convergence. This approach also accounts for the TCA shift
due to the manoeuvre, disregarded by most of the other methods. The achieved results are
promising, with computation times in the order of tenths of a second that mainly depend on
the expansion order and the number of impulses (or low-thrust arcs). Ref. [71] extends the
method to handle multiple constraints. Since it is used to address the multiple encounters
scenario, its discussion follows in Section 3.3.
Lee et al. [62] are among the ﬁrst to propose a Genetic Algorithm (GA) to ﬁnd the optimal
CAM. They include Station Keeping (SK) constraints for GEO satellites and ground-track
maintenance ones for LEO and the option to design the manoeuvre a long time before TCA.
This approach has been proven unnecessarily conservative by research in the following decade,
as most operators prefer to manoeuvre close to the conjunction. Their method is a precursor
to following studies on multiple encounters; therefore, a more detailed discussion follows in
Section 3.3. S´anchez and Vasile [66] implement a linear approximation of dynamics with im-
pulsive manoeuvres. In their methodology, PoC is computed under a short-term encounter
assumption and integrates epistemic uncertainty in key parameters - such as MD and covari-
ance components. By accounting for this uncertainty, the encounter produces a range of PoC
values reﬂecting variations in the parameters. Thus, the problem reduces to determining
the minimum manoeuvre needed to minimize the maximum collision risk—speciﬁcally under

the worst-case scenario within the uncertain conjunction family. The optimal manoeuvre
direction is calculated for the worst-case PoC arising from epistemic uncertainty along the
direction of the minimum eigenvector of the combined covariance. After each manoeuvre
computation, the conjunction geometry is updated, necessitating a recalculation of the ex-
tremal value of PoC arising from the uncertain variable set. This iterative approach continues
until the algorithm converges and the maximum PoC stabilises. A line search strategy is in-
tegrated into the iteration to adjust the magnitude of the ∆v, ensuring that the maximum
PoC post-manoeuvre falls below the selected risk mitigation threshold. Additionally, the
paper extends this framework to a heuristic, suboptimal strategy for low-thrust scenarios.
This is achieved by discretising ﬁring intervals and assuming a ﬁxed optimal thrust direc-
tion for each interval, which is determined using the impulsive strategy for the respective
sub-interval. The manoeuvre design strategy remains similar to the impulsive case, with the
exception that the ﬁnal position of the primary in the conjunction plane after the manoeuvre
is determined via a semi-analytical propagation and the optimal direction within each arc
is ﬁxed. This ﬁxed-direction assumption leads to the suboptimal nature of the method. As
with the impulsive case, the thrust arc magnitudes or manoeuvre amplitudes are adjusted
via line search during the iterative solution of the minimax problem.
Pi˜neiro et al. [61] propose a low dimensional Non-Linear Program (NLP) solution through
a direct collocation method based on pseudo-spectral approximation of the controls and
states. The solution aims at minimising the low-thrust fuel consumption while meeting a
ﬁnal state constraint linked to MD. In fact, the approach avoids PoC estimation and instead
selects an appropriate semi-major axis variation to ensure safety against state and manoeuvre
execution uncertainties at TCA. The preliminary results focus solely on planar transfers from
circular reference LEO orbits, facilitating validation against classical Hohmann transfers.
A diﬀerent take to the problem is proposed by Greco et al. [63] who adopt a Bayesian
paradigm and importance sampling to determine the optimal impulsive CAM in the presence
of uncertainties in its execution (i.e., using Gates model). In particular, PoC is computed as
the expectation of the indicator function that assumes value 1 if the MD is smaller than a
threshold and 0 otherwise. To obtain the optimal manoeuvre in this framework, the authors
resort to a stochastic shooting approach with direct transcription.
Recently, convex optimisation has emerged as a promising approach for CAM design. This
branch of direct optimisation is particularly attractive for solving trajectory design problems
due to its established success in other aerospace applications [72, 73, 74].
These studies
highlight the advantages of formulating the convexiﬁed problem as a Second-Order Cone
Program (SOCP), which facilitates the minimisation of the l2 norm of the control history
- a critical objective in spacecraft trajectory optimisation. To achieve this, all non-convex
elements of the original OCP must be convexiﬁed [75], including the fuel-optimal objective
function, the non-linear dynamics constraint, and the PoC constraint.
In Ref. [47], the author applies the projection and linearisation method proposed by Mao
in Ref. [76] and later reﬁned in Ref. [77] to the short-term CAM problem. This approach
utilises DA for automatic diﬀerentiation of the multi-impulsive dynamics and implements two
nested iteration cycles to iteratively linearise the PoC constraint based on a ﬁxed dynamics
reference solution. Furthermore, as in Ref. [69], the expansion of the conjunction time map
is used to understand how the manoeuvre inﬂuences TCA.
The author also emphasises
the signiﬁcance of the initial trajectory guess, noting that it can cause the optimiser to
converge to a local optimum on the avoidance ellipse. Consequently, while the method reliably
produces locally optimal solutions, it does not guarantee a globally optimal solution to the

iterations. As with all SCP methods, there is no theoretical proof for the convergence of
the algorithm. However, by means of a vast simulation campaign, the author shows that
convergence is reached in most of the considered cases. Armellin’s method is the basis for

Although all of the previously mentioned works are based on the use of some form of PoC,
Dutta and Misra [64] solve the short-term CAM using the IPoC. By employing the 3D COLA
ellipsoid, they use the same approximation of [49] to ﬁnd a constant-PDF approximation of
IPoC. This allows them to deﬁne the CAM constraint on SMD, similarly to the conversion
from PoC in Ref. [47]. Diﬀerently from the previously cited method, however, Dutta and
Misra eliminate the need for successive projection and linearisation of the CAM constraint by
expanding the SMD around a reference point. This linearisation leads to a more conservative
solution, so in general, the method always ﬁnds a sub-optimal solution. However, given that
the algorithm encompasses a single SOCP instance without sequences of convexiﬁcations,
it is guaranteed to converge to the local optimum of the original OCP. Nonetheless, the
use of IPoC instead of PoC is a limiting factor, and the reduction of the ∆v is obtained at
the price of a lower reduction of the risk. In Ref. [65], the authors extend the formulation
to include a non-linear propagation of the covariance of the two objects. The covariance
evolution is modelled using a Monte Carlo approach for the secondary and a UT for the
primary. Given the non-Gaussian nature of the uncertainty cloud at TCA, SMD cannot be
used, and an analogous metric based on the cumulative density function is adopted to ensure
that the sigma points of the UT respect the avoidance requirement. Because of the high
computational load required by the Monte Carlo and by the UT, the method is expensive,
with runtimes that reach 2 hours.
At last, Ref. [70] explores the Theory of Functional Connections (TFC) to design energy-
optimal CAMs, framing the problem as an unconstrained optimization. A comparison is
made between the TFC-based approach and the analytical approach from Ref. [79], evalu-
ating performance in terms of computational cost, accuracy, and manoeuvre cost while also
analysing the impact of various TFC conﬁguration parameters. Despite [79], detailed in Sec-
tion 4.2, oﬀering higher computational eﬃciency, TFC paves the way for incorporating any
constraint without the need for dynamics and constraint convexiﬁcation.

rather than a single optimal solution.

one that derives a single manoeuvre based solely on the worst-case scenario, and another that

impulse, whereas the second computes individual manoeuvres for each conjunction, requiring
PoC recalculations for subsequent events after each CAM. This method primarily focuses
on proposing and ranking various potential CAMs under diﬀerent operational and execution
constraints. The authors employ rigorous multi-criteria decision-making techniques to evalu-
ate collision risk and prioritise the candidate CAMs. While this approach is advantageous for
ground operators in selecting the most appropriate CAM for a given scenario, its high com-
putational demands and reliance on human intervention render it unsuitable for autonomous
manoeuvres.

4. Analytical and Semi-Analytical CAM Design Methods
This section introduces analytical and semi-analytical COLA algorithms, encompassing
both impulsive and low-thrust propulsion strategies. These methods utilise closed-form solu-
tions for CAM problems, oﬀering signiﬁcantly improved computational eﬃciency compared
to traditional numerical techniques.
The following discussion is organised into two parts: the ﬁrst addresses impulsive strate-
gies, while the second explores low-thrust manoeuvrers. Both analytical and semi-analytical
formulations are examined within each subsection.
4.1. Impulsive Methods
Impulsive trajectory optimisation, typically used to design manoeuvrers for chemical
thrusters, assumes that manoeuvres can be executed instantaneously with a change in ve-
locity at discrete points in time. This kind of CAMs often involves solving a sequence of
Boundary Value Problem (BVP), where the goal is to determine the optimal times, mag-
nitudes, and directions of impulses to achieve a desired mission objective. In Table 4, the
methods discussed in this section are summarized.
The literature on this topic has a strong foundation with a key contribution by Bom-
bardelli, who assessed the challenges of impulsive CAMs assuming elliptical Keplerian orbits
in [93]. Leveraging the B-plane dynamics at TCA, closed-form analytical expressions are
17


## Page 18


developed to increase MD with a three-dimensional manoeuvre. The accuracy of these algo-
rithms is tested under various orbital conﬁgurations, assessing its relevance for practical sce-
narios like space debris avoidance and satellite operations, where last-minute, non-tangential
manoeuvres might be necessary. Building upon this work, Bombardelli and Hernando-Ayuso
in [94] present a high-accuracy analytical formulation to compute the MD and PoC of two
approaching objects after an impulsive CAM. The manoeuvre optimisation is turned into
an eigenvalue problem coupled with a non-linear algebraic equation by exploiting the linear
relation between applied impulse and objects’ relative motion in the B-plane. As an opti-
misation variable, the cost of the manoeuvre is chosen in terms of ∆v magnitude to match
imposed thresholds on either MD or PoC.
In Ref.
[95], Gonzalo et al.
introduce an analytical formulation for spacecraft CAM
with space debris, oﬀering solutions for both maximum deviation and minimum PoC scenar-
ios. Employing Gauss’ planetary equations and relative motion dynamics, the model maps
manoeuvres at speciﬁc times into displacements at predicted close approaches. The formula-
tion is extended to propagate covariance matrices, reducing the optimisation problem to an
eigenvalue problem. Comparisons between MD and PoC methodologies are presented using
data from the ESA’s MASTER-2009 model. The analysis in the B-plane shows that both
uncertainties and maximum deviation increase with lead time, limiting PoC reduction over
extended time frames.
A further advancement [97] extends impulsive CAM strategies by introducing frame-
works that seamlessly transition between ECI or B-plane coordinates while incorporating
constraints such as MD, SMD or PoC. Single-impulse formulations are developed using NLP
to obtain closed-form solutions to the problem. These methodologies are further reﬁned for
the direct impact scenario, taking into account both ﬁxed and free thrust direction con-
straints. A tri-impulsive analytical formulation is then introduced to return to the nominal
trajectory: the ﬁrst is applied at a predeﬁned epoch to achieve lower PoC, the second at
TCA to redirect the trajectory back toward the original orbit, and the third one at the point
of intersection with the original orbit to achieve the SK condition. These methods are tested

4.2. Low-Thrust Methods
Low-thrust trajectory optimisation involves continuous thrusting over long durations,
typically using electromagnetic propulsion systems, solar sails, or similar mechanisms. A
key feature of low-thrust propulsion is the gradual but continuous acceleration, allowing for
extended mission lifespans due to higher speciﬁc impulse and available ∆v. This optimisation
is particularly suited for CAMs with suﬃcient warning time or for integrating them with SK
routines during close encounters. A noteworthy distinction exists between Energy-Optimal
(EO) and Fuel-Optimal (FO) formulations.
The former minimises energy usage without
thrust limitations, while the latter typically follows a bang-bang proﬁle, maintaining constant
acceleration throughout the manoeuvring windows. Moreover, EO strategies often serve as a
starting point for FO optimisations through techniques like homotopy transformations [99].

constraints enforced on MD, SMD and PoC. Comparatively, EO solutions in the B-plane
exhibit slightly reduced accuracy due to the ﬁrst-order approximation of dynamics. Optimal
manoeuvrers, consistent with prior research, are typically near-tangential when planned in
advance. Additionally, EO solutions can be leveraged to solve FO problems with bounded
acceleration, achieving propellant eﬃciency. The algorithms were validated on realistic short-
term conjunction scenarios in LEO, demonstrating that the linearisations introduce negligible
constraint violations, even for highly elliptical orbits, and that gravitational and atmospheric
drag perturbations eﬀects have a limited impact on the manoeuvres’ safety and eﬀectiveness.
A step forward is achieved in [105] where previous algorithms are extended by incorporating
return on the initial orbit with a point-to-point and a point-to-orbit strategy. In both policies,
analytical solutions are retrieved by combining a series of EO problems. Finally, in the GEO
regime, [104] introduces algorithms that integrate low-thrust CAMs and SK by concurrently
meeting a target PoC and maximising the permanence time within the box. An EO problem

Gonzalo et al. [107] introduce analytical and semi-analytical models for CAMs using av-

with minimal added complexity. The model’s eﬃciency is demonstrated through sensitivity

components to optimise manoeuvre design. The method reduces PoC using a quasi-optimal,
piecewise constant thrust control proﬁle derived from an eigenproblem approach. Moreover,

and semi-analytical models for low-thrust CAM computation and design. With the aid of
single-averaging of Keplerian motion equations, the secular and oscillatory components of or-

piecewise-constant thrust proﬁles are derived based on their impulsive model counterparts.
Str¨obel et al. [106] propose an analytical method to determine the required coasting arc
or burn duration to achieve a speciﬁed PoC, modelled using Chan’s series expansion. The
method is based on the Hill-Clohessy-Wiltshire equations, which yield closed-form analytical
solutions for the coasting arc following a short-duration manoeuvre. The approach assumes
negligible perturbations, nearly circular orbits, minimal deviation between the object and the
reference trajectory, and thrust applied in the along-track direction. The method eﬀectively
reduces PoC when CAMs are executed several revolutions before the TCA, with execution
time strongly inﬂuenced by encounter geometry. While achieving accuracy comparable to
numerical simulations, the authors suggest its use as a preliminary estimate for more precise
numerical methods.
Reiter et al. [101] addresses the challenge of COLA in LEO under minimal notiﬁcation
times. It assumes scenarios with less than half an orbital period to plan and execute ma-
noeuvrers, treating debris as a point mass with position uncertainty aligned along its velocity
vector. The study recurs to ﬁnite burns with constant thrust magnitude to optimise thrust lo-
cation, direction, and duration, aiming to minimise fuel usage while achieving safe separation,
also expressed as PoC threshold. A key contribution is the development of a semi-analytical
formulation that links thrust duration, PoC, and separation distance, facilitating rapid on-
board decision-making. Case studies demonstrate that when notiﬁcation times are below 20
minutes, reducing collision probability is prioritized over fuel consumption. Conversely, for
longer notiﬁcation times, minimizing manoeuvre duration becomes the primary objective.
Regarding semi-analytical techniques, Lee et al. [100] theorize how to generate near-
optimal collision-free trajectories for active spacecraft. By incorporating a penalty function
into the performance index, there is no need for iterative processes and initial guesses required

design during orbit-raising scenarios. The manoeuvre is achieved by determining the engine’s
optimal switch-oﬀtime, after which the subsequent drifting phase is analytically propagated
until the anticipated TCA is reached, with its duration being determined to ensure that the
target SMD is realised.

tangential about half an orbit earlier. These studies oﬀer valuable insights into the operational

of results to thrust magnitude and conjunction geometry. The computational eﬃciency of
the method and its applicability to high-risk, low-warning time scenarios make it a promising
candidate for future autonomous applications.

6. Future Trends
This section explores recent CAM advancements that we believe will become more and
more signiﬁcant in the future. In particular, CAMs in the Cislunar domain and in thrustless
applications (such as micro- and nanosats) are discussed.
6.1. Cislunar Collision Avoidance
In response to growing concerns about space debris and orbital congestion, as highlighted
in Section 1, both space agencies and private enterprises are increasingly investigating alter-
native orbital regimes for future space-based infrastructures. Among these, Cislunar space
has garnered considerable attention thanks to its proximity, vast expanse, and critical scien-
tiﬁc, strategic, and economic signiﬁcance. As a result, numerous missions and infrastructures
are being planned for this region. However, the challenges encountered in near-Earth orbits,
such as the necessity for COLA, may similarly emerge in this new orbital regime.
The literature on this topic is still in its early stages. In Ref. [116]. the authors introduce
an analytical method for impulsive CAMs in Cislunar space using the Circular Restricted
Three Body Problem (CR3BP) model. A closed-form solution is then derived for the EO
formulation with free-ﬁring direction, providing educated guess for a semi-analytical FO
CAM with an ON-OFF proﬁle. This last solution employs DA for the optimisation of the
ﬁring window to retain some computational eﬃciency. Despite results typically obtained in
two-body problems, resulting thrust proﬁles do not show any preference for the tangential
direction. Ultimately, these algorithms adopt MD, SMD and PoC as terminal constraints and
are tested on a custom-designed test case in the L1-Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbits (NRHO),
demonstrating highly promising computational times and accuracy.
The same test case
proposed in Ref. [116] is used in Ref. [69] to show that the recursive PP implementation can
deal with Cislunar dynamics, obtaining very similar results to the original work.
The challenges of conjunction assessment in the cislunar space is further developed by S.
Crum et al. in [117], where authors evaluate collision risk and the need for CAMs in this
region. The results indicate that satellites in Low Lunar Orbit (LLO) might require up to
four manoeuvres annually to avoid collisions, with most close encounters occurring near the
lunar equator. The analysis emphasises the signiﬁcant impact of covariance matrix size on
collision risk, stressing the need to improve the cislunar space domain awareness to mitigate
future risks as lunar exploration increases. Another work by T. Lechtenberg et al. [118]
addresses the same topic by using internal cislunar population models and spatial-density
approaches to estimate long-term collision risks.
It also introduces techniques to ensure
safety compliance despite gaps in state uncertainty data, tackling the increasing number of
missions planned for the Moon and Cislunar space.
Ultimately, [119] leverage two dynamical models for CAM design in this realm: CR3BP
and a higher-Fidelity Ephemeris Model (HFEM), both simulating spacecraft motion in the
Earth-Moon system. The CR3BP is used for initial analysis, while the HFEM incorporates
additional gravitational forces for greater accuracy. Collision avoidance is achieved through
impulsive manoeuvres designed using the STM, ensuring the spacecraft returns to the refer-
ence orbit after diversion, with evaluations conducted using both models.
6.2. Thrustless CAM
When performing a CAM, the natural environmental forces present in orbit – like SRP
or atmospheric drag – are viable alternatives to actively thrusting. These solutions allow for
28


## Page 29


changing the satellite’s trajectory by increasing its ballistic coeﬃcient or its SRP equivalent
area through attitude control or devices like drag/solar sails to maximise the environmental
actions in the desired direction. The previously cited work in [102] shows the possibility of
employing SRP and drag as a means to modify the orbit shape to avoid a close encounter,
paving the way for CAM capabilities also for small spacecraft by mounting solar or drag sails.
Mishne et al. investigate in [120] the feasibility of drag and SRP for COLA in LEO satel-
lites. By orienting the satellite to maximise the eﬀect of these natural forces, changes in the
semi-major axis are achieved, creating suﬃcient along-track deviation to avoid collisions. It
solely relies on attitude control and the diﬀerence between the satellite’s maximum and min-
imum cross-sectional areas. Numerical simulations demonstrate that within three days, the
technique can reduce PoC below acceptable thresholds for satellites at altitudes between 600
and 800 km. In [121], authors propose a way for COLA based on controlling the spacecraft’s
ballistic coeﬃcient to adjust its orbit. A simple analytical solution computes the diﬀerence in
mean anomaly required to achieve a desired along-track miss distance, which is further reﬁned
numerically by modulating the ballistic coeﬃcient using drag devices. The authors empha-
sise the importance of initiating manoeuvres early to minimise orbital deviations. Monte
Carlo analysis demonstrates the robustness of the CAM algorithm for operational scenarios,
conﬁrming the validity of the underlying assumptions. Building upon this work, F. Turco
et al. in [122] explore the use of atmospheric drag as an in-track COLA strategy in LEO
by adjusting the satellite’s ballistic coeﬃcient through attitude changes. The method esti-
mates the achievable separation distance between satellites experiencing varying drag forces,
assuming circular orbits and a non-rotating atmosphere. The study incorporates constraints
such as battery charging phases and conjunction geometry. The feasibility of the method is
demonstrated using the Flying Laptop satellite, with results showing the feasibility of the
method in reducing PoC, despite some limitations connected to space weather and uncer-
tainties. Similarly, [123] presents a novel COLA strategy for satellites in Very Low Earth
Orbit (VLEO), leveraging aerodynamic drag modulation as a propellant-free alternative for
unmanoeuvrable satellites. The approach balances two conﬂicting objectives – increasing MD
while minimising orbital decay – via a composed cost function. By employing the SOURCE
CubeSat as a test case, the method is proved capable of achieving a 22 km in-track sepa-
ration with 165 m orbital decay in a 24-hour warning period. Key contributions include a
realistic model incorporating atmospheric dynamics and an OCP ensuring practical control
proﬁles. The study highlights its adaptability for future debris-congested environments and
sustainable satellite operations.
Ultimately, in [124] the study explores the use of ionospheric drag as a fuel-less mechanism
for satellite COLA in LEO. By utilising an in-house propagation suite, the study investigates
the impact of ionospheric and neutral drag on along-track separation for satellites at altitudes
between 350 and 500 km. It further compares charged and uncharged methods, revealing that
ionospheric drag can reduce the time to achieve NASA’s approved collision risk reduction
by at least 60%. The results suggest that ionospheric drag oﬀers a feasible alternative for
COLA, particularly for small satellites operating in LEO.
7. Conclusions
This review provided a comprehensive analysis of computationally eﬃcient collision avoid-
ance manoeuvre (CAM) routines, focusing on their applicability in autonomous settings. Var-
ious numerical, semi-analytical, and analytical approaches have been examined, emphasizing
