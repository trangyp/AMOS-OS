WHERE THE TREES FALL: MACROECONOMIC FORECASTS FOR FOREST-
RELIANT STATES 
Andrew Crawley^, Adam Daigneault*, Jonathan Gendron+ 
 
Abstract 
 
Several key states in various regions of the U.S. have experienced recent sawtimber as well as 
pulp and paper mill closures, which raises an important policy question: how have and will key 
macroeconomic and industry specific indicators within the U.S. forest sector likely to change 
over time? This study provides empirical evidence to support forest-sector policy design by 
using a vector error correction (VEC) model to forecast economic trends in three major 
industries – forestry and logging, wood manufacturing, and paper manufacturing – across six of 
the most forest-dependent states found by the location quotient (LQ) measure: Alabama, 
Arkansas, Maine, Mississippi, Oregon, and Wisconsin. Overall, the results suggest a general 
decline in employment and the number of firms in the forestry and logging industry as well as 
the paper manufacturing industry, while wood manufacturing is projected to see modest 
employment gains. These results also offer key insights for regional policymakers, industry 
leaders, and local economic development officials: communities dependent on timber-based 
manufacturing may be more resilient than other forestry-based industries in the face of economic 
disruptions. Our findings can help prioritize targeted policy interventions and inform regional 
economic resilience strategies. We show distinct differences across forest-dependent industries 
and/or state sectors and geographies, highlighting that policies may have to be specific to each 
sector and/or geographical area. Finally, our VEC modeling framework is adaptable to other 
resource-dependent industries that serve as regional economic pillars such as mining, agriculture, 
and energy production offering a transferable tool for policy analysis in regions with similar 
economic structures. 
 
Key Words: Forest policy analysis, natural resource dependence, regional economic forecasting, 
vector error correction model (VECM), forest sector economics, industry-level modeling, 
macroeconomic forecasting

1. Introduction 
 
Forest-dependent industries have long served a crucial role in the economic vitality of 
many rural U.S. communities across various regions, often serving as foundational pillars of 
local employment and public revenue (Balogh, 2018). However, these industries have undergone 
significant structural changes, such as market consolidation, global supply chain shifts, 
technological shifts, and declining demand for traditional wood products such as newsprint and 
paper (Toppinen & Kuuluvainen, 2010, Woodall et al, 2011). The consequences ripple through 
these communities leading to mill closures, job losses, declining tax bases, and reduced 
economic resilience, particularly in states heavily reliant on the forest sector (Sherman & 
Daigneault, 2023). These disruptions pose an urgent question for policymakers and economic 
development officials: how are key macroeconomic and sector-specific indicators likely to 
evolve in forest-reliant communities, and what interventions can improve resilience?   
 
This paper aims to support evidence-based forest sector policy by offering industry-
specific, state-level economic forecasts using a cointegration-based econometric framework with 
six historically forestry-reliant states: Alabama, Arkansas, Maine, Mississippi, Oregon, and 
Wisconsin. Specifically, we apply a vector error correction (VEC) model (e.g., Parajuli et al., 
2016; Hetemaki et al., 2004) to forecast employment, output, wages, and firm dynamics across 
three core forest sector industries: (1) forestry and logging (NAICS 113), which encompasses 
tree cultivation, harvesting, and timber transport; (2) wood manufacturing (NAICS 321); and (3) 
paper manufacturing (NAICS 322), both of which involve processing harvested timber in a mill 
into lumber and paper products, respectively (USDA, 2011). While the literature often uses terms 
such as “timber”, “logging”, and “forestry” interchangeably, this study distinguishes between 
these core subsectors to more precisely identify which components of the forest economy are 
most vulnerable or resilient to future economic shifts. To identify the most forest-dependent 
states, we first calculate location quotients (LQs) for all U.S. states with measurable forest 
industry activity. These LQs quantify each state’s relative economic dependence on the forest 
sector and guide the selection of states most in need of targeted policy attention. This approach 
provides a generalizable foundation for identifying other natural resource-based dependent 
industries. We then apply state and industry VECs for each highly forest-dependent state in our 
study. 
 
Generating forward-looking region-specific economic forecasts, our analysis helps 
policymakers, industry leaders, and local development officials by providing information and 
strategies to best anticipate and mitigate disruptions with actions such as labor planning, 
economic diversification strategies, and targeted support programs for forest- and other natural 
resource-dependent communities (Anderson et al., 2002). Importantly, our analysis highlights 
that the impacts of structural change are not uniform: some industries (i.e. wood manufacturing) 
show modest resilience, while others (i.e. forestry and paper manufacturing) are expected to 
contract, requiring different policy responses. Furthermore, this modeling framework is 
transferrable and can be used to evaluate similar risks in other resource-dependent sectors such 
as mining, agriculture, or energy (e.g., Michieka & Gearhart 2015; Jiang & Liu, 2014; Bekhet & 
Yusop, 2009). The same forecasting techniques applied here can be used to assess economic 
resilience in other sectors or regions facing resource dependency (such as mining, agriculture, or 
energy). These sectors often share similar vulnerabilities to technological change, price shocks,

and regulatory shifts. Understanding where economic vulnerabilities are likely to emerge enables 
more strategic allocation of public resources and more effective long-term planning. These 
vulnerabilities have been long studied in empirical economic analyses, particularly in the 
contexts of forecasting, labor dynamics, policy evaluation, price analysis, and trade. Forecasting 
models are central to this work: they help decision-makers understand not just where industries 
are today, but where they are heading. In practice, regional forecasts often inform state-level 
budgeting, labor force planning, infrastructure investment, and policy prioritization for rural and 
resource-dependent areas (Pattanayak et al., 2002; Federal Reserve System, 2016). Although all 
forecasting methods involve a degree of measurement error and respective uncertainty, selecting 
the most appropriate econometric model that can handle the particular characteristics of the data 
(such as long-run cointegration between variables) enhances accuracy and validity. This 
enhancement accordingly greatly improves the usefulness of forecasts in real-world policy 
design and decision making. 
 
Existing literature already documents the high economic sensitivity of forest-reliant 
communities; for example, in Maine mill openings and closures have immediate and measurable 
effects on local economies (Gendron et al., 2019). Oregon provides a similar example, with the 
forestry sector contributing up to $375 million in annual state and local tax revenues as early as 
2005 (Associated Oregon Loggers, 2021). In Mississippi, forest-based industries generated 
$925.73 million in tax revenue in 2018 and have yielded over $1 billion annually in timber 
harvests since 1993 (Henderson & Munn, 2008; Tanger & Measells, 2020). These industries also 
support thousands of direct and indirect jobs throughout the state (Munn, 1997). Wood and paper 
manufacturing, while downstream from forestry, are similarly vulnerable to supply shocks, price 
fluctuations and mill openings/closures, given their reliance on harvested raw materials. These 
industries are particularly significant in Alabama, Arkansas, Maine, Mississippi, Wisconsin, and 
Oregon. These states stand to benefit most from evidence-based policymaking tools that 
anticipate and mitigate the economic consequences of sectoral disruptions and also provide 
targeted support to its affected communities. Alabama alone contributes an estimated $7.9 billion 
to the economy in value added output from the forest sector (Fickle, 2017). Arkansas ranks first 
among southern states and fourth nationally in lumber production, with a total economic impact 
exceeding $12 billion as early as 2001 (Pelkki, 2005). Oregon’s lumber industry accounts for 
more than half of all manufacturing employment in the state and has led the nation in wood 
product production since 1938 (Cloughesy, 2021; Robbins, 2021). While Oregon is less reliant 
on paper manufacturing, this industry is a significant contributor to economic activity in 
Alabama, Arkansas, Maine, Mississippi, and Wisconsin –states that consistently rank among the 
top in U.S. paper production (Ballweg, 2017).

literature on forest sector forecasting and related policy analysis. Section 3 describes the data and 
methodology, with an emphasis on the Vector Error Correction (VEC) model’s capacity to 
analyze long-run relationships and forecast structural trends. Section 4 presents and interprets the 
results from a policy and resilience standpoint. Section 5 concludes with a discussion of 
actionable insights for forest sector stakeholders and opportunities to apply this approach to other 
resource-dependent economies that rely on industries such as mining, agriculture, and energy

2. Literature Review 
 
Forecasting macroeconomic trends is a well-established approach for understanding and 
responding to real-world economic challenges. These forecasts are often used to support policy 
decisions aimed at stabilizing or stimulating key economic indicators such as employment and 
output (Anderson et al., 2002; Eicher et al., 2019; Poudel & Dahal, 2025). For example, the U.S. 
Federal Reserve routinely produces macroeconomic forecasts to guide monetary policy and 
assess broader economic conditions across the globe (Federal Reserve System, 2016). At more 
localized levels, similar forecasting methodologies have been applied to state or regional 
economies and to specific industries - including the forest sector – where shifts in output, 
employment, and firm activity can significantly affect the wellbeing of resource-dependent 
communities. In such contexts, policy intervention informed by accurate forecasts can help 
mitigate economic downturns and support community resilience. 
 
Selecting an appropriate econometric model is crucial to generating reliable forecasts. An 
essential first step is determining whether the underlying data is stationary or non-stationary. 
Non-stationary (or cointegrated) data indicates that variables move with each other due to an 
underlying long-run relationship, whereas stationary data suggests that variables fluctuate 
independently over time (Engle & Granger, 1987). Mistaking these properties can result in 
model specification and accordingly unreliable inferences, undermining the reliability of policy 
recommendations (Hill et al., 2008). Even when short-run dynamics suggest independence 
between variables, long-run equilibrium relationships may exist and must be modeled 
accordingly as cointegrated. Since traditional stationary models cannot properly account for 
cointegrated relationships, more advanced econometric techniques have been developed 
including the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), error correction (EC) and 
vector error correction (VEC) models (Engle & Granger, 1987). Beyond these standard 
approaches, hybrid and unique models, such as behavioral adaptive expectation models, have 
also been explored (Haji-Othman, 1991). Formal statistical tests such as the Johansen test and the 
Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test for stationarity are widely used to guide model selection 
(Johansen, 1988; Al-Ballaa, 2005).  
 
Table 1 summarizes common econometric methods used in the forest sector forecasting. 
EC models are often favored for their ability to handle cointegrated data. For instance, Polemis 
(2007) analyzed energy demand in Greece with an EC, and Song et al. (2011) used a stationary 
two-stage least squares (2SLS) model. In the forest sector, EC models have been utilized to 
analyze sawlog stumpage prices in the southern U.S. (Mei et al., 2010). However, EC models 
can only accommodate a single cointegrated equation, making them less flexible when modeling 
more complex systems like multiple interdependent variables (Hill et al., 2008; Lambert et al., 
2013). In such cases, vector autoregression (VAR) and VEC models are capable of handling 
multiple cointegrated equations. Comparative studies have shown that VAR and VEC models 
outperform ARIMA and other specifications in terms of forecasting accuracy, particularly in 
contexts involving complex interrelationships between multiple economic indicators (Hetemaki 
et al., 2004; Malaty et al., 2006; Mei et al., 2010). In the forest sector, where employment, 
output, firm activity and wages often evolve together, VEC models offer a powerful framework 
for capturing both short-run policy-relevant fluctuations and long-run structural dynamics 
(Parajuli & Chang, 2015; Kolo & Tzanova, 2017; Hernandez-Diaz et al., 2024).

3. Methodology 
 
 
This study employs a VEC model to forecast key macroeconomic and industry-specific 
variables for U.S. states where the forestry and logging industry (NAICS 113) and its related 
sectors – wood manufacturing (NAICS 321) and paper manufacturing (NAICS 322) play a 
significant economic role. The primary objective of this analysis is to examine how these 
variables are expected to evolve in states where these industries are particularly important, as 
determined by their LQ value. Figure 1 below illustrates the modeling with a simple workflow. 
The remainder of this section is structured as follows: Section 3.1 formally defines the LQ value 
and its role in identifying industry-dependent states. Section 3.2 describes the data sources and 
variables included in the VEC model. Section 3.3 presents the theoretical foundation of the 
model, while Section 3.4 details how the empirical model is specified and estimated based on the 
data.

3.2. Data 
 
To analyze state-level impacts in the U.S. forest sector, we use data from NAICS 113, 
321 and 322. This dataset consists of quarterly macroeconomic data spanning from 2001Q1 to 
2018Q4, with variable descriptions and sources summarized in Table 2. Recent data beyond 
2018 was excluded following the forecasting literature to avoid distortions caused by the non-
systemic shocks of the COVID-19 pandemic (Eicher et al., 2019; Bobeica & Hartwig, 2023; 
Schorfheide & Song, 2024). The variables included in the analysis represent key supply and 
demand factors: price and employment impact demand while output, wages, number of firms and 
price impact supply. To measure wages, total wages were used instead of wages per capita, as 
the latter assumes uniform earnings across workers – an overly restrictive assumption.

crucial factor that can impact both supply and demand. While this substitution will likely 
introduce some measurement error, especially in regions where input costs or product markets

We use a VEC model because it allows us to measure bi-directional and cross directional 
relationships between variables (unlike standard regression models, which assume a single-
directional relationship). In addition, a VEC model allows us to handle multiple cointegrated 
equations, which are essential given the interconnected nature of supply and demand in the forest 
sector. By utilizing a VEC model, we can simultaneously estimate long-run equilibrium 
relationships and short-run adjustments for key macroeconomic variables. Our model primarily 
focuses on employment, output and wages, capturing both supply and demand dynamics in the 
forest sector. These estimated relationships are then used for forecasting, an essential tool for 
projecting future trends in forest related industries (Pattanayak et al., 2002; Xu et al., 2004; Baek, 
2012). Ultimately, the VEC model enables us to provide a comprehensive empirical framework 
for understanding the interdependencies within the forest sector and forecasting its future 
economic trajectory (Toppinen, 1998; Toppinen & Kuuluvainen, 2010; Baek, 2012; Parajuli & 
Chang, 2015; Daigneault et al., 2016; Parajuli et al., 2016).

To analyze dynamic interactions among variables, we employ impulse response functions 
(IRFs), a common tool in macroeconomic and VEC modeling. IRFs trace the effect of a one-time 
shock to one variable on the future values of all variables in the system, allowing us to 
distinguish both short-run adjustments and long-run responses. Following Gendron et al. (2019), 
we use these IRFs to generate out-of-sample forecasts based on how the system responds to 
shocks over time. To illustrate how this works in practice, consider a one-time shock to 
employment. The example IRF plot for Maine employment shown in Figure 2 simulates how this 
employment shock propagates through the rest of the system. For instance, wages experience an 
immediate spike that then levels off after two quarters. Interestingly, we also observe a seasonal 
oscillation: each year, wages tend to spike in the first quarter and level off by the fourth. These 
patterns emerge naturally from the estimated relationships among variables in the VEC model 
and reflect the historical dynamics captured in the data. These detailed dynamics provide insight 
into how one variable affects the others over time, and this is a key strength of VEC models. 
IRFs not only help us interpret short and long run interdependencies but also provide a 
foundation for generating realistic shock-based forecasts. 
 
This approach mirrors methodologies used in the lumber forecasting literature, where 
VEC models have been applied to project supply and demand dynamics across regions and 
countries. By integrating VEC based forecasting and IRFs, this empirical strategy provides a 
comprehensive understanding of the macroeconomic forces shaping the forest sector, offering 
valuable insights for policymakers and industry leaders. These insights can then be used for best 
informed policy intervention to mitigate the damage of economic shifts and supply support to its 
affected communities.

4. Results and Discussion 
 
We forecasted trends for the 5 empirical models of NAICS 113 (forestry and logging), 
the 6 empirical models of NAICS 321 (wood product manufacturing), and the 5 empirical 
models of NAICS 322 (paper manufacturing) and compared them to historical data to assess 
forecast accuracy4. The forecasting horizon spans five years (2018Q1 to 2023Q1), using data 
from 2001Q1-2018Q1 for model estimation. Forecasts are compared to historical trends to assess 
accuracy and highlight implications for regional policy and industry sustainability. Importantly, 
these forecasts do not account for the COVID-19 pandemic, as it represents a non-systemic 
shock that would distort long-run industry trends. As highlighted in the methodology within 
Section 3, wages in the forestry sector exhibit a cyclical pattern. After adjusting for this cyclical 
behavior, only a modest upward trend persists. 
 
Our study focuses on the six states where LQs are consistently the highest across all 3 
NAICS industries (forestry, wood and paper manufacturing): Alabama, Arkansas, Maine, 
Mississippi, Oregon, and Wisconsin (see Table 3). These states represent a diverse geographic 
range, covering the Northeast, North Central, South Central, and Pacific West regions. The high 
economic dependence of communities in these states on the forestry sector makes them 
particularly vulnerable to structural changes, highlighting the importance of regional policy 
interventions for their economic stability and industry sustainability. Our vector error correction 
(VEC) model proves effective in capturing long-run dynamics in employment, output, wages, 
and firm count, offering valuable insights for policymakers and stakeholders seeking to improve 
regional resilience, develop workforce transition strategies, or prioritize infrastructure 
investment. The following subsections summarize key industry-level forecasts and discuss 
implications for targeted policy intervention: Section 4.1 presents and discusses the results for 
NAICS 113, followed by Section 4.2 for NAICS 321, and Section 4.3 for NAICS 322. Section 
4.4 outlines both the limitations and advantages of the empirical framework used in these 
forecasts, and Section 4.5 provides some suggestions for future study. Lastly, Section 4.6 
provides some suggestions for policy-makers of the states we empirically modeled. 
 
4.1 NAICS 113 (Forestry and Logging) 
 
Forecasts for NAICS 113 shown in Figure 3-4 indicate a general decline in employment 
and the number of firms, while output remains stable and wages show a modest upward trend. 
Maine is the only state where employment exhibits seasonal patterns. Oregon is a notable outlier 
with significantly higher levels of employment, output, and wages, although the direction and 
rate of change are consistent with the broader group. The contraction in employment and 
business activity pose a serious challenge to communities with tax bases and household incomes 
dependent on this sector. However, the observed stability in output and wage growth suggests 
that the remaining firms are either more productive or benefit more from improved pricing or 
efficiency. Policymakers and industry leaders may interpret these findings as a call to action: 
targeted policy intervention and/or economic diversification efforts may be required in 
communities where mill closures and labor market contraction are likely, while those 
maintaining output may benefit from workforce retraining programs to transition displaced 
workers into higher-value roles within the sector.

4.2 NAICS 321 (Wood Product Manufacturing) 
 
The results for NAICS 321 as shown in Figure 5-7 provide various insights. Employment 
and wages fell sharply during the financial crisis (2008-2010) but rebounded gradually over time 
in most states. Post-crisis employment continued to rise steadily in Alabama and Oregon, while 
wages in Alabama increased at a faster rate compared to the other states in this analysis. Firm 
count has been steadily declining for most states with the exception of Alabama and Oregon 
whom have instead leveled off. Output followed a similar trajectory – dropping during the 
financial crisis, recovering to pre-crisis levels over time, and then stabilizing. Arkansas saw 
particularly volatile output growth between 2012 and 2015, while Alabama, Mississippi, and 
Wisconsin experience similar but more moderate output surges from 2014 to 2018. 
 
In summary, NAICS 321 exhibits more favorable labor market dynamics than NAICS 
113 does. The upward trend in employment is a promising sign for local economic development, 
suggesting that targeted support to sustain firm viability – such as infrastructure improvements or 
capital investment programs – may yield significant returns. However persistent declines in the 
number of firms in some regions signal underlying structural challenges. As such, policy 
measures that reduce entry barriers, incentivize mill modernization, or provide technical 
assistance could play a key role in supporting not only stability, but also industry longevity, and 
employment growth.

4.3 NAICS 322 (Paper Manufacturing) 
 
The results for NAICS 322 shown in Figures 8-9 have a concerning trajectory. Although 
wages remain relatively stable with seasonal fluctuations, employment, output, and the number 
of firms are all forecasted to decline. Maine shows a particularly steep decline across all 
indicators, suggesting that this sector is under acute strain. Wisconsin, by contrast, has much 
higher levels across all indicators, but shares a similar rate of decline most of the other states in 
this analysis exhibit. A noteworthy exception is Arkansas in that its output does not decrease like 
the other states but instead remains level. These results highlight the urgency for policy support 
in regions where the paper manufacturing industry remains a major employer. In particular, 
Maine’s sharp contradictions could result in substantial economic dislocation unless mitigated by 
targeted investment or retraining programs. The stability in wages – despite firm and job losses – 
suggests that remaining jobs may be higher-skilled or more capital-intensive, reinforcing the 
need for human capital development strategies. Additionally, the relatively resilient performance 
of Wisconsin suggests a potential case study for how public and private sector collaboration can 
sustain a mature industry in transition.

bidirectional relationships unlike constrained EC models – essential for understanding how 
changes in employment, wages, and output interact over time. Forecasts were validated by 
comparing predicted values with actual data for 2016-2018 (see Figures A1-A3). Aside from 
Oregon, which had a larger degree of volatility, the forecasts aligned closely with observed 
trends, affirming the models sustainability for regional industry forecasting. However, some 
limitations should be noted. First, state-level RGDP data is only reported annually, requiring us 
to proxy quarterly output using U.S. RGDP (Mei et al., 2010). This trade-off allowed for more 
robust forecasting but introduces some aggregation error. Price data was another constraint, so 
we used the 2017 PPI for lumber and wood products, as done by Baek (2012) and Nanang (2000) 
in the absence of more granular price data. Moreover, there was no data available for capital, 
meaning we could not account for how capital investments might affect mill operations and log 
demand. This omission is important since it misses the key balance between labor and capital, 
and how improvements in capital via automation and efficiency significantly changed demand. 
Further research incorporating capital data would be highly valuable. Furthermore, our analysis 
focused on six states; future research should examine other forest-intensive regions such as 
Kentucky, Louisiana, and Tennessee since they have consistently high LQ values for all three 
industries. 
 
A second limitation is the sample size of the available data: 72 observations for NAICS 
113 and 60 observations for NAICS 321 and 322. While these sample sizes are comparable to 
previous studies, additional data would enhance statistical power robustness, accordingly 
improving the reliability of forecasts (Toppinen, 1998; Malaty et al., 2006). The literature does 
not have a consensus on the ideal sample size for econometric forecasting, but related studies 
often work with between 57 to 69 observations (Toppinen, 1998; Nanang, 2000; Stordal & 
Nyrud, 2003; Hetemaki et al., 2004; Parajuli & Chang, 2015; Parajuli et al., 2016; Gendron et al., 
2019;). Some related studies have been conducted with less than five years of data (Toppinen, 
1998; Stordal & Nyrud, 2003; Yin & Xu, 2003; Malaty et al., 2006).  
 
4.5. Future Study 
 
While this study focused on six of the most forest-dependent U.S. states, we attempted to 
expand this analysis to a broader regional scope – covering approximately 15 states for NAICS 
113, and 40 states for NAICS 321 and 322. However, the results were inconclusive, likely due to 
heterogeneity within each region, potential differences in data quality, and potential regional 
economic dynamics. Future research should aim to refine the structure of regional analysis 
beyond geographic groupings, this could include shared forest composition, mill capacity and/or 
labor force characteristics. This could be further enhanced with the use of spatial econometric 
techniques which could potentially capture spillover effects across neighboring states. These 
interlinked supply chains and/or shared labor markets are a crucial factor for policymakers to 
manage.

4.6. Suggested Policy Actions 
 
As we have illustrated, there are distinct differences at the state and/or industry level, so 
policy will need to be tailored to particular scenarios. Accordingly, we provide suggested policy 
actions per scenario: 
 
Scenario 1: Output stable; employment and firm count declining; wages rising modestly 
(e.g., NAICS 113 – Forestry and Logging) 
 
When output remains steady despite declining employment and number of firms, 
policymakers should prioritize workforce retraining and upskilling programs. These 
initiatives can help transition displaced workers into higher-skill roles within or beyond 
the sector, improving labor market resilience without compromising productivity.  
 
Scenario 2: Employment and wages increasing; firm count declining or leveling 
(e.g., NAICS 321 – Wood Product Manufacturing) 
 
In cases where labor market indicators are strong but the number of firms is shrinking, 
policymakers should consider lowering entry barriers, offering mill modernization grants, 
and/or providing technical assistance to encourage entrepreneurship and bolster firm 
survival. Supporting firm formation and continuity can help sustain the employment 
momentum already underway (and respectively wages). 
 
Scenario 3: Wages stable; employment, output, and firm count declining 
(e.g., NAICS 322 – Paper Manufacturing) 
 
This scenario suggests structural contraction. In such cases, targeted support from 
policymakers could take two forms. First, targeted investment in at-risk firms could 
prevent further losses in firm counts, and respectively employment and output. Second, 
transition assistance and retraining programs could redeploy affected workers into other 
segments of the forest sector or adjacent industries, preserving community-level 
economic vitality. 
 
5. Summary & Conclusions 
 
During the past three decades, industries in the U.S. forest sector have faced substantial 
challenges due profound structural changes driven by mill closures, technological shifts, and 
changes in global demand (Toppinen & Kuuluvainen, 2010; Crandall et al., 2017; Woodall et al. 
2011; Conrad et al., 2018; Hodges et al., 2011; Poudel & Dahal, 2025). These changes have 
disproportionately impacted rural communities where forestry-related industries serve as 
economic linchpins. This study provides region-specific forward-looking evidence to support 
more informed, proactive policymaking, particularly in rural areas where these industries are 
economic cornerstones. We forecast key macroeconomic trends for three core forest sector 
industries within the U.S.: forestry and logging (NAICS 113), wood manufacturing (NAICS 321) 
and paper manufacturing (NAICS 322). We focused on six key states as being the most forest-


## Page 23


23 
 
dependent in the country, Alabama, Arkansas, Maine, Mississippi, Oregon and Wisconsin – 
identified using a location quotient measure and supported in prior literature.  
 
To capture both supply and demand dynamics, we modeled state-level industry variables 
including employment, wages, number of firms, output (proxied with state-level real gross 
domestic product), and prices for U.S. lumber and wood products (Andrei & Andrei, 2015). 
Using quarterly data from 2001Q1 to 2018Q4, we constructed 16 state-industry VEC models, 
chosen for their ability to estimate long-run equilibrium relationships in cointegrated time series 
data such as the U.S. forest sector (Nanang, 2000; Stordal & Nyrud, 2003; Yin & Baek, 2005; 
Song et al., 2011). While data constraints precluded the inclusion of stumpage prices or capital 
investment variables, the modeling framework selected remains one of the most appropriate for 
this sector. In addition to forecasting, the VEC model enables insight into cross-sector 
interactions, such as how labor market shifts may influence output or firm dynamics (Zhang & 
Xie, 2019). 
 
Our forecasts indicate that the forestry and logging industry is likely to experience 
continued declines in both employment and firm counts, even as output remains steady and 
wages gradually rise. This suggests a form of industrial resilience that may not translate to labor 
market stability, thereby highlighting the need for targeted state-level policy responses to 
mitigate job losses and strengthen rural economies. Policymakers should anticipate the ripple 
effects of declining employment in mill regions and consider economic diversification strategies 
to stabilize community tax bases and employment. The wood product manufacturing industry 
exhibits similar trends, but with a more optimistic outlook: employment is expected to rise. This 
trend suggests a comparatively resilient subsector where less intervention may be needed, 
allowing policymakers to reallocate limited public resources to more vulnerable subsectors, such 
as the forestry and logging sector. In contrast, the paper manufacturing industry presents a more 
pessimistic trajectory, with declines expected in employment, output, and the number of firms, 
while wages remain stagnant. Note that this trend is especially severe for Maine. These 
projections signal a need for proactive intervention to buffer communities at risk – through 
retraining programs, business development grants, or infrastructure investments that support 
industrial adaptation.  
 
Given the regional diversity of the states analyzed – spanning the Northeast, North 
Central, South Central, and Pacific West – further research is warranted to explore regional 
economic differences in greater detail. Additionally, these findings have implications beyond the 
U.S., particularly for other resource-dependent economies such as Canada, Germany, Sweden, 
and Finland (Hetemaki et al., 2004; Malaty et al., 2006; Zhang & Parajuli, 2016). More broadly, 
the methodology used in this study can be adapted to other industries that serve as economic 
anchors for specific regions, such as mining, agriculture, and energy production (Andrei & 
Andrei, 2015; Apostu et al., 2022; Asnawi et al., 2025). This offers a valuable tool for analyzing 
economic resilience in industry-dependent communities and essential information for policy 
makers in these industries outside of the forestry sector. Evidence-based forecasting is especially 
vital in designing resilient policy responses tailored to local conditions at both the state and 
industry level.
