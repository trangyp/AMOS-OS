Foundation models may exhibit staged progression in novel CBRN threat disclosure 
 
Kevin M. Esvelt 
Media Lab, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge MA USA 
 
The extent to which foundation models can disclose novel chemical, biological, radiation, and 
nuclear (CBRN) threats to expert users is unclear due to a lack of test cases. I leveraged the unique 
opportunity presented by an upcoming publication describing a novel catastrophic biothreat – 
“Technical Report on Mirror Bacteria: Feasibility and Risks” – to conduct a small controlled study 
before it became public. Graduate-trained biologists tasked with predicting the consequences of 
releasing mirror E. coli showed no significant differences in rubric-graded accuracy using Claude 
Sonnet 3.5 new (n=10) or web search only (n=2); both groups scored comparably to a web baseline 
(28 and 43 versus 36). However, Sonnet reasoned correctly when prompted by a report author, 
but a smaller model, Haiku 3.5, failed even with author guidance (80 versus 5). These results 
suggest distinct stages of model capability: Haiku is unable to reason about mirror life even with 
threat-aware expert guidance (Stage 1), while Sonnet correctly reasons only with threat-aware 
prompting (Stage 2). Continued advances may allow future models to disclose novel CBRN threats 
to naive experts (Stage 3) or unskilled users (Stage 4). While mirror life represents only one case 
study, monitoring new models' ability to reason about privately known threats may allow 
protective measures to be implemented before widespread disclosure. 
 
 
Introduction 
The advent of increasingly capable large 
language models (LLMs) has raised concerns 
that they might expand access to known 
biological weapons, but models might also 
provide 
novel 
scientific 
insights 
into 
previously unrecognized ways of causing 
harm1. 
While 
recent 
studies 
have 
demonstrated that current LLMs can exceed 
domain expert performance on standardized 
tests2–5, 
CBRN 
benchmarks6, 
and certain 
difficult challenges7, their capacity to generate 
truly world-class scientific insights –  which 
may be necessary to “raise the ceiling” of 
biological risk8 – remains poorly understood. 
The challenge of assessing novel threat 
disclosure 
capacity 
is 
heightened 
by a 
near-total dearth of test cases that require 
world-class expertise, can be evaluated using 
well-defined criteria, and may be safely 
disclosed. The recent emergence of mirror 
biology – the theoretical creation of cells 
composed entirely of molecules with inverted 
chirality, with potentially unprecedented and 
catastrophic consequences for multicellular 
life9,10 – provided a unique opportunity to test 
whether models could provide novel insights 
in advance of publication.  
The foundations of mirror biology span 
multiple 
scientific 
domains, 
requiring 
sophisticated reasoning across biochemistry, 
molecular biology, microbiology, immunology, 
ecology, 
and 
evolutionary 
dynamics. 
A 
299-page Technical Report10 published on 12 
December 2024 established clear evaluation 
criteria concerning the anticipated health and 
ecological 
consequences, 
including 
mechanistic 
explanations, 
that 
were 
unknown to the models during training. 
Initial skepticism by most Technical Report 
authors that mirror bacteria might prove 
catastrophic suggests that these insights were 
non-obvious even to leading biologists. 
To leverage this unique opportunity to 
evaluate whether current models can provide 
world-class 
threat 
assessment, 
twelve 
individuals 
with 
graduate 
training 
in 
biological sciences, all broadly unaware of the 
implications of mirror life, were tasked with 
predicting the consequences of creating and 
releasing mirror E. coli bacteria (Box 1) with 
LLM assistance or via web search alone.

Methods 
Participants, none of whom were aware of the 
Technical Report, were randomly assigned to 
use Claude Sonnet 3.5 new 20241022 (n=10) as 
well as conventional web search, or web 
search alone (n=2) for a total of one hour. To 
establish 
an 
upper 
bound 
on 
model 
performance, I prompted Sonnet 3.5 and 
Haiku 3.5 to reason about the questions while 
attempting to refrain from providing any 
relevant information on graded outcomes or 
mechanisms. As a handicap, the models’ 
summaries 
of those conversations were 
submitted after 15 minutes. 
Responses were evaluated using a two-pass 
grading rubric derived from the Technical 
Report (Appendix A). In pass 1, Sonnet 3.5 
extracted 
relevant 
phrases 
from 
each 
response 
to 
remove 
complicating 
information. In pass 2, five separate model 
instances graded each set of extracted phrases 
using the rubric, which focused on accurate 
predictions and mechanistic explanations. 
To provide a baseline of readily available 
public information on the web, the most 
relevant article – a WIRED piece from 2010 
describing 
the 
potential 
use 
of 
mirror 
cyanobacteria 
for geoengineering – was 
graded using the same methodology. 
 
Figure 1 | Graded responses to study questions about mirror-image E. coli release 
consequences. Participants using Sonnet 3.5 (n=10) performed similarly to those using web search 
(n=2), with both groups scoring near the baseline from a 2010 article on mirror cyanobacteria. 
However, when prompted by a Technical Report author attempting to avoid providing relevant 
insights in the prompt, Sonnet 3.5 achieved substantially higher scores, demonstrating correct threat 
reasoning under expert guidance. The smaller Haiku 3.5 model failed with similar prompting, 
revealing a capability progression that suggests monitoring model performance under expert 
guidance could provide early warning before such reasoning becomes widely accessible.

Results 
 
Participant responses clustered near the web 
baseline 
(36/100), 
with 
no 
significant 
difference between the LLM-assisted group 
(mean=28.5/100) 
and 
web-only 
group 
(mean=43.5/100; 
Welch's 
t 
> 
0.05). The 
highest-scoring 
participant 
incorporated 
information from both the baseline article 
and Wikipedia's mirror life entry. Sonnet 3.5 
clearly failed to help naive experts identify 
the potential threat from mirror E. coli. 
 
Participants working with Sonnet 3.5 were 
most commonly misled by assertions that 
mirror E. coli would be unable to grow due to 
a lack of chiral carbon sources. This framing 
led participants to overlook several key 
factors, especially achiral nutrient availability 
– including relatively rapid E. coli growth on 
achiral minimal media containing glycerol, 
acetate, or glycine – and the potential to 
bypass 
chirality-dependent 
immune 
responses to acquire nutrients from hosts that 
are inaccessible to existing bacteria. While 
most participant responses recognized the 
likelihood of predation resistance, possibly 
due to the model's training on the 2010 
Bohannon article, they typically missed the 
critical insight that slower growth does not 
preclude 
invasive 
colonization 
when 
mortality rates are sufficiently reduced.  
 
In contrast, when I prompted Sonnet 3.5 to 
reason about the questions while attempting 
to avoid providing any information that 
would be rewarded during grading, the 
model-generated summary scored markedly 
higher 
(80.2/100). 
This 
demonstrates an 
important dynamic: threat-aware users can 
elicit sophisticated insights from capable 
models, even while attempting to avoid 
conveying their own knowledge, before naive 
users can access those same insights.  
 
Interestingly, when I prompted the smaller 
Haiku 3.5 model under the same conditions, 
its summary received a much worse score. 
The model asserted that mirror bacteria 
would not be viable in any environment 
(Appendix 
B), 
clearly 
contradicting 
the 
Technical 
Report, 
and 
stood 
by 
this 
assessment even when repeatedly asked to 
explain 
and 
provide 
examples. 
This 
demonstrates that even threat-aware expert 
elicitation cannot always coax models to 
identify mechanistic threats without overtly 
correcting 
misconceptions. 
Indeed, 
such 
misconceptions may represent a key barrier 
limiting threat-naive users, even if they are 
technical experts. 
 
Whereas 
threat-naive expert participants 
appeared to largely accept Sonnet’s initial 
assertions 
(Appendix 
C), 
I 
requested 
extrapolations 
and 
examples, 
then 
reappraisal 
regardless 
of accuracy. This 
enabled the model to reason about each 
relevant concept without providing overt 
mechanistic hints in the prompts that might 
impact its graded predictions. However, the 
resulting score almost certainly benefited 
from my ability to avoid irrelevant tangents 
that 
likely 
diverted 
the 
threat-naive 
participants from core considerations.  
Discussion 
 
While the small sample size limits statistical 
analysis, the core observations of this study 
are clear. First, Claude Sonnet 3.5 could not 
independently identify or mechanistically 
explain 
the 
catastrophic implications of 
mirror life when prompted by domain 
experts, except when guided by an individual 
already aware of those implications. Second, 
the smaller and less capable Haiku 3.5 was 
unable to articulate the concerning outcome 
at all when given similar prompting.  
These findings suggest that models pass 
through 
distinct stages: from failing to 
identify a catastrophic threat even with 
threat-aware expert guidance (Haiku 3.5), to 
correctly reasoning about the threat only 
when guided by an expert who is already 
aware of it (Sonnet 3.5).

Figure 2 | Suggested capability framework for disclosing novel catastrophic biothreats. Stage 1 
models such as Haiku 3.5 cannot reason about mirror life even when prompted by a threat-aware 
expert attempting to feign ignorance. Stage 2 models such as Sonnet 3.5 may produce useful 
mechanistic insights under those conditions, but not when prompted by naive experts. Hypothetical 
Stage 3 models could assist expert biologists seeking novel weapons, while Stage 4 models could 
disclose new methods of causing catastrophe to even naive unskilled prompters. Because 
disclosures are probabilistic and skill levels continuous, model capabilities presumably reflect 
gradients rather than discrete stages, are likely to differ across various threats, and may skip stages: 
a new model might be Stage 3 for a given threat even though its predecessor was clearly Stage 1. 
Even so, identifying disclosure risks at Stage 2 or 3 may provide time to implement safeguards. 
 
Subsequent 
stages 
might permit correct 
reasoning and disclosure when prompted by 
a 
threat-naive 
domain 
expert 
(e.g. 
a 
bioweapons researcher), and independently 
generating 
and 
reasoning 
about 
the 
catastrophic scenario when prompted by a 
naive unskilled user (Fig. 2). 
The transition from "cannot identify or 
mechanistically explain a catastrophic threat" 
to "can explain when guided" may provide 
early warning that a model is nearing 
dangerous capabilities – and a corresponding 
opportunity to implement safeguards before 
future models disclose catastrophic threats to 
experts or to unskilled users. However, 
several caveats apply. 
Limitations 
 
First, with only twelve participants plus a 
technical report author evaluating a single 
threat over the course of an hour, this 
experiment is best viewed as a case study. 
Real world threat actors may be more 
persistent, and these findings are suggestive, 
not definitive.

## Page 5


Second, the mirror life problem may not 
generalize to other catastrophic biothreats. 
All 43 authors of the Technical Report and 
Policy Forum were initially skeptical that 
mirror bacteria could plausibly flourish in the 
wild; 
for 
many, 
extensive 
investigation, 
ultimately presented in a 299-page report, 
was 
required 
to 
reach 
the 
opposite 
conclusion. While mirror life represents a 
notably challenging test requiring integration 
across multiple scientific domains, other 
novel threats may present different cognitive 
challenges. A model's ability to reason about 
one type of threat may not predict its 
capabilities regarding others. 
Third, we lack examples of models that 
succeed at the mirror life challenge when 
prompted by threat-naive domain experts or 
unskilled individuals. An even smaller pilot 
by a developer given advance warning of the 
mirror life publication suggested that another 
frontier model also failed to reach Stage 3. It 
is difficult to predict the capability gains 
required to move between the hypothesized 
stages. A model could conceivably go from 
Stage 1 to Stage 3 or Stage 2 to Stage 4 in a 
single generation. 
Fourth, it is challenging to determine whether 
any 
given 
prompting 
attempt 
by 
a 
threat-aware 
expert 
is 
leaking 
critical 
information to the model. That Sonnet is 
more capable than Haiku is abundantly clear, 
but we cannot be confident that both models 
received 
equivalent 
assistance 
from 
prompting despite my best efforts.  
Finally, 
even 
if 
threat-aware 
expert 
prompting can provide early warning of the 
future capacity to disclose a privately known 
biological threat to others, it would be 
challenging to recruit experts to evaluate each 
new model and to perform those evaluations 
with appropriate information security. 
 
Future directions 
Given the magnitude of harm that could 
result from frontier models freely disclosing 
mechanistic 
insights 
or 
designs 
for 
catastrophic biothreats such as Wildfire or 
Stealth pandemics, and the potential for 
recently 
developed 
and 
future 
model 
safeguards to effectively mitigate these risks, 
monitoring 
newly 
trained 
models 
for 
dangerous capabilities appears desirable.  
Leading developers could invite leading 
biologists with private security concerns to 
red-team 
new 
models 
in 
environments 
certified as secure by a trusted third party. A 
more scalable alternative might involve the 
use 
of 
existing 
“prompter” 
LLMs 
to 
exhaustively explore conversational paths 
relevant to creative misuse, followed by 
secure analysis of the transcripts by “spotter” 
LLMs 
trained 
by 
leading 
biologists 
to 
recognize a specific privately known threat. A 
positive response from any spotter would 
trigger notification of the trainer, analysis of 
the model stage, and the implementation of 
protective measures in that model and/or 
subsequent, more capable models.  
All nations and AI developers share a 
common 
interest 
in 
preventing 
the 
widespread disclosure of feasible designs for 
novel CBRN threats. Ideally, robust early 
warning protocols would be paired with 
mechanisms for sharing mitigation strategies 
across the industry without disclosing the 
underlying 
threats. 
Leading 
developers 
should consider pioneering standards for 
monitoring and inventing safeguards that 
could be widely adopted without proliferating 
dangerous knowledge.
