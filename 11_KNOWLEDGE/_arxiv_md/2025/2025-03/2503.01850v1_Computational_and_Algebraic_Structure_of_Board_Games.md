Abstract
We provide two methodologies in the area of computation theory to solve optimal
strategies for board games such as Xi Gua Qi and Go. From experimental results,
we find relevance to graph theory, matrix representation, and mathematical con-
sciousness. We prove that the decision strategy of movement for Xi Gua Qi and
Chinese checker games belongs to a subset that is neither a ring nor a group over
set Y={-1,0,1}. Additionally, the movement for any board game with two players
belongs to a subset that is neither a ring nor a group from the razor of Occam.
We derive the closed form of the transition matrix for any board game with two
players such as chess and Chinese chess. We discover that the element of the
transition matrix belongs to a rational number. We propose a different method-
ology based on algebra theory to analyze the complexity of board games in their
entirety, instead of being limited solely to endgame results. It is probable that
similar decision processes of people may also belong to a matrix representation
that is neither a ring nor a group.

1 Introduction
Pebble games (Kfoury and Stolboushkin, 1997; Lee and Streinu, 2008) and relational
games (Venema, 1990) are first order logic (Kfoury and Stolboushkin, 1997; Halpern
and Weissman, 2008). In this paper, we use a traditional board game, Xi Gua Qi, to
discuss the phenomena about mathematics and computation theory. The playing rules
of Xi Gua Qi are similar to the game Go, such that when an opponent’s pieces are
blocked, they become captured, and the endgame result has three possible states of a
win, loss, or draw.
Go and Xi Gua Qi are tri-valued logic, differing from Chinese checkers which is
fourth-valued logic. We proposed two methods that can be applied to solve games such
as Xi Gua Qi and Go.
In addition, we demonstrate that the movement of pieces for any board game
with two players such as Chinese chess, chess, or Go belongs to a subset of algebra
structure that is neither a ring nor a group, in which the transition matrix is only with
different dimensions of complexity over Q, the rational numbers. We apply matrix
representation theory in abstract algebra to analyze the complexity of board games
as a novel methodology to analyze the complexity of whole board games.

or CG.

3.1 Method 1: Game Tree
We constructed an objective function that optimized the degree of freedom (DoF), i.e.,
the number of game pieces that are not captured by the opponent. The optimization
objective value for a player is to maximize its DoF while minimizing its opponent’s
DoF. It is a general algorithm that can be applied with a min-max game tree (Rivest,

3.2 Method 2: Probabilistic Neural Network Rule Models
We proposed PBCR1 and PBCR2 algorithms (Hwang and Oyang, 2023) to extract
probabilistic Boolean rule models from Deep Neural Networks (DNN), which can also
be applied to predict game movement. It basically can be applied to auto-inference
(Hwang and Oyang, 2023).
We recorded the 100 playing games of pieces movements, and among these games,
the win ratio of red color to yellow color is 50 to 50, and as a result we regarded it
as a binary classification problem. There were a total of 4985 piece movements, with
2519 win (positive) cases and 2466 loss (negative) cases. The training features of this
problem were the movement of pieces. The input was the current chess state, the next
state, and the current playing color. The current state was composed of 21 nodes.
Each game piece used a triple value to denote it, red, yellow, and the empty game
pieces respectively. We use 2 bits to store the triple value. The current and the next
state needed 42 nodes, and they were composed of 42×2=84 bits, with 1 bit also used
to denote the current playing color. As a result, there were a total of 85 inputs and 1
binary output (win or loss).

5 Discussion
We found some interesting phenomena during different kinds of board games. If we
regard each snapshot of the board game as a vector point, it can be regarded as the
vertex of a graph. Every piece movement is the edge of the graph, The edge is a
function mapping or the transition matrix f : A 7→A, where A = X21, X = {1, 2, 3},
in which 1 and 2 represent the different colors of the chess pieces, and 3 represents
the empty, such that the whole game composed a graph. The win or loss can be either

a directed acyclic graph (DAG) or a cyclic graph (CG), such that if there were no
duplicated vertices of the graph, it would be a DAG, and a CG otherwise. However,
the drawn game is a CG. The cardinality of the vertex would be infinite but with a
finite element of the vertex set. Therefore, the graph composed from a drawn game
must contain duplicate vertices. As a result, it is a CG.
Theorem 1 (Neither A Ring nor A Group) If we view the snapshot of the board game as
a vertex of a graph, the edge of this graph represents a piece movement, and the edge is an
operator (a matrix) or a function mapping f, f : A 7→A, where A = X21, X = {1, 2, 3},
1, and 2 are the different colors of the chess pieces, and 3 represents the empty, then the
following properties hold:
1. There exists a function mapping f belonging to a subset of D = M21(Y ) \
{I21×21, 021×21, Z21×21}, where M21(Y ) is the set of 21 × 21 matrices over Y ,
Y = {−1, 0, 1}, I21×21 is the identity matrix, 021×21 is the zero matrix, and Z21×21
is a matrix that contains at least one zero row vector.
2. M21(Y ) is a noncommutative ring in abstract algebra.
3. M21(Y ) is an abelian group under addition.
4. D = M21(Y ) \ {I21×21, 021×21, Z21×21} is neither a ring nor a group.
Proof 1. For a vertex a = [ai], where i = 0, . . . , 20, if we move the game piece from p
to q, then either ap = 1 or ap = 2. Furthermore, aq = 3 as q is empty. The function
mapping f is a 21 × 21 matrix M21.
First, consider the case that ap = 1. Suppose that after the game movement
from p to q, we will get a vertex b = [bi] = M21a, with the properties that for
game pieces bp = 3 and bq = 1. Pieces with indices k ∈K are captured with this
movement, resulting in bk = 3. Similarly, if the movement does not cause the game
pieces to be captured, then bi = ai for ∀i ̸= p, q, and i /∈K.
Next, we show how to construct the matrix M21 = [σij] with Occam’s razor
principle. Suppose that the game piece s belongs to the opponent, i.e., as = 2, for
the p-th row vector rp of M21, we can set σpp = 1, σps = 1, and σpj = 0, ∀j ̸= p, s.
Thus, we can construct the transformation that bp = 3.
For the q-th row vector rq of M21, we can set σqq = 1, σqs = −1, and σqj =
0, ∀q ̸= p, s. Hence, we can construct the transformation where bq = 1.
For the pieces k ∈K that are captured, which can be obtained from the tree
from Method 1, the k-th row vector rk of M21 is of the form σkk = 1, σkp = 1, and
σkj = 0, ∀j ̸= k. Therefore, we can construct the transformation in which bk = 3.
For the pieces i that are not captured and do not equal p, q, and do not belong
to K, the i-th row vector ri of M21 is of the form σii = 1, σij = 0, ∀j ̸= i, ∀i ̸= p, q,
and i /∈K.
Hence, we construct the transformation that bi = ai for ∀i ̸= p, q, and i /∈K.
Similarly, we can construct the matrix M21 for the case that ap = 2. Finally, we
observed that the element of the matrix M21, σij ∈{−1, 0, 1}.
The board vertex will be the same with omitting a movement by the trans-
formation of an identity matrix, I21×21. The board vertex will be filled with the

6 Conclusions and Future Works
We are able to discuss the complexity of different board games from Theorem 1, 2,
and
3. From Theorem 1, we found that the movements in Xi Gua Qi belong to
D = M21(Y ) \ {I21×21, 021×21, Z21×21}. From Theorem 2, we found that the game
movements of Chinese checker belong to Mn(Y ) \ {In×n, 0n×n, Zn×n}, where n is the
number of the game board lattices. From Theorem 3, we found that the movements
of any board game with two players belong to Mn(Q) \ {In×n, 0n×n, Zn×n}, where Q
represents the rational numbers and n is the numbers of the game board lattices.
In Theorem 1, we proved that the set of the function mapping f is neither a ring
nor a group. It is an interesting topic about the algebraic structure of the operator
f, for example, whether it is with an interesting structure similar to SU(2) ( Moffat,
2020; Rovelli and Vidotto, 2015). We found the transition matrix is composed of
the element from set Y = {−1, 0, 1}, and it is a sparse matrix. Chess games might
be related to reductive groups (Minchenko and Ovchinnikov, 2011), compact groups

(Varj´u, 2012), Lie groups (Breuillard, 2004; Versendaal, 2019) and random walks on
groups and random transformations (Furman, 2002) in abstract algebra.

on the dual space (Moffat, 2020). We may develop a quantum game theory method
(Zhang, 2012; Ghosh, 2021a; Ghosh, 2021b; Ghosh, 2021c) via an approach that is
similar to computational spin networks (Moffat, 2020). Every state corresponds to a
vector. It is probable that the human decision-making process for other strategies not
limited to playing chess can be represented as a matrix operator that neither belongs to
a ring nor a group, Mn(R)\{In×n, 0n×n, Zn×n}, where R represents the real numbers.
