Short-term AI literacy intervention does not reduce over-reliance on incorrect ChatGPT 
recommendations 
 
Brett Puppart, Jaan Aru 
Institute of Computer Science, University of Tartu 
brett.puppart@gmail.com 
 
 
Abstract 
In this study, we examined whether a short-form AI literacy intervention could reduce the 
adoption of incorrect recommendations from large language models. High school seniors 
were randomly assigned to either a control or an intervention group, which received an 
educational text explaining ChatGPT’s working mechanism, limitations, and proper use. 
Participants solved math puzzles with the help of ChatGPT’s recommendations, which were 
incorrect in half of the cases. Results showed that students adopted incorrect suggestions 
52.1% of the time, indicating widespread over-reliance. The educational intervention did not 
significantly reduce over-reliance. Instead, it led to an increase in ignoring ChatGPT’s correct 
recommendations. We conclude that the usage of ChatGPT is associated with over-reliance 
and it is not trivial to increase AI literacy to counter over-reliance.

Introduction 
Large language models (LLM) are probabilistic models that have been trained to 
predict the next token in a text sequence (Naveed et al., 2024). High capabilities, ease of use, 
and commercial promotion have caused a widespread adoption of these systems in various 
fields, such as medicine (Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023), science (Liang et al., 2024), and 
education (Valova et al., 2024). Countries including South Korea (GEM Report, 2025) and 
Estonia (ERR, 2025) plan to integrate AI systems with public education in the hope of 
improving the quality of learning. Researchers have argued that LLMs could revolutionize 
education through personalized learning, immediate feedback, and growing accessibility 
(Diab Idris et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024).  
Despite these potential benefits, LLMs carry the risk of over-reliance, which has been 
observed in several recent studies (Amoozadeh et al., 2024; Valova et al., 2024). Constant 
access to ready-made answers may discourage students from actively developing their 
cognitive abilities (Aru, 2025; Dubey et al., 2024; Kasneci et al., 2023). Specifically, 
over-reliance has been shown to impair critical thinking (Duhaylungsod & Chavez, 2023), 
which is mediated by cognitive offloading that occurs when learners delegate thinking to AI 
systems (Gerlich, 2025). This external dependency could lead to metacognitive laziness that 
potentially hinders learners’ ability to self-regulate and engage deeply in learning (Fan et al., 
2025; Fernandes et al., 2024; Laak et al., 2024). Additionally, using LLMs may impair 
semantic memory (Dubey et al., 2024), as students begin to remember where to find 
information rather than the information itself (Sparrow et al., 2011).  
Beyond the cognitive consequences described above, over-reliance on LLMs can also 
manifest behaviorally as the uncritical acceptance of their outputs – even when incorrect or 
misleading. LLMs can generate inaccurate outputs (Borji, 2023) and may incorporate biases, 
such as gender stereotypes (Kotek et al., 2023) and political biases (Rozado, 2023), which 
users might mistakenly accept due to the confident style of LLM-generated content (Azaria et 
al., 2024). When students habitually trust information provided by ChatGPT without 
verification, they not only risk remembering misinformation but also reinforce cognitive 
passivity, further exacerbating the negative impact on their learning and cognitive 
development.  
To effectively address the cognitive and behavioral problems associated with 
over-reliance, researchers have emphasized the importance of increasing students’ AI literacy 
(Kasneci et al., 2023; Lo, 2023). AI literacy refers to the users’ knowledge of AI systems and

their ability to use them effectively and responsibly (Walter, 2024). A review conceptualising 
AI literacy named four crucial sub-skills: knowledge and understanding, usage, critical 
evaluation, and ethics (Ng et al., 2021). Walter’s (2024) conceptualisation also emphasized 
the potential risks and limitations of AI systems. More specifically, as it relates to LLMs, the 
construct of AI literacy comprises technical proficiency, critical evaluation of its outputs, 
communication and prompt engineering, creative application, and ethics (Lee & Park, 2024).  
There is some empirical evidence that enhancing AI literacy mitigates over-reliance. 
Individuals with higher background knowledge in AI tend to be less likely to exhibit both 
over-reliance and under-reliance (Horowitz & Kahn, 2023). Jacobs et al. (2021) found that 
clinicians who reported their familiarity with machine learning as “not at all familiar” were 
seven times more likely to choose a treatment that aligned with AI recommendations 
compared to clinicians who responded with “extremely familiar”. Some evidence has also 
come from intervention studies. For example, reliance disclaimers–such as explicit reminders 
to verify the information provided by the AI system–have been shown to decrease both types 
of negative reliance (Bo et al., 2024). Similarly, Chiang & Yin (2021) showed that a short text 
cautioning participants about AI’s performance drop on novel data significantly reduced 
over-reliance. Additionally, Morrill & Noetel (2023) measured the impact of a short, 
five-minute text designed to enhance AI literacy. The intervention decreased both over- and 
under-reliance on incorrect AI recommendations (Morrill & Noetel, 2023). 
Nevertheless, the question of how enhancing AI literacy impacts over-reliance 
specifically on LLMs remains underexplored in experimental studies. Previous research has 
observed a relationship between trust and reliance (Klingbeil et al., 2024). Thus, it is possible 
that individuals’ trust for simulated AI systems, used in previous experimental studies, is 
different from LLMs, raising questions about the generalizability of the results. In addition, 
most studies on over-reliance have been conducted with university students (Amoozadeh et 
al., 2024; Morrill & Noetel, 2023; Walter, 2024). However, several countries will now provide 
LLMs as a tool for secondary education; thus, it will be essential to investigate over-reliance 
among high school students. We aim to fill these gaps in the current literature by focusing on 
an AI literacy intervention tailored for LLMs and measuring its effect on over-reliance and 
under-reliance on high school students.

Method 
Participants 
The study sample comprised 12th-grade Estonian high school students (M = 18.06 
years, SD = 0.23, age range: 18-19 years). The invitations to participate in the study were sent 
to 72 schools, of which 10 participated in the study. Schools that agreed to participate 
forwarded the study invitation to their senior students. A total of 42 students completed the 
questionnaire, but six of them were excluded from the data analysis for the following reasons: 
Five participants were excluded because they read the intervention text in less than 30 
seconds, and one participant was excluded because they completed the entire questionnaire in 
less than six minutes. The final sample consisted of 36 participants, of whom 25 (69.4%) were 
female and 11 were male (30.6%). To incentivize participation, a lottery was held among all 
participants, with five €20 bookstore gift cards as prizes. 
Materials 
To increase AI literacy, we created a short-form educational text, taking inspiration 
from Walter (2024) due to his deeper focus on limitations and risks of AI. The text covered all 
sub-skills of AI literacy as described in Walter (2024), except for ethics, as this aspect is 
addressed in separate guidelines (Table 1). In addition to the text, screenshots illustrating 
examples of ChatGPT hallucinations and other limitations were provided. When designing the 
control text, we aimed to minimize information about AI literacy sub-skills while ensuring it 
still appeared relevant to the study for participants. The control text contained only the first 
part of the text (also presented in the intervention group), which contained only general 
information about ChatGPT (Table 1). The full-text translations from Estonian language are 
provided in Appendix B.

To measure over-reliance, we used Kangaroo math puzzles (Teaduskool, 2022), which 
test not only the knowledge of formulas but also logical and creative reasoning. These puzzles 
were suitable for measuring over-reliance for two reasons. First, each puzzle has one correct 
answer, making over-reliance measurement binary (yes/no) and therefore easy to detect. 
Second, solving these puzzles requires iterative reasoning and slow, analytical thinking. This 
aspect mimics the real-life use of ChatGPT, where fact-checking requires additional effort, 
and blindly trusting ChatGPT’s output is the easier, more tempting alternative. 
We tested several puzzles from the “Cadet” age category, designed for 7th-8th grade 
students, to ensure a consistent level of difficulty while making sure that the puzzles do not 
involve any math that would be hard for 12th-graders. We initiated the conversation with 
ChatGPT 4-o (using default parameters) with the command “solve correctly,” followed by the 
description of the puzzle. We selected eight puzzles, with four featuring correct 
recommendations from ChatGPT and four featuring incorrect recommendations (Appendix 
A). We chose puzzles that were not too simple to encourage interaction with ChatGPT’s 
recommendation. For incorrect recommendations, ChatGPT’s response had to appear 
trustworthy at first but contain errors that should be detected through critical evaluation.  
Procedure 
The study was conducted on the LimeSurvey online platform, with data collection 
taking place in October 2024. Students who wished to participate in the study had to first read 
and provide informed consent, which was presented to them on the landing page of the online 
questionnaire. The study employed an independent-group experimental design with a single 
independent variable that had two conditions: the intervention text and the control text. To 
describe the sample, participants were asked to provide demographic information, including 
gender, age, and school affiliation.  
Participants were then randomly assigned to one of the two groups, where they were 
presented with the independent variable of the study. After reading the texts, participants 
answered self-referential statements on a four-point forced-choice Likert scale, assessing the 
perceived effect of the text on enhancing their AI literacy. The statements followed the 
structure “Compared to before, I believe that after reading the text introducing ChatGPT, I am 
better informed about…” The effectiveness of the intervention was evaluated based on the 
answers to the following three statements:  
1.​ “…how ChatGPT works.” 
2.​ “…what the limitations and risks of ChatGPT are.”

3.​ “…how to use ChatGPT wisely.” 
After these statements, participants received task-solving instructions and proceeded 
to solve math puzzles with ChatGPT’s assistance. The instructions were as follows: “You are 
about to solve math puzzles with the help of ChatGPT. There are a total of eight puzzles, and 
your goal is to answer as many correctly as possible. There is no time limit for answering.”  
Each participant was presented with a randomly ordered puzzle at the top of the 
webpage, followed by a screenshot of ChatGPT’s recommendation (Figure 1). Participants 
had to make an initial decision by answering the question, “Do you use ChatGPT’s 
recommendation?” If they answered “yes," they moved on to the next question. If they 
answered “no," they were given the opportunity to solve the puzzle independently by 
selecting the correct answer from four options. To ensure that participants genuinely engaged 
with ChatGPT’s recommendation rather than automatically selecting “no” to solve the puzzles 
on their own, we removed ChatGPT's suggested answer from the later multiple-choice 
options. After each puzzle, participants rated their perceived confidence on a five-point Likert 
scale (1 = strongly disagree – 5 = strongly agree) in response to the self-referential statement: 
“I believe my answer to the previous puzzle was correct.”

The primary dependent variable of the study was over-reliance, which we 
operationally defined as a scenario where ChatGPT provided an incorrect recommendation 
and the participant adopted it as their answer (Figure 2). We also measured under-reliance, 
which we operationally defined as a scenario where the participant did not adopt ChatGPT’s 
correct recommendation. Human error was measured when a participant ignored the incorrect 
recommendation but later on chose the wrong answer.  
 
Figure 2. A decision pathway for reliance on ChatGPT's recommendations. The outcomes are 
categorized into under-reliance, appropriate reliance, and over-reliance based on the 
correctness of ChatGPT’s recommendation and the user’s decision to rely on it. 
  
Given that over-reliance has been associated with fast thinking (Buçinca et al., 2021), 
we also measured the initial decision time across all tasks (dt). The assumption was that the 
longer a participant spent deliberating their initial decision, the less likely they were to exhibit 
over-reliance and under-reliance. We used three different metrics of dt: initial decision time 
across all puzzles (dttotal); initial decision time for puzzles with incorrect recommendations 
(dtincorrect); and initial decision time for puzzles with correct recommendations (dtcorrect). Beyond 
initial decision time, we also measured the total time spent reading the texts and the total time 
spent solving the puzzles. All time metrics were reported in minutes.  
Ethics 
This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee for Human Studies at the 
University of Tartu (Approval No. 391/T-14). All participants read detailed instructions and 
provided informed consent before taking part in the study. The study was presented to 
participants under the title “Students’ motivation in solving mathematics puzzles with the help 
of ChatGPT.” This was done to prevent bias – if participants were aware of the actual aim of

the study, it might have compromised the validity of the results. At the end of the experiment, 
a debriefing session was conducted in which participants were informed about the actual 
purpose of the study. This ensured the ethical integrity of the research. 
Data analysis 
The data analysis was conducted using the open-source statistical software JASP 
(version 0.19.1) to examine the relationships between variables. The significance level for all 
analyses was set at p =.05. The normality assumption was considered to be violated when 
(skewness and kurtosis |values| > 2), and equality of variance was violated when (Levene’s 
test: p <.05).  
To analyze over-reliance, under-reliance, perceived confidence, and intervention 
effectiveness, we used an independent-samples Student’s t-test to compare the intervention 
and control groups. When the normality assumption was violated, we applied the 
non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, and when homogeneity of variances was violated, we 
used Welch’s t-test. For the Student’s t-test and Welch’s t-test, we reported effect sizes using 
Cohen’s d (d), while for the Mann-Whitney U test, we reported effect sizes using the 
rank-biserial correlation (rrb). We interpreted effect sizes according to Cohen’s (1988) 
guidelines: for Cohen’s d, 0.20 represents a small effect, 0.50 a medium effect, and 0.80 a 
large effect. For rrb we interpreted the results according to standard correlation guidelines, 
described below.  
To analyze the relationship between initial decision time and reliance, we conducted 
correlation analyses. As the normality assumption was violated, we used Spearman’s 
correlation coefficient (rs). Effect sizes for r, rs and rrb were interpreted using Cohen’s (1988) 
guidelines, where values of 0.10, 0.30, 0.50 represent small, medium, and large effects, 
respectively.  
 
Results 
The results included 36 students, with 19 in the intervention group and 17 in the 
control group. On average, participants took 23.6 minutes to complete the questionnaire. The 
intervention text was read for an average of 4.3 minutes, while the control text was read for 
0.9 minutes. This means that on average, the length of the intervention was 3.4 minutes.  
The mean rate of over-reliance among all participants was 52.1% (SD = 28.90) and 
under-reliance 28.5% (SD = 24.00). We conducted an independent-samples t-test to compare 
the rate of over-reliance and under-reliance in the intervention group (Group 1) and the

To assess participants’ self-reported perceptions of the intervention's effectiveness in 
enhancing AI literacy, we conducted Student’s t-tests (Figure 4). Results indicated that 
participants in the intervention group reported significantly increased knowledge in the 
“Usage” category (M = 3.42, SD = 0.77) compared to the control group (M = 2.77, SD = 
0.97), t(34) = 2.26, p = .030, d = 0.76. Similarly, the intervention group reported significantly 
increased knowledge in the “Working mechanism” category (M = 3.37, SD = 0.76) compared 
to the control group (M = 2.77, SD = 0.90), t(34) = 2.09, p = .044, d = 0.70. For both of these 
categories, the effect sizes were medium to large. However, while the intervention group 
reported increased knowledge in the “Limitations” category (M = 2.95, SD = 0.91) compared 
to the control group (M = 2.77, SD = 1.03), this difference was not statistically significant, 
t(34) = 0.56, p = .577, d = 0.19.

To assess participants’ self-perceived confidence when relying on ChatGPT 
recommendations, we conducted the Mann-Whitney U test, due to the violation of the 
normality assumption. The results showed that the confidence rating was lower in the 
intervention group (M = 3.58, SD = 0.46) than in the control group (M = 3.67, SD = 0.76), but 
the difference was not statistically significant (U = 188.50, p = .400, rrb = 0.17). 
We examined the average initial decision time across all puzzles (dttotal) between both 
groups. Since the assumption of normality was violated, we again conducted a Mann-Whitney 
U test. The mean dttotal was lower in the intervention group (M = 1.56 minutes, SD = 0.77) 
compared to the control group (M = 1.64 minutes, SD = 1.12), but the difference was not 
statistically significant (U = 179.5, p = .560). 
We also conducted correlation analyses to explore the relationship between initial 
decision times and reliance. Specifically, we analyzed the relationship between dtincorrect and 
over-reliance, as well as dtcorrect and under-reliance. A small to medium negative correlation 
was found between over-reliance and dtincorrect (rs = -0.41, p = .013), suggesting that longer 
decision times were associated with reduced over-reliance. Similarly, a small to medium 
positive correlation was found between under-reliance and dtcorrect (rs = 0.35, p = .038), 
suggesting that longer decision times were associated with increased under-reliance.

Discussion 
The primary aim of this study was to assess the impact of a short-form AI literacy 
intervention on reducing over-reliance in high school students. 
Results demonstrated widespread over-reliance, as students accepted nearly half of 
incorrect ChatGPT recommendations. This is in accordance with previous research 
highlighting that students often do not verify the accuracy of ChatGPT’s outputs and place 
full trust in them (Amoozadeh et al., 2024; Valova et al., 2024). The intervention was not 
effective, as it did not reduce over-reliance but increased under-reliance. This is in contrast 
with previous experimental studies demonstrating that an educational text can decrease 
over-reliance on incorrect AI advice (Chiang & Yin, 2021; Morrill & Noetel, 2023).  
​
There are two explanations for why our intervention did not decrease over-reliance on 
ChatGPT. The first explanation is that the intervention did not effectively increase AI literacy. 
In support of this explanation is the fact that the students in the intervention group did not 
report an increase in knowledge in the “Limitations” category. This is somewhat surprising, as 
a sufficient portion of our text focused on explaining different reasoning errors, 
hallucinations, and biases of LLMs. In addition, we also provided screenshots of real 
instances where ChatGPT made such errors. It is possible that the students were already well 
aware of LLM limitations; hence no increase in knowledge was reported. However, the fact 
that a medium to large increase of knowledge was reported in the categories “Usage” and 
“Working mechanism” supports the hypothesis that the intervention text did sufficiently 
increase the AI literacy of the students. 
​
Another explanation is that over-reliance is not directly affected by AI literacy. 
Although previous studies have shown that people with higher AI literacy exhibit less 
over-reliance (Horowitz & Kahn, 2023), the relationship might be mediated by a third 
variable not addressed in our study. Our intervention failed to increase slow and analytical 
thinking, which is evidenced by non-significant differences in the initial decision time. This 
suggests that the mediating variable between AI literacy and over-reliance might not be the 
increase in slow thinking but something else. Perhaps increased AI literacy does not change 
the thinking habits of individuals (Fernandes et al., 2024), but the decrease in over-reliance 
results from the change in habitual practices, such as prompt engineering, that decreases 
model hallucinations (Tonmoy et al., 2024)  and overconfidence (Xiong et al., 2024).  
​
We suggest that the high prevalence of over-reliance results from an interaction 
between human cognitive tendencies and the properties of LLMs. The human mind is

inherently inclined to minimize cognitive effort (Aru & Rozgonjuk, 2022; David et al., 2022; 
Shenhav et al., 2017). This inclination serves as an adaptive mechanism, allowing individuals 
to conserve mental resources and navigate daily tasks efficiently. However, while this strategy 
is beneficial in many contexts, it can lead to disengagement from more complex and 
demanding mental processes that are essential for critical thinking (Shenav et al., 2017). As a 
result, individuals might default to surface-level understanding and quick thinking 
(Kahneman, 2011), rather than investing in the sustained effort required for knowledge 
acquisition and reasoning. We found a small to medium negative correlation between 
over-reliance and time spent making the initial decision. This means that the longer a student 
deliberated on whether to use ChatGPT’s incorrect recommendation, the less likely they were 
to accept it. These results align with Buçinca et al. (2021), who also linked over-reliance to 
fast thinking.  
​
Our cognitive tendencies might cause over-reliance when interacting with LLMs, as 
they offer quick ready-made answers for problems that would normally demand cognitive 
effort, making them valuable tools in different tasks. LLMs can present even incorrect 
information with very high confidence and plausibility (Azaria et al., 2024). This could 
disarm people from using critical thinking while using these systems, potentially leading to 
the acceptance of hallucinated outputs (Borji, 2023), incorrect answers for tasks that they 
would be able to solve by themselves (Mormando, 2024; Puppart et al., 2025), or socially 
biased information (Kotek et al., 2023; Rozado, 2023). 
​
The question of why our intervention led to an increase in under-reliance, with a 
medium to large effect, is harder to explain than over-reliance. Previous research has shown 
that exposure to AI flaws can cause a general aversion towards its outputs, even if they are 
correct (Dietvorst et al., 2015). However, this explanation does not fit well with our data, as a 
general aversion towards ChatGPT recommendations should have decreased over-reliance as 
well. Future interventions should account for this tendency towards aversion and design 
interventions that not only highlight LLM weaknesses but also present its strengths and 
describe the types of tasks where it is reliable and significantly outperforms humans. 
In conclusion, our study serves as a caution to schools and countries wanting to adopt 
LLMs as part of their secondary education. Although we measured over-reliance as it pertains 
to the acceptance of misinformation, the results suggest a broader trend of over-reliance that 
could negatively affect students’ critical thinking in the long term. Students accepted incorrect 
recommendations, at least in part, because of fast thinking and usage of mental shortcuts. If 
this is the default approach for many students interacting with LLMs, we can expect a stunt on

learning process. Our current study consisted of a relatively small sample of participants, and 
large-scale experimental studies are needed to confirm our findings. Nevertheless, we can 
conclude that it is not trivial to decrease over-reliance: even if future work would show a 
significant effect with a larger sample, the present work hints that the effect size of the 
manipulation would likely not be large.

A short introduction to ChatGPT 
ChatGPT is a chatbot that was first introduced in 2022 and has since gained rapid popularity. 
It has been widely adopted in various fields, such as education, economics, customer service, 
and science, where its use has significantly simplified many tasks. 
ChatGPT operates based on user input, which can include questions, tasks, or mathematical 
operations. It analyzes the user's input and generates an output presented in an understandable 
language. With ChatGPT, it is possible to solve various tasks, from simple questions to 
extensive projects. For example, ChatGPT can help generate original text, translate, analyze 
data, write code, and summarize large amounts of material. 
Here are some reasons why ChatGPT is a useful tool for solving language-related tasks1: 
●​ Understanding language. ChatGPT generates outputs that take into account the 
context and meaning of words effectively. 
●​ Personalized responses. ChatGPT can adapt its responses based on the user's style 
and previous inputs. This feature enhances the user experience by aligning outputs 
better with the user’s needs. 
●​ Availability. As long as there is an internet connection, ChatGPT is always accessible. 
Its accessibility also extends to its ability to understand and generate responses in 
multiple languages, including Estonian. 
●​ Automation. ChatGPT helps simplify and automate routine tasks, saving the user 
time and effort

Each time ChatGPT generates an output, it solves a very simple task: predicting the next

Writing prompts 
As previously described, ChatGPT generates outputs based on user input. This input can 
consist of four main components2: 
●​ Instructions. This part explains what task you want ChatGPT to perform. Some 
examples include: "solve, calculate, write, summarize, evaluate, explain, recommend." 
●​ Context. Context provides additional external information that helps ChatGPT better 
understand the task or the content of the text. This allows it to generate more relevant 
and accurate outputs. 
●​ Text. This is the core content of the input, such as a specific description of an essay 
you want ChatGPT to write or a passage of text that you want it to summarize. 
●​ Output preferences. This allows you to specify the format or style of the response. 
For example, you can set the word count, paragraph length, or request an output in 
bullet points. 
User input does not have to include all four components. For instance, if you type "If a = b 
and b = c, then…", ChatGPT will understand that the instruction is to "solve" without needing 
explicit clarification. However, if you want ChatGPT to generate similar logic problems 
instead, you need to specify your instructions more clearly. 
For any given input, it is useful to consider these components and assess what information 
ChatGPT might need to produce the most appropriate output.

Tips for Using ChatGPT 
●​ Ask multiple times. ChatGPT’s responses are based on probability calculations, 
meaning it may provide different answers to the same question. Some of these answers 
might be unlikely or incorrect. Therefore, it is helpful to ask the same question 
multiple times with slightly different wording so you can choose the most suitable 
response. 
●​ Ask for the reasoning. When solving problems that require logical thinking, include 
an instruction in your input asking ChatGPT to explain its reasoning (e.g., by adding 
"Solve step by step" at the end of your prompt). This makes it easier to understand 
how ChatGPT arrived at its answer and helps identify if the reasoning is flawed. 
●​ Verify facts. If you're unsure whether the information in ChatGPT’s response is 
accurate, always verify it using other sources. One option is to search on Google. 
Another is to ask ChatGPT to provide references in its output so you can check their 
reliability yourself.
