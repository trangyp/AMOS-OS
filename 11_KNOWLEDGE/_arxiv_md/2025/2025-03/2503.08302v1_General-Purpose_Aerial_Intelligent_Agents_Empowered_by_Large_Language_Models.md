General-Purpose Aerial Intelligent Agents Empowered by
Large Language Models
Ji Zhao and Xiao Lin
Abstract— The emergence of large language models (LLMs)
opens new frontiers for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAVs), yet
existing systems remain confined to predefined tasks due to
hardware-software co-design challenges. This paper presents
the first aerial intelligent agent capable of open-world task
execution through tight integration of LLM-based reasoning
and robotic autonomy. Our hardware-software co-designed
system addresses two fundamental limitations: (1) Onboard
LLM operation via an edge-optimized computing platform,
achieving 5-6 tokens/sec inference for 14B-parameter models
at 220W peak power; (2) A bidirectional cognitive architecture
that synergizes slow deliberative planning (LLM task plan-
ning) with fast reactive control (state estimation, mapping,
obstacle avoidance, and motion planning). Validated through
preliminary results using our prototype, the system demon-
strates reliable task planning and scene understanding in
communication-constrained environments, such as sugarcane
monitoring, power grid inspection, mine tunnel exploration,
and biological observation applications. This work establishes
a novel framework for embodied aerial artificial intelligence,
bridging the gap between task planning and robotic autonomy
in open environments.
I. INTRODUCTION
Although UAVs have advanced significantly, they are
still mostly limited to performing tasks that are specifically
designed and pre-programmed. Typical applications of UAVs
include precision agriculture, power grid inspection, mining
industry, biological observations, etc. With the rise of LLMs
and visual-language models (VLMs), an important question
arises: Can UAVs carry out tasks based on open-set language
instructions and interact with open-set objects? Is it possible
in a few-shot even a zero-shot manner? We refer to such
LLM-empowered UAVs that can perform general-purpose
tasks in open-world as aerial intelligent agents (AIA).
To build an aerial intelligent agent, the integration of fast
reactive behaviors and slow deliberative reasoning remains a
critical challenge, particularly in open environments requir-
ing real-time responsiveness and a long-horizon planning. In-
spired by the dual-process theory of human cognition [1], this
paper proposes a hardware-software co-designed framework.
It embeds fast thinking for agile reaction and slow reasoning
for long-horizon task-planning into aerial intelligent agents.
There are several challenges to implement an aerial intel-
ligent agent. First, LLMs are characterized by their vast pa-
rameter sizes and training on internet-scale datasets. Due to
limitations in weight, power consumption, and size of UAVs,
designing an appropriate edge computing platform and se-
lecting an proper LLM is crucial. Current UAVs designed

Recent efforts to integrate LLMs with UAVs predom-
inantly focus on cloud-dependent architectures or offline
task planning [4], which fundamentally constrain real-time
adaptability in open environments. While edge computing so-

power-weight tradeoffs and vibration-induced computational

aerial intelligent agents, we present a hardware platform and
a prompt-design framework for LLM-empowered UAVs. The
major contributions in this work are summarized as follows:
• A novel hardware-software co-design framework fea-

embedded computing module enables efficient LLM
inference (14B parameters DeepSeek-R1 LLM [6] or
11B parameters Meta Llama 3.2 Vision [7] at 5-6 to-

peak consumption).
• We propose a prompt design framework that integrates
LLM-based reasoning with conventional UAV auton-
omy modules. Our architecture achieves bidirectional
integration between high-level task planning (via LLMs)
and low-level reaction pipelines (state estimation, mo-
tion planning, control, etc).
• Using the prototype of our hardware platform, we
validate the effectiveness of LLMs/VLMs running on-
board. We test the performance through mission-critical
applications, including sugarcane monitoring, power
grid inspection, mine tunnel exploration, and biological
observation, demonstrating the generalization ability of
task planning and scene understanding in real-world
scenarios.

guiding an agent to navigate through an environment based
on natural language instructions and visual inputs. In [11],
a novel agent tailored for embodied question answering

hierarchical task execution. Recently, some datasets for
UAV’s VLN have been proposed in [12], [13]. Swarm-GPT
enables users to automatically generate synchronized drone
performances through natural language instructions [14]. The
TPML method [15] uses LLMs as a command interface
to comprehend operators’ intentions and translate them into
executable codes. However, LLMs run on cloud services or
ground stations in these works.
III. FRAMEWORK
The core ability of aerial intelligent agent is long-horizon
planning for general-purpose tasks in open environments.
First of all, we need to clarify two levels of planning, i.e.,
task planning and motion planning. The whole planning
procedure involves organizing actions to solve given tasks,
typically through generating a sequence of high-level sym-
bolic operators (i.e., task planning) followed by executing
them using low-level motion planning. Motion planning
refers to the process of generating a path by computing
sequential waypoints within configuration or task spaces.
The work flow of the aerial intelligent agent is shown
in Fig. 1. The prompt design for LLMs/VLMs is the key
to unleash the power of LLMs. Our method includes two
stages. The first stage involves accepting the mission and for-
mulating the task plan, with human-in-the-loop operations.
At this stage, we pose a question to the LLM (DeepSeek-
R1 [6] is used in this paper) via a prompt, providing it
with a description of the task and requesting it to generate
a detailed plan. The answer provided by the LLM is then
reviewed and confirmed by a human operator. If the plan is
deemed reasonable, the process proceeds to the next stage.

If not, the prompt is rerun to generate a revised response.
Drawing on the vast knowledge acquired through LLMs, it
possesses common sense and can provide rational planning
for the mission, even if some details are not mentioned in
the prompt. The common sense is considered one of the keys
to achieving machine intelligence [19].
The second stage involves the UAV autonomously exe-
cuting the mission. We take the sugarcane monitoring as an
example. The images, LiDAR point clouds, and GNSS/IMU
data collected by the UAV are sent to the perception, state
estimation and mapping modules. The perception module can
be a small deep-learning model to detect obstacles, such as
high-voltage power lines, buildings, etc. The state estimation
and mapping module is used to provide the ego pose of
the UAV and a local point cloud map. This module can be
implemented by a LiDAR-inertial odometry. We use Fast-
LIO2 [2] in our system. Then the UAV’s ego pose, local
map, and the obstacle detection results are fused and saved
into a file. It provides the UAV’s pose and the attributes of
the obstacle, including category, position, distance, etc.
At the second stage, the images are also sent to a VLM to
generate scene descriptions of the images, which are also
written into a file. In addition, there are two fixed files
that record the geodetic information of the sugarcane field
and the action set of the UAV, respectively. The content
from these four files is substituted into a text template,
forms a prompt that allows the LLM to act as a Motion
Planner. It makes decision by selecting an action from the
action set and generates waypoints for motion planning.
The generated waypoints are input into the flight control
unit (FCU) to produce commands for controlling the UAV.
The VLM does not require high-frequency execution; it is
typically performed every few seconds to dozens of seconds.
In this paper, the Meta Llama 3.2 Vision 11B [7] is used for
both VLM and LLM at this stage.
The action set of the UAV contains three main parts. (1)
Basic flight control, including takeoff, land, hover, emer-
gency stop. (2) Three-axis motion control, including linear
motion and angular motion. (3) Dynamic parameter control,
including accelerate, decelerate, altitude hold, speed lock. In
addition there are some advanced modes, including waypoint
navigation, return to home (RTH), orbit, etc.
IV. HARDWARE DESIGN
The hardware platform of the aerial intelligent agent is
shown in Fig. 2(a). Its mechanical structure forms a hexa-
copter drone, which is called Bumblebee LLM UAV 1.0. It has
a 850-millimeter wheelbase. The weight is between 10 and
15 kg, depending on the payloads. Drawing inspiration from
the human body structure and its functions, the hardware can
be divided into 6 domains as introduced below.
Intelligent Computing Domain (Brain): Our UAV is
equipped with a combination of a 16-core 5GHz Intel CPU
and a 12-core 2.5GHz ARM processor. It is also equipped
with a dual-layer GPU architecture, capable of delivering
up to 10 TFLOPS of general-purpose computing power and
40 TOPS (Int8) of embedded computing power. Our UAV
features a substantial 96GB DDR5 memory. Additionally,
its NVIDIA Jetson Orin module is equipped with 16GB of
LPDDR memory and 8GB of high-speed GDDR memory,
further enhancing its overall performance capabilities. The
hardware and software platform is capable of running the
DeepSeek 14B-parameter model at a speed of 5-6 tokens
per second (CPU mode), with a power consumption of

approximately 220W.
Energy Supply Domain (Heart): This domain contains
the battery compartment and power supply system. Currently,
it can provide a peak power consumption of 1000Wh to the
intelligent computing domain, allowing continuous operation
of large models for 4 hours. It can also provide 22000WmAh
(or 700Wh) for the flight domain. A mobile power supply
platform (mobile charging station) is provided, capable of
charging up to 12 batteries simultaneously, meeting the daily
work requirements.
Flying Domain (Body, Skeleton, and Limbs): This
domain includes a FCU and propellers. Each propeller can
provide a thrust of 3kg, and with a 6-propeller configuration,
it can lift a maximum weight of 18kg.
Communication & GPS Domain (Ears and Mouth):
The 2×2 MIMO 5G communication module is equipped and
can provide a theoretical bandwidth of 2Gbps, with an actual
bandwidth of 400Mbps. This is 10 times the communication
bandwidth of current commercial drone data transmission
modules. The RTK module can provide centimeter-level
navigation accuracy. The video transmission module can
achieve a transmission distance of 30 km, which is a 30% in-
crease compared to the DJI M350 RTK (with a transmission
distance of 20 km) [16].
Perception Domain (Eyes and Tactile Sense): This
domain is equipped with the following sensors, which can
be abbreviated as 14V+6T+1L.
• 14 cameras (14V). It includes 6 stereo camera systems,
one wide-angle camera with 48 million pixels, and one
4K ultra-high-definition telephoto camera;
• 6 Time-of-Flight (ToF) cameras (6T);
• 4D LiDAR (1L);
• An IMU. The FCU integrates with an IMU.
Payload Domain (Work Tools): It is equipped with a
diverse range of payloads, including one infrared thermal
camera, laser rangefinder (1.5KM), 1080p FPV camera, 3-
axis gimbal, searchlight (for night operations), wide-angle
night vision device, telephoto night vision device, wide-angle
camera, telephoto camera, etc.
The flowchart of the hardware platform is shown in Fig. 3.
All hardware components in our prototype are off-the-shelf
products. As a result, the hardware platform can ensure
performance and reliability. Additionally, it demonstrates
significant cost advantages by eliminating the need to pay
the high Non-Recurring Engineering (NRE) fees associated
with customized components.
The comparison of our prototype with state-of-the-art
UAVs is shown in Fig. 2(b)(c). It can be seen that different
UAV products have their own emphases in design philosophy
and application scenarios. The SUPER [3] has advantages
in computing power and safety. The Agilicious [18] has its
own strength in power consumption control and embedded
applications. DJI drones [16] stand out in sensor configura-
tion and positioning capabilities, making them particularly
suitable for aerial photography. By contrast, our prototype
has comprehensive advantages in computing power, sensing,
Fig. 3: Flowchart of the hardware platform.
safety, and communication capabilities, and can meet the
needs of a variety of complex mission scenarios.
Our prototype and DJI drones are weak in size and
agility, primarily due to the following reasons. First, to
achieve high endurance, large-capacity batteries are required
to provide sufficient propulsion, and larger lift is needed
to overcome their own weight, which in turn necessitates
bigger propellers. Second, in open fields and over the sea,
considering the impact of wind, UAVs need to have enough
power and weight to counteract it and ensure stable flight.
Lastly, powerful computing platforms require extensive cool-
ing measures. A compact design may lead to ineffective heat
dissipation, thereby affecting operational stability.
The key innovations of the our hardware platform is
summarized below. First, for the first time in 10-15Kg
class UAVs, an hardware platform for running LLMs has
been introduced, supporting a 14B-parameter LLM at the
edge. The computing power is enhanced by 2-3 orders of
magnitude compared to existing commercial UAV platforms.
Second, the platform is equipped with cameras, ToF LiDARs,
and 4D LiDAR modules to enable real-time perception,
improving the UAV’s obstacle avoidance capabilities and its
potential to operate autonomously in challenging weather
conditions. The hardware of our prototype will be open.

V. EXPERIMENTS
We implement a prototype of an aerial intelligent agent
based on the proposed prompt design method and hardware
platform. We evaluate its task planning and scene understand-
ing abilities using its onboard computing. To demonstrate its
effectiveness to deal with complex tasks, the performance
is evaluated for four different tasks, including sugarcane
monitoring, power grid inspection, mine tunnel exploration,
and biological observation of whale surfaces, as shown in
Fig. 4.
A. Task Definition
Sugarcane Monitoring: In the field of agricultural breed-
ing, aerial intelligent agents have the potential to utilize their
high-precision measurement and perception capabilities to
conduct lodging measurement and growth monitoring of sug-
arcane. Through the collaborative operation of fast-thinking
and slow-thinking systems, agents are able to autonomously
identify the growth status of sugarcane, promptly detect
lodging and other issues, and provide strong support for
agricultural industrialization.
Power Grid Inspection: Aerial intelligent agents have
the potential to achieve autonomous flight following and
defect recognition. The fast-thinking system is responsible
for obstacle avoidance and path planning, ensuring that the
aerial agent flies safely and efficiently along the power
lines. Meanwhile, the slow-thinking system conducts in-
depth analysis of the inspection data, identifies potential
defects, and provides crucial references for the maintenance
of power facilities.
Mine Tunnel Exploration: Aerial intelligent agents can
leverage their LiDAR SLAM technology for precise navi-
gation and mapping. By integrating the real-time perception
and path-planning capabilities of the fast-thinking system [3]
with the comprehensive analytical and processing power of
the slow-thinking system, UAVs are able to autonomously
traverse complex mine environments and conduct all-round
monitoring. This provides a solid basis for the safe exploita-
tion and sustainable utilization of mine tunnels.
Biological Observation of Whale Surfaces: Rendezvous
with sperm whales for biological observations is made
challenging by their prolonged dive patterns [20]. Whale
sightings at the surface, known as rendezvous, are critical for
connecting behavioral data with tagged whales but can easily
be missed. The fast-thinking system employs real-time multi-
sensor fusion to maintain good positional accuracy during
whale surfacing events. The slow-thinking system conducts
behavioral forecasting, integrating historical dive patterns
with oceanographic data to predict the surfacing zones.
B. Task Planning Results at the First Stage
Task planning results at the first stage for different appli-
cations are shown in the following four boxes. We can see
that the LLM [6] can generate reasonable task planning for
all the applications, including mission objectives, preparation
phase, mission planning, etc. Meanwhile, certain steps in
task planning vary according to the specific task at hand and
incorporate common sense. Take the takeoff point selection
as an example, sugarcane monitoring application selects an
open area near the sugarcane field as the takeoff point. By
contrast, tunnel exploration application sets the takeoff point
at the entrance of the mine tunnel and ensure that there are
no obstacles in the area. In addition, the LLM noticed that
there is no GPS signal inside the mine tunnels, even though
we did not mention this point in the prompt. Please refer to
the red text in the box below. We can see that the reasoning
abilities of LLMs enable task planning to address a wide
range of general-purpose tasks effectively.

C. Scene Understanding Results at the Second Stage
Using real-world images of corner cases, We test the
onboard VLM’s [7] scene understanding ability. Examples
for sugarcane monitoring and power grid inspection are
shown in Fig. 5, respectively. We can see that the VLM
successfully detect the the lodging condition of a sugarcane
field and potential hazards of the power grid.
From these experiments, the aerial intelligent has shown
its potential to replace humans in performing tasks that are
dangerous or monotonous. Our results has two implications.
First, compared to the expensive costs of human operators,
aerial intelligent agents have enabled the realization of an
agent with common sense using relatively low-cost hardware.
Second, considering VLMs and LLMs typically do not
require high-frequency execution, a UAV can be powered
with one day of slow thinking capability using just one
kilowatt-hour of electricity. Our solution thus offers great
potential, providing plenty of room for a wide range of
applications
VI. CONCLUSION
We present a framework for aerial intelligent agents
through the integration of LLMs with UAV systems. The
proposed framework achieves three critical advancements:
1) Edge deployment of 14B-parameter LLMs via optimized
hardware design, overcoming traditional power-compute
constraints; 2) A two-stage prompt design framework en-
abling effective interaction between LLM-guided task plan-
ning and low-level reaction abilities; 3) Effectiveness in
mission-critical applications requiring both environmental
awareness and operational precision. Our experiments reveal
two key insights: First, the bidirectional information flow be-
tween LLM reasoning and reaction modules can be applied to
general-purpose tasks with specific-design methods. Second,
onboard LLM running is beneficial for UAVs, especially for
safety-critical operations.
In the future, we will further reduce the UAV weight. The
current computing platform uses an aluminum alloy frame.
