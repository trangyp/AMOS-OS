CartoAgent: a multimodal large language model-powered multi-agent 
cartographic framework for map style transfer and evaluation 
The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) presents new 
opportunities to advance the cartographic process. Previous studies have either 
overlooked the artistic aspects of maps or faced challenges in creating both 
accurate and informative maps. In this study, we propose CartoAgent, a novel 
multi-agent cartographic framework powered by multimodal large language 
models (MLLMs). This framework simulates three key stages in cartographic 
practice: preparation, map design, and evaluation. At each stage, different 
MLLMs act as agents with distinct roles to collaborate, discuss, and utilize tools 
for specific purposes. In particular, CartoAgent leverages MLLMs’ visual 
aesthetic capability and world knowledge to generate maps that are both visually 
appealing and informative. By separating style from geographic data, it can focus 
on designing stylesheets without modifying the vector-based data, thereby 
ensuring geographic accuracy. We applied CartoAgent to a specific task centered 
on map restyling—namely, map style transfer and evaluation. The effectiveness 
of this framework was validated through extensive experiments and a human 
evaluation study. CartoAgent can be extended to support a variety of cartographic 
design decisions and inform future integrations of GenAI in cartography. 
Keywords: multimodal large language model; autonomous multi-agent system; 
map style transfer and evaluation

1. Introduction 
Maps have significantly shaped human understanding of space and are recognized as 
one of the most important geographic ideas that have changed the world (Hanson, 
1997). Cartography, the study and practice of making and using maps, has long been 
defined as a mixture of art, science, and technology (Krygier, 1995). For decades, 
cartographers have been exploring the use of cartographic language (such as symbols, 
annotations) to design maps that are both visually appealing and capable of effectively 
conveying accurate information with efficient map-making tools (Andrews, 1990; Li & 
Huang, 2002; Morrison, 1997; Nikmah et al., 2022). The development of information 
and communication technologies has significantly enhanced the richness of map-
making tools and benefited the cartographic process. This evolution spans from 
traditional handcrafted maps, such as historical atlases, to digital mapping using 
geographic information system (GIS) software, and further to interactive web mapping 
platforms like OpenStreetMap1. Recently, the emergence of Generative AI (GenAI) has 
demonstrated significant potential across various applications (Mai et al., 2025; Messeri 
& Crockett, 2024; Milano et al., 2023; Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023) and opened up new 
opportunities to cartography (Ai, 2021; Kang et al., 2024; Zhang & Zhu, 2024). GenAI, 
exemplified by text-to-text large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT2, Gemini3, 
and LLaMA4, as well as image generation models like Stable Diffusion5, DALL·E6, and

between objects. Informativeness, on the other hand, measures how effectively a map 
conveys meaningful and interpretable geographic information. A map is considered 
informative if it clearly differentiates between object types, uses intuitive visual 
variables, and presents legible labels and icons that enhance understanding. However, 
image generation models often struggle with these aspects (Courtial et al., 2023; Kang 
et al., 2023; Lin & Zhao, 2024; Wu et al., 2022a; Wu et al., 2022b; Zhao et al., 2021).

The emergence of multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), particularly the groundbreaking

geographic data record the precise locations of objects, while design styles dictate how

accuracy of maps and could potentially be automated using multiple MLLMs. Second, 
MLLMs are pre-trained on vast amounts of text from the Internet and possess extensive 
world knowledge. When applied to map-making, these models can interpret the real-
world meanings of map elements, thereby facilitating the creation of informative maps. 
Third, due to their visual aesthetic capability, MLLMs can analyze maps and provide 
feedback on layout and design. This helps create maps that are not only accurate and 
informative but also visually appealing.

2. Literature review 
In this section, we first review existing research on map style transfer and its evaluation, 
and highlight their respective strengths and limitations. We then introduce MLLMs and 
autonomous agents, along with their current applications in GIS and cartography. 
2.1 Map style transfer 
A map style is a collection of distinct yet aesthetically cohesive features in cartographic 
design (Kent & Vujakovic, 2017). Map style transfer refers to the process of 
reproducing a specific style—whether from an existing map or an artistic work—onto 
new map data (Kang et al., 2024). Previous studies have developed methods for map 
style transfer using both vector and raster data. 
Vector data represents geographic entities as points, polylines, and polygons 
with precise locations and associated attributes. In vector map production, design styles 
are typically kept separate from geographic data. Cartographers have developed 
cartographic grammars that define style specifications and visual variables to guide map 
design decisions (Diamond, 2019; Griffin, 2021; Nestel, 2019; White, 2017). 
Researchers have used color pickers to extract color schemes from Monet’s landscape 
paintings and applied these schemes to vector data for map style transfer 
(Friedmannová, 2009). Furthermore, Christophe and Hoarau (2012) highlighted the 
importance of considering color proportions and complementary principles in the 
process of map style transfer. Wu et al. (2022a) developed an optimization algorithm for 
transferring colors from arbitrary images to vector maps. However, previous studies 
have faced limitations in automating map style transfer. They have primarily focused on 
the color dimension while neglecting other elements such as size and shape, which are 
also important in map design. Moreover, they often overlook the semantic context of


## Page 8


8 
 
objects on the map, which makes it difficult for users to intuitively extract meaningful 
information. All these challenges highlight an opportunity to explore the integration of 
GenAI in map design. 
A raster map typically consists of an array of pixels arranged in a rectangular 
grid, with each pixel storing numerical values that represent geographic information 
(Williams, 2019). The use of AI for map style transfer is rooted in raster-based maps. 
For example, researchers have developed advanced Generative Adversarial Networks 
(GANs) to transfer styles between map images within identical geographical contexts, 
as well as to apply artistic styles of famous paintings to maps (Bogucka & Meng, 2019; 
Kang et al., 2019; Christophe et al., 2022). Although these models show promise in 
manipulating the artistic aspects of cartography, they lack a deep understanding of 
spatial structures and real-world connotations of map objects. As a result, the raster-
based outputs often contain blurred areas, fictitious regions, distorted relationships, and 
nonsensical symbols or annotations (Wu et al., 2022b; Kang et al., 2024), which makes 
it difficult for users to determine true geographic locations and extract meaningful 
information. 
Therefore, our proposed CartoAgent aims to address these limitations. In line 
with common cartographic practices, we first separate style from geographic data to 
maintain accuracy. CartoAgent then incorporates two specialized agents that fully 
leverage the world knowledge and visual aesthetic capability of MLLMs. One agent, the 
stylesheet designer, is responsible for defining visual variables of objects in textual 
stylesheets, while the other, the map reviewer, evaluates and provides feedback on 
layout and design. This practice enables CartoAgent to automatically refine style 
specifications and ensures that maps are not only visually appealing but also accurate 
and informative.

2.2 Map style evaluation 
Map evaluation aims to enhance the quality and effectiveness of cartographic design 
through critical analysis (Bjørke, 1996; Robinson & Nelson, 2015). In particular, the 
evaluation of map styles should guide the map style transfer process to produce styled 
maps that are visually appealing, accurate, and informative (Wu et al., 2022b). Current 
metrics for evaluating map styles can be categorized into objective and subjective 
measures. 
Objective metrics. Early explorations in this field were inspired by image quality 
assessment methods, where mathematical rules are defined to evaluate styled images. 
For example, some researchers introduced the structural similarity index from computer 
science to assess the effectiveness of styled map tile generation (Chen et al., 2020). 
More recently, researchers have begun integrating cartographic design constraints, such 
as smoothness and coalescence reduction, into the evaluation framework for styled 
maps (Courtial et al., 2022). Additionally, efforts have been made to directly develop AI 
models for map style evaluation. For instance, Kang et al. (2019) curated natural images 
and maps to train a binary classifier that determines whether distinct map characteristics 
are retained after map style transfer. These objective metrics are useful for conducting 
large-scale evaluations of styled maps. However, they have several limitations. On the 
one hand, due to the inherent complexity of map design, these metrics often provide 
only a superficial assessment. They usually focus on surface-level attributes like visual 
similarity, but fail to capture deeper and more critical aspects of cartographic quality, 
such as user perception and contextual relevance. On the other hand, scholars have 
pointed out that while machines can easily compute these metrics, cartographers may 
find them difficult to interpret and apply effectively in their decision-making processes 
(Kang et al., 2024).

10 
 
Subjective metrics. Due to the limitations of objective metrics, researchers often 
turn to user evaluation studies. For instance, Chen et al. (2020) invited GIS/Cartography 
graduates to participate in a cognitive experiment where they evaluated and rated styled 
maps. The statistical measures derived from these evaluations (such as the average 
score) were used to assess the effectiveness of the corresponding approaches. 
Additionally, researchers have collected a large volume of assessment data from 
participants and trained models to simulate user preferences. For example, in evaluating 
the quality of sequential color schemes, Chen et al. (2021) first curated a dataset 
consisting of 781 five-color schemes, accompanied by assessments from 26 
undergraduate students. An AdaBoost model was subsequently trained to align with the 
ratings provided by the undergraduates. Furthermore, Wang et al. (2024a) utilized 
Siamese neural networks to capture readers’ perceptions of map information load, 
thereby guiding map design. While these metrics provide a more in-depth assessment, 
they are not without challenges. On the one hand, such studies require significant time 
and human resources. On the other hand, aggregating diverse perspectives and 
preferences from different experts may lead to an averaging effect, which could 
potentially overlooks individual differences in design preferences. 
In this study, to address these challenges, we leverage visual aesthetic capability 
of MLLMs and their world knowledge to conduct map style evaluations. This approach 
enhances efficiency and enables in-depth assessments. Additionally, since MLLMs can 
utilize tools, they can perform objective measurements, such as comparing the visual 
similarity between images.

2.4 Autonomous agents and their applications in GIS/Cartography 
An autonomous agent is a system that interacts with its environment by perceiving its 
surroundings and taking actions over time to achieve specific goals (Wang et al., 2023). 
The development of such powerful agents has been a long-standing objective in both 
industry and academia. However, earlier efforts primarily focused on deploying models 
in isolated environments with limited knowledge, which greatly differed from human 
learning processes and hindered agents from making human-like decisions (Mnih et al., 
2015). Recently, the integration of extensive world knowledge into LLMs and MLLMs 
has led to significant advancements in their capabilities and attracted considerable 
attention within the autonomous agent community. Researchers have begun exploring 
the use of LLMs and MLLMs as central controllers to build agents capable of human-
like decision-making (Qin et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2023). Over time, a relatively unified 
framework—encompassing modules for profiling, memory, planning, and action—has 
emerged and been widely adopted (Wang et al., 2023). 
Researchers have developed various autonomous agents, such as ChemCrow, 
which can assist in conducting chemical experiments (Bran et al., 2023), and ChatLaw, 
a legal AI consultant (Cui et al., 2023). This exploration has also extended into GIS and 
cartography research and led to a series of outstanding works. For example, Zhang et al. 
(2024a) introduced GeoGPT, a novel framework that leverages LLMs to understand 
user needs and autonomously invoke predefined GIS tools for geospatial data 
collection, processing, and analysis. Similarly, Li & Ning (2023) adopted LLMs as

controllers and enabled them to automate spatial data collection, analysis, and 
visualization. In cartography, Zhang et al. (2024b) proposed the MapGPT framework, 
which integrates LLMs with multiple professional mapping tools. Users can interact 
with MapGPT through conversation to adjust map elements. These studies have 
significantly enhanced the automation of spatial analysis and visualization and lowered

integrate visual aesthetic capability and world knowledge of MLLMs with coordination 
capability of multi-agent systems to generate high-quality, styled maps that are visually 
appealing, accurate, and informative.

introduce our conceptual CartoAgent framework and discuss how it contributes to the 
intelligent map-making.

3.1 Major steps in the map-making process 
The process of map-making involves several stages, including conceptual design, 
iterative prototyping, and comprehensive evaluation (Roth, 2019). In line with standard 
cartographic practices, we distill the process into three fundamental steps (Figure 1): 
preparation, map design, and map evaluation. 
Preparation. In this initial step, cartographers define their objectives and 
carefully analyze geographic data along with other relevant materials. It is essential for 
them to consider fundamental cartographic principles and choose appropriate 
cartographic language to convey information effectively. Keeping map readers in mind


## Page 15


15 
 
is crucial, as it ensures that geographic information is accurately interpreted and users 
can form a reliable representation of the real world through maps. 
Map design. Once the objectives are clarified and the datasets are prepared, the 
map design process begins. Cartographers often separate style from geographic data. 
They manipulate visual variables such as color, contrast, and contiguity within 
stylesheets and incorporate symbols and annotations to enhance visual appeal. 
Manipulating visual variables does not involve editing the geographic data itself, which 
can preserve spatial relationships between objects and ensure geographic accuracy. 
Additionally, since the function of a map is to communicate information to readers, a 
good map should be intuitive and easy to read. Therefore, cartographers often consider 
real-world connotations of objects when designing maps—for example, using cool tones 
to represent bodies of water. 
Map evaluation. After map design, the next step is to assess the quality and 
effectiveness of maps and conduct map critiques. Map reviewers analyze whether the 
visual variables—such as size, color,  and shape—are appropriately configured and 
whether the map as a whole is visually appealing. Additionally, they evaluate whether 
the cartographic design decisions make the maps accurate and informative. 
It should be emphasized that the ‘map design–map evaluation’ process may 
need to be repeated multiple times. Cartographers often adjust visual variables and 
refine the visual hierarchy iteratively to ensure the final design effectively meets the 
map’s intended objectives. Following this conceptual framework, we introduce 
CartoAgent—an MLLM-powered multi-agent system designed to support each step 
with specialized agents.

3.2 CartoAgent: an MLLM-powered multi-agent cartographic system 
Given the strong capabilities of MLLMs in both text processing and visual aesthetics, as 
well as their extensive world knowledge, we can design multiple MLLMs with distinct 
roles to handle specific subtasks in the cartographic process. For instance, one MLLM 
could focus on analyzing relevant materials, while another could be designed to 
generate a textual stylesheet. During the map evaluation stage, an MLLM could assess 
the quality and effectiveness of maps, as well as critique their style and layout. By 
deploying a series of MLLMs, we can develop a multi-agent autonomous system for 
map-making, which we term CartoAgent. Figure 1 provides an overview of these 
agents, including their architectures and interactions. Notably, a specific CartoAgent is 
introduced in Section 4 for map style transfer and evaluation. However, the conceptual 
framework discussed here is adaptable and can be leveraged to support a wide range of 
cartographic design decisions. 
Agent architecture. In line with common practices in agent-related studies, our 
proposed CartoAgent consists of four modules: a profiling module, a memory module, a 
planning module, and an action module. In CartoAgent, the profiling module is 
implemented by crafting prompts that clarify roles of agents (e.g., a stylesheet designer, 
a map reviewer) and outline their specific map-making skills and responsibilities (e.g., 
data analysis, map design, and evaluation). For instance, we can initialize a stylesheet 
designer to create textual stylesheets and a map reviewer to assess the quality and 
effectiveness of maps. The memory module preserves content gathered from various 
inputs. In CartoAgent, this module primarily determines whether to memorize map 
styles that have been entered into the system and whether to retain feedback on 
previously styled maps. For example, when we prompt the stylesheet designer to


## Page 17


17 
 
modify the style of maps, the previous stylesheets can be stored in memory and 
referenced. 
The planning module can be used to decompose and dynamically adjust tasks, 
similar to how humans adapt to their environment. It employs techniques such as chain-
of-thought (Wei et al., 2022), tree-of-thought (Yao et al., 2023), and graph-of-thought 
(Besta et al., 2024) for effective task decomposition and adaptation. In CartoAgent, this 
module efficiently breaks down the cartographic process into manageable subtasks and 
simplifies complex cartographic design decisions. For example, the agent can divide 
map evaluation into two key dimensions: objective and subjective assessments. During 
the objective evaluation process, the agent can analyze the color histogram of styled 
maps by invoking external tools. Finally, the action module translates agents’ decisions 
into concrete actions. For example, by clearly defining the action sets corresponding to 
each decision (e.g., external APIs), we can ensure that CartoAgent effectively executes 
multiple tasks across the three stages of map-making. 
Agent interactions. Each agent is designed to handle a specific subtask in the 
cartographic process. CartoAgent, as a multi-agent system, facilitates various 
interactions among these agents, including collaboration, discussion, and tool 
utilization. Collaboration involves multiple agents with unique capabilities and 
expertise working together to tackle complex tasks. In CartoAgent, one agent may 
specialize in designing stylesheets, while another may focus on analyzing relevant 
materials. These agents collaborate to ensure that the map’s style aligns with relevant 
materials. Discussion refers to multiple rounds of communication between agents to 
refine outcomes. Agents review each other’s outputs and provide feedback to improve 
map design. For instance, in CartoAgent, through multiple interactions between the map 
design agent and the map critique agent, the system can iteratively enhance the quality

and effectiveness of the maps. Beyond interactions among agents within CartoAgent, 
some agents may also utilize external tools to assist in completing their tasks. For 
example, an agent might use GIS software like Mapbox to visualize map data (Figure 
1). Properly specifying these agent interactions is crucial for the system to successfully 
execute complex tasks.

6. Discussions 
In this section, we list several takeaways from the study and discuss the potential of 
MLLMs in advancing cartography. 
6.1 What knowledge is required for map style transfer? 
When using CartoAgent for map style transfer and evaluation, users only need to upload 
an inspiration source and map data to generate a styled map, without requiring any prior 
knowledge. Since map style transfer involves more than just artistic decisions—maps 
must be visually appealing, accurate, and informative—we have made every effort to 
fully leverage the knowledge embedded in MLLMs and integrate additional expertise 
into the agent workflow. This approach enhances both the quality and effectiveness of 
the styled maps. 
Specifically, we fully utilize three types of knowledge. First, we integrate 
cartographic knowledge into the agent workflow. For example, cartographers typically 
separate style from geographic data and manipulate visual variables to achieve effective 
map design. Following this common cartographic practice, we incorporate a specialized 
agent, the stylesheet designer, responsible for defining the visual variables of objects in 
stylesheets through textual descriptions. This approach mitigates a prevalent issue in 
AI-generated maps, which often contain blurred areas, fictitious regions, distorted 
relationships, and nonsensical symbols or annotations—all of which compromise 
geographic accuracy (Wu et al., 2022b; Kang et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2024). Second, 
we leverage the world knowledge embedded in MLLMs. Since MLLMs are pre-trained 
on vast amounts of text, they inherently possess knowledge about various objects and 
their attributes. For instance, they understand that bodies of water, such as oceans and 
lakes, typically appear blue due to the absorption and scattering of light. By explicitly


## Page 43


43 
 
instructing the model to focus on the semantic information of maps, we can harness this 
knowledge to enhance the quality and effectiveness of map design. Third, we explore 
the aesthetic knowledge of MLLMs. Although aesthetics are subjective, and defining 
what makes a map beautiful is challenging (Jiang & Sui, 2014; Jiang & de Rijke, 2021; 
Kent, 2017), assessing the stylistic similarity between a given inspiration source and a 
styled map is relatively straightforward. Since MLLMs have been fine-tuned on text-
image pairs, they can analyze an inspiration source, recognize its content, color 
schemes, themes, and design, and evaluate a map’s contrast and visual hierarchy. By 
instructing the model to value visual appeal, we can activate this aesthetic awareness 
during map style transfer. These three types of knowledge contribute to the generation 
of styled maps that are visually appealing, accurate, and informative. 
Despite these advancements, there is still room for improvement. For example, 
integrating more specialized cartographic knowledge—such as the information theory 
of cartography (Li et al., 2021)—or insights from neuroscience (Wu et al., 2025) could 
enhance map design and visual quality. Moreover, map styles are influenced not only by 
size, color, and shape but also by the topological organization and orientation of 
geometric elements, human cognition and emotions (Jiang, 2020; Jiang & de Rijke, 
2023; Ortag, 2009). Future research should enable agents to adjust a broader range of 
visual variables and conduct more extensive cognitive experiments to deepen our 
understanding of how visual design influences map perception. 
6.2 How to evaluate the results of map style transfer? 
Maps should prioritize and balance visual appeal, accuracy, and informativeness—
fundamental principles that guide our evaluation of map styles. Unlike generating maps 
directly using image generation models in prior studies, our approach separates style

and informative, we must consider these two aspects during the evaluation process. On 
the one hand, the styled maps should closely resemble their inspiration sources; on the 
other hand, they must effectively convey spatial information without causing visual 
confusion. Since both aspects are explicitly outlined in the prompt, the agent can adhere 
to them strictly. 
Furthermore, to assess the ability of MLLMs in evaluating map styles, 
conducting a human evaluation study is essential (Chen et al., 2025; Voas et al., 2023; 
Zhuang et al., 2024). In this study, we recruited 17 experts and students to participate in 
a cognitive experiment. The results indicate that the evaluation criteria of MLLMs 
largely align with human judgment and suggest that MLLMs have the potential to 
leverage their visual aesthetic capability and world knowledge to assist humans in 
evaluating map styles. 
However, there is still room for improvement. Due to the inherent trade-off 
between communicative quality and aesthetic appeal, map style transfer can be viewed 
as a max-max optimization problem (Wu et al., 2022a). Some styled maps may 
prioritize communicative clarity over strict visual resemblance to their inspiration 
sources, while others may closely mimic the styles at the expense of slight reductions in 
communicative quality. Future advancements could enhance user experience by 
generating multiple styled map options and allowing users to choose the one that best 
aligns with their preferences and needs. This approach would enable greater


## Page 45


45 
 
personalization in map-making and cater to diverse aesthetic and functional 
requirements. 
6.3 How to build a robust multi-agent cartographic system? 
A well-designed multi-agent cartographic system should be reliable and resilient while 
maintaining optimal performance. To enhance its robustness, we have carefully adhered 
to several key design principles throughout its development. 
• Explainability. We require MLLMs to provide necessary explanations during the 
cartographic process. These explanations can help cartographers understand the 
rationale behind the models’ actions and reduce the “black box” nature of deep 
learning models. 
• Agents Interactions. Just like humans, agents can make mistakes, such as 
misidentifying the theme of an inspiration source. To address this, we allow 
agents to actively engage in discussions. For example, map reviewers evaluate 
the quality and effectiveness of the styled maps and provide detailed, actionable 
suggestions to the stylesheet designers. The final styled maps are produced after 
multiple rounds of revision, which significantly reduces the impact of any single 
mistake within the system. 
• Review mechanism. Each time we conduct a map style evaluation, we clear the 
map reviewer’s memory. In other words, we initiate a new session, assign GPT-
4o the role of map reviewer through a predefined prompt, and upload the 
inspiration source and styled map. This ensures that the agent reassesses the 
quality and effectiveness of the styled map from scratch without being 
influenced by previous comments or feedback. This approach prevents the 
reviewer from lowering its standards due to prior interactions or shared memory


## Page 46


46 
 
with other agents. Given that MLLMs may perceive the same map differently 
due to variations in their pre-training datasets, future work could incorporate 
multiple MLLMs to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Additionally, 
combining human evaluation studies and human-in-the-loop processes could 
help ensure the outputs better align with human perceptions. 
6.4 Ethical issues related to map style transfer 
This application addresses the public’s demand for customized maps, as AI-generated 
maps are more personalized than those created using existing resources. However, 
ethical concerns, particularly regarding copyright issues, cannot be overlooked (Zhao et 
al., 2021; Kang et al., 2024). Several potential stakeholders may claim copyright in this 
process. First, the system builder, who configures the model and provides 
computational resources, may assert a claim to copyright ownership. Second, the user, 
who contributes to open-source map data and selects inspiration sources, could argue 
for copyright based on their creative input in shaping the final styled map. Third, the 
original artists behind these inspiration sources may also contend for copyright over 
their artistic creations. This complex interplay of contributions raises significant legal 
and ethical considerations in cartography. Although current technology may not fully 
resolve these copyright challenges, there is an urgent need for intellectual property 
frameworks and policies specifically designed for AI-generated maps.

7. Conclusions and future work 
In this study, we explored how advanced GenAI can be leveraged to create visually 
appealing, accurate, and informative maps. Specifically, we proposed CartoAgent, a 
multi-agent cartographic framework powered by MLLMs. We applied this framework 
to a task centered on the restyling aspect of map-making—map style transfer and 
evaluation. To support the three stages of the task—preparation, map style transfer, and 
evaluation—five agents were designed with distinct roles: an image appreciator, a 
stylesheet designer, an icon designer, a file implementer, and a map reviewer. These 
agents collaborated, discussed, and utilized tools to generate and evaluate maps. By 
harnessing the visual aesthetic capability of MLLMs, along with their extensive world 
knowledge, CartoAgent can produce maps that are both visually appealing and highly 
informative. More importantly, this approach ensures geographic accuracy as it focuses 
solely on designing stylesheets without modifying the vector-based data. To evaluate 
the feasibility of our approach, we conducted extensive experiments on multi-scale and 
multi-source map style transfers. Additionally, we recruited 17 experts and students and 
conducted a human evaluation study to confirm that the results align with human 
perceptions. Designed for users without cartographic expertise, CartoAgent enables 
personalized map creation and makes high-quality cartographic design accessible to a 
broader audience. 
The contributions of this study are multifaceted. First, we proposed CartoAgent, 
which integrates the visual aesthetic capability and world knowledge of MLLMs with 
the coordination capability of multi-agent systems to generate high-quality and styled 
maps. Second, we demonstrated how GenAI models can benefit the future of 
cartography in terms of both its artistic dimension and scientific informativeness. Third, 
our study contributes to the existing literature on map style transfer and evaluation.

Unlike earlier approaches that exhibited issues such as blurred areas, fictitious regions, 
distorted relationships, and nonsensical symbols or annotations (Wu et al., 2022b; Kang 
et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2024), our framework ensures precise geographic 
representation by separating style from geographic data. Our approach customizes map 
styles by manipulating visual variables directly, without editing the vector-based data.  
Although CartoAgent has presently been applied to map style transfer and 
evaluation, it can be extended to support a wide range of cartographic design decisions. 
For instance, agents could be designed to automatically acquire data, select appropriate 
map projections, and generate thematic maps, rather than just general reference maps. 
We believe the proposed CartoAgent framework offers significant opportunities across 
diverse domains, such as disaster response, urban planning, land use, and climate 
studies.
