Strategic Effort and Bandwagon Effects in Finite
Multi-Stage Games with Non-Linear Externalities:
Evidence from Triathlon using a Leave-One-Out IV Strategy
Felix Reichel*
May 2025
Abstract
This paper examines strategic effort and positioning choices resulting in bandwagon ef-
fects under externalities in finite multi-stage games using causal evidence from triathlon
(Reichel , 2025). Focusing on open-water swim drafting—where athletes reduce drag
most effectively by swimming directly behind peers—we estimate its performance ef-
fects through a structural contest framework with endogenous, deterministic effort and
drafting position. Leveraging exogenous variation from COVID-19 drafting bans in
Austrian triathlons, we apply a panel leave-one-out (LOO/LOTO) group ability instru-
mental variables (IV) strategy to isolate the causal non-linear effect of drafting. Results
from restricted sample analysis and pooled estimated bandwagon IV effects show sub-
stantial and nonlinear gains: in small (group size < 10) drafting swim groups/clusters,
each deeper position improves finishing rank on average by over 30%, with rapidly
diminishing returns in larger groups. Leading however is consistently more costly
than optimal positioning, aligning with theoretical predictions of energy expenditure
(metabolic costs).
Keywords: Bandwagon effects, externalities, effort choice, contests, finite games, multi-
stage games, endurance sports, triathlon, instrumental variables, drafting, swim drafting

Introduction
Drafting plays a crucial role in race dynamics and performance outcomes in endurance and
multi-endurance sports. In triathlon - where a race events consist of several finite stages,
namely: swimming, cycling, and running - drafting introduces a strategic externality
that greatly varies by discipline, race format and race regulation. Although drafting in
professional cycling offers the most visible and studied drafting benefits, hydrodynamic
drag reduction in the swim segment in triathlon are said to enhance subsequent cycling
and thus overall race performance (Delextrat , 2003) offer an unique empirical setting.
This paper investigates the performance implications of open water swim drafting using
rich panel data from triathlon races by modeling drafting in the first segment as a two-stage
deterministic game. In the first stage, athletes exert some deterministic swimming effort; in
the second stage, they position themselves optimally within a group, potentially benefiting
heterogeneously from reduced drag behind other swimmers due to the inversly decaying
nature of hydrodynamic benefits and rare overtakings (resorting) during the swim segment
(Reichel (2025)).
Empirical estimates of drafting effects in the swim segment are still limited by mea-
surement challenges. Group formations are likely endogenous and cannot be observed
directly, and drafting exposure may correlate with unobserved ability (sorting/selection
bias). Laboratory and biomechanical studies (e.g., Chatard (1990, 2003); Delextrat (2003);
Bolon (2020)) focus on energy expenditure (metabolic) and performance gains from draft-
ing, but large-scale causal estimates from race data have only been documented recently
(in Reichel (2025)).
In our theoretical framework, we extend contest theory and incorporate models of
strategic externalities (Tullock (1980); Moldovanu (2001); Konrad (2009); Che (2003)) to
develop a structural model in which athletes first select a deterministic level of swimming
effort, followed by an endogenous choice of drafting position. Drafting benefits are cap-
tured through a non-linear function that exhibits diminishing marginal returns beyond a
certain positional threshold. The structural implications of the model are then integrated
into an empirical two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimation framework. To address en-
dogeneity in within-group drafting positions, we employ a leave-one-out (LOO/LOTO)
average of peer swimming ability as an instrumental variable (IV).
Our analysis provides the first large-scale heterogeneous causal estimate of open-water
drafting effects using rich panel race data. This study contributes to the broader literature on
strategy in multi-stage games within performance environments by offering a simple two-
stage model of effort choice and externality constraints. The model aligns with frameworks

Data
We use a rich administrative dataset provided from Triathlon Statistics Austria, covering
all official triathlon races from 2010 to 2024. These include Sprint, Short, Middle, and Long
distance formats, with athlete-level performance data on swim-out segment times, overall
times and the final overall race ranks (after T1, cycling, T2 and the running segment).
2.1
Data Structure and Variables
The dataset is structured across three relational tables:
• Athlete Table: Contains athlete identifiers, gender, and birth year.
• Event Table: Includes event identifiers, calendar dates, and event category.
• Result Table: Reports swim-out time, total race time, and final rank for each athlete-
event observation.
We merge these tables using the respective foreign keys to construct a combined panel
dataset indexed by athlete and event. Derived variables then include age (computed as
time-dependent event year minus birth year), age squared, and binary indicators for gender
and periods (pre-COVID, COVID, post-COVID). Events are grouped into pandemic periods
based on their timing, capturing staggered start policies in 2020 and their subsequent
relaxation into default state.
The initial dataset includes 175,740 observations. After removing records with missing
values, DNFs (Did Not Finish), and DNSs (Did Not Start) athlete-level race outcomes, we
obtain a cleaned panel of 168,391 athlete-event entries.
2.2 Drafting Group Construction
Drafting groups can not be observed directly in the data. We thus infer drafting clusters
using a single-linkage hierarchical agglomerative clustering algorithm approach applied
to swim-out segment times within each event. Athletes are grouped into the same swim
cluster if their exit times fall within a 5-second distance thresholds recorded. Earlier trials
with complete linkage and wider thresholds (10–15 seconds) produced overly broad or
unstable groupings. (Reichel (2025))
Within each cluster, the fastest athlete is designated as the leader, and all others are
assigned ordinal positions (e.g., second drafter, third drafter) based on swim-out time
segment order. We construct both continuous drafting position variables (di) and binary
indicators (e.g., first drafter, last drafter).

Identification Strategy and Empirical Framework
4.1
Research Objective
We want to estimate the causal effect of within-group drafting position Dieg on final overall
race performance Yie, leveraging a leave-one-out (LOO/LOTO) instrumental variable (IV)
strategy. This is embedded in a finite-horizon, multi-stage game with endogenous effort,
group formation. We use following notation:
• i: athlete, e: event, g: drafting group;
• Yie: observed performance outcome (e.g., log finishing rank + 1);
• Dieg ∈{1, . . . , dmax}: within-group drafting position;
• Sie: pre-draft swim time (a proxy for initial effort/ability);
• Xie: athlete and event-level controls.
Because drafting positions reflect strategic sorting and group formation dynamics,
naive OLS is likely biased. We thus use a two-stage least squares (2SLS) framework with
a leave-one-out group swim-time instrument to estimate a local average treatment effect
(LATE) of drafting benefits (functional form from the theoretic model) or drafting positions
on performance.

This 2SLS framework identifies the local average treatment effect (LATE) of drafting benefit

Assumption 2 (Identification Assumptions).
• Instrument relevance: Cov(Zie, Dieg) ̸= 0;

This empirical structure provides consistent estimates of the causal effect of drafting

4.4.2
Structural Validation and Interpretation
The coefficient β1 reflects the average marginal return to drafting benefit. The increasing
and concave structure implies that:
• Greatest positive net gains occur when transitioning from front positions backwards;
• Diminishing returns occur deeper into the pack;
• The optimal position is interior, as predicted by the theoretical model.
Empirical evidence of this shape supports the game’s interior equilibrium logic and
nonlinear (dis-)utility (drafting benefit structure) topology.

Conclusion
This paper contributes comprehensive analysis on Strategic Effort and Bandwagon Effects
in Finite Multi-Stage Games with Non-linear Externalities and draws on empirical causal
evidence using rich panel data from drafting in endurance sports competitions, predicting
effects on final stage rank outcomes focusing on competitive athletes. We develop and
estimate log-linear models with endogenous drafting position, using a leave-one-out group
swim ability as an instrument (Angrist , 2009; Manski , 1993; ?). The results validate the
strategic and nonlinear nature of drafting benefits in multi-stage competitions such as
triathlon.
1. Drafting deeper in the pack improves performance: In the full sample, a one-
position drop in the drafting line improves expected finishing rank by 1.1% (^β =
−0.011). In small groups (size < 10), this effect grows to 62.1% (^β = −0.972, p < 0.01).
In very small groups (< 5), the effect reaches 90.7% (^β = −2.378, p < 0.1).
2. Theory-based drafting benefits follow nonlinear decay: In smaller groups, a one-
unit increase in uncalibrated theoretical drafting benefit B(di) improves performance
by 22–30 log-rank points. These results validate nonlinear decay in marginal gains
beyond the third drafting position, in line with the optimal interior solution from the
theoretic model.
3. Leading is costly: While leader coefficients are negative, IV estimates in smaller
clusters propose that leading imposes performance costs due to higher energy ex-
penditure—reinforcing the value of tactical positioning behind the leader (Delextrat
, 2003).
4. Pooled bandwagon effects highlight the stylized fact that overall race performance
improves with latter drafting position: In a series of binary IV comparisons across
adjacent drafting position bands (e.g., 1–2 vs 3–4, 2–3 vs 4–5), we find large, statistically
significant reductions in adjusted rank—ranging from 31.8% to 60.3%—associated
with moving into a latter drafting position group. That is, athletes further back in
the pack tend to finish with better ranks, consistent with drafting-based performance
spillovers. However, this benefit plateaus at some point and becomes statistically
insignificant beyond positions 7–8, indicating a capped or diminishing bandwagon
effect or aligned with the in theory derived interior optimum.
By focusing on capped/limited outcome ranks, we reduce the influence of extreme
values and measurement error in the fat tails. The findings offer strong empirical support

To estimate the causal effect of drafting position on relative athlete performance, we specify a
two-stage least squares (2SLS) model with a log-linear functional form. The structural equation of
interest is:
log(Rank∗
it) = α + β · Treatit + γi + δt + εit
(1)
where:
• Rank∗
it = Rankit −
¯
Rankt + c is the event-centered rank for athlete i in event t, shifted by a
constant c > 0 to ensure positivity.
• Treatit ∈{0, 1} is a binary variable equal to 1 if the athlete is in the worse of two pooled
drafting positions (e.g., positions 3–4 rather than 1–2).
• γi and δt are cluster and event fixed effects, respectively.
• εit captures unobserved performance shocks.
Because Treatit may be endogenous—potentially correlated with εit—we instrument for it using
the exogenous variation in Group group swim ability. The first-stage equation is:
Treatit = π · GroupSwimMeanit + ηi + θt + uit
(2)
where:
• GroupSwimMeanit is the leave-one-out average swim score of athlete i’s drafting cluster in
event t.
• ηi, θt are the same fixed effects, and uit is the first-stage error term.
Under the standard IV assumptions—instrument relevance (π ̸= 0), instrument exogeneity
(E[GroupSwimMeanit · εit] = 0), and monotonicity—the parameter β captures the local aver-
age treatment effect (IV-LATE) of being a drafter in latter pooled drafter position band on log-
transformed, adjusted rank.
