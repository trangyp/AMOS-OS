Abstract—Fingerprint-based passive localization enables high
localization accuracy using low-cost UWB IoT radio sensors.
However, fingerprinting demands extensive effort for data ac-
quisition. The concept of channel charting reduces this effort
by modeling and projecting the manifold of channel state
information (CSI) onto a 2D coordinate space. So far, researchers
only applied this concept to active radio localization, where a
mobile device intentionally and actively emits a specific signal.
In this paper, we apply channel charting to passive localization.
We use a pedestrian dead reckoning (PDR) system to estimate a
target’s velocity and derive a distance matrix from it. We then
use this matrix to learn a distance-preserving embedding in 2D
space, which serves as a fingerprinting model. In our experiments,
we deploy six nodes in a fully connected ultra-wideband (UWB)
mesh network to show that our method achieves high localization
accuracy, with an average error of just 0.24 m, even when we
train and test on different targets.
Index Terms—Device-free, UWB Mesh, Channel Impulse Re-
sponse, Deep Learning, Passive Sensing, Channel Charting.
I. INTRODUCTION
The widespread deployment and rapid expansion of IoT
radio devices and infrastructure, such as WiFi and ultra-
wideband (UWB), have transformed indoor environments into
dense, radio-rich spaces [1]. At the same time, device-free,
passive radio sensing enables the tracking of objects or in-
dividuals without requiring them to carry any devices [2].
Together, these developments create strong potential for a wide
range of passive sensing applications, including monitoring,
surveillance, presence detection, activity recognition, and even
sensitive tasks like vital sign monitoring [3], [4]. Such applica-
tions are especially relevant in areas like security [5], personal
health [6], and elderly care [7].
Most related work relies on radio strength indicator (RSSI)
measurements from various radio systems such as WiFi [8],
Bluetooth [9], and UWB [4]. However, RSSI provides limited
environmental information, resulting in restricted sensing ac-
curacy [10]. To overcome this, recent approaches use channel
state information (CSI), which offers finer-grained insights
into environmental effects such as reflections, scattering, and
shadowing [8], [10]–[12]. This richer data enables higher
accuracy, especially with high-bandwidth systems such as
UWB [13]. For passive localization, analytic, i.e., non-data-
driven methods extract reflections from a target, e.g., a person
or automated guided vehicle [14], [15], or model the target’s
impact on multipath propagation [16]. However, these methods
typically require line-of-sight (LOS) or detailed environmental
models, limiting their applicability in cluttered or complex
spaces. To enable localization in non-line-of-sight (NLOS)
environments, fingerprinting (FP) can be used [17]. Unlike
analytic methods, FP maps recorded CSI directly to positions,
making it robust to NLOS conditions and environmental com-
plexity. However, FP requires costly data acquisition with ref-
erence positions [18]. Environmental changes, e.g., movement
of large objects, invalidate fingerprints, require updated refer-
ence data, and raise lifecycle management costs [19]. Recently,
the concept of channel charting (CC) was proposed to reduce
or eliminate the need for reference data [20], enabling simpler
deployment and easier updates of FP models. CC models the
manifold of CSI and maps it to 2D coordinates using only
radio signals, either in a self-supervised manner [21] or with
additional context, such as physical motion assumptions [22]
or low-cost sensor data [23]. However, prior work only applied
CC to active localization with mobile transmitters.
Alongside Euchner et al. [24], we propose the concept of
passive channel charting (PCC) for passive localization. While
they apply PCC using a distributed Wi-Fi multi-antenna setup,
we focus on a fully connected UWB mesh. Our approach lo-
calizes a mobile target based on its influence on environmental
reflections and direct signal reflections. Due to the smooth
movement of the target, the resulting variations in the radio
signals remain smooth, allowing us to model the CSI manifold
and project it into spatial coordinates, analogous to active
CC. We implement velocity-based CC [23] that estimates the
target’s velocity using a pedestrian dead reckoning (PDR)
system. Our experiments demonstrate that we achieve high
localization accuracy, with a mean absolute error (MAE) of
up to 0.24 m, even when training and testing on different
individual targets. We also study the mesh configuration to
provide insights for optimal system design.

II. RELATED WORK
Active localization algorithms such as classical Round-
Trip Time (RTT) and Time Difference of Arrival (TDoA)
multilateration [25], [26] perform worse than FP methods
in multipath-rich environments [27]. However, FP methods
need CSI databases with reference positions, which increase
costs [28]. Unsupervised approaches solve this problem and
remove the need for labeled training data [29]. Studer et
al. [20] introduce CC as an unsupervised alternative to tra-
ditional FP. CC models the CSI manifold and maps it to
2D coordinates through manifold-preserving dimensionality
reduction. This enables fully unsupervised FP and captures
environmental reflections. Many CC methods use distance
matrices to represent spatial relationships in CSI data [30],
[31]. Although CC lacks global consistency, Stahlke et al. [21]
achieve it with geodesic distances derived from CSI. Self-
supervised CC reaches moderate localization accuracy [20],
[32], but additional sensor data improves results. Low-cost
sensors such as IMU or odometry estimate relative velocities.
They provide inter-sample distances and help CC match the
accuracy of supervised fingerprinting [23].
Passive localization algorithms locate people by using
signal reflections and geometric models. Sensor noise reduces
positioning accuracy. Zhang et al. [33] study geometric meth-
ods that reduce the effect of noise from a WiFi RSSI sensor
array in device free localization (DFL). Their methods support
both single- and multi-target tracking. Ledergerber et al. [34]
use high-bandwidth UWB systems to detect reflections and
measure delays in CIR for outdoor tracking. Bocus et al. [15]
adapt this to indoor environments. They remove clutter to
isolate reflections from a target. Gentner et al. [16] use envi-
ronmental reflections, assign multi path components (MPCs) to
virtual anchors, and solve localization with geometric models.
These methods often require LOS or detailed maps, which
limits their use in cluttered areas. Zhang et al. [35] improve
accuracy in complex environments. They divide space based
on statistical features, train local DFL models, and merge
them with a variance-constrained method. Rao et al. [18]
propose a low-complexity method. They use calibrated CSI
phases from a single link to run DFL and reduce hardware
costs. Their approach depends on earlier phase calibration [36]
and manual tuning. Recent unsupervised DFL methods use
probabilistic RSSI models to estimate presence and position.
Al-Husseiny et al. [37] apply a hidden Markov model. Hillyard
et al. [38] extend this with a Bayesian method that adapts
to changes. Kaltiokallio et al. [39] use Gaussian smoothing
to increase accuracy and efficiency. These methods rely on
iterative inference and do not scale well. They also depend on
LOS RSSI links, which require dense sensor deployments and
remain vulnerable to MPC interference.
Unlike all existing approaches, we draw inspiration from
CC to achieve accurate localization of moving people by
combining radio CSI with additional information such as
odometry measurements, and we integrate these ideas with
the concept of passive localization.
III. METHODOLOGY
CSI dependents on the surrounding environment due to
effects such as reflections, scattering, and blockage, and it
changes smoothly w.r.t. the movement of the transmitter. As
a result, the radio signals lie on a low-dimensional manifold
corresponding to positions in space. CC leverages this property
to create a mapping from high-dimensional CSI data to spatial
coordinates [20]. However, existing CC approaches have only
been applied in active localization scenarios, where a target
carries a transmitting device.
In passive localization, a target does not carry any active
transmitter. Instead, the CSI varies due to the target’s influence
on the radio propagation, either by blocking existing paths
or acting as a reflector, thereby affecting the signals in the
environment. Due to the target’s smooth movement, the CSI
recorded by the static infrastructure also changes smoothly.
Hence, as in active CC, the CSI in passive setups lies on a
spatial manifold. We extend this concept by projecting the
manifold of static CSI measurements to spatial positions, an
approach we refer to as passive channel charting (PCC).
Fig. 1 illustrates the end-to-end positioning pipeline of
our PCC method. We deploy a static UWB mesh in the
environment, while a target moves within it, influencing the
radio signals by blocking and reflecting them. Most CC
algorithms estimate pairwise distances between measurements
and learn a 2D embedding that preserves those distances.
Following the approach of Stahlke et al. [23], we estimate a
distance matrix based on the target’s velocity, which we obtain
using a PDR system attached via an interial measurement unit
(IMU) [40]. We compute the distance between measurements
by integrating the velocity over time:

IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
This Section describes the UWB mesh (Sec. IV-A), the
PDR system (Sec. IV-B), the Siamese Network configuration
(Sec. IV-C), and the datasets (Sec. IV-D).
A. UWB Mesh
The system operates at a center frequency of 4.5 GHz (band-
width = 499.99 MHz) with 6 DW1000-based nodes forming
a mesh network. 4 nodes (0–3) act as transceivers, and 2
nodes (4, 5) act as receivers, forming a multi-static radar setup
with 14 total links. A time division multiple access (TDMA)
protocol assigns non-overlapping time slots for transmissions.
The system records the CIR for each link and limits it to
90 ns (90 samples at 1 ns resolution), covering about 30 m of
multipath propagation. The system records CIRs at 40 Hz.
B. PDR
An IMU (XSense MTI 300) attached to the foot estimates
a person’s velocity. We apply the Gaitmap toolset [42] for
velocity estimation. As the IMU is mounted in a random ori-
entation, we align roll and pitch using gravity and correct yaw
with a principal component analysis. For trajectory estimation,
we use angular velocity, acceleration, and magnetic field. We
apply an Error-State Kalman Filter [43] together with Rauch-
Tung-Striebel smoothing [44] to estimate the target’s velocity.
C. Siamese network architecture
We use a ResNet-based Siamese network with 5.6 M pa-
rameters. Tab. I lists the full configuration, that includes all
UWB links, i.e., number of antennas. The input dimensions
vary depending on the setup. We start with a 1×1 CNN
layer to expand the channel dimension from 1 to 64. We
then apply 9 ResNet blocks. After each ResNet block group
(3 blocks), the network doubles the channel dimension and
reduces the time and antenna dimensions with a stride of 2.
Each CNN layer uses a kernel size of 3 and padding of 1.
Global average pooling follows across the antenna and time
dimensions, leaving a 256-dimensional channel vector. 2 linear
layers with ReLU reduce this to the 2D target position.

V. EVALUATION
We evaluate our PDR-based PCC for different PDR window
sizes (Sec. V-A) and analyze how the mesh composition affects
localization accuracy (Sec. V-B).
As an upper bound, we compare our method to supervised
FP [17]. The FP model uses the same Siamese network
architecture (Sec. IV-C) and directly fits the output to the
reference positions of the CSI values using mean squared
error (MSE) loss. We measure accuracy using Mean average
error (MAE) and the 90th percentile of the error distribution
(CE90), where the error is the Euclidean distance between the
predicted and reference positions. Each model trains for 500
epochs using the AdamW optimizer [45] with a learning rate
of 0.0001 and a batch size of 128. A learning rate scheduler
reduces the learning rate by factor 2 if the training does not
improve for 50 consecutive epochs. We train the models on
data from 1 target and test on 2 others (P1, P2) to evaluate
generalization. We fit a linear transformation to map the 2D
manifold of the PCC method into absolute coordinates, for
which we use 20 reference positions from the training set.
A. PDR-based PCC
We evaluate the PDR-based PCC for different window sizes
on the fully connected UWB mesh, where all node links
contribute to PCC. Tab. II shows the results; Fig. 3 visualizes
them for P1 (blue) and P2 (orange) with solid lines. As an
lower bound, we include supervised FP results as dashed lines.
Supervised FP achieves highest accuracy: CE90 = 0.31 m
and MAE = 0.17 m for P1; CE90 = 0.23 m and MAE =
0.13 m for P2. The small 0.08 m gap between P1 and P2
shows strong generalization. PCC shows a similar pattern, with
an average error difference of 0.07 m across window sizes,
enabling target-independent localization.

= 0.26 m for P2. This results in a CE90 difference of 0.12 m
for P1 and 0.14 m for P2 compared to supervised FP. Window
size strongly impacts accuracy. Small windows (1–2 s) produce

VI. CONCLUSION
To enable passive localization of moving people with an
UWB mesh, we propose PCC, which builds FP models using
only a few reference positions. Our PDR-based PCC approach
achieves high accuracy, with CE90 up to 0.50 m on unknown
targets, showing strong generalization.
We evaluated different UWB configurations, varying the
number of nodes and their connections. We found that spatial
distribution is as important as the number of links, creating a
trade-off between hardware cost and link budget.
