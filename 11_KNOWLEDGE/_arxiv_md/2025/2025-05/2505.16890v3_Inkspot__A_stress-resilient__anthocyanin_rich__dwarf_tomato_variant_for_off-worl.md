Inkspot: A stress-resilient, anthocyanin rich, dwarf tomato variant for off-world cultivation. 
Sarah Lang1, A'nya Buckner1, Solomon Jones1, Gabrielle Erwin1, Sally Lee1 and Rafael 
Loureiro1* 1Department of Biological Sciences, Winston-Salem State University, Winston-Salem, 
NC, USA *Corresponding author: Rafael Loureiro (loureirorr@wssu.edu) 
 
Abstract 
As humanity prepares for sustained off-world habitation, the development of regolith-based 
agriculture (RBA) is essential for achieving self-sufficiency in space crop production. However, 
lunar regolith's alkaline pH, poor water retention, and high metal content pose severe physiological 
and biochemical challenges to plant growth. This study evaluates the performance of Solanum 
lycopersicum ‘Inkspot’, a stress-adaptive, anthocyanin-rich tomato variant, in comparison to its 
progenitor ‘Tiny Tim’, under control and simulated lunar regolith (LHS-2) conditions. A 
randomized complete block design was employed to assess germination dynamics, morphological 
and physiological traits, fruit quality, antioxidant defense, and root system architecture across 80 
biological replicates over 65 days in controlled environment chambers. 
Inkspot maintained high germination rates (85% in regolith) with low variation (CV = 14%) and 
exhibited only moderate reductions in plant height and biomass under regolith stress, while Tiny 
Tim suffered a 45% reduction in biomass and a 60% decline in fruit yield. Anthocyanin content in 
Inkspot fruits increased 2.5-fold under regolith conditions, serving as both a stress-response 
mechanism and potential bioindicator of substrate health. Physiological assessments revealed that 
Inkspot preserved chlorophyll content, Fv/Fm photochemical efficiency, and stomatal conductance 
to a greater degree than Tiny Tim, correlating with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD 
and CAT) and reduced lipid peroxidation (MDA levels). Root imaging and morphometric analysis 
demonstrated a significant expansion in Inkspot’s root length and surface area in regolith, whereas 
Tiny Tim exhibited severe root contraction and loss of complexity. 
These results underscore Inkspot’s capacity to withstand abiotic stress induced by lunar regolith 
simulants through a coordinated suite of morphological, physiological, and biochemical 
adaptations. This cultivar offers a promising model for closed-loop agricultural systems and ISRU 
strategies aimed at supporting future lunar and Martian settlements.

1. 
Introduction 
Humanity's ambitions to establish a sustained presence beyond Earth have grown considerably in 
recent years, driven by advancements in space exploration and the increasing feasibility of long- 
duration missions to the Moon and Mars. As the focus shifts from short-term expeditions to 
establishing permanent or semi-permanent settlements, ensuring a reliable and sustainable food 
supply becomes one of the most pressing challenges for space agencies and private enterprises 
alike. Traditional approaches to space food supply, which relies on periodic resupply missions 
from Earth, are costly, logistically challenging, and ultimately unsustainable for prolonged off- 
worldhabitation. Consequently, the development of in situ resource utilization (ISRU) techniques, 
including regolith-based agriculture (RBA), is of paramount importance for future off-world 
settlements (NASA, 2022). 
The utilization of lunar regolith as a growth substrate presents an opportunity to achieve self- 
sufficiency in space farming. However, the successful implementation of regolith-based 
agriculture faces numerous challenges. Lunar regolith, primarily composed of silicate minerals, is 
characterized by extreme physical and chemical properties that are not conducive to plant growth, 
including high levels of heavy metals, poor water retention, and the absence of essential organic 
nutrients (Fackrell et al., 2024). 
Regolith’s sharp, electrostatically charged particles pose risks to plant root systems, leading to 
mechanical stress and reduced nutrient uptake. Studies have shown that plant responses to these 
stressors involve complex physiological and biochemical adaptations, including alterations in root 
architecture, antioxidant responses, and secondary metabolite production (Elardo et al., 2022). 
Despite these challenges, identifying plant cultivars with adaptive resilience to such hostile 
environments remains a critical focus of space agricultural research. 
Tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum), due to their relatively short growth cycle, high nutritional 
value, and versatility, have been widely studied in the context of space agriculture (Massa et al., 
2016). Several tomato cultivars have been evaluated for their adaptability to microgravity and 
simulated regolith conditions. Among them, dwarf and stress-adaptive tomato variants have shown 
significant promise in providing a sustainable source of essential vitamins and antioxidants. In 
particular, the Inkspot tomato variant, selectively bred for compact growth, continuous fruit


## Page 3


production, and a unique pigmentation response to environmental stressors, presents an innovative 
solution to the challenges posed by regolith agriculture. 
Despite the growing body of research on plant responses to off-world environments, significant 
knowledge gaps remain regarding the physiological and biochemical performance of stress- 
adaptive tomato variants in regolith-based substrates (Fackrell et al., 2024). 
Previous studies have primarily focused on traditional crop species, often with limited success in 
achieving sustained productivity under space-relevant conditions (Cerqueira et al., 2023). To 
address these challenges, research has increasingly focused on identifying crop species and 
genotypes with enhanced stress resilience. Among various cultivars, dwarf and stress-adaptive 
variants offer particular promise due to their compact morphology and increased tolerance to 
environmental stressors. 
This study hypothesizes that Inkspot will exhibit superior growth performance, germination rate, 
and fruit production under regolith conditions compared to the parent Tiny Tim cultivar. 
Additionally, it is expected that Inkspot’s stress-induced anthocyanin accumulation will correlate 
with oxidative stress mitigation and that differences in plant performance will be significantly 
influenced by substrate composition, including nutrient availability, water retention, and pH 
stability. A comprehensive evaluation of Inkspot’s physiological, biochemical, and growth 
responses under simulated lunar regolith conditions will provide insights into plant adaptability in 
off-world substrates and inform future strategies for sustained food production in space (Llorente 
et al., 2016). 
The insights gained from this research will be instrumental in advancing future ISRU-based 
agricultural systems, optimizing plant growth in off-world environments, and informing the


## Page 4


selection of suitable crops for long-duration missions. As space agencies such as NASA and ESA 
continue to prioritize sustainable exploration strategies, the findings of this study may provide 
critical guidance for the development of closed-loop agricultural systems that can support human 
life on the Moon and beyond. 
2. 
Methodology 
2.1 Plant Material and Growth Conditions 
The Inkspot tomato (Solanum lycopersicum ‘Inkspot’) used in this study is a Tiny Tim-derived 
cultivar selectively bred for compact growth, continuous fruiting, and anthocyanin-based stress 
response mechanisms. Seeds for the comparative Tiny Tim cultivar were obtained from Eden 
Brothers (USA). The other dwarf variants used in the cross-breeding program were Krasnaya 
Shapochka and Helsing Junction Blue all acquired through private donors. 
To eliminate microbial contamination, seeds were surface-sterilized using a 70% ethanol wash for 
one minute, followed by a five-minute treatment in a 10% sodium hypochlorite solution, and were 
then rinsed with sterile deionized water. Plants were cultivated in two distinct growth media: a 
commercial potting mix (ProMix BX) serving as the control and the LHS-2 lunar regolith simulant 
obtained from Exolith Lab - Batch # 001-11-001-1223 -(USA). 
Growth trials were conducted for 65 days in climate-controlled growth chambers (Percival 
Scientific) under a standardized photoperiod of 16-hour light and 8-hour dark cycles. Full- 
spectrum LED lighting provided a photosynthetic photon flux density of 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, while 
chamber temperatures were maintained at 22 ± 2°C during the day and 18 ± 2°C at night, with 
relative humidity controlled at 65-70%. Carbon dioxide concentrations were maintained at 400 ± 
10 ppm to approximate Earth-like conditions. 
A critical consideration in regolith-based agriculture is water retention capacity. To ensure 
controlled hydration and proper seed imbibition time, substrate moisture levels were maintained 
at 30% field capacity through precision irrigation using Milli-Q water (Milli-Q ® IQ 7000). Soil 
volumetric water content was monitored daily using a time-domain reflectometry (TDR) soil 
moisture sensor (HOBO) to ensure consistency across treatments in 500 ml pots. No amendments 
(e.g., nutrients) were introduced, as the primary objective was to isolate the effects of regolith- 
induced stress on plant development. However, to account for potential nutrient disparities 
between potting mix and regolith, a compositional analysis of both substrates was conducted, 
including pH, electrical conductivity, and elemental composition.


## Page 5


2.2 Experimental Design 
A randomized complete block design (RCBD) was implemented with two primary treatment 
groups: control and lunar regolith simulant (LHS-2). Each treatment included 20 per plant cultivar 
replicates (n=20) and a total of 80 plants (CTT control tiny-tim; RTT regolith tiny-tim; CTI control 
inkspot; RTI regolith treatment inkspot). 
2.3 Data Collection and Measurements 
2.3.1 Germination and Growth Parameters 
Germination rate was recorded daily, and the time required to reach 50% germination (T50) was 
calculated. Germination uniformity was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV) of 
germination time. Plant height (canopy-substrate), number of leaves (day 20), and biomass (dry 
weight) were measured weekly using an analytical balance (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) and a 
digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Japan). 
2.3.2 Fruit Production and Quality Assessments 
The number of fruits per plant was recorded at maturity. Anthocyanin content was quantified via 
spectrophotometric analysis at 530/700 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular 
Devices, USA). Brix levels were measured using a PAL-1 digital refractometer (Atago, Japan). 
2.3.3 Physiological Assessments 
Chlorophyll content was measured using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter (Konica Minolta, Japan). 
Photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm ratio) was assessed with a MINI-PAM-II pulse-modulated 
fluorometer (Walz, Germany). Stomatal conductance was measured using an LI-6800 portable gas 
exchange analyzer (LI-COR, USA). All parameters were collected three times a day (8:00 a.m. – 
1:00 p.m. – 6:00 p.m.). 
2.3.4 Biochemical Analysis 
Antioxidant enzyme activity, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), was 
determined through spectrophotometric assays using a UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, 
Japan). Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was quantified via the thiobarbituric acid reactive 
substances (TBARS) assay to assess lipid peroxidation (Feldman, 2019) 
2.4 Microscopic and Molecular Analysis 
Root morphology was analyzed using RhizoVision Analyzer software (RhizoVision, USA). Cross- 
sections of leaves were obtained using a microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany) and examined


## Page 6


under a Leica DM500 light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to assess anatomical 
differences. Total RNA was extracted from leaf tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), 
and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 system 
(Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to assess the expression of stress-responsive genes, including 
those associated with anthocyanin biosynthesis (SlAN2-like) and oxidative stress response. 
2.5 Regolith Characterization 
LHS-2 regolith simulant and control soil were analyzed for pH, electrical conductivity, and 
elemental composition using an ICP-MS NexION 2000 (PerkinElmer, USA). Soil pH was 
measured using a HI5522 pH meter (Hanna Instruments, USA). 
2.6 Statistical Analysis 
All collected data were subjected to statistical analysis using R statistical software (version 4.1.2, 
R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Data normality was tested using the Shapiro- 
Wilk test prior to analysis. Differences between treatment means were analyzed using two-way 
ANOVA with treatment and time as factors. Tukey's post-hoc test was applied for pairwise 
comparisons at a significance level of p < 0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to 
evaluate relationships between anthocyanin content, physiological parameters, and stress markers. 
2.7 Ethical Considerations 
All plant experiments were conducted in compliance with institutional guidelines for plant 
research (40 CFR Part 160). No genetically modified organisms (GMOs) were used in the study. 
3. 
Results 
3.1 Germination and Early Growth Parameters 
Germination rates were significantly influenced by genotype and substrate composition (p < 0.05). 
Inkspot demonstrated a high germination rate across both treatments, reaching 90% in the control 
and 85% in LHS-2 regolith. However, germination in regolith was slightly delayed, with the time 
to 50% germination (T50) increasing from 2 days in the control to 4 days in regolith. The 
coefficient of variation (CV) for germination time remained low in Inkspot, measuring 12% in the 
control and 14% in regolith, indicating relatively uniform germination under both conditions. 
In contrast, Tiny Tim exhibited a significantly reduced germination rate in regolith, achieving only 
50% germination compared to 92% in the control. The delay in T50 was more pronounced, 
increasing by 3 days under regolith conditions (6 days vs. 2 days in control, p < 0.01). Germination 
uniformity was also markedly reduced, as indicated by a higher CV in regolith (25% vs. 10% in


## Page 7


control), suggesting increased variability and inconsistency in seedling emergence under stress 
conditions. 
Differences in plant growth parameters further underscored these genotype-specific responses (p 
< 0.001). Inkspot exhibited a moderate reduction in final height when grown in regolith (29.8 cm 
vs. 35.2 cm in control), but total biomass remained relatively stable (11.1 g vs. 12.3 g in control), 
suggesting an acclimatory response to substrate-induced stress. Leaf count on day 20 showed 
minimal variation, with plants in regolith producing an average of 15 leaves compared to 18 in the 
control (p < 0.472) 
In contrast, Tiny Tim experienced significant growth suppression under regolith conditions. Final 
plant height was reduced by 32%, decreasing from 32.5 cm in the control to 22.1 cm in regolith (p 
< 0.001). Total biomass declined by 45% (6.2 g vs. 10.8 g, p < 0.001), and leaf production at day 
20 was reduced by 44% (9 in regolith vs. 16 in control, p < 0.001), indicating a severe physiological 
impact of regolith-induced stress.

3.2 Fruit Production and Quality 
Fruit productivity varied significantly across treatments (p < 0.001). Inkspot maintained a high 
fruit yield under regolith conditions, producing an average of 16 fruits per plant (range: 14–18), 
which was 80% of its control yield (20 fruits per plant, range: 18–22). In contrast, Tiny Tim 
suffered a drastic decline in fruit production, yielding only 7 fruits per plant (range: 5–9) in 
regolith, a 60% reduction from its control condition (18 fruits per plant, range: 16–20). 
Anthocyanin accumulation in fruit tissues increased significantly in response to regolith stress (p 
< 0.001). Inkspot exhibited a 2.5-fold increase in anthocyanin content (3.0 AU at 530/700 nm vs. 
1.2 AU in control), leading to visibly darker pigmentation. Conversely, Tiny Tim failed to exhibit 
a significant anthocyanin response (1.1 AU in regolith vs. 1.0 AU in control, p = 0.12), suggesting 
a lower capacity for stress-induced pigment accumulation. 
Soluble sugar content, measured via Brix levels, followed a similar trend. Inkspot exhibited an 8% 
increase in Brix levels in regolith (7.0% vs. 6.5% in control, p < 0.05), suggesting an osmotic 
adjustment. In contrast, Tiny Tim experienced a 12% decline (5.3% in regolith vs. 6.0% in control, 
p < 0.05), indicating impaired carbohydrate metabolism (fig.2).

3.4 Physiological Performance 
Physiological assessments revealed genotype-dependent stress responses (p < 0.01). Chlorophyll 
content (SPAD readings) decreased by 10% in Inkspot under regolith conditions (40.8 vs. 45.2 in 
control), but values remained within the optimal range for photosynthesis. Conversely, Tiny Tim 
showed a significant 25% reduction in SPAD values (31.4 in regolith vs. 42.5 in control, p < 
0.001), indicating accelerated chlorophyll degradation. 
The Fv/Fm ratio, a measure of photoinhibition, was significantly lower in regolith-grown plants 
(p < 0.05). Inkspot exhibited a moderate reduction (0.74 in regolith vs. 0.80 in control), suggesting 
mild photoinhibition. In contrast, Tiny Tim displayed a pronounced decline (0.63 in regolith vs. 
0.78 in control, p < 0.001), reflecting severe stress-related damage to photosystems. 
Stomatal conductance followed a similar pattern, decreasing 15% in Inkspot (298 mmol/m²s vs. 
350 mmol/m²s, p < 0.05) and 35% in Tiny Tim (205 mmol/m²s vs. 320 mmol/m²s, p < 0.001), 
indicating substantial reductions in gas exchange under stress.

3.5 Biochemical Stress Responses 
Stress markers varied significantly between genotypes (p < 0.01). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) 
activity increased 2.1-fold in Inkspot (12.1 U/mg vs. 5.8 U/mg in control, p < 0.001), whereas Tiny 
Tim showed only a 1.2-fold increase (6.8 U/mg vs. 5.5 U/mg, p = 0.08). 
Catalase (CAT) activity followed a similar trend, with Inkspot exhibiting a 1.8-fold increase (7.2 
U/mg vs. 3.9 U/mg, p < 0.01), while Tiny Tim showed only a marginal increase (4.1 U/mg vs. 3.6 
U/mg, p = 0.12). 
Lipid peroxidation, assessed via malondialdehyde (MDA) content, was 3.5-fold higher in Tiny 
Tim under regolith conditions (7.8 nmol/g FW vs. 2.3 nmol/g FW, p < 0.001), reflecting severe 
oxidative stress. In contrast, Inkspot exhibited only a 1.6-fold increase (3.5 nmol/g FW vs. 2.1 
nmol/g FW, p < 0.05), indicating a more effective antioxidant defense.

3.6 Root Morphology and Regolith Characterization 
Root morphology analysis (p < 0.001) revealed significant differences in stress adaptation. Inkspot 
developed a larger and more compact root system under regolith conditions, with an increase in 
root length (17.8 cm vs. 15.2 cm in control) and root surface area (52.3 cm² vs. 45.1 cm², p < 0.01). 
Tiny Tim, however, exhibited a 36% reduction in root length (9.2 cm vs. 14.5 cm, p < 0.001) and 
a 26% decrease in root surface area (29.8 cm² vs. 40.5 cm², p < 0.01), indicating a compromised 
ability to explore the substrate. 
Regolith characterization confirmed alkaline conditions (pH = 9.2, range: 9.1–9.3) and low 
electrical conductivity (0.15 mS/cm, range: 0.14–0.16), consistent with previous lunar regolith 
simulant analyses. Elemental analysis revealed high calcium (5400 mg/kg), aluminum (8700 
mg/kg), and iron (3200 mg/kg) levels, all of which may contribute to nutrient imbalances.

4. Discussion 
Our findings indicate that the Inkspot tomato variant (Solanum lycopersicum ‘Inkspot’) possesses 
noteworthy resilience in a simulated regolith substrate, exhibiting comparatively stable biomass 
accumulation, elevated antioxidant enzyme activity, and significant anthocyanin biosynthesis 
relative to the Tiny Tim cultivar. These outcomes align with recent work emphasizing the ability


## Page 12


of tomatoes and related Solanaceae to adapt to a range of abiotic challenges, whether in terrestrial 
fields or in experimental off-world substrates (Alaguero-Cordovilla et al. 2018; Tapia et al. 2022; 
Du et al. 2024). Inkspot’s enhanced anthocyanin accumulation stands out as a central adaptive 
mechanism to high- pH and low-nutrient regolith conditions. This response is consistent with 
earlier reports that anthocyanin production is elevated under multiple abiotic stresses, including 
salinity and UV-B exposure (Borghesi et al. 2011; Tapia et al. 2022; Li & Ahammed, 2018; 
Cappellini et al. 2021). In the context of off-world agriculture, anthocyanins may play dual 
roles—acting as photoprotective pigments in high-radiation environments and as potent 
antioxidants, mitigating reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage. Such stress mitigation parallels 
findings in other species where anthocyanin induction has been correlated with improved 
tolerance to extreme soil conditions and radiation stress (Borghesi et al. 2011; Du et al. 2024). 
The Inkspot cultivar’s greater superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activity under 
regolith conditions suggests a coordinated antioxidant machinery that preserves membrane 
integrity. This aligns with the demonstration by Borghesi et al. (2011) that elevated carotenoid and 
anthocyanin levels frequently co-occur with enhanced enzymatic protection in stressed tomato 
lines. Studies of wild tomato species (Tapia et al., 2022) also emphasize that water deficits and 
strong light can trigger the upregulated expression of genes like DFR and AN2, mirroring the 
patterns observed here for Inkspot’s anthocyanin-related genes. The net effect is improved 
photosynthetic efficiency, despite the challenging substrate, supporting the hypothesis that robust 
antioxidant defense constitutes a key factor in adaptation to off-world horticulture (Du et al., 
2024; Manai et al., 2014; Foyer & Noctor, 2000). 
Beyond physiological and biochemical attributes, root system architecture and canopy 
morphology significantly influence tolerance to harsh substrates (Maia et al. 2018; Morris et al. 
2017). Our microscopic observations suggest that Inkspot’s root expansion under LHS-2 may 
facilitate better water and nutrient uptake—critical in regolith-based agriculture. Similar 
morphological plasticity has been observed in dwarf tomato lines selected for short internodes yet 
retained yield (Finzi et al. 2017). In synergy with anthocyanin-driven photoprotection, these 
anatomical adaptations further enable stable growth under substrate stress (Du et al. 2024). 
The possibility of leveraging local in situ regolith for plant-based life support has gained renewed 
traction with the aim of establishing self-sustaining settlements on the Moon or Mars (Fackrell et 
al. 2024; Paul et al. 2022; Buckner et al. 2025). However, as our data and other studies show (e.g.,


## Page 13


Mazhar et al. 2024), lunar or Martian regoliths are not inert or “soil-like” media. Rather, they 
present chemical and physical hurdles including high pH, low organic matter, and potential metal 
toxicities, all of which may trigger unique stress response pathways in plants (Paul et al. 2022). A 
multi-tiered approach—combining substrate amendments, controlled irrigation, and selection of 
cultivars genetically predisposed to high antioxidant or anthocyanin production may be required 
to mitigate these substrates’ inhospitable traits (Tapia et al. 2022; Du et al. 2024). 
Although our findings support Inkspot’s relative success in regolith simulant, long-term and 
multifactor studies remain necessary to account for the space environment’s full complexity, 
especially microgravity, cosmic radiation, or partial gravity on the lunar or Martian surface (Maffei 
et al. 2024). Future work should also explore how beneficial microbes (Maia et al. 2018) might 
modulate nutrient acquisition or alleviate oxidative and ionic stresses, further enhancing yields. 
Additionally, integrated breeding programs using advanced gene editing and phenomics could 
systematically optimize traits like dwarfism, antioxidant capacity, and root architecture for off- 
world cultivation (Borghesi et al. 2011; Finzi et al. 2017; Zhu et al. 2020). 
5. Conclusion 
The present study underscores that Inkspot possesses both robust antioxidant defenses and stress- 
induced anthocyanin accumulation that facilitate relatively stable growth under simulated lunar 
regolith conditions. However, this substrate remains far from benign, necessitating continued 
refinement of regolith-based agricultural strategies, from nutrient supplementation to cultivar 
development. Insights gleaned from anthocyanin-rich tomatoes and dwarf variants suggest that 
stress-responsive phenotypes can be harnessed for successful off-world horticulture provided that 
plant substrate interactions are systematically understood and managed. These findings lay the 
groundwork for more sophisticated regolith-based systems, which are poised to become a central 
pillar of future extra-terrestrial plant production and sustained human exploration.
