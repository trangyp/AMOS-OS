Abstract 
This paper introduces the Human-AI Governance (HAIG) framework, contributing to the AI 
Governance (AIG) field by foregrounding the relational dynamics between human and AI actors 
rather than treating AI systems as objects of governance alone. Current categorical frameworks 
(e.g., ‘human-in-the-loop’ models) inadequately capture how AI systems evolve from ‘tools’ to 
‘partners’, particularly as foundation models demonstrate emergent capabilities and multi-agent 
systems exhibit autonomous goal-setting behaviours. As systems are deployed across contexts, 
agency redistributes in complex patterns that are better represented as positions along continua 
rather than discrete categories. The HAIG framework operates across three levels: dimensions 
(Decision Authority, Process Autonomy, and Accountability Configuration), continua (continuous 
positional spectra along each dimension), and thresholds (critical points along the continua where 
governance requirements shift qualitatively). The framework's dimensional architecture is level-
agnostic, applicable from individual deployment decisions and organisational governance through to 
sectorial comparison and national/international regulatory design. Unlike risk-based or principle-
based approaches that treat governance primarily as a constraint on AI deployment, HAIG adopts a 
trust-utility orientation – reframing governance as the condition under which human-AI collaboration 
can realise its potential, calibrating oversight to specific relational contexts rather than 
predetermined categories. The analysis reveals how technical advances in self-supervision, 
reasoning authority, and distributed decision-making drive context-dependent and non-uniform 
evolution in trustworthiness conditions — with consequences for the trust attitudes of relevant 
stakeholders that existing categorical frameworks are ill-equipped to track. Case studies in 
healthcare and European regulation demonstrate how HAIG complements existing frameworks 
while offering a foundation for adaptive regulatory design that anticipates governance challenges 
before they emerge. 
 
Keywords: Human-AI Governance (HAIG), trust-utility, agentic AI, dimensional governance, trust 
dynamics, AI oversight, foundation models

1. Introduction: Rethinking Human-AI Relationships 
The governance of AI systems increasingly demands frameworks capable of accounting for 
dynamic, non-uniform shifts in how authority, autonomy, and accountability are distributed between 
human and AI actors. As algorithmic systems take on greater roles in shaping decisions, allocating 
resources, and influencing public life, the question is not merely whether we trust them, but whether 
they are designed and maintained to warrant that trust. What is required is neither blind faith in 
machines nor paralysing scepticism, but a governance model that can continually calibrate how 
much trust is appropriate, and in which contexts. 
 
Artificial intelligence is fundamentally reshaping the landscape of organisational decision-making, 
public service delivery, and institutional governance (Engin et al., 2025; Rudko et al., 2024; 
Shrestha et al., 2019). Across sectors, it is rapidly evolving from basic automation to increasingly 
sophisticated forms of governance partnership (Rahwan et al., 2019; Pedreschi et al., 2025; Liu et 
al., 2025), aiming for human–AI complementarity where collaboration produces greater value than 
either alone  (Stephany & Teutloff, 2024; Akata et al., 2020; Donahue et al., 2022; van Breda & 
Barry, 2020). This evolution is particularly evident in technical advances such as foundation models 
with emergent capabilities, self-supervised learning systems that critique their own outputs, and 
multi-agent architectures that exhibit collective behaviours beyond their individual components. 
These technical developments fundamentally challenge traditional governance frameworks by 
introducing dynamic capability boundaries and distributed agency patterns that resist static 
categorisation. However, our conceptual frameworks have not kept pace with this evolution (Goos & 
Savona, 2024).   
 
This limitation becomes particularly evident as AI systems increasingly permeate domains 
traditionally dominated by human judgment. In healthcare, diagnostic AI systems gradually shift 
from flagging anomalies to making preliminary diagnoses (Yelne et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2024); in 
financial services, credit algorithms evolve from augmenting human judgment to making 
autonomous decisions for standard applications (Gsenger & Strle, 2021); in transportation, self-
driving systems dynamically redistribute authority between human and machine based on 
environmental conditions (Ansari et al., 2022). Even in knowledge work requiring complex 
reasoning—legal practice, academic research, strategic decision-making, and creative production—
AI systems now operate as collaborative partners rather than mere tools (Sowa & Przegalinska, 
2025; Koehler & Sauermann, 2024), progressively assuming greater agency. Multi-agent systems 
embedded in infrastructure further demonstrate this evolution, with interconnected algorithms 
collectively reshaping environments through distributed decision-making (Cugurullo & Xu, 2025; 
Luusua et al., 2023; Engin et al., 2020). Perhaps most consequentially, AI is increasingly embedded 
in processes central to democratic governance —from content moderation that shapes public 
discourse, to algorithmic allocation of public resources, to automated enforcement of regulations, to 
decision support systems that inform policy development (Engin & Treleaven, 2019; Dunleavy & 
Margetts, 2023). AI systems in these contexts fundamentally challenge traditional frameworks for 
understanding accountability, oversight, and democratic control (Coglianese & Lehr, 2017; 
Gritsenko & Wood, 2020; Stokel, 2025) as they incrementally assume greater decision-making 
authority without corresponding evolutions in oversight frameworks. 
 
Current approaches to understanding human-AI relationships rely on discrete categories—tools 
versus agents, decision support versus automated decision-making, or human-in-the-loop versus

This paper introduces the Human-AI Governance (HAIG) framework for analysing trust dynamics 
across evolving human-AI relationships. Situated within but extending the emerging field of AI 
governance (AIG), which Birkstedt et al. (2023) characterise as spanning ethical, regulatory, 
organisational, and technical domains, HAIG foregrounds the relational dimension: how decision 
authority, process autonomy, and accountability configuration are dynamically distributed between 
human and AI actors across evolving deployment contexts. Where existing AIG frameworks are 
predominantly harm-preventive — designed to identify risks, assign categories, and impose 
constraints on AI deployment (Kaminski, 2023; Ebers, 2024) — HAIG's trust-utility orientation 
reframes this: governance is not only a constraint on AI capability but a condition for realising its 
potential. Utility here encompasses not only technical effectiveness but democratic legitimacy and 
public confidence; performance gains achieved at the cost of accountability diffusion do not satisfy 
the trust-utility criterion. By calibrating oversight to the trust requirements of specific human-AI 
relationships rather than to predetermined risk categories, HAIG makes proactive governance 
tractable — anticipating where existing mechanisms will become insufficient before capabilities 
advance past them, rather than responding reactively once governance gaps have become acute. 
HAIG operates across three complementary levels: dimensions, continua, and thresholds, providing 
both theoretical comprehensiveness and practical flexibility by acknowledging continuous agency 
redistribution while identifying meaningful transition points requiring governance adaptation. 
Throughout, human-AI relationship refers to the governance relationship between human and AI 
actors — how authority, autonomy, and accountability are distributed across a given deployment 
context — rather than to the interactional or psychological dimensions of human-AI engagement.

2. Research Approach and Nature of Contribution 
The HAIG framework is a conceptual contribution in the tradition of integrative theory development 
(Jaakkola, 2020): it synthesises existing bodies of knowledge into a new analytical architecture 
rather than generating new empirical data. The governance gap it addresses — a structural 
mismatch between the continuous, multi-dimensional nature of human-AI relationships and the 
static categorical frameworks developed to govern them — motivates a dimensional approach in 
which governance relationships are represented as positions along continuous axes rather than as 
members of discrete categories. That gap has two structural foundations that any adequate 
response must address (Narayanan and Kapoor, 2024). First, risk and governance requirements 
are not properties of AI models but emerge from deployment context: the same system may present 
negligible governance concerns in one application and require stringent oversight in another. 
Second, unlike traditional regulatory objects such as pharmaceuticals or aircraft, AI systems are not

static artefacts — they are continuously updated, fine-tuned, and version-dependent, such that a 
system approved or categorised under one configuration may behave substantially differently after 
subsequent modification. Static categorical frameworks, by design, cannot track either form of 
variation systematically. Dimensional positioning can. 
 
Framework development proceeded through structured synthesis of three bodies of scholarship. 
Human-automation interaction theory (Parasuraman et al., 2000; Vagia et al., 2016; Methnani et al., 
2021) provides the theoretical vocabulary for continuous representation of authority and autonomy. 
AI governance and regulatory scholarship (Mäntymäki et al., 2022; Birkstedt et al., 2023; Kaminski, 
2023; Smuha, 2021) establishes the governance gap the framework addresses and situates HAIG 
primarily within the organisational and regulatory levels of AIG analysis. Trust theory as applied to 
automated systems (Lee & See, 2004; Toreini et al., 2020; Ferrario et al. 2024; Laux et al., 2024) 
grounds the trust-utility orientation and the threshold architecture. These three clusters have largely 
developed in parallel; their integration is itself a substantive contribution. The dimensional approach 
it produces is not novel in principle — Wilson et al. (2025) demonstrate its analytical value within 
clinical decision support — but HAIG applies it at a different level of abstraction: a cross-sector 
governance architecture built on three theoretically orthogonal axes of Authority, Autonomy, and 
Accountability (Engin & Hand, 2025). 
 
By design, HAIG's dimensional architecture is level-agnostic: the same continua apply whether 
calibrating oversight for a single deployment, comparing governance positioning across sectors, or 
evaluating the implications of an emerging regulatory paradigm. The organisational and institutional 
level (Mäntymäki et al., 2022) is the primary locus of directly actionable outputs in this paper — 
reflected in the framework's deployment guidance (Section 4.4) and the healthcare case study 
(Section 5.1) — while the sectorial and national regulatory levels are addressed through Figure 2 
and the EU AI Act analysis (Sections 5.2–5.3). The individual and international levels represent 
natural extensions of the framework's logic, and are identified as future research directions in 
Section 6. 
 
HAIG’s most significant limitation is the absence of longitudinal empirical validation tracking how 
governance frameworks evolve alongside AI capabilities across real deployments — the primary 
future research direction identified in Section 6. A related limitation concerns operationalisation: 
authority, autonomy, and accountability do not correspond to simple physical measurements, and 
developing robust validated measures for them is identified as important future work. The trust 
thresholds in Section 4.3 are designed to generate testable propositions toward this end, making 
specific predictions about where governance frameworks will encounter difficulty that can be 
evaluated against empirical evidence as it accumulates. 
 
To guard against the risk that disciplinary integration coherent in the abstract may fail to capture the 
practical texture of the phenomena it describes, the framework is grounded in cross-sector 
governance practice — spanning applied research collaborations in healthcare, public 
administration, and regulatory contexts; sustained editorial engagement with interdisciplinary 
scholarship at the intersection of data science and public policy; and leadership roles within 
international AI governance networks. The recurring patterns this practice surfaces — static 
categorical frameworks that break down at boundaries, trust mechanisms that fail to scale, 
accountability structures that cannot accommodate distributed agency — are not theoretical 
constructs but observed governance failures that motivate the framework's design, consistent with 
design science approaches to framework development (Hevner et al., 2004).

3. Governing Human-AI Relationships: Current 
Approaches and Their Limitations 
AI integration across public and commercial domains demands new approaches to human-AI 
relationships (Lai et al., 2023; Rahwan, 2018; Wilson et al., 2025). This section maps the landscape 
of existing approaches across four strands that collectively motivate the HAIG framework — not 
arbitrarily, but because their limitations, taken together, define the governance gap it addresses. 
Governance philosophy scholarship (Section 3.1) establishes what existing regulatory approaches 
can and cannot do. Trust and accountability studies (Section 3.2) reveal how confidence in 
algorithmic systems is built and eroded over time. Agentic AI scholarship (Section 3.3) documents 
the technical developments outpacing those frameworks. Section 3.4 then synthesises these 
limitations. 
3.1. Governance Philosophies 
As AI systems increasingly permeate decision-making contexts, several distinct governance 
approaches have emerged to address the challenges of human-AI relationships. These approaches 
reflect different priorities, disciplinary perspectives, and governance philosophies that shape how we 
conceptualise, implement, and regulate AI systems across society. 
Risk-based approaches exemplify the tension between clarity and adaptability. The EU's AI Act 
categorises systems by potential harm (unacceptable to minimal risk), yet struggles with evolving 
systems and contextual variation (Kaminski, 2023; Laux et al., 2024). Foundation models 
particularly challenge this approach with their 'multifunctionality' across deployment contexts 
(Coglianese & Crum, 2025). Rights-based frameworks centre on protecting fundamental human 
rights in AI deployment (Smuha, 2021; UN Human Rights Council, 2024; Toronto Declaration, 
2018). While establishing essential normative foundations, they often lack specific implementation 
guidance for complex socio-technical systems. Principle-based governance, advanced by 
organisations like the OECD and IEEE, establishes broad normative guidelines focusing on values 
like fairness, transparency, and accountability. While creating useful ethical frameworks, these 
approaches often struggle with operationalisation and enforcement in complex technical contexts 
(Mittelstadt, 2019). Trust-centred approaches position trust dynamics as fundamental to effective 
human-AI relationships. These frameworks, including work by Winfield and Jirotka (2018) and 
Toreini et al. (2020), address the social and psychological dimensions of how humans develop 
appropriate confidence in AI systems, though they sometimes lack the regulatory specificity of risk-
based approaches. 
Within these broader governance philosophies, more specific categorical frameworks have emerged 
to address human-algorithm relationships. The dominant approach has been to distinguish systems 
based on automation/autonomy levels or human involvement. 
"Levels of Automation/Autonomy" (LOA) frameworks span a spectrum from manual to autonomous 
operation, with automation frameworks focusing on task allocation (Parasuraman et al., 2000) whilst 
autonomy frameworks emphasise independent decision-making (Vagia et al., 2016). Both struggle 
with contextual variation, positioning systems in static categories rather than recognising their 
dynamic nature. Alkhatib and Bernstein's (2019) research demonstrated that real implementations

consider not just task distribution, but the quality of agency and epistemic relationships within

The widely used "human-in-the-loop," "human-on-the-loop," and "human-out-of-the-loop" trichotomy

human oversight often fails to deliver meaningful accountability in automated decision systems. 
Technical implementations further challenge these distinctions. Horvitz's (1999) work on mixed-
initiative interfaces shows how systems can dynamically adjust the distribution of initiative between 
human and AI components, implementing fluid agency exchange rather than fixed categorical 
roles—approaches now visible in everything from customer service chatbots to judicial decision 
support tools. 
These limitations of rigid categorical frameworks point to the need for more nuanced approaches 
that can address the complex relationships between trust, accountability, and evolving AI 
capabilities across all domains where AI impacts innovation trajectories and institutional 
arrangements.  
3.2. Trust and Accountability in Algorithmic Governance 
This section explores how trust dynamics specifically manifest in accountability frameworks and how 
they evolve alongside AI capabilities. Before mapping these dynamics, a conceptual distinction 
requires precise statement that will be held constant throughout the remainder of the paper. Trust 
and trustworthiness are frequently used interchangeably in the literature, but the distinction matters 
analytically. Trust denotes a relational attitude held by a human actor — individual, institutional, or 
public — characterised by willingness to rely on an AI system's outputs within a defined context 
(Lee & See, 2004); trustworthiness denotes a property of the system, reflecting the degree to which 
its technical characteristics warrant that reliance (Toreini et al., 2020; Winfield & Jirotka, 2018). The 
two are related but not equivalent: a technically trustworthy system may fail to achieve warranted 
trust if appropriate governance structures are absent, and trust may be extended to systems that do 
not warrant it. Accountability mechanisms, as this section explores, mediate between the two. 
The interrelationship between trust and accountability in algorithmic systems operates as a mutually 
reinforcing cycle (trust spirals). Accountability mechanisms serve as the structural foundation upon 
which trust is built and maintained, while trust levels influence accountability demands. Lee and 
See's (2004) foundational work established this perspective, showing how trust in algorithmic 
systems develops through cyclical processes where system performance and accountability 
mechanisms gradually build or erode confidence – a dynamic process rather than a binary state. 
These dynamics operate similarly whether in commercial recommendation engines or welfare 
eligibility systems, consistently demonstrating that trust and accountability exist along continuums 
rather than in discrete states. 
Accountability frameworks have evolved from simple transparency requirements (Pasquale, 2015) 
to more sophisticated models, such as Raji et al.'s (2020) SMACTR framework that address

generative AI, provenance watermarking systems verify content authenticity (Rijsbosch et al., 2025). 
Taken together, these developments reflect the recognition that static assessments are insufficient 
for continuously evolving AI systems. 
This dynamic nature reveals a fundamental misalignment between how trust and accountability 
actually function and what static categorical frameworks can accommodate (Tolmeijer et al., 2022). 
Governance frameworks that treat trust as a threshold to be crossed rather than a relationship to be 
maintained will struggle to track the continuous, context-sensitive evolution this section has 
documented — a structural limitation that HAIG is designed to address.  
3.3. The Rise of “Agentic AI” 
The concept of machine agency has evolved significantly over time, from early discussions of 
automation levels to today's more sophisticated understanding of fluid authority distribution. While 
early frameworks like Parasuraman et al.'s (2000) taxonomies introduced degrees of automation, 
they relied on discrete classifications that positioned systems in static categories. Bradshaw et al. 
(2013) advanced the field by conceptualising autonomous capabilities along continuous dimensions 
of 'self-directedness' and 'self-sufficiency' that adapt dynamically to changing contexts, laying 
groundwork for more fluid conceptions of AI agency. Wei et al. (2022) identify 'emergent abilities' in 
foundation models that appear non-linearly as systems scale, creating discontinuous jumps in 
capability requiring more nuanced governance approaches. Liu (2021) demonstrates that subtle 
variations in "agency locus" —whether an AI follows human-made or machine-learned rules— 
significantly affect user trust and perceived uncertainty, suggesting agency exists on a spectrum 
rather than in discrete states. 
The emergence of what is now termed "agentic AI" represents a qualitative shift in this landscape 
(Acharya et al., 2025; Xi et al., 2025). Unlike traditional autonomy frameworks, which emphasise 
task execution independence, agentic AI systems dynamically redistribute decision authority and 
goal-setting capabilities in real-time, often across contexts. These systems, built primarily on 
foundation models, challenge traditional authority distributions by combining autonomous planning, 
reasoning, and execution capabilities (Yang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Liu et al. (2024) 
document the self-supervision capabilities—where models critique and revise their own outputs 
without human intervention—creating fluid boundaries between human and AI control that defy 
traditional governance categories. What fundamentally distinguishes these systems is their potential 
to dynamically redistribute decision authority based on contextual factors, sometimes autonomously 
reducing their own agency when encountering scenarios beyond their validated capabilities.

These developments have profound governance implications (Kolt, 2025; Shavit et al., 2023). 
Existing frameworks struggle with the variable agency distributions of agentic systems precisely 
because they treat agency as a unified, actor-level attribute — something a system either 
possesses or does not. This framing obscures the failure modes that governance most urgently 
needs to address: systems that exercise high operational autonomy without legitimate authority, or 
that act with apparent authority whilst accountability remains diffuse or unassigned. As Dafoe et al. 
(2021) argue, modern AI systems must learn to negotiate agency distributions rather than operating 
at fixed authority levels — a dynamic that static categorical assignments cannot adequately capture 
and that demands governance approaches capable of accommodating continuous negotiation. 
Addressing these gaps requires disaggregating agency into its governance-relevant components 
rather than treating it as a unified construct. Authority concerns the legitimate entitlement to act or 
decide; Autonomy captures the degree to which action is self-directed rather than delegated or 
constrained; and Accountability addresses the attribution of responsibility for outcomes 
independently of both. These are analytically separable, and their separation matters: it is precisely 
the misalignment between them — authority without accountability, autonomy without legitimate 
authority — that constitutes the central governance challenge of agentic AI. Few existing 
frameworks address how these relationships evolve over time, connect governance requirements to 
specific technological capabilities, or account for the institutional dimensions that shape how 
authority and accountability are distributed across human-AI partnerships. The HAIG framework 
introduced in Section 4. addresses these gaps directly, reconceptualising governance along 
continuous dimensions that can capture the variable and dynamic nature of agency distribution in 
advanced AI systems — not by replacing the concept of agency, but by making its governance-
relevant components tractable. 
3.4. The Governance Gap 
The governance gap manifests in three critical limitations. 
First, rigid categorical frameworks fundamentally misrepresent the continuous nature of agency 
evolution. Transitions between governance states often happen incrementally rather than through 
discrete jumps between categories. 
Second, existing frameworks struggle to account for contextual variation. Regulatory approaches 
like the EU AI Act categorise applications rather than technologies, but discrete risk categories 
cannot capture the dynamic nature of AI agency — the same foundation model may simultaneously 
operate across multiple risk profiles or exercise varying levels of decision authority across 
deployment contexts, requiring more fluid governance approaches than current categorical 
frameworks provide. 
Third, current frameworks lack mechanisms to address the dynamic trust relationships that mediate 
between technical capabilities and effective governance. Without explicitly incorporating trust 
dynamics, governance approaches risk becoming increasingly misaligned with actual system 
capabilities as AI systems develop enhanced reasoning authority and self-supervision capabilities. 
Taken together, these three limitations define a structural governance deficit: existing frameworks 
cannot systematically track context-dependence, incremental agency redistribution, or the trust

4. The HAIG Framework: Dimensions, Continua, and 
Trust Thresholds 
Having established the governance gap that motivates HAIG and the methodological approach from 
which it derives, this section elaborates the framework in full. 
4.1. The Three Governance Dimensions: Authority, Autonomy, and 
Accountability 
The HAIG framework operates across three governance dimensions — Decision Authority 
Distribution, Process Autonomy, and Accountability Configuration — each representing a continuum 
along which human-AI relationships transition. The selection of these three dimensions reflects two 
complementary considerations. First, they are theoretically orthogonal (Engin & Hand, 2025): each 
captures a distinct and non-reducible aspect of the governance relationship, such that a system can 
occupy very different positions on each axis independently (a point Figure 2 illustrates across 
sectors). In practice, correlations between dimensions are commonly observed, and high co-
movement across all three is itself a governance signal worth tracking: when authority, autonomy, 
and accountability migrate together without independent oversight of each, the asymmetries that 
HAIG is designed to detect risk going unnoticed. Second, and more fundamentally, the three 
dimensions correspond to the foundational questions any governance arrangement must answer: 
who decides (Decision Authority), how much human involvement is required in execution (Process 
Autonomy), and who is answerable for outcomes (Accountability Configuration). Other governance-
relevant properties — transparency, explainability, contestability, fairness — are treated within HAIG 
as conditions that bear on how these dimensions should be configured, rather than as independent 
dimensions in the same sense. This framing accommodates them without inflating the framework's 
dimensional architecture beyond what the governance relationship itself requires. 
Decision Authority Distribution: This dimension captures how decision-making authority is 
allocated between human and AI actors, with corresponding implications for how trust must be 
established and maintained. Along this continuum, authority shifts incrementally from humans to AI 
systems—as exemplified in the progression from medical diagnosis (where humans typically retain 
final authority) to content curation (where authority is often shared) to automated trading (where AI 
may exercise near-complete decision authority). This is distinct from epistemic authority — the 
capacity to generate credible knowledge claims (Ferrario et al., 2024) — which HAIG treats as a 
driver of decision authority migration along the continuum rather than as a primary governance 
dimension in its own right. Wilson et al.'s (2025) control relations typology similarly addresses 
authority at the level of the individual human-machine dyad — describing where override capability 
sits within a specific deployment — whereas HAIG's Decision Authority dimension operates at the 
level of governance architecture: the conditions under which any such allocation is legitimate, 
auditable, and revisable across contexts. 
As systems move along this continuum, trustworthiness mechanisms must adapt from process 
verification to outcome validation. For foundation models, this dimension extends to include what we

With generative AI systems, process autonomy is complicated by self-supervision capabilities, 
where models can critique and revise their own outputs, creating a dynamic state where boundaries 
between human and AI control become increasingly fluid. 
Process autonomy directly influences monitoring requirements and intervention capabilities that 
safeguard system operation. As autonomy increases, governance frameworks must shift from 
continuous oversight to exception-based monitoring with clearly defined intervention triggers. This 
shift requires sophisticated boundary detection mechanisms that can identify when systems 
approach operational limits or encounter novel scenarios that exceed their validated capabilities. 
Organisations must calibrate these boundaries to balance operational efficiency against risk 
tolerance, with different thresholds appropriate for different application domains and stakeholder 
impacts. 
Accountability Configuration: This dimension addresses how responsibility for decisions and 
outcomes is distributed across human and AI components, with trustworthiness implications and 
corresponding trust consequences for how accountability is maintained and demonstrated to 
stakeholders. For foundation models, accountability configurations must address emergent 
capabilities, where new functionalities appear through interaction patterns rather than explicit 
design. 
As systems progress along this continuum, accountability mechanisms must evolve from 
straightforward assignment of responsibility to more complex frameworks that accommodate 
distributed agency. Cases such as AI systems that creatively circumvent content policies through 
unforeseen language patterns or medical diagnostic tools that independently begin recognising 
comorbidities beyond their intended scope illustrate this challenge. Emergent capabilities present 
particular challenges for accountability as they often manifest in ways not anticipated by either 
designers or users, as seen when automated moderation systems develop unexpected biases or 
when virtual assistants demonstrate novel problem-solving strategies that bypass established safety 
protocols. 
Accountability configuration determines the legal, organisational, and technical mechanisms 
required to ensure appropriate system behaviour throughout the AI lifecycle. Effective accountability 
requires not only clear responsibility assignment but also practical enforceability through technical 
controls, organisational policies, and legal frameworks. As accountability becomes more distributed, 
organisations must develop multi-stakeholder governance structures that engage system creators, 
operators, users, and affected communities. These structures must be capable of detecting, 
attributing, and addressing consequences arising from complex human-AI interactions rather than 
simple linear cause-effect relationships.

4.2. Trust Dynamics Across the HAIG Continua 
While Section 4.1 examines the fundamental dimensions of human-AI governance relationships, 
this section explores how trust characteristics vary as systems are positioned along those 
dimensions across a human-led to AI-led governance spectrum. The HAIG continua are positional 
rather than temporal: they describe the current distribution of authority, autonomy, and 
accountability in a given deployment context, not a trajectory that systems inevitably follow. The 
same system may occupy different positions simultaneously across deployment contexts, and 
movement in either direction reflects governance choices as much as technical development. That 
positioning is shaped by multiple factors — technological capability, organisational choice, 
regulatory context, and stakeholder trust — of which technical development is the most 
systematically studied but not the only determinant. 
 
Among these drivers, the AI field's explicit and sustained trajectory toward greater autonomy and 
agency warrants particular attention. The progressive development of self-supervised learning, 
emergent reasoning capabilities, and multi-agent architectures reflects a deliberate research 
direction aimed at reducing human intervention requirements and expanding the scope of AI 
decision-making. This creates persistent and directional pressure on governance arrangements: as 
technical capabilities expand the possible range of AI authority, organisational, regulatory, and trust-
based factors determine how much of that range is actually exercised in any given deployment. 
HAIG is designed to track that determination — not to assume it follows technological capability 
automatically. Critically, it is also designed to resist importing the field's own developmental 
narrative into the governance framework itself. When governance frameworks adopt the progressive 
vocabulary of the technology sector — treating greater autonomy as a natural next stage rather than 
a governance choice — they risk normalising a trajectory rather than evaluating it. The capability 
configuration framing is a deliberate methodological response to this risk: each configuration 
describes a cluster of technical properties and governance arrangements that tend to co-occur, 
without encoding any claim that movement from one to another constitutes progress in a 
governance-relevant sense. 
This trajectory is usefully illustrated through four capability configurations — rule-based systems, 
advanced ML, foundation models, and multi-agent systems — that reflect characteristic clusters of 
technical properties and governance arrangements recurring across sectors and contexts. These 
configurations are not stages in an inevitable developmental sequence, nor do they exhaust the 
range of positions a system may occupy on the HAIG continua. They serve as analytical reference

their technical capabilities would otherwise suggest. 
Even where movement along the continua does occur, it rarely proceeds smoothly. Technological

4.2.1. Contextual Variation in Trust Requirements 
A critical aspect of trust dynamics across the HAIG continua is contextual variation—the 
phenomenon where the same AI system operates at different positions along each dimension 
depending on specific deployment contexts, user needs, or task domains. This variation creates 
variable trust requirements for identical systems across different applications. 
This contextual variation is driven not merely by technological capabilities but by domain-specific 
risk tolerances, stakeholder expectations, and regulatory requirements that shape how much 
agency AI can exercise in different settings. This is particularly evident with foundation models, 
which might simultaneously operate at different positions along the decision authority continuum 
based solely on deployment context—not because their capabilities differ, but because the 
governance thresholds at which warranted trust can be sustained vary dramatically across 
deployment contexts. A single large language model might exercise minimal authority when 
providing information for research purposes, moderate authority when generating creative content 
for low-stakes applications, and significant authority when deployed for automated content 
moderation or other consequential decision-making. Similarly, healthcare AI systems might operate 
with high process autonomy for wellness recommendations but maintain tight human supervision for 
clinical interventions. 
This contextual variation appears across all capability configurations but becomes increasingly 
pronounced in advanced systems. Organisations must develop trust mechanisms that 
accommodate this variation rather than assuming fixed relationships between humans and AI 
components.

4.2.2. Non-Sequential Configuration Adoption and the Limits of Accumulated 
Governance Experience 
The same functional purpose can be served by systems occupying very different positions on the 
HAIG continua, and each positional shift carries distinct trustworthiness conditions even when the 
organisational mission remains constant. Medical diagnostics illustrates this clearly. The diagnostic 
function has remained stable across decades, but the governance position of systems serving that 
function has shifted substantially as rule-based expert systems have given way to statistical ML and 
then to foundation model-based multimodal analysis. Each configuration introduces distinct 
trustworthiness conditions (see Table 1) that are not reducible to one another and cannot be 
addressed by governance frameworks designed for a prior configuration. The application context is 
constant; the governance requirements are not. 
 
Where institutions have moved through configurations in sequence, accumulated governance 
experience can inform trustworthiness assessment of successor configurations — transferring not 
the specific mechanisms but the institutional capacity to recognise when governance frameworks 
are becoming misaligned with technical capabilities. This is, however, the exception rather than the 
rule. The more consequential and more common pattern is that institutions adopt new configurations 
out of sequence: skipping intermediate configurations under competitive or regulatory pressure, 
deploying new capabilities before governance frameworks for prior configurations are fully 
consolidated, or finding that deep familiarity with one configuration creates institutional resistance to 
recognising the governance demands of a successor. In these circumstances — which are precisely 
the circumstances HAIG is designed to address — accumulated sequential experience is absent, 
partial, or actively misleading. Positional tracking across the HAIG continua matters most where 
orderly progression cannot be assumed, because it provides governance orientation that experience 
transfer cannot supply. 
This dynamic complements contextual variation by identifying a second axis along which 
governance requirements vary independently of technical capability: the accumulated governance 
readiness of the deploying institution. Taken together, 4.2.1 and 4.2.2 establish that position on the 
HAIG continua is jointly determined by deployment context, institutional readiness, regulatory 
environment, and stakeholder trust — with technical configuration as one input among several 
rather than a determinant. 
4.2.3. Bi-Directional Movement and Non-Uniform Progression 
Configuration adoption patterns are not only non-sequential — movement along the HAIG continua 
is also non-uniform in direction and rate. Organisations do not simply advance toward greater AI 
authority and autonomy; they frequently reverse course, and they rarely progress across all three 
dimensions simultaneously. 
Bi-directional movement is well documented in practice. Content moderation provides a clear 
example — several major platforms initially progressed toward AI-dominant moderation but 
subsequently reintroduced stronger human oversight when stakeholder trust was damaged by false 
positives. This reversal was not a governance failure but a governance response: public and 
institutional acceptance functioned as a constraint on progression regardless of technical capability, 
and organisations that had advanced along the Decision Authority dimension retreated when that 
advancement proved unsustainable. The direction of movement is therefore not simply a function of

Non-uniform progression across dimensions is equally significant — and arguably more 
consequential for governance design. Organisations typically advance at different rates along each 
of the three HAIG dimensions, producing what might be termed governance asymmetries: 
configurations in which, for instance, Process Autonomy has advanced substantially while Decision 
Authority remains constrained. Financial services illustrate this clearly — many institutions have 
granted AI systems significant process autonomy for fraud detection while maintaining tight 
constraints on decision authority, a pattern driven by the interplay between competitive pressures 
(which accelerate Process Autonomy) and regulatory frameworks (which simultaneously constrain 
Decision Authority). Such asymmetries are not anomalies to be corrected but characteristic 
governance patterns that HAIG is designed to surface and track. 
The governance implication is that dimensional calibration cannot be reduced to a single axis. 
Organisations must assess their positioning — and the direction and rate of movement — across all 
three dimensions simultaneously. Systems approaching the verification-to-delegation threshold on 
Process Autonomy while remaining well below the information-to-authority threshold on Decision 
Authority present a qualitatively different governance challenge than systems advancing uniformly. 
The threshold architecture introduced in Section 4.3 is designed precisely to provide orientation in 
these asymmetric cases — marking the points where movement along any single dimension 
produces qualitative discontinuity in governance requirements, regardless of where the other two 
dimensions stand. 
4.3. Critical Trust Thresholds Along the HAIG Dimensions 
While movement along the HAIG continua is continuous and bi-directional, it is not uniformly 
smooth. Within and between capability configurations, AI systems reach critical points where 
existing governance mechanisms become insufficient and qualitatively different approaches are 
required. These points are termed trust thresholds rather than trustworthiness thresholds because 
they are irreducibly relational: they mark points where shifts in system-level trustworthiness 
conditions interact with the trust attitudes of relevant stakeholders — institutional, professional, or 
public — to produce a qualitative discontinuity in governance requirements that neither dimension 
alone determines. Trust thresholds therefore represent not merely quantitative increases in 
capability or agency but qualitative shifts in the conditions under which warranted trust can be 
sustained, and consequently in the governance arrangements needed to maintain it. Four primary 
thresholds are identified, representing fundamental transitions in human-AI governance 
relationships, supported by several secondary thresholds that further delineate the governance 
landscape (Figure 3).

maintained. In tax administration, for example, when systems cross this threshold, governance 
mechanisms shift from comprehensive review of all algorithmic determinations to sample-based 
auditing, altering the basis on which warranted trust can be maintained.

engagement with community stakeholders in governance.

4.4. Trust-Building Strategies Across the HAIG Framework 
As organisations navigate the HAIG dimensions, trust-building approaches must evolve alongside 
both technological configurations and deployment context. 
Effective trust assessment begins with relationship mapping — identifying stakeholder requirements 
across internal users, oversight bodies, affected citizens, and partner organisations, each 
demanding different trust types from technical reliability to value alignment. Organisations must 
identify which trust thresholds they are approaching, since governance arrangements nearing 
verification-to-delegation require fundamentally different mechanisms than those approaching the 
process-to-outcome threshold. Gap analysis typically reveals significant discrepancies, with 
technical teams holding confidence that affected citizens do not share. 
Different positions along the continua require distinct approaches. At early positions, trustworthiness 
rests on transparency and comprehensive verification. Middle positions — recommendation 
systems with shared authority — require demonstrated reliability and confidence calibration. 
Advanced positions need outcome-focused governance with effective exception handling. Complex 
multi-agent positions must address emergent collective behaviours through demonstrated control 
over system interactions. 
Trust in public sector AI must anchor in democratic values across all continuum positions. This 
requires transparency calibrated to stakeholder needs and system position rather than applied 
uniformly; contestability mechanisms through which individuals can challenge outcomes, with rights 
scaling to AI authority levels; and stakeholder participation that ensures governance reflects 
democratic values beyond technical considerations. Regular value alignment reviews — intensifying 
as systems advance — identify misalignments before they undermine institutional legitimacy.

5.1. The Evolving Physician-AI Relationship: Healthcare Through the

Healthcare presents an ideal domain for applying the HAIG framework due to its graduated 
complexity, high-stakes decisions, and evolving regulatory landscape (Dennstädt et al., 2025; Lee et 
al., 2024). This abbreviated case study demonstrates how the HAIG framework's dimensions and 
thresholds can be operationalised in a concrete domain, illustrating the analytical advantages of a 
dimensional approach over categorical frameworks while acknowledging that comprehensive 
analysis would require more extensive examination than this paper permits. 
 
Healthcare AI has progressed through distinctive phases across various medical domains, with 
radiology serving as a particularly illustrative example. The evolution spans from rule-based clinical 
decision support systems with minimal decision authority (Papadopoulos et al., 2022), to advanced 
ML systems with greater process autonomy (Wang & Summers, 2012), to foundation model-based 
diagnostic partners (Qiu et al., 2024), and emerging multi-agent care coordination systems (Moritz 
et al., 2025). In radiology specifically, early computer-aided detection systems utilised rule-based 
pattern matching (Giger et al., 2008), then evolved toward convolutional neural networks requiring 
statistical validation (Chartrand et al., 2017), and most recently to foundation model-based 
diagnostic systems introducing challenges around emergent capabilities (Thieme et al., 2023)—all 
while serving the same fundamental diagnostic purpose. This technological evolution within a stable 
application context (Steiner et al., 2021) demonstrates why dimensional governance approaches 
provide more precise calibration than categorical frameworks. 
 
The Decision Authority continuum in healthcare spans from purely informational systems to those 
with significant diagnostic authority. Examples range from basic reference tools like drug interaction 
databases  (Knox et al., 2024), to early warning systems generating alerts (Henry et al., 2015), to 
diagnostic systems for standardised image interpretation with significant classification authority 
(McKinney et al., 2020). Stroke diagnosis AI systems exemplify this evolution, progressing from 
simple support tools to systems with differentiated authority based on case complexity (American 
Heart Organisation, 2024). Importantly, this movement is bi-directional in healthcare settings. Some 
systems initially progressed toward greater AI decision authority in medication management but 
subsequently reintroduced stronger human oversight after encountering unexpected edge cases 
(Lyell & Coiera, 2017), while automated sepsis alert systems were often scaled back after 
experiencing alert fatigue (Ginestra et al., 2019), demonstrating how organisations calibrate 
authority distribution through experimentation and learning.

5.3. HAIG as a Basis for Adaptive Regulatory Design 
Beyond complementing categorical frameworks, HAIG can serve as an alternative regulatory 
foundation for jurisdictions seeking governance approaches that evolve alongside technological 
capabilities rather than requiring periodic legislative revision (e.g. NIST AI RMF, 2023). 
A HAIG-based regulatory approach would establish graduated requirements that scale with a 
system's position across the three governance dimensions rather than assigning fixed requirements 
by application type. Its modular structure — admitting new dimensions, additional thresholds, and 
refined trajectories as implementation experience accumulates — provides adaptability that 
categorical frameworks structurally cannot achieve. Governance intensity remains proportional to 
trust requirements rather than predetermined risk categories, balancing innovation benefits against 
potential harms without requiring frequent legislative revision.

6. Discussion and Conclusions 
The HAIG framework addresses a governance deficit that the existing AI governance literature has 
identified but not resolved. Birkstedt et al. (2023) document persistent knowledge gaps around how 
governance frameworks evolve alongside AI capabilities; Laux et al. (2024) show that the EU AI Act 
conflates trustworthiness with acceptable risk, losing the relational dimension of trust; Coglianese 
and Lehr (2017) diagnose the core accountability challenge of algorithmic governance as 
incremental authority migration that escapes categorical notice. HAIG responds to all three. By 
representing governance relationships as positions along three continuous dimensions rather than 
as members of discrete categories, the continuum architecture tracks how authority, autonomy, and 
accountability are actually distributed and how that distribution evolves. Its trust-utility orientation 
restores the relational logic that risk-based approaches suppress. And its threshold system provides 
structured early warning precisely where categorical frameworks go blind: at the points where 
quantitative shifts in any dimension produce qualitative discontinuities in governance requirements. 
The framework’s three analytical contributions are distinct. The first is the governance asymmetry 
diagnostic: tracking the three dimensions simultaneously makes visible the misalignments — rapid 
Process Autonomy advancement against constrained Decision Authority; accountability frameworks 
lagging both — that single-axis approaches systematically miss. The second is the non-sequential 
configuration insight: institutions that adopt new AI configurations out of sequence cannot rely on 
accumulated governance experience to orient them, and positional tracking provides the orientation 
that experience transfer cannot. The third is the threshold architecture as a research programme: 
rather than producing principles to be endorsed or categories to be assigned, HAIG generates 
specific, empirically evaluable propositions about where governance mechanisms will encounter 
difficulty — the response to Mittelstadt’s (2019) observation that principles alone cannot guarantee 
ethical AI. 
The framework’s implications differ by audience. For regulators, the EU AI Act analysis 
demonstrates that dimensional calibration and categorical compliance are complementary, not 
competing: categorical frameworks establish baseline requirements; HAIG provides the finer-
grained calibration within categories that static tiers structurally cannot. For deploying organisations, 
the key practical contribution is the diagnostic function — governance choices about how much of 
the authority space technical capabilities open up to actually exercise should be made deliberately, 
with attention to positioning across all three dimensions simultaneously. For oversight bodies, the 
threshold architecture offers what Coglianese and Lehr’s diagnosis demands: a mechanism for 
anticipating authority migration rather than responding to it after the fact. 
The framework’s primary limitation is the absence of longitudinal empirical validation. This is also its 
most significant research direction: whether the trust thresholds predict governance failure points in 
practice, whether asymmetry patterns recur characteristically across sectors, and how institutional 
governance readiness mediates configuration transitions are all open empirical questions. The 
threshold architecture is designed to generate testable propositions toward this end. Secondary 
directions include operationalising dimensional measurement, extending the framework to the 
individual and international levels, and developing governance theory for the multi-agent frontier that 
the Individual to Collective AI threshold marks. 
As AI systems continue to evolve in capability and deployment scope, the governance challenge is 
not primarily one of identifying new risks but of maintaining alignment between technical capability,

Several implementation domains would particularly benefit from a continua approach to human-AI 
relationships rather than strict categorical frameworks (see Table 3). These sectors share 
characteristics that make the nuanced perspective of dimensions and continua especially valuable. 
From public services balancing democratic accountability with efficient delivery, to healthcare 
systems navigating graduated clinical authority, to financial services adapting to dynamic risk

The HAIG approach particularly valuable for sectors/applications with: high consequence decisions 
with varying stakes across contexts, professional judgement requirements that cannot be fully

frameworks that already recognise degrees of responsibility, and risk profiles that vary across

alone could offer. These sector-specific applications provide valuable comparative examples, 
demonstrating that the multidimensional HAIG framework has broad applicability across domains
