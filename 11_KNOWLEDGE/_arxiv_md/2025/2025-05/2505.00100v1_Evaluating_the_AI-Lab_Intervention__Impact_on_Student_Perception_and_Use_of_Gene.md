Background and Context. Generative AI (GenAI) is becoming increasingly common in computer science education, yet its effects on
student learning, skill development, and perceptions remain relatively underexplored. Despite widespread concerns about overreliance
on GenAI, few studies have systematically examined how structured scaffolding can shift students’ engagement with GenAI tools in
formal educational settings.
Objectives. We investigate whether and how a dedicated “AI-Lab” intervention, which emphasizes scaffolding and mindful usage,
shapes students’ understanding, perceptions, and applications of GenAI tools in undergraduate computer science and first-year
engineering courses. Our main goal is to determine whether students develop a more deliberate approach to using GenAI that preserves
and enhances essential skill building.
Method. Over three regular semesters at Purdue University, we implemented the AI-Lab in two mandatory Data Structures and
Algorithms courses, one elective Competitive Programming course, and one first-year engineering course, yielding 831 valid student
responses. Using a mixed-methods approach, we collected and analyzed quantitative survey data (pre- and post-intervention) as well
as qualitative focus group transcripts to capture both broad trends and in-depth perspectives.
Findings. While the overall frequency of GenAI usage for homework or programming projects remained largely stable, we
observed large effect sizes in comfort and openness across conceptual, debugging, and homework problems. Notably, usage patterns for
debugging also shifted statistically significantly, reflecting students’ more mindful and deliberate approach. Focus group discussions
corroborated these results, suggesting that the intervention “bridged the gap” between naive GenAI usage and more nuanced, reflective
integration of AI tools into coursework, ultimately heightening students’ awareness of their own skill development.
Implications. Our findings underscore that structured scaffolding – such as the AI-Lab – can help students harness GenAI’s
benefits without undermining core problem-solving abilities. By demonstrating how student perceptions and practices evolve through
targeted intervention, we provide evidence-based guidance for educators seeking to integrate GenAI effectively and responsibly into
computing curricula, and we highlight directions for further research on GenAI-supported pedagogy.

students to generate human-like text based on prompted instructions, raising new pedagogical and ethical challenges

Existing guidance on integrating GenAI in education primarily adopts a broad academic perspective, focusing on

process.
To support AI literacy in computing education, the SEAME framework has been introduced, structuring lessons
around five key dimensions: social impacts, ethical considerations, application of AI, mechanisms of AI functionality,
and the evaluation of AI systems [12]. Similarly, researchers at Aalto University in Finland have explored the use of
GenAI to provide personalized learning experiences and automated feedback in computing education. Their approach
emphasizes training students to write effective prompts and leveraging AI tools to improve programming skills [16].
Several studies highlight the necessity of educating students on the ethical implications and limitations of GenAI
[1, 18]. Key concerns include safeguarding sensitive information, recognizing the potential inaccuracies of AI-generated
content, understanding AI’s capacity to produce harmful material, and adhering to platform-specific terms of use.
In the spirit of protecting the integrity of CS education, we use the AI-Lab Intervention, first introduced by [8] in
2023 on the preprint server arXiv.com and published the following year, in second-year, collegiate computer science
and first-year engineering courses and provide the first empirical evaluation of its effectiveness over the course of two
semesters. We study it using a mixed methods model with pre- and post-surveys as well as focus groups, and present
our findings in Section 4. Detailed breakdown of student population and courses can be found in Section 3.1.
1.1
AI-Lab
The AI-Lab framework [8] offers a structured approach for instructors to introduce GenAI to students in a pedagogically
effective manner. Grounded in Vygotsky’s More Knowledgeable Other theory, this framework positions the instructor
as an expert in the course’s technical content while acknowledging the evolving nature of GenAI tools. Rather than
assuming absolute authority over AI applications, instructors are encouraged to engage with these tools in the classroom
at the same level as students, providing guidance and scaffolding to facilitate meaningful learning experiences.
The AI-Lab framework is structured into multiple stages (see Figure 1 from [3]) to facilitate a guided exploration of
GenAI in education. The first stage (Instructor Preparation) involves the instructor selecting an AI tool and a relevant
topic, allowing them to assess the nature and accuracy of the tool’s outputs. Ideally, the chosen topic should expose AI
limitations, producing incorrect or ambiguous responses to prompt critical analysis.
In the second stage (Prelab), students engage with offline preparatory materials designed to familiarize them with
fundamental AI concepts, prompting strategies, and the generation of scholarly content. At this stage, students also
complete an anonymous survey to report their prior usage and perceptions of AI tools in their learning process.
The third stage (In-class Lab) consists of an in-class intervention in which the instructor integrates AI into the lesson
to generate responses to basic definitions, examples, and analytical scenarios. This demonstration is followed by a
structured discussion on the appropriate and ethical use of AI in education. Students are then encouraged to experiment
independently with the AI tool by posing more complex queries related to the course topics. Peer discussion is central

1.2
Research Questions
Since the authors’ introduction to it, the AI-Lab framework has been implemented across multiple semesters. However,
due to course enrollments and sample size constraints, strong empirical evidence of its impact on computer science edu-
cation remained limited. Dickey et al. [8] provided experiences to gauge the validation of the framework, demonstrating
its effectiveness. Additionally, Bejarano et al. [3] investigated shifts in students’ usage and perceptions of GenAI during
the Spring 2024 semester in two Data Structures and Algorithms courses after running the AI-Lab. Although promising,
the effect of the framework on Computer Science students required more insightful and rigorous analysis.
To this end, we propose two research questions to guide our work:
(RQ1) How and how much are students using GenAI before and after the AI-Lab intervention?
(RQ2) Are Students Open to Using GenAI in Their Learning Processes? If So, In What Scenarios?

METHODS
In this study, we utilized a mixed-methods approach to thoroughly investigate how students use and perceive GenAI
in academic settings. Our methodology integrates quantitative surveys with qualitative focus group discussions to
provide both statistical rigor and rich, contextual insights. The quantitative component involves administering pre-
and post-intervention surveys across multiple courses and semesters, allowing us to track changes in both usage
patterns and attitudes toward GenAI. We then complement this data with in-depth focus groups, which capture nuanced
reflections and experiences that surveys alone might overlook. The focus groups are further enriched by happening
several weeks after the post-surveys are administered, allowing for an evaluation of the more permanent effect the
intervention had.
This section details our overall methodological framework, including survey design, data collection strategies,
statistical analyses, and qualitative procedures. By employing paired sample tests and effect size estimations for our
quantitative data, and thematic analysis for our qualitative findings, we ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the
intervention’s impact on student learning.
2.1
Survey Design and Data Collection
This study examines student usage and perceptions of GenAI through surveys conducted before and after an academic
intervention. Four surveys were administered, focusing on Pre- and Post-Usage and Pre- and Post-Perception of GenAI.
Data were collected in Spring and Fall 2024. Students in multiple courses could complete surveys for each course,
allowing analysis across different academic contexts. Only students who completed the intervention and both pre- and
post-assessments were included in this analysis.
2.2
Statistical Analysis
2.2.1
Paired Sample Analysis. A before-and-after study design was employed to evaluate the impact of the intervention
on measured outcomes. The same participants were assessed at two time points: before (pre-test) and after (post-test)
the intervention. To determine whether significant differences existed between pre- and post-intervention responses, a
Paired Sample t-test was conducted.
The assumptions for the paired sample t-test include:
(1) The differences between paired samples follow a normal distribution.

(2) The samples are paired, meaning the same participants were measured under two different conditions.
To assess the normality assumption, a Shapiro-Wilk test was performed on the distribution of paired differences. If
the assumption was violated, a Paired Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was used as a non-parametric alternative, which
evaluates the median difference rather than the mean difference.
2.2.2
Effect Size Estimation. To quantify the magnitude of observed differences, Rank-Biserial Correlation (𝑟𝑟𝑏) was
used as the effect size measure. Rank-Biserial Correlation is the most appropriate effect size metric for paired data
because it directly reflects the proportion of favorable and unfavorable differences within matched samples. Using the
paired Wilcoxon test statistic, W, it provides an interpretable measure of how strongly values increased or decreased
between pre and post-intervention.
Effect size interpretation (Rank-Biserial Correlation metric 𝑟𝑟𝑏):
• ±0.00 −±0.10 →Negligible effect
• ±0.10 −±0.30 →Small effect
• ±0.30 −±0.50 →Medium effect
• > ±0.50 →Large effect
2.2.3
Significance Threshold. All statistical analyses were conducted using two-tailed tests, with a significance threshold
of p < 0.05 (95% confidence level).
2.3
Focus Groups and Data Collection
This study employs a mixed methods approach, integrating qualitative data to enrich insights into perceptions, strategies,
and reflections of computer science and engineering students on GenAI usage. Qualitative data were drawn from
focus group discussions post-intervention with questions that encouraged students to reflect on their use of AI before
and after participating in the study. Focus groups allow for more flexible and in-depth data collection than individual
assessments, encouraging natural participant interaction [11].
Given the study’s focus on capturing nuanced changes in student behavior and attitudes, we used qualitative methods
to supplement survey data. This allowed for a more comprehensive understanding of how the AI-Lab intervention
influenced students’ learning experiences. Qualitative approaches help explore complex social processes, capture
underlying motivations, and provide a deeper understanding of participants’ perspectives, which are often difficult to
measure quantitatively [6].
On average, the focus groups occurred 5-7 weeks after the intervention at the end of the Fall semester, allowing
students to experience the full scope of the intervention before reflecting during the focus group discussion. This
approach gave students reasonable time to reflect on their AI Lab experience, capturing longer-term perceptions rather
than just immediate responses. This enabled richer insights into retained learning and behavioral changes.
We provided equal opportunity for participants to join the focus group interviews. They were recruited by email
with information about how to sign up, and there was a monetary incentive for participation. We scheduled the group
interviews based on students’ availability and conducted six sessions, each with a different number of participants.
Before data collection, participants signed consent forms. During the focus group interviews, we followed the
protocol by informing students that their participation was voluntary and that they could leave at any time if they felt
uncomfortable. We also clarified that they were not required to answer any questions they did not feel comfortable
answering. Their names were anonymized to address ethical concerns and prevent conflicts of interest with two

2.4
Qualitative Analysis
We transcribed and analyzed data from the focus group interviews using thematic analysis, which offers a flexible yet
rigorous approach for identifying, analyzing, and interpreting patterns in qualitative data, particularly suitable for
exploring participants perceptions and experiences [4, 20].
We began the analysis using a deductive approach, capturing the language from the research questions to develop
initial themes. This approach guided our early coding framework. After our initial glance at the data, we engaged in
theme refinement through discussion, going back and forth between the data and the emerging codes. This occurred
through an iterative process with theme development evolving through dialogue between two researchers and their
interpretations. The researchers coded the same two transcripts first, comparing results and reaching agreement before
moving on independently. This builds intercoder agreement and supports credibility in the findings. This also supports
researcher triangulation, where multiple researchers provide diverse interpretations, increasing trustworthiness. Moving
forward, we refined theme names and definitions and when we reached an agreement, we organized codes, applying
them to transcripts using NVivo software. Please see Section 3.3 for this analysis and Section 5.1 for the questionnaire
used.
Our final set of themes captured broad trends and subtle tensions in the data. These included:
• Exploration and Strategic Use
• Stable Frequency, usage evolvement
• Improved Understanding of AI’s limitations
• AI as a Support Tool for Learning
• AI avoidance and boundaries
• Ethical Concerns and Internal Tension
The final themes served as the foundation for our qualitative findings presented in Section 3.3. These findings
illustrate how students engaged with GenAI tools and how their perceptions shifted after the intervention.

Student population
This intervention was performed at Purdue University in three computer science (CS) courses and one engineering
(ENGR) course over Spring 2024 (S24) and Fall 2024 (F24). Both CS and ENGR programs are highly competitive, rigorous
programs. Not all courses were held every semester. DSA-CS (CS 25100, Data Structures And Algorithms, for CS majors)

(CS 21100, Competitive Programming I) is an elective credit typically taken by second year CS students, not required by
any degree plan. ENGR (ENGR 13000, Transforming Ideas Into Innovations) is a first-year engineering course covering
skills in managing complex problems related to design, systems analysis with computational tools, and academic
professional development. ENGR is left out of the data unless explicitly noted otherwise, due to the primary research
focus being on CS undergraduates.
We also analyzed the differences across demographics like gender, race/ethnicity, residency, and classification
(sophomore/junior/senior) and found no significant differences between these groups.

Effect Size. For statistically significant questions, we conduct post-hoc effect size analysis using Rank Biserial

substantial and meaningful changes in student responses. This reinforces the practical significance of the findings,
demonstrating not only statistical significance but also consistent and impactful improvements across all measured
areas. The ability to assess paired differences at an individual level further strengthens the interpretation, providing
clear evidence of the intervention’s effectiveness on a student-by-student basis.

Findings from Focus Groups Discussion
We analyzed focus group data from multiple courses during Spring 2024 to explore how students understood and

3.3.1
RQ1: How and how much are students using GenAI before and after the AI-Lab intervention?
From Exploration to Strategic Use. Students reported they initially used GenAI in a trial-and-error manner and their
prompts often lacked structure. Before the intervention, they mainly used such tools for summarization, research, and
translation. Many struggled with unclear prompts and doubtful AI responses. This caused frustration and confusion,
particularly due to the inaccuracy of AI tools. Thus, before the AI Lab, students lacked prompting skills and often
abandoned AI when it didn’t work immediately, as described by these participants:
“Usually whenever I try to use ChatGPT, I sort of just give up on it really fast if it doesn’t give me the
right answer.” – Focus Group 5, Speaker 3
“Before we hadn’t really been given guidelines on how to use it... I thought it was helpful in guiding us,
giving different examples of how it could be applied.” – Focus Group 1, Speaker 3
After intervention, students reported learning how to refine prompts, add context, and engage in more interactive,
iterative conversations with GenAI tools. They began using more structured prompting techniques:
“Now I give it, for example, everything I do know... don’t change those answers. Based on what I have,
give me the answer to A.” – Focus Group 6, Speaker 2
“I felt like it did much better once it was given clear rules... especially if I would explicitly dictate what I
wanted it to tell me.” – Focus Group 5, Speaker 1
These reflections illustrate that while the frequency of use may not have increased for all students, their approach
shifted substantially from casual to more purposeful.
Frequency Remained Stable, Yet Rationale for Use Evolved. While participants clearly demonstrated a significant shift
in their approach, which shows the intervention improved prompting practices, it did not lead to a major change in
how often students used GenAI. For instance, before the intervention, some students used AI frequently, while others
rarely interacted with it. After the intervention, those students reported maintaining a similar level of use, although
engaging with the tools more intentionally and effectively. As one student shared:
“It’s pretty much the same. I just learned better ways to use it.” – Focus Group 1, Speaker 3.
Another student corroborated this point, emphasizing how their approach became more refined:
“I don’t think I use it more, but I definitely use it better. Now I know how to refine my prompts instead of
wasting time rewriting them over and over.” – Focus Group 3, Speaker 2

DISCUSSION
This study examined the impact of a GenAI-focused instructional intervention on undergraduate students’ understanding,
perceptions, and usage of GenAI tools across computing and engineering courses. This was the first time testing the AI
Lab framework with actual students across different classes and semesters, using survey and interview data. Although
the intervention was built on a strong theory, this study provides the first validation of the framework from an empirical
perspective.
In analyzing the survey data, we discovered significant changes across multiple measures of students’ comfort and
openness to GenAI, with Rank-Biserial Correlations indicating large effect sizes in several key areas. Rather than
inducing a uniform shift in overall usage, our results suggest that the AI-Lab fostered more focused and deliberate
attitudes toward GenAI. This aligns with the intervention’s dual emphasis: enabling students to experience potential
failures of GenAI firsthand while also offering best practices for using the tools responsibly. The strong effect sizes
highlight that this approach did meaningfully shape student perspectives, especially regarding why and how to use
GenAI, without prompting a dramatic spike in frequency of use. In other words, the framework appears to have
succeeded in promoting mindful experimentation and reflective practice, rather than rote adoption.
In particular, the large effect sizes for comfort and openness to GenAI indicate that many students underwent a
meaningful shift in attitude, even though frequency of use for homework or programming projects did not significantly
increase. This partially dispels the concern that explicit AI instruction would inevitably encourage more academic
dishonesty or superficial reliance on GenAI. Rather, students did become more willing and prepared to integrate
GenAI into their learning process, but they remained cautious about substituting it for their own problem solving. The
absence of major changes in usage frequency thus suggests that students retained healthy skepticism or simply found
a comfortable equilibrium in line with existing course policies. Coupled with the qualitative data, these large effect
sizes for comfort and openness strongly support our interpretation that the AI-Lab intervention primarily enhanced
students’ understanding and critical use of GenAI, rather than pushing them toward the extremes of overreliance.
Qualitatively, students described significant improvement in their use approach, learning to prompt GenAI tools more
effectively by being more strategic and applying critical thinking when evaluating AI outputs. While most students

4.1
Differences in Courses
In addition to second-year computer science classes, we also performed the AI-Lab intervention in a first-year engineering
course ENGR, previously introduced in Section 3.1. The analysis of ENGR showed a higher intervention impact than
any other course by number of questions with significant p-values, when bucketed by course over both S24 and F24, as
seen in Section 4.1. To further explore the impact of ENGR, one can observe in Section 4.1 that by adding ENGR to
the CS courses in F24, we gain statistical significance in comfort using GenAI for homework problems. This makes
intuitive sense because on that question, ENGR had a p-value of 0.0219, CP had 0.0018, and DSA-CS had 0.2761. With
the combined CP and DSA-CS p-value of 0.0592 being nearly significant, ENGR pushed it over the edge. Sample sizes
for this data can be found in Tables 16 and 17.
In short, when the AI-Lab was applied to a non-CS discipline, we found that it had an even higher impact than it did
in CS, strongly supporting the generalizability of the AI-Lab intervention’s positive results. Anecdotally, this follows
from the notion that computer science undergrads tend to be more willing to experiment with and use cutting-edge
technologies than other disciplines. Extending to engineering students provides a structured way for those students to
experiment with new technology, that they may not have known how to begin using, in an educationally positive way,
potentially leading to even higher educational outcomes for those students.
Based on Figure 4b, one can see that each course had a different average perceived shift in their desire to use GenAI.
Explicitly, CP, DSA-CS, and ENGR skewed towards more usage, while DSA-DSAI skewed towards less usage. Based on
student interactions and focus groups, we hypothesize that DSA-DSAI, as DS/AI majors, were likely to be overreliant
on GenAI coming into the intervention, due to their specific major structure and the perceived difficulty of their classes.
On the other hand, CS and ENGR majors were more cautious to begin with, erring on the side of under-use, whereas
we wanted to encourage all students to use these tools for their educational benefit.
Future studies are necessary to better analyze differences between courses, as this study was primarily designed to
measure the overall impact and success of the AI-Lab intervention on CS undergraduates.

4.3
What We Didn’t See (Null Findings)
At this point we return to data analysis focused on only CS courses. Although we saw shifts in 6 of the 9 questions,
we did not see significant differences in openness to using GenAI to help with debugging as well as how frequently
students use GenAI on programming projects and homework problems.
Looking at the plots for debugging openness (Table 4), it is clear that students were already very open to using GenAI
to help debug, and the AI-Lab intervention was not primarily designed to encourage or discourage use of GenAI for
debugging. Comparing to openness of conceptual questions and homework problems, Tables 3 and 5, the intervention
was directly designed to help students experience the benefits of using GenAI for conceptual questions and caution
them against overreliance, and there was a significant change in those two categories. Furthermore, debugging is very
rarely, if ever, explicitly taught in CS collegiate curriculum. educators expect students to learn it on their own as they
progress through the degree, and as a consequence, a student is never graded on such activities. As a student, it then
becomes much less risky and much less taboo to use the new shiny tool on a non-graded, non-taught activity that
is a frequent pain point. It is worth noting that one of the primary ways CS educators implement learning through

In summary, the unchanged frequency of GenAI use in programming projects and homework suggests that students
already exhibit well-established, cautious practices in these contexts. Their behavior, likely shaped by ingrained study
habits and the academic stakes involved, appears robust and resistant to intervention.
4.4
If I teach them GenAI, will they use it to cheat more?
One frequent concern among faculty is that explicitly teaching students to use GenAI might encourage more academic
dishonesty. However, our findings do not support this worry. If the intervention had spurred widespread “cheating”
behaviors, we would have expected significant increases in GenAI usage on graded work. Instead, not only did we fail
to observe any statistical rise in frequency of use for homework or projects, but the only notable (albeit small) change
was actually a slight shift from “moderate” or “very frequent” usage down to “on occasion.” In other words, rather than
pushing students toward reliance on AI for answers, the intervention appears to have encouraged a more restrained and
reflective approach, reinforcing that these tools should supplement rather than replace their own intellectual efforts.
4.5
Conclusions and Future Outlooks
Overall, our findings reinforce that a carefully designed GenAI intervention – here, the AI-Lab framework – can promote
more mindful and educationally beneficial usage of AI tools. Students became measurably more comfortable and open
to GenAI for conceptual and debugging help, and concerns about it boosting plagiarism were not represented in our
data. Instead, they typically used GenAI in moderation, adopting it as a support tool rather than a shortcut. While
usage frequency remained relatively stable, students gained deeper insights into GenAI’s limitations and capabilities,
suggesting that structured scaffolding successfully encouraged reflective practice. Looking ahead, future work should
explore how these effects evolve with larger and more diverse student populations, track and examine semester-to-
semester shifts as student perspectives and usage patterns continue to change, and investigate how different instructional
contexts or disciplines beyond computer science and engineering might be able to adapt and leverage the AI-Lab
approach.
