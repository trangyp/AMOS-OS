Abstract
In this study, MLP models with dynamic structure are applied to factor mod-
els for asset pricing tasks. Concretely, the MLP pyramid model structure was
employed on firm characteristic sorted portfolio factors for modelling the large
capital US stocks. It was further developed as a practicable factor investing
strategy based on the predictions. The main findings in this chapter were
evaluated from 2 angles: model performance and investing performance, which
were compared from the periods with and without COVID-19. The empirical
results indicated that with the restrictions of the data size, the MLP models
never perform ‘deeper, better’ anymore, while the proposed MLP models with
2 and 3 hidden layers have higher flexibility to model the factors in this case.
This study also verified the idea of previous works that MLP models for factor
investing have more meaning in the downside risk control than in pursuing the
absolute annual returns.

1 Introduction
As early as 1961, the capital asset pricing model (CAPM)[1] was developed as the first
factor model, which gave people an indication of how the underlying risks of the market

could affect the expected returns of assets.Treynor [1] employed a univariate linear
model on the market portfolio excess returns as a single observed factor to explain the
expected return of assets. This model explained the asset returns according to market
factors to some extent, but it led to the development of further research on how to
deal with the information that market factors cannot explain. Later, in 1978, Ross [2]
proposed the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT), which deployed the linear model on
multi-factors. The model frame therefore became:
Ri = αi + βi1F1 + βi2F2 + · · · + βikFk + ϵi
(1)
Where F1 to Fk are factors, βi1 to βik are coefficients which measure the sensitivity
of the factors. αi is the intercept of the linear model which can be interpreted as
the pricing error in the asset pricing modeling. The most well-known factor model
is the Fama-French 3-factor (FF3) model[3]. It proved that market factors together
with factors organized by the top-bottom difference between firms’ size and the top-
bottom difference of book-to-market ratio can provide a better interpretation of the
asset excess returns. The FF3 models triggered the enthusiasm for factor exploration
in later research. The factor exploration was separated into two groups: researchers
in academia were attempting to explore more factors that can give the asset excess
returns a better interpretation, which shows as lower or insignificant alpha, while the
practitioners were developing the factors for searching the extra gain beyond factors,
which shows as higher alpha. Due to the motivation from these two groups, the num-
ber of factors explored increased dramatically. Since then, four hundred more factors
have been explored for stock pricing or factor investing [4]. In this work, a ‘factor zoo’
was developed. However, a challenge was that the increasing complexity of factors
led to a ‘dimensionality disaster’ for the na¨ıve linear model. Although, in the early
stage, researchers noticed the non-linearity of the factor models, for instance, the
consumption CAPM model [5] and its advanced forms [6, 7] or ‘disaster as a factor’
[8], none of them attempt to solve the issue of dimensional reduction for factors.
Nonetheless, the development of machine learning (ML) algorithms in the past two
decades has sparked a revolution in the factor model for asset pricing, which has shed
light on the dimensional reduction task of factor models. Since 2017, ML algorithms
have gained significant interest from researchers in finance and economics due to
their fabulous predictability and flexibility. For example, Baek et al. [9], Chang et al.
[10], Sarmento and Horta [11] all utilized the ML technique for constructing financial
trading strategies. In the asset pricing domain,Gu et al. [12] tested the most classic
ML algorithms such as Elastic Net, random forest (RF), gradient-boosted regression
trees and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), benchmarked with OLS, principal compo-
nents regression (PCR), and partial least square (PLS) on asset pricing tasks of US
stocks. It is considered the seminal work of ML application for asset pricing (AP)
assignment, which made it the most cited paper in that domain. Their models are
also known as ‘GKX2020’ of later papers [13]. They found that the MLP model with
4 hidden layers with the fixed pyramid structure (32,16,8,4 neurons in each hidden
layer respectively) has the highest out-of-sample average fit (R2). The study also
managed the balance of computational costs of ML algorithms and the predictive fit
of the ML models, which gave a taste of ML application on asset pricing. It is a great

2 Related works
Before Treynor [1] created the CAPM model, people had fuzzy notions about how the
risks could affect the expected returns of assets. He proposed a single-factor linear
model for interpreting expected stock returns via a factor generated from the difference
between expected market return and the risk-free rate. Specifically, it can be written
as:
E(Ri) = Rf + βi (E(Rm) −Rf)
(2)
Where E(Ri) is the expected return of asset i, Rf is the risk-free rate, βi is the asset’s
beta, representing its sensitivity to the market, and E(Rm) is the expected return
of the market. Although it is the cornerstone of the asset pricing theory, people cast
doubts on the explainability of the market factor to the individual assets or portfolios.
Thus, Ross [2] reckoned asset returns were driven by the combined effect of factors,
hence proposed a generalized factor model’s frame:
Ri = αi + βi1F1 + βi2F2 + · · · + βikFk + ϵi
(3)
Where F1 to Fk are factors, βi1 to βik are coefficients which measure the sensitiv-
ity of the factors, also known as factor loadings. αi is the intercept of the linear
model, it can be interpreted as a pricing error, and the residual ϵi can present the
firm-specific risks. This theory suggests that if the pricing mismatches the factors’
offers, investors can take advantage of the arbitrage. It is known as Arbitrage Pricing
Theory (APT). The profit from the arbitrage is equal to αi which is that the returns
cannot be explained fully by the factors. The model frame released the single-factor
assumption from CAPM, hence, it increased the flexibility of the model. However,
with such a frame, scholars still struggle with questions such as what factors should
be present and how many factors should appear in a single frame. The most well-
known multi-factor model is the Fama-French 3-factor model (FF3) [3]. It deployed
the market factor together with size and value (book-to-market ratio) factors to
the APT frame, which improved the explanation of the sorted portfolios. The FF3
model led to an increased enthusiasm for research on factor exploration afterwards.
Researchers developed new factors and models based on the APT frame and had the
massive competition competed models on the their interpretability factors and their
combinations’ interpretability which normally presented as the higher coefficient of
determination and insignificant value of α. For example, Carhart [20] extended FF3
to 4 factors by adding the momentum factor, while Novy-Marx [21] proposed adding
a gross profit factor to the FF3 frame. Further development led to the Fama French
5 factor (FF5) model [22] which added profitability and investment factors to FF3.
Hou et al. [23] optimized the FF5’s investment factor by Tobin’s Q theory [24] which
4

utilized the ratio of the market value of the company and replaced the cost of capital
to measure the quality of investment. They offered great ideas on how to interpret
asset or portfolio returns. However, with continuously emerging new factors and
countless combinations brought by these factors, it remains a key research challenge
to assess the reliability of different factors. There are four hundred plus factors which
play a role in stock pricing [25]. Handpicking factors may cause the ‘omitted variable’
issue, which means important variables are neglected intentionally or unintentionally.
However, inserting all factors in the APT frame causes the ‘dimensionality disaster’
since it leads to the heavy multicollinearity issue of the linear APT frame, which
lowers the interpretability of the factors.
Therefore, a branch of the researchers devotes themselves to developing non-linear
model forms or adding constraints to the linear form to satisfy the increasing factor
size. Stock and Watson [26] introduced the use of principal components analysis
(PCA) to subtract factors from a large dimension of predictors. PCA projects the
predictors into the new coordinate system via a linear transformation and reor-
ganizes the predictors into components. It subtracts components which have the
highest explanation for the variance as the new factors for the APT frame. The
entire process is named principal components regression (PCR), and the pre-defined
parameter which indicates how many new factors should be taken is known as the
hyperparameter of PCR. PCA is the most widely applied technique for dimension
reduction tasks, but it has limitations as the linear assumption of predictors, new
factors generated based on a linear combination of the original predictors may not be
easy to interpret and require normalization. Later, Kelly et al. [27] utilized the incre-
mental PCA (IPCA) which upgraded from the PCA for predictor abstracting. IPCA
employed a dynamic process on PCA, which only analyses a small batch of the entire
dataset each time and updates the new factors gradually, ultimately approaching the
optimum factors for the full dataset. Although IPCA improved the quality of new
factors, the shortcomings of the PCA persist in IPCA.
Furthermore, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) known
as l1 penalty and ridge regression known as l2 penalty as two commonly applied con-
strains for linear models. They are employed for controlling the oversized predictors.
Kelly and Pruitt [28] proposed a new pricing method via market expectation which
relies on LASSO for noise reduction. Feng et al. [29] introduced the concept of ‘factor
zoo’ and an applied LASSO-based examination method. Chinco et al. [30] has also
verified LASSO’s ability to denoise datasets. They proved that LASSO regression can
significantly reduce the noise created by ineffective factors and improve model fitness
and robustness. Whereas the classic LASSO has limitations such as subtracting only
one factor from a group of highly correlated factors, its limitation is that it cannot
jointly measure multiple correlated or equally important factors. This leads to infor-
mation loss. Therefore, some papers turn to the constraint of classic ridge regression.
For example, Giglio and Xiu [31] applied ridge regression to the study of how investor
beliefs have an impact on portfolio decisions from a survey of wealthy retail investors.
It matched the survey data to investors’ portfolios, trading activities, and online
5


## Page 6


behaviors data. It proved that the l2 penalty can identify which factors should be
the prioritized factors, identifying potential multicollinearity and moderating model
overfitting in the dataset. Nonetheless, classic ridge regression has limitations such
as it is impossible to eliminate the ineffective factors since it never fails to shrink
the coefficients to zero, and it penalizes all correlated factors when it addresses the
multicollinearity, which makes it difficult to interpret the individual effect of factors
with high correlation. Moreover, for sparse factors, where only a few variables have
significant effects, ridge regression shows less priority than LASSO due to the effect of
non-zero coefficient shrinkage. Both classic LASSO and ridge regression face the same
limitations of being computationally expensive and time-consuming for searching
hyperparameters and are therefore not suitable for non-linear data and are sensitive
to noises. Yuan and Lin [32], Freyberger et al. [33], Bertelsen [34] later extended
LASSO to group LASSO, adaptive group LASSO and prior adaptive LASSO respec-
tively, for moderating the aforementioned issues of classic LASSO, but it still has not
been fundamentally resolved.
Fortunately, the development of machine learning (ML) has led to the progression
of the factor model’s frame exploration. The earliest neural network (NN) models
for financial application can be traced back to the 1990s, where Hutchinson et al.
[35] used a new non-parametric NN model for simulating Black-Scholes option price.
They proved that the model recovered the Black-Scholes formula from a two-year
training set of daily options prices, and it can be used on both out-of-sample price
and delta-hedge options, which gave hope to later research that NN models may con-
quer some limitations of traditional econometric methods. Later, Heaton et al. [36]
tested classic deep NN structures on financial time series prediction tasks, from MLP
to Autoencoder, and found deep neural network structures significantly improved
the performance of financial series prediction. Gu et al. [12] examined the classic
ML methods, including MLP, decision tree and random forest, on 30,000 US stocks
for 60 years. They found MLP with 3-4 hidden layers outperform all other models
by comparing the average out-of-sample (OOS) fitness indicator R2. Their models
in later works are also named ‘GKX2020’ Gu et al. [13]. Rresearch in this chapter
has developed from Gu et al. [13]’s MLP models. By following their footpath, Wang
[17] deployed their methods on the cryptocurrencies and found similar results. Giglio
and Xiu [31] solved the omitted variables issue of the asset pricing factor model by
proposing an innovative ML model for dimensional reduction tasks. More advanced,
Gu et al. [13] extended the idea from IPCA, which developed an autoencoder model
for the dimension reduction. Adopting an autoencoder structure in factor models can
be seen as a generalized PCA model which has the capability to relax the linearity
assumption between original factors. Avramov et al. [37] did comparative research
for previous ML on factor models, where they demonstrated ML methods can consis-
tently identify mispriced stocks and handle the anomalies better, albeit acknowledging
the black-box effect of ML models. Also, they indicated that the trading signals
generated by deep learning methods are more profitable, especially for long positions.
Moreover, the deep learning methods moderated downside risk significantly. Giglio
et al. [15] did a comprehensive literature review on ML-based asset pricing research.

They concluded that although ML assisted in improving the model performance,
pure ML techniques are far from solving the asset pricing assignment, and researchers
should consider exploring the economic and financial meanings behind models. They
also suggested that macroeconomic and microeconomic activities should be adopted.
Kelly and Xiu [38] contributed a more general literature review in ML for the finan-
cial application domain. They mentioned that ML for finance is already extended to
the speech and textual recognition fields, and it could be exciting for the attempt
at corporate finance research. More theoretically, Chen et al. [39] used Generative
Adversarial Networks (GAN) for searching the most appropriate function form for
the stochastic discount factor (SGD) of factor models. It is the seminal paper on the
application of advanced models such as GAN in the asset pricing domain which trig-
gered the initial interest in the GAN and its variation in factor models Wang and Guo
[40]. Eckerli and Osterrieder [41] overviewed the recent development of GAN-based
models in financial applications. Practically, Coqueret and Guida [19] discussed the
implementation of traditional ML models such as random forest, MLP, autoencoder
and reinforcement learning for factor investing trading strategy designing. It also
provided examples with R codes. Dixon et al. [42] well matched the two angles for
ML: theory and practice, providing the mechanism presentation and implementation
in general financial application. It introduced the classic ML models and how they
accommodate tasks such as decision-making and sequential learning.
3 Data Description
The responses, or labels in ML terminology, used in this research contain 420 large-
capital stock excess returns from January 1957 to December 2021. These stocks come
from the US NASDAQ and NYSE markets which are derived from CRSP of Wharton
Research Data Services (WRDS). These 420 stocks originally are filtered from the
top 15% of the largest market capital (MC) which covers 85% of total MC for the
two markets. They satisfy the conditions of having no missing data in the testing
period and less than 50% of values missing during the training period, so it can meet
the requirement of the ‘going concern’ [43] and data representability. As a benefit
of the assumption of ‘too-big-to-fail’ [44] and ‘going concern’ , this setting is more
realistic for practitioners since it protects the investors from systematic risks such as
companies who delisted unexpectedly due to insolvency (e.g. Lehman Brothers). The
overall coverage of market capital from these stocks is 21.38% in the US stock markets
of National Association of Securities Dealers Automated Quotations (NASDAQ) and
the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). The risk-free rate for constructing the excess
returns and evaluating the Sharp ratio (SR) and Sortino ratio (SO) are derived from
the Kenneth R. French Data Library.
The predictors, also known as features for the ML terminology, organized by 182
explored firm characteristic sorted portfolio factors from asset pricing literature
offered by Andrew Y.; Zimmermann [45] with at least 60% of available values in
the training period. The entire data set is split into training, validation and testing

4 Models
To begin this section, we will introduce the general additive prediction error model
commonly used for asset pricing assignment:
ri,t+1 = Et [ri,t+1] + εi,t+1
(4)
Where ri,t+1 is the ri,t+1 as asset excess returns at time t+1 with the assumption that
Et[ri,t+1] = 0 for t ∈[1, T −1],i ∈[1, N], N is the number of assets. Concerning the
work of GKX2020 [12], the conditional expectation of ri,t+1 can also be expressed as
a function of a vector fi,t which is generated from encompassed P features (factors).
fi,t can be directly equal to the factors if the number of factors is small and suited
for linear models, but if the number of factors is large, such as the ‘factor zoo’ issue
[4], the additional process as principal components analysis (PCA) is required for
shrinking the dimension of factors. This process is also known as ‘feature engineering
or abstracting’ in ML terminology. Equation (5) gives the general idea of a factor
model. The earliest literature on asset pricing normally uses the ordinary least squares
(OLS) linear form with less than 10 factors, but later, other function forms are applied
due to the increasing factor numbers. In this chapter, Equation (5) is implemented
with an MLP function form.
ri,t+1 = g (fi,t; θ) + εi,t+1
(5)
4.1 Multilayer Perceptron Algorithms (MLPs)

factors. In this chapter, we employ the MLP model with a pyramid structure as a
substitution for the traditional linear form of the factor model. The mechanism of
MLP will be presented with an example, while the application of the factor model for
asset pricing will be explained in line with the work of GKX2020.

4.4 Early Stopping and batch normalization
One parameter searching for all training data points in a batch is defined as one
epoch. Epoch is a one-time gradient descent process. Normally, when the epoch num-
ber increases, the MSE of training and validation will decrease. However, as epochs
are a hyperparameter of the MLP model, it is difficult to define before attempting
the model. Oversize epochs will cause the overfitting issue as well because the dif-
ference of MSE for both training and validation data increases after epochs reach a
critical minimum value. Early-stopping is a process for stopping the epochs when
14


## Page 15


the performance on the validation data cannot be improved further, then logging the
parameters for prediction.
Batch normalization is a vertical normalization, where each observation in the batch
removes the batch’s mean and is divided by its standard deviation; the training data
in one batch’s mean is zero, and the variance is equal to 1. Batch normalization can
accelerate and stabilize the training process, moderate the issue of gradient vanishing
and explosion [52], which is explained in Section 4.5, and reduce the sensitivity towards
the initial weights.
4.5 Gradient vanishing and explosion
Gradient vanishing usually happens during the backpropagation process. When the
hidden layer number of the MLP model increased to a certain level, the gradient was
extremely small or equal to zero. Subsequently, when it is passed back to the first
hidden layer, the gradient vanishes, causing the parameters to pause updating, leading
to poor performance of the model. This was especially prevalent when the activation
function was using Sigmoid or Tanh. Whereas ReLU is the activation function which
can prevent this issue since its derivative is most likely to be 1, batch normalization
is another approach that also helps with this. Opposite to the scenario of gradient
vanishing, if the initial learning rate is not properly set or an inappropriate depth to
the MLP structure is deployed, the gradient could be extremely large. In that sense,
if the learning rate causes excessively large parameter updating, the parameters do
not converge the loss function to the global minimum but diverge from the global
minimum. This phenomenon is known as gradient explosion, and the loss function
encountering gradient explosion tends to be infinite. Testing the initial learning rate
and batch normalization can be applied as adjustment methods for this issue.

6 Conclusion and Discussion
This chapter revisits and develops the GKX2020’s MLP models amid employing a
dynamic MLP structure introduced by Coqueret and Guida [19] and features as firm
characteristics sorted factors provided by Andrew Y.; Zimmermann [45], re-evaluating
the model and portfolio performance during the testing period of COVID-19, restrict-
ing the stocks with consideration of more realistic US stock markets conditions such
as high liquidity, ‘going concern’, variable borrowing rates and available borrowing
shares from the brokers. Similar to the findings in the original work of GKX2020,
with data length limitation, the MLP models have no ‘deeper, better’ performance.
It does not like the applications in computer vision, speech recognition and biological
engineering which contain sufficient data length, but GKX2020’s MLP models more
rely on the depth of the structure. The MLP models’ performance on our dataset
peaked at MLP with 2 hidden layers and faded after MLP with 3 hidden layers. As
discussed in GKX2020, data size is a great challenge in ML-based factor modelling.
However, the proposed MLP models have a better OOS fitness than GKX2020’s work.
In addition, the firm characteristic-sorted factors compared with firm characteristics
as observable factors are more accessible and stable for MLP model training in factor
modelling. The most powerful predictor in all proposed MLP models is size followed
by Earnings forecast revisions (REV6), Earnings announcement return (Announce-
mentReturn), and Cash flow to market (CF) on our data settings which matched the
findings from the original paper of GKX2020. Albeit, the long-only setting portfolio
has lower Sharpe ratios and Sortino ratios than the two-way portfolio, it is more
practicable for factor investors from the systematic risk management perspective.
Our best-performed models achieve a great balance on the investing profitability and
market fluctuation risks whether the COVID-19 period is applied.
36


## Page 37


Our work also verified the GKX2020’s notion that MLP models without signal filter-
ing mechanisms would be highly affected by the market timing noises and found that
MLP models which suffer from serious overfitting fail to capture the trading signals.
The investing performance can be improved by finding the solutions for these market
timing challenges such as adding a signal filtering mechanism. The MLP models
in our case have considerable meanings in moderating the downside risks with the
relatively highest gain, which verified the results from Avramov et al. [37], rather
than achieving the absolute annual returns when experiencing the steep increasing
trend which covers the drawdowns caused by events with medium-term effects. In
this sense, apart from the signal filtering, the stock selection has a high impact on
portfolio performance as well. All models examined have significant α to some extent
which indicates the profitability from factors.
Overall, MLP models bring promising performance on downside risk control and
relatively high annual return after transaction cost for factor investing in all scenar-
ios of our case, especially during the COVID-19 period. The design of ours highly
approached the real practicable conditions which makes it more applicable to the
market practitioners. It highlights the untility of new inspirations on neural network
models on the asset pricing assignment for both industrial applications and academic
research. Whereas, the existing challenges of limited data length, trading signal fil-
tering, model hyperparameters tuning and stock selection which highly influence the
model and portfolio performance. Topics such as how to moderate the restriction of
data length, improve the trading signal quality and tune the model hyperparameter
effectively are still worth further exploration.
