Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit remarkable capabilities across a spectrum of tasks
in financial services, including report generation, chatbots, sentiment analysis, regulatory
compliance, algorithmic trading enhancement, investment advisory, financial knowledge re-
trieval, and summarization. However, their intrinsic complexity and lack of transparency
pose significant challenges, especially in the highly regulated financial sector, where inter-
pretability, fairness, and accountability are critical.
As far as we are aware, this paper presents the first application in the finance do-
main of understanding and utilizing the inner workings of LLMs through mechanistic
interpretability, addressing the pressing need for transparency and control in AI systems.
Mechanistic interpretability is the most intuitive and transparent way to understand LLM
behavior by reverse-engineering their internal workings. By dissecting the activations and
circuits within these models, it provides insights into how specific features or components
influence predictions—making it possible not only to observe but also to modify model
behavior.
In this paper, we explore the theoretical aspects of mechanistic interpretability and demon-
strate its practical relevance through a range of financial use cases and experiments, includ-
ing applications in trading strategies, sentiment analysis, bias, and hallucination
detection.
While not yet widely adopted, mechanistic interpretability is expected to become increas-
ingly vital as adoption of LLMs increases. Advanced interpretability tools can ensure AI
systems remain ethical, transparent, and aligned with evolving financial regulations. In this
paper, we have put special emphasis on how these techniques can help unlock interpretabil-
ity requirements for regulatory and compliance purposes—addressing both current needs
and anticipating future expectations from financial regulators globally.

Hallucination, Artificial Intelligence, Sentiment Analysis, Fraud Detection, Bias Reduction,
Financial Applications, Model Validation, Regulatory Compliance

Introduction
As large language models (LLMs) and other AI systems become more sophisticated and deeply embedded
in financial decision-making, ensuring their transparency, reliability, and alignment with regulatory and
ethical standards is paramount. Financial institutions deploy AI models including LLMs across risk man-
agement, fraud detection, credit scoring, regulatory compliance, and algorithmic trading, often relying on
complex black-box models (Černevičien˙e & Kabašinskas, 2024). The lack of interpretability in these models
poses significant risks, including bias, hallucinations, non-compliant decisions, and opaque reasoning, which
can lead to regulatory penalties and financial losses (Guidotti et al., 2018). These concerns have driven
the increasing adoption of Explainable AI (XAI) methodologies, with a significant shift toward mechanis-
tic interpretability—a discipline that aims to reverse-engineer AI models and LLMs into understandable
components rather than relying on post hoc explanations (Räuker et al., 2023).

Historically, interpretability in finance relied on feature importance methods, Shapley values (SHAP), and
rule-based explainability frameworks (Linardatos et al., 2020). While these methods provided insights into
model behavior, they lacked the ability to unravel the underlying computations and internal representations
governing AI-driven financial decisions. Recent breakthroughs in mechanistic interpretability have enabled
researchers to dissect large AI systems, such as transformers and deep neural networks, at a granular level,
exposing how models understand several concepts (Olsson et al., 2024). Notably, OpenAI’s discovery of a
“sentiment neuron” within an unsupervised language model exemplifies how specific neurons can encapsulate
complex features like sentiment, highlighting the potential for mechanistic interpretability to reveal such
structures (Radford et al., 2017).
Mechanistic interpretability provides two primary approaches: observation and intervention. Observation
techniques, such as logit attribution and feature retrieval, enable researchers to identify which model com-
ponents contribute to a given prediction. Intervention techniques, such as activation patching and feature
steering via sparse autoencoders, allow targeted modifications to mitigate bias, reduce hallucinations, and
enhance regulatory compliance. Sparse autoencoders, in particular, play a transformative role by extracting
sparse feature representations, which not only enable the retrieval of relevant financial signals but also facil-
itate the suppression of unwanted or non-compliant outputs (Bereska & Gavves, 2024). A related approach
is dictionary learning, as discussed by Hinton et al. (2023), where models learn to represent data using a
set of basis functions or atoms, enabling more efficient representation and retrieval of financial signals. This
approach can be particularly useful in financial models that require interpretable feature extraction, as it
focuses on identifying distinct, monosemantic features that can explain complex predictions.
This shift toward mechanistic analysis is particularly critical for high-stakes financial applications. Tradi-
tional black-box approaches often require extensive prompt engineering or model fine-tuning to align AI
outputs with compliance standards and risk frameworks. Mechanistic interpretability, by contrast, enables
dynamic output adjustments while preserving transparency, significantly reducing the need for manual in-
tervention. We demonstrate the impact of these techniques across key financial applications.
This paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides a detailed background on mechanistic interpretabil-
ity, including foundational concepts and prior interpretability approaches in finance. Section 3 outlines
core interpretability techniques—logit attribution, activation patching, and sparse autoencoder-based tech-
niques - applying both observation and intervention methodologies. Section 4 introduces how mechanistic
interpretability concepts can be applied in finance, while Sections 5 and 6 demonstrate the results of
our research across real-world applications including credit risk analysis, bias detection, and hallucination
control.

Background
2.1
Interpretability In General
In order to promote the integration of AI into critical decision-making processes, interpretability and trans-
parency have emerged as key priorities. Explainable AI (XAI) aims to improve trust and accountability by
enabling users to understand, validate, and control AI models (Adadi & Berrada, 2018; Arrieta et al., 2020).
Early rule-based expert systems provided inherent interpretability, but the rise of deep learning (DL) models
introduced a trade-off between performance and explainability (Saeed & Omlin, 2023).
Black-box models, such as deep neural networks (DNNs) deliver state-of-the-art accuracy but lack trans-
parency, raising concerns in high-risk applications like finance and healthcare (Linardatos et al., 2020).
In contrast, white-box models, including linear models, decision trees, and rule-based algorithms, provide
structured and interpretable outcomes but may lack predictive power and fail to capture intricate feature
dependencies.
XAI techniques are categorized by their scope (local vs. global), timing (ante-hoc vs. post-hoc), and de-
pendency (model-specific vs. model-agnostic). Ante-hoc methods integrate interpretability during training,
while post-hoc approaches explain pre-trained models through techniques like SHAP (Shapley Additive Ex-
planations), LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations), and attention visualization (Saranya

& Subhashini, 2023). Model-agnostic methods generalize across architectures, whereas model-specific tech-
niques leverage internal mechanisms to enhance transparency (Zolanvari et al., 2021).
Regulatory frameworks such as the European Union’s AI Act emphasize the need for fairness, transparency,
and accountability in AI-driven decision-making (European Commission, 2020).
However, achieving ex-
plainability remains a challenge, as increasing model complexity often leads to reduced interpretability.
Researchers continue to explore ways to balance explainability, performance, and computational efficiency,
ensuring AI remains both powerful and accountable (Frasca et al., 2024).
2.2
Interpretability Categories
We encounter a spectrum of interpretability paradigms for decoding AI systems’ decision-making, ranging
from external black-box techniques to internal analysis as shown in Figure 1. We contrast these paradigms
with mechanistic interpretability, highlighting its distinct causal bottom-up perspective within the broader
interpretability landscape.
Feature Attribution Based Interpretability
Feature attribution methods evaluate the significance of each input feature in influencing a model’s predic-
tions, helping understand decision boundaries and model behavior. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP)
(Lundberg & Lee, 2017) allocate feature contributions using cooperative game theory, while Local Inter-
pretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) (Ribeiro et al., 2016) approximate complex models with
locally interpretable linear models. Gradient-based techniques such as Integrated Gradients, DeepLIFT, and
GradCAM (Sundararajan et al., 2017) quantify how changes in input affect outputs. These methods enhance
interpretability by identifying key decision-making factors, though they often fail to capture deeper causal
structures within neural networks.
Model Simplification-Based Interpretability
Model simplification techniques aim to retain predictive power while improving transparency by approximat-
ing black-box models with interpretable alternatives. Decision trees and rule-based models (Hayashi et al.,
2016) generate structured, human-readable rules for applications such as credit risk assessment. Knowledge
distillation (Zhang et al., 2020) compresses knowledge from complex models into simpler ones, preserving
accuracy while improving interpretability. Although effective, these methods often fail to capture intricate
feature dependencies present in high-dimensional financial data.
Visual Explanations-Based Interpretability
Visual Explanations aim to create visual representations that simplify understanding how a model works,
even when dealing with complex dimensions.
These methods highlight the interaction among features,
identify the regions of an input that influence model outputs and use activation maps (Selvaraju et al., 2016)
to aid transparency.
Behavioral Interpretability
Behavioral interpretability treats AI models as black boxes, analyzing how outputs change in response to
different inputs. Techniques such as minimal pair analysis (Warstadt et al., 2020) compare model responses
to slight input modifications, while perturbation and sensitivity testing (Casalicchio et al., 2018) assess model
robustness to distributional shifts. Although useful for identifying biases and performance vulnerabilities,
these methods provide limited insights into internal model structures.
Mechanistic Interpretability
Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer AI models by analyzing circuits, neurons, and compu-
tational pathways, making it distinct from post-hoc explanations. Circuit-level analysis (Elhage et al., 2021;
Nanda et al., 2023) identifies how specific patterns of attention heads and MLP neurons implement distinct

subfunctions of a model, such as induction heads or induction circuits. Sparse autoencoders (Bereska &
Gavves, 2024) extract interpretable and disentangled features from model activations, enabling applications
such as bias correction, steering, and concept-based debugging. Activation patching (Elhage et al., 2021)

2.3
Interpretability In Finance
Financial AI models are integral to modern decision-making, driving risk management, fraud detection,
credit assessments, and algorithmic trading and many other financial applications. However, their complex-
ity raises concerns about transparency, compliance, and accountability. This section explores how inter-
pretability techniques are applied in finance, aligning them with the key categories of explainability: feature
attribution, model simplification, visual and concept-based methods, behavioral analysis, and mechanistic
interpretability. By mapping explainability techniques to financial tasks, we illustrate how interpretability
ensures regulatory compliance, enhances transparency, and mitigates financial risks.
Feature Attribution
Feature attribution techniques assess the influence of individual financial attributes on model predictions,
ensuring fairness and transparency. For example, models like SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and LIME
(Ribeiro et al., 2016) quantify the impact of financial variables such as income, debt levels, and credit history
in determining loan approvals. Additionally, SHAP values are used in fraud detection models (Zhang et al.,
2020) to analyze the impact of transaction patterns on fraud risk. Gradient-based attribution techniques like
Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) highlight critical risk factors affecting financial forecasts,
though they do not explain deeper causal relationships.
Model Simplification
Model simplification methods approximate complex AI models with interpretable surrogates, balancing accu-
racy and transparency. In regulatory compliance, decision trees and rule-based models (Hayashi et al., 2016)
offer structured rules for credit risk assessment, bankruptcy prediction, and fraud detection. Additionally,
techniques like distillation (Zhang et al., 2020) compress deep learning models into interpretable structures,
making AI-driven financial systems more transparent while retaining predictive accuracy.

Visual interpretability helps investors and analysts understand AI-driven market predictions. For example,
attention mechanisms (Selvaraju et al., 2016) highlight key market indicators affecting stock movements.

Behavioral interpretability treats AI models as black boxes, examining how they react to input changes.

Mechanistic Interpretability - Concepts And Tools
Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer neural networks by identifying the precise computa-
tional pathways contributing to decision-making. Unlike other explainability methods, which analyze models
after predictions are made, mechanistic interpretability aims to understand the internal mechanisms at a
fine-grained level. This approach is crucial for ensuring AI safety, reliability, and transparency, particularly
in high-stakes applications such as finance and healthcare.
A fundamental challenge in mechanistic interpretability is that modern AI models, particularly transformers,
store far more information than they have neurons or activations available. This compression of knowledge
leads to a phenomenon called superposition, where multiple independent concepts are stored within the
same activation space. As a result, individual neurons often become polysemantic, meaning they activate
for multiple, seemingly unrelated features, making direct interpretation difficult.1
These concepts were
formalized in the influential research by Anthropic’s Transformer Circuits team, particularly in their article
on Superposition (Elhage et al., 2022a).
Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are among the most promising tools for representing disentangled and inter-
pretable concepts. Given their importance and the focus of our experiments, we begin this section with an
overview of SAEs, followed by several observation and intervention-based techniques in mechanistic inter-
pretability, including methods such as Logit Lens and Attribution Patching.
We will explore these tools and concepts through practical experiments described in Section 5 and Section 6.
These include the use of Logit Lens and Attribution Patching on GPT-2 models, and sparse autoencoders
on Gemma to reveal and manipulate interpretable features relevant to financial tasks.
3.1
Sparse Autoencoders
Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained to maximize their efficiency, so they compress information into
the fewest dimensions possible, allowing a limited number of neurons to encode a massive number of features.
Instead of identifying a one-to-one mapping between neurons and concepts, researchers must use alternative
techniques to uncover hidden structures within activations.
Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are utilized to mitigate the issue of polysemantic neurons that activate for
multiple, unrelated features. An SAE is a type of autoencoder that applies sparsity-promoting regularization

wine etc. activate, reflecting high-level conceptual associations. Sparse autoencoders learn to isolate such
interpretable concepts by compressing activations into a small number of specialized latent features.

Mechanistic Interpretability Methods: Observation vs. Intervention
Mechanistic interpretability research is divided into two major categories as shown in Figure 3:
• Observation-Based Methods – Analyze model activations without modifying the model.
• Intervention-Based Methods – Actively modify neural computations to establish causal rela-
tionships.
Figure 3: Interpretability Methods
3.3
Observation-based Methods
Observation-based methods in mechanistic interpretability analyze a large language model’s (LLM) internal
activations without modifying their parameters. Sparse autoencoder-based feature detection helps reveal
hidden patterns, showing how models organize and process information across layers.
These techniques
identify emerging features and the mechanisms driving predictions, enhancing interpretability.
They can be broadly categorized into different frameworks: Logit Lens, Sparse Autoencoders, and
Other Methods such as structured probing and feature visualization.
Given that Sparse Autoencoders
were introduced in Section 3.1, this section covers Logit Lens and other observation-based techniques in
mechanistic interpretability.
3.3.1
Logit Lens
One of the fundamental approaches to observing neural activations is the Logit Lens which applies the next-
word prediction at every layer. This enables researchers to analyze how confident a model is at predicting
outputs at different layers, offering a glimpse into the step-by-step evolution of predictions. Mathematically,
for an intermediate activation hℓat layer ℓ, the Logit Lens maps it to vocabulary space using the final model
output projection matrix Wout and bias bout from the pre-trained model:
ˆyℓ= Wouthℓ+ bout
where ˆyℓrepresents the logits at layer ℓ. Here, Wout and bout are directly inherited from the model’s original
language modeling head, trained to predict the next token at the final layer. For example, in GPT models,
these parameters are optimized to map the last hidden state to vocabulary logits during training. If earlier
layer logits closely resemble final outputs, it suggests that the model forms predictions incrementally rather
than exclusively at the last layer.

sentence prompts.

3.3.2
Other Observational Techniques
Probing for Features: Feature probing involves training external classifiers on intermediate activations
to test whether a model encodes specific properties. Simple linear probes (Alain & Bengio, 2016) examine
linear separability, while structured probes attempt to identify high-level concepts.
Feature Visualization: Feature visualization techniques generate synthetic inputs that maximally activate
neurons, revealing their sensitivity to different patterns. Activation Maximization (Zeiler & Fergus, 2014)
iteratively adjusts inputs to highlight neuron-preferred features.
Attention Analysis: Observing attention distributions can offer interpretability insights in transformer
models. Attention Lens (Sakarvadia et al., 2023) maps attention head activations to vocabulary tokens to
uncover hidden feature relationships.
3.4
Intervention-Based Methods
Interventional techniques provide a causal framework for understanding and modifying model behavior by
perturbing activations or weights and measuring the resulting effects.
Intervention techniques in mechanistic interpretability include methods such as activation patching, steering
sparse autoencoders, and causal abstraction. Given that steering sparse autoencoders was already covered
in Section 3.1.3, we now turn to the remaining intervention techniques in the following section.
3.4.1
Attribution Patching
One of the most widely used interventional methods is attribution patching, which involves modifying input
data to determine how specific features contribute to the model’s predictions.
This technique helps to
attribute the model’s decisions to particular components of the input, providing insights into which aspects
of the data are most responsible for the model’s outputs. Attribution patching can be linked to techniques
such as feature attribution (Sundararajan et al., 2017), and Shapley value-based analysis (Lundberg & Lee,
2017).
Attribution patching involves the following key steps:
1. Running the model with a clean input to obtain the baseline predictions.
2. Modifying or corrupting certain parts of the input, effectively "patching" or removing specific
features.
3. Running the model again on the modified input and observing the changes in the output.
4. The attribution of importance is determined by measuring how much the modified input alters
the predictions, thereby indicating the significance of each part of the input.
As illustrated in Figure 4, this process compares an original model (left) correctly outputting buy, when
evaluating Tesla stock data against a modified version (right) with patched activations, producing sell with
the central intervention point represented by the Patch node that modifies specific neural components. The
methodology follows a structured four-step attribution patching approach: identifying relevant components
(price/volume features, technical indicators), implementing targeted activation patches, measuring resulting
signal differences, and establishing causal relationships between internal mechanisms and trading decisions.
This rigorous approach extends beyond correlational analysis to determine which components are causally
necessary for specific computational pathways, enabling researchers to trace information flow through trading
algorithms and precisely identify the mechanisms responsible for particular buy or sell recommendations.
Section 5.2 demonstrates our experiment on how attribution patching reveals influential heads and layers in
financial sentence predictions using clean and corrupted prompts.

3.4.2
Other Intervention Techniques
In addition to attribution patching and SAE steering, several alternative intervention techniques exist:
• Causal Abstraction: Maps neural computations onto human-understandable structures, making
high-level AI reasoning transparent (Xia & Bareinboim, 2024).
• Resample Ablation: Replaces activations with randomized values to test the stability of circuits
in transformers (Zhang & Smith, 2023).
• Feature Injection: Introduces synthetic activations to study how specific concepts influence out-
puts (Lin et al., 2024).

Applications in Financial Services
As mentioned in Section 3.2, Mechanistic Interpretability techniques can be broadly classified into
observation-based methods and intervention-based methods. Observation techniques passively ana-
lyze how a model processes information, whereas intervention techniques actively modify its activations to
test causal effects.
Although these methods, such as sparse autoencoder-based feature analysis, activation steering, and attri-
bution techniques, show promise in enhancing transparency, bias mitigation, and compliance, they are not
yet widely known or implemented in real-world financial systems. They represent potential applications
that could improve AI-driven decision-making in areas like credit scoring, fraud detection, and algorithmic
trading.
Some potential use cases for applying mechanistic interpretability techniques in finance are mentioned below
and their detailed applications are illustrated in Table 2.

Observation-Based

Feature Discovery And Identification
Observation-based techniques like Logit Lens mentioned in Section 3.3.1 and sparse autoencoders men-

The Logit Lens applies the
next-word prediction every layer, showing how the model’s guesses evolve and become more refined across
layers. This helps understand model decision-making in areas such as credit scoring and fraud detection.
Sparse autoencoders, on the other hand could recover monosemantic features from a small one-layer trans-
former. Together, these methods increase model transparency and risk mitigation.

4.1.2
Feature Activation-Based
The magnitude of feature activation of SAE essentially provides a quantifiable measure of the model’s
focus on specific aspects of the input or output, enabling a deeper understanding of its decision-making
process and facilitating diverse applications across various domains. By analyzing activation patterns, models
can identify inconsistencies in generated content, detect biases in decision-making, and extract insights for
trading strategies. In hallucination detection, feature activation helps flag potentially incorrect outputs.
For bias detection, it highlights sensitive attributes that may influence model decisions, ensuring fairer
outcomes.
In trading strategies, feature activation aids in mapping financial news sentiment to market
movements, enabling more informed predictions. This approach enhances transparency and reliability in
AI-driven financial analysis.
4.2
Intervention-Based
4.2.1
Attribution Patching
As mentioned in Section 3.4.1, attribution patching tests the impact of neuron activations by modifying
specific layers in a model. A typical example is using corrupted and clean financial reports—where a model
predicts bankruptcy for one company based on a report, and then a corrupted version of the same report
is used to analyze which activations determine the final prediction. This method helps in identifying which
components of a model contribute most significantly to financial distress predictions and fraud detection.
4.2.2
Steering With SAEs
Feature steering as described in Section 3.1.3 modifies model behavior internally, reducing reliance on prompt
engineering and fine-tuning. In sentiment analysis, for example, it can direct models toward specific risk
assessments or investment strategies. This enables precise AI-driven financial decisions without costly re-
training.
However, fine-tuning is necessary when introducing new knowledge since feature steering only
adjusts existing activations rather than incorporating external information.

In this experiment, we aimed to enhance the reliability of the language model’s output by mitigating potential
hallucinations, specifically in the context of financial information. We utilized a feature selection method
based on the activation magnitudes of various SAE features within the Google Gemma-2B model.
By
analyzing the features’ activation levels, we selected those most relevant to the financial domain to help
guide the hallucination reduction process. In addition to reviewing activation magnitudes, we also reviewed
the labels assigned to the features to ensure that the selected features were meaningfully connected to
financial concepts and terminology.
Table 6 shows the top features, including their mean activations. Features like 15857 (financial terms and
information) and 15354 (financial news related to the stock market) were selected for their strong connection
to the financial domain. These features are useful in distinguishing between accurate financial insights and
potential hallucinations.
The methodology involves calculating the percentage of activation for selected
features relative to the total activation across all features for the given output. If the computed percentage
falls below a predefined threshold, an additional retrieval step is triggered.
Specifically, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is used, a method that retrieves semantically relevant
information from external sources to supplement the model’s internal knowledge and improve factual ground-
ing. For this experiment, a vector database containing processed SEC filings and financial documents was
prepared; embeddings of document chunks were stored to allow efficient semantic retrieval when needed.

Challenges
Mechanistic interpretability in finance faces key challenges related to scalability, generalization, and feature-
level transparency. While methods such as logit attribution, activation patching, and sparse autoencoder-
based steering have improved AI explainability, scalability remains a major concern, especially as financial
models grow to GPT-4 scale and beyond. Manual circuit analysis is infeasible for large models, and automated
interpretability tools are still in early development. Furthermore, many existing techniques fail to generalize
effectively to real-world financial applications, as models often rely on abstract representations that do
not directly map to human-understandable financial concepts. The lack of ground-truth benchmarks for
financial interpretability further complicates evaluating and validating AI-driven decision-making (Elhage
et al., 2022b).
Another critical challenge is the evolving nature of financial markets and regulations. AI models trained
on historical data may struggle to adapt to changing risk environments, new financial instruments, and
updated compliance laws. Dynamic interpretability pipelines that allow for real-time steering and adaptive
model explanations are necessary to ensure AI models remain aligned with financial industry standards.
The reliance on human evaluations in interpretability research also presents risks, as subjective analyses can
introduce inconsistencies when explaining financial model decisions (Räuker et al., 2023).
To address these challenges, fully integrated interpretability tooling must combine techniques from various
research efforts, such as Distill-style visualizations, Redwood’s tools, and Nanda’s TransformerLens (Nanda,
2022). A unified framework could streamline analysis workflows, making it easier for financial professionals to
debug models, trace decisions, and refine AI-driven predictions. Mechanistic interpretability is particularly
valuable for financial institutions looking to improve credit risk modeling, algorithmic trading transparency,
fraud detection, and regulatory compliance. By enhancing AI explainability in finance, institutions can build
trust, improve governance, and ensure models remain robust in dynamic economic conditions (Hendrycks,
2023).

Conclusion
Mechanistic interpretability offers a promising route to dissect and guide AI-driven financial decisions with
greater transparency and control. By combining observation-based methods (like logit attribution and sparse
autoencoders) with intervention-based techniques (such as activation patching and feature steering), financial
practitioners can pinpoint the neurons and attention heads most influential in tasks such as fraud detection,
credit scoring, and compliance.
Our experiments showed that these interpretability tools not only help
isolate crucial risk or sentiment indicators but also allow dynamic model adjustments—cutting down on
costly fine-tuning and enabling more precise responses in high-stakes scenarios.
In practice, mechanistic interpretability enhances AI reliability in finance. By replacing extensive prompt
engineering and reducing reliance on fine-tuning, steering provides a cost-effective alternative for refining
financial AI applications dynamically. These insights reinforce the need for continued research in the field of
financial AI safety, particularly in the alignment of LLMs with regulatory standards and risk governance
frameworks.
Despite its benefits, this approach faces notable challenges. Large-scale financial models can exceed current
methods’ capacity to manually analyze circuits, and automated interpretability frameworks remain a work
in progress. Financial features often blend abstract economic factors that are difficult to map to human-
understandable concepts, and evolving markets or regulatory changes can outpace models’ training data.
Additionally, identifying a particular neuron or attention head does not always clarify the exact influence
of features such as credit score or volatility on the final decision—requiring integration with traditional
model-agnostic methods for a truly comprehensive explanation.
8.1
Future Work
Looking ahead, the adoption of integrated interpretability pipelines will be crucial for ensuring AI-driven
finance remains both transparent and adaptive. Combining mechanistic interpretability with feature-level
attributions and real-time intervention tools can mitigate risks stemming from bias or market shifts. Future
work can focus on integrating mechanistic interpretability with feature-level attributions, ensuring that
AI decisions remain explainable not just at the neuron level but also in terms of real-world financial indicators.
One promising direction is to integrate traditional statistical modeling techniques that utilize financial time
series data with unstructured data processed by LLMs. This hybrid approach offers an explainable framework
for combining structured and unstructured data, unlocking access to the vast potential of unstructured
information.
As these methods mature, they will pave the way for robust, interpretable financial AI applications that can
be safely aligned with regulatory standards and responsive to the dynamic demands of modern finance.
