Abstract 
Using real-world food price and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions data for locally available food 
items in 171 countries, we measure how healthy diets could be obtained with the lowest possible 
emissions, compared to costs and emissions of the least expensive options and foods most 
commonly consumed. We find that foods with the lowest GHG emissions for a healthy diet 
would emit 0.67 kg CO₂e. A healthy diet using the least expensive items in each country would 
emit 1.65 kg CO₂e and cost $3.68 in 2021, while using foods most commonly consumed would 
emit 2.44 kg CO₂e and cost $9.96. Ninety-one percent of the difference in emissions between the 
lowest-cost and lowest-emissions diets is driven by animal-source foods and starchy staples. 
Other food groups, especially fruits and vegetables, vary widely in cost but not in emissions. 
Results show how changes in food policy and choice can most cost-effectively support healthier 
and more sustainable diets worldwide.

Environmental impacts and monetary costs of healthy diets worldwide 
 
Since 2020, the Cost and Affordability of a Healthy Diet (CoAHD) has been adopted by FAO1 
and the World Bank2 for global food security monitoring25, and used within countries to track 
subnational variation over time and space3. This work reveals that about 2.6 billion people 
worldwide cannot afford the least expensive locally available items in sufficient quantities from 
each food group required for health4.  CoAHD is a diagnostic indicator, revealing the degree to 
which poor diet quality might be caused by limited supply of low-cost options in each food 
group, insufficient income available for food, or displacement of healthy diets by other foods5. 
All three factors influence malnutrition to varying degrees6, and the usefulness of CoAHD has 
led to its adoption by a variety of national government agencies, international organizations and 
research institutions to guide food policies7 and nutrition interventions8 towards availability, 
access, and consumption of healthy diets worldwide9. 
 
Identifying low-cost options for a healthy diet is an essential step towards allowing all people to 
meet their nutritional needs. This study extends prior work on foods with the lowest monetary 
cost to consider foods with the lowest greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, which is increasingly 
important10,11,12. Prior work has shown that within nutritionally similar food groups, lower-cost 
items generally have lower GHG emissions13, so healthy diets that follow dietary guidelines and 
are composed of lower-cost options tend to be more environmentally sustainable than the higher-
cost diets commonly consumed in higher-income settings. Prior work has also shown that the 
least-cost diets identified to compute the CoAHD have comparable GHG emissions and water 
use to the EAT-Lancet diet, a diet pattern developed with sustainability targets14,18. However, 
prior studies have not yet quantified the lowest possible emissions following the CoAHD 
framework, and what type of substitutions would have the greatest reductions in GHG per unit of 
cost.  
 
This study measures the frontier of the lowest possible GHG emissions for a healthy diet, defined 
as the minimum CO2-equivalent emissions per day required to meet global Healthy Diet Basket 
(HDB) targets14. We compare this benchmark to the emissions and cost of healthy diets using the 
least expensive and then the most commonly consumed items in each country12,13. The HDB is 
an international dietary standard introduced in 2022 to define the types and quantities of food 
groups needed for an active and healthy life, based on commonalities among national dietary 
guidelines15. HDB targets serve as the foundation of measuring the Cost of a Healthy Diet 
(CoHD), which tracks economic access to sufficiently nutritious food using retail price data. In 
this study we quantify the emissions and cost of different ways to meet HDB targets using foods 
commonly sold around the world, revealing the range of possibilities through which the existing 
global food system can meet human nutritional requirements.

Our analysis uses the 2021 cycle of International Comparison Program (ICP) data, providing the 
most recent global coverage of item availability and price in almost every country of the world16.   
To quantify the role of food choice in diet cost and emissions, we compare five benchmark item 
selection rules, choosing foods with each country’s (1) lowest monetary cost, (2) lowest GHG 
emissions, (3) most commonly consumed items, (4) all commonly consumed items weighted by 
that product’s share of consumption, and (5) all commonly consumed items equally weighted.  
 
The five diets we construct for this study all adhere to the same nutritional standard set by the 
global reference HDB, thereby revealing trade-offs between affordability, sustainability and 
other criteria when meeting human health needs as specified by UN agencies17 in ways that can 
be compared to national dietary guidelines and private initiatives such as the EAT-Lancet 
Commission targets18,19. Diets 1 and 2 are benchmarks showing the frontier of what is possible: 
diet 1 uses the least expensive locally available items in each food group, while diet 2 uses the 
items with lowest GHG emissions. Diets 3 and 4 are based on actual consumption patterns within 
food groups, showing the most realistic way to meet Healthy Diet Basket targets under current 
conditions in each country: diet 3 uses the products that are most consumed in each country, 
while diet 4 uses all products in proportion to actual consumption as identified by food balance 
sheets. Diet 5 uses those same products in equal proportions to show the full range of 
possibilities. Both diets 4 and 5 include all retail items reported to be available for each Healthy 
Diet Basket food group, using Monte Carlo sampling to capture the full distribution of costs and 
GHG emissions of locally available foods in each country.

Focusing on emissions shown in Panel B, animal-source foods have both the highest levels and the 
greatest variation in GHG emissions per day. When choosing the least expensive items in each 
country (diet 1), mean daily GHG emissions for animal-source foods were 0.97 kg CO₂e (SD = 
0.58, IQR: 0.59-1.43) in 2021. Selecting low-GHG food items reduces emissions in most food 
groups but by far the largest reductions in emissions are from animal-source foods and starchy 
staples, which account for 91% of the reduction in emissions between the lowest-cost (diet 1) and 
lowest emissions (diet 2) scenarios. The relatively high contribution from starchy staples compared 
to other food groups reflects the large daily recommended intake of starchy staples required to 
meet national dietary guidelines and the global HDB targets (Suppl. Table 6), rather than high 
emissions per kilogram. In contrast, the total emissions and variation in emissions from fruits, 
vegetables, legumes, and oils were minimal (Figure 2B). Shifting to lower-emission items within 
these food groups would have a limited effect on total diet-related emissions. However, items in 
these food groups vary widely in price, so shifting to lower emissions items could increase diet 
cost significantly. For example, fruits cost $0.77 (SD = 0.29, IQR: 0.60-0.88) for diet 1 and $1.57 
(SD = 0.79, IQR: 1.03-1.87) for diet 2, accounting for 24% of the difference in cost between these 
two diet scenarios. However, fruits accounted for only 5% of the reduction in emissions between 
the lowest cost (diet 1) and lowest emissions (diet 2) scenarios. A diet composed of the lowest-
emissions starchy staples and animal-source foods and lowest-cost items in other food groups 
would cost $5.77 on average and have just 0.1 kg CO₂e higher emissions than the lowest-emissions 
diet.

Discussion 
 
We estimate the greenhouse gas emissions and monetary cost of foods needed to meet a global 
standard for healthy diets, using locally available items in 171 countries around the world to 
compare five criteria for item selection in each country. Our first benchmark uses only the least 
expensive locally available items, which is the criterion used internationally by UN agencies, the 
World Bank, and country governments for global monitoring of food access based on the 
monetary cost and affordability of healthy diets. We then compare the cost and GHG emissions 
of lowest-cost diets to four other options: (a) the lowest GHG emissions items available in each 
country, (b) the most commonly consumed items in each country, (c) all items selected in 
proportion to use, and (d) all items selected randomly.  
Our principal finding is that differences in the cost and GHG emissions between these four diet 
scenarios are due primarily to choices within just two food groups: animal source foods and 
starchy staples. Within these two food groups, higher cost items usually but do not always have 
higher emissions. For example, among animal source foods, milk and poultry meat are often 
among the least costly items for a healthy diet, while the least emitting items often include small 
fish. Other items have higher cost and higher emissions, including commonly consumed foods 
such as beef. Selection among animal-source foods is important due to their wide range and high 
levels of both costs and emissions, while selection among starchy staples is important due to the 
much larger quantity needed for a healthy diet. In contrast, fruits, vegetables, oils and fats, or 
legumes, nuts and seeds vary more widely in terms of monetary cost than in terms of emissions. 
These findings extend prior work that focuses on the monetary cost and affordability of healthy 
diets3,4, and complement prior work that focuses on shifts between food groups18,19,20.  
The alternatives compared in this study reveal how the foods needed for health differ in terms of 
both environmental impacts and monetary cost. These results underscore the importance of 
reducing emissions from the animal source foods and starchy staples needed in a healthy diet, in 
addition to reducing the monetary cost of the most affordable options for improved access to all 
required food groups, including vegetables and fruits that are relatively expensive. Actual 
choices depend not only on price and income but also other factors such as taste and preferences, 
time use and cooking costs, or habits and aspirations. In each country, demand, local production, 
trade patterns, and government policies and programs all influence which foods are consumed 
most frequently. Our comparisons between the least emitting and least cost options to commonly 
consumed diets reveal how locally available foods in each country could support shifts toward 
healthier and more sustainable diets that are also more affordable.

The role of food choice is particularly important for cost and sustainability when selecting 
among animal-source foods, and to a lesser extent starchy staples. Other plant-source foods are 
already low in emissions but vary widely in cost. In response to climate change, many 
government agencies, international organizations, and civil society groups have sought to 
incorporate emission reductions into decision making. Our findings confirm that in most cases, 
choosing lower cost items within the animal source food group also leads to lower emissions, 
although the absolute least cost items may not have the absolutely lowest GHG emissions. This 
study shows how there are expensive, high-emissions food items in all countries that are 
substitutable, revealing opportunities to meet health needs in more sustainable and affordable 
ways. The methods shown here can be used to guide policies and investments towards more 
sustainable as well as healthier diets at each location around the world.  
Limitations of the study include the data on availability and price of commonly used foods in 
each country. At any market location there are often thousands of distinct items for sale, each 
from a variety of origins being sold at different prices over time. For this study we use 
availability and annual average prices for the most commonly sold items in up to 173 countries, 
accounting for over 97% of the global population (Suppl. Table 8). These ICP data are the only 
source of retail food prices and availability with worldwide coverage, but by design the data are 
limited to standardized items sold in multiple countries. This includes foods sold in just a few 
countries such as amaranth leaves, but omits items that are widely consumed in only one country 
such as teff in Ethiopia. Future work looking within countries could use the even more detailed 
market data, capturing seasonal and spatial variation in emissions as previously done only for 
diet costs. For this baseline study, observed national average prices in 2021 are static and do not 
respond to dietary shifts. If widespread dietary shifts were to occur, for example due to a change 
in government policies, prices would adjust, potentially changing which products are the least 
expensive options in each food group. Future work could model specific scenarios using partial 
or general equilibrium frameworks that track how consumers and producers might adjust to each 
change. 
 
A second limitation of the study concerns the data on GHG emissions, which could vary 
depending on how each food is produced and distributed to consumers at each place and time. For 
this study we use the most recent and complete peer-reviewed compilation global average 
estimates of GHG emissions, based on studies from 78 countries on 6 continents, all harmonized 
to represent emissions from the farm to point of retail sale. Country-level estimates of the GHG 
emissions of many of the food items in our analysis are not currently available. Future work could 
incorporate more granular estimates of the climate footprints that may be produced, transported, 
processed and stored in different ways prior to consumption, potentially extended to other 
environmental or social impacts as in other work on sustainable diets.  
 
A third limitation is methodological, regarding item selection criteria other than the least 
expensive or least emitting food choices. Observed diets depend on a wide range of factors

Page 15 of 40 
 
driving the consumption of each type of food, leading to different dietary patterns for each 
person over time. In this study, we use national average food supplies as measured by food 
balance sheets, which infer each country’s total consumption per person of each product category 
from a country’s total production plus imports minus exports, nonfood uses, and losses to the 
point of retail sale. The consumption patterns revealed by these data are a result of the interaction 
of demand and supply with trade opportunities and government policies. Foods actually 
consumed are not necessarily those most preferred by taste or aspiration, as choices from the 
available options are constrained by available income and prices as well as convenience and time 
constraints, cooking methods and other factors. Future work could address changes in 
availability and price as well as food choice using individual dietary intake or household 
consumption surveys, including differences in kitchen and plate waste, to understand variation in 
what and how food is consumed.  
 
Methods 
Data 
This study combines three kinds of data about each food item: (1) availability and price in each 
country; (2) share of that product in the country’s food supply; and (3) global average GHG 
emissions associated with that product. Sources for each are described below. 
1. Food item availability and price  
We use retail food prices and availability from the International Comparison Program (ICP)14. 
Our analysis of ICP data excludes discretionary items not needed in healthy diets as defined by 
national dietary guidelines, such as salty snacks, cured meats, and sugar-sweetened products. We 
also exclude infant foods, beverages, herbs and spices, condiments, culinary ingredients, mixed 
dishes, and items lacking food composition data. The resulting database provides national 
average prices for a global total of 440 distinct food items across 173 countries in 2021, with 
each country reporting an average of 105 items across the six food groups needed for a healthy 
diet (Suppl. Table 8). These ICP data are reported by each national statistical organization 
through the World Bank, with the explicit goal of representing as many of the world’s commonly 
consumed foods for as much of the global population as possible. Each price is a national 
average for the 2021 calendar year, reported in local currency per unit sold.  We then converted 
those data into real monetary cost per unit of dietary energy for each type of food, expressed in 
purchasing power parity (PPP) dollars per kilocalorie (kcal) per person per day using the 
following steps: 
1) Converting to standard units with food composition data: The weight or volume or each 
item as sold was standardized into kilograms of edible matter and then kilocalories of dietary 
energy by matching ICP item descriptions to food composition tables, including USDA 
Standard Reference 28, the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies, the West


## Page 16


Page 16 of 40 
 
Africa Food Composition Table (FCT), and national FCTs from Bangladesh, India, Japan, 
and the United Kingdom. 
2) Calculating cost per day: Prices were converted to cost per day based on dietary energy 
required from each food group as specified in the Healthy Diet Basket (HDB). The HDB is 
derived from requirements specified in national dietary guidelines, defining a balanced diet 
as 11 items in 6 food groups adding up to 2,330 kcals/day, of which 1,160 kcal is from two 
different starchy staples, 300 kcal from any type of legumes, nuts or seeds, 110 kcal from 
three different vegetables, 160 kcal from two fruits, 300 kcal from two animal source foods,

To quantify the greenhouse gas emissions associated with each diet, ICP items were matched to

day per person, by country and year. Food product categories used for the FBS and SUA were 
matched to ICP food items, often with multiple ICP items in each food category, and multiple 
food categories in each food group needed for a healthy diet.

Affordability measure 
The Prevalence of Unaffordability (PUA) indicator estimates the percentage of individuals in a 
population whose disposable income, net of the amount required to meet basic non-food 
expenditures, is insufficient to afford the least expensive healthy diet in their country. Basic non-


## Page 20


Page 20 of 40 
 
food expenditures are calculated as the average non-food expenditure shares of low -income 
consumers, multiplied by the international poverty lines set by the World Bank. 
For low-income and lower-middle-income countries, non-food expenditure shares are derived 
from the second quintile of consumers, estimated at 37% and 44%, respectively. For upper-
middle-income and high-income countries, non-food expenditure shares are based on the first 
quintile, estimated at 54%, according to recent household survey data for 71 countries, compiled 
by the World Bank. The income distribution data used to calculate PUA are sourced from the 
World Bank’s Poverty and Inequality Platform (PIP)26 and are based on 2017 PPPs. The method 
is summarized in Table S2.3 of the UN agencies’ official report using the CoAHD method3, with 
details and provided in the FAO background paper for that report27.
