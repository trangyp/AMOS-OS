Human cognition is supported by brain structural connectivity wherein weak connectivity 
with weights several orders of magnitude smaller than those of strong connectivity, has been 
treated as noise and ignored from analysis over a long time. We here propose that weak 
connectivity plays roles to cognitive abilities by nonlinearly amplifying its small weights. 
Using the HCP dataset (n=999) and multiple tractography algorithms, we found that weak 
connectivity involves high individual variability and contributes to predictions of general 
cognitive ability and memory, and it is also critical for brain functional connectivity 
simulation and structure-function coupling. Importantly, we fused two post-tractography 
filtering methods to generate more reliable connectivity that preserves weak links and 
outperforms conventional thresholding. At the network level, we showed that weak 
connectivity expands the operational capacity of brain networks to enhance both global

diverse cognitive abilities. Finally, we identified a specific type of weak connectivity mainly 
linking visual/motor to limbic areas with negative gene co-expression, which has a 
disproportionately large functional impact. Our findings demonstrate groundbreaking 
evidence of underestimated but crucial role of weak connectivity in human cognition, 
providing a refined approach to reliably reveal brain structural connectivity.

1. Introduction 
Human brain is a complex network system composed of a mosaic of cortical areas as nodes 
and white matter fibers as edges 1,2. The noninvasive tractography based on diffusion MRI 
data is currently the only way to map the fiber projections, called structural connectivity (SC) 
of the in vivo human brains 3,4. Most state-of-the-art tractography algorithms had high 
accuracy in producing ground truth bundles (a group of streamlines) 4. However, 
tractographies are also seriously challenged by the false-positive problem 3-6. Even so, 
noninvasive tractography indeed provides an unprecedent opportunity to reconstruct the short 
and long-range fiber connections. These connections are essential for the investigation of the 
in vivo human brains 4,7-10, as well as for the worldwide large-scale brain simulation 
platforms, i.e., Virtual Brain 11 and Digital Twin Brain 12. These applications regard streamline 
number (termed as connectivity weight) as fiber strength 13 underlying neural signal 
transmission, even though strictly speaking this is not strongly grounded 3-6. Although current 
tractography methods are incapable of accurately reconstructing ground-truth streamlines and 
determining fiber strength 3,4, diffusion MRI mapping has indeed advanced the understanding 
of the functional roles of SC in brain dynamics, cognition and neuropsychiatric disorders as 
the new frontier of neuroscience.   
 
Noninvasive tractography generates a significant degree of noise in weak, long-range 
connectivity 14. With the general assumption that longer-range streamlines have less reliability 
from tractography, several post-filtering methods have been proposed to address the false-
positive problem (e.g., sift2 15 and commit2 3) by compressing the long-range streamlines, 
generating more reliable but spare connectivity, e.g., 30.2% density (53 areas) from the 
commit2 3. Tackling the issue of low reliability, the majority of operations in network 
neuroscience take the rough approach of directly thresholding out weak connectivity to retain 
a low network density (e.g., 10% or even lower, depending on resolution of brain parcellation) 
16-22, because the connectivity weights from tractography spans several orders of magnitudes 
and weak connectivity is believed to insignificantly affect the brain network topological 
measures and dynamics. Indeed, it was shown that the removal of even the 70–90% weakest 
connectivity had no statistical effect on graph-theoretical properties (weighted versions) 16 and 
removing the ∼50% weakest connectivity negligibly affected the neural signal propagation 
ratio in the dynamic model of rat brains 23. Consequently, neglecting weak connectivity seems 
to be a consensus in the fields of human brain tractography and network neuroscience. 
However, the sparse connectivity in the human brain is strongly contradictory with their high

5 
density in mammalian animal brains (e.g., 62.43% for 55 areas of marmoset monkey brains, 
66% for 29 areas in macaque brains, 97% for 47 areas in mouse brains) using reliable 
retrograde tracer injection 24-27. Furthermore, the connectivity weights (i.e., numbers of axon 
projections) in animals also span several orders of magnitudes in those reliable data 24-26,28, 
and the positive correlation between streamline number and tract-tracing strength has also 
been found 29-31. These evidences across species indicate the natural existence of weak 
connectivity 26,32,33, and cast doubt on the practice of direct exclusion of weak connectivity 
and using only low network density in the human brain research 16-22. Therefore, the apparent 
dilemma leads us to ask a critical, counter-intuitive open question: whether the tractography-
based weak connectivity really does not contain any useful information nor does it have an 
impact on brain dynamics, functions and cognitive abilities.  
 
In response to the question, we proposed to assess the importance of weak connectivity to 
human cognitive abilities and brain functions through dynamical modeling and machine 
learning analyses on diffusion MRI data. To perform the analyses systematically, we here set 
the connectivity with weight smaller than the threshold that generates ∼10% density in brain 
SC networks as relatively weak, because network neuroscience studies often focused on the 
10% backbone strong connectivity and discarded the weaker portion 16-22. But notably, ‘weak’ 
is a relative definition to facilitate subsequent analyses and there is no accepted definition of 
weak connectivity in network neuroscience, especially due to a broad distribution of 
connectivity weighting over several orders of magnitude. The connectivity weight has a 
dependence on space distance 32 and long-range fibers are usually more difficult to trace 34, 
except for corpus callosum fibers. Consequently, the weight of weak connectivity may appear 
to be even weaker in tractography, and then, considering the linear weight of connectivity, 
weak connectivity was unimportant for brain network topologies and dynamics16,23. Thus, in 
large-scale brain networks, we hypothesized a potential nonlinear functioning manner of SC 
to human cognitive abilities and brain functions, such that weak connectivity could play more 
important roles than its small weights appear to. In order to test such a possibility, we 
proposed a phenomenological model to validate this potential nonlinear functioning manner 
by applying a scaling parameter β to all connectivity from tractography (i.e., 𝑤𝑖𝑗
𝛽): β = 1.0 
means that the streamline number is taken as the fiber strength, the same as previous studies 
16-22; and 0.0<β<1.0 indicates the potential nonlinear dependence of fiber strength and 
functional effects on streamline numbers.

Here, we utilized the large-sample dataset (sample size n=999) from the Human Connectome 
Project (HCP) 35. First, we applied multiple probabilistic tractography algorithms to map the 
streamlines from seed to target regions which were further filtered by the sift2 and commit2 
methods. Consistent with previous studies 16-22, we regarded the reconstructed streamlines as 
the SC of the in vivo human brains, even though its weight does not strictly equal to the real 
fiber strength 3-6. Second, we constructed a machine learning model to investigate whether 
weak connectivity with scaling parameter β is predictive of diverse cognitive abilities in 
individuals. Third, we fused the tractography and filtering methods to extract potentially more 
reliable fused SC and tested it in a test-retest dataset (simple size n=44). Using a large-scale 
brain dynamic model, we confirmed the advantages of fused SC and weak connectivity’s 
nonlinearly contribution to more realistic simulation of brain functional connectivity and 
structure-function relationship than the thresholded SC. Then, we conducted an eigenmode 
analysis without thresholding to reflect the effect of weak connectivity across hierarchical 
levels of connectome activation modes, as a potential mechanistic explanation of its 
functional roles in cognitive abilities. Finally, we examined the distribution principle of weak 
connectivity in the brain in terms of functional systems and gene co-expressions, and 
investigated the heterogeneous functions of weak connectivity in relation to brain dynamics 
and cognitive abilities.  
 
2. Results  
2.1. Weak connectivity in a whole-brain structural connectome with diffusion MRI data.  
In the tractography, we used the probabilistic algorithms 36 to track the streamline and 
constructed the whole-brain basic SC networks (N=360 regions, binary density: 56.3±3.8%, 
Figure 1A). The weight of SC spans several orders of magnitude in individuals, consistent 
with observations of other mammal brains using reliable retrograde tracer injections 24-26,28. 
Two state of the art post-filtering methods (sift2 15 and commit2 3) were further applied to 
address the false-positive problem. Sift2 15 modulates the streamlines to optimize the 
similarity between streamline density and fiber orientation distribution (FOD)  lobe (i.e., 
primary diffusion direction). This method does not change the binary density of SC networks, 
but mainly decreases the weights of weak connectivity and also increases weights of some 
stronger connectivity (Figures 1B and S1). Commit2 3 deals with the false-positive issue by 
(1) considering the contribution of each streamline to voxel signal proportionally to its length, 
and (2) gathering the streamlines belonging to the same bundle together that connect the same 
pairs of regions; however, it does not have the prior knowledge of true-positive and false-

positive bundles 3. By controlling the bundle constraint through a regularization coefficient 
𝜆= [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2] (see Materials and Methods), commit2 could exclude weak 
connectivity from the basic SC to generate different network densities varying from 56.3% to 
27.9% (Figure 1D), wherein the weight of the retained weak connectivity also becomes 
smaller (Figure 1C). These results show that there is a significant amount of weak 
connectivity, which is prone to the false-positive problem and the methods for dealing with it. 
More importantly, the inter-individual coefficients of variation (CV, standard deviation/mean) 
of the connectivity decay with the mean weights of the connectivity (Figures 1E and S2). In 
a test-retest HCP dataset with 44 individuals having two DWI scans, we calculated the 
intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to evaluate the reliability of each connectivity and also 
found this association between CV and weight for relatively reliable connectivity (i.e., 
ICC>0.6, Figure S3). Thus, strong backbone connectivity is relatively consistent across 
individuals 37, and weaker connectivity shows higher individual variability. Apart from the 
tractography noise, the large variance in weak connectivity may have functional significance 
for variations in individual cognitive abilities.

2.2. Nonlinear contribution of weak connectivity to the predictions of individual 
cognitive abilities 
We proposed that SC can be functionally significant in a nonlinear manner by introducing the 
same scaling parameter on all SC (i.e., 𝑤𝑖𝑗
𝛽, see Materials and Methods). A lower value of 𝛽<
1.0 indicates that weak connectivity could have functional impact higher than that reflected 
by its traditionally used linear weight. To demonstrate the significance, we first constructed a 
structural equation model (SEM) involving four latent cognitive abilities (i.e., crystallized 
intelligence: cry; processing speed: spd; general cognitive ability: g; and memory: mem) 
based on nine cognitive behavioral tasks (Figure 2A) and extracted individual estimates of 
these cognitive ability factors. These four cognitive abilities cover diverse functional demands 
on brain network segregation (required by crystallized intelligence and processing speed), 
integration (required by general cognitive ability) and their balance (required by memory) 38. 
We then constructed a connectome-based prediction model (CPM) 39 to predict cognitive 
abilities from individual SC with the scaled 𝑤𝑖𝑗
𝛽 (Figure 2B). It is expected that 𝛽< 1.0 
would generate better predictions than 𝛽= 1.0 wherein the connection weights are used in a 
linear manner. Although this nonlinear functioning model would also enlarge noise and false-
positive connectivity, such noisy, random portion of connectivity should not be systematically 
associated with cognitive abilities across individuals, which could be partially compressed 
when predicting individual cognitive abilities. 
 
We found that general cognitive ability is well predicted from basic SC, memory and 
crystallized intelligence show only modest predictive associations (Figure 2C), and 
processing speed cannot be predicted (Figure S4). More importantly, 𝛽< 1 seems generate 
better predictions. To test this prediction improvement but avoiding the optimistic bias, we 
introduced an inner loop to select the best β inside a fully nested cross-validation in the CPM 
(see Materials and Methods). The best predictions are at 𝛽= 0.703 (95% 𝐶𝐼, 0.641 −

We also predicted specific task performance and further confirmed that 𝛽< 1.0 generates 
better predictions than 𝛽= 1.0 (Figure S7). These observations of nonlinear functioning 
manner in SC are robustly retained for (1) different post-filtering methods (i.e., sift2 and

the desired number of streamlines as 50 million in tractography (Figure S8); (3) the SC 
reconstructed by a different tractography algorithm 40 (density: 90.6±3.2%, Figure S8). In 
addition, in an independent UCLA dataset (sample size, n=122), we extracted the executive 
control ability from three task performances and also found better predictions of the executive 
control ability and three task scores with 𝛽< 1.0 than those with 𝛽= 1.0 (Figure S9). These 
systematic findings for diverse cognitive abilities, tractography algorithms, post-filtering 
methods and network densities provide ample evidence that in a nonlinear manner, SC has 
selective implications for cognitive abilities, especially for general ability and memory, such 
that weak connectivity has stronger functional impact than what its small weight implies.  
 
To highlight the role of weak connectivity, we examined the distribution of the connectivity 
selected in the CPM. In the best predictions, most of the selected connectivity in the CPM is 
weak (i.e., the weight smaller than the threshold for generating ~10% density) for general 
ability (basic: 88.98%, sift2: 87.90%, commit2: 79.54%), memory (basic: 86.71%, sift2:

genuine individual differences. Ignoring such weak connectivity in the majority of network 
neuroscience studies 16-22 thus may have lost crucial information about individual differences 
and strongly limited the understanding of the brain network organization and functions.

2.3. Towards more reliable brain structural connectivity 
We were interested to see whether different filtering methods could be integrated to obtain 
more reliable brain SC but retain weak connectivity. Different from the thresholding method 
to remove weak connectivity 16-22, commit2 introduces biological constrains on the formation 
of bundles  to exclude an amount of false-positive connectivity 3 while retaining some weak 
connectivity. However, comparing to basic/sift2 SC, commit2 SC (𝜆= 0.05) has clearly 
worse prediction performance for general ability (two-sample t-test, basic: t(198) = -9.451, p 
< 0.001;sift2: t(198) = -8.908, p < 0.001, Figure 2C, D) and memory (basic: t(198) = -2.620, 
p =0.009; sift2: t(198) = -2.522, p =0.012), but not for crystallized intelligence (basic: t(198) = 
-0.736, p =0.4623; sift2: t(198) = -1.877, p=0.062). Since all connectivity weights were 
adjusted in commit2 SC relative to basic/sift2 SC and the number of links were greatly

decreased (i.e., smaller density and fewer features to CPM), it is unclear whether the 
decreased predictions are due to the reduction of feature number or the weight modulation. To 
investigate this issue, we created a “fused SC” (Figure 3A) by retaining the existed link (i.e., 
𝑤𝑖𝑗≠0) in commit2 SC networks (i.e., commit2 masks), while replacing the weight of the 
link with the corresponding sift2 SC weight. Interestingly, consistent across different 𝜆 to 
obtain commit2 masks, the predictions of general ability and memory from fused SC are 
significantly higher than those from commit2 SC networks (Figure 3B, Table S1). 
Alternatively, we obtained a “combined SC network” (Figure 3A). We retained all links in the 
sift2 network (i.e., sift2 partial mask), assigned commit2 SC weights to the overlapping links 
between sift2 and commit2 SC, and preserved sift2 weights for non-overlapping links. This 
combined SC network has the same density but different weight as the sift2 SC network. The 
predictions of cognitive abilities from the combined SC are much smaller than those from 
sift2 SC (Figure 3B, red dashed line) and fused SC (Table S1). These results suggest that the 
reduced prediction of cognitive abilities for commit2 SC come from the modulation of 
connectivity weight, indicating that the modification of the connectivity weights by commit2 
may have degraded some useful information regarding individual differences. However, the 
robust prediction by fused SC with reduced network density suggests that the removed weak 
connectivity by commit2 could be largely false-positive. Therefore, combining the sift2 
connectivity weight and commit2 mask (i.e., fused SC network) could be a potential way to 
obtain more reliable connectivity, reducing the false-positive connectivity while retaining 
useful individual differences.       
 
In the test-retest dataset, the ICCs of both high (ICC>0.6) and low (ICC<0.4) reliable 
connectivity are always higher for fused SC than commit2 SC (Figure 3C), suggesting an 
improvement of reliability. With λ increasing, the ICC for low-reliable connectivity increases, 
indicating that fusing sift2 and commit2 indeed improves the reliability of less-reliable 
connectivity. However, as 𝜆 increases, the biological constraint of bundles in commit2 is 
stronger and that may discard the true-positive connectivity, especially for streamlines that 
may not follow the bundles34,42. Thus, larger λ would remove more connectivity with high 
reliability and induce the decrease of ICC for high-reliable connectivity (Figure 3C). It is thus 
expected that an optimal 𝜆 would reduce the false-positive connectivity to the greatest extent, 
but meanwhile, the SC network retains the ability to predict cognitive abilities, showing a 
trade-off between biological constraints and reliability. Indeed, with 𝜆 increasing to 0.2, the 
predictions of general ability and memory by fused SC are first retained and then decreased

2.4. Nonlinear contribution of weak connectivity to brain functional organizations 
We further tested whether SC plays nonlinear roles in brain functions. First, we constructed a 
whole-brain dynamic model to simulate functional connectivity (FC) based on the fused SC 
(𝜆= 0.05, density: 39.6%). In the model, there are two tuning parameters: global coupling c 
and β. We optimized the parameters to ensure the highest similarity between simulated FC 
matrices and real FC matrices that were constructed by concentrating four sessions of resting-
state fMRI data 13,38. In the c-β parameter space, the highest similarity (smallest Euclidean 
distance) between simulated and empirical FC matrices appears around β =0.2. To test the 
model performance without an optimistic bias, we generated two FC matrices by 
concentrating two fMRI series in each individual. One of the FC matrices was randomly 
selected to fit the model, and the other was used to test. The best β is at 0.181 (95% CI, 0.178-
0.183, Figure 4A), with the FC distance significantly smaller than that for β = 1.0 wherein the 
connectivity weights play a linear role (pair-wise t-test, t(998) = -101.70, p < 0.001). To prove 
that this performance improvement of model fitting is not due to the increase of model 
complexity, we calculated the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information 
Criterion (BIC, see Supplementary Information). The model for the best β has significantly 
lower AIC (t(997)=98.86, p<0.001) and BIC (t(997)=98.86, p<0.001) than that for β=1.0 
(Figure S19). These results are also robust for a nonlinear Jansen Rit model and the


## Page 17


1
log(SC+1) function (Figure S19 and S20). Then, we focused on the brain structure-function 
relationship measured by Pearson correlation between empirical SC scaled by β and FC in 
each individual. The best SC-FC correlation in empirical data appears at β = 0.319 (95% CI, 
0.315-0.322), which is significantly larger than that for β = 1.0 (t(998) = 151.300, p < 0.001, 
Figure 4B). Finally, we predicted the fused SC (𝜆= 0.05) from FC using a deep learning 
model 43 (see Materials and Methods). The correlation between predicted and existed fused 
SC is the best for 𝛽= 0.44 (95% CI, 0.32-0.54), which is significantly higher than that for β 
= 1.0 (pair-wise t-test, t(99)=30.591, p<0.001, Figure 4C). These results indicate that scaling 
SC seems to allow weak connectivity to play more important roles in brain FC and structure-
function relationship than what its small weight value would suggest, and this range is 
consistent with the best prediction of cognitive abilities (Figure 2), offering a mechanistic 
explanation.  
 
Considering the trade-off between network density and prediction power of cognitive abilities 
(Figure 3B), we then studied whether fused SC at 𝜆=0.05 is also optimal for brain functional 
organization in terms of segregation, integration and their balance that empowers the diverse 
cognitive abilities 38. The nested spectral partition (NSP) method was used to identify the 
segregation-integration balance 38, i.e., HB = 0. Positive HB corresponds to bias to integration, 
and negative HB measures bias to segregation (see Materials and Methods). The resting-state 
brains of healthy young adults maintain a segregation-integration balance 38, as further 
confirmed by the HB being close to zero for the empirical FC and simulated FC (Figure 4D, 
E). For the fused SC at each 𝜆, model parameters c and β were optimized to generate the 
smallest distance between the empirical FC and the best-simulated FC. Subsequently, the 
absolute difference of global HB between simulated and empirical FC matrices was calculated. 
With 𝜆 increasing, the global HB difference is first retained until 𝜆 ~ 0.05 and then greatly 
increases (𝜆= 0.1 vs. 𝜆= 0, t(998) = 3.033, p = 0.002, Figure 4F). More importantly, when 
adding more noises to connectivity by setting the desired number of streamlines as 50 million, 
the global HB difference first decreases to the minimum value at  𝜆= 0.01~0.05 and then 
increases with increasing 𝜆 (Figure 4F). It seems that the fused SC at 𝜆= 0.01~0.05 (we 
selected 0.05) indeed reduces the false-positive connectivity to the greatest extent, while 
retaining the powers of predicting cognitive abilities and simulating FC, providing more 
reliable brain SC.

2.5. Weak connectivity contributes to stronger large-scale integration but finer-scale 
local segregation 
We further studied the potential dynamic mechanisms underlying the selective function of 
weak connectivity to cognitive abilities in terms of segregation and integration. In the large-
scale model (𝛽= 0.2), when weak connectivity was removed by thresholding in fused SC 
(𝜆= 0.05), HB was reduced for the fused SC network (Figure 5B), suggesting that removing 
weak connectivity would break down the balance and generate higher network segregation. 
This is especially serious when the connectivity density is reduced to 10% which is typically  
applied in network neuroscience studies 16-22. Thus, the above results consistently demonstrate 
that with the nonlinear functioning manner (𝛽< 1), weak connectivity selectively contributes 
to the prediction of general cognitive ability and memory, which are in turn supported by 
higher integration and the segregation-integration balance 38; however, this nonlinear 
functioning manner of weak connectivity does not contribute to the crystallized intelligence 
which requires higher segregation. Thus, these results suggest that weak connectivity 
contributes to the brain functional balance by primarily enhancing network integration.


## Page 20


2
 
The segregation and integration are generated by activating the intrinsic structural eigenmodes 
of brain SC networks 13,44. The first mode, with an eigenvalue of zero, corresponds to the 
homogeneous activation of whole brain regions, while higher-order modes describe activation 
of local regions at different spatial-temporal scales. We defined the active size in each mode 
as the number of regions with squared eigenvector component values larger than a threshold 
(i.e., the standard deviation, Figure 5A). A higher active size denotes coactivation of more 
regions and corresponds to larger-scale integration. As the order of modes increases, the 
active size decreases (Figure 5C), reflecting that lower-order modes are responsible for 
larger-scale integration and that higher-order modes correspond to more segregated activation 
of local regions. When weak connectivity was removed from the fused SC network by 
thresholding (e.g., 10% density), the active size of low-order modes decreased while the size 
of high-order modes increased (Figure 5C). This phenomenon can be clearly seen in the 
example of two specific structural modes, wherein the low-order mode in the 10% density 
network has a smaller activation range, and the high-order mode has a larger range (Figure 
5D) when compared to the original fused SC network. Thus, a wide range of regions cannot 
be coactivated to generate large-scale integration in the low-order mode in the 10% density 
network without including weak connectivity. However, compared to the fused SC network, 
the high-order modes activate more regions and appear to induce broader local integrations. 
By calculating the mean active sizes of low-order and high-order modes, we found that with 
increasing network density when including weak connectivity, the active size of low-order 
modes increased and that of high-order modes decreased (Figure 5E), revealing that weak 
connectivity contributes to global integration by increasing the active sizes in low-order 
modes, while it also contributes to finer-scale segregations in high-order modes. This counter-
intuitive result is a rather interesting but reasonable finding, as local circuits without the 
projections and interference of weak connectivity from other circuits may become more 
cohesive due to strong local connections. And the local circuits do not support finer-scale 
segregation that may be induced by (weak) projections from other distant regions. Thus, 
adding weak connectivity would greatly expand the hierarchy of brain SC network to enhance 
its processing capacity, as supported by the highly heterogeneous active sizes across modes 
(Figure 5F).  
 
Therefore, weak connectivity not only contributes to integration at the scale of the whole 
brain, but also induces finer-scaled segregation in high-order modes, which can overall

2
expand the processing capacity of the brain network hierarchy. With the nonlinear functional 
manner for  < 1.0, removing weak connectivity to retain 10% density only would have 
greater impact on the increase of active size of high-order modes (Figure S22), suggesting 
that weak connectivity consequently has higher impact on finer-scale segregations via 
nonlinear interactions.

2.6. Basic organization principles of weak connectivity 
We finally investigated how the functional implication of weak connectivity is related to its 
basic organization in the brain. The brain was partitioned into seven resting-state networks 
(RSNs) 45, and we found that the connectivity weight in the group-averaged fused SC network 
(𝜆= 0.05) between RSNs is significantly smaller than that within RSNs (Figure 6A). When 
the threshold was applied to remove weak connectivity (yielding ~10% density), the 
connectivity between RSNs was excluded noticeably more than that within RSNs (Figure 6A, 
82.02% vs. 17.98%, pair-wise t-test, t(998) = 1877.27, p < 0.001). We calculated the binary 
connectivity densities within and between RSNs in individual SC networks, measured by the 
number of existing connectivity divided by the maximum number of possible connectivity 
(irrespective of the connection weight). With the removal of weak connectivity, both inter- 
and intra-RSN densities decreased (Figure 6C), but the inter-RSN density decreased more 
rapidly than the intra-RSN density from the fused network to 10% density networks (relative 
change: 77.9% vs. 56.6%, t(998) = 558.32, p<0.001). These results clearly show that weak 
connectivity is distributed between RSNs more than within each RSN, matching to its 
meaningful contribution to cognitive abilities. 
 
We further investigated the organization principle of weak connectivity in terms of gene 
expression. At the whole-brain scale, we used public gene expression data to construct a 
group-averaged gene co-expression (GC) network (Figure 6B). GC was positively correlated 
with the log-scaled SC weight (Figure S23), reflecting that weaker connectivity linking brain 
regions with less cooperative gene co-expression. We further studied the organization 
principle of weak connectivity with respect to brain hierarchy by calculating the GC 
hierarchical gradient (see Materials and Methods). The principal gradient of GC corresponds 
to the hierarchy from posterior to anterior cortical regions (Figure 6B), consistent with the 
primary–transmodal gradient from other imaging measures 46-49. By rearranging the SC matrix 
according to the GC gradient order, we found that the regions with negative GC have a 
significantly longer gradient distance than those with positive GC (Figures 6B and S24). 
Importantly, weak connectivity primarily connects the long-distance gradient locations 
(Figure 6B), i.e., dissimilar brain regions between the posterior and anterior cortex. By 
calculating the ratio of weak connectivity with negative GC to all connectivity for each 
region, we observed a U-shape relationship between the ratio and the GC gradient (nonlinear 
fitting 𝑦~𝑥2 + 𝑥 yielding 𝑅2 = 0.56, F(2,357) =232.58, p < 0.001, see Figure 6D; 
permutation test of Moran spectral randomization, p < 0.001, see Figure S25) wherein

visual/motor and limbic systems have higher ratios than other systems. Moreover, the ratio of 
weak connectivity with negative GC is more distributed in inter-system than in intra-system 
(t(998) = 410.71, p < 0.001, Figure 6E). Therefore, weak connectivity corresponding to less 
cooperation between regional gene profiles are more likely to project to or from 
visual/motor/limbic regions, reflecting a typical visual-limbic pathway 50, suggesting that it 
may serve specific brain functions.  
 
Our gradient analyses showed that there may be different types of weak connectivity 
according to gene co-expression profiles. To assess potentially differentiable functional roles 
of these types, we deleted weak connectivity with positive or negative GC separately, and 
studied whether they have different impacts on the brain dynamics and predictions of 
cognitive abilities. Deleting the weak connectivity with negative GC induced stronger

3. Discussion 
In this work, we addressed the apparent paradox that weak structural connectivity is densely 
distributed in the human brain but is considered functionally insignificant and largely ignored 
in the majority of network neuroscience studies. We proposed a theory that weak connectivity 
has strong functional implications and contributes to human cognitive abilities in a nonlinear 
manner despite its small weight. First, under this theoretical framework, we found that general 
ability, memory and crystallized intelligence can be better predicted, demonstrating that weak 
connectivity contributes to individual cognitive abilities. In addition, with the nonlinear effect 
of weak connectivity, we revealed stronger brain structure-function relationship and showed 
that the whole-brain dynamic model generates more realistic functional connectivity. Second, 
by fusing two post-filtering methods (sift2 and commit2) to reduce the false-positive 
connectivity, we obtained more reliable structural connectivity that contributes to robust 
predictions of cognitive abilities and more accurate simulation of brain functional 
connectivity. More importantly, this fused connectivity retains the reliable weak link with 
several orders of magnitude and more effectively reveals brain structure-function relationship 
and more accurately simulates functional connectivity than the thresholded connectivity that 
simply discards weak connectivity. Then, we elucidated that weak connectivity not only 
enhances functional integration and contributes to the segregation-integration balance, but 
also counterintuitively induces finer segregated brain activation patterns in high-order 
activation modes, overall expanding the functional capacity of brain network hierarchy. 
Finally, we revealed that weak connectivity mainly follows an intersystem distribution pattern 
and is strongly associated with less cooperation of gene profiles of the linked regions. 
Particularly for weak connectivity that links the visual/motor regions with limbic regions, it 
has negative gene co-expression and is distant across the principal hierarchical gradients of 
the brain. This type of weak connectivity has particularly great impact on brain structure and 
dynamics, and also dominantly contributes to the predictions of cognitive abilities. 
Collectively, we provided a systematic understanding of the importance of weak connectivity


## Page 26


2
in supporting cognitive abilities and brain functional organization, which is otherwise 
seriously underrated in network neuroscience studies. Our results reveal a newly discovered 
mechanism of structural connectivity for amplifying neural signal transmission and 
regulation, with distinct roles of weak connectivity depending on gene profiles. The 
application of fusing tractography and filtering methods on diffusion MRI dataset resulted in a 
reliable brain connectome for analyzing and modeling large-scale brain networks to reveal 
meaningful individual differences in brain cognition and disorders. 
3.1. Nonlinear functioning manner of structural connectivity 
Weak connectivity in the human brain structural connectome, with weight spanning orders of 
magnitude, is largely treated as noise and further omitted from analysis by thresholding 
methods in previous studies 16-22,37,51,52. This widely accepted operation is largely driven by 
the false-positive problem of non-invasive tractography 3-6. Meanwhile, studies using the 
traditional method based on a linear combination of connectivity weights in graph measures 
or dynamical modeling have concluded that weak connectivity is not important 16,23. In fact, 
weak connectivity has been included in CPM to predict cognitive abilities 53,54, trait anxiety 55 
and hearing ability 56, but its functional role was rarely explicitly discussed. On the other 
hand, weak connectivity in macaque brain connectome can be predicted from strong links 57, 
and a group-averaged study also reported that weak connectivity promotes complex dynamics 
by diversifying the inputs and outputs to brain areas 32, suggesting that weak connectivity 
shall not be ignored. In some studies, the log-scaled form of SC was considered 58,59, but there 
is still a lack of understanding on how weak connectivity is functionally significant. To 
address the question, we hereby proposed a theory that weak connectivity has significant 
functional implications for human cognitive abilities and brain functions in a nonlinear 
manner. To examine this, we applied a scaling parameter 𝛽 to SC. Such a nonlinear manner 
with 𝛽< 1 reveals the crucial functional roles of weak connectivity, as demonstrated by its 
contribution to more accurate predictions of cognitive abilities, a stronger relationship of brain 
structural and functional connectivity and a more realistic simulation of brain functional 
networks, segregation and integration in the whole-brain dynamic model. These results 
advanced our understanding that weak connectivity contributes to brain dynamics and 
cognition in a nonlinear manner. Even suffering from false-positive challenges 3-6, weak 
connectivity should not be simply regraded as noises and neglected in network analyses. This 
nonlinear amplification of weak connectivity are supported by various factors: (1) the weight 
of weak connectivity in tractography streamlines may be even weaker than the actual fiber 
projections especially for long-range ones 32,34; (2) the functional impact of synapses can be


## Page 27


2
amplified by recurrent excitations and spatial clustering of co-activations 60-62; For example, 
in layer 4 in the visual cortex 61, the spatial clustering of co-activations reserved in weak 
excitatory synapses amplifies the synaptic drives and enhances the contribution of the large 
number of weak synapses to selective responses of primary visual cortex (V1) neurons to 
visual stimuli; (3) ample evidence has shown that neural dynamics in local or global scales are 
organized near critical states which are sensitive to small stimulations 13,63, thus activation 
projecting through weak connectivity may still induce sizable responses. Future studies using 
modeling may help to elucidate the interplay between these and other factors and reveal the 
nonlinear functional roles of weak connectivity in brain connectome. Currently, how neural 
activities regulate the amplification of neural signal transmission through weak white matter 
fibers in the large-scale brain connectome has not yet been tested in animal experiments, and 
thus our theory is limited by the lack of direct experimental evidence. But these results 
suggested that the pure viewpoint of graph network analysis is far from being sufficient to 
understand the complex interactions in the brain connectome, and the future development of 
network neuroscience should consider more realistic neural biological processes. 
3.2. Filtering more reliable connectivity 
There is no doubt that weak connectivity widely exists and has functional significance no 
matter in human or animal brains 26,32,33. However, current tractographies are greatly 
challenged by the false-positive problem 3-6 and structural connectivity has a significant 
degree of noise, especially in weak connectivity. To tackle the false-positive problem and 
enhance the accuracy in tracing true fibers, the post-filtering methods of sift2 15 and commit2 
3 were recently developed based on different tract-tracing frameworks and hypotheses. Sift2 
focuses on the maximal similarity between streamline density and FOD lobe in the same 
direction of fibers, and commit2 3 aims to enhancing the sensitivity/specificity in tractography 
by incorporating more prior knowledge about brain anatomy that streamlines represent 
neuronal fibers and naturally organize into bundles. In terms of cognitive ability predictions, 
we found that sift2 improves the quality of connectivity weight and commit2 provides the 
potential mask of truth connectivity by excluding the noisy connectivity. Fusing the 
connectivity weight of sift2 and the connectivity mask of commit2 provides the powers in 
predicting cognitive abilities and simulating brain functional organization, and meanwhile, 
removing noisy false-positive connectivity as much as possible, indicating a pathway to 
generate more reliable connectivity. Compared to the thresholding method of directly 
removing weak connectivity 16-22, this fusing method does not need an artificial threshold and 
can retain weak connectivity, especially effective in preserving meaningful individual


## Page 28


2
differences in cognitive performances. More importantly, fused SC contributes to better 
revealing the brain structure-function relationship and simulating brain functional networks in 
a whole-brain dynamic model, providing further evidence that weak connectivity has 
significant impact on brain functions and cognition. Therefore, even though there is no ground 
truth human whole-brain connectome for directly validating the fused SC, our approaches 
provide convincing evidence that fused SC could provide a practical solution for reliably 
revealing human brain structural connectivity, which may be applied in the analysis and 
modeling of brain cognition and disorders. However, it should be noted that this fusion is 
performed at the level of connectivity and is greatly dependent on the tract tracing methods 
(i.e., sift2 and commit2) at the level of streamlines, and fused SC weight should also not be 
confused with the real fiber strength. 
3.3. Weak connectivity contributes to brain functional integration 
We found that weak connectivity is mainly distributed between functional systems, in 
consistent with other literature 64. From a network perspective, weak connectivity plays a role 
in functional integration and in balancing segregation of systems. Our analysis confirmed this 
role quantitatively. This may explain why general cognitive ability and memory requiring 
integration and balance involve weak connectivity with the nonlinear functioning manner 
(i.e., β < 1), but crystallized intelligence that requires higher segregation is insensitive to β. 
More importantly, eigenmode analysis revealed that weak connectivity also contributes to 
finer-scale segregation in local regions. Thus, including weak connectivity can allow for the 
integration of small modules into large modules to support larger-scale integration. 
Concurrently, weak connectivity expands the hierarchies of the activation modes and allows 
the generation of finer modules differentially linking with other systems, as reflected by 
higher coherence in high-order mode levels. Overall, the dynamic range and functional 
capacity of the brain network can be expanded by the presence of weak connectivity. These 
results greatly expand the understanding of the role of weak connectivity in brain dynamics 
and ultimately provide a high potential dimension for studying personalized connectome in 
cognitive neuroscience and abnormalities related to brain disorders 8,65.  
3.4. Two types of weak connectivity 
Crucially, weak connectivity can be classified into two types with distinct functions, one with 
negative gene co-expression and the other with positive gene co-expression. We found that the 
type with negative gene co-expression is mainly linked to visual/motor and limbic systems. It 
is now widely accepted that the cortex has hierarchies involving the asymmetric forward and 
backward pathways 66,67, e.g., across visual, sensory and motor cortexes 60. In particular, in


## Page 29


2
macaque visual cortex, forward pathways were found to have a higher weight and cross fewer 
hierarchical levels compared to backward pathways 68. Human visual system has positive 
gene co-expression with the motor and dorsal attention systems, consistent with the bottom-up 
salience pathway 69, and it has negative gene co-expression with the limbic and DMN 
systems, matching the top-down control pathway 69. Thus, weak intersystem connectivity with 
positive and negative gene co-expression may partially correspond to forward and backward 
functional projections. In the analysis of brain functional connectivity, Granger causality has 
been used to study the effective forward and backward paths 66,70, but in the human structural 
connectome, it is still challenging to study forward and backward processing through fiber 
paths obtained from noninvasive diffusion imaging 71 which cannot provide directionality of 
projections. Reproducing similar analysis in animal brains may contribute to addressing this 
relationship. Meanwhile, based on the primate cortical laminar structure and 
weighted/directed connectivity data, Mejias and colleagues constructed a large-scale 
computational model to reveal feedforward-associated gamma oscillations and feedback-
related alpha/low-beta oscillations 72. Thus, the development of a computational neuroscience 
model with forward and backward projections is promising for understanding the dynamic 
and functional relevance of weak connectivity that is revealed by our empirical association 
analysis. Additionally, while structural connectivity in the dynamic model typically enhances 
correlations between regions, long-range weak connectivity, primarily believed to transfer 
excitatory signals, can activate both excitatory and inhibitory neurons 73 and plays a 
potentially inhibitory function. Thus, weak connectivity linking different functional systems 
could have complex implications for information transmission and regulation. Understanding 
of this biological background should be a priority in network neuroscience and further 
dynamic model analysis should be conducted to reveal the important roles of weak 
connectivity. 
4. Conclusion 
In summary, our proposed theory suggests that weak connectivity functionally contributes to 
cognitive abilities by integrating different systems to maintain a balance between local 
segregation and global integration, with the functional impact amplified in a nonlinear 
manner. Fusing multiple filtering methods for fibers is a promising way to select more truthful 
connectivity in brain structural connectome, especially preserving weak connectivity that 
contains important information of individual differences. The fused connectivity reliably 
enables the study of individual differences in cognition and disorders. The identified 
organization principles provide insights into the existence of two types of weak connectivity


## Page 30


3
that potentially support forward and backward pathways in the brain. These findings reveal 
the necessities of weak connectivity for brain functional interactions and filled a significant 
knowledge gap about weak connectivity in the current network neuroscience literature. This 
work also reveals the urgency of developing effective synergy between experimental design 
and network tools, and to support the next generation of network neuroscience. 
5. Materials and Methods 
Data acquisition. The dataset was obtained from the WU-Minn Human Connectome Project 
(HCP) Young Adult database 35, including data of structural magnetic resonance imaging 
(MRI), diffusion MRI, resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) and nine behavioral measures 
collected from 999 healthy young adults (22-35 years old). For each participant, the resting-
state fMRI data were recorded for 864 s (1,200 frames) in four high-resolution scanning 
sessions across two days. Details of the MRI data acquisition are extensively described in 
35,74. 
Diffusion MRI data processing. The MRtrix3 software was used to process the DWI data that 
has underwent the standard HCP minimal preprocessing pipeline 36. The multi-tissue response 
functions for white matter (WM), gray matter (GM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were 
estimated using the dhollander algorithm, followed by multi-shell, multi-tissue constrained 
spherical deconvolution to compute fiber orientation distributions (FODs) for each tissue 
type. A five-tissue-type segmentation was generated from the T1-weighted image for 
anatomically constrained tractography. Whole-brain probabilistic streamlines were then 
reconstructed using the iFOD2 algorithm, seeding dynamically based on the WM FOD and 
producing 10 million streamlines with backtracking enabled (the streamline number of 50 
million was also chosen). Then, the brain was partitioned into 360 regions (180 per 
hemisphere) according to the Multi-Modal Parcellation (MMP) atlas 75. The resulting maps 
from seed to target regions indicate the percentage of streamlines and represent the likelihood 
of the connection, but a high likelihood does not necessarily mean a strong white matter fiber 
6. Despite the limitations of the tractographies, they are currently the only way to map the 
structural connectivity of the in vivo human brain. Therefore, we used the average probability 
of streamlines obtained by taking one area as the seed and the other as the target (vice versa) 
to define the weight of connection between two regions, termed as structural connectivity 
(SC). The resulting adjacency matrix 𝑊 was defined as the brain SC network, where the 
element represents the connection between regions i and j. Notably, even tracking weak 
connectivity theoretically may suffer from more accumulated error problems in probabilistic 
algorithm. The key point is that such weak connectivity was largely ignored in network

3
neuroscience, but may not be merely noises due to technical limitations. Instead, it may reflect 
true individual differences in cognitive abilities. 
At the streamline level, the spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (Sift2)

Because selecting the best β by comparing the predictions among different β would constitute 
an optimistic bias, we designed the CPM to select the best β strictly within the training data, 
while keeping the test data completely unseen until the final evaluation. This model consists 
of an outer loop and an inner loop. In the outer loop, we employed a 10-fold cross-validation 
and repeated 10 times, resulting in 100 independent outer splits. In each split, the data was 
divided into an outer training set (90%) and an outer test set (10%, the results are similar for 
50%:50%, Figure S16). To enforce the family-wise splitting, we utilized a Group K-Fold 
strategy to ensure the subjects from the same family strictly in the same fold. In the inner 
loop, we performed a further 5-fold internal cross-validation within each outer training set. 
We iterated through the candidate β∈[0.1,1.5], trained the CPM model on the inner training 
folds, and evaluated them on the inner validation folds. The β value that yielded the highest 
mean correlation across these inner folds was selected as the optimal β* for that specific outer 
iteration. This optimal β* was used to train the final model on the entire outer training set and 
the model was subsequently applied to the untouched outer test set to generate the predictions. 
Within each of the 100 independent outer splits, we recorded the prediction performance 
(measured by the Pearson correlation between the predicted and true ability scores) for the 
optimal β* and compared it with the baseline β=1 using the pair-wise t-test. This strictly 
nested approach eliminates optimistic bias by ensuring that the data used to select the optimal 
β* is entirely independent of the data used to evaluate prediction accuracy. Because the best 
β* is different at different iterations, we finally reported the mean value of the best β and its 
95% confidence interval (CI).

determination) between real and rewired networks. The statistical p-value was obtained across 
1000 permutations. The permutation test of Moran spectral randomization was based on 
BrainSpace toolbox.
