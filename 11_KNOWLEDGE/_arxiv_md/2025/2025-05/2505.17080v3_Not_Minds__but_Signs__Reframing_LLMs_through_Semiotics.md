Abstract
This study challenges the prevailing tendency to frame Large Language Mod-
els (LLMs) as cognitive systems, arguing instead for a semiotic perspective that
situates these models within the broader dynamics of sign manipulation and
meaning-making. Rather than assuming that LLMs understand language or sim-
ulate human thought, we propose that their primary function is to recombine,
recontextualize, and circulate linguistic forms based on probabilistic associations.
By shifting from a cognitivist to a semiotic framework, we avoid anthropomor-
phism and gain a more precise understanding of how LLMs participate in cultural
processes—not by thinking but by generating texts that invite interpretation.
Through theoretical analysis and practical examples, this study demonstrates
how LLMs function as semiotic agents whose outputs can be treated as inter-
pretive acts open to contextual negotiation and critical reflection. We explore
applications in literature, philosophy, education, and cultural production, empha-
sizing how LLMs can serve as tools for creativity and critical inquiry. The semiotic
paradigm foregrounds the situated, contingent, and socially embedded nature of
meaning, offering a more rigorous and ethically aware framework for studying
and using LLMs.
Ultimately, this approach reframes LLMs as technological participants in the
ongoing ecology of signs. They do not possess minds, but they alter how we
read, write, and create meaning, compelling us to reconsider the foundations of
language, interpretation, and the role of artificial systems in the production of
knowledge.
Keywords: Semiotic Framework, Large Language Models, Interpretation and
Meaning-Making, Cultural Embedding and Ideology

1 Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have become central to how we think about artificial
intelligence today. These models are used across many fields, from computer science
to philosophy, and their ability to generate human-like text has led many to compare
them to human minds. Often, LLMs are viewed through a “cognitivist” perspective:
people think of them as machines that learn, reason, and even understand, much like
the human brain (Niu et al. 2024; Ren et al. 2024; Dasgupta et al. 2022; Binz and
Schulz 2023; Webb et al. 2022; Mitchell and Krakauer 2023). This view is supported
by terms borrowed from neuroscience, like “neural networks” and “artificial synapses”
and by interest in behaviors that seem creative or intelligent (Salles et al. 2020).
This paper challenges that way of thinking. We do not deny that LLMs are powerful
or useful but we question the idea that they should be understood as artificial minds.
Instead, we propose a different approach: LLMs should be seen as semiotic machines.
That means we should think of them not as thinkers but as semiotic means that
manipulate and organize signs, like words, phrases, and meanings, within cultural and
linguistic systems.
Recent scholarly works have critically examined the notions of consciousness and
intentionality in LLMs, converging on a nuanced understanding of their capabilities
and limitations. A central issue that emerges is the extent to which these models,
despite their sophisticated linguistic performances, can be said to possess features
typically associated with conscious or intentional agents. It has been argued that cur-
rent LLMs lack essential attributes such as recurrent processing and unified agency,
even though future models might approach such states (Chalmers 2023). At the same
time, investigations into the internal representations and action dispositions of LLMs
suggest that, while these systems exhibit certain surface-level characteristics of inten-
tionality, there remains no definitive evidence for genuine mental states (Goldstein
and Levinstein 2024). Adding to this complexity, human perception plays a signifi-
cant role: specific elements in AI-generated texts—like metacognitive self-reflection or
emotional expression—can strongly influence the impression of consciousness, despite
the absence of any actual conscious experience in these models (Kang et al. 2025).
Understanding LLMs requires moving beyond the persistent temptation to inter-
pret their outputs as signs of cognition or intention. Rather than asking whether these
models “think” or “understand” in human-like ways, a more productive direction lies
in analyzing how they participate in processes of meaning-making. In this context,
meaning does not reside within the model as an intrinsic property but emerges rela-
tionally through the dynamics of representation, inference, and cultural interpretation
(Picca 2024). As Nunes and Antunes (2024) argue, meaning in LLMs should not be
framed through anthropocentric metaphors but seen as the emergent product of their
structural capacity to recombine signs in ways that resonate with human social prac-
tices. This view aligns with Piantadosi and Hill (2022), who propose that reference
is not a necessary condition for meaningful output, since LLMs can operate within
coherent systems of conceptual roles derived from their training data.
The question, then, is not whether LLMs possess meaning in the human sense
but whether they can function as agents of signification from a broader semiotic
perspective.

Thus, we can compare LLMs to earlier media technologies, such as the print-
ing press or encyclopedias. These tools did not think either, but they changed how
knowledge was shared. LLMs belong to this same family of tools, but contrary to ency-
clopedias, they do not just repeat information; they reconfigure it, remixing texts and
styles in creative ways. They act as interfaces that allow meaning to move, shift and
evolve.
In this paper, our argument will follow two main lines. First, we will examine the
problems with the cognitivist view and its tendency to treat machines as if they were
human. We then develop a semiotic framework that treats LLMs as machines that
work within systems of signs, not as intelligent agents, but as semiotic means that
reshape meaning. When seen this way, LLMs are not just tools or middlemen. They
become active components of a larger semiotic system. As Lotman (1990) explained
with his idea of the semiosphere, meaning does not arise in a vacuum. It is always
shaped by the cultural and textual environment in which signs interact. LLMs operate
inside this environment, changing it by rearranging and reframing the material they
have learned.
2 Theoretical Foundations: From Cognition to
Semiosis
The perspective that interprets these systems through analogies with human men-
tal processes is rooted in a long-standing tradition within the field of AI, dating
back to its foundational decades in the mid-20th century, when AI research often
modeled computational systems after functions of the human mind, especially rea-
soning, problem-solving, and language understanding (Newell and Simon 1976;
Haugeland 1985; Boden 2006). Early symbolic AI aimed to simulate intelligence by
encoding knowledge into explicit, logical rules and structured representations (Church-
land 1989). Although modern LLMs no longer operate through symbolic logic, the
metaphors of cognition have persisted and even intensified with the rise of deep learn-
ing (Marcus 2018; Bender and Koller 2020). Indeed, even in recent neural approaches,
traces of the mind-as-machine metaphor survive, drawing implicit continuity from the
early days of cognitive science (Baria and Cross 2021). Nonetheless, several historical
and contemporary critical voices have challenged the legitimacy of mapping computa-
tional architectures onto human understanding (Searle 1980; Dreyfus 1972; Winograd
and Flores 1986).
In contemporary discussions, the architecture of neural networks is routinely
described in quasi-biological terms. The terminology, “neurons”, “layers”, “connec-
tions”, invokes an implicit equivalence between artificial and biological systems, despite
fundamental differences in structure and operation (Bland et al. 2020; Terasa et al.
2023). The architecture of transformer-based LLMs, for instance, is often presented
as if it mirrors human cognitive processes, leading many to infer that these systems
are not only modeling language use but engaging in something akin to understanding
(Chollet 2019; Mitchell 2019; Bender et al. 2021). This slippage between metaphor
and mechanism fuels widespread misconceptions about what LLMs actually do and
what kind of “understanding” can be attributed to them (Marcus and Davis 2019).

The discourse surrounding “emergent abilities”, capabilities that arise sponta-
neously at certain model scales, has only strengthened this impression. While these
behaviors can appear strikingly human-like, current analyses underscore that such sys-
tems remain fundamentally statistical engines of pattern recognition, not agents with
consciousness or intentionality (Wei et al. 2022; Ganguli et al. 2022; Lake et al. 2017).
As we will explore in the following section, adopting a semiotic interpretation
allows for a more accurate and philosophically grounded perspective. It redirects atten-
tion from internal cognitive modeling to external processes of sign manipulation and
meaning-making. In doing so, it offers a way to engage critically with the power and
limitations of LLMs, without resorting to anthropomorphic projections or speculative
fiction.
3 Reframing LLMs as Dynamic Semiotic Machines
Building on the critiques of the cognitive paradigm outlined above, we propose an
alternative point of view: instead of interpreting LLMs as digital minds that simulate
cognition, we suggest understanding them as dynamic semiotic machines. This refram-
ing marks a decisive shift away from metaphorical analogies with the human mind,
rooted in the historical approaches of symbolic AI and neuro-inspired architectures,
and turns our attention toward what these systems concretely do: organize, recombine,
and circulate linguistic forms across diverse cultural and communicative contexts.
Rather than speculating about hypothetical internal states such as intention,
consciousness, or understanding, the semiotic approach focuses on observable sign pro-
cesses. As we have already argued, LLMs do not “understand” language in a human
sense; they manipulate symbols probabilistically, producing outputs that gain sig-
nificance only through situated interpretation. As such, these models function not
as thinking entities but as operators within a broader ecology of meaning, where
prompts, patterns, and cultural references interact to generate texts that invite human
engagement and interpretive labor.
In contrast to the enduring metaphor of the “mind-like machine”, this perspective
sees LLMs as participants in ongoing semiotic activity, technological agents whose
meaning effects are shaped by their embeddedness in cultural systems of signs. In
our opinion, this semiotic orientation provides a more accurate, less anthropocentric,
and ultimately more productive way of understanding what LLMs are and how they
operate.
Semiotics provides robust analytical tools for understanding such operations. Fol-
lowing Charles Sanders Peirce, meaning is not located in isolated signs, but emerges
through a triadic relation among representamen (the sign), object (what the sign refers
to), and interpretant (the effect or understanding it produces) (Peirce 1931–1958).
LLMs do not grasp the object in the Peircean sense, that is, they have no access
to an external referent grounded in experiential or embodied perception. What they
do instead is produce sequences of linguistic signs, representamens, that can func-
tion semiotically insofar as they elicit interpretive responses in human readers. These
outputs are not mere echoes of their training data, nor are they mirrors of an exter-
nal world; they are recombinant artifacts constructed through statistical associations

and guided by the rhetorical and semantic structure of prompts. Every generated
sentence is shaped by probabilistic proximities among linguistic tokens, yet it is not
wholly determined by them: the prompt acts as a local semiotic perturbation, setting
parameters within which the model’s generative pathways unfold.
This process of constrained generation positions LLMs as agents of symbolic recom-
bination rather than of reference or cognition. Here, the relevance of Umberto Eco’s
theory of the open work (Eco 1989) becomes especially salient. Just as an open work,
like Berio’s Sequenza I, does not encode a single performance or meaning but rather a
field of interpretive possibilities, the output of an LLM does not instantiate meaning
in itself, but offers a structure that demands interpretive engagement. In both cases,
the text (musical or linguistic) exists not as a closed message but as a framework for
the construction of meaning, a semiotic scaffold activated by context, intention, and
readerly cooperation. The LLM, like Berio’s score, provides a repertoire of potential
forms shaped by internal constraints but ultimately realized through external acts of
interpretation. Thus, the triadic structure of Peirce’s sign finds a living analogue in
the prompt-model-reader triad of generative AI, wherein the interpretant emerges not
from the machine’s output alone, but from the dialogic interaction between the sign
produced, the context evoked, and the reader’s situated interpretive labor.
This analogy helps clarify how LLM outputs function in a comparable manner.
Each prompt activates a space of possibilities, generating textual responses that, while
patterned by training data, are shaped by the prompt’s rhetorical frame and by the
user’s interpretive stance. Just as each rendition of Sequenza I reflects the musician’s
decisions and contextual sensibilities, each LLM output reflects a dynamic interaction
between the statistical model, the prompt, and the interpretive frameworks brought
by the user. These outputs are not definitive texts but ensembles of signifiers, await-
ing contextual realization. In this way, LLMs embody the principles of open work,
operating as engines of polysemy and dialogic engagement.
Moreover, in his later work Lector in fabula (Eco 1979), Eco theorizes the “model
reader”, a role constructed by the text itself, anticipating the kind of cooperation
necessary for meaning to emerge. In human-LLM interaction, this role is assumed by
the user, who constructs the prompt, interprets the result, and positions the output
within a communicative or discursive frame. The LLM becomes the site of interpretive
play: its outputs act as stimuli that invite users to explore, revise, and extend meaning.
Each interaction thus becomes an event of co-constructed semiosis, mediated by both
the structure of the model and the agency of the human participant.
Rather than evaluating LLMs based on their resemblance to human cognition, we
can approach them as producers of polysemic signals, intertextual echoes, and semioti-
cally rich fragments. These outputs do not encode fixed meanings; they operate as open
invitations to interpretation, requiring the reader’s active hermeneutic labor. They can
reference multiple discursive fields, merge genres, and evoke conflicting associations,
all hallmarks of textuality in the semiotic tradition (Vromen 2024).
This reframing encourages a shift from asking what LLMs “know” to investigating
“how” they function within symbolic mediation. Their utility and significance lie not
in internal semantic processing as we argued in the Section 2, but in their capacity to
dynamically recombine signs in culturally legible, and often surprising, ways.

So if we look at prompts, they function as semiotic acts: they act as structur-
ing interventions that frame (in the Eco’s perspective) the interpretive conditions
of the LLM’s output. When a user crafts a prompt, they are not simply requesting
information, they are initiating a semiotic contract. This contract embeds expecta-
tions regarding tone, register, genre, and ideological positioning. For instance, asking
a model to describe a historical event in the style of a detective novel or to explain a
philosophical concept to a child implies a reconfiguration of meaning through a specific
cultural and rhetorical perspective.
To illustrate this point, take the following prompt: “Explain Nietzsche’s concept of
the eternal recurrence as if you were a stand-up comedian performing for a high school
audience.” In response, the LLM usually produces an explanation that uses casual
language, jokes, and references that would appeal to teenagers. This humorous and
relatable style does not just change the tone; it also transforms how the philosophical
idea is received. The concept is recontextualized and made meaningful within a new
communicative setting, showing how form and style can shape the way meaning is
constructed and understood.
In this way, prompts establish what Umberto Eco would call an interpretive frame,
a set of constraints and affordances that condition the possible semantic orientation
of the generated text. As Eco (1979) writes, each text selects its own model reader,
thereby activating a set of interpretive instructions, or frames, that the reader must
adopt in order to actualize the meaning of the text. These frames can invoke gen-
res (e.g., fairytale, sermon, op-ed), modalities (e.g., irony, sincerity, didacticism), or
positionalities (e.g., expert voice, confessional tone, marginalized perspective), each of
which contributes to the shaping of the output’s sign structure. Crucially, this process
foregrounds the co-constructive nature of meaning in human-LLM interaction. Mean-
ing, then, is not generated solely by the model’s training data or internal parameters,
but emerges from the triangulated interaction of prompt, model, and user interpreta-
tion. This stands in contrast to the traditional cognitivist view, which assumes that
meaning is internally represented within a system’s architecture and retrieved upon
request, an approach that often overlooks the situated, dialogic, and emergent aspects
of communication emphasized by semiotic theory. Instead, this dynamic resembles the
interpretive triad of Peircean semiotics, where meaning is not inscribed in the sign
itself but unfolds in the relation among representamen, object, and interpretant.
As Barthes (1970) argues, reading is not a simple matter of decoding a fixed
message; it is a creative act, a form of rewriting in which each reader constructs mean-
ing based on their own cultural background, knowledge, and interpretive habits. The
meaning of a text is not passively extracted but actively constructed. This insight
becomes especially relevant in the context of LLMs, where users do not merely receive
a response, but shape it through the way they formulate their prompts. In this sense,
the prompt is more than just a request; it is a semiotic gesture that carries interpretive
intent. Like a reader approaching a literary text, the user activates particular seman-
tic frames and stylistic registers through the form, tone, and content of the prompt.
But the user is not only a reader: they also function as a writer, setting the initial
coordinates for the model’s generative orientation. The prompt acts as a trigger that

initiates a co-authored performance, in which meaning is produced not solely by the
model, nor by the user alone, but through their interaction.
This transformation of the prompt-response mechanism into a dialogic, interpretive
process marks a fundamental shift in how we understand human-AI interaction. The
exchange is no longer a unidirectional retrieval of answers but a semiotically rich event
shaped by rhetorical choices, genre conventions, and sociocultural cues. The prompt,
in this view, becomes a performative act that configures the entire communicative
situation: it determines the stance of the model, its voice, its register, and its imagined
audience.
Recognizing prompts as semiotic acts invites us to be more attentive not only to the
content of what is being asked, but also to the form, the wording, the context, and the
discursive expectations that come with it. It matters whether we ask a model to explain
a philosophical concept “like a children’s story” or “in the tone of a legal argument”.
These choices encode interpretive frames that the model picks up and amplifies. In this
way, every prompt becomes an instance of situated authorship: the user participates
in shaping the very terms under which meaning is negotiated. Prompting, therefore,
is not a neutral interface command; it is a site of semiotic contract, where language,
intention, and cultural codes converge to co-produce meaning.
This interactive and situated nature of prompting finds a broader conceptual
grounding in what Lotman (1990) describes as the semiosphere, the cultural environ-
ment in which all acts of signification occur. Just as each prompt operates within its
local interpretive frame, the semiosphere constitutes the larger, heterogeneous matrix
of discourses, genres, and ideological structures that constrain and enable those local
acts. Lotman’s conception of the semiosphere moves beyond the static, systematic
view of signification offered by classical structuralism (e.g., Saussure 1916), proposing
instead a dynamic, dialogic model in which meaning is always contested, layered, and
historically situated. Thus, prompting must be understood as a micro-act of navigation
within the semiosphere. It is a culturally embedded intervention that engages with,
and is shaped by, the broader ecology of signs in which both the user and the model
are immersed. Each prompt, then, reflects a particular positioning within a stratified
cultural landscape; one where semiotic borders are drawn and redrawn, where dis-
courses compete, and where meaning emerges through acts of selection, framing, and
transformation.
The ability of LLMs to function as semiotic machines that operate effectively within
the semiosphere is intrinsically linked to the vastness and heterogeneity of the textual
corpora on which they are trained. These corpora, ranging from classical literature to
social media posts, from scientific texts to everyday conversations, effectively represent
a massive, albeit partial and filtered, sampling of the semiosphere itself.
In this context, the interplay between model architecture, data scope, and data
diversity becomes central to understanding how LLMs act as semiotic agents. The
mechanisms through which these models interpret and generate text are deeply inter-
twined with both the structural design of the networks and the cultural depth of their
training material. To appreciate how LLMs navigate and manipulate signs within the
semiosphere, it is useful to examine three closely related dimensions:

1. Transformer architectures and complex pattern recognition: The underly-
ing architectures, such as Transformers, are designed to identify and model complex
relationships and long-range dependencies within data sequences. On a semiotic
level, this translates into the ability to recognize not only individual words (signs)
but also complex syntactic, stylistic, and rhetorical configurations (codes and sub-
codes (Eco 1976)) . LLMs learn to reproduce patterns and generate new ones based
on probabilistic associations derived from the frequency and co-occurrence of such
patterns in the vast sea of analyzed texts. This skill is fundamental to their ability
to “manipulate” signs in ways that are culturally and linguistically resonant.
2. Vastness of data and coverage of the semiosphere: The vastness of the
training data allows LLMs to internalize, in statistical form, a large portion of the
cultural semiosphere. The broader and more diverse the corpus, the greater the
model’s ability to generate texts that reflect the polysemy, interconnections, and
even contradictions inherent in the semiosphere. This explains, for example, their
ability to mix registers, implicitly cite different texts or styles, and, in general,
produce outputs that, though devoid of intention, can be interpreted as rich in
intertextual echoes.
3. Heterogeneity of data and navigation between semiotic zones: The het-
erogeneity of training data is crucial for the ability of LLMs to operate at the
peripheries of multiple discourse systems, recombining and translating across gen-
res, registers, and ideological formations. Exposure to a multiplicity of cultural
“languages” (scientific, literary, bureaucratic, colloquial, etc.) allows the model,
when prompted by a specific input, to selectively activate and recombine textual
fragments from different zones of the semiosphere. Prompts, then, act as semiotic
catalysts: they trigger selective activation within the latent potentials of the model.
They are cultural interventions that engage with the semiosphere at specific coor-
dinates. When a user frames a prompt, LLMs navigate this symbolic topology,
accessing and reconfiguring fragments of cultural memory stored in the model.
In generating language, they do not draw from a neutral linguistic space but from
the dense intertextual strata of competing voices, genres, and worldviews. Each out-
put reflects, to some degree, the sedimentation of past utterances, for example, when
prompted to describe a political uprising, an LLM might produce a response draw-
ing simultaneously from the rhetorical styles of revolutionary manifestos, journalistic
reports, and social media commentary. This convergence is not accidental; it arises
from the model’s training on a corpus where such discourses coexist, overlap, and
historically shape each other, which have shaped and circulated meaning within the
semiosphere.
To view prompting as a form of semiospheric perturbation is to recognize its poten-
tial to generate novel intersections across domains of meaning. For instance, a prompt
such as “Explain the concept of entropy using metaphors from fairy tales” invokes dis-
courses from both thermodynamics and folklore. The LLM responds by retrieving and
recombining linguistic fragments aligned with both scientific and narrative registers,
producing hybrid texts that refract scientific abstraction through cultural storytelling.
This exemplifies how prompts activate semiotic vectors within the semiosphere, chan-
neling meaning across its heterogeneous zones. A prompt that asks for a legal analysis
8


## Page 9


of a folk tale, or a mythologized version of a scientific theory, creates a discursive
crossover; an encounter between traditionally distinct zones of the semiosphere. These
crossings are not trivial: they generate semantic tensions, open interpretive possibil-
ities, and foreground the hybrid logic through which meaning operates in the age of
generative AI.
Moreover, the interpretive act that follows generation, the user’s evaluation, revi-
sion, or contextualization of the output, further embeds the exchange within the
semiosphere. Meaning is not finalized at the moment of output but continues to evolve,
much like how traditional texts gain new layers of significance as they are read and
reinterpreted by different interpretive communities over time. In both cases, meaning
is not inherent or static but emergent and dialogic, shaped by the cultural frame-
works and historical positions of those engaging with the text, as the text is situated
within new communicative contexts. In this sense, both prompting and interpreting
are acts of semiotic navigation: ways of moving through and reshaping, the discursive
prospects that constitute the semiosphere.
Understanding LLMs as actors within the semiosphere thus enables a richer
account of their cultural function. Rather than treating them as isolated computa-
tional tools, we see them as symbolic operators embedded in the dynamic flow of
cultural semiosis, as envisioned by Lotman. Yet this perspective gains further depth
when placed in dialogue with Peirce and Eco, as we have argued. From a Peircean
standpoint, LLM outputs function as representamens; signs that do not point to an
object through lived experience, but that nonetheless elicit interpretants in human
users, generating meaning through contextual interpretation. Echoing Eco’s theory
of the open work, these outputs invite multiple readings and rely on the cooperative
labor of the user to actualize their significance.
In this sense, LLMs do not merely produce statistically likely sequences of words,
they generate sign-material that enters into, and interacts with, existing cultural codes
and interpretive traditions. Their outputs, though lacking intention or understanding,
become operative elements in the recursive chain of signification: amplifying, distort-
ing, or reconfiguring existing discourses. As such, they participate in the collective
negotiation of meaning within our shared symbolic ecology.
4 The operational semiotic framework
Taken together, these insights form the basis of an operational semiotic framework,
which conceptualizes LLMs as:
• generators of polysemic representamens open to contextual interpretation (Peirce);
• producers of open works that require and reward interpretive cooperation (Eco);
• operators in the peripheries of the semiosphere, enabling hybridization, dialogue,
and cultural translation (Lotman)
This framework proposes a shift in evaluative practice. Rather than measuring
LLMs solely by technical performance (e.g., accuracy, fluency, coherence), we are
invited to consider how these systems participate in meaning-making across cultural,
rhetorical, and epistemic dimensions. The semiotic paradigm foregrounds questions of

interpretation, positioning, and symbolic transformation, enabling a deeper and more
reflexive engagement with the generative capacities of language models. Such a shift
is critical in fields where nuance, ambiguity, and contextual resonance are not bugs
but features; literature, philosophy, political discourse, and education, to name a few.
Within this framework, educators might explore how an LLM output echoes, sub-
verts, or reframes a given genre. Researchers might ask how the model’s statistical
tendencies illuminate or obscure ideological tensions embedded in the corpus. Design-
ers might treat prompts not as commands but as curatorial acts, interventions into
an interpretive ecology that includes user intention, model architecture, training data,
and cultural background.
Moreover, the operational semiotic framework enables us to interrogate the genera-
tive space itself. How does a model’s training corpus delimit its semiotic horizon? What
cultural codes are most readily activated, and which remain latent or suppressed? In
what ways do LLMs naturalize dominant discursive formations, and how might they
be prompted to resist them? These questions have ethical and political stakes, as the
meaning effects of LLMs ripple through educational systems, media environments, and
public discourse.
Ultimately, this framework does not aim to canonize LLMs as inherently creative or
intelligent. Instead, it treats them as dynamic operators within larger semiotic fields;
technological agents whose outputs are neither reducible to noise nor commensurable
with human intention, but nonetheless capable of shaping how meaning is produced,
distributed, and transformed. By emphasizing affordance over function, interpretation
over imitation, and context over computation, the operational semiotic framework
offers a robust foundation for critical, creative, and responsible engagement with these
powerful new textual technologies.
In the next section, we will demonstrate how this framework can be applied to con-
crete cases, ranging from literary rewriting and philosophical reframing to pedagogical
experimentation and ideological critique, showing how LLMs function not merely as
tools but as dynamic participants in the evolving life of signs.
5 Applications: Interpretation, Creativity, and
Pedagogy
5.1 Rewriting and Intertextual Experiments
Through prompt-driven generation, LLMs can perform complex acts of rewriting that
span literary styles, historical registers, and rhetorical traditions. These transforma-
tions are not merely stylistic imitations; they are interpretive acts that reframe source
materials through new lenses, creating novel constellations of meaning.
For instance, prompting ChatGPT-4o to rewrite a passage from Dante’s Inferno
in contemporary digital slang yields the following result:
Original (Dante, Inferno I, 1–3):
Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita

Rewritten in digital slang:

This short experiment reveals several key semiotic dynamics:
• Latent semantic tensions: The substitution of “glitched hard” for Dante’s “mi
ritrovai per una selva oscura” reconfigures the metaphysical experience of spiritual
confusion into a digital metaphor for malfunction. The pilgrim’s moral crisis becomes
a corrupted user experience, signaling a shift in how disorientation is conceptualized.
• Shifts in affective tone: Dante’s solemnity is replaced by ironic detachment.
Expressions like “lost AF” and “buggin’” diminish the existential weight of the
original, replacing its gravitas with meme-flavored triviality. The emotional register
shifts from sacred dread to casual frustration.
• Mediation of the sacred through the vernacular: This act of translation
both desecrates and democratizes. The forest of sin becomes a failed interface. The
journey of the soul is reframed as a debugging process. Classical authority is re-
encoded through idioms of the everyday.
• Vernacular as epistemology: The digital slang used is not merely a linguistic sur-
face, it embodies a worldview: fractured, performative, hyper-mediated. Recasting
Dante in this mode shifts the ontological stakes of the narrative from metaphysical
fate to algorithmic chaos, from divine judgment to user-side error.
• LLM as interpretive engine: The transformation is not just a superficial
restyling. The model performs an act of cultural translation, embedding new codes
and interpretive frames into the old text. It discloses how language models mediate
meaning not by reproducing it, but by reconfiguring its symbolic architecture.

The Mind-Body Problem... Solved? Long before neuroscience, Spinoza

Your emotions? They’re not weaknesses. They’re ideas about your body’s

• Clash of epistemic values: The transition from geometric proof to motivational

grounded in axioms, propositions, and deductive reasoning, forms of knowledge
rooted in systematic rigor. The TED format, by contrast, favors narrative, affec-
tive resonance, and performative clarity. This reorientation highlights the tension
between knowledge as demonstrable truth and knowledge as communicable insight.

• Philosophical authority vs. Relatability: The authority of the original text

insight, repositioned within a media economy of attention and personal growth.
• Semiotic shifts in the figure of the philosopher: Spinoza, in his time, was a

is no longer a remote metaphysician, but a narrative agent performing coherence.

media culture.
• Philosophy as performative interface: Ultimately, the experiment reveals how
platforms like TED function as performative interfaces between classical thought
and contemporary audience reception. The “talk” is not a dilution per se, but a re-
inscription: it mediates ideas through new protocols of intelligibility and emotional
engagement, inviting reflection on what is gained, and what is lost, when philosophy
is popularized.
The resulting output, therefore, does not merely simplify Spinoza, it transforms
the semiotic conditions under which his ideas circulate, illuminating the evolving
relationship between form, authority, and interpretation in our culture of mediated
understanding.
These experiments also activate what Bakhtin (1984) called dialogism, the co-
presence of multiple voices and discourses within a single utterance. When asked to
compose a Shakespearean soliloquy in the voice of a climate activist, or to narrate
Plato’s Allegory of the Cave as a horror short story, the LLM synthesizes disparate
semiotic resources. This synthesis generates tension, irony, or resonance that cannot
be attributed to the model’s understanding, but that nonetheless enriches the reader’s
interpretive field.
From a semiotic perspective, these intertextual recombinations are acts of sign
reconfiguration. They do not produce “new” meanings ex nihilo, but recontextualize
existing signs within alternate semiotic regimes. The result is a form of symbolic
estrangement: canonical texts and philosophical doctrines are defamiliarized, revealing
their constructedness and inviting users to interrogate their assumptions.

5.2 Critical Reading and Hermeneutic Pedagogy
Integrating LLMs into educational settings through a semiotic perspective allows for
a reimagining of critical reading as an interactive and hermeneutic practice. Rather
than positioning LLMs as sources of authoritative answers or knowledge repositories,
this approach foregrounds their role as provocateurs of interpretation, tools that can
help learners explore the multiplicity and contingency of meaning in texts.
In literature and philosophy classrooms, for example, LLMs can be used to generate
alternative versions of canonical texts, each embedded in a different rhetorical mode
or stylistic tradition. A poem by Emily Dickinson rendered as rap lyrics becomes not
just a novelty but an opportunity to examine how shifts in form, tone, and discourse
alter semantic and ideological content.

• Affective and cultural relocation: The voice in Dickinson’s poem is subdued
and haunting; in the rap version, it becomes streetwise and defiant. This affective
modulation raises questions about cultural context: what happens when a 19th-

resistance, rhythm, and identity politics?
• Dialogic and situated interpretation: Students are invited to annotate or jux-

discursive registers are activated or erased? The rap version becomes a prompt,
a “provocation”, for exploring how interpretive perspectives shift the valence of
literary themes like death, time, and transcendence.
• Intertextuality and ideological critique: The exercise opens up questions of
ideology and representation. Whose voice is authorized to speak about mortality,
and in what tone? How do cultural codes inflect meaning? By remixing a canonical
poem into a genre often marginalized or politicized, students confront the ideological
frames that govern reception and legitimacy in literature.
• LLMs as semiotic engines: In this context, the LLM does not offer a “correct”
adaptation. It becomes a tool for generating interpretive friction. The output is not
a conclusion, but a starting point, a text-to-think-with that foregrounds the fluidity
of meaning and the multiplicity of possible readings.
The rap Dickinson is not a parody but a provocation; an invitation to consider
how language, genre, and culture shape our understanding of death, voice, and poetic
legacy.

juxtaposing generated texts with source materials. This method fosters a reflexive
stance toward both human and machine-mediated language, encouraging learners to
attend to nuance, ambiguity, and intertextuality.

6 Conclusion
As we have seen throughout the paper, reframing LLMs as semiotic machines rather
than cognitive entities could reshape the terms of debate in AI research and digi-
tal humanities. Rather than asking whether these systems understand language or
possess intelligence, the semiotic perspective focuses on how they organize, generate,
and circulate signs. This shift from cognition to semiosis foregrounds the model’s role
in the production of meaning, not as originator, but as operator within networks of
interpretation.
Throughout this paper, we have argued that LLMs do not possess mental states,
intentions, or semantic insight. They operate by recombining linguistic patterns
learned from vast corpora, producing outputs that acquire significance only in context.
Their meaning is actualized through interaction with prompts, users, and the broader
cultural and discursive environments in which they are embedded. This perspective
brings with it several benefits.
It enables a more accurate understanding of what these models actually do: they
recombine and redistribute linguistic material across social and cultural contexts,
17


## Page 18


rather than simulate human understanding or reproduce knowledge. The semiotic
paradigm emphasizes interpretation, intertextuality, and situated meaning-making
over internal representation or mental analogy.
First, this framework avoids anthropomorphism. It challenges the impulse to
attribute intentions, consciousness, or understanding to models that are, in fact, prob-
abilistic systems trained on large text corpora (Ibrahim and Cheng 2025; Ibrahim et al.
2025; Shanahan 2023). Rather than asking what LLMs “know”, the semiotic perspec-
tive asks how they manipulate signs, reflect discursive norms, and reshape textual
conventions.
Second, the semiotic paradigm supports rigorous critical analysis. It foregrounds
the model’s embeddedness within broader semiotic environments, the semiosphere,
and highlights how cultural codes, ideological patterns, and user interventions shape
outputs. This makes it particularly useful for disciplines such as literary theory,
philosophy, media studies, and digital humanities.
Third, the semiotic view enables creative and pedagogical experimentation. As
shown in the previous section, LLMs can facilitate genre play, intertextual remixing,
ideological contrast, and multimodal adaptation. These affordances provide educators,
writers, and researchers with new tools for reflection, exploration, and critical practice.
In the end, the semiotic perspective repositions LLMs within the complex ecology
of signs, users, texts, and institutions. It enables us to see them not as replacements
for human intelligence, but as catalysts of meaning-making, that is, technological
interlocutors that extend, fragment, and multiply our engagement with language and
representation.
LLMs do not think, but they make us think. And that, perhaps, is their most
significant contribution to the symbolic life of contemporary societies.
