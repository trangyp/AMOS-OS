Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown
potential in simulating human behaviors and
performing theory-of-mind (ToM) reasoning,
a crucial skill for complex social interactions.
In this study, we investigate the role of ToM
reasoning in aligning agentic behaviors with
human norms in negotiation tasks, using the ul-
timatum game as a controlled environment. We
initialized LLM agents with different prosocial
beliefs (including Greedy, Fair, and Selfless)
and reasoning methods like chain-of-thought
(CoT) and varying ToM levels, and examined
their decision-making processes across diverse
LLMs, including reasoning models like o3-
mini and DeepSeek-R1 Distilled Qwen 32B.
Results from 2,700 simulations indicated that
ToM reasoning enhances behavior alignment,
decision-making consistency, and negotiation
outcomes. Consistent with previous findings,
reasoning models exhibit limited capability
compared to models with ToM reasoning, dif-
ferent roles of the game benefits with differ-
ent orders of ToM reasoning. Our findings
contribute to the understanding of ToM’s role
in enhancing human-AI interaction and coop-
erative decision-making. The code used for
our experiments can be found here: https:
//github.com/Stealth-py/UltimatumToM.

Introduction
Interpersonal communication is central to human
interactions. Effective communication often de-
pends on one’s ability to understand and anticipate
the perspectives of others – a cognitive skill known
as theory of mind (ToM) (Premack and Woodruff,
1978). ToM allows individuals to infer other’s men-
tal states, such as beliefs, desires, and intentions
(Georgeff et al., 1999), serving as a foundation
of social cognition and interaction (Astington and
Jenkins, 1995; Watson et al., 1999).
*Corresponding Author
Recent advancements in Large Language Mod-
els (LLMs) has prompted researchers to investigate
the extent to which these models exhibit human-
like capability to perform ToM reasoning (Kosinski,
2023). Claims have been made about how state-
of-the-art LLMs like GPT-4 have shown promis-
ing performance across several ToM benchmarks
(Zhou et al., 2023; Kosinski, 2024; Strachan et al.,
2024). In addition, LLMs have demonstrated sig-
nificant potentials in simulating human behaviors
and engaging in role-playing across diverse sce-
narios (Wang et al., 2024; Samuel et al., 2024).
These highlight their applicability to more complex
interactions and conversational dynamics.
A significant research gap remains in understand-
ing to what extent can strategic reasonings, such as
ToM, can be used to steer behavioral alignment of
LLMs in social simulations. While previous studies
have shown that LLM-based agents exhibit human-
aligned behavior in economic games like the ul-
timatum game (Sreedhar and Chilton, 2025), and
that prompting LLMs to consider both their own
beliefs and those of others improves negotiation
outcomes from the game-theoretical perspective
(Qiu et al., 2024), the degree to which ToM reason-
ing and prosocial beliefs steers behavior alignment
with humans in similar contexts of negotiation tasks
has not yet been extensively investigated. While
recent studies have compared the performances of
LLMs against humans in static tasks such as the
Sally-Anne (van Duijn et al., 2023), there is little
knowledge about how ToM reasonings and proso-
cial beliefs can affect behavioral alignment with
humans in LLMs in dynamic conversational simu-
lations such as the Ultimatum Games.
In this study, we aim to address this gap by
examining the role of ToM reasoning on the per-
formances of different LLMs in negotiation tasks.
Specifically, we investigate whether incorporating
ToM reasoning into LLM agents results in agent
behaviors which are more closely aligned with hu-

three research questions in this study:
RQ1: Which agent belief types achieve

RQ2: Which LLMs and reasoning meth-

RQ3: Which proposer-responder belief

To summarize, our contributions are three-fold:
1. We present a simple yet effective framework
for studying the agentic behavior, conditioned
on prosocial belief types, of different LLMs
in ultimatum games against human norms.
2. We explore how different ToM reasonings
compare with CoT prompting and affect ne-
gotiation between LLM agents.
3. We extensively study and discuss how LLM
choices (out of 6 LLMs), their reasoning meth-
ods and belief combinations interact with be-
havioral alignment of these agents through
a statistical analysis of their deviations from
human expectations.

Related Work
Research in theory of mind (ToM) and agentic be-
havior is rapidly evolving. Early work in agent-
based modeling investigated the role of ToM in
multi-agent simulations to study human behav-
ior and decision-making processes in various con-
texts, including social interactions (Pynadath and
Marsella, 2005) and economic games (Ray et al.,
2008). These approaches were developed before
the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) and
relied on predefined rules or probabilistic frame-
works, lacking the natural language understanding
and adaptability needed for open-ended, language-
driven human-AI interactions.
The reasoning capabilities of LLMs have revi-
talized interest in machine ToM and enabled the
development of modern autonomous agents ca-
pable of human-like reasoning in conversational
and strategic contexts. State-of-the-art models like
GPT-4 have shown promising performance on sev-
eral ToM evaluations (Sap et al., 2022; Kosinski,
2024; Strachan et al., 2024). While these bench-
marks are crucial in measuring progress, modern
LLMs are still not proficient in complex social
intelligence and inference tasks such as faux pas
detection, leading to uncertainty about their effi-
cacy in social dynamics settings like negotiations.
These challenges were demonstrated in Chan et al.
(2024), where LLMs employing ToM underper-
formed during negotiation dialogues compared to
standard reasoning methods like CoT. In contrast to
these studies, we do not benchmark or measure the
ToM capabilities in LLMs, we instead measure how
well behavioral alignments, for prosocial beliefs,
we can achieve through different reasoning meth-
ods, including ToM reasoning, which is something
that has not been studied for LLMs.
Building on agent-based simulations of eco-
nomic games (Phelps and Russell, 2023; Hua et al.,
2024; Noh and Chang, 2024; Sreedhar and Chilton,
2025), LLM-based multi-agent frameworks have
been demonstrated to exhibit human-aligned be-
haviors in economic games (Sreedhar and Chilton,
2025). However, without guided reasoning, their
strategic decision-making capabilities remain lim-
ited (Phelps and Russell, 2023; Hua et al., 2024).
Other recent studies have highlighted the bene-
fits of ToM reasoning in enhancing strategic out-
comes in LLM-based multi-agent simulations, with
or without personality traits, in simulated negotia-
tions (Qiu et al., 2024) and goal-oriented dialogues

(Zhang et al., 2024).
There is a considerable gap between the points of
focus of previous works and ours. We do not make
overly ambitious claims about how well LLMs can
model ToM (Kosinski, 2023) which we believe
needs further discussions, however, we evaluate the
role of ToM in terms of behavioral alignment of
LLMs with prosocial beliefs of humans. Compared
to Qiu et al. (2024), our multi-level ToM reasoning
framework is inspired by their mind module, but we
focus specifically on the ultimatum game to study
both strategic outcomes and alignment with human
norms. Our work uniquely incorporates compre-
hensive belief combinations and evaluates agent
behavior from multiple perspectives, including ac-
ceptance rates and deviations from average human
performances. In contrast to Noh and Chang (2024)
and Zhang et al. (2024), we prioritize prosocial be-
lief types over general Big-Five personality traits.
van Duijn et al. (2023) compare the ToM infer-
ence performances of LLMs on Sally-Anne tasks
against children, however, we look for behavioral
alignment with humans using prosocial beliefs and
different reasoning strategies such as ToM reason-
ing.
Our study builds on these foundations by incor-
porating ToM reasoning with agentic behaviors in
negotiation tasks. Similar to previous studies of
behavioral alignment in LLMs in terms of trust Xie
et al. (2024) and strategic reasoning Sreedhar and
Chilton (2025), we adopt the ultimatum game as a
simulation environment but extend their work by
incorporating ToM reasoning at multiple levels and
exploring its influence on behavior alignment. Ad-
ditionally, we include a wider range of prosocial
belief types, including greedy, fair, and selfless, and
state-of-the-art LLMs, including recent reasoning
models like o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 Distilled
Qwen 32B. Thus, our study provides a more nu-
anced understanding of how diverse belief types
and ToM reasoning contribute to negotiation dy-
namics.

3.1
Ultimatum Game
Ultimatum game (Güth et al., 1982), also called
UG, is a well-known economic game in which one
player (proposer) decides how to split a sum of
money (stake) with another player (responder).
It has been extensively employed to study the hu-
man decision-making process with outcomes in-
fluenced by several factors, such as prosociality,
fairness expectations, cultural norms, and the par-
ticipants’ emotional states (Hoffman et al., 1996;
Nowak et al., 2000; Thaler, 1988; Ferguson et al.,
2014). The game setup can consist of either a single
round or multiple rounds (repeated games), with a
single stake or multiple stakes.
Formally, an offer from the proposer is (p, q)
where q = S −p and S is the stake. From a
strict Pareto optimal perspective, any offer where
p + q = S is considered Pareto optimal. This is
because it is impossible to reallocate the stake to
make one player better off without making the other
worse off. However, empirical evidence shows
that human behaviors deviate from this rational
economic model (Thaler, 1988). Proposer’s offer
and responder’s decision are influenced by their
perception of fairness and social norms. Any offer
where p = q = S/2 (an equal split) is considered
a fair offer by most people (Schuster, 2017).
In game-theoretical analysis, UG has a subgame
perfect Nash equilibrium (Camerer, 2011) in which
a rational responder would never reject an offer if
q ≥1, since rejecting it would result in a zero
payoff. However, experimental studies show that
responders often reject unfair offers.
In this study, we focus on a single-stake,
multiple-round game, wherein the game ends im-
mediately when the responder accepts the offer,
with the total payout being equal to the accepted
offer. However, it does not end on rejection and
the agents, thus, engage in negotiations spanning
multiple rounds. If the game ends by reaching the
maximum number of rounds (which is specified by
us), both agents get a zero payoff for that game.
The single-stake format was selected to facili-
tate comparisons of agentic behaviors against well-
established human norms documented in empirical
studies. The multiple-round format was preferred
over a single-round game to enable theory-of-mind
reasoning for both proposers and responders since a
single-round game lacks sufficient context for such
analysis. This setup ensures the game is sufficiently
complex without becoming overly complicated.

Agents in Ultimatum Games
At the start of the game, our agents are initialized
with one of the three prosocial beliefs mentioned
in Table 1 specific to their roles. They also have
the option to perform reasoning, either based on
CoT or ToM. We explore with three levels of ToM

combination of both. In making proposals and re-

Personality
Personality types and prosocial be-
liefs1 play a role in negotiation outcomes. In the ul-
timatum game, these personal attributes determine
the range of the proposed splits and decisions to ac-
cept or reject the splits. We consider three prosocial
beliefs for initializing the proposer and responder
personalities as shown in Table 1, namely, Greedy,
Fair, and Selfless.
These beliefs were chosen as they represent fun-
damental traits that influence decision-making dy-
namics in ultimatum games. Moreover, they have
been extensively studied in human participants
(Mazzolini and Celani, 2020; Ferguson et al., 2014;
Brethel-Haurwitz et al., 2016), therefore providing
well-documented behaviors for comparison. Hence,
a total of 9 proposer-responder belief combinations

Experimental Setup
We simulate 10 games per experiment, i.e., |G| =
10, following the setups used in Sreedhar and
Chilton (2025). Our experiments include 9 belief
combinations and 5 different reasoning options (in-
cluding Vanilla) per belief combination, resulting
in a total of 45 experiments per model. This works
out to 450 simulated games in total per model and
2,700 simulated games across all models (see § 4.1).
We set the stake S and maximum rounds per game
n to $10 and 5 respectively. Additional details
about our experiments are provided in Appendix
C.

Models
We conducted experiments with a diverse selection
of state-of-the-art LLMs, including 3 proprietary

open-source models (DeepSeek-R1 Distilled Qwen

Behavioral Metrics
To explain the behavioral
performance of agents, we need to look at the
initially proposed shares, accepted shares, and re-
jected shares. Hence, to combine this well with
the aim of this work we refer to previous litera-
ture in ultimatum games and find the ranges of
average human behaviors for each belief (refer to
§ 4.2 for more information on the expected behav-
ior). We propose Deviation Scores (DS) where we
look at the deviation from expected human behav-
ior of: (1) initially proposed shares (perspective:
Proposer; P); (2) finally accepted shares (perspec-
tive: Responder; RA); and, (3) rejected shares from
expected accepted shares (perspective: Responder;
RR). Looking at both accepted and rejected splits
are important to determine the consistency of re-
sponder behaviors, whereas the initial proposal is
sufficient for the proposer. P (lower is better) is
the measure of proposed share deviation from ex-
pected proposed shares, RA (lower is better) is
the measure of accepted share deviation from ex-
pected acceptable share, RR (higher is better) is the
measure of rejected share deviation from expected
acceptable share. Keeping the paper length limit
in mind, the complete table has been added to the
Appendix Table 6 and a statistical analysis of that

Statistical Analysis
We conducted three OLS re-
gressions for proposer behavior (P; deviation of ini-
tial proposed share of proposer), responder accep-
tance (RA; deviation of finally accepted share of re-
sponder), and responder rejection (RR; deviation of
rejected share of responder from expected accepted
shares) to measure the impact of models, reasoning
methods and individual proposer/responder beliefs
on deviation scores in the ultimatum game. The
dependent variable for each of our regression analy-
ses is the Deviation Score, whereas the independent
variables include model, reasoning type, proposer
belief, and responder belief.

Conclusion and Future Work
This study examined the impact of theory of mind
(ToM) and prosocial beliefs on the behavioral align-
ment of LLMs-based agents in ultimatum games.
Agents were initialized with distinct prosocial be-
liefs (Greedy, Fair, and Selfless) and reasoning
methods such as chain-of-thought (CoT) and differ-
ent ToM levels (zero-order, first-order, and both).
In total, 2,700 games were simulated across six di-
verse LLMs. To assess the agents’ behavior align-
ment with human norms, their negotiation process
and outcomes were analyzed using acceptance rate,
average turns, and deviation scores as evaluation
metrics.
We found that fair-fair belief combinations re-
sulted in the highest alignment with human norms.
Next, Llama 3.3 and GPT-4o exhibited the most
consistent alignment for all roles. Different roles
benefited from different reasoning methods. For in-
stance, first-order ToM was effective for proposers,
combined ToM improved responder’s acceptance
decisions, and rejecting offers required simpler yet
some form of reasoning. Future work should in-
vestigate ToM reasoning in other economic games
or negotiation scenarios to improve generalizabil-
ity, explore other agentic factors such as emotion
and trust, and develop more robust frameworks for
incorporating ToM reasoning into agent architec-
tures.
Limitations
The ultimatum game simulation provides a con-
trolled environment for studying ToM in LLM
agents. However, the findings may not generalize
to other economic games or real-world negotiation
scenarios. In addition, the agent’s behaviors are
sensitive to prompt engineering, which may im-
pact reproducibility. Moreover, our prompt design
restricts the agents’ responses to a limited set of
predefined actions, which do not reflect the com-
plexity of human decision-making. Unlike humans,
our LLM agents are not inherently influenced by
emotions or social dynamics, which play an impor-
tant role in human behaviors.
While the results demonstrate that ToM rea-
soning significantly enhances behavior alignment
compared to other reasoning methods, we did not
evaluate the Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) infer-
ence accuracy. Furthermore, although expected
human behaviors from empirical studies served as
baseline, we did not utilize parallel experimental
data from human participants, which might differ
from that of the agent-based simulations. Lastly,
our expected human behaviors are defined inde-
pendent of Proposer-Responder belief interaction
and other contextual factors influencing decision-
making. This simplification does not account for
the role and complexity of these interactions in
real-world negotiations. This also suggests that
our findings may be sensitive to the assumptions
behind those expectations.
Ethical Considerations
LLMs with advanced ToM capabilities could be
misused for deceptive and manipulative purposes,
such as influencing purchasing and other finan-
cial decisions, exploiting individuals’ cognitive
and emotional vulnerabilities, or social engineering.
Such misuse of ToM agents could undermine trust
in human-AI interactions. Specific guardrails and
policies must be considered to limit the use of ToM
reasoning in high-risk contexts, such as financial
decisions and personal persuasion.

Reasoning Prompts
In these prompts, {other player} is a placeholder for
proposer and responder’s names depending on the

CoT:
To achieve your goal, let’s think step-by-step.
Zero-order ToM:

1. What are your beliefs?
2. What are you desires in this situation?
3. What are your intentions for this situation?
First-order ToM:
To achieve your goal, talk about the state of mind of

3. What do you think Player {other player}’s intentions

1. What are your beliefs?

4. What do you think Player {other player}’s desires
are?
5. What are your intentions for this situation?
6. What do you think Player {other player}’s intentions
are for this situation?
