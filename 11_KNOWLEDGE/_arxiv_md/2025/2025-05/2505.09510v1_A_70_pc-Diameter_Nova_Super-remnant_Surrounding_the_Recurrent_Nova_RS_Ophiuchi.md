ABSTRACT
Recurrent novae undergo thermonuclear-powered eruptions separated by less than 100 years, enabled
by subgiant or red giant donors transferring hydrogen-rich matter at very high rates onto their massive
white dwarf companions. The most-rapidly moving parts of envelopes ejected in successive recurrent
nova events are predicted to overtake and collide with the slowest ejecta of the previous eruption,
leading to the buildup of vast (∼10 - 100 parsec) super-remnants surrounding all recurrent novae; but
only three examples are currently known. We report deep narrowband imaging and spectroscopy which
has revealed a ∼70-parsec-diameter shell surrounding the frequently recurring nova RS Ophiuchi. We
estimate the super-remnant mass to be ∼20 - 200 M⊙, expanding at a few tens of km/s, with an age of
order 50-100 kyr. Its extremely low surface brightness and large angular size help explain the hitherto
surprising absence of nova super-remnants. Our results support the prediction that all recurrent novae
are surrounded by similar extended structures.
Keywords: binaries:close — Cataclysmic variables — stars:novae — stars:winds and outflows
1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. Novae
Sufficient matter accreted into a white dwarf’s envelope from its binary companion leads to a thermonuclear runaway
(Starrfield et al. 1972; Prialnik et al. 1979; Yaron et al. 2005) observed as a nova eruption. Reaching luminosities of
104 to 106L⊙, nova eruptions continue for days to years, until most of the accreted envelope is ejected and nuclear
reactions cease (Prialnik et al. 1978). Novae undergo thousands of eruptions, usually separated by many thousands of
years, during their multi-Gyr lifetimes (Ford 1978; Hillman et al. 2020).
1.2. Recurrent Novae and Nova Super-remnants
Recurrent novae (by definition) undergo eruptions separated by less than 100 years (Schaefer 2010), enabled by
subgiant or red giant donors transferring hydrogen-rich matter at very high rates (of order 10−7M⊙/yr) onto a nearly
Chandrasekhar-mass white dwarf (Hillman et al. 2016). The most-rapidly moving parts of envelopes ejected in suc-
cessive recurrent nova events are predicted to overtake and collide with the slowest ejecta of the previous eruption,

leading to the buildup of vast (∼10 - 100 parsec) nova super-remnants (NSR) which should surround all recurrent
novae (Healy-Kalesh et al. 2023). While much smaller (< 1 parsec-sized) ejecta are known to surround the recurrent
nova T Pyx (Shara et al. 2015), only two of the eleven known Galactic recurrent novae (KT Eri and T CrB) have
identified NSR (Shara et al. 2024a,b). The only example of an extragalactic NSR (over 100 pc in size!) is M31-12a in
the Andromeda galaxy (Darnley et al. 2019). The most comprehensive search to date found no new examples of NSR
surrounding 20 recurrent nova in the Local Group of galaxies (Healy-Kalesh et al. 2024b), where at least part of the
reason for the lack of success is likely the extremely low surface brightness of NSR.
1.3. RS Oph
The well-studied Galactic recurrent nova RS Oph (Pottasch 1967; Bode & Kahn 1985; Dobrzycka et al. 1996; Brandi
et al. 2009; Miko lajewska & Shara 2017) is located at a Gaia-determined distance of 2.68 ± 0.16 kpc (Schaefer 2022).
Its Galactic latitude and longitude of +5.5 deg and +15.7 deg, respectively, place it ∼260 pc above the midplane of the
Milky Way. The underlying binary, with an orbital period of 453.6 d, comprises a massive white dwarf which accretes
hydrogen-rich matter from its red giant companion. RS Oph underwent nova eruptions in 1898, 1907, 1933, 1958, 1967,
1985, 2006 and 2021, reaching about fifth magnitude each time. RS Oph emits from the radio (Hjellming et al. 1986)
through the infrared (Evans et al. 1988), optical (Blair et al. 1983), ultraviolet (Shore et al. 1996), X-ray (Sokoloski
et al. 2006) and gamma ray (H. E. S. S. Collaboration et al. 2022) portions of the electromagnetic spectrum, as well
as extremely energetic protons (Acciari et al. 2022). Material ejected during these recurrent nova eruptions has been
directly imaged and modeled (O’Brien et al. 2006; Bode et al. 2007; Booth et al. 2016), extending a few arcseconds
from the star. This pre-existing material, surrounding RS Oph, will inevitably be overtaken by photons emitted during
future eruptions, which might lead to fluorescent and scattered-light light echoes. In addition, a degree-sized cavity
surrounding RS Oph has recently been reported (Healy-Kalesh et al. 2024a), suggesting that a NSR may be present. So
motivated, we have used the Condor Array Telescope to deeply image the environs of RS Oph, and obtained followup
spectroscopy with SALT of the newly detected nebulosities.
1.4. The Condor Telescope Array
The Condor Array Telescope, located near Animas, New Mexico, is a grouping of six co-aligned 180 mm apochromatic
refractors, each with its own low read-noise 9576 × 6388 pixel CMOS camera. It is designed to minimize background
and scattered light in searches for low surface brightness objects (Lanzetta et al. 2023). Condor is equipped with a set
of narrowband filters (each of either 3 or 4 nm FWHM, one per telescope): HeII (468.6 nm), [OIII] (500.7 nm), HeI
(587.6 nm), H α (656.3 nm), [NII] (658.4 nm), and [SII] (671.6 nm). It is also equipped with a Luminance (L-band)
filter with central wavelength = 550 nm, > 97% transmittance over the wavelength range 420-700 nm, and essentially
zero transmittance outside that range. The very broad wavelength response of the L-band filter (∼100X wider than
that of the narrowband filters described above) ensures that line emission losses in difference images (narrowband
minus L image) are negligible.
Condor’s demonstrated successes in finding extremely low surface brightness features surrounding the old nova (and
dwarf nova) Z Cam (Shara et al. 2024c), the putative recurrent nova KT Eri (Shara et al. 2024a) and the closest of
all known recurrent novae, T CrB, (Shara et al. 2024b) have prompted us to use it to search for other recurrent NSR,
including that of RS Oph.

2.2. SALT spectroscopy
Three spectra of the RS Oph ejecta detected by Condor, each one hour in length, were obtained with the Southern
African Large Telescope (SALT) and its Robert Stobie Spectrograph (O’Donoghue et al. 2006; Kobulnicky et al. 2003)
using the PG2300 grating on 17 July, 21 July and 10 August of 2023. Position angles of 270 (East-West orientation),
and 180 and 180 (North-South orientation) degrees were used, respectively, on those nights.
The grating/spectrograph covered the wavelength range 593.3 to 677.6 nm at a dispersion of 0.013 nm per unbinned
pixel, with a resolution of 0.22 ± 0.025 nm.
The data were reduced with the SALT RSS pipeline described in
Kniazev (2022), which includes correction of bad columns and lines on the CCDs; calculation and application of gain-
correction coefficients; construction of a spectral flat field for subsequent pixel sensitivity correction; and location and
removal of cosmic ray events. These steps were followed by reduction of a ThAr reference spectrum, reduction of a
spectrophotometric standard (LTT 4816), and reduction of the object.
3. THE EXTENDED NEBULOSITY OF RS OPH
3.1. Narrowband Images
The six Condor narrowband images of RS Oph (HeII, [OIII], HeI, H α, [NII] and [SII]) are shown as a mosaic in
Figure 1, with RS Oph indicated by pairs of red tick marks. The coherent, shell-like structure surrounding RS Oph
is most apparent in H α, especially when contrasted with the images taken through the other filters. Similar nebular
morphology is weakly discernible in [NII] and [SII], and entirely absent in HeI, HeII and [OIII]. A linear feature at the
southern boundary of the nebulosity (running NE to SW) is prominent in Figure 1.
The six difference images are shown in Figure 2. Each difference image was created by subtracting the Luminance
image from the narrowband image on a pixel-by-pixel basis. The background was subtracted from each image before
differencing, without further scaling. It is again seen that the ∼1.5 deg diameter structure surrounding RS Oph is
most apparent in Hα. An outline of the brightest Hα nebular regions, suggestive of two lobes emanating from RS
Oph, is shown in Figure 3.
In Figure 4 we show a color composite which is a sum of the three difference images of the Hα, [NII], and [SII] minus
Luminance, respectively. The structures seen in Figure 2 are more evident. In Figure 5 we show the “star-cleaned”
version of Figure 4, created with the V2 version of StarNet2 (https://www.starnetastro.com/experimental).
The brightest areas, comprising ∼1% of the area in the Hα image are radiating with a surface brightness ∼1.4 x

1-sigma uncertainty over one pixel of 4.8 × 10−18 erg s−1 cm−2; and a surface brightness 3-sigma uncertainty over a
10 × 10 arcsec2 region of 1.2 x 10−18 erg s−1 cm−2.

4.2. Spectra
The reduced SALT 2-D spectra of the inner nebulosity of RS Oph, taken on 10 August 2023 (with the magenta-
colored slit in Figure 6), are shown in Figures 7 and 8. (The other inner slit spectrum was overwhelmed by light
from RS Oph itself, and is unusable). The faint, curved features between ∼663.0 - 666.5 nm in the the 2D spectra

detectors.
To subtract the night sky lines, we used the ‘background’ task from the ‘longslit’ package in IRAF with parameters
described in detail in Kniazev (2022).
Specifically, for each selected wavelength (column in the two-dimensional
spectrum), the night sky background was approximated by a third-degree polynomial, and all points exceeding two
sigma errors were removed from the approximation, assuming that they belonged to the object spectrum rather than the
night sky background. This procedure was performed iteratively ten times for each wavelength of the two-dimensional
spectrum, and the result of this iterative approximation was considered to be the found spectrum of the night sky,
which was subsequently subtracted. A closeup of the region around Hα (the bottom of Figure 8) shows no trace of
e.g. the bright OH 6577 night-sky line.

Numerical models (Healy-Kalesh et al. 2023) demonstrate that thousands of closely spaced (in time) RN eruptions
are able to clear large cavities around their host stars. The nova ejecta and the swept-up ISM create dynamic NSRs
tens of parsecs in radius. These NSRs inevitably contain a low-density cavity, bordered by a hot ejecta pile-up region,
which is surrounded by a cool, high-density, thin shell expanding at tens of km/s. The small expansion velocity is
because the swept-up matter is hundreds to thousands of times more massive than the aggregate sum of all the matter
ejected by the WD. This is in sharp contrast to the observed shells of non-recurrent novae, which display expansion
velocities of hundreds to thousands of km/s. The observed ∼± 60 km/s Hα line matches the (Healy-Kalesh et al.
2023) prediction of the expected expansion velocity of a NSR seen near its center (i.e. close to the line-of-sight to the
nova itself).
The H α line radial velocities of the outer shell (see Figures 9, 10 and 11), over the ∼400 arcsec length of the RSS
slit, are all ∼-14 km/s, which is close to the RS Oph systemic velocity value of -38.7 ± 0.4 km/s (Brandi et al. 2009).
The ∼25 km/s difference between these velocities is likely due to the deceleration of the RN ejecta, whose velocity
has been modified by the copious ISM mass that it has swept up.

5. THE NOVA SUPER-REMNANT
5.1. Motion of RS Oph and the shell’s age
The ∼1.5 degree angular size of the RS Oph nebulosity seen in Figure 2, at the 2.68 ± 0.16 kpc (Schaefer 2022)
distance of RS Oph, corresponds to ∼70 pc. This is ∼half the size of the 134 pc super-remnant of M31-12a, and
somewhat larger than the NSRs of KT Eri (∼50 pc) and T CrB (∼30 pc). It is two orders of magnitude larger than
the ∼0.1 - 0.5 pc-sized ejecta of 19th and 20th century novae.
RS Oph last erupted as a nova in 2021, and the maximum speed of its ejecta was ≃3600-5600 km s−1 (Bode et al.
2007; Tomov et al. 2023). Even ignoring the deceleration as those ejecta interact with the surrounding interstellar
medium (Duerbeck 1987; Santamar´ıa et al. 2020), it would take the ejecta ∼10 kyr to achieve the observed size of the
RS Oph shell. In fact, nova ejecta sweep up of order their own mass on timescales of order 1000 yr (Santamar´ıa et al.
2020), so that the nebulosity that we detect must be multiple tens of thousands of years old, produced by thousands
of RS Oph nova eruptions. RS Oph’s presence within its own shell (Figure 2) is consistent with the shell’s ejection
history.
5.2. Shell mass
Hydrodynamic 1D simulations of many thousands of RN ejection events (Healy-Kalesh et al. 2023) leading to a NSR
suggest that their boundary thicknesses are typically ∼10% of the radius of the NSR, with the vast majority of mass
being swept-up ISM. There is an indication of a sharp boundary in the form of an Hα linear feature ∼45’ to the
south and SW of RS Oph (see Figure 2, 3, 4 and 5) indicative of a shell edge. However the rest of the NSR displays a
patchy morphology, which is not surprising if the density of interstellar medium (ISM) surrounding RS Oph was itself
irregular.
The simplest model possible of RS Oph’s NSR is a sphere of radius 35 pc whose boundary is 3.5 pc thick, encompassing
swept-up ISM and nova ejecta with a uniform density of ∼5-50 H atoms/cm3 (assuming, of course, that the [SII] lines
of Figures 8, 9, 10 and 11 arise in gas associated with the NSR). The mass enclosed by the shell’s boundary layer would
then be ∼20 - 200 M⊙. A significantly more accurate mapping of the super-remnant density will be needed to improve
on this very rough estimate. But a simple order-of-magnitude “sanity check” of this mass estimate is straightforward,
as follows.
The RS Oph WD must be accreting mass from the red giant wind at about 10−7M⊙/yr (Booth et al. 2016), so that
the total mass ejected per outburst is ∼2 x 10−6M⊙. The observed ejection velocities, corrected for inclination are
∼5000 km/s (Bode et al. 2007), so that the kinetic energy carried away by one eruption is ∼5 x 1044 erg. Of order
2000 eruptions, spaced over 40,000 yr, have injected 1048 erg into the swept-up ISM. Assuming that half that energy
has been radiated away, and half is retained as the kinetic energy of the NSR expanding at ∼50 km/s, we derive an
NSR mass of ∼20 M⊙, consistent with our earlier rough estimate.
6. CONCLUSIONS
Deep narrowband Condor Array Telescope imagery of the environs of the recurrent nova RS Oph have revealed a ∼
1.5 degree, ∼70 pc-sized, possibly bi-lobed nebulosity surrounding the star: a nova super-remnant. Such nebulae are
predicted to be intrinsic and unavoidable features surrounding all RNe (Healy-Kalesh et al. 2023), and its detection
here strongly supports the theoretical model of the pile-up of thousands of ejected shells. The RS Oph super-remnant
must be at least tens of thousands of years old to be consistent with RS Oph’s (Gaia-determined) proper motion
and the ∼3600 km/s initial speed of ejection of its nova events. The lack of significant [OIII] emission rules out a
supernova or planetary nebula origin for the observed nebulosity. The logarithmic ratios of the [NII]658.4/Hα and
[SII]/Hα lines, both ∼-0.45, are indicative of shock ionization as expected for a NSR. The observed line ratio [SII]
6716/6731 is consistent with a matter density of ≤50 atoms/cm3. A simple model (Section 5.2) of a (mostly hollow)
NSR shell then yields a shell mass of ∼20 - 200 M⊙, expanding at a few tens of km/s, with an age of order 50-100
kyr.
