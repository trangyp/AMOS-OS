Coverage Path Planning for Holonomic UAVs via Uniaxial-Feasible,
Gap-Severity Guided Decomposition
Pedro Antonio Alarcon Granadeno1 and Jane Cleland-Huang2
Abstract— Modern coverage path planning (CPP) for holo-
nomic UAVs in emergency response must contend with diverse
environments where regions of interest (ROIs) often take the
form of highly irregular polygons, characterized by asymmetric
shapes, dense clusters of concavities, and multiple internal
holes. Modern CPP pipelines typically rely on decomposition
strategies that overfragment such polygons into numerous
subregions. This increases the number of sweep segments and
connectors, which in turn adds inter-region travel and forces
more frequent reorientation. These effects ultimately result
in longer completion times and degraded trajectory quality.
We address this with a decomposition strategy that applies a
recursive dual-axis monotonicity criterion with cuts guided by
a cumulative gap severity metric. This approach distributes
clusters of concavities more evenly across subregions and pro-
duces a minimal set of partitions that remain sweepable under
a parallel-track maneuver. We pair this with a global optimizer
that jointly selects sweep paths and inter-partition transitions
to minimize total path length, transition overhead, and turn
count. We demonstrate that our proposed approach achieves
the lowest mean overhead in path length and completion time
across 13 notable CPP pipelines.
I. INTRODUCTION
Coverage Path Planning (CPP) generates trajectories that
fully observe a Region of Interest (ROI) with minimal cost,
typically measured in path length, turns, execution time, or
energy use. A dominant strategy in aerial and ground robotics
is parallel-track coverage, where back-and-forth sweeps (e.g.,
boustrophedon) tile the region. For convex ROIs, selecting
an optimal sweep direction is straightforward. Huang [1]
showed that the direction perpendicular to the polygon’s
minimal altitude minimizes turns, a widely accepted sur-
rogate for maneuvering effort, execution time, and energy
consumption [2].
Real-world ROIs, however, are rarely convex. This leads to
the need for polygon decomposition, where the region is sub-
divided into simpler subregions (often convex or monotone
in a chosen direction), each assigned a local sweep pattern.
Early strategies such as trapezoidal decomposition [3] and
boustrophedon cellular decomposition (BCD) [4] provided
simple and topologically sound methods for this. Trapezoidal
decomposition projects vertical rays from vertices to the
boundary, yielding convex but often overfragmented paritions

and solve a global sequencing problem (often modeled as a
TSP or GTSP) to jointly minimize coverage and transition
costs [6], [10], [9].
These strategies have shown strong performance on con-
cave regions that are geometrically simple, such as polygons
with rectangular outlines, moderate symmetry, or limited
branching. These types of geometries commonly appear in
synthetic benchmarks or agricultural setting where the spatial
layout remains relatively uniform. However, this assumption

fails in real-world aerial scenarios, particularly in applica-

from landcover annotations, which are semantic segmenta-

Several core issues drive this overfragmentation: (i) width-

objectives, where minimizing per-region turn cost often
comes at the expense of a larger, more fragmented partition
set; (ii) fixed global sweep axes (as in [3], [4]) misalign with

merging is tightly constrained, typically allowed only when
the merged region remains convex and yields a strictly better
width cost (as in [6], [1], [9], [8]).
To address these limitations, we introduce a recursive
decomposition strategy built around a relaxed geometric
criterion. A region is considered sweepable if it is monotone
with respect to at least one cardinal axis, a property we
refer to as uniaxial feasibility. This criterion replaces stricter
assumptions such as convexity, global sweep alignment, or
width minimization, and is used both to determine when
a region must be split and whether adjacent regions can
be merged. When a region violates feasibility, we apply a
recursive partitioning process guided by a cumulative gap
severity metric that identifies and distributes clusters of con-
cavity. Unlike prior methods that prioritize turn cost or axis
alignment during decomposition, we aim to distribute regions
of discontinuity evenly across resulting partitions. This yields
a smaller partition set composed of larger, sweepable regions,
leading to lower global path length and execution time as
compared to contemporary CPP pipelines
II. RELATED WORK
Decomposition is a core strategy in modern Coverage
Path Planning (CPP) for simplifying coverage over complex
(e.g. convex) regions. Recent CPP surveys document that a
recurring theme is the use of polygonal width as a proxy
for turn cost [2]. Width is defined as the shortest distance
between two parallel lines enclosing the polygon, orthog-
onal to a candidate sweep direction.Smaller width implies
denser coverage passes and more turns. Since turns dominate
energy use and execution time, minimizing width offers a
practical proxy for reducing maneuvering effort. One of the
most influential examples that pioneered width-surrogates
decomposition strategies is Huang’s Multi-Strip Approxi-
mation (MSA) [1], which extends classical Boustrophedon
decomposition. MSA sweeps orthogonal to each polygon
edge, places dividers at topological changes, and overlays
the resulting dividers to form a set of monotone partitions.
A dynamic program then evaluates possible mergers and
assigns sweep directions to minimize total width.
Later approaches introduced width surrogates at different
stages of the planning pipeline. Li et al. [6] and Yu and Hung
[7] apply width-based decisions during decomposition. Li et
al. apply a greedy recursive strategy that places axis-aligned
cuts at reflex vertices. Each candidate cut is scored based
on the total width of the resulting sub-polygons, and the
cut minimizing this cumulative cost is chosen. The process
repeats until all partitions are convex. Yu and Hung instead
modify trapezoidal decomposition by defining four event
types (floor and ceiling, convex and concave) and apply this
scheme across multiple orientations, each orthogonal to a
polygon or obstacle edge. Every orientation yields a convex
decomposition, and the one with the lowest total width is
selected. Both methods optionally merge adjacent convex
regions that share a common sweep direction. Other methods
postpone width optimization to the merging stage. Nielsen
et al. [8] generate convex partitions by extending incident
edges at reflex vertices until they intersect the boundary, then
enumerate all valid convex unions and compute their widths.
An integer linear program selects a subset of merged regions
that together minimize total width. Bochkarev and Smith [9]
start from an existing convex decomposition and iteratively
refine it. At each reflex vertex, they define a feasible cut
space within the visible cone formed by adjacent edges,
sample chords, and evaluate each by the sum of minimum
altitudes. Cuts that lower total width replace previous ones,
and the process continues until convergence.
Other approaches emphasize global optimization over ex-
isting partitions instead of decomposition. Torres et al. [10]
define four path alternatives per region, combining sweep
orientation and traversal direction to determine entry and
exit points. A global planner selects visitation order and
local configuration to minimize total path length, jointly
considering in-region coverage and inter-region transitions,
while heuristically pruning the solution space. Jimenez et al.
[11] take a genetic approach, encoding the entire plan as a
chromosome. The head specifies region order and entry/exit
configurations, while the body selects local paths from a
template library. A fitness function combining travel and
estimated turn cost guides the evolution of efficient plans.
Despite their effectiveness on well-structured regions,
width-guided strategies struggle on irregular geometries due
to competing objectives. Cuts that reduce turn cost often
increase partition count, sweep-axis churn, and connector
complexity. Post-hoc merging offers limited relief but is
highly sensitive to early partitioning decisions and typically
is constrained by stringent admissibility tests that accept
unions only when surrogate width metrics improve. In con-
trast, our method avoids width-based during decomposition
and merging. We introduce a cumulative gap severity metric
that evaluates sweep inefficiencies along both axes and
guides recursive cuts to spread clustered concavities across
subregions. A dual-axis monotonicity criterion governs both
decomposition and merging: a region is preserved if it is
monotone along at least one cardinal axis, and split only
when both fail. This flexibility retains complex but sweep-
able regions that traditional convex or width-driven methods

final stitching phase that jointly optimizes path length, turn
cost, and inter-region transitions across all partitions.
III. CPP PROBLEM FORMULATION
Coverage path planning (CPP) seeks a trajectory that
enables a robot to observe every part of a planar region
of interest (ROI), denoted eR ⊂R2. Unlike ground-based
robots that must remain within strict workspace boundaries
due to physical constraints, this work considers a holonomic
multirotor UAV, capable of concurrent linear and rotational
motion at any planar heading. The UAV may also slightly
exceed the ROI boundary to improve coverage quality or
reduce path overhead. Hence, they are not subject to non-
holonomic constraints such as a minimum turning radius or
path-smoothness assumptions, where the trajectory is often
required to satisfy C3 continuity and curvature bounds (e.g.,
kmax = 1/rmin) [12].
The UAV is equiopped with a nadir-pointing camera with
an effective symmetric field-of-view (FOV) half-angle ϕ/2
at altitude h. Under a flat-ground, pinhole-camera model, the
cross-track footprint width becomes w = 2h tan(ϕ/2). We
adopt an approximate cellular decomposition setting [13],
where the ROI is modeled as a bounded polygonal region eR
that may contain internal holes. Let O = {O1, O2, . . . , OK}
denote the set of polygonal holes, each representing an
obstacle or exclusion zone. A uniform grid of square cells
with resolution equal to the UAV’s footprint (w × w) is
overlaid onto eR. All cells that intersect eR but do not lie
entirely within any hole Ok ∈O are retained. The union
of these retained cells defines an expanded rectilinear region
R. By construction, R ⊆eR, which guarantees full coverage
albeit at the cost of some negligible excess area along the
boundary.
A region is considered fully covered once every retained
cell in R has been observed. A cell is marked as covered
when the cumulative camera footprint intersects it beyond a
threshold ratio α (typically close to 1). This defines the core
principle of complete coverage in CPP. Additional objectives
usually include minimizing path length (L), execution time
(T), and energy use (E). In emergency response scenarios,
which motivate this work, fast execution is essential. Victim
survival rates decline sharply with time; for instance, 93.6%
of live extrications after earthquakes occur within the first
24 hours [14]. Accordingly, after guaranteeing complete
coverage, this study adopts execution time as the primary
evaluation metric when comparing coverage strategies.
IV. COVERAGE PATH PLANNING ALGORITHM
A. Uniaxial Feasible, Gap-Severity Guided Decomposition
We operate on a rectilinear polygonal region P ⊂R2,
defined over a uniform grid of square cells with resolution
equal to the UAV’s camera footprint width w. The region
may contain interior holes, which define non-observable
zones. Our objective is to determine where P can be swept
directly using parallel-track maneuver, or whether we must
decompose into simpler subregions. To do so, we apply a re-
cursive, line-sweep-inspired strategy that partitions a polygon
only when it violates dual-axis monotonicity. Cut placements
are guided by a cumulative gap severity metric that quantifies
how inefficient a traversal without decomposition would be
inside regions of discontinuity.
Many decomposition pipelines rely on stringent condi-
tions: they fix a global sweep direction a priori (e.g., TD,
BCD), decompose whenever a region is non-convex (e.g.,
Li), and merge only when convexity is preserved and width
cost improves (e.g., Li, Huang). These constraints fragment
regions that could otherwise be swept directly. To avoid
premature cuts and preserve larger sweepable regions, we
instead adopt a more permissive geometric rule called the
uniaxial feasibility criterion, defined as follows:
Criterion 1 (Uniaxial Feasibility Test). A polygon P is
acceptable if it is monotone with respect to at least one of
the two cardinal axes (horizontal or vertical). This criterion
governs both decomposition and merging, that is, a region
is decomposed only if it violates this condition, and adjacent
regions are merged only if their union satisfies it.
This condition retains many concave shapes that would
be discarded under convexity or width-based tests, while
still guaranteeing parallel-track coverage. To evaluate axis
monotonicity, we construct a finite family of axis-aligned
sweep lines over the polygon boundary. Note that a rectilin-
ear polygon is composed of individual grid cells of width w,
corrsponding to the UAV’s footprint as defined in Section III.

vast majority of polygonal regions encountered in practice.
V. EVALUATION
A. Experimental Setup & Baselines
Our benchmark evaluates a suite of CPP pipelines formed
by combining several well-known decomposition algorithms
with route optimizers that have shaped much of CPP liter-
ature. Each pipeline consists of two components, namely a
(1) decomposition module (and optional post-hoc merging)
and (2) a global optimizer. The decomposition algorithm
divides the input polygon into smaller subregions (partitions)
that are amenable to coverage via parallel sweeps. For each
partition, a set of candidate sweep paths is generated, each
defined by a direction and entry/exit point. The optimizer
selects one candidate per partition and determines the order
in which partitions are visited. Its goal is to construct a global
trajectory that minimizes a combination of costs, which may
include the total sweep path length within each partition, the
connector distances between partitions, and the number of
turns in the full path.
For decomposition, we evaluate three classical strategies,
namely Boustrophedon Cellular Decomposition (BCD) [4],
Trapezoidal Decomposition (TD) [3], and Li’s vertex-edge
minimum-width decomposition [6]. BCD and TD are im-
plemented via the open-source Fields2Cover library [16],
which provides faithful implementations of these canonical
algorithms. Both BCD and TD remain widely used in CPP
pipelines, and many modern approaches either adopt them
directly into their pipelines. Li’s method is used through the
coverageDecomposition tool from MathWorks [17], which
implements a vertex-edge strategy that recursively partitions
the region to minimize the maximum width of each cell.
Originally inspired by Huang’s minimal-altitude criterion,
this approach serves as a key baseline in our study, as
it illustrates the trade-off between reduced within-partition
turn cost and significant overfragmentation, and broadly
represents a class of width-based decomposition strategies
that continue to shape the direction of CPP research.
For the optimization stage, we include Torres’ heuristic
[10], a state-of-the-art method that exhaustively enumer-
ates traversal sequences based on sweep orientations and
entry/exit directions, implemented using the MathWork’s
uavCoveragePlanner module [18]. We include Google OR-
Tools [19], the default TSP solver used by the Fields2Cover
library. Finally, we reimplemented three metaheuristic plan-
ners, namely, Genetic Algorithm (GA), Ant Colony Op-
timization (ACO), and Threshold Accepting (TA), which
closely follows the encoding and cost formulation described
by Jimenez et al. [11]. In [11], each solution encodes a

candidates, guided by pheromone levels accumulated from
prior iterations. In TA, we use partition swaps and candidate
flips and accept a neighbor only if the cost increase stays
within a decreasing threshold.
As an additional baseline, we include a Nearest-Neighbor
(NN) strategy that bypasses decomposition entirely. Instead
of relying on partitioned sweep segments, NN treats all
raw waypoints from the original track set as independent
targets and greedily constructs a tour by chaining the closest
unvisited point.
Each pipeline ultimately produces a global trajectory,
defined as a sequence of waypoints such that full coverage
of the ROI (i.e. polygon) is guaranteed. To estimate execu-
tion time under realistic motion constraints, we use Ruckig
[20], [21] to perform offline time parameterization of each
global segment. This process respects user-defined velocity,
acceleration, and jerk limits, and accounts for multi-axis
synchronization (including concurrent yaw and linear motion
for holonomic systems). The resulting duration reflects ac-
celeration and deceleration phases, jerk-bounded transitions,
and coupled motion across all active degrees of freedom.
This time cost serves as our primary performance metric.
We additionally report total path length, partition count per
decomposition strategy, and number of turns.
All reported metrics are presented as relative overheads
with respect to the best-known value per polygon. For-
mally, the overhead for a metric M is computed as (M −
Mmin)/Mmin. This normalization allows for fair comparison
across polygons with different scales and inherent difficulty.
We evaluate all baselines pipelines and our proposed solution
(as outlined in Section IV) on a benchmark of 13 polygons.
The benchmark includes polygons with varying shapes,
scales, aspect ratios, holes, and clusters of concavities. These
benchmark polygons were designed to mimic observed land-
cover features (e.g., woodlands, large water bodies, clustered
buildings) and to exhibit geometrical complexity that poses
nontrivial challenges for coverage path planning pipelines.

irregular polygons hinges on balancing geometric flexibil-
ity with partition simplicity. By deferring sweep direction
selection and replacing width-based splitting with a cu-
mulative gap severity metric, our method preserves larger,
more sweep-efficient regions. This reduces both path length
and execution time without incurring the penalties of over-
fragmentation.
VI. CONCLUSION
This work introduced a decomposition and global opti-
mization strategy tailored to the challenges of holonomic
UAV-based CPP in highly irregular environments, such as
those found in emergency response missions. We depart from
conventional width-based approaches that dominate the CPP
literature, and instead we proposed a viable alternative built
on a uniaxial feasibility criterion and a cut selection process
