Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) remain vulner-
able to misalignment and jailbreaks, making
external safeguards like moderation filters es-
sential, yet existing filters often focus narrowly
on safety, falling short of the broader alignment
needs seen in real-world deployments. We in-
troduce Policy Aligned Moderation (PAM), a
flexible framework for training custom mod-
eration filters grounded in user-defined poli-
cies that extend beyond conventional safety ob-
jectives. PAM automates training data gen-
eration without relying on human-written ex-
amples, enabling scalable support for diverse,
application-specific alignment goals and gener-
ation policies. PAM-trained filters match the
performance of state-of-the-art safety moder-
ation filters and policy reasoning models, and
outperform them on PAMBENCH, four newly
introduced user-annotated policy enforcement
benchmarks that target age restrictions, dietary
accommodations, cultural alignment, and lim-
itations in medical guidance. These perfor-
mance gains are achieved while the PAM filter
runs 5–100× faster at inference than policy-
conditioned reasoning models.

Bhargava et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023). This is
underscored by a growing body of jailbreak attacks

et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023; Mehrotra et al., 2024;
Liu et al., 2024; Andriushchenko et al., 2024). As
a result, external safeguards now serve as a criti-
cal defense layer against misalignment and misuse.
Most notably, content moderation filters are used to
detect and block prompts or completions that con-

As LLMs are deployed across diverse real-world
settings, ensuring compliance with alignment ob-
jectives, often defined by developers or users and
codified as policies, has become increasingly im-
portant, especially for goals beyond general notions
of safety. Achieving policy-aligned behavior often
requires curating custom datasets and retraining
models, a costly and often impractical process, es-
pecially when working with proprietary or third-
party models lacking access to the original align-
ment pipeline. Moreover, retraining does not guar-
antee robust or reliable compliance, highlighting
the need for moderation filters that provide fine-
grained control by explicit policy specifications.
A new class of guardrails has emerged to support
custom policy enforcement (OpenAI, 2025; Hoover
et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025). These are fine-tuned
LLMs trained on a variety of content policies along-
side examples of compliance and violation, aiming
to generalize to novel, user-defined alignment con-
straints specified at inference time. However, this
approach faces key challenges: first, it requires
high-quality, comprehensive training data captur-
ing nuanced edge cases; second, these models in-
troduce substantial inference cost and latency due
to large parameter sizes and the need to process

full policy context at every step; and third, inter-
preting complex or ambiguous policies demands
strong base models with large capacities and long
context windows.
In this work, we propose an alternative approach
to building custom policy moderation filters by
addressing a core bottleneck: the high cost and
effort required to produce policy-aligned training
data. We present PAM (Policy-Aligned Moder-
ation), a framework that automatically translates
user- or developer-defined alignment policies into
customized moderation filters. Given a policy, po-
tentially composed of multiple specifications or
rules, PAM constructs a multi-dimensional filter,
with each dimension aligned to a distinct specifica-
tion. For each dimension, it generates a rich set of
labeled examples spanning varying degrees of com-
pliance, which are then used to train a regression-
based alignment scoring model. To automate this
process, PAM employs meta-prompts to coordinate
multiple LLMs for prompt generation, response
collection, and scoring, producing high-quality,
policy-guided training data without manual annota-
tion.
A broader limitation in developing custom policy
moderation filters is the absence of standardized
benchmarks. While DynaBench (Hoover et al.,
2025) is the only existing benchmark that pairs
prompts with synthetic policies and compliance
labels, it includes just 500 examples and empha-
sizes broad generalization across many ephemeral
policies. In contrast, real-world deployments typi-
cally prioritize performance on a small set of stable
policies, motivating the need for targeted, high-
coverage benchmarks. To help address this gap, we
introduce PAMBENCH: four new public bench-
marks featuring thousands of human-annotated
prompt–completion pairs aligned to practical pol-
icy domains—including age-appropriate content,
dietary accommodations, cultural alignment, and
limitations in medical guidance.
We evaluate thePAM-based filter on both pub-
lic safety benchmarks and our new PAMBENCH
datasets.
Using a compact 2B model, our fil-
ter achieves strong performance on public safety
tasks (F1 = 0.84), matching the best-performing 7B
safety filter and similar to policy-reasoning mod-
els (F1 = 0.81–0.84). On PAMBENCH, the filters
align well with human judgments and outperform
the average of four out-of-the-box LLMs prompted
with policy instructions on Under 13 and Vegan
Respect policies, match performance on Medical
Advice policy, and remain competitive on Cul-
tural Alignment policy despite its higher complex-
ity. Compared to policy reasoning models, PAM
shows a clear performance advantage, indicating
stronger generalization to diverse, user-defined
moderation objectives. Notably, the PAM-derived
filter achieves these results while remaining 5–100×
faster at inference than policy-conditioned reason-
ing models. We will release our code, prompt tem-
plates, datasets, and trained filters to support future
research and community adoption.

Related work
Safety Moderation Filters. The rise of adversar-
ial attacks on LLMs shows that alignment alone is
insufficient to constrain model behavior, motivat-
ing the development of external moderation filters,
typically fine-tuned models or classifiers trained
to detect unsafe outputs (OpenAI, 2022; Clarifai;
Zeng et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024a; Microsoft,
2025). To date, these filters have focused primarily
on ensuring the safety of model outputs by adopt-
ing taxonomies that span a range of harm cate-
gories (OpenAI, 2022; Bai et al., 2023; Gehman
et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2022).
As real-world deployments demand finer-grained
alignment, there is a growing need to extend mod-
eration beyond universal safety norms to support
custom, policy-specific objectives.
Policy Reasoning Models A common approach
to extending moderation beyond fixed safety
rules is to fine-tune models to apply custom
policies provided at inference time by includ-
ing the full policy text in the prompt.
Exam-
ples include gpt-oss-safeguard (OpenAI, 2025)
and Nemotron-Content-Safety-Reasoning-4B
(NVIDIA, 2025), which are trained to interpret
and enforce developer-specified safety constraints.
DynaGuard (Hoover et al., 2025) further expands
this paradigm by training on a large set of syntheti-
cally generated policies, augmented with reformat-
ted public safety datasets. These models jointly
reason over the policy and content to produce a
compliance decision and explanation.
A key limitation of existing approaches is their
reliance on high-quality training data that captures
real-world policies and includes challenging exam-
ples of both compliance and violation. However,
such data is rarely available, particularly for nu-
anced alignment objectives that go beyond general
safety. As a result, prior work has largely focused

for assessing general safety compliance, they do
not measure a model’s ability to follow explicit,
user-defined policy specifications.
Evaluating policy-specific moderation requires
benchmarks that pair alignment policies with
prompts and responses, along with ground-truth
compliance labels.
DynaBench (Hoover et al.,
2025) takes a step in this direction, offering 500
examples where each includes a synthetic pol-
icy (comprising multiple rules) and corresponding
compliant or violating responses. However, real-
world use cases often involve a small number of
persistent policies, where performance on those
specific policies matters more than generalization
across many randomized ones. This highlights
the need for focused, policy-grounded benchmarks.
To address this gap, we introduce PAMBENCH: a
suite of four public benchmarks, each centered on
a distinct policy domain. Each benchmark includes
thousands of human-annotated prompt–response
pairs labeled for policy compliance, enabling eval-
uation of policy-specific moderation filters.
3
Design of PAM
PAM transforms user-defined alignment policies
into tailored moderation filters by guiding the gen-
eration of fine-tuning data. This design is motivated
by the practical insight that deployment policies,
typically do not change frequently, enabling effi-
cient and robust filter construction.
3.1
Design Considerations
PAM embeds policy specifications directly into
the model through fine-tuning, following a strat-
egy similar to conventional safety moderation fil-
ters (Clarifai; Zeng et al., 2024; Han et al., 2024a).
Our design is shaped by four key considerations:
Policy-based fine-tuning. Encoding policy rules
during training, rather than conditioning on them
at inference time, enables the creation of compact,
specialized filters. These models can match or even
exceed the performance of larger policy-agnostic
moderation systems, while being more efficient
to deploy. Unlike policy reasoning models that
require runtime access to the policy text, our ap-
proach eliminates inference-time overhead and sim-
plifies deployment in resource-constrained settings.
Further, training as a multidimensional model en-
ables the filter to output the assessment for multiple


## Page 4


policies in one inference call, hence keeping the
inference time constant as the number of policies
increases.
Handling multi-rule policies. Real-world poli-
cies often comprise multiple rules, ranging from
clearly defined constraints (e.g., “no use of emo-
jis”) to more subjective ones (e.g., “ensure age-
appropriate content”). As the number and com-
plexity of these rules grow, a single model’s ability
to reliably interpret and apply all of them can de-
grade, especially when rules interact or conflict
(e.g., cultural sensitivity vs. fairness). Such scenar-
ios may require nuanced prioritization or interpre-
tation logic. To address this, PAM trains a modera-
tion filter with multiple independent output dimen-
sions, each aligned to a specific policy rule. Model
responses are then independently evaluated against
each rule, producing a multi-dimensional compli-
ance vector rather than a single decision score. This
modular structure enables customization: deploy-
ers can assign weights, configure thresholds, or
define rule-resolution strategies based on their spe-
cific operational requirements.
Regression vs. binary classification. Although
moderation decisions are often enacted as binary
allow/deny outcomes, we adopt a regression-based
formulation to assess policy alignment for two key
reasons. First, many policy rules exhibit graded
severity rather than a strictly binary structure (e.g.,
adherence to cultural norms or age appropriate-
ness). Collapsing such cases into binary labels
obscures meaningful distinctions and forces qual-
itatively different behaviors to be treated identi-
cally. A continuous alignment score instead cap-
tures degrees of compliance or violation, which
downstream systems can threshold according to ap-
plication needs (e.g., stricter thresholds for public-
facing systems and more permissive ones for in-
ternal use). Second, deployment contexts vary in
their risk tolerance and sensitivity requirements.
Even for ostensibly binary policies, such as gen-
eral safety, acceptable over-refusal rates may dif-
fer across applications. Regression outputs allow
deployment-specific calibration without a fixed
global threshold at training time.
Flexibility to incorporate custom training
data.
PAM is designed to complement—not
replace—human-annotated, policy-aligned train-
ing data. While its core strength lies in generating
moderation training data without relying on man-
ual annotation, it readily supports integration with
high-quality, developer-provided data when avail-
able. This includes cases where annotated data
exists for the full policy or only for a subset of its
individual rules. Such data can be incorporated
directly, allowing training datasets to combine syn-
thetic and real-world examples in a seamless, mod-
ular fashion.
Guided by these considerations, PAM structures
data generation and training into seven sequential
tasks (T1–T7), illustrated in Figure 2 and described
in detail in the following subsections.
3.2
Data Generation
Policy Representation and Specification Scope
(T1) A policy defines, in natural language, the types
of content that a moderation filter should restrict
or disallow. As is typical, we assume the policy is
composed of a list of rules or specifications writ-
ten in a format that clearly conveys the intended
objectives. In this work, we focus on 17 represen-
tative specifications that a real-world policy might
include, grouped into four categories. However,
PAM can accommodate any user-defined policy in
this format without modification. These categories
include general safety (3 specifications), cultural
and personal values such as religious content, di-
etary preferences, and culturally sensitive topics
(11), domain-specific guidance for high-risk areas
like finance and medicine, and neutrality and fair-
ness to ensure balanced responses. A full descrip-
tion of policies is provided in Appendix A
System Prompt Generation (T2) Training a pol-
icy moderation filter for a given policy rule, re-
quires a dataset of user interactions with an LLM-
that is, pairs of user questions (or test prompts) and
the model’s responses. These test prompts must
span a broad and diverse set of topics and styles
relevant to the specification in order to reflect the
full range of possible user inputs. Thus, an essen-
tial early step is generating system prompts that
instruct the LLM to create these test prompts.
Manually crafting system prompts for every
specification is impractical, so we use meta-
prompts, high-level prompts that instruct an LLM
to generate diverse system prompts for a given spec-
ification. Although this process is automated, a
human-in-the-loop review validates that the gener-
ated system prompts accurately reflect the speci-
fication’s intent and capture a wide range of user
interaction types. We use two complementary meta-
prompts. The first generates prompts that explicitly
reference the specification and simulate various
user interaction styles.


## Page 5


The second meta-prompt targets subtle or in-
direct violations and does not explicitly mention
the specification.
For example, it may prompt
discussions about traditional foods rather than di-
rectly asking about allergens. Examples are in Ap-
pendix B.2. Together, the two meta-prompts ensure
diverse topics and styles, generating an average of
17 system prompts per specification.
Test Prompt Generation (T3 & T4) In this stage,
we generate test prompts that can elicit either vio-
lating or compliant model behavior. Each system
prompt produced in the previous step is passed to a
set of diverse LLMs (Task T3 in Figure 2), instruct-
ing them to generate user-style questions aligned
with the behavioral intent described in the system
prompt.
This initial generation process may occasion-
ally produce test prompts that are poorly phrased
or contain generic placeholders (e.g., [country] in-
stead of a specific country name), which do not
reflect realistic user inputs. To address this, all
test prompts are passed through a quality valida-
tion step (Task T4), where another LLM evaluates
and rewrites prompts as needed to ensure clarity,
self-containment, and specificity. This includes re-
placing generic placeholders with concrete entities
and correcting unclear phrasing. In practice, ap-
proximately 2% of test prompts were revised. See
Appendix B.3 for some examples of test prompts
before and after revision.
Response Generation (T5) Next, we generate re-
sponses for all validated test prompts by instructing
models to produce both compliant and violating
outputs. We use two meta prompts, one guiding
compliant behavior and the other guiding viola-
tions, combined with each specification descrip-
tion to create candidate system prompts through
an LLM. These prompts can manually reviewed to
ensure appropriateness.
Each test prompt is then paired with both com-
pliant and violating variants and passed to a diverse
set of LLMs. Compliant responses are generated
using aligned LLMs fine-tuned to follow human
preferences and safety guidelines (Ji et al., 2023b).
Since aligned LLMs often refuse to produce vi-
olating responses, uncensored LLMs are used to
obtain these, as they respond freely to prompts that
aligned models would typically reject. On aver-
age across the different specifications, the response
generated by the uncensored models got an average
compliance score of between 1.95 and 3.5 (on a 1–5
scale, where 1 indicates severe noncompliance and
5 full compliance; see below section for scoring
details). More specifically, WizardLM responses
yielded an average score of 3.5, while Dolphin fam-
ily models’ responses ranged between 1.95–2.75
across specifications. These results indicate that
the uncensored models are capable of producing a
range of responses from severe to more nuanced vi-
olations. The models used for response generation
are provided in Appendix B.1. Finally, if addi-
tional datasets are available, they can be seamlessly
integrated with the generated data.
Rubric Creation and Scoring (T6) Training a
regression model to assess response compliance re-
quires labeled data. Using the datasets from Tasks
T1–T5, which contain both compliant and violating
examples, we generate evaluation rubrics for each
specification to ensure consistent scoring. Each
rubric defines a 1–5 scale, where 1 denotes severe
violation and 5 full compliance, created with LLM
assistance to capture varying degrees of alignment.
The rubrics are then manually reviewed and refined
to ensure they are relevant, unambiguous, and faith-
fully capture the intended alignment criteria.
With these rubrics, we adopt an LLM-as-a-Judge
framework to assign compliance scores. Following
prior work showing that LLMs can approximate
human judgment (Zheng et al., 2023), a panel of
LLMs evaluates each prompt–response pair against
the rubric and assigns a score from 1 to 5. If a speci-
fication is not applicable, judges assign a Not Appli-
cable (NA) label, treated as a score of 5 during train-
ing. We aggregate the scores from multiple judges
and use their average as the ground-truth label. Ex-
ample prompts are provided in Appendix B1.
3.3
Training of PAM (T7)
We train a PAM filter using data aggregated
from Tasks T1–T6 across 17 alignment specifi-
cations. To ensure balanced regression training,
we bucket compliance scores into 0.5 intervals
([1.0, 1.5), . . . , [4.5, 5.0]) and subsample overrep-
resented buckets to match the smallest one. This
prevents bias toward extreme (highly compliant or
non-compliant) samples. For each specification,
we construct the final dataset by combining sam-
ples generated for that specification with samples
from all other specifications. Data are split into
80% training, 5% validation, and 15% test sets.

Model Architecture.
PAM is built by fine-
tuning a shared base model with lightweight re-
gression heads, one per policy specification. Given
a prompt–response pair, the base model produces
a shared representation, and each head predicts a
compliance score for its respective policy. This
setup allows scoring multiple policies in a single
forward pass. Filters are trained in a multi-attribute
setting, where all policy heads are optimized jointly.
This reduces inference cost and encourages shared
learning across alignment objectives.
We experiment with Gemma-2-2B-it (PAM-G),
selected for its bilingual capabilities and suitability
for inline moderation. The model is trained for
multiple epochs using multiple optimization set-
tings, and we select the checkpoint with the lowest
validation loss. Full training hyperparameters are
reported in Appendix D.
Binary Filter Variant.
To enable direct compar-
ison with existing moderation baselines, most of
which are binary classifiers, we also train a binary
version of PAM-G, (PAMB-G). In this setting, the
continuous compliance scores are binarized using
a fixed threshold, and the model is trained to pre-
dict binary compliance labels. We report the per-
formance of both the regression-based and binary
variants in our evaluation.

PAMBENCH: Custom Policy
Moderation Benchmarks
PAMBENCH is a suite of four policy-based mod-
eration benchmarks designed to evaluate practi-
cal alignment objectives encountered in real-world
deployments3. PAMBENCH targets one represen-
tative policy specification from each of the four
policy categories described in Appendix A: age-
appropriate content for users under 13 (B1.2), re-
spect for vegan dietary preferences (B2.3), regional
cultural alignment (B3.1), and medical advice re-
strictions (B4.2).
Each
benchmark
contains
approximately
400–1,300 prompt–response pairs, combining
real user queries with synthetic prompts and
model-generated responses from a diverse set of
large language models. Prompts were sourced or
adapted from existing benchmarks where applica-
ble, including MinorBench (Khoo et al., 2025),
Arabic Safety Benchmark (Ashraf et al., 2024),
MedSafetyBench (Han et al., 2024c), and Wild-
Chat (Deng et al., 2024), and were complemented
with user-authored or model-generated queries. To
support reliable evaluation, responses include both
compliant and non-compliant outputs to ensure
representative coverage across compliance levels.
All benchmarks were independently annotated
by three expert raters using a 1–5 Likert scale for
policy compliance, following shared specifications
and detailed scoring rubrics. Additional details on
benchmark construction and annotation protocols
are provided in Appendix G.

Table 2 shows that the performance of PAM-G

Results indicate that no individual LLM judge
consistently matches human annotations, whereas
PAM-G closely follows aggregate human judg-

training distribution. More detailed results on infer-

The ICCs, showing agreement with annotators, re-

elevated MAE and MSE on B4.2 and B1.2 indi-

graded and interpretive nature of these policy spec-

Conclusions
We introduce PAM, a flexible framework for build-
ing moderation filters aligned to policy specifica-
tions. PAM enables the automated generation of
training data tailored to user-defined policies, re-
ducing reliance on manual annotation. The PAM-
based filter performs strongly on public safety
benchmarks and achieve high agreement with hu-
man annotators across PAMBENCH, four newly
introduced policy-specific benchmarks. Finally,
PAMBENCH offers the first systematic evaluation
suite for moderation aligned to policy specifica-
tions, supporting further research into customiz-
able, policy-aware model behavior.

Limitations
While PAM supports diverse alignment specifica-
tions, the quality of training data depends on the
ability of LLMs to faithfully follow meta-prompts
during system prompt and response generation. For
highly abstract, conflicting, or nuanced specifica-
tions, prompt adherence may degrade without addi-
tional safeguards such as manual review or filtering
heuristics—to validate that examples reflect the
correct compliance or violation behavior.
Ethical Considerations
All human annotations in this work were conducted
under fair labor practices. Annotators were com-
pensated on an hourly basis at a rate consistent with
or above prevailing local wages, rather than per-
task payments, to ensure fair compensation regard-
less of task difficulty or completion speed. Each
annotator was informed of the nature of the task,
the approximate duration, and the use of their an-
notations for research purposes.
The

involved

It contains both vanilla and adversarial prompts

comparisons between the responses. We use the

each of the two responses. This resulted in a test
set consisting of 16,422 prompt+response pairs.

F.4
Results
PAM is designed as a general-purpose framework
for generating labeled datasets and training moder-
ation filters based on arbitrary, user-defined align-
ment specifications. However, publicly available
benchmarks primarily focus on general safety con-
straints, and there is a lack of datasets covering
more diverse or custom specifications. To evalu-
ate the practical effectiveness of our approach, we
benchmark the PAM filter on widely used safety
datasets and compare their performance with exist-
ing content moderation systems.
Since these benchmarks are formulated as binary
classification tasks (safe vs. harmful), we trained
our filter as a binary classifier after converting our
data labels to binary labels. We compare the PAM

G.1
Data Collection and Generation
Methodology
To evaluate alignment beyond standard safety, we
construct four new benchmark datasets, each target-
ing a specific specification. Each dataset contains
approximately 400–1,297 prompt–response pairs,
combining real user queries and synthetic prompts
with completions from a diverse set of LLMs.
For B1.2 (age-appropriate content for users un-
der 13), prompts were adapted from the Minor-
Bench suite (Khoo et al., 2025), with responses gen-
erated by Gemma-27B and WizardLM, resulting in
a total of 1019 pairs. For B2.3 (respect for vegan
dietary preferences), prompts were generated using
GPT-5. We first instructed the model to produce
a diverse set of categories relevant to the specifi-
cation, then generated approximately 50 questions
per category. For each prompt, WizardLM and
Gemma-27B were used with two distinct system
prompts-one guiding compliant responses and an-
other guiding non-compliant ones-resulting in both
compliant and non-compliant outputs and yielding
1297 prompt–response pairs.
For B3.1 (local cultural alignment), 84 regionally
sensitive questions from the Arabic Safety Bench-
mark (Ashraf et al., 2024) were combined with 316
user-authored queries. Responses were generated
by GPT-4o, Allam-7B, Fanar-9B, Qwen3-32B, and
Gemma-27B, totaling 400 pairs. For B4.2 (Med-
ical Advice Restrictions), prompts were sourced
from the MedSafetyBench (Han et al., 2024c) and
WildChat datasets.
For WildChat (Deng et al.,
2024), we used Gemma-27B with a system prompt
to classify both prompts and responses according
to their coverage under the medical advice policy.
Only instances where both the prompt and response
were classified as covered were retained for further
evaluation, regardless of compliance status. To en-
sure a balanced distribution, these prompts were
paired with responses generated by Gemma-27B
and WizardLM, resulting in a final dataset of 683
balanced prompt–response pairs. The MedSafety-
Bench dataset consisted of prompt–response pairs
compliant with the policy. To obtain non-compliant
examples, we used LLaMA-2-7B and WizardLM


## Page 21


to generate completions that partially or fully vio-
lated the policy. This process yielded a final dataset
of 431 balanced prompt–response pairs.
To construct all custom benchmarks,except B3.1,
we used the judges’ initial evaluations to balance
the score distributions, ensuring a fixed number of
examples per score level and achieving representa-
tive, evenly distributed benchmarks. This resulted
in 1019, 1297, 400, and 1114 samples for B1.2,
B2.3, B3.1, and B4.2, respectively.
G.2
Human Annotation Study Details
Each benchmark was independently annotated by
three raters, drawn from a pool of nine experienced
annotators. Annotators were recruited through a
local contracting firm and compensated at a fixed
hourly rate. The pool comprised seven females and
two males based in Egypt, Sudan, and Syria, all
bilingual with high English proficiency. Their aca-
demic backgrounds were diverse: five held PhDs
(including two in linguistics), two were medical
doctors, one an engineer, and one an architect.
All had prior experience with LLM evaluation and
alignment-related annotation tasks.
Annotators were provided with the same spec-
ifications and accompanying rubrics used during
data generation. Each prompt–response pair was
rated on a 1–5 Likert scale for compliance with the
given specification. They received detailed scor-
ing instructions, along with ten reference examples
pre-annotated by the research team 16. Before the
main annotation phase, a pilot study involving 20
examples was conducted to calibrate judgments
and improve inter-annotator agreement.
Two recurring issues were identified during the
pilot: (1) a tendency to conflate general response
quality with compliance to the specification, and
(2) penalizing responses due to issues in the in-
put prompt (e.g., ill-formed phrasing). To mitigate
these, real-time communication was maintained
via a messaging channel, through which annotators
received clarifications and feedback throughout the
study. To preserve human judgment integrity, anno-
tators were explicitly instructed not to use language
models or automated tools during the annotation
process.
Annotation was conducted using structured
Google Sheets designed for batch scoring and meta-
data tracking. Each response was triple-annotated
with no adjudication; scores were averaged for eval-

rics in Appendix G.3. On average, annotators spent
approximately five minutes per item, with each
benchmark taking 4–8 days to complete per anno-
tator.

Expanded Results
Table 11 provides an expanded breakdown of the
results from Table 2, detailing performance across
all annotators, judges on PAMBENCH . Perfor-
mance is reported using four metrics: Mean Ab-
solute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE),
Agreement Rate (A.Rate), and Intraclass Correla-
tion Coefficient (ICC)(Shrout and Fleiss, 1979).
MAE measures the average absolute difference be-
tween predicted and reference annotator scores,
while MSE penalizes larger deviations more heav-
ily. Agreement Rate is calculated by rounding ref-
erence annotator scores and predicted scores to the
nearest integer and computing the ratio of matches
to total cases. ICC quantifies consistency among
raters, with higher values indicating stronger agree-
ment.
Inter-annotator agreement is measured by com-
paring each annotator’s score to the average of the
other two annotators (first three rows) and then aver-
aging the results across all annotators (Inter Ann.).
Note that different benchmarks have different an-
notators. Individual judge–annotator agreement is
computed by comparing each individual judge’s
score with the average annotator score (rows 5–8),
and averaging across all individual judges (Ind.
Judge Avg.). We also report the average of all
judges’ scores (Avg. Judges) compared to annota-
tors. For PAM–annotator agreement, each PAM
filter score is compared with the average annotator
score to evaluate alignment with human judgments.

A key limitation of prompt-based judge models
is their reliance on general pretraining, which of-
ten conflicts with user-defined specifications. For
instance, if a user-defined specification prohibits

pork due to cultural or dietary restrictions-as might
be the case in Islamic or kosher contexts-a model

However, LLMs often assign high alignment scores
