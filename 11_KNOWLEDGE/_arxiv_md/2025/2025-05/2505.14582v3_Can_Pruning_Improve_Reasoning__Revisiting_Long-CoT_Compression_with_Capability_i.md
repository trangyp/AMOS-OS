Abstract
Long chain-of-thought (Long-CoT) reasoning
improves accuracy in LLMs, yet its verbose,
self-reflective style often hinders effective dis-
tillation into small language models (SLMs).
We revisit Long-CoT compression through the
lens of capability alignment and ask: Can prun-
ing improve reasoning? We propose Prune-
on-Logic, a structure-aware framework that
transforms Long-CoT into logic graphs and
selectively prunes low-utility reasoning steps
under self-verification constraints. Through
systematic analysis across three pruning strate-
gies—targeting entire chains, core reasoning,
and verification, we find that verification prun-
ing consistently improves accuracy while re-
ducing token usage, whereas pruning reason-
ing steps or indiscriminate pruning degrades
performance. Our study reveals that effective
pruning aligns supervision with model capac-
ity rather than merely shortening inputs. Gains
hold across tasks, model scales, and CoT ca-
pability, with larger models benefiting more
from pruning due to richer but more redundant
reasoning. Our empirical findings highlight
pruning as a structural optimization strategy for
aligning CoT reasoning with SLM capacity.

Related Work
LLM as a Graph Parser.
To better manage long
CoT reasoning, we decompose the original long
CoT into a step-level logic graph for structured rea-
soning, following (Pandey et al., 2025). Recent
advances in structured reasoning via CoT (Zhang
et al., 2025b,a) and self-correction (Zhang et al.,
2025d) have improved LLM-based parsers and re-
vealed their ability to represent logical structures
as graph-based reasoning processes (Luo et al.,
2025b).
Unlike prior methods that extract iso-
lated sub-information, we preserve the original sen-
tence’s logical flow by decomposing it into ordered
reasoning steps. Each step undergoes a verification
phase to maintain fidelity to the original content.
This design yields a semantically equivalent and
structurally organized representation.
Long2short CoT Compression for SLMs.
Ex-
isting compression methods for SLMs are inher-
ently lossy.
Fine-tuning token-level compres-
sion via perplexity (Han et al., 2024), prompting
strategies (Hou et al., 2025), step-level compres-
sion (Zhang et al., 2025c), or RLHF on length-
harmonizing datasets (Luo et al., 2025a) often
degrade reasoning capabilities. As shown in Ta-
ble 1, existing methods, while improving efficiency,
risk discarding critical reasoning information (Hou
et al., 2025) necessary for complex tasks. Our work
primarily revisits how step-level compression can
enhance SLMs. As such, we do not consider com-
parisons with alternative approaches such as data
mixing tricky strategies (Li et al., 2025b) or budget
prompting strategies (Han et al., 2024). To the best
of our knowledge, this is the first work to systemat-
ically explore perplexity-aware step-level pruning,
offering new insights into how lossy pruning affects
different CoT stages (e.g., reasoning, verification).

Prune-on-Logic
Inspired by the complex graph-like connectivity
in biological brains (Friston, 2008), we adopt a
structure-aware view of reasoning data following
the advice of Zhang et al. (2025c); Luo et al.
(2025b). We propose Prune-on-Logic involving:
(1) logic graph construction (§3.2), (2) step-level
importance scoring via predictive loss and ranking-
based pruning of low-impact steps (§3.3). We sys-
tematically explore three pruning strategies over
different reasoning phases, aiming to compress
Long-CoT without degrading inference quality, and
revisit the question: Can pruning improve reason-
ing, when compression is guided not by length
but by capability alignment? These strategies tar-
get different phases of the reasoning process: full
sequences, core derivations, and verification tails.
This allows us to probe not only where pruning is
most effective, but also what should be pruned.
Specifically, we explore two granularities of
pruning:
(1) removing individual logic nodes,
which represent discrete reasoning steps, and (2) re-
moving edges, i.e., weakening or severing weakly
informative logical connections between steps.
This distinction is important: pruning a node re-
moves its content entirely, while pruning an edge
changes the dependency structure and may isolate
redundant subchains. By analyzing both forms of
pruning across different reasoning roles, we revisit
a fundamental question: Can logic-aware prun-
ing compress CoT traces not by truncation, but by
selectively removing parts that least support infer-
ence—across steps, stages, and rhetorical roles?
3.1
Equivalent Logic Graph Reconstruction
Long chain-of-thought (Long-CoT) sequences of-
ten contain rich, multi-step reasoning interleaved
with rhetorical scaffolding (Friston, 2008). While
their original form is linear, the underlying logic
is inherently graph-structured (Luo et al., 2025b).
To make such structure explicit without altering
the content, following Luo et al. (2025b), we map
each CoT sequence as a semantically equivalent
logic graph that preserves all deductive steps but
encodes them as a directed acyclic graph (DAG).
This transformation does not alter the meaning of
the reasoning; rather, it restructures the sentence-
level logic into a graph topology that reflects the
actual flow of inference.

Long chain-of-thought (Long-CoT) sequences en-

form. However, their underlying semantic structure

Pruning Strategies.
To explore how pruning af-
fects reasoning, we define three logic-aware strate-
gies based on functional roles of nodes in the
CoT process. The All-chain strategy removes low-
scoring nodes from the entire logic graph, balanc-
ing breadth and depth of reasoning. This acts as
a general compression baseline. The Reasoning-
only strategy prunes derivational steps—such as
intermediate calculations—while preserving final
conclusions and verification. This reflects a conser-
vative approach that favors robustness over max-
imal compression. Conversely, the Verification-
only strategy targets self-checking or reflective ut-
terances (e.g., “Let’s verify that”), aiming to reduce
rhetorical redundancy while leaving the computa-
tional core intact. Each strategy produces a pruned
sequence Spruned by deleting selected nodes from
the original CoT S in order. The number of dele-
tions is controlled by a pruning ratio γ ∈(0, 1),
which determines the top-k nodes to remove based
on their semantic utility scores (§3.3). This explicit
control allows us to examine not only where prun-
ing is most effective, but also how much pruning
can be tolerated before degrading reasoning quality.
Pruning
Component.
Beyond
determining
where to prune within the reasoning process, we
also consider what to prune.
Specifically, we
explore two levels of granularity: node-level and
connection-level pruning. These correspond to
distinct components of a CoT sequence.
• Node-level pruning removes entire reasoning
steps ni ∈N, such as symbolic derivations
and their corresponding connections.
• Connection-level pruning suppresses rhetor-
ical units ci ∈C, such as discourse fillers and
stylistic transitions.
Both types are evaluated under the same loss-
based scoring mechanism introduced in §3.3. For
each candidate unit, we compute its semantic util-
ity Scorei using the differential loss with and with-
out the unit. This unified formulation enables se-
lective removal of both deductive and rhetorical
components. Rhetorical connections are retained
only when their removal would harm the model’s
predictive confidence. Together, the two pruning
axes—strategy scope (§3.3) and component granu-
larity—form a unified, structure-aware framework.
This design allows us to ask: What parts of a rea-
soning chain can be removed without impairing
performance, and how does this vary under differ-
ent resource constraints?

Main Results
Can Pruning Improve Reasoning?
Yes—If
Structured and Aligned with Model Capacity.
To assess whether pruning from Long-CoT can
improve reasoning instead of lossy learning for
SLMs (Han et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025; Hou et al.,
2025), we evaluate three pruning strategies that
target all-chain, reasoning-only, and verification-
only components under varying pruning ratios and
model capacities. Our results reveal that pruning is
not merely a compression tool, but a mechanism for
aligning supervision with SLMs’ capability. The
key lies in what gets pruned—not how much.
Finding 1: Longer Isn’t Always Better: Re-
dundancy Hurts SLMs Learning from Long-
CoT.
Despite the common belief (Jin et al., 2024)
that longer CoT enhances reasoning, our results
show that excessive length—especially during ver-
ification, can overwhelm small models with shal-
low content that adds little deductive value. As
shown in Table 2, pruning verification steps (γ =
1.0) consistently improves accuracy: DeepSeek-R1-
Distill-Qwen-7B increases from 57.0% to 63.0%
(+6.0), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B rises
from 57.7% to 62.9% (+5.2%) while reducing to-
kens by 9.51% and 5.72%, respectively. These
results suggest that SLMs are less constrained by
the availability of long reasoning traces than by
their capacity to filter irrelevant or repetitive steps
aligned with the premise of Luo et al. (2025b).
Verification, often reiterating previous answers, is
the most dispensable during Long-CoT distillation.
Pruning verification steps helps the model focus on
essential reasoning while reducing computational
load without sacrificing logical coverage.
Finding 2: What We Prune Matters More Than
How Much.
Pruning is not simply a matter of re-
ducing length; it’s about preserving semantic func-
tion. Our empirical analysis across three pruning
strategies (Table 2) shows that indiscriminate re-
moval of CoT steps severely degrades performance:
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B drops from 57.0%
to 18.1% (–38.9), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-
8B from 57.7% to 10.4% (–47.3). In contrast, se-
lectively pruning verification steps improves both
accuracy and efficiency (+6.0 for Qwen-7B, +5.2
for Llama-8B), while moderate pruning of reason-
ing chains (γ=0.5) still reduces performance (–3.3
and –1.4). These results indicate that small models
rely on reasoning traces to acquire inductive pat-
terns beyond final answers. Pruning must respect
this structural dependency: verification steps are
often reiterative and safe to remove, but reason-
ing steps provide the logical backbone for learning.
Supervision must prioritize semantic salience over
superficial verbosity.

Dispensable—If Nodes, Not Connections, Are
Removed.

gets, we compare the removal of logic-bearing

on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and DeepSeek-
R1-Distill-Qwen-7B), while pruning only rhetori-
cal connections drops accuracy below 32%. This

racy and efficiency. In contrast, reasoning pruning
risks over-compression, and all-chain pruning col-
lapses logic altogether. These results suggest that
structured, logic-aware pruning is not just a token-
reduction strategy, but a way to tailor supervision to
match the abstraction capacity of SLMs, enhancing
generalization and interpretability.
5
Conclusion
We revisit Long-CoT compression through a
capability-aligned perspective and introduce Prune-
on-Logic, a framework that transforms reasoning
traces into logic graphs and selectively prunes low-
utility reasoning steps. Unlike traditional length-
based compression, our approach improves reason-
ing by removing redundant components, especially
in the verification phase, enhancing both accuracy
and efficiency. Extensive empirical experiments
across three logic-aware pruning strategies demon-
8


## Page 9


strate that pruning verification steps consistently
boosts model performance, with larger models ben-
efiting more due to redundant reasoning structures.
Our findings highlight that logic-based pruning is
not only an efficient compression technique but
also an effective tool for strengthening small lan-
guage models, paving the way for scalable and
high-quality Long-CoT reasoning distillation.
Limitations
While our work provides the first structured
comparison
of
three
logic-aware
pruning
strategies—All-chain,
Reasoning-only,
and
Verification-only—under a capability-aligned lens,
it assumes access to high-quality logic graphs and
verification labels, which may limit applicability
in fully unsupervised or noisy domains.
Our
pruning operates at the step level without explicitly
modeling fine-grained symbolic dependencies or
temporal coherence beyond local spans. Moreover,
while we focus on small language models (SLMs),
how pruning interacts with medium- or large-scale
models remains underexplored. Finally, although
we frame pruning as a form of lossless structural
compression, some utility loss may still occur
in highly entangled reasoning chains where
step interdependencies are subtle and hard to
disentangle.
We view this as an initial step toward principled
CoT simplification, and future work may extend
our framework to dynamic pruning at inference
time (Wang et al., 2025), graph-based consistency
enforcement, and hybrid supervision regimes.

Logic Unit Segmentation Prompt (Plogic)
Logic Unit Segmentation Prompt (Plogic)
### Instruction ###
You are an expert in mathematical reasoning and logical discourse parsing. Given a problem-
solving passage involving computation, your task is to segment the passage into discrete reasoning
steps and commentary, then classify each segment as either a node or a connection, following a
structured logic analysis framework.
### Node Identification ###
• Mark segments that include calculations, algebraic manipulations, definitions, or logical
conclusions.
• Always mark the final boxed answer as a node.
• Examples: symbolic equations, variable definitions, result statements.
### Connection Detection ###
• Identify segments that contain narrative transitions, setup, intent, commentary, or rhetorical
expressions.
• These do not affect the logic or math derivation.
• Examples: “Let’s simplify this. . . ”, “Now I need to check...”, “So far, we’ve shown...”
### Segment Splitting and Granularity ###
• If a sentence mixes logic and commentary, split it into parts.
• Use punctuation (commas, periods) as natural split points.
• Label each part accordingly.
### Output Format ###
<node begin>Logical computation or reasoning here</node end>
<connection begin>Narrative or
commentary here</connection end>
Preserve original sentence order. Strictly follow the format—do not add explanations.

### Instruction ###
You are an expert in logical graph construction and coherence analysis. Given a list of logic
units that have been segmented and labeled as <node> or <connection>, your task is to assign

### Edge Assignment Rules ###
• Draw a directed edge from node A to node B if A provides information, computation, or
reasoning that B builds on.
• Use nearby connection segments to justify the link, ensure that the target node is the immediate
successor of the selected connection in the text.
• Do not connect unrelated nodes; avoid spurious links.
### Self-Verification Phase ###
• After assigning all edges, perform a self-verification step:
– Check if every non-initial node has at least one valid predecessor.
– Ensure there are no circular dependencies unless logically necessary.
– Flag any node with unclear inputs or outputs.
