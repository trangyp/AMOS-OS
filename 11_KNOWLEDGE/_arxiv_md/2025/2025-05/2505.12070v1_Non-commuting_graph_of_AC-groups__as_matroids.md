Abstract
Let G be a non-abelian group and let Z(G) be the center of G. Associate a graph
ΓG (called non-commuting graph of G) as follows: Take G\Z(G) as the vertices
of ΓG and join x and y, whenever xy ̸= yx. In this paper, we show that a finite
group G is an AC-group, if and only if, the associated non-commuting graph
of G is a matroid. Leveraging the properties of matroids, we further delve into
the characteristics of AC-groups. Additionally, we provide a formula to compute
the clique number of the non-commuting graph of AC-groups, offering a new
perspective on the structure of these groups.
Keywords: AC-group, CC-group, matroid, non-commuting graph.
MSC Classification: 20B05; 20B30; 20D60
1 Introduction
Given a group G, there exist numerous methods to associate a graph G (see, for
example, [1, 4, 10]). By studying the properties of these associated graphs, we can
uncover intriguing characteristics of the algebraic structures they represent. One such
class of graphs is matroids, defined as graphs whose complement is a disjoint union of
complete graphs. Matroids abstract and generalize the concept of linear independence

found in vector spaces. We adopt one of the many equivalent definitions of matroids
for our study (see [9]). Under this definition, matroids are identified as graphs whose
complement forms a disjoint union of complete graphs.
Among all graphs associated with finite groups, we focus on non-commuting
graphs. We aim to investigate the properties of finite groups whose non-commutative
graph is a matroid. We demonstrate that the terminology of matroid theory can
simplify the expression of complex combinatorial results.
While this paper primarily considers non-commuting graphs, the results can be
readily generalized to any graph associated with a finite group.
The paper is structured as follows: The first section introduces preliminaries about
graphs, matroids, and finite groups. The second section provides the technical proofs
required for our main results. The third section presents the main results. We conclude
the paper with a conclusion and some illustrative examples.
2 Preliminaries
All groups in this paper considered to be finite. Let G be a finite group. For x, y ∈G
the commutator of x, y denoted by [x, y], is xyx−1y−1. Let G be a non-abelian group
and let Z(G) be the center of G. Associate a graph ΓG (called non-commuting graph
of G) in the following manner: Take G\Z(G) as the vertices of ΓG and join x and
y, whenever xy ̸= yx. The concept of a non-commuting graph of a group was first
introduced in [1] in 2006.
Definition 2.1. A group G is called an AC-group, if every non-central element of G
has an abelian centralizer. Similarly, a finite group G is called a CC-group, if every
non-central element of G has a cyclic centralizer.
By CG(a) we mean the centralizer of a in G. We also denote by Cm, a cyclic group
of order m. For any natural number n , the alternating group of degree n is denoted
by An , and the symmetric group of degree n by Sn. . We further define Q4n as the
generalized quaternion group of order 4n. It is presented as follows:
Q4n =< x, y |x2n = 1, y2 = xn, y−1xy = x−1 >
. This notation provides a comprehensive description of the group structure.
For any simple graph G (undirected, with no loops or multiple edges), we denote
the sets of the vertices and the edges of G by V (G) and E(G), respectively. The degree
deg(v) of a vertex v in G is the number of edges incident to v. A connected component
of G is a maximal connected subgraph, meaning it’s not possible to add any more
vertices or edges to the subgraph without breaking its connectivity or connecting it
to other components. We denote by c(G) the number of connected components of G.
A subset X of the vertices of G is called a clique if the induced subgraph on X is a
complete graph. The maximum size of a clique in a graph G is called the clique number
of G and denoted by ω(G). A subset X of the vertices of G is called an independent set,
if the induced subgraph on X has no edges. The maximum size of an independent set
in a graph G is called the independence number of G and denoted by α(G). Matroids
were defined independently in 1930 by Nakasawa [8] and Whitney [11]. There is a close
similarity between certain results in graph theory and their analogues in transversal

Definition 2.2. Let V be a finite non empty set and let H ⊂2V . We say that H =
(V, H) is an (abstract) simplicial complex (or hereditary collection) if H is nonempty
and closed under taking subsets.
A nonempty element I ∈H is called a simplex or a face. Its dimension is dim I =
|I|−1. A maximal face of H (under inclusion) is called a facet. We use the convention
dim∅= −1. Then the dimension of H is defined by
dim H = max{dim X|X ∈H}.
For every k ≥0, we write
Pk(V ) = {X ⊂V ||X| = k} and P≤k(V ) = {X ⊂V ||X| ≤k}.
Definition 2.3. A simplicial complex H = (V, H) is called trim, if it contains all
singleton subsets of V .
A simplicial complex H satisfies the exchange property if:
(EP) For all I, J ∈H with |I| = |J| + 1, there exists some i ∈I such that J ∪{i} = I.
Definition 2.4. A simplicial complex satisfying the exchange property is called a
matroid.
We can view (finite undirected) graphs as trim simplicial complexes of dimension
less than or equal to one. If H = (V, H) is such a complex, we view V (identified often
with P1(V )) as the set of vertices and E = P2(V ) ∩H as the set of edges. Note that
this definition excludes the existence of loops or multiple edges, so we are meaning
graphs in the strictest sense of the word.

Clearly, within a graph G, not every clique necessarily extends to a maximum
clique. However, in the following Lemma, we demonstrate that such an extension is
possible if G is a matroid.
Lemma 3.2. Let the graph G is a matroid and ω(G) = n. Then every clique in G is
extendable to a clique of size n.
Proof. Let H be a clique of size k < n in G. Therefore, H in the complement of G is
an independent set of size k. On the other hand, the complement of G is the union
of n complete graphs. Then there exist n −k connected components of ¯G whose do
not intersect H. Adding one element from each one of those connected components
we reach a maximal independent set in the complement of G which is a clique of size
n in G.
Lemma 3.3. Let every clique in the graph G be extendable to a clique of maximum
size. Then for every v ∈V (G), we have deg(v) ≥ω(G) −1.

be a clique of size ω(G). It means that deg v0 ≥ω(G) −1.

Theorem 4.3. The group G is an AC-group, if and only if ΓG is a matroid.
Proof. Let G be an AC-group. Let {a1, a2, · · · , an} be the maximal subset of pairwise
non-commuting elements of G. By Lemma 3.4, we know that G\Z(G) is the disjoint
union of CG(ai)\Z(G), for 1 ≤i ≤n. Now, it is easy to check that, for 1 ≤i ≤n,
CG(ai)\Z(G) are connected components of ¯ΓG and they are complete graphs. Then,
by Theorem 3.1, ΓG is a matroid. Now, let ΓG be a matroid. Suppose, by contradiction,
that the group G is not an AC-group. Then, for some a ∈G, the centralizer a, i.e.
CG(a), is not abelian. Hence, there exist x, y ∈CG(a), such that xy ̸= yx while xa =
ax, ya = ay. It means that the graph ΓG is not a matroid, which is a contradiction.

6 Conclusion
Let χ be a property of a finite group G. We define the graph Γχ such that its vertices
are the elements of the group, and two vertices x and y are connected if and only if
< x, y > forms a finite group with the property χ. We define a χ-group as a group
where all centralizers possess the property χ. Analogous to the proof of Theorem 4.3,
we can establish that a group G is a χ-group if and only if the associated graph Γχ is
a matroid.
