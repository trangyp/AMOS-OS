Understanding nature’s choice of genetic languages
Apoorva D. Patela,b
aCentre for High Energy Physics, Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru, 560012, India
bInternational Centre for Theoretical Sciences, Bengaluru, 560089, India
Abstract
All living organisms use the same genetic languages in their molecular bi-
ology machinery. They can be understood as the optimal solutions to the
replication tasks involving DNA and proteins. These solutions perfectly ﬁt
the pattern predicted by Grover’s algorithm, which is more eﬃcient than
the best Boolean search algorithm (i.e. binary tree search) that predicts a
diﬀerent pattern. The challenge is to demonstrate how Grover’s algorithm
would be executed in vivo by the living organisms.
Keywords:
Boolean algorithm, Database search, Genetic languages,
Grover’s algorithm, Wave dynamics
1. Biological Facts
Studies in molecular biology have established certain general properties
of the genetic languages that encode the hereditary information of living or-
ganisms [1]. They have important implications about how these languages
may have naturally evolved:
• The langauges of genes and proteins are universal. The same 4 nucleotide
bases and 20 amino acids are used by all living organisms, despite the fact
that other nucleotide bases and amino acids exist in living cells. Clearly,
evolution has selected speciﬁc languages.
• The genetic information is encoded close to the data compression limit (for
genes) and maximal packing (for proteins). This indicates that optimisation
of information storage has occurred.
• Mutations occur during evolution. Darwinian selection can eliminate sub-
optimal competitors, provided there is suﬃcient time to explore various pos-
sibilities. That can make the surviving languages universal, instead of their
appearance as an accidental event.

The only task that the genetic languages implement, i.e. replication by
sequentially assembling complementary building blocks on top of pre-existing
master templates, is simple enough to explicitly determine their optimal
structure in various algorithmic settings. The complementary base-pairing
with the master template is a binary step, either it happens or it does not.
Since the building blocks are distinguishable, this step can be called a binary
search oracle query. The optimisation problem is then to ﬁnd the suitable
complementary building blocks using as few binary oracle queries as possible.
2. Optimal Search Algorithm
For a database of size N, the best Boolean search algorithm takes log2 N
binary oracle queries.
It is a straightfoward binary tree search, and one
complmentary pair of nucleotide bases is suﬃcient to encode the genetic
information. It requires the database to be sorted, but we may assume that
nature would have found a way to do that were this algorithm advantageous.
In reality, nature has selected more complicated genetic languages than the
minimal binary ones.

3. Physical Implementations of Grover’s Algorithm
Grover’s algorithm starts with an unbiased uniform superposition state,
and applies two reﬂection operators alternately for a number of iterations, un-
til the target state is reached. One of the reﬂection operators is the response
to the binary oracle query, and the other is the reﬂection across the uniform
superposition state. A speciﬁc criterion is needed to stop the iterative steps,
when the target state is reached.
The key feature of the algorithm is wave dynamics that allows superposi-
tion. Once coherent wave modes are available, the algorithm needs nothing
more than the two reﬂection operations. Figure 1 illustrates how the algo-
rithm works in the simplest instance, unambiguously identifying one out of
four items in the database using a single binary oracle query. In contrast, a
single binary oracle query in a Boolean setting would only identify one out
of two items in the database.
The ingredients required to implement Grover’s algorithm are quite sim-
ple. It suﬃces to have coherent wave modes that can be superposed and
phase-shifted, and so the algorithm can be implemented using classical wave
dynamics as well [4, 5]. Figure 1 also points out the corresponding physical
steps. Generically, synchronised oscillations perturbed by scattering from an
impurity lead to amplitude ampliﬁcation at the impurity site.
Note that Grover’s algorithm is referred to as a search algorithm due to
the quantum interpretation of |amplitude|2 as probability. In the classical
wave dynamics setting, however, |amplitude|2 represents energy of the wave
mode. In a coupled system of oscillators, the centre-of-mass mode plays the
role of the unbiased uniform superposition state, and reﬂection operations can
be implemented as elastic collisions. Such a scenario has been demonstrated
in a high school science project [6]. Furthermore, energy ampliﬁcation in a
particular mode beyond a threshold can start a new reaction that eﬀectively
terminates the algorithm. The consequences can be dramatic in processes
whose rates are governed by the Boltzmann factor, exp(−E/kT), where the
energy appears in the exponent.

4. Grover’s Algorithm in Natural Setting
At the molecular level, biological systems have features appropriate for
implementing Grover’s algorithm in the classical wave version:
• Various biomolecules are obtained by digesting food, and then reassembled
according to speciﬁc prescriptions. In this metabolic process, the elementary
components are randomly ﬂoating around in the cellular environment, and
their assembly in a speciﬁc order is unsorted database search.
• Time is highly precious while space is fairly expendable, in the biological
context. A small diﬀerence in population growth rates, even an advantage
of a fraction of a percent, is suﬃcient for one species to overwhelm another
over many generations. The number of seeds produced by a plant, and the
number of cells in a tissue, are examples of large scale parallelisation of the
eﬀort required for carrying out the desired task. The additional cost of spa-

tial resources can indeed be tolerable for small values of N, and the best
Boolean algorithm can be beaten in the race for temporal advantage.
• The tasks of eﬃcient manipulations of energy have high priority in the
non-equilibrium metabolic processes of living organisms. The kinetic isotope
eﬀect demonstrates that the rates of processes are inﬂuenced by the vibra-
tional degrees of freedom [7], while signatures of coupled vibrational and
electronic degrees of freedom have been observed in enzyme catalysis [8].
Beyond these features, it is worth noting that:
(a) Grover’s algorithm is quite robust against perturbations.
Due to its
optimal nature, its outcome is aﬀected only at the second order in physical
disturbances.
(b) Q = 2 for N = 10 is an approximate solution of Eq.(1). It agrees with
the partition of the amino acids, into two classes of ten each, by a doublet
genetic code that is likely to have preceded the current triplet genetic code
during evolution [9, 10, 11].
(c) For its success, Grover’s algorithm requires coherent superposition of wave
modes to survive long enough. But an apparent superposition would suﬃce,
where the molecular diﬀusion cycling time between diﬀerent selection options
is short compared to the overall base-pairing time.
Replacement of the fragile quantum version of Grover’s algorithm by the
stable classical wave version keeps its temporal advantage unchanged. Given
the simplicity of the algorithm (the required steps are only those illustrated
in Figure 1), it is perfectly plausible that biological evolution discovered
it by trial-and-error exploration, and thereafter its optimality made it an
inalienable part of life.
5. Experimental Veriﬁcation
The arguments of the preceding sections make a compelling case that
the classical wave version of Grover’s algorithm is fundamentally important
for the structure of genetic languages. Several scenarios of how that can be
have been proposed [12], but the ultimate test must come from experimental
veriﬁcation. Two possibilities come to mind:
(1) An indirect, and easier to implement, test would check the optimality of
the algorithm by a competition between the natural genetic language and
artiﬁcally constructed competitors [13]. The competitors would use less or
more number of letters in their genetic alphabet, while encoding the same
amount of genetic information. Technology for performing such tests is avail-

(2) A direct test would observe the intermediate steps of the genetic repli-

Such a conﬁrmation would tell us that indeed the advantage of classical
wave search made nature select the universal genetic languages observed
today, compared to the minimal Boolean ones.
