Joint Reconstruction of Activity and Attenuation in
PET by Diffusion Posterior Sampling in Wavelet
Coefficient Space
Cl´ementine Phung-Ngoc, Alexandre Bousse, Antoine De Paepe, Thibaut Merlin, Baptiste Laurent,
Hong-Phuong Dang, Olivier Saut, Catherine Cheze-Le-Rest and Dimitris Visvikis, Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—Attenuation correction (AC) is necessary for accurate
activity quantification in positron emission tomography (PET).
Conventional reconstruction methods typically rely on attenuation
maps derived from a co-registered computed tomography (CT) or
magnetic resonance (MR) scan. However, this additional scan may
complicate the imaging workflow, introduce misalignment artifacts
and increase radiation exposure. In this paper, we propose a
joint reconstruction of activity and attenuation (JRAA) approach
that eliminates the need for auxiliary anatomical imaging by
relying solely on emission data. This framework combines wavelet
diffusion model (WDM) and diffusion posterior sampling (DPS)
to reconstruct fully three-dimensional (3-D) data. Experimental
results on simulated data show our method outperforms maximum
likelihood activity and attenuation (MLAA) and MLAA-UNet
with U-Net-based post processing, and yields high-quality noise-
free reconstructions across various count settings with time-
of-flight (TOF). It is also able to reconstruct non-TOF data,
although the reconstruction quality significantly degrades in
low-count (LC) conditions, limiting its practical effectiveness
in such settings. Nonetheless, a non-TOF Biograph mMR real
data reconstruction with joint scatter estimation highlights
the potential of the method for clinical applications. This
approach represents a step towards stand-alone PET imaging
by reducing the dependence on anatomical modalities while
maintaining quantification accuracy, even in LC scenarios when
TOF information is available. Our code is available on GitHub
at https://github.com/clemphg/jraa-dps.
Index Terms—Positron emission tomography (PET), Deep
learning (DL), Joint reconstruction of activity and attenuation
(JRAA).

OSITRON emission tomography (PET) is a nuclear
medical imaging technique used to visualize functional
mechanisms within the body. It involves injecting a positron-
emitting radiopharmaceutical as a tracer, designed specifically
for the targeted function. This tracer concentrates in the regions
of interest via the metabolism, and pairs of γ photons resulting
from the electron-positron annihilation events are detected by
an array of ring detectors placed around the patient. Finally,
an image of the activity distribution is reconstructed from the
emission data. PET has a wide range of applications in fields
such as oncology, cardiology or neurology.
PET technology has undergone significant advancements
since its introduction in the 1970s [1], particularly in terms
of sensitivity, spatial resolution, and quantitative accuracy.
Modern PET systems are typically integrated with anatomical
imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) or
magnetic resonance (MR), enabling the generation of γ photon
attenuation maps for attenuation correction (AC)1 and scatter
estimation. These hybrid systems not only facilitate more
accurate quantitative imaging but also provide complementary
functional and anatomical information, thereby assisting clini-
cians in diagnosis. Furthermore, contemporary PET systems
increasingly leverage time-of-flight (TOF) information to
enhance spatial localization.
In recent years, several researchers have explored the
possibility of performing joint reconstruction of activity and
attenuation (JRAA) using emission data alone. Defrise et al. [2]
demonstrated that, in TOF PET, it is theoretically feasible
to estimate both the activity distribution and the attenuation
coefficients up to a multiplicative constant, provided the TOF
resolution is sufficiently high. Building on this foundation,
numerous JRAA methods have been proposed with promising
results, including maximum likelihood activity and attenuation
(MLAA) [3] and other similar methods [4] (see Berker et
al. [5] for a review). These advancements open new avenues
for improving PET imaging, such as reducing patient radiation
dose, which could enable more frequent scans for improved
monitoring and patient screening. They also offer opportunities
for enhanced respiratory motion management, as the reliance on
CT snapshots at each respiratory phase could be mitigated [6].
The above-mentioned JRAA techniques are not without
limitations. In particular, they suffer from activity–attenuation

Artificial intelligence (AI) and more particularly deep
learning (DL) techniques have changed the paradigm in PET
imaging, for image reconstruction [7] and post-processing [8].
DL has also been investigated for AC in PET, where approaches
for PET AC from emission data only can be divided in two
main categories [9].
Direct methods use deep models to directly estimate the
AC activity image from the non-AC image [10], [11]. They
are particularly sensitive to the characteristics of the training
data, since they learn a direct image-to-image mapping. In
such approaches, the measured emission data are not explicitly
used to guide the reconstruction once the non-AC image has
been formed. Although these methods can provide good results
when applied to data that are well represented in the training
set, they do not enforce consistency with the measured PET
data and may therefore be less robust in out-of-distribution
cases, for example in the presence of unusual anatomy, lesions,
or different count levels.
Indirect AC methods aim to predict a synthetic attenuation
map, which can then be incorporated into a conventional itera-
tive reconstruction algorithm such as expectation-maximization
(EM). As a result, the final activity reconstruction remains
guided by the emission measurements. The attenuation map
can be predicted from the non-AC PET image [12], [13], or
from an MLAA reconstruction [14]. However, both strategies
still depend on intermediate reconstructions that are themselves
noisy, and the quality of the final attenuation map remains
strongly linked to the acquisition settings represented in the
training data.
One way to mitigate this dependence on acquisition settings
is to use diffusion-prior-based JRAA methods. Diffusion models
(DMs) have arisen as an alternative to generative adversarial
networks (GANs) for image generation and other tasks [15],
and have been widely explored in medical imaging [16].
Score-based DMs are a generative technique which consists in
gradually transforming a collection of images into white noise
via a stochastic differential equation (SDE), then, in learning
the inverse SDE by score matching using a convolutional neural
network (CNN), and finally to generate images from random
white noise. The trained DM can be used to solve inverse
problems with approaches such as diffusion posterior sampling
(DPS) and have been used in image reconstruction such as
in PET [17], [18] and CT [19], [20]. In this setting, the DM
acts as a prior in the reconstruction algorithm rather than
as a direct supervised predictor. Consequently, acquisition-
specific factors, including scan duration, noise level, TOF
resolution, and scanner geometry, are explicitly accounted
for in the forward model and data-consistency term. This
makes the approach less dependent on the specific acquisition
conditions represented in the training set, while ensuring that
the reconstructed images remain consistent with the measured
emission data.
In this work, we propose to leverage DMs models for the
task of JRAA via DPS, using a model trained on activity–
attenuation image pair. The proposed JRAA-DPS method (cf.
Figure 1) extends our preliminary study [21], which was limited
to two-dimensional (2-D) phantoms. Bae et al. [22] presented
a similar approach, although their DM operates on attenuation
images only.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
• Building on our previous study, we propose a DM-based
JRAA method, namely JRAA-DPS, which leverages a
diffusion prior learned from activity–attenuation pairs for
the joint reconstruction of activity and attenuation from
emission data alone.
• The proposed method is agnostic to acquisition parameters
such as scan duration, TOF resolution, and scanner
geometry. Therefore, a single trained model can be applied
to various clinical scenarios without retraining.
• To process large three-dimensional (3-D) volumes, we
employed a wavelet diffusion model (WDM) trained on
eight-channel wavelet coefficient images [23].
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section II
provides background on JRAA. Section III describes our
proposed method, namely JRAA-DPS, followed by details of
the experimental setup on simulated and real data in Section IV.
Section V presents the experimental results, and Section VI
discusses the limitations of JRAA-DPS. Finally, Section VII
concludes the paper.

C. 3-D Wavelet Diffusion Models
The utilization of DMs to 3-D volumes is a challenging task
as it requires considerably more computational resources [37].
A common workaround consists in using 2-D DMs trained on
individual slices combined with a hand-crafted regularization
to promote in-between slice consistency [17], [38]. Similarly,
several 2-D DMs can be leveraged across two or three
orthogonal directions [39]. One can also feed the DM adjacent
slices to mitigate axial artifacts [40], [41]. Finally, latent
diffusion model (LDM) use data compression methods to train
diffusion in a lower-dimensional latent space [42], [43].

D. Joint Scatter Estimation
PET reconstruction requires corrections for scanner sensi-
tivity, scatter, and random coincidences. While sensitivity and
random events can be estimated from the scanner and raw
measurements [46], most scatter estimation methods utilize
the attenuation map, such as single scatter simulation (SSS)
[47] and Monte-Carlo simulations [48]. However, in absence
of a pre-estimated attenuation map, scatter must be estimated
differently. AI approaches have been developed [49], [50],
but they require ACFs. Alternatively, an energy-based scatter
estimation technique could be used [51]. In this work, we
propose to deploy a joint scatter estimation method in which
scatter is progressively refined during DPS, similarly to the
approach proposed by Lorenzon et al. [52].

The proposed framework can reconstruct data with diverse
acquisition characteristics, provided that these are appropriately
modeled. In particular, factors such as scan duration (i.e., noise
level), scanner geometry, TOF information, normalization, and
other corrections are explicitly incorporated into the forward
model (2), allowing a single trained DM to be applied across
different settings without retraining. Furthermore, the joint
scatter estimation step is optional and can be omitted when
scatter is negligible or when a scatter estimate is available,
resulting in a simplified sampling procedure.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
Model training and reconstruction were implemented with
PyTorch. The hyperparameters of JRAA-DPS were finely tuned
to optimize the output images. We used parallelproj [53] to
perform projections and backprojections with 4-mm full width
at half maximum (FWHM) detector resolution. JRAA-DPS was
implemented with 10 subsets for the activity and attenuation
updates (Step 8 and Step 9 in Algorithm 1).
We evaluated our method on both simulated (Experiment
1 in Section IV-D) and real PET data (Experiment 2 in
Section IV-E).
Retrospective patient data were used for model training,
raw data simulation, and image reconstruction. All patients
provided informed consent for the use of their data.
A. Patient Data Images
We used 360 [18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/CT
patient 3-D images with a 500-mm field of view (FOV)
provided by CHU Poitiers to conduct our experiments. PET
images were first upsampled to match the 1.50×0.97×0.97-mm3
CT resolution. CT scans were converted from HU to 511-keV
attenuation maps and beds were removed [54]. The images
were then downsampled to a 2.00×1.95×1.95-mm3 resolution
(256×256 slices). Vertically trimming from shoulders to pelvis
instead of head to mid-thighs was applied. Finally, PET images
were clipped to 1 × 105 Bq/mL while attenuation maps were
clipped to 0.025 mm-1 to remove outliers.

1) Normalization: Attenuation maps were linearly mapped
to the [−1, 1] range while PET images were normalized as

While attenuation map values occupy a relatively compact
physical range, PET images typically have a wide and a
right-skewed intensity distribution. The cube-root transform
compresses the wide dynamic range of PET, limiting the
influence of a few high uptake voxels while retaining contrast
in lower-activity regions. The resulting normalized PET range
of [-1, 2] aims to make the activity and attenuation histograms
comparable, and to facilitate training on the activity–attenuation
image pairs. This normalization strategy is similar to the
logarithmic linear normalization proposed by Sun et al. [55],
which was shown to be effective for DM training on PET
images by compressing the dynamic range.
2) Training parameters: We used 300 patient cases for train-
ing and 30 for model validation. We extracted 128×256×256
sub-volumes, resulting in 1,168 volumes for training and 112
for validation. JRAA-DPS uses a linear variance schedule with
T = 1000, β1 = 10−4, and βT = 0.02, as described by Ho et

C. Methods for Comparison
We compared our method with two JRAA reconstruction
approaches: (i) MLAA [3], and (ii) the DL-based approach
proposed by Toyonaga et al. [56] which we refer to as MLAA-
UNet. The latter uses a U-Net to produce a pseudo-attenuation
map from the MLAA attenuation estimate, which is then used
to perform AC reconstruction via OSEM.
In this study, we did not consider JRAA-DPS with DM priors
trained separately on λ and µ, as prior results have shown that
a jointly trained prior over (λ, µ) consistently achieves better
performance [21].
To avoid strong influence of isolated voxels with high values,
we clipped each activity reconstruction to the 99.9% quantile
of the reference activity image.
D. Experiment 1—Simulated Data
1) Raw Data Simulation and Reconstruction: We used 30
patients for testing, cropping each volume pair x = (λ, µ)
to 128×256×256 voxels. Emission data y were simulated
following (1) from activity–attenuation pairs.
We varied scan duration τ to simulate different count levels.
High-count (HC) data were simulated with a scan duration
τ = 1 × 10−5, and low-count (LC) data were simulated with
τ = 1 × 10−7 (1% of the counts). Random and scatter events
were not included in the simulations.
The parallelproj projector used in simulations included 48
rings, each with 300 detectors, and a scanner diameter of
700 mm. The maximum ring difference was set to 44 rings
and a radial trim of 30 LORs was applied. Each projection
consists of 241 radial elements, 150 angles of view, and 2,292
sinograms. When applicable, projections also include 13 TOF
bins with a 58-mm (≈400 ps) FWHM TOF resolution.
MLAA and MLAA-UNet were evaluated with TOF data
only, while JRAA-DPS were evaluated on both TOF and non-
TOF data for reconstruction. For MLAA (and subsequently
MLAA-UNet), attenuation was initialized and updated within a
predefined support region, which is obtained from an initial non-
AC TOF PET reconstruction. Reconstruction hyperparameters
are summarized in Table I.

V. EVALUATION
A. Experiment 1—Simulated Data Reconstructions
HC and LC reconstructions are respectively shown in
Figures 2 and 3 for two different patients. Metrics averaged
over the testing dataset are shown in Table III, while scatter
plots of SSIM against PSNR are shown in Figure 4.
With regards to PSNR, SSIM and NRMSE, JRAA-DPS
outperforms other methods for activity reconstruction in both
count settings. Omitting TOF information degrades JRAA-DPS
performance, but it still achieves better reconstructions than
MLAA and MLAA-UNet with TOF.
In the LC setting, MLAA-UNet outperforms JRAA-DPS
on attenuation map reconstruction across the full test set (cf.
Table III). MLAA-UNet reconstructs a smooth attenuation map
while JRAA-DPS adds in some details which can be erroneous,
thus driving metrics down (cf. Figures 3(c) and 3(d)). In spite of
that, JRAA-DPS recovers the activity image better (cf. Table III
and Figure 3(d)), suggesting those erroneous details do not alter
significantly the quality of the reconstructed activity image.
MLAA and MLAA-UNet activity reconstructions suffer
from noise amplification and partial volume effect (PVE) as
a result of the limited resolution of the imaging system. This
is especially noticeable in the LC setting (cf. Figure 3(b) and
3(c)). Conversely, JRAA-DPS with TOF recovers smooth and
noiseless activity images (cf. Figure 3(d)).
JRAA-DPS without TOF in the HC setting still yields a
reasonably accurate activity reconstruction (cf. Figure 2(e) and
Figure 4). In the LC setting, the image is degraded and the
reconstructed attenuation map is visibly distorted, highlighting
the limited applicability of JRAA-DPS in such conditions (cf.
Figure 3(e)).
Absolute bias is shown against STD in Figure 5, for the HC
and LC settings. JRAA-DPS achieves a better bias–variance
trade-off than MLAA and MLAA-UNet in both count settings
for activity reconstruction, although the performance gain is
clearer in the LC setting. JRAA-DPS has lower bias than
MLAA for attenuation map reconstruction, but its variance is
on the higher end. However, STD of JRAA-DPS attenuation
reconstructions remains similar to the STD of JRAA-DPS
activity reconstructions.
The RC and the CRC of the added hot lesion is shown in
Figure 6. While MLAA achieves the lowest RC and CRC in
the HC setting, JRAA-DPS is able to accurately recover both
the intensity and the contrast in the studied ROI. Furthermore,
our proposed method seems robust to hyperparameter selection
in HC, as varying ζt produces a limited spread in RC and CRC
values. In a LC setting, JRAA-DPS outperforms both MLAA
and MLAA-UNet, provided the measurement-contribution
parameter ζt is sufficiently high.
Reconstruction variability of JRAA-DPS is illustrated in (cf.
Figure 7), where mean and STD images are computed from 30
reconstructions obtained from a single measurement under both
count settings. We observe that variability increases in the LC
regime and in the absence of TOF information. This behavior
arises because the DM-based stochastic prior strength increases
as the measurement contributions ζt and ξt are reduced to limit
noise in the reconstructions (cf. Table I). We further observe
that variability is highest in regions of elevated tracer uptake in
the reconstructed PET images, whereas it is most pronounced at
body–background and lung–body boundaries in the attenuation
maps.
Finally, sampling with DM is known to be computationally
expensive and we observe the proposed JRAA-DPS method is
approximately 14 times slower than MLAA and MLAA-UNet
in the HC setting with TOF (cf. Table IV).

training to accommodate variations in geometry, noise level,
or the absence of TOF information. In addition, a joint scatter
estimation strategy can be integrated into the framework at
sampling time, without requiring additional training. Therefore,
a single trained DM can be applied to reconstruct data with
diverse characteristics (e.g., different count levels, TOF or
non-TOF acquisitions, and varying SFs).
However, the diffusion prior is learned from a specific
image distribution and may be sensitive to domain shifts
not captured by the forward model, such as differences in
radiopharmaceutical uptake patterns. In this work, the DM
was trained and evaluated using data from a single tracer (i.e.,
18F-FDG). Extending the approach to other imaging conditions
may therefore require additional training. This limitation could
be addressed by training the DM on multi-tracer data (e.g.,
with a tracer-conditioning mechanism), or by developing tracer-
specific models through targeted training or fine-tuning.
The joint scatter estimation strategy we used was able to
partially mitigate the impact of scatter on reconstructions, but
tended to underestimate the scatter contribution, which led to
overestimation of the activity in low-uptake regions. In the
end, on the real patient dataset, MLAA-UNet yielded a higher-
quality activity reconstruction than JRAA-DPS on the clinical

Generative models are prone to hallucination, meaning they
produce features that are not supported by the measurements.
In our experiments, these were especially noticeable in the
reconstructed attenuation maps: examples include missing bone
detail, poorly recovered small air-filled regions, incoherent
structures in the lungs, and shifted or extended arms. Although
these artifacts did not appear to substantially affect the activity
reconstruction in our experiments, especially when they were
confined to peripheral or out-of-FOV regions, they remain a
limitation of the method. However, such artifacts had little effect
on the reconstructed activity images in our tests. JRAA-DPS
was able to recover high image quality activity reconstructions,
even without TOF information, provided the count level was
sufficient. In practice, small errors in the attenuation map
may not significantly impact the reconstructed activity image,
especially given the limited spatial resolution of PET systems.
The attenuation map obtained in PET/MR systems is prone
to errors (e.g., missing bones, simplified structures) due to
the challenges of deriving accurate attenuation information
from MR images, yet is considered clinically acceptable
[59]. In addition, activity–attenuation cross-talk is reduced
when using TOF information, which improves robustness
to attenuation map errors [60]. Nonetheless, quantifying the
frequency, magnitude and clinical impact of such hallucinations
on activity reconstructions is important to assess the clinical
viability of diffusion-based reconstruction methods such as
JRAA-DPS.
An important limitation of JRAA-DPS concerns the recon-
struction duration. Sampling from DMs is known to be time-
consuming, particularly when using the standard DDPM, and
reconstructing a sample of simulated data with JRAA-DPS
currently takes around 30 minutes. Employing the denoising
diffusion implicit model (DDIM) with its accelerated inference
variant could significantly reduce reconstruction time [31].
Additionally, the computation of the gradient of the data fidelity
term can also be accelerated [34], [35]. However, comparative
evaluation would be necessary to assess the impact of this
acceleration on reconstruction accuracy.
Finally, CT-less PET reconstruction methods are also
attractive for respiratory motion compensation, since they
can eliminate the need for gated-CT acquisitions. Previous
studies have investigated joint PET reconstruction and motion
estimation using a single, potentially mismatched, attenuation
map [61], [62]. Building on these works, Elhamiasl et al.
proposed two methods for the joint estimation of activity,
attenuation and motion [6]. Such strategies could be integrated
with our proposed JRAA-DPS framework to enable diffusion-
based motion-compensated CT-less PET reconstruction.
VII. CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduce a novel method for 3-D CT-less
reconstruction of PET. This approach reconstructs accurate
and noise-free PET images without attenuation information.
The framework relies on a prior consisting of a DM trained
to generate activity-attenuation image pairs. Additionally, we
used a WDM to process the large 3-D volumes. Overall, this
method enables radiation dose reduction by eliminating the
need for a CT scan for attenuation correction, and remains
effective even in LC settings. Coupled with a joint scatter
estimation strategy, JRAA-DPS reconstructs real non-TOF
clinical data with high quality, demonstrating its potential for
clinical application. Therefore, it is a promising approach to
generate high-quality PET images from low-dose or non-TOF
PET data without attenuation information. Future work will
focus on exploring sampling acceleration methods to reduce
reconstruction duration and respiratory motion-compensated
JRAA.
