Abstract 
We have developed Aitomia – a platform powered by AI to assist in performing AI-driven 
atomistic and quantum chemical (QC) simulations. This evolving intelligent assistant 
platform is equipped with chatbots and AI agents to help experts and guide non-experts in 
setting up and running atomistic simulations, analyzing simulation results, and summarizing 
them for the user in both textual and graphical forms. Aitomia combines LLM-based agents 
with the MLatom platform to support AI-driven atomistic simulations as well as conventional 
quantum-chemical calculations, including DFT, semiempirical methods such as GFN2-xTB, 
and selected high-level wavefunction-based methods, through interfaces to widely used 
programs such as Gaussian, ORCA, PySCF, and xtb, covering tasks from ground-state and 
excited-state calculations to geometry optimization, thermochemistry, and spectra 
simulations. The multi-agent implementation enables autonomous execution of complex 
computational workflows, such as reaction enthalpy calculations. Aitomia was the first 
intelligent assistant publicly launched on cloud computing platforms for broad-scope 
atomistic simulations (Aitomistic Lab@XMU at https://atom.xmu.edu.cn and Aitomistic Hub 
at https://aitomistic.xyz). Aitomia lowers the barrier to performing atomistic simulations, 
thereby democratizing simulations and accelerating research and development in relevant 
fields.

Introduction 
Computational chemistry has played a transformative role in advancing chemical research 
over the past decades, enabling spectra simulations and interpretation, the elucidation of 
reaction mechanisms, and the generation of training datasets for artificial intelligence (AI)-
assisted design and discovery.1-5 The backbone of such atomistic simulations is quantum 
chemistry (QC) based on first principles, solving the Schrödinger equation by employing 
numerous approximate methods that balance speed and accuracy. AI/machine learning (ML) 
methods are also increasingly used for AI-driven atomistic simulations. However, the 
growing sophistication of computational chemistry tools presents significant challenges for 
researchers. 
Proficiency with these tools requires not only familiarity with complex underlying 
theories and package-specific options but, often, skills in Linux and access to high-
performance computing (HPC) resources. Moreover, simulating realistic chemical processes 
often necessitates the integration of multiple computational packages, compounding the 
complexity for users across the chemical sciences. Despite progress in automating 
computational workflows,6 two critical barriers remain. First, users must manually configure 
crucial simulation parameters, often requiring consultation of extensive and technical user 
manuals. Second, many workflows depend on HPC resources, which are not readily available 
to non-computational researchers, including experimental chemists and students. 
Some of the aforementioned issues are alleviated with graphical user interfaces (GUIs) 
designed for the specific software packages. For example, a popular Gaussian program7for 
QC calculations includes GaussView as its official GUI, which facilitates the creation of 
input files and the analysis of calculation results. Many other packages, such as Materials 
Studio and ADF8, were built to provide seamless experiences for computing job preparation, 
performing, and analyzing the computations. Unfortunately, GUIs do not fully solve the 
above problems, as researchers may still need to learn the GUI's options in addition to those 
of the underlying software, submit jobs to HPC clusters, and seek expert advice. 
Another useful solution lies in the growing number of web-based platforms that provide 
simplified ways to submit and run calculations, often with web GUIs; examples include 
TeraChem9 and ChemCompute.10 We are also involved in the development of similar 
platforms, Aitomistic Hub11 and Aitomistic Lab@XMU12 (successor to the Xiamen 
Atomistic Computing Suite,13 XACS), which enable QC- and AI-driven atomistic 
calculations on HPC clusters via a web browser. An interesting platform is Chemvox,14 
which enables researchers to run calculations using AI-powered speech recognition, allowing 
them to perform basic calculations via voice commands. Another attempt to bring QC 
calculations to general users, including students, is the mobile app MoleculAR, which 
converts 2D handwritten structures into 3D visualizations to improve understanding of 
molecular structures.15 The platforms mentioned above often provide free HPC resources for 
basic use, such as education, and are convenient options for performing QC and ML 
simulations; however, users still need to understand the software's background and the 
supported theoretical methods. These website platforms also provide limited support for 
interpreting and analyzing the results.

With the success of large language models (LLMs) such as the GPT series 16-18from 
OpenAI and Qwen19, it is natural to ask whether LLMs can provide an ultimate solution for 
making QC- and ML-driven atomistic simulations accessible to a wider research community. 
Many studies have been combining LLMs with professional chemistry-related fields in recent 
years,20-23and, unsurprisingly, this trend also extends to atomistic simulations. One recent 
noteworthy attempt is the AutoSolvateWeb platform24 for submitting jobs to simulate 
explicitly solvated molecules. This platform uses an AI agent that guides the user in selecting 
calculation parameters and provides explanations for basic queries; however, it is limited to 
submitting jobs to specific cloud computing services and provides neither analysis of the 
results nor any technical discussion of the calculations. This approach resembles a rule-based 
method to guide the users with rather limited explanations. The feasibility of using LLMs for 
autonomous simulations, from the design of calculations to the reporting of results and 
analyses, has been demonstrated in polymer research.25 LLM assistant MDcrow was recently 
developed to automate molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.26 A more general multi-agent 
system, El Agente, is powered by LLMs to generate and execute complex workflows, e.g., 
involving several ground-state single-point energy calculations, geometry optimizations, and 
frequency analysis using DFT and semi-empirical quantum-mechanical (QM) methods.27 
Efforts toward LLM-powered autonomous simulations are gaining momentum, as evidenced 
by the fact that the above works appeared online within five months, and the latter 
independent preprint on El Agente is dated a few days before the first version28 of this 
preprint. 
While the above research shows the great advancement of LLM accelerating the 
automation in QC field, at the time of the writing, until our work, there has been no online 
accessible platform providing an integrated, all-in-one, solution for both AI/ML and QC 
simulations powered by AI agents, and providing a comprehensive support of a wide range of 
typical simulation tasks ranging from ground- to excited-state single-point calculations, 
geometry optimizations, spectra simulations, etc. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a 
user-friendly platform that reduces technical and computational barriers, enabling a wider 
audience to effectively leverage computational chemistry tools.

Results and discussion 
The scope of atomistic simulations 
Our goal is to design an intelligent assistant capable of performing most key computational 
chemistry tasks using a wide variety of state-of-the-art AI atomistic models and conventional 
QC methods. This is an ambitious goal that presents a formidable challenge, as it requires 
supporting many different software packages and theoretical methods, both QC- and AI-
driven. Luckily, we can leverage the existing MLatom software ecosystem,29 which is 
particularly well-suited to this goal because it seamlessly supports both QC and AI-driven 
atomistic simulations. Hence, our intelligent assistant, Aitomia, achieves its goal by 
leveraging MLatom’s wide range of features. The current capabilities of Aitomia are 
summarized in Figure 2.

Currently, Aitomia can perform tasks such as single-point calculations, geometry 
optimizations, frequency and thermochemistry calculations, and IR and UV/vis spectra 
simulations. For this, it leverages many state-of-the-art pre-trained universal ML models, 
such as the AIQM,30-33 ANI,34-37 OMNI-P,38, 39 and AIMNet40 series. Some of these models,

Aitomia as an assistant for performing standard computational tasks 
Typically, performing single computational tasks, such as geometry optimizations or 
frequency calculations, requires users to be familiar with the corresponding software input 
formats and options. Learning them is a labor-intensive process, impeding the widespread 
adoption of various software and new techniques. To overcome this barrier, we have built AI 
agents for Aitomia, which invoke LLMs to interact with the user, choose an appropriate 
computational task requested by the user, submit the computing job to the HPC server, 
retrieve the output, analyze it, and report the results back to the user in text and graphical 
format. Additionally, we implemented quality-of-life features that enable Aitomia to retrieve 
molecular structures from user-provided chemical names. With Aitomia’s assistance, AI-
driven atomistic and QC calculations can be executed and analyzed autonomously, with the 
input and output files and scripts organized in a clear directory structure for the user’s review 
and results retrieval. 
We show an example in Figure 3. The user interacts with Aitomia and requests the 
structure of the hexanol molecule, which Aitomia returns, allowing the user to analyze it in 
the web GUI (Figure 3a). Later, the user requests infrared (IR) spectra calculations for this 
molecule, and Aitomia calculates them using the AIQM2 method33 (Figure 3b). The 
calculations finish in seconds, and the resulting spectrum is very close to the experimental 
spectrum retrieved from the NIST website41 (Figure 3c). Importantly, calculations of similar 
quality would take much longer with DFT, whereas analogous spectra simulations with semi-
empirical QM methods would not be as accurate.42 Interactions can be performed in many 
other languages, such as English, Chinese, and Ukrainian, which we tested. Videos of this 
and other examples are available on the Aitomia website at http://mlatom.com/aitomia. 
We note that Aitomia prefers using the AIQM methods (e.g., the choice of AIQM2 in the 
above IR calculation), because they are among a few methods that allow fast, accurate, and 
robust simulations for a broad range of systems, while other approaches such as DFT are 
often too slow (particularly for the free online platform), or too inaccurate (e.g., semi-
empirical methods such as GFN2-xTB and others, which are less accurate than AIQM series 
according to numerous benchmarks)30-33, 43, 44, or not robust and transferable enough (e.g., 
more recent universal machine learning potentials which often break down in uncommon 
situations).43

learn and write input files for various software packages. However, the researchers often need 
to perform multiple such computations, e.g., to calculate reaction and isomerization energies, 
binding energies, electron affinities, etc. This necessitates the development of a more 
autonomous assistant capable of handling such complex workflows automatically.

Design of autonomous workflow and execution with multi-agent Aitomia 
Beyond simplifying typical single computational tasks, the goal of Aitomia is to assist with 
performing full computational workflows comprising multiple tasks. We enable these 
workflows via an autonomous, multi-agent extension of Aitomia. It is capable of designing 
workflows and executing them with minimal manual intervention.  
 
Figure 4. The architecture of multi-agent Aitomia enabling complex computational workflows. 
First, the multi-agent system evaluates the user’s intent to determine whether a 
computational task is required. If computation is needed, the system validates the relevant 
computational parameters and, under the LangGraph-based orchestration layer's control, 
activates the executor agent. The executor agent initializes the working directory, stores the 
user-provided initial molecular structure (if available), constructs the overall computational 
workflow, and dispatches subtasks to subordinate agents. 
Each sub-agent acquires the necessary contextual information, including the working 
directory. If the user does not provide an initial molecular structure, the sub-agent invokes a 
structure-retrieval agent to obtain the required molecular geometry. Within the structure-
retrieval agent, the molecular species required for the current task are first identified, after 
which the agent determines whether the corresponding structure should be retrieved from 
external sources or reused from previously generated results. The retrieved structure is then 
validated to ensure its chemical plausibility before being returned to the calling sub-agent. 
For sub-agents responsible for simpler tasks, they directly call MLatom-based scripts to 
perform the required calculations. In contrast, sub-agents handling more complex tasks 
further refine the computational workflow and may invoke additional specialized agents to 
decompose the task and execute specific components. 
After completing all assigned computations, each sub-agent performs local result 
analysis and returns the processed outputs to the executor agent. Once all sub-agent responses 
are collected, the executor agent conducts an integrated analysis, summarizes the key

terminates the current computational workflow. The entire process concludes with a report to

We note that the multi-agent system, e.g., the use of multi-agent Aitomia, requires 
extensive LLM-based reasoning and, hence, is slower and more resource-intensive than using 
Aitomia for single-task computations. Also, the autonomous use of Aitomia for increasingly 
complex workflows is more prone to occasional hallucinations and mistakes. For example, in 
our initial tests, we observed that when running the same prompt for the Diels–Alder reaction 
(see example above), it sometimes failed to properly convert hartree to kcal/mol (the value in 
hartree was still correct). We fixed this unit-conversion problem; in general, the user is 
advised to critically evaluate the responses from the multi-agent Aitomia. More benchmarks 
are provided next. 
Benchmark on atomistic simulations tasks 
A major challenge for Aitomia is correctly interpreting user queries, determining which 
information is missing and which calculations are required, and finally organizing the 
corresponding computational procedures to obtain reliable results. Thus, we conducted a 
systematic benchmark of Aitomia on a set of representative theoretical chemistry tasks, 
assigning each task a difficulty level based on the degree of autonomy required to answer the 
given questions. The full overview of the tasks is provided in the SI benchmark spreadsheet. 
Tasks with low autonomy typically provide clear instructions regarding the type of 
calculations needed and involve relatively simple execution with minimal orchestration. 
Medium-level autonomy tasks require a higher level of routing to organize multiple 
computational steps, but still contain explicit instructions describing the necessary procedures. 
In terms of high-level autonomy, little information is provided to decide which tasks to 
perform, and the queries are much closer to what we as humans face every day. It requires a 
deep understanding of the user’s questions and the correct calculation procedure to obtain the 
results. We used low and part of medium-level autonomy to implement Aitomia; hence, they 
are marked ‘seen’ in the SI benchmark spreadsheet, while all the others were not seen by 
developers and are marked accordingly. 
A task is considered successfully completed if it terminates normally and the deviation 
between the results generated by Aitomia and those obtained from manual calculations is 
sufficiently small. Specifically, for tasks requiring a precise numerical answer, the energy 
deviation must be less than 10−6 hartree, and deviations for other properties (geometries, 
frequencies, etc.) must be below 10−4. In addition, the final analysis provided by the system 
must reasonably address the user’s query and avoid significant chemical inaccuracies, which 
we also assess. To assess robustness and reproducibility, each benchmark task was 
independently executed 10 times under identical settings. The results demonstrate consistent 
performance across repeated trials, indicating stable agent orchestration and reliable task 
execution. The specific task types, representative examples for each category, and the 
corresponding benchmark results are summarized in Table 1. The full task descriptions, their 
corresponding abbreviations, and the success rates across independent runs are provided in 
the SI spreadsheet. Because Aitomia is now actively being developed with improved 
intelligence, the benchmark results reflect only its performance using versions from January 
to February 2026.

It should be noted that, although challenging tasks demonstrate increased flexibility, they 
are fundamentally composed of basic agents. Consequently, complex tasks that rely on 
fundamental computational capabilities not supported by the benchmarked version of 
Aitomia—such as pKa calculations or solvation free energy evaluations that require 
unimplemented capabilities of the base agents—were beyond its scope.  
In general, Aitomia achieves excellent stability on low-difficulty tasks, with a success 
rate approaching 100%. It provides a solid foundation for more complex tasks composed of 
these basic operations. For medium-difficulty tasks, which involve more complex workflows 
but still provide explicit instructions, Aitomia achieves a success rate of 70.9%. In contrast, 
the performance on high-difficulty tasks drops to 45.6%. An examination of failed or 
partially successful cases indicates that several systematic factors contribute to performance 
degradation. 
One important factor is the absence of an automated self-correction mechanism in the 
current implementation. The present framework follows a relatively straightforward 
execution pipeline in which intermediate computational results are not actively validated. 
Consequently, errors introduced in earlier stages can propagate to the final output. For 
example, if a geometry optimization yields a structure with imaginary frequencies, the 
workflow proceeds without triggering reoptimization or structural correction, thereby 
compromising the reliability of subsequent thermodynamic or vibrational analyses. This 
behavior can be observed in several benchmark cases (e.g., MED.EWG, and 
HIGH.Gas.Vibrational, see the benchmarks in the SI for definitions and prompts). Notably, 
inspection of the generated reasoning indicates that the system often identifies the underlying 
source of the error in its final analysis. This observation indicates that integrating a self-
correction module capable of diagnosing common computational issues and invoking 
specialized correction agents (e.g., imaginary-frequency removal or workflow redesign) 
could substantially improve the system's robustness. 
A second source of error arises from the molecular retrieval stage, where the system 
occasionally fails to distinguish between isomeric and identical structures correctly. When the 
user-provided molecular description is ambiguous, the system may incorrectly interpret 
identical species as different isomers or, conversely, confuse distinct isomers as the same 
structure. This issue is particularly evident in cases such as HIGH.StericVsStereoelectronic. 
Even when the molecular identity is described relatively clearly, occasional misassignments 
still occur (e.g., MED.AxPref.StereoElec), suggesting that the current molecular 
identification procedure lacks sufficiently rigorous structural validation. In addition, this 
problem affects cases involving different conformational states of the same molecule (e.g., 
HIGH.CONF.HB-vs-nHB). When the user does not explicitly provide a molecular structure, 
the system retrieves molecules from a database based primarily on the molecular name.

However, such name-based retrieval typically returns only a single reference geometry and 
does not account for alternative conformations. Consequently, when tasks require comparing 
structurally distinct conformers—such as intramolecularly hydrogen-bonded versus non-
hydrogen-bonded conformers—the system may inadvertently use the same retrieved structure 
for both calculations. This results in identical optimized geometries and energies, precluding 
a meaningful comparison. 
In several benchmark tasks, the quantum chemical calculations themselves complete 
successfully, yet inaccuracies appear in the AI-generated chemical interpretation. These 
issues mainly arise in the post-analysis stage and typically involve minor errors in spectral 
assignments or mechanistic interpretation. For instance, in one of the benchmark runs (RUN7) 
of the HIGH.AminoSubstitution test case, small inaccuracies appear in the assignment of 
spectral features, while in (RUN10) of the same test, the electronic transition assignments for 
benzene were reversed. Although these issues do not affect the underlying numerical 
calculations, they reduce the reliability of the generated chemical explanations. 
Another issue arises when the reaction equation is not explicitly specified by the user. In 
some cases (e.g., MED.ΔGdeprot), the LLM may infer an incomplete reaction scheme, such 
as omitting the H⁺ species in deprotonation calculations. 
Finally, a small number of failures are attributable to infrastructure-related issues, such 
as instability of the large language model service or unexpected server interruptions during 
task execution. While these factors are external to the computational framework itself, their 
impact could be mitigated through simple fault-tolerant mechanisms such as automatic task 
retry. 
Taken together, these results suggest that Aitomia performs robustly on well-defined 
computational chemistry tasks, while performance decreases in scenarios involving 
ambiguous problem definitions, complex workflows, or automatic reaction inference. The 
benchmark analysis highlights several promising directions for future development, including 
incorporating automated self-correction mechanisms, improving molecular validation 
procedures, and adopting more robust reasoning strategies. 
Aitomia serves as a look-up tool for atomistic calculations 
Even with a powerful, intelligent assistant at hand, to ensure scientifically reliable 
results, users themselves must have background knowledge of simulations, how they are 
performed, and which QC methods or AI models are appropriate for the task. Before the rise 
of LLMs, the only ways were either learning from manuals and tutorials or from a human 
teacher. This severely limits the speed of knowledge acquisition, which might also be limited 
in access. It is also inconvenient to look up the information when needed in the lengthy 
manuals. 
The challenge here is to create an LLM-driven lookup tool that provides reliable 
information based on the domain knowledge about the underlying MLatom capabilities 
exploited by Aitomia. We solve this challenge by exploiting the Retrieval-Augmented 
Generation (RAG) technique to improve the LLM's robustness by providing access to

Limitations 
Despite Aitomia's demonstrated flexibility, the presented capabilities were inherently 
constrained by the scope of the integrated fundamental functional agents. At that stage, 
Aitomia primarily focused on establishing a robust foundation for complex AI-driven 
atomistic and quantum chemical workflows, rather than exhaustively covering all possible 
simulation tasks. 
Aitomia operated by decomposing complex scientific problems into a set of basic 
computational tasks. For example, reaction enthalpy calculations were automatically 
formulated as combinations of geometry optimization and frequency calculations for multiple 
molecular systems, with the overall task structure orchestrated by LLMs. While this design 
allowed for a high degree of generality and adaptability, the range of problems it could 
address remained limited by the availability, maturity, and reliability of these basic task 
modules. 
Furthermore, the implementation supported only a limited set of molecular input formats, 
primarily XYZ and JSON representations. This restriction limited interoperability with 
existing simulation pipelines and user workflows, particularly when alternative or more 
specialized file formats were required. 
Additionally, the integration of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) focused 
primarily on answering MLatom-related queries, whereas the calculation agents underused it. 
Conclusions 
We have introduced an intelligent AI assistant, Aitomia, that helps researchers perform 
atomistic simulations tailored to their needs, even without specialized knowledge of the 
underlying software. It draws on the versatility of the MLatom software ecosystem to enable 
a broad range of computational chemistry tasks, spanning both traditional QM (DFT, HF, and 
post-HF; semi-empirical methods) and AI atomistic models (universal ML interatomic 
potentials and beyond). 
Aitomia can assist with typical tasks such as geometry optimizations of minima and 
transition states, frequency and thermochemistry calculations, and simulations of IR and 
UV/vis spectra. Because of the extensive use of AI atomistic models, calculations are often 
much faster than DFT, with better or comparable accuracy. In addition, Aitomia can 
autonomously design and execute complex computational workflows, enabling convenient 
calculations of reaction enthalpies, atomization energies, proton affinities, and related 
quantities using single-task AI agents. Aitomia can also be used as an expert advisor for 
performing atomistic simulations. Its ability to analyze calculation results and answer 
common QC and ML questions plays a promotional role, helping professionals and non-

Methodology 
LangGraph-based multi-agent architecture 
In the implementation of Aitomia, single computational tasks rely on the native tool-
calling capabilities of large language models to parse user-provided parameters and invoke 
the corresponding computational functions. This version uses a lightweight, robust logic with 
relatively low latency in language-level parsing; consequently, the primary computational 
cost arises from the calculations themselves. However, this design offers limited flexibility 
and requires users to manually guide the workflow step by step to complete the desired 
computations.

The Atomia multi-agent architecture is implemented using LangGraph, and the overall 
structure of task execution is illustrated in Figure 4. For the agent system, we adopt a graph-
controlled dynamic routing mechanism. Specifically, a dedicated decision agent makes a 
judgment on which agent should be called next by using an LLM tool to output a structured 
decision. This decision is then returned to LangGraph, allowing the graph engine itself to 
perform the transition. 
In this design, the LLM participates in dynamic decision-making, but the execution flow 
remains explicitly governed by the StateGraph, ensuring reproducibility and clear separation 
between reasoning and control logic.  The agents communicate through graph nodes, with the 
system featuring a main orchestrating agent responsible for analyzing user queries, designing 
the workflow, and orchestrating execution by calling upon corresponding single-task agents 
for specific calculations and file management. Each single-task agent is dedicated to an 
independent computational module (e.g., geometry optimization or reaction simulations) and 
possesses its own internal workflow. For simpler tasks (e.g., geometry optimization), the 
workflow follows sequential logic. The agent prepares the necessary inputs—such as 
acquiring molecular structures and setting calculation parameters—in order, before finally 
invoking the MLatom code to execute the calculation. For more complex tasks (e.g., reaction 
energy calculation), the agent employs a reasoning process and workflow design similar to 
that of the orchestrating agent, involving the invocation of various tools to manage 
complexity.

Integration with MLatom 
The connection between Aitomia and the MLatom package is primarily achieved through 
function calls. We adopted this approach because LLMs are prone to hallucinations when 
generating code for functionalities with insufficient training data. Since computational 
stability is paramount, we chose not to delegate code generation to an LLM; instead, we 
generated an input file or Python script from a predefined template for a task. 
The flexibility of our approach currently relies on a flexible combination of the single-
task agents operating as described above. This design offers a viable strategy for working 
with code packages where the LLM may be under-trained or lack sufficient exposure. While 
this approach inevitably reduces some flexibility, we mitigate this by breaking tasks down 
into smaller, single-purpose units. One agent is responsible for one specific, granular task, 
and complex tasks are achieved by orchestrating these smaller units.

and support for a wide range of computational chemistry tasks. The integration with MLatom

Integration with HPC clusters 
The Aitomia system adopts a node-local execution model. When Aitomia is submitted 
via the web interface, the scheduling system launches the main service script on an assigned 
compute node within the HPC cluster. All subsequent computational commands are executed 
from that node's local shell environment using system command calls. This approach ensures 
that computations directly utilize the node's allocated resources and the centrally maintained 
software stack accessible via the shared filesystem. 
Enhancing model robustness through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) 
Although the LLM can, at the date of writing, often provide reasonable answers about 
computational chemistry and machine learning, it currently often hallucinates due to the 
vastness of the relevant knowledge. For example, it provides wrong answers about the 
capabilities of MLatom, which is at the core of our implementations. To address these issues 
and improve the model's performance, we also integrate RAG to enhance Aitomia’s 
robustness as a lookup tool by providing access to external knowledge, thereby reducing 
hallucinations and improving factual accuracy (Figure 8). In Aitomia, external knowledge is 
first chunked and then represented as embeddings generated by the text-embedding-v3 model 
via the API provided by Aliyun. 
 
Figure 8. The Adaptive-RAG pipeline: retrieving external knowledge for enhanced generation. 
Traditional single-step RAG, while technically the simplest and most feasible approach, 
is inaccurate for complex queries because it performs only a single retrieval step before 
generating an answer.46, 47 The multi-step variant performs much better on complex queries 
but is inefficient with simple queries. 
Adaptive-RAG presents a promising alternative,48 which we have implemented in 
Aitomia. For straightforward queries, it can answer them directly without consulting the 
database; for simple queries, it checks the database only once to ensure efficiency; for 
complex queries, it can retrieve from the database multiple times until the queries are fully 
answered.48

When RAG is activated, Aitomia determines whether the question is related to the 
database's knowledge. If so, Aitomia retrieves information from the RAG database. Then 
LLM answers the user’s query based on the retrieved information. After getting an 
intermediate answer, Aitomia judges whether it is enough to answer the query. If not, 
Aitomia updates the query and retrieves more information from the database. Finally, 
Aitomia makes a summary and provides a complete answer to the initial query. 
By using this strategy, it retrieves only information relevant to the database, thereby 
reducing the number of retrievals and mitigating the model's tendency to hallucinate. 
Therefore, we integrate Adaptive-RAG to enhance the robustness of our model. The 
integration of Adaptive-RAG reduces the probability of model hallucinations and improves 
the model performance based on user needs.
