ReverbFX: A Dataset of Room Impulse Responses Derived from Reverb Effect
Plugins for Singing Voice Dereverberation
Julius Richter∗, Till Svajda∗, Timo Gerkmann
Signal Processing Group, University of Hamburg, Germany
Email: {julius.richter,timo.gerkmann}@uni-hamburg.de
Abstract
We present ReverbFX, a new room impulse response (RIR)
dataset designed for singing voice dereverberation research.
Unlike existing datasets based on real recorded RIRs, Re-
verbFX features a diverse collection of RIRs captured from
various reverb audio effect plugins commonly used in music
production. We conduct comprehensive experiments using
the proposed dataset to benchmark the challenge of derever-
beration of singing voice recordings affected by artificial
reverbs. We train two state-of-the-art generative models
using ReverbFX and demonstrate that models trained with
plugin-derived RIRs outperform those trained on realistic
RIRs in artificial reverb scenarios.
1 Introduction
Room impulse responses (RIRs) play a crucial role in audio
signal processing, as they characterize how sound propa-
gates and decays in a given environment [1]. To obtain
RIRs that characterize natural reverberation, they can either
be measured in actual rooms or simulated using established
acoustic models [2]. These RIRs reflect the physical proper-
ties and geometries of actual spaces, yielding reverberation
with consistent time decay, energy distribution, and fre-
quency response characteristics. Accordingly, most RIR
datasets and dereverberation benchmarks are based on this
natural room acoustics paradigm, aiming to improve tech-
nologies such as speech recognition, hearing aids, and audio
enhancement in real-world environments [3].
However, there is a discrepancy between natural rever-
beration and artificial reverb effects used in modern music
production [4, 5]. Unlike real-world acoustics, music pro-
duction often relies on digital reverb effects that are directly
applied to individual tracks, such as vocals, using software
plugins. These plugins can employ convolution-based meth-
ods using recorded or synthesized RIRs, or algorithmic ap-
proaches that simulate reverberation through mathematical
models. Artificial reverbs are unconstrained by the laws
of physics and can exhibit a wide range of exotic behav-
iors: extremely long or modulated decays, non-monotonic
frequency responses, non-linear characteristics, and even
deliberately unnatural acoustics. These reverberation ef-
fects are part of the creative palette, shaping the aesthetics
of contemporary music. As a consequence, dereverberation
approaches trained and evaluated exclusively on natural
RIR datasets may not generalize well to these artificial re-
verberation scenarios.
To address this gap, we introduce ReverbFX, a novel
dataset of RIRs entirely generated from a diverse set of pro-
fessional reverb audio effect plugins. Unlike existing RIR
datasets, ReverbFX captures the diversity and complexity
of artificial reverbs frequently used in music production.
This unique dataset enables, for the first time, the study and

these artificial environments.
We use ReverbFX to create a new benchmark, Singing-
ReverbFX, for evaluating singing voice dereverberation
under artificial reverberation conditions. Using this data,
we train two state-of-the-art generative models [6, 7] and
demonstrate that, when trained on SingingReverbFX, these
models can effectively dereverberate singing voices recorded
with artificial reverbs, significantly outperforming counter-
parts trained solely on natural RIRs (i.e., in an off-domain
setting). Our experiments establish ReverbFX as an essen-
tial resource for advancing dereverberation research beyond
natural acoustics and towards applications in music produc-
tion, where artificial reverbs predominate.
In summary, our contributions are three-fold:
1. We motivate and construct ReverbFX, the first dataset
focused on artificial reverberation from audio effect
plugins.
2. We systematically compare two generative dereverbera-
tion models trained on artificial versus natural reverber-
ation.
3. We establish a new benchmark for singing voice derever-
beration in artificial reverb scenarios, paving the way for
more robust and musically relevant audio enhancement
technologies.
2 Background
Reverberation, or reverb for short, is a phenomenon that
occurs in enclosed spaces when sound signals reflect off
walls and other surfaces [3]. In music production, reverb is
one of the most commonly used effects, applied to almost
every track in a mix. This is especially true for vocals,
where reverb is used to create tension, add texture, or sim-
ply achieve a more natural and immersive sound. Reverb
can make vocals sound wider and significantly influence
the emotional response of listeners; for example, music
with long, dense reverb often evokes an epic or dreamlike
atmosphere.
Music datasets often provide audio in separate stems,
such as vocals, drums, bass, and accompaniment. These
stems can be used for training source separation models [8],
stem mixing and mastering [9], and other music informa-
tion retrieval tasks [10]. However, vocal stems are often
provided with added reverb, and truly anechoic (dry) voice
recordings are not always available. Therefore, singing
voice dereverberation is necessary to retrieve the original
anechoic voice. In turn, the resulting dry singing voice can
be convolved with a variety of RIRs to generate additional
data for training purposes–a common data augmentation
strategy. Moreover, in music production, it is usually prefer-
able to apply reverb to non-reverberant vocals, as this allows
precise parameter control and greater creative flexibility.
Removing reverb from a signal, especially from vocals
or speech, is a challenging problem in the field of signal
processing. Singing voice dereverberation is conceptually

Early approaches to vocal and music dereverberation
primarily rely on signal processing techniques such as linear
prediction [12–15], or source models designed to capture
the harmonic structure of musical signals [16, 17]. These
methods typically assume a structured representation of the
source and exploit music’s inherent periodicity and tonal
characteristics to mitigate reverberation effects.
Recent research in machine learning has introduced
a variety of new approaches, including end-to-end deep
neural networks [18]. These supervised methods tend to
learn the characteristics of specific reverbs present in the
training data. This leads to reduced performance when
encountering unseen reverb types, a limitation given the
wide range of possible reverberation scenarios in real-world
applications. To address this challenge, more recent work
has increasingly focused on generative approaches, such as
diffusion models, which can be supervised or unsupervised
and aim to achieve robust generalization across diverse
reverb conditions [19].
Feedback Delay Networks
One of the foundational concepts behind algorithmic re-
verb plugins was introduced by Stautner and Puckette in
1982 [20]. Their approach feeds multiple copies of the input
signal into a network of delay lines with varying lengths.
The outputs from these delays are then weighted and re-
cursively routed back into the system through a feedback
delay matrix, laying the groundwork for what would later be
formalized as feedback delay networks (FDNs) [21]. This
architecture allows the signal to circulate within the net-
work, mimicking the complex reflections in natural acous-
tic environments. The feedback matrix is typically unitary
to ensure filter stability, resulting in a lossless FDN. The
reverb tail’s envelope is generally shaped using a combi-
nation of gain-based decay and low-pass filtering on the
delay lines, simulating the gradual energy loss, especially
of high frequencies, that occurs with multiple reflections in
real spaces.
Beyond the feedback process, some algorithms also in-
clude a feedforward path that routes the input signal through
the delay lines once before reaching the output. This com-
ponent helps approximate early reflections and provides
additional dry/wet balance control.
3 ReverbFX Dataset
We release the ReverbFX dataset1, comprising 1,846 RIRs,
with RT60 times ranging from 0.31 to 52.08 seconds. A
histogram of RT60 times is shown in Figure 1. All RIRs
are generated using four publicly available audio plugins
(see Section 3.1) and measured using a discrete-time unit
impulse as the input signal. The reverb effects are applied
via DawDreamer [22], a Python API providing DAW func-
tionality, including support for VST plugins.
For each plugin, we utilize both factory and custom
presets. To further increase variation in the resulting audio
effects, we generate 14 randomized versions of each preset
by modifying two randomly selected parameters from a
predefined subset (e.g., delay time and low cut). This pro-
cedure results in a total of 15 impulse responses per preset.
For all configurations, the dry/wet parameter is set to 1 to
ensure a fully wet signal.
A rigorous validation procedure is employed to filter out
anomalous or corrupted data to guarantee the quality and
reliability of the RIRs. Each RIR underwent the following
checks:
• Numerical Integrity: Verification for invalid numerical
values such as NaNs or infinite entries.
• DC Offset: Ensuring the mean value is close to zero
(threshold: 10−4)
• Energy Threshold: Discarding RIRs with an energy
below 10−16
• Length Validation: Excluding empty or zero-length
RIRs.
• RT60 Consistency: Retaining only those RIRs with a
computed RT60 in the range of 0.1 to 100 seconds.
• Hilbert Envelope: Evaluating the amplitude envelope
via the Hilbert transform to exclude RIRs with exces-
sively short decay times.
3.1 Reverb Audio Effect Plugins
Protoverb (v1.0.1)
The Protoverb plugin is proprietary software distributed
by u-he Heckmann Audio GmbH2 and licensed for free,
non-commercial use. Contrary to traditional algorithmic
reverbs that aim to minimize resonances, Protoverb builds
up as many room resonances as possible, modeling the
body of air in a room [30]. It is based on a delay network,
whose structure is controlled by a text-based randomization
feature, allowing users to generate unique reverb settings.
For our study, we created 200 presets with different random
strings in the reverb section to capture a diverse set of RIRs.
SkyNet (v0.4)
SkyNet Reverb by Shift Line3 is proprietary software of-
fered under a Pay-What-You-Want model. It is based on
the two reverb algorithms SkyNet-1 and SkyNet-2 from
the Astronaut space reverb pedal, and is designed to create
expansive soundscapes with long decay times and diffuse
sound [31]. The plugin offers a parameter to control the
mix ratio between the two algorithms.

4 SingingReverbFX Benchmark
We introduce SingingReverbFX, a singing voice dereverber-
ation benchmark focused on restoring reverberant vocals
processed with audio reverb effects commonly used in mu-
sic production. To this end, we convolve singing voice
data with our new ReverbFX dataset of RIRs captured from
reverb effect plugins.
4.1 Data
Inspired by Lemercier et al. [34], we utilize a variety of
singing voice datasets, including OpenSinger [23], M4Sing-
er [24], CSD [25], PJS [26], OpenCpop [27], NUS-48E [28],

5https://valhalladsp.com
and NHSS [29]. These datasets feature studio-quality record-
ings with balanced gender and phoneme distributions and
include vocals in multiple languages. The combined vocal
data amounts to a total of 94 hours. An overview of the
datasets and their key characteristics is provided in Table 1.
To prepare data for training, validation, and the test,
we partition the ReverbFX dataset using stratified sampling
based on RT60 bins, ensuring that each subset is represen-
tative of the overall distribution of reverberation times. The
data is split into training, validation, and test sets with a
ratio of 1446:200:200. The vocal training set includes all
recordings except those set aside for validation and testing.
For validation, we select 980 gender-balanced files from
the OpenSinger dataset. The entire NHSS dataset is used
exclusively for testing.
For every split, each vocal recording is paired with a
randomly selected RIR from the corresponding subset. To
ensure compatibility for convolution, vocal files are first
resampled to 48 kHz. Each RIR is truncated at its maximum
index to remove trailing silence or noise, then normalized,
converted to mono by taking the left channel, and convolved
with the vocal signal.
After convolution, we apply dry/wet mixing, a stan-
dard parameter in digital reverb plugins, which defines the
balance between the original (dry) and reverberated (wet)
vocals. At each iteration, the mixing ratio α is randomly
sampled from a uniform distribution α ∈[0.1,1], resulting
in
y(t) = (1−α)x(t)+α(x(t)∗h(t)),
(1)
where y(t) is the final mixture, x(t) is the dry vocal, and
h(t) is the RIR.
Our approach, convolving vocals with RIRs, leverages
the principles of linearity and time invariance (LTI), mean-
ing that only linear, time-invariant reverb characteristics
can be captured. Some reverb plugins, however, include
time-varying or nonlinear elements (e.g., Chorus or Pitch
Modulation) that cannot be replicated through RIR convolu-
tion. Nevertheless, using RIRs offers a straightforward way
to construct the dataset while still modeling essential reverb
parameters, such as delay time, intensity, and absorption
characteristics.

NVIDIA RTX A6000 graphics processing units (GPUs),
with a batch size of 6. Training is stopped after 350k steps.

Score-based Generative Models
Richter et al. [6] introduced SGMSE+, a score-based gener-
ative model for speech enhancement and dereverberation.
In a supervised setting, SGMSE+ learns clean speech pos-
teriors conditioned on noisy or reverberant inputs. The
model operates in the complex short-time Fourier trans-
form (STFT) domain, utilizing a diffusion process and a
neural network to estimate the score function, which guides
the reverse generative process.
In a follow-up study [35], the authors adapted the model
for 48 kHz audio and conducted an extensive hyperparam-
eter search to optimize the noise schedule and amplitude
compression of the input representation. We adopt these
optimal hyperparameters to train SGMSE+ on SingingRe-
verbFX using the official implementation.6 As an ablation,
we also train SGMSE+ on natural RIRs using the datasets
employed in [35] to illustrate the domain shift between
natural and artificial reverberation.
Schrödinger Bridge
Juki´c et al. [7] extended SGMSE+ by employing a tractable
Schrödinger bridge as the diffusion process for speech en-
hancement and dereverberation. The Schrödinger bridge
transforms the input speech distribution into the clean speech
distribution by formulating and solving an optimal transport
problem. Richter et al. [36, 37] later adapted this model
for 48 kHz audio and integrated it into their code repos-
itory, which we use for training the Schrödinger bridge
on SingingReverbFX. We utilize the stochastic differential
equation (SDE) sampler, as it has been shown to provide
superior performance for dereverberation tasks [7].
4.3 Metrics
Currently, there are no metrics specifically designed for
singing voice dereverberation. Therefore, we adopt a set of
established metrics from speech enhancement to evaluate
both perceptual quality, intelligibility, and signal fidelity:
• POLQA [38]: An intrusive, model-based standard (ITU-
T P.863) for perceptual audio quality, considered a suc-
cessor to PESQ and widely used in speech evaluation.
• PESQ [39]: An intrusive metric that estimates per-
ceived speech quality on a scale from 1 to 4.5, originally
developed for telephony.
• ESTOI [40]: Measures intelligibility by comparing
short-time spectral features between the reference and
output, giving a score from 0 (unintelligible) to 1 (per-
fect).
• MAE: Mean absolute error in the STFT domain, pro-
viding a direct, objective comparison between estimated
and reference signals.
6https://github.com/sp-uhh/sgmse
• OVRL [41]: A non-intrusive neural network-based es-
timator that predicts mean opinion score (MOS) for
overall quality.
• REVERB [41]: A non-intrusive neural network-based
estimator that predicts MOS for perceived reverbera-
tion.
Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive evalua-
tion framework to assess both the perceptual and objective
quality of dereverberated singing voice signals.
5 Results
In Table 2, we present the singing voice dereverberation
results obtained using SingingReverbFX. It can be seen
that all methods yield improvements over the reverberant
test set across all metrics. Furthermore, training SGMSE+
on natural reverb (with mismatched RIRs) leads to a per-
formance decrease compared to training SGMSE+ with
in-domain data from the SingingReverbFX training set. The
Schrödinger bridge also demonstrates strong performance
but is slightly outperformed by SGMSE+. This may be
attributed to the extensive hyperparameter tuning applied to
the 48 kHz model in [35].
6 Conclusions
In this work, we introduced ReverbFX, the first dataset ded-
icated to artificial reverberation effects commonly used in
music production. Through comprehensive benchmarking
with state-of-the-art generative models, we demonstrated
that models trained on plugin-derived RIRs significantly
outperform those trained on traditional, natural RIRs when
faced with dereverberation of singing voices under artificial
reverb conditions. Our results highlight the importance of
domain-specific data for dereverberation tasks and establish
ReverbFX and the Singing-ReverbFX benchmark as vital
resources for developing and evaluating audio enhancement
models tailored to modern music production environments.
