An AI Capability Threshold for Rent-Funded Universal Basic Income in an
AI-Automated Economy
Aran Nayebi
Machine Learning Department and Neuroscience & Robotics Institutes,
School of Computer Science, Carnegie Mellon University
Correspondence: anayebi@cs.cmu.edu
Abstract
We derive the first closed-form condition under which ar-
tificial intelligence (AI) capital profits could sustainably fi-
nance a universal basic income (UBI) without relying on new
taxation or the creation of new jobs. In a Solow-Zeira task-
automation economy with a CES aggregator σ < 1, we in-
troduce an AI capability parameter that scales the productiv-
ity of automatable tasks and obtain a tractable expression for
the AI capability threshold—the minimum productivity of AI
relative to pre-AI automation required for a balanced transfer.
Using current U.S. economic parameters, we find that even in
the conservative scenario where no new tasks or jobs emerge,
AI systems would only need to reach only 5-7 times today’s
automation productivity to fund an 11%-of-GDP UBI.
Our analysis also reveals some specific policy levers: raising
public revenue share (e.g. profit taxation) of AI capital from
the current 15% to about 33% halves the required AI capa-
bility threshold to attain UBI to 3 times existing automation
productivity, but gains diminish beyond 50% public revenue
share, especially if regulatory costs increase. Market struc-
ture also strongly affects outcomes: monopolistic or concen-
trated oligopolistic markets reduce the threshold by increas-
ing economic rents, whereas heightened competition signifi-
cantly raises it.
These results therefore offer a rigorous benchmark for assess-
ing when advancing AI capabilities might sustainably finance
social transfers in an increasingly automated economy.

Introduction
The rise in capabilities of artificial intelligence (AI) has
brought with it concerns of mass unemployment, especially
if these systems replace humans in many (or all) economic
sectors. We consider a “worst-case scenario”, where even if
this happens and no new jobs were created, then under what
conditions on AI capabilities would there be sufficient capi-
tal to finance a universal basic income (UBI). We do not ar-
gue here for the merits (or dismerits) of a UBI, nor suggest
it as a substitute for individual “purpose”, but rather view
it as a general mechanism to be able to fund the populace
in principle from the capital generated by AI, without creat-
ing new jobs. We also do not argue for how, or whether, it
should be distributed, of which a UBI is one possible famil-
iar dispersion mechanism among several (e.g. a sovereign
wealth dividend, a negative income tax, or a refundable tax
credit). We find that this mechanism is theoretically possi-
ble, and directly related to the average productivity of an AI
system relative to pre-AI automation, which we parametrize
as γt ∈R≥1. Furthermore, we find that when a small group
of firms controls most advanced AI systems, the larger eco-
nomic rents they generate make it easier to fund a UBI.
By contrast, intense price competition thins those rents and
raises the bar. Ensuring that government captures a meaning-
ful slice of AI profits further lowers the capability threshold
without requiring full government ownership, so society can
finance a broad transfer without waiting for science-fiction-
level breakthroughs, but rather within the range of foresee-
able progress.
The AI economics literature is relatively young. While
economists have studied the macro impact of technology for
decades (e.g., Solow (1956, 1957)), and have modeled au-
tomation explicitly since at least Zeira (1998), systematic,
model-based work that treats artificial intelligence itself as
an endogenous driver of growth, rents, or policy has begun
to be explored only in the past ten-fifteen years. Acemoglu
and Restrepo (2018) address a key empirical limitation of
Zeira’s model—that it predicts continuously rising growth
rates and capital shares—by introducing an explicit param-
eter, α, representing the fraction of tasks that have been au-
tomated. By modeling innovation as a balance between au-
tomating existing tasks and creating new ones, they show
how α can remain stable, aligning the theoretical predictions
with historical evidence of constant growth rates and cap-
ital shares. Yudkowsky (2013)’s Intelligence Explosion Mi-
croeconomics first articulated the idea that an ultra-advanced
AI could reinvest its cognitive output to make itself smarter,
generating what Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2017) later chris-
tened a “Type II” take-off (their “Type I” take-off refers to
a growth surge driven by automating new tasks rather than
recursive self-improvement). Yudkowsky’s technical note is
not a standard growth model—there is no closed-form theo-
rem—but it does formalize key parameters (e.g. the conver-
sion of additional compute into capability) and shows how
different functional forms of “returns to intelligence” map to
slow growth, rapid take-off, or outright singularity. Erdil and
Besiroglu (2023) recently surveyed the literature on “explo-

Unlike the “AI-explosion” models that endogenize the
creation of entirely new tasks and jobs (e.g., Aghion, Jones,
and Jones (2017); Erdil and Besiroglu (2023)), we study a
worst-case environment in which automation substitutes for
labor without spawning fresh job categories. Concretely, we
treat the automated-task share ¯α as fixed, and no new tasks
enter the CES (constant elasticity of substitution) aggrega-
tor. This consideration puts full weight on existing AI rents:
if universal basic income is fiscally viable even under this
zero-job-creation scenario, it is a fortiori viable in richer set-
tings where new labor demand emerges.

the analytical results, and Section 4 concludes with simula-

4.1
When Could AI Capabilities Reach the UBI
Threshold?
There has been much discussion lately about the scaling
laws for AI capabilities, although it is far from certain what
a suitable scaling law should be for all automatable tasks
in the current economy. Instead, we observe that these pre-
dictions depend on (1) the type of tasks that are automated
by AI, and (2) the observable used to characterize this capa-
bility. For example, Kwa et al. (2025), a recent study from
METR (Model Evaluation & Threat Research), found that
at least on automatically scoreable software tasks from a
few distributions, that AI agent performance on the length
of these tasks roughly doubles every 7 months. When hard-
ware is used to characterize this capability, a study by Epoch
AI (2025a) found that machine learning hardware perfor-
mance (FLOPS/s) has doubled roughly every 2.3 years. If
we instead use compute as the observable, the amount used
for frontier AI models has been observed to grow 4.6×/year
since 2010 (Epoch AI 2025b). Combining this with the ca-
pability (measured in terms of test loss) exponent of 0.05 as
a function of compute for current language models (Kaplan
et al. 2020, Figure 1), we get that (4.6)0.05 ≈1.0793, im-
plying that capabilities would increase 7.93% a year. This
implies a doubling time of roughly ln 2/ ln 1.0793 ≈9.1
years.
As a result, in Figure 1, we start from a conservative min-
imal baseline γ0 ≡1, and vary these capability predictions
(of doubling between 1-10 years) to simulate when they will
reach the threshold γ⋆
t , which we estimate and compute from
extant economic data.
Specifically, we consider a basic UBI of $12k/year in the
U.S. in 2025 dollars ($1,000/month), which is estimated
to cost ∼$3.1 trillion when limited to adults (UBI Cen-
ter 2019). Given a current GDP of $29.35 trillion in 2024
Q3 (U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis 2024c), this would

to realizing that advantage.
5
Discussion
In conclusion, our simulations highlight three central in-
sights:
1. Only moderate AI growth is needed: At today’s eco-
nomic parameters, AI systems would need to reach
roughly 5-7 times pre-AI automation to underwrite an
11%-of-GDP UBI. Depending on the pace of AI capa-
bility growth, the necessary productivity level for AI to
sustainably fund a substantial UBI from economic rents
alone, without new jobs, could be reached in principle
between the early 2030s and the mid 21st century.
2. Small oligopolies are permissible: Market structure sig-
nificantly influences these outcomes: while monopolistic
or highly concentrated oligopolist AI markets substan-
tially lower the productivity threshold required, even per-
fectly competitive AI markets do not entirely eliminate
rents, though they do raise the required AI capability sig-
nificantly.
3. Full-scale nationalization of AI profits is not required
in principle: Expanding public revenue share or captur-
ing AI-generated profits greatly reduces the capability
level needed for AI to support UBI. Specifically, increas-
ing the public stake in AI capital from today’s ≈15%
to one-third halves the UBI threshold, yet the gain is
partly undone if heavier oversight lifts operating costs;
beyond about 50% ownership further gains taper off.
Furthermore, the effectiveness of this approach depends
critically on operational efficiency, as increased public
oversight or bureaucratic management costs could offset
some of these benefits.
Thus, should a UBI be desired, successfully navigating
the balance between fostering innovation, ensuring market
competitiveness, and efficiently capturing AI rents for pub-
lic benefit will be essential to leveraging advancing AI capa-
bilities for this in the 21st century.
