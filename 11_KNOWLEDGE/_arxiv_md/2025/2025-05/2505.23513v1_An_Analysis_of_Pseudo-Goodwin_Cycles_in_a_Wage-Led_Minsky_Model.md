An Analysis of Pseudo-Goodwin Cycles
in a Wage-Led Minsky Model
Johannes Buchner
June 11, 2025
Abstract
The goal of these notes is to make mathematically more precise some of the ideas
presented in Stockhammer and Michell [2014]. At first the title seems like a contradiction
to have a wage-led model and still find goodwin cycles in it, but the point we try to make
in the paper is that those are only ‘pseudo-goodwin’ cycles, and not real goodwin cycles.
1
Acknowledgements
This paper is based on discussions with Charlie Brummit, who I am deeply indebted, especially
for performing the simulations. I have coined the name ”pseudo goodwin cycles” in reference
to ”pseudo trajectories”, which play an important role in numerical mathematics of dynamical
systems. I would like to thank Engelbert Stockhammer, who introduced me to the topic.

Definitions
2.1
Variables
1. Time t ∈R is continuous.
2. Output of the economy is denoted by y(t) ≥0.
3. A portion w(t) of output y(t) is distributed to wages. [The quantity w(t) will also be
called the distribution.]. The remainder, y(t) −w(t), is profits.
4. Financial fragility f(t) is the ratio of debt-to-income of firms.
Notation 1. Derivatives with respect to time, d/dt, will denoted by an overdot, such as ˙y(t) ≡
dy(t)/dt. For short, we will just write ˙y.
Notation 2. We will denote the nonnegative real numbers by R+ ≡{x ∈R : x ≥0}.
2.2
Differential equations
We will consider a system of three coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of the form
˙y = F(y, w, f)
(1a)
˙w = G(y, w, f)
(1b)
˙f = H(y, w, f),
(1c)

3
A simplified version of the Goodwin model
The Goodwin model [Goodwin, 1967] posits that a business cycle occurs from “the interaction
between a profit-led demand function and a reserve army distribution function”.
Definition 1. A profit-led demand function means that ∂˙y/∂w ≡∂F(y, w, f)/∂w < 0 for all
sufficiently large t.
Remark 1. The economic reasoning is that an increase in the wage share w(t) is associated
with a decline in demand because investment is driven by the profit share y(t) −w(t).
3.1
Reserve army effect
Assumption 1 (Output varies positively with employment). Higher levels of output y(t) are
associated with higher levels of employment.
Assumption 2 (Reserve army assumption). “[A]s unemployment increases the bargaining
power of labour is diminished, leading to a fall in the wage rate” w(t). And vice versa: as
unemployment falls, the bargaining power of labour increases, leading to a rise in the wage rate
w(t).
Combining Assumptions 1 and 2 gives
Assumption 3 (Large output y(t) is associated with rising wage share w(t)). When output
y(t) is large, employment is large by Assumption 1, so by Assumption 2 the wage share w(t)
rises.
Now we make Assumption 3 mathematically precise:
Definition 2 (Reserve army effect). When y(t) is sufficiently large, we have ˙w > 0. That is,
there exists a constant κ > 0 such that y(t) > κ =⇒
˙w > 0.
3.2
Profit squeeze theory of accumulation
Definition 3 (Profit squeeze). For sufficiently large wage share w(t), output y(t) falls. That
is, there exists a constant ω > 0 such that w(t) > ω =⇒˙y < 0.
This definition is motivated by the following assumptions (footnote 1 on page 2 of Stock-
hammer and Michell [2014]):
Assumption 4. “[W]orkers consume all wages and capitalists invest all profits, the marginal
productivity of labour is constant and the capital-output ratio is fixed. Given these assumptions,
a rise in the real wage will lead to a reduction in the growth of profits and - since investment is
equal to profit - a reduction in the rate of output growth.”

3.3
A simplified version of the Goodwin model
Let

(2a)
˙w = GGoodwin(y, w, f) := w(−c + ry),

Definition 5 (Minsky financial fragility). Financial fragility f(t) exhibits the Minsky effect if
the rate of change of f(t) increases with output y(t), i.e., if ∂˙f/∂y ≡∂H/∂y > 0.
Definition 6 (Inverse relationship between output growth and financial fragility). The rate of
change of output decreases with financial fragility, i.e., ∂˙y/∂f ≡∂H (y, w, f) /∂f < 0 for all
y(t), w(t), f(t) ≥0.
Let
˙y = FMinsky(y, w, f) := y(1 −f)
(3a)
˙f = HMinsky(y, w, f) := f(−1 + py),
(3b)
where p is a positive constant. Note that Eq. (3b) satisfies the Minsky effect (Definition 5)
provided that f(t) ≥0, and note that Eq. (3a) satisfies the inverse relationship between output
growth ˙y and financial fragility f(t) (Definition 6) provided that f(t) ≥0.
System (5) exhibits countercyclical closed orbits much like the Goodwin model (2) does, as
illustrated in Figure 4 of Stockhammer and Michell [2014].
Remark 2. If we ignore the economic interpretations, then the Minsky model (3) is a special
case of the Goodwin model of the Goodwin model (2) with the replacement of variables w →
f, c →1, r →p, so it is straightforward to see why closed orbits appear in both models.

4.2
Minsky cycle with a reserve army effect
We augment System (3) with dynamics for the wage share that exhibits the reserve army effect:
˙w = GMinsky-reserve-army(y, w, f) := w(−c + ry −w).
(4)
Note that the wage distribution w(t) has no effect on demand. Said mathematically, w(t)
is enslaved to y(t); Eq. (4) is a slave equation, while Eq. (3a) is called a master equation.
Stockhammer and Michell [2014] also invoke the language of a scavenger from biological models
to describe the slave equation (4).

Definition 7 (Pseudo-Goodwin cycle). A pseudo-Goodwin cycle is a counterclockwise closed
orbit in (y(t), w(t)) such that y(t) and w(t) form a master–slave system.
Lemma 1. A pseudo-Goodwin cycle necessarily has at least one more variable that is neither
enslaved to y(t) nor enslaves y(t).
Proof. Follows from the fact that the solution to a first-order ODE ˙x = F(x) cannot oscillate.

A Minsky model with distribution function and a wage-led effect
in the demand function
Now we retain Eqs. (3b) and (4) in the previous model (i.e. the Minsky model with a reserve

4.4
Next steps
Some possible ways to proceed:
1. Consider a fast–slow system with |s| ≪1? Stockhammer and Michell [2014] seem to have
this case in mind because in their numerical example on page 12, they take s := 0.02.
2. Stockhammer and Michell [2014] claim on page 12 that “The cyclical behaviour of the
model is still driven by the interaction between y and f (Figure 6b).” I think this statement
could be made more precise. It’s certainly true when s = 0, because then there is no
feedback from w to either y or to f (i.e., w is enslaved by y and f). If we let s be negative
but “close” to zero, then we have two possible ways for cycles to appear, in the interaction
between w and y [as in the Goodwin model (2)] or in the interaction between f and y
[as in the Minsky model (3)]. Then we could ask whether there are stable limit cycles or
centers or unstable orbits in that model.

5
Hopf bifurcation in System (5) as s crosses 0
Figure 9 shows the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix of System (5) evaluated at the unique
interior fixed point (y∗, w∗, f ∗) = (1/p, r/p −c, rs/p −cs + 1) as a function of s from −2 (dark
blue) to 2 (light blue). Here, the parameters are the same as those used by Stockhammer and
Michell [2014, page 12]: c = 3/2, p = 2, r = 5.
At s = 0, the first eigenvalue λ1 = −1, and the other two eigenvalues λ2 = ¯λ3 cross the
imaginary axis. This crossing of the imaginary axis by a pair of complex-conjugate eigenvalues
suggests that a Hopf bifurcation occurs at s = 0. Figure 10 shows an orbit for s = 0; the closed
orbit of y, f drives cyclic behavior in the enslaved variable w.
For s > 0, the complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues have positive real part, i.e., ℜλ2 =
ℜλ3 > 0, while the third eigenvalue λ3 < −1. As a result, the dynamics (at least in the lin-
ear approximation near the interior fixed point) are unstable. This instability is also observed
by Stockhammer and Michell [2014, page 13], who note that “this cyclical motion is now un-
stable, so that outward spirals are generated in the phase space of all three pairs of variables”.
Figure 11 shows an orbit for s = 0.03 that spirals outward. Eventually, the system blows up
and escapes to ∞.
For s < 0 , the complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues have negative real part, i.e., ℜλ2 =
ℜλ3 < 0, and the other eigenvalue, λ1, is negative if s < p/(cp−r) and positive if s > p/(cp−r)
(see the orange text in Fig. 9). Numerically, I find that all three variables converge to a fixed
point (and oscillate on the way to the fixed point). In the appendix, some simulations and
illustrations are presented.
