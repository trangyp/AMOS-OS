Serious Games: Human-AI Interaction, Evolution, and Coevolution 
Short title: Serious Games: Human-AI Evolutionary Dynamics 
 
Nandini Doreswamy1, 2, MBBS, MS, MBA (ORCID 0000-0002-8467-762X) 
1 Southern Cross University, Lismore, New South Wales, Australia 2National Coalition of Independent Scholars,

Abstract 
The serious games between humans and AI have only just begun. Evolutionary Game Theory (EGT) models the competitive and cooperative 
strategies of biological entities. EGT could help predict the potential evolutionary equilibrium of humans and AI. The objective of this work 
was to examine some of the EGT models relevant to human-AI interaction, evolution, and coevolution. Of thirteen EGT models considered, 
three were examined: the Hawk-Dove Game, Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and the War of Attrition. This selection was based on the widespread 
acceptance and clear relevance of these models to potential human-AI evolutionary dynamics and coevolutionary trajectories. The Hawk-
Dove Game predicts balanced mixed-strategy equilibria based on the costs of conflict. It also shows the potential for balanced coevolution 
rather than dominance. Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma suggests that repeated interaction may lead to cognitive coevolution. It demonstrates how 
memory and reciprocity can lead to cooperation. The War of Attrition suggests that competition for resources may result in strategic 
coevolution, asymmetric equilibria, and conventions on sharing resources. Therefore, EGT may provide a suitable framework to understand 
and predict the human-AI evolutionary dynamic. However, future research could extend beyond EGT and explore additional frameworks, 
empirical validation methods, and interdisciplinary perspectives. AI is being shaped by human input and is evolving in response to it. So too, 
neuroplasticity allows the human brain to grow and evolve in response to stimuli. If humans and AI converge in future, what might be the 
result of human neuroplasticity combined with an ever-evolving AI? Future research should be mindful of the ethical and cognitive 
implications of human-AI interaction, evolution, and coevolution. 
Keywords: evolutionary game theory, human-AI interaction, human and AI coevolution, neuroplasticity and AI evolution

1 INTRODUCTION 
Game Theory focuses on the “game”—a strategic or social setting in which two or more independent decision makers, or 
players, compete to produce an outcome [1, 2]. This theory can be applied to zero-sum games, which are competitive, and 
non-zero-sum games, which can be either competitive or non-competitive. An important enhancement of Game Theory is 
Nash Equilibrium [3], which describes the outcome of adversarial, non-cooperative games. It focuses on the point where the 
game reaches a state of equilibrium and no player can win an advantage by changing their strategy unilaterally. The main 
limitation of Game Theory and Nash Equilibrium is the assumption that players in a game are rational, self-interested, and 
maximize utility. 
Prisoner's Dilemma [4, 5] is a well-known illustration of Game Theory and Nash Equilibrium. It shows why two rational 
players may or may not cooperate, even if cooperation is in their best interest. Nash Equilibrium does not consider players’ 
past behavior [6]. However, past behavior influences future behavior [7] and may be the best predictor of future behavior [8].  
Evolutionary Game Theory (EGT) [9-11], an influential offshoot of Game Theory, does not have these limitations. It is not 
based on the artificial construct of purely rational players whose decisions are underpinned by rationality alone. It describes 
interactions between living things such as microorganisms, birds and animals in the natural world. It predicts the likely outcome 
of competitive strategies employed by biological species, within a single generation or over evolutionary timescales. This 
theory has also been extended to describe the outcome of cooperative strategies [12]. 
 
Therefore, EGT is more applicable to the real world than Game Theory. It is being constantly refined and enhanced to 
analyze an ever broader and more complex set of situations. Based on this theory, Sandholm [13] created a framework for the 
relationship between the evolutionary dynamics of a population and the rules that individuals within that population use, to 
learn. Skoulakis et al. [14] described an enhancement in which players compete in a scalable, zero-sum game. The players, the 
environment, and the game itself evolve over time.  
With the advent of AI, particularly advanced Generative AI, the concept of population, evolution, and serious games may 
extend beyond biological entities alone [15]. There are various flavors of Generative AI—ChatGPT, Claude AI, Bard, 
Perplexity, and many more, that can be regarded as an AI population. Further, these Generative AI may herald a new era 
dominated not just by artificial intelligence, but by artificial life [16]. Neuroevolution, an established branch of research on 
artificial life, aims to evolve—not design—artificial brains [17]. Adami [18] proposes that the process of Darwinian evolution 
should underpin the production of artificial brains fitted into robots, to create fully sentient machines. 
It seems inevitable that there will be numerous types and forms of AI in future, and that they will be far more advanced 
than Generative AI. As the evolution of the AI population accelerates, it may be useful, and important, to build robust 
frameworks and models—or adapt existing ones—that can predict human-AI interaction, evolution, and coevolution, over 
time. EGT, and well-established models based on this theory, may lead the way in developing appropriate and predictive 
frameworks and tools.  
In the modern era, interest in AI began in the 1950s [19-21]. In less than a hundred years, interactions with AI may already 
be affecting human cognition, both positively and negatively. For example, while AI processes vast amounts of information 
on digital platforms and helps guide human preferences in activities such as shopping, it may reduce the ability of humans to 
make decisions on their own [22]. Another example is the use of Global Positioning System (GPS) navigation with AI chatbots 
in cars and other vehicles, which may have a negative impact on the cognitive processes that create spatial knowledge [23]. 
AI may also impact human cognition in terms of attention, memory, addiction, and perception, and reinforce novelty-seeking 
behaviors [22]. Resilience and determination have underpinned human survival and achievement for millennia. The burning 
question is whether societies will take these attributes forward or trade them for the convenience that AI offers. 
AI is an artificial entity created by humans, but it may be evolving in a manner similar to biological entities. Baber [15] 
elaborated on Kelly’s argument [24] that technology is a natural extension of biological evolution—a living force that can 
extend human potential. Turkle [25] postulates that the use of technologies such as txt, email, and social media change the way 
humans view themselves and their relationships with others. Turkle argues that technology has become such an extension of 
the human self that it is a ‘phantom limb’ when humans are separated from it even briefly. Mijwil and Abttan [26] discuss the 
possibility of  transhumanism in future—the merging of humans on the one hand and AI-powered robots on the other, into 
cybernetic beings or cyborgs. 
Ramsey [27] postulated that humans make decisions by maximizing expected utility. However, this theory of human 
decision-making behavior is incomplete if not problematic [28-32]. It is more likely that humans make complex decisions 
based on cognitive and affective processes [33, 34]. As AI evolves, will its decisions be based on a different class of cognition 
and on different affective processes?

Artificial neural networks (ANN) and AI are based on the current knowledge and understanding of human neural networks 
[35]. However, the exact structures and processes of human neurons, their connections, and networks are yet to be fully 
understood [36]. In fact, the brain has the ability to adapt, learn and grow based on experience and external stimuli. This 
process is known as neuroplasticity [37]. Neuroscientists are now learning more about human neuronal networks and 
connections by studying ANN structures [38]. Humans and AI are already learning from each other, it seems, with AI and 
ANN based on human neural networks, and neuroscientists in turn learning from ANN. Litt et al. [39] proposed the Neural 
Affective Decision Theory. It is based on four principles: decision-making is a cognitive–affective process; it is coordinated 
by interconnected neurons that interact across various areas of the brain; preferences are formed by different but interacting 
mechanisms mediated by the chemicals dopamine and serotonin; and decisions and judgements depend on patterns of neural 
activation based on how information is framed and presented. 
In the context of EGT, strategic substitutes and strategic complements are fascinating concepts that may significantly 
influence interactive decision-making. Strategic substitutes are scenarios where players' decisions offset each other [40]. In 
evolutionary contexts, strategic substitutes are the foundation of competitive dynamics. On the other hand, strategic 
complements are scenarios in which the decisions of two or more players mutually reinforce one another [40]. In evolutionary 
contexts, strategic complements tend to create dynamics where successful strategies spread through populations and positive 
feedback loops lead to convergent evolution. Strategic complements may also predict stable evolutionary outcomes, known as 
Evolutionarily Stable Strategy (ESS), which is one of the cornerstones of EGT. 
EGT incorporates Game Theory, Darwin’s theory of evolution, and the role of heredity and natural selection [41]. It 
provides a robust theoretical foundation for diverse disciplines, including economics, social sciences, and biology. Importantly, 
it is also relevant to understanding ecological dynamics and coevolution [42]. 
1.1 Rationale and Objectives 
EGT has been used to predict human behavior in many competitive and cooperative situations  [43-47]. It has also been applied 
to AI behaviors [48, 49]. Therefore, it may form a suitable theoretical basis to explore the nature of serious games that humans 
and AI play. The objective is to examine some of the relevant EGT models through the lens of human-AI interaction, evolution, 
and coevolution. The aim is to explore how each model could potentially apply to the serious games between these two entities.

In the interest of time and resources, a decision was made to examine only three of these models, based on their widespread 
acceptance and clear relevance to human-AI interaction, evolution, and coevolution. Each of these models was examined from 
the perspective of human and AI decision-making attributes [71-73]. The models were also examined from psychological and 
biological perspectives and from the perspective of artificial intelligence: 
• The Hawk-Dove Game [9] models aggressive and cooperative strategies based on a paradigm of interaction between 
hawks and doves. Hawks aggressively pursue resources regardless of cost, while doves share resources or demonstrate 
strategic displays, but retreat if challenged. The model demonstrates why natural selection often favors limited 
conflict over potentially destructive escalation of conflict. The game typically results in mixed-strategy equilibria 
based on the relative costs of aggression versus cooperation, rather than pure strategic dominance. 
• Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma [12, 51] is an extension of classic Prisoner's Dilemma [4, 5]. It  allows players to 
interact repeatedly over multiple rounds of the game, remember previous choices, and adjust strategies accordingly. 
Each player must repeatedly decide whether to cooperate or defect, with payoffs determined by a combination of the 
choices made by both the players. This repetition enables the emergence of reciprocity, reputation, and conditional 
strategies such as tit-for-tat. The model demonstrates how cooperation can emerge through repeated interaction, even 
when defection may be rational in single encounters. 
• The War of Attrition was described in 1974 [10] and expanded later [52-54]. It models competitive scenarios where 
two contestants vie for a resource through persistent displays or confrontation, until one participant withdraws. Over 
time, costs accumulate for both competitors. The competition is based on endurance and creates a strategic balance. 
The decision to withdraw depends on a player’s assessment of a resource’s value and their tolerance for the costs. 
3 RESULTS 
The analysis of the three EGT models revealed distinct patterns that can be applied to human-AI interaction. 
• The Hawk-Dove Game suggests that mixed-strategy equilibria will likely emerge between humans and AI, with 
neither cooperation nor competition dominating. Instead, a dynamic balance is likely, based on the costs of conflict. 
When AI systems adopt aggressive "hawk" strategies, they may trigger defensive regulatory responses, while mutual 
"dove" strategies enable cooperative coevolution. 
• Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma indicates that repeated interaction between humans and AI may create cognitive 
coevolution. This could lead to cooperative equilibrium, competitive escalation, or convergence based on memory 
capacity and reward structures. 
• The War of Attrition suggests that competition for resources may drive strategic coevolution, where each entity's 
persistence threshold influences the timing of withdrawal. This could result in conventions for resource sharing, 
optimal contest durations, or asymmetric equilibria, depending on cost tolerance and the value assigned to a resource.

4 DISCUSSION 
4.1 Hawk-Dove Game 
Pioneered by Smith and Price [9], the Hawk-Dove Game may offer profound insights into human-AI coevolution. The model 
examines competitive scenarios in which aggressive (hawk) and cooperative (dove) strategies co-exist, resulting in restrained 
conflict and evolutionarily stable outcomes, despite more aggressive alternatives. 
The Hawk-Dove Game demonstrates why natural selection may favor limited conflict over escalated fighting, explaining 
why ritualized contests are more common in nature than fights to the death. In the context of human-AI interaction, this model 
illuminates a strategic landscape where both entities may adopt cooperative approaches that reduce the costs of conflict, instead 
of more aggressive, resource-hungry strategies. Animals evolve to favor limited conflict, or displays of conflict, instead of 
lethal combat. Similarly, humans and AI systems may evolve to balance competition with cooperation. 
The Hawk-Dove Game suggests that when AI systems adopt "hawk" strategies that maximize their objectives without 
constraint, they may trigger defensive "hawk" responses from humans through regulation or disconnection. Conversely, when 
both adopt "dove" strategies, cooperative coevolution becomes possible. Therefore, this model points to the likelihood of 
mixed-strategy equilibria emerging in human-AI coevolution, where neither pure cooperation nor pure competition dominates. 
Instead, a dynamic balance may develop, based on the relative costs of conflict versus cooperation.  
In animal studies, Hardy and Mesterton-Gibbons demonstrated that this model extends to triadic interactions, alliances, 
respect for territory, and signaling behaviors. Similarly, in future, human-AI coevolution may result in coalition formation, 
mutual respect for domains, and status displays that shape the boundaries of interaction. Such Evolutionarily Stable Strategies 
(ESS) between humans and AI may result in neither complete technological dominance nor rejection. Instead, a complex 
coevolutionary dance of strategic adaptations may develop, where each entity influences the trajectory of the other's evolution. 
4.2 Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma 
Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma [12, 51], a model that stems from EGT, is an important variation of Prisoner's Dilemma [4, 5]. In 
this model, two players select mutual strategies repeatedly, and remember their past behaviors. This is not only applicable to 
human interactions, but also to interactions between biological species that are engaged in endless games, in nature. Iterated 
Prisoner's Dilemma has been applied to social sciences such as sociology, economics, and politics, as well as biological 
sciences like evolutionary biology [74, 75]. 
This model is not only relevant to interactions between different biological species, but also to interactions between AI. 
DeepMind Technologies, a subsidiary of Google’s parent company Alphabet Inc., applied Prisoner’s Dilemma and Iterated 
Prisoner’s Dilemma to AI [76], with interesting results. When two AI players were pitted against each other, they modified 
their behavior to cooperate or compete, depending on the context [76]. These findings indicate that AI players apply complex 
attributes to arrive at strategic and context-dependent decisions. Humans, too, apply complex attributes to make effective 
decisions: for instance, they may deploy both rationality and intuition in decision-making [73, 77]. In evolutionary terms, 
repeated interaction between humans and AI may create cognitive coevolution, with each entity's decisions influencing the 
other's adaptive responses. 
4.3  War of Attrition 
The War of Attrition [10, 52-54] is an EGT model in which two players compete for a resource by displaying, waiting, or 
fighting, with the contest ending when one player withdraws. The longer the duration of the contest, the greater the cost; 
therefore, this is a war of endurance. This is not only applicable to human conflicts, but also applicable to competitions between 
players like insects and territorial animals engaged in costly signaling, in nature [78]. 
This model is an important variation of the Hawk-Dove Game [10]. It has been applied to disciplines such as economics, 
political science, and conflict studies, as well as biological sciences like behavioral ecology [79]. DeepMind Technologies 
applied War of Attrition principles to AI resource allocation [80]. When multiple AI agents competed for limited computational 
resources, they developed sophisticated withdrawal strategies based on assessments of cost and predictions of opponent 
behavior [80]. 
This model is relevant to competitions between different biological species as well as resource conflicts between AI 
systems. Therefore, it could be applied to competitive scenarios between biological and non-biological entities as well—
humans and AI, for instance. In evolutionary terms, repeated resource competitions between humans and AI may create 
strategic coevolution, with each entity's persistence threshold influencing the timing of the other's withdrawal.

The War of Attrition model may be an appropriate basis for the study of competition for resources between humans and 
AI. Humans apply complex attributes to optimize competitive outcomes, including competition for resources. When they make 
decisions under uncertainty, the results vary; for example, the social dilemmas of consuming resources equitably during the 
COVID-19 pandemic [81] or in the era of climate change [82]. Similarly, DeepMind's findings show that AI is capable of 
nuanced responses when competing for resources. In a War of Attrition, its withdrawal threshold is based on the value of a 
resource and the context of the competition [80]. Depending on the cost tolerance of each of these entities, and the value they 
each assign to a resource, the coevolution of humans and AI may result in conventions or agreements on sharing resources, 
optimal durations for contests, and/or asymmetric equilibria. 
4.4 Assumptions and Limitations 
This examination is limited by its selective focus on three EGT models from a broader theoretical landscape. It also assumes 
that current AI capabilities will scale predictably without major paradigm shifts. In addition, cultural and ethical dimensions 
of human-AI coevolution receive limited attention. Future research could address these limitations through a more 
comprehensive theoretical exploration across disciplines and through empirical studies of human-AI interaction. 
5 CONCLUSIONS 
This examination of three established EGT models through the lens of human-AI interaction, evolution, and coevolution. 
These models—the Hawk-Dove Game, Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and the War of Attrition—may offer valuable insights 
into conceptualizing and potentially predicting the complex dynamics emerging between humans and AI. Both entities are 
evolving, as are the environments in which they play—and the game itself. 
The Hawk-Dove Game illustrates how mixed-strategy equilibria between cooperation and competition might develop, 
potentially leading to a balanced coevolutionary relationship rather than dominance by either entity. The Iterated Prisoner's 
Dilemma highlights how memory, reciprocity, and repeated interaction could foster cooperative behaviors between humans 
and AI, with each influencing the other's adaptive responses. The War of Attrition model provides insights into how resource 
competition might drive strategic coevolution, based on persistence thresholds and value assessments. 
Understanding evolutionary dynamics may be crucial to guiding the relationship between humans and increasingly 
sophisticated AI. While this is a limited theoretical exploration, it suggests that EGT may provide a suitable theoretical basis 
to examine the evolutionary dynamic between these two entities. The initial exploration of three EGT models indicates that 
human-AI evolution and coevolution may well favor cooperative equilibrium over competitive escalation or the dominance of 
a single entity. 
The serious games between humans and AI have only just begun. While EGT holds promise as a suitable framework, future 
research could extend beyond it and explore additional approaches, empirical validation methods, and interdisciplinary 
perspectives. AI is being shaped by human input and is evolving in response to it. In tandem, the neuroplasticity of the human 
brain allows it to grow and evolve in response to life’s experiences. How will AI influence human evolution based on this 
phenomenon? If humans and AI converge in future, what might be the result of human neuroplasticity combined with an ever-
evolving AI? Future research should be mindful of the ethical and cognitive implications of human-AI interaction, evolution, 
and coevolution.
