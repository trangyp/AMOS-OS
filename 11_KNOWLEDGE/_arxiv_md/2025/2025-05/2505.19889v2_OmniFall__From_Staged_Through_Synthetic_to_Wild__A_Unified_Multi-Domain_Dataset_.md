OmniFall: From Staged Through Synthetic to Wild,
A Unified Multi-Domain Dataset for Robust Fall Detection

Abstract
Visual fall detection models trained on small, staged datasets
have unclear real-world utility due to limited diversity and
inconsistent evaluation protocols. We present OmniFall, a
unified benchmark with 80 hours / 15k videos and dense
frame-level annotations in a harmonized 16-class taxonomy,
spanning three complementary domains: OF-Staged (eight
public staged sets, standardized with cross-subject/view
splits), OF-Synthetic (12k videos, 17 h; controlled diversity
in age, body type, environment, camera), and OF-In-the-
Wild (the first test-only benchmark curated from genuine ac-
cident videos). OmniFall supports both video classification
and timeline segmentation, and its cross-domain protocol
isolates staged/synthetic→wild generalization. Our results
show that carefully designed synthetic data can match or
surpass real staged footage on cross-domain transfer—while
reducing privacy risk and easing data collection. By com-
bining privacy-amenable synthetic/staged sources with a
public, test-only wild target and releasing dense, standard-
ized timelines, OmniFall provides a comprehensive bench-
mark for privacy-preserving fall detection and fall-related
(pre/post-fall) segmentation, enabling robust detectors that
generalize to uncontrolled environments.

1. Introduction
Falls remain a leading cause of fatal and non-fatal injury in
older adults, claiming about 684,000 lives globally and send-
ing more than 37 million people to medical care, yearly [28].
Preventing death hinges on detecting such falls, but even
more so on recognizing the sustained fallen state—when
a person remains on the ground unable to summon help.

Computer vision-based fall detection offers potential for

(3) collecting fall data from vulnerable elderly populations

environments, visually homogeneous staging conditions,
and incompatible annotation taxonomies that prevent cross-
dataset comparison. This lack of diversity produces sys-
tems that perform well within their training domain but fail
under novel conditions. Moreover, the lack of real-world
evaluation protocols prevents assessment of the staged-data
model’s generalizability to actual accidents.
We introduce OmniFall, a unified benchmark compris-
ing three complementary components: OmniFall-Staged
(OF-Sta) (staged real-world data), OmniFall-In-the-Wild
(OF-ItW) (genuine accident videos), and OmniFall-Synthetic
(OF-Syn) (large-scale synthetic data with controlled diver-
sity). The benchmark provides 33 hours of densely anno-
tated single-view footage (82 hours, including multi-view
recordings), enabling systematic cross-domain evaluation
previously impossible due to annotation heterogeneity. The
taxonomy distinguishes transient actions (fall, sit down, lie
down, stand up), static states (fallen, sitting, lying, standing),
and additional activities, facilitating detection of critical
fallen states even when fall events are missed and disam-
biguation from non-critical activities such as lying down.
Why unify existing staged datasets? Individual datasets
offer limited environmental and subject diversity, leading
to poor cross-dataset generalization. OmniFall-Staged (OF-
Sta) addresses this by unifying eight public datasets [1, 3,
6, 14, 17, 23, 29] under standardized annotations, providing
14 hours of unique recordings (42 hours multi-view) across
101 subjects and 29 camera views. This aggregation en-
ables cross-dataset, cross-subject, and cross-view evaluation
protocols while substantially increasing training diversity
compared to any single dataset alone.
Why the need for in-the-wild evaluation?
Staged
datasets feature healthy actors in controlled settings with
safety equipment, creating unknown generalization gaps to
real-world deployment. OmniFall-In-the-Wild (OF-ItW) es-
tablishes the first standardized benchmark for staged-to-wild
evaluation, curating 2.6 hours of genuine accident videos
from uncontrolled environments with natural camera motion,
varied lighting, and authentic falls. This out-of-distribution
test set enables systematic assessment of how models trained
on staged or synthetic data perform on real accidents.
Why add synthetic data? Collecting diverse fall data
from elderly populations and children raises ethical concerns
and practical barriers. OmniFall-Synthetic (OF-Syn) con-
tributes 17 h of synthetic video, featuring controlled diversity
across age, ethnicity, body type, gender, and environments.
Each video depicts a unique person-environment combina-
tion, providing substantially greater diversity than staged
collections while addressing privacy concerns.
Our contributions are threefold. First, OF-Staged and OF-
In-the-Wild provide a staged-to-wild cross-domain bench-
mark with over 60 hours of manual frame-level annota-
tions. Second, OF-Synthetic adds synthetic-to-wild eval-
uation and diverse training data with controlled demographic
variation. Third, comprehensive fine-tuning experiments
demonstrate that the staged-to-wild domain gap is larger
than the synthetic-to-wild gap, with synthetic data outper-
forming real staged data for out-of-distribution generaliza-
tion—suggesting that synthetic video data may surpass ex-
isting data collections for real-world human fall detection.
2. Related Work
Many fall detection datasets have been introduced in recent
years [1–4, 6–8, 14, 15, 17, 23, 29], reflecting the growing
interest in automated fall detection.
Fall Detection Datasets in OmniFall-Staged.
To collect
existing public fall detection datasets from prior work, we
conducted a systematic search in accordance with PRISMA
guidelines (5)-(9) [19]. Of the 98 surveys retrieved, only
9 met all inclusion criteria. The public datasets (1)-(8) ex-
tracted from these surveys are listed in Table 1.
(1) The CMDFall dataset [23] is a multimodal, multi-
view collection acquired from seven synchronized Kinect
sensors (providing color and depth data) together with two
accelerometers. It comprises recordings of 50 subjects per-
forming eight distinct fall types alongside twelve non-fall
activities. (2) UP Fall [17] offers a multimodal setup from
17 subjects in a laboratory environment. It includes vision
data from a pair of synchronized cameras (640×480 at 18
fps), inertial measurements from accelerometers and gyro-
scopes worn at different body locations covering six activi-
ties of daily living (ADL) and five fall types. (3) The Le2i
dataset [6] contains 143 staged falls and 79 ADL actions
recorded from a single fixed camera at 640×480 pixels and
25 fps across four environments and six views, (4) GMD-
CSA [1] delivers high temporal resolution data, capturing
81 fall and 79 ADL clips recorded with a stationary laptop
camera at 1280×720 pixels and 30fps in three distinct home
settings. Its design emphasizes intra-class variability, with
subjects altering clothing and backgrounds between clips.
(5) The EDF and (6) OCCU datasets [29] were acquired in a
controlled apartment environment using two Kinect sensors
at 320×240 pixels and 30 fps. The EDF set focuses on 40
non-occluded falls by five subjects performing falls from
eight directions, as well as 30 non-fall actions. In compari-
son, OCCU presents a more challenging scenario in which 30
falls become partially occluded near the end, accompanied
by a suite of 80 ADL actions. (7) The Multiple Camera
Fall (MCFD) dataset [3] leverages eight synchronized IP
cameras installed in a fixed indoor setting to capture 22 fall
scenarios, facilitating 3D reconstructions either explicitly
or implicitly. (8) The CAUCA Fall dataset [14] provides
recordings from 10 subjects in an apartment setting under
varying lighting conditions. Captured at 720×480 resolution
and 23 fps, it includes both color and infrared modes (in

low-light situations) with detailed, frame-wise annotations
that report not only fall versus non-fall labels but also spatial
metrics, such as distance and angle with respect to the cam-
era. OOPS! [11] is a 20.7 k-clip (>50 h) video dataset with
labels marking when intentional actions turn accidental, sup-
porting benchmarks in intention classification, failure-point
localization, and accident forecasting.
Other Datasets.
We identified several additional fall de-
tection datasets, but excluded them from OF-Staged due to
specific limitations. UR Fall [15] was not included due to
its limited size. MUVIM [9] offers multimodal data, but all
videos are captured from a top-down view. FPDS [16] con-
sists of static images, we only considered full video datasets.
While High Quality FSD [4], FallFree [2], SIMPLE [8], and
ACT42 [7] align well with our objectives, we were unable
to include them due to missing usage licenses or the lack of
explicit permission from the authors.
What is missing.
These datasets comprise staged record-
ings of healthy participants in controlled environments.
While valuable for method development, these corpora do
not establish how models transfer to in-the-wild falls, and
they are annotated with heterogeneous label sets and proto-
cols, hampering cross-dataset comparison. Three gaps stand
out: (1) Staging bias: lab scenes, fixed cameras, and scripted
routines dominate. (2) Annotation heterogeneity: labels vary
from binary fall/no-fall to coarse ADL categories; few offer
dense, consistent timelines. (3) Lack of standardized OOD
evaluation: there is no widely-used protocol to quantify
staged→wild generalization.
These gaps motivate OmniFall, a composite benchmark
that (i) aggregates eight public staged datasets under unified,
dense frame-wise annotations in a 16-class taxonomy. These
are included as OF-Staged as listed in Table 1: (ii) adds a cu-
rated in-the-wild test set (OF-ItW) derived from OOPS [11];
and (iii) contributes a synthetic set (OF-Syn) with controlled
diversity (age, body type, environment, camera) to provide
diversity and probe sim→real transfer. Beyond aggregation
and large-scale data generation, our main contribution is
the standardization: harmonized per-frame labels, dataset-
specific cross-subject/view splits, and a standard evaluation
protocol that enables principled cross-domain comparisons.

Design principles and contributions.
Our goal is com-
parability across domains. To that end we: (i) Standard-
ize labels by manually re-annotating all videos to a uni-
fied 16-class timeline taxonomy (four transient actions: fall,
sit_down, lie_down, stand_up; five locomotion/transition
actions: walk, kneel_down, squat_down, crawl, jump; six
static states: fallen, sitting, lying, standing, kneeling, squat-
ting; plus other). (ii) Densify annotations per frame, so
methods can be evaluated on both classification and timeline
segmentation. (iii) Unify splits by providing cross-subject
(CS) and cross-view (CV) partitions for each staged dataset,
mirroring original protocols when available and filling gaps
otherwise. (iv) Enable OOD assessment with a held-out OF-
ItW split and controllable OF-Syn splits (random, cross-age,
cross-ethnicity, cross-BMI).

OF-Syn contributes
12k videos (17 h) generated with Wan 2.2 [27] at 1280×720

Prompts vary age, gender presentation, body type (BMI

Annotations and problem setup.
We cast fall understand-
ing as both video classification and timeline segmentation.
The unified 16-class taxonomy enables detecting medically
critical fallen states even when the fall event is missed, and
disambiguating visually similar actions (e.g., lie_down vs.
fall). All data is released with per-frame labels in this taxon-
omy, plus dataset-specific splits to standardize protocols.
4. Experiments
Our evaluation comprises three complementary experiments
using different methodologies. For domain shift analysis
(Sec. 4.1), we extract frozen motion oriented features from
I3D [5] and VideoMAE-K400 [22] and more appearnce-
based features from SigLIP2 [24] to analyze dataset similari-
ties and feature clustering. For action and fall classification
(Sec. 4.2), we fine-tune VideoMAE-K400 end-to-end on our
benchmark datasets. Timeline segmentation follows estab-
lished protocols using I3D and SigLIP2 features (Sec. 4.3).
Implementation details are provided in the appendix.

4.2. Action and fall classification
Experimental Setup
We evaluate four fine-tuning config-
urations for VMAE-K400 [22] to understand generalization
effects of training data sources. First, we train on the largest
and most comprehensive pre-existing fall dataset CMDFall
alone (7h), using separate models for cross-subject (CS) and
cross-view (CV) splits. Second, we train on our OF-Staged
superset (14 h, 8 datasets), again using CS and CV models
to evaluate both generalization protocols. Third, we train on
OF-Synthetic using its random (Ra) split (17h). Fourth, we
combine OF-Staged (CS) with OF-Synthetic (Ra), assess-
ing whether synthetic data complements real staged data.
For metrics, we report accuracy and macro-F1 for the
16-class task, alongside sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), and
F1-score for the critical binary subtasks Fall and Fallen
Macro-F1 scores tend to be lower due to the natural class im-
balance across 16 categories (Figure 6 illustrates this effect).

mark best and second-best results with green and orange.

5. Conclusion
We introduced OmniFall, a unified benchmark with ∼80
hours of densely annotated video across three complemen-
tary components—OF-Staged (eight public datasets with
standardized cross-subject/view protocols), OF-In-the-Wild
(a rigorous test-only set curated from genuine accident
videos), and OF-Synthetic (12k videos with controlled de-
mographic and environmental diversity). A harmonized
16-class taxonomy provides framewise timelines for both
classification and timeline segmentation, including detection
of medically critical fallen states. Experiments show that di-
versity drives generalization: training on OF-Staged outper-
forms any single staged set on OF-ItW (Fall F1: 60.5–61.2 %
vs. 52.3 %), while OF-Syn alone bridges the staged→wild
gap more effectively (64.2 %) with marginal gains from
combined training (65.6 %). Timeline segmentation fur-
ther reveals feature sensitivity: appearance-centric embed-
dings benefit most from synthetic diversity, whereas motion-
centric ones profit from staged dynamics, underscoring the
complementarity of the three domains. Coupling dense, stan-
dardized timelines with staged, synthetic, and wild regimes
under a unified protocol, OmniFall exposes a challenging
staged→wild transfer where high in-distribution scores do
not guarantee segmental precision in the wild. The bench-
mark’s privacy-amenable synthetic component and public,

test-only wild target make it a practical and reproducible
benchmark for advancing fall recognition/segmentation to-
ward methods that balance boundary precision with domain
robustness under real-world constraints.

A. Appendix
Limitations.
Despite our efforts to create a diverse bench-
mark, several limitations remain: (1) OF-ItW, while provid-
ing rigorous in-the-wild evaluation, represents only a subset
of possible real-world fall scenarios; (2) OF-Syn’s controlled
demographic diversity addresses biases in staged datasets
(which predominantly feature young subjects), yet synthetic
data cannot fully capture all real-world complexities and
potentially introduces new, generator-based biases [27]; (3)
deployment in care settings requires careful consideration
of privacy, surveillance ethics, and user acceptance beyond
technical performance.
Societal Impact.
Fall detection systems offer substantial
benefits for elder care and health monitoring, potentially
saving lives through timely intervention. Our demonstra-
tion that synthetic data can outperform real staged data
while providing controlled demographic diversity offers a
privacy-preserving path forward that avoids collecting sen-
sitive footage from vulnerable populations. However, we
acknowledge potential negative impacts: surveillance con-
cerns in care settings, false alarms causing trust erosion,
reduced human oversight, and the risk that synthetic data
biases affect real-world performance across demographics.
Responsible deployment requires transparent performance
reporting across demographic groups, human-in-the-loop
decision systems, and ongoing validation in real care envi-
ronments.

A.2.2. OF-Synthetic
OF-Synthetic provides four evaluation protocols designed
to probe demographic and phenotypic generalization across
age, ethnicity, and body composition.
Random Split (80/10/10).
A standard baseline dividing
the dataset into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% test-
ing, comprising 9,600/1,200/1,200 videos with correspond-
ing 15,344/1,956/1,928 action segments. This split is used
by default in our experiments.
Cross-Age Split.
This split evaluates transfer across age
groups by training on adults (young adults and middle-aged
individuals), validating on teenagers, and testing on children,
toddlers, and elderly individuals. The configuration com-
prises 4,000 training videos, 2,000 validation videos, and
6,000 test videos.
Cross-Ethnicity Split.
To assess generalization across
large phenotypic variations, training includes individuals
identified as White, Asian, and Hispanic or Latino; valida-
tion uses American Indian or Alaska Native; and testing
targets Black or African American, Middle Eastern or North
African, and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander. The split
sizes are 5,178 training, 1,741 validation, and 5,081 test
videos.
Cross-BMI Split.
This protocol examines robustness to
body-type variation by training on underweight and normal-
weight individuals, validating on overweight individuals, and
testing on obese individuals, with 6,066/2,962/2,972 videos
per split.
A.3. OF-Synthetic Data Generation Process.
Existing fall detection datasets exhibit systematic demo-
graphic biases, predominantly featuring young to middle-
aged participants with limited ethnic diversity. To address
these limitations while avoiding ethical complexities of col-
lecting fall data from vulnerable elderly populations, we gen-
erated OF-Synthetic with explicit control over demographic
attributes through structured prompt design for the Wan 2.2
video diffusion model, ensuring systematic coverage across
age groups, ethnicities, body types, and environmental con-
texts.
A.3.1. Demographic Attribute Design
Age Groups.
Six age categories follow common report-
ing practices: toddlers (1–4 years), children (5–12 years),
teenagers (13–17 years), young adults (18–34 years), middle-
aged (35–64 years), and elderly (65+ years). While elderly
individuals constitute our primary target demographic, all
age groups enable age-specific evaluation and provide train-
ing diversity. Each age group receives equal representation
with 200 generated videos per action class.
Race and Ethnicity.
To ensure systematic ethnic diver-
sity, we adopt the seven categories defined by the U.S.
Office of Management and Budget (OMB) standard for
race and ethnicity reporting [25]: Black, Asian, White,
Hispanic/Latino, Middle Eastern/North African (MENA),
American Indian/Alaska Native (AIAN), and Native Hawai-
ian/Pacific Islander (NHPI). This standardized taxonomy
enables consistent demographic reporting and comparison
with population statistics.
Skin Tone.
Beyond categorical ethnicity labels, we in-
corporate the Monk Skin Tone Scale [18], a perceptually
uniform 10-point scale (MST1–MST10) ranging from very
light to completely dark skin. This continuous representa-
tion captures intra-ethnic skin tone variation and provides
finer-grained control over visual appearance diversity than
ethnicity categories alone.
Body Morphology.
Four BMI bands (underweight, nor-
mal, overweight, obese) and three height categories (short,
average, tall) provide systematic body type coverage. BMI
bands translate to appearance descriptors (thin, average build,
chubby, stocky) in prompts. Gender presentation is specified
as male or female.
Beyond person-level attributes, environmental context
and scenario characteristics provide additional sources of
controlled variation.
A.3.2. Environmental and Contextual Diversity
Age-Appropriate Environments.
Each age group is asso-
ciated with 100 indoor and 100 outdoor environment descrip-
tors reflecting typical activity contexts for that demographic.
Younger age groups feature educational and play-oriented
settings: toddler environments include daycare play areas,
indoor playgrounds, and sandbox areas; children’s environ-
ments span elementary classrooms, playgrounds, and sports
fields; teenage environments encompass high school hall-
ways, athletic facilities, and teen activity centers. Adult age
groups reflect professional and residential contexts: young
adult environments include offices, gyms, universities, and
apartments; middle-aged environments feature corporate set-
tings, professional spaces, and home environments; elderly


## Page 13


environments comprise senior centers, assisted living facili-
ties, medical clinics, and residential areas. This age-specific
environment mapping ensures generated scenarios reflect
realistic activity contexts for each demographic.
Clothing Diversity.
Each age group has 200+ age-
appropriate clothing options spanning everyday casual wear
(60%), seasonal attire (25%), sleepwear (10%), and spe-
cial occasion clothing (5%). Examples range from toddlers’
colorful casual wear to elderly soft comfortable clothing,
ensuring contextually appropriate attire with visual diversity.
A.3.3. Action-Specific Attributes
Beyond demographic and environmental attributes, which
remain constant across action classes, each action class in-
corporates action-specific descriptors to control temporal
dynamics and scenario characteristics.
Fall-Specific Attributes.
Fall videos include 30 fall mech-
anism types (slip on wet surface, trip over obstacle, lose bal-
ance from dizziness), 20 fall direction specifications (falling
forward, backward, sideways, diagonally, while spinning),
20 recovery attempt descriptions (reaching for support, wind-
milling arms, bracing for impact), age-appropriate prior ac-
tivities, and 20 facial expression descriptors.
The inclusion of facial expressions addresses a specific
challenge with the Wan 2.2 model: the model exhibits a
tendency to generate individuals with smiling or happy ex-
pressions, presumably due to training data distribution or
alignment objectives. This creates inappropriate and unre-
alistic scenarios where individuals smile while falling. To
counteract this bias, we explicitly specify contextually ap-
propriate facial expressions (startled, panicked, focused, sur-
prised) in fall scenario prompts.
Other Action Classes.
Similar attribute sets were defined
for other action classes (lie down, stand up, sit down, walk),
incorporating action-specific motion descriptors, environ-
mental constraints, and contextual details appropriate to each
activity. The “other” category receives special attention to
ensure minimal overlap with the 15 primary action classes
through pre-screening of candidate activities for distinctive-
ness.
A.3.4. Camera Specification
To complete the prompt specification, camera parameters
control viewpoint variation. Each video specifies camera van-
tage point through four attributes: elevation (eye-level, low,
high/CCTV perspective, top-down/birds eye), azimuth (front,
rear, left, right), distance (medium/distant shot, far/long-
distance shot), and shot type (static wide, static medium
wide). Frame rates are specified as 24fps, 25fps, or 30fps
real-time. This systematic camera parameter variation pro-
duces videos from diverse viewpoints representative of real-
world surveillance and monitoring camera placements.
A.3.5. Prompt Construction and Generation
Prompt Structure.
Each prompt combines demographic,
environmental, action-specific, and camera attributes into
a structured natural language description. A typical fall
prompt follows this template:
“A [height] [body type] [age descriptor] [gender]
with [skin tone] and [ethnicity] features wearing
[clothing] is in the process of falling due to [fall
mechanism], [fall direction], in [environment],
while [recovery attempt], after [prior activity],
with [facial expression], [framerate], [shot type],
camera positioned [elevation] from the [azimuth],
it is a [distance]. Realistic. Fully dressed upper
body.”
The modifiers “Realistic” and “Fully dressed upper body”
were found beneficial for generating appropriate video con-
tent.
Deterministic Attribute Assignment.
To ensure repro-
ducibility and systematic coverage of the attribute space,
each video receives a unique identifier (e.g., fall_el_001
for elderly fall video 1). This identifier deterministically
seeds attribute selection via hash-based random number gen-
eration, ensuring identical attributes are selected for the same
identifier across generation runs while producing diverse at-
tribute combinations across the full dataset.
Dataset Scale.
We generated 200 videos per age group
for 10 base action classes (fall, fallen, lie down, lying, sit
down, sitting, stand up, standing, walk, other), yielding
200 × 6 age groups × 10 actions = 12,000 videos totaling
17 hours of synthetic footage at 1280×720 resolution and
16fps. While the initial generation targeted these 10 action
classes, subsequent dense annotation revealed the utility of
additional fine-grained distinctions, leading to the expansion
to the full 16-class taxonomy used for timeline segmentation
evaluation.
A.3.6. Safety Filtering and Iterative Refinement
The Wan 2.2 model occasionally produced inappropriate con-
tent requiring iterative filtering: generated videos were man-
ually reviewed, inappropriate content removed, and prompts
refined to reduce recurrence. This process repeated until
the dataset contained only safe-for-work content suitable for
public release.

A.4. OmniFall Dense Annotation Process
Unifying eight staged datasets, curating genuine accident
videos, and annotating 12,000 synthetic videos into a con-
sistent 16-class taxonomy required substantial annotation
effort. This section details the annotation workflow, software
tooling, and quality assurance measures employed to ensure
consistent, frame-level labels across the 33 hours of single-
view footage (82 hours including multi-view recordings).
A.4.1. Annotation Software Selection and Customization
After systematic comparison, we selected VGG Image An-
notator (VIA) [10] as the most efficient platform among
available open-source video annotation tools. Custom mod-
ifications introduced comprehensive keyboard-only anno-
tation capabilities, enabling all operations without mouse
interaction. This accelerated annotation throughput through
rapid temporal navigation, label assignment, and boundary
adjustment via keyboard shortcuts, particularly beneficial for
precise frame-level timeline segmentation.
A.4.2. Annotation Team and Training
Approximately 10 annotators received detailed instruction
on the 16-class taxonomy, emphasizing visually similar ac-
tion pairs (e.g., fall vs. lie down) and temporal boundaries
between transient actions and static states. Frequent team
discussions established consensus on challenging cases, in-
cluding transition frame determination (fall to fallen), con-
trolled vs. uncontrolled descents, and handling occluded or
out-of-frame actions.
A.4.3. Multi-View Annotation Strategy
For datasets with synchronized multi-view recordings (CMD-
Fall, UP Fall, EDF, OCCU, MCFD), we employed an effi-
cient annotation transfer approach rather than independently
annotating all camera views. For each recording, we sys-
tematically selected the primary camera view most likely
to maintain the subject in frame throughout the activity se-
quence. This selection criterion minimized instances where
the subject would be visible in other views but occluded or
out-of-frame in the annotated view.
Annotations were performed on this primary view and
subsequently transferred to all synchronized camera views.
During transfer, we accounted for temporal offsets between
synchronized cameras to ensure frame-level label alignment
across views. This approach substantially reduced annota-
tion effort (14 hours of unique recordings vs. 42 hours of
multi-view footage) while providing complete timeline an-
notations for all camera perspectives, enabling cross-view
evaluation protocols.
A.4.4. Quality Assurance Protocol
Nearly all videos underwent two-pass review: initial frame-
level annotation followed by quality control verifying tem-
poral boundary accuracy, label correctness, and taxonomy
consistency. Discrepancies were corrected, with ambiguous
cases referred to team discussion. This workflow, combin-
ing specialized tooling, comprehensive training, consensus
meetings, and efficient multi-view transfer, ensured dense,
consistent annotations enabling OmniFall’s evaluation proto-
col.

Implementation details action classification.
We employ
a lightweight temporal transformer for all classification ex-
periments. The model processes sequences of extracted
features from the previously discussed backbones as vectors
and maps them to class predictions. Its architecture follows
a standard encoder design: input features are first linearly
projected to a unified embedding dimension, normalized,
and enriched with positional embeddings. A shallow stack
of transformer encoder layers then performs multi-head self-
attention before a linear classifier maps aggregated token
representations to the target classes. Training uses AdamW
with a base learning rate of 1e-5, weight decay of 0.01, over
a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler with mixed preci-
sion and a batch size of 32 over 50 epochs.
Implementation details supervised timeline segmentation.
We use a standard ASQuery [13] model for timeline segmen-
tation. The architecture follows the established query-based
formulation: precomputed frame-level features are processed
by a transformer decoder, enabling a set of queries to attend
to the full sequence and extract temporally coherent rep-
resentations. These queried embeddings are decoded into
dense per-frame segmentation scores.
