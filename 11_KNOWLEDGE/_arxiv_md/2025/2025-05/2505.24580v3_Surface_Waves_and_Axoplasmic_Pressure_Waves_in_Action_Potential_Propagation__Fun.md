Potential Propagation: Fundamentally Di®erent Physics or
Two Sides of the Same Coin?

In this commentary, we argue that El Hady and Machta's “surface wave” model1 for mechanical
waves accompanying action potential (AP) propagation describes the same underlying process as
the “axoplasmic pressure wave” model introduced earlier by Rvachev.2 Both models describe me-
chanical modes thatstore potentialenergy in theelastic components ofthe axon (axonal membrane,
cytoskeleton,bulkaxoplasmic deformation), with kineticenergy carried bythe axoplasmic °uidand
axoplasmic viscosity playing a signi¯cant role. The “surface wave” model quantitatively considers
driving by the traveling electrical depolarization wave of the AP, whereas the “axoplasmic pressure
wave” model qualitatively considers driving not only by the AP's electrical depolarization but also
by other mechanisms, such as cytoskeletal actomyosin contractility. In addition, the “axoplasmic
pressure wave” model considers mechanisms for synchronizing the depolarization wave and the
pressure wave. Although derived using di®erent approaches, the two models yield identical
dependencies for the mechanical modes in key limits. The confusion in the literature, which treats
these models as describing distinct processes, needs to be resolved to improve comprehensive
understanding of the AP phenomenon and to guide future research.
Keywords: Neurons; action potential; biophysical modeling; signal propagation; ion channels;
surface waves; axoplasmic pressure waves.
For more than seventy years, action potentials (APs) propagating along the neu-
ronal axon surface have been modeled and experimented on largely within the
con¯nes of the electrical framework introduced by Hodgkin and Huxley (HH) in the
1950s.3

manifestations cannot be straightforwardly accommodated within the irreversible,

framework have also been proposed.5
An explicit challenge to the HH model came from Heimburg and Jackson,6 who in
2005, building on earlier work by Kaufmann,7 proposed that APs are adiabatic
density pulses (solitons) in the axonal membrane, propagating via nonlinear, dis-
persive mechanics. In 2007, they extended their model, publishing in this journal,8 to
show how anesthetics a®ect membrane phase behavior and pulse propagation.
In 2010, also in this journal, Marat Rvachev presented an \axoplasmic pressure
wave" model, suggesting that HH-type AP propagation is accompanied by a pressure
pulse traveling through the axoplasm along the axon's length.2 The Rvachev model
proposed a mechanism that allows the electrical and mechanical waves to synchronize
via stretch modulation of voltage-gated ion channels. For pressure pulse generation
and ampli¯cation, several mechanisms were suggested, including a Ca2þ-driven pro-
cess that may act via actomyosin contractility, as well as a simpler mechanism of
electro-mechanical coupling of the membrane to the HH voltage spike, as further
discussed below.
This was followed in 2015 by Ahmed El Hady and Benjamin Machta, who
proposed a \surface wave" model that considered the mechanical (and other
nonelectrical) aspects of the AP as driven by the electrical aspect, irrespective of
how the electrical wave is generated.1 In their words, \Our model does not require
an underlying theory of how this electrical component arises. We emphasize that
any travelling electrical wave will induce a co-propagating mechanical wave
. . .."1
However, in their e®ort to promote the novelty of their model, El Hady and
Machta moreover stated that \Our model di®ers from most other models of the
mechanical response existing in the literature, in that it is electrically driven by the
depolarization wave that forms the AP. Where driving has been explicitly consid-
ered, it has been taken to arise from actomyosin contractility."1 With this latter
statement, El Hady and Machta speci¯cally refer to the Rvachev model.2 Contrary
to the claim of El Hady and Machta, however, the \axoplasmic pressure wave" model
does speci¯cally consider the electrical driving of axoplasmic pressure waves, albeit,
along with actomyosin contractility. It states: \We also posit that generation and
ampli¯cation of the axoplasmic pressure pulse may proceed through electro-
mechanical coupling such as voltage-induced membrane movement9 resulting from

While there are some relatively minor di®erences between the model formulations,
they do not fundamentally alter the underlying physics of passive pressure wave
propagation:
(1) El Hady and Machta speci¯cally punish radial elastic deformations, motivated
by experiments showing evenly spaced actin rings.11
(2) El Hady and Machta explicitly incorporate the e®ects of extracellular °uid.
(3) Rvachev accounts for the bulk compressibility of the axoplasm.
In addition, a key idea in the Rvachev model is the stretch modulation of voltage-
gated sodium (Nav) channels, which allows the electrical and mechanical waves to
co-propagate at a velocity higher than in the classical purely electrical Nav activation
mechanism and may be close to the velocity of the mechanical wave, regardless of
whether the mechanical driving force is actomyosin contractility, voltage-induced
membrane movement, or another axoplasmic disturbance caused by the AP. The
model treats all these driving forces qualitatively, while the El Hady and Machta
model provides quantitative estimates (including thermal aspects) for voltage-
induced membrane movementestimates that remain valid even if the electrical
and mechanical components propagate at di®erent velocities.
In fact, El Hady and Machta state: \We emphasize that the driven waves we
consider will travel at the speed of the electrical AP that drives them, cAP, and not
at their undriven propagation speed cpr."1 However, a mechanical pressure wave or
pulse cannot be constrained to propagate at an arbitrary cAP, just as when a
loudspeaker vibrates, the resulting sound waves propagate at the speed of sound in
the medium (air), not at the velocity of the loudspeaker. That the membrane
displacement (and the associated axoplasmic pressure pulse) propagates ahead of
the AP in cases where cpr > cAP can, in fact, be seen in El Hady and Machta's
results (Figs. 2(a)–2(f)), consistent with the Rvachev model. While the experi-
mentally observed magnitude of the membrane displacement appears to be well
described by electromechanical coupling with the AP in the El Hady and Machta
model, the velocity of the driven mechanical modes is, in our opinion, also similar to
the axoplasmic pressure wave modes in the Rvachev model, which, like the El Hady
and Machta model, assumes that the duration of the axoplasmic pressure pulse is
comparable to that of the AP (both models, however, su®er from imprecise
knowledge of the mechanical properties of axons). Although the El Hady and
Machta model can be considered more general than the Rvachev model, in the sense
that it does not necessarily prescribe a functional role for axoplasmic pressure waves
(particularly in cases where cpr < cAP), El Hady and Machta, like Rvachev, focus
primarily on the potentially functional implications of cases where cpr > cAP,
stating: \. . . it seems likely that biology would take advantage of this co-propa-
gating information, especially if real neurons are indeed in the regime where cpr >
cAP in which the mechanical component arrives before the electrical AP. . . . We
foresee future theoretical and experimental work will highlight the extent to which

Proper mathematical modeling and physical explanation of the propagating AP as a
multiphysical phenomenon mediating signal transmission and information proces-
sing in the nervous system remains a contentious topic debated by various groups of
theoretical and experimental neurophysicists/physiologists.5 In these discussions, the
\surface waves" model of El Hady and Machta and the earlier \axoplasmic pressure
waves" model of Rvachev continue to be treated as describing distinct physical
processes,12 sometimes used together in formulating a general unifying account of AP
propagation.13 However, as argued here, this is based on a misconception introduced
by El Hady and Machta, misattributing aspects of the \Rvachev model" to their own
work. In light of current confusion, the many unfortunate misunderstandings which
already riddle the debate on the physics of the AP,5 and the importance of the
propagating AP as a central concept in current scienti¯c understanding of neuronal
signaling, this misconception should be addressed and resolved. Doing so would also
contribute to re¯ning recent ideas about the potential role of intracellular pressure
integration in dendritic signal processing.14
