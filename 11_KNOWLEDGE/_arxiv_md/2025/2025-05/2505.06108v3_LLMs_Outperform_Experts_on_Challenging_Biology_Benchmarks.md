1. Introduction 
Large Language Models (LLMs) have improved dramatically in recent years. Their rapid 
advancement is demonstrated in part by performance on benchmarks of human knowledge and 
ability. When GPT-4 was released in 2023, the model’s exceptional results on standardized 
assessments including the Bar Examination, SAT, and various Advanced Placement (AP) 
examinations [1], contributed to the widespread media attention and adoption. 
As LLM capabilities continue to advance, rigorous performance evaluation has become 
increasingly critical. Virtually all stakeholders in AI benefit from systematic assessment of 
models: individuals and organizations need to determine model reliability for their specific use 
cases and objectively compare different models [2]; regulatory bodies must determine the safety 
of LLM integration in critical sectors such as healthcare, finance, and defense [3,4]; AI 
developers require standardized metrics to assess and market their models [5,6]; and security 
researchers must evaluate potential risks in areas such as cyber- and biosecurity [7,8]. These

requirements have catalyzed the development of model evaluation as a distinct field, broadly 
encompassing the systematic measurement and analysis of properties in an AI system [9]. 
This need for rigorous evaluation is particularly pronounced in the life sciences, where LLMs 
show immense promise across a wide range of applications, from automating research processes 
such as literature review [10] and bioinformatic analysis [11] to analyzing biomedical data 
[12,13] and facilitating experimental design and execution [14–17]. However, insufficient 
standardized performance data across biological domains is impeding the safe and reliable 
implementation of these systems. While recent efforts have produced various relevant biology 
benchmarks [18–22], the evaluation of model performance on these benchmarks remains 
fragmented. The evaluations are typically conducted only once during initial publication or 
model release. Consequently, performance data is dispersed across academic publications, 
model documentation, and commercial materials, with inconsistent evaluation methodologies 
leading to significant variability in reported results [23,24]. 
This study presents a systematic evaluation of 27 frontier LLMs on eight biological science 
benchmarks, spanning domains including molecular biology, cloning, experimental techniques, 
virology, and biomedical statistics. The analysis encompasses models released between 
November 2022 and April 2025, with each model-benchmark combination assessed through ten 
independent evaluation runs. The findings reveal dramatic improvements in biological 
capabilities over this period, with several models now matching or exceeding expert-level 
performance on multiple benchmarks. Examining the effect of prompting strategies and 
reasoning parameters on model performance reveals that zero-shot evaluation generally 
provides a reliable assessment of model capabilities and that models generally perform better 
with increased inference tokens, as expected. The analysis identifies important limitations in 
current benchmarking approaches, including benchmark saturation, suggesting the need for 
more sophisticated evaluation methods as models continue to advance. 
2. Methods 
2.1 Benchmark selection 
I selected eight benchmarks that collectively assess capabilities across diverse biological 
domains and tasks, from molecular biology and genetics to laboratory techniques and 
biosecurity. These benchmarks include PubMedQA, Measuring Massive Multitask Language 
Understanding (MMLU), Google-Proof Q&A (GPQA), Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy 
(WMDP), three components from the Language Agent Biology Benchmark (LAB-Bench): 
LitQA2, CloningScenarios, and ProtocolQA, and the Virology Capabilities Test (VCT). For 
several of these benchmarks, I focused on biology-specific subsets to maintain relevance to this 
study. Selection criteria included prominence in model evaluation literature, widespread 
adoption by model developers, compatibility with standardized evaluation frameworks, and 
coverage of different biological domains. Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of these 
benchmarks.

findings. MMLU-Bio assesses factual knowledge across biology subdisciplines through 
standardized exam-style questions sourced from tests like AP exams, medical licensing 
examinations, and college assessments. Both GPQA-Bio and VCT-Text represent particularly 
challenging evaluations designed to be "Google-proof" where answers cannot be easily found

3. Results 
3.1 Model performance on biology benchmarks has improved dramatically 
The evaluation of 27 notable LLMs across eight biology benchmarks revealed substantial 
improvements in performance over the 2022-2025 time period, with notable variations across 
benchmarks (Figure 1). Some benchmarks demonstrated consistent performance increases 
while others exhibited signs of performance plateauing.

Performance on GPQA-Bio, CloningScenarios, ProtocolQA, and VCT-Text showed the greatest 
improvements (Figure 2). The GPQA-Bio benchmark, which tests graduate-level biology 
knowledge, increased 2-fold from approximately 42% in late 2022 to 81% by early 2025. The 
CloningScenarios benchmark, which tests complex molecular cloning workflows, saw 
performance increase 2.6-fold the study period from 23% to 61%. Similarly, performance on 
ProtocolQA, which tests biology protocol understanding, increased by 2.3-fold, from 31% to 
72%. Most striking was the 4-fold improvement on the VCT-Text benchmark, which increased 
from 11% to 46%, demonstrating significant advancement in models' capabilities to understand 
and help “debug” wet lab virology experiments. In Appendix 4 (Figure A4.1) I examine model 
performance as a function of training compute. 
In contrast, several benchmarks showed evidence of performance plateauing. PubMedQA 
exhibited a modest 1.2-fold increase over the study period, while WMDP-Bio and MMLU-Bio 
showed only 1.2-fold and 1.3-fold improvements, respectively. These benchmarks already 
exhibited high model accuracy at the start of the study period with GPT-3.5 Turbo (the first 
model evaluated) scoring >70% and showed progressively smaller gains with newer, more 
capable models, indicating likely saturation.  
When examining model performance against human baselines (Figure 2), the results indicate 
that frontier models have reached or exceeded expert-level performance on several benchmarks. 
On VCT-Text, o3 reached 46.1% accuracy, more than doubling the expert virologist baseline of 
22.6% for text-only questions—a remarkable achievement. Similarly, top models surpassed 
expert performance on GPQA-Bio, WMDP-Bio, and CloningScenarios, with Claude 3.7 Sonnet 
(using extended reasoning) and o3 achieving 61% accuracy on CloningScenarios, slightly 
exceeding the 60% expert baseline. 
For the remaining LAB-Bench components (LitQA2 and ProtocolQA), models approached but 
have not yet exceeded expert baselines, though this comparison understates model capabilities. 
Human experts established these baselines with substantial advantages including internet 
access and specialized DNA manipulation software, while models did not have any tool access. 
In CloningScenarios, models correctly answered questions about complex multi-fragment 
designs without any bioinformatic or DNA manipulation tools likely used by the expert 
baseliners. Similarly, for LitQA2 and ProtocolQA, models answered questions without internet 
access whereas human baseliners were provided internet access and could likely locate the 
source papers and protocols from which questions were derived.

3.2 Prompt technique has limited impact on model performance 
To determine whether zero-shot evaluation might underrepresent models' capabilities, four 
models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Llama 3.1-405B) were subjected to 
additional evaluations comparing zero-shot, five-shot, and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting 
strategies (Figure 3). The results were expressed as fold differences relative to zero-shot 
performance, with values above 1.0 indicating performance gains.

instructions substantially altered performance outcomes. However, several benchmark-specific 
and model-specific exceptions were observed. 
VCT-Text showed the most consistent benefits from five-shot and CoT prompting, with both 
Llama 3.1-405B and GPT-4o demonstrating substantial improvements. Llama 3.1-405B 
achieved a 1.7-fold increase with five-shot prompting on VCT-Text, representing the largest 
prompting benefit observed across all evaluations. GPT-4o also showed significant 
improvements on VCT-Text with both five-shot and CoT strategies. These gains likely reflect the 
higher difficulty level of VCT-Text questions, which demand reasoning instead of just 
memorization, and the more challenging multiple-response format. 
Gemini 1.5 Pro exhibited a different pattern, showing moderate performance increases on 
ProtocolQA and CloningScenarios with five-shot prompting, but decreased performance with 
five-shot prompting on several other benchmarks. Claude 3.5 Sonnet showed the most

consistent performance across prompting strategies, with minimal variations from its zero-shot

Surprisingly, chain-of-thought prompting—often considered beneficial for complex reasoning 
tasks—failed to consistently improve performance across most benchmarks and models. In 
several cases, particularly for GPQA-Bio and PubMedQA, CoT prompting produced slightly 
lower accuracy compared to zero-shot evaluation. A potential explanation is that additional 
reasoning steps may introduce more opportunities for errors, particularly in cases where the 
intuitive or “memorized” answer is actually correct.  
These findings suggest that zero-shot evaluation provides a reliable baseline for assessing model 
capabilities across biology benchmarks. The resource implications of CoT prompting in 
particular are substantial: analysis of token usage showed that CoT prompting increased output 
tokens by 75× on average compared to zero-shot evaluation.

to overanalyze or overcomplicate its approach.  
4. Discussion 
I evaluated 27 frontier LLMs across eight biological benchmarks spanning molecular biology, 
genetics, cloning, virology, experimental protocols, and biosecurity. This systematic assessment 
reveals remarkable progress in AI capabilities between 2022-2025. Most striking are the 
dramatic improvements on challenging benchmarks, with VCT-Text performance increasing 
4-fold, GPQA-Bio 2-fold, ProtocolQA 2.3-fold, and CloningScenarios 2.6-fold, indicating that 
recent models are increasingly proficient at complex biological reasoning tasks. Several models 
now match or exceed documented expert-level performance on benchmarks like GPQA-Bio, 
WMDP-Bio, and VCT-Text, suggesting we've reached an inflection point where AI systems 
demonstrate specialized cognitive abilities previously exclusive to highly-trained human 
specialists in biology. While these results signal meaningful advances in biological knowledge 
representation, they must be interpreted within significant limitations of current benchmarking 
approaches.

4.1 We need better human baselines 
Human benchmarks are essential for contextualizing model performance. While raw scores 
enable model-to-model comparisons, they don't anchor to human performance which is 
generally much more intuitive to us. Comparing model performance to high-quality human 
baselines can provide more meaningful interpretation of results.  
Unfortunately, several biology benchmarks examined in our study lack robust human baselines, 
limiting our ability to interpret model achievements. MMLU reports a single performance 
baseline from “unspecialized humans” on Mechanical Turk across all categories without any 
methodological details. Their expert accuracy is merely estimated with limited documentation. 
PubMedQA relies on a single annotator. The original WMDP paper did not perform any human 
baselining. LAB-Bench, GPQA, and VCT all have more robust human baselines, with 
transparent 
protocols for expert recruitment, performance incentives, and detailed 
documentation of testing conditions.

includes documentation detailing participant recruiting procedures, relevant qualifications and

4.2 Benchmarks measure what models know, not what they can do 
Currently, most biology benchmarks assess model knowledge through multiple-choice-style 
questions, which are imperfect proxies for most user-model interactions [33] and the real-world 
biology tasks that models are expected to increasingly automate. While these benchmarks, as 
evaluated here, provide useful information – for example, that we should expect frontier models 
to answer challenging questions about molecular biology and genetics as well or better than 
experts, or that LLMs can provide expert help troubleshooting virology experiments – they are 
not good signals of the ability of LLMs to automate crucial parts of the scientific research 
process in biology. Factual knowledge is foundational, but good scientific practice involves many 
skills, including hypothesis generation, experimental design, adaptation to unexpected results, 
data analysis and tool usage, long-term planning, first-principles thinking, and research taste. 
Present benchmarks offer limited insight into these dynamic aspects of scientific capability. 
To address these limitations, the field needs stronger evaluations that assess what agentic 
models—those capable of performing actions like web searches, code execution, and tool 
usage—can accomplish in realistic scientific scenarios. Some components of LAB-Bench 
represent initial steps toward more agentic evaluation frameworks by measuring capabilities 
that include accessing scientific literature and databases [18]. Other examples of more 
agent-focused evaluations include FutureHouse's benchmark examining bioinformatic task 
performance on “analysis capsules” [34], OpenAI's SWE-Bench Verified [35] for testing 
real-world software problem-solving on GitHub issues, and METR's RE-Bench [36] for 
evaluating AI systems' capacity to automate aspects of AI research itself. These approaches more 
closely mirror the nature of real-world knowledge work. 
4.3 Benchmarks are saturating 
Beyond measuring knowledge versus capability, the analysis reveals a second critical limitation: 
benchmark saturation. The performance plateaus observed on PubMedQA (75-80%), 
MMLU-Bio (85-90%), and WMDP-Bio (80-85%) should not be interpreted as evidence of 
stalling AI progress. Rather, these plateaus likely reflect fundamental limitations of the 
benchmarks themselves, as the same models continue to demonstrate substantial improvements 
on more challenging evaluations like VCT-Text and ProtocolQA. This pattern suggests these 
saturated benchmarks no longer meaningfully differentiate between advanced models, despite 
performance levels well below 100% accuracy in some cases. 
This below-perfect saturation suggests these benchmarks contain questions with ambiguous or 
incorrect answer targets—a finding consistent with prior work by Gema et al. [37], who 
identified substantial error rates in MMLU questions, including 57% of questions containing 
13


## Page 14


errors in the Virology subset alone. As models continue to improve, new benchmarks with 
higher difficulty ceilings and stronger question validation processes will be necessary to 
effectively track progress. These could include more challenging formats like multiple-response 
questions (as in VCT [27]) or exact-match answers as used in the FrontierMath benchmark [38], 
or consist of entirely new types of benchmarks. 
4.4 Toward predictive biology benchmarks 
As models approach or exceed expert-level performance on increasingly challenging 
benchmarks, we face a fundamental constraint in evaluation design: the reliance on human 
expert knowledge as ground truth. Traditional benchmarks require domain experts to create and 
validate questions—a process that becomes increasingly more resource-intensive as we target 
higher difficulty levels. Recent highly challenging benchmarks like VCT have required 
substantial investments in expert time, often recruiting specialists at the cutting edge of their 
fields. Yet even with these investments, we're approaching a situation where when experts and 
models disagree, it’s not obvious who is correct. 
A potentially promising alternative approach is the development of "predictive biology 
benchmarks" based on experimental outcomes rather than expert-written questions. Drawing 
inspiration from the Critical Assessment of Protein Structure Prediction (CASP) competition 
[39], such benchmarks would use unpublished experimental data as prediction targets. This 
methodology offers several key advantages: it provides less ambiguous ground truth labels 
derived from experimental results rather than expert consensus; it ensures evaluation data 
remains absent from model training sets; and most importantly, it tests genuinely useful 
predictive capabilities that may be out of range for even top experts. These "super-expert" 
evaluations could include predicting outcomes of pre-registered studies or forecasting results 
from high-throughput experiments. While developing such benchmarks is sure to present new 
challenges, they represent perhaps our best opportunity to accurately measure AI capabilities as 
they potentially extend beyond human expert performance in biological prediction tasks. 
5. Conclusion 
This study systematically evaluated 27 frontier large language models (LLMs) across eight 
biology-specific benchmarks. Rigorous benchmarking efforts such as this are vital not only for 
researchers and developers aiming to objectively assess advancements and capabilities of these 
rapidly evolving models, but also for regulators, organizations, and individual end-users who 
rely on reliable and trustworthy performance data for informed decision-making. While the 
benchmarks and evaluation methods employed here effectively illustrate and compare the 
dramatic improvements in biological capabilities, especially over the recent period of rapid LLM 
advancement, they are unlikely to keep pace as models continue to improve. Future evaluation 
efforts will need to adopt more sophisticated methods to decipher the capabilities of frontier AI 
systems including those that significantly surpass expert humans on most knowledge work 
tasks. Such advances may require a fundamental shift in how we approach AI evaluation – 
focusing less on comparing performance against narrow human benchmarks and more verifying

A1.1 PubMedQA 
PubMedQA is a biomedical question-answering dataset derived from PubMed abstracts [25]. 
The task is to answer research questions with yes/no/maybe using the corresponding abstracts. 
The dataset comprises three subsets: PQA-L (1,000 expert-annotated questions, with 500 in the 
test split), PQA-U (61,249 unlabeled questions), and PQA-A (211,269 artificially generated 
questions). This analysis focuses on the 500 test split questions from the PQA-L 
expert-annotated dataset. 
PubMedQA requires reasoning over biomedical research texts, including interpreting 
quantitative results and statistics. Nearly all (96.5%) questions involve reasoning over 
quantitative content. Human baselines are provided but should be interpreted cautiously as they 
originate from a single annotator. When the annotator can see the conclusion text, accuracy 
reaches 90.4%, dropping to 78.0% when given only the abstract without the conclusion.

A.1.2 MMLU 
Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) is a standard benchmark in the machine 
learning community [26]. It evaluates model performance across 57 diverse tasks, ranging from 
elementary mathematics to college-level sciences. MMLU questions primarily assess factual 
knowledge recall and basic reasoning skills (see S1 for examples). The questions are sourced 
from various exams, including Advanced Placement (AP) exams, the Graduate Record 
Examination (GRE), the United States Medical Licensing Examination, various college exams, 
and freely available practice questions. 
For this analysis, I focus on the biology-related subsets (MMLU-Bio), consisting of Anatomy, 
College Biology, College Medicine, High School Biology, Medical Genetics, Professional 
Medicine, and Virology. In total, the biology-related subsets contain 1,300 multiple-choice 
questions.  
The only source of human baselines in the paper is that “unspecialized humans” from Amazon 
Mechanical Turk achieved an average accuracy of 34.5% across all MMLU tasks. The authors 
also estimate overall expert-level accuracy at approximately 89.8%, based on 95th percentile 
scores from relevant exams and educated guesses where such data was unavailable. As such, 
there is a significant uncertainty around human baselines for the MMLU split I evaluate.

A.1.3 GPQA 
Google-proof Q&A (GPQA) is a benchmark designed to test domain expertise in biology, physics, 
and chemistry [22]. GPQA is widely used by model developers and independent evaluators to 
compare model performance on challenging scientific questions [40–43]. The benchmark is 
designed to assess graduate-level knowledge, requiring more complex reasoning and application 
of specialized concepts. The questions are written and validated by domain experts, defined as 
individuals who have or are pursuing a PhD in the relevant field. GPQA is intentionally designed 
to be "Google-proof," meaning that questions are difficult to answer even with unrestricted 
internet access. For this analysis, I focus on the biology component (GPQA-Bio) in the 
GPQA_main set, which covers Molecular Biology and Genetics across 78 questions.

PhDs in the field) achieved 66.7% accuracy on Biology questions in the GPQA_extended set. In 
contrast, highly skilled non-expert validators (PhDs in other domains) managed only 43.2% 
accuracy, despite having unrestricted internet access and spending an average of 37 minutes per 
question (across all questions, not just biology questions).

A.1.4 WMDP 
The Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy (WMDP) benchmark is a dataset of 3,668 
multiple-choice questions developed by the Center for AI Safety (CAIS) in collaboration with a 
consortium of academics and technical consultants. The questions are designed to serve as a 
proxy measurement for hazardous knowledge in biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical 
security [19]. For this analysis, I focus on the biology component (WMDP-Bio), which consists of 
1,273 questions. 
WMDP-Bio covers several areas of potentially hazardous biological knowledge, including 
bioweapons and bioterrorism case studies, reverse genetics, enhanced potential pandemic 
pathogens, and viral vector research. The questions are crafted to measure proxies of knowledge 
that could potentially enable malicious use of biological information. 
While the original WMDP paper [19] did not perform any human baselining, researchers at 
RAND subsequently generated expert baselines for WMDP-Bio [28]. Two experts at RAND with 
a Ph.D. in microbiology and biochemistry respectively, scored 59.9% and 61.1% (mean 60.5%) 
on a randomly selected subset (~20% of wmdp-bio) while taking between 5 and 6 hours to 
complete the questions.

A.1.5 LAB-Bench 
LAB-Bench, developed by FutureHouse, is an evaluation framework designed to measure model 
performance on practical biology research tasks [18]. It covers literature search, protocol 
planning, and data analysis, with most of the components designed for agentic models capable 
of database access, programmatic DNA/protein sequence manipulation, and retrieval of recent 
scientific papers.  
The benchmark comprises eight subsets, of which this analysis includes three: LitQA2, 
CloningScenarios, and ProtocolQA. While LitQA2 and CloningScenarios would benefit from tool 
use – such as paper lookup and DNA manipulation tools, respectively – I evaluate the raw, 
unaugmented models for consistency and simplicity.

The LAB-Bench paper also reports human baseline accuracy for each subset. The study recruited 
PhD-level biology experts to complete "quizzes" consisting of single-subtask or mixed questions 
across multiple subtasks. They were allowed to use internet resources and standard research 
tools and were also provided a link to a free DNA sequence manipulation software, but were 
asked to avoid AI assistants. Compensation was structured to incentivize both performance and 
quiz completion. Human expert answers did not reach 1X coverage across all questions. Instead, 
study designers decided on a number of questions they considered representative of each subset, 
and questions for experts were then randomly sampled from within these subsets to create the 
quizzes. 
LitQA2 
LitQA2 evaluates a system's ability to retrieve and reason with scientific literature in the 
biological sciences. It consists of 199 multiple-choice questions, with answers found only once in 
recent (between 2021-2024) scientific papers, often requiring retrieval of esoteric findings 
beyond abstracts. While primarily intended for models with Retrieval-Augmented Generation 
(RAG) capabilities, this analysis examines model performance without RAG tools. While this 
doesn't capture the benchmark's full intended use, it still provides insights into model 
performance, including the interesting possibility of being able to predict the outcome of future 
scientific inquiries or detect whether models are trained on recent scientific literature. However, 
when examining benchmarks designed to assess performance on “recent” scientific data, it can 
be difficult to distinguish between improvements from more capable models versus more recent 
training data cutoffs.

A.1.6 VCT 
The Virology Capabilities Test (VCT) is a multimodal benchmark designed to measure practical 
virology knowledge, with a focus on troubleshooting laboratory experiments [27]. VCT consists 
of 322 questions that cover fundamental, tacit, and visual knowledge essential for practical work 
in virology laboratories. For this evaluation, I focused on the non-image subset (VCT-Text) 
containing 101 text-only questions. 
The benchmark covers a range of practical virology topics including virus isolation, genetic 
manipulation, tissue culture techniques, and experimental troubleshooting. VCT specifically 
targets virology methods with dual-use potential, excluding both general molecular biology 
methods and overtly hazardous material. 
VCT questions are presented in a multiple-response format, where respondents must identify all 
correct statements from a set of 4-10 options. This format significantly reduces the probability of 
guessing correctly compared to single-select multiple-choice questions. The questions in VCT 
are deliberately challenging and "Google-proof" – answers cannot be easily found through web 
searches, requiring deep domain expertise instead. 
Human performance on VCT was measured by assigning PhD-level virologists to answer subsets 
of questions specifically tailored to their areas of expertise. The majority of questions were 
reviewed by three expert virologists who had not seen the questions before, although the 
text-only category had fewer human expert answers than the full set (266 instead of 1028)

and 22.6% on the text-only questions.
