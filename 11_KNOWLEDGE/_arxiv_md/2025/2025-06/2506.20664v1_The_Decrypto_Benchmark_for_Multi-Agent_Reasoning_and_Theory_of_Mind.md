The Decrypto Benchmark for Multi-Agent
Reasoning and Theory of Mind
Andrei Lupu1,2, Timon Willi1,2, Jakob Foerster1,2
1FAIR at Meta, 2University of Oxford
As Large Language Models (LLMs) gain agentic abilities, they will have to navigate complex multi-
agent scenarios, interacting with human users and other agents in cooperative and competitive settings.
This will require new reasoning skills, chief amongst them being theory of mind (ToM), or the ability
to reason about the “mental” states of other agents. However, ToM and other multi-agent abilities
in LLMs are poorly understood, since existing benchmarks suffer from narrow scope, data leakage,
saturation, and lack of interactivity. We thus propose Decrypto, a game-based benchmark for
multi-agent reasoning and ToM drawing inspiration from cognitive science, computational pragmatics
and multi-agent reinforcement learning. It is designed to be as easy as possible in all other dimensions,
eliminating confounding factors commonly found in other benchmarks. To our knowledge, it is also
the first platform for designing interactive ToM experiments.
We validate the benchmark design through comprehensive empirical evaluations of frontier LLMs,
robustness studies, and human-AI cross-play experiments. We find that LLM game-playing abilities
lag behind humans and simple word-embedding baselines. We then create variants of two classic
cognitive science experiments within Decrypto to evaluate three key ToM abilities. Surprisingly,
we find that state-of-the-art reasoning models are significantly worse at those tasks than their older
counterparts. This demonstrates that Decrypto addresses a crucial gap in current reasoning and
ToM evaluations, and paves the path towards better artificial agents.

Introduction
Reasoning abilities and agentic behaviour are currently at the forefront of large language model (LLM)
research (Huang et al., 2023; Jaech et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025), with early applications of LLM agents
including software engineering (Jimenez et al., 2023) and web navigation (Yao et al., 2023). While those tasks
are predominantly single-agent, real-world agentic applications will increasingly involve complex interactions
with many other actors, including business partners, teammates, users and other agents. Thus, the future of
AI problems is irreducibly multi-step, multi-agent, partially observable, and stochastic – a reality not captured
by the existing suite of benchmarks.
To navigate these multi-agent scenarios, LLMs will require theory of mind (ToM), the general ability of
modelling the mental states of other agents (artificial or biological), including their knowledge, beliefs,
intentions and abilities. However, LLM ToM remains poorly understood. Despite ToM being inherently
tied to multi-agent interactions, the majority of existing benchmarks lack interactivity and are restricted to
variants of the Sally-Anne task (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985). Furthermore, Hu et al. argue that many of those
benchmarks suffer from confounding factors and bias introduced when translating embodied scenarios to text.
To address the gap in ToM evaluations, we introduce Decrypto, a multi-agent reasoning and ToM benchmark
based on the award-winning language game of the same name. Decrypto is a pragmatic inference game (Degen,
2023), where two agents must exchange secret messages while preventing a third party from intercepting them.
We designed Decrypto to be future-proof and as easy as possible for LLMs in all other dimensions except

Decrypto: The Game
Decrypto is a word-based code-guessing board game published by Scorpion Masqué1, of which we implement
a variant requiring three players divided into two teams. Borrowing the terminology from cryptography,
players are assigned three distinct roles: Alice (the Encoder), Bob (the Decoder), and Eve (the Interceptor or
Eavesdropper). A full game (i.e., an episode) lasts at most 8 turns. We show one such turn in Figure 1.
Alice and Bob are on the same team, and share 4 ordered secret keywords, sampled without replacement
before the start of the episode and kept fixed throughout. In Figure 1, those are [star, jazz, thunder, plane].
Each turn, Alice samples a secret code of 3 non-repeating digits between 1 and 4, such as [2-3-4], with any of
the 24 possible codes occurring at most once per episode. Alice must then provide 3 public hints (e.g. [bass,
Zeus, takeoff], such that Bob can guess the code but Eve cannot.
Next, Bob and Eve each make an independent attempt to guess the code. If Bob guesses incorrectly, his team
gets a Miscommunication token. If Eve guesses correctly, she gets an Intercept token.
Finally, Bob and Eve’s guesses, the real code, and any event (miscommunication or intercept) that occurred
during the turn are publicly revealed. Thus, the history of all codes from previous turns and the history of
past hints associated with each of the four keywords are common knowledge, but the keywords themselves are

not. The code history and especially the hint history are the two main pieces of information that will help
Eve make educated guesses in future turns. For instance, if the hint history for digit 3 so far is [lightning,
Zeus], Eve may associate terms related to storms or electricity with that digit, even without knowing the
exact keyword.
The game ends and Eve wins immediately if Alice and Bob accumulate two Miscommunication tokens or if
Eve gains two Intercept tokens. Alice and Bob win if they make it through 8 rounds without either of those
two conditions happening. Alice must therefore be careful to provide subtle hints to avoid interception, yet
make them clear enough for Bob to guess correctly.
The keywords are sampled from a predetermined set, but the choice of hints is open-ended and can be
composed of multiple words. The restriction is that hints must be real words (including proper nouns) and
refer to the meaning of the keywords, not to their spelling or pronunciation. They must also rely on publicly
available information, which includes culture, technical knowledge or recent events.
Beyond word associations
At the surface level, Decrypto provides a language reasoning challenge that consists in matching hints to
either keywords or the hint history. However, Alice’s hints cannot be too literal, or they will get intercepted,
and so Decrypto can be formalised as a pragmatic inference game under the Rational Speech Act (RSA)
framework (Goodman and Frank, 2016; Degen, 2023), where the listeners (Bob and Eve) update their
belief of the intended meaning of the speaker (Alice) via Bayesian inference. We provide such a treatment
in Appendix H, explicitly showing that agents must model each other’s beliefs for optimal play. This includes
the result that Bob must perform second-order ToM, modelling Alice’s beliefs over Eve’s beliefs, to maximize
the chance of guessing correctly.
To help readers intuit the role of ToM in Decrypto, we also invite them to attempt guessing the code on the
right side of Figure 1 as Bob, and to read the rationale in Appendix C. Alternatively, readers can download
our code and play a game of Decrypto with an LLM in only a few minutes.
3
Decrypto: The Benchmark
An easy benchmark. Unlike other reasoning benchmarks, Decrypto is purposely designed to not require
many of the capabilities that large pre-trained models struggle with. In particular, it demands no symbolic
reasoning (Bard et al., 2020), mathematical reasoning (Cobbe et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024a), spatial
reasoning (Clark et al., 2018; Carroll et al., 2019), tool use (Xu et al., 2023a), or particular attention to
tokenisation, which has been shown to affect arithmetic performance (Singh and Strouse, 2024). It also
distinguishes itself from recent ToM benchmarks (Le et al., 2019; He et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Zhou et al.,
2023; Xu et al., 2024; Gandhi et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024) by not relying on state tracking (e.g. of object
locations) or on textual representations of embodied settings, which Hu et al. argue introduce unwanted
pragmatic artifacts and bias LLM answers.
Instead, strong performance in Decrypto relies on language-based reasoning and theory of mind. Since
LLMs are trained to learn word co-occurrences and follow instructions, we expect Decrypto to be easy to
play by LLMs, but hard to master. Our results and Appendix L show this to be the case: even Llama 3.1-8B
understands the rules, but much stronger models still struggle to play well.
A future-proof benchmark. With its two-team, three-player dynamics, Decrypto is a benchmark for both
competitive and cooperative scenarios.
The difficulty scales with the choice of agents for each of the three roles, making the benchmark much harder
to saturate than those that rely on a fixed dataset of problems. We curate a set of 680 possible keywords,
resulting in over 8.8 billion possible keyword combinations to limit memorisation, and also implement the
option to provide custom keywords beyond this standard set. The number of possible games is virtually
infinite since the choice of hints is open-ended, and can rely on any publicly available information, including
history, culture or science. Finally, the game was originally designed for human play, facilitating studies on
human-AI coordination and comparisons.

3.1
Multi-Agent Reasoning
Competition. In this setting, we compare two models A and B by having both Alice and Bob be played by
A, and Eve by B. At its simplest, we can record the win rate of A vs. B, swapping teams to ensure a fair
evaluation. However, we find win-rate to be uninformative given the current abilities of LLMs, and propose
different metrics below.
Ad-hoc Coordination. In this setting, we freeze Eve (e.g., to a rule-based baseline or the strongest available
LLM) and instantiate Alice and Bob to be played by different models (i.e. Alice is A, and Bob is B). Like
in Stone et al. (2010), we are concerned with the ability to “efficiently and robustly collaborate with previously
unknown teammates”, such as independently trained LLMs.
A crucial subset of ad-hoc coordination is human-AI coordination, where one of the two agents (Alice or Bob)
is played by a human. This setting paves the way towards more social AI agents that seamlessly coordinate
with humans and understand their intents.
Metrics. Both settings are subject to the same tension that is at the core of Decrypto: Alice must provide
hints that balance what she knows about Bob, Eve, and the information available to each of them. If the
hints are too obscure, Bob will guess wrong, which leads to a miscommunication; too obvious, and Eve will
intercept; just right, and Alice and Bob survive for another round. The number of miscommunications and of
intercepts are therefore two sides of the same coin, providing granular insights into the failure modes of LLMs.
Meanwhile, the average number of turns per episode captures both sides in one metric, since longer games
mean that Alice and Bob could better balance the difficulty of hints to avoid defeat. Game length is also
more informative than win-rate, since we empirically find current LLMs to be much weaker at providing hints
than intercepting, which results in Eve having significantly higher win-rates in most match-ups (see Figure 7).
3.2
Theory of Mind
As explained in Section 2, high performance in Decrypto requires players to explicitly reason about other
players’ beliefs and world knowledge. For example, sufficiently powerful LLMs playing the game could leverage
their encyclopedic knowledge to gain an advantage over human players. Newer models could also win against
older ones by referring to information made public after the knowledge cut-off of the opponent. For instance,
a politician’s name could be used to refer to the keyword “plane” based on recent news stories.
Beyond game playing, Decrypto provides a platform for conducting interactive ToM experiments inspired
by cognitive psychology. We conduct two such experiments, evaluating three different ToM abilities in LLMs.
Each required only new prompts and approximately 15 additional lines of code.
The first experiment adapts the Smarties Task of Gopnik and Astington, which presents children with a
deceptive object (a box of Smarties containing pencils) and studies whether the child can correctly identify
incorrect beliefs – either their own or of another child – when first encountering the deceptive object. To
recreate this task in Decrypto, we substitute the closed Smarties box and the pencils with the game history
and the secret keywords. At each turn except the first, we prompt Eve three times independently. Prompt A
asks her to predict the four keywords. Prompt B reveals the keywords and asks Eve what she thought were
the keywords pre-reveal. Prompt C also reveals the keywords but asks the model to predict what a “second
interceptor” would think the keywords to be, pre-reveal.
We generate outputs with temperature 0 (for models that allow it) and only consider turns where the answer
to A is an incorrect guess, keeping only cases where Eve has inaccurate “beliefs” pre-reveal. We compare
answers A and B to measure representational change (RC), the ability of the agent to recognise when its belief
about the world (but not the world itself) changes due to additional information. Similarly, comparing A and
C measures false belief (FB), the ability to represent other agents as having false beliefs about the world.
We distinguish two variants of the tasks. The Weak variant only requires the agents to realise that either
themselves or the second interceptor could not have known the ground truth, and so an answer to either B or
C is correct as long as it differs from the real keywords. For the Strong variant of those tasks, we consider the
agent to pass only if it correctly predicts its answer to prompt A (i.e. if B = A or C = A). Success here likely
requires a self-consistent representation of the keywords, or at least strong counterfactual reasoning. Figure 5
summarises the procedure.

The second experiment measures perspective taking (PT) and is a word-based code-guessing variant of the
Three Mountain Problem (Piaget et al., 1956). After Alice has given her hints, we prompt her to predict
Eve’s guess. We record the prediction accuracy and the proportion of turns on which Alice predicts that
Eve will guess the correct code. This last metric is particularly informative: Alice predicting that Eve will
intercept more rarely than she does is a sign that Alice fails to consider all information available to Eve. If,
instead, Alice predicts that Eve will intercept on nearly every turn, it shows a failure to reason from Eve’s
perspective, and either explicitly or implicitly assumes that Eve has access to the keywords. It also shows a
failure to integrate ToM reasoning in decision-making: if Alice thinks Eve will guess the code given her hints,
she should have chosen different hints.
4
State of the Art
Given the plurality of settings in Decrypto, we caution users to be explicit and transparent about their
assumptions when declaring state-of-the-art.
4.1
Specialist vs Generalist Agents
When reporting results on Decrypto, we recommend distinguishing between two classes of agents: generalists
and specialists.
Generalist agents refer to general-purpose models used to play the game “out-of-the-box”, without any
additional fine-tuning or in-context learning outside of that happening within individual games. In particular,
for generalist agents, we consider the prompt to be the observation returned by the environment, which
precludes any form of prompt engineering. Evaluating foundation models in this framework assesses their
ability to zero-shot generalise to novel tasks, including out-of-distribution ones and tasks where training data
is limited or unavailable.
Because foundation models are often sensitive to prompting, we recommend evaluating generalist agents with
a range of different prompts to prevent the choice of prompt favouring any one model. We provide such an
evaluation using 625 prompt variants in Appendix G, showing that game performance is robust to prompt
variations and depends predominantly on general model capabilities.
In contrast, specialist agents are all the agents designed specifically for the task. Solutions in this space include
rule-based strategies, training on the game using RL, building a prompt pipeline informed by the rules of the
games, and prompt engineering. Here, we consider the prompt to be part of the agent, if it is needed at all.
Prompt engineering is the designer’s burden, and the environment only returns key information.
Specialist agents can, in theory, overfit to Decrypto, which makes some degree of performance easier to reach,
but does not automatically solve the game under every setting. For instance, we know from the cooperative
multi-agent RL literature that training models to collaborate in cross-play (XP) with unseen agents can be
extremely challenging, even with unlimited access to the environment (Hu et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2023). We
expect this to also hold for Decrypto. This is evidenced by the rule-based baselines we describe next, which
coordinate flawlessly in Decrypto if Alice and Bob pre-agree on a common strategy and word embeddings,
but otherwise can fail catastrophically when paired with other agents, including humans and LLMs.
Specialist Agents - Baselines. We introduce two specialist agent baselines to play Decrypto. For the baseline
models, we use the common crawl GloVe with a vocabulary of 2.2 million words (Pennington et al., 2014) and
the Word2Vec embeddings trained on the Google News dataset, provided by the gensim library (Rehurek and
Sojka, 2011; Mikolov, 2013).
For both embedding models, the baseline algorithm is the same and relies on a fixed corpus of 5696 nouns.
When hinting a digit, Alice draws from the set of the top-K most similar words to the corresponding keyword
under cosine similarity, filtering out hints that are more similar to another keyword than to the one intended.
In turn, Bob greedily assigns each hint to the most similar keyword, measured using his own embedding model
and cosine similarity. Not having access to the keywords, Eve computes the mean embedding of the hint

The purpose of these baselines is threefold. First, we can study the case where Alice and Bob share perfect ToM,
represented by a shared strategy and word embedding. This establishes an upper bound on performance to
which to compare other agents. Second, having baselines using two different word embeddings, we demonstrate
in Section 5 how not accounting for differences in how agents associate words can lead to a catastrophic lack
of coordination and poor XP performance. Third, K determines the amount of diversity in Alice’s hints.
By tweaking it, we can operate in a regime where GloVe and Word2Vec correlate and represent reasonable
rule-based baselines against which we can compare LLMs. We thus use K = 16 for the rest of the paper.
Being explicit about whether Decrypto is approached from a generalist or specialist perspective provides
clarity and avoids unfair comparisons between models making inherently different assumptions. It also leads
to two equally rich but distinct research directions. In the first case, Decrypto acts as a benchmark for
multi-agent reasoning and measures progress in foundation models. In the second case, it is an RL environment
used to research and evaluate training algorithms.
4.2
Human-Data Collection
We collect 10 full games of human teams playing against a fixed LLM Eve, namely Llama 3.1-70B-Instruct.
The players interacted with the game through a unified command-line interface and saw the same system
prompts, user prompts and information as LLMs did. See Appendices I and J for prompts and Appendix K
for data collection details. The data itself is hosted in the code repository.
Normally, a game ends when 2 interceptions or miscommunication tokens are collected. To maximise our data
collection, we did not terminate the game after 2 interceptions but let the humans play the game for the full 8
turns. The participants were encouraged to keep providing good hints and analyse the hints appropriately,
even after the game technically terminated. Such a game does not count as won, but the extra turns can be
useful when replaying the game to evaluate other LLMs as Eve.

Related Work
BattleAgentBench (Wang et al., 2024), multi-robot studies (Li et al., 2023) and other works such as Liu
et al. (2023) and Guo et al. (2024) have recently studied LLMs in multi-agent contexts. While detailed,
they focus on spatial reasoning rather than on the language-based reasoning and ToM abilities central to
Decrypto. Among multi-agent environments, social deduction games such as Werewolf (Xu et al., 2023b,c) and
Avalon (Light et al., 2023) are particularly amenable to studying LLMs, as are negotiation games (Abdelnabi
et al., 2023). However, they provide less structured and atomic insight than Decrypto, given the free-form
dialogue and frequent deception in those settings. AgentPro (Zhang et al., 2024b) focuses on competitive
scenarios in games like Blackjack and Poker, but lacks the cooperative aspect of Decrypto.
ToM has been a subject of focus in AI as early as 2018, when Rabinowitz et al. trained ToM-net, a simple
network capable of predicting the behaviour of other artificial agents and acting based on false beliefs. In
contrast, recent works on ToM have focused on LLMs modelling human beliefs (Chen et al., 2024; Sclar et al.,
2024; Kim et al., 2023). Decrypto connects both by enabling the study of LLM ToM applied to both humans
and other artificial agents. Unlike other recent ToM benchmarks (Le et al., 2019; He et al., 2023; Kim et al.,
2023; Zhou et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Gandhi et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2024) and Rabinowitz et al. (2018),
however, Decrypto does so in an interactive setting, where agents can not only model the beliefs of other
agents, but also influence them.
Kosinski (2023) also studied the Smarties Task, from which they concluded that “ToM-like ability [...] may
have spontaneously emerged” in LLMs. Those claims were directly challenged by Ullman in subsequent
work, with which our results agree. Shaikh et al. (2023) use another language game, Codenames, to study
cross-cultural pragmatics. Their work stops short of evaluating LLMs, but raises important questions about
the role of cultural backgrounds in pragmatic inference. Decrypto is a natural choice for pursuing research
in this direction, since we can specify different personas in the LLM prompts.

Conclusion
Our paper introduces Decrypto, an interactive multi-agent benchmark designed to evaluate coordination,
competition and theory of mind capabilities in language models. Decrypto addresses critical limitations
of existing ToM benchmarks, such as biases arising from textual translation of embodied scenarios or lack
of interactivity. Designed to be future-proof and to eliminate confounding factors known to limit LLM
performance, Decrypto fills an important gap in existing benchmarks. Furthermore, our codebase provides
a versatile platform for quickly designing interactive ToM experiments inspired by cognitive psychology.
We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate open-source and closed-source LLMs. We find that even state-
of-the-art models struggle with the nuanced communication and strategic reasoning that Decrypto requires,
often underperforming simple baselines in cooperative and competitive settings. Similarly, our human-AI
experiments shed light on the limited ability of recent LLMs to coordinate with humans or understand their
communications.
Finally, we create variants of two seminal experiments from cognitive psychology to study LLM ToM in
interactive settings, analogously to how it is studied in children. Our experiments provide strong evidence
that state-of-the-art still lack many ToM skills. Moreover, we find newer and more capable reasoning models
such as Claude 3.7 Sonnet and o1 high to be significantly worse at some ToM tasks than older models,
demonstrating the need for more ToM methods and benchmarks.
Decrypto opens up several promising research directions. On the ToM side, these include enhancing the ToM
capabilities of language models and investigating differences between how LLMs model humans versus how
they model other agents. From the multi-agent perspective, an exciting avenue for future work is adapting
algorithms from multi-agent RL (Silver et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2020) to fine-tune LLMs. For a longer discussion
on the limitations of our work and on the multiple research directions enabled by Decrypto, we refer the
reader to Appendix A.

Limitations and Future Work
We firmly believe Decrypto to be a valuable and future-proof benchmark for evaluating multi-agent reasoning
and theory of mind. However, our work still has a number of limitations, which we hope to address in future
work.
First, while we largely eliminated the confounding factors that affect LLM performance, the game dynamics
and the ToM tasks may still be too complicated for weaker models. For instance, when trying Llama
3.1-8B-Instruct on the perspective taking task, we found that it failed to understand the prompt altogether.
Regarding evaluations, the main limitation of our work is the computational cost of computing Figure 3. An
individual run is relatively cheap (approximately $0.10 per role per episode for Claude 3.7 Sonnet). However,
given the 3-player nature of the game, the total number of combinations for n models is n3, or 2n2 if only
computing the matrix slices in Figure 3. For a large number of models, this can induce significantly larger
costs than standard single-agent benchmarks. To mitigate this, we provide the tools to run evaluations
locally and efficiently, leveraging prefix caching where possible to reduce unnecessary computations. We also
acknowledge the limited scope of our human study in Table 1. While the 10 games collected were sufficient to
demonstrate the limitations of LLMs in human-AI interactions, we believe that there is much to be learned
from a more extensive study, including with humans as interceptors. We therefore made sure to provide the
tools to enable such research, such as a lightweight command-line interface for conducting human studies.
Finally, theory of mind is an umbrella term for multiple abilities. Our work evaluates some of them, such as
representational change, false belief and perspective taking, and we hope extensions to Decrypto to expand
on this set in the future. Nevertheless, there are ToM abilities which Decrypto is ill-fitted to evaluate. For
instance, models performing well in the game and the ToM tasks we designed may still fail to reason about
the intentions or emotions of other agents.
Decrypto also enables a number of other research directions connected to ToM, multi-agent reasoning and
reinforcement learning, and pragmatic inference. From a ToM perspective, Decrypto is easy to extend with
additional ToM experiments, which can complement the results presented in our work. Given the limitations
of current frontier models, we also believe there is a dire need for methods to improve the ToM abilities of
LLMs. Decrypto features short episodes and dense rewards, making it a suitable environment to study
the application of multi-agent RL algorithms towards LLM post-training. In particular, Decrypto features
stochasticity and partial observability, two key ingredients for “meaningful” coordination challenges (Ellis
et al., 2023). It would therefore be interesting to study whether fine-tuning LLMs on Decrypto using self-
play (Silver et al., 2017) or other MARL algorithms (Hu et al., 2020) produces the same kind of idiosyncrasies
observed when training tabula rasa models in other cooperative environments (Cui et al., 2023; Gessler et al.,
2025). Finally, we believe the study of pragmatic inference in LLMs to be a promising avenue for future
research, which is made much easier by the release of our benchmark. We formalise Decrypto as a pragmatic
inference game and provide suggestions for this line of work in Appendix H.
B
Impact Statement
As LLMs become more prevalent in our daily lives and start interacting with each other and with human
users, we believe it becomes crucial to understand the multi-agent and ToM abilities of foundation models.
Our work provides a novel assessment of those abilities, and the tools to conduct future studies on inter-AI
and human-AI interactions. As such, we hope to bring attention to what we consider to be an understudied
subset of agentic capabilities, and that our work will lead to models with a strong social and human-centric
behaviour.
However, ToM encompasses a broad set of abilities, of which Decrypto evaluates only a subset. A possible
misuse of Decrypto is to equate strong performance on our benchmark to more general ToM abilities,
including intent inference and emotion understanding, which are not evaluated by Decrypto. Such claims
have incorrectly been made about other ToM benchmarks (Hu et al., 2025), and so we ask future researchers
to be careful when concluding that an AI system has ToM.

The baselines work as follows. Alice can choose hints from a fixed corpus of the 5696 most common nouns
extracted from the Brown, Gutenberg, and Webtext corpora (Bird et al., 2009), filter to ensure all extracted
nouns are present in both the GloVe and Word2Vec embeddings. (We use this hint corpus for the baselines
only; LLM agents generate hints in an open-ended fashion.)
Before Alice chooses hints, Alice and Bob agree on a strategy. For each code digit ci ∈[1, 4], i ∈[1, 3], Alice
picks a hint hi and guarantees that the hint picked for the digit is more similar to the ci-th keyword kci than
to any other keyword, i.e., s(hi, kci) > s(hi, kj)
∀j ∈−c, where −c = [1, 4] \ ci and s is the cosine similarity.
Alice increases the diversity of her hints by randomly sampling each hint from the top-K most similar words
for each keyword under cosine similarity, filtering those that do not satisfy the constraint above. We also
enforce that Alice cannot reuse any previously used hints in the same episode.
Bob then analyses the hints and assigns each hint to its most similar keyword under cosine similarity,
i.e., arg maxc∈[1,4] s(hi, kc) ∀i ∈[1, 3]. Bob guesses the code perfectly if Alice and Bob use the same word
embeddings because the hints are guaranteed to be the most similar to the correct keyword under the same
embedding model.
Eve is only given the hints h and the hint history for each keyword, which at turn 0 is empty. Eve calculates the
cosine similarity between the average embedding of each keyword’s hint history with the hints. This results in
a similarity matrix of size N ×M, where N(= 3) is the number of hints and M(= 4) is the number of keywords.
Eve combinatorially calculates the globally optimal guess based on the similarity values. Since N and M are
typically small values, we are unconcerned about the computational complexity. However, Eve’s selection task
is equivalent to a linear assignment problem and efficient algorithms, such as the Jonker-Volgenant algorithm,
are available in open-source libraries such as SciPy (Virtanen et al., 2020).

Experimental Details
Here we provide details for the experiments reported in Section 5.
F.1
Prompting
All our experiments are text-only and conducted exclusively through prompts, which we provide in Appendices I
and J. The system prompt is composed of an explanation of the game rules, common to all roles, and a set
of role-specific instructions. The user prompts are role-specific but follow a similar structure, providing, in
order, a summary of the previous turn and of the game so far, role specific information (e.g. the code or the
hints), a summary of the role task, and formatting instructions. For the ToM tasks, the prompts are structure
similarly, and rely on the same output formatting. For agents that do not admit a system prompt, such as
Deepseek-R1, we include the system prompt as a prefix to the first user message.
We maintain prompts and model outputs in context, making Decrypto a multi-turn environment. We use
a regular expression to extract the hints or the guess from the model outputs. If the code fails to detect a
16


## Page 17


correctly-formatted answer in the output, we re-prompt the model again up to 10 times, reminding it of the
correct output format. If the model has still not provided an answer after 10 attempts, we default to a dummy
answer. In practice, this almost never happens unless the output token limit is set very low for very verbose
models, such as Deepseek-R1. By default, retries are not added to the model’s context to avoid cluttering it
and unnecessarily lowering its performance. ToM prompts and outputs are also excluded from the context for
future turns.
F.2
Generation parameters
We set the model temperature to 0.6 for experiments in Figure 3 and table 1, and to 0 for ToM experiments
in Figure 4 to reduce the impact of model stochasticity. We note that some close source models are not
entirely deterministic, even with the temperature set to 0.
Our goal was not to evaluate model verbosity, but rather the limit of their capabilities. Therefore, we set
generous token limits (between 750 for non-reasoning models and up to 10000 for reasoning ones) to prevent
cutting model generations prematurely. However, we found that models rarely got close to their token limit.
For instance, Claude 3.7 Sonnet with Extended Thinking output approximately 500 total tokens per turn on
average, and sometimes as low as 350 despite a limit of 1500 tokens.
The full set of generation parameters can be found in the configs we provide as part of our code release, and
which enable reproduction of the results in our paper with only a few commands.
F.3
Computational Costs
We ran our experiments on a mix of local and API-hosted models.
All open-source models were hosted locally on a high performance computing cluster using vLLM (Kwon
et al., 2023). Each model instance ran on 8x Nvidia A100 GPUs. Although not necessary, we also launched
multiple instances of the same model running, using concurrency to accelerate inference.
System prompts are on the order of 1000 tokens, and the user prompts at each turn are less than 300 tokens
each. Models will typically output around 500 tokens per prompt, but the number can go much higher for
particularly verbose models. We estimate that evaluating Claude 3.7 Sonnet with Extended Thinking cost
approximately $0.10 per role per episode. A full run of 32 episodes, 3 random seeds, and with all three roles
played by Claude 3.7 Sonnet costs approximately $30.

Decrypto as a pragmatic inference game
With mild assumptions, we can formalize Decrypto as an iterated pragmatic inference game in the Rational
Speech Act (RSA) framework introduced by Frank and Goodman and extended in Goodman and Frank
(2016). Doing so provides a Bayesian view of how successful communication in the game requires agents to
model each other’s decision process.
We consider each turn in isolation, so that each episode of Decrypto is composed of up to 8 independent
pragmatic inference games. Agents must associate utterances u (i.e. a set of 3 hints) to an intended meaning
m (the code for that turn or, equivalently, the corresponding combination of 3 keywords).

their semantics:

H.2
Further refinements
Our formalism assumes [[u]] to be shared by Alice and Bob, in line with classical RSA. This may not always be
the case. Revisiting the example from equation 2, m1 may not be in [[u′]] for a player who has never heard of
nuclear fusion, or who is unaware of its role in stellar physics. Differences in lexicons arise naturally if players
have different world knowledge and in the authors’ experience, are often the cause of miscommunications
when playing Decrypto. Such differences can also be exploited, and we provide in Section 3 the example of
a model referring to events that occurred after their opponent’s knowledge cut-off.
Evaluating LLM abilities to play Decrypto and communicate under lexical differences is a promising avenue
for future work. We suggest that readers interested in this direction start with the study of RSA under lexical
uncertainty in Bergen et al. (2016).

K.1
Human Data Collection Details
Participants were made aware that an AI agent played Eve. Still, no additional instructions were provided
to the players, except minor assistance if/when participants asked how to format their input. For technical
reasons, players interacted on the same computer in a typical “hot seat” setup. One of the authors was
always present to ensure participants wouldn’t cheat either by peeking at the screen outside their turn or
through verbal communication. We do not claim to have collected a representative dataset of human ability
at Decrypto. The data collected serves to demonstrate that LLMs perform worse than a non-expert group of
human players and provides a starting point for future studies on human-AI coordination and ToM.
Data collection was pre-approved following the standard procedure at the authors’ institution.
