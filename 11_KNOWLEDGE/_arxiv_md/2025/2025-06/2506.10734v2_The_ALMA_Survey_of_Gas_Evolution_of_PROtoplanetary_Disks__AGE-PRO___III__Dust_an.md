ABSTRACT
We present Band 6 and Band 7 observations of 10 Lupus disks around M3-K6 stars from the ALMA
survey of Gas Evolution in PROtoplanetary disks (AGE-PRO) Large Program. In addition to contin-
uum emission in both bands, our Band 6 setup covers the 12CO, 13CO and C18O J=2-1 lines, while
our Band 7 setup covers the N2H+ J=3-2 line. All of our sources are detected in 12CO and 13CO, 7
out of 10 are detected in C18O, and 3 are detected in N2H+. We find strong correlations between the
CO isotopologue line fluxes and the continuum flux densities. With the exception of one disk, we also
identify a strong correlation between the C18O J=2-1 and N2H+ J=3-2 fluxes, indicating similar CO
abundances across this sample. For the two sources with well-resolved continuum and 12CO J=2-1
images, we find that their gas-to-dust size ratio is consistent with the median value of ∼2 inferred
from a larger sample of Lupus disks. We derive dust disk masses from continuum flux densities. We
estimate gas disk masses by comparing C18O J=2-1 line fluxes with those predicted by the limited grid
of self-consistent disk models of Ruaud et al. (2022). A comparison of these mass estimates with those
derived by Trapman et al. (2025a), using a combination of CO isotopologue and N2H+ line emission,
shows that the masses are consistent with each other. Some discrepancies appear for small and faint
disks, but they are still within the uncertainties. Both methods find gas disk masses increase with dust
disk masses, and gas-to-dust mass ratios are between 10 −100 in the AGE-PRO Lupus sample.

1. INTRODUCTION
Disks around young stars are the sites of planet forma-
tion, hence the name protoplanetary disks. Among their
different properties, disk masses and sizes are among
the most critical ones to test disk evolution and planet
formation models (see Drka˙zkowska et al. 2023; Man-
ara et al. 2023, for recent reviews). For example, the
initial dust and gas disk masses determine what type
of planetary systems could form (e.g., Mordasini et al.
2012).
Disk sizes as a function of time can be used
to distinguish disk evolutionary models.
In the clas-
sic disk viscous accretion models (e.g., Pringle 1981),
the angular momentum is redistributed outward which
leads to larger gas disk sizes over time. In the case of
the magneto-hydrodynamic (MHD) wind-driven accre-
tion scenario (e.g., Pascucci et al. 2023, for a recent re-
view), the angular momentum is extracted from the disk
through the wind (e.g., Suzuki et al. 2016; Tabone et al.
2022) so that gas disk sizes are not expected to increase
with time. Dust disk sizes cannot directly probe gas disk
evolution, but, when coupled with gas disk sizes, they
can indicate the extent of radial drift of millimeter-size
dust particles, often called pebbles (e.g., Trapman et al.
2019, 2020; Toci et al. 2021).
In the past decade, the characterization of disk masses
and sizes for both the gas and dust components has
been significantly improved by the Atacama Large Mil-
limeter/Submillimeter Array (ALMA; see Miotello et al.
2023 for a recent review). Initial surveys of nearby star-
forming regions detected emission from pebble-sized
grains in most disks around a large range of stellar
masses (from ∼0.1 to 1 M⊙; e.g., Ansdell et al. 2016;
Barenfeld et al. 2016; Pascucci et al. 2016). These sur-
veys revealed that the millimeter flux, a proxy for the
dust disk mass, scales with the mass of the central star
with a relation that steepens with time. They also en-
abled measuring dust disk sizes and showed that they are
smaller in older regions (Hendler et al. 2020). Several
ALMA surveys have also probed the gaseous component,
particularly in carbon monoxide (CO) and its isotopo-
logues. Spatially-resolved 12CO observations were used
to measure gas disk sizes and the optically thin line emis-
sion from rarer CO isotopologues (e.g., 13CO, C18O)
was used to derive gas disk masses when combined with
thermo-chemical disk models (e.g., Miotello et al. 2017;
Woitke et al. 2019; Ruaud et al. 2022; Deng et al. 2023).
However, the detected CO and its rarer isotopologues
have been found to be weaker than what were expected
from early models (e.g., Ansdell et al. 2016, 2018; Baren-
feld et al. 2016; Long et al. 2017). As such, gas disk
mass estimates are only available for the bright disks in
each star-forming region where their 13CO and/or C18O
lines were detected, corresponding to ∼50% of the Lu-
pus disks in the literature (e.g., Miotello et al. 2017;
Zhang et al. 2021; Schwarz et al. 2021; Deng et al. 2023;
Pascucci et al. 2023).
The ALMA survey of Gas Evolution in PROtoplane-
tary disks (AGE-PRO; Project ID: 2021.1.00128.L, PI:
K. Zhang) is an ALMA Cycle 8 Large Program. It builds
on the initial lower-sensitivity surveys but goes much
deeper to detect gas emission from the rarer CO iso-
topologues and N2H+. The survey is designed to study
disks at different ages in a consistent observational set
up, aiming to systematically trace the evolution of gas
masses and sizes throughout the lifetime of protoplan-
etary disks (Zhang et al. 2025). The disks in the Lu-
pus star-forming region have been selected to represent
disks at the age of ∼1 −3 Myrs because of the richness
of literature data and well-characterized stellar and disk
parameters.
The Lupus molecular cloud complex is one of the clos-
est low-mass star-forming regions, and it is part of the
Scorpius-Centaurus OB association (Comer´on 2008). It
consists of four main clouds with active star formation
(Lupus I-IV) located at slightly different distances of
∼150 −160 pc. The mean distance to the Lupus re-
gion is ∼158 pc (Luhman & Esplin 2020; Galli et al.
2020; Gaia Collaboration 2022). The average age of the
region is ∼1 −3 Myrs: the youngest region is Lupus I
(median age ∼1.2 Myrs), the middle age is Lupus III-
IV (∼2.4 −2.5 Myrs), and older targets are off cloud
(∼3.2 Myrs, Galli et al. 2020).
There are many initial and near-complete surveys
characterizing the protoplanetary disks and their stars
in the Lupus star-forming region. Observations in the
near- and mid- infrared with Spitzer (Mer´ın et al. 2008;
Evans et al. 2009; Dunham et al. 2015) and far-infrared
with Herschel (Bustamante et al. 2015) found a disk
fraction of ∼70 −80%.
Later, Ansdell et al. (2016)
carried out the first ALMA survey of disks in Lupus,
followed by other extended surveys (e.g., Ansdell et al.
2018; Miotello et al. 2021) as well as several deep ob-
servations focusing on specific targets and lines (e.g.,
Cleeves et al. 2016; Tsukagoshi et al. 2019). Moreover,
Alcal´a et al. (2014, 2017, 2019) conducted a spectro-
scopic survey using VLT/X-Shooter, which derived stel-

2. TARGETS AND OBSERVATIONS
2.1. Targets in Lupus Star-forming Region
The AGE-PRO disks in the Lupus star-forming region
were selected based on the following criteria:
• Stars were chosen to have spectral types (SpT)
between M3-K6, roughly corresponding to stellar
masses (M⋆) ∼0.3 −0.8 M⊙according to evolu-
tionary tracks (e.g., Feiden 2016).
• Stars identified as close-binaries in previous liter-
ature were excluded.
• Class II disks were selected based on the their
Spectral Energy Distributions (SEDs) and their
infrared spectral indices −1.6 < αIR < −0.3
(Williams & Cieza 2011).
• Sources were chosen to have ALMA Band 7 de-
tections in the continuum and 13CO J = 3-2 line
(Ansdell et al. 2016, 2018).
• Known highly-inclined (inclination angle > 75◦)
and edge-on disks were excluded because the prop-
erties of their central stars cannot be well con-
strained.

3. DATA REDUCTION
In AGE-PRO, we carry out a similar data reduction
strategy for disks across different star-forming regions to
ensure consistency in the products. The data reduction
procedure is described in detail in the overview paper
(Zhang et al. 2025, Section 3), and the data reduction
scripts are released on the AGE-PRO website. Here we
summarize main procedures and the special steps that
are taken for the 10 disks in the Lupus star-forming
region.
3.1. Calibration Strategy
The execution blocks were initially calibrated individ-
ually by the ALMA staff using the CASA pipeline ver-
sion 6.2.1-7-pipeline for both short and long base-
line Band 6 and Band 7 data, and 5.6.1-8-pipeline
for archival Band 7 data.
Self-calibration of the continuum visibilities is per-
formed for all the sources in the AGE-PRO Lupus sam-
ple. The continuum visibilities are first created by flag-
ging any possible line emission in each spectral window
and averaging into a maximum 125 MHz channel width.

resulting peak intensity and image SNR are used to de-

last step, one amplitude self-calibration is attempted,
usually with a solution interval equal to the scan length.
Self-calibration improves the SNR for the disks in AGE-
PRO Lupus sample by ∼50% within a few iterations.
These alignments and calibration tables are also ap-
plied to the original visibilities that do not have any
spectral averaging and include the spectral lines. Then
we subtract the continuum from these aligned and self-
calibrated visibilities to provide a continuum-subtracted
measurement set for each source for further analysis on
different spectral lines.
The data products, continuum+line measurement sets
and data reduction scripts are available for download
from the AGE-PRO website.
3.2. Imaging Strategy
The continuum images in both Bands 6 (234 GHz)
and 7 (285 GHz) are generated while the self-calibration
is conducted. For the final continuum image product
after self-calibration, we use the tclean task with an
elliptical mask, and a Briggs robust parameter of 0.5 to
achieve an angular resolution of ∼0.35 ′′ for Band 6 and
∼0.70 ′′ for Band 7.
Before the line imaging, we split the data into individ-
ual measurement sets for each target line. We also use
the constraint of uvrange = ‘>50klambda’ for Band 6
data to remove the cloud contamination in large-scale
structures ≥4′′ that heavily affect 12CO (2 −1) line
emission, and sometimes in the 13CO (2 −1) line. The
choice of the 4′′criteria is based on the initial images,
where we see the 12CO (2 −1) line emission size of the
disk is < 4′′, but cloud contamination structures can
be more extended.
We do not apply such constraint
for V1094 Sco (Lupus 10), because it has a large disk
(see Section 4). Then the split measurement sets are re-
sampled onto a common velocity grid using the cvel2
task. A velocity spacing of 0.1 km s−1 is used for Band 6,
12CO (2−1) line, and 0.2 km s−1 is used for all other lines
in both Band 6 and 7, unless otherwise stated. Only a
few targets have a previously measured system veloc-
ity (vsys), thus we set a large range of channels from
−19.0 km s−1 to +21 km s−1 to capture the entire disk
emission.
The line images are then produced with the tclean
task. We start by using the tclean tasks with a Briggs
robust parameter of 0.5 for all line images to achieve the
same high spatial resolution of ∼0.35 ′′ as the contin-
uum images, which also gives high image fidelity for the
12CO. However, for other weaker lines including 13CO,
C18O and N2H+, the robust value of 0.5 resulted in a
low SNR. Therefore, to boost the SNR and provide more
accurate total fluxes, a robust value of 1.0 is chosen to
generate images for other lines. This choice results in
a larger beam size of ∼0.50 ′′ (Band 6) and ∼0.80 ′′
(Band 7).

4. RESULTS
4.1. Band 6 Images
The AGE-PRO Band 6 set up includes the continuum
image at 234 GHz, three CO isotopologues 12CO J=2-1
(230.5 GHz), 13CO J=2-1 (220.3 GHz), and C18O J=2-1
(219.6 GHz), and other lines in this wavelength coverage:
H2CO J=303-202 (218.2 GHz), DCN J=3-2 (217.2 GHz),
and N2D+ J=3-2 (231.3 GHz).
All sources are detected in the continuum (with SNR
≳20), 12CO J=2-1 (SNR ≳10), and 13CO J=2-1 (SNR
≳5 except Lupus 9 with SNR ∼3). C18O J=2-1 emis-
sion is detected with SNR > 3σ for Lupus 1, 2, 4, 6, 7,
8 and 10, and with SNR ∼1 −3σ for Lupus 3, 5 and 9.
Figure 5 shows the Band 6 continuum images and the
zeroth-moment (integrated flux) maps of CO isotopo-
logue lines. For Lupus 10, a zoom-out image with size
of ±5′′ is shown in Figure 6 to present its full gas disk.
Figure 7 shows the spectra of each target. We use the
aperture corresponding to the gas disk radii defined in
12CO (see Section 4.3), and smaller apertures for 13CO
and C18O, and the adopted apertures are shown on the
upper-right corner on each panel. To make these spec-
tra, we re-bin the channel width of 13CO and C18O to
0.4 and 0.8 km s−1 respectively to boost the SNR in each
channel.
In the zeroth-moment (moment 0) maps and the line
spectrum of 12CO images, the emission is asymmetric
for Lupus 1, and 2.
This is due to cloud contamina-
tion, which absorbs the emission from the disk in certain
velocity channels. For example, a clear cloud contami-
nation can be seen in the channels around 5 km s−1 for
the Lupus 2 12CO (Figure 7), which leads to a clear
absorption of the 12CO emission, and thus creating an
asymmetric line profile. An asymmetry is also presented
in the first-moment (moment 1) maps (Figure B1 in Ap-
pendix B). Although we removed the smallest baselines
to reduce contamination from large scale structures, ab-
sorption from the nearby cloud is still an issue and
strongly affects part of the channels in the 12CO images.
We further discuss how to correct for cloud contamina-
tion in Section 4.3 when measuring the disk emission
and size from the 12CO images.
The 13CO emission
experiences a similar cloud contamination, though the
extent is significantly less pronounced – with more sym-
metric line spectra, moment 0 and moment 1 maps –
and therefore we do not attempt to remove the contam-
ination there.
While C18O does not show evidence of cloud contam-
ination in the images or spectra, there are some struc-
tures present in the moment 0 maps: a ‘spiral-like’ struc-
ture in Lupus 2, and ‘tail-like’ structure in Lupus 4 and
7.
These structures only appear in C18O but not in
the other two CO isotopologues, and could be signa-
tures of possible interaction with the cloud (e.g., Pineda
et al. 2023; Winter et al. 2024). However, the flux level
of these structures is just ∼1 −2 times of the RMS.
Therefore, these structures may not be real kinematic
structures, and are most likely due to the low sensitivity
in those regions. The confirmation of such substructures
needs deep follow-up observations.

4.3. Fluxes and Disk Radii
The total flux and the radius of the continuum and gas
emission lines are measured from the curve-of-growth
method following the same method adopted in the lit-
erature (e.g., Ansdell et al. 2016, 2018; Andrews et al.
2018; Sanchis et al. 2021).
For gas emission lines, the moment 0 maps are made
with a Keplerian mask using the parameters given in
Table 2 with disk inclination and position angle from
Vioque et al. (2025), but with 1.5× larger Rmax to make
sure all the emission is included. Then, we create a se-
ries of elliptical apertures upon the moment 0 maps. The
apertures correspond to the circular aperture projected
to the corresponding disk inclination and position angle.
The apertures are set up with different radii centered at
the stellar location, and the flux inside each aperture is
measured to compile a curve-of-growth of flux vs. radius
r. The disk total flux is measured when the curve-of-
growth reaches the first peak of the oscillating plateau
modulated by noise, and the variation of total flux after
the peak does not exceed the noise.
Flux uncertain-
ties are estimated as the square root of the number of
beams that are inside the aperture for measuring the
total flux, and multiplied by the per-beam noise mea-
sured from an emission free part of the image outside
of that aperture. To validate these uncertainties esti-
mated from root-mean-square (RMS), we also carry out
a bootstrapping method on three CO isotopologue lines
(described in Appendix E). We find the uncertainties
from both methods are in agree with each other, except
for the large disk around Lupus 10 (Figure E6 and Ap-

5. DISCUSSION
While our selection criteria for the Lupus AGE-PRO
disks were based on the shallow and moderate resolu-
tion Band 7 survey by Ansdell et al. (2016), the Lupus
region was also observed in Band 6 in the continuum
and main CO isotopologue lines (Andrews et al. 2018;
Ansdell et al. 2018). When comparing our results with
those obtained from the shallower Band 6 survey, we
find that AGE-PRO increases the detection rates of the
rare isotopologue 13CO and C18O from 10% to 100%
and from 0% to 90%, respectively. When sources are
detected in both surveys, their fluxes agree to within
20%, with the exceptions of Lupus 5, 6, and 10, where

observations (see Appendix F for details). We also com-

5.1. Correlations between the dust emission and gas
line fluxes
There have been many shallow surveys of nearby star-
forming regions where the continuum flux is often de-
tected while gas lines are more rarely detected, es-
pecially for the rarer CO isotopologues (see Miotello
et al. 2023; Manara et al. 2023, as reviews).
With
the higher sensitivity of AGE-PRO, we have many de-
tections of CO isotopologues, which enable us to test
whether gas line fluxes are correlated with the contin-
uum emission.
If these correlations exist, they could
aid in planning future gas observations in other star-
forming regions. Besides CO isotopologues, AGE-PRO
also provides more N2H+ (3 −2) line detections, which
is an ionization tracer in disk mid-planes, and can be
used to evaluate the CO abundance in the disk together
with C18O (2 −1) (e.g., Trapman et al. 2022; Trapman
et al. 2025a). Thus, we also compare the N2H+ (3 −2)
fluxes with the C18O (2 −1) fluxes.
Figure 11 shows line fluxes from three CO isotoplogues
vs the Band 6 continuum flux density (F234 GHz cont.) and
the N2H+ (3−2) fluxes. The figure suggests that all CO
isotopologue fluxes are correlated with the F234 GHz cont.,
while the FC18O (2−1) appears to be less well correlated
with the FN2H+ (3−2).
To test whether correlations are present we follow Pas-
cucci et al. (2023) and use the pymccorrelation routine
v0.2.55 which carries out the non-parametric Kendall’s
τ test with censored data (Isobe et al. 1986). This rou-
tine is capable of including uncertainties and upper lim-
its. The resulting median values of the Kendall’s τ and
the percent probability p are presented in each panel: τ
gives the direction of the correlation (positive correla-

5.2. Gas-dust disk size and implications for radial drift
The ratio between gas and dust disk radii can be used
to identify disks with significant dust evolution and ra-
dial drift (e.g., Trapman et al. 2019, 2020; Toci et al.
2021). Figure 12 compares the 12CO radii and the Band
6 continuum radii at 234 GHz, where they represent gas
disk radii (Rgas) and dust disk radii (Rdust), respectively.
The beam sizes for both tracers are ∼0.35 ′′ and they
are represented by vertical and horizontal green dashed
lines in each panel of Figure 12.
For the two targets that are well resolved in the images
of both 12CO (2 −1) and Band 6 continuum – Lupus 2
and 10 – their CO gas radii are about two times their
dust radii (this is true for both R68 and R90). This result
is consistent with the trend reported in the literature
for Lupus disks as well as disks in other star-forming
regions (e.g., Ansdell et al. 2018; Kurtovic et al. 2021;
Sanchis et al. 2021; Long et al. 2022), and it is below the
region where dust evolution and radial drift dominates
(Rgas/Rdust > 4, see Trapman et al. 2019).
However, the majority of the Lupus disks have dust
disk radii close to the beam size. This means that we can
only place an upper limit of ∼100 AU to their dust disk
sizes and, from the image plane, we cannot conclude
whether or not the gas is significantly more extended
than the dust.
In an accompanying publication, Vioque et al. (2025)
carried out visibility fittings of the Band 6 continuum
data to obtain dust disk radii and Trapman et al.
(2025b) estimated beam-corrected deconvolved gas disk
radii. More discussions on the gas-dust disk size differ-
ences are presented in Trapman et al. (2025b). To give
a general overview, the gas and dust radii from their
works are compared with those measured here in the
image plane along with literature values in Appendix F.
Here we conclude that there is no evidence of signif-
icant radial drift for the large disks in our sample. In
an accompanying paper, Kurtovic et al. (2025) demon-
strates that the radial drift is not very significant even
in compact disks.

6. SUMMARY
In this work, we present ALMA Band 6 and 7 observa-
tions and analysis for 10 disks in the Lupus star-forming
region. These disks are part of the ALMA large program
AGE-PRO, which aims to study gas disk evolution with
the Lupus sample tracing a population of ∼1 −3 Myr-
old disks. These disks are selected based on the spec-
tral type of their host stars (M3-K6), their broad-band
SED (indicating Class-II systems), and literature obser-
vations which suggest they are not close-binaries, not
edge-on disks, and also had previous 13CO detections.
The high-sensitivity AGE-PRO observations enable us
to measure their disk masses and sizes with great pre-
cision.
Combining these values with results from the
younger Ophiuchus (Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025) and
the older Upper Sco region (Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025)
gives us the opportunity to study the evolution of dust
and gas disks through time (Trapman et al. 2025a; Kur-
tovic et al. 2025; Tabone et al. 2025). Our main conclu-
sions for the AGE-PRO Lupus disks are as follows:
1. We find that the Lupus AGE-PRO sample is rep-
resentative for all disks around M3-K6 stars in the
Lupus region with respect to mass accretion rates,
millimeter fluxes, and spectral indices.
2. In ALMA Band 6 observations, all disks are de-
tected in the 234 GHz continuum, 12CO (2 −1)
and 13CO (2 −1) with SNR ≳3.
C18O (2 −1)
emission is detected in 4 targets with SNR > 3σ,
in other 5 with SNR ∼2 −3σ, and for 1 target
with SNR ∼1σ. We also have > 3 σ detections of
H2CO J= 303 −202 in three targets, DCN J=3-2
in two targets, and N2D+ J=3-2 in one target.
3. In ALMA Band 7 observations, all disks are de-
tected in the 285 GHz continuum. N2H+ (3 −2)
emission is detected in 3 disks with SNR > 5σ, 1
disk with SNR∼3σ, and in other 4 disks with SNR
< 3σ. No any N2H+ (3−2) emission is found from
the other 2 disks. We also have > 3 σ detections
of DCO+ J=4-3, DCN J=4-3, H2CO J = 404−303
in three targets, and C34S J=6-5 in one target.
4. We measure the total flux (densities) from the
curve-of-growth method for dust continuum in
both ALMA Bands 6 and 7, CO isotopologue line
emission, and N2H+ (3−2) emission. We find that
there are positive correlations between all CO iso-
topologue line emission and the dust continuum
flux density, and also a positive correlation be-
tween C18O (2 −1) and N2H+ (3 −2), with the
exception of Lupus 3. While the sample on the
whole appears to have similar CO abundances, Lu-
pus 3 shows enhanced N2H+ emission and may be
depleted in CO.
5. We also measure the 68% and 90% radii for Band 6
dust continuum and 12CO (2 −1) emission with
curve-of-growth method from the image plane.
We find that only the two largest disks are well-
resolved in the image, and they follow the relation-
ship of Rgas ∼2 × Rdust, indicating no significant
radial drift. For other targets that are not well-
resolved we refer to Trapman et al. (2025b) and
Vioque et al. (2025) for the gas and dust sizes re-
spectively.
6. We estimate gas disk masses from thermochemi-
cal models and find higher disk masses compared
with the earlier DALI grid of Miotello et al. (2017).
Our C18O (2−1) derived mass estimates based on
the Ruaud et al. (2022) model tracks are compared
with an updated DALI grid using a combination of
CO isotopologue and N2H+ (3−2) fluxes in accom-
panying work by Trapman et al. (2025a). Masses
estimated from both approaches are very similar
for large and more massive disks. For smaller and
fainter disks, discrepancies are larger but all within
the quoted uncertainties.
More analysis and/or
deeper observations are needed to better constrain
the mass estimates for small disks.

In the Band 6 12CO images and spectra, we notice

seem to be asymmetric, but their emission from both

The most interesting target is Lupus 10, where we can
see two clear red lobes perpendicular to the disk plane

disk outflows from this system. But because this first-

Here we outline how the method is implemented:

which are all channels with velocities outside a
user-specified velocity range that contains line

that the noise bootstrapping should be done with a

data.
2. A subset of equal velocity width as the line-
containing data cube is selected from the line-free
data cube, and we refer to this subset as the “noise
cube”. By default, the channels in the noise cube
are selected at random, but it is possible to se-

G. RELATIONSHIPS BETWEEN FLUXES, FLUX
DENSITIES, AND RADII.
In Section 5.1, we find positive correlations between
different CO isotopologue lines and the Band 6 con-
tinuum flux densities at 234 GHz; and we also find
correlation between the optically thin FC18O (2−1) with
FN2H+ (3−2) for the Lupus disks in our sample. Here,
we also present the comparisons between the three CO
isotopologue lines in Band 6 with the Band 7 contin-
uum flux densities at 285 GHz (Figure G9). Following
the same tests that carried out in Section 5.1, we find
strong positive correlations between the F285 GHz cont.
and F12CO (2−1) as well as F13CO (2−1), where we find pos-
itive τ and small p < 1% from Kendall’s τ test. Lupus 3
appears to be an outlier again in the correlation between
F285 GHz cont. and FC18O (2−1) and because of its very
small FC18O (2−1) compared to its other CO isotopologue
fluxes. Once we exclude Lupus 3, the F285 GHz cont. is also
strongly correlated with the FC18O (2−1) with p < 1%.
We also test the correlations between the continuum
flux densities, CO isotopologue fluxes, and FN2H+ (3−2)
with the measured 90% radius from the 12CO (2 −1)
images (Figure G10). We find positive correlations for
all of them, and the continuum flux densities from both
bands, F12CO (2−1) and F13CO (2−1) are with p < 3% from
the Kendall’s τ tests, suggesting they are all likely to
be optically thick, while FC18O (2−1) and FN2H+ (3−2) are
optically thin as they are less likely to be correlated with
the disk sizes. But none of these tests show significant
correlations (with p < 1%), which could be due to the
fact that more than half of the disks in our sample are
not well-resolved in the images and their measured disk
sizes are the beam sizes. Therefore, observations with
higher angular-resolution that can resolve these small
disks in our sample could help further conduct detailed
analysis on the fluxes and the disk sizes, and confirm
the correlations found here.
