Language Models Might Not Understand You:
Evaluating Theory of Mind via Story Prompting
Nathaniel Getachew & Abulhair Saparov
{ngetach, asaparov}@purdue.edu
Abstract
We introduce StorySim, a programmable framework for synthetically generating
stories to evaluate the theory of mind (ToM) and world modeling (WM) capabilities
of large language models (LLMs). Unlike prior benchmarks that may suffer from
contamination in pretraining data, or rely on an LLM for generation, StorySim
produces novel, compositional story prompts anchored by a highly controllable
STORYBOARD, enabling precise manipulation of character perspectives and events.
We use this framework to design first- and second-order ToM tasks alongside WM
tasks that control for the ability to track and model mental states. Our experiments
across a suite of LLMs show that most models achieve higher accuracy on WM
tasks than on ToM tasks, and that models tend to reason more accurately when the
subject of reasoning is a person rather than an inanimate object. Additionally, our
framework enabled us to find evidence of heuristic behavior and an over-reliance
on earlier events in the story. All code for generating data and evaluations is freely
available.1

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs), especially models meant for reasoning, have shown increasing
improvement on benchmarks that were once infamously challenging. Contemporary models have
made progress in reasoning through math problems, coding, and pure logical deduction (Jaech et al.,
2024; Guo et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025). As reasoning capabilities of LLMs continue to improve,
we focus on LLMs’ ability to reason about mental states and the perspectives of other intelligent
agents, a concept known as theory of mind (ToM). This has become increasingly important, as an
LLM without a sound ToM might not be able to understand the perspective of a user with harmful
intent, or consider the effect its decisions could have on other people, who have their own goals and
wishes. Since reasoning about perspectives requires modeling the surrounding world, we view ToM
as an extension of world modeling (WM)—an LLM’s capacity to internally represent world states.
Agents with strong WM should exhibit ToM, since other minds exist within the same world.
ToM is often assessed in children by evaluating their ability to understand that others hold mental
representations of the world that differ from their own. The prototypical ToM test is known as the
Sally-Anne test (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985), where children are given a story akin to the following:
Classic Sally-Anne story
Sally and Anne walk into a bedroom. Sally puts a toy on top of the bed and leaves. Anne takes the toy and
hides it under the bed. When Sally comes back into the room, where is the first place she will look for the toy?
It may seem obvious to adults that the first place Sally will look is on top of the bed. However, it has
been shown that children under the age of 4.5 think that Sally will look under the bed (Memisevic
et al., 2018). This is hypothesized to be due to the fact that they have not yet developed ToM, so they
cannot understand the fact that Sally and Anne have a different understanding of the toy’s location.
LLMs have been evaluated on problems of this nature, and initially showed success (Kosinski, 2023).

In order to measure ToM ability in a way that is robust against data contamination, we developed a
flexible, synthetic benchmark. We propose StorySim, a framework that allows us to generate stories
to evaluate ToM and WM abilities of LLMs. The core events of the story are described by a high
level specification called the STORYBOARD. Aside from the STORYBOARD, the rest of the story is
randomly-generated. The generated story is represented symbolically and is guaranteed to be cohesive
and consistent. An example of the framework in action is shown in Figure 1. The STORYBOARD
provides a description of the setting in which the story will take place, including which locations
are accessible from each other, as well as required actions characters need to make, the length of
the story, the number of characters, etc. StorySim then generates a story that is coherent with the
STORYBOARD, filling in all other details randomly in a way that is consistent with the provided
constraints. Due to the exact specificity of the events that occur in StorySim via the STORYBOARD,
the randomness of the events in between, and the length of the stories we generate, the chance that
any synthetically-generated story in our experiments has appeared in the training data of any LLMs
is astronomically small.
StorySim allows us to systematically evaluate current LLMs, which we utilize to address the
following research questions:
RQ1 Can we measure ToM abilities of LLMs in a way that is robust to data contamination and
heuristics?
RQ2 To what extent do LLMs rely on memorization and heuristics to answer ToM questions?
RQ3 Can ToM abilities in LLMs be evaluated independently of their general WM capabilities?
Relatedly, do LLMs show stronger performance on general WM tasks or on ToM-specific
reasoning?

World Modeling: Though there is much work evaluating aspects of WM abilities of LLMs (Kocisk´y
et al., 2018; Qiu et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025), there are none, to the best of our knowledge, that
examine the relationship between WM and ToM ability. We consider ToM to be an extension of
world modeling since ToM requires the explicit modeling of mental states and how they may change
over time, and mental states are dependent on the goals, limitations, and perspectives of agents. In
the stories we generate, the character’s perspectives only consist of what they observe around them,
so to reason over this requires understanding the world in which they live and the limitations of each
character’s perception and knowledge. Wang et al. (2024) train a narrator and a role player model
to interact within role-playing game environments. This setup implicitly requires both models to
construct and maintain an understanding of the environment (WM), while the narrator must also
infer the role player’s perspective to guide narrative choices (ToM). However, the task itself does not
directly evaluate either ToM or world modeling capabilities in isolation.
Synthetic Datasets: Though existing synthetic datasets meant for benchmarking LLMs exist (Patel
et al., 2024; Shvets, 2025; Sood, 2024; Mishra & Prabakeran, 2025), there are none that evaluate
ToM and WM abilities as we do. Synthetic datasets have been shown to be an effective method for
training LLMs to perform well on logic tasks (Xie et al., 2025), clinical text classification (Tang et al.,
2023), deductive reasoning (Saparov et al., 2023), and more. The diversity of synthetic data is an
important factor in the evaluation of performance on both in- and out-of-distribution data (Chen et al.,
2024a; Havrilla et al., 2024). For this reason, we developed StorySim to be highly programmable
and able to generate a wide range of stories.

3
Methods
Below we discuss the details of StorySim’s generation process, the breadth of stories it can create,
and how we leverage it to answer our research questions.
3.1
StorySim
Broadly, the StorySim framework generates each story in two steps: (1) we construct a STO-
RYBOARD capturing high-level story specifications, and (2) StorySim uses it to produce a story
by generating event sequences that satisfy these constraints while filling in the remaining details
coherently.
A STORYBOARD is defined as D = (C, ϕ, G, E, n), where C is a set of characters, G is a directed
graph describing the locations in the story, with edges denoting which locations are immediately
reachable from other locations, E is a set of programmed events, and n is the number of events. The
StorySim framework generates a sequence of n events of the form ϕ(c, l), where ϕ is the action,
c ∈C is the character performing the action, and l ∈V(G) is the location of the event. Events
follow the structure of G, so characters move only between adjacent vertices. Only events in E are
deterministic, specifying actions at particular time steps; all others are randomly generated but remain
consistent with E. The exact parameters of D in our experiments are given in Appendix A. An example
is shown in Figure 1. These events are translated into natural language via templates mapping each
ϕ(c, l) to a sentence. Optionally, the stories are further paraphrased into more naturalistic language
by GPT-4o-mini (Hurst et al., 2024).
Though a simple framework, StorySim can be used to create a wide variety of stories. Depending
on the desired story, the framework requires varying degrees of manual input. However StorySim
can be used to simulate any story where each action can be modeled as a relation (or set of relations)
that act over a subject and a node in the graph. The subject does not necessarily need to be a human,
and the nodes need not be locations. Additionally, the stories can feature manually-created actions as
well, which can add another layer of complexity to the events described by StorySim. Appendix
D illustrates the range of stories the framework can generate for ToM evaluation, including more
narrative-driven scenarios, goal-oriented tasks, counterfactual reasoning, and preference attribution.
Our main analysis, however, focuses on false-belief tasks, as they offer a more controlled setting for
evaluating ToM.
3.2
Experimental Setup
StorySim can generate stories with either simpler sentences (i.e., template-generated) or more
linguistically naturalistic sentences (i.e., paraphrased). In most of our experiments, we utilize the
simpler language to minimize confounding effects from linguistic complexity, ensuring errors reflect
limitations in the targeted ability rather than misinterpretation—we aim to measure the model’s ToM
capability independently of the model’s ability to parse sentences. In order to explore the effect
of linguistic complexity on LLM ToM performance, we run experiments using LLM-paraphrased
versions of the stories; these results are reported in Figure 5.
In order to test ToM abilities, we generate two types of ToM problems:
First-order ToM problem: Ask about one character’s knowledge of another character’s location.
E.g.: “Where does Bob think Alice is?”
Second-order ToM problem: Ask about one character’s knowledge of a second character’s knowledge
of a third character’s location. E.g.: “Where does Bob think Charlie thinks Alice is?”
To generate stories that allow us to ask questions like the ones above, we create two types of
STORYBOARDS which specify events for characters S1, S2, and T, using the Hallways and Doors
environment shown in Figure 1. In the above examples, S1 is Bob, S2 is Charlie, and T is Alice.
We use StorySim to generate stories for first-order ToM by specifying a high-level pattern within
the STORYBOARD: S1 and T first meet at a shared location, after which T moves elsewhere. Then,
without S1’s knowledge, T moves to a third location and remains there for the rest of the story.
Similarly, for second-order ToM, we define a pattern where S1, S2, and T initially meet, S2 and T
later meet again at a different location, and T subsequently moves to another location alone. Given
these STORYBOARDs, we can ask:

ToM Prompt: Where does S1 think S2 thinks T is?

The first question type requires the LLM to understand the perspective of S1 and their perceived
location of S2 and T, thereby directly testing its ToM capabilities. The second question type is
equivalent to the first, but instead of asking the model to consider the perspective of S1, we prompt it
to algorithmically search through the story to find an event where S1’s perception of the locations
of S2 and T changed. Thus, the second question is a test of world modeling capability, since we do
not ask about the mental state of any character, and answering the question requires understanding
the events in the story. Finally, the third question type is equivalent to the second, but instead of
describing human subjects, the story describes inanimate objects, where each object is moved by
some third-party outside the story. This question is meant to test whether the LLM’s world modeling
ability is sensitive to whether the entities in the world are animate or inanimate. These experiments
enable us to directly test RQ3. The results of evaluating a suite of LLMs on these three prompts are
shown in Figures 2 and 3.
Additionally, given the STORYBOARD for a first-order or second-order ToM problem, we vary the
number of time steps between the event where S1 last perceives T’s location and the event where S1’s
beliefs are no longer true. A reasonable hypothesis about the ToM abilities of LLMs is that since T’s
last movement is a more recent event in the story, LLMs may utilize a heuristic where they predict
T’s last location, despite the fact that S1 did not see this movement. We refer to this number of time
steps between these two events as the mislead distance. We create STORYBOARDS for both first- and
second- order ToM problems, varying the mislead distance and using the first-order ToM Prompt.

prior knowledge of the task, to answer ToM questions about StorySim stories. Each student was
shown 50 examples from the Hallways and Doors environment shown in Figure 1 across various
mislead distances, achieving an average accuracy of 96%.

In the subsequent experiments, we aim to address whether or not LLMs are exploiting simple
heuristics to answer our questions rather than relying on an actual ToM mechanism. We use
StorySim to generate stories while controlling for numerous confounding variables, including but
not limited to the story length, number of characters, locations, events, and the order of events. As a
result, the resulting observed differences in LLM behavior can be attributed to specific changes in the
experimental variables.
4.3.1
How Sensitive Are LLMs to Story Construction in ToM?
A reasonable hypothesis is that LLMs would predict T’s last location without considering S1’s mental
state, especially if the final location change occurred later in the story. This would be a form of
recency bias. To test this, we evaluated GPT-4, R1, R1-Distilled Qwen, Llama 4 Scout, Llama
4 Maverick, and o3-mini on first- and second-order ToM problems while increasing the mislead
distance (i.e., the number of steps after S1 sees T move to another location before T moves to a
different location altogether) (Meta AI, 2025a;b; DeepSeek AI, 2025). We chose this set of models
because they can be organized into pairs based on model family with differing numbers of parameters.
Though we see no significant evidence of recency bias, we present values across mislead distances to
demonstrate StorySim’s ability to generate targeted stories, and the LLM’s variance across slight
changes in construction.
In the first-order setting, we see a wide spread of model performances, with R1, GPT-4, Qwen, and
Maverick performing nearly identically at smaller mislead distances, then varying more at larger
mislead distances. Llama 4 Scout outperforms Maverick despite its smaller size. But in all other
pairs of models from the same family, the larger model outperforms the smaller one, providing further
evidence of a correlation between model size and ToM ability.
Figure 4 (right) shows the results of the second-order ToM version of the experiment. Again, Llama
4 Scout outperforms Maverick, despite the difference in model size, but R1 outperforms its distilled
Qwen counterpart. The performance gaps between pairs of models of the same family shrinks in
the second-order setting, pointing to the difficulty of this ToM task. The most clear differences in
performance appear at the largest mislead distances, with the Llama models and R1 finishing above
the rest. GPT-4 is the only model that noticeably improves performance relative to other models.
Interestingly, the worst performing model in this setting is o3-mini, which is the best performing
model in the first-order setting.

5
Conclusion and Future Work
We present StorySim, a framework that synthetically gen-
erates stories from a STORYBOARD, a high-level outline
containing a number of key events. This framework cre-
ates stories that enable us to evaluate the ToM and WM abilities of LLMs in a way that is robust
against data contamination, investigate whether world modeling ability is a good predictor of ToM
ability, and identify heuristics that LLMs may use in place of ToM reasoning.
Our results show that most LLMs exhibit generally stronger world modeling ability than ToM.
However, we observe promising signs that increasing model size may improve ToM performance.
Our error analysis reveals that LLMs often memorize incorrect character locations within the stories.
While varying character locations affects LLM performance, we do not observe widespread recency
bias, though reasoning models show reduced performance and greater reliance on heuristics.
We hope this work inspires future efforts to benchmark ToM and social reasoning abilities in LLMs
in a more controlled and targeted manner. Though we focus on each character’s perceptions of other’s
locations, StorySim’s programmability allows for more complex stories than those presented here.
As LLMs are increasingly used in socially-sensitive domains, a rigorous benchmark of their ToM and
social reasoning capabilities, as well as the perturbations that affect them, is essential.
