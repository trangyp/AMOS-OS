Abstract
We propose utilizing match-level suspense and surprise—which capture the entertain-
ment utility created by competitive balance and outcome uncertainty for sports specta-
tors—as alternative policy targets for league organizers and managers. Through simulations,
we derive a benchmark range for suspense and surprise based on a perfectly balanced match
before analyzing over 25,000 men’s matches (2010/11–2023/24) and 725 women’s matches
(2023/24) from Europe’s top football leagues. Our findings reveal that an average match
generates lower suspense compared to the benchmark range, particularly for top teams,
while surprise values consistently align with the benchmark. Moreover, we observe nuanced
trends over time in men’s football and highlight notable differences across leagues and clubs
in both men’s and women’s competitions. These insights enhance our understanding of how
the attractiveness of matches arises from competitive balance and carry important policy
implications.
Keywords: OR in sports, Competitive balance, Consumption of sport, Football, Scoring
Rate, Suspense, Surprise.

Introduction
Professional sports are an entertainment product that regularly attracts widespread attention.
For several decades, one key appeal for consumers has been attributed to outcome uncertainty,
a concept rooted in the seminal works of Rottenberg (1956) and Neale (1964), who argue that
the unpredictability of competitive outcomes enhances fan welfare. Conceptually, competitive
balance, which refers to the relative strengths of teams, translates into outcome uncertainty at
various levels: a single match (short-term perspective), a sub-competition such as the cham-
pionship race (mid-term perspective), or the dominance of a few teams over time (long-term
perspective) (e.g., Szymanski, 2003). Subsequently, greater outcome uncertainty is assumed to
lead to higher demand for sports. This relationship is known in the literature as the Uncer-
tainty of Outcome Hypothesis (UOH), which primarily refers to the relation between outcome
uncertainty and demand at the match level (e.g., Fort, 2022; Pawlowski, Nalbantis, & Coates,
2018).
Based on the premise that outcome uncertainty enhances entertainment value and is de-
sirable for sports fans, league organizers frequently (re)design competitions, and policymakers
justify market interventions such as salary caps, draft systems, or revenue redistribution schemes
(e.g., Fort & Quirk, 1995), which are prohibited or heavily restricted in most industries outside
of professional sports (e.g., Budzinski, 2012; Mehra & Zuercher, 2006). Moreover, numerous
studies investigate factors influencing outcome uncertainty, including competition design ele-
ments (e.g., Scarf, Yusof, & Bilbao, 2009), sporting rules (e.g., Baker, Chadwick, Parma, &
Scarf, 2022), point-scoring systems (e.g., Percy, 2009), and scoring rates (e.g., Scarf, Parma, &
McHale, 2019). However, prior research has failed to provide consistent evidence that outcome
uncertainty is a key driver of stadium attendance and TV viewership, particularly in European
football (Pawlowski & Nalbantis, 2019; Collins & Humphreys, 2022; Johnson & Fort, 2022). Ac-
cordingly, monitoring outcome uncertainty over time and treating it as a policy target appears
misleading.
In this paper, we change perspectives and propose suspense and surprise (Ely, Frankel, &
Kamenica, 2015) as promising alternative policy targets. Both measures capture the entertain-
ment utility that originates from the relative strengths of the teams in a match and unfolds
through the resolution of information during the game. Suspense is a forward-looking concept
that reflects the excitement or anxiety about future events and arises from the variance in beliefs
about the outcome of the game in the next period. Surprise, in contrast, is a backward-looking
concept that captures the emotional response to an unexpected event, resulting from the shift
between the current and previous beliefs about the game’s outcome (Ely et al., 2015).
A key reason for employing both measures is their clear link to sports demand.1 Moreover,
both measures are closely tied to outcome uncertainty, albeit in opposite ways: suspense tends
to increase with higher uncertainty, whereas the potential for surprise rises when uncertainty is

captures two distinct dimensions of entertainment utility within the broader concept of outcome
uncertainty. This perspective may help explain the mixed empirical support for the UOH, as
it suggests that the relationship between match outcome uncertainty and demand operates
through at least two distinct mechanisms. Finally, the calculation of suspense and surprise
follows a bottom-up approach to competitive balance and outcome uncertainty (Basini, Tsouli,

club or league level, thereby allowing for a more precise examination of patterns and trends.
We examine the status quo and the development of suspense and surprise in European foot-
ball—the most extensively analyzed context for the UOH (Collins & Humphreys, 2022)—and
pose three questions: (i) How do match-level suspense and surprise depend on the (relative)
strengths of the teams? (ii) How do suspense and surprise in European football matches com-
pare to those in a perfectly balanced match? (iii) How have suspense and surprise evolved
over time? Addressing these questions enables league organizers and policymakers to make in-
formed regulatory and league design decisions by considering the origins, levels, and trends of
demand-relevant suspense and surprise.
We begin by analyzing how match-level suspense and surprise are influenced by the strengths
of the competing teams. This is important because team strength variation captures competitive
balance (Baker et al., 2022). To investigate this, we simulate matches between teams with
varying scoring rates, which reflect the underlying strengths of the competing teams (Scarf,
Khare, & Alotaibi, 2022). A match is considered “perfectly balanced” when both teams have
equal strength and, consequently, equal scoring rates. Our simulations reveal a key trade-off:
suspense in a perfectly balanced match is higher when scoring rates are lower, whereas surprise
increases with higher scoring rates. Based on this, we establish a benchmark range of suspense
and surprise, derived from simulating perfectly balanced matches at varying scoring rates.
In the second step, we derive empirical values of suspense and surprise from over 25,000
matches played in the top five European leagues: the English Premier League, the German
Bundesliga, the Spanish La Liga, the Italian Serie A, and the French Ligue 1, covering the
seasons from 2010/11 to 2023/24. Additionally, we include data from the 2023/24 season of the
women’s top divisions in the same five countries.2
Our findings suggest that an average match in European football generates lower suspense
values compared to the benchmark range derived from perfectly balanced matches. This pattern
is particularly evident in matches involving top teams, whereas matches involving non-top teams
generally do not exhibit average suspense levels significantly below the lower bound of the
benchmark. In contrast, average surprise values typically lie within the benchmark range.
In terms of the development of suspense and surprise over time, we observe substantial
within-league dynamics. While the overall changes in suspense and surprise within each league
are modest or even negligible, matches involving Manchester City and Paris Saint-Germain show
a considerable decrease in both suspense and surprise during our observation period. In contrast,
matches involving Barcelona and Real Madrid exhibit a significant increase in both suspense and
surprise over time. In Germany, we find lower but persistent levels of suspense and surprise in FC

need for intervention in European football.

2
General Set-up
The procedures for determining suspense and surprise values in both simulated and real matches
build on the conceptual framework proposed by Ely et al. (2015). Suspense is induced by the
variance in beliefs about the next period, whereas surprise results from the change in belief
between the previous and current periods. In the context of football, these beliefs pertain to
the final outcome of the match, i.e., the winner. Following the approach outlined by Buraimo
et al. (2020), we implement an in-play model to estimate outcome probabilities, namely the
probability of a home win, a draw, or an away win, calculated on a minute-by-minute basis for
each match.
For each game, we assume that a European football match resembles a ”Poisson match”
(e.g., Csat´o, 2023; Scarf et al., 2019, 2022), where goals are scored independently by the home
and away teams, following univariate Poisson distributions. Specifically, the number of goals
scored by the home team, X ∼Poisson(λH), and the number of goals scored by the away
team, Y ∼Poisson(λA). We distribute the team-specific scoring rates, λH and λA, across the
match using historical, league-specific minute-by-minute empirical goal distributions to derive
time-specific scoring rates. These minute-by-minute goal-scoring rates are combined with the
timing of major in-game events, namely goals scored and red cards received, to estimate the
outcome probabilities for each match at any given minute. Specifically, scoring in a given minute
is simulated by drawing from a Bernoulli distribution, where the success probability is set to
the estimated minute-by-minute scoring rate. That is, Gt ∼Bernoulli(pS

Simulated Matches
To investigate how suspense and surprise are influenced by team strengths, we simulate matches
between teams with varying scoring rates. We consider 1,326 unique scoring rate combinations,
covering all pairings of λH and λA ranging from 0 and 5 in increments of 0.1.5 For each pairing,
we simulate 10,000 matches, resulting in a total of 13,260,000 hypothetical matches. The simula-
tions assume no red cards and use historical minute-by-minute empirical goal distributions from
the English Premier League to determine the minute-by-minute scoring rates.6 Subsequently, we
calculate the suspense and surprise values for each simulated match, following the methodology
outlined in Section 2.

low (indicated in blue) when scoring rates are highly unequal, as one team is overwhelmingly
dominant and almost certain to win the match. Conversely, suspense tends to be high (indicated

Importantly, even in a perfectly balanced match, the level of suspense depends on the abso-
lute values of the scoring rates. When both rates are close to zero, the probability of a draw is
high, as neither team is likely to score. As the scoring rates increase together while remaining
equal, the likelihood of a draw decreases, and the probability of either a home or away win
increases (Scarf et al., 2022). Therefore, different levels of equal scoring rates translate into
different levels of suspense. Suspense increases for 0 < λH = λA < 0.5, reaches a maximum
at λH = λA = 0.5, and decreases again for λH = λA > 0.5, as the two rates continue to rise
together. This finding adds nuance to the conjectures of Scarf et al. (2019), who suggest that
suspense strictly decreases as scoring rates increase.
Interestingly, surprise follows the opposite pattern, increasing strictly with higher and more
equal scoring rates. It is zero when λH = λA = 0, as no goals are expected and a draw is certain.
In our simulations, the maximum level of surprise occurs at λH = λA = 5.7 Remarkably, higher
but unequal scoring rates can produce levels of surprise similar to those observed under lower
but equal scoring rates. For example, the surprise generated in a match with λH = λA = 1
is approximately the same as that from a match with λH = 3 and λA = 1.5. These findings
contradict the conjecture by Scarf et al. (2019), who suggest that higher scoring rates always
reduce surprise.
Overall, the insights from Figure 1 highlight the trade-off between suspense and surprise:
suspense in perfectly balanced matches is higher at lower scoring rates (except when rates are
close to zero), whereas surprise is higher at higher scoring rates. Moreover, there are many
instances where an increase in the scoring rate of only one team produces opposing effects on

To contextualize the current empirical values of suspense and surprise in European football
(see Section 4), we use the simulation results to derive a benchmark range for both metrics
in perfectly balanced matches with varying absolute scoring rates. For low scoring rates, we
select λH = λA = 0.5, as this corresponds to the 10th percentile of the empirical distribution of
the average number of goals scored per team per match in our sample. Notably, equal scoring
rates of 0.5 for both teams also represent the point at which suspense is maximized in our
simulated matches. For high scoring rates, we select λH = λA = 2.5, which corresponds to the
90th percentile of the empirical distribution in our sample. Since surprise increases with rising
(equal) scoring rates, it reaches its highest levels at this upper bound.8

4.1
Data and Sample
Our empirical dataset comprises over 25,000 men’s football matches played in the top five
European leagues between the 2010/11 and 2023/24 seasons. Depending on the league, the
dataset includes between 4,284 and 5,320 matches per league.9
To estimate the scoring rates λH and λA for each game, we follow the methodology of
Buraimo et al. (2020) and use an optimization function that assumes Poisson distributions for
goals scored. To ensure precise probability calculations, the overround of the pre-match betting
odds is removed, allowing for an exact determination of the implied probabilities for a home
win, draw, and away win. While these closing odds already encapsulate some information about
the expected number of goals—since a team more likely to win is also more likely to score
more—over/under (O/U) odds provide additional, independent constraints on the distribution
of total goals scored. Specifically, O/U odds for thresholds ranging from 0.5 to 5.5 goals are
converted into implied probabilities and incorporated into the same optimization procedure,
ensuring that the selected scoring rates not only match expected match outcomes but also
align with market expectations about total goals. Using the procedure outlined in Section 2, we
calculate match-level suspense and surprise values for all 25,389 matches played.10
Table 2 presents descriptive statistics for suspense and surprise across leagues. League com-
parisons reveal several differences, with average suspense ranging from 5.74 in the Premier
League to 5.99 in Ligue 1 over the observation period. Similarly, average surprise varies from
1.38 in the Premier League to 1.43 in the Bundesliga. Notably, the relatively large standard
deviations indicate substantial variation over time and across matches. The last column of Table
2 indicates whether the average suspense and surprise values fall significantly below the lower
bound of the benchmark range for perfectly balanced matches, based on a one-sided t-test. The
results show that average suspense values are significantly lower than the benchmark’s lower
bound. In contrast, average surprise values across all five leagues remain above the lower bound
of the benchmark range. This suggests that, while an average match in a top-five European
league generates comparatively low suspense, the level of surprise remains within the expected
range for a perfectly balanced match.11

Suspense and Surprise Trends in the English Premier League
We begin our time trend analysis by examining the development of suspense and surprise in the
English Premier League. Figure 2 presents box plots showing the average suspense and surprise
per game across teams and seasons. We visualize the three top teams separately, defined as
those that accumulated the most points during our observation period from 2010/11 to 2023/24,
while grouping all remaining teams together. According to this definition, the top three teams
in the Premier League were Manchester City, Liverpool, and Arsenal. The grey horizontal band
represents the benchmark range for a perfectly balanced match, and an asterisk (*) indicates
whether the average suspense or surprise values for each team and season are significantly below
the lower bound of the benchmark range (p < 0.05), based on a one-sided t-test.
Figure 2a shows that the average suspense values for Manchester City matches within a
season tend to be the lowest and are almost always significantly below the lower benchmark
bound derived from a perfectly balanced match. For Liverpool and Arsenal, a similar pattern
emerges, though it is less pronounced. For other teams, average suspense values only rarely
fall significantly below the benchmark range, indicating that the average suspense level across
more than 70% of all Premier League matches is comparable to that of matches with perfectly
balanced team strength and high scoring rates of 2.5. With regard to surprise, Figure 2b shows
that average values generally fall within the benchmark range, except for Manchester City
matches in the 2018/19 season.

Discussion and Conclusion
We present match-level suspense and surprise as measures that more effectively capture the
entertainment utility generated by competitive balance and outcome uncertainty for sports
spectators, as they account for how outcome uncertainty unfolds throughout the course of a
match. These measures are valuable because they offer a more accurate understanding of both
the current state and the dynamics of match attractiveness associated with competitive balance
across clubs and seasons. Consequently, suspense and surprise appear to be promising policy
targets for regulatory interventions and decision-making regarding league designs.
Through simulation analysis, we identify a trade-off between suspense and surprise even in
perfectly balanced matches: suspense tends to be higher at lower absolute scoring rates, while
surprise increases with higher ones. Building on this insight, we establish benchmark ranges
for both metrics based on perfectly balanced matches with varying absolute scoring rates. Our
analysis of the top five men’s European football leagues indicates that the average level of
suspense is generally below the benchmark range, particularly in games involving top teams.
In contrast, matches involving non-top teams tend to show average suspense values that are
close to the benchmark’s lower bound or fall within the benchmark range. Meanwhile, average
surprise values mostly remain within the benchmark range.
In contrast to other studies that report a significant decline in competitive balance in Euro-
pean football over time (e.g., Avila-Cano & Triguero-Ruiz, 2023; Basini et al., 2023; Pawlowski
et al., 2010; Poli et al., 2018), our analyses reveal three distinct patterns across and within
the leagues. First, we find leagues where both suspense and surprise considerably decreased for
matches of a single top team, while matches featuring other teams in the same league have
remained relatively stable. This applies to Manchester City in the Premier League and Paris
Saint-Germain in Ligue 1. However, it is important to note that lower levels of suspense and
surprise in matches featuring top teams do not currently appear to reduce overall sporting
interest. The negative impact may be offset by positive influences such as star appeal or the
quality of play (e.g., Buraimo et al., 2022). Second, we observe a modest overall decline in both
suspense and surprise in the German Bundesliga, which is not driven by top teams. Third, we
identify a significant increase in average suspense and surprise over time in Spain’s La Liga, a
trend accentuated by matches involving Real Madrid and Barcelona.
Extending our analysis to include the top women’s leagues in the 2023/24 season provides
additional insights into the current state of competitive balance in European football. On av-
erage, women’s leagues exhibit lower levels of suspense and surprise compared to their male
counterparts. A particularly striking result is the pronounced disparity in these measures be-
tween Barcelona and the rest of the teams in Spain’s Liga F, highlighting extreme dominance
within the league.
Our paper provides a practical framework for assessing the state and evolution of suspense
and surprise in European football. This approach not only advances the theoretical understand-
ing of how competitive balance and outcome uncertainty translate into sports demand, but also
offers actionable insights. In particular, we outline three key practical implications.
17


## Page 19


First, policymakers and league organizers should consider the trade-offs between suspense
and surprise when designing interventions aimed at increasing the attractiveness of their compe-
tition. For example, efforts to equalize team strengths may increase suspense but reduce surprise,
depending on the absolute level of scoring rates. Second, our findings highlight the importance of
examining not only structural differences across leagues but also club-level variation over time.
Average values of these measures can differ substantially even within a single league, suggesting
that league-wide trends may obscure important patterns at the individual club level. Third, al-
though few top men’s clubs financially dominate their leagues, this dominance is only partially
reflected in lower suspense and surprise values, which continue to exhibit substantial match-
level variation. One possible explanation lies in the low-scoring nature of football, where chance
plays a significant role (e.g., Brechot & Flepp, 2020; Gauriot & Page, 2019; Wunderlich, Seck,
& Memmert, 2021). As a result, the current levels of suspense and surprise in men’s football do
not suggest an urgent need for regulatory intervention. Nevertheless, continued monitoring is
warranted, particularly for matches involving Manchester City and Paris Saint-Germain, where
we observe significant downward trends. In contrast, the values for Barcelona’s women’s team
may be more concerning, as both suspense and surprise are substantially lower than for any
other team in either women’s or men’s football.
We conclude by acknowledging several limitations of our study and highlighting promising
directions for future research. One limitation lies in our focus on match-level suspense and
surprise, which captures only short-term, match-specific dynamics. Extending this perspective
to the mid-term, such as the cumulative impact on league outcomes or title races, could offer
valuable insights into how season-long narratives influence sporting demand. A broader temporal
lens is particularly relevant for our analysis of women’s football, which is limited to a single
season (2023/24) and thus provides only a cross-sectional view. Without longitudinal data, we
are unable to assess trends over time or determine whether dominance patterns, such as those
seen in Liga F, are consistent or evolving. Incorporating multiple seasons would allow for a more
robust understanding of how suspense and surprise develop in the women’s game.
Methodologically, our in-play probability model incorporates only goals and red cards, omit-
ting other potentially influential in-game events. Incorporating data on missed scoring oppor-
tunities, possession changes, or defensive interventions could lead to more refined measures of
suspense and surprise. In this context, recent advancements in modeling expected possession
values (e.g., Fern´andez, Bornn, & Cervone, 2021) represent a promising avenue for further de-
velopment. Moreover, our analysis adopts a neutral perspective, without accounting for the
emotional attachment that fans (or ”haters”) may have toward different clubs. These emotional
factors likely shape the perception of suspense and surprise, making fan loyalty an impor-
tant consideration for future research, particularly in the context of league governance (e.g.,
Pawlowski et al., 2024).
Although our study is focused on football, the concepts of suspense and surprise are broadly
applicable across sports. Comparative studies could offer valuable insights into how suspense
and surprise function across diverse sporting environments, thereby enriching our broader un-
derstanding of professional sport as an entertainment product.

A
TV Demand Analysis
We begin our validity check of suspense and surprise by merging our match-level measures
for the Premier League with the original dataset used by Buraimo et al. (2022). This dataset
includes all matches broadcast in the UK market on Sky Sports and BT Sport between the
second half of the 2013/14 season and the end of the 2018/19 season (n = 790).17
The match audience size is defined as the average per-minute audience size, measured from
kick-off to 110 minutes later, accounting for half-time (15 minutes) and added extra time (5
minutes). Additionally, the dataset includes new match-level measures of player quality, derived
from an expected points plus-minus ratings model, as well as match significance measures based
on match forecasting models. For a detailed description of the variables used in the TV demand
model, we refer to Buraimo et al. (2022).
Table A.1 presents the results for the TV demand model. In Column (1), we first replicate
the specification of Buraimo et al. (2022) using the natural log of audience size during the match
as the dependent variable, and obtain almost identical results. Most importantly for our study,
the coefficient of Outcome uncertainty, measured pre-match, is non-significant. In Column (2)
of Table A.1, we add the natural log of suspense to the model. The coefficient is positive and
statistically significant (β = 0.042, p = 0.015), indicating that a 1% increase in suspense is
associated with a 0.042% increase in audience size.
In Column (3) of Table A.1, we add the natural log of surprise to the baseline model. The
coefficient is positive and significant (β = 0.050, p = 0.004), indicating that a 1% increase
in surprise is associated with a 0.05% increase in audience size. In Column (4), we include
both Ln(suspense) and Ln(surprise) in the model. While both coefficients remain positive, the
coefficient of Ln(suspense) is not estimated precisely enough to reach statistical significance at
the conventional level, which may be due to the relatively low number of observations.18
Overall, the results of this subsample analysis suggest that both suspense and surprise are
positively associated with TV demand, whereas the most commonly used measure of outcome
uncertainty, based on the absolute difference in pre-match win probabilities for either team, has
no effect.
