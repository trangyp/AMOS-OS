Wireless Propagation Parameter Estimation with
Convolutional Neural Networks
Steffen Schieler , Student Member, IEEE, Sebastian Semper , and Reiner Thom¨a , Life Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—Wireless channel propagation parameter estimation
forms the foundation of channel sounding, estimation, modeling,
and sensing. This paper introduces a Deep Learning approach
for joint delay- and Doppler estimation from frequency and time
samples of a radio channel transfer function.
Our work estimates the two-dimensional path parameters from
a channel impulse response containing an unknown number of
paths. Compared to existing deep learning-based methods, the
parameters are not estimated via classification but in a quasi-grid-
free manner. We employ a deterministic preprocessing scheme
that incorporates a multi-channel windowing to increase the
estimator’s robustness and enables the use of a Convolutional
Neural Network (CNN) architecture. The proposed architecture
then jointly estimates the number of paths along with the respective
delay and Doppler-shift parameters of the paths. Hence, it jointly
solves the model order selection and parameter estimation task.
We also integrate the CNN into an existing maximum-likelihood
estimator framework for efficient initialization of a gradient-based
iteration, to provide more accurate estimates.
In the analysis, we compare our approach to other methods in
terms of estimate accuracy and model order error on synthetic data.
Finally, we demonstrate its applicability to real-world measurement
data from a anechoic bi-static RADAR emulation measurement.
Index Terms—Parameter Estimation, Convolutional Neural
Networks, Delay-Doppler Estimation, Harmonic Retrieval
I. INTRODUCTION
Wireless channel parameter estimation is a problem encoun-
tered in many signal processing tasks, e.g., channel estima-
tion [1], radar localization, and direction finding. Available
solutions for the task include subspace algorithms, like Multiple
Signal Classification (MUSIC) [2] or Estimation of Signal
Parameters via Rotational Invariance Techniques (ESPRIT) [3],
iterative maximum likelihood (ML) [4], and Sparse Signal
Recovery (SSR) [5] and Deep Neural Network (DNN)-based
algorithms [6]–[11]. We focus on the latter category, presenting
a CNN approach that is also compatible with existing model-
based optimization techniques. Our review of similar works in
this category, namely those discussed below, includes spectral-
and Direction of Arrival (DoA)-estimation due to their similar
algebraic structure.

However, classification methods suffer from inherent estima-

grids are highly interesting, especially if we wish to achieve
super-resolution. Comparably, [11] performs DoA estimation
by combining a regression task, solved by a DNN, with
gradient steps on the data’s likelihood function. The grid-
free estimates from the DNN are used as initial guesses for
a second-order Newton method. Hence, it combines a DNN
and maximum-likelihood methods to improve the fast, but
approximate initial estimates from the DNN with an iterative
high-resolution method. The results highlight the decrease in
computational complexity and improvements in performance
from the combination. However, the presented method considers
only up to 3 stochastic paths estimated from multiple snapshots,
which is insufficient in many practical scenarios, especially if
paths are modeled as deterministic. More significantly, the used
antenna array geometries have a very small aperture, rendering
the initialization of the Newton method very well conditioned
since it tolerates high deviations of the initialization from the
true solution.
B. Our Contributions
Comparably, we estimate an unknown number of determinis-
tic paths (also known as model order selection) from a single
snapshot of frequency-time data recorded from a Single Input
Single Output (SISO) system. Additionally, we jointly estimate
each path’s propagation parameters, namely delay and Doppler-
shift.1. We use supervised learning, combined with a signal
model to generate synthetic data, where a full groundtruth
for the estimates is available compared to measurement data.

II. SIGNAL MODEL
Our task is to characterize a SISO channel by estimation of
its paths’ propagation parameters. Therefore, we first define
the channel and signal models used to describe the measured
observation recorded by the receiver (Rx).
We assume an underspread channel that can be modeled as
a Linear Time-Variant (LTV) system composed of 1..P ∈N
specular paths, each represented by a planar wave impinging on
the Rx. Each path is parametrized by its magnitude and phase-
shift (jointly expressed as γp ∈C), delay τp ∈R and Doppler-
shift αp ∈R. At the Rx only an electric field proportional to
the superposition of all the planar waves is observed, due to
the channel linearity. Our task is to identify each of the P
specular propagation paths characterizing the frequency and
time impulse response of the LTV channel.
At the Rx, we model the sampled wireless channel transfer-
function of bandwidth B with Nf ∈N frequency samples and
Nt ∈N snapshots under the narrowband assumption (B ≪fc).
The sampling process is characterized by the sampling intervals
in frequency ∆f > 0 and time ∆t > 0 with S sampled

III. NEURAL NETWORK
This section introduces the preprocessing scheme applied
to the data Y , the off-grid parameter encoding used for the
labels, and the CNN architecture.
A. Preprocessing
Our preprocessing stage aims to provide the neural network
with informative and diverse input values via two steps, multi-
windowing and subsequent 2D-DFT.
In the first step, multiple views of the data Y are created
by filtering with NW windows and stacking the results into
YW ∈CNW ×Nf ×Nt. The motivation for the multi-window
approach is to obtain different information from the same
observation. Window functions are typically an application-
specific choice, as different windows create different benefits
and drawbacks. For example, rectangular windows achieve the
maximum SNR and provide a narrow pulse shape but also result
in high sidelobes after the DFT, potentially introducing ghost
paths. Other filters, such as Hann windows, reduce the sidelobes,
i.e., the probability of ghost paths, but increase the mainlobe
width and usually result in higher estimation variances. Hence,
it is recommended to select a Hann window for situations where
paths are sufficiently separated from neighboring paths (that
would otherwise be masked by the wider mainlobe) and have
sufficient SNR. Vice versa, a rectangular window is suitable for
situations in which a narrow mainlobe is required to separate
close paths or the maximum SNR is required, while strong
sidelobes are not a concern. These two cases demonstrate the
choice of the optimal window is always dependent on the
situation at hand, and is only obvious if the result is already
known. To alleviate the dependency on one specific window,
we apply multiple windows in parallel by processing them as
different channels in the CNN, similar to color channels of
images. Consequently, the CNN can jointly utilize the different
views. We used NW = 4 different windowing functions, i.e., a
Tukey, Cosine, Hann, and the Rectangular window.
The second preprocessing step is a 2D-DFT over the two
data dimensions, transforming it to the target parameter domain
in delay- and Doppler, the Fourier domains corresponding to
frequency- and time, respectively. If desired, the DFT output
can be cropped to those parts of the scattering function which
are of interest instead of the full unambiguous range in delay-

C. Network Architecture
Our network architecture is split into two trainable stages,
we loosely term them encoder and refinement, illustrated in
Figure 1. We embed the convolutional layers into blocks,
which each consist of a 2D convolutional layer, followed
by batch normalization and a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)
activation function, denoted as CBA-block in the following.
The encoder reduces the dimensionality of the data through
downsampling. For the downsampling, the 2D convolutional
layers are parameterized with a stride of 2 and a kernel size

The synthetic dataset is split into three subsets, a training,
validation, and test set. Each sample in a dataset contains a

a uniform distribution; magnitudes from a range of 0 dB to
−30 dB and phases from 0 to 2π. Similarly, the corresponding
delay-Doppler parameters are also sampled randomly from
uniform distributions. The additional noise N is generated
randomly with different random noise variance σ for every
snapshot, such that the SNR is in the range of −30 dB to

The postprocessing recovers the estimated parameters from
their encoded form and reconstructs the corresponding complex
path gains ˆγ. This is done by first sorting the ˆµ and keeping
only those exceeding the threshold δ. Applying the inverse of
the grid encoding Equation (5) returns the non-linear parameters

IV. ANALYSIS
We conduct a two-fold evaluation of our approach: first,
by assessing its estimation performance compared to state-
of-the-art algorithms and theoretic bounds, and second, by
examining its applicability to real-world measurement data. To
assess the estimation performance of our approach, we perform
an isolated study with respect to the MSE of the parameter
estimates and the accuracy of the estimated model order. As
these comparisons mandate the availability of a groundtruth,
we perform them on synthetic data from a test dataset. Second,
we apply the approach to previously published measurement
data (see [14]), captured in an anechoic chamber and compare
the results to the available groundtruth where possible.
A. Synthetic Data
To assess the estimation accuracy, we create a random
single path scenario and evaluate it at different SNRs from
−50 dB to 20 dB. For each SNR we average the squared
error in the parameter domain of the estimates from 10 000
different noise realizations to obtain an estimate of the MSE.
The comparison includes the raw estimates obtained from the
CNN and those obtained with the additional gradient-based
optimization. For reference, we provide comparisons with an
iterative ML estimator from [4] and the CRB as a theoretical
lower bound for unbiased estimators.
The results in Figure 4 show that our approach successfully
estimates the delay-Doppler parameters, with the raw and
optimized estimates showing similar performance in low SNR.
At higher SNRs, the raw estimates’ accuracy of our approach
starts to saturate around −20 dB SNR. This indicates that the
approach produces a bias in the estimates, whose source is

B. Measurement Data
So far, the analysis is limited to synthetic data, generated from
the observation model in eq. (3). It allowed an assessment of
the overall feasibility and theoretical performance, but not real-
world performance. Therefore, the question remains: If a model
trained on such synthetic data, is it applicable to measurement
data? And if so, how is its performance affected by adverse
effects, such as imperfect synchronization or additional phase
noise?
To answer these questions, we utilize measurement data
bi-static delay-Doppler emulation measurement in an anechoic
chamber, previously presented in [14] and evaluated in [18]. The
setup features two metallic spheres (12 cm diameter) mounted
at the ends of a 3 m beam rotating at 60 rpm. They are
illuminated in a bi-static setup by a transmitter (TX) and
Rx spaced 2.24 m apart, schematically shown in Figure 6b.
The Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM)-like
signal is transmitted at fc = 5.9 GHz with a bandwidth of
160 MHz and a symbol length of 64 µs. It is modulated with
a Newman sequence providing constant spectral amplitude and
minimal Crest factor [19]. To provide a groundtruth for the
performance evaluation, the sphere positions are recorded by
an angular sensor in the motor. From these positions and the
known rotation speed, the path parameters τ and α of the
spheres can be computed and evaluated against the estimates.
We divide the recorded data of one full rotation of the
spherical targets into consecutive snapshots, each with Nt = 100
successive measurements, to form the 2D data according to
eq. (3). The associated sampling processes result in a Rayleigh
resolution of 6.25 ns in delay and 156.25 Hz in Doppler-shift.
While rotating, the spheres result in two paths with oscillating
delay and Doppler parameters, illustrated by the black lines in
Figure 6a and Figure 6c. Despite the anechoic environment,
the Line of Sight (LOS) at 7.4 ns, motor, and other objects (at
37.5 ns) contribute further static paths, creating a challenging
task for the detection and estimation with our approach.
As before, we use our approach for the joint estimation of
the delay and Doppler-shifts. We utilized the same network
as in section IV-A, including the additional optimization with
the Gauss-Newton scheme during the postprocessing and a
threshold of δ = 0.8 for the model order estimation. Most
notably, this CNN was trained solely on synthetic data and
never exposed to any measurement data during its training.
The plots in Figure 6a and fig. 6c illustrate the estimation
results for one full rotation of both spheres (from motor
angle 0◦to 360◦). It is immediately visible that our approach
can accurately estimate the spheres’ parameters. A notable
exception are motor rotation angles from 90◦to 135◦, where
one sphere is masked by a strong beam reflection, visible
by the linearly increasing estimates in fig. 6c (labeled beam).
Furthermore, other static paths, such as the motor reflection,
are also estimated. Using a groundtruth filter and matching the
remaining estimates to the respective sphere parameters, we
can compute the average RMSE for the parameter estimates of
the spheres. This comes out to 1.1 ns and 30 Hz in Doppler-
shift, and hence, well below the inherent resolution limit of
the original sampling process (illustrated by the y-grid lines in
Figure 6a and Figure 6c), showing super-resolution capabilities.
However, the most significant insight is that despite being trained
solely on synthetic data, the CNN generalized sufficiently well
to be applicable even in measurement applications.

dimensional wireless propagation parameter and model order

especially in the low-SNR regime, already shown in previous

the applicability of synthetically trained CNN to measurement

feature, as it indicates that extensive, site-specific measurement
data collection and labeling are not necessary for the successful
application of Machine Learning algorithms in the field, if
trained carefully.

well to more than two dimensions, as demonstrated in [20].
Ultimately, this could allow processing of full multiple input
multiple output (MIMO) measurements, and hence, also spatially
resolved and polarimetric channel sounding data, similar to [4].
For this, the preprocessing must be extended with realistic
antenna beampatterns by a suitable beamspace transformation
as facilitated by the Effective Aperture Distribution Function
(EADF) [21]. Moreover, an architecture ablation study is
required to quantify the performance impacts of the individual
architecture blocks, possibly alleviating the aforementioned bias,
whose source currently remains unknown. A further opportunity
is in the area of wideband channel estimation, where additional
dispersion in delay and Doppler domains, as well as coupling of
the two domains and violation of the narrowband assumption,
present an even more challenging task.
