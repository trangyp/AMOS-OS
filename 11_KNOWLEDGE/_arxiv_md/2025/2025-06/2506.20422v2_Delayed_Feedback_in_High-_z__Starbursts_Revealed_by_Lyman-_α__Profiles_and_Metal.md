Delayed Feedback in High-z Starbursts Revealed by Lyman-α Profiles and Metal Line Diagnostics
James Nianias
,1 Jeremy Lim
,1 Yik Lok Wong,2 and Gordon Wong1
1Department of Physics, University of Hong Kong, Pokfulam Road, Hong Kong
2Department of Physics and Astronomy, The University of Manchester, Oxford Road, Manchester M13 9PL, UK
ABSTRACT
Lyman-α emission, which owing to its resonant nature strongly couples the emergent line profile
to gas kinematics, is a key observable for probing outflows from star-forming galaxies in the early
universe. Inferences of outflow properties from Lyman-α, however, often lack contextual comparisons
with more direct outflow diagnostics from down-the-barrel metal absorption lines and driving-source
properties from metal emission lines. Here, we make such checks by taking advantage of the lens-
ing magnification provided by galaxy clusters for 338 Lyman-α sources observed with the Multi-Unit
Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE). Using metal emission lines to measure systemic redshifts, we confirm
that the Lyman-α profiles are consistent with outflowing gas: single peaks redshifted relative to, or
double peaks straddling, the systemic redshift. In cases where metal absorption lines are detected,
blueshifted velocities indicate outflows, while line ratios point to absorption by a clumpy medium. We
find systematic differences in both metal absorption and emission lines associated with single- versus
double-peaked Lyman-α profiles, such that the latter are preferentially associated with weaker and nar-
rower metal absorption profiles, but stronger emission lines indicating younger stellar ages (≲4 Myr
for double-peaked Lyman-α vs ≳10 Myr for single-peaked Lyman-α). Double-peaked Lyman-α pro-
files may therefore reflect weaker feedback in extremely young starbursts due to the delayed onset of
core-collapse supernovae. Fitting model Lyman-α profiles based on simple expanding shell geometry
to those observed, we find that such models successfully reproduce the data, yet systematically overes-
timate systemic redshifts and yield unphysical parameters – calling for caution when inferring outflow
properties from such models.
Keywords: stellar feedback; early universe; outflows; galactic winds
1. INTRODUCTION
Feedback from star formation is believed to play a
pivotal role in regulating galaxy evolution, particu-
larly in the early universe where intense starbursts can
drive powerful outflows that suppress further star for-
mation and eject metals into the circumgalactic medium
(CGM) and/or intergalactic medium (IGM). The most
widely used tracer of outflows in the early universe
is blueshifted absorption from gas accelerated along
the line of sight (“down-the-barrel” absorption), which
provides unambiguous evidence of outflowing material.
Rest-frame UV absorption lines (accessible by ground-
based observatories at z ≳2) have been used to diag-

Jones et al. 2012), and Lyman-α (Lyα)-emitting galaxies
(Berry et al. 2012). However, detections of such absorp-
tion signatures in individual high-z galaxies are limited
to a relatively small number of highly luminous systems
(e.g.
Pettini et al. 2002, Shibuya et al. 2014), which
are unlikely to be representative of typical star-forming
galaxies (SFGs) in the early universe.
To search for outflows in individual low-luminosity
SFGs in the early universe, observers have instead
turned to the Lyα emission line. Due to the abundant
Lyα photons produced in star forming regions via re-
processing of extreme UV radiation (λ < 912 ˚A) from
young stars, even SFGs that are extremely faint in
the continuum can be detected via the Lyα line (Par-
tridge & Peebles 1967).
Moreover, Lyα’s large scat-

that the emergent line profile is strongly coupled to the
kinematics of the interstellar medium (ISM) and CGM,
and hence encodes information about outflows (see re-
views by Erb 2015 and Ouchi et al. 2020). Most Lyα-
emitting galaxies display asymmetric Lyα peaks that
are redshifted relative to the systemic redshift of the
host galaxy (where measured), sometimes accompanied
by additional weaker blueshifted peaks (e.g. Kulas et al.
2012, Trainor et al. 2015, Rivera-Thorsen et al. 2015).
These distinctive line profiles can be reproduced by ra-

shells/halos of gas (e.g.

2006, Orsi et al. 2012, Duval et al. 2014), thus provid-

Fitting such models to observed Lyα profiles provides
estimates of outflow parameters such as expansion ve-
locity and neutral column density (Gronke et al. 2015;
Orlitov´a et al. 2018; Gurung-L´opez et al. 2019, 2022),
but is fraught with degeneracies and uncertainties owing
to the complexity of radiative transfer in the Lyα line
(Gronke et al. 2016; Li & Gronke 2022). Comparisons
between Lyα lines and down-the-barrel absorption lines
– that could be used to better understand the impact
of outflow properties on the emergent Lyα line profile –
are lacking, particularly in the early universe.
In this paper, we study a large sample (N = 338)
of gravitationally-lensed Lyα-emitting galaxies observed
with the Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE; Ba-
con et al. 2010) and cataloged by Richard et al. (2021)
(R21 henceforth). Drawing on MUSE observations of 12
galaxy clusters, with accompanying deep Hubble Space
Telescope (HST) observations, R21 uncovered a large
number of Lyα-emitting galaxies at 2.9 < z < 6.7.
The intrinsic (i.e.
un-lensed) morphologies of these
sources were subsequently studied in further detail by
Claeyssens et al. (2022). Thanks to the magnification
provided by gravitational lensing, some of these ob-
jects are among the faintest and most compact galax-
ies yet seen in the early universe (see, e.g., Vanzella
et al. 2017; Meˇstri´c et al. 2022; Messa et al. 2024; Iani
et al. 2024). The boost in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
provided by gravitational lensing also enables the de-
tection of faint spectral features that may otherwise be
undetected: UV nebular emission lines (besides Lyα)
and gas-phase metal absorption lines that may originate
from the ISM, CGM, and/or outflows. We analyze the
spectra of these objects to search for evidence of outflows
by (i) using optically-thin UV emission lines (where de-
tected) to trace their systemic velocities, against which
the Lyα profiles can be compared, and (ii) searching
for evidence of blueshifted metal absorption lines from
outflowing gas. We then investigate the relationship be-
tween metal absorption profiles and emergent Lyα pro-
files, developing an empirically-driven understanding of
how to interpret Lyα profiles in terms of outflow proper-
ties. Comparing Lyα profiles with metal emission lines
from H II regions as well as P-Cygni profiles from mas-
sive young stars, we investigate how outflows are affected
by the evolution of the stellar populations that drive
them. We complement this analysis by fitting expanding
shell RT models to the Lyα profiles, testing the ability
of such idealized models to infer outflow properties by
comparing them against the observables obtained from
metal emission and absorption lines.
In Section 2.1, we provide details about the catalogs
of R21, on which our analysis is based. In Section 2, we
provide basic details of the MUSE observations, from
which we extract our spectra, and provide details of the
fitting routines and flagging procedures that we use to
detect and verify emission/absorption lines. In Section
3, we present the results of our fitting, which suggest
that clumpy, multiphase outflows are widespread, if not
ubiquitous, among high-z Lyα-emitting galaxies.
We
also show – for the first time – evidence for strength-
ening feedback within the first ∼10 Myr of an episode
of star formation, presumably due to the onset of su-
pernova explosions (SNe). We then go on to fit homo-
geneous expanding shell RT models to the Lyα profiles
and assess their success in reproducing the observed out-
flow properties as revealed by metal absorption lines.
We find that, while these models can generally repro-
duce the Lyα profiles well, the fitted parameters are in
many cases unphysical, which can likely be attributed
to discrepancies between homogeneous thin shell models
and real outflow geometry. We provide a discussion of
our findings and interpretation in Section 4. In Section
5, we briefly summarize our main findings. Through-
out this paper, we adopt a flat ΛCDM cosmology with
H0 = 68 km s−1 Mpc−1 and ΩM = 0.31 (Planck Collab-
oration et al. 2014).

2.3. Sample Selection and Spectrum Extraction
We selected all sources from the R21 catalogs that
have reported Lyα SNR greater than 3.0.
To better
confine the spectra to the brightest continuum regions
of the respective sources, where features such as metal
absorption lines and optically-thin emission lines may be
more easily detected, we extracted spectra from aper-
tures rather than the entire segmentation map of each
source as in R21. We set the aperture diameter to be
2× the FWHM of the point-spread function (PSF) of the
corresponding MUSE cube at 7000 ˚A as listed in R21.
We placed the apertures at the source positions listed in
the R21 catalogs and extracted 1D spectra along with
uncertainties using MPDAF. This resulted in 842 spectra
in total.
The sources exhibit a wide range of Lyα spectral
profiles as well as Lyα spatial morphologies, some of
which are clearly distorted by strong gravitational lens-
ing. The metal emission lines, on the other hand, gener-
ally appear to arise from more compact, often spatially-
unresolved regions (justifying our approach of extract-
ing spectra from tighter apertures rather than the entire
segmentation map). This is unsurprising given that the
Lyα halos often extend far beyond the UV continuum of
the associated object (Claeyssens et al. 2022), though in
some cases the apparent compactness of the metal emis-
sion regions may also be due to poor SNR. We show
examples illustrating the range of Lyα spatial morphol-
ogy and spectral profiles, as well as the compactness of
the metal emission line sources in Figure 1.

2.5. Flagging potential contaminants
Following fitting, we cleaned the results of potential
contamination from lower-redshift objects.
This can
happen either due to (i) the misidentification of the
[O II]λλ3726, 3729 ˚A doublet for Lyα in the original cat-
alogs of R21, or (ii) contamination of the spectrum of
a genuine Lyα source by emission/absorption lines from
foreground galaxies. To identify cases wherein the [O II]
doublet masquerades as double-peaked Lyα emission, we
first searched for putative Lyα profiles that are indistin-
guishable from [O II] both in the ratio of the central
wavelengths (which is 3728.8/3726.1 = 1.0007 for the
[O II] doublet) as well as the ratio of the red to blue peak
(which can vary between 0.35 and 1.5 for the [O II] dou-
blet, depending on the gas density). If neither of these
values was more than 3σ away from the corresponding
value (or range) for the [O II] doublet, we then checked
for additional spectral lines that could confirm the red-
shift of the source independently of the Lyα line. If no
such lines existed, the spectrum was flagged and not in-
cluded in subsequent analysis. In total we identified just
four potential [O II] contaminants in this way.

low all Lyα emitters in our sample) whose centroids fall
within 10.0′′ of each source. We then identified which

target source to estimate how much flux is contributed
by the potential contaminating object within the aper-
ture used to extract the target spectrum. We then used
this value to normalize the spectrum3 of the potential
contaminating object and hence estimate the degree of
spectral contamination at the position of the target. In
the final step, we determined whether the potential con-

2.6. Identifying and Aggregating Lensed Counterparts
To maximize the SNR of our spectra, as well as to
prevent repeated entries in our spectral catalogs, we ag-
gregated together spectra from multiply-lensed images
of the same source. We did this by comparing the Lyα
profiles of our extracted spectra: if two or more spectra
from the same cluster were found to have Lyα profiles
that were not significantly different (i.e. having all fitted
parameters including peak wavelengths, i.e. redshifts,
within 3σ of each other) then we assumed them to be-
long to the same source and group them together. Due
to the very diverse range of Lyα profiles and typically
small uncertainty in Lyα peak redshift (≲10−4), it is
unlikely that two unrelated systems could be matched
by this procedure. In each matched group, we then dis-
carded any spectra with emission or absorption lines
that were previously identified as likely contaminated
(see Section 2.5) to prevent the contamination being
carried forward to the aggregated spectrum. Next, we
took the sum of all the spectra in each group and re-
fitted all spectral lines using the exact same procedure
as described in Section 2.4, except now using the fit-
ted parameters from the initial round of fitting as initial
guesses for lines previously detected at > 3σ. We found
that this averaging had a mildly positive impact on the
detection rate of faint spectral features overall (as com-
pared with simply discarding the lensed counterparts
and retaining only that with the highest magnification),
with 16 newly detected lines at 5σ against 6 lost, and
5 newly detected blue Lyα peaks against 3 lost. After
accounting for multiply-lensed images in this way, we
were left with 639 spectra.
Next, we removed from our results all those spectra
that could not be confidently identified as true Lyα emit-
ters. Those excluded have low-confidence spectral red-
shifts according to R21 (those with redshift confidence
below the highest rating of 3), did not show statisti-
cally significant asymmetry in our fitted Lyα profiles (i.e
the asymmetry parameter a was within 3σ of zero), and
furthermore had fewer than two high-confidence (> 4σ)
detections of other spectral lines. This left us with 524
spectra in total.
Inevitably, there are a few cases of multiple spectra
that come from the same object but are not grouped
together by our procedure to identify lensed counter-
parts. This can happen when a single lensed image is
highly spatially extended and/or clumpy, in which case
there can be multiple catalog entries from R21 point-
ing to different parts of the same lensed image. Alter-
natively, the apertures placed on different lensed im-
ages of the same source may cover different parts of
the target due to differences in the lensed morpholo-
gies/magnifications of each image. When there is suf-
ficient variation in Lyα profiles across the source, the
resulting fitted Lyα profiles can have statistically sig-
nificant differences. After aggregating our spectra, we
cleared any such remaining repeated entries by simply
removing spectra from the same cluster with Lyα peak
wavelengths within 300 km s−1 of one another (retaining
whichever spectrum has the higher total SNR in emis-
sion plus absorption lines, or, if none are detected, the
higher SNR in Lyα). This left us with 338 spectra in
total.

3.2.2. Lyα vs Metal Absorption Line Profiles
We now turn our attention to developing an empirical
understanding of how outflow properties (as traced by
metal absorption lines) influence Lyα profiles. Here, we
show that, when compared to DP sources, SP sources
host outflows with greater neutral gas covering fraction
and (likely) faster and/or more turbulent outflows.
To understand the relationship between Lyα profiles
and outflow properties without resorting to idealized ex-
panding shell models, we generated stacked metal ab-
sorption line profiles for SP and DP sources. To allow
us to distinguish between the predominantly ionized and
neutral gas phases, we made separate stacks for low-
and high-ionization metal absorption lines.
To make
the low-ionization stacks, we used the Si IIλ1260 and
Si IIλ1527 lines – we also tried adding the C IIλ1334
line into the stack, but this reduced the overall SNR
of the absorption profiles (qualitatively our conclusions
remain unchanged regardless of whether we include
C IIλ1334, albeit at lower statistical significance). To
make the high-ionization stacks, we used both lines of
the Si IVλλ1394, 1403 doublet. We selected sources to
be included in these stacks based on the following crite-
ria:
(i) We considered only those 45 spectra where the sys-
temic redshifts had been measured with optically-
thin emission lines, as without these measurements
it is impossible to align the spectra in velocity
space and obtain meaningful kinematic results.
(ii) We found the best SNR in the absorption pro-
files was obtained by including only those spec-
tra with 1σ uncertainties in systemic velocity <
50 km s−1, a criterion that only excluded 4 sources,
and weighting each spectrum in the stack by in-
verse median variance.
(iii) We also excluded any SP sources with z > 4.0, as
these may be affected by IGM attenuation blue-

cluding:
(i) Excluding the three SP sources with the strongest

nificance of the SP low-ionization absorption to
< 3σ, though the results remain consistent with
those of the original stacks).

(iii) Including sources without measured systemic red-

proxy for the systemic: while the resulting line

unreliable, the total integrated absorption still re-

line widths – and hence faster/more turbulent outflows

greater neutral column densities and faster/more turbu-

3.2.3. Lyα Profiles vs Stellar/ISM Properties
With a clear understanding of how outflow properties
influence emergent Lyα profiles, we now search for dif-
ferences in stellar/ISM properties of SP and DP sources,
again making use of metal emission lines.
Based on
stellar population/photoionization modeling of emission
lines and P-Cygni features from stellar winds, we show
that DP sources host younger stellar populations com-
pared with SP sources, the two source types having
characteristic ages of ≲4 Myr and ≳15 Myr, respec-
tively. Considering the slower, less massive outflows in
DP sources discussed in Section 3.2.2, we propose a pic-
ture in which delayed onset of core-collapse SNe gives
rise to weaker feedback in extremely young (i.e. DP)
sources.
In Section 3.2.1, we presented evidence that DP
sources tend to have greater metal emission line EWs
as compared with SP sources (see Figure 9). To scru-
tinize this relationship in greater detail, we generated
stacked profiles for the following emission lines from SP
and DP sources: C III]λλ1907, 1909, C IVλλ1548, 1551,
O III]λλ1661, 1666, He IIλ1640, Si III]λλ1883, 1892, and
N Vλλ1238, 1243. To enhance overall SNR of the stacks,
we included only spectra with highly significant Lyα de-
tections (Lyα SNR ≳20). As in the stacked absorp-
tion lines in Section 3.2.2, we excluded any SP sources
with z < 4, which may be intrinsically DP sources af-
fected by IGM attenuation bluewards of the systemic
redshift. We included sources both with and without
measured systemic velocities from optically-thin emis-
sion lines.
We prefer this blind stacking to choosing
only sources with emission lines, as the latter is biased
towards the youngest systems with the most extreme
ionization conditions. We aligned the spectra in veloc-
ity space using the redshift of the Lyα peak as a proxy
for the systemic redshift. Because the offset of the Lyα
relative to the systemic varies from source to source,
the metal emission lines thus stacked are imperfectly
aligned, or scattered. To characterize the extent of this
scattering effect, we checked the velocity offsets of the
Lyα lines in the 45 spectra with high-confidence metal
emission lines. Figure 11 shows the distribution in off-
sets, which ranges between ∼0 and ∼300 km s−1, and
shows no difference for SP and DP sources. While this
scatter affects the shape of the stacked lines – making
them wider – the integrated emission line flux and con-
tinuum level still reflect the average for each sub-sample,
allowing us to compare the lines from SP and DP sources
in terms of EW.
The stacked emission line profiles are shown in
Figure 12, clearly demonstrating a significantly en-
hanced emission line EW in DP sources compared to
SP sources in C III]λλ1907, 1909, C IVλλ1548, 1551,
O III]λλ1661, 1666, and He IIλ1640 (in agreement with
the results from individual spectra shown in Figure 9).
We measured the EW of these lines by fitting gaussian
profiles and using bootstrapping to obtain uncertainties.
Though no significant nebular emission can be seen in
the N Vλλ1238, 1243 stacked spectra (see Figure 12(a)),
they instead show broad P-Cygni features, with the DP
stack having the more pronounced of the two (relative
to the continuum level). This feature arises from stel-
lar winds from massive stars, and has been observed in
the spectra of SFGs at both low and intermediate red-
shift (Chisholm et al. 2019). Moreover, Chisholm et al.
(2019) show that this feature weakens with increasing
stellar population age, disappearing altogether within a
few Myr, making it a sensitive probe of stellar popula-
tion age.
We find qualitatively similar results to those shown
in Figure 12 when stacking only sources with systemic
redshifts traced by metal emission lines, but with a re-
duced contrast between SP and DP stacks (as may be
expected due to the aforementioned bias towards the
youngest systems when considering only those with de-
tectable metal emission lines).

Age:
The lower age ascribed to the DP spectra
can be attributed to two factors.
Firstly, the higher
emission line EWs, particularly the He IIλ1640 and
C IVλλ1548, 1551 lines, require more EUV photons,
which are only produced by the most massive stars that
live for just a few Myr. Secondly, the deeper P-Cygni
profile in the DP spectrum suggests a higher proportion
of extremely young massive stars (with ages ≲4 Myr)
relative to slightly older stars (with ages 10 – 60 Myr).
Comparing this result with the weaker and narrower
metal absorption lines in DP sources suggests that ex-
tremely young starbursts drive slower/less turbulent and
less massive outflows. Moreover, the difference in age
between SP and DP sources aligns remarkably well with
the expected onset of SNe feedback, which can only be-
gin after the most massive stars begin to undergo core-
collapse at ∼4 Myr (see, e.g., Woosley et al. 2002; Lei-
therer et al. 2014). This suggests that outflows in DP
sources may be driven primarily by early SNe from rare
very massive stars or even pre-SNe feedback mechanisms
such as stellar winds and radiation pressure. Once SNe
feedback begins in earnest after ∼10 Myr (Kim et al.
2020, Hopkins et al. 2023), outflows become more mas-
sive, faster, and more turbulent (greater ISM turbulence
being a well-studied consequence of SNe shocks – see
Mac Low & Klessen 2004, Klessen & Glover 2016), which
in turn tends to give rise to SP Lyα profiles.

The posteriors in log U ten-
tatively favor a higher ionization parameter for DP
sources.

specifically the ionization parameter evaluated at the
Str¨omgren radius, and thus primarily reflects the geom-
etry of the ionized gas, with larger values correspond-
ing to more compact H II regions closer to the ioniz-
ing source. Hence lower U in SP sources may also be
driven by strengthening feedback due to the onset of
core-collapse SNe, which disrupt the ISM surrounding
star-forming regions and drive it outward.
Dust depletion factor: Our models tentatively favor a
higher dust depletion in DP relative to SP sources, likely
due to the relatively weak stacked Si III]λλ1883, 1892
emission in DP sources. The weak Si III]λλ1883, 1892 in
DP sources be related to their very young ages, which
may not allow sufficient time for dispersal of Si into the
ISM via SNe.

For each

We take the median

approximate 1σ uncertainties.
In most cases, we find good agreement between the
best-fit models found by zELDA and the observed spec-
tra, with a median reduced-χ2 of 1.32 and only 14 cases
with reduced-χ2 > 3.
Nevertheless, these poorly-fit
cases are valuable to understanding the limitations of
idealized HTS models.
We break the poor fits down
into three categories:
1. Cases where the Lyα line is qualitatively well-
reproduced, but due to extreme SNR the deviation
between the model and spectrum is highly signifi-
cant (four cases). An example is shown in Figure
14(a).
2. Cases where the model fails to qualitatively re-
produce the spectrum – this happens either when
there is a blue Lyα peak which the model does
not reproduce, when the model includes a blue
peak not seen in the actual spectrum, and/or when
the model includes a blueshifted absorption com-
ponent not seen in the spectrum (six cases). An
example is shown in Figure 14b.
3. Cases affected by systematics – significant fluctu-
ations in the spectrum beyond the spectral uncer-
tainties, likely due to sky lines (four cases). An
example is shown in Figure 14c.
Cases in category 1 demonstrate that, when SNR is
sufficiently high, deviations between HTS models and
real Lyα profiles can become apparent, as may be ex-
pected for any idealised model. It is worth noting here
that there are also four cases with SNR > 100 among the
good fits with reduced-χ2 < 2, showing that some Lyα
profiles are reproduced by HTS models with remarkably
high precision. Cases in category 2 are unusual in that
the continuum level surrounding the Lyα line is high –
all but one are in the top 10% of the sample in terms of
continuum flux density. These spectra may suffer from
contamination by foreground cluster members, render-
ing it impossible to simultaneously reproduce the Lyα
and continuum level. We exclude sources from all three
categories from further analysis, leaving us with 255 fit-
ted Lyα profiles for which reduced-χ2 < 3.

to underestimate the velocity offset of the red Lyα peak
relative to the systemic (i.e. placing the systemic too
close to the red Lyα peak in velocity space). This raises
three major potential problems:
(i) The inferred outflow kinematics may be inaccu-
rate, since the proximity of the redshifted peak
relative to the systemic scales with the expansion
velocity (e.g. Verhamme et al. 2006).
(ii) If there are also metal absorption lines present,
their blueshift relative to the systemic velocity will
be exaggerated, leading to overestimation of out-
flow velocities.
(iii) The amount of Lyα emission at the systemic ve-
locity may be overestimated. This has important
consequences, as it has been found that – at low
redshift – systemic Lyα emission can be used as
a proxy for LyC escape (Verhamme et al. 2015).
Any future studies employing this relationship and
wishing to use expanding shell models to estimate
systemic redshifts may consequently overestimate
LyC leakage.
The same over-estimation of systemic redshift has also
been reported by Orlitov´a et al. (2018), who fit similar
expanding shell models to low-redshift Green Pea galax-
ies.
4. DISCUSSION
4.1. Evidence of Delayed SNe Feedback
Our modeling of stacked emission lines from SP and
DP sources in Section 3.2.3 suggests that starbursts in
DP sources tend to be younger, while at the same time
also showing weaker outflow signatures in metal absorp-
tion lines as shown in Section 3.2.2. We interpret this as
evidence of weaker feedback in the first ∼4 Myr of star
formation, the time scale aligning well with expectations
of the onset of SNe feedback from the most massive stars
(see Woosley et al. 2002; Leitherer et al. 2014 for de-
tails of the relevant stellar evolutionary models, Agertz
et al. 2013; Hopkins et al. 2023 for implementation into
galaxy simulations). Moreover, recent studies of young
star clusters in nearby galaxies with JWST NIRCam and
MIRI find a similar time scale of ∼3 Myr for the dis-
ruption of photo-dissociation regions by stellar feedback
(Pedrini et al. 2024). Of course, such young clusters are
far less massive than the galaxies in our sample (103 –
104 M⊙vs 106 M⊙or more). However, the contrast in
physical size is much less – the nearby young star clus-
ters can be a few tens of pc in size, similar to some of
the smaller objects in our sample (see (Claeyssens et al.
2022)) – suggesting that feedback timescales may also
be similar.
Similar trends to those we find here have been re-
ported in low-z Lyα-emitting galaxies by Hayes et al.
(2023), who find that galaxies with greater Lyα EW
tend to have younger stellar ages and weaker low-
ionization metal absorption lines. However, they con-
clude that a greater abundance of ionizing photons from
very young stars may be responsible for this correla-
tion by simultaneously powering greater Lyα emission
and ionizing the ISM, thus reducing the neutral col-
umn density along the line of sight to the observer.
While this may indeed partially explain the associa-
tion between young stellar age and low neutral column
density, it does not explain why younger sources (i.e.
DP sources) drive slower/less turbulent outflows as sug-
gested by our stacked metal absorption lines, as well
as RT models more generally.
Moreover, we do not
find any evidence of enhanced high-ionization absorp-
tion from Si IVλλ1394, 1403 in DP sources as may be
expected if the difference is merely in ionization state of
the ISM. Rather, our analysis suggests a relationship be-
tween stellar population age and ISM kinematics, possi-
bly complementary to any relationship between age and
ISM ionization.
Another factor that may influence the relationship be-
tween age and outflow rate is galaxy mass. Lower-mass
SFGs will tend to have lower neutral column density
simply by virtue of having lower gas mass, drive slower
outflows due to lower escape velocities, and yet may
also undergo more bursty star formation and hence have
younger average ages. Burstiness is also driven primarily
by the timescale of SNe feedback, with the shortest fea-
sible burst being set by the age at which SNe feedback
kicks. In the extreme scenario in which SNe feedback
halts star formation completely, star formation episodes
in DP sources may simply not survive past ∼4 Myr
in most cases (as opposed to a scenario in which DP
sources evolve into SP as feedback strengthens).

check whether SP sources may indeed be more massive
than DP sources, we compared the distributions in lu-

the emergent Lyα profile. Radiation-hydrodynamic sim-

to anisotropic neutral column density/ISM kinematics.
While viewing angle likely contributes considerable scat-
ter to the relationship between age and interstellar ab-
sorption line profiles, its effect may be mitigated some-
what by the generally low masses of our target sources
(∼106 −109 M⊙; see, e.g., Iani et al. 2024), which

(e.g. Strickland & Heckman 2009). Incidentally, these
same simulations show a similar relationship between
Lyα profile and down-the-barrel absorption profiles as
we find here, with sources with relatively weak blue Lyα
peaks having broader and more strongly blueshifted ab-
sorption profiles.
Finally, we consider the impact of stellar evolution-
ary tracks on our results. The stellar population tem-
plates of Bruzual & Charlot (2003) used by default by
BEAGLE do not include the effects of binary evolution,
which may influence the ages we infer in our model star
formation histories in Section 3.2.3. However, as shown
by Chisholm et al. (2019), binarity seems to accelerate
the decay of the N Vλλ1238, 1243 P-Cygni profile that
is the strongest indicator of an age difference between
SP and DP sources. Consequently, the inclusion of bi-
nary evolution is unlikely to have any qualitative effect
on our inference of younger stellar ages in DP sources.
5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
We analyzed the spectra of gravitationally-lensed Lyα
emitters spectroscopically identified by R21. We fitted
analytic profiles to the Lyα lines as well as a number
of rest-frame UV metal emission and absorption lines
from both high- and low-ionization species.
We then
generated stacks of these emission and absorption lines
from spectra with and without detected blue Lyα peaks.
We find that
(i) These sources show spectral signatures suggesting
near ubiquitous outflows.
Single Lyα peaks are
redshifted relative to the systemic velocity as mea-
sured using metal emission lines, or, when there is
also a blue peak, the two peaks straddle the sys-
temic. metal absorption lines from both low- and
high-ionization species are blueshifted relative to
the systemic redshift in most cases where the latter
can be measured (8/10).
(ii) Both low- and high-ionization absorption lines ex-
hibit line ratios indicative of saturation, suggesting
that the outflows consist of optically thick clumps
of neutral gas as well as warm ionized gas with lim-
ited covering fractions. The sometimes-saturated
nature of the high-ionization absorption lines is
particularly interesting as it suggests clumps of
warm ionized gas embedded in a still hotter phase.
(iii) Sources with double-peaked Lyα lines have weaker
low- and high-ionization absorption lines relative
to those with single-peaks, suggesting they are as-
sociated with less massive outflows.
They also


## Page 25


25
show smaller absorption line widths than single-
peaked sources, suggesting slower/less turbulent
outflows.
Taken together, the weaker and nar-
rower absorption in double-peaked sources sug-
gests significantly weaker stellar feedback.
(iv) At the same time, double-peaked Lyα sources also
show nebular metal emission lines indicative of ex-
tremely young ages (just a few Myr), while single-
peaked Lyα sources are characterized by ages ≳
10 Myr. This suggests a picture in which outflows
strengthen significantly as SNe feedback builds in
intensity, consistent with theoretical expectations.
(v) Though qualitative agreement between Lyα pro-
files and homogeneous expanding shell RT mod-
els is good, the models return unphysical out-
flow parameters (at least when not constrained
by any priors) and tend to overestimate systemic
redshifts. Results obtained by fitting such simple
models should be taken with a grain of salt: even
excellent goodness-of-fit is no guarantee of physi-
cally meaningful fitted parameters.
Our most salient result – that stellar feedback appears
to strengthen dramatically after ∼4 Myr – provides a
critical empirical constraint on feedback timescales in
young galaxies.
This timescale, likely tied to the on-
set of core-collapse supernovae, has significant implica-
tions for models of early galaxy formation, where the
efficiency and timing of feedback govern predictions for
star formation, gas ejection, and chemical enrichment.
While our findings suggest a feedback-driven transition,
two scenarios remain plausible: (i) DP sources evolve
into SP systems as SNe-driven outflows develop, or (ii)
SP sources represent more massive systems capable of
sustaining longer star formation episodes. JWST imag-
ing of these lensing clusters – already available for sev-
eral targets – may soon resolve this degeneracy through
robust stellar mass estimates.

A. AUTOREGRESSION MODEL FOR FITTING ABSORPTION LINES
When fitting gaussian profiles to search for absorption lines, we found a high proportion of cases were affected by
correlated noise. To eliminate spurious fits, we included the effect of autocorrelation when generating uncertainties
via bootstrapping. We estimated the autocorrelation length of each spectrum as the maximum pair-wise displacement
between spectral channels where the measured autocorrelation function was greater than 0.33. We then generated
correlated bootstrapped uncertainties using an autoregression model where the strength of the dependence between
adjacent channels scales with the autocorrelation length:
ϵn+1 = ϵne−2/lcorr
where ϵn is the uncertainty in the nth spectral channel, and lcorr is the estimates autocorrelation length of the spectrum.
