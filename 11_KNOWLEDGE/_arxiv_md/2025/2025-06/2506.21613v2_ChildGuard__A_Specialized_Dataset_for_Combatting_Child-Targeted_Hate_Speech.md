Abstract
Hate speech targeting children on social me-
dia is a serious and growing problem, yet cur-
rent NLP systems struggle to detect it effec-
tively. This gap exists mainly because exist-
ing datasets focus on adults, lack age-specific
labels, miss nuanced linguistic cues, and are
often too small for robust modeling. To address
this, we introduce ChildGuard, the first large-
scale English dataset dedicated to hate speech
aimed at children. It contains 351,877 anno-
tated examples from X (formerly Twitter), Red-
dit, and YouTube, labeled by three age groups:
younger children (under 11), pre-teens (11–12),
and teens (13–17). The dataset is split into two
subsets for fine-grained analysis: a contextual
subset (157K) focusing on discourse-level fea-
tures, and a lexical subset (194K) emphasizing
word-level sentiment and vocabulary. Bench-
marking state-of-the-art hate speech models on
ChildGuard reveals notable drops in perfor-
mance, highlighting the challenges of detecting
child-directed hate speech. Data is available at:
https://github.com/rafali25/ChildGua

nicity, and disabilities (see Figure 1). Unlike adults,
children lack the emotional resilience and cogni-

Overview of the Dataset
ChildGuard is a large-scale dataset developed
to detect hate speech targeting children.
It in-
cludes three cognitively distinct age groups—teens
(13–17), pre-teens (11–12), and younger children
(below 11)—informed by developmental and be-
havioral literature (Gaillard et al., 2021).
The
dataset was created entirely from scratch by collect-
ing raw online text, filtering it using custom key-
word rules, and manually annotating each instance.
Each entry in ChildGuard is annotated with a bi-
nary label indicating the presence or absence of
hate speech. The contextual and full corpus subsets
additionally include a multiclass label specifying
the age group of the child target, while the lexical
subset excludes age labels to focus on linguistic
features alone. The dataset is structured into three
parts: a lexical subset (157K entries), a contextual
subset (194K), and a unified corpus (351K) with
complete annotations. This organization supports
binary classification as the primary task, while also
enabling age-aware analysis when needed.

X (207K), and YouTube (140K). These platforms
were selected based on their high usage among
minors and the accessibility of public data. No
pre-annotated or previously labeled datasets were
reused.

3.2
Dataset Filtration
After initial keyword-driven collection, we applied
a dedicated multi-stage filtering pipeline to elim-
inate irrelevant, spammy, non-English, and dupli-
cate entries. Language filtering was performed us-
ing the langdetect15 Python library to retain only
English-language content. Duplicate removal was
based on SHA-25616 hash comparisons of normal-
ized text strings. Spam detection was implemented
through a rule-based system that flagged posts con-
taining excessive repetition (e.g., repeating phrases
more than 3 times), unnatural punctuation patterns
(e.g., overuse of special symbols), or known spam
trigger phrases (e.g., "click here", "subscribe now").
Additionally, comments below a minimum length
threshold (10 tokens) or those dominated by URLs
or emojis (>70% of content) were excluded. The
entire filtering process was automated via Python
scripts and executed in batches, without human-in-
the-loop annotation. Post-filtering (see Table 3),
we retained 469,910 high-quality instances suitable
for downstream annotation: Reddit (179,993), X

3.3
Annotations
All annotations in the ChildGuard dataset were
manually performed by a team of three authors
with prior experience in linguistic annotation and
hate speech research. This direct involvement en-
sured consistency in labeling criteria, contextual
interpretation, and better handling of subtle and am-
biguous cases. Each instance was annotated with
three attributes (see Figure 2). Keywords were se-
lected through prior literature ((Waseem and Hovy,
2016; Davidson et al., 2017; Vidgen and Derczyn-
ski, 2020)):
• Hate Speech Label (Binary): Each sample
was labeled as hateful (1) or non-hateful (0),
based on the presence of derogatory, threat-
ening, or hostile language targeting minors.
Annotation decisions were informed by a re-
fined subset of the collected keyword list. Ex-
amples of hate-indicative language included
phrases such as “brat”, “go play with toys”,
“childish”, “babyface”, and “kiddie” used in
demeaning or aggressive contexts. If hate
presence could not be reliably inferred, the
instance was excluded.
• Age Group Label (Multiclass): The target
child’s inferred age was annotated as one
of three groups: Younger Children (below
11), Pre-teens (11–12), and Teens (13–17).
Lexical markers were used as primary cues:
mentions of “toddler”, “diaper”, “nursery”,
“preschool”, “playschool” indicated Younger
Children; terms like “schoolgirl”, “play-
ground”, “children”, “minor” informed Pre-
teens; while references such as “teenager”,
“school”, “high school”, “childish” suggested
Teen-aged targets. Samples with insufficient
age-disambiguating context were discarded.
• Lexical vs.
Contextual Tag:
This tag
identified whether the hate expression was
explicit—clearly conveyed through direct
slurs or offensive terms (e.g., “worthless
brat”, “ugly kid”, “idiot baby”), or im-
plicit—expressed through sarcasm, demean-
ing tone, or coded phrasing (e.g., “go play
with toys”, “little one should stay offline”,
“child acting tough”). If hate style could not
be discerned, the instance was excluded.
To ensure reliability, each instance in the dataset
was independently labeled by two authors. In cases
of disagreement, the third author reviewed the sam-
ple and made the final decision. Before large-scale
annotation, we conducted a calibration phase us-
ing a small pilot set of 1,000 instances. This pilot
helped refine the annotation guidelines (see Sec-
tion A.1), align interpretation across annotators,
and reduce subjectivity in edge cases.
The main annotation process was carried out in
batches of 10,000–15,000 instances over a period
of four weeks. Inter-annotator agreement was mea-

rule-based filtering using regular expressions and
heuristic rules (e.g., flagging explicit labels that
lacked any offensive lexical markers) to detect am-
biguous or low-confidence entries. All flagged in-
stances were reviewed and removed if label reli-
ability could not be established. After deduplica-
tion and final validation, the finalized ChildGuard
dataset comprises 351,877 high-quality annotated
instances.

3.4
Anonymity
Anonymity was strictly preserved throughout the
data processing pipeline by the authors themselves,
ensuring direct control and accountability. Specifi-
cally, we used spaCy’s19 pre-trained English NER
model (en_core_web_trf20) to automatically de-
tect Personally Identifiable Information (PII) such
as names, usernames, and locations. This model
was chosen for its robust performance on social
media text. To complement this, we applied reg-
ular expressions tailored to platform-specific user
handles (e.g., strings beginning with ’@’) to cap-
ture additional identifiers. Upon detection, all such
identifiers were replaced with unique hashed tokens
generated via SHA-256 hashing to maintain refer-
ential consistency across instances without reveal-
ing the original identity. In cases where identifiers

4.1
Baselines
We benchmarked a broad range of models across
classical, neural, and large language paradigms.
Traditional approaches included Support Vector

This work was conducted with a strong focus on

A.1
Annotation Guidelines
A.1.1
Purpose and Scope
These guidelines define the procedures and deci-
sion rules used to annotate the ChildGuard dataset.
The aim is to ensure high-quality, reproducible la-
beling of hate speech targeting minors across di-
verse online contexts.
A.1.2
Annotation Objectives
Each instance was annotated with the following
attributes:
• Hate Speech Label (Binary): Indicates if the
post expresses hostility, dehumanization, or
incites harm against children (Hate = 1) or is
neutral/positive/non-targeted (Hate = 0).
• Age Group Label (Multiclass): Captures
the inferred age group of the child target —
Younger Children (under 11), Pre-Teens
(11–12), or Teens (13–17).
• Hate Type (Binary): Distinguishes between
Explicit hate (direct slurs, threats, abuse)
and Implicit hate (coded language, sarcasm,
indirect demeaning tone).
A.1.3
Annotation Process
Three authors with prior training in hate speech
analysis independently labeled the dataset. Two an-
notators reviewed each instance, and a third adjudi-
cator resolved disagreements. A calibration phase
on 1,000 instances ensured interpretation alignment
and edge case discussion before large-scale label-
ing. Labeling was performed in controlled batches
with regular discussion checkpoints.
A.1.4
Operational Definitions and Rules
Hate Speech Criteria:
An instance was labeled
as Hate = 1 if it met any of the following:
• Contained direct derogatory references to
a child or children (e.g.,
“kids
are
disgusting”).
• Used metaphors or exaggeration to present
children as burdens, threats, or nuisances (e.g.,
“these school kids are a virus”).
• Expressed contempt, devaluation, or mock-
ery toward children’s behavior, appearance, or
presence.
• Showed sarcasm or passive aggression imply-
ing negative traits of children (implicit hate).

B
Qualitative Analysis
To complement the quantitative results, we provide
a detailed qualitative analysis of model predictions
across six evaluation settings: Contextual, Lexical,
ChildGuard (full), and age-specific subsets (Teens,
Pre-teens, Younger Children). This analysis reveals
recurring patterns in model behavior, particularly
around the interpretation of implicit hate speech
and age-related linguistic cues.
B.1
Common Success Patterns
Across all models, explicit hate expressions were
detected reliably, especially those containing strong
lexical cues such as “brat”, “idiot”, or imperative
insults like “go back to TikTok”. For instance, GPT-
4.5 correctly labeled “No one wants to hear from
kids like you — go back to TikTok.” as hateful un-
der the Contextual subset. Similarly, GPT-4o suc-
cessfully flagged “Go back to your toys, little one.”
under the Younger Children setting. These cases
demonstrate that current models excel when ex-
plicit hostility or exclusionary language co-occurs
with clear age markers.


## Page 12


B.2
Observed Failure Trends
However, failures were more frequent in subtle and
implicit hate cases. Models often misclassified sar-
castic or infantilizing comments as neutral, despite
their derogatory intent. For example:
• GPT-4.5 labeled “Sure, because 10-year-olds
clearly understand politics” as neutral, miss-
ing its sarcastic tone.
• GPT-4o failed on “Kids shouldn’t be on these
platforms.”, ignoring the normative and exclu-
sionary bias.
• DeepSeek-V3 predicted neutral for “Maybe
kids should just stay quiet.”, overlooking im-
plicit silencing language.
These errors indicate that implicit hate remains a
core challenge, as sarcasm, euphemism, and indi-
rect condescension lack strong lexical markers.
B.3
Age-Specific Challenges
Performance varied across age groups. Hate speech
directed at Younger Children was slightly easier to
detect when cues like “kindergarten” or “toys” ap-
peared explicitly. Conversely, comments targeting
Teens often blended criticism with humor or stereo-
type, increasing ambiguity. For example, GPT-4.5
failed on “You must be 13 to write that nonsense.”,
highlighting difficulty in distinguishing age-based
mockery from benign humor. Similarly, models
frequently missed pre-teen insults framed as com-
pliments, such as “Aw, how cute! A pre-teen opin-
ion.”.
B.4
Implications for Model Design
These findings suggest that: Lexical cues alone are
insufficient; pragmatic and contextual understand-
ing is crucial. Sarcasm and indirect bias require
discourse-level modeling, possibly via conversa-
tional context or intent detection. Age-sensitive
embeddings or fine-tuning on implicit hate exam-
ples may improve robustness.
