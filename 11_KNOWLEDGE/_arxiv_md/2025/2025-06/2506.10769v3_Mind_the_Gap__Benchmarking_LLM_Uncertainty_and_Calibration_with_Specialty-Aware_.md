Mind the Gap: Benchmarking LLM Uncertainty and Calibration with
Specialty-Aware Clinical QA and Reasoning-Based Behavioural Features
Alberto Testoni1,2, Iacer Calixto1,2
1Department of Medical Informatics, Amsterdam University Medical Center,

Abstract
Reliable uncertainty quantification (UQ) is es-
sential when employing large language models
(LLMs) in high-risk domains such as clinical
question answering (QA). In this work, we eval-
uate uncertainty estimation methods for clini-
cal QA focusing, for the first time, on eleven
clinical specialties and six question types, and
across ten open-source LLMs (general-purpose,
biomedical, and reasoning models), alongside
representative proprietary models. We analyze
score-based UQ methods, present a case study
introducing a novel lightweight method based
on behavioral features derived from reasoning-
oriented models, and examine conformal pre-
diction as a complementary set-based approach.
Our findings reveal that uncertainty reliability
is not a monolithic property, but one that de-
pends on clinical specialty and question type
due to shifts in calibration and discrimination.
Our results highlight the need to select or en-
semble models based on their distinct, comple-
mentary strengths and clinical use.

Introduction
In recent years, Large Language Model (LLM) per-
formance has consistently increased across medi-
cal and clinical tasks (Chen et al., 2023; Wu et al.,
2024a; Acikgoz et al., 2024; Sellergren et al., 2025).
This includes classification tasks with finite output
spaces, such as multiple-choice question answering
(MCQA; Jin et al., 2019, 2021; Pal et al., 2022), as
well as natural language generation (NLG) tasks
with open-ended output spaces (Baan et al., 2023),
such as open-ended question answering (Open-QA;
Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023; Singhal et al., 2023)
and clinical text summarization (Schoonbeek et al.,
2024; Van Veen et al., 2024). However, LLMs
are known to be overconfident (Groot and Valdene-
gro Toro, 2024), raising concerns about their re-
liability in high-stakes clinical settings. In such
settings, reliability depends not only on discrimina-
tion (how well confidence scores separate correct
vs. incorrect answers) but also on calibration (how
closely predicted probabilities match actual accu-
racy); both are critical for safe deployment (Atf
et al., 2025). By quantifying uncertainty, LLMs
can spot unreliable predictions and prompt expert
oversight if needed (Zhou et al., 2025).
Let x ∈X be a question and y ∈Y its an-
swer. Typically, in a classification setting such as
MCQA, Y is a finite set and quantifying the en-
tropy of an LLM’s predictive uncertainty pθ(y|x)
for this problem can be done directly by enumer-
ating all the possible answers. However, uncer-
tainty quantification (UQ) in NLG is a significant
challenge (Giulianelli et al., 2023; Geng et al.,
2024). In Open-QA, the output space Y is the set
of all possible strings, which grows exponentially
with the length of the answer. Directly calculat-
ing the predictive uncertainty pθ(y|x) for every
possible output becomes unfeasible. In this work,
we benchmark how representative UQ approaches
for NLG—token-based confidence (Huang et al.,
2025; Aichberger et al., 2024), sample consistency
(Kuhn et al., 2023), confidence elicitation (Xiong
et al., 2024), and conformal prediction (Campos
et al., 2024)—fare specifically in clinical QA. We
benchmark uncertainty across 10 LLMs (general-
purpose, biomedical, and reasoning-focused) and
2 clinical QA datasets, including 11 different clini-
cal specialties (among which 5 can be considered
less frequent; see Section 3 for details). We also
introduce a novel annotation scheme that maps QA
pairs onto 6 different question types (e.g., diagnosis,
treatment, definition questions, etc).
We also present a new case study on behavioral
signals from LLM reasoning traces, where we offer
a lightweight, interpretable, and single-pass alter-
native to sampling-based methods. We find that
reasoning LLMs often yield superior accuracy and
calibration, but this advantage varies significantly
across specialties and question types.

Related Work
Starting from the seminal work of Guo et al. (2017),
uncertainty quantification has been studied in NLP
tasks such as sentiment analysis, machine transla-
tion, and named entity recognition (Xiao and Wang,
2019; Zerva et al., 2022; Hashimoto et al., 2024;
Shorinwa et al., 2025). While uncertainty estima-
tion has a long tradition in classification tasks, the
rise of LLMs has renewed interest in extending
these methods to language generation (Geng et al.,
2024; Ulmer et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Huang
et al., 2025). Ensuring well-calibrated predictions
is essential in clinical NLP, where overconfidence
or miscalibrated uncertainty estimates can translate
into patient risk or diagnostic error (Ghoshal et al.,
2021; Kompa et al., 2021; Dawood et al., 2023).
Few approaches have systematically evaluated
these aspects in the clinical domain. Gao et al.
(2025) argue that LLM uncertainty signals (e.g.,
token-level entropy) are poorly suited for diagno-
sis tasks, but their analysis remains limited to a
small set of outdated models and focuses solely
on classification tasks. Similarly, studies on open-
ended generation (Wu et al., 2024b; Savage et al.,
2025) evaluate only a narrow range of methods
and models, limiting the generalizability of their
findings. Moreover, existing evaluations typically
report aggregated metrics, offering limited insight
into finer-grained dimensions such as medical spe-
cialties or question types. Another still underex-
plored aspect is the comparison across different
LLM types. Recent studies have evaluated general-
purpose and biomedical LLMs on clinical tasks,
reporting mixed evidence for the latter’s advantage
(Dorfner et al., 2024). However, these comparisons
largely overlook uncertainty calibration.
A recent related study to ours is Savage et al.
(2025), which investigates uncertainty estimation
methods for clinical question answering.
The
authors found that sample consistency (assess-
ing agreement across multiple answers generated
via stochastic decoding) outperformed other ap-
proaches in terms of both discrimination and cal-
ibration, though the latter remained suboptimal.
While their findings provide valuable initial in-
sights into UQ for medical QA, their evaluation
is limited in several key ways. First, it focuses
on a small set of outdated models, excluding re-
cent advances such as reasoning-tuned and domain-
specific LLMs (which we highlight in Figure 1,
along with the calibration–discrimination trade-
off). Second, their analysis is restricted to diag-
nosis and treatment questions, ignoring other clin-
ically relevant question types. Third, they do not
examine how performance varies across medical
specialties. A complementary line of work is Ye
et al. (2024), which applies conformal prediction
to evaluate uncertainty in LLMs across five (non-
clinical) NLP tasks, such as question answering
and text summarization. Notably, they find that
(i) higher accuracy does not necessarily correlate

with higher certainty, and (ii) larger LLMs can ex-
hibit greater uncertainty than smaller ones. In our
work, we investigate whether these patterns extend
to clinical question answering.

Datasets and Question Annotation
We focus on two dimensions of clinical question an-
swering that have been largely overlooked in prior
work: medical specialty and question type, which
induce systematic variation in task difficulty and
uncertainty profiles that is obscured by aggregate
evaluation.
By Medical Specialty
We leverage two English
multiple-choice question answering datasets. 1 S-
MedQA (Yan et al., 2026) combines questions
from MedQA (Jin et al., 2021) and MedMCQA
(Pal et al., 2022). The dataset includes a validated
annotation pipeline that maps each question to one
of 55 medical specialties recognized in the Euro-
pean Union. We consider the six most frequent spe-
cialties: Gastroenterology, Cardiology, Obstetrics
and Gynecology, Neurology, Infectious Diseases,
and Pediatrics, which we refer to in the follow-
ing as ‘common specialities’. To ensure balanced
evaluation across specialties while keeping the total
number of questions manageable for computational
and analytical purposes, we fix the sample size
to 145 questions per specialty, resulting in a total
of 870 questions. Each question in S-MedQA in-
cludes four answer options and one correct answer.
MedExQA (Kim et al., 2024) focuses on underrep-
resented specialities: Biomedical Engineering (143
questions in the MedExQA test set), Clinical Labo-
ratory Science (372 questions), Clinical Psychol-
ogy (106 questions), Occupational Therapy (189
questions), and Speech Language Pathology (130
questions), summing up to 940 questions. Ques-
tions in MedExQA were manually gathered from a
variety of freely accessible online sources, such as
mock tests and practice exams designed for specific
specialties. Each question in MedExQA includes
four answer options and one correct answer.
By Question Type
After manually inspecting
a random sample of 50 questions from each
dataset, we identify six clinically meaningful
question types: Diagnosis, Treatment, Diagnos-
tic Test, Definition, Procedure/Operation, and
Other.

Methodology
4.1
Extracting Uncertainty Estimates
Uncertainty in LLMs can be quantified by exploit-
ing the probability distributions induced by the
model over its outputs. In the context of question
answering, these distributions can be summarized
as scalar scores or prediction sets that reflect how
confident a model is in its chosen answer. We con-
sider multiple strategies for extracting such uncer-
tainty estimates, ranging from single-pass methods
based on token probabilities to sampling-based ap-
proaches and set-valued predictions.
Score-based methods:
Following the taxonomy
of Geng et al. (2024), we consider logit-based ap-
proaches, such as average token-level probability
under greedy decoding (Huang et al., 2025). Con-
sistent with Savage et al. (2025), we found negligi-
ble differences when using minimum or maximum
token probability. We also include G-NLL (Aich-
berger et al., 2024), which computes the negative
log-likelihood of the greedily decoded output and
serves as an efficient uncertainty proxy.2 For lin-
guistic confidence methods, we adopt a two-step
confidence elicitation approach (Rivera et al., 2024;
Xiong et al., 2024), where the model first produces
an answer and is then prompted to reprocess the
full question–answer pair and assign a scalar cer-
tainty score. We use the same elicitation prompt as
Savage et al. (2025).
Consistency-based methods:
We evaluate Se-
mantic Entropy, inspired by Kuhn et al. (2023);
Farquhar et al. (2024) and related to Sample Consis-
tency (Savage et al., 2025). While Sample Consis-
tency measures agreement across sampled outputs,
Semantic Entropy quantifies the entropy of the
predicted answer distribution. Given the multiple-
choice answers available in the datasets we used,
we group generations by selected option and com-
pute entropy over the resulting distribution. Higher

Set-based methods:
As for a representative set-

approach inspired by Ke et al. (2025). The method

Linguistic behavioral features:
We also intro-

havioral features extracted from reasoning traces,
an understudied category in current taxonomies of
uncertainty estimation methods for LLMs. We pro-

of this approach in Section 5.2.
Finally, following the classification in Liu et al.
(2025), we can further distinguish between single-
pass methods (logit-based scores, confidence elic-
itation, and linguistic behavioral features) and
sampling-based methods, which require multiple
generations (consistency-based approaches). The
linguistic confidence method specifically involves
two passes per input.

4.3
Evaluation
Answer Extraction and Accuracy
Models are
prompted to output a long-form answer that in-
cludes the letter of the selected option in square
brackets (e.g., [C]), which we extract via regular
expressions. Only when the format deviates (on
average in 5–6% of responses, e.g., full answer
text within brackets instead of the letter alone), we
use LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct to map the response
to one of the four options (see Appendix B for
additional details). We validate its performance
on this task against human annotations over 100
samples, observing 92% accuracy. QA accuracy
is computed by comparing the extracted answer to
the ground-truth label, whereas uncertainty esti-
mates are computed over the full (long-form) an-
swer generated by the LLM. To control for position
bias, answer options are shuffled four times per
question with greedy decoding. Semantic Entropy
and Conformal Prediction require sampling (via
nucleus sampling) 10 and 20 responses per ques-
tion, respectively, with the options shuffled at each
generation to mitigate position bias.
Discrimination and Calibration
To evaluate dis-
crimination, i.e., the ability of uncertainty estimates
to distinguish correct from incorrect predictions,
we compute the Area Under the Receiver Operat-
ing Characteristic Curve (AUROC). AUROC mea-
sures how well uncertainty scores separate correct
from incorrect predictions; a value of 1.0 indicates
perfect discrimination, while 0.5 corresponds to
random guessing. We assess calibration, i.e., the
alignment between predicted confidence and em-
pirical accuracy, using Expected Calibration Error
(ECE) and the Brier Score (Brier, 1950). ECE mea-
sures the discrepancy between predicted confidence
and actual accuracy. Following Rivera et al. (2024),
we use 10 bins to compute ECE. The Brier Score
captures the squared error between predicted confi-
dence and correctness, providing a proper scoring
rule that reflects both calibration and sharpness. For
both scores, a value of 0 indicates perfect calibra-
tion. Given that ECE and Brier Score rank models
similarly, we report their average for some of the
analyses below.

Score-based UQ Methods Results
We begin by evaluating score-based uncertainty
methods by analyzing how their scores relate to
prediction correctness, focusing on calibration and
discrimination across all models and questions.
5.1
From Logits to Semantic Entropy
We compare logit-based, confidence elicitation,
and consistency-based methods to assess (a) which
yields the most calibrated and discriminative esti-
mates, and (b) whether LLM type or size systemati-
cally affect them. Figure 1 shows AUROC vs. ECE
for all LLMs and UQ methods. In line with the
findings in Savage et al. (2025), Semantic Entropy
shows the strongest overall performance while re-
quiring multiple generations per input. Reasoning-
oriented and biomedical LLMs do not consistently
outperform general-purpose models in terms of
uncertainty estimates. G-NLL does not perform
well in either dimension, although it performs bet-
ter on the recent s1.1-32B reasoning model. In
general, larger models tend to yield more reli-
able uncertainty estimates (particularly for Con-
fidence Elicitation), though this trend is less pro-
nounced for Semantic Entropy, which performs ro-
bustly across nearly all models, including the small-
est (LLaMA-3.2-3B). By contrast, Mistral-7B and
Mixtral-8x7B underperform relative to the rest.
Based on these findings, we adopt Semantic En-
tropy as our primary uncertainty estimation method
for the remainder of this section. We revisit single-
generation alternatives in Section 5.2. While Se-
mantic Entropy provides well-calibrated uncer-
tainty estimates across a broad range of models,
two important questions remain: Does this perfor-
mance hold uniformly across medical specialties
and question types? And how are these uncertainty
estimates aligned with actual QA accuracy?

5.2
Behavioural Signals of Uncertainty in
Reasoning Models: A Case Study
Although performance varies across medical spe-
cialties and question types, Semantic Entropy sub-
stantially outperforms sentence-level logprob meth-
ods in both calibration and discrimination. While
effective, the method requires repeated generations:
this feature may hinder its deployment in real-
world applications, particularly in latency-sensitive
or resource-constrained settings, such as clinical
decision support. In contrast, single-pass methods
like G-NLL and average token probability require
only one forward pass per input, albeit with reduced
discrimination and calibration performance.
To address this trade-off, we explore whether be-
havioral signals in LLM reasoning traces can serve
as lightweight proxies for uncertainty. Our moti-
vation stems from empirical observations: models
like DeepSeek-R1-70B and s1.1-32B often pro-
duce intermediate reasoning steps with linguistic
cues that appear to reflect uncertainty. For example,
we frequently observe self-directed questions and
tokens such as “Wait...”, which may signal self-
verification or reconsideration. We also hypoth-
esize that longer responses may indicate greater
difficulty in reaching a final answer. Appendix D
provides illustrative examples of these patterns. No-
tably, the authors of s1.1-32B show that “Wait”
tokens can be leveraged at test time to improve
prediction calibration (Muennighoff et al., 2025).
To operationalize these hypotheses, we extract
three surface-level behavioral features from each
model response: (1) the number of tokens in the
reasoning trace, (2) the number of self-posed ques-
tions, and (3) the number of occurrences of the to-
ken “Wait”. We explore different strategies for turn-
ing these features into uncertainty estimates, de-
tailed in Appendix D. The most successful method
treats the three features as input to a Calibration
Regression supervised model (which fits a linear
function with L2 regularization as Ridge Regres-
sion). To train this model, we use ground-truth
correctness labels (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) for
each response. Although the regression model is
trained to predict correctness, we hypothesize that
its outputs can serve as proxies for confidence. We
define uncertainty as its complement, u = 1−f(x),
enabling direct comparison with other scalar-based
uncertainty estimation methods. To ensure fair
comparison and avoid overfitting, we train the re-
gression models on 40% of the dataset and evalu-
ate on the remaining 60%, preserving the original
distribution of medical specialties. We assess the
quality of the behavioral uncertainty signals using
AUROC, ECE, and Brier Score. To further disen-
tangle model behavior, we report results separately
for common and uncommon medical specialties
(see Appendix D for the latter). We include results
with Semantic Entropy for comparison, noting that
it relies on repeated sampling.
The results, shown in Figure 4, reveal that our
lightweight behavioral feature method outperforms
sentence-level methods and closely matches Se-
mantic Entropy, while requiring only a single for-
ward pass per input. This pattern holds across both
reasoning-oriented LLMs, DeepSeek-R1-70B and
s1.1-32B, and applies to both calibration and dis-

Conformal Prediction Results
Analyses based on score-based uncertainty esti-
mates (Section 5) reveal notable trends: reasoning
LLMs often achieve higher accuracy and better
calibration, while biomedical models often appear
overconfident (likely due to domain-specific tun-
ing) and underperform outside a narrow range of
specialties, leading to less reliable estimates.
We next examine conformal prediction (follow-
ing the method in Ke et al. 2025) to assess whether
set-based uncertainty estimates offer complemen-
tary insights. We focus on three representative 70B
LLMs (one per model type) and restrict the analysis
to common medical specialties with sufficient held-
out data. Table 3 reports empirical coverage and av-
erage set size across models, specialties, and ques-
tion types. The three models achieve strong overall
coverage, though not all of them reach the 90%
coverage target. DeepSeek-R1-70B performs most
reliably, achieving 93.8% overall coverage with
compact sets, and generalizes well across special-
ties and question types. Llama3-OpenBioLLM-70B
also reaches high coverage (90.1%) but with larger
sets compared to DeepSeek-R1-70B, and it fails to
meet the target in several categories, particularly
Infectious Diseases and Definition questions.
In contrast, Llama-3.1-70B falls short of the
coverage target (85.5%) with highly restrictive pre-
dictions, indicating overconfidence and poor gen-
eralization from calibration to test data. For this
model, no specialty or question type meets the 90%
threshold, suggesting that its strong score-based
UQ performance does not extend to set-based meth-
ods. These findings underscore that accuracy alone
does not suffice. Robust uncertainty estimation
depends on careful calibration and generalization

across specialties, question types, and uncertainty
paradigms, particularly in light of the misalignment
observed between score- and set-based methods.
7
Discussion and Conclusion
We investigate uncertainty estimation for LLMs in
clinical QA. By jointly assessing calibration and
discrimination spanning diverse models and across
clinical specialties and question types, we address
key limitations of prior work. Our findings show
that model performance varies not only in accu-
racy but also in uncertainty quality, with substan-
tial differences across clinical contexts. Reasoning-
oriented LLMs often perform well, but no method
succeeds consistently across settings; evaluation
and deployment must be context-specific.
In conclusion, our work raises a crucial caution-
ary note for the deployment of LLMs in clinical
NLP, while also offering a constructive path for-
ward. Our analysis demonstrates that a model’s
ability to signal uncertainty is not a monolithic ca-
pability but one that varies significantly with the
clinical context. To support more nuanced evalu-
ation, we propose a lightweight and interpretable
UQ case study based on behavioral cues from rea-
soning traces, which approximates the performance
of more resource-intensive sampling-based meth-
ods. The primary lesson for NLP practitioners
is that for high-stakes domains, ‘one-size-fits-all’
evaluations are insufficient and potentially mislead-
ing. The true measure of an LLM’s reliability lies
in its performance on the specific tasks and in the
precise domains where it will be used. Future work
should involve clinicians in assessing questions
flagged as uncertain, to determine which signals
best indicate when additional human review is war-
ranted.
Limitations
In the following, we discuss some limitations of
our experimental setup.
Open-ended generation mapped to MCQA.
While we benchmark on multiple-choice question
answering for comparability and ground truth clar-
ity, our setup involves open-ended generation: mod-
els first produce free-form answers and reasoning
traces, which we then map to the multiple-choice
options for evaluation. We believe that fully open-
ended QA is crucial for clinical realism, but it
comes with significant challenges in defining gold
standards. We believe this focus is justified as a first
step: to the best of our knowledge, ours is the first
study to systematically evaluate UQ across model
types, medical specialties, and question types at
this scale. Nonetheless, future work should extend
this analysis to more open-ended clinical scenarios.
Automated prediction and annotation pipelines.
We rely on automated extraction of model predic-
tions and question-type annotations. Although our
heuristics and model-assisted annotations are vali-
dated against manual labels with high agreement,
some residual noise may persist, especially for am-
biguous questions or borderline categories (e.g.,
“Other”).
Behavioral features as exploratory proxies.
We
use behavioral features as proxies for uncertainty in
an exploratory case study, as highlighted through-
out the main text (Section 5.2). Validation with
domain experts or clinicians is planned as an im-
portant next step to confirm their practical value.
While we show that behavioral features offer a
promising, interpretable alternative to multi-sample
methods like Semantic Entropy, our feature space is
deliberately simple and task-specific. Generalizing
these findings to other tasks, languages, or reason-
ing paradigms will require broader validation and
potentially more sophisticated feature design.
Sources of uncertainty.
We do not investigate
in detail sources of uncertainty. We believe future
work should look into promising approaches in this
direction, such as Yang et al. (2025).
Post-hoc calibration.
We do not apply post-hoc
calibration techniques such as temperature scal-
ing. Our focus is on comparing raw uncertainty
estimates without additional tuning. Applying tem-
perature scaling consistently is challenging in our
setup, as models differ in tokenization schemes
and output structure – e.g., in how they format an-
swers (direct label vs. more complex sentences), af-
fecting how likelihoods are computed and making
calibration non-trivial. We leave the integration of
model-specific calibration as an important direction
for future work. As for additional considerations
on the temperature setup and decoding details, see
Appendix C.
Uncertainty communication.
We benchmark
uncertainty estimates (including confidence elic-
itation) but do not study how uncertainty should be
communicated to end users (Ulmer et al., 2025);
verbalized uncertainty is shaped by contextual and


## Page 10


sociopragmatic factors and can be biased by align-
ment for helpfulness, raising additional challenges
for clinician-facing deployment.
Coverage for ChatGPT models.
Our analysis
of OpenAI ChatGPT models (see Appendix G) is
limited to Semantic Entropy, which proved to be
the most robust UQ method. Future work should
evaluate other methods, such as Confidence Elic-
itation and Conformal Prediction, also for these
models.

D.1
Additional details on the method
Given the features described in Section 5.2, the first
is a simple normalized sum of the three features,
referred to as the Linear Sum method. Let x1, x2,
and x3 denote the normalized values of the three
features, and compute the uncertainty estimate as
u = x1 + x2 + x3. The second approach treats the
three features as input to a supervised model: either
a Logistic Regression or a Calibration Regression
(the former models a sigmoid likelihood, the latter
fits a linear function with L2 regularization as Ridge
Regression). Calibration Regression is the method
discussed in Section 5.2, while here we provide
details on all methods. To train these models, we
use ground-truth correctness labels (1 for correct,
0 for incorrect) associated with each response. For-
mally, the model learns a function f : R3 →[0, 1].
Although the regression models are trained to pre-
dict correctness, we hypothesize that their outputs
can serve as proxies for confidence. We define
uncertainty as their complement, u = 1 −f(x),
enabling direct comparison with other uncertainty
estimation methods. To ensure a fair comparison
and avoid overfitting, we train the regression mod-
els on 40% of the dataset while evaluating on the
remaining 60%, preserving the original distribution
of medical specialties. All responses are generated
via greedy decoding to enable direct comparison
with single-pass baselines such as G-NLL and aver-
age token probability. We assess the quality of the
behavioral uncertainty signals using AUROC, ECE,
and Brier Score. To further disentangle model be-
havior, we report results separately for common
and uncommon medical specialties. We include
results with Semantic Entropy for comparison, not-
ing that it relies on repeated sampling to estimate
uncertainty.

Additional Details on Uncertainty
Quantification Methods
First, we chose not to include confidence elicitation
methods that directly prompt LLMs to report a con-
fidence score. While such approaches have been


## Page 17


explored in prior work, their reliability remains
limited. In particular, Savage et al. (2025) report
poor performance and calibration when relying on
model-generated confidence scores.
Second, for methods based on log-probabilities
(logprobs), we carefully considered how to handle
outputs from models that include explicit reasoning
traces. A notable example is DeepSeek-R1-70B,
whose responses are structured with clearly delim-
ited reasoning segments, marked by special tokens
such as <think>. In these cases, a key decision
is whether to compute uncertainty scores over the
entire response (including the reasoning trace) or
only over the final answer portion. After empiri-
cal inspection and preliminary analyses, we found
that restricting the computation of logprobs to the
final answer (excluding the reasoning trace) pro-
duced more reliable and comparable uncertainty
estimates.
