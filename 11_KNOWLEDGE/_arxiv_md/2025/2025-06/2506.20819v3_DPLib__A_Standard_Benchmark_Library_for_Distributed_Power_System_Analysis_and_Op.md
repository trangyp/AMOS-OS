DPLib: A Standard Benchmark Library for
Distributed Power System Analysis and Optimization
Milad Hasanzadeh, Graduate Student Member, IEEE and Amin Kargarian, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—DPLib is an open-source MATLAB-based benchmark
library created to support research and development in distributed
and decentralized power system analysis and optimization.
Distributed and decentralized methods offer scalability, privacy
preservation, and resilience to single points of failure, making them
increasingly important for modern power systems. However, unlike
centralized tools such as PGLib, no general-purpose, reproducible
data library package currently exists for distributed power system
studies. DPLib, available at GitHub, fills this gap by providing
a standard power system library featuring over 20 multi-region
benchmark test cases of varying sizes, along with a graph-based
partitioning toolkit that decomposes any MATPOWER test system
into multiple regions. The partitioning toolkit, an easy-to-use
MATLAB code, generates standardized .mat and .m files, along
with region visualizations for intuitive understanding. We also
provide modular, easy-to-use distributed optimal power flow
(OPF) solvers: an alternating direction method of multipliers
(ADMM)-based DC OPF solver implemented in YALMIP, and an
ADMM-based AC OPF solver leveraging IPOPT. These solvers
validate the generated test systems for distributed optimization
applications. Numerical results validate the generated test cases,
establishing DPLib as a foundation for reproducible distributed
power system research.
Index Terms—Distributed power grid test systems, standard
benchmark library, MATPOWER, distributed optimization.
I. INTRODUCTION
C
ENTRALIZED frameworks have long served as the
dominant approach for modeling and solving large-scale
power system problems, including optimal power flow (OPF),
planning, control, and unit commitment. A key enabler behind
the success and widespread adoption of centralized optimization
and analysis in power systems is the availability of standardized
benchmark test cases and libraries. Among these, MATPOWER
has emerged as a widely accepted benchmark tool, offering
consistent, well-structured data for centralized studies [1]. Its
role in enabling reproducibility, fair performance comparisons,
and methodological development has been fundamental to the
evolution of centralized power system analysis.

relaxations for AC OPF and validated their results on
MATPOWER test cases. [3] provided a comprehensive review
of relaxation techniques for OPF, with all benchmarks
sourced from MATPOWER. [4] introduced QC relaxation for
OPF using MATPOWER data. More recently, [5] developed
robust optimization methods for corrective control under
uncertainty, relying on MATPOWER cases for validation.
[6] proposed the NESTA archive to enrich MATPOWER
with more realistic data, yet retained full MATPOWER
compatibility. These works and many more underscore the

II. GRAPH-BASED PARTITIONING AND TOOLKIT
DESCRIPTION
In many power systems studies, realistic standard test cases
in the MATPOWER format are commonly used for testing and
validation. This practice ensures that developed methods are
evaluated on realistic cases that reflect the complexity of actual
power systems. To maintain this standard, researchers working
on distributed algorithms should also rely on realistic standard
test systems and datasets rather than arbitrarily constructed or
manually partitioned grids.
In practice, real power networks often exhibit a naturally
regional structure. For example, a network covering several
regions will typically have strong internal connectivity within
each region and only a few tie-lines connecting areas to one
another. This results in regions that are strongly connected
internally but weakly connected to the other areas. A proper
partitioning method should be able to observe these loosely
connected boundaries. We use graph-based partitioning to
provide a principled and effective framework for capturing
such structures and dividing the network in a way that aligns
with real systems’ physical and topological characteristics.
A. Power Network Modeling and Spectral Clustering
To prepare the network for spectral partitioning, the power
system is modeled as an undirected graph G = (V, E), where
buses form the nodes and transmission lines form the edges.
The graph representation is encoded through the adjacency
matrix A, and two forms of this matrix may be constructed
depending on how connectivity is interpreted.
In its simplest form, the network is viewed purely through
its topological structure. Each transmission line indicates the
presence of a connection, and the adjacency matrix is as

A second option introduces electrical heterogeneity explicitly.

Although the toolkit supports a weighted Laplacian that
incorporates electrical heterogeneity, we treat this option as
an auxiliary feature rather than the default mode. Weighted
formulations can highlight electrically strong corridors, but
doing so requires a choice of normalization, scaling, and
aggregation of heterogeneous parameters—decisions that are
not standardized across power-system studies. Because the
purpose of DPLib is to produce baseline multi-region datasets
for benchmarking distributed algorithms, we use the unweighted
formulation to ensure that all results are comparable, repeatable,
and free of case-dependent parameter tuning. Researchers who
wish to emphasize electrical strength rather than topological
connectivity may enable the weighted variant, which the toolkit
provides for completeness.

Laplacian matrix L encodes the power network connectivity and
topology. For a connected graph, L is symmetric and positive
semi-definite, meaning all its eigenvalues are non-negative.
Importantly, the smallest eigenvalue of L is always zero,
corresponding to the trivial uniform eigenvector.
To divide the system into k regions, we use spectral
clustering, which leverages the properties of L. The first
step is to compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of L.
Among these, the k smallest nonzero eigenvalues are selected.
Each eigenvalue is associated with an eigenvector that varies
smoothly across the graph, meaning that buses that are strongly
connected in the physical system will have similar eigenvalues.
Therefore, these eigenvectors reflect the underlying structure
of the network.
We form a matrix U ∈Rn×k by placing the k selected
eigenvectors as columns. Each row ui ∈Rk corresponds to a

bus and represents it in a new k-dimensional spectral space,

To assign buses into one of k regions, we apply the k-means
clustering algorithm to the rows of U. This unsupervised
learning algorithm operates by:
• Randomly initializing k centroids (in our case, from the
rows of U);
• Assigning each bus to the nearest centroid based on
Euclidean distance;
• Recalculating the centroid of each cluster;
• Repeating the assignment and centroid update steps until
convergence.
The clustering result yields a partition of all buses into k
regions. Each cluster groups strongly connected buses, leading
to balanced regions where each has its generation. After
forming the clusters, inter-regional tie-lines (lines that connect
buses belonging to different clusters) are identified. To improve
quality, the algorithm repeats the clustering multiple times
and select the configuration that minimizes the number of
inter-regional tie-lines.
B. DPLib Partitioning Toolkit
The DPLib partitioning toolkit implements the graph-based
clustering framework described in the previous subsection
and automates the full workflow for constructing multi-region
MATPOWER datasets. The toolkit supports both adjacency
representations introduced earlier, allowing the Laplacian to
be formed either from the purely topological structure of the
network or from a weighted graph that incorporates electrical
parameters. In the unweighted mode, each transmission line
contributes a unit entry to the adjacency matrix, so the
Laplacian reflects the combinatorial connectivity of the system.
In the weighted mode, each existing edge is assigned a
composite weight constructed from line reactance, line charging
susceptance, and thermal capacity after normalization, allowing
the Laplacian to embed relative electrical strength directly into
the spectral geometry.
Although both modes are supported, the unweighted
Laplacian is used as the default for generating standardized
multi-region test cases. This choice ensures that the resulting
partitions depend only on the physical topology of the
MATPOWER system and remain independent of parameter
scaling, model variations, or dataset-dependent numerical
ranges. The unweighted construction also aligns with how
regional boundaries appear in real-world transmission networks,
where geographical separations and sparse tie-lines primarily
determine structural decomposition. Using the unweighted
version therefore provides consistent, reproducible partitions
across different datasets and preserves compatibility with
studies that compare methods on topologically equivalent cases.

is loaded, and if the original bus numbering is not sequential,
all buses, branches, and generators are renumbered from 1 to

constructed using either the binary definition or the weighted
formulation. The degree matrix and Laplacian are then formed,
and the k Laplacian eigenvectors associated with the smallest
nonzero eigenvalues are computed to obtain the spectral
embedding. Multiple runs of k-means are applied to the rows of

III. VALIDATING DPLIB TEST SYSTEMS USING OPF

V. CONCLUSION
This work presented DPLib, a reproducible and open-source
benchmark library tailored for distributed and decentralized
power system studies. DPLib addresses the gap in standardized
datasets for distributed OPF research by offering a graph-based
partitioning toolkit and over 20 multi-region test cases
derived from MATPOWER. The library is complemented
by modular ADMM-based DC and AC OPF solvers, which
enable scalable validation across diverse case sizes. Numerical
results demonstrate the accuracy of the generated test cases
and convergence of the solvers, making DPLib a practical
foundation for future research in scalable, privacy-preserving,
and resilient power system analysis.
To support long-term usability, we are committed to
maintaining DPLib as an actively developed open-source
project. Future updates will include bug fixes, feature
enhancements, expanded test systems, and improvements to the
distributed OPF solvers as new algorithms become available.
The DPLib repository will continue to be updated and versioned
through GitHub, where users may report issues, request features,
and contribute to the codebase.
