ABSTRACT
Within disk galaxies, the velocity dispersion, σ, of stars increases with age, τ, as measured in the
Milky Way (MW) and nearby galaxies. This relation provides a key window into galactic formation
history, tracing both the kinematics of stars at birth and the dynamical heating of stars after birth.
We compile and compare observational measurements of the MW, M31, M33, and 16 galaxies from
the PHANGS survey. The MW exhibits significantly colder stellar kinematics, with 2-3 times lower
σ(τ)/vϕ,0 at a given age, than all but one other observed galaxy. Therefore, the MW is a kinematic
outlier. To assess how measurement effects influence σ(τ), we analyze the FIRE-2 cosmological simu-
lations, quantifying the impact of uncertainties in stellar age, aperture size, galactocentric radius, and
galaxy inclination. Aperture size and galactocentric radius affect σ(τ) by up to a factor of ≈2 for
stars younger than 100 Myr, with milder effects on older stars. Age uncertainties up to 40% change
the value of σ(τ) at a given age by ≲20% but can reshape the relation with age and erase merger
signatures. We compare σ(τ)/vϕ,0 in FIRE-2 simulations with observations. FIRE-2 agrees well with
M31 and M33 at all measured ages, and with PHANGS for stars older than ≈500 Myr. The average
σ(τ)/vϕ,0 in FIRE-2 is about two times higher than the MW at most ages, but the youngest stars
show better agreement. The velocity ratios (σϕ/σR, σZ/σϕ, σZ/σR) in FIRE-2 broadly agree with
the MW. We conclude that σ(τ) in FIRE-2, and most cosmological zoom-in simulations, reasonably
matches observed nearby galaxies, but matching the MW is rare, because it is a kinematic outlier.
1. INTRODUCTION
Galactic discs form and grow over cosmic time, and the
stars within them retain a lasting record of this dynam-
ical evolution. Nowhere is this stellar record richer than
in the Milky Way (MW), and much work has focused
on measuring the velocity dispersion, σ, as a function
of stellar age, τ, in the solar neighborhood.1
Almost
all works agree that σ typically increases with age, that
is, young stars are kinematically cold, exhibiting tidily
circular orbits, while older stars are on less coherently
circular orbits and therefore have higher σ.
However,
despite decades of extensive study, a fundamental ques-
tion persists: What is the physical origin of στ?
The present-day orbits of stars, including σ of a popu-
lation at a given age, reflect two key aspects of a galaxy’s
formation history: the conditions of the (star-forming)
interstellar medium (ISM) over time, which sets the ini-
tial orbits of stars at birth, and their subsequent dy-
namical evolution. Both processes affect σ(τ), but their
relative contributions remain uncertain.
In the canonical picture of Spitzer & Schwarzschild
(1951), stars form with the same low σ at all ages, but
repeated scattering interactions with (then undetected)
giant molecular clouds (GMCs) gradually increase σ as
a stellar population ages. However, analytical models of
GMC-driven heating failed to match observation, moti-
vating the study and acceptance of additional dynamical
heating processes, such as transient spiral arms (Barba-
nis & Woltjer 1967; Carlberg & Sellwood 1985; Minchev

by satellite galaxies (Bird et al. 2012; D’Onghia et al.

standard heating models fail to reach the high σ observed
for the MW’s older stars. This discrepancy is unsurpris-
ing, because traditional models assume that the MW’s
present-day conditions are representative of its earlier
states, such that stars formed with the same low σ over
cosmic time and subsequently experienced the same dy-
namical heating processes.
In contrast, nearly all recent observations and theoret-
ical models of galaxy formation find that the dynamical
state of the ISM evolves substantially over cosmic time.
At higher redshifts (z ≳1), disk galaxies are consistently
thicker, clumpier, and more turbulent than local disks
(for example F¨orster Schreiber et al. 2009; Birkin et al.
2023), probably due to their higher gas fractions and
star-formation, accretion, and merger rates (Conselice
et al. 2005; Swinbank et al. 2012; Tacconi et al. 2013;
Genzel et al. 2017; Stott et al. 2016; F¨orster Schreiber
& Wuyts 2020).
Spatially resolved studies of ionized
gas kinematics find that vϕ/σ, which measures the de-
gree of rotational-support or diskiness, is often as high
as 10 −20 in the local Universe but decreases with in-
creased redshift, with average values of ≈2 at z = 3 (see
recent compilation in Danhaive et al. 2025). Similarly,
σ increases from ≈20 km s−1 in disk galaxies today to
≈50 km s−1 at z = 3 (Kassin et al. 2012; Wisnioski et al.

works indicate that disks “settled” over cosmic time, such
that their gaseous disks became progressively less turbu-
lent and more rotationally supported. Theoretical mod-
els, including cosmological simulations, strongly support
these observations, generally finding that the progenitors
of MW-mass galaxies typically formed long-lived disks at
z ≲1 −2, with such disks growing radially “inside-out”
and vertically “upside-down” (for example Bird et al.
2012; Pillepich et al. 2019).
This observational picture indicates that stars form
with different dynamics over cosmic time, given that
stars inherit their initial kinematics from their progen-
itor gas. That said, the same conditions that drive in-
creased ISM turbulence – including substantial gas frac-
tions, higher merger rates, and burstier star formation
– also affect post-formation dynamical heating. For ex-
ample, feedback-driven outflows and the resulting po-
tential fluctuations can inject additional energy into the
stellar component, leading to rapid, non-adiabatic heat-
ing that further amplifies stellar velocity dispersions (El-
Badry et al. 2018a). Such ISM conditions also increase
the prevalence of scattering from long-lived massive gas
clumps and GMCs (for example Bournaud et al. 2014;
Johnson et al. 2018; Price et al. 2020).
Disentangling
the degree to which present-day velocity dispersions re-
flect hotter birth conditions versus cumulative dynamical
heating is challenging, as both processes can yield sim-
ilar observational signatures. For example, Ting & Rix
(2019) argue that the MW’s vertical σ(τ) is consistent
with stars forming with a constant, low σ over the past 8
Gyr, but experiencing stronger GMC-driven dynamical
heating at these earlier times.
This already tangled picture has recently been further
complicated, as some recent observations now challenge
our understanding of (gaseous) disk evolution. ALMA
has revealed cold, rotationally supported molecular gas
discs out to z ∼7, including systems with v/σ0 ≳4
(Rizzo et al. 2020; Lelli et al. 2021; Pope et al. 2023),
and even as high as v/σ0 ∼11 at z = 7.3 (Rowland et al.
2024). JWST now extends this picture to ionized gas,
detecting kinematically cold discs across a broad range
of stellar masses and star formation rates (Nelson et al.
2023, 2024; Tohill et al. 2024; Robertson et al. 2023; de
Graaff et al. 2024). Although these findings suggest that
dynamically cold disks can form, at least temporarily, in
gas, an open question is how representative these disks
are at early times. For example, Danhaive et al. (2025)
showed that almost all of these early disks are massive,
Mstar > 1010 M⊙, and thus not representative of a typical
MW-mass progenitor at these redshifts.
A key limitation of high-redshift studies is that they
generally only provide a statistical view of galaxy pop-
ulations and cannot reveal how any individual galaxy
continuously evolved across cosmic time.
In contrast,
detailed measurements of resolved stars (or stellar pop-
ulations) in nearby galaxies can provide a view of the
entire formation history of a galaxy. In particular, the
advent of Gaia (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016), along
with large spectroscopic surveys, such as APOGEE (Ma-
jewski et al. 2017), GALAH (De Silva et al. 2015; Buder
et al. 2018), and LAMOST (Cui et al. 2012), now pro-
vides up to 6D orbital phase-space coordinates and ele-
mental abundances for millions of stars throughout the
Galaxy. In parallel, the growth of asteroseismology (Pin-
sonneault et al. 2018; Miglio et al. 2021), in combination
with high-quality spectroscopic measurements and data-
driven models (Ho et al. 2017; Ness et al. 2016; Sharma
et al. 2018; Queiroz et al. 2023), has driven remarkable
progress in our ability to measure the ages of stars. Such
progress enables measurements of σ(τ), which was histor-
ically limited to the immediate solar neighborhood and
beholden to unclear systematics and age uncertainties
(Nordstr¨om et al. 2004; Holmberg et al. 2007; Casagrande
et al. 2011), to large swathes across the disk with un-
precedented precision (for example Lagarde et al. 2021;
Anders et al. 2023; Kordopatis et al. 2023).
These advancements have revolutionized our view of
the MW’s formation history. Indeed, a general picture
has emerged that the MW began as a turbulent galaxy,
subject to a bombardment of mergers and bursts of star
formation (Myeong et al. 2019; Rix et al. 2022; Chan-
dra et al. 2023).
It then rapidly “spun up”, forming
a coherently rotating disk ≈12 Gyr ago (Belokurov &
Kravtsov 2022; Conroy et al. 2022; Xiang & Rix 2022;
Xiang et al. 2025).
The young disk then faced, and
ultimately survived, a head-on collision with a massive
satellite ≈8−11 Gyr ago: this event, the “Gaia-Sausage-
Enceladus” (GSE) merger (Belokurov et al. 2018; Helmi
et al. 2018), scrambled and “splashed” the pre-existing
disk, but also brought in fresh fuel for star formation
and potentially seeded the formation of the thin disk
(Belokurov et al. 2020; Grand et al. 2020, for example).
While many aspects of the GSE merger, including its
impact on the MW’s disk and σ(τ), remain unclear (for
example Di Matteo et al. 2019; Fern´andez-Alvar et al.
2025; Sk´ulad´ottir et al. 2025), it is generally agreed to be
the MW’s last major merger to date (though see Donlon
et al. 2024, about the timing of this event).
Furthermore, there is growing consensus that certain
aspects of the MW’s formation history – namely its early
disk formation and quiescent merger history since z∼2 –
are atypical among present-day disk galaxies (Hammer
et al. 2007; Evans et al. 2020; Mackereth et al. 2018;
Kruijssen et al. 2019a; Semenov et al. 2024a). Given how
much of our understanding of galaxy formation, and in
particular post-formation dynamical heating, is bench-
marked against the MW, it is imperative to understand
whether the MW is indeed an outlier or simply appears
so due to observational bias remains an open question.
Because we view it from the inside, we cannot measure it
using the same methods applied to other galaxies, com-
plicating efforts to draw meaningful comparisons (though
see Fielder et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024).
One of the best ways to distinguish these possibilities
is via spatially resolved measurements of σ(τ) in nearby
disk galaxies. M31 and M33, the only other disk galax-
ies in the Local Group (LG), provide our best oppor-
tunities, motivating surveys by the Hubble Space Tele-
scope (HST) (Dalcanton et al. 2012; Williams et al. 2021)
and ground-based observatories (Guhathakurta et al.
2006; McConnachie et al. 2009, 2018; Bhattacharya et al.
2019).
In many ways, M31 is similar to the MW: a
slightly more massive disk galaxy with a central bar, spi-
ral arms, and modest star formation (Yin et al. 2009;
Mutch et al. 2011).
In other ways, M31 is a foil to
the quiescent MW: it likely experienced a major (≈4: 1)
merger ≈2.5 Gyr ago (Hammer et al. 2018; D’Souza &

Beyond the LG, integral-field spectroscopy (IFS) pro-
vides an intermediate view between galaxy-wide inte-
grated properties and individual stars. While many IFS
surveys, including MANGA (Bundy et al. 2015), SAMI
(Croom et al. 2012), and CALIFA (S´anchez et al. 2012),
strike a middle ground between survey scope and depth,
measuring spatially resolved kinematics for up to 10,000
galaxies at intermediate resolution (0.5 −1 kpc), some
surveys focus on higher-resolution (≲100 pc) measure-
ments for smaller samples. The PHANGS-MUSE survey
(Emsellem et al. 2022) obtained stellar kinematics for 19
star-forming galaxies at cloud-scale (≈50 −100 pc) res-
olution, measuring σ(τ) in disk galaxies outside the LG
for the first time (Pessa et al. 2023). These results, along
with those of other MUSE surveys, including MAD (den
Brok et al. 2020), TIMER (Gadotti et al. 2019), and
GECKOS (van de Sande et al. 2024), allow us to place
the detailed dynamical record of the MW (and the LG)
in a greater cosmological context.
Cosmological baryonic simulations are valuable tools
for interpreting observations of stellar kinematics, as they
now produce realistic disk galaxies spanning a wide range
of formation histories (for reviews see Naab & Ostriker
2017; Vogelsberger et al. 2020; Crain & van de Voort
2023). Zoom-in simulations, in particular, contain both
cosmological nonequilibrium perturbations like mergers
and mass growth, with the resolution needed to model
the multiphase ISM, enabling star formation and feed-
back to be treated self-consistently in a broad context.
Notably, these simulations resolve both global galaxy
properties (e.g., morphology, disk dynamics) and the
smaller-scale structures (e.g., GMCs, spiral arms, bars)
that drive post-formation dynamical heating (Benincasa
et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2022; Ansar et al. 2025). This en-
ables detailed analysis of the evolving strength and bal-
ance between birth kinematics and subsequent heating
across cosmic time. By tracing galaxy evolution across
time and comparing simulated galaxies with each other
and with observations, these models reveal how present-
day kinematics reflect both birth conditions and cumu-
lative dynamical processes imprinted on σ(τ).
In this work, we focus on σ(τ) in the MW and nearby
disk galaxies of similar mass, using FIRE-2 simulations
(described in §2) as a comparison baseline.
Although
some of these works (for example Dorman et al. 2015)
note that the observed σ(τ) in M31 differs from that
of the MW, no work has compiled and compared all
available measurements of σ(τ) in nearby MW-mass disk
galaxies. In this work, we aim to (1) put these observa-
tions on more equal footing, and (2) provide a cogent
view of the state of the literature.
In §3, we assess how measurement considerations, in-
cluding aperture size, galaxy inclination, age uncertain-
ties, and galactocentric radius, affect the inferred σ(τ).
In §4, we compile existing observations, focusing exclu-
sively on star-forming disk galaxies; we exclude non-disky
low-mass systems such as the LMC, SMC, and other MW
satellites (for example Cole et al. 2005; Olsen & Massey
2007; Leaman et al. 2017), as well as lenticular/S0 galax-
ies (Poci et al. 2019, 2021). We summarize and interpret
these results in the discussion (§5).
2. METHODS
2.1. FIRE-2 Simulations
We study the stellar disks of 11 simulated MW-
mass galaxies from the Feedback in Realistic Environ-
ments (FIRE) project 2, including 5 isolated galaxies
from the Latte suite (Wetzel et al. 2016) and 3 LG-
like pairs from the ELVIS on FIRE suite (Garrison-
Kimmel et al. 2018). These baryonic cosmological sim-
ulations model the physics of stars and gas (in addition
to dark matter) in a “zoom-in” region embedded within
a lower-resolution cosmological background.
Crucially,
this zoom-in method enables these simulations to reach
the resolution needed to resolve the multiphase ISM and
GMCs.
These simulations use the zoom-in technique to simu-
late MW-mass galaxies at high resolution within a fully
cosmological context. We first run dark-matter-only cos-
mological simulations within a periodic volume of side
length 70.4−172 Mpc, assuming a flat ΛCDM cosmology
with parameters broadly consistent with Planck Collab-
oration et al. (2020): h = 0.68−0.71, ΩΛ = 0.69−0.734,
Ωm = 0.266−0.31, Ωb = 0.0455−0.048, σ8 = 0.801−0.82
and ns = 0.961 −0.97. After selecting a region centered
on a halo (or pair of halos) of interest at z = 0, we use
MUSIC (Hahn & Abel 2011) to identify the particles
within the region, trace them back to z ≈99, and re-
generate the initial conditions for that region at higher
resolution and including baryons.
For the Latte suite, halo selection is based solely on
their mass at z = 0 (M200m ≈1 −2 × 1012 M⊙) and
relative isolation from similarly massive halos.
The
ELVIS on FIRE suite selection is a pair of neighboring
(d < 600−1000 kpc), massive (M200m ≈1−3×1012 M⊙
halos approaching each other, to mimic the LG. We em-
phasize that the selection of these halos was agnostic to
any halo properties beyond mass (and LG-like environ-
ment), including formation history, concentration, spin,
or satellite population. That is, there was no attempt to
recreate the MW, or any other individual galaxy, or the
larger-scale environment (beyond the LG). We exclude
three galaxies (m12r, m12w, and m12z) from our analy-
sis, because they lack coherent (rotationally-dominated)
stellar disks, making them poor analogs for the observed
disk galaxies we compare against.
We ran the simulations using GIZMO3, a multi-
method gravity plus (magneto)hydrodynamics code, in
its meshless finite-mass (MFM) mode. This mesh-free

FIRE-2 simulations can resolve key phase structures in
the ISM, allowing gas to collapse into GMCs (Benin-

self-consistently generate 11 elements (H, He, C, N, O,
Ne, Mg, Si, S, Ca, Fe), including a model for sub-grid
mixing/diffusion via turbulence (Hopkins 2016; Su et al.
2017; Escala et al. 2018).
Star formation occurs in gas that is dense (n >
1000 cm−3), self-gravitating (following Hopkins et al.
2013), self-shielding and molecular (following Krumholz
& Gnedin 2011), and Jeans unstable. If all of these cri-
teria are met, a gas cell probabilistically converts to a
star particle in a local gravitational free-fall time. Each
star particle represents a single stellar population sam-
pled from a Kroupa (2001) initial mass function, with
mass and metallicity inherited from its progenitor gas
cell.
Once formed, star particles follow stellar evolu-
tion models that tabulate feedback event rates, luminosi-
ties, energies, and mass-loss rates from STARBURST99
v7.0 (Leitherer et al. 1999). FIRE-2 implements all of
the major channels for stellar feedback, including core-
collapse and white-dwarf (Type Ia) supernovae, stellar
winds, radiation pressure, photoionization, and photo-
electric heating.
FIRE-2 also includes photoionization
and heating from a spatially uniform, redshift-dependent
UV background (Faucher-Gigu`ere et al. 2009) that reion-
izes the Universe at z ≈10.
2.2. Selecting Stars and Measuring Velocity Dispersion
We measure all positions and velocities in galactocen-
tric cylindrical coordinates (R, ϕ, Z). To determine the
center of each galaxy – and thus our coordinate system –
we iteratively compute the mean center-of-mass position
of all stars using the “shrinking spheres” method until
the spherical radius drops below 10 pc. We then align
our coordinate system with the principal axes of the stel-
lar disk, as defined by the moment-of-inertia tensor of the
25% youngest stars inside a radius that encloses 90% of
the total stellar mass within 10 kpc. Although we define
our coordinate system using the disk-scale properties of
young stars, we measure stellar kinematics on local aper-
tures, which in principle can have distinct alignments
relative to other apertures and the galaxy as a whole.
However, we found that our results are insensitive to the
specifics of our coordinate system, for example, if we in-
stead assign our axes using just the young stars within
that aperture, or if we use gas or different definitions for
young stars.
McCluskey et al. (2024) showed that, in these FIRE-2
simulations, σ of cold gas and young stars at formation
strongly depend on the spatial scale over which one mea-
sures them. Specifically, when measured in apertures of
radius 250 pc, σ of cold gas and young stars at forma-
tion are 3 times and 2 times lower than when measured
across an entire galactocentric annulus. We now extend
this analysis to the present-day σ of stars of various ages.
Within each aperture, we compute the velocity disper-
sion, σ, for a given velocity component (vR, vϕ, or vZ,
as half of the mass-weighted 16th–84th inter-percentile
range, which is equivalent to the standard deviation for
normal distribution.

σ within individual apertures occasionally depends on
this threshold, with higher Nmin restricting our results
toward denser apertures, which are generally (but not

2.3. Age Binning
In Section 3, we show results for stars in five represen-
tative age ranges: 0−0.1, 0.1−0.6, 1.75−2.25, 5.75−6.25,
and 10.75 −11.75 Gyr. We choose these age ranges to
sample the full dynamical history of the disk and also
to complement observations of resolved stars and stellar
populations in nearby galaxies.
The youngest age bin, 0 −100 Myr allows us to study
the kinematics of stars near birth and thus probe the dy-
namical state of the star-forming ISM. Although stars in-
herit the kinematics of their progenitor gas cells, dynami-
cal heating of stars quickly can increase and decouple the
(effectively dissipationless) stars from the (dissipational)
gas. This narrow age range also allows us to compare
with observations of young stars in nearby galaxies.
The youngest age bins probed by observations of M31
and M33 have mean ages of 30 and 80 Myr, while P23
presented σ(τ) of stellar populations in PHANGS galax-
ies with luminosity-weighted ages < 100 Myr.
Our second age bin is 100−600 Myr. While still young,
these stars have evolved over 1-3 disk dynamical times, so
they provide a window into the internal heating processes
that shape stellar dynamics in the disk. This age range
also overlaps with the youngest age bins used in MW
observations, and it bookends the ages of young AGB
stars in M31 (which have a mean age of 400 Myr), and
exactly matches the “intermediate” age bin of P23 for
PHANGS galaxies.
For the three older age bins, we chose age ranges that
correspond to the typical midpoints of the disk dynami-
cal eras that we presented for the FIRE-2 simulations in
McCluskey et al. (2024). Briefly, McCluskey et al. (2024)
found that the formation of stellar disks – as traced by
the vϕ/σ of newly formed stars – proceeded in three dis-
tinct phases.
Stars in the “Pre-Disk” Era formed on
non-disk, dispersion-dominated orbits. Next, the “Early-
Disk” Era commenced once stars began to form (perma-
nently) on rotation-dominated orbits. These early disks
were thick and kinematically hot, with high σ. Subse-
quent generations of stars formed on increasingly rota-
tionally dominated orbits, and eventually the galaxies
settled into their “Late-Disk” Era, forming stars on con-
sistently dynamically cold orbits.

3. MEASUREMENT EFFECTS
In this section, we use the FIRE-2 simulations to inves-
tigate how uncertainties, systematics, and measurement
choices impact the measured stellar velocity dispersions.
We seek to provide guidance for interpreting observa-
tions, comparing them against each other, and compar-
ing them against simulations.
3.1. Age Uncertainties
Stellar ages are difficult to measure precisely, and their
uncertainties are generally ≳20 −30% (for example
Soderblom 2010). Fortunately, the synergy of astromet-
ric and spectroscopic surveys with asteroseismological
data has driven tremendous progress in the past decade
(for example Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Mackereth et al.
2019).
Nevertheless, stellar-age uncertainties continue
to hinder our ability to accurately reconstruct the for-
mation history of the MW and nearby galaxies, both
by obscuring merger features (Martig et al. 2014; Buck
et al. 2020) and by decreasing inferred dynamical heating
(Aumer et al. 2016).
We explore the impact of age uncertainties on the mea-
sured σ(τ).
To model this, we generate new ages for
each star particle, randomly drawing “measured” ages
from Gaussian distributions centered on the true age
with standard deviations corresponding to 10, 20, 30,
and 40% (fractional) uncertainties. We then recompute
σ in each age bin using these ages.
The degree to which age uncertainties influence σ(τ)
depends mainly on the steepness and shape of the true

dependence on age. In general, if σ(τ) is smooth and
slowly varying, modest age uncertainties do little to dis-
tort the overall trend. However, by mixing populations
across age bins, uncertainties generally lead to an in-
ferred dependence on age that is shallower than truth.
Furthermore, if σ(τ) exhibits sharp features – such as
spikes or breaks in slope – age uncertainties can smear
these signatures by mixing stars across bins with different
intrinsic kinematics. The impact also varies with age: for
a fixed fractional age uncertainty, absolute uncertainties
are smaller at younger ages. Accordingly, we expect min-
imal effects from age errors at young ages, even when the
slope is steep, and more noticeable smoothing at older
ages, where the population size is limited, absolute un-
certainties are larger, and slopes are (occasionally) steep.
3.1.1. Case Studies
Figure 1 shows the total stellar velocity dispersion,
σ3D, versus stellar age, τ in four of our galaxies: m12m
(top left), m12f (top right), Romeo (bottom left), and
Louise (bottom right).
We selected these galaxies to
bracket the mass range of the observed systems that we
compare against in Section 4 – namely the MW, M31,
and M33 – and to serve as useful analogs: Louise is com-
parable in mass to M33, Romeo resembles the MW, and
m12m is a comparable stellar mass to M31. We also in-
clude m12f to illustrate a merger-driven spike in its σ(τ)
and to demonstrate how such features are affected by age
uncertainties.
All galaxies show a characteristic rise in σ(τ) with age,
the result of disk settling and post-formation dynamical
heating, as (McCluskey et al. 2024) explored for these
galaxies. The youngest stars exhibit the most rapid in-
crease, because heating is most efficient for dynamically
cold populations (Binney & Tremaine 2008). However,
the exact shape of σ(τ) reflects the unique history of each
galaxy and, because of this, age uncertainties impart dif-
ferent effects on each galaxy.
With a stellar mass of 1011 M⊙, m12m is our most
massive galaxy and the closest to M31’s stellar mass of
10.3 ± 2.3 × 1010 M⊙(Sick et al. 2015). In m12m, σ rises
steadily with age between 1 and 7 Gyr, which in this
case primarily reflects the continued settling of the star-
forming ISM (McCluskey et al. 2024). For stars younger
than 6 Gyr, the impact of age uncertainties is weak, while
for older ages, age uncertainties reduce σ and flatten its
age dependence.
m12f highlights the dramatic impact that a gas-rich
merger can have and the equally dramatic impact that
age uncertainties have on the observability of this feature.
m12f underwent a merger (of stellar mass ratio ≈1:13)
≈6.9 Gyr ago, after it entered its Early-Disk Era, but
before it settled into a thin disk ≈4.6 Gyr ago. This
merger led to a significant spike in the present-day σ
of stars formed around that time. When measured with
perfect age precision, this spike is dramatic, and it would
provide a direct way to age-date the merger. However,
even 10% age uncertainties reduce this spike to a gentle
bump, and 20% age uncertainties wash it out entirely.
Not all mergers drive such spikes in σ today. m12f un-
derwent another merger ≈500 Myr ago with an LMC-
mass satellite. This merger was by no means negligible:
for example, Necib et al. (2019) shows that this merger
accounts for 53% of the accreted stellar mass in the so-
lar circle, while Yu et al. (2021) show that this merger
triggered a significant burst of star formation.
Never-
theless, σ(τ) remains comparatively smooth, even in the
absence of age uncertainties.
Thus, merger signatures
in σ(τ) depend on the timing and orbit of the merger.
The fact that mergers that coincide with (and perhaps
facilitate) disk formation can lead to spikes in σ(τ) is
particularly relevant for the MW, given that its disk for-
mation was potentially coincident with the GSE merger
(for example Conroy et al. 2022). Correctly character-
izing such merger signatures require age uncertainties of
≲10%, with the earlier timing of GSE (≈10 Gyr ago)
potentially necessitating even tighter constraints within
an already enigmatic age regime.
Romeo is our earliest-forming galaxy, both in terms
of stellar mass assembly and the onset of disk forma-
tion, which occurred ≈11 Gyr ago (McCluskey et al.
2024). Thus, in many ways, it provides our best MW
analog, in terms of an early-forming disk (Belokurov &
Kravtsov 2022; Xiang & Rix 2022), being in an LG-like
environment, and having a stellar mass of 5.9×1010 M⊙.
In the absence of age uncertainties, Romeo’s σ3D in-
creases steadily with age, and with a sharper rise ≈8
and 12 Gyr ago, which is the steepest relation for old
stars among our sample.
Thus, for Romeo, fractional
age uncertainties significantly reduce σ in older stars by
up to 50 −75 km s−1.
Given that Romeo is our best
dynamical analog to the MW, this suggests particular
caution in interpreting σ of old stars in the MW.
Louise is the lowest-mass galaxy in our sample, with
Mstar ≈2.3×1010 M⊙, and is thus closest to approaching
the even lower mass of M33 – 4.8 × 109 M⊙(Corbelli
et al. 2014). For Louise, σ rises gradually with age up to
6 Gyr, but σ increases rapidly between ≈6 and 9 Gyr
ago, reflecting the rapid settling of its disk. However,
increasing age uncertainties shift this rapid rise to occur
more recently, by up to 2 Gyr. In contrast, at ages ≳
9 Gyr, the flatness of σ(τ) leads to only minor effects for
age uncertainties.
Ultimately, the impact of age uncertainties varies be-
tween galaxies and depends on a galaxy’s formation his-
tory. Age uncertainties can change our inference about
the ISM settling history and dynamical heating of old
stars (weakly for m12m, strongly for Romeo), the merger
history (m12f), and the disk formation/settling time
(Louise).

up to ≈10%, and σZ changes by ≲5%. The dispro-

shows the strongest time evolution (both across all com-

the (generally rapid) onset of disk formation. Although
the median vR and vZ of ∼4 and 8 Gyr old stars are
usually broadly similar at ∼0 km/s, the median vϕ can
differ by up to ∼200 km/s. Thus, the faulty inclusion
of older, non-disky stars artificially increases σϕ. Lastly,
age uncertainties reduce σ by up to 10−20% for Pre-Disk

inflating its value by up to 30% at intermediate ages
(4 −8 Gyr old). In comparison, the effects on σR and
σZ are generally weaker.
Ultimately, the effect of age
uncertainties on the total σ3D is generally ≲15% at any
given age. However, we repeat that these are the average
shifts in σ at fixed age across our galaxy sample. Within
individual galaxies, the effect of age uncertainties can be
more dramatic, shifting the inferred ages of certain kine-
matic features (as shown in Figure 1).
3.2. Aperture Size
Next, we study how measurements of σ(τ) depend on
physical scale. The long-known size-linewidth relation,
that σGMC ∝l0.5 (Larson 1981), indicates that σ of
molecular gas increases with the size of the region, often
as a power law (Heyer & Dame 2015; Rice et al. 2016;
Rosolowsky et al. 2021; Leroy et al. 2025). This arises
because the star-forming ISM is supersonically turbu-
lent, and GMCs reflect this hierarchical turbulent struc-
ture. The FIRE-2 simulations show this behavior in the
ISM, with various works confirming that star-forming gas
and GMCs in the simulations follow the observed size-
linewidth relation (Hopkins et al. 2018; Hung et al. 2019;
Guszejnov et al. 2020).
Stars form from dense gas and thus inherit the clus-
tered distribution of their natal ISM. In turn, σ of newly-
formed stars is correspondingly scale-dependent (Lada
& Lada 2003; Krumholz et al. 2019; Maschmann et al.
2024). However, this inherited scale dependence rapidly
evolves and is erased on timescales of ≈100 Myr, as stel-
lar orbits mix and evolve (Grasha et al. 2019; Peltonen
et al. 2023).
McCluskey et al. (2024) showed in these FIRE-2 sim-
ulations that σ of young stars (and cold gas) increased
by ≈2× when measured across an annulus versus when
measured in small (150 pc) apertures, in part because
larger apertures incorporate later-scale disk dynamical
structures such as spiral arms, bars, and warps.
This
aperture dependence provides an important context for
interpreting and comparing observational work. Histor-
ically, many studies of the MW focused on stars within
a few 10’s of pc of the Sun, while more recent surveys

youngest stars (age < 100 Myr) have the strongest ra-

The youngest stars (age<100 Myr) show the strongest
radial dependence: young stars in the inner galaxy have
nearly twice the σ as their cohorts in the outer galaxy.
This strong radial dependence is largely – though not
exclusively – inherited from the star-forming ISM. In
these FIRE-2 simulations, the gas surface density, Σgas,
generally decreases by an order of magnitude between
R = 2 −12 kpc (Graf et al. 2024). This outward radial
decline in σ also results from radially-dependent post-
formation heating, and post-formation dynamical heat-
ing may be stronger in the inner galaxy (Sch¨onrich &
Binney 2009; Aumer et al. 2016).
These trends also reflect the radial redistribution of
these populations.
Stars do not necessarily remain at
the R where they formed, but, via various dynamical pro-
cesses, can redistribute across multiple kpc (for example
Sellwood & Binney 2002; Roˇskar et al. 2012; Vera-Ciro
et al. 2014; Daniel & Wyse 2018; El-Badry et al. 2018a;
Frankel et al. 2020). In contrast to dynamical heating,
post-formation radial redistribution has a negligible ef-
fect on stars younger than ≈100 Myr. This is not neces-
sarily the case for older stellar populations. Indeed, 400
Myr and 2 Gyr old stars show the weakest radial depen-
dence, despite forming with a radial dependence similar
to that of 100 Myr old stars, due to radial redistribution.
While stars from the cold outer disk exhibit a net inward
redistribution, stars from the hot inner disk exhibit a
net outward distribution (Bellardini et al. in prep.). In
turn, this mixing subsequently weakens the strong radial
dependence inherited at formation.
Stars 6 and 11 Gyr old exhibit a stronger radial de-
pendence than those 400 Myr and 2 Gyr old.
This
might seem to contradict our earlier claim, given that
these stars had more time to redistribute and weaken
any initial radial dependence. However, when these stars
formed, these galaxies were more radially compact, ver-
tically thick, and highly turbulent (for example El-Badry
et al. 2018b; Horta et al. 2024). Over time, these galaxies
grew and formed disks, with subsequent generations of
stars forming in increasingly thin and radially extended
disk (Yu et al. 2022; Carrillo et al. 2023; Bellardini et al.
2022). Thus, while Late-Disk stars formed throughout
this radial range (R = 2 −12 kpc), older stars predomi-
nantly formed in the (now) inner galaxy.
The stronger radial dependence exhibited by stars that
formed in the Early-Disk and Pre-Disk Eras compared
with those that formed in the Late-Disk Era (save the
youngest population) agrees with observations. For ex-
ample, Aniyan et al. (2018) shows that in the nearby
spiral galaxy, NGC 628, stars older than 3 Gyr exhibit
a ≈50% stronger radial dependence than their younger
counterparts.

fits-all correction can misrepresent the intrinsic kinemat-

all, one must apply age-dependent corrections before in-
terpreting trends such as dynamical heating or other evo-
lutionary processes.
4. OBSERVATIONAL COMPARISONS
Thus far, we used FIRE-2 simulations to investigate
how age uncertainties, aperture size, galactocentric ra-
dius, and galaxy inclination influence measurements of
stellar velocity dispersion. These results provide us with
a framework for combining and comparing disparate ob-
servations of σ(τ) in observed galaxies. We now compile
observations from the literature, including the MW, M31,
M33, and PHANGS galaxies, and we compare σ(τ) in
these galaxies. We then use these observations to bench-
mark how well the FIRE-2 simulations model σ(τ).

Like the MW, M31

For example, M31’s stellar halo is ≈10× more

containing significant tidal debris and a metal-rich com-

population (Dorman et al. 2013; Escala et al. 2023). Sim-

RGB stars having a scale height of ≈770 pc (Dalcanton
et al. 2023). Simulations indicate that these properties
are consistent with M31 experiencing a major (≈4 : 1)
merger ≈2.5 Gyr ago (D’Souza & Bell 2018; Hammer
et al. 2018), which is likely related to M31’s contem-
poraneous global burst of star formation (for example
Williams et al. 2015).
Much of this knowledge of M31 results from large-scale
surveys of resolved stellar populations throughout M31’s
disk and halo. Two notable surveys of M31’s disk are
the Panchromatic Hubble Andromeda Treasury (PHAT)
survey (Dalcanton et al. 2012; Williams et al. 2014), a
Hubble Space Telescope (HST) MultiCycle Treasury pro-
gram that provided six-filter photometry for 138 million
stars, and the Spectroscopic and Panchromatic Land-
scape of Andromeda’s Stellar Halo (SPLASH) survey
(Guhathakurta et al. 2006; Gilbert et al. 2012; Dorman
et al. 2012), which obtained Keck/DEIMOS spectra for
≈10, 000 stars. These two surveys allowed for the first
measurements of σ(τ) in a disk galaxy other than the
MW.
M31’s largest satellite, M33, allows us to study a disk
at lower masses. Lying 859 kpc from the MW, M33 offers
a holistic view and resolved stellar populations.
With
a stellar mass of 3 × 1010 M⊙, M33 provides a crucial
perspective on the formation of galactic disks, as this is
precisely the “mass of disk formation”, that is, the mass
where the observed (stellar mass) Tully-Fisher relation
transitions (Simons et al. 2015). HST provided six-filter
photometry for millions of disk stars, here for ≈22 mil-
lion stars via the Panchromatic Hubble Andromeda Trea-
sury: Triangulum Extended Region (PHATTER) sur-
vey (Williams et al. 2021), while complementary spectro-
scopic surveys provide line-of-sight velocities for a small
fraction of the photometric sample.
For example, the
TRiangulum EXtended Survey (TREX) survey (Quirk
et al. 2022, hereafter Q22) measured spectra for ≈7000
stars across the disk of M33. Importantly, these two sur-
veys allowed Q22 to measure σ(τ) in M33 using ≈3000
stars.
Within the LG, we can resolve individual stars, but
the number of disk galaxies is limited.
Fortunately,
integral-field spectroscopy (IFS) of nearby galaxies of-
fers a compelling new window, with IFS instruments like
MUSE on the VLT providing spatially resolved spec-
tra of stellar populations on ≈100 pc scales.
Re-
cently, P23 used MUSE to measure σ(τ) in the 19 star-
forming galaxies comprising the PHANGS-MUSE sam-
ple. These galaxies lie within ≲20 Mpc of the MW, have
low to moderate inclinations (< 60◦), and have stellar
masses of log(M⋆/M⊙) = 9.4 −11.0, with a median of
log(M⋆/M⊙) = 10.5. Although P23 measured σ(τ) for
all 19 of these galaxies, we only include results from the
16 that also have reported rotational velocity measure-
ments from Lang et al. (2020). An important caveat for
interpreting the results of this work is that these σ from
MUSE are likely overestimated at small intrinsic values
(and thus at young ages), as we discuss in Section 4.3.

We make this cut in α-element populations for two
reasons. First, the relative fractions of high- and low-α
stars are almost always far from equal at any given age.
Given that the vast majority of high-α stars have ages
≳8 Gyr while most low-α stars exhibit ages spanning
0 −10 Gyr, this cut provides a broadly sensible distinc-
tion. That said, a significant number of stars seemingly
depart from the simple heuristic that high-α stars are
old and low-α stars are young. Much debate has sur-
rounded the nature of reportedly young (age < 6 Gyr)
high-α stars, with a general consensus that these stars
are not genuinely young, but are instead the product of
binary evolution, having gained mass through mergers
or accretion (for example Miglio et al. 2021; Jofr´e et al.
2023; Grisoni et al. 2024). On the other end, the nature
of old (> 10 Gyr) low-α stars remains unclear, poten-
tially because the formation of these populations truly
overlapped (Silva Aguirre et al. 2018), or because signifi-
cant older high-alpha stars are subject to significant age
uncertainties (Haywood et al. 2013). See Buder et al.
(2019) for further discussion. Nevertheless, the mean σ
for stars with ages ≳8 Gyr is largely unaffected by this
cut, with the inclusion or exclusion of old low-α stars
changing the mean σ by ≲5%.
In Figure 6, all three components (σR, σϕ, σZ) exhibit
a broadly similar age dependence: a steady rise for stars
younger than ≈8 Gyr followed by a shallower slope at
older ages. This flattening is most apparent in σR and σϕ,
whereas σZ continues to increase with age more notice-
ably, albeit more gradually than it does at younger ages.
Across all ages, the radial component dominates, with
the mean σR exceeding the means of both σϕ and σZ by
at least 50%. For stars younger than ≈8 Gyr, σϕ > σZ,
while the two components become roughly comparable
for older populations.
These trends – σZ’s continued
rise, σR’s dominance, and σϕ and σZ’s convergence at
old ages – are in remarkable agreement with the results
of McCluskey et al. (2024) for FIRE-2 (see their Figure
4). We further explore the physical drivers of the rela-
tive strength and evolution of each component of σ in
Section 4.4.2.
Despite the general agreement among the different ob-
servational datasets that each component of σ increases
with age, there is considerable scatter in both the nor-
malization and slope of the relation. Even after discount-
ing potentially biased measurements from high- or low-α
samples, the total 3D velocity dispersion at fixed age
can vary by ≈25%, with σZ alone varying by up to 50%.
Nevertheless, none of the observational trends show evi-
dence of a factor of two increase in σ over the age range.

crepancy at young ages likely results from the fact that
the observed σLOS in PHANGS is overestimated, for two
reasons.
First, the true velocity dispersion, σtrue, of young pop-
ulations is typically lower than the spectral resolution
of the MUSE instrument, σinst ≈62 km s−1 (Emsellem
et al. 2022). If σtrue < σinst, the observed line profile
is dominated by the spectrograph’s line-spread function,
effectively masking the intrinsic broadening of the nar-
rower σLOS.
The tendency of Penalized Pixel Fitting
(pPXF) to systematically overestimate σ when σtrue is
close to or near instrumental dispersions is well studied
(for example Cappellari et al. 2011; Falc´on-Barroso et al.
2017; van de Sande et al. 2017; Varidel et al. 2020): vari-
ous works found that this bias increases as σtrue decreases
but largely disappears as σtrue exceeds the instrumental
resolution.
Second, PHANGS measurements are not for individ-
ual stars, but for aggregate stellar populations within
≈100 pc apertures. For each aperture, the reported age
is the luminosity-weighted average age of its constituent
stars; moreover, because young stars dominate the light,
these ages are biased by younger, brighter populations.
Thus, even apertures deemed very young could include a
significant number of older stars. In turn, the PHANGS
measurement will overestimate σ of “young populations”,
because even though older stars contribute negligibly to
the light, they can contribute substantially to the line
broadening (Wang et al. 2024). Together, these results
indicate that σ is typically overestimated by ≳10 km s−1
relative to its true value in the youngest age bin, with the
intermediate age bin likely showing weaker, but non-zero,
systematic biases, while the oldest age bin is unaffected.
Therefore, one should take the observed values at the
youngest ages shown in Figure 8 (right) with a grain
of salt, and to the extent that they are lower in real-
ity, they would agree better with FIRE-2. At the oldest
ages, where these instrumental effects are less important,
FIRE-2 and PHANGS agree well.
Overall, σ(τ) in the FIRE-2 simulations exhibits re-
markable similarity to those observed in M31 and M33,
and is largely consistent with those in PHANGS galax-
ies, considering measurement effects. This concordance
goes against the standard lore that cosmological simula-
tions have hotter kinematics than observed galaxies (for
example House et al. 2011). However, as we will show,
this newfound harmony between (the stellar disk dynam-
ics in) FIRE-2 and observations is not replicated for the
MW.

5. SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION
5.1. Summary of Results
We investigated the stellar age velocity (dispersion) re-
lation, σ(τ), which is a key tracer of both stellar birth
conditions (from the star-forming ISM) and subsequent
dynamical heating of stars in disk galaxies. We used a
suite of 11 MW–mass galaxies from the FIRE-2 cosmo-
logical zoom-in simulations to assess how various obser-
vational effects influence the measured σ(τ), finding:
• Age uncertainties of up to 40% alter σ by typically
≲20% at any given age. However, age uncertain-
ties can significantly affect the slope of the relation
between σ and τ, and age uncertainties >10% can
erase sharp features of major mergers.
• For young stars (age ≲100 Myr), σ increases sig-
nificantly with the measured aperture radius, rap,
out to > 1 kpc, reflecting turbulence in the star-
forming ISM. However, for stars older than this, σ
is essentially independent (to within a few percent)
of aperture radius, at least at rap ≳250 pc, where
FIRE-2 simulations spatially resolve σ at that age.
• σ increases with decreasing galactocentric radius
at all ages, by a factor of 1.5 −2 from R = 12 to
2 kpc. The youngest stars have the strongest ra-
dial dependence: stars are born dynamically hot-
ter towards the galactic center. However, slightly
older stars (≈400 Myr old) show the weakest de-
pendence, likely from radial redistribution, while
progressively older stars show somewhat stronger
radial dependence.
• Galaxy inclination affects measured line-of-sight
dispersion, σLOS, by up to a factor of 2, with
larger σLOS for inclinations > 50◦compared to
face-on observations.
This is because generally
σR > σϕ > σZ, so a face-on disk generally leads
to the smallest σLOS.
This effect is the largest
for fairly young stars (0.4 −2 Gyr) but weaker for
newly-formed stars (ages ≲100 Myr are affected
up to 40%). Stars ≳6 Gyr old, being the most
isotropic, are affected by at most 25-30%.
We compiled observational measurements of σ(τ) from
the literature: 13 analyses of the MW and analyses of
M31, M33, and 16 PHANGS galaxies.
We compared
them primarily in terms of σ(τ)/vϕ,0, where vϕ,0 is the
rotation velocity today, because this dimensionless ratio
provides the most robust way to compare these galaxies
that span over an order of magnitude in stellar mass. We
found:
• All analyses of the MW show an increase in σ with
age for all velocity components (vR, vϕ, vZ), by a
factor of 2 −3 over the last ≈13 Gyr. However,
these works show substantial scatter (≈50%), in
both the normalization of σ at fixed age, and shape
of the relation of σ with age. Within the MW, σR
dominates, being at least 50% greater than σϕ and
σZ at all ages.
• M31, M33, and the average PHANGS galaxy ex-
hibit remarkably consistent values of σ(τ)/vϕ,0.
M31 and M33 are nearly identical and generally
within the 1σ range of the PHANGS sample.
• Thus, among this sample of 19 nearby disk galaxies,
the MW is a strong kinematic outlier. The MW
is dynamically colder than M31, M33, and 15 of
the 16 PHANGS galaxies. The MW is similar to
one PHANGS galaxy, NGC 1433, so importantly,
it is possible to measure at least one other nearby
galaxy as dynamically cold as the MW¿
Finally, we compared σ(τ)/vϕ,0 in the FIRE-2 simula-
tions against these observations, accounting for the ob-
servational effects that we explored. We found:
• FIRE-2 galaxies show good agreement with M31
and M33 across all measured ages.
FIRE-2
matches PHANGS galaxies at older ages, but at
the youngest ages (≲100 Myr), σ/vϕ,0 is lower in
FIRE-2 by a factor of 4.
However, this discrep-
ancy is likely not physical, but instead reflects the
(well-known) limitations of measuring small σ with
MUSE.
• FIRE-2 galaxies show σ(τ)/vϕ,0 typically 2 −3×
higher than the MW at a given age. The youngest
stars show the best agreement, indicating that
post-formation dynamical heating is more rapid in
FIRE-2 than in the MW.
• The ratios of components of σ in FIRE-2 agree with
the MW within the 1σ scatter, though there are
offsets in the average relations.
In both FIRE-2
and the MW, σR > σϕ > σZ at essentially all ages.

the existence of a similarly cold galaxy actually strength-

Furthermore, recent observational works arrive at simi-
lar conclusions regarding the MW’s typicality. Bershady
et al. (2024) estimated σ(τ) for 500 star-forming disk
galaxies in the MANGA survey by parameterizing their
observed asymmetric drift, σAD, which is the lag between
the tangential speeds of stars and gas. Bershady et al.
(2024) also derived analogous σAD relations for the MW,
M31, and M33 from literature data, finding that while
M31 and M33 are broadly consistent with the typical
trends in MANGA, the MW falls far below the stan-
dard relation.
These findings also provide further in-
sight on the origin of the MW’s discrepancy, because
Bershady et al. (2024) parameterized their exponential
heating model via both the radial σ of young stars and a
heating index or “stratification rate”. These results indi-
cate that although young stars in the MW are atypically
cold, with similarly-massive MANGA galaxies exhibiting
radial σ nearly 3 times higher – the rate of heating in the
MW (over the past few Gyr) is well within the range of
observed values.
The unusual stellar kinematics of the MW likely re-
lates to its unique history, mainly, its early forming disk
and paucity of major mergers. As we discuss below (and
tritely summarize now), these factors allowed the major-
ity of the MW’s stars to form “disky” and subsequently
stay (reasonably) “disky” throughout the Galaxy’s life-
time.
The earliest epoch of the MW’s history has been
the subject of extensive recent study (for reviews see
Helmi 2020; Deason & Belokurov 2024).
Such studies
primarily focus on the orbit and abundance distribu-
tions of the Galaxy’s oldest, and thus, most metal poor,
stars.Notably, Belokurov & Kravtsov (2022) identify a
large sample of in-situ stars from APOGEE DR17 and
Gaia EDR3 that span iron abundances down to [Fe/H]
= −1.5, and show that the mean vϕ of these stars rapidly
increased from [Fe/H] of −1.2 to −1. They argue that
this increase traces the MW’s transition from a proto-
galaxy with negligible net rotation to a coherent disk
with vϕ ≈150 km s−1 within 1 −2 Gyr. Subsequently,
Conroy et al. (2022) used the H3 survey to extend this
analysis to even lower metallicities, and by measuring
the ages of these stars, argued that the disk formed
≈10 −12 Gyr ago. On the other hand, Xiang & Rix
(2022) and Xiang et al. (2025) studied 250,000 subgiant
stars in LAMOST (with a remarkably low typical age
uncertainty of ≈8%) and concluded that the MW’s disk
emerged ≳13 Gyr ago, formed a total stellar mass of
3.7×109M⊙by 13 Gyr ago, and then further experienced
a 2-3 Gyr period of intense star formation.
Indeed, the exact timing of the formation of the MW’s
disk remains rather uncertain (for example Viswanathan
et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024a; Horta & Schiavon 2025).
For example, age uncertainties of 10% can shift inferred
disk formation times to 1-2 Gyr earlier than in reality
(Zhang et al. 2024b), and accreted stars can masquerade
as in-situ disk populations (Santistevan et al. 2021; Mar-
dini et al. 2022). Furthermore, the stellar orbits that we
measure today do not necessarily reflect those at birth,
especially for the oldest stars: McCluskey et al. (2024)
showed that stars need not form with disk-like kinemat-
ics to exhibit significant coherent rotation today, given
possible subsequent torquing by mergers, satellite inter-
actions, or bars (see also Chandra et al. 2024). Neverthe-
less, most works agree that the MW’s (long-lived) disk

probably formed no later than 9 −10 Gyr ago.
This places the MW at the earliest end of the distribu-

Only ≈10 −13% of the MW-mass analogs
in the TNG50 and Artemis simulations form disks in
stars at such low metallicities (Dillamore et al. 2024;
Semenov et al. 2024a), and this is only 6% among the
E-MOSAICS simulations (Kruijssen et al. 2019b). We
note that some high redshift simulations form coherent
disks even earlier, at z = 4 −7 (Tamfal et al. 2022; Ko-
handel et al. 2024; van Donkelaar et al. 2024); however,
many of these are already massive at these early times,
with Mstar ≳1010 M⊙, and thus are not representative
of the typical MW-mass progenitors. Furthermore, when
comparing against the MW and nearby galaxies, it is crit-
ical to compare with simulations that have been run to
the present day and thus include the effects of long-term
dynamical heating of stars.
The MW’s early disk formation likely reflects its rapid
early mass assembly.5 Works based on the MW’s recon-
structed SFH (Snaith et al. 2014) and globular cluster
properties (Kruijssen et al. 2019b; Trujillo-Gomez et al.
2021) consistently predict that our Galaxy assembled
50% of its z=0 stellar mass by 10-10.5 (± 1.5) Gyr ago.
In contrast, typical MW-mass halos reached 50% of their
final stellar mass 2 −3 Gyr later than this, based on
semi-empirical models (Behroozi et al. 2019), abundance
matching of observed galaxy populations (Papovich et al.
2015), and reconstructing star-formation histories of low-
redshift galaxies (Pacifici et al. 2016).
Once a disk forms, it tends to self-regulate near Toomre
Q ≈1, enabling subsequent generations of stars to
form with progressively colder kinematics, provided its
gas fraction declines (as expected) (for example Gur-
vich et al. 2020). For example, McCluskey et al. (2024)
showed that FIRE-2 galaxies with earlier disk formation
times tend to form stars with colder present-day kine-
matics than those with later-forming disks (see also Se-
menov et al. 2024a).
Forming a disk is only half the battle; preserving it is
equally critical. Once disks have formed, mergers act to
dynamically heat existing stellar populations, potentially
reorienting and even destroying pre-existing disks (for ex-
ample Semenov et al. 2024c). In the limit that disks are
maintained, simulations consistently show that mergers
induce noticeable increases in stellar velocity dispersion
and imprint discontinuities in σ(τ) (Martig et al. 2014;
Grand et al. 2020; Khoperskov et al. 2023). The MW
likely underwent a major merger ≈10 Gyr ago (Helmi
et al. 2018; Belokurov et al. 2018). Since then, its merg-
ing history has been atypically tranquil for a galaxy of
its present-day mass (Hammer et al. 2007): for exam-
ple, Evans et al. (2020) found that only 5% of MW-mass
galaxies in the large-volume EAGLE simulation experi-
enced an early major merger with no subsequent massive
mergers since z=1 (which reduces to 0.65% if an LMC-
like satellite is also required). 6

quiescent merger history, as various works find earlier

These constrained simulations also help us to understand

though M31-mass and MW-mass galaxies formed sim-

& Bell 2018; Bhattacharya et al. 2023; Tsakonas et al.
2024). That said, M33’s merger history is more ambigu-
ous.
Its warped stellar and HI disks, coupled with a
burst of star formation in both M31 and M33 roughly
2–3 Gyr ago, have long been interpreted as signatures of
a past fly-by interaction with M31 (McConnachie et al.
2009; Bernard et al. 2012; Williams et al. 2017; Corbelli
& Burkert 2024). However, recent observations and or-
bital modeling strongly suggest that M33 is on its first
infall into the Local Group and has yet to undergo a close
pericentric passage with M31 (Patel et al. 2017; van der
Marel et al. 2019; Patel et al. 2025).
Lastly, we note that the MW’s cold kinematics, early
formation, and quiescent merger history are only a few
of its potentially atypical properties. For example, many
works argue that the MW is abnormally compact for
its stellar mass, having a remarkably short disk scale
radius (Rix & Bovy 2013; Licquia et al. 2016; Bland-
Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016). However, recent work ar-
gues that the MW’s size is actually typical, and that its
apparent compactness reflects the faulty assumption that
its disk follows a simple exponential profile (Lian et al.
2024).
When (Lian et al. 2024) fitted the MW’s sur-
face brightness profile nonparametrically, the Galaxy’s
measured half-light radius doubles, and its disk size is
consistent with the overall galaxy population. However,
this increased disk size makes the MW even more of an
outlier in terms of its (abnormally) steep radial gradient
in metallicity (Boardman et al. 2020; Lian et al. 2023).

galaxy, including the dynamically cold MW. FIRE-2 rep-

and representative histories of MW-mass galaxies.
As
such, it may not be surprising that among 11 FIRE-2
MW-mass galaxies, none of them agrees well with the
normalization of σ(τ) in the MW, while they age better
with larger samples of observed galaxies. As McCluskey
et al. (2024) discussed, and consistent with our discussion
in the previous subsection, the LG-like FIRE-2 simula-
tions tend to form disks earlier (in particular, Romeo), so
they tend to have lower σ(τ)/vϕ,0 today and thus among
the sample are closest to the MW.
That said, the FIRE-2 simulations in this paper do not
model all the physics that could be relevant. These sim-
ulations do not model magnetohydrodynamics (MHD)
nor self-consistent cosmic-ray injection, transport, and
feedback. Hopkins et al. (2020) show that including cos-
mic rays in these simulations can lower the stellar mass
(growth) of the galaxy, especially at late times, although
the strength of the effect depends sensitively on the de-
tails of the assumed diffusion/transport of cosmic rays.
Cosmic-ray feedback can reduce a galaxy’s present-day
stellar mass and SFR, which can lead to a lower σ in gas
(Chan et al. 2021). However, McCluskey et al. (2024)
show that this also leads to a slight reduction in the de-
gree of rotational support of these galaxies, in terms of
vϕ/σ, because this shift occurs primarily along the ex-
isting stellar mass-kinematics relation (see Figure A2 in
McCluskey et al. 2024). Thus, cosmic-ray feedback, as
implemented in FIRE-2, does not make these disks mean-
ingfully dynamically colder.
Potentially more important is that these FIRE-2 simu-
lations do not model supermassive black holes and feed-
back via active galactic nuclei (AGN), although some
recent FIRE simulations have (Wellons et al. 2023;
Mercedes-Feliz et al. 2023). Although AGN feedback is
most critical for more massive galaxies, growing work
finds that AGN can affect the structural and dynamical
properties of MW-mass galaxies (for example Irodotou
et al. 2022).
The lack of AGN feedback may explain
how these FIRE-2 galaxies display high average SFR:
the average SFR in these FIRE-2 simulations today is
≈5 −10 M⊙yr−1, higher than most (although not all)
observational inferences at these masses (Gandhi et al.
2022). In particular, the central regions of FIRE-2 galax-
ies display intense star formation stemming from strong
gas inflows. In real galaxies, these gas inflows would also
feed the central black hole, and the resulting AGN feed-
back would regulate further inflows and star formation in
the central regions. Indeed, Marasco et al. (2025) argue
that the lack of AGN feedback in FIRE-2 causes them to
exhibit more complex and broader HI line profiles than
in most observed galaxies.
Given the intimate connection between bar forma-
tion and gas dynamics in the inner galaxy (for example
Athanassoula et al. 2013; Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2020;
Fragkoudi et al. 2025), the lack of AGN feedback may
also affect bars in these simulations. For example, high
gas fractions and star formation in the inner galaxy can
drive fluctuations of the gravitational potential that dis-
rupt bars as they form (Weinberg 2024). Bars do form
in about half of these FIRE-2 simulations, but they are
generally weaker, more ellipsoidal, and shorter-lived than
in observed galaxies (Ansar et al. 2025). Bars can affect
stars throughout the galaxy and can be a driver of post-
formation dynamical heating, either directly (for exam-
ple Grand et al. 2016), or through resonant overlap with
spiral structure (Minchev & Famaey 2010; Marques et al.
2025). As such, stronger bars might increase σ, especially
σϕ (Asano et al. 2022; Khoperskov & Gerhard 2022; Dil-
lamore et al. 2023; Filion et al. 2023).
Lastly, much of our analysis focused on quantifying the
impact of measurement effects – age uncertainties, aper-
ture radius, galactocentric radius, and inclination angle –
applied to the “truth” of the FIRE-2 simulations. How-
ever, we did not use synthetic observations that modeled
observable photometry and spectroscopy, including mag-
nitude limits, dust, etc. In future work we will explore
in more detail how both synthetic measurements of re-
solved stars and of IFS-like synthetic measurements of
stellar population affect these results.
5.2.3. Comparison to Other Simulations
We now discuss our results from the FIRE-2 simula-
tions in relation to other cosmological zoom-in simula-
tions of MW-mass galaxies. We identified six other stud-
ies reporting σ(τ), spanning 48 MW-mass galaxies: 16
from Auriga (Grand et al. 2016), 19 from RaDES (Few
et al. 2012; Ruiz-Lara et al. 2016), five from NIHAO-UHD
(Buck et al. 2020), one from VINTERGATAN (Agertz
et al. 2021), one (h277) from the g14 suite (Bird et al.
2021), and 6 from HESTIA (Khoperskov et al. 2023). We
also included results for 11 FIRE-2 galaxies.
A crit-
ical point that we emphasize: among these 59 cosmo-
logical zoom-in simulations of MW-mass galaxies across
seven suites, only a single galaxy, h277, matches the
MW’s σ(τ). In contrast, these simulated galaxies gen-
erally agree better with the larger sample of M31, M33,
and PHANGS.
We first note important caveats to this statement.
As mentioned, we limit this comparison to cosmologi-
cal zoom-in simulations of MW-mass galaxies, meaning
we do not include results for isolated (non-cosmological)
simulations (for example Kumamoto et al. 2017; van
Donkelaar et al. 2022), non-hydrodynamic simulations,
including those using sticky-particle algorithms (for ex-
ample Martig et al. 2014), larger-volume simulations (for
example Pillepich et al. 2019, 2024), or simulations tar-
geting significantly different masses than the MW (for
example Brook et al. 2012).
Second, the galaxies for
which the authors measure σ(τ) in the above simula-
tion suites sometimes represent only a subset of the en-
tire suite. HESTIA features 13 LG-like pairs (Libeskind
et al. 2020), Auriga encompasses 30 galaxies (Grand et al.
2017), and the g14 suite includes about a dozen other-
wise well-studied galaxies (for example Loebman et al.
2012; Munshi et al. 2013; Kassin et al. 2014; Christensen
et al. 2016; Brooks et al. 2017), of which h277 has by far
the smallest σ(τ).
Also striking is that nearly all of these different simu-
lation suites (FIRE-2, Auriga, NIHAO-UHD, VINTER-
GATAN, HESTIA, RaDES) show remarkable quantita-
tive consistency among them for their average σ(τ), all

Most of these 59 galaxies have σZ ≈15 – 20 km/s for the

This
quantitative agreement may be surprising, given the di-
versity of models for the ISM, star formation, and feed-
back used across these simulation suites and their diver-
sity of initial conditions, and thus formation histories.
These σZ values generally match M31, M33, and (at least
for stars older than ≈1 Gyr) PHANGS. In contrast, σZ
in the MW is only ≈12 and 20 km/s at the same ages.
Therefore, tension with the MW is somewhat stronger
at older ages, which may reflect the MW’s quiescent
merger history and early-forming disk. However, these
explanations do not obviously apply to younger stars,
which in almost all simulations formed in non-merging,
rotationally-dominated disks.
The key exception is h277, which, as Bird et al. (2021)
show, exhibits a σ(τ) similar to that of the Solar neigh-
borhood of the MW. Furthermore, as Bird et al. (2021)
noted, h277 is unusual among the larger g14 simulation
suite of over a dozen galaxies. Brooks et al. (2009) intro-
duced h277, and Zolotov et al. (2009) notes its particu-
larly quiescent merger history. However, the original ver-
sion of h277 did not stand out kinematically: House et al.
(2011) showed that it had the highest σZ (≈28 km s−1)
among young stars in their simulated sample. The up-
dated version of h277 analyzed in Bird et al. (2021) had
twice better spatial resolution (173 pc), eight times bet-
ter mass resolution (5800 M⊙), and updated ISM and
star formation models from Christensen et al. (2012),
which directly link star formation to H2.
Bird et al. (2021) argues that the low σZ ≈7 km s−1
today arose primarily from their updated star-formation
prescription, which limits star formation to dense gas
(n > 100 cm−3). The connection between star forma-
tion thresholds and σ of stars at birth may account for
unrealistic kinematics in earlier simulations (House et al.
2011). However, the relationship between high-density
star formation thresholds and low σ in newly formed
stars is not clear across different simulations.
For ex-
ample, the FIRE-2 simulations we analyze here adopt a
minimum density for star formation of n = 1000 cm−3,
along with a requirement of gas being self-gravitating.
An alternative expectation is that higher density thresh-
olds combined with a self-gravitating criterion lead to
more clustered star formation, which increases the im-
pact of stellar feedback and dynamical perturbations on
short timescales (Fielding et al. 2018; Martizzi 2020; Orr
et al. 2022). An even broader explanation is that while
stars form from the densest self-gravitating gas, this gas
is still not as kinematically cold as the gas in the MW
(or h277).
The cold kinematics of h277 likely relate to the simula-
tion’s MW-like, yet cosmologically rare, early formation
history and absence of late-time mergers. This is cou-
pled with h277’s relatively low SFR, ≈1 M⊙yr−1, over
the last 6 Gyr, which is broadly consistent with obser-
vational estimates of the MW’s star-formation history
(Smith et al. 2012; Ruiz-Lara et al. 2020), because star
formation and its associated feedback drive turbulence
in the ISM, increasing σ in gas. The FIRE-2 galaxies we
analyze have an average SFR today of ≈5 −10 M⊙yr−1
(Gandhi et al. 2022). (See also Figure 9 of Iyer et al. 2020,
for a comparison of SFRs in m12m, m12f, and h277).
Indeed, among the sample of cosmological zoom-in sim-
ulations we discuss above, most have higher SFRs than
the MW and h277: for example, 60% of Auriga galaxies
have SFRs of 5–10 M⊙yr−1, though individual galaxies
occasionally have 1–2 M⊙yr−1. Few simulations exhibit
such low SFR over a long (≳6 Gyr) timescale, poten-
tially indicating that this may be necessary to reproduce
the MW’s σ(τ).
Reinforcing this idea is that the one
PHANGS galaxy with MW-like kinematics, NGC 1433,
has a recent SFR of 1.1 M⊙yr−1, the lowest among the
PHANGS-MUSSE sample.
We thus conclude that, based only on observational
comparisons, the MW is a kinematic outlier with un-
usually low σ(τ)/vϕ,0 among this sample of 19 observed
nearby galaxies. As such, it may not be surprising that
among the sample of 59 cosmological zoom-in simula-
tions with published σ(τ), only one (h277) matches the
MW, while most simulations reasonably match the much
larger sample of M31, M33, and (at least for stars older
than ≈500 Myr) PHANGS. This highlights the poten-
tial dangers in benchmarking simulations only against
the MW, but this also highlights the remarkable value
of ongoing measurements of stellar kinematics and their
histories within nearby galaxies.

COMPARING FIRE-2 SIMULATIONS AND OBSERVATIONS WITHOUT SCALING
In Section 4.4, we compared σ(τ) in the FIRE-2 simulations against observed galaxies, scaling each galaxy by its
rotational velocity, vϕ,0, such that we compare σ(τ)/vϕ,0. This dimensionless metric provides a fairer comparison of
galaxy samples that span a factor of 10 in stellar mass. However, many studies examine only σ(τ). Therefore, here we
show versions of Figure A1 and Figure 9 unscaled by vϕ,0. Note that the scaled versus unscaled versions differ only
in normalization, not in shape, because vϕ,0 is rotational velocity today and does not vary with age. The results are
qualitatively similar, because these FIRE-2 galaxies have stellar masses (and thus rotational velocities) similar to the
MW, M31, and the median mass of the PHANGS-MUSE sample. The primary difference is for M33, given its much
lower stellar mass.
OBSERVATIONS OF NEARBY GALAXIES
Observations of M31
We now discuss the observations of Dorman et al. (2015) (D15) in detail.
D15 measured the line-of-sight σ(τ)
relation across the northern half of M31’s disk using a sample of ≈5800 individual stars with combined SPLASH
spectroscopy and PHAT photometry. The photometric accuracy and precision afforded by deep HST observations
allow clean color-magnitude-based separation of RGB, AGB, and MS stars. In turn, D15 split stars from their sample
into 4 broad age bins based on their position on the optical CMD; these age bins have distinct mean ages (0.03, 0.4, 2,
and 4 Gyr) despite their overlapping age distributions. For each star, D15 first measured σ in a surrounding circular
patch of radius ≈760 pc. If the patch contained at least 15 stars in the same age bin, they calculated σ from the
second moment of the velocity distribution (of same-aged stars) using a kernel density estimator, while patches with
fewer than 15 same-aged neighbors were ignored. The final reported σ is the mean of all aperture-based σ in each age
bin.
Observations of M33
Similarly, we now discuss the observations of M33 by Quirk et al. (2022) (Q22) in more depth. The procedure of
Q22 largely followed that of D15, although their exact parameters necessarily differed. Specifically, Q22 divided their
sample into 3 age bins with corresponding mean ages of ≈0.08, 1, and 4 Gyr. As in D15, the age distributions within
each bin are broad and overlap, having 16th–84th percentile ranges of ≈20 – 180 Myr, 0.56-2.2 Gyr, and 1.4-9 Gyr.
Furthermore, the aperture/patch radius over which Q22 measured σ differed between age groups, with median values
of ≈350, 500, and 420 pc for the young, intermediate, and old populations, respectively.

PHANGS-MUSE Survey
We now discuss – in greater detail – the PHANGS-MUSE sample and the procedure that Pessa et al. (2023) (P23)
used to measure σ(τ) in 19 galaxies.
The PHANGS-MUSE sample lies within ≲20 Mpc with low to moderate
inclinations (< 60◦) and stellar masses of log(M⋆/M⊙) = 9.4 −11.0, with a median of log(M⋆/M⊙) = 10.5.
As we discuss, one way to better compare the kinematics of different galaxies is to scale the dispersion by the galaxy’s
rotational velocity, vϕ,0 . Although P23 does not measure vϕ,0, earlier work by Lang et al. (2020) provided rotation
curves for 67 galaxies in the larger PHANGS-ALMA sample using high-resolution CO (2-1) maps. By parameterizing
these rotation curves with smooth analytic fits, Lang et al. (2020) determined the maximum (asymptotic) rotational
velocities; however, three of the PHANGS-MUSE galaxies (NGC 2835, NGC 5068, and IC 5332) lacked robust smooth
fits, leaving their maximum rotational velocities unreported. Consequently, our analysis focuses on the remaining 16
PHANGS-MUSE galaxies, which we hereafter refer to as the “PHANGS galaxies/sample”.
Unlike the previously mentioned observations, these measurements are for stellar populations across ≈100 pc scales,
not individual stars. To extract stellar properties and kinematics, P23 used the popular Penalized PiXel-Fitting (PPXf)
code introduced by Cappellari & Emsellem (2004) and significantly upgraded by Cappellari (2017). To measure stellar
kinematics, they fit the observed Vornoi-binned spectrum to stellar templates (here the E-MILES templates of Vazdekis
et al. (2012) and their young extensions from Asa’d et al. (2017)) convolved with a line-of-sight velocity distribution
parameterized by a Gauss-Hermite expansion assuming a single stellar kinematic component.
The high spatial resolution and wide coverage of the PHANGS survey allowed P23 to study stellar populations
across multiple galactic environments separately. To more fairly compare different galaxies and account for radial and
environmental trends, the σ(τ) relations in P23 included only disk stars within 0.25 −0.30R25, where R25 is the 25th
magnitude isophotal B-band radius. For this sample, R25 ranges from 7 −34 kpc with a mean value of ≈15 kpc, such
that 0.25 −0.30R25 corresponds to ≈3.75 −4.25 kpc. After making this geometric selection, P23 calculated the mean
for populations with luminosity-weighted ages within three age bins: young (< 100 Myr), intermediate (100−600 Myr),
and old (≳3 Gyr).
Observations of the Milky Way
Finally, we describe the 13 works that we compiled that measured σ(τ) of the MW. Nordstr¨om et al. (2004) introduced
the Geneva-Copenhagen Survey (GCS). The GCS combined Stromgren photometry and radial velocity measurements
with Hipparcos proper motions and parallaxes to measure late-type stars in the Solar neighborhood, ultimately creating
an all-sky, kinematically-unbiased catalog of ≈16, 500 F and G stars that is volume complete within 40 pc of the Sun.
Since Nordstr¨om et al. (2004), the GCS catalog has undergone multiple revisions. First, Holmberg et al. (2007)
used new temperature and metallicity calibrations to update the metallicites and ages. Second, Holmberg et al. (2009)
implemented newly revised Hipparcos parallaxes to significantly improve distance measurements, resulting in improved
kinematics and ages as well. Lastly, Casagrande et al. (2011) adopted a corrected effective temperature scale to provide
more accurate and precise metallicities and ages. These updated ages are generally ≈1 Gyr younger than their earlier
values, with the oldest ages often decreasing by ≈3−6 Gyr. However, a substantial number of stars previously deemed
young (age < 1 Gyr) now have ages ranging between 0.5 −10 Gyr (see Figure A2 of Casagrande et al. 2011). Despite
these changes, all of these works show similar σ(τ). We include Nordstr¨om et al. (2004), Holmberg et al. (2009), and
Casagrande et al. (2011) in our compilation, given their prevalence in the literature, and to show how different age
determinations impact the measured σ(τ) for the same stellar sample.
All of the GCS analyses used the Galactic coordinate system and a heliocentric Cartesian system, so they quoted
σ in Galactic space-velocities (U,V,W), where U points towards the Galactic center, V the direction of rotation, and
W the north Galactic pole. However, with the exception of Silva Aguirre et al. (2018), the other works (including
our own) used the Galactocentric coordinate system (R, ϕ, Z). We tested how using the Galactic coordinate system
impacts the σ measured in FIRE-2, and we found that for aperture-based measurements, the median vertical (W) and
3D σ were generally unchanged, as expected. However, the median σ in U is typically ≈15 −20% lower than σ in vR,
while V is typically ≈20 −25% higher than σ in vϕ, across all ages. Although the in-plane components of σ are not
exactly analogous for the two coordinate systems, we show the U and V velocity dispersions as σR and σϕ in Figure 6
for consistency.
We know discuss works that measured σ(τ) in the Gaia era, grouping similar works together rather than adopting
a purely chronological ordering. Yu & Liu (2018) analyzed a sample of ≈3, 500 SGB/RGB stars that have LAM-
OST spectroscopy and Gaia DR1 astrometry (stars in the Tycho-Gaia Astrometric Solution catalog). Following the
likelihood technique of Liu et al. (2015), Yu & Liu (2018) estimated stellar ages by comparing the observed stellar
parameters with theoretical isochrones, leading to typical age uncertainties of ≈30%. This sample is fairly local:
requiring reliable astrometric measurements limits the sample to within 1 kpc of the solar neighborhood. Furthermore,
Yu & Liu (2018) divided their sample into two equal populations based on the stars’ distance from the mid-plane:
≈1780 stars with |Z| < 270 pc and 1780 with |Z| > 270 pc. Both populations exhibit similar 3D and radial σ, with

the population at |Z| < 270 pc having a larger azimuthal σ and a smaller vertical σ at most ages. For brevity, we
include only the population at |Z| < 270 pc.
Kordopatis et al. (2023) derived stellar parameters, including orbital properties, masses, and isochrone ages, for
≈5 million stars with radial velocities from Gaia-EDR3, by combining Gaia-EDR3 spectroscopy, photometry, and
astrometry with 2MASS infrared photometry. Kordopatis et al. (2023) presented σ(τ) for RGB and MSTO stars
within 1 kpc of the Sun. They claim that their stellar ages are most reliable for MSTO stars, with all but the least-
massive MSTO stars having age errors ≲50%. Therefore, we include only their MSTO data, sampling their trendline
every 0.5 Gyr in Figure 6.
Sharma et al. (2021) compared the results of multiple complementary surveys, including GALAH, LAMOST,
APOGEE, Kepler, and Gaia, to explore how the σ(τ) depends on age, angular momentum, metallicity, and |Z|.
However, Sharma et al. (2021) mainly presented (dimensionless) normalized σ, in part to compare the relative profiles
of these different works. That said, they presented raw σ from GALAH DR3 for ≈100, 000 MSTO stars in the ex-
tended solar neighborhood, defined via |R−R⊙| and |Z|, which we sample every 0.5 Gyr. The GALAH+ DR3 catalog
(Buder et al. 2021) provides stellar ages estimated using the Bayesian Stellar Parameter Estimation code from Sharma
et al. (2018), which provides a Bayesian estimate of stellar parameters from (PARSEC-COLIBRI) isochrones. When
we adopt the same quality cuts as Sharma et al. (2021), we find an average age uncertainty of 16.7%. Interestingly,
Sharma et al. (2021) compared σ(τ) of RGB and MSTO stars in GALAH DR3 and LAMOST DR4, finding that while
RGB stars from both surveys and MSTO stars in LAMOST had σ that significantly flattened or even decreased with
age above 8 Gyrs, σ of MSTO stars from GALAH continued to increase monotonically with age. The authors posited
that this behavior may stem from the lower age uncertainties of the GALAH MSTO sample.
Also using GALAH DR3 results, Hayden et al. (2022) derived new ages for ≈215, 000 stars using their measured
elemental abundances and overall metallicities. They quote typical age uncertainties for their abundance-based ages
of ≈1 −2 Gyr. However, unlike for other methods, these uncertainties are largely age-independent, so older stars
have more accurate ages than younger stars. Hayden et al. (2022) showed σ(τ) for stars at R = 7.1 −9.1 kpc and
|Z| < 0.5 kpc. We sample the σ of their “All GALAH Stars” population every 0.5 Gyr.
Silva Aguirre et al. (2018) combined optical and infrared photometry, APOGEE spectroscopy, and Kepler astroseis-
mology to construct a photometrically complete sample of ≈1200 stars across 2 kpc of the solar annulus. The sample
has a median age uncertainty of 28.5%. Silva Aguirre et al. (2018) also used the Galactic coordinate system.
Miglio et al. (2021) and Lagarde et al. (2021) considered overlapping samples of red-giant-branch (RGB) stars with
reliable constraints from Kepler astroseismology, APOGEE spectroscopy, and Gaia astrometry. However, each work
further refined their sample and determined stellar properties, in particular stellar ages, differently.
Miglio et al.
(2021) selected only giant stars with robust mass measurements, producing a final sample of ≈3, 300 stars with
a median random age uncertainty of 23%. From this sample, Lagarde et al. (2021) further selected for stars in the
APOKASC catalog, yielding a final sample of ≈2, 800 giant stars. Both works divided their sample into high- and low-
α populations, with Lagarde et al. (2021) further dividing their low-α population by [Fe/H]. We show both populations
from Miglio et al. (2021), and we show the low-α and the metal-poor high-α populations from Lagarde et al. (2021),
omitting their metal-rich high-α population for brevity, given that its σ lie between those of the other populations.
As in Yu & Liu (2018), Sun et al. (2024) also combined LAMOST and Gaia observations. However, the improved
proper motions that Gaia DR2 and EDR3 provided allow them to measure red clump stars throughout much of the
disk, with a sample of ≈130, 000 stars spanning R = 6 −15 kpc and |Z| < 3 −4 kpc. They determined stellar ages
from a machine-learning model trained on a sample of thousands of red clump stars in the LAMOST-KEPLER field
with accurate astroseismic ages (following Huang et al. (2018)), leading to a typical age uncertainty of ≈30%. In
their analysis, Sun et al. (2024) subdivided their entire sample into various spatial and population bins, providing 3D
kinematic maps and σ(τ) for each. For this comparison, we use their “thin” and “thick” disk measurements for stars
within R = 7 −9 kpc and |Z| < 1 kpc (shown in the bottom-left panels of their Figure 9), where they determined the
low-α and high-α disk populations using an empirical cut in the [Fe/H]–[α/Fe] plane.
Similarly, Mackereth et al. (2019) also provided a comprehensive dissection of the low- and high-α disks using stars
with spectra from APOGEE DR14 and ages determined using machine learning models trained on APOGEE stars
that have robust asteroseismic ages from Kepler. As in Sun et al. (2024), the improved astrometry of Gaia DR2
allowed Mackereth et al. (2019) to probe stars throughout a large volume of the disk: R = 4−13 kpc and |Z| < 2 kpc.
Mackereth et al. (2019) presented σ(τ) for stars across R = 6 −11 kpc. We make no further radial selection and
include their data for all mean radii, because we cannot determine these stars’ current radii from their mean radii.
However, low- and high-α populations generally have slightly different radial ranges, with the low-α data spanned the
entire radial range, while the high-α data had mean radii limited to R ≈6 −7 kpc. Interestingly, σR showed little
dependence on the mean orbital radius, while σZ increased with increasing mean radius at a fixed age. The final
sample of ≈66, 000 stars has a median age uncertainty ≈30 −35%, though the authors note that for stars older than
10 Gyr their ages are likely to be underestimated and subject to large uncertainties.
