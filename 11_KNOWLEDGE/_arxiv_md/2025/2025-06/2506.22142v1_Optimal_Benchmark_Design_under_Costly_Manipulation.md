Optimal Benchmark Design under Costly Manipulation∗
´Angel Hernando-Veciana†

Abstract
Price benchmarks are used to incorporate market price trends into contracts, but their use can
create opportunities for manipulation by parties involved in the contract. This paper examines
this issue using a realistic and tractable model inspired by smart contracts on blockchains like
Ethereum. In our model, manipulation costs depend on two factors: the magnitude of adjustments
to individual prices (variable costs) and the number of prices adjusted (fixed costs).
We find
that a weighted mean is the optimal benchmark when fixed costs are negligible, while the median
is optimal when variable costs are negligible. In cases where both fixed and variable costs are
significant, the optimal benchmark can be implemented as a trimmed mean, with the degree of
trimming increasing as fixed costs become more important relative to variable costs. Furthermore,
we show that the optimal weights for a mean-based benchmark are proportional to the marginal
manipulation costs, whereas the median remains optimal without weighting, even when fixed costs
differ across prices.
Key Words: Financial benchmarks, oracles, decentralised finance.

Introduction
A (price) benchmark provides a summary of the price in the market at a given moment in time, quite
often computed as a statistic from a sample of prices or from opinion polls.1 They are computed for
the majority of the more relevant financial markets and include popular benchmarks like the “4pm
Fix”, based on sampled prices,2 or the original version of the LIBOR and EURIBOR, based on opinion
polls.3 Benchmarks play a crucial role in financial markets as they allow to write contracts contingent
on the evolution of prices, like derivatives, floating rate loans, portfolio evaluations... However, the
notional value of these contracts has become so large that it is often the case that one of the parties
may benefit from manipulating the benchmark. Indeed, there is ample evidence that this has been the
case. See, for instance, the discussions in the media, Sch¨afer et al. (2008), and in academic papers,
Evans (2018).
More recently, the advent of decentralised finance (DeFi) has added a new dimension to this
problem: its main building block is smart contracts4 whose execution depends crucially on the input
from some financial benchmarks, also called price oracles5 in this context.6 Consider, for instance, one
of the most basic financial contracts: lending. Due to the lack of enforcing courts, lending is always
collateralised and an oracle is used to determine the value of the collateral. In a well documented

Indeed, our first main result (Proposition 2) is that the optimal benchmark is the median or the
mean, respectively, when either the variable costs or the fixed costs are negligible. This is intuitive.
In the former case, the main trouble may come from a few prices large price increases, which are
better handled with the median than with the mean. Quite the contrary, if it is fixed costs that are
negligible, the mean is better to handle manipulations than the median as the median is very sensitive
to the manipulation of a large proportion of prices.
Proving this result in general is technically challenging, as the problem formally corresponds to
an optimal contract with multitask moral hazard. In this context, the manipulator acts as the agent,
deciding how much to manipulate each of the market prices that feed into the benchmark. The analysis
becomes even more complex in the presence of fixed costs, as the manipulator’s objective function is
non-convex, even under linear contracts. Consequently, the manipulator’s optimal behavior cannot be
fully characterized using only first-order conditions.
Our approach takes a different perspective. Consider the case with no fixed costs and a mean
benchmark. Convex variable costs of manipulation result in the optimal manipulation strategy of
uniformly increasing all prices in the market by the same amount. This uniform manipulation would
also increase by the same amount any benchmark that satisfies basic reasonable properties.
For
instance, symmetric benchmarks, those equal to the point of symmetry if the distribution is symmetric,
would increase the same amount if the original distribution was symmetric. However, other symmetric
benchmarks may allow for alternative, less costly manipulation strategies. In this sense, the mean is
optimal, as it maximizes the cost of manipulation among all symmetric benchmarks.
The case without variable costs is similar, though slightly more complex.
Here, the optimal
manipulation strategy of the median involves raising a sufficient mass of prices such that only half
the total mass remains below the target. With careful adjustment, this minimal mass can be shifted
in a way that preserves symmetry, ensuring that if the original price distribution was symmetric, the
manipulated distribution remains symmetric as well. As in the previous case, this implies that the
median maximizes the cost of manipulation among all symmetric benchmarks, making it optimal in
this sense.

Extending the analysis beyond these extreme cases is challenging. However, in the specific scenario
where the initial distribution of prices is concentrated at a single point, we can generally characterize
benchmarks that induce optimal manipulations that preserve symmetry, thereby ensuring optimality
among symmetric benchmarks. This turn out to be trimmed means13, where the extent of trimming
increases as the fixed costs grow larger relative to the variable costs.
Finally, we study how our results extend to the case in which the prices fed into the benchmark
may come from subpopulations with different observable characteristics. For instance, the sampled
prices may correspond to different moments in time in which liquidity may have varied, and differences
in liquidity may imply differences in the variable cost of manipulation. Similarly, different moments in
time may be associated to different gas prices and thus differences in the fixed cost of manipulation.
In this case, our results for the extreme cases of no fixed costs or no variable costs are very clear
cut. In the former case, the optimal benchmark is a weighted mean with weights proportional to the
marginal costs of manipulation, whereas in the latter case the standard median remains optimal.
The most closely related papers are those of Duffie & Dworczak (2021), and Baldauf et al. (2022)
that we already referred to. As we have already noted, the main difference is that they assume that
manipulation has no fixed costs that grow with the number of prices manipulated. Extending their
analysis to the case of fixed cost is technically challenging as the manipulator problem is no longer
convex and thus we cannot work with first order conditions, as in the literature of optimal contract
design, see Bolton & Dewatripont (2004).
Our work is also related to the literature on robust statistics, see Huber (2004) for a comprehensive
review. This literature study the statistic that minimizes deviations from the true value subject to
a perturbation chosen to maximize deviations. This problem, like ours, is formulated as a minimax,
but the difference is that the assumptions that define the set of feasible deviations are reasonable for
exogenous outliers but not for the manipulation problems that arise in financial markets. In particular,
these deviations are not compatible with the existence of a fixed cost of manipulation, one of the main
elements of our analysis.
Finally, our work is also related to the fast growing literature on Decentralised Finance and in
particular to the analysis of oracles and their vulnerabilities, see Werner et al. (2022). This literature,
however, does not analyse the performance of the possible rules used to aggregate prices but instead

The Model
In our investigation, we assume a population of unmanipulated prices described by a probability
distribution F. For some results we assume that the distribution is a degenerate distribution at a
price that we normalise to be zero. We refer to this case as the case of no dispersion. For the rest of
the paper, we assume that F has a single-peaked density f symmetric around zero. A strategic agent,
the manipulator, can manipulate these prices at a cost. In particular, the manipulator’s strategy can
be described with a conditional distribution function Gc(·|p), for any p ∈R, that describes how the
prices of all the markets with unmanipulated price p are shifted into a new distribution of prices.
In the case of no manipulation, Gc(·|p) is simply a degenerate distribution at p. The manipulator’s
strategy gives rise to the final distribution of prices:

A benchmark is a unidimensional statistic computed from the final distribution of prices. In our
analysis, either we require very little structure on the set of rules that may be used to compute the
benchmark or restrict attention to particular families of rules that have been used in practice. In
particular, we call symmetric benchmarks those that are equal to the point of symmetry in the case
of a symmetric distribution of prices. Note that this definition does not impose any structure on
distributions that are not symmetric. We define the τ-trimmed mean, for τ ∈[0, 0.5], as the average
calculated over the modified distribution obtained by truncating the original distribution at the τ and
1−τ quantiles. Clearly, τ = 0 and τ = 1/2, renders the τ−trimmed mean equal to the standard mean
and the median, respectively. Clearly, the τ-trimmed means are symmetric benchmarks.
As a realistic example, we can think of the distribution of prices as the marginal prices offered by
a set of automated market makers (AMM) at different moments in time. This marginal prices can be
manipulated by submitting trade orders to the AMM whose only purpose is to upset the computed

benchmark computed for the final distribution of prices.

Optimal Benchmarks
The criterium we use to evaluate a benchmark is the minimum cost of manipulation necessary to
implement a certain price P, the greater this minimum cost is, the better the benchmark. This is
consistent with the example of collateralised loans that we provide in the introduction. A manipulator
minimizes the cost to reach the price at which the liquidation clause is activated, whereas the bench-
mark designer maximizes this cost to make this manipulation less attractive. This is also a first step
to study alternative objective functions for the manipulator. For instance, for the case in which the
manipulator’s objective is to maximize the price, the optimal benchmark maximizes the marginal cost
of raising it, which under some regularity conditions coincides with the benchmark that maximizes

Definition:
• A benchmark is optimal at price P if it is the symmetric benchmark that maximizes the minimum
cost of manipulating the benchmark up to P. A benchmark is optimal if it is optimal at any
price P.
• A benchmark dominates another benchmark at price P if the former has a greater minimum
cost of its manipulation to P than the latter. A benchmark dominates another benchmark if it
dominates the latter for any P.
In our analysis, we shall build on an intuitive property.
Proposition 1. A benchmark is optimal at P if its optimal manipulation strategy induces a final
distribution of prices symmetric around P.
Proof. Since the manipulation strategy of the proposition induces a symmetric final distribution of
prices, it not only implements P for the proposed benchmark but also for any symmetric benchmark.
This manipulation strategy is optimal for the proposed benchmark, but other symmetric benchmarks
manipulators may have less costly manipulation strategies that also implement P, which implies the
proposition.

Conclusion
This paper investigates how the design of price benchmarks can mitigate the risks of manipulation, with
a particular focus on the unique challenges posed by decentralized finance (DeFi). We demonstrate that
the choice of benchmark formula—mean, median, or trimmed mean—plays a critical role in balancing
15


## Page 16


the costs of manipulation. Our results highlight that while the mean is optimal when variable costs
dominate, the median is preferable when fixed costs are significant. In intermediate cases, trimmed
means emerge as optimal, with the degree of trimming reflecting the relative importance of fixed costs.
By formalizing the trade-offs between fixed and variable manipulation costs, our work offers a
framework to enhance benchmark robustness in both traditional finance and DeFi. Our findings are
particularly relevant for the development of DeFi, since reliable price benchmarks are essential to the
trustworthiness of smart contracts and the stability of DeFi systems.
A key direction for future research is the joint optimization of sampling strategies and benchmark
formulas. While this paper focuses on the formula used to aggregate sampled prices, the sampling
process itself is equally critical, particularly in decentralized exchanges where liquidity and transaction
timing can vary widely. Exploring how sampling protocols interact with benchmark formulas could
yield further insights into designing benchmarks that are both manipulation-resistant and statistically
efficient.
