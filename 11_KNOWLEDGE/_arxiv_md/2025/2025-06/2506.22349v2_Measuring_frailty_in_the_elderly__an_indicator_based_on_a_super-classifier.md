Measuring frailty in the elderly: an indicator based on a
super-classiﬁer
Preprint
Sara Rebottini∗† and Pietro Belloni‡§
Version: October 2025
Abstract
Identifying frail older adults in an ageing population is essential for improving healthcare services. This study
proposes a composite indicator to assess individual frailty levels using administrative healthcare data. Given the
complex and multidimensional nature of frailty, a multi-outcome approach is adopted. Following an extensive
literature review, a set of adverse health events is selected as proxies for frailty. These events were modelled
using logistic classiﬁers, with frailty determinants (associated to adverse health events, selected using a gradient
tree boosting) serving as covariates. The sensitivity and speciﬁcity of each classiﬁer is used to compose their
combined likelihood. From this, we derive an indicator capable of quantifying frailty across the population.
The indicator shows robust performance across multiple outcomes and over time. Its primary innovation lies in
allowing the use of diverse and outcome-speciﬁc sets of frailty determinants without any structural constraint.
Overall, we oﬀer an eﬀective tool for quantifying frailty among older adults, potentially supporting health
authorities in the prevention of frailty-related adverse events.
Keywords: Frailty, Classiﬁer’s combinations, Administrative data, Composite indicator

Introduction
1.1
The role of frailty in ageing populations
Healthcare systems worldwide face a multitude of daily challenges, but none more critical than safeguarding
the well-being of the population they oversee. This responsibility has become even more pressing nowadays,
since the average age of the population is increasing due to the higher life expectancy and the lower fertility
rates. An ageing demographic translates into a growing demand for healthcare services and a greater ﬁnancial
burden to prevent and manage age-related conditions. Among older adults, the most vulnerable subgroup,
known as frail, faces a signiﬁcantly heightened risk of adverse health outcomes, including the onset of dementia,
hip fractures and even death [Mulasso et al., 2016]. In this context, frailty has emerged as a central concern in
geriatric care and a key public health priority [Cesari et al., 2016, Bessa et al., 2021]. Addressing this challenge
demands a proactive approach: early identiﬁcation of frail individuals is essential to enable timely interventions
and to strengthen preventive medicine strategies [Gobbens et al., 2010a]. Only by quantifying frailty, healthcare
systems can sustain quality healthcare during the demographic shifts of the global population.
For this reason, the concept of frailty has attracted growing interest over time.
Although there is still
no single deﬁnition accepted within the scientiﬁc community [Hoogendijk et al., 2019], frailty is commonly
deﬁned as a complex and dynamic condition of increased vulnerability in a person, which usually aﬀects older
adults [Fried et al., 2001]. It can be caused by various factors, including exposure to stressful events, and can
increase the risk of developing diseases of diﬀerent severity [Fried et al., 2004]. Due to its complexity, there are
numerous deﬁnitions in the scientiﬁc literature that capture diﬀerent aspects of frailty itself. These deﬁnitions

are grouped into three paradigms that diﬀer according to their theoretical framework [Cavazza and Malvi, 2014]:
the biomedical paradigm, the cumulative deﬁcit paradigm and the bio-psycho-social paradigm.
According to the biomedical paradigm, frailty is deﬁned as a biological syndrome in which functional reserve
and resistance to external stressors are reduced [Fried et al., 2001]. These reductions may cause a progressive
decline in physiological systems, resulting in increased vulnerability and the onset of adverse health events.
However, this deﬁnition captures only one aspect of frailty (the biomedical one) and ignores the complex and
multidimensional nature of this concept. To measure frailty using this deﬁnition, the main indicator used by
the scientiﬁc community is the Fried Frailty Phenotype. An individual is considered frail when at least three of
the following elements are present: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slowness and low physical
activity.
Another way to describe frailty is through the cumulative deﬁcit paradigm, which deﬁnes it as “state of
chaotic disorganisation of physiological systems that can be assessed by evaluating functional status, diseases,
physical and cognitive deﬁcits, psychosocial risk factors and geriatric syndromes, with a view to building as
complete a picture as possible of the risk situations for adverse events” [Rockwood et al., 1994]. According to
this deﬁnition, a non-frail person is in a state of balance between factors that promote independence, such as
functional abilities, good health and a positive attitude towards health. On the other hand, a frail person might
have deﬁcits that reduce autonomy, such as disability, chronic illness and dependence on others to perform
activities of daily living.
A more comprehensive view is oﬀered by the bio-psycho-social paradigm, which deﬁnes frailty as a multi-
dimensional and dynamic condition [Gobbens et al., 2010a]. Speciﬁcally, according to this deﬁnition the state
of frailty aﬀects a person who experiences losses in one or more functional domains (physical, mental, social)
caused by the interaction of several variables that increase the risk of adverse health outcomes. This formula-
tion allows to combine the essential elements of each of the other existing deﬁnitions. Moreover, this deﬁnition
highlights the multidimensional aspect of frailty, considering not only physical frailty but also psychological and
social frailty. It also particularly emphasises its dynamic nature, described as a degenerative process that can
have both positive and negative implications, generated by both individual characteristics and environmental
factors. The association of frailty with adverse events is another feature that distinguishes this paradigm. A
person classiﬁed as frail has a higher risk of developing adverse events related to frailty itself, such as death,
hip fracture or the onset of disability, compared to a person who is not frail. This connection allows frailty to
be studied from a predictive perspective: a person can be deﬁned as frail when there is a high probability of
experiencing adverse events, commonly referred to as (adverse) outcomes.
1.2
The importance of frailty indicators
The change in the age structure of the world’s population has led to a slow but steady general ageing process,
which translates into the need to adapt the health services oﬀered to citizens. In fact, since the early 2000s,
there has been an increase in life expectancy at birth: from 66.8 years in 2000 to 73.1 in 2019 [World Health
Organization, 2024]. This increase is associated with a greater likelihood of illness and disability, reducing
the self-suﬃciency of older adults.
This is why prevention and protection of the older population play an
important role: the early identiﬁcation of a frail individual reduces the number of unnecessary hospitalizations
and therefore saves time and resources. To this end, it is essential to have tools capable of identifying frail older
adults at an early stage. In the prevention phase, it is also important to minimise the number of frail older
adults who are not recognised as such, even if this means including some individuals who are not frail.
Our aim is to estimate the frailty level for each subject of a population in order to provide important support
for prevention and public health procedures. Composite indicators are one of the most widely used tools for
this purpose: through a composite indicator, a public authority (such as a health authority) has access to a
single value that synthesises the characteristics of a person, a community or a geographical area. Then, this
single indicator can be used to plan new public policies or improve current ones. Despite this great advantage,
it is essential that the indicator is constructed appropriately, because if the concept to be measured is not fully
described, the implemented policies may be oﬀ-target.
In general, the nature of the data used inﬂuences how the indicator is constructed. These tools can be
divided into two categories depending on the type of data: indicators based on sample data and indicators
based on administrative data. In this work, we use administrative data with the goal of overcoming some of the
critical issues found in similar frailty indicators already in use. Administrative data are preferred because of
their reliability and accuracy, which is hardly achieved using other data sources [Saltelli, 2007]. Furthermore,
administrative data are often already available to health authorities, unlike sample data (which often require a
new survey to be conducted), and therefore guarantee greater ease of use once collected.

Among the most widely used administrative data-based indicators in the literature is FI-POS [Silan et al.,
2022], which is appreciated not only for its high predictive performance compared to other indicators in the same
class, but also for its data parsimony. However, it has some limitations related to its construction methodology:
on one hand, it always uses the same subset of frailty determinants to predict the onset of diﬀerent adverse
events; on the other hand, it does not allow the use of non-ordered statistical variables (such as sex) to be
included among the frailty determinants. Our work stems from the need to ﬁnd a solution to these problems.
We propose a frailty indicator that captures the multidimensionality of frailty and, at the same time, selects
the determinants that best predict the onset of each outcome using a diﬀerent subgroup for each adverse event.
The article is organised as follows. Section 2 describes indicators used in the scientiﬁc literature, focusing
on the FI-POS as a benchmark for the subsequent analysis. Section 3 introduces a new approach for frailty
indicators composition, focusing on the aggregation phase of the indicator construction. Section 4 is the appli-
cation of our methodology to real healthcare administrative data, also including some preliminary descriptive
consideration about the indicator. Section 5 describes and validates the indicator, showing its ability to predict
the onset of adverse events. Section 6 provides several considerations about strength and limitations of our
indicator proposal and possible future work.
2
How to represent frailty
Since frailty results from a combination of several clinical outcomes, it is by deﬁnition a multidimensional,
complex and latent (not directly observable) concept.
These characteristics make it diﬃcult to move from
theoretical to practical deﬁnitions when it comes to frailty. Frailty can be deﬁned as a health condition common
in individuals with certain risk factors (frailty determinants), which increase the probability of observing adverse
events that characterize this condition. Based on this deﬁnition, frailty can be represented with an indicator.
To begin with, it is necessary to identify the relevant information to construct such an indicator. As argued
in Gobbens et al. [2010a], the operational deﬁnition of frailty depends on the available data. Using data from
diﬀerent sources highlights diﬀerent aspects of frailty and may lead to diﬀerent intervention strategies.
Administrative data contain a wide range of information related to the health status of patients who require
access to public health services. Through local health databases, various pieces of information are collected
about the patient, the type of service requested, and the service provided by the healthcare system. These
data capture events occurring in hospitals or other healthcare facilities, oﬀering greater accuracy while reducing
collection costs, as they are initially gathered for oﬃcial purposes and updated periodically.
On one hand, the use of already collected data have great advantages, but on the other hand, it can lead
to some problems. Since the primary purpose of administrative data collection is administrative rather than
clinical, some information relevant to the construction of frailty indicators may be missing. Moreover, when
dealing with sensitive data that can make individuals identiﬁable, it is essential to implement precautions to
ensure proper use.
Several indicators have been proposed in the literature to identify frailty using administrative data. These
indicators diﬀer based on their intended purpose: some are designed to predict adverse outcomes related to
frailty, while others assess the association between frailty and adverse events. Among the noteworthy indicators
based on administrative data are the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS) [Gilbert et al., 2018], the Elders Risk
Assessment (ERA) [Crane et al., 2010], the Frailty Index (D-FI) [Drubbel et al., 2013], the Risk Prediction
Model (RPM-S) [Soong et al., 2019], the Electronic Frailty Index (eFI) [Clegg et al., 2016], and the POSET
Frailty Index (FI-POS) [Silan et al., 2019, 2022].
The HFRS indicator aims to predict three outcomes: 30-day mortality, prolonged hospitalization and emer-
gency hospitalization within 30 days. Built using electronic hospital records of older individuals (aged 75 and
above), the indicator is calculated as the sum of scores assigned to a person’s diseases. These scores are propor-
tional to each disease’s ability to predict whether an individual belongs to a frailty cluster. The clusters were
identiﬁed through a cluster analysis, grouping individuals based on known frailty markers from the literature.
This indicator predicts adverse events with an Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) of 0.69 for 30-day mortality,
0.73 for prolonged hospitalization, and 0.61 for emergency hospitalization within 30 days.
The ERA is a prognostic indicator based on data from the Mayo Clinic (USA). Constructed from information
on demographic characteristics, health conditions and medical history relating to the two-year period 2003-2004,
it aims to predict the identiﬁcation of older adults at risk of hospitalisation or emergency room admissions in
the following two years.
The main risk factors were identiﬁed using statistical models, each of which was
assigned a score proportional to the estimated eﬀect. The frailty indicator varies between -7 and 32 and reﬂects
the individual risk level. However, the AUC of the model (0.678 overall, 0.640 for hospitalisation, 0.705 for
emergency room) indicates only moderate predictive performance.

The D-FI is developed on a sample of 1679 Dutch subjects over the age of 60.
It aims to predict the
occurrence of an adverse event between death, surgical emergency room visit, after-hours visit to the family
doctor and admission to a nursing home. The indicator values vary between 0 and 1 and is calculated as the
number of deﬁcits present out of the total of 36 considered. This indicator, with an AUC of 0.69, also shows a
moderate predictive ability with regard to the risk of at least one adverse event occurring.
The RPM-S is constructed on a sample of 2099252 individuals aged 65 years and older from clinical in-
formation in the UK Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) database. This indicator is developed with the aim
of predicting three adverse events: hospital mortality, emergency readmission within 30 days of discharge and
institutionalisation. To estimate the risk of these events, logistic regression models are used, which include as
predictors certain a priori selected risk factors, such as gender, age, cognitive and functional problems, comor-
bidities and hospitalisation. The indicator shows moderate predictive performance, with AUCs between 0.62
and 0.66 for hospital mortality, between 0.63 and 0.65 for institutionalisation, and between 0.57 and 0.63 for
30-day readmission.
The eFI is constructed using medical record data from the ResearchOne (for index estimation) and The
Health Improvement Network (for validation) databases. With the aim of predicting death, emergency admission
and nursing home admission several years later, the selection of variables is based on the scientiﬁc literature
and the deﬁcit accumulation paradigm, excluding factors with low prevalence. The ﬁnal indicator comprises 36
variables and assigns each subject a value between 0 and 1, calculated as the ratio of the number of deﬁcits
present to the total. The index shows good predictive ability in all the years considered and is now one of the
most widely used in the literature. Some extensions have been proposed in the United States [Pajewski et al.,
2019] and Japan [Nishimura et al., 2022].
The FI-POS aggregates 7 variables using the POSET method. The variables are selected through a series
of logistic models from a large set of health-related features obtained by cross-referencing multiple healthcare
databases. A partially ordered set (POSET) refers to a set of elements where an order relation can be estab-
lished, it is deﬁned as partially ordered because not all pairs of elements are comparable [Br¨uggemann et al.,
2004, Br¨uggemann and Patil, 2011, De Loof et al., 2011].
Recently, the POSET method has been applied
in various contexts, primarily in ranking theory, construction of composite indicators and causal inference in
multi-treatment cases [Silan et al., 2021, 2023]. The FI-POS indicator has been evaluated in predicting the
occurrence of six adverse events: death (AUC = 0.82), emergency room (ER) access with maximum priority
(AUC = 0.71), urgent hospitalisation (AUC = 0.69), avoidable hospitalisation (AUC = 0.77), hip fracture (AUC
= 0.75) and disability onset (AUC = 0.78).
Indeed, the FI-POS provides a benchmark for frailty indicators based on administrative data not only because
it has the best predictive performance among its class of indicators, but also because it is very parsimonious
(requiring a small set of variables easily available in any public health database). Its main disadvantage is that
only sortable variables can be used in the indicator construction since they need to be ordered by the POSET
method. This leads to the exclusion of some important subjects characteristics, ﬁrst and foremost their sex.
Our work aims to overcome this issue by proposing an indicator composed with administrative data, with a
predictive performance similar to that obtained from FI-POS, but generalisable to any kind of variable (ordinal
or not). This allows the full potential of administrative data to be exploited, not excluding any variable a priori.
As mentioned above, frailty indicators can also be obtained from survey data using ad-hoc interviews or
periodic surveys, which gather health-related information through questionnaires or physical tests. This type
of indicator will not be covered in our work. For further information, refer to Fried et al. [2001], Mitnitski et al.
[2001], Gobbens et al. [2010a,b, 2012], Santiago et al. [2018].
3
Constructing a super-classiﬁer for frailty representation
Key steps in constructing a composite indicator include selecting the variables to be aggregated and the aggrega-
tion procedure. To tackle both, we propose an innovative and eﬃcient methodology using classiﬁcation models
as elementary indicators to predict the probability of occurrence of health adverse events. Speciﬁcally, our main
contribution lies in the aggregation phase, while for the selection step, we provide a preliminary proposal that
can be customised depending on the context of the application.
3.1
Variable selection to reduce the number of frailty determinants
To reduce the number of frailty determinants used to predict the occurrence of an adverse event, we rely on a
measure of variable importance generated by a gradient tree boosting. Among all possible classiﬁers, we choose
the gradient tree boosting because of its computational eﬃciency in estimating the classiﬁcation rule and for the
4

ease of calculation of a measure to represent the variables importance, used to ordered the frailty determinants
according to their importance for the ﬁnal classiﬁcation.
Boosting is an iterative procedure designed to improve the predictive ability of classiﬁcation models (deci-
sional trees) by successively combining multiple classiﬁers to construct a more robust classiﬁcation rule. Each
iteration is estimated with a diﬀerent data set: the ﬁrst iteration starts with a model training on the original
data set; in the following iterations, misclassiﬁed observations enter the training procedure with greater weight
to improve the classiﬁcation rule [Friedman, 2001]. The ﬁnal boosting classiﬁcation rule is the combination of
the models, trained at each iteration and weighted by a measure of misclassiﬁcation: models with higher clas-
siﬁcation error will have less weight than those with lower classiﬁcation error. In particular, we use XGBoost,
a speciﬁc training algorithm of the gradient tree boosting where, at each iteration, a second order Taylor’s
approximation is used to quickly optimise the loss function [Chen and Guestrin, 2016].
The choice of this
algorithm is driven by its eﬃciency for sparse data.
The key aspect in the selection phase is the measure of importance. During the training process, at each
node of each tree of the gradient boosting, the q frailty determinants are evaluated to identify the best split
in the sample as the variable that brings the greatest reduction in the loss function. Therefore, if a variable
appears frequently as a split, it suggests that it is highly relevant for describing the observations, and we can
use this frequency as a measure of its importance. This importance can be statistically tested to determine
which variables are most signiﬁcant, using an approach similar to that of Rachid Zaim et al. [2020] for random
forests. While trees in random forests are independent, they are grown sequentially in boosting models and
can exhibit dependency. Therefore, we introduce randomness at each split to prevent this issue and extend the
testing of importance to gradient tree boosting. Speciﬁcally, for each tree, we randomly sample both a subset
of variables and a subset of observations; this randomisation helps reduce the dependency between splits. As a
result, under the null hypothesis, we assume independence between splits. The null hypothesis states that each
variable has the same probability of being selected as any other.
Formally, let Xp be the random variable representing the number of times the p−th frailty determinant is
used as a split across all the trees. It is approximately distributed as a binomial distribution Xp ∼Bin(np, θp),
where np is the number of nodes and θp is the probability of using the p-th explanatory variable as a split. We
can test the hypothesis that the probability associated with the p-th variable is the same as the one associated
with the other variables (H0 : ˆθp = θ0), whereas the alternative hypothesis states that this probability is greater
than the one of the other variables (H1 : ˆθp > θ0).
Under the null hypothesis of independence, θ0 is the probability that Xp is chosen as a split, given by
θ0 = 1/q, where q is the number of candidate variables at each node. The binomial test uses the statistic

Construction and use of the frailty indicator
The construction of the indicator can be summarised in a step-by-step procedure:
1. The data sources are identiﬁed (Section 4.1);
2. The selection of frailty determinants is carried out in four phases: a priori selection, exclusion of low-
prevalence variables, exclusion of variables positively associated with the outcomes and application of the
binomial test on the variable importance. See Section 4.2 for details;
3. The model is estimated by splitting the data into estimation and validation sets.
Then, the desired
outcome j (with j = 1, . . . , 6) is identiﬁed and a logistic regression is ﬁtted on the estimation set, which is
balanced through under-sampling: 20% of the sub-sample observations are equal to 1 for outcome j, and
80% are equal to −1. This procedure is further discussed in Section 4.3;
4. The quantities αj, γj, and cj are calculated by partitioning the estimation set into ten sub-sets. For
each unit in each group, the probability of developing the adverse event j is computed. One group k is
chosen (with k = 1, . . . , 10) and the cut-oﬀcjk is selected as the value that maximises both sensitivity
and speciﬁcity. The average of these cut-oﬀs is then used to determine cj. The predictive ability of each
logistic regression model is evaluated using cj as the threshold for outcome j. For each group k of the
estimation set, sensitivity, speciﬁcity, and AUC are computed, and average values of these parameters,
reported in Table 2, are used to determine sensj and specj. Finally, Equation (10) is applied to calculate
the quantities αj and γj. This phase is illustrated in Section 4.4;
5. For each adverse event j, the probability of occurrence fj(xi) is computed for each individual. The values
cj, ˆfj(xi), αj, and γj are then plugged into Equation (14) and a single indicator score is obtained for each
individual. The indicator score is then normalised using Equation (15), described in Section 4.5.

represent graphically in Figure 1 the data partitioning process.

population. Target population data come from the administrative health databases of the Epidemiological Unit
of the Prevention Department. We use data of two cohorts: one that received any form of medical assistance

are considered older adults with an age of 65 years and over. Frailty determinants were measured in 2016-2017
and 2017-2018, and adverse events in 2018 and 2019, respectively. Using a deterministic record linkage, fully

• Hospital discharge records;

• Local psychiatry registry;

• Drug payment exemption database;

4.2
Frailty determinants selection
We begin the frailty determinants selection process with 75 potential variables (see supplementary Table S1).
These include demographic characteristics of the patients, binary variables indicating the occurrence of a health
condition and count variables measuring the access to public health services. First, we remove 9 variables due to
their redundancy (duplicate variables, other composite frailty indicators, measures of health service duration).
Since determinants with low prevalence do not help to explain the health of population as a whole, even if
they might be relevant for a small group of individuals [Silan et al., 2022], we exclude 19 determinants with
low prevalence in our population (less than 1%). Subsequently, we discard the determinants with a positive
association with each speciﬁc outcome, as we are interested in constructing a frailty indicator that detects only
the deterioration of an individual’s health status. In order to identify the protective determinants, we take the
odds ratio with each adverse event as a univariate measure of association, considering as protective factors those
for which the odds ratio is signiﬁcantly < 1, with a 95% conﬁdence interval (see supplementary Table S2). For
adverse outcomes such as dementia and disability, we conduct the analyses only in the sub-population in which
the diagnosis of dementia and disability has not yet been made. In order to better understand its eﬀect on frailty,

sex is always included in the selection regardless of its eﬀect on the outcome. After this initial selection, we obtain
diﬀerent groups of frailty determinants for each outcome. Forty-three possible determinants are considered for
death, 45 for hip fractures, 47 for access to the emergency room with maximum priority, hospitalisation and
onset of disability and 44 for onset of dementia.
Subsequently, we proceed with the selection based on the binomial test. As mentioned in Section 3.1, this
selection relies on an importance measure obtained from a gradient tree boosting model. To ﬁt a model for each
outcome, we choose a set of optimal hyper-parameters via cross-validation, reported in supplementary Table S3.
We ﬁx the number of boosting iterations at 2000 to allow low-prevalence determinants to be considered among
the candidate split variables during the trees construction with a suﬃciently high probability. Then, for each
frailty determinant, we compute its importance by multiplying the proportion of times it was used as a split in
the trees by the total number of splits across all trees. Finally, we apply the binomial test with a 5% signiﬁcance
level to identify the most important frailty determinants for each outcome. Using this method, we end up with
15 selected frailty determinants for death, 16 for access to ER with the maximum level of emergency, 12 for hip
fracture, 16 for hospitalisation, 12 for the onset of dementia, and 16 for the onset of disability (see supplementary
Table S4).
4.3
Eﬀect of frailty determinants on adverse outcomes
Using the set of selected frailty determinants, we train the classiﬁcation rule, fj(x), used to construct the
indicator as described in Equation (13). To this end, we choose a logistic regression model, as it allows for a
direct interpretation of the eﬀect of each frailty determinant in predicting the occurrence of the outcome. The
logistic regression models are independently estimated on 75% of the total population (training set) to avoid
overﬁtting. When the outcome of interest is disability, individuals who already have a diagnosis of disability
are excluded from the sample before the splitting. Similarly, when the outcome is dementia, those with a prior
diagnosis of dementia are excluded. Since some outcomes show a low prevalence in our data, we undersample
the training set separately for each outcome. As a result, each of the six models is trained on a data sub-set
whose 20% of the observations is positive to the adverse event and 80% is not.
Then, we perform a more in-depth analysis on the variables with a protective eﬀect in the multivariate
logistic models. Since a frailty indicator aims to capture the health deterioration of the individual’s physical,
social and psychological condition, our interest is to understand the reasons behind the changes in the eﬀect of
some frailty determinants. At ﬁrst, for each outcome, we select the explanatory variables whose eﬀects change
in the multivariate context. Then, we conduct a speciﬁc analysis for each of these variables. We begin by
training a model using only one of these variables as a covariate. Based on the correlation matrix of the frailty
determinants (see supplementary Tables from S5 to S10), we sequentially add the most correlated variables
until the change in the eﬀect is observed. In this way, we can understand which combinations of determinants,
and in what order, modify the initial association between a determinant of frailty and the outcome.
Considering the death outcome, the ﬁrst frailty determinant showing a reverse eﬀect is related to the en-
docrine disorders. The reversal of the sign of the coeﬃcient occurs when the variables related to diabetes and
the number of multiple drugs prescriptions are included in the model. This result is likely due to the structure
of the variable associated with endocrine diseases, which is a binary indicator representing a macro condition:
it takes the value 1 if at least one of several underlying conditions, including diabetes, is present. As for mul-
tiple drugs prescriptions, the intake of a high number of medications is often a consequence of having multiple
chronic conditions, thus explaining the correlation with endocrine diseases. Then, we consider musculoskeletal
diseases, which are signiﬁcantly correlated with the number of emergency room visits. The inclusion of this
latter variable in the model is suﬃcient to cause a reversal in the eﬀect on the risk of death. In this case as well,
the variable is aggregated and captures the presence of speciﬁc musculoskeletal conditions, including fracture
diagnoses. It is plausible that the observed eﬀect reﬂects the tendency of individuals with suspected fractures to
visit the emergency room for further evaluation. As for urinary system diseases, no strong correlations (below
0.20) with other determinants emerges; however, a reversal in the eﬀect occurs in the presence of a combination
of variables: number of hospital admissions, emergency room visits, multiple drugs prescriptions, age and sex.
The same approach is also applied to the other adverse events, with the decision to analyse only the rela-
tionships that had not yet been investigated. Regarding the outcome of emergency room visits with a maximum
priority, we observe a reversal in the eﬀect of digestive system diseases, particularly after the inclusion of vari-
ables such as number of hospital admissions, emergency room visits, and multiple drugs prescriptions. This
reversal may reﬂect the frequency with which these conditions lead to acute episodes requiring urgent care,
especially among older individuals [ISTAT, 2020].

4.4
Parameters calculation
Once the frailty determinants and the classiﬁcation rules have been established, the next step consists in the
calculation of α, γ, and c for the aggregation phase. Data use is one of the main features of this phase, in
order to prevent possible overﬁtting. Given the low prevalence of some outcomes, it is not possible to split the
data in two (one for the regression models estimation and another for calculating the parameters) with a classic
train/test sets scheme because there would be a consistent probability that no events would be observed in either
set. Therefore, we use an approach similar to cross-validation. We utilise the models previously estimated on
the entire estimation set as classiﬁcation rules, and we divide the estimation set into ten groups, treating each
one in turn as a validation set.
To calculate cj for each outcome j, with j = 1, . . . , 6, we begin from a sequence of 500 values between 0 and
1. Then, we estimate the probability of developing the adverse event j for the ﬁrst subgroup of the estimation
set. Using each value of the sequence as a cut-oﬀto dichotomise the probability of developing the adverse event
j, we choose cj1 as the cut-oﬀfor the ﬁrst group, as the value that maximises the sensitivity and speciﬁcity
of the classiﬁcation rule. We repeat the procedure for all ten groups and we use the average of cj1, . . . , cj10 to
obtain a ﬁnal value for cj. For the calculation of αj and γj, we proceed similarly: using cj as the threshold
to dichotomise the probability of developing the adverse event j, for each subgroup of the estimation set we
calculate the sensitivity and speciﬁcity. We then use the average of the 10 sensitivities and speciﬁcities to
calculate sensj and specj respectively, which we substitute into Equation (10) to determine αj and γj. The
estimated parameters are reported in Table 2. We can observe that the estimated models overall show good
predictive capabilities, except for the one for the prediction of hospitalisation and disability, which shows the
lowest values in terms of AUC. This may be due to the selection of the determinants. Disability is among
the outcomes with the fewest explanatory variables, while hospitalisation is a condition that may result from
various factors not directly related to frailty.

Super-classiﬁer construction and normalisation
After calculating all quantities, we obtain the values of the indicator for each individual in the validation set.
We then use the outcome-speciﬁc models to calculate the probabilities that each individual will develop each
outcome and, using the quantities reported in Table 2, we calculate the frailty level for all individuals in the
new set. The distribution of the frailty indicator after the min-max transformation is shown in Figure 2. The
distribution highlights the presence of a large number of individuals with low frailty: approximately 50% of
the sample have an indicator smaller than 0.1.
These individuals are mostly under 75 years old and have
not developed many of the health conditions included in the indicator yet. Diﬀerences in the indicator based

et al., 2022]. To examine this, we standardise the frailty indicator by age and observe higher values for men
than for women. Nevertheless, to remain consistent with the guidelines provided by Rockwood [2005] regarding
the characteristics a frailty indicator must have, we adopt the non-standardised version of the indicator in the
subsequent analyses.

So far, we identiﬁed a value between 0 and 1 that reﬂects the individual’s frailty level. However, we are
not immediately able to classify a person as frail or not frail, and this is particularly relevant when identifying
targets of interventions [Royston et al., 2006]. To do this, it is useful to identify a threshold frailty level that
allows for a distinction between frail and non-frail individuals. Despite its importance, there is still debate
in the literature about how to identify this threshold. The aggregation method presented in Section 3 allows
the direct construction of a dichotomous indicator, if the formulation in Equation (13) is adopted instead of
that in Equation (15). Speciﬁcally, for each outcome, we assess the indicator’s ability to correctly predict the
occurrence (1) or non-occurrence (−1) of the adverse event using the F1 score (the harmonic mean of sensitivity
and speciﬁcity) and the false negative rate (FNR) of classiﬁcation (Table 4).
Given the low false negative rates, it turns out that most of the classiﬁcation errors are false positives.
Therefore, many individuals are classiﬁed as frail, and therefore at risk of adverse events, even though they
do not actually experience them. This suggests that the our indicator tends to classify individuals as frail
rather than as healthy. However, in the context of frailty estimation, a high percentage of false positives is not
excessively problematic, provided that the percentage of false negatives remains low.
Given the importance of maintaining a low number of false negatives, we ﬁnally assess whether the indicator
systematically fails to identify speciﬁc individuals as frail, i.e., whether it excludes certain subgroups of the
population more frequently. Using the number of individuals classiﬁed as true positives, true negatives, false
positives and false negatives stratiﬁed by frailty determinants and outcome-speciﬁc variables (see supplementary
Table from S11 to S16), we evaluate whether the prevalence of each frailty determinant within the false negative

group diﬀers signiﬁcantly from its prevalence in the general population. This evaluation is based on Wilson

related to diseases of the blood and the hematopoietic system are more prevalent among false negatives. In the
case of emergency room visits, respiratory system diseases also show a higher prevalence in this group. For hip
fractures and the onset of disability, nervous system diseases appear more frequently among those the indicator
fails to identify as frail. Regarding the onset of dementia, mental and behavioural disorders are more common
in the false negative group. Finally, for hospitalisations, individuals aﬀected by neoplasms, cancer and mental
and behavioural disorders are disproportionately represented among the false negatives.
6
Discussion and conclusion
6.1
Key strengths
In this work, we propose a new method to construct a frailty indicator using administrative data with the aim
of stratifying the population according to the frailty status of each individual. Our main contribution lies in the
indicator construction pipeline, speciﬁcally in the frailty determinants aggregation phase. The indicator is based
on a combination of classiﬁcation models (classiﬁers), each of which predicts the probability of developing a
speciﬁc adverse event associated with frailty. The accuracy measures of these classiﬁers, in particular sensitivity
and speciﬁcity, are used as weights for the ﬁnal aggregation. Since the classiﬁcation rules are independent, we
can include all outcomes that we consider important in explaining the frailty condition (six, using the data
described in Section 4.1). In addition, we can use a small set of determinants, speciﬁc for each outcome, to
predict the occurrence of the adverse event: 10 determinants are used to predict death, 9 to predict emergency
room access, 5 to predict hip fracture, 13 to predict hospitalisation, 6 to predict the onset of dementia and 9
to predict the onset of disability. This procedure allows us to diﬀerentiate and characterise the explanatory
variables, generating a more accurate indicator. For the frailty determinants selection process, we use a gradient
tree boosting, but it is also possible to use any method capable of performing a variable selection. The freedom
in the choice of the selection method and the number of outcomes to be considered leaves this methodology
the possibility to be improved and also to adjust to diﬀerent needs. In addition, selecting a diﬀerent subgroup
of frailty determinants for each outcome allows us to include variables whose eﬀects on the probability of
experiencing adverse events diﬀer depending on the outcomes. For instance, women are more likely than men to
experience a hip fracture or the onset of dementia, while they are less likely to develop other adverse outcomes.
Comparing our indicator to the FI-POS indicator (a good benchmark among the indicators obtained from
administrative data), our proposal is capable of including the sex of the individual, and potentially any other
non-ordered variable, into the set of frailty determinants. We also tried to construct the frailty indicator again
excluding the sex from the set of frailty determinants to better compare the AUCs with those obtained from
the FI-POS indicator. This is also useful to understand how important the contribution of sex is in our frailty
estimation process. If we exclude the sex, we obtain slightly lower values of AUC in all the outcomes (0.857
for death, 0.819 for emergency room visits with maximum priority, 0.654 for hospitalisation, 0.761 for disability
onset and 0.817 for dementia onset), with the exception of the hip fracture (AUC = 0.787) adverse event.
6.2
Limits and improvements
As already mentioned, our indicator’s prediction of the hospitalisation event shows a lower AUC compared to
the other events’ prediction, suggesting that the model may not be optimal for predicting this outcome, as it
is very generic and can have many diﬀerent causes, not necessarily related to frailty. Indeed, among the main
causes of hospitalisation is trauma, a condition that also involves other spheres of the older person’s health
beyond frailty [ISTAT, 2020]. To improve the hospitalisation predictive capacity, it might be useful to include
additional explanatory variables in the classiﬁer; in particular, determinants variables not directly related to
the frailty condition could be included if useful to explain the process underlying hospitalisation. However,
it is worth noting that this limitation ultimately does not lie in the aggregation method but in the choice of
outcomes.
At the variable selection stage, some problems could arise in using the binomial test to identify the most
important determinants of frailty. The test performed on the number of times a variable is used as a split
in gradient tree boosting does not take into account the decrease in the variance of the residuals. This could
result in the selection of a determinant that is used many times as a split in the ﬁnal part of the trees, but
whose contribution to decreasing variance is less than a determinant used a smaller number of times as a split
15


## Page 16


in the top part of the trees. In addition, our ﬁnal selection identiﬁes a very small number of determinants for
the hip fracture and onset of dementia adverse events, a number that may not fully describe the underlying
frailty mechanism. This over-selection could be due to the low prevalence of such outcomes in the population.
Therefore, a diﬀerent methodology could be used to select the most important determinants of frailty that can
better handle the low population prevalence of some adverse events.
Our work applied the frailty indicator exclusively to administrative data from a speciﬁc health authority.
To further evaluate its robustness, it would be better to test the indicator using diﬀerent data. Applying our
pipeline to build the indicator to diﬀerent administrative data should be straightforward, as the reduced set
of variables we selected are commonly collected across health authorities. This process may present additional
challenges, but could provide further insights on the indicator’s versatility and reliability. Finally, in order to
have greater consistency of the frailty indicator over time, it would be recommended to update α and γ using
new data, including consecutive data batches in the estimation set, and then updating the indicator with the
new information. This could increase the predictive capabilities of the indicator, but it could also increase its
dependence on the data used.
6.3
Conclusion
With the increase in the older population worldwide, especially in Italy, there has also been an increase in the
prevalence of age-related diseases. These diseases reduce healthy life expectancy with important consequences
on the use of healthcare and welfare resources. Frailty is a condition typical of older individuals that involves
multiple aspects of a person’s life, starting with a deterioration in overall health, such as loss of autonomy,
or increased vulnerability to the development of adverse events, such as hospitalisation, onset of dementia or
disability. Among the older population, frail individuals are those most in need of care, so it is essential to identify
them early to implement prevention policies to improve their quality of life [Bergman et al., 2002]. Therefore,
the use of a tool capable of measuring the level of frailty of individuals using the healthcare administrative data
plays a fundamental role.
To this end, we develop an indicator to measure the frailty level in the older population based on administra-
tive data. We deﬁne frail individuals as those who have an increased risk of developing adverse events related to
their health condition. The indicator is based on the risk of adverse events like death, hip fracture, emergency
room visits, hospitalisation, dementia and disability. The frailty determinants are selected using the impor-
tance measure of a gradient boosting model, and we use a novel aggregation method to combine single-outcome
classiﬁers, weighted by accuracy measures, to generate a frailty score between 0 and 1. The indicator shows
good predictive ability for adverse events in 2018 and 2019, comparable to other state-of-the-art indicators, such
as the FI-POS. It also shows the possibility to predict adverse events two years after the frailty determinant
recording, conﬁrming its robustness over time. One of the main advantages of our method is the possibility
to use non-ordered variables (such as sex) as frailty determinants, which was not possible with the FI-POS
aggregation technique. To conclude, our indicator can be an important improvement for healthcare authorities,
as it helps to identify vulnerable individuals using common variables easily available from administrative data
collected for other purposes, without increasing costs and resources.
