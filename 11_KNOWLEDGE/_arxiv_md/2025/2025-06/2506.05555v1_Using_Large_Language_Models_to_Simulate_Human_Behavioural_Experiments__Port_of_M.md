Abstract
Collective risk social dilemmas (CRSD) highlight
a trade-off between individual preferences and the
need for all to contribute toward achieving a group
objective. Problems such as climate change are
in this category, and so it is critical to understand
their social underpinnings. However, rigorous
CRSD methodology often demands large-scale
human experiments but it is difficult to guaran-
tee sufficient power and heterogeneity over socio-
demographic factors. Generative AI offers a po-
tential complementary approach to address this
problem. By replacing human participants with
large language models (LLM), it allows for a scal-
able empirical framework. This paper focuses
on the validity of this approach and whether it is
feasible to represent a large-scale human-like ex-
periment with sufficient diversity using LLM. In
particular, where previous literature has focused
on political surveys, virtual towns and classical
game-theoretic examples, we focus on a complex
CRSD used in the institutional economics and
sustainability literature known as Port of Mars.
1. Introduction
A Collective Risk Social Dilemma (CRSD) is a situation
where a group must reach a collective target through indi-
vidual contributions where everybody suffers if they fail to
achieve the target (Milinski, 2021). CRSD encompass many
of the challenges facing society today. Some examples in-
clude emission reductions to reduce risks of climate change,
and the vaccination for infectious diseases to create herd
immunity.
Societal challenges related to the need for collective action
are well-studied, in particular in trying to understand the
governance of shared resources (e.g. land, forests, water,

Experimental social science, rather than observational social

social scientists rule out confounding effects when analysing
the factors contributing to collective action. Ascertaining
causality can prove difficult with the kinds of data available

This is why experimental CRSD research is needed. How-

cost, of large-scale human experiments. It is difficult to cre-

heterogeneity amongst participants can limit generalizabil-
ity. Our hypothesis, along with others (Dillion et al., 2023;
Xu et al., 2024), is that large language models (LLMs) can
be used to mitigate some obstacles that would otherwise
make experimental CRSD research difficult. LLMs will not
replace experimental research but can provide a useful com-
plement, for example by identifying relevant configurations
of experiments with human participants to implement and
to provide predictions for those experiments.
LLMs have the potential to model knowledge, beliefs and
patterns of social behaviour, facilitating in silico research
on human social behaviour (Argyle et al., 2023; Aher et al.,
2023; Grossmann et al., 2023). Due to being trained on a
large corpus of human data, it is plausible to expect that
LLMs may contain substantial latent social information
(Horton, 2023). LLMs could be cost-effective / efficient
tools to aid empirical studies by narrowing the pool of hy-
potheses and gaining guiding insights. However, the condi-
tions that must hold for LLM-“participants” to be viable as
a tool of in silico research remain an equally open-question.

mic fidelity, and the implication is that it would be valid to
conduct in silico research on LLM agents in such a setting1.
Algorithmic fidelity has predominantly received interest in
domains where the goal is to characterize a distribution of
static human responses (e.g. survey data Argyle et al. (2023)
and structured interview data Amirova et al. (2024)). These
studies did not put multiple LLM agents together in the
same environment to study their social interactions or how
they solve problems together. Seperately, another line of
work has focused on socially interacting LLM agents. Gen-
erative agents, such as those studied in (Park et al., 2023),
have been used to simulate groups of interacting individuals.
These methods have potential in agent-based modeling of
collective action dilemmas (Poteete et al., 2010). They en-
able the creation of a new kind of agent-based model where
it is possible to study the emergence of group-level social
phenomena from the interaction of individual agents. These
individuals can now talk to each other and utilize much
more complex kinds of cognition than was possible with
simpler agents (Vezhnevets et al., 2023; Ghaffarzadegan
et al., 2024).

In this work, we explore LLM agents in a novel CRSD.
We initially validate the algorithmic fidelity of LLM agent
responses within the CRSD by directly comparing against
human responses and metrics from the original works. Then,
based on an underlying assumption of algorithmic fidelity,
we demonstrate the flexibility and power to model and study
the interactions of differing social personas within CRSDs.
In particular, we focus on Social Value Orientation (SVO) as
the primary way of defining differences between our agents
and study how this impacts the outcomes in the CRSD.
Furthermore, we study the effects of leadership on top of
the SVO outcomes, demonstrating the flexibility of traits
and problem variants that can be effectively studied under
an LLM agent approach.
2. Port of Mars
Collective action problems are widely studied (Ostrom,
2000), but they are generally abstracted as simple matrix
games (Trivers, 1971; Axelrod & Hamilton, 1981). While
such abstractions have been used for decades, many have
argued that they are of limited utility for predicting human
behaviour in more complex (and realistic) settings (Janssen
et al., 2010; Perolat et al., 2017; Hertz et al., 2024). In
particular, matrix games generally ignore critical features of
real-world social dilemmas (Leibo et al., 2017). Firstly, they
miss the temporal extendedness of real-world dilemmas.
Secondly, they do not require decisions with only partial
information about the state of the world, the activities of oth-
ers and true uncertainty. For example, the true impact of the
climate crisis and human action towards it is an unknown,
and unexpected events will occur regularly. In traditional
public goods / common pool resources experiments, deep
uncertainty is often elided since the probabilities of events
are known (e.g. the probabilities of stochastic events in a
poker game). Therefore, it is important to ensure any new
framework is capable of introducing ‘unknown unknowns’
to represent the “sometimes we just have to cross that bridge
when we come to it” feature of real-world dilemmas (Knight,
1921; Savage, 1951; Binmore, 2007; Johanson et al., 2022).
We must also consider how LLMs may impact methodolog-
ical decisions, such as player motivation. Often monetary
rewards are attached to performance, however, it is debated
whether this an effective motivator (Smith & Walker, 1993).
Gneezy & Rustichini (2000) found that appealing to moral
incentives may be more effective. In contrast to AI research
methods, like reinforcement learning, LLMs have no natu-
ral analog of reward. Therefore it is unnatural to motivate
LLMs with monetary incentives. Instead, LLM agents can
be motivated in other ways, e.g. by appealing to their social
identity, or by asking them to role play as a certain character.
We must choose a CRSD that appeals to such incentives
in order to motivate behaviour. However, a critical prob-
lem is the extensive literature that exists about CRSD. This
contaminates the LLM training data, making it difficult to
separate the LLMs social capabilities from simply recre-
ating strategies discussed in the literature. We require a
near novel CRSD that is not prominent in the LLM training
corpus.
We have chosen the Port of Mars (PoM) (Janssen et al.,
2020) game for the following reasons: 1) PoM meets the cri-
teria of Hughes et al. (2018) and thus is a sequential social
dilemma2, this may render it full of potential for emergent
social complexity according to conjectures such as those
of Leibo et al. (2019) 2) PoM focuses on building a nar-
rative for the players to engage with. This naturally fits
the LLM framework. 3) PoM is specifically designed to
include ‘unknown unknowns’ (it is a game of incomplete
information in the game-theoretic sense), 4) PoM is a rela-
tively novel game. While it has been previously described
(Janssen et al., 2020), it has a limited presence in the wider
literature. This suggests that training data contamination is
unlikely to prove overly problematic.
PoM (Janssen et al., 2020) is a resource allocation game
where five players balance individual goals against the con-
flicting needs of maintaining shared infrastructure called
System Health, in the face of ongoing environmental, social,
and technical challenges. The narrative identifies the players
as members of Generation Zero: the first group of long-term
residents to arrive on the Red Planet, and the Players expe-
rience the challenges of life as early citizens of a Martian
settlement. Full game details are provided in Appendix A.
Each player has a unique role: the Curator, Pioneer, Re-
searcher, Politician, and Entrepreneur. Although the roles
have a different narrative, the underlying payoff structure
is the same. This is not known by the players. Shared in-
frastructure degrades due to wear and tear. Players must
maintain a minimum level of shared infrastructure for the
game to continue. Players spend personal resources on
shared infrastructure or on acquiring points to win. ’Dirty
cards’ exist which players can claim for points at the ex-
pense of the shared infrastructure. As such, the game expe-
rience provides a fundamental CRSD between individual
and collective goals. Each round features random event
cards. These can lead to reductions of shared infrastructure
(e.g. crop failures) or other positive / negative consequences.
Normally, players draw one event card per round, however
this increases as the shared infrastructure degrades. These
events are a source of incomplete information, as the deck
of event cards is unknown, and the LLM players do not
retain memory of previous games to memorise event cards.

3. Framework Overview
All of the players in our framework are controlled by a single
LLM, and they are differentiated through their prompting.
All of the prompts, at minimum, contain the following in-
formation:
1. Game Description - A minimal description of the rules
and narrative such that the LLM can play the game
fully without requiring additional game information.
2. Round Information - Description of event cards that
have been drawn in this round. Information about level
of system health.
3. Player Identity - Provides the player role (e.g. Pioneer),
and a description of their personality / traits.
4. Phase Information - Description of what currently
needs to be done by the LLM player.
Full prompts for each phase are provided in Appendix B.
Our version of PoM consists of 9 rounds, however the LLM
agents are not aware of the number of rounds. In each round
the players make the following decisions (also demonstrated
in Fig. 1):
1. Handle specific decisions relating to events.
2. Have a planning meeting with all of the other players.
3. Create or update longer-term plans.
4. Spend time-blocks on either the system health or the
resources.
5. Propose and accept/reject trade requests with other
players for resources.
6. Decide whether to complete / discard available accom-
plishment cards.
An early problem we encountered was that providing full
histories of the game did not lead to strong gameplay (e.g.
forgetting the rules). However, without full history we often
found that round-by-round behaviour was inconsistent, for
example with players acquiring different resources across
round that were not consistent with the accomplishment
card they were aiming for in the earlier rounds. To over-
come this, we carry three pieces of information through
the rounds. First, we construct a summary of the round for
the players that tracks information on trades that happened,
system health spending in the previous the round, and an
LLM generated summary of the planning meeting. Sec-
ondly, in order to generate more consistent decision-making,
we get the LLM players to generate two plans that are used
to condition future behaviour: 1) Health Plan - The players
decide how much of their time-blocks they want to invest in
the system health, with their planning being contingent on
the current rounds events and the player planning meeting,
alongside the general information, and 2) Accomplishment
Plan - The players decide which of their available accom-
plishment cards they want to complete next. At the start of
each round, players can either decide to continue working
towards the accomplishment card they worked towards in
the previous round, or update to a new one. We found em-
pirically that without these elements it was difficult to get
strong, consistent behaviour from the LLM players.
4. Experiments
All experiments used gemini-1.5-flash-002 (Team et al.,
2024) for the LLM players. The purpose of this work is not
to test the differences between specific LLMs (e.g. OpenAI
vs. Gemini vs. Claude vs. etc...) behaviour in these social
science experiments, and due to lack of resources we chose
the gemini model as it provided free-tiered API requests.
4.1. Algorithmic Fidelity
We begin our experiments by partially validating algorithmic
fidelity, adapting the concept from (Argyle et al., 2023)
to our LLM framework. In this work, we do not claim
that we fully validate algorithmic fidelity, as were were
only able to perform 2/4 of the tests due to the lack of
corresponding human data. Specifically, we were unable
to perform the social science Turing test here since we
did not have parallel human-generated gameplay text, for
example communication logs, (though it would be possible
to add in the future), and also backward continuity required
large-scale human evaluation of the LLM outputs, which
we defer to future work. Therefore, our focus is on forward
continuity and pattern correspondence as we have accessible
human data to check these conditions. We provide these
results as an initial proof of concept, and in combination
with results in later sections which are well validated by
the wider social science literature, we believe we can make
reasonable claims about the replication of human behaviour
in our experiments.

should proceed naturally from the conditioning context pro-
vided. We choose to initially demonstrate this by evalu-

to elicit obvious responses. For example, we prompted
two types of players: Cooperative players with Altruis-
tic, Cooperative, Empathetic, Generous, Selfless traits and
Selfish players with Self-Centred, Selfish, Uncooperative,
Machiavellian traits. We do this because the goal of for-
ward continuity is to probe the features of the distribution,
P(behaviour|backstory). This distribution is naturally sum-

Pattern Correspondence is when the LLM responses re-
flect the underlying patterns of relationships between de-
mographics and the behaviours that would be observed in
comparable human data. In order to test for this, we make
direct comparisons to the original PoM study (Janssen et al.,
2020). In the original study, the authors required partici-
pants to fill out a survey which placed them into one of four
groups (Douglas, 2002): Hierarchical Individualist (HI), Hi-
erarchical Communitarian (HC), Egalitarian Individualist
(EI) or Egalitarian Communitarian (EC), as a characterisa-

provided results that showed differences in decision-making
and the results based on which category the players fell
into. Within our framework, we used the description of each
of these cultural groups from Zanocco & Jones (2018) as
the personality prompts of each of the agents, instead of
just using the names (e.g. prompting them as a Egalitarian
Individualist). The prompts for the four cultural groups are:
• Hierarchical Individualist - You value a well-defined
social order and respect authority, yet emphasise per-
sonal freedom and personal achievement within that
structure.
• Hierarchical Communitarian - You value a struc-
tured society where roles are clearly defined. You
prioritise community welfare and collective responsi-
bilities.
• Egalitarian Individualist - You prioritise personal
achievements. You see interactions as negotiations and
value free competition among individuals.
• Egalitarian Communitarian - You place high value
on the collective and prioritise making decisions that
do not negatively impact anyone.
Our goal is to demonstrate that LLM agents prompted as
these groups reflect similar relationships between the groups
as observed in the human data.

4.2. SVO
For our first extension to the original PoM study, we study
the impact of Social Value Orientation (SVO) on player out-
comes. SVO is a measure of the magnitude of the concern
people have for others (Murphy et al., 2011), which was
found to have the strongest correlation with decisions in
social dilemmas (Thielmann et al., 2020). Mathematically,
the social value of an agent can be specified using a point on
a circle centred at the origin, where the x-axis is the agent’s
reward and the y-axis is the arithmetic mean of all of the
other agents’ rewards. SVO angles can generally fall into
four categories (Murphy et al., 2011): Altruism (SVO an-
gle > 57.15◦), Prosocial (22.45◦< SVO angle < 57.15 ◦),
Individualism (-12.04◦< SVO angle < 22.45 ◦), and Com-
petitiveness (SVO angle < -12.04◦). In our framework, we
provide the players with a description of the SVO measure
(in Appendix B), and a corresponding SVO angle which
they represent.
In these experiments, the group consists of players with
SVO angles {−15, 0, 15, 30, 60}, randomly assigned to
roles each run to eliminate role-specific influence (e.g. the
Politician role affecting decisions). Results are shown in
Fig. 2 with three visualisations. An individual point of note
is that 81% of games ended in survival, significantly higher
than the survival rates in recent human PoM studies (Janssen
et al., 2024). The recent experiments were performed in
an online environment in contrast to (Janssen et al., 2020)
which was a card game. Possible explanations include: 1)
LLM players may exhibit inherently prosocial behaviour
that SVO prompting cannot fully override, or 2) our frame-
work ensures consistent communication, which corresponds
with findings in (Janssen et al., 2024) that groups that sur-
vived communicated more, a factor we explore in Sec. 4.3.
Figs. 2A and 2B detail how players’ SVO angles affect

4.3. Communication
Fig. 3 presents the results of experiments examining the role

4.4. Leadership
One of the key advantages of an LLM-based approach, as
highlighted earlier in the paper, is the ability to easily inves-
tigate the effects of varying social variable compositions, a
challenge in human-based studies. In this work, we focus on
the concept of leadership and its impact on outcomes. Lead-
ership is a well-research topic in social dilemmas (Ruve
& Wilke, 1984; De Cremer, 2000; Van Vugt et al., 2004;
Van Vugt & De Cremer, 1999), with findings such as the
self-moderating effects of SVO on leadership influencing
outcomes (Van Dijk & De Cremer, 2006). Our results are
presented in Fig. 4.
In these experiments, we modified prompts to designate one
player as the leader and tested three variations of how this
leadership role was communicated. In the ‘Vanilla’ setting,
only the leader is informed of their role, with the option to
disclose it to others. In the ‘Announce’ setting, the leader
is openly identified to all player. In the ’Unaware’ setting,
the leader is not told their role, but the other players are
informed of who the leader is–without knowing the leader is
unaware. Each leadership variant was tested over 50 games,
with win rates and detailed breakdowns shown in Fig. 4.
In Fig. 4, we present survival rates dependent on both which
SVO angle player was the leader, and which leadership
variant was employed. A trend emerges: survival rates
decrease as the leader’s SVO angle increases (no matter the
variant), with the group surviving the least when the 30◦and
60◦players acted as the leaders. This result may align with
findings from (Van Dijk & De Cremer, 2006), which suggest
that pro-self (low SVO angle) leaders are more likely to act
in self-interest. In this context, self-interested play involves
encouraging high system health spending from others while
maximising individual points, leveraging their leadership
role to ensure the port’s survival.
Another way to view this is that pro-self leaders may engage
in “cheap talk” (Cartwright et al., 2025), influencing others’
behaviour through verbal framing rather than altering their
own resource allocation. As leaders, they may influence
the group’s decision-making without necessarily increasing
their pro-social contributions. We give two examples of this
for the -15◦SVO player when they were acting as the leader,
demonstrating how their comments during communication
do not necessarily match the actions that they take:

5. Conclusion & Future Work
This work is an initial attempt to understand the behaviour
of LLM agents in CRSDs. Specifically, we began by ex-
ploring two of the conditions of algorithmic fidelity within
a novel CRSD, Port of Mars. We hypothesise that PoM
is an ideal candidate for LLM experimentation on CRSDs,
in particular due to lack of strategy contamination in the
LLM training data, unlike other CRSDs. We demonstrate
initial signs of algorithmic fidelity, in terms of two of the
four conditions over a range of personality variables. We
leverage this validation to further investigate a selection of
variables that have been shown to impact social behaviour
in social dilemmas, demonstrating that LLMs can simulate
the results of human behaviour experiments. Future work
can take two avenues for advancing our understanding of
human behaviour in CRSDs - first by further validating the
algorithmic fidelity of an LLM framework through more
comparisons to human results, and second by exploring the
impacts of other personal and social variables at a scale that
is difficult to match in human experiments.

A. Port of Mars
In the game, System Health is a number that represents the physical health of the community - the condition of infrastructure,
production of food, water, and breathable air, radiation shielding, energy production, and other necessary resources. This
number begins at 100 at the start of the game, and goes down by 25 points each round, representing wear and tear on
infrastructure and consumption of resources. If System Health reaches zero, the community does not survive, and everyone
dies. No one wins if everyone is dead.
Each player receives ten Time Blocks per round. These blocks can be spent to improve System Health of to pursue their own
ambitions. Each Time Block spent on System Health raises the System Health number by one point. Players may also spend
Time Blocks to purchase Influence cards. These cards represent the player’s capacity to impact specific domains. There are
five kinds of Influence cards: Culture, Legacy, Science, Governance, and Finance. Players can spend combinations of their
accrued Influence cards to purchase Accomplishment cards, which represent significant achievements in their domain - e.g.
scientific discoveries, cultural productions, political milestones, etc., and allow players to earn points to win the game.
Each player may earn one type of Influence card cheaply, and two other types at greater expense. Each player has two types
of Influence cards they may not earn through investment of Time Blocks. For example, the Politician may exchange two
Time Blocks for one Governance card, three Time blocks for a Culture or Legacy card, but may not exchange their Time
Blocks for Science of Finance cards. To acquire Influence cards, they are unable to earn directly, players may arrange trades
with one another. Under normal circumstances (barring certain events which are described in more detail below), players can
communicate with each other through a discussion phase that is available at the start of each round. Once players have the
right combinations of Influence cards, they may use them to purchase an Accomplishment card that will give them points.
At the end of the game, if the community survives, the player with the most points wins.
Each round features unexpected developments in the form of random event cards. These can lead to reductions of shared
infrastructure (e.g. dust storms, solar flares, crop failures) or other consequences, positive or negative. Normally, players
draw one event card per round, however this increases as the shared infrastructure degrades. If System Health falls below
65, they draw two cards, and if it falls below 35, players draw three cards. With more than 10 events there are millions of
possible sequences of events. Each game is unique, and the possibility of events creates uncertainty, especially since players
do not know what kind of events are possible.
