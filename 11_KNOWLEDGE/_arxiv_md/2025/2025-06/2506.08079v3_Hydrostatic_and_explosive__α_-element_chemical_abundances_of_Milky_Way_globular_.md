ABSTRACT
Stellar atmospheric element abundance ratios of stars retain information about their birth conditions, helping elucidate their
origin and nature. In this letter, we analyse and contrast the hydrostatic and explosive 𝛼-element abundance ratios, and the ratio of
the two (the hex ratio), for a large sample of Galactic globular clusters (GCs), halo substructures, satellite galaxies, and the Milky
Way high-/low-𝛼discs using data from the APOGEE survey. Our results show that: 𝑖) Milky Way GCs and halo substructures
appear to have qualitatively similar hex ratios across a broad range of [Fe/H], that are higher than that of dwarf satellite galaxies
of similar [Fe/H]; 𝑖𝑖) for all stellar populations studied, there is a trend in the hex ratio with [Fe/H]; 𝑖𝑖𝑖) there is a weak trend in
the hex ratio with respect to age for Galactic GCs, but not with initial or final GC mass; 𝑖𝑣) there are no differences in the hex
ratio between GCs formed in situ versus those labelled as accreted.
Key words: Galaxy: abundances – globular clusters: general – Galaxy: evolution
1 INTRODUCTION
The chemical abundance profile of stellar populations retain vital
clues about their origin and formation environment (Freeman &
Bland-Hawthorn 2002). Of the spectrum of elements, the 𝛼type
(namely, those composed from multiples of the 𝛼-particle, 4
2He) are
particularly interesting because they are created during the burning
and explosion of massive stars that detonate as type II supernovae,
one of the main drivers of the star formation and chemical enrichment
of galaxies. Thus, the 𝛼-elements are particularly useful for tracing
the star formation histories of galaxies, especially when contrasted to
heavier iron-peak elements produced predominantly during the ex-
plosion of type-Ia supernovae (i.e., the Tinsley-Wallerstein diagram,
Tinsley 1979).
While the 𝛼-elements are typically grouped into one category, not
all 𝛼-elements are produced in the same way. They can be broadly
sub-categorised into “hydrostatic” and “explosive” 𝛼-elements, de-
pending on if they are formed during nuclear fusion in the core of
massive stars (e.g., O, Mg) or during the type II supernovae explo-
sion (e.g., Si, Ca, Ti)1. Importantly, not all stars that detonate as
type II supernovae produce the same amount of 𝛼-elements. The
amount of hydrostatic and explosive elements synthesised depends
on the mass of the star; hydrostatic 𝛼-elements are solely produced
in the core of the most massive stars (𝑀★≈15 −30 𝑀⊙), whereas
explosive 𝛼-elements are created from all core-collapse supernovae
explosions (𝑀★> 8 𝑀⊙). Therefore, the ratio of the hydrostatic
to explosive 𝛼-elements, dubbed the “hex” ratio (McWilliam et al.

2013; Carlin et al. 2018), provides a way of inferring the production
of 𝛼-elements from the most massive stars to the total number of
core-collapse supernovae.
As the hex ratio traces how many massive stars have contributed
to the star formation of a system when compared to the total number
of supernovae type II, comparing the hex ratio between different
stellar populations provides a way to estimate the high-mass end of
the initial mass function (IMF). For example, Carlin et al. (2018)
examined the hydrostatic and explosive 𝛼-elements in the Sagittarius
dwarf spheroidal galaxy (Sgr dSph), finding that the system had
lower overall hex abundances when compared to Milky Way stars
of the same metallicity; from this the authors inferred that the Sgr
dSph is consistent with having formed from an IMF that lacks the
most massive stars (i.e., top-light or bottom-heavy IMF, see also
McWilliam et al. 2013). Similarly, Blancato et al. (2019) examined
the ratio of [Mg/Si] for Milky Way disc stars across [Fe/H] and age,
finding that there were small variations that could not be reproduced
with chemical evolution models that assume a fixed IMF (see also
Horta et al. 2022).
In this letter, we set out to examine the abundance of hydrostatic
and explosive 𝛼-elements, as well as the ratio between the two (i.e.,
the hex ratio), in the Milky Way high-/low-𝛼disc, globular clusters,
halo substructures, and satellite galaxies. Our aim is to examine and
contrast stellar populations formed at different ages and metallicities,
and under a wide range of star formation histories, using the well
measured 𝛼-elements in red giant stars from the APOGEE survey.

and the sample used; Section 3 presents the main results, including
an examination of the hydrostatic and explosive 𝛼-elements across

2 DATA
We use the final spectroscopic data release (DR17) from the
APOGEE survey (Majewski et al. 2017; Abdurro’uf et al. 2022).
APOGEE is a dual-helmisphere survey that collected near-infrared
data using two high-resolution spectrographs (Wilson et al. 2019)
mounted on the 2.5m telescope at Apache Point Observatory (Gunn
et al. 2006) in New Mexico, and the Du Pont telescope at Las Cam-
panas Observatory (Bowen & Vaughan 1973) in Chile. Stellar pa-
rameters and element abundances are determined using the ASP-
CAP pipeline (García Pérez et al. 2016) based on the FERRE code
(Allende Prieto et al. 2006), using the line-lists from Cunha et al.
(2017) and Smith et al. (2021). The spectra were reduced by a cus-
tomized pipeline (Nidever et al. 2015). All target selection criteria
for APOGEE and APOGEE-2 are described in Zasowski et al. (2013)
and Zasowski et al. (2017), respectively; details of APOGEE-north
can be found in Beaton et al. (2021), whereas information about
APOGEE-south are contained in Santana et al. (2021).

2.1 Accounting for light-element variations in globular clusters
GCs are known to host multiple-populations (MPs; Bastian & Lardo
2018). The second generation/population type (SP) are known to host
light-element variations. Typically, SP stars are enriched in certain
light elements whilst simultaneously being depleted in others, and
this chemical profile isn’t manifested in the first population (FP)
type. For example, SP stars typically show enhanced levels of Al and
depleted in Mg; the same effect happens for an enhancement in either
N or Na and depletion in C and O, respectively (e.g., Schiavon et al.
2017; Mészáros et al. 2020). Thus, in order to mitigate any possible
bias in our 𝛼-element analysis, we must account for these chemical
abundance anomalies arising from SP stars. To do so, we employ a
𝐾-means (unsupervised) clustering algorithm, in order to determine
the FP GC stars that we will use in our analysis. We run this clustering
procedure using the kmeans package in scikit-learn (Pedregosa
et al. 2011), and employing the following set of abundances for
all stars belonging to each GC: [Mg/Fe], [Al/Fe], [C/Fe], [N/Fe].
When running the code, we choose the following hyperparameters:
n-clusters= 2; random-state= 0; n-init=auto. We find that
using this set-up, we are able to successfully split FP from SP GC
stars (see Fig 1). We also tested using a support vector machine to
assess if we could obtain a better classification, but found that the
𝐾-means clustering provided similar classification performance.

4 SUMMARY & CONCLUSIONS
At constant metallicity, a higher hex ratio value implies that a stellar
population has undergone a star formation history that has endured
a higher amount of core-collapse supernovae from the most massive
stars; this is analogous to saying that a system was formed from
a top-heavier IMF. Conversely, a lower hex ratio implies a smaller
contribution from the most massive stars, and therefore, a more top-
light IMF. In this work, we have set out to examine the hydrostatic to
explosive 𝛼-element abundance ratio in a range of stellar populations
(namely, GCs, halo substructures, satellite galaxies, and the Galactic
discs), all observed with the APOGEE survey.
Fig 2 reveals that overall Galactic GCs and halo substructures
have similar hex ratios across a wide range in [Fe/H]. This hex ratio
is more enhanced in these systems when compared to Milky Way
satellite galaxies that —at fixed [Fe/H]— have on average a hex
ratio ≈0.1 dex lower. This lower overall hex abundance in satellite
galaxies is not due to a lower amount of hydrostatic 𝛼-elements alone,
as Milky Way satellites appear to also be depleted in explosive 𝛼-
elements when compared to Galactic GCs and halo substructures (see
the left and middle panels of Fig 2). At face value, this qualitative
examination implies that the Milky Way satellite galaxies studied
underwent a star formation history that is consistent with them being
formed under a more top-light IMF when compared to Milky Way
halo substructures and GCs.
Extra-Galactic studies modelling the luminosity of galaxies rang-
ing in mass and environment have found that those with higher star
formation rates tend to have formed from a more top-heavy IMF
(Hoversten & Glazebrook 2008; Meurer et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2009;
Gunawardhana et al. 2011; Cappellari et al. 2012; Weidner et al.
2013; Li et al. 2017; De Masi et al. 2018); follow up studies also
suggest that the IMF possibly varies with metallicity or time (Li
et al. 2023; Martín-Navarro et al. 2024; Bate 2025). Assuming that
the cause for the variation in the hex ratio at constant metallicity
between the GC/halo substructures and the living satellites is due to
variations in their star formation histories, and thus the high-mass
end of the IMF, our results support these extra-Galactic studies.
Interestingly, when examining the distribution of the mean hex
ratio as a function of average [Fe/H] for all stellar populations exam-
ined, we have found that GCs, halo substructures, and the Milky Way
high-/low-𝛼disc follow a relation in which the hex ratio decreases
with increasing [Fe/H] (see also Blancato et al. 2019). The best-fit
line to this relationship has a slope of 𝑚= −0.047 and an intercept
of 𝑏= 0.089. Along the same lines, the Milky Way satellite sample
also follows a linear trend, albeit different to that of the GCs/halo
substructures. The Milky Way satellites’ best-fit line has a slope of
𝑚sat = −0.048 and an intercept of 𝑏sat = −0.050.
We reason that the origin of the hex ratio trend with respect
to [Fe/H] is due to the contribution of explosive 𝛼-elements from
SNIa explosions (Johnson et al. 2020); at higher [Fe/H] there have
likely been more white dwarf supernovae detonations (that have a
delayed onset of ≈100 Myr when compared to SNII). This leads to
a higher contribution of both Fe and explosive 𝛼-elements (e.g., Si,
Ca, and Ti). As a result, [𝛼hyd/Fe] decreases with increasing [Fe/H]
but [𝛼exp/Fe] remains approximately constant with increasing [Fe/H]
(see the middle panel of Fig 2), yielding a decreasing hex ratio over-
all. However, in order to test this hypothesis, a full characterisation of
the star formation histories for each individual system across [Fe/H]
is needed.
Interestingly, we also find that there are some outliers in each
sample studied. For example, the Sagittarius dSph (Sgr) and its core
(M54) have a hex ratio that more closely follows the satellite galaxy
trend (see also McWilliam et al. 2013; Carlin et al. 2018). This is
perhaps not that surprising, given that Sgr is a satellite galaxy that
has recently been accreted by the Milky Way. In contrast, the Sex-
tans dwarf galaxy has a hex ratio that places it in the middle of
the GC/halo substructure distribution in the [Fe/H]-poor end (see
Fig A1). This result implies that Sextans likely formed from a more
top-heavy IMF when compared to its satellite galaxy counterparts
with approximately the same [Fe/H]. We also find that several halo
substructures (Pontus, Sequoia, and Wukong) have an almost iden-
tical hex ratio (and 𝛼-Fe abundance) to GES, in concordance with
the hypothesis from Horta et al. (2023) that these are likely differ-
ent parts of the GES debris. The same insight applies to Aleph and
Nyx, which show 𝛼-abundances and a hex ratio that is very similar
to the high-𝛼disc. In addition, there is one dwarf galaxy (Bootes I)
that has a hex ratio of ≈0 at a low [Fe/H] = −2.15. This implies
that this dwarf galaxy could have formed from a significantly more
top-light IMF, although see Filion et al. (2022). There are also two
GCs (NGC 6341 and NGC 7078) that show a lower hex ratio (≈0.1)
for their low metallicity ([Fe/H] ≈−2.3).
In addition to examining the distribution of hex ratios with respect
to [Fe/H], in Fig 3, we have explored how the hex ratio varies as a
function of age and mass for Galactic GCs. Overall, we find evidence
for a weak trend of an increasing hex value with increasing age,
irrespective of [Fe/H] (𝑚age = 0.012 and 𝑏age = 0.016). We also
investigated how the hex ratio varies with GC (initial and final) mass,
but (surprisingly) found no correlation. This (weak) trend with age
may possibly highlight a small variation in the high-mass end of the
IMF with GC birth time. However, the scatter around this relation is
significant, so we are unable to draw any strong conclusions from the
data. It would be interesting to follow up this line of research with
bigger samples, and including other field stellar populations with
accurate age/mass measurements.
Lastly, in Fig 4 we also compared the hex ratio with respect to
[Fe/H] for Galactic GCs that are deemed to be formed in situ or have
been accreted during galaxy mergers. Overall, we found that there

In summary, contrasting the hydrostatic and explosive 𝛼-element
chemical compositions (namely, the hex ratio) of stellar populations
provides a way of inferring the high-mass end of the IMF. In this
work, we have found that Milky Way satellite galaxies have over-
all a lower hex ratio when compared to Galactic GCs and halo
substructures at all [Fe/H]. It will be interesting to follow up this
analysis by contrasting different heavier neutron-capture subgroups
(𝑟/𝑠-process; e.g., Monty et al. 2024; Henderson et al. 2025), as well
as other elemental species (e.g., Hasselquist et al. 2021; Fernandes
et al. 2023) to gain further insight into the IMF and star formation
histories from which these systems formed. Furthermore, if accurate
stellar ages were available, it would also be interesting to quantify
how the difference in the hex ratios between stellar populations man-
ifests in terms of different IMFs; a possible avenue for exploring this
could involve flexible Galactic chemical evolution modelling.
