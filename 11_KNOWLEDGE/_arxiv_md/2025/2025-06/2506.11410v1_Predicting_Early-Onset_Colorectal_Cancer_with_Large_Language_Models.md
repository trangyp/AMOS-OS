Introduc.on 
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a signiﬁcant public health concern, ranking as the second leading cause of cancer-related 
deaths and the 4th most common new cancer diagnosis in the U.S. in 2024.1 While CRC has historically been 
considered a disease of older adults, there has been an increase in colorectal cancer diagnosed in individuals under 
50. Between 2011 and 2019, CRC incidence rates increased by 1.9% per year in people younger than 50 years.2,3 
Furthermore, between 2012 and 2021, among individuals aged 20 to 49, the incidence of advanced-stage colorectal 
cancer increased by approximately 3% per year.2,4 Because current screening guidelines recommend ini.a.ng rou.ne 
CRC screening at age 45, younger adults are oQen diagnosed at later, less treatable stages.5,6 Given these concerning 
trends, early iden.ﬁca.on of high-risk individuals is crucial for .mely interven.on and improved pa.ent outcomes. 
Beyond standard screening, addi.onal strategies are necessary to enhance the preven.on and early detec.on of CRC 
in younger popula.on. One promising approach is leveraging electronic health records (EHR) to iden.fy individuals 
at increased risk.7,8,9 By integra.ng diverse clinical and demographic data, EHR-based predic.ve approaches have the 
poten.al to enhance early detec.on eﬀorts, increase awareness among both pa.ents and healthcare providers, and 
facilitate .mely medical evalua.ons, ul.mately reducing the burden of EoCRC. 
Colorectal Cancer Risk Predic.on: Machine learning (ML) methods oﬀer considerable advantages by modeling 
complex, non-linear rela.onships and interac.ons within large datasets. ML-based approaches in CRC predic.on 
oQen demonstrate strong predic.ve performance and can handle noisy data, large sample sizes, and a higher number 
of predic.ve variables.10,11,12 Despite these advantages, many ML-based models are s.ll constrained by the 
incorporated features and the popula.ons they are trained on. 
Most exis.ng ML studies on CRC focus on a limited set of well-established risk indicators, including demographic data 
(age, race, family history), lifestyle factors (BMI, alcohol consump.on, red meat intake), and overt symptoms (rectal 
bleeding, blood in the stool, changes in bowel habits, abdominal pain, and blood count abnormali.es).11,13,14 While 
these factors are important, their u.lity for early detec.on is limited. Demographic-based models may not generalize 
well across diverse popula.ons, and symptom-based models depend on clinical signs that typically emerge in later 
disease stages.15 Younger individuals, in par.cular, may under-report or overlook symptoms, further delaying 
diagnosis.16 This narrow focus poten.ally obscures a broader picture that could be revealed by considering a more 
comprehensive range of health record data. Addi.onally, the low prevalence of CRC in the general popula.on

Methods 
The aim of this study is to predict the onset of CRC in the younger popula.on between 18 and 44 years of age, by 
employing both sta.s.cal machine learning models (ML) and large language models (LLM). The inclusion criteria for 
the CRC pa.ent group were: 1) conﬁrmed diagnoses of CRC in the EHR; 2) diagnosed at age 18-44. The inclusion 
criteria for the Non-CRC control group were: 1) absence of CRC diagnoses; 2) age between 18 and 44. The exclusion 
criteria for both groups consisted of: 1) any personal or family history of CRC; 2) diagnoses of Crohns disease, Lynch 
syndrome, or ulcera.ve coli.s. To train the machine learning models, we used pa.ent condi.ons, lab results, and 
observa.ons from the 2nd to the 7th month prior to the CRC diagnoses. We inten.onally excluded pa.ent data 
within the last month prior to diagnoses in order to avoid signals that were highly indica.ve of an impending 
diagnosis of CRC. As such, any pa.ents without 7 months of medical condi.ons, lab results and observa.ons would 
be excluded from the study. A set of ICD and SNOMED-CT codes related to each of the criteria were curated and 
reviewed by a gastroenterologist. We used these codes to iden.fy the cohorts in the Truveta database where all 
pa.ent data were already deiden.ﬁed. 
Dataset: A total of 1953 pa.ents were iden.ﬁed for the CRC group, which was less than 1% of the popula.on within 
that age range. Table 1 shows the sta.s.cs of the pa.ent demographics. Unlike the older CRC pa.ent popula.on 
between age 50 and 64,2 more women (66.34%) were found in our CRC pa.ent group, and 6% of them were pregnant. 
Their regular prenatal checkups provided more than 7 months of pa.ent journey in EHR and consequently made 
them eligible in our study.

We created a balanced training set with 1853 CRC pa.ents and 1853 Non-CRC pa.ents randomly sampled from the 
popula.on. The idea was to improve the predic.ve performance on the CRC group by under-sampling the 
overpresented Non-CRC pa.ents. Some studies detec.ng rare diseases applied synthe.c minority over-sampling 
technique (SMOTE) to up-sample the rare cases by interpola.on in feature space.20,21 Since LLMs process natural 
languages rather than numeric feature vectors, synthe.c longitudinal pa.ent events with realis.c EHR medical coding 
would be needed to provide augmented samples for the CRC pa.ent group.22 We leave this for future work. For the 
test set, we split the rest of the 100 CRC pa.ents into 10 diﬀerent runs, each combined with 990 Non-CRC pa.ents,

precision (or posi.ve predic.ve value (PPV)), nega.ve predic.ve value (NPV) and F1 measures across these 10 test

All available features, including condi.ons, laboratory results, and observa.onal data from EHR, were incorporated 
to examine the intricate rela.onships between variables comprehensively. Missing values were not removed; 
instead, they were treated as “not speciﬁed” to preserve poten.al signal. Feature engineering involved 
transforma.on of demographic variables into one-hot encoded vectors, indica.ng their presence or absence. 
Condi.ons were represented by how oQen they were diagnosed within the relevant .me period. For con.nuous lab 
results and condi.on values, mean values were used. 
To prevent informa.on leakage, the training and test sets were processed independently, leading to varia.ons in 
feature counts across datasets. The training set contained 11,509 features, while the 10 test sets had between 5,209 
and 5,783 features. This discrepancy stemmed from diﬀerences in sample sizes. To ensure a consistent feature space 
across all models, any features present in the training set but missing in a par.cular test set were added to that test 
set, with values set to zero. To mi.gate the poten.al for overﬁ`ng due to this sparsity, feature regulariza.on 
techniques were applied during model training (e.g., L1 or L2 regulariza.on, depending on the speciﬁc algorithm).23

The decision threshold for each model was op.mized to balance sensi.vity (recall) and speciﬁcity, given the very low 
prevalence of CRC cases in our test sets. The op.mal threshold was determined using Youden’s J Index (J = Sensi.vity 
+ Speciﬁcity - 1) calculated from the Receiver Opera.ng Characteris.c (ROC) curve.24,25 To ensure a robust and 
generalizable threshold, and to avoid overﬁ`ng, we performed 10-fold cross-valida.on, using imbalanced valida.on 
sets within each fold that mimicked the target CRC propor.on (approximately 1% CRC cases). 
Large Language Models: For the LLM approach, we employed OpenAI’s GPT-4o (version released 2024-08-06). Pa.ent 
informa.on, including demographics, was input as plain text. Medical condi.ons, lab results and observa.ons were 
represented by the names of their corresponding ICD, SNOMED-CT and LOINC codes in order to capture the clinical 
context. Although pa.ents could be reported with the same condi.ons or observa.ons from mul.ple encounters 
within 6 months prior to the onset of CRC, only one instance of condi.ons and observa.ons would be used in the 
input when promp.ng the LLM. The values of the lab results and their units of measure (when available) were 
included. However, only the latest value of the same lab tests would be used. 
The prompt comprised ﬁve components: 1) deﬁni.on of the AI assistant’s role; 2) Chain-of-Thought26 reasoning steps 
for CRC determina.on; 3) general guidelines for CRC diagnosis, synthesized from the U.S. Preven.ve Services Task 
Force recommenda.ons;27 4) a speciﬁed output format; and 5) pa.ent-speciﬁc input. This template, shown in Figure 
1, was designed to facilitate the processing of complex, long-context pa.ent informa.on that required extended 
reasoning and holis.c considera.on. Wong et al. proposed similar promp.ng approach, namely 
UNIMEDABSTRACTOR (UMA), to extract informa.on from clinical notes.28 When promp.ng LLMs, we set max tokens 
to 4096 and temperature to 0 in the chat comple.on, in addi.on to all the default parameters. 
We evaluated the performance of the base GPT-4o LLM and also the ﬁne-tuned version. Using the same training set 
as in the ML approaches, we ﬁne-tuned the LLM for 2 epochs.

Table 3 shows a compara.ve analysis of the average performance across 10 test runs for both the ML models and

speciﬁcity. RF also exhibited a balanced performance that was comparable to that of GPT4-o (Base). In contrast, 
methods such as LightGBM and HGBoost tended to achieve very high sensi.vity at the expense of speciﬁcity,

Conclusion 
Given the rising incidences of CRC in younger individuals, the American Cancer Sociey (ACS) lowered the age 
recommenda.on for stool-based test and structural examina.on from 50 to 45.34 One study based on Markov model 
analysis suggested that ini.a.ng CRC screening at age 45 years could be cost eﬀec.ve, and could prevent 29,400 CRC 
cases and 11,100 CRC deaths over the next 5 years, despite the addi.onal cost of $10.4 billion in colonoscopies.35 
Discussion on the implementa.on of this recommenda.on at the na.onal level remained inconclusive. In par.cular, 
the screen pool would be an.cipated to increase by 22%, and the balance of screening burden to beneﬁt was 
unclear.36,37 This same argument could extend and be manifested to an even younger age group (18-44), with a larger

burden-to-beneﬁt ra.o. This presents an opportunity to leverage machine learning to alleviate the burden of 
screening cost for the younger popula.on. 
In this context, our study demonstrated the poten.al of ML and LLMs, speciﬁcally a ﬁne-tuned GPT-4o, to predict 
early-onset CRC (EoCRC) risk using electronic health record (EHR) data. Sta.s.cal ML models (LR, XGBoost, and SVC) 
detected 81–91% of CRC cases but correctly iden.ﬁed only 56–64% of non-CRC pa.ents, resul.ng in lower F1 scores 
and precision compared to LLMs. Interes.ngly, the base GPT-4o model performed comparably to RF, despite receiving 
no explicit training data. This highlights the inherent medical knowledge embedded within LLMs and their poten.al 
applicability in clinical se`ngs. While the GPT-4o base model contained general medical knowledge, supervised ﬁne-
tuning with explicit guideline enhanced its predic.ve capabili.es on EoCRC. The ﬁne-tuned LLM was able to detect 
on average 73% cases of CRC pa.ents, and 91% of Non-CRC pa.ents. The accuracy of iden.fying the Non-CRC cases 
was at 99.7%. The performance was comparable to faecal immunochemical tests (FITs), while the sensi.vity was 34% 
higher than the results from guaiac fecal occult blood tests (gFOBTs) for colorectal cancer screening (age ≥ 40).38 
Furthermore, the explana.on in natural language from the LLM was easier to understand than the degree of feature 
contribu.on in sta.s.cal ML models. 
Despite these promising ﬁndings, this study has some limita.ons. First, it is a retrospec.ve study where the pa.ent 
informa.on was extracted from the coded data in EHR. The inherent informa.on bias could be due to missing data 
issues and inaccurate coding.39 Second, we only experimented with OpenAI’s ﬂagship model, GPT4-o LLM. In future 
work, we plan to evaluate other LLMs, like Meta’s Llama3 and Google’s Gemini to gain beger understanding of LLMs’ 
reasoning capability in CRC predic.on. Lastly, the study was limited to the pa.ents with 7 months of medical history 
of clinical data including condi.ons, lab results and observa.ons, in order to provide suﬃcient training signals for the 
models to learn. However, we found many cases where the pa.ents were diagnosed CRC within one month aQer 
their ﬁrst visit and therefore they were not eligible to be in the study. This could be agributed to the general good 
health in the younger popula.on, and the absence of other health issues that required medical agen.on. Many 
pa.ents also had irregular visits, or no visits in a few months. The data sparsity in pa.ent journey with irregular 
pagerns could be challenging to learn. One poten.al avenue for future research is to pre-train a clinical language 
model that captures the nuances in diﬀerent pa.ent journeys through a large amount of longitudinal medical 
records. By learning the joint probability distribu.on between events in the pa.ent journeys, beger performance 
could be achieved without abundant and highly accurate training data.40,41
