Abstract
We present the first radio–continuum detection of the circumstellar shell around the well-known WN8 type Wolf-Rayet star WR16 at
943.5 MHz using the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP) Evolutionary Map of the Universe (EMU) survey. At this
frequency, the shell has a measured flux density of 72.2±7.2 mJy. Using previous Australia Telescope Compact Array (ATCA) measurements
at 2.4, 4.8, and 8.64 GHz, as well as the EMU observations of the star itself, we determine a spectral index of α = +0.74 ± 0.02, indicating
thermal emission. We propose that the shell and star both exhibit thermal emission, supported by the its appearance in near-infrared and
Hα observations. The latest Gaia parallax is used to determine a distance of 2.28±0.09 kpc. This star is well-known for its surrounding
circular nebulosity, and using the distance and an angular diameter of 8.′42, we determine the shell size to be 5.57±0.22 pc. We use the Gaia
proper motions (PMs) of WR16 to determine peculiar velocities of the star as Vα(pec) = –45.3±5.4 km s–1 and Vδ(pec) = 22.8±4.7 km s–1,
which indicates that the star is moving in a north-west direction, and translates to a peculiar tangential velocity to be 50.7±6.9 km s–1. We
also use these PMs to determine the shell’s origin, estimate an age of ∼9500 ± 1300 yr, and determine its average expansion velocity to be
280 ± 40 km s–1. This average expansion velocity suggests that the previous transitional phase is a Luminous Blue Variable (LBV) phase, rather
than a Red Super Giant (RSG) phase. We also use the measured flux at 943.5 MHz to determine a mass-loss rate of 1.753 × 10–5 M⊙yr–1, and
use this to determine a lower-limit on ionising photons of NUV > 1.406 × 1047 s–1.
Keywords: radio continuum emission, Wolf Rayet stars, WN stars, nebulae
1.
Introduction
Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars are massive late stage stars with broad
emission lines (Crowther, 2007). They are categorised by
their dominating elemental emission: carbon (WC), nitrogen
(WN), and oxygen (WO). WR stars follow an evolutionary
cycle that is identifiable by its previous mass–loss history, where
mass-loss outbursts are inconsistent unlike O-stars, and caused
by internal pulsations (Langer et al., 1994). The previous mass–
loss history of these stars is easily identifiable by outbursts of

WR16 is a WR star of the WN8 type (Shara et al., 2009) at
RA (J2000) = 09:54:52.91, Dec (J2000) = –57:43:38.30 (Toalá
et al., 2015), which is well known for its ring-like nebula (Ci-
chowolski et al., 2020). While the inner nebula is circular, it is
proposed that two more arc-like features outside of this inner
nebula share the same origin (Figure 1; Marston, 1995). It is
likely that winds from the previous O-type star main sequence
would have swept the surrounding ISM over several Myrs, cre-
ating a wind-blown shell (Langer et al., 1994). Then, after the
main sequence, the star would have entered a transitional phase,

suffering a number of mass-loss events/outbursts (Crowther,
2007). The ejecta from these outbursts would have expanded
freely within the cavity carved by the main sequence wind.
Finally, when the star became a WR, the WR wind pushed
and compressed the ejecta, allowing its most recent outburst
to expand symmetrically (Marston, 1995).
The Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP)
(Hotan et al., 2021) Evolutionary Map of the Universe (EMU)
(Norris et al., 2011, 2021; Hopkins et al., 2025) survey is
mapping the entire southern sky at 943.5 MHz and is cur-
rently ∼25% complete. The ASKAP telescope has good sur-
face brightness sensitivity that allows us to see characteris-
tics of objects not detected before at this frequency. Previ-
ously, extended sources with a similar low-surface brightness
to WR16’s shell have been characterised such as Supernova
Remnants (SNRs); J0624–6948 (Filipović et al., 2022; Sasaki
et al., 2025), G288.8–6.3 (Ancora; Filipović et al., 2023; Burger-
Scheidlin et al., 2024), G308.7+1.4 (Raspberry; Lazarević et al.,
2024a), G312.6+ 2.8 (Unicycle; Smeaton et al., 2024a); and a
pulsar wind nebula (PWN) (Potoroo; Lazarević et al., 2024b).
We present an analysis of the star WR16 as seen by ASKAP
during mapping for the EMU survey. In section 2, we discuss
the observing parameters of the survey, as well as outlining
other data used. In section 3 we explore the results from
analysing the EMU tile, as well as exploring the movement of
the shell and star, the shell’s expansion, and the presence of a
CO bubble. Finally, we give our summary and conclusions in
section 4.
2.
Data
2.1
ASKAP EMU
The object WR16 has been seen in three ASKAP EMU obser-
vations. SB46953 observed the tile EMU_1017-60 on Decem-
ber 12th 2022, SB51428 observed the tile EMU_0954-55 on
July 13th 2023, and SB53568 observed the tile EMU_0936-60
on October 7th 2023. SB46953 is excluded from analysis due
to its proximity to the tile’s edge, which can cause a drop in
sensitivity.
The data were reduced using the standard ASKAP pipeline,
ASKAPSoft, using multi-frequency synthesis imaging, multi-
scale cleaning, self-calibration and convolution to a common
beam size (Guzman et al., 2019). As the source was observed
in two usable EMU fields, the images were combined with
the radio imaging software Miriad (Sault et al., 1995) using
the task IMCOMB. The final image was created by combining
the observations with equal weighting. We measure a num-
ber of background regions around WR16 to find the back-
ground difference in the images. We find a median rms value
of 37.5 µJy beam–1 and a mean value of 37 µJy beam–1, com-
pared with the previous values of median = 36 µJy beam–1 and
mean = 43.5 µJy beam–1 (SB51428), and median = 46.5 µJy beam–1
and mean = 50.5 µJy beam–1 (SB53568). This background
noise level is slightly higher than typical for EMU (σ = 20 –
30 µJy beam–1; Hopkins et al., 2025), which can be attributed
to WR16 being in an area of higher background Galactic emis-
sion (b = –2.55). The final image is shown in Figure 1 with
a resulting sensitivity of σ=37 µJy beam–1 and a synthesised
beam of 15′′×15′′.
2.2
Gaia
We use measurements made by the Gaia astrometry satellite
(Gaia Collaboration et al., 2016) for WR16. We obtain proper
motion (PM) and parallax values from Data Release 3 (DR3)
(Gaia Collaboration et al., 2023), and use them to determine
the star’s movement and distance. Discussion surrounding the
use of this specific data release is found in section 3.1.
2.3
Nanten CO
In order to explore the distribution of molecular clouds sur-
rounding WR16, we analysed the archival 12CO(J = 1–0) data
taken by the NANTEN 4-m radio telescope (Mizuno & Fukui,
2004). The angular resolution of the data cube was 2.′6. The
typical noise fluctuations were ∼0.3 K at a velocity resolution
of 0.65 km s–1.
2.4
Other Data
We include observations from the SuperCOSMOS (Hambly
et al., 2001) and Wide-Field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE)
(Wright et al., 2010) sky surveys to compare the nebulosity
surrounding WR16 at 656.281 nm and 22 µm (Figure 2).
We also use previous flux density measurements using
Australia Telescope Compact Array (ATCA) from Leitherer
et al. (1995), Leitherer et al. (1997), and Chapman et al. (1999).
These measurements are included in Table 1, and discussed in
section 3.1.
We have searched for high-energy associations to WR16
and its shell. Fermi-LAT Data Release 4 (Abdollahi et al.,
2022; Ballet et al., 2023) shows no corresponding Gamma-Ray
emission. The SRG/eROSITA all-sky survey (eRASS) Data
Release 1 (Merloni et al., 2024), and observations taken with
XMM-Newton (Strüder et al., 2001; Turner et al., 2001) show
no corresponding X-Ray emission.
3.
Results and Discussion
3.1
Measurements
Using a Gaia parallax of 0.4380±0.0168 mas (Gaia Collabora-
tion, 2020), we estimate the distance of WR16 to be 2.28±0.09 kpc.
This is in accordance with the photometric distance calculated
in van der Hucht (2001) of 2.37 kpc, and with a distance of
2.23±0.39 kpc derived using Ca II lines (Megier et al., 2009).
Previously, Gaia Data Release 2 (DR2) (Gaia Collaboration,
2018) provided a distance of 2.66±0.23 kpc (Bailer-Jones et al.,
2018). Considering that Gaia DR3 provides a distance that is
concordant with other measurements, we adopt 2.28±0.09 kpc
to be the most accurate distance value.
We obtain PMs from Gaia DR3 (Gaia Collaboration, 2020),
for WR16, which are µα: –9.458±0.021 mas yr–1 and µδ: 5.054±
0.018 mas yr–1. Using the Gaia parallax and proper motion
values, we calculate the peculiar velocity with respect to the lo-
cal interstellar medium following Comerón & Pasquali (2007)

We measure the inner WR16 nebula to have a flux density
of 72.2±7.2 mJy at 943.5 MHz, taking an error of 10% (as
discussed in Smeaton et al., 2024b; Filipović et al., 2024). We
also measure the flux density of the central star at 943.5 MHz,
and find it to be 0.35±0.04 mJy. We combine this with the
ATCA flux density measurements listed in Table 1 and section
2.4 to compute a spectral index for the star. The radio spectral
index is defined as S ∝να, where S is flux density, ν is
the frequency and α is the spectral index. We calculate the
spectral index using the LINREGRESSa function in the Python
SCIPY (Virtanen et al., 2020) library. The function uses the
linear least-squares regression method to find a best fit line of
α = +0.74±0.02 for WR16. This is very close to the canonical
spectral index of an isothermal, spherical stellar wind (α = +0.6,
(Panagia & Felli, 1975; Wright & Barlow, 1975)), and is similar
to that of Dougherty & Williams (2000) for similar WR stars
that also exhibit thermal characteristics.
We fit a circular region to the outer edge of the shell and
measure an angular diameter of 8.′42, which is similar to the
angular size estimated in Cichowolski et al. (2020). We use the
derived distance and the angular size measured on the EMU
image (Figure 1) to determine the shell’s true linear size and
find that the shell has a diameter of 5.57±0.22 pc. Due to the
shell’s presence in near–infrared and Hα (Figure 2) we infer
that the shell also has thermal origin. Because the shell has not
yet been observed at other frequencies, we cannot confirm
thermal origin from the spectral index.

3.2
Large-Scale Wind-Blown Bubble of CO
Figure 3(a) shows the velocity-integrated intensity map of
CO toward WR16 with an integration range of from –7 to
0 km s–1. We found a new candidate for a large-scale wind-
blown bubble whose radius is approximately 0.◦75 or ∼30 pc at
the distance of WR16. Since the inner circular shell of WR16
is approximately placed at the geometric center of the CO
bubble, they appear to be physically associated to each other.
Figures 3(b) and 3(c) show the position–velocity diagrams
of CO. We found clear evidence of an expanding gaseous
bubble whose expanding velocity is ∼7 km s–1. The fact that
the spatial extent in the Declination direction on the position–
velocity diagram varies depending on the integrated Right
Ascension range is also roughly consistent with the three-
dimensional expansion motion of the molecular clouds.
We argue that the dynamical timescale of this expanding
gaseous bubble can be naturally understood if it is assumed to
have been driven by WR16. The dynamical time scale of the
expanding bubble is estimated to be (bubble radius)/(expanding
velocity) ∼30 pc / 7 km s–1 ∼4 Myr. This time scale is
roughly consistent with the typical lifetime of a massive star,
suggesting that the CO expanding bubble was likely formed
by stellar feedback effects such as strong stellar winds from the
progenitor of WR16.

to a linear size of 8.62 × 1013 km (2.79 pc). Dividing this by
the years travelled (∼9500± 1300 yr) and dividing by seconds
in a year, we determine the average expansion velocity of the
circular shell to be 280 ± 40 km s–1. It is important to note
that this only assumes a 2–dimensional plane, and is therefore
a lower limit on age values and an upper limit on expansion
values.
We take our calculated expansion velocity to be the average
rate of expansion because it is presumed that the shell is slowing
down the more it expands and interacts with the surrounding
interstellar medium. WR16 also has a wind terminal velocity
(v∞) of 630 km s–1 (Toalá et al., 2015), so it is likely that
the wind-driven shell started with velocities similar to this,
and has slowed over the ∼9500 ± 1300 yr timescale. This
velocity sits conveniently between the typical expansion speed
of Luminous Blue Variable (LBV) shells of ∼50 km s–1 (Weis,
2011), like that of AG Car (Smith, 1991), and the faster ejecta
of Eta Car (600 km s–1, Steffen et al., 2014). This supports the
idea that the shell originated in a previous LBV phase.
3.4
Mass-Loss Rate
Based on the observed parameters of WR16 at 943.5 MHz, we
are able to determine a mass-loss rate ( ˙M) at this frequency
using the equations outlined in Wright & Barlow (1975, Equa-
tion 20) and Leitherer et al. (1995, Equation 3). We calculate
a Gaunt factor, gv, at 943.5 MHz to be 5.99, assuming a gas
temperature of 104 K and a Z of 1. Using the same constants
for our calculation as Leitherer et al. (1995), and substituting
our flux, distance, and frequency, we derive a mass-loss rate of
1.753 × 10–5 M⊙yr–1.
This is lower than the calculated mass-loss rate from Lei-
therer et al. (1995) of 3.981 × 10–5 M⊙yr–1. However, re-
calculating their value using our Gaia distance measurement
of 2.28 kpc, we get a result of 2.188 × 10–5 M⊙yr–1. These
measurements are quite similar, and may indicate that the

3.5
Similarities to Other Objects
WR16 presents an almost symmetrical circular shell similar to
the Galactic SNR Teleios (Greek τϵλϵιoσ – meaning perfect,
Filipović et al. submitted). Although they share similarities,
they are unique objects for their given type. This shows that
different objects can share the same morphologies, and can
possibly be equated to similar formation processes.
Another object similar to the WR16 shell is the radio ring
Kýklos, discussed in Bordiu et al. (2024).They discuss the like-
lihood of the ring-like object being an outburst of a WR star.
This object is also situated in our Galaxy, but is significantly
smaller than WR16 (WR16: 8.′42, Kýklos: 80′′). Both systems
show evidence of thermal emission, however, Kýklos presents a
ring-like structure rather than a diffuse circle like WR16. The
ring-like structure and smaller size may indicate that Kýklos is
at an earlier developmental stage compared to WR16.
We see significant brightening toward the north–west part

4.
Conclusion
We present the detection of the nebulosity surrounding the
Wolf-Rayet star WR16 and its circular shell at 943.5 MHz
using the ASKAP EMU survey. We find that the shell has a
measured flux density of 72.2±7.2 mJy, and WR16 has a flux
density of 0.35±0.04 mJy. By combining this with archival
ATCA data, we determine a spectral index of +0.74±0.02 for
the star, which indicates thermal origin of the emission. We
also infer that the shell is thermal from its presence in IR and
Hα. We use Gaia DR3 measurements to determine the star’s
direction and distance. WR16 is moving in a north-western
direction, coinciding with the brighter edge of the nebulosity.
We also find that the star is at a distance of 2.28±0.09 kpc,
and measure the shell to be 8.′42 in angular diameter, which
translates to a linear size of 5.57±0.22 pc. The shell as seen
by EMU matches well with WISE and SuperCOSMOS data,
further confirming thermal emission. By mapping PM, we
are able to determine the origin point of the shell and calculate
the age of the shell to be ∼9500 ± 1300 yr, with an average
expansion velocity of 280 ± 40 km s–1. It is expected that
the brighter edge of the shell will become brighter as WR16
moves towards it.
We also discuss the discovery of a large wind-blown bubble
seen in CO observations, which is proposed to be related to
WR16 during its main-sequence phase. We calculate a mass-
loss rate of 1.753 × 10–5 M⊙yr–1, which is remarkably close
to the canonical value of the mass-loss rate for WN8 type
stars. Using this we were able to determine the amount of
UV-ionising photons emanating from WR16: NUV > 1.406×
1047 s–1.
