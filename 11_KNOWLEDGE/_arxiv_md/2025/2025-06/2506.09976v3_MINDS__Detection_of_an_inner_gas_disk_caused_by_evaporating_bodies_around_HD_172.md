ABSTRACT
Mechanisms such as collisions of rocky bodies or cometary activity give rise to dusty debris disks. De-
bris disks trace the leftover building blocks of planets, and thus also planetary composition. HD 172555,
a stellar twin of β Pic, hosts a debris disk thought to have resulted from a giant collision. It is known
for its extreme mid-infrared silica dust feature, indicating a warm population of silica-rich grains in
the asteroid belt (∼5 au), cold CO observed by ALMA, and small bodies evaporating as they approach
close to the star. Our JWST MIRI MRS observations now reveal emission from an inner gaseous disk
(<0.5 au) that arises from the evaporation of close-in material. For the first time in a debris disk, we
detect neutral atomic chlorine and sulfur, as well as ionized nickel. We recovered the neutral sulfur line
in ∼20-year-old Spitzer data, showing it is long-lived and stable. Ionized iron, previously seen only in
β Pic, is also detected. All lines are broadened by Keplerian rotation, pinpointing the gas location. The
HD 172555 system serves as a unique laboratory to study the composition of planetesimals, asteroids,
and comets beyond the Solar System. The comparison to β Pic reveals that the gas in HD 172555 is
hotter, closer to the star, and poor in argon – suggesting it originates from evaporating rocky bodies
near the star, while β Pic’s gas may trace volatile-rich bodies from larger separations.

1. INTRODUCTION
Stars that have undergone planet formation can host
detectable debris disks. The dust in these disks is pri-
marily produced either by a continuous collisional cas-
cade, or a singular massive collision taking place in the
disk; however, cometary activity can also produce cir-
cumstellar material (e.g., Wyatt 2008; Hughes et al.
2018). Debris disks are most often identified by excess
infrared emission arising from dust, and little to no gas.
They can have warm and cold components, where the
temperature correlates with their separation from the
star. In the Solar System, these correspond to the main
asteroid belt at 2–3.5 au and the Edgeworth–Kuiper belt
between 30 and 48 au (Wyatt 2008). Interferometric ob-
servations sometimes also show evidence of hot exozodii,
namely hot dust very close to the star (Kral et al. 2017).
Infrared excess at 22–24 µm around main-sequence stars
is relatively common – detectable around ∼25% of main-
sequence stars, up to ∼50% depending on the sample
(Chen et al. 2020). However, warm dust asteroid belts
that show detectable strong solid-state dust emission
features (so-called 10 µm features) are rarer (e.g., Chen
et al. 2007; Mittal et al. 2015). These features originate
from large amounts of small micrometer-sized grains at
about 200–300 K (Lisse et al. 2009; Johnson et al. 2012)
and are thought to be a potential result of recent massive
collisions or a giant impact occurring in these systems.
In recent years, it has been discovered that a subset of
debris disks also contains volatiles in the form of cold CO
gas (e.g., K´osp´al et al. 2013; Mo´or et al. 2017; Hughes
et al. 2018; Mo´or et al. 2019). There is still debate about
its origin as either primordial or a byproduct of the col-
lisions in the debris disk (e.g., Smirnov-Pinchukov et al.
2022; Cataldi et al. 2023).
The focus of our study is HD 172555, a nearby
(28.33 ± 0.19 pc, Gaia Collaboration 2020) A7V (Gray
et al. 2006) star and 20 ± 4 million year old member
of the β Pic moving group (e.g., Barrado y Navascu´es
et al. 1999; Miret-Roig et al. 2020). It is one of the best
studied warm belt systems and a near stellar twin to
β Pic (A6V; Gray et al. 2006). Both stars host close to
edge-on debris disks (HD 172555: ∼76.5◦(Engler et al.
2018), β Pic: ∼89◦(Heap et al. 2000)).
The similarity of these two members of the same
moving group makes them a comparative laboratory
to understand where differences in their debris disks
arise from.
While the overall stellar properties are
similar, they differ in their disk and planet system
properties.
β Pic has two observed giant planets (b:
9.9 au, ∼11.9 MJup; c: 2.7 au, ∼8.9 MJup, Lacour et al.
2021).
No massive companions were detected in ex-
isting VLT/SPHERE coronagraphic imaging data for
HD 172555 (Flasseur et al. 2020).
In the following, we will compare HD 172555 and β Pic
in terms of circumstellar dust, gas, and exocomet activ-
ity.
1.1. Dust
HD 172555
has
a
strong
excess
in
the
mid-IR
(LIR/L∗= 7.2 × 10−4; Mittal et al. 2015), typical of
young A-type stars (Su et al. 2006). The fractional IR
excess is a factor of 3–4 lower than in β Pic (2.7×10−3).
However, while HD 172555 primarily has a strong warm
dust component (about 200–350 K) corresponding to
an asteroid-belt analog, it lacks a significant cold and
extended dust component (∼100 K). β Pic hosts both
warm and cold dust components, but the spectral energy
distribution (SED) peaks at ∼60 µm (Chen et al. 2007)
and is dominated by the cold dust component.
The
warm dust of HD 172555 has been spatially resolved in
the mid-IR with TReCS at Gemini-South (Smith et al.
2012) and in the visible with polarimetric imaging us-
ing SPHERE/ZIMPOL at the VLT (Engler et al. 2018).
These studies show that the dust outer radius is between
8.5 and 11.3 au (Engler et al. 2018). Based on the SED
and dust models, Lisse et al. (2009) expected the bulk
of the warm dust to be located at around 5.8 ± 0.6 au.
A dust mass for submillimeter dust of about 4 × 1019–
2 × 1020 kg, equivalent to a 150–200 km radius aster-
oid, was used to explain the mid-IR spectrum (Lisse
et al. 2009). The millimeter dust mass was estimated
to be (1.8 ± 0.6) × 10−4 M⊕(Schneiderman et al. 2021).
HD 172555 has strongly pronounced silica and silicate
features that show an unusual composition indicative of
minerals that are created at high temperatures, consis-
tent with obsidian and tektite (Lisse et al. 2009; Johnson
et al. 2012). This led to the hypothesis that the debris
disk could be the direct result of a recent hyperveloc-
ity impact between two large bodies, as relative veloci-
ties of <10 km s−1 would not produce the observed silica
(SiO2), but rather the more typical magnesium (and/or
iron) containing silicates olivine and pyroxene. Such a
high-velocity impact would completely melt the incident
body and strip the mantel material off the impacted ob-
ject (Lisse et al. 2009).
The dust features detected in the HD 172555 disk dif-
fer from the β Pic disk spectrum, which is dominated by
magnesium silicate features (Chen et al. 2024). These

1.2. Exocomet activity
High-resolution spectroscopy in the optical and UV
has shown nonphotospheric absorption features with a
stable as well as variable component indicating the pres-
ence of released gas from star-grazing evaporating bod-
ies, also called “falling evaporating bodies” (FEBs), in
the HD 172555 system. In the literature, the terms ex-
ocomets and FEBs are often used interchangeably, re-
gardless of whether the evaporating bodies are predom-
inantly icy or rocky in nature.
In this work, we will
differentiate between falling evaporating comets and as-
teroids, based on their supposed reservoir of origin.
In HD 172555, Kiefer et al. (2014) established the pres-
ence of stable and variable [Ca ii] and [Na ii] absorption
at radial velocities consistent with the star. Grady et al.
(2018) detected absorption lines of [Si iii] and [Si iv],
[C ii] and [C iv], and [O i] using the Hubble Space Tele-
scope (HST)/Cosmic Origins Spectrograph (COS) and
STIS instruments that they associate with star-grazing
objects with high covering factors up to 58%–68% of
the stellar light. Furthermore, Kiefer et al. (2023) found
hints of an exocomet transit in CHEOPS lightcurves,
that points to a 2.5 km radius evaporating body pass-
ing at 6.8 ± 1.4 R⊙(or 0.05 ± 0.01 au) close to the star.
These detections are reminiscent of the well-studied cir-
cumstellar absorption in the β Pictoris system, which
has been monitored for more than 25 yr (e.g., Vrig-
naud et al. 2024, and references therein).
Transiting
exocomets have also been observed in β Pic (Zieba et al.
2019), underscoring a broader phenomenon of evapo-
rating bodies around young stars. Given the extensive
observational history of β Pic, it provides an invaluable
baseline for comparing system architectures.
1.3. Gas
CO gas has been observed by the Atacama Large
Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) in HD 172555
in a ring from ∼4.2–10.8 au (Schneiderman et al. 2021)
and was taken as further evidence for a recent giant im-
pact between volatile-rich bodies, as asteroids at these
separations should not retain CO. Schneiderman et al.
(2021) determined a CO mass of (0.45 −1.21) × 10−5
M⊕for gas temperatures between 100 and 250 K. An
optically thick cold disk model can result in much higher
CO masses of ∼5×10−2 M⊕. New higher spatial resolu-
tion ALMA data indicate that, while the bulk of CO gas
is located at a similar separation to the warm dust belt,
the CO disk could extend closer to the star (L. Matra,
private communication). No other molecules have been
found using ALMA in this disk (Smirnov-Pinchukov
et al. 2022).
Forbidden neutral atomic oxygen ([O i])
has been observed in Herschel/PACS spectra (Riviere-
Marichalar et al. 2012) similar to β Pic (Brandeker et al.
2016), whereas no [C II] was found (Riviere-Marichalar
et al. 2014) (see Table 1).
In this paper, we present the first JWST Mid-InfraRed
Instrument (MIRI) Medium Resolution Spectroscopy
(MRS) observations of HD 172555.
They allow us to
conduct the most sensitive search for mid-IR gas emis-
sion lines around HD 172555 to date at much higher
spectral resolution.
Section 2 describes the data and
data reduction.
Section 3 presents the emission lines
we have discovered and discusses their nature and likely
origin as evaporating rocky material close to the star.
In Section 4 we discuss our discoveries in the con-
text of other observations and compare the results to
HD 172555’s twin star β Pic.
Finally, we conclude in
Section 5.

3.3. Origin of atomic gas lines
The detection of gas lines raises the question of their
origin. Most of these forbidden lines (Fe II, Ni II, S I,
Cl I) are well known from recent observations with
JWST of jets and outflows of high-mass protostars
(Beuther et al. 2023; Gieser et al. 2023) and low-mass
protostars (Tychoniec et al. 2024; Narang et al. 2024;
Caratti o Garatti et al. 2024; Nisini et al. 2024), where
they trace shock regions. However, it is the first time
that these lines, except for [Fe ii], observed in β Pic, are
seen in a debris disk.
In HD 172555, the lines appear to be a direct tracer
of evaporating bodies or dust releasing material into the
gas phase. The analysis of the line broadening (see Ta-
ble 2) shows that the lines originate very close to the
star. The equilibrium temperature of an irradiated body
without heat redistribution is approximated by

means that a temperature of about 720 K, enough to
break apart troilite (FeS; Lauretta et al. 1995), which
is the simplest refractory compound that can release
sulfur, is reached at a separation of around 0.8 au (as-
suming A = 0.1).
At 0.3 au, temperatures approach
1200 K, which is high enough to break apart most sili-
cates (Lenzuni et al. 1995). It therefore stands to reason
that the gas is probing the region in which rocky mate-
rial can sublimate.
3.3.1. Impact of stellar radiation on the stability of the gas
reservoir
Once gas is released, it is important to assess whether
the detected species can form a stable reservoir in the
presence of the strong stellar radiation field of the A-
type star. Atoms and ions can experience acceleration
by absorbing stellar photons, which may remove them
from the inner disk.
Chlorine does not experience strong radiation pres-
sure from the star, as neither its neutral nor ionized
form posses abundant and strong transitions in the UV
or optical range, where the photon flux of the A-type
host star is high (Lehtmets et al. 2024).
Fern´andez
et al. (2006) showed that while ionized species of iron
and nickel do experience outward acceleration due to
radiation pressure in β Pic, they could be self-breaking
due to enhanced carbon abundance. The newly discov-
ered neutral sulfur may also be affected by radiative
blowout, as it has a β = Frad/Fgrav > 1 in Lehtmets et
al. 2025, in preparation, for an A6V star, and β = 0.56
in Fern´andez et al. (2006) for β Pic.
We expect sim-
ilar chromospheric activity in HD 172555 compared to
β Pic (Grady et al. 2018), and therefore a radiation
field of comparable strength (also see Appendix H for
an analysis of the extreme-UV (XUV) and X-ray fields).
Therefore, an additional breaking mechanism, like the
self-breaking due to carbon, may also be necessary to
explain the strong and stable sulfur detection (see Sec-
tion 3.3.3). Circumstellar carbon has been observed in
absorption around HD 172555 by Grady et al. (2018).

3.3.2. Ionized Iron (Fe ii) and Nickel (Ni ii)
We detect atomic nickel emission in the mid-infrared
spectrum of a debris disk for the first time. [Fe ii] emis-
sion has previously been observed in only one other de-
bris disk, β Pic (Worthen et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025).
Iron and nickel are abundant in both meteorites and
comets and have been observed in the gas phase in
cometary comae, even at large heliocentric distances
(Manfroid et al. 2021).
The Solar System nickel-to-
iron abundance ratio is log(Ni/Fe) = −1.25±0.04 (Lod-
ders 2020), although slightly higher values have been re-
ported for Sun-grazing comet Ikeya–Seki (−1.11 ± 0.09)
and near-solar ratios (−0.06 ± 0.31) for comets at 0.6–
3 au (Manfroid et al. 2021). In meteoritic material, iron
occurs in silicates, sulfides, and metallic phases, with
silicates and metallic iron sublimating at higher tem-
peratures (∼1200 K) than sulfides (∼720 K). Nickel, by
contrast, is found only in sulfides and metallic phases
(Larimer & Anders 1970; Grossman & Larimer 1974).
Sublimation at temperatures below 1000 K therefore can
favor a higher Ni/Fe ratio in the released gas, potentially
explaining the elevated Ni/Fe ratios observed in some
cometary comae (Manfroid et al. 2021). Cometary Fe-
and Ni-bearing materials, such as Ni-rich sulfides (e.g.,
pentlandite), are often present as nanometer-sized par-
ticles (e.g., Berger et al. 2011). These particles can ex-
perience transient heating events, such as superheating
of fluffy aggregates or high-velocity collisions, reaching
temperatures exceeding 1000 K, which could explain the
detection of atomic gas in cometary atmospheres in the
Solar System even at large separations.
Although the detection of both Fe and Ni provides an
opportunity to probe the composition of the gas, deter-
mining a precise Ni/Fe abundance ratio for HD 172555
requires detailed modeling of the gas excitation condi-
tions and the local UV radiation field, which is beyond
the scope of this work. In Section 3.4, we use iron line
ratios to constrain the physical conditions of the gas and
compare them to those in β Pic.
3.3.3. Neutral sulfur S i
This is the first time that atomic sulfur is reported
in the mid-IR spectrum of a debris disk.
The line is
located at 25.249 µm and is the strongest emission line
in the spectrum. It is the only line that appears to be
asymmetric in the amplitude of the two Gaussian com-
ponents (see zoom-in in Fig. 1 and the ∼2-σ residuals
in the symmetric Keplerian model fit in the Appendix
Fig. 8 e), potentially hinting at a stronger blue shifted
component.
From the archival Spitzer/IRS LH (R ∼600) we suc-
ceeded to detect the [S i] line (see Appendix A). None of
the other lines are detectable in Spitzer due to insuffi-
cient signal-to-noise ratio and resolution. This detection
of the line in JWST after 20-years, at a similar or slightly
higher flux level, means that not only is the dust emis-
sion stable on these timescales (see Sect. 3.6 and also Su
et al. 2020), but so too is the sulfur gas emission. The
slight increase in the silicate emission (Fig. 1) may also
be consistent with a slight increase in sulfur emission of
about 20% (see Appendix A), but this is hard to conclu-
sively show given the larger uncertainties on the Spitzer
data.
Troilite (FeS) is predicted to form as the result of the
first reaction that incorporates sulfur into solid material
in a cooling solar-composition gas from the combination
of solid iron with H2S gas and first becomes stable at
716.5 K (Lauretta et al. 1995). But at solar composi-
tions (95% Fe, 5% Ni), nickel sulfide (NiS) can also be
produced. It has recently been invoked as a candidate
to explain the polarization properties of the debris ring
around HD 181327 in scattered light observations (Milli
et al. 2024). Small particles can more easily be heated to
temperatures above ∼700 K, necessary to release sulfur
into the gas phase (see Fig. 7 in the Appendix G); how-
ever, dust particles between 0.1–1.5 µm are expected to
be quickly blown out within less than one year (John-
son et al. 2012). For larger grains >1.5 µm a separation
of ∼0.8 au is enough to heat them above 715 K. This
is larger than the separation we infer for the gas disk
(see Table 2), which could indicate that FeS dust is not
the origin for the gas, unless the dust is released from
asteroid-sized bodies at smaller radii.
In the Solar System, the organic sulfur molecular con-
tent has been measured directly in samples of the aster-
oid 162173 Ryugu, where the total sulfur abundance was
between ∼2.5 and 6.2 weight percent (Yoshimura et al.
2023). Many carbonaceous chondrites found on Earth
have similar bulk abundances between 3 and 6 weight
percent (Alexander et al. 2022).
The near-Sun asteroid (3200) Phaeton (Jewitt & Li
2010), the likely parent body of the Geminid meteoroid
stream, could be a good analog to study evaporating
exo-asteroids. With a perihelion distance of 0.14 au, it
undergoes periodic heating episodes up to ∼750 K, show-
ing comet-like activity.
A recent laboratory study by
Suttle et al. (2024) showed that the retention of sulfur-
bearing gas may play a crucial role in limiting thermal
decomposition, allowing FeS to survive repeated heating
cycles. This could provide a viable mechanism for a pop-
ulation of bodies to survive repeated noncatastrophic
thermal decomposition events and release volatiles over
an extended period of time.

3.3.4. Neutral chlorine Cl i
Atomic chlorine is seen in the mid-IR spectrum of
a debris disk for the first time.
The line, located at
11.333 µm is the second strongest emission line in the
spectrum after neutral sulfur at 25.249 µm. The [Cl i]
line has recently been found to trace jets and shocked
knots in protostars (Tychoniec et al. 2024; Nisini et al.
2024; Caratti o Garatti et al. 2024). In the Solar Sys-
tem, evidence for chlorine has been found in ammonium
salts (NH4Cl, “salmiac salt”) in comet 67P (Altwegg
et al. 2020), which is thought to sublimate at a temper-
ature of around 473 K (Clementi & Gayles 1967). In the
coma of comet 67P, the volatile fraction of chlorine rel-
ative to oxygen is ∼1.2 × 10−4 (Dhooghe et al. 2017),
whereas in chondrites the ratio is ∼6.8 ± 1.2 × 10−4
(Lodders 2010). Chlorine bearing salts have also been
directly measured in samples of asteroid 162173 Ryugu
(Yoshimura et al. 2023). The chlorine-to-oxygen ratio
could therefore be a potential indicator for the type of
objects that produce the observed chlorine signature.
Neutral oxygen has been detected in emission with Her-
schel around HD 172555 (see Table 1). However, it is
unclear what radial separation the emission traces. If
a giant impact is the origin of the oxygen, then the
probed oxygen reservoir may not be colocated with the
measured chlorine. However, the oxygen could also be
released over time from evaporating rocky material sim-
ilarly to the observed chlorine.
The comparatively low sublimation temperature of
the ammonium salts begs the question why the broad-
ening of the lines and derived gas kinematics are consis-
tent with the more refractory elements iron and nickel.
It could be that the bulk of chlorine is only released in
large enough quantities when an asteroid/comet reaches
high enough temperatures to disintegrate.

4. DISCUSSION
The new MIRI MRS data adds a new fascinating layer
to our understanding of the system’s architecture. Pre-
viously, ALMA has seen the warm (169 K) dust contin-
uum extending out to 10 au, similar in spatial distribu-
tion to the CO gas (Schneiderman et al. 2021). The bulk
gas and dust detected by ALMA are expected to be at
separation between around 4 and 7 au, which is consis-
tent with the location of the warm dust as determined
from the silicate feature (Lisse et al. 2009; Johnson et al.
2012). Small grains of sizes from 1.25–3.9 µm are also de-
tected out to about 10 au in resolved polarimetric high-
contrast observation (Engler et al. 2018) and modeling
based on unresolved polarimetry (Marshall et al. 2020).
Our newly detected gas disk with JWST MIRI is much
closer-in than the bulk of the gas and dust previously ob-
served. Ertel et al. (2014) reported a tentative detection
of hot dust around HD 172555 with VLTI/PIONIER,
which, if real, may be produced by the same mechanism
as the gas reported in our study.
4.1. Is the atomic gas tracing a different population of
bodies than in β Pictoris?
We have reduced and continuum-subtracted the MIRI
MRS data of β Pic (Worthen et al. 2024) using the
same methodology as for HD 172555. The continuum-
subtracted spectra, scaled to the distance of HD 172555,
are shown in the Appendix E (Fig. 5). Although both
stars belong to the same moving group with similar ages
and spectral types, they have different system architec-
tures and debris disk properties.
The differences in
detected emission lines indicate that we may be probing
distinct populations of bodies.
4.1.1. Line Profiles and Physical Conditions
Wu et al. (2025) reported the detection of [Fe ii] lines
at 17.9 and 26 µm in β Pictoris, which we also observe
in HD 172555. Our analysis shows that these lines in
β Pic (see Table 5) also appear Doppler broadened, but
show velocities roughly half of those in HD 172555. This
implies that the bulk of the [Fe ii] emission originates
at larger separations (>0.8 au; see Appendix Fig. 13),
consistent with the assessment of Wu et al. (2025) that
this gas may be part of the stable gas disk compo-
nent.
The conclusion that the gas probes larger sep-
arations is reinforced by the [Fe ii] line ratios (Fig. 2;
Section 3.4), which indicate that the electron tempera-
ture in HD 172555 is about twice as high as in β Pic.
In addition to the lines reported in Wu et al. (2025),
we detect ionized nickel emission in β Pic for the
first time at 10.68 µm with a flux of (3.0 ± 0.6) ×
10−15 erg s−1 cm−2 and with velocity broadening consis-
tent with the [Fe ii] line at 17.9 µm. This is a different
transition from the one detected in HD 172555 at 6.6 µm
line and deserves further study.
4.1.2. Implications for the Nature of the Evaporating
Bodies
In HD 172555, observations are consistent with evap-
orating rocky bodies originating in the inner sys-
tem.
These bodies sublimate and thermally decom-
pose, releasing refractory components and any remain-
ing volatiles, while any outer volatile layers (such as ice
mantles) have likely already been lost. Several scenarios
may explain the gas release in HD 172555:
(i) Perturbed asteroid-like bodies.
Bodies are scat-
tered into close stellar encounters, crossing different sub-
limation thresholds that trigger gas release. This mecha-
nism is supported by the detection of falling evaporating
bodies (FEBs) (Kiefer et al. 2014; Grady et al. 2018),
with Grady et al. (2018) noting that the simultaneous
detection of carbon and Ca ii ions in absorption suggests
a lower volatile-to-refractory ratio compared to Solar
System comets. However, the observed stability of the
inner gas disk in HD 172555 indicates that continuous
or quasi-continuous gas production from stable close-in
material may be required, rather than solely transient
events (see Section 4.1.3).
(ii) In situ collisional cascades. Collisions of bodies
near the star continuously produce small dust that is
rapidly evaporated by stellar heating. However, main-
taining a significant population of parent bodies at such
close separations is challenging due to their short colli-
sional time scale (on the order of thousands of years),
compared to the system age ∼20 Myr (Wyatt et al. 2007;
Kral et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2025).
(iii) Outgassing of a large body. A single large ob-
ject, for instance an evaporating rocky world such as
BD+054868 Ab (Hon et al. 2025), may continuously sup-
ply gas through outgassing and thermal decomposition.
(iv) Evaporation of dust. Su et al. (2020) suggested
that the giant impact invoked to explain the systems’
mineralogy and CO content could generate an opti-
cally thick clump of mm-sized vapor condensates. This
clump would provide shielding from radiation pressure
blowout, while submicron-sized grains are produced in
large quantities in the clump, which can explain the
mid-IR spectral feature. They would persist after the

systems: in HD 172555, an ongoing supply from rocky,

in β Pic, a mixture of short-lived exocometary gas and
an extended, stable, carbon-rich disk.

bodies releasing it formed in cold regions (T ≤35 K)
where argon could be trapped in ices (e.g., Bar-Nun

This is consistent with β Pic host-

substructures—such as the Cat’s Tail and a misaligned
subdisk—that may arise from collisions in the outer sys-
tem (Rebollido et al. 2024).
The pronounced neutral chlorine and sulfur emission
features in HD 172555, which are not detected in β Pic,
may arise from differences in ionization conditions or
production mechanisms. In β Pic, a higher UV/X-ray
flux or a difference in the disk structure could lead to
lower abundances of neutral Cl and S. We find an ∼50%
higher FUV flux in archival β Pic HST/COS spectra
compared to HD 172555.
The XUV and X-ray fields
of both sources could be similar based on their spectral
types and ages; however, HD 172555 is not clearly de-
tected in existing X-ray archival data (see Appendix H).
The atomic species observed in the MRS spectra have
widely varying ionization potentials (Appendix Table 9).
The observed pattern of detections and nondetections
in the two systems is not straightforwardly explained by
a simple change in the number of hardness of ionizing
photons.
4.1.4. Summary of Gas Origin Scenarios
One important aspect requiring explanation is the sta-
bility of an inner gas disk at much smaller separations in
HD 172555 than in β Pic, despite the presence of FEBs
in both systems.
This favors a scenario in which gas
is produced by material on close-in, low-eccentricity or-
bits, enabling the formation of a long-lived inner gas
disk.
Potential sources include a population of close-
in bodies, the continuous outgassing of a large in situ
rocky planet, or the slow inward drift and sublimation
of fine dust at the sublimation radius.
For dust, the
location of the sublimation radius strongly depends on
composition and particle size. FeS larger than 1.5 µm
would evaporate at radii of ∼0.1–0.3 au (see Appendix
Fig. 7), comparable to the inferred gas disk location.
Smaller particles would be vulnerable to blowout and
would sublimate at larger separation (>1 au). Johnson
et al. (2012) suggested that 0.02 µm-sized obsidian (SiO)
grains would resist blowout, but these would not release
the atomic species observed here. A detailed study re-
mains needed to explore the combination of grain com-
positions, sizes, and morphologies, that would allow ef-

while transient events involving perturbed asteroid-like

this scenario cannot be fully ruled out, since the cascade
could have been triggered recently in the lifetime of the
system, potentially dynamically linked to the giant im-
pact. Continuous gas production from a close-in evapo-
rating rocky planet, and/or inward-drifting dust grains
that evaporate at small radii remain plausible scenarios.
Future observations and modeling of dust properties and
gas dynamics will further constrain these scenarios.
5. CONCLUSIONS
This work presents the first detection of mid-IR emis-
sion from an inner disk of hot atomic gas around
HD 172555. The system is known for its warm dust belt
in the terrestrial planet zone, thought to be the result
of a giant impact. It is known to host star-grazing bod-
ies (so-called FEBs) seen via absorption lines and tran-
sit events. Our MIRI MRS observations reveal mid-IR
emission of nickel, chlorine, and sulfur for the first time
in any debris disk, providing direct evidence that large
amounts of rocky material are thermally disintegrating
near the star and releasing iron and other elements into
the gas phase.
We compared our discovery to HD 172555’s near-twin
of the same moving group, β Pic. Both systems share
similar stellar properties yet differ in disk and planetary
architecture, offering an excellent testbed for exploring
how this gas relates to planet formation. The compari-
son shows that the inner gas disk in HD 172555 lies much
closer to the star, despite the presence of FEBs in both
systems. This suggests that the gas in HD 172555 is pro-
duced predominantly by an in situ reservoir, rather than
from material falling inward from larger separation.
We discuss four possible scenarios for the origin of the
gas disk: (1) perturbed asteroid-like bodies originating
from a closer-in reservoir; (2) an in situ collisional cas-
cades; (3) outgassing from a large body on a stable orbit,
such as an evaporating rocky world; or (4) evaporation
of dust drifting inward from the main debris belt. The
presence of a close-in source of gas could plausibly be
linked to the dynamical processes associated with the
giant impact that is proposed to explain the warm dust
belt. Such an event could have generated fragments and
possibly led to enhanced dynamical activity in the inner
system, including increased eccentricities and collisional
interactions (Kral et al. 2015), or produced dust capa-
ble of drifting inward and sublimating (Su et al. 2020).
However, these scenarios are challenging to distinguish
and will require further multiwavelength observations
and more detailed modeling to fully disentangle.
The nondetection of H2 alongside the presence of
atomic neutral sulfur (with similar excitation condi-
tions), demonstrates that the inner gas disk is not pri-
mordial. Furthermore, the lack of argon in HD 172555
reinforces that the evaporating material is primarily ice-
poor.
Our JWST data open up a valuable opportunity to di-
rectly constrain the chemical composition of rocky ma-
terial in exoplanetary systems — an approach indepen-
dent and complementary to measurements of polluted
white dwarfs, and that can be compared to protoplane-
tary disk chemistry and evolution. Finally, we note that
the observed gas and dust production in HD 172555,
together with the detection of FEBs, supports the no-
tion of ongoing dynamical stirring in the system, unless
the observed gas is produced by an in situ evaporat-
ing planet. Previous studies (e.g., Grady et al. 2018)
have suggested that such stirring could be driven by the
presence of planets. Our findings reinforce this picture,
although a direct detection of planets in the system re-
mains outstanding.

A. DETECTION OF [S I] IN SPITZER/IRS LH
DATA
We have recalibrated the Spitzer/IRS LH (long wave-
length, high-resolution) data taken on the 2004-03-22
(AOR 3563264) following the same approach as outlined
in Sect. 2.1. For the high-resolution data, the PSF-based
extraction method is more reliable than aperture-based
extraction, which was used for the low-resolution spec-
tra. The sensitivity and spectral resolution are insuffi-
cient to detect any of the [Ni ii], [Cl I], and [Fe ii] lines.
However, we have recovered the [S i] line at 25.249 mi-
cron, the strongest line in the spectrum.
The line is
detected in two orders (15 and 16) of the Spitzer/IRS
LH data.
Figure 4 shows the continuum-subtracted
emission line in JWST and the two IRS orders.
The
line is clearly detected in both orders.
However, the
width of the line is different.
The line width indi-
cates an instrument resolving power of R∼590 in or-
der 15 and R∼350 in order 16. The expected resolv-
ing power according to the instrument documentation
should be close to 600, which could indicate that the
order 15 spectrum is more reliable.
The integrated
flux is measured in the same way as described in Ap-
pendix B.2 by fitting a linear trend (as the continuum)
simultaneous to a (single) Gaussian.
The window in
which the fitting is done is 10 and 17 spectral chan-
nels wide for order 15 and 16, respectively, owing to
different proximity to the edge of the order. The flux
in order 15 is F = 1.59 ± 0.15 × 10−14 erg s−1 cm2.
In order 16 it is F = 2.67 ± 0.91 × 10−14 erg s−1 cm2,
driven by the larger width. The JWST measured flux is
F = 1.99 ± 0.06 × 10−14 erg s−1 cm2. Overall, the fluxes
are similar between the Spitzer and JWST data, even

In
order
to
confirm
whether
the
observed
broadened/double-peaked profiles can be explained with
a rotating Keplerian gas disk, we have implemented a
toy model similar to the one used by Schneiderman
et al. (2021) to model the optically thin ALMA CO
line observations for this target. In short, we draw a
large number of samples from a uniform density distri-
bution in a ring around the star. The ring has an inner
and outer radius and an inclination. For the sampled
points, we compute the projected Keplerian velocity
and compute a velocity histogram. We then transform
the velocities into wavelength offsets with respect to the
line center to get the expected line profile shape, which
we convolve with the instrument resolution. Finally, we
resample the model line profile to the MRS wavelengths
to compare it to the data.
For all lines, we assume the same disk inclination
(i = 102◦), the best fit from Schneiderman et al. (2021),
which is also consistent with the scattered light obser-
vations i = 76.5◦of Engler et al. (2018)—as this is a
symmetric and optically thin model, which side of the
disk is facing us does not matter. We also assume the
same stellar mass M∗= 1.76 M⊙. For β Pic, we use a
stellar mass M∗= 1.8 M⊙and an inclination of i = 90◦.
We fit this line profile of the rotating disk model to the
spectra using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo package
emcee (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013) to derive posterior
parameter distributions for the gas disk of each species.
Flat priors are used for all parameters, except for the
resolving power R, for which we use a Gaussian prior
with a σ of 2%. A lower limit on Rinner equal to a stellar
radius of R∗= 1.55 R⊙or 7.2 × 10−3 au (Kiefer et al.
2023) is imposed. Router is required to be larger than
Rinner.
The data uncertainties are scaled by a factor
to match the mean value of the surrounding continuum
scatter after continuum subtraction, unless this scaling
factor is smaller than 1. In the fit, we allow the resolving
power to vary by up to ±5%, to account for remaining
uncertainties in the derivation of the resolving power.
Lastly, we fit an overall offset of the profile to account
for potential systematic blue- or redshifts.

F. H2 MASS CONSTRAINTS
The nondetection of H2 allows us to put an upper
limit on the possible H2 mass in the disk. The caveat
is, however, that H2 rotational lines trace primarily the
warm gas and are very sensitive to the temperature that
is unknown and is expected to vary across the disk. In
Fig. 6 we therefore show the upper limit of the mass of
H2 as a function of the assumed gas kinetic temperature,
assuming optically thin emission in LTE. The limit cor-
responds to the maximum mass of H2 for which all of the
predicted line fluxes are below the 3-σ detection limits
of each individual transition of H2 shown in Table 6. An
ongoing debate about the origin of gas in debris disks
is whether it is primordial gas or secondary.
A deci-
sive test is to measure the ratio between the metals and
hydrogen. The mass of the gas is likely dominated by
the reservoir traced by CO detected by ALMA, but the
temperature is likely too low there to bring a solid con-
clusion. We only note that if the gas temperature at
4–7 au is above 150 K, the CO/H2 abundance would be
higher than 10−4, ruling out a primordial gas origin.

H. ESTIMATE OF STELLAR UV AND X-RAY
FLUXES
If the observed ions are produced by photoionization
from short-wavelength stellar radiation, their first ion-
ization potentials (Table 9) imply that significant radi-
ation fields shortward of ∼100 nm are required, and for
[Ar ii], even wavelengths shorter than 80 nm. For the
undetected [Ne ii] 12.81 µm line, the first ionization po-
tential of Ne is 21.56 eV, corresponding to a limiting
wavelength of 57.5 nm.
Such extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) and X-ray radiation
can originate from the stellar photosphere or a magne-
tized corona. Although A-type stars are generally X-
ray faint due to weak or absent convective zones, some
show weak and very soft X-ray emission with luminosi-
ties and coronal temperatures significantly lower than
those of active solar-type stars (G¨udel 2004; Schr¨oder
et al. 2008). In many cases, strong X-ray detections of
A stars are instead attributed to unresolved, later-type
companions (Schr¨oder & Schmitt 2007).
The potential X-ray emission from HD 172555 can be
constrained using observations from Chandra. Schr¨oder
et al. (2008) reported a nondetection, placing an upper
limit on the X-ray luminosity of LX < 5.7×1026 erg s−1,
assuming a coronal temperature of 0.9 keV (≈107 K), a
value rather high for an A-type star. For a cooler corona
with T = 1 × 106 K, more typical of A-type stars, the
upper limit becomes LX < 4.3 × 1028 erg s−1, as simu-
lated using XSPEC (Arnaud 1996) with an APEC coro-
nal model and solar abundances, applying the Chandra
ACIS-I response over the standard 0.1–2.4 keV energy
band.
To gain further insight, we examined β Pic, a close
analog to HD 172555 (spectral types A6V and A7V,
respectively).
Hempel et al. (2005) reported a detec-
tion with the XMM-Newton EPIC PN camera (ObsID
0044740601), but attributed it to a detector UV leak.
However, a weak excess at the energy of the O vii triplet
(565 eV) detected in the EPIC MOS cameras could not
be explained by UV contamination.
Combining the
O vii flux with an O vi flux from FUSE observations,
they derived a coronal temperature of 6 × 105 K and
an emission measure of 1052 cm−3, corresponding to an
X-ray luminosity of 1.8 × 1029 erg s−1 (0.1–10 keV),
assuming solar abundances. For a metallicity of 0.5×
solar, the luminosity would reduce to 9.7×1028 erg s−1.
Later, G¨unther et al. (2012) used the Chandra HRC-I
detector, less affected by UV leakage, to obtain a reliable
detection of β Pic. They derived a coronal temperature
of 1.1×106 K and a luminosity of LX = 3×1027 erg s−1
(0.06–5 keV) and 3 × 1026 erg s−1 (0.2–5 keV).

tion, and spectral extraction procedures. The extracted
spectrum was modeled with the APEC coronal model
(XSPEC) using global abundances of 0.5× solar.

coronal temperature of T = 1.4 × 106 K, close to that
derived from Chandra and EPIC MOS data. This fit
