Introduction
The discovery that our Universe is undergoing a phase of accelerated expansion stands as one of the
most signiﬁcant breakthroughs in modern cosmology. This observation was ﬁrst inferred from luminosity-
distance measurements of Type Ia supernovae [1, 2], and has since been corroborated by several in-
dependent observational probes, including measurements of the cosmic microwave background (CMB)
anisotropies [3], baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) [4] and large-scale structure surveys [5]. Within the
standard cosmological model, based on general relativity (GR), this acceleration is typically ascribed to
an exotic component known as dark energy (DE), characterized by a strong negative pressure. The most
straightforward interpretation of dark energy is a cosmological constant Λ, representing vacuum energy
with a constant equation of state ω = −1.
While the ΛCDM model remains remarkably consistent
with most observational data, it faces substantial theoretical challenges—most notably, the cosmological
constant problem, which refers to the vast discrepancy between the observed value of Λ and theoretical
predictions from quantum ﬁeld theory [6], and the coincidence problem, which questions why the densities
of dark energy and matter are of the same order precisely in the current epoch [7]. These open issues
suggest that a deeper understanding of gravity, possibly through geometric modiﬁcations to GR, may be
necessary to explain the origin of cosmic acceleration.
The discovery of the late-time acceleration of the Universe has motivated the development of modiﬁed
theories of gravity that go beyond the standard Einstein–Hilbert action. These theories aim to explain
cosmic acceleration without invoking exotic dark energy components. In the curvature-based approach,
generalizations such as f(R) gravity [8,9], f(G) gravity involving the Gauss-Bonnet invariant [10,11] and
more general f(R, G) models [12], have been widely studied. These retain the Levi-Civita connection and

energy eﬀects [27,28]. Importantly, the redshift evolution of Om(z) allows one to infer the nature of the
dark energy equation of state ω(z): an increasing Om(z) with redshift is typically associated with phantom
behavior (ω < −1), while a decreasing trend indicates quintessence-like characteristics (ω > −1) [29].

to better capture the dynamics of cosmic acceleration [30–35].

the stateﬁnder diagnostic shows that the model evolution closely follows that of ΛCDM, reinforcing its
credibility within the context of f(T, TG) gravity as a promising alternative framework to explain late-time
cosmic acceleration.
