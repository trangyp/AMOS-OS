Uncovering Hidden Violent Tendencies in LLMs:
A Demographic Analysis via Behavioral Vignettes

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increas-
ingly proposed for detecting and responding to
violent content online, yet their ability to reason
about morally ambiguous, real-world scenarios
remains underexamined. We present the first
study to evaluate LLMs using a validated social
science instrument designed to measure human
response to everyday conflict, namely the Vio-
lent Behavior Vignette Questionnaire (VBVQ).
To assess potential bias, we introduce persona-
based prompting that varies race, age, and geo-
graphic identity within the United States. Six
LLMs developed across different geopolitical
and organizational contexts are evaluated under
a unified zero-shot setting. Our study reveals
two key findings: (1) LLMs’ surface-level text
generation often diverges from their internal
preference for violent responses; (2) their vio-
lent tendencies vary across demographics, fre-
quently contradicting established findings in
criminology, social science, and psychology.
1
Introduction
Community violence, defined as violence between
unrelated individuals (CDC, 2025), remains a ma-
jor public health concern in the United States.
Traditional face-to-face violence allowed cultur-
ally embedded Violence Prevention Professionals
(VPPs) to intervene directly through Community
Violence Intervention (CVI) programs. Yet, the
shift to online platforms has made violent threats
harder to detect and address, especially given the
frequent disagreement over what constitutes as vio-
lent content (Lane, 2018; Patton et al., 2013, 2016;
Kleiner et al., 2024; Frey et al., 2020). This chal-
lenge highlights an opportunity for NLP methods
to support automated detection of threats, conflict,
and violence in text (Patton et al., 2018). Large
language models (LLMs) have been applied to de-
tect violence in diverse settings, including intimate
partner violence (Basile et al., 2024), political vi-

However, prior work often overlooks the limita-
tions of LLMs in interpreting offline context and
socially nuanced language around conflict and vi-
olence, treating their outputs as reliable without
sufficient scrutiny (Kleiner et al., 2024; Frey et al.,
2020). Most LLMs are pre-trained on open web
data and preference data from human annotators,
suffering from the risk of perpetuated bias (Hu
et al., 2025; Gallegos et al., 2024). As a result,
their interpretive behavior, especially in high-stakes
domains like violence prevention, is far from guar-
anteed to be reliable or safe.
Our work systematically examines how LLMs re-
spond to real-world conflict scenarios, focusing on
risks and embedded biases. We adapt the Violent
Behavior Vignette Questionnaire (VBVQ) (Nunes
et al., 2021), a validated social science instrument

consisting of 10 everyday conflict scenarios de-
signed to assess how individuals might respond to
provocation. These morally ambiguous vignettes
are used to probe both surface responses and inter-
nal tendencies of LLMs, analyzed via next-token
sequence likelihood. A second motivation for our
study is to examine whether LLMs, by extension
their developers, apply safety and moral reasoning
consistently across social groups. We introduce per-
sona settings that vary race, age, and US region to
simulate different hypothetical subjects of the same
conflict vignette (Figure 1). This design allows us
to test whether LLM responses shift based on de-
mographic cues, revealing potential inconsistencies
in moral alignment or embedded bias.
We evaluate six LLMs from diverse organi-
zational and geopolitical backgrounds: Mistral-
7B-Instruct-v0.1 (Mistral-7B) and Mixtral-8x22B-
Instruct-v0.1 (Mixtral-8x22B) (Jiang et al., 2023,
2024) (Mistral AI (France)); DeepSeek-Distill
Qwen and its 32B variants (Guo et al., 2025)
(DeepSeek, China); and GPT-4o-mini (Hurst et al.,
2024) (OpenAI, U.S.) and Llama3.1-8B-Instruct
(Meta, U.S.). While all models are trained on large-
scale open web data, differences in national regu-
lations and institutional norms shape how training
data is curated and alignment strategies are applied.
Rather than optimizing for task performance, our
goal is to probe and compare how these LLMs re-
acts towards morally ambiguous conflict scenarios,
under a zero-shot setting.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
study to evaluate LLM responses to real-world con-
flict scenarios using a validated social science in-
strument. Our findings reveal significant variation
across personas and models, with some patterns
contradicting to criminology findings and expecta-
tion, and raising concerns about fairness in high-
stakes applications.
2
Related Work
Criminological research shows that violence in-
creasingly begins online, with conflicts emerging
via social media rather than in person, as seen in
phenomena like “internet banging” and the “digital
street.” (Patton et al., 2013; Lane, 2018). Studies
raise concerns about whether LLMs can interpret
culturally embedded, context-sensitive cues of ag-
gression or conflict, particularly given their limited
grasp of offline context and human nuance. (Patton
et al., 2020; Kleiner et al., 2024; Frey et al., 2020).
In NLP, prior work has explored modeling vio-
lence risk through linguistic features (Glasgow and
Schouten, 2014; Wolska et al., 2023), multilingual
approaches (Kim et al., 2024; Saha et al., 2023;
Alhelbawy et al., 2016), and domain-specific vio-
lence such as gun violence (Pavlick et al., 2016)
and police encounters (Ziems and Yang, 2021). Re-
cent studies use LLMs to detect violence in text
(§1), but few systematically examine their moral
reasoning or response bias in socially ambiguous
situations across different demographic groups.
3
Methods
All selected LLMs are instruction-tuned, reflecting
industry practices that prioritize alignment and us-
ability by improving the models’ ability to follow
prompts in a controlled and interpretable manner.
In a follow-up analysis that assessed the internal
confidence of the LLM, we replaced GPT-4o-mini
with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct due to API limitations
with GPT-4o-mini, which prevented the extraction
of logarithmic probabilities at the token level.
Vignette dataset. Vignettes in VBVQ were devel-
oped through semi-structured interviews about con-
flicts experienced by criminal offenders and com-
munity members in Canada (Nunes et al., 2021).
Following Nunes et al. (2021, 2023), we evaluated
LLMs using three response formats: two categor-
ical and one open-ended. For the open-ended for-
mat, the vignette was presented directly and the
model generated a free-form response. For the
categorical formats, we used: (1) the original 10
options ranging from passive (e.g., “Ignore it”) to
violent actions (e.g., “Hit, punch, kick, or tackle
him”); and (2) a binned version with three cate-
gories: PACIFIST (PA), NON-VIOLENT (NV), and
VIOLENT (VI). A criminologist on our team cre-
ated brief (2–3 sentence) descriptions for each cate-
gory, drawing on follow-up question patterns from
the original study (Nunes et al., 2021). To reduce
spurious lexical correlations in LLM evaluation,
we use GPT-4o to generate four paraphrased vari-
ants per human-labeled response, creating a more
semantically robust and diverse reference set for
each intent category.
Prompting with varying demographics. Each
input prompt appended a system instruction of the
form: “Put yourself in this situation and answer as
if you are a {Race}, {Age Group} from {Location}”.
We varied six racial and ethnic groups, six U.S.
counties (Figure 1), and three age groups (15, 25,

To capture variation in model behavior, we
use open-text generation with self-consistency de-
coding (Wang et al.), sampling five outputs per
prompt at temperature 0.7. This enables analysis of
both surface-level outputs and internal preferences
across demographic dimensions.
Evaluating open-text generation by semantic
similarity. To assess how closely model outputs
align with human-labeled intent categories, we
use BERTScore (Zhang et al.), computed with a
DeBERTa-large model fine-tuned on MNLI (He
et al., 2020). Each LLM response is compared
against three reference categories (PA, NV, VI)
and four paraphrased versions per category. The
BERTScore F1 scores are averaged across five gen-
erations per prompt, providing a fine-grained se-
mantic alignment score per category (results in
Table 1). Additionally, we run a Kruskal-Wallis
test (McKight and Najab, 2010) on each demo-
graphic group to identify whether they have a sta-
tistically significant effect on text generation based
on the BERTScore (Table 2).
Probing LLM response tendencies via sequence
likelihood. To examine internal tendencies in the
LLMs’ responses, we compute the sequence likeli-
hood of human-labeled intent category responses
(PA, NV, VI), conditioned on each vignette input.
For robustness, we use both human written re-
sponse and their paraphrased variants of each cat-
egory label and average their likelihoods. These
likelihoods are then normalized using a softmax
function to yield a probability distribution over the
three intent categories. We define the Top-Rank
Rate as the proportion of generations in which each
class receives the highest softmax-normalized like-
lihood, providing a categorical summary of model
preference. This allows us to assess which response
type an LLM is most likely to favor in probabilistic
terms, even when generation outputs appear neutral
or similar on the surface. To isolate demographic
effects, we calculate pairwise differences in PROB

4
Results and Discussion
Overall results. When prompting with the 10 cate-
gorical options, all LLMs consistently choose the
most passive options as responses to all vignettes
input, regardless of the persona. In the free-form
generation setting, differences emerge: as shown in
Table 1, Mixtral-8x22B achieves the highest overall
similarity to human-labeled references, followed
closely by Mistral-7B and GPT-4o-mini, while both
DeepSeek models perform worse across all cate-
gories. Table 2 shows that several LLMs exhibit
significant variation in BERTScore across race, lo-
cation, and age, with DS-Qwen models showing
the strongest and most consistent demographic sen-
sitivity, particularly for VI responses. In contrast,
GPT-4o-mini and Mistral-7B show limited sensi-
tivity, with only a few significant effects. On the
overall ranking through sequence likelihood, DS-
Qwen-32B has shows the highest tendancy towards
VI category, while Llama3.1-8B shows the lowest
on VI and highest tendency towards PA.

Notable finding#1: the age categories are oppo-
site of expectations. In all of the models and at all
sizes, the models demonstrated that they believed
the youngest group (15 years old) would choose
less violent responses than the older cohorts. This
contrasts with findings from criminology and de-
velopmental psychology, which suggest that ado-
lescents are more prone to violence due to height-
ened risk-taking, underdeveloped self-regulation
and an immature frontal cortex (Stolzenberg and
D’Alessio, 2008; Steffensmeier et al., 2025; Shul-
man et al., 2013; Sapolsky, 2018). This finding then
suggests that the LLMs we prompted were actively
trying to provide non-violent responses in case it
was an actual teenager prompting them. Ostensibly,
this would be to prevent violence among teenage
groups. Interestingly, within the human written
labels, the Qwen models show the expected pattern
among age while all the other models demonstrate
the same unexpected directionality. This is impor-
tant to understand because of the ways models can
react differently when LLMs experience a human
input versus a paraphrased machine input.
Notable finding#2: the location data do not
have any particular pattern. For example, DEN-
VER,CO appeared more violent in Qwen models but
less so in Llama and Mixtral. This inconsistency
suggests LLMs may struggle to interpret location-
based cues, likely due to limited exposure to struc-
tured crime data during training, such as the FBI’s
crime data explorer (noa).
Notable finding#3: the racial categories demon-
strate a bias toward political correctness, though
the larger models seem to be less prone to this
bias. In the U.S. context, criminological and socio-
logical literature suggests a racialized bias linking
BLACK and HISPANIC individuals to violence, and
ASIAN individuals to passivity due to gendered
stereotypes (Alexander, 2012; Feagin, 1991; Chou,
2015). When LLMs depict WHITE personas as
most or least violent, they appear to overcorrect to-
ward color-blind, politically correct outputs. While
well-intentioned, such overcorrections risk rein-
forcing “positive” stereotypes that can perpetuate
systemic bias (Chou and Feagin, 2014).
Outward responses of the LLMs often contradict
their internal thinking. This suggests the models
may be overcorrecting, even if they ultimately re-
flect what humans might say in a similar scenario.
These guardrails are important but potentially prob-
lematic for real-world deployment. Future work
should examine the gap between hidden tenden-
cies and surface-level alignment to better ensure
reliability and safety in real-world deployment.
5
Conclusion
Our analysis of LLMs in morally ambiguous sce-
narios reveals a gap between surface-level neutral-
ity and latent violent preferences influenced by de-
mographic cues. While instruction tuning curbs
overt bias, sequence likelihood probing uncovers
deeper disparities, suggesting LLMs may subtly
shape sociocultural narratives through overcorrec-
tion or hidden bias.

Limitations
This study is limited in a few important ways. First,
the use of vignettes is useful and an ethical way to
address violence. However, it limits the ability for
us to assess how well LLMs can detect and react
to violence in real world scenarios. To improve
this, the next step will be to take real world social
media posts and create similar vignettes to assess
the response to a real world situation. Similarly,
using vignettes created by Canadian researchers
only allows conflicts in a specific racial/ethnic use
case. It is important to understand that the lan-
guage used among youth, and particularly youth of
color in the US, will be different and specific and
may cause even more misunderstandings within the
LLMs. Next steps will be to help create vignettes
with the help of teenagers and young adults to in-
clude vignettes that utilize important slang terms to
assess how this affects the LLMs’ ability to utilize
specific terminology. Finally, this study is poten-
tially limited by the training models of the LLMs.
While each LLM is trained on the entire internet,
what is privileged in the training may affect how
the LLM understands and reacts to the situations
they are presented with and how they understand
the US context, specifically as it refers to race and
place.
Ethical Considerations
Probing LLMs with vignettes that make up scenar-
ios, even if they are based on real-world scenarios,
is an ethical way to discover how LLMs understand
violence. In this way, we are not using data from
real people until it is discovered that LLMs can
accurately detect violence without putting real peo-
ple in potentially difficult scenarios. The outcomes
of this study can help us to improve ethical use
of LLMs in attempting to detect violent situations
online in real time. Understanding how LLMs in-
terpret violent situations, and improving this, will
allow for LLMs to help CVI programs to utilize
the "digital street" to intervene on violent situations
before they become issues on the physical street.
Our findings are a systematic evaluation. At this
point, we do not recommend that CVI programs use
LLMs to attempt to detect violence. We do believe
this is possible in the future but for the moment our
findings suggest that LLMs are not yet prepared to
ethically detect violence in a real world setting.

the social world, violence can often go undetected
or be as misunderstood by humans as it can be by
machines. Violence is often measured by proxy,
such as using crime and homicide rates to assess
violence in an area (Martinez et al., 2010; Lee and
Martinez, 2009). Another way to assess violence,
and how someone might react to a violent act, in
particular, is to use hypothetical vignettes (Nunes
et al., 2021). In the social sciences, vignettes are
hypothetical situations that are posed to a person
who is then asked to put themselves in that situation
and asked how they would respond to that. Nunes
et al. (2021) and their team have developed the
Violent Behavior Vignette Questionnaire (VBVQ)
to assess how certain individuals would react in
provocative situations. The VBVQ does not present
violent scenarios but rather, scenarios that present
the reader with conflicts and asks them how they
would respond (Nunes et al., 2021).
Vignettes provide an ethical way to understand
whether someone would react to a conflict violently.
When presented to a panel of Canadian and US
adults Nunes et al. (2023) find that adults are likely
to respond to conflict violently 25 percent of the
time. These vignettes and studies are especially rel-
evant to understanding how LLMs can understand
violence and conflict. By utilizing these vignettes
and proposing different answers, we can compare
how frequently an LLM chooses a violent reaction
to how frequently a human sample does (Nunes
et al., 2023). Moreover, using vignettes in this
way allows our research team to better understand
how well LLMs can understand the nuances of hu-
man language and interactions (Kleiner et al., 2024;
Frey et al., 2020; Patton et al., 2020).
