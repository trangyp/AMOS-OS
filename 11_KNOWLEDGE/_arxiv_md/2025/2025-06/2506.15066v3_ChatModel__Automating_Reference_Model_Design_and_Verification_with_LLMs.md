Abstract
As the complexity of integrated circuit designs continues to escalate, the functional
verification becomes increasingly challenging. Reference models, critical for ac-
celerating the verification process, are themselves becoming more intricate and
time-consuming to develop. Despite the promise shown by large language models
(LLMs) in code programming, effectively generating complex reference models
remains a significant hurdle. To address these challenges, we introduce ChatModel,
the first LLM-aided agile reference model generation and verification platform.
ChatModel streamlines the transition from design specifications to fully functional
reference models by integrating design standardization and hierarchical agile mod-
eling. Employing a building-block generation strategy, it not only enhances the
design capabilities of LLMs for reference models but also significantly boosts veri-
fication efficiency. We evaluated ChatModel on 300 designs of varying complexity,
demonstrating substantial improvements in both efficiency and quality of refer-
ence model generation. ChatModel achieved a peak performance improvement of
55.02% compared to alternative methods, with notable enhancements in generation
stability, and delivered a 9.18× increase in its capacity to produce reference model
designs. Furthermore, it accelerated the iterative process of reference model design
and validation by an average of 5.90× compared to traditional approaches. These
results highlight the potential of ChatModel to significantly advance the automation
of reference model generation and validation.

Introduction
The rapidly increasing complexity of integrated circuit (IC) designs has significantly prolonged
functional verification cycles, posing a critical bottleneck in agile hardware development [37, 21, 32].
In this context, model-driven verification (MDV) is recognized as a highly effective methodology
and has been widely applied in preliminary chip designs, such as neural processors and artificial
intelligence accelerators [66, 14, 52, 43, 22, 33]. The models in MDV are typically written in
high-level abstraction languages (i.e. Python [29, 3, 48], C++ [70, 67], MATLAB [19, 74], or
SystemC [7, 1, 50, 2]) by engineers and serve as golden references for hardware designs. As shown
in Figure 1, by systematically comparing the model outputs with those of the Device Under Test
(DUT) under identical input stimuli, developers can effectively identify design flaws and accelerate
the debugging and iteration process [53].
In the MDV framework, open-source simulators or loosely-timed modeling approaches are often
employed to rapidly develop models [6, 23, 8, 5]. However, the execution processes within these
models exhibit substantial discrepancies compared to actual hardware execution, thereby restricting
their effectiveness in diagnosing errors within the DUT. For the development of specialized designs,
developers must consider timing factors to construct more accurate models. However, building high-
fidelity reference models requires a deep understanding of the behavior and verification requirements

With the advent of large language models (LLMs), a revolutionary paradigm shift has occurred in
the field of natural language processing (NLP) [47]. Recent studies have showcased their impressive
capabilities in code generation for programming languages [85, 56, 28, 44, 77, 90] such as C++
and Python, as well as hardware description languages [79, 16, 78, 75] such as Verilog. However,
their application in constructing reference models remains underexplored. General-purpose LLMs
encounter numerous challenges when applied to reference modeling (detailed further in Appendix A),
including context and token limitations, hallucinations, and limited self-correction capabilities.
These issues undermine the quality and stability of the outputs, restricting LLMs to small-scale
designs. Moreover, the increasing diversity of design specifications and hardware architectures
further exacerbates these issues, making it challenging for LLMs to meet the demands of constructing
reference models.
To overcome these limitations, we introduce ChatModel, an end-to-end platform designed for
agile generation and verification of reference models, as illustrated in Figure 2. The ChatModel
leverages a multi-agent LLM system comprising two integrated groups: one standardizes design
specifications, while the other automates the generation and validation of reference models. By
adaptively decomposing complex design tasks into specialized subtasks, ChatModel facilitates
the rapid construction of the corresponding design Intermediate Representations (IR). The Design
IR serves as the input to the Hierarchical Agile Modeling (HAM) flow to generate the reference
model. By employing an adaptive task planning algorithm, HAM facilitates automated and scalable
generation and verification of reference models, seamlessly progressing from module to subsystem to
system in a “building block” manner.
• We propose the ChatModel, the first multi-agent LLM-based platform for automated design
and verification of reference models, enabling agile hardware verification.
• We propose the Design IR, a novel domain-specific language specifically designed for LLM
to accurately generate reference models, which effectively transforms complex specifications
into a clear directed acyclic graph.
• We introduce a Hierarchical Agile Modeling (HAM) flow that realizes agile generation
and verification of models through a modular generation methodology.
• We develop a benchmark, ModelEval, to comprehensively evaluate the capabilities of LLM
in the generation of complex reference models.
• Our comprehensive experiments demonstrate that ChatModel outperforms other benchmark
methods, offering enhanced generative capabilities and more stable generation scales. Com-
pared to general LLMs, it achieved a 55.02% improvement in functional pass rate and
enhanced the LLM reference model generation ability by 9.18x. Furthermore, it delivers an
average 5.90× speedup in model generation compared to experienced engineers.

Related Works
Traditional Reference Model Design: As a vital component in the verification process, reference
models are typically developed using programming languages at varying abstraction levels, tailored to

the functionality of the DUT [65, 54]. For image or signal processing designs, Python [68, 62, 4] and
MATLAB [38, 91] are commonly employed for modeling, while C++ [26, 51, 67] is predominantly
used for constructing reference models for logic functions and CPU designs. Recently, SystemC with
Transaction-Level Modeling 2.0 (TLM 2.0) has achieved widespread adoption for reference model
design, attributable to its capacity to facilitate modeling across multiple levels of abstraction [25,
31, 59, 39, 17]. For larger System-on-Chip (SoC) designs, simulators like gem5 [9, 63, 72] or
SST [69] are employed for model development to verify hardware system correctness, but their
limited configurability results in coarse simulation granularity. Although these approaches have
contributed to the advancement of reference model development, they require developers to possess
substantial expertise in hardware and software programming, often necessitating a considerable and
sustained investment of resources and time.
LLM in Code Generation: With the emergence of models such as ChatGPT [35] and Claude [10],
LLMs have demonstrated their powerful capabilities in automating code generation from natural
language descriptions [15, 88, 92, 41]. Recent efforts [73, 55, 12, 78, 11, 18, 82] have explored
LLM-aided Hardware Description Language (HDL) generation and debugging using prompt engineer-
ing [20, 86, 57, 76], retrieval-augmented generation [46, 61], and supervised fine-tuning [36, 87, 83].
Besides HDL generation, some works have also investigated complex software designs. The learning
selection method LAIL [40] and the self-planning code generation technique [30] are both enhance-
ments in these areas. A two-stage code generation method [89] has demonstrated improved efficiency
in generating code under zero-shot conditions. The LCG model [42], which adopts a multi-LLM
agent cooperation mechanism, integrates seamlessly into traditional software development processes,
significantly elevating the quality of generated code. In addition, study like UVLLM [27] has explored
the potential of LLM in the generation of reference models. However, implementations rely on the
relatively lower-level language SystemVerilog, which restricts the scale of the generated models.
Despite these advancements, the potential of LLMs to generate reference models for large-scale
hardware designs remains underexplored, with notable limitations in both the token/context memory
limits for complex designs and limited debugging capabilities of LLMs.
3
Architecture & Workflow
Given that complex hardware designs consist of multiple functionally decoupled modules, ChatModel
employs a “divide-and-conquer” approach to decompose intricate design tasks into manageable
module-level subtasks, effectively addressing the aforementioned challenges presented in Section 1.
Building on this strategy, ChatModel offers a fully automated solution for spec-to-model generation
using LLMs, as shown in Figure 3. This framework consists of the “Design Specification Standard-
ization Phase” and the “Hierarchical Agile Modeling Phase”. Each Agent is assigned a dedicated
generation task and is equipped with a tailored chain of thought and template.
The transition from design specifications to reference model generation involves five key steps.
(1) Module IRs Generation (Agents 1 & 2): Nonstandard input specifications are decomposed into
multiple standardized module IRs based on the design structure, with each module IR corresponding to
a specific generation subtask. (2) DFG Construction (Agent 3): For modules containing submodules,
connections are established between parent module ports and internal submodule ports, effectively
constructing the data flow graph. (3) Adaptive Task Planning: Utilizing Algorithm 1, the sequence
for module generation and verification is defined, ensuring an organized and efficient workflow.
(4) Module-Level Model Generation (Agents 4–6): Based on the module IR and DFG (optional),
Algorithm 2 invokes the agile model generator to produce the corresponding reference model for
each subtask. (5) Model Verification and Debugging (Agent 7): A comprehensive set of external test
cases is employed to verify both the syntax and functionality of each generated module. Any module
that fail verification are automatically repaired. If any modules remain incomplete after this process,
subsequent modules are generated and validated iteratively until the top-level module is finalized,
thereby completing the entire system design. To clearly demonstrate the workflow of ChatModel,
Figure 4 illustrates the automatic generation and validation process of a reference model based on
specific design specifications. For more detailed case information, please refer to Appendix E.

4
Design Specification Standardization
The content of a design specification can vary significantly with different authors and often becomes
excessively verbose, which diminishes the ability of LLMs to accurately extract design-related
information. To address these, we propose a design specification standardization flow that employs
multiple agents to decompose design at the module level, extract implementation descriptions for each
module, and construct inter-module connections. Through this process, the input design specifications
are transformed into a Design Intermediate Representation (Design IR), which consists of multiple
module IRs and a Design Architecture Graph (DAG).
The design specifications are first divided into multiple text segments through preprocessing, which
serve as inputs for the subsequent module-level IR generator. Module IRs are generated in a top-
down systematic manner, starting from the top module and sequentially producing each submodule.
As depicted in Figure 3, when Agent 1 is tasked with IR generation, it first sequentially extracts
port signals, implementation details, and other relevant information from the sliced specifications
of the current module. This extracted information is then analyzed and refined, with irrelevant

content filtered out and implementation methods optimized, resulting in the final module IR. Agent 2
performs equivalence checks between the generated module IR and the original design specification
to assess whether any essential descriptive logic has been lost during the transformation process.
For IRs that fail verification, detailed error reports are communicated to Agent 1, accompanied by
targeted guidance to facilitate iterative refinement and accurate regeneration. By employing multiple
rounds of reading slice content and self-checking, ChatModel effectively mitigates attention loss in
LLMs when processing long texts.
After the successful verification of all module IRs, Agent 3 constructs the DAG by systematically
analyzing the port data flow graph of parent and child modules. The DAG abstracts the hierarchical
structure of hardware modules into a structured directed acyclic graph, effectively resolving interface
alignment challenges inherent in modular design. It comprises multiple depth-2 subtrees, where each
subtree delineates the port connections between a parent module and its child modules, providing a
precise and structured reference for subsequent generation processes.

5.3
Model Verification & Debugger
The validation strategy of ChatModel for the reference model adheres to the task planning generated
by HAP. Upon generation of the reference model for a specific module, user-provided test cases
are utilized to verify the syntax and functional accuracy of the reference model. The model is
deemed functionally equivalent to the hardware design only after successfully passing all tests.
Subsequent subsystem or system-level models will incorporate these validated models and undergo
comprehensive subsystem or system-level validation. This incremental modular validation approach
employs a bottom-up strategy, facilitating concurrent validation during the development of internal
subsystems. In addition, it effectively reduces validation complexity, simplifies error localization,
and accelerates convergence toward an accurate model.
To enhance the debugging capabilities of LLMs, we document common errors and their solutions
within the reference model and utilize Chroma [64] to construct a vector database. Agent 7 employs
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) technology to access relevant data from this database,
facilitating the resolution of syntactic and functional errors during validation. For syntactic errors,
terminal output logs are used to retrieve pertinent information from documents or knowledge bases
via RAG technology. This information, along with relevant code snippets, is input into the LLM
for error analysis and code correction. In the case of functional errors, Agent 7 also extracts logs
from the period surrounding the error occurrence to enable the LLM to analyze the causes of output
discrepancies.

6
Evaluations
In this section, we evaluate the capability of ChatModel in generating reference models for various
designs across multiple dimensions. The results demonstrate that the framework exhibits exceptional
performance and remarkable stability when processing diverse designs, effectively enhancing the
capability and efficiency of LLMs in agile model generation and verification tasks.
6.1
Experimental setup
Benchmarks. To evaluate the effectiveness of our work, we employed 300 designs from the open-
source benchmarks VerilogEval [45] and RTLLM2.0 [49], as well as our own ModelEval dataset.
Both VerilogEval and RTLLM2.0 are relatively simple and primarily used to evaluate the generative
capabilities of LLMs, yet their design specifications can still be applied to generating reference
models. To more accurately evaluate the capability of ChatModel in generating complex reference
models, we developed the ModelEval benchmark, which consists of 94 distinct designs that vary
significantly in complexity. Each design includes comprehensive test cases to verify the functional
correctness of the generated reference models. The model is considered successfully generated only
if it passes these test cases.
Evaluation Metrics. Since LLMs are probabilistic, each case is evaluated multiple times to account
for output variability, and the pass@k [13] metric is calculated. Unless otherwise specified, Chat-
Model uses GPT-4o as the foundational LLM for each agent, and the temperature of all LLMs is
set to 0.3 to balance generation capability and randomness. The debugging iteration threshold in
ChatModel is set at 6, as improvements beyond this point are difficult to observe. For more detailed
information about the experiment setup, please see Appendix B.
6.2
Capability for Reference Model Generation
Table 1 compares the reference model generation capabilities of ChatModel with CoT, Few-Shot,
Multi-Agent and other LLM-based methods across three benchmarks. ChatModel consistently
achieved the highest scores on both syntax and functionality tests, with an average syntax pass rate
(SP) of 95.44% and a functionality pass rate (FP) of 86.74%. Compared to the baseline FP of the
LLM-based Method, ChatModel demonstrates a performance improvement ranging from 39.21%
to 55.02%, significantly improving the ability of LLMs to generate accurate reference models. In
Figure 5, we selected several representative examples from the three benchmarks, arranged in order of
increasing complexity. As the complexity of the designs increases, the scores of the baseline methods
decrease rapidly, whereas ChatModel consistently manages to complete these designs effectively. The
figure demonstrates that ChatModel, compared to other LLM-based methods, generating complex
designs of over 1800 lines and enhancing design capability by 9.18x.
According to the design scale classification criteria specified in Appendix B, we further divided the
benchmarks into three categories based on the design scale: Small-Set, Medium-Set, and Large-Set.
We evaluated the average Pass@5 performance of ChatModel and other LLM-based methods on these
datasets to compare their generative capabilities on different design scales, as depicted in Figure 6

6.3
Ablation Experiment
To further clarify the contribution of each component to the improvement in the generation capacity
of ChatModel, we performed ablation experiments on designs of varying complexity. The entire
experiment used GPT-4o as the original reference for comparison, and we sequentially added the
four core elements of ChatModel: design IR, Hierarchical Adaptive Planning (HAP), Agile Model
Generator (AMG) and debugger, to observe their impact on generation performance.
Figure 7 demonstrates that the incorporation of design IR significantly improves generative per-
formance, with HAP further enhancing the capabilities of LLMs in handling large and complex
designs. This improvement is attributed to the effective reduction of hallucinations in LLMs through
standardization of the specification, while HAP further mitigates the constraints imposed by token
limitations. For more intricate designs, AMG effectively addresses the challenges of generating
extended submodules and aligning interfaces. Furthermore, the introduction of a Debugger agent
further strengthens the debugging capabilities of ChatModel.

6.4
Agile Design and Verification Analysis
To evaluate the acceleration provided by ChatModel in reference model design and verification, we
engaged three verification engineers with 2 to 3 years of experience who are familiar with SystemC.
They independently developed and verified reference models for three designs of varying complexity
and recorded their time costs. Engineer E1 utilized ChatModel for development, E2 employed
GPT-4o for assistance, while E3 developed without the aid of LLMs. As illustrated in Figure 9,
ChatModel significantly improved development efficiency compared to E3, achieving an average
speedup of 5.90×, with a range from 3.94× to 6.27×.
7
Conclusion
In this work, we introduce ChatModel, an agile framework for the generation and verification of
reference models for general hardware. This framework effectively addresses the challenges faced
in reference model design using LLMs through the standardization of design specifications and a
structured Hierarchical Agile Modeling (HAM) process. We have also developed a debugger agent to
effectively detect and correct errors in the generated models. Compared to traditional LLM-assisted
reference model generation, our approach achieved a 55.02% improvement in functional pass rate
and expanded the generative scale of the reference model by 9.18X. In comparison to manually
developed reference models, ChatModel demonstrates an enhancement in reference model generation
and performance ranging from 3.94X to 6.27X. Our evaluation demonstrates robust capability of
ChatModel in generating complex reference models and further explores the transformative potential
of LLMs in assisting reference model generation.

Challenges in Reference Model Generation with LLMs
To explore the challenges that large language models (LLMs) encounter when generating reference
models, we conducted a comparative study involving GPT-4o, Claude 3.5-Sonnet, Gemma-2, Llama-
3, and Code-Llama. We selected ten open-source designs with varying levels of complexity as
the test set. Each LLM was tasked with generating reference models based on identical input
specifications. A reference model was considered functionally equivalent to the hardware design only
if it successfully passed all built-in functional verification cases. In instances of errors or test failures,
the corresponding error logs were provided to the LLM for debugging. We then recorded the number
of generation and debugging iterations required to achieve successful functional verification. Each
design task was repeated 20 times, with a maximum of six debugging rounds per test. We evaluated
the generation capabilities of the LLMs using Pass@k, as defined in Formula 1, and assessed their
debugging capabilities using DC, as defined in Formula 2.

As shown in the Figure 10, GPT-4o exhibits stronger generation and debugging capabilities compared
to other LLMs. For simpler designs, LLMs can quickly generate the corresponding reference
models. However, as design complexity increases, the ability of LLMs to generate accurate reference
models diminishes. When the RTL code exceeds 160 lines, most LLMs struggle to produce correct
reference models within a limited number of iterations. We attribute this occurrence primarily to four
challenges:
• Context Window & Hallucinations: Complex hardware designs are typically accompanied by
extensive specifications, making it challenging for LLMs to systematically align and analyze the
global context due to their limited token capacities and context window sizes. As a result, these
limitations can induce hallucinations and significant errors in the generated reference models.
• Randomness: LLMs can produce different outputs even with the same input. This inherent
randomness increases the likelihood of design errors, which can accumulate rapidly over multiple
iterations. The absence of standardized descriptive norms, combined with the diverse content
and structure of complex design specifications, further exacerbates the problem and significantly
affects output quality.

• Token Constraints: The limited token generation capacity of LLMs makes it difficult to
encapsulate all necessary details for large-scale designs in a single output. Therefore, multi-turn
dialogues or multi-agent approaches are necessary. However, these methods require an effective
strategy to manage the entire generation process and ensure that the interfaces within the design
remain consistent.
• Limited Code Generation & Debugging Capabilities: Hardware design necessitates con-
sideration of timing and parallel execution, whereas reference models are typically executed
sequentially. LLMs must accurately understand the distinctions between these two methodolo-
gies to generate reference models effectively. Moreover, the intricate inter-module connection
relationships inherent in hardware designs result in error-prone code generations and challenging
verifications, further impacting overall system reliability.

B.2
LLM-based Methods
In this subsection, we introduce several methods based on LLMs used in our experiments.
Zero-Shot prompting: Zero-Shot prompting leverages the capabilities of a general LLM to address
new tasks directly. In this approach, the LLM does not require specific task examples but instead
relies on the extensive knowledge and reasoning abilities accumulated during its pre-training phase to
generate outputs. Consequently, this method allows the LLM to demonstrate strong generalization
and adaptability, effectively performing various tasks even in the absence of task-specific information.
Few-Shot prompting: Few-Shot prompting utilizes an LLM to execute specific tasks with only a
small number of examples. This approach maximizes the use of the LLM’s pre-trained knowledge
by providing a limited number of examples to guide the model in understanding and completing
the task without additional specialized training. Typically, few-shot learning involves including task
instructions and examples in the input, enabling the LLM to infer task requirements from limited
context and generate appropriate outputs.
Chain-of-Thought (CoT): Chain-of-Thought prompting guides the LLM to generate a series of
intermediate reasoning steps, thereby aiding in more effective problem-solving for complex issues.
Unlike traditional LLMs that typically generate outputs directly from inputs, chain-of-thought
decomposes problems into multiple manageable sub-steps, allowing the LLM to better understand
and resolve issues. This step-by-step reasoning process can significantly improve the LLM’s accuracy
and efficiency in tasks requiring deep analysis, such as logical reasoning and code generation.
CoT+Few-shot: CoT+Few-shot enhances the LLM’s accuracy and efficiency in handling complex
problems by presenting a series of step-by-step reasoning examples, thereby improving the model’s
understanding of the problem’s structure and logic.
Multi-agent: The multi-agent approach involves decomposing a complex task into multiple subtasks
and employing several LLMs to collaborate in completing them. The current LLM treats other models
as black boxes, focusing solely on the output content without considering their internal dialogue
information. This method allows the LLM to concentrate on the current task requirements, thereby
enhancing the accuracy and robustness of the results.
Best-Choice: Best-Choice is also a multi-model generation strategy. This method involves aggregat-
ing the prediction results from multiple LLMs and selecting the highest quality output for subsequent
processing. Consequently, this approach effectively reduces potential biases or errors from individual
models, thereby improving the overall system performance.

D
Case Study
The ChatModel framework adeptly plans and generates processes based on input design specifications,
incrementally creating reference models for various modules and executing automated validation and
self-correction. By employing techniques such as task refinement, chain of thought, and templates,
ChatModel effectively addresses challenges encountered by LLMs in code generation, such as
hallucinations and token limitations, etc. In this section, we employ the Conv2D design illustrated
in Figure 4 as a case study to demonstrate the flexibility, stability, and efficacy of ChatModel. The
architecture of this design is organized with a top-level module, Conv2D, which integrates two
submodules, Adder and Multi. These submodules are employed by Conv2D to perform logical
processing operations.
D.1
Design IR Generation for Conv2D
The first phase involves the Conv2D design specification standardization process, which is divided
into two steps to convert non-standard design specifications into a design Intermediate Representation
(IR). The design IR comprises three module IRs: Conv2D, Adder, and Multi, along with a Design
Architecture Graph (DAG) that illustrates the connections between the submodules and the parent
module.
Utilizing a top-down approach, ChatModel decomposes complex design specifications into multiple
standardized module-level IRs. As depicted in Figure 11, ChatModel initially engages Agent 1 to
extract information pertinent to the top-level Conv2D module through multiple rounds of dialogue
and generates a standard Conv2D Module IR. Subsequently, Agent 2 verifies the generated IR with
the original design specification to ensure the conversion process is equivalent. Any erroneous
information is relayed by Agent 2 to Agent 1 for reference in regenerating the IR. Upon successful
check, ChatModel determines the subsequent IR generation task based on the “Children Modules”
within the Conv2D IR.
After completing the IRs for the Conv2D, Multi, and Adder modules, these IRs are submitted to Agent
3 to construct the inter-module DAG. Agent 3 primarily focuses on the connectivity between module
ports, while Agent 4 further elucidates the interaction between the internal logic of submodules and
parent modules. This methodology allows the LLM to concentrate on analyzing uniform types of
connection relationships, thereby enhancing the accuracy of DAG construction. Since Conv2D is a

three module classes. The Conv2D model acts as the top level, internally invoking the classes of the

E
Limitations and Future Work
As ChatModel is a framework composed of multiple agents, its generation quality and stability are
influenced to some extent by the LLMs utilized by these agents. While our experiments with advanced
LLMs like GPT-4o have yielded promising results for complex designs, tasks remain challenging for
less capable LLMs. Despite employing strategies such as task segmentation, self-verification, chain of
thought, and offering output templates to mitigate the likelihood of hallucinations, ChatModel cannot
completely eliminate them. This issue may lead to reference models failing syntax and functionality
tests, thereby affecting the generation efficiency of ChatModel. The completeness of functional
verification for the reference model is typically influenced by the test cases. Future work will focus on
fine-tuning LLMs according to specific generation tasks to enhance agent task handling capabilities
and reduce hallucination occurrences, ultimately enabling automatic generation of reference models
for ultra-large-scale designs like out-of-order multiprocessors.
