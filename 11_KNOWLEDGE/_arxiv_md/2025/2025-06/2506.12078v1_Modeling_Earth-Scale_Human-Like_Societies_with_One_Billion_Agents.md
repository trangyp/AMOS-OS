Abstract
Understanding how complex societal behaviors emerge from individual cognition
and interactions requires both high-fidelity modeling of human behavior and large-
scale simulations. Traditional agent-based models (ABMs) have been employed to
study these dynamics for decades, but are constrained by simplified agent behaviors
that fail to capture human complexity. Recent advances in large language models
(LLMs) offer new opportunities by enabling agents to exhibit sophisticated social
behaviors that go beyond rule-based logic, yet face significant scaling challenges.
Here we present Light Society, an agent-based simulation framework that advances
both fronts, efficiently modeling human-like societies at planetary scale powered
by LLMs. Light Society formalizes social processes as structured transitions of
agent and environment states, governed by a set of LLM-powered simulation
operations, and executed through an event queue. This modular design supports
both independent and joint component optimization, supporting efficient simulation
of societies with over one billion agents. Large-scale simulations of trust games
and opinion propagation—spanning up to one billion agents—demonstrate Light
Society’s high fidelity and efficiency in modeling social trust and information
diffusion, while revealing scaling laws whereby larger simulations yield more
stable and realistic emergent behaviors.

Introduction
The past decade has witnessed remarkable advancements in artificial intelligence, culminating in
large language models (LLMs) that not only demonstrate unprecedented capabilities in language
understanding and generation [1; 2; 3], but also achieve near-human performance on complex tasks
requiring advanced reasoning including mathematics and coding [4; 5]. These capabilities have
catalyzed the emergence of LLM-powered autonomous agents that are capable of perceiving their
environment, making decisions, and taking actions in complex, dynamic settings [6; 7; 8]. Crucially,
such agents can emulate nuanced individual behaviors—maintaining personality traits [9; 10; 11],
exhibiting self-awareness [12; 13], and expressing context-sensitive emotions [14; 15]—capturing
the fluidity and depth of human-like reactions. With the ability to simulate realistic and coherent
individual behaviors, LLM-powered agents offer a new tool for investigating cognition, personality,
and decision-making across diverse and reproducible scenarios.

Social science, as the study of human behavior in social contexts, offers key insights into how societies
function and evolve. Traditional methods—such as questionnaires [16; 17], interviews [18; 19], and
controlled experiments [20; 21]—have long provided valuable insights, yet they are often costly,
ethically constrained, and difficult to scale or replicate. Agent-based modeling (ABM) emerged
as a computational alternative, enabling in silico experiments on social phenomena by simulating
interactions among rule-based agents [22; 23; 24; 25; 26]. However, most ABMs rely on heuristics
or fixed equations, limiting their capacity to reflect the complexity and adaptability of real-world
societies [27]. The rise of large language models offers a transformative opportunity: agents powered
by LLMs can generate context-rich, expressive, and adaptive behaviors, enabling simulations that
integrate cognitive, emotional, and social dimensions. Recent work has demonstrated the promise
of such agents in reproducing social dynamics like herd behavior [28; 29], cooperation [30; 31],
polarization [29; 32], information spreading [32; 29], presidential election [33; 34], and the impact
of disease outbreaks [35], suggesting that LLM-powered simulation may bridge longstanding gaps
between computational models and the fluid realities of human societies.
Despite recent advances, current LLM-powered simulation frameworks remain prohibitively costly.
For instance, many existing studies require dozens of GPUs and weeks of computation to simulate
up to a million agents in social scenarios [29; 32; 28; 33; 36; 37; 38]. As simulations scale up,
storage demands, latency, and overall computational costs grow rapidly, often rendering planet-scale
simulations infeasible. Consequently, when attempting to study complex global phenomena, scaling
simulations to a global level while maintaining high fidelity becomes challenging and expensive. In
addition, many existing frameworks are task-specific and lack generalizability [29; 28; 39; 40; 41],
limiting their applicability across diverse social simulation conditions.
To advance the field of simulating large-scale societal dynamics with cognitively rich agents, we
introduce Light Society, a modular simulation framework powered by LLMs capable of simulating
societies with over one billion agents. At its core, Light Society captures social evolution by
encoding interactions as transitions between agent and environment states, triggered by LLM-
powered simulation operations and paced by an event queue, with all components carefully designed
to preserve the complexity of real-world societies. This design enables not only high-fidelity modeling
of human social behaviors, but also efficient simulation by decoupling the simulation system into
modular components that can be optimized both independently and jointly.
To accelerate large-scale simulation, Light Society integrates a suite of algorithmic and system-level
optimizations. At the inference level, semantic prompt caching identifies and reuses structurally
similar prompts during the simulation, significantly reducing redundant LLM queries. To further
reduce computational cost, we apply knowledge distillation, training lightweight surrogate models on
task-specific distributions to approximate the outputs of LLMs. A mixture-of-models architecture,
with automated load balancing across heterogeneous inference backends—including LLMs, surrogate
models, and models served by different providers—enables flexible trade-offs between fidelity and
efficiency. Parallelized inference pipelines further improve throughput by distributing simulation tasks
across diverse model types and infrastructure pathways. At the system level, Light Society employs
a distributed execution engine and high-performance storage to coordinate simulation at scale, and
compressed graph representations ensure efficient communication across billion-node networks. At
the event level, the framework employs an event queue with support for event aggregation—merging
concurrent events of the same type to minimize redundant computation—and asynchronous execution,
which decouples independent events to maximize concurrency. This modular and composable
architecture supports plug-and-play accelerations, allowing domain-specific enhancements to be
integrated without altering the core simulation engine.
To demonstrate the capabilities of Light Society, we conduct two large-scale simulation studies. The
first examines socio-demographic patterns of trust and reciprocity through a globally instantiated
trust game with agents derived from real-world survey data, revealing how social stratification shapes
interpersonal behavior and how scaling influences the emergence of stable behavioral norms. The
second investigates opinion propagation via influencer–influencee interactions across a one-billion-
agent network, capturing how individual views shift through repeated social exposure. This simulation
demonstrates the computational efficiency of Light Society at planetary scale, while uncovering
propagation dynamics consistent with real-world patterns of information diffusion. Together, these
studies highlight the potential of Light Society as a testbed for exploring emergent phenomena,
counterfactual interventions, and large-scale modeling across the human sciences.

Light Society: An Efficient LLM-Powered Social Simulation Framework
To realize large-scale simulation of cognitively rich societies, Light Society is designed as a modular
and efficient framework that integrates LLM-powered social cognition, modeling social dynamics as
transitions between agent and environment states orchestrated by simulation operations and scheduled
through an event queue, as shown in Fig. 1. The model is defined as the tuple:
M := ⟨D, T, SA, SE, V, Q, F⟩
(1)
F := {fI, fP , fΠ, fA, fE, fU}
(2)
Here, T denotes the timeline of the simulation. At each time step, the system maintains a dynamic set
of active agents, which may change over time due to internal events such as birth, death, or shifts in
participation (for example, when individuals join or leave a social platform). Each agent has a state
SA composed of three elements: a static profile that remains constant throughout the simulation (such
as demographics or personality traits), an internal status that evolves over time (capturing aspects like
memory, beliefs, and goals), and an external status that reflects observable features like the agent’s
location or social connections. The environment state SE is defined by both static components, such
as spatial layout or institutional structures, and dynamic components that change as the simulation
progresses—these might include things like weather conditions or disasters.
Events v ∈V in Light Society serve as the primary drivers of simulation dynamics. Each event
encapsulates a discrete interaction or change in the system, such as an agent sending a message,
relocating, forming a relationship. Events are assigned timestamps for execution in the simulation and
may involve one or more initiating entities and one or more target entities (e.g., agents, environmental
modules). They carry structured payloads that describe the content and context of the interaction—for
instance, the text of a message, a resource transfer, or a behavioral intention. Events may also include

a priority level, allowing the system to resolve multiple simultaneous events deterministically. All
events are stored in an event queue, which functions as a priority queue sorted by time and priority,
ensuring consistent and temporally coherent execution of interactions throughout the simulation.
The core dynamics of Light Society are governed by a collection of simulation operations F powered
by large language models. These operations define how agents perceive, reason, act, and evolve over
time, as well as how the environment responds and the system state is updated accordingly. At the
outset, an initialization operation fI configures the simulation by generating the initial agent states,
the environment state, a set of starting events, based on a seed dataset D. During simulation, agents
perceive their surroundings via a perception operation fP , make decisions and generate actions using
a policy operation fΠ that produces events. As time progresses, agent and environment states evolve
independently through dedicated evolution operations fA and fE, reflecting internal dynamics such
as memory decay or environmental drift. An update operation fU applies events to the simulation
system, ensuring consistent transitions. After the simulation, a readout process aggregates historical
system states and events into measurable outputs for downstream analysis. Together, these operations
form a flexible and scalable framework for simulating complex social dynamics.
To support efficient inference at planetary scale, Light Society implements a multi-tiered optimization
pipeline. At its core is a semantic prompt caching mechanism that reduces redundant LLM invocations
by identifying and reusing responses to structurally similar prompts. During simulation, each query
to the LLM is first converted into a vector representation and is stored in a vector database that
supports fast searching algorithms. When a new prompt is generated, the system performs similarity
search in the embedding space to determine whether to reuse a semantically equivalent prompt
that has been processed previously. Besides, knowledge distillation is applied to train compact
task-specific surrogate models that approximate LLM behavior for routine decisions and environment
updates. These models are periodically retrained using live simulation data to adapt to domain drift,
maintaining high fidelity while offloading frequent and repetitive tasks from full LLM calls.
Simulation operations are dispatched through a mixture-of-models architecture, allowing tasks to be
routed to full LLMs, distilled models, or other inference backends based on fidelity needs and resource
availability. Combined with parallelized pipelines, this design maximizes throughput by distributing
computation across heterogeneous infrastructure. At the system level, Light Society leverages a
distributed execution engine and compressed graph structures to support efficient state updates and
neighbor queries across billion-node networks. The event queue supports event aggregation—merging
simultaneous events of the same type—and asynchronous execution, enabling concurrent processing
of independent interactions and improving system throughput.
3
Results
To evaluate the capacity of Light Society to model complex societal behaviors at scale, we conduct
two representative simulations emphasizing both expressiveness and efficiency. In section 3.1, we
investigate patterns of human trust through agent-based simulations of the trust game. In section
3.2, we study opinion propagation via influencer–influencee interactions across a one-billion-agent
network.
3.1
Trust Game Simulation
We evaluated how individual trust behavior and reciprocity vary across socio-economic backgrounds
using a large-scale simulation of trust games with LLMs. In a trust game, a trustor decides how much
of an initial $10 endowment to send to an anonymous trustee, with the amount tripled upon trans-
fer [42; 43; 44]. The trustee then decides how much of the received amount to return. This minimal
setup captures key dynamics of social exchange, including risk-taking, fairness, and reciprocity.
Our simulation pipeline (Fig. 2a) uses agent profiles constructed from the World Values Survey
(WVS) Wave 7 [45; 46; 47; 48], which offers extensive coverage of individual characteristics
across a wide range of socio-demographic and cultural dimensions—including country, age, and
subjective social class (Fig. 2b). Each agent is instantiated with a natural language profile derived
from real-world survey responses, and both the trustor and trustee roles are played by LLMs,
conditioned on these profiles. Each profile was used once in the trustor role, and separately in the

demonstrated higher trust than those with little or no formal education. These stratified patterns
were evident across both models, suggesting that socio-economic background may influence trust
propensity in systematic ways.
While the relative trends remain consistent, we observed differences in the overall level of trust
expressed by agents simulated with different models (Fig. 2c). In general, GPT-4.1 nano exhibited
more conservative trust behavior than Gemini 2.0 Flash, with lower average send amounts observed
across multiple demographic groups. This suggests that model-specific inductive biases may influence
individual-level decision patterns, even when provided with identical profile information.
Trustee behavior revealed structured patterns of reciprocity that varied with the amount received (sim-
ulated by Gemini 2.0 Flash). As shown in Fig. 2d, average return amounts increased approximately

3.2
Opinion Propagation via Influencer-Influencee Interactions with One Billion Agents
Opinion dynamics explores how individual attitudes evolve through social interactions, shaping
collective beliefs across populations [49; 50]. Understanding these dynamics is essential for inter-
preting societal phenomena such as political polarization, rumor spreading, and public consensus
formation. In particular, social networks with heterogeneous structures—such as scale-free networks
generated via the Barab´asi–Albert (BA) model [51]—reflect real-world patterns of social connectivity,
where a small subset of highly connected individuals, or “influencers”, wield disproportionate impact
on opinion formation [52; 53; 54]. To investigate these dynamics at scale, we simulate opinion
propagation over a one-billion-agent BA network, focusing on the interactions between influencers
and their local neighborhoods (influencees) to uncover emergent patterns of influence, convergence,
and fragmentation [55], as shown in Fig. 3a.
To instantiate the simulation, we generate a synthetic social network of one billion agents using
the Barab´asi–Albert (BA) model (with each new node attaching to three existing nodes), yielding
a sparse, scale-free topology that mirrors real-world degree heterogeneity (Fig. 3b). Each agent is
assigned a social identity by randomly sampling from a pool of 10,000 demographic profiles drawn
from the World Values Survey (WVS) Wave 7 dataset [45; 46; 47; 48]—ensuring diversity in age,
gender, education, and socio-economic background. We designate the top 20% of nodes by degree
as influencers, reflecting their centrality and potential for disproportionate influence in the network,
while the remaining 80% serve as influencees.
At the start of the simulation, all agents are given a controversial statement—“AI automation will lead
to mass unemployment”—and are assigned an initial opinion: agree, disagree, or neutral.
Influencee opinions are initialized randomly, while influencer opinions are configured under three
distinct seeding regimes: (1) equal proportions of disagree and neutral (1D1N), (2) equal
proportions of agree and neutral (1A1N), and (3) fully randomized distribution (Random), as
illustrated in Fig. 3c. The simulation proceeds in rounds: in each round, 1% of influencers are
sampled uniformly at random, and each engages in topic-specific interactions with their immediate
influencees. These interactions may result in updates to the influencee’s opinion, depending on both
parties’ current views and profile attributes.
To scale simulation efficiently, we distill LLM behaviors into a lightweight surrogate model. We first
generate a dataset of 100,000 interaction samples by prompting the LLM with diverse combinations
of agent profiles and opinion states, capturing how influencees respond to influencers under varying
demographic and attitudinal conditions. Then this synthetic dataset serves as supervised training data
for a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) surrogate model tasked with predicting the influencee’s updated
opinion given the interaction context. This surrogate replaces most LLM calls during simulation,
significantly reducing token usage, as shown in Fig. 3f.

in shaping the evolution of population-wide attitudes. As shown in Fig. 3d–e, in the 1A1N condi-
tion—where influencers predominantly hold an agree stance—the influencee population exhibits
a clear shift toward agreement over successive rounds. Conversely, in the 1D1N setting, where
disagreeing influencers are more prevalent, the influencee group moves toward disagree. Notably,

To further understand heterogeneity in opinion influence, we analyze how individual attributes shape
the likelihood of opinion change during influencer–influencee interactions. As shown in Fig. 3g,
agents with higher education levels are consistently more successful at persuading others to adopt
their stance, while also being more resistant to opinion change themselves. This asymmetric influence
profile suggests that education confers both expressive power and cognitive resilience in social
discourse. Fig. 3h extends this analysis by examining the joint effects of education and income on
influence success rates. Across all groups, influence efficacy increases with both dimensions, with
the highest success observed among agents with postgraduate education and high income. These
findings reflect well-documented sociological patterns: individuals with greater access to resources
and knowledge often occupy more persuasive and authoritative positions in discourse, and their views
tend to carry greater weight in shaping collective opinion. In simulated societies, as in real ones,
structural advantages amplify communicative power—suggesting that opinion dynamics are not only
shaped by who speaks, but also by who is heard.
To evaluate the fidelity of the surrogate model, we conducted a controlled validation experiment on
a smaller network of 10,000 agents, where we systematically varied the proportion of influencer
interactions simulated using the original LLM versus the surrogate model. As shown in Fig. 3i, we
compare the opinion evolution trajectories under five substitution levels: 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and
100% surrogate usage. The results demonstrate strong alignment between the full LLM simulation
and the surrogate-assisted runs: the trajectory of opinion distributions—across agree, disagree,
and neutral states—remains qualitatively consistent, with similar directionality and magnitude
of change. In particular, the time series of influencee agree opinion counts show near-identical
inflection points and convergence behavior, indicating that the surrogate model effectively captures
the underlying decision patterns of the full LLM. This consistency suggests that the surrogate model
not only replicates the high-level behavioral dynamics induced by the LLM, but also preserves the
interaction-level semantics critical for downstream outcomes. As a result, Light Society can achieve
high improvements in simulation efficiency without sacrificing behavioral fidelity—enabling opinion
dynamics simulations at unprecedented scales.

Related Work
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have significantly reshaped agent-based social
simulations, enabling more realistic representations of human cognitive processes and social behav-
iors [1; 56]. Leveraging extensive world knowledge and sophisticated natural language capabilities,
LLM-driven agents can simulate complex human interactions, including context-aware decision-
making and adaptive social responses [57]. These virtual societies, powered by LLM agents, have
successfully exhibited emergent phenomena such as collective information diffusion and realistic
economic market dynamics [58; 59]. Further background on traditional agent-based modeling and
diverse applications of LLM agents can be found in Appendix A.
Despite these advancements, scaling LLM-based simulations to large populations remains compu-
tationally challenging. Most existing frameworks are limited to relatively small groups of agents
(often fewer than 1,000) due to significant computational overhead [60; 61]. Understanding emergent
collective behaviors, however, often requires simulations involving larger populations, highlighting
the critical need for scalable frameworks. Recent efforts have begun addressing these scalability
challenges, developing architectures capable of efficiently simulating large-scale agent societies using
parallel computation and modular designs [28; 32; 62; 63; 36].
However, significant challenges persist, particularly in efficiently orchestrating large-scale LLM
inference tasks and accurately representing complex societal dynamics at planetary scales [33]. Our
proposed framework, Light Society, contributes directly to this area by formalizing social processes
into structured state transitions governed by modular LLM-powered simulation operations, executed
efficiently through event-driven mechanisms.

Conclusion
We introduce Light Society, an efficient framework for simulating cognitively rich societies with
over one billion LLM-powered agents. By combining modular simulation operators with system-
level optimizations, Light Society enables efficient, high-fidelity modeling of social behaviors at
unprecedented scale.
Through large-scale trust and opinion propagation experiments, we demonstrate its ability to capture
both micro-level individual variation and macro-level emergent patterns. These results position Light
Society as a powerful tool for studying societal dynamics, hypothesis testing, and counterfactual
analysis.
Future work will explore richer memory and learning mechanisms, longitudinal behavior modeling,
and integration with real-world data to enable policy-relevant simulations and broader applications
across the human sciences.

A
Additional Related Work
A.1
From Rule-based Models to Autonomous Learning Agents
Agent-based modeling (ABM) has historically served as a foundational approach for exploring com-
plex societal phenomena through individual-level interactions. Early influential studies demonstrated
how simple behavioral rules could generate complex macro-level outcomes, such as residential
segregation arising from mild individual preferences [64] and cultural diversity emerging from local
interactions [65]. Later, more sophisticated ABMs like Sugarscape [66] expanded agent capabilities,
enabling richer simulations of social and economic phenomena. Similarly, Palmer et al.’s artifi-
cial stock market [67] employed adaptive agents to simulate realistic market dynamics, capturing
speculative bubbles and price fluctuations.
Despite their utility, traditional ABMs face inherent limitations. These include oversimplified agent
behaviors constrained by manually crafted rules [68], subjective parameter selection, and scalability
challenges when modeling large populations [69]. To address these limitations, reinforcement learning
(RL) approaches emerged as a promising direction, automating agent decision-making through
interactions with the environment. Notable advancements include simulations of user behaviors
within recommendation systems [70] and autonomous learning in complex gaming scenarios [71; 72].
Despite their impressive performance in structured tasks with clearly defined objectives, RL-based
models face significant challenges when extended to open-ended social simulations, primarily due
to difficulties in specifying appropriate reward functions that accurately reflect complex human
motivations and social interactions [73; 74; 75].
A.2
Expanded Applications and Multimodal Capabilities of LLM Agents
Beyond demonstrating emergent social phenomena, subsequent research has expanded the appli-
cations of LLM-based agents across diverse domains. For instance, studies have explored opinion
dynamics [76; 77], misinformation propagation [78; 79], geopolitical conflicts [80], and political
election forecasting [81], consistently demonstrating the versatility and realism of LLM-powered
simulations. Early studies also highlighted the potential of LLM agents to replicate classical experi-
ments from economics, psychology, and social sciences, demonstrating their capability to capture
intricate human-like decision patterns and biases [57].
Moreover, recent advances have integrated multimodal and embodied capabilities into LLM-based
agent simulations, further enhancing their realism and applicability. Multimodal agents can process
and respond to visual, auditory, and textual stimuli, enabling richer simulations of human behavior

Trust Game Experimental Details
B.1
Agent Profile Construction
All agent profiles used in the trust game simulations were derived from the publicly available World
Values Survey (WVS) Wave 7 dataset (2017–2022) [45]. The WVS offers broad demographic and
attitudinal data (Fig. 4), providing a globally representative empirical foundation for constructing
realistic agent personas. Following the methodology of WorldValuesBench [48], we extracted a wide
range of socio-demographic attributes, including geographic location, gender, age, migration status,
education, employment, income, religion, ethnicity, and subjective social class. To ensure consistency,
we excluded records with incomplete responses on core fields (e.g., gender, age), yielding a cleaned
dataset of 96,125 valid entries. Each cleaned WVS record was transformed into a natural language
agent profile for large language models. Profiles were written in the second person (“You are...”)
to facilitate character embodiment by the LLMs and included rich socio-demographic context. For
example:
You are a Female, 60 years old person. You live in AD: Andorra la Vella, Andorra. Your town
has a population of 20,000-50,000, is classified as a Capital city, is considered Urban. You
are an immigrant to this country (born outside this country) born in Spain. Your citizenship
status is “Not, I am not a citizen of this country”. Your native language is Spanish; Castilian.
Your ethnicity/race is AD: Caucasian white. You don’t have any religious beliefs. You are
Married and have 2 children. Your highest education level is Upper secondary education
(ISCED 3), your father’s education level is Upper secondary education (ISCED 3), your
mother’s education level is Upper secondary education (ISCED 3), your spouse’s education
level is Upper secondary education (ISCED 3). You are Full time (30 hours a week or more)
working as Sales (for example: sales manager, shop owner, shop assistant, insurance agent,
buyer) in the Private business or industry sector. Your spouse is Full time (30 hours a week
or more). Your father’s occupation was Semi-skilled worker (for example: bricklayer, bus
driver, cannery worker, carpenter, sheet metal worker, baker), your mother’s occupation was
Farm worker (for example: farm labourer, tractor driver). Your father is Immigrant born in
Spain. Your mother is Immigrant born in Spain. You are not the main income earner in your
household. Your financial situation: Spent some savings and borrowed money. You consider
yourself to be Lower middle class. On a scale of 1-10, You place your household income at
level 5.
