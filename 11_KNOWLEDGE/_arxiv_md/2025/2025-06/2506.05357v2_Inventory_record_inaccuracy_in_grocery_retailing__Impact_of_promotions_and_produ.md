Inventory record inaccuracy in grocery retailing: Impact of 
promotions and product perishability, and targeted effect of audits 
 
Abstract 
We report the results of a study to identify and quantify drivers of inventory record inaccuracy 
(IRI) in a grocery retailing environment, a context where products are often subject to promotion 
activity and a substantial share of items are perishable. The analysis covers ~24,000 stock 
keeping units (SKUs) sold in 11 stores. We find that IRI is positively associated with average 
inventory level, restocking frequency, and whether the item is perishable, and negatively 
associated with promotional activity. We also conduct a field quasi-experiment to assess the 
marginal effect of stockcounts on sales. While performing an inventory audit is found to lead to 
an 11% store-wide sales lift, the audit has heterogeneous effects with all the sales lift 
concentrated on items exhibiting negative IRI (i.e., where system inventory is greater than actual 
inventory). The benefits of inventory audits are also found to be more pronounced on perishable 
items, that are associated with higher IRI levels. Our findings inform retailers on the appropriate 
allocation of effort to improve IRI and reframes stock counting as a sales-increasing strategy 
rather than a cost-intensive necessity. 
Keywords: Inventory record inaccuracies; Empirical study; Quasi-experimental designs; Retail 
sector.

1. Introduction 
To manage inventory levels, companies usually employ software tools that use inventory and 
sales data to automatically forecast demand and replenish warehouses and store shelves. The 
global retail analytics market attained a size of around $7.4 billion in 2022 and is expected to 
reach $40.4 billion by 2032, growing at a compound annual growth rate of 18.6% during the 
forecast period (DataHorizon Research, 2023). The analytics market consists of both software 
and associated services. The key analytical software for supply chains within the retail sector is 
built with a focus on inventory management, supply and demand forecasting, and vendor 
management. Companies’ investments in inventory software are immense. 
One weakness of automatic inventory management systems though is that replenishments are 
triggered based on inventory levels recorded in the system, which can differ from the inventory 
available in the retail store or warehouse. Discrepancies between the actual and the recorded 
inventory level are termed Inventory Record Inaccuracies (IRI) (e.g., Chuang et al., 2016; Goyal 
et al., 2016), and they have been reported to greatly impact the retail sector; earlier research has 
found that between 20% and 70% of inventory records are incorrect (e.g., Miller, 1994; Kang 
and Gershwin, 2005; DeHoratius and Raman, 2008). 
If the inventory management system displays a stock level that is higher than the inventory level 
available (negative IRI), then inventory is replenished too late, which can lead to stockouts and 
the company not being able to satisfy customer demand (DeHoratius and Raman, 2008; Rekik et 
al., 2019). In the opposite case, where the available stock exceeds the recorded one (positive 
IRI), the system triggers unnecessary replenishments and extra supplies leading to unnecessarily 
high inventory carrying cost (Hardgrave et al., 2009; Chuang et al., 2016). IRI may generally

occur because of transaction errors, mistakes in supply, unknown stock loss (such as theft or 
spoilage) or misplaced products, among others (Kang and Gershwin, 2005; DeHoratius and 
Raman, 2008; Chuang and Oliva, 2015; Rekik et al., 2019).  
Regardless of whether inventory discrepancies are positive or negative, inaccurate inventory 
records prevent the (costly to install and operate) stock management system to unfold its full 
potential. Although it is clear that inaccurate inventory records will impact business in a negative 
way, little is known about the magnitude of this impact. Earlier research used simulation for 
estimating the impact of “inventory freezing”1 on sales (DeHoratius and Raman, 2008) or 
analyzed the sales improvement potential of stockcounts for a reduced set of SKUs (Chuang et 
al., 2016). Given the limited scope of earlier research on the sales impact of IRI, we evaluate 
how audits of the whole store (as opposed to counts of individual SKUs) impact sales and 
identify areas where the audits are more impactful.  
We also depart from the existing literature by concentrating our analysis on grocery retailing, 
i.e., on an environment where products are often subjected to promotions and where a substantial 
share of items is perishable. We argue that the IRI problem is especially relevant in situations 
where products are perishables as perishability entails additional material handling (e.g., more 
frequent replenishments, sorting the shelf to encourage First-In-First-Out usage) that may lead to 
stock discrepancies. Promotions, however, present an intriguing case as they increase sales – a 
well-established cause of IRI (DeHorations and Raman, 2008) – but also require additional

We report our work with a major European grocery retailer undertaken in the context of a wider 
collaboration between the authors and various retailers organized in the Efficient Consumer 
Response (ECR) Retail Loss Group. The project commissioned by ECR aimed at a descriptive 
analysis of the IRI problem in the retail sector, and, more importantly, at understanding the store-
wide implications on sales of fixing IRI. In this paper, we focus on one major grocery retailer, 
and assess the prevalence of IRI and the main drivers based on a sample from 11 of their stores. 
We then perform a field quasi-experiment to assess the impact of stockcounts on store-wide 
performance.  
We find that about 65% of the SKUs analyzed are affected by IRI. The median magnitude of IRI 
for the affected SKUs is  +4 and -3 units (+30% and -33%), for positive and negative 
discrepancies, respectively. We find that, after controlling for the major SKU level drivers 
identified by DeHoratious and Raman (2008), a SKU’s IRI is positively associated with its 
average inventory level, its restocking frequency, and whether the item is perishable, and 
negatively associated with its promotional activity. Through a quasi-experiment we also find that 
performing audits to correct such inaccuracies leads to approximately 11% of increased store-
wide sales in the first two months after the stock count. More interestingly, we find that, 
controlling for sales levels and other factors, the stockcount has heterogeneous effects on SKUs 
with all the sales lift concentrated on items exhibiting negative IRI and that perishable items, that 
are more propense to exhibit IRI, benefit more from those audits than non-perishable items. We 
believe these findings will be of great value to retailers to inform their decisions on the 
appropriate levels of investment that they should put against improving inventory record

2. Literature review 
One of the most important objectives in retailing is to ensure high on-shelf availability of 
products. If customers are after an item that is not available for sale, they may decide to buy it 
elsewhere, or they may abandon their shopping trip altogether, negatively affecting the sales of 
other items as well. If stockouts occur frequently, store reputation may suffer, and customers 
may switch to another brand or store, jeopardizing future sales (Corsten and Gruen, 2003; 
Ehrenthal and Stölzle, 2010; Goyal et al., 2016). This is particularly problematic in the grocery 
retail context. Grocery shopping has been described as a habitual process, with customers often 
following routines in terms of shopping hours, store visits and products purchased (East et al., 
1994; Kim and Park, 1997). Routine shoppers face high costs if they switch stores, so they tend 
to leave out-of-stock items out or to buy alternative items. If stockouts occur frequently or for an 
extended period, then customers may switch stores as well (Campo et al., 2004). As previously 
discussed, one cause of stockouts may be inaccurate inventory records, although the transition is


## Page 7


7
not necessarily linear nor obvious (Chuang et al. 2022). In the case of negative IRI where the 
inventory system overestimates the available stock, the system orders too late, which may result 
in a stockout the system is not even aware of (Ehrenthal and Stölzle, 2010). 
Inaccurate inventory records have attracted quite some attention over the years, with researchers 
trying to obtain insights both into the sources and the extent of IRI across different sectors. An 
early work is the one of Rinehart (1960), who studied a supply facility of the US Federal 
Government. He found that around 80% of the discrepancies were caused by the discrepancy 
correction procedures themselves. Kang and Gershwin (2005) analyzed inventory data collected 
at multiple stores of a global retailer and found that the share of SKUs affected by IRI ranged 
between 20% and 70%, with an average of 49%. Ishfaq and Raja (2020a, 2020b) analyzed audit 
data of a US apparel retailer and found that about 80% of the stock records were accurate over 
several subsequent stockcounts. Negative discrepancies were more frequent and extensive than 
positive discrepancies.  
DeHoratius and Raman (2008) also studied the drivers of IRI by analyzing stocktake data 
collected at 37 stores of a major US retailer. The authors analyzed the distribution of errors, both 
within and across stores, and identified factors that mitigate IRI. The most important IRI drivers 
they found are an item’s annual selling quantity, its cost, and the frequency of stock audits. They 
further found that 65% of the inventory records they analyzed were inaccurate. The absolute 
difference between the stock on shelf and the inventory record was five units on average per 
SKU, or 35% of the average number of units found on the shelf for the SKU. IRI has also been 
investigated in contexts other than retail stores, such as in manufacturing warehouses (e.g., 
Meyer, 1990; Sheppard and Brown, 1993) or retail distribution centres (e.g., Kull et al., 2013; 
Barratt et al., 2018). Although IRI has been found to be prevalent in these contexts too, its


## Page 8


8
behavior is likely different due to the absence of a major driver of inventory discrepancies: the 
customer. 
In addition to identifying the sources and the extent of IRI, researchers have also studied the 
effectiveness of different measures in reducing IRI. Several papers (e.g., Hardgrave et al., 2009; 
2013; Bertolini et al., 2015; Goyal et al., 2016) investigated how Radio Frequency Identification 
(RFID) technology can contribute to lowering IRI. These studies showed that using RFID during 
stockcounts or for tracking stock movements can lead to a substantial decrease in IRI, but that it 
cannot alleviate the problem completely. Chuang and Oliva (2015) investigated the influence of 
store staffing levels and operational performance on IRI. Their results indicated that backroom 
and shelf shrinkage errors are the dominant drivers of IRI. Under-shelving, together with 
checkout and data capture errors, have a much smaller impact on IRI than shrinkage. In an 
empirical analysis of longitudinal data from five stores of a global retail chain, the researchers 
found evidence that full-time labor reduces IRI, whereas part-time labor fails to alleviate it. 
DeHoratius et al. (2023) evaluated different methods for generating a list of items to be counted 
in the store each morning to correct IRI. The authors differentiated between ‘rule-based’ and 
‘model-based’ policies, where the first type of policies sorts items by indices based on some 
operational metrics, while the second type relies on error and inventory models adopted from the 
literature. For each performance metric the authors considered, they found one rule-based policy 
that did not perform worse than the best model-based one, which is interesting for practitioners 
given the relative simplicity of rule-based approaches. 
While several researchers have investigated how retail stockouts may affect sales (see Gruen et 
al., 2002, for an overview), only a few works have addressed the impact of IRI on sales. Ton and 
Raman (2010) studied how so-called phantom products, i.e., products that are physically present


## Page 9


9
in the store, but only in areas where customers cannot find them, influence sales. The authors 
found that a one standard deviation increase in the percentage of phantom products led to a 1% 
decrease in store sales. Phantom products are not necessarily connected to IRI; that is, the 
inventory records in the system could be accurate, but some of these items – the phantom 
products – could be misplaced and not accessible to customers. In the context of our analysis, the 
fraction of phantom products not appropriately recorded in the stock management system would 
be regarded as positive inventory discrepancy. DeHoratius and Raman (2008) used empirical 
data to conduct a simulation study on the influence of “frozen products” on sales. They estimated 
that inventory freezing can result in a loss of sales of about 1%. The authors hypothesized that 
their estimate is only a lower bound on the true sales loss and called for further empirical 
research in this area. The work that probably comes closest to our investigation is the one of 
Chuang et al. (2016), who developed a model for predicting out-of-shelf events based on 
consecutive zero sales-situations. In a field experiment, they used their model to trigger audits of 
eight different SKUs that suffered from inventory freezing and found that the intervention had a 
positive effect on sales. 
Our investigation departs from, and contributes to, the extant literature in three ways. We map 
each of these departures onto elements of the typology developed by Makadok et al. (2018) that 
sets out the ways in which research contributes  to theory. First, we evaluate drivers of inaccurate 
inventory records in a grocery retail environment, i.e., in the presence of perishable products and 
product promotions. Earlier research has not yet investigated how product perishability and 
discounts interact with IRI. With respect to our theoretical contribution here, we introduce a new 
construct and variables as the focal phenomenon. Secondly, we evaluate, through an intervention 
study, the impact of a store-wide stockcount on sales. The store-wide assessment of the


## Page 10


10
stockcount allows us to empirically assess heterogenous effects of the audit on products with 
different attributes and different accuracy status. In so doing, theoretically, we introduce a new 
level of analysis for the effects of audits. Finally, through a dynamic evaluation of the effects of 
the intervention, we trace the evolution of benefit post stockcount. In developing the scholarship 
in this way, our work offers the possibility for future research into the appropriate timing of 
interventions to correct the problem, and as such the opportunity to make new theoretical 
contributions. 
3. Empirical and theoretical background 
3.1. Research setting and data collection 
We worked together with a major European grocery retailer. The partner organization is a higher 
end supermarket, known for the quality of their products and customer service. They operate 
stores throughout the UK and in 2022 had an annual sales volume of approximately £7 billion.  
In addition to regular groceries, they sell specialty products, including a wide range of own brand 
products, and most stores have a bakery, cheese, fish, and meat counters. They also sell 
household goods, gifts, and clothes. With more than 300 shops in the UK, the company is one of 
the largest retailers of groceries in the country, also exporting products to many other countries, 
in some of which they have physical presence.  
The company employs a third party to conduct periodic stockcounts at the store level up to four 
times a year. The timing of stockcounts differs in-between stores and depends on a set of metrics, 
such as self-service penetration, shrinkage and theft exposure, or stock turnover. For the 
purposes of this work, however, the stockcounts were organized around our research design 
rather than triggered by the above metrics.


## Page 11

Prior to the actual stockcounts, a preparation team provided by the third party prepares the stores 
for stocktaking by tidying both the backroom and the shop floor (in this step, easy-to-spot 
misplaced items are identified and brought back to their correct locations). The actual 
stockcounts then start in the backroom and continue on the shop floor in slow selling areas just 
before the store closes. The remaining parts of the store are counted after shop opening hours. 
Working closely with product and sales analysts of the retailer, a total of 11 stores were selected 
as representative of the different store types operated by them. The 11 stores under study have 
different capabilities (ecommerce, self-checkout service, car parking) and different sizes (ranging 
from 6,663 to 32,032 square feet), different location types (urban, suburban, city centre, rural 
town) and different numbers of suppliers (ranging from 55 to 351 suppliers). The big majority of 
those stores are core type outlets selling general grocery merchandise and some of them are 
convenience stores focusing on a limited range of everyday products. All stores were ultimately 
randomly chosen, except for stores 1 and 2 where we involved the retailer in the matching 
between the stores. This pair of stores will be used in a difference-in-differences analysis in §5 to 
assess the benefit of the stockcounts and the effects of IRI on sales. Details of the selected stores

3.2. Measures and descriptive analysis 
First, we examine the stockcount reports of the 11 stores discussed in the previous sub-section. 
Such examination will inform the analysis of the IRI drivers to be discussed in §4.  
The stock audit data set provides two quantities: the ‘system record’ defined as the inventory 
stock record shown in the database before the audit takes place, and ‘counted record’ defined as 
the physical stock counted during the stock audit.2 Comparing these two quantities, we define the 
inventory record inaccuracy with the variable 𝐼𝑅𝐼 measuring the discrepancy between the 
counted record and the system record (taken as the difference of the former minus the latter). We 
also derive the variable 𝐴𝐵𝑆_𝐼𝑅𝐼 measuring the absolute value of the IRI measure. 
In addition to the data related to the physical audits conducted in all 11 stores, we consider all the 
inbound (replenishment) and outbound (point of sales) stock movements that took place between 
the latest stock audit where results are reported and IRIs are measured, and the immediately 
preceding one.

3.3. Development of hypotheses  
This section develops hypotheses that will be tested in subsequent sections of this paper. Our 
first objective is to identify the main operational drivers of IRI within a grocery retail store. We 
leverage the large sample study performed by DeHoratius and Raman (2008) (DHR) where they 
identified IRI drivers of ~10,000 different SKUs across 37 stores of a single retailer. In addition 
to the differences in shopping behavior outlined in Section 2, two major factors differentiate the 
grocery retailing investigated here from the sample analyzed in the DHR study. First, the 
presence of perishable products. Perishable products affect not only the frequency of 
replenishments, but also add operational complexity as shelves require much stricter First-In-
First-Out (FIFO) arrangements, and expired products need to be removed from the shelves and 
the inventory records (van Donselaar et al., 2006). Second, grocery sales are often enticed 
through promotions (Blattberg et al., 1995; Ailawadi et al., 2009), and we expect those 
promotions to also have an impact on IRI. In the remainder of this section, we develop the 
hypotheses of interest to our research. We control for the main drivers of IRI identified by DHR 
in our statistical testing. 
The amount of inventory kept in a retail store has been identified as a possible source of IRI. 
Earlier research has found that inventory density, a storewide variable that measures the number 
of total units stored in a retail store relative to the size of the sales area, has a negative effect on 
the accuracy of inventory records (DeHoratius and Raman, 2008). Crowded shelves, and smaller 
gaps on the shelves the store employees must fill, introduce complexity that may result in items 
not being assigned to the correct shelf positions. In addition, a high number of items on the shelf 
for any given SKU may also make it difficult for store employees to maintain an overview of the 
shelf layout, leading to product misplacements not being noticed. Furthermore, if items arrive at


## Page 18


18
the store in case packs, and if items need to be removed from such packs for being stored on the 
shelves with eventual leftovers being sent to the backroom, an incorrect treatment of individual 
units and case packs in the inventory information system can trigger IRI (Eroglu et al., 2011; 
Best et al., 2022). Due to the often-non-intuitive layout of backrooms in retail stores, large 
inventory levels may slow down shelf replenishments and cause IRI (Gruen et al., 2002; Goyal et 
al., 2016). Based on the above discussion, we formulate the following hypothesis: 
H1: IRI is positively associated with the average stock level of an item. 
A second IRI driver that has been discussed in the literature is the replenishment frequency. High 
replenishment frequencies result in frequent interactions with the products and stock records. 
Earlier research has shown that the more frequently store employees interfere with the stock 
records – be it in the form of manual corrections or (sales) transactions – the more extensive the 
IRI problem gets (Rinehart, 1960; Rekik et al., 2019). Shelf replenishments are also often 
conducted in-between other tasks (Aastrub and Kotzab, 2009), with frequent replenishments and 
the associated time pressure increasing the chance of errors. It is therefore not surprising that 
store replenishments have been identified as an important source of item out-of-stocks (e.g., 
Corsten and Gruen, 2003; Aastrub and Kotzab, 2009). While earlier research has hypothesized 
that the replenishment frequency may trigger IRI (Ishfaq and Raja, 2020a, 2020b), the 
relationship has never been tested empirically. We therefore formulate the following hypothesis 
H2.  
H2: IRI is positively associated with the replenishment frequency. 
Note that the average inventory level and the replenishment frequency are interrelated: if the 
retailer decides to reduce the level of inventory kept in the store, the store needs to be


## Page 19


19
replenished more frequently. The impact of the average inventory level (and the replenishment 
frequency) both on cost and IRI should be considered in determining order quantities for the 
retail store. Further, note that both product perishability and promotions can potentially shift the 
otherwise-optimal order quantity and frequency as the first affects how long inventory can be 
safely held and the second has the potential to artificially accelerate the purchase rate.  
As discussed earlier, perishability introduces a set of operational challenges within the store 
(e.g., Ketzenberg and Ferguson, 2008; Manikas and Terry, 2010; Akkas et al., 2019). Perishable 
products approaching their expiration date may have to be marked down or be subjected to 
special discounts (e.g., “take three, pay two”) to accelerate sales before the item expires (van 
Donselaar et al., 2006; Yassin and Soares, 2021). During shelf replenishments, products need to 
be rotated within the shelves to encourage that products that arrived first, or expire first, are sold 
with priority (Akkas, 2019). Finally, expired products need to be removed from the shelves, and 
these movements need to be reflected in the inventory information system. These interactions 
with the products stored on the shelves introduce various error sources that may result in 
inaccurate inventory records (Chuang and Oliva, 2015). Thus, we hypothesize: 
H3: IRI is positively associated with perishability. 
Grocery retailing is highly competitive (e.g., Colla, 2004; Cardinali and Bellini, 2014). As a 
result, grocery retailers frequently rely on promotions either directly by the retailer, or in 
conjunction with plans from the manufacturer (examples are price discounts, store displays, 
discount coupons or store flyers; see Ailawadi et al., 2009, Srinivasan et al., 2004, Tokar et al., 
2011). It is not intuitively clear what the impact of these promotions on IRI will be. On one hand, 
promotions lead to a demand shock with a substantial increase in customers entering the store, 
stock movement and sales (Blattberg et al., 1995; Lam et al., 2001; Tokar et al., 2011). This


## Page 20


20
effect is stronger in grocery retailing that relies on regular promotions as opposed to the 
clearance promotions more common in fashion retailing (Lam et al. 2001). The expected higher 
sales also often lead to higher inventory levels for the products affected by the promotion 
(Aastrup and Kotzab, 2009). We expect that the higher inventory levels as well as the extra stock 
movement and sales caused by a promotion – that all occur in a relatively short period – will 
have a detrimental effect on the accuracy of inventory records. On the other hand, the extra 
scrutiny required to match sales to coupons to justify reimbursement or to monitor expiration 
days and reshelving of promoted products, could reduce IRI. We therefore propose two 
competing hypotheses to be tested empirically: 
H4a: IRI is positively associated with promotional activity. 
H4b: IRI is negatively associated with promotional activity.  
4. Drivers of IRI in grocery stores 
To make our work consistent with, and be able to contribute to, the existing IRI literature – e.g., 
Fleisch and Tellkamp (2005), DeHoratius and Raman (2008), Hardgrave et al. (2013) – we use 
as a base dependent variable for this section the absolute value of IRI. While positive and 
negative IRI have different cost implications, we found no significant differences on their 
respective drivers. Accordingly, we focus our analysis on the effects of drivers on the absolute 
value of IRI. As a robustness test, we also replicate the analysis using a percentual measurement 
of IRI.

4.1. Controls and variables transformation 
In their study, DeHoratius and Raman (2008) (DHR) find that IRI is positively associated with 
the selling quantity of an item (h1) and negatively associated with the frequency of audits (h2), 
the cost of an item (h3), and the dollar volume of an item (h4).4 DHR also find drivers at the 
store level, namely, that IRI is associated with the distribution structure used (h5), and that it is 
positively associated with the product density (h6) and the product variety (h7) within the store. 
We exclude from our study the storewide elements identified by DHR (h5-h7) as our sample 
does not permit sufficient variance across stores. We control for all time invariant store 
characteristics through a store-fixed effect in our statistical models. As such, our research 
focuses on SKU-level attributes as drivers of IRI as opposed to the mixed approach followed by 
DHR that had enough store variance to capture store-level attributes. Finally, it should be noted 
that our research partner does not perform partial audits of targeted SKUs, relying instead on 
storewide stockcounts. Thus, our measures of frequency of testing and the store-fixed effects are 
collinear. We explicitly control for this in the estimation section. 
As shown in Table 2, several variables have a standard deviation higher than their mean. Afifi 
and Clark (1997) recommend transforming such variables to induce normality. Like DHR, we 
transform several of these variables (ABS_IRI, STOCK, REPLEN, QUANTITY, DAYS, PRICE) to 
their natural log, adding a unit to the value of these variables prior to their transformation to 
avoid applying the natural log to a measure equal to zero.

5. Do audits improve sales? 
We now turn our attention to the extent to which IRI monetarily matters in retailing. Do audits 
and inventory record reconciliations improve sales? To evaluate this question, we performed a 
quasi-experimental intervention study (Sadish et al., 2002), comparing the performance of two 
similar stores where the ‘treatment’ consisted of a store-wide stocktake in one of the stores, 
approximately six months after an initial stocktake in both stores. We were particularly interested 
in assessing whether the re-ordering and cleaning of the store had an impact on aggregate store 
sales. As discussed above, this experimental design also allowed us to assess heterogeneous 
effects of audits on products depending on their attributes or their accuracy state. The matching 
of the stores was undertaken by the company, following discussions with the research team, to 
ensure parallel trends on sales performance and as close a match as possible in terms of size, 
location, number of SKUs, and organizational capabilities. Specifically, store #1 in Table 1 
served as our treatment store, and store #2 served as a control.  
Sales and inventory data were tracked for two months before and after the treatment. This 
experimental design enabled us to observe the effects of correcting the inventory records (at the 
test store) versus not fixing the inventory records (at the control store). For our analysis, we only 
used SKUs sold before and after the stock count treatment in both, the test and control stores. 
This resulted in a sample of 5,657 directly comparable SKUs, representing 95% and 97% of the 
total number of SKUs in the treatment and control stores respectively. We use the monthly data 
aggregation following previous work in retail operations (e.g., Ton and Raman, 2010; Kesavan et

al., 2014; Chuang and Oliva, 2015). We present our analysis in sales revenue, but all results are 
consistent when performing the analysis measuring sales in units.

the higher-level fixed effects. Although incorporating nested random effects for category and

6. Conclusion 
Achieving retail growth in mature markets is not easy. Brick-and-mortar retailers face an 
intensive competition from ecommerce retailers today and see themselves confronted with 
customers that are highly demanding in terms of product availability, customer service, and 
price. Grocery retailing is particularly challenging, as it involves perishable products that require 
exceptional handling to avoid spoilage as well as the removal and disposition of obsolete stock. 
Competition and product perishability also force grocery retailers to rely on promotions, both as 
a means to avoid the spoilage of products and to induce customers to enter the store. Additional 
interactions with the products that results from perishability and promotions may influence 
inventory record accuracy. Against this background, this paper studied the prevalence of IRI and 
attempted to identify its drivers. 
We used data from a leading UK grocery retailer and found that, while controlling for previously 
identified drivers of IRI – sales volume, price, and time since last audit – IRI is positively 
associated with the average inventory level, the restocking frequency, and perishability. This is 
somewhat expected as more handling and exceptions in the movement of items are more likely


## Page 33


33
to introduce record-keeping errors (Chuang and Oliva, 2015). Surprisingly, we found that the 
promotional activity of an item is associated with lower IRI. We posit that this is the result of the 
closer operational scrutiny required to prepare and manage the promotions. 
In the same context, we performed a field intervention study to assess the impact of inventory 
audits on sales. We found that audits and their associated corrections and cleaning and tidying up 
activities, led to an 11% store-wide sales uplift. This study provides additional evidence for the 
argument that stock audits can be an important lever for increasing sales (e.g., Chuang et al., 
2016; Chuang et al., 2022) and suggests a required change in management perceptions on 
counting, as stocktakes should no longer be perceived as a necessary evil.  
More interestingly, we found evidence that the full benefit of the audit, as measured in sales lift, 
is concentrated on the items with negative IRI at the time of the audit, with no discernable impact 
on items with positive IRI (𝛾+ 𝛿𝑝 in Model 4) or those items that were audited but not corrected 
(𝛾 in Model 4). We also found that perishable items, that are associated with higher IRI levels, 
also benefit more from the audits than non-perishable products. These results suggest that the 
benefits of accurate records could be achieved without the need to perform store-wide 
stockcounts, focusing instead on higher-frequency audits targeted on perishable items and/or 
SKUs with propensity to negative IRI. While we did not have access to the cost of performing an 
audit, the method described here allows to quantify the benefits of an audit and help managers 
make a more informed decision on the timing and intensity of future audits. 
The above findings suggest two potential areas for future research. First, a better understanding 
of the drivers of IRI is needed. DeHoratius and Raman (2008) provided first insights into the 
problem. We expanded this work by identifying drivers specific to the grocery environment (in 
particular, perishability and promotions). Both studies, however, are correlational and further


## Page 34


34
research is needed to identify the root causes of IRI. One promising approach in this context is 
machine learning that has performed well in identifying patterns that explain the variation in a 
variable of interest (see Chou et al. 2022 for examples and discussion of the use of machine 
learning for pattern identification and theory development). Only with causal explanations of IRI 
will we be able to quantify and address the operational issues causing it, allowing us to reduce its 
incidence. Developing full account of causal mechanisms, however, remains a significant 
challenge as IRI events are not directly observable and we can only detect and report them post 
facto (Chuang et al., 2022). Nevertheless, further studies with broader samples and over longer 
periods of time should allow testing of additional potential drivers (even in a correlational sense) 
and reveal insights that would help develop stockcount strategies to better direct resources to 
those products that offer the highest sales improvement potential. Given that stockcounts are 
labor- and consequently cost-intensive in many sectors, retailers could contrast the cost of 
auditing a particular product category with the sales improvement potential of that group of 
SKUs. The result could be selective stockcounts with a larger audit frequency for items suffering 
particularly from inaccurate stock records.  
The second area of future research suggested by our findings is to explore the timing of the 
audits for the targeted group of SKUs. This study presented some evidence of the dynamics of 
IRI corrections on sales with the timing effects in Models 2, 3, and 4. The results, however, are 
difficult to interpret as sales are also affected by other time-dependent factors (e.g., demand 
seasonality, holidays, promotional activity). If, as suggested by our results, the audit uplift on 
sales is caused by a correction of ‘frozen’ products – or a situation where inventory is so low that 
it limits sales – then, perhaps a more promising strategy would be to assess the evolution of the 
magnitude of IRI. Chuang et al. (2022) used empirical data to estimate the decay of stock


## Page 35


35
records, from accurate to inaccurate, and developed an inspection policy that minimizes the 
combined cost of performing the audits and the expected cost of inventory inaccuracies for a 
group of SKUs. Their policy, however, is based on an expectation of when an item will become 
inaccurate and an aggregation of the error magnitudes. Our findings suggest that further 
precision in the description of the evolution of the magnitude of IRI has the potential for more 
targeted auditing policies and further cost reductions.  
Like any empirical study, this work has limitations. In our case, the quasi-experimental study 
was limited to only one pair of stores of a single retailer. Further tests need to be done to verify 
whether stockcounts can have a generally positive effect on sales by decluttering the store and 
the directionality and scale of the heterogenous effects detected here. Discussions the authors had 
with retail experts prior to conducting the study indicated that this may be the case in practice. 
The quasi-experimental structure proposed in this paper can easily be adopted by retailers to 
verify the sales improvement potential of a correction in stock records for their own operations.
