Challenges in Grounding Language in the Real World

Figure 1: A Collaborative System that combines the memory and reasoning

Abstract 
A long-term goal of Artificial Intelligence is to build a language 
understanding system that allows a human to collaborate with a physical 
robot using language that is natural to the human. In this paper we 
highlight some of the challenges in doing this, and propose a solution that 
integrates the abilities of a cognitive agent capable of interactive task 
learning in a physical robot with the linguistic abilities of a large language 
model. We also point the way to an initial implementation of this approach.


## Page 2


1. Introduction 
A long-term goal of Artificial Intelligence research is to develop autonomous robotic 
agents that can act as collaborative assistants to a human in physical, real-world 
environments (Nilsson, 1984). A key capability of such an agent is to understand 
instructions from its human partner in language that is as natural as possible to the 
human. This paper outlines some of the challenges in implementing such a capability, 
then proposes an approach that combines the strengths of an intelligent agent based 
on a cognitive architecture with knowledge it can extract and verify from a large 
language model (LLM). Finally, we describe a research program to test the viability of 
such a combined system. 
Figure 1 is a sketch of the type of system we envision. A human supervisor 
communicates with a cognitive agent. That agent controls a robotic actor and maintains 
knowledge of the situation and the environment. It uses requests to an LLM to help it 
understand the human’s natural language, and gather general world knowledge. Our 
premise is that this combined system can perform better in assisting the human than 
either the cognitive agent by itself or an LLM by itself. 
2. The Language Grounding Problem 
Humans are accustomed to using language to communicate with each other, including 
about real-world activities that a robotic physical assistant would be expected to 
participate in. There are several challenges in building an agent that can interact with 
a human partner using language as natural to the human as possible and act correctly 
in the world based on those interactions (Bansal et al., 2024). Consider several specific 
issues. 
2.1 
Identifying Objects 
A human may refer to objects in the environment in many different ways. We have 
identified seven aspects of linguistic expressions that can be used to identify specific 
objects in a scene when combined with knowledge about the objects and the scene. 
1. Category – Usually a head noun, such as drawer or spoon. These can be resolved 
given a list of object representations that include a category identifier, along with a 
mapping of the given noun onto a category identifier. 
2. Physical properties – Adjectives such as red, large, or square. These can make further 
selection from the list of objects if the representations contain information about these 
properties. 
3. Deictic properties – References based on pointing or other gestures, such as this, that, 
or the one over there. Resolving these requires knowledge of the human’s pointing 
gestures relative to the objects in the environment.


## Page 3


4. Spatial relations – Many prepositions, such as in, on, to the left of, above, below, etc. 
refer to spatial relations. Resolving these requires knowledge of both the physical 
positions of objects in the scene and ways to calculate each possible linguistic reference 
based on these positions. 
5. Positional properties – Many terms such as top, bottom, or middle also require 
knowledge of object positions and how to calculate whether a given object satisfies the 
given condition. 
6. Functional properties – Another class of language expressions identify an object based 
on how it is used. Examples include the knife drawer, a dessert spoon, or the cabinet 
where we keep the plates. Resolving these requires knowledge of the situation, the kinds 
of tasks to be performed, what objects are needed for them, and preferences of the 
humans involved. 
7. Discourse properties – Often a person in an ongoing dialog will refer to an object based 
on previous references in that dialog, such as the knife meaning the knife previously 
used to slice the bread. Resolving these references requires detailed knowledge of the 
dialog history. 
2.2 
Understanding Action Verbs in Context 
Consider the following sentences using the verb cook and what they may mean in 
context. 
1. Cook. – This could be a command to turn on the microwave to cook an object already 
in it. 
2. Cook the potato in the microwave. – This describes a task that, in addition to the low-
level cook operation, involves finding the potato and putting it in the microwave. 
3. Cook the potato. – Here the task is more complicated. The agent must either know 
from prior knowledge or engage in dialog to find out what manner of cooking is desired 
using what kind of instrument. 
4. Cook an egg. – This example emphasizes the fact that how to cook depends on what 
kind of object is to be cooked, and the fact that there are many ways to cook an egg. It 
could be hard-boiled, soft-boiled, scrambled, fried, over easy, poached, etc. 
5. Cook dinner. – My wife will understand this sentence, as long as I say it as Could you 
please cook dinner? Our agent, however, will need a lot of background situational 
knowledge to know how to perform this complex task. 
In general, the kinds of knowledge needed to perform a complex task labeled with 
the verb cook include at least: who is being cooked for, what kinds of objects are to be 
cooked, which of several possible results is desired for an item like an egg, what 
ingredients are required, what instrument(s) and what method(s) are needed for each 
type of cooking, where to find the ingredients and the instruments, and what the 
personal preferences of the people involved are.

2.3 
Developing a Common Ground 
A large part of understanding natural language and being able to act on it involves 
having common knowledge between the speaker and the hearer. For our artificial agent, 
this will require the ability to learn situational knowledge gradually and incrementally 
through its ongoing interactions with a human. This would include the sorts of 
knowledge mentioned above in describing referring expressions and verbs in context. 
2.4 
Understanding Free-form Language 
Humans often speak in ways that are ambiguous, without situational and dialog 
context, are not formally grammatical, leave out assumed common knowledge, and don’t 
state intentions clearly. We would like our agent to be able to deal with at least many of 
these natural variations of human speech rather than having to teach the human how 
to speak a restricted language understood by the agent. 
3. Related Work 
The problem of grounding language consists of mapping linguistic expressions to objects 
and actions in the physical world, and is fundamental to building intelligent cognitive 
agents (Williams et al., 2009; Harnad, 1990). Without grounding, words are only 
symbols, and their meanings are only defined in terms of other symbols such as in 
LLMs. Instead, words need to connect to and be grounded to real-world sensory and 
motor experiences  for natural language understanding (Barsalou, 2008).  Early efforts 
in language grounding were Winograd’s (1972) SHRDLU system, that mapped natural 
language to objects and actions in a simulated blocks world using predefined symbolic 
representations.  SHRDLU understands and executes commands like put the red block 
on the green cube. Although SHRDLU demonstrated how language could be grounded 
in a limited environment, it is unable to generalize to new environments and ambiguous 
language. 
Subsequent research on language grounding has focused on grounding single 
words such as nouns, adjectives, and prepositions, as well as referring expressions that 
combine these elements in realistic environments. For instance, Roy and Pentland 
(2002) built a system where a robot learned the meanings of words like red and ball by 
connecting spoken words with what it saw. Tellex et al. (2011) introduced a probabilistic 
grounding graph that allowed robots to map spatial prepositions like on, next to, or 
under to actual locations in their environment. A robot system called  INGRESS 
(Shridhar & Hsu, 2018) grounds expressions like the red cup on the left by generating 
descriptions for each object and comparing them to the input instruction. It handles 
both object attributes (like color and type) and spatial relations (like on the left of), and 
can ask clarification questions when unsure. However, it is limited to small, 
unconstrained environments where the set of possible referents is limited.  
Grounding verbs is  more difficult, as it requires understanding dynamic actions 
and their effects in context (see Cohen et al., 2024 for a survey on language grounding 
in robotics). Many approaches use parsers to transform natural language instructions

Another challenge in grounding language is the establishment of a common 
ground—the shared knowledge and assumptions between the human and the agent 
about the environment and the task (Clark  & Brennan, 1991). Humans rely heavily on 
common ground to communicate efficiently, using context-dependent expressions like 
put it over there (a deictic reference) or do the same as before (depends on dialog history). 
Without a model of shared context, an agent is likely to misinterpret such language. 
Lindes et al. (2017) and Thomason et al. (2015) both explored dialogue-based 
approaches where agents incrementally build common ground through an interactive 
conversation with a human. 
Recently, researchers are using machine learning and deep learning techniques 
to address grounding in more dynamic, realistic, and complex environments. The 
ALFRED benchmark introduced by Shridhar et al. (2020) enabled learning from 
instruction-action pairs in simulated home environments. Agents learn to interpret 
sequences of high-level instructions and translate them into low-level actions. Similarly, 
Kirk et al. (2024) introduced methods for grounding ambiguous instructions using 
perceptual context and visual feedback. More recently, large language models (LLMs) 
are  used to improve agents’ ability to process a wide range of natural language (Ahn et 
al., 2022, Chen et al., 2020). For example, Ahn et al. (2022) used LLMs to map natural 
instructions to action sequences in real-world robot tasks in their SayCan framework. 
These models can bridge the gap between informal language and structured 
representations. However, LLMs often operate without real-time grounding in the 
physical environment and lack mechanisms for maintaining common ground over time. 
Lawley and MacClellan (2024) report on an agentic LLM system with similar goals to 
ours, but it lacks the abilities of a full cognitive agent. 
4. Using an LLM to Gather Knowledge 
A large language model (LLM) plays an essential part in the approach we are suggesting. 
It serves two primary purposes: as a source of general or common-sense knowledge, and 
as a tool to translate free-form language from a human into either simpler language or 
a formalism that the agent can understand and act on. In this section we explore briefly 
the idea of gathering general or common-sense knowledge from an LLM. 
 
Previous work (Kirk et al., 20241, 2023) has explored using LLMs to extract 
knowledge needed for learning novel tasks. Figure 2 gives a specific example of asking 
an LLM a high level question about how to prepare scrambled eggs.

not explicit in the language. Thus, we need another layer of translation to understand 
not only the free-form language that a human collaborator might use, but also to 
understand the free-form language that an LLM typically produces to answer a query 
about general or common-sense knowledge.

5. Using an LLM to Understand Free-form Language 
To explore the space of the ways humans use language to describe and teach tasks, a 
dataset of such human instructions would make a good reference. The ALFRED dataset 
(Shridhar et al., 2020) has over 8,000 “demonstrations” of a robot in the AI2-Thor 
simulator performing one of seven types of tasks. Amazon turk “annotators” observed 
videos of the agent performing each task, and were asked to write both a single sentence 
describing the goal of the task and a detailed set of instructions based on the video to 
tell the agent how to perform the task. Each demonstration has such annotations made 
by three different human subjects.

6. Our Proposed Approach 
The approach we propose is to build an AI system centered on an embodied cognitive 
agent that interacts with both a human collaborator and an LLM, learning incrementally 
the situational knowledge needed to act autonomously in a complex environment (see 
Figure 1). This section describes the main components of our proposed system and how 
they can work together. Section 7 suggests a possible implementation. 
6.1 
Capabilities of a Cognitive Agent 
Here we define a cognitive agent as an instance of a cognitive architecture with internal 
memories for knowledge of its situation, a way to control the perception and action of a 
physical robot, a means of communicating with a human in natural language, and a 
connection to make queries of an LLM such as ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2023). 
Such an agent has the abilities to: 1) perceive the physical world, 2) perform 
primitive actions, 3) understand limited or formal language, 4) remember situational 
knowledge, 5) learn from experience, 6) do planning and reasoning, 7) have situational 
awareness, 8) orchestrate interactions, and 9) remember its history. 
6.2 
Capabilities of a Large Language Model 
Large language models, on the other hand, have complementary abilities to: 1) handle 
free-form natural language, 2) ground NL to a given context, 3) translate NL to a formal 
representation, 4) provide general world knowledge, and 5) retain a dialog history to 
ground to.

6.3 
A Combined Approach 
Our primary hypothesis is that it is possible to develop a cognitive agent that can learn 
the large amount of situational knowledge needed to collaborate with a human on 
complex tasks in a complex, real-world environment from multiple interactions with a 
human and queries to an LLM. 
The basic operation of the system begins with receiving an utterance from the 
human requesting an action or providing information, sending that sentence along with 
appropriate prompts and context information to an LLM, and receiving back a formal 
representation of the meaning of the sentence. Given this meaning representation, the 
agent will use its internal knowledge to ground the formal representation to objects in 
its scene, access task-related knowledge, and decide what to do next. Its next action 
could be adding to its knowledge base, asking for further clarification from the LLM or 
the human, or using its knowledge and planning capabilities to go ahead and execute 
the desired task in the world. 
Consider one aspect of how this will work, the grounding of referring expressions 
in language. Section 2.1 gives seven ways of identifying objects based on language. 
Preliminary informal experiments that have been performed (Kirk, 2024) suggest that a 
modern LLM can ground language references to the agent’s internal object identifiers 
based on object category if given a list of objects labeled with this information. 
Grounding based on spatial relations and positional properties is more 
complicated, involving mathematical calculations on the physical position of objects in 
the scene. In other preliminary experiments we have found that a cognitive agent can 
do the math to build a graph representing neighbor relations among objects. With this 
graph references like the cabinet to the left of the microwave or the third drawer down 
next to the stove can be resolved using only symbolic calculations. The cognitive agent 
can easily do this resolution if given a formal representation of the reference, which the 
LLM should be able to provide. Alternatively, the LLM may be able to do the full 
resolution itself if given the neighbor graph. 
Resolving functional references, such as in Get a knife from the drawer when 
there are many drawers and only one is used for storing knives, requires specific 
knowledge of how things are organized in this particular kitchen. Certainly, the LLM will 
not have that knowledge. The cognitive agent should be able to learn that knowledge 
either by inquiring of the human or by exploration. Once it has that knowledge, these 
references can be resolved easily using the agent’s reasoning from the formal form of 
the reference. 
Finally, what about references to objects previously mentioned in the dialog? 
Suppose the agent has used a knife to slice a potato, put the knife down somewhere to 
be able to move the potato slices to the pot, and now needs to get the knife again to slice 
an onion. Where is the knife? A cognitive agent with episodic memory should be able to 
remember where it left the knife. An LLM like ChatGPT (Open AI, 2023) has a context


## Page 12


window that remembers a lot of the dialog, and it may be able to ground such reference 
to that. We intend to experiment with these alternatives. 
Section 2.2 discussed a variety of issues related to understanding tasks based on 
the use of verbs in context. Human instructions such as I’m hungry, what’s for 
breakfast? or even just Cook me an egg. require extensive situational knowledge to 
perform. To perform one of these actions, the agent will need knowledge of ingredients, 
recipes, storage locations, tools, procedures, personal preferences, and more. 
Some parts of this knowledge may be gleaned by consulting the LLM, such as 
different ways to cook an egg. Much of it, though, can only be acquired by interaction 
with the human. The LLM will be an important part of this interaction to transform the 
free-form natural language the human uses into formal terms the agent can understand. 
The agent will also need its reasoning and planning abilities to verify the LLM’s 
responses, orchestrate all these interactions, and plan the sequences of primitive 
actions needed to accomplish a given task. 
7. Conclusions and Going Forward 
This paper proposes an approach to tackling the problem of making a robotic agent 
capable of collaborating with a human in a real-world environment where much of their 
interaction is through natural language. The approach is based on using the proven 
capabilities of both cognitive agents and large language models, with each contributing 
its strengths to the outcome. 
We have just begun to move forward with the implementation of a research 
prototype of the system shown in Figure 1. We intend to build the system in the 
simulated environment provided by AI2-Thor (Kolve et al., 2022), which provides a 
complex environment with many objects, a simulated robot that can navigate and 
manipulate objects in the environment, and good visuals in real time. This will be 
connected to an instance of the Soar cognitive architecture (Laird, 2012), which provides 
memories and reasoning capabilities that have been interfaced to a number of physical 
and simulated robots. We expect to use the dataset of natural human instructions 
produced using AI2-Thor in ALFRED (Shridhar et al., 2020) for learning and testing. We 
believe that this combination will allow us to demonstrate unprecedented abilities for a 
robotic agent to use natural language to collaborate with humans in real physical 
environments. 
Limitations 
First, we present a proposed approach to accomplishing goals of this paper, but there 
is no full implementation of the approach as yet. Second, the planned implementation 
is in simulation rather than with a physical robot. Third, we intend to work with the 
ALFRED dataset, but our agent will be using the symbolic data from AI2-Thor to 
simulate real visual perception, while their language data was produced by humans 
viewing a visual presentation.
