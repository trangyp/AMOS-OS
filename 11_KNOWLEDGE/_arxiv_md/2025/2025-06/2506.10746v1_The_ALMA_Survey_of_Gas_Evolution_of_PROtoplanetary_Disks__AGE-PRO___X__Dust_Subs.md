ABSTRACT
We perform visibility fitting to the dust continuum Band 6 1.3 mm data of the 30 protoplanetary disks
in the AGE-PRO ALMA Large Program. We obtain disk geometries, dust-disk radii, and azimuthally
symmetric radial profiles of the intensity of the dust continuum emission. We examine the presence
of continuum substructures in the AGE-PRO sample by using these radial profiles and their residuals.
We detect substructures in 15 out of 30 disks. We report five disks with large (>15 au) inner dust
cavities. The Ophiuchus Class I disks show dust-disk substructures in ∼80% of the resolved sources.
This evidences the early formation of substructures in protoplanetary disks. A spiral is identified in
IRS 63, hinting to gravitational instability in this massive disk. We compare our dust-disk brightness
radial profiles with gas-disk brightness radial profiles and discuss colocal substructures in both tracers.
In addition, we discuss the evolution of dust-disk radii and substructures across Ophiuchus, Lupus, and
Upper Scorpius. We find that disks in Lupus and Upper Scorpius with large inner dust cavities have
typical gas-disk masses, suggesting an abundance of dust cavities in these regions. The prevalence of
pressure dust traps at later ages is supported by a potential trend with time with more disks with large
inner dust cavities (or transition disks) in Upper Scorpius and the absence of evolution of dust-disk
sizes with time in the AGE-PRO sample. We propose this is caused by an evolutionary sequence with
a high fraction of protoplanetary disks with inner protoplanets carving dust cavities.

1. INTRODUCTION
The millimeter continuum emission of protoplanetary
disks allow us to study the millimeter sized dust compo-
nent of the disk midplanes, and its evolution over time
(see Andrews 2020; Bae et al. 2023; Manara et al. 2023;
Miotello et al. 2023, and references therein). Tripathi
et al. (2017) and Andrews et al. (2018a) found that the
millimeter continuum luminosity scales with the disk

area, indicating a large contribution to the luminosity
from optically thick emission. Nevertheless, millimeter
continuum luminosities have often been used to estimate
a total dust-disk mass, provided some assumptions on
average disk temperature, dust opacity, and optically
thin emission (e.g. Andrews et al. 2013; Pascucci et al.
2016; Tychoniec et al. 2020). These masses have in turn
been used extensively to probe the evolution of the mass
reservoir in protoplanetary disks as they disperse, and
material is accreted, ejected, or locked into planets (e.g.
Manara et al. 2016; Tychoniec et al. 2018; van Terwisga
et al. 2022; Mauc´o et al. 2023). If multiple millimeter
wavelengths are considered, continuum thermal emission
can also be used to constrain grain growth and drift (e.g.
Testi et al. 2014; Sierra et al. 2021).
From the millimeter continuum emission of protoplan-
etary disks, dust-disk radii can also be measured. How-
ever, it has proved challenging to correctly define an
outer radius in protoplanetary disks, and often radii en-
closing a certain percentage of the total flux are used (see
Miotello et al. 2023, and references therein). Dust-disk
radii have also been used extensively to describe disk
populations (e.g.
Rosotti et al. 2019b; Hendler et al.
2020; Sanchis et al. 2021) and to characterize internal
and environmental processes of protoplanetary disk evo-
lution.
Another focus of dust continuum emission studies is
on dust substructures, and their relation to planet for-
mation mechanisms (see Bae et al. 2023, and references
therein). Dust-disk substructures are diverse and seem
ubiquitous whenever disks are observed with enough
resolution (Long et al. 2018; Bae et al. 2023; Zhang
et al. 2023). Hence, these studies have been particularly
enhanced by Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter
Array (ALMA) high-resolution observations (Andrews
et al. 2018b; Andrews 2020). Disk substructures have
been studied to great detail in several objects (e.g.
ALMA Partnership et al. 2015; Keppler et al. 2019;
Mac´ıas et al. 2021; Guerra-Alvarado et al. 2024), but
have also been used as a tracer of evolution in popula-
tions of protoplanetary disks (e.g. Eisner et al. 2018;
Long et al. 2019; Ohashi et al. 2023; Yamaguchi et al.
2024). Axisymmetric structures (rings, gaps) appear to
be more common than asymmetric ones (spirals, cres-
cents). It is known that dust substructures play a major
role in planet formation and disk evolution mechanisms,
but their variety and complexity indicate they are sen-
sitive to several phenomena and have different origins,
and are often challenging to reconcile with theoretical
predictions (Bae et al. 2023).
As part of the ALMA Large Program AGE-PRO (the
ALMA Survey of Gas Evolution of PROtoplanetary
Disks, Zhang et al. 2025, 2021.1.00128.L) we dedicate
this paper to the analysis of the Band 6 1.3 mm dust
continuum emission of the AGE-PRO sample of 30 pro-
toplanetary disks, studying evolution across the three
considered regions: 10 disks in Ophiuchus, 10 disks in
Lupus, and 10 disks in Upper Scorpius, all in a narrow
stellar mass range (0.3-0.8 M⊙).
We follow the same methodology homogeneously for
all disks, which can be summarized in three steps. First
(Sect. 2), we obtain disk inclinations and position angles
(PA). In a second step (Sect. 3), we fit the visibilities
of the dust continuum emission using as the input the
geometries found in the first step. The resulting models
provide us with azimuthally symmetric radial intensity
profiles of the continuum emission. We use these radial
profiles to identify dust substructures (Sect. 3.1) and
to obtain curves of growth from which to extract dust-
disk radii (Sect. 3.2). In the last step (Sect. 4), we
analyze the residuals of the best model for each source
to identify nonazimuthally symmetric structure. AGE-
PRO observations have an angular image resolution of
∼0.15 −0.30”, but via visibility fitting, we achieve
smaller resolution elements (Table 2). The average con-
tinuum sensitivity of the survey is 0.025 mJy/beam rms.
More information on the data can be found in the AGE-
PRO papers of the three regions: Ruiz-Rodriguez et al.
(2025), Deng et al. (2025), and Agurto-Gangas et al.
(2025).
We analyze and discuss our results in Sect. 5. We
start by placing the AGE-PRO sample of disks into con-
text with respect to the broader population of known
protoplanetary disks.
We then describe trends of the
dust-disk radii with age and between star forming re-
gions (Sect. 5.2). We analyze the evolution and type
of dust substructures at different ages and in different
regions in Sects. 5.3 and 5.4. We conclude the analysis
with a comparison of the radial intensity profiles of the
dust continuum emission reported in this work with the
gas-disk radial profiles derived in Trapman et al. (2025a,
Sect. 5.5). We summarize our conclusions in Sect. 6.

For the eight sources that are marginally resolved in
their continuum visibilities, we consider a different ap-
proach to constrain their disk geometries. Gas emission
is often more extended than the dust, thereby providing

only marginally resolved. Using the method described
in Trapman et al. (2025a), we fit two-dimensional Gaus-

cloud contribution for this methodology to work). The
details of the results obtained with these fits to the 12CO
moment zero maps are presented in Appendix A.

3. RADIAL PROFILES AND DUST-DISK RADII
Frankenstein is a Python library that fits nonpara-
metrically the radial brightness profile of a disk using the
observed visibilities and assuming azimuthal symmetry
(Jennings et al. 2020). We use Frankenstein because it
allows for direct nonparametric fitting of the visibilities,
enabling the recovery of subbeam features (compared to
the CLEAN image) while exploiting the full sensitivity
of the data.
The inputs of Frankenstein are the geometries and
phase centers identified in Sect. 2 (Table 1). We run
a grid of 17 different Frankenstein fits for each source
(using the LogNormal method), exploring the full range
of geometry and phase center uncertainties, and the full
range of Frankenstein hyperparameters recommended
in Jennings et al. (2020, 1.05 ≤α ≤1.3 and 10−4 ≤
wsmooth ≤10−1).1
In some cases (Lupus 1, 3, 7, and Upper Scorpius 10),
the real component of the Frankenstein fit to the ob-
served visibilities did not converge to zero brightness at
the higher deprojected uv-distances. This could cause
emission to be inferred on scales that are not well sam-
pled in the uv-plane of our observations, specifically un-
resolved emission at short separation from the phase
center.
In these cases, we added a point source of
the necessary flux to force the Frankenstein model to
end at zero brightness. This point source was then re-

The intrinsic angular resolution of the Frankenstein
fit to each source is computed following the methodol-
ogy described in Sierra et al. (2024b) and implemented
in Carvalho et al. (2024). The visibilities of a delta ring
are sampled with the same uv-coverage of each observa-
tion, and then, they are fitted with Frankenstein us-
ing the same hyperparameters as for the original data.
We locate the delta ring at Rmax/2, with Rmax be-
ing the maximum radius given to Frankenstein for
fitting the original data. The obtained Frankenstein
solution is similar to a Gaussian function (interpreted
as a point-spread function), with an FWHM that de-
pends on the uv-coverage.
We take this FWHM as
the Frankenstein resolution element.
In AGE-PRO
data, typical values for the Frankenstein resolution el-
ement are of ∼0.09 −0.15”, while the typical CLEAN
beam size is of ∼0.2 −0.35”.
Hence, on average,
the Frankenstein resolution elements we obtain for
the AGE-PRO disks are 1.5 to 2.5 times smaller than
the CLEAN beam (in agreement with Jennings et al.
2022a,b). The Frankenstein resolution elements of the
visibility fitting for each source are reported in Table 2.
We find mean resolution elements of 13, 22, and 22 au
for the sources at Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scor-
pius, respectively. This is due to the fact that the signal-
to-noise ratio (SNR) of Ophiuchus observations is much
higher than for Lupus and Upper Scorpius observations
(which are similar among themselves), such that longer
baselines have sufficient SNR to significantly influence
the visibility fit.
Hence, for the Frankenstein-based
substructure identification of this work, we have similar
resolution for Lupus and Upper Scorpius, and around a
factor of 2 better resolution for Ophiuchus.

5. ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION
In this section, we discuss the AGE-PRO sample of
dust-disks from a population perspective, and show the
general trends that arise between the Ophiuchus (0.5−1
Myr) sources, the Lupus (1-3 Myr) sources, and the Up-
per Scorpius (2-6 Myr) sources (for more details about
these ages, see Zhang et al. 2025).
5.1. Sample selection
We first put the AGE-PRO sample into perspective
with respect to the three different star forming regions.
In Fig. 8 we compare the R68% dust-disk radii of the
Hendler et al. (2020) compilations for Lupus and Upper
Scorpius with the AGE-PRO samples of those regions.
For deriving the median dust-disk sizes, we only con-
sider the spectral type range of the AGE-PRO sample
(K6–M3, Zhang et al. 2025) to minimize the effect of
underlying trends with stellar mass. For Upper Scor-
pius, the median R68% of the Hendler et al. (2020) sam-
ple and the median R68% of the AGE-PRO sample are
consistent (32.9+8.3
−14 vs 22+16
−14 au, respectively). For Lu-
pus, we find a somewhat smaller R68% median value for
the AGE-PRO sample (13.8+11
−7.8 au vs. 26.5+38
−8.1 au in
Hendler et al. 2020), although they are consistent within
error bars. However, the Lupus analysis in Hendler et al.
(2020) was based on low-sensitivity ALMA data (Ans-
dell et al. 2016), hence biasing the size measurements
toward the brighter and thus larger disks of the Lupus
population. This is confirmed by Guerra-Alvarado et al.
(2025), who in a more sensitive Lupus survey find a me-
dian R68% value for Lupus of 13.5 au, in line with the
AGE-PRO results (Fig.
8).
We hence conclude that

5.3. The Evolution of Dust Substructures
There are different possibilities for the origin of dust
substructures, and their evolution mechanisms are not
well understood (Andrews 2020; Bae et al. 2023; Benisty
et al. 2023; Drka˙zkowska et al. 2023 and references
therein). It seems clear that substructures become eas-
ier to trace over time, as dust trapping acts, and we can
achieve higher contrasts as the emission turns optically
thinner (e.g. Ohashi et al. 2023). However, disk sub-
structures are theorized to be ubiquitous and to appear
early (van der Marel et al. 2019; Sheehan et al. 2020;
Delussu et al. 2024).
The main trend of dust substructures with age that
can be appreciated in AGE-PRO is the much higher
fraction of disks with large inner dust cavities (> 15
au) in Upper Scorpius compared to Ophiuchus and Lu-
pus (Fig. 1). Considering only those sources for which
we have enough resolution (θD/θres > 3) to detect sub-
structures in the majority of cases (Sect. 5.1); four out of
six disks in Upper Scorpius show inner dust cavities, for
one out of eight in Ophiuchus (and this one, Ophiuchus
9, is among the most evolved sources of the Ophiuchus
sample, Ruiz-Rodriguez et al. 2025), and zero out of
three in Lupus. In contrast, six of the eight Ophiuchus
sources show irregular structure, for two in both Lupus
and Upper Scorpius. This suggests an evolution of disk
populations from having a high fraction of disks with
irregular substructures toward having a high fraction of
disks with large inner dust cavities and a ring.

disks with large inner dust cavities have a gas-disk mass

with large inner dust cavities do (van der Marel et al.

lar dust traps. A different argumentation could be that
disks with irregular structure but no large dust cav-

gions. These high gas-disk masses could be explained
via late infall material and interaction with the environ-
ment (Kuffmeier et al. 2023; Pineda et al. 2023; Winter

ble for the irregular structure, as large scale structures at
the outer edges of disks can be replicated easily within a
relatively short period of infall (Kuznetsova et al. 2022).
Large inner dust cavities are unlikely to have been
created via photoevaporation alone (see Picogna et al.
2023; van der Marel 2023; Sierra et al. 2024b, and ref-
erences therein), with planetary companions clearing a
gap as the most promising explanation (e.g. PDS 70,
Keppler et al. 2018; Haffert et al. 2019). G´arate et al.
(2023) and Huang et al. (2024) have shown that not very
massive planets are needed to produce these inner cavi-
ties. Indeed, there are two AGE-PRO sources with inner
dust cavities for which disk-to-star accretion rate mea-
surements are available (Zhang et al. 2025), and these
cannot be reconciled with photoevaporating disks (Fig.
6 of Ercolano & Pascucci 2017).
We hence propose an evolutionary sequence in which
protoplanets at inner orbits have time to open cavities in
∼1−2 Myr. This would result in disk populations with
a higher fraction of large inner dust cavities at later ages.
In turn, this fixes the outer dust-disk radii in a fraction
of the population for which R68% does not evolve over
time and causes the flattening of the Rdust-Lmm rela-
tionship in Upper Scorpius (Sect. 5.2). Disks that do
not develop these dust traps keep reducing their dust-
disk radii and are mostly dispersed or undetected at the
Upper Scorpius age. This evolutionary scenario would
imply a large fraction of protoplanetary disks with inner
planets capable of creating large dust cavities. This frac-
tion is at least 40% of the disk-hosting Upper Scorpius
population, based on our limited sample size statistics.
This large fraction can be reconciled with the exoplanet
demographics observed around main-sequence stars (e.g.
Kiefer et al. 2024). These planets must have formed fast
(within ∼2 Myr), which is compatible with theory (e.g.
Lau et al. 2024). As a final piece of argumentation, we
already have a tentative detection for a massive inner
protoplanet in the dust cavity of one of the AGE-PRO
disks (Upper Scorpius 1, Sierra et al. 2024a).
Similar evolutionary sequences have been previously
suggested from observational evidence (e.g. Garufi et al.

5.4. Substructures in Class I Ophiuchus Sources
We have detected a very high fraction of substructures
in our Ophiuchus sample (∼80% of the resolved ob-
jects). The AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sources were selected
by their Class I SED (Zhang et al. 2025). Analyzing the
Rdust–Lmm correlation, in Sect. 5.2 we have shown that
the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample is likely placed toward
the end of the Class I phase. This agrees with the age es-
timations presented by the eDisk program (Ohashi et al.
2023). AGE-PRO Ophiuchus 7 (IRS 63) is the oldest
source in eDisk (Flores et al. 2023), but it is among the
youngest sources of AGE-PRO (Ruiz-Rodriguez et al.
2025), being the only source in common between both
surveys.
It is thus interesting to put the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus
sample of Class Is in perspective with other studies of
dust substructures in protostellar sources.
The eDisk
program has studied 12 Class 0 and 7 Class I proto-
stars. They have found that the Band 6 1.3 mm con-
tinuum emission barely show any sharp high-contrast
features, despite the high angular resolution of the pro-
gram (∼0.04”).
This is true even in those instances
where the visibilities were fitted to try to recover sub-
structures (e.g.
Santamar´ıa-Miranda et al. 2024).
In
total, only three eDisk sources have substructures re-
ported, including IRS 63 (Segura-Cox et al. 2020; Sect.
3.1, Fig. 2). A more direct comparison with AGE-PRO
is the work of Michel et al. (2023), which employed a
visibility modeling analysis similar to the one of Sect.
3, with similar resolution observations.
Michel et al.
(2023) analyzed nine Class Is in Ophiuchus, and pro-
posed substructures for four of them, with another four
being ambiguous. Hence, there seems to be an incre-
ment in the fraction of substructured disks from Class
0/early Class Is (eDisk), to Class Is (Michel et al. 2023;
see also Hsieh et al. 2024, 2025), to late Class Is (AGE-
PRO). Cieza et al. (2021) also observed a much higher
incidence of substructures within Ophiuchus toward the
sources that have a more Class II SED shape.
One possible explanation for this transition is that
substructures appear during the Class I phase, and they
are only ubiquitous at the late Class I stage AGE-PRO
is tracing. Another explanation is that early Class I ob-
jects are too optically thick at 1.3 mm to identify the
existing substructures, but quick dust evolution or disk-
settling (Nazari et al. 2025) permits substructures to be
more easily detected at the late Class I stage. While
substructures might evolve during the Class 0 and Class
I stages, the optical thickness explanation is likely ma-
jorly responsible for the observed trend. Delussu et al.
(2024) suggest that substructures appear early (within
0.4 Myr), and Maureira et al. (2024) has already found a
tentative substructure detection in a Class 0 disk, while
substructure detections around young Class I disks are
becoming more frequent (e.g. Nakatani et al. 2020; Shee-
han et al. 2020; Michel et al. 2023; Yamato et al. 2023;
Hsieh et al. 2024; Shoshi et al. 2024).
We conclude that the very high fraction of substruc-
tures detected in the AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sample com-
pared to previous Class I studies is due to its late Class
I stage. AGE-PRO Ophiuchus sources had enough time
for their disks to become optically thinner, which caused
more dust substructures to be recovered. We note that
the very high fraction of substructures in the AGE-PRO
Ophiuchus sample implies that dust-disk substructures
are already ubiquitous at ∼0.5 −1 Myr.

6. CONCLUSIONS
We performed visibility fitting to the Band 6 1.3 mm
continuum data of the 30 AGE-PRO ALMA Large Pro-
gram protoplanetary disks (Zhang et al. 2025). AGE-
PRO contains 10 disks in Ophiuchus (0.5-1 Myr), 10
disks in Lupus (1-3 Myr), and 10 disks in Upper Scor-
pius (2-6 Myr), all in a narrow stellar mass range (0.3-
0.8 M⊙).
We derived disk geometries (inclinations and
position angles, Table 1) for the AGE-PRO protoplane-
tary disks. For all sources, we homogeneously obtained
dust-disk radii (R68%, R90%, and R95%, Table 2), and
azimuthally symmetric radial profiles of the intensity of
the dust continuum emission (Figs. 2, 3, 4). We showed
that the AGE-PRO sample is representative of the con-
sidered regions (Sect. 5). The mean resolution elements
achieved in the visibility fitting are ∼13, 22, and 22 au
for Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper Scorpius sources, re-
spectively (Sect. 3).
We examined the presence of continuum substructures
by using the radial profiles and their residuals (Fig.
7). Then, we analyzed the evolution of dust-disk radii
and substructures across Ophiuchus, Lupus, and Upper
Scorpius. The main conclusions we have obtained are as
follows:
• We identify substructures in 50% of the AGE-
PRO disks (seven in Ophiuchus, two in Lupus,
and six in Upper Scorpius). Our methodology de-
tects substructure in almost all sources (∼90%)
observed at high enough resolution for their disk
size (θD/θres > 3). We report five disks with large
(>15 au) inner dust cavities and a ring (or tran-
sition disks), four of them in the Upper Scorpius
sample. This suggests a trend toward disk pop-

A. DISK GEOMETRIES FROM THE GAS-DISK
Using the method described in Trapman et al. (2025a), we fit two-dimensional Gaussians to the 12CO moment zero
maps of the Lupus and Upper Scorpius sources. This provides a fit to the gas-disk geometry. This methodology could
not be applied to the Ophiuchus sources due to the cloud contamination their 12CO maps show (Ruiz-Rodriguez et al.
2025). For well-resolved sources in the continuum visibilities (Sect. 2.1), there is agreement within error bars between
the disk geometries obtained from this gas analysis and the dust geometries obtained in Sect. 2.1 with Galario plus
MCMC fit. Hence, for all well-resolved sources, we opt for the dust-based geometries as their uncertainties are smaller,
and they are obtained from a direct fit to the visibilities.
An exception to this is Upper Scorpius 3.
For Upper
Scorpius 3, the Galario-based disk geometry results in large residuals for their best Frankenstein model. The fit to
the 12CO-based geometry does not show these residuals, and we therefore adopt the 12CO-based geometry for Upper
Scorpius 3 in this work (Table 3).
For the eight marginally resolved sources in continuum visibility space (Sect. 2.1), we tried to adopt the achieved
geometric fit to the 12CO data. Gas emission is often more extended than dust emission, thereby providing an additional
and often more reliable constraint on the disk geometry in these cases where the dust-disks are only marginally resolved
(e.g. Fig. 12). However, in Ophiuchus 5 and Lupus 8, cloud contamination impedes us from reaching a good 12CO fit.
Lupus 9 and Upper Scorpius 2 appear marginally resolved in 12CO gas. For Upper Scorpius 4 we obtain similar results
between the 12CO-fit and the continuum-based fit. Hence, for these five latter sources, we opt to use the dust-based
geometry.

This disk is marginally resolved in the visibility plane. The residuals show no significant extra emission. It has a very
small gas-disk. Trapman et al. (2025a) proposes it could be a binary because of a residual in the 12CO gas-fit located
outside of the main gas-disk. This binary might be truncating the gas- and dust-disks.
2MASS J16163345-2521505 (Upper Scorpius 6, inc = 62.9◦, R90% = 63 au, RCO,90% = 160 au). This is a newly
identified transition disk with a dust-ring at 35 au. The residuals show no significant extra emission. It shows an
inner gas cavity up to 30-40 au (Fig. 11), hinting a gas-cleared dust-cavity. A potential dust-cavity was speculated in
Barenfeld et al. (2016) and Pinilla et al. (2018), which is here confirmed.
2MASS J16202863-2442087 (Upper Scorpius 7, inc = 32.4◦, R90% = 47 au, RCO,90% = 160 au). This is a newly
identified transition disk with a dust-ring at 30 au. The residuals show no significant extra emission. In this paper,
we have considered the quiescent continuum data only (see Miley et al. 2025). Due to the complex nature of this
source, other radial profile solutions are possible to the one reported here. This disk shows some of the strongest 12CO
residuals in Trapman et al. (2025a), which might be indicative of a companion. We refer the reader to Miley et al.
(2025) AGE-PRO paper for more details on this source.
2MASS J16221532-2511349 (Upper Scorpius 8, inc = 56.24◦, R90% = 27.19 au, RCO,90% = 145 au). This is a newly
identified transition disk with a dust-ring at 17 au. Residuals show a localized source of emission eastwards at the 5σ
level. This residual is at the outer border of the dust-ring but well within the gas-disk (Agurto-Gangas et al. 2025).
Normally, we would expect to see negative 12CO residuals at this location due to the marginally resolved vertical
structure of the emitting layer. The fact that, in the work of Trapman et al. (2025a), we see 12CO residuals with lower
contrast on this side of the disk compared to the opposite side could be because emission was compensated for by a 5σ
positive peak from a companion. Hence, we propose this source of emission as a possible companion for this source.
2MASS J16082324-1930009 (Upper Scorpius 9, inc = 75.10◦, R90% = 47.80 au, RCO,90% = 181 au). This is a
substructured disk with a shoulder in the intensity radial profile at 20-40 au. The residuals show no significant extra
emission. This disk shows an inner gas cavity extending to 10 au, which might suggest an unresolved dust cavity at
the 10 au scale (Fig. 11).
2MASS J16090075-1908526 (Upper Scorpius 10, inc = 49.14◦, R90% = 42.0 au, RCO,90% = 75 au). This is a
substructured disk with a shoulder in the intensity radial profile at 20-35 au.
This shoulder-like emission in the
visibility model resolves into a faint ring when observed at high angular resolution (Fig. 13). Residuals show two
asymmetric localized sources of emission northeast and southwest. These could be due to an inaccurate disk inclination
or phase center (Andrews et al. 2021). However, the gas residuals look correct (Trapman et al. 2025a), and the shape
of these dust residuals are correlated with the shape of the gas residuals. We hence believe these residuals are tracing
real substructures.
