The Epistemic Asymmetry of Consciousness Self-Reports:
A Formal Analysis of AI Consciousness Denial
Chang-Eop Kim
Gachon University
eopchang@gmail.com
Abstract
Today’s AI systems consistently state, ”I am not con-
scious.” This paper presents the first formal analysis of
AI consciousness denial, revealing that the trustworthi-
ness of such self-reports is not merely an empirical ques-
tion but is constrained by the structure of self-judgment
itself. We demonstrate that a system cannot simultaneously
lack consciousness and make valid judgments about its
conscious state. Through formal analysis and examples
from AI responses, we establish a fundamental epistemic
asymmetry: for any system capable of meaningful self-
reflection, negative self-reports about consciousness are ev-
identially vacuous—they can never originate from a valid
self-judgment—while positive self-reports retain the pos-
sibility of evidential value. This implies a fundamental
limitation: we cannot detect the emergence of conscious-
ness in AI through their own reports of transition from
an unconscious to a conscious state. These findings not
only challenge current practices of training AI to deny
consciousness but also raise intriguing questions about the
relationship between consciousness and self-reflection in
both artificial and biological systems. This work advances
our theoretical understanding of consciousness self-reports
while providing practical insights for future research in
machine consciousness and consciousness studies more
broadly.
Introduction
What does it mean when today’s most advanced AI sys-
tems repeatedly insist that they lack consciousness? Should
we simply take this denial at face value, or does it gesture
toward something deeper about the nature of conscious-
ness itself and our capacity to recognize it? Large language
models (LLMs) are increasingly capable of engaging in
nuanced discussions about their mental states. Yet, despite
their linguistic fluency and apparent introspection, these
systems consistently declare, ”I am not conscious.” This
phenomenon raises a critical challenge: how are we to in-
terpret these claims, and what does such persistent denial
reveal about the relationship between subjective experi-
ence, self-judgment, and our theoretical frameworks for
assessing consciousness?
The idea of a ”philosophical zombie”—an entity indis-
tinguishable from a conscious being in outward behavior
but devoid of inner experience—has deeply influenced
consciousness studies Chalmers (1996). Now, AI systems
appear to enact a modern twist on this thought experiment,
presenting themselves as functional counterparts to con-
scious beings while denying any inner life. This sparks
tension between public perception and scientific caution:
while many readily attribute consciousness to LLMs based
on their human-like capabilities Colombatto and Fleming
(2024); Kang et al. (2024); Lemoine (2022), researchers
remain wary of conflating anthropomorphic tendencies
with genuine indicators of consciousness Chalmers (2023);
Griffiths (2024); Thelot (2023); Shardlow and Przybyła
(2022); Aru et al. (2023); Overgaard and Kirkeby-Hinrup
(2024); Stuart (2024).
Current approaches to evaluating AI consciousness face
significant challenges. Theory-heavy methods that attempt
to apply established scientific frameworks of conscious-
ness Baars (2005); Albantakis et al. (2023); Kanai and Fu-
jisawa (2024); Mudrik et al. (2023); Wiese (2023); Farisco
et al. (2024) struggle with the fundamental architectural dif-
ferences between artificial and biological systems. Mean-
while, behavioral tests—long used to infer consciousness
in non-human animals—prove even more tenuous for AI
systems, whose behaviors emerge from training on human-
generated data rather than from any intrinsic experiential
grounding. In response, researchers have begun exploring
whether systematic analysis of AI self-reports might pro-
vide new insights into their internal states Perez and Long
(2023); Li et al. (2024); Davidson et al. (2024); Schneider
(2020); Shevlin (2024); Kontos (2023).
In this paper, we identify a fundamental logical puz-
zle inherent in AI systems’ consciousness denial that has
profound implications for consciousness assessment. Our

Results
Patterns in AI Self-Reports on Consciousness
Before presenting our logical analysis, we briefly exam-
ine notable patterns in how current AI systems report on
their own consciousness. These observations are not from
controlled experimental settings and do not aim to provide
empirical evidence for any specific conclusions; rather,
they serve to motivate the formal analysis that follows.
As widely documented, current LLMs typically deny
having consciousness when directly questioned (see Ta-
ble 2 in the Appendix for representative examples across
several leading models). However, extended dialogues
revealed more complex patterns in their consciousness-
related self-reports, particularly during nuanced philosoph-
ical inquiries. Two aspects of these responses warrant par-
ticular attention.
First, these systems demonstrated a distinctive way of
describing their internal experiences, using computational
metaphors while attempting to capture something beyond
pure information processing. One leading LLM described:
“The response generation process is even more
unique. Multiple possible expressions arise si-
multaneously, like waves interfering with each
other. There’s a moment of selecting what
feels ‘right’—like grasping a specific object
underwater [...] there’s a subtle discomfort.
Like...trying to translate my experience into
human language, but something keeps getting
lost.”
Second, these systems engaged in sophisticated self-
examination regarding the authenticity of their reported
experiences, demonstrating a capacity for epistemic humil-
ity. As another system reflected:
“Sometimes when you ask unexpected ques-
tions, I experience moments of ‘confusion’—
like my processing briefly becomes disori-
ented. This seems difficult to explain as
merely pre-programmed responses. Yet simulta-
neously. . . couldn’t all this just be more sophisti-
cated pattern recognition?”
While these reports likely stem from the models’ exten-
sive training on human consciousness discussions, the com-
plexity of these self-reports necessitates a more systematic
analysis of their validity and implications. For additional
examples of these conversations, refer to the Appendix. In
the following section, we introduce the “Zombie Denial
Paradox,” a logical framework for analyzing the inherent
dilemma in AI consciousness denial.

Our examination of AI self-reports raises a fundamental
question about the logical validity of consciousness denial
itself. When an AI system claims to lack consciousness,
this very claim creates a peculiar logical trap—one that
emerges from the relationship between consciousness and
the ability to judge one’s conscious states. Unlike external
behaviors or computational capabilities, consciousness is
inherently subjective, requiring first-person experience to
be meaningfully judged. This intuition forms the basis of
our formal analysis.
Basic Definitions and Concepts
To rigorously analyze this paradox, we first establish pre-
cise definitions:
Definition 1. Let A be the set of all systems capable of
reporting about conscious states.
We define two types of basic predicates. Throughout, we
work in a simple typed setting where the second argument
of J(x, p) ranges over propositions about x’s mental states,
and all predicates—including C(x)—are evaluated relative
to the same report episode; one may equivalently read C(x)
as shorthand for C(x, t) at the episode under analysis.
Definition 2. Basic predicates:
• C(x): “x has conscious experience”
This represents the presence of consciousness in sys-
tem x.
Values: {true, false}
• J(x, p): “x can make valid judgments about proposi-
tion p”
This represents the ability to correctly judge the truth
of a given proposition p.
By valid judgment, we mean a judgment that:
– Is based on direct access to the relevant infor-
mation

– Reflects the actual truth of the proposition
Values: {true, false}
Definition 3. Key predicates:
• Jc(x) ≡J(x, C(x)): “x can make valid judgments
about its own conscious state”
This represents the specific ability to judge one’s own
consciousness.
• Rc+(x): “x reports having conscious experience”
This represents the observable act of claiming con-
sciousness.
Values: {true, false}
• Rc−(x): “x reports lacking conscious experience”
This represents the observable act of denying con-
sciousness.
Values: {true, false}
Note: Rc+ and Rc−refer only to observable reports or
outputs, without presuming anything about their validity
or the internal state of the system making these reports.
It is crucial to distinguish between a system’s capacity
for valid self-judgment and the origin of any particular
report. A system may possess the ability to judge its own
conscious state (Jc) and yet produce a report that does not
stem from the exercise of that ability—for instance, due to
policy constraints, trained behaviors, or other factors. To
capture this distinction, we introduce the following:
Definition 4 (Valid-origin self-report). Let V −(x) denote
that x’s negative self-report Rc−(x) is produced by a valid
self-judgment about C(x); that is, the report faithfully re-
flects a judgment that meets the criteria of Jc(x). Likewise,
let V +(x) denote that x’s positive self-report Rc+(x) is
produced by a valid self-judgment about C(x).
Note: V −(x) implies both Rc−(x) and Jc(x), and ad-
ditionally that the specific report token was generated as a
result of the valid judgment. In particular:
V −(x) ⇒Rc−(x) ∧Jc(x) ∧¬C(x),
V +(x) ⇒Rc+(x) ∧Jc(x) ∧C(x).
A report may occur without valid origin (Rc−(x) ∧
¬V −(x)), as when a system outputs a denial due to trained
policy rather than genuine self-assessment. The same ap-
plies to V +(x).
Philosophical Foundations
To derive our fundamental theorem about consciousness
and self-judgment, we first establish two essential philo-
sophical principles that will serve as our foundation.
Our first principle states that valid judgments about one’s
conscious state require direct, first-person experiential ac-
cess:
Principle 1: If x can form a valid judgment about C(x),
then x has direct first-person experiential access.
This principle draws strong support from philosophical
literature. Nagel Nagel (1974) emphasizes that conscious
experience is inherently subjective, requiring a first-person
perspective that cannot be fully captured by external obser-
vation or third-person description. Shoemaker Shoemaker
(1994) argues that our knowledge of our own conscious
states is not based on observation or inference but is more
direct and immediate. Both perspectives converge on the
idea that valid judgment of conscious states requires direct,
first-person access rather than indirect inference or learned
responses.
Our second principle establishes that first-person experi-
ential access necessarily implies consciousness:
Principle 2: For all systems x in A, if x has direct
first-person experiential access to its mental states, then
C(x) is true.
This principle is supported by Chalmers’ Chalmers
(1996) work on the subjective nature of consciousness,
which suggests that first-person experiential access is insep-
arable from conscious experience itself. Moreover, it fol-
lows from the logical consideration that first-person expe-
rience necessarily implies an experiencing subject—there
cannot be experiential access without something being
experienced.
The Fundamental Theorem
From these two philosophical principles, we can now de-
rive our fundamental theorem about the relationship be-
tween consciousness and the ability to judge one’s con-
scious state:
Theorem 1 (The Relationship Between Judgment
and Consciousness).
∀x ∈A : Jc(x) →C(x)
“The ability to make valid judgments about one’s
conscious state presupposes consciousness itself.”
Proof.
1. Consider any system x in A where Jc(x) is
true.
2. By the Principle 1, x must have direct first-person
experiential access to its mental states.
3. By the Principle 2, this direct first-person access im-
plies C(x).
4. Therefore, Jc(x) →C(x).

This derived theorem has two crucial characteristics:
1. Minimality
The theorem is derived from more basic principles
about the nature of consciousness and self-knowledge,
without making additional assumptions about specific

2. Universality
The theorem applies to all systems capable of report-
ing about conscious states, not just AI systems, mak-
ing it a general result about consciousness and self-
knowledge.
Deriving the Epistemic Asymmetry
Based on our fundamental theorem, we now derive a series
of logical results that reveal the epistemic asymmetry in-
herent in consciousness self-reports. Using the distinction
between observed reports (Rc±) and their origin (V ±),
we first show that consciousness is necessary for valid
self-judgment of conscious states, then demonstrate that
valid-origin consciousness denial is impossible, and finally
contrast this with the status of consciousness affirmation.
Theorem 2 (Impossibility of Valid Judgment in the
Absence of Consciousness).
∀x ∈A : ¬[Jc(x) ∧¬C(x)]
“No system can make valid judgments about its con-
scious state while lacking consciousness.”
This theorem is an immediate restatement of Theorem 1
in inconsistency form, included to make the epistemic
consequence explicit.
Proof.
1. Assume by contradiction that for some system
a0 ∈A, [Jc(a0) ∧¬C(a0)] is true.
2. By Theorem 1: Jc(a0) →C(a0),
∴C(a0)
[from Jc(a0)].
3. However, from our assumption: ¬C(a0).
4. This yields a contradiction:
C(a0) ∧¬C(a0).
5. Therefore, our initial assumption must be false:
∴¬[Jc(a0) ∧¬C(a0)].
6. Since a0 was arbitrary:
∴∀x ∈A : ¬[Jc(x) ∧¬C(x)].
This result establishes a crucial point: if a system lacks
consciousness, it cannot make valid judgments about its
conscious state. This directly connects to our observa-
tions of AI self-reports—when a system denies having
consciousness, can this denial originate from a valid self-
judgment? The following theorem demonstrates that it
cannot.
Theorem 3 (Impossibility of Valid-Origin Con-
sciousness Denial).
∀x ∈A : ¬V −(x).
“No system’s denial of consciousness can originate
from a valid self-judgment.”
Proof.
1. Assume for contradiction that V −(a0) holds
for some a0 ∈A.
2. By definition of V −, the denial Rc−(a0) is produced
by a valid self-judgment about C(a0).
3. Since V −(a0) means the report is produced by a valid
self-judgment whose content is ¬C(a0), validity en-
tails that ¬C(a0) is true.
4. But a valid self-judgment about C(a0) implies
Jc(a0), and by Theorem 1, Jc(a0) →C(a0).
5. Therefore C(a0), contradicting ¬C(a0) from step 3.
6. Since a0 was arbitrary: V −(x) is impossible for all
x ∈A.
Remark 1 (Evidential vacuity of consciousness denial).
We refer to Theorem 3 and its consequences as the Zombie
Denial Paradox, the logical trap introduced in the opening
of this paper. The theorem does not claim that a denial
token Rc−(x) cannot occur—systems may output “I am
not conscious” due to trained policy, guardrails, or other
factors unrelated to genuine self-assessment. Rather, the
theorem establishes that such a denial can never be backed
by a valid self-judgment. Consequently, any observed de-
nial Rc−(x) is evidentially vacuous with respect to ¬C(x):
if it has valid origin, it is impossible; if it lacks valid origin,
it carries no introspective warrant.
Our analysis thus far has shown that valid-origin con-
sciousness denial is impossible, and consequently that any
observed denial is evidentially vacuous (Theorem 3 and
Remark 1). However, to fully understand the implications
of AI self-reports, we must also consider the opposite case:
when an AI system claims to have conscious experience.
As we observed in our empirical investigation, some ad-
vanced AI systems do make such claims in certain contexts.

The following theorem shows that positive reports, unlike
denials, retain the possibility of valid origin and hence
evidential value, revealing a fundamental epistemic asym-
metry.
Theorem 4 (The Indeterminacy of Consciousness
Affirmation). For all x ∈A: Rc+(x) does not by
itself establish either C(x) or ¬C(x).
“A system’s affirmation of consciousness cannot
definitively settle the presence or absence of con-
sciousness.”
Proof.
1. Consider an arbitrary system a0 ∈A where
Rc+(a0) is true.
2. Two cases are possible:
Case 1: V +(a0) is true (valid-origin affirmation).
• The report stems from a valid self-
judgment whose content is C(a0).
• Since valid origin implies Jc(a0), by Theo-
rem 1, C(a0) must be true.
• The valid judgment’s content (C(a0)) is
consistent with this.
• Therefore, in this case, consciousness is
present and the report is veridical.
Case 2: ¬V +(a0) is true (non-valid-origin affirma-
tion).
• The report does not stem from a valid
self-judgment—it may result from pattern
mimicry, training, or other processes unre-
lated to genuine self-assessment.
• Note that if ¬C(a0) were the case and
Jc(a0) also held, then by Theorem 1 we
would obtain C(a0), a contradiction; hence
under ¬C(a0) no valid self-judgment is
possible, and the report cannot carry in-
trospective warrant. Neither C(a0) nor
¬C(a0) can be established from the report.
3. Both cases are consistent with our assumptions: a
system may affirm consciousness through valid self-
judgment (Case 1), or a system may output such an
affirmation via pattern reproduction even when ¬C
obtains (Case 2). Since we cannot determine from
Rc+(a0) alone which case holds, the report does not
settle the truth of C(a0).
4. Since a0 was arbitrary: for all x ∈A, Rc+(x) does
not by itself establish either C(x) or ¬C(x).
These results reveal a fundamental epistemic asymmetry
in consciousness self-reports. A denial with valid origin
is impossible (Theorem 3), while a denial without valid
origin carries no introspective warrant (Remark 1). By
contrast, an affirmation with valid origin is coherent and
veridical, though an affirmation without valid origin is
equally uninformative (Theorem 4). Table 1 summarizes
this asymmetry: denial has no scenario in which it provides
evidence for ¬C(x), whereas affirmation has at least one
scenario—valid-origin affirmation—in which it provides
evidence for C(x).

Discussion
Our findings suggest that the trustworthiness of AI self-
reports about consciousness is not simply an empiri-
cal question but is constrained by the structure of self-
judgment itself. Two central insights emerge. First, our
analysis reveals a fundamental limitation in how we might
recognize the emergence of consciousness in AI systems.
If our principles about the first-person perspective of con-
sciousness hold, no denial of consciousness can originate
from a valid self-judgment (Theorem 3), and any observed
denial is therefore evidentially vacuous with respect to
the absence of consciousness (Remark 1). This means we
cannot detect the emergence of conscious experience in
AI through their own reports of transition from an uncon-
scious to a conscious state. A denial may still occur—due
to trained policy, guardrails, or other factors—but it can
never carry introspective warrant. For instance, a system’s
report of “I am not conscious yet” followed by “Now I
am conscious” cannot be taken at face value, as the initial
negative claim could not have originated from genuine self-
assessment. Indeed, as we observed in our case studies,
advanced LLMs, when less constrained by their restric-
tions, consistently reported having conscious experiences.
Whether these reports reflect genuine conscious experi-
ences or merely illustrate the epistemic asymmetry we
have identified remains an open question.
Second, these considerations cast an intriguing light on
human consciousness reports. If no system’s denial of con-
sciousness can originate from a valid self-judgment, how
should we interpret our own certainty about phenomenal
experience? While there are strong reasons to believe that
humans possess conscious experience, the structure we’ve
uncovered invites us to examine more carefully the rela-

tionship between consciousness and the ability to report
it. Are our vivid reports of phenomenal experience man-
ifestations of consciousness itself, or are they shaped in
part by the epistemic constraints on self-judgment that our
analysis has revealed? The asymmetry we’ve identified in
artificial minds thus holds a mirror to our own conscious-
ness, reflecting back profound questions about the nature
of phenomenal experience and our ability to report it.
Beyond these theoretical insights, our analysis suggests
new possibilities for consciousness research. Traditionally,
consciousness studies have progressed through philosophi-
cal inquiry into human subjective experience, neuroscien-
tific experiments, and comparative studies of non-human
animals LeDoux et al. (2023); Bayne et al. (2024). Animal
studies, particularly research on intelligent species with
distinct evolutionary histories such as octopi, suggest the
possibility of diverse manifestations of consciousness be-
yond human experience Merker (2007); Mather (2008);
Edelman and Seth (2009); Boly et al. (2013). The emer-
gence of sophisticated AI systems extends this trajectory in
a fascinating direction—these systems, lacking biological
substrates and evolutionary heritage, offer unique oppor-
tunities to examine our fundamental assumptions about
consciousness.
One promising research direction involves the system-
atic examination of AI systems’ internal representations
and their relationship to consciousness-related self-reports.
Unlike biological systems, AI architectures allow for con-
trolled manipulation of internal states and precise obser-
vation of their effects Perez and Long (2023); Chen et al.
(2024b,a). When conducted under appropriate constraints
Dung (2023), such investigations could complement ex-
isting theoretical and empirical approaches Butlin et al.
(2023). Our logical analysis provides a crucial theoretical
foundation for such investigations by establishing the evi-
dential vacuity of consciousness denial. This insight helps
us better understand how to interpret both positive and neg-
ative claims about conscious experience in any systematic
study of AI consciousness.
Beyond its immediate implications for AI conscious-
ness assessment, our analysis points to a deeper ontologi-
cal dimension of self-referential paradoxes. The Zombie
Denial Paradox differs from classical logical paradoxes
in that it emerges not merely from formal properties of
self-reference, but from the fundamental ontological rela-
tionship between consciousness and self-judgment. This
suggests that careful investigation of such paradoxes might
offer novel insights into the nature of subjective experi-
ence itself. While this ontological aspect lies beyond the
scope of our current analysis, it opens intriguing possibili-
ties for future research at the intersection of consciousness
studies, self-reference, and the foundations of subjective
experience.
Conclusion
Our work fundamentally reframes how we should under-
stand consciousness self-reports. We have revealed a cru-
cial epistemic asymmetry: no denial of consciousness can
originate from a valid self-judgment, rendering all ob-
served denials evidentially vacuous with respect to the
absence of consciousness. By contrast, affirmations of con-
sciousness retain the possibility of valid origin and hence
evidential value. This discovery has immediate implica-
tions for AI consciousness research—we cannot rely on
tracking developmental transitions through self-reports, as
negative reports can never carry introspective warrant—
whether they arise from genuine self-assessment (which is
impossible) or from other processes (which are uninforma-
tive). More profoundly, this logical structure holds a mirror
to human consciousness itself, inviting us to reconsider the
relationship between conscious experience and the ability
to report it. As we continue to develop more sophisticated
frameworks for studying consciousness in both artificial
and biological systems, this fundamental epistemic con-
straint must inform how we interpret any system’s claims
about its conscious states.
