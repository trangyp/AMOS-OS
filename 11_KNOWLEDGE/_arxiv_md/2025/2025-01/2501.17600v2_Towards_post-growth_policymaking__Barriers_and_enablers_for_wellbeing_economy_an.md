Abstract 
Providing wellbeing for all while safeguarding planetary boundaries may require 
governments to pursue post-growth policies. To understand how post-growth 
policymaking can be fostered, we examine wellbeing economy and Doughnut 
economics government initiatives across governance scales in Europe, New 
Zealand, and Canada. To identify political dimensions of barriers and enablers as 
well as priorities for future action, we apply a framework that distinguishes polity, 
politics, and policy to analyze the data.  
We find that the main barriers are polity-related contextual factors while the main 
enablers are the political agency of key individuals and positive framings of post-
growth visions. Despite the focus of the post-growth literature on policies, our 
results suggest they have limited transformative potential in the current system. 
The overarching economic growth paradigm severely limits the initiatives’ scope of 
action. Highly motivated individuals and high-level political support are more 
essential in driving initiatives than pressure from civil society. Practitioners who 
promote growth-critical perspectives often face tensions: they need to appeal to 
broad stakeholder groups while avoiding cooptation. Overall, our findings suggest 
that further structural changes are required to support post-growth initiatives: a 
greater presence of post-growth approaches in economics education and the 
media is required for post-growth discourses to become more accepted, and to 
build understandings of how growth dependencies can be addressed. 
Key words: Post-growth, transformations, state, wellbeing economy, Doughnut 
economics

1. Introduction 
Providing wellbeing for all while staying within planetary boundaries would require moving 
towards post-growth societies in which the provisioning of human wellbeing and the 
safeguarding of ecological outcomes are prioritized over economic growth (D’Alisa et al., 
2015; Jackson, 2021; Slameršak et al., 2024). Post-growth approaches include degrowth, 
a-growth, Doughnut economics, and post-growth-aligned wellbeing economy perspectives 
(Hayden, 2025; Kallis et al., 2025).  
Various actors and strategies of transformations could contribute to transforming current 
economic systems towards post-growth. “Symbiotic approaches” work within established 
political institutions of the state to transform the current system from within (Wright, 2019). 
Several scholars suggest that post-growth-aligned policy decisions must form part of and 
can foster wider transformations towards post-growth societies, together with strategies 
pursued by civil society actors outside of established political institutions (e.g. Hirvilammi, 
2020; Koch, 2022).  
However, so far, government-led post-growth initiatives remain restricted to "window-
dressing activities" (McCartney et al., 2023), and “weak post-growth narratives” (Mason & 
Büchs, 2023). To pave the way for a wider political adoption of post-growth approaches we 
ask the question: What are the most important enablers of and barriers for government-led 
post-growth initiatives?  
To help answer this question and identify priorities for future actions, we examine wellbeing 
economy and Doughnut economics initiatives within governments. Wellbeing economy 
refers to an economy geared towards the provision of human wellbeing within ecological 
limits (Fioramonti et al., 2022). Doughnut economics outlines a “safe and just space for 
humanity” in which basic human needs are met without transgressing ecological 
boundaries (Raworth, 2017).  
Existing empirical studies of wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics initiatives are 
based on few empirical cases and do not jointly examine wellbeing economy and Doughnut 
economics initiatives. In addition, current analysis of barriers and enablers has not applied 
clear frameworks to assess their relative importance. We address this gap in the literature 
by, first, collecting primary data from 14 wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics 
initiatives across 9 European countries, New Zealand and Canada across local, regional, 
and national governance scales. Second, we employ an explicit analytical framework which 
allows us to classify the relevance of barriers and enablers.  
We analyze barriers and enablers across the political dimensions of polity, politics, and 
policy (hereafter PPP). Polity refers to the constituting structures of society that give rise to 
the processes (politics) through which regulations and activities (policies) emerge (Pichler, 
2023; Rohe, 1994). Thus, the framework encompasses different aspects of the state. 
Barriers related to all three political dimensions need to be addressed for radical changes 
in policymaking, and societal transformation more broadly (Patterson et al., 2017; Pichler, 
2023). However, understanding which barriers are structural, and thus the most relevant, 
allows us to identify priorities for future actions. Overall, our research empirically

2. Literature review 
In this section, we review previous literature on the role of the state in post-growth 
transformations. Then, we focus on relevant empirical literature on the enablers of, and 
barriers to, post-growth-leaning government initiatives. 
2.1 The role of the state in post-growth transformations 
The literature presents different roles of the state in social-ecological transformations 
depending on the respective theoretical and ideological perspectives taken (Brand, 2016). 
Recent contributions based on materialist state theories advocate the state as a relevant 
actor to steer transformations, alongside civil society which operates outside state 
institutions (Bärnthaler, 2024a; D’Alisa & Kallis, 2020; Koch, 2020). Koch (2020, p. 127) 
argues that post-growth transformations will require “a combination of bottom-up 
mobilisations and action and top-down regulation”. In addition, he identifies internal 
struggles within the state as an important terrain of post-growth transformations. Several 
post-growth scholars highlight the role of the state for transformation. For instance, 
according to Eckersley (2021), the state is uniquely placed to facilitate and enforce far-
reaching changes in societal organization due to its regulatory power and Bärnthaler 
(2024a) argues that acquiring top–down  within the state must form an essential part of 
strategizing, if degrowth-aligned ideas are to be implemented on a societal scale. However, 
it remains unclear to what extent state actors can promote post-growth transformations. 
This article aims to contribute to this debate. 
2.2 Barriers and enablers for post-growth-leaning government 
initiatives  
Recent empirical studies have examined the barriers and enablers of wellbeing economy 
(Bärnthaler et al., 2024; Hayden & Dasilva, 2022; Mason & Büchs, 2023; Trebeck, 2024) and 
Doughnut economics (Cattaneo et al., 2025; Khmara & Kronenberg, 2023; Schmid, 2025; 
Turner & Wills, 2022) initiatives. Apart from national WE initiatives, most research focuses 
on local or regional levels. We also review related studies on barriers and enablers in social-
ecological transformations of provisioning (Hirvilammi et al., 2023; Kreinin et al., 2024) as 
these initiatives pursue similar objectives and policies.


## Page 4


The wellbeing economy studies rely either on small interview samples (Mason & Büchs, 
2023; Trebeck, 2024) or solely on secondary data (Bärnthaler et al., 2024; Hayden & Dasilva, 
2022). The Doughnut economics studies are all case-based. Two wellbeing economy 
studies explore power dynamics, concentrating on narratives (Mason & Büchs, 2023) and 
regulation theory (Bärnthaler et al., 2024). Hayden and Dasilva (2022) discuss their findings 
in relation to the sufficiency literature while Trebeck’s (2024) report does not apply an 
explicit analytical framework.  
The Doughnut economics studies focus on urban transformations, drawing on ecological 
economics (Cattaneo et al., 2025), municipalism (Schmid, 2025), urban degrowth 
economics (Khmara & Kronenberg, 2023) and sustainability governance literature (Turner 
& Wills, 2022). The two provisioning systems studies engage with structural barriers to 
transforming provisioning systems (Kreinin et al., 2024) and with enablers for an eco-social 
welfare state (Hirvilammi et al., 2023). 
To our knowledge, no study draws on primary data from wellbeing economy and Doughnut 
economics initiatives across governance scales and country contexts. In addition, previous 
studies have not classified the enablers and barriers according to the political dimension 
that they address. The present study aims to address this gap.  
In the next two sections (Sections 2.2.1 and 2.2.2), we summarize relevant empirical 
findings on the barriers to and enablers of respective initiatives.  
2.2.1 Barriers 
Existing studies identify key barriers for post-growth initiatives, including the entrenched 
economic growth paradigm, institutional inertia, power imbalances, and limited capacity 
and resources.  
The economic growth paradigm characterizes capitalist economies and claims that 
economic growth is “desirable, imperative, and essentially limitless” (Schmelzer, 2015, p. 
264). It is deeply institutionalized through structural growth dependencies and political 
priorities (Kreinin et al., 2024; Mason & Büchs, 2023; Schmid, 2025). Furthermore, it is 
culturally entrenched, for instance, through the dominance of neoclassical economics in 
education and the widespread association of economic growth with wellbeing (Hayden & 
Dasilva, 2022; Kreinin et al., 2024; Mason & Büchs, 2023).  
Path dependencies and entrenched ways of working in political institutions further restrict 
changes, for example through short-termism, siloed organization, and a general tendency 
of the system to pull towards the status quo (Cattaneo et al., 2025; Mason & Büchs, 2023; 
Trebeck, 2024; Turner & Wills, 2022).  
Post-growth initiatives lack broad-based political and public support, facing powerful 
opposing interests in their implementation, also within government (Trebeck, 2024). For 
instance, Doughnut economics initiatives sometimes prioritize political feasibility over 
scientific recommendations (Cattaneo et al., 2025; Turner & Wills, 2022). Even in the six 
Wellbeing Economy Governments (see Section 4.1), post-growth proponents remain a 
minority within public administrations (Mason & Büchs, 2023). Economic actors and vested


## Page 5


interests also pose significant resistance to wellbeing economy initiatives more broadly 
(Bärnthaler et al., 2024; Mason & Büchs, 2023).  
Finally, capacity constraints, especially at the local level, limit the ability to implement 
changes in a multi-level governance context (Trebeck, 2024; Turner & Wills, 2022). 
Hirvilammi et al. (2023) underscore the need for adequate funding, knowledge-sharing, and 
learning opportunities to support these efforts. 
2.2.2 Enablers 
Existing studies identify several key enablers: the agreeability of frameworks, the formation 
of broad alliances, participatory governance, and coherence across governance levels. 
In Doughnut economics initiatives, accessibility of the visualization, breadth and 
agreeability of the approach have been useful to unite a broad base of stakeholders 
(Cattaneo et al., 2025; Khmara & Kronenberg, 2023; Schmid, 2025; Turner & Wills, 2022). 
However, referring to the wellbeing economy, studies suggest the breadth could also risk 
cooptation with pro-growth meanings and policies (Mason & Büchs, 2023; Trebeck, 2024). 
Building unconventional and non-partisan collaborations (Bärnthaler et al., 2024; 
Hirvilammi et al., 2023; Turner & Wills, 2022), and establishing participatory democratic 
processes (Bärnthaler et al., 2024; Kreinin et al., 2024), have been identified as useful 
means to strengthen the inclusiveness of initiatives and the influence of non-corporate 
actors. In addition, the re-politization of political responses to multiple crises through 
pressure from civil society could foster the implementation of transformative regulations 
(Kreinin et al., 2024; Trebeck, 2024).  
Moreover, several authors suggest that acting coherently across governance scales 
through consistent policy framework and goal setting, is necessary to facilitate systemic 
changes in economic and political structures (Bärnthaler et al., 2024; Hirvilammi et al., 
2023; Trebeck, 2024; Turner & Wills, 2022).  
While previous studies have identified various barriers and enablers, we lack an 
understanding of their relative importance. The present study aims to address this gap by 
applying the PPP framework.  
3. The PPP framework 
The threefold distinction between polity, politics and policy is a conceptual framework from 
political science. It allows us to understand political phenomena in their multiple 
dimensions.  Outlining political dimensions of social-ecological transformations, Görg et 
al. (2017, p. 9) argue that “beside the focus on policies (e.g., certain environmental 
measures) the very structures of polity (i.e., institutionalised forms of policies) and of 
politics (i.e., actors and conflicts about structures and political strategies) needs to be 
transformed towards sustainability.” Thus, we apply the PPP framework to gain a holistic 
understanding of barriers and enablers for wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics 
initiatives across political dimensions. This allows us, first, to identify which barriers are


## Page 6


structural, and therefore more difficult to tackle, and second, potential entry points for 
symbiotic strategies.  
We draw on Pichler’s (2023) understanding of polity which includes both political and 
economic structures. In this view, necessary changes in political structures are dependent 
on and have the potential to lead to changes in wider capitalist structures. Thus, the 
dimension of polity encompasses the economic growth imperative and structural features 
of political institutions (Pichler, 2023).  
Politics refers to the processes through which actors pursue competing interests and 
negotiate change. Transformation efforts are inherently contested and shaped by both the 
institutional context and underlying power relations (Patterson et al., 2017; Pichler, 2023). 
While agency focused definitions of power treat it as mainly as part of politics (Pichler, 
2023), we account also for the structural power inherent in norms and institutions which 
form the polity. 
Policy includes the instruments and outcomes of political decision-making. These are 
shaped by existing institutional structures and actor dynamics but can, in turn, reshape 
political practice, either perpetuating unsustainability or driving changes (Pichler, 2023). 
Thus, despite referring to the outcomes of policymaking processes, we included “policy” 
as a dimension of enablers and barriers. For example, Pichler (2023) names eco-social 
policies and phase-out policies as transformative policy examples.  
We know from theoretical and empirical literature on transformations that crises can foster 
opportunities for change (e.g. Buch-Hansen, 2018; Trebeck, 2024). Crises can be external 
to political and economic structures (e.g. the COVID-19 pandemic). However, many crises 
are caused by, and part of, existing political and economic structures. In addition, external 
crises can trigger “internal” crises of legitimacy within political and economic structures. 
External and internal crises can have various impacts on the three political dimensions: 
Within polity, crises may expose institutional failures and reduce trust in or acceptance of 
institutions; in politics, they can trigger the loss of support for political leaders and heighten 
conflicts between interest groups; and in policy, they may create pressure to introduce new 
policies. However, whether “external” crises lead to change depends on how actors 
interpret and respond to them. Thus, while crises themselves are not necessarily part of 
one specific dimension, we capture their effects in the framework.   
4. Methods 
Qualitative research allows us to investigate process-oriented questions in depth 
(Derrington, 2019). We chose expert interviews to gain access to first-hand experiences of 
processes as well as contextual knowledge about the initiatives (Meuser & Nagel, 2009). 
Table A1 in the appendix provides an overview of interviews.  
4.1 Data collection  
We prepared the sampling process by conducting desk research on WE and DE initiatives. 
The Wellbeing Economy Governments (WEGo), an informal forum of the governments of


## Page 7


Wales, Scotland, Finland, Iceland, New Zealand and Canada dedicated to fostering 
wellbeing economy (Mason & Büchs, 2023), and Doughnut economics initiatives in regions 
and cities (Doughnut Economics Action Lab, 2023), were the starting point for our sample. 
We chose these initiatives due to their transformative potential building on post-growth 
visions and their uptake in different localities.  
The data collection was part of a wider sample that included “post-growth” and “green 
growth” oriented initiatives based on their publicly available self-descriptions. While we 
limit the data in this study to “post-growth” oriented initiatives, it is important to flag that 
the official label that initiatives give themselves may not always reflect the mindsets of all 
actors involved or the priorities in actual decision-making.  
To be included in this study’s sample, each initiative had to fulfill the following selection 
criteria: 
• 
The initiative is based on the Doughnut economics or wellbeing economy concept. 
• 
The initiative is based at the national or sub-national governance level. 
• 
The initiative is based in Europe or in a WEGo country.  
• 
The initiative is implemented by the government or public administration. 
The final data included in this study consist of 18 interviews from 14 initiatives across local, 
regional, and national scales in 11 countries (for an overview see Table A1 in the appendix). 
Initiatives include all six WEGos and Doughnut economics initiatives at city and regional 
levels. Interviewees included policymakers, policy experts, and two academics who had 
close involvement with an initiative. Speaking to practitioners involved in the initiatives 
allowed us to access expert knowledge about the processes of their emergence and 
implementation. Including external experts in the sample complemented the data with 
more systematic accounts of barriers and enablers across contexts (Smith & Elger, 2014).  
The semi-structured interviews were conducted online between November 2023 and 
March 2024 and lasted between 40 and 80 minutes. The interviews were recorded and 
transcribed using the “intelligent verbatim” approach and supported by the automatic 
transcripts generated by recording software. Interviews were conducted in English, except 
for one interview conducted in German and translated to English. All transcripts were 
pseudonymized before analysis and each interview was given a number and a code 
identifying the type of initiative (see Table A1 in the appendix for an explanation of the 
codes). 
4.2 Data analysis  
The interviews were analyzed following a reflexive thematic analysis approach (Braun & 
Clarke, 2022) with support of the qualitative data analysis software N-Vivo. In line with our 
overarching research question, and based on the three political dimensions outlined 
above, we posed the following analytical question to the data:  
What are polity-, politics-, and policy-related enablers of — and barriers to — wellbeing 
economy and Doughnut economics initiatives?


## Page 8


After familiarizing ourselves with the data, the interviews were coded deductively, starting 
with the codes of “policy”, “politics”, and “policy”, “barriers” and “enablers”. Statements 
qualified to be included as a barrier or enabler if they described actualized experiences of 
the interviewee, rather than potential or theoretical enablers or barriers. Throughout the 
coding process, detailed memos were written to record which factors were described and 
their relations to the political dimensions. After coding the interviews, the notes and the 
codes were reviewed. Then, in an iterative process of reviewing barriers and enablers from 
the literature and rereading the coded data segments, enablers and barriers were clustered 
across political dimensions.

5.1 Polity 
5.1.1 Barriers 
Interviewees described the initiatives’ embeddedness in a growth-dependent economic 
system and the structuring of government as barriers.  
Several interviewees mentioned the embeddedness in a growth-dependent economic 
system as a barrier. For instance, a representative of a wellbeing economy initiative stated 
that this embeddedness limits “the capacity for any single country to ultimately transform 
or deal with, let's say, the climate crisis” (Interview 12, WE/E). Interviewees at the regional 
and local scale underscored the limited ability of subnational scales to move beyond 
framework conditions set by the national level because “at the moment, the growth idea, 
economic growth, is so embedded in kind of everything” (Interview 5, DE/E/R). One 
interviewee stated that within their institution economic growth was seen as “more 
important than nature” (Interview 16, WE/P/N) because it was understood as necessary to 
achieve social outcomes.  
Across interviews, the organization of governmental institutions was described as 
hampering integrative, post-growth-oriented policymaking. For example, a lack of 
monetary resources and a lack of capacity of practitioners to take on additional workload 
were common barriers. A lack of structural funding even led to the foreseeable end of 
multiple post-growth leaning initiatives. This was explicitly compared to other departments 
within government that were more aligned with economic priorities: “for example, in the 
Ministry of Finance, the thinking is quite old school and [...] they have the money, so they 
have the power" (Interview 16, WE/P/N).  
Siloed ways of working in government was pointed out as another key barrier. In interview 
14 (WE/E) the interviewee stated, “even though the wellbeing economy is meant to be this 
overarching thing, that's about aligning and designing the whole of government, they've 
effectively managed to silo it as well.” Other interviewees referred to silos related to 
thinking about environmental and social challenges separately and limited responsibilities 
of people working on the wellbeing economy. In addition, short-term thinking from 
politicians who “want to see short term results” (Interview 3, WE/P/R) was a challenge. 
5.1.2 Enablers 
Interviewees describe the exposure to external crises, in particular, the COVID-19 crisis 
and related societal changes, as placing wellbeing higher on the public and government 
agenda and triggering system rethinking. For example, Interviewee 1 (DE/P/L) remembered 
how “some exceptional actions” related to the energy crisis and the war in Ukraine, as well 
as the “double context of accelerating alerts on global change etc. made it really important 
for the city to really take action and to decide to move forward within [the Doughnut 
economics] framework”.


## Page 10


Passing enabling legislation that provides the framework for governments to prioritize 
human wellbeing and environmental outcomes was suggested by two interviewees as a 
useful step to enforce post-growth approaches long-term. Interviewee 3 (WE/P/R) shared 
the experience that in their constituency the “big and enabling legislative framework […] is 
dictating everything we do and everything we want to achieve in the next years, decades and 
years to come.” Interviewee 10 (WE/E) agreed that “legal frameworks and change is really 
important, like rights of nature and ecocide law to kind of say, hey look, these are the 
boundaries within which every government needs to operate.” 
5.2 Politics 
5.2.1 Barriers  
Interviewees described various expressions of hegemonic pro-growth discourses. 
Discourses can act as context, predefining actors’ behavior and problem framing (polity), 
while also becoming a site of ideological struggle (politics). Since the contestation and 
proactive shaping of discourses is a central point of conflict between actors in political 
processes, we understand them to be part of politics. Individuals sympathetic to post-
growth working in administrations reported that they are confronted with attitudes by 
colleagues which imply that “sustainability, eco [...] that's all well and good, but the 
numbers, that's what counts” (Interview 7, DE/P/L). Having conversations in which the 
pursuit of economic growth would be questioned was described as highly polarizing, 
because “it scares so much people to talk directly on that question” (Interview 13, DE/P/L) 
and “if you say that explicitly, you are completely shutting off the conversation with your 
conversation partner” (Interview 18, WE/A).  
With respect to the approaches to policymaking within political institutions, interviewees 
indicated that “a lot of people are just very resistant to change in general” (Interview 17, 
DE/P/L). For example, discussing the implementation of a Doughnut initiative, an 
interviewee stated: “So what I see happening if we were to implement the tool I mentioned 
is that people were going ‘ohh well, that will be so much work’, and ‘don't we do a lot of good 
things already’ and ‘why do we need to use this’.” (Interview 17, DE/P/L). 
Changes in or termination of a supportive government led to challenges for, or even the 
end of, multiple initiatives. Interviewees described a partisan perception of the initiatives 
as particularly challenging.1 For example, Interviewee 4 (PG/P/R), recalled the tension that 
comes with high-level political support: “So […] on one hand, it [support from a governing 
party] has helped us to accelerate and to give us the possibility via subsidies to start the 
work. But on the other hand, it's also a bottleneck […] because it doesn't allow us to go 
broader”. 
Powerful opposing actors are a key barrier for initiatives. These were mainly economic 
actors, including “multinationals and big organizations” (Interview 10, WE/E), “some

5.2.2 Enablers 
Interviewees described how multiple crises led to a shift in narratives and a perceived 
urgency to act. People’s experience of multiple crises “just makes it easier and easier 
obviously to tell the story that the system is not working, our economy is not working 
because everybody can see it now” (Interview 14, WE/E). Interviewee 20 (WE/E) reported 
that for one WEGo “[...] COVID and that increasing public dissatisfaction, that was a big 
impetus and catalyst [...]”. 
Another enabler was the emergence of a discourse around alternative measures of welfare 
at the international level influenced by the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi report (2009). At the same 
time, the introduction of initiatives influenced political norms within institutions: “you can 
see the change internally in the type of conversations that colleagues have with each other, 
the types of considerations that get raised and then the tone and the focus of the papers 
that get produced and the balance that’s struck between competing objectives” (Interview 
15, WE/P/N). 
Initiatives depend on key individuals who are intrinsically motivated to drive the initiative, 
as well as on high-level political support. An interviewee from a WEGo observed that 
“anywhere around the world where this has taken hold there usually is a president or a 
prime minister or a finance ministry or treasury, or a king who makes this their thing that 
they push” (Interview 11, WE/P/N). This enabler directly relates to the reported barrier of 
vulnerability to changes in government. In Interview 17 (DE/P/L) the interviewee described 
that they “feel it's very dependent on people. So if you have people in a department that […] 
go like ‘we need to do something with this, I'm gonna set this up […]”. 
Building alliances between peer initiatives (e.g. through the WEGos or Doughnut 
Economics Action Lab), as well as support from international organizations (e.g. the 
OECD), were considered useful for peer learning and capacity building. While civil society 
action as a driving force was rather the exception, Interviewee 12 (WE/E) described how in 
one WEGo initiative “it was really like a mid-level civil servant who […] sort of rallied and 
developed a really strategic group of citizens and advocacy organizations to put pressure 
on the government to ultimately pass this type of [WE] legislation”.  
Having a wide variety of societal actors take part in the initiative was considered a necessity 
for them to be successful. Initiatives drew for example on participatory approaches to 
shape government strategies. These approaches were discussed as theoretically having 
the potential to contribute to redistributing power to citizens, raising public support, and 
providing legitimacy for the initiative with the public. However, interviewees provided a 
mixed picture as to whether the recommendations of respective bodies were being 
considered in decision-making. Moreover, interviewees criticized problems of accessibility 
and representation in participatory processes.

Furthermore, the breadth and agreeability of the wellbeing economy and Doughnut 
economics allowed for a broad support base. Interviewees reported that they would start 
their work from easily agreeable points and frame their work in a non-confrontational way: 
“quite a lot of people can sign up to [the WE] I think, which is the strength and the weakness

5.2 Policy  
5.2.1 
Barriers 
The broadness of the concepts and signs of cooptation were reflected in a disconnect 
between the established tools and the actual priorities in policymaking. The lack of a 
common definition of the core concepts within government has allowed for a stretch of the 
wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics concepts towards pre-established, 
economic growth–focused policy goals. As Interviewee 10 (WE/E) put it: “I feel like a lot of 
the wellbeing frameworks that we're seeing are not really conceptual frameworks. They're 
like selections of indicators […] but nothing in them really says that our economies are 
embedded within our societies and our environments. And I think that's a huge paradigm 
shift that we’re still missing”.  
In addition, there is a lack of coherence. On the one hand, this refers to the multiplicity of 
sustainability visions used in policymaking within the same government. For example, in 
Interview 16 (WE/P/N) the interviewee reported that ”people don't understand […] why don't 
we talk about the […] Sustainable Development Goals or why don't we talk about green 
transition? […] And the problem is that there are too many concepts like in a competition.” 
On the other hand, there is also a lack of coherence on the policy level related to the siloed 
ways of working within government. For example, in Interview 13 (DE/P/L) the interviewee 
suggested that the Doughnut portrait developed in the environmental department of the 
city will not lead to “an economic strategy in that sense also because the city council, 
parallel to this developed [its] own economic strategy. And both things don't talk too much.”  
5.3.2 Enablers 
We did not identify enablers in the dimension of polity in our interview data. Interviewees 
did not mention pre-established tools or policies that enable the emergence or 
implementation of post-growth-aligned initiatives (apart from legislative frameworks, see 
section 5.1.2).  
6. Discussion 
6.1 Main findings 
Overall, we find that wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics initiatives within 
government remain very restricted in their scope of action by the overarching economic 
growth paradigm. This is reflected in their dependence on growth-based economic


## Page 13


structures, the hegemony of a pro-growth discourse (which makes it inopportune to 
criticize growth), and the continued importance of GDP as an indicator in policymaking. 
Thus, shifts in polity-related contextual factors and hegemonic pro-growth discourses are 
necessary for initiatives to gain more transformative potential.  
Practitioners aiming to introduce growth-critical perspectives face tensions between 
broadening stakeholder support and maintaining project longevity on the one hand and 
advocating bold changes and avoiding cooptation on the other. Therefore, the breadth and 
agreeability of the wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics concepts can be seen as 
a strength and as a weakness. 
Our findings highlight the importance of highly motivated individuals and internal high-level 
support in advancing post-growth agendas. The initiatives rely heavily on the power of key 
individuals and high-level political support from incumbent governments.  In the interviews 
this support emerged as more important than external civil society pressure. This finding 
illustrates that, even within pro-growth institutions, individual actors retain some agency to 
promote post-growth approaches. However, the reliance on individuals’ power makes the 
initiatives vulnerable to political changes and thereby counteracts the possibilities of 
fostering long-term transformations. In addition, their influence is limited by structural 
power asymmetries and entrenched ways of working. Our findings suggest a lack of power 
of post-growth initiatives relative to opposing interests. The lack of power is evident in the 
lack of funding, also relative to other departments within government. Building stronger 
alliances with civil society may help reinforce the initiatives and enhance accountability to 
their post-growth ambitions. Moreover, gaining discursive power could be a first entry point 
for the initiatives to strengthen their position.  
6.2 Polity 
Changes in contextual-polity related factors are a precondition for transformative shifts 
across politics and policy. By their very nature governmental institutions favor dominant 
ideas and therefore tend to have a conserving function (Buch-Hansen, 2018; Jessop, 
2016). To tackle the engrained growth paradigm within institutions, changes beyond the 
institutional structures themselves are necessary. Hammond (2025) suggests that the 
development of a social consciousness of the current (growth) hegemony would be the 
starting point to transform the state through its imperative for democratic legitimation. As 
some interviewees suggested, the unfolding of multiple crises may accelerate processes 
of new “meaning making”.  
Enshrining post-growth priorities in a legislative framework could directly address the 
barriers of siloed ways of working and short-term thinking by formulating long-term, cross-
governmental priorities. Moreover, to be able to adopt integrated ways of working, 
practitioners in government institutions require sufficient resources — including time, 
funding, and education. From our perspective, better funding and staffing of public 
administration needs to be part of the post-growth movement’s demands if government 
institutions are to have the capacity to move towards post-growth policymaking.


## Page 14


6.3 Politics 
Interviewees noted a growing awareness of multiple crises and an increased focus on 
wellbeing as a policy goal, both within governments and civil society. This suggests that 
there could be potential for post-growth discourses to gain traction. Framings that 
emphasize increases in wellbeing and equality in post-growth futures (Paulson & Büchs, 
2022) and underscore the benefits of post-growth policies that will be shared by the 
majority of the population (Strunz & Schindler, 2018) could resonate with a wide range of 
people in society.  
Our findings support Schmid’s (2025) observation that practitioners in Doughnut 
economics initiatives strategically avoid direct critiques of economic growth to maintain 
broader appeal. However, this avoidance of direct critique may leave initiatives vulnerable 
to green growth co-optation. To support practitioners in introducing a growth-critical 
discourse, Schmid (2025) proposes a five-step strategy: (1) Clarifying what is meant by 
growth (disambiguation);  (2) examining how prioritization of growth currently informs 
policymaking (scrutinization); (3) acknowledging where prioritizing growth contradicts 
social and environmental objectives (acknowledgement); (4) identifying areas where 
growth orientation is counter-productive and exploring alternative approaches 
(partitioning); and (5) exploring (municipal) growth-dependencies and alternative strategies 
(generalization). This stepwise approach may enable more constructive debates about the 
challenges of the continuous pursuit of economic growth.  
Broad-based alliance building has also been identified as a necessary precondition for 
transformative changes (e.g. Bärnthaler, 2024a; Buch-Hansen, 2018). Some interviewees 
discussed pressure from civil society as an enabler. Overall, however, proactive 
engagement by interviewees with civil society actors was limited. A strategic alliance 
between post-growth-minded practitioners and civil society actors could be a promising 
avenue to challenge institutionalized growth-based logics of policymaking and to put 
pressure on governments. In addition, it could contribute to changing mentalities and 
create consent for post-growth transformations in both political and civil society 
(Bärnthaler, 2024b).  
6.4 Policy 
We did not identify specific enablers in the policy dimension: existing barriers have so far 
prevented their implementation. To date, much post-growth research focuses on 
identifying policies for post-growth societies (e.g. Cosme et al., 2017; Fitzpatrick et al., 
2022; Kallis et al., 2025). However, policies have limited transformative potential in the 
given polity and politics. Thus, our findings suggest that identifying the institutional and 
political conditions under which transformative changes could be implemented might be 
more effective in fostering transformations. 
Nevertheless, if incremental policy changes were chosen strategically, they could 
contribute to more ambitious changes later on (Paulson & Büchs, 2022). For example, 
universal basic services could provide first-hand “experience” of alternative provisioning to 
the majority of people, appealing to high-income and low-income groups alike as well as to


## Page 15


people with a variety of values (Bärnthaler, 2024a; Paulson & Büchs, 2022).  In addition, 
addressing siloed ways of working and fostering growth-critical discourse could also 
reduce policy fragmentation and strengthen the connection between declared goals and 
actual implementation. 
6.5 Applying the PPP framework to post-growth transformations 
The PPP framework enables us to gain a more nuanced understanding of various barriers 
and enablers for post-growth initiatives across institutional structures, political processes, 
and policy outcomes.  However, the framework is limited in a number of ways.  
First, the framework allows classification of different aspects, but it does not provide a 
theory of change. In principle, the institutional structures (i.e. polity) shape the processes 
(politics) that lead to policy outcomes. However, this is not a one-way causality. In practice, 
there are feedback loops between dimensions and entry points for transformative changes 
that are split across polity and politics. Both dimensions contain deep leverage points. 
From a leverage points perspective, polity determines the rules of the system, while politics 
sets its goals. The items related to policies are rather shallow leverage points (Meadows, 
1999). Thus, it could be polity or politics or their intersection that could enable systemic 
changes. However, the PPP framework does not provide information about the interaction 
between dimensions, nor about how enablers might address respective barriers. Therefore, 
the framework itself does not allow detailed conclusions about the causalities of change.  
Second, some barriers and enablers relate to more than one political dimension (e.g. 
economic growth paradigm, discourses). The classification of some of the barriers and 
enablers into only one of the categories risks invisibilising their influence across 
dimensions. Other influencing factors are not captured by the PPP framework altogether 
(e.g. external crises). We have addressed this shortcoming by outlining our understanding 
of how respective concepts relate to the framework. However, future research applying the 
PPP framework to post-growth transformations could address this shortcoming by 
introducing a category of “external factors” that captures how external factors influence 
barriers and enablers within the three political dimensions (see figure 1).

6.6 Contributions, limitations and future research  
Our research empirically contributes to understanding the role of government-led post-
growth initiatives in post-growth transformations in several ways. First, existing literature 
has not assessed the relative importance of barriers and enablers. We employ the PPP 
framework to investigate which barriers are structural as these are more difficult to tackle 
than non-structural ones. Second, while previous research is based on a small number of 
empirical cases of either wellbeing economy or Doughnut economics initiatives, we


## Page 17


collected primary data from wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics initiatives 
across governance levels in 9 European countries, plus New Zealand and Canada. Third, 
we provide a reflection on the application of the PPP framework to inform future empirical 
research on post-growth transformations. Finally, we formulate recommendations on how 
post-growth initiatives and post-growth policymaking could be fostered.  
It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. As we draw on insights from a 
range of countries and governance scales, we cannot provide in-depth analysis of the 
institutional settings, actor constellations, or power dynamics in respective contexts and 
governance scales. Moreover, this study does not provide an analysis of the difference in 
enablers and barriers between wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics initiatives. 
As our findings underscore the influential role of key individuals in initiating initiatives within 
government institutions, future research could explore which strategies agents of change 
in political institutions take to promote post-growth approaches in policymaking. In 
addition, future case studies of government-led post-growth initiatives would allow for the 
investigation of the power relations and possible alliances that enable or restrain post-
growth policymaking in specific contexts. Moreover, our findings could inform further 
theoretical contributions on the role of the state in post-growth transformations.  
7. Conclusion 
In this study, we have asked the question: What are the most important enablers of and 
barriers to government-led post-growth initiatives? To answer this question, we collected 
primary data from 18 interviews with practitioners and experts across local, regional, and 
national scales. We employed the three dimensions of polity, politics, and policy to 
systematize barriers and enablers.  
Overall, we find that wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics initiatives remain 
restricted in their scope of action by polity-related structures and the overarching 
economic growth paradigm. These are the main barriers to post-growth policymaking which 
will have to be addressed in the long term. Highly motivated individuals and high-level 
support from within political institutions appear to be more essential in driving the 
initiatives than pressure from civil society. However, those practitioners who aim to foster 
growth-critical perspectives face tensions between appealing to a broad base of 
stakeholders and ensuring the longevity of the project on the one hand while aiming to 
advocate bold changes and avoid cooptation on the other.  
Structural changes are required to address the greatest overarching barrier: the economic 
growth paradigm. For instance, a greater presence of post-growth approaches in 
economics education and the media would be required for post-growth discourses to 
become more accepted, and to facilitate understandings of how growth dependencies can 
be addressed. Wellbeing economy and Doughnut economics initiatives themselves could 
aim to openly criticize the continuous pursuit of economic growth and collaborate with 
initiatives that support post-growth-aligned mindsets among citizens. At the same time,


## Page 18


initiatives could strive to support economic approaches that focus on the direct 
provisioning of human needs. These economic approaches could eventually challenge the 
sociocultural hegemony of economic growth and open spaces for more ambitious post-
growth policies in the long term.
