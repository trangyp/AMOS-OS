Abstract
Analyzing data from multiple sources offers valuable opportunities to improve the esti-
mation efficiency of causal estimands. However, this analysis also poses many challenges due
to population heterogeneity and data privacy constraints. While several advanced methods
for causal inference in federated settings have been developed in recent years, many focus on
difference-based averaged causal effects and are not designed to study effect modification. In
this study, we introduce a novel targeted-federated learning framework to study the hetero-
geneity of treatment effects (HTEs) for a targeted population by proposing a projection-based
estimand. This HTE framework integrates information from multiple data sources without
sharing raw data, while accounting for covariate distribution shifts among sources. Our pro-
posed approach is shown to be doubly robust, conveniently supporting both difference-based
estimands for continuous outcomes and odds ratio-based estimands for binary outcomes.
Furthermore, we develop a communication-efficient bootstrap-based selection procedure to
detect non-transportable data sources, thereby enhancing robust information aggregation
without introducing bias. The superior performance of the proposed estimator over exist-
ing methods is demonstrated through extensive simulation studies, and the utility of our
approach has been shown in a real-world data application using nationwide Medicare-linked
data.
Keywords: Categorical outcomes; Doubly robust estimation; Federated learning; Heterogeneity of
treatment effects.

INTRODUCTION
Precision medicine aims to tailor healthcare decisions to individual patient characteristics, recog-
nizing that treatment effects often vary across different subpopulations. This variation, known
as heterogeneity of treatment effects (HTEs), has critical implications for clinical practice, health
policy, and equitable care delivery [18]. A one-size-fits-all estimate of treatment efficacy, such as
the average treatment effect (ATE), may mask important differences in how subgroups respond
to interventions, potentially leading to suboptimal or even harmful decisions for certain individu-
als. As a result, there is growing emphasis on identifying and quantifying HTEs to better inform
precision treatment strategies.
From a statistical perspective, HTEs can be formulated as the treatment effect as a function of
certain covariates [17, 23, 25]. In the past decade, an increasing number of methodologies have been
developed to better characterize these effects, including T-learner [19], R-learner [25], DR-learner
[17], among others. Most of these approaches primarily focus on difference-based estimands for
a continuous outcome, though extensions to other outcome types, such as conditional estimands
for time-to-event outcomes, have also been explored [23]. To estimate HTEs, existing methods
typically rely on either the propensity score (PS) or outcome regression (OR) model. When both
models are used, researchers are interested in a so-called doubly robust approach [29, 1], which
will yield a consistent estimate if either PS or OR is correctly specified, but not necessarily both.
This feature offers more protection against model specification in real data analysis and has proven
valuable in modern clinical research [31].
Furthermore, most existing literature on HTEs relies on a single dataset, which often limits
statistical power due to small sample sizes, particularly in a single-site study. With the grow-
ing availability of clinical data across institutions, there is a burgeoning interest in leveraging
multi-source data to enhance the estimation of HTEs. Federated learning has emerged as a pow-
erful paradigm for this purpose, enabling collaborative analysis across multiple institutions/study
groups/study years without the need to pool individual-level data [8]. By preserving data privacy
and complying with institutional or regulatory constraints, federated learning facilitates large-scale
inference while mitigating the risks and complexities associated with centralized data sharing.
While the majority of federated learning methods to date have focused on prediction [3, 9, 21, 13]
and classification tasks [15, 36], recent advancements have extended federated learning frameworks
2


## Page 3


to support causal inference [35, 38, 16]. However, these approaches primarily target the estimation
of ATE for the overall study population, rather than focusing on a prespecified target population
that is often of greatest interest to researchers. Such target populations may include patients
admitted in the most recent year, subpopulations from specific geographic regions, individuals
with particular baseline characteristics or comorbidities, or historically underrepresented groups
in clinical research [20, 14]. Tailoring causal inference to these specific populations can yield more
relevant and actionable insights for decision-making and policy development. More recently, [14]
developed a targeted-federated learning approach for the mean potential outcome by adapting
transfer learning techniques to leverage information from external source data and provided an
estimate of target ATE. To the best of our knowledge, however, a significant methodological gap
remains in the literature regarding the development of targeted-federated learning approaches for
estimating HTEs.
To address these methodological gaps, we aim to develop a novel targeted-federated learning
framework for estimating HTEs within a prespecified targeted population. We formally define
the underlying HTE based on a projection-based estimand, which is broadly applicable across
different outcome types, including count and binary outcomes. This new estimand is based on a
working structural model that can serve as a reasonable approximation to the underlying HTE
function. Building on this framework, we develop two doubly robust estimators: one relies solely
on the target dataset, the other integrates information from multiple datasets under the targeted-
federated paradigm. The latter estimator is doubly robust in the sense that it yields a consistent
estimate if either the OR model is correctly specified or both the PS and density ratio model are
correctly specified, but not necessarily all three. The density ratio model plays a crucial role in
calibrating the covariate distribution shift across datasets [26, 14, 4]. To further safeguard against
negative transfer and enhance robustness, we introduce a communication-efficient bootstrap-based
selection procedure designed to detect and exclude non-transportable data sources that could bias
estimation. This step ensures that only compatible information is integrated, supporting valid
and efficient information aggregation in federated settings.
A preview of the proposed framework highlights several innovations that distinguish it from
existing literature. First, the proposed HTE estimand and working structural model are novel
contributions to the HTE literature, offering a flexible yet interpretable way to capture the rela-

Before concluding this section, we describe the Neyman-Rubin causal paradigm [33, 30]. Let
Yi(a) be the potential outcome for individual i under treatment assignment Ai = a, with a ∈{0, 1}.
In practice, only one of the two potential outcomes is observed for each individual. The observed
outcome is linked to the potential outcomes through the relationship Yi = AiYi(1)+(1−Ai)Yi(0).
To enable valid causal inference, we adopt the following standard assumptions, collectively known
as the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA):
Assumption 1 (No interference) The potential outcomes of any individual are unrelated to the
treatment assignment of other individuals.
Assumption 2 (Consistency) For each individual i, if Ai = a, Yi = Yi(a).
Assumption 1 requires no interference between individuals, which is reasonable in our case study
since most of the medicare beneficiaries in our dataset who experience a hip fracture are treated
and live independently. Assumption 2 states that the observed outcome is equal to the potential
outcome under the assigned treatment, and there are no multiple versions of a treatment, which
is also plausible in our application.
2.2
Heterogeneity of treatment effect via a working structural model
In this section, we define the underlying HTE estimand of interest and introduce a novel structural
model that reasonably approximates the estimand. Under the potential outcome framework, our
focus is on the expectation of the potential outcome with A = a, for a ∈{0, 1}, conditional

on a subset of covariates ˜X ⊂X that may serve as potential effect modifiers, with the target
population defined as individuals from the source M = 1: E(Y (a)|˜X, M = 1). The estimand of
our interest is similar to those defined in the existing literature [5, 22], except that it focuses on the
targeted population (M = 1) rather than the entire population. However, the proposed estimand
poses challenges for non-parametric calculation due to its infinite dimensionality and complex
interpretation regarding modifier effects. In practice, clinical researchers are often more interested
in evaluating how specific covariates in ˜X influence the outcome, including relative risk and odds
ratio when the outcome is categorical.

interpretable results, instead of non-parametrically estimating E(Y (a)|˜X, M = 1), we consider a

2.3.2
Doubly robust targeted-federated learning
The strategy of using only target data may be underpowered to capture the heterogeneity of
treatment effects due to the limited sample size and insufficient population diversity in a single data
source. Integrating information from other data sources can help achieve a more precise estimate.

We propose a doubly robust targeted-federated estimator that achieves three goals: (1) flexibly
accommodating heterogeneity in covariate distributions across data sources, (2) preserving data
privacy by eliminating the need to share individual-level data, and (3) minimizing communication
costs and distribution times among different sites.

Assumption 5 (Positivity of treatment assignment): 0 < Pr(Ai = 1|Xi = x, Mi = m) < 1 for

2.4
Method extension
The federated estimation framework introduced in Section 2.3.2 relies on a critical assumption: the
mean conditional potential outcome is exchangeable across data sources (Assumption 8). While
this assumption is plausible for some applications, it may not hold universally, particularly in the
presence of substantial heterogeneity in source-specific populations, clinical practices, or healthcare
policies. Additionally, the proposed doubly robust estimator requires either the OR models or both
the density ratio and PS models to be correctly specified. This requirement can be challenging
to satisfy when the number of data sources is large.
Under these circumstances, integrating
information from non-transportable datasets may lead to biased estimates, a phenomenon often
referred to as “negative transfer” in the machine learning literature [37]. These practical concerns
motivate the extension of our framework to enhance its robustness and applicability in real-

REAL DATA APPLICATION
Our research interest lies in analyzing recovery outcomes among older adults who experienced a hip
fracture. Notably, hip fracture is widely recognized as one of the most debilitating injuries in older
populations, often leading to a cascade of aging-related complications, such as disability, functional
decline, loss of independence, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality [2]. However, recovery
trajectories vary considerably among patients, reflecting substantial heterogeneity in post-fracture
outcomes [7, 12]. Therefore, it is important to identify potential “driven” factors that may partially
explain poor recovery and help guide targeted interventions.
Cohort, outcome, and exposure. To maximize the utility of large-scale Medicare-linked
data while acknowledging patient-level heterogeneity, our analysis specifically focuses on a minor-
ity subgroup: Black older adults who have been shown to have worse outcomes after hip fracture in
general, but are largely unrepresented in major clinical studies making inference to more variable
patient-centered outcomes difficult [11]. Moreover, we focused on evaluating the potential differ-
ential impact of two common treatments for hip fracture: open reduction with internal fixation
15


## Page 16


(OR/IF, A = 1) vs hip arthroplasty (HA, A = 0). A recent literature found that hip arthroplasty
was associated with more favorable outcomes than internal fixation, particularly regarding post-
operative functional scoring and reoperation [6]. However, the substantial clinical heterogeneity
among hip fracture survivors highlights the need to identify moderators that drive variation in
surgical outcomes, motivating our investigation into the heterogeneity of treatment effects.
We focused on a patient-centered outcome associated with the concept of “aging in place” and
widely applied in recent literature: Days at Home (DAH) [12, 24, 32]. This claims-based metric
DAH can be defined by subtracting the total number of days spent in hospitals, skilled nursing
facilities, hospital observation, and emergency department visits from the total number of days
alive. To reflect extremely unfavorable recovery outcomes, we defined the outcome Y as one if
the total DAH was fewer than 10 days or death occurred within 12 months after a hip fracture,
and zero otherwise. For our analysis, we used the 2017 cohort (20% random samples of national
beneficiaries) that was available to our team as the target dataset and treated seven cohorts from
2010-2016 that were available to our collaborator as source datasets (5% random samples).
The study inclusion criteria required beneficiaries to be 65 years or older and have experienced
a traumatic hip fracture. Hip fractures were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes
for femoral neck or intertrochanteric fractures, while excluding those involving the pelvic rim and
acetabulum. We excluded individuals who were not community-dwelling at baseline (i.e., long-stay
nursing home residents prior to admission), those who died during their index admission for hip
fracture, were discharged against medical advice, or were hospitalized on a U.S. territory (e.g.,
Puerto Rico). Additionally, we excluded individuals who did not maintain continuous Medicare
fee-for-service enrollment for 6 months prior to and 12 months following the hip fracture admission.
In addition, states with fewer than five enrolled patients were excluded to ensure data reliability
and adequate representation. These criteria resulted in a final sample size of 1, 830 patients for
the 2017 cohort, with annual cohort sizes of 137, 213, 216, 219, 199, 195, 187, and 464 from 2010
to 2017, respectively.
Covariates and potential effect modifiers. To account for potential confounding effects,
both the propensity score and outcome regression model incorporated a comprehensive set of
covariates, including demographic characteristics, hospital-level factors, and state of residence.
Baseline patient-level factors included age group (0: ages 65–75; 1: 76–85; 2: 85+), sex (0:
16


## Page 17


male; 1: female), pre-fracture diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD),
multimorbidity evaluated by Elixhauser Comorbidity score during the 6 months prior to fracture,
and total DAH within 6 months prior to fracture. Hospital-level factors included tertiles of hospital
bed count (0: < 230 beds; 1: 231-424 beds; 2: > 425 beds) and teaching hospital status. Among
these variables, we are particularly interested in examining sex and ADRD status as potential effect
modifiers, as both have been shown to be strongly associated with post-fracture DAH outcomes
[24, 12].
Model assessment.
To support the validity of applying our method, we conducted two
assessments for assumptions. First, we examined the balance of baseline covariates in the target
dataset. The love plot in Figure 1(b) revealed that all covariates were well balanced after PS
weighting, with absolute standardized mean differences all below 0.1. This suggests that the PS
model was likely to be correctly specified for the target data analysis, and thus, the estimator
based solely on the target dataset is likely to yield consistent estimates. Second, we observed that
several covariates, such as ADRD status and age categories, exhibited distributional changes over
the years (Figure 1(c)). This finding provides evidence of covariate shifts among multiple source
datasets. After applying the density ratio weighting, the distributions of these baseline covariate
were well balanced among datasets (Figure 1(d)), indicating that the density ratio model is likely
to be correctly specified.
Results.
For the targeted-federated learning analysis, we applied the proposed bootstrap
procedure described in Section 2.4 and found that all datasets from 2010−2016 were transportable
to the analysis of the year 2017.
The estimated log odds ratios from three approaches, i.e.,
Target only, Fed-SSnaive, and Fed-BS, are summarized in Figure 2, with corresponding 95%
confidence intervals constructed via bootstrapping. All three estimators detected a significant
positive main effect from OR/IF and a significant negative interaction effect from OR/IF by sex.
These findings suggest that OR/IF is more likely to result in unfavorable recovery than HA for
Black patients without pre-fracture diagnosis of ADRD, and that sex acts as a significant effect
modifier, leading to differential treatment effects (Figure 3). Additionally, both the Fed-BS and
Fed-SSnaive approaches captured a significant association between pre-facture ADRD and worse
recovery outcome, whereas the target-only estimator did not identify this effect as significant. In
general, Fed-BS and Fed-SSnaive produced narrower confidence intervals, indicating the utility of
17


## Page 18


federated learning in improving estimation efficiency. However, in this particular case study, we
did not observe a significant difference in point estimate or uncertainty between the Fed-BS and
Fed-SSnaive estimators.
Moreover, we also observe consistent results in sensitivity analyses where we considered fewer
than 5 days or 20 days as thresholds in the definition of the outcome, in place of the 10-day cutoff
used in the primary analysis (Section 2.1 of the Supplementary Material).

DISCUSSION
In this paper, we developed a novel targeted-federated learning framework for estimating HTEs.
This method fills the methodology gaps in the federated learning literature, where most existing
approaches have limited their scope to prediction, classification, and ATE-based causal inference.
Our developed framework requires only a single round of communication across data sources and
achieves double robustness while mitigating negative data transfer, making it both statistically
rigorous and communication-efficient for real-world federated applications. Findings from our data
application suggest that HA may result in significantly better post-fracture recovery compared to
IF/OR procedure. In addition, pre-fracture ADRD status appears to be a potential prognostic
factor contributing to differential surgery effects. These findings offer valuable evidence to improve
our understanding of the heterogeneous impact of surgical treatments for older adults with a hip
fracture.
Despite its advancements, the current framework has several limitations that warrant further
investigation. One limitation is the potential for unmeasured confounding, particularly because
surgery type is often dictated by fracture characteristics (e.g., severity), which are not consistently
captured in Medicare claims data. Additionally, conducting a formal theoretical assessment of the
proposed bootstrap-based selection procedure is worth investigation. We view both of these areas
as directions for future research.
