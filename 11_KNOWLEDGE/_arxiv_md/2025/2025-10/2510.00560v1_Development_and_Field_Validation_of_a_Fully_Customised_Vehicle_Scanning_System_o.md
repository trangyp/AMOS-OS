Abstract 
Ensuring the structural integrity of bridges is essential for maintaining infrastructure safety 
and promoting long-term sustainability. In this context, Indirect Structural Health Mon- 
itoring (ISHM) through drive-by bridge inspection emerges as a promising alternative to 
traditional inspection methods, offering a cost-effective and scalable solution by using vehicle- 
mounted sensors to assess the condition of bridges without requiring direct instrumentation. 
This study introduces the first purpose-built electric inspection vehicle specifically designed 
for drive-by bridge inspection. The autonomous platform is capable of maintaining a con- 
stant low speed and offers customisable operational parameters to maximise the accuracy and 
repeatability of indirect sensing — capabilities not achieved in previous studies. The vehicle 
is deployed within an ISHM framework and tested on two full-scale bridges to evaluate its 
effectiveness in capturing structural dynamic responses. Two unsupervised frameworks are 
then employed to analyse the collected data to identify features indicative of bridge properties 
and structural condition. The promising findings from this study demonstrate the practical 
feasibility of the approach. The study also shows the potential of ISHM as a viable tool for 
eﬀicient bridge monitoring, contributing to the development of next-generation structural 
health monitoring systems that can enhance safety, optimise maintenance strategies, and 
support the longevity of critical infrastructure. 
Keywords: Drive-by bridge inspection, deep learning, adversarial autoencoders, matrix 
profile, inspection vehicle, bridge monitoring, damage assessment.

15  remains the main preemptive approach, typically conducted every two years [5]. Despite its

17  being subjective, labour-intensive, expensive, and inherently restricted in scope due to its 
18 dependence on the inspector [6]. Consequently, the demand for automated, objective, and 
19  continuous Structural Health Monitoring Systems (SHMS) has grown considerably [7, 8]. 
20 
To address these limitations, Indirect Structural Health Monitoring (ISHM) using drive- 
21 by bridge inspection has emerged as a promising alternative to visual inspections and con- 
22  ventional SHMS, which rely on fixed sensors installed directly on the structure [9]. In con- 
23  trast, drive-by bridge inspection involves instrumented vehicles crossing the bridge, using 
24  the dynamic response measured by mounted sensors, to infer the condition of the bridge 
25  [10]. This approach enables the extraction of modal properties, such as natural frequencies, 
26  damping characteristics, and potential damage indicators. Its advantages include low cost, 
27 particularly in the face of scalability across many structures, and reduced deployment and 
28  maintenance effort [2]. 
29 
Over the past two decades, numerous laboratory studies have contributed to validating 
30  drive-by methodologies under controlled conditions. Zhang et al. [11] used wavelet-based 
31  time-frequency analysis to extract mode shapes from a scaled beam, while Kim et al. [12] 
32  introduced artificial damage through saw cuts and added masses to demonstrate damage 
33 identification using frequency and damping changes. Cerda et al. [5, 13] conducted scaled 
34  laboratory tests where damage was simulated by changing boundary conditions and adding 
35  mass, validating classification-based identification techniques using frequency-domain anal- 
36  ysis. Mei et al. [14] applied Mel-frequency spectral coeﬀicients (MFCC) and Principal Com- 
37  ponent Analysis (PCA) to detect damage in a scaled setup using a two-axle instrumented 
38  vehicle. 
39 
Recent studies have focused on improving robustness under variable operating conditions. 
40 Liu and Xu [15] proposed a multi-task unsupervised learning framework that uses Short- 
41  Time Fourier Transform (STFT) signals from multiple vehicles to identify, localise, and 
42  quantify bridge damage, achieving high identification accuracy across different test scenarios. 
43  Li et al. [16] combined MFCCs with Support Vector Machines (SVM) to achieve high 
44  detection sensitivity and low computational cost in a scaled experiment. Singh and Sadhu 
45 [17] introduced a hybrid signal processing technique that effectively isolates bridge dynamics 
46 from noisy vehicle signals using Wavelet Packet Transform (WPT) and Synchro-Extracting 
47  Transform (SET), even under varying speeds. Yang et al. [18] demonstrated the effectiveness 
48 of Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD) in extracting modal frequencies despite strong 
49  vehicle-induced noise. 
50 
In terms of model generalisation and scalability, Liu et al. [19] developed a transfer

Although laboratory validations are promising, real-world applications are more complex 
59  and remain limited. Most experimental tests in real bridges focus on the identification of 
60  dynamic properties. Yang et al. [22] and Lin et al. [23] extracted bridge natural frequencies

64  [25] conducted field testing on the Turtle Mountain Bridge using frequency-domain analysis 
65  of contact point accelerations to extract dynamic properties, providing practical validation 
66  of vehicle-bridge interaction models. Additional field validations by Kong et al. [26] and 
67  Xu et al. [27] confirmed the effectiveness of tractor-trailer and contact-point acceleration 
68  approaches, respectively, for identifying bridge frequencies without fixed sensors. McGetrick 
69 et al. [28] emphasised the challenges of simulating repeatable artificial damage in operational 
70 bridges, which remains a key limitation for full-scale ISHM validation. 
71 
Despite these successes, ongoing research in advanced signal processing, robust machine 
72  learning techniques, and optimised vehicle configurations continues to enhance the feasibility 
73 of these methods for real-world applications. 
74 
This study advances drive-by bridge inspection for full-scale, operational structures by 
75  building on two rigorous ISHM frameworks [29, 30, 31]. For the first time, we introduce a 
76  purpose-built autonomous inspection vehicle and demonstrate its application in field tests to 
77  identify key dynamic properties and evaluate its practical potential for damage assessment. 
78  The outcomes of this work contribute to the development of more scalable, cost-effective 
79  structural health monitoring systems that support the long-term safety, maintenance, and 
80  sustainability of bridge infrastructure.

104 3. A Novel Autonomous Inspection Vehicle 
105  This section presents the inspection vehicle setup, specifically designed and developed to 
106  perform drive-by bridge inspections. The inspection vehicle is a customised 1:5 scale electric 
107  RC car, adapted to carry the necessary instrumentation for bridge monitoring. Figure 1 
108  illustrates the complete inspection vehicle and its data management system. Specifically, 
109  it is equipped with four PCB-ICP393B05 accelerometers, one mounted on the suspension 
110  system of each wheel, capable of measuring within a ±0.5 g range and operating across a 
111  frequency range of 0.7 to 450 Hz. These sensors effectively capture vehicle dynamic responses, 
112  which are crucial for assessing bridge conditions. The setup also includes four Honeywell 
113  Model 31 Series load cells (capacity 0.5–5,000 N), mounted on a custom-designed aluminium 
114  suspension tower. This frame not only houses the sensors but also allows adjustment of the 
115  pivoting point to accommodate various load configurations (see Figure 2b). The sensing 
116  data are collected using a NI cDAQ-9185 CompactDAQ system and transmitted wirelessly 
117  to a remote computer via a 300M PIX-LINK WR09Q wireless repeater. 
The data are 
118  accessed through NI MAX and recorded using NI-FlexLogger, ensuring a reliable workflow 
119  for acquisition, processing, and storage of dynamic measurements. 
120 
The autonomous control architecture of the vehicle ensures both precise path following 
121 and a stable inspection speed, critical for consistent data collection for drive-by bridge scan- 
122 ning purposes. Steering guidance is achieved using a RoboteQ MGSW1600 magnetic guide 
123  sensor, which continuously tracks a magnetic tape embedded along the inspection route. Its 
124  real-time output interfaces directly with the steering servo, enabling precise path tracking 
125  and delivering high accuracy in lateral positioning, while also facilitating smooth naviga- 
126  tion even over uneven surfaces. A closed-loop feedback system handles speed regulation: 
127 an AMS Hall-effect sensor mounted on the drive train shaft measures rotational speed and 
128  relays this data to an ESP32 microcontroller. The ESP32 implements a PID control algo- 
129  rithm that adjusts the motor speed via a standard R/C electronic speed controller (ESC), 
130  working in tandem with a reduction gearbox to convert high-RPM motor output into the 
131  slow, torque-rich speeds required for scanning. A double-pole double-throw (DPDT) switch 
132  provides seamless toggling between manual and autonomous modes for both steering and 
133  motor drive control, allowing the operator to intervene for greater flexibility in operation, if 
134  required. This integrated system maintains target speeds, maximises data repeatability, and 
135  minimises operator workload during extended field campaigns. This architecture is shown in

5. Methodology 
After successfully identifying the fundamental frequency of the bridge using vehicle-mounted 
accelerometers – and confirming consistency with direct sensing – this section focuses further 
explores the potential for bridge health monitoring of drive-by sensing. To this aim, two 
state-of-the-art unsupervised damage assessment frameworks, previously established in [29] 
and [30, 31], will be used and are detailed next. 
5.1. Indirect SHM Using Drive-by Bridge Inspection 
This section demonstrates the potential application of the drive-by bridge inspection setup 
to SHM. In this stage, the data recorded by the inspection vehicle from the target bridges is 
processed using state-of-the-art damage assessment techniques. The first methodology, pre- 
sented in [29], employs an Adversarial Autoencoder (AAE) to identify and evaluate damage. 
In that study, the authors compared the proposed approach with other state-of-the-art tech- 
niques, demonstrating its enhanced performance. Although the methodology was validated 
through a scaled experimental test, its applicability in more realistic scenarios has not yet 
been demonstrated. 
The second methodology, presented in [30, 31], involves a time-series-based analysis using 
an unsupervised semantic segmentation technique based on Matrix Profile (MP). Although 
promising results were obtained using the matrix profile, previous work was limited to direct 
sensing and simplified laboratory experiments. 
The current study aims to further validate the frameworks proposed in [29, 30, 31] using 
real data from these two bridges, specifically concerning the proposed sensing platform de- 
scribed earlier. In the first case study, the nominal condition of the bridge is intentionally 
varied by adding localised mass to simulate a variation on the bridge condition. However, in 
the second case study, such modification could not be performed due to the significant mass

5.3. Matrix Profile (MP) 
The second approach is based on the framework presented in [30, 31]. In drive-by inspection, 
the amount of available data is often limited due to constraints in testing opportunities or 
operational conditions. This presents a challenge for the AAE approach, which relies on 
large training datasets. One potential solution is the MP method [36], which can detect 
change points in bridge states using limited observations. Therefore, this study investigates 
the use of this technique as a second approach. 
Figure 19 provides a schematic overview of the MP-based framework. The MP method 
is typically used in combination with the Corrected Arc Crossings (CAC) to detect potential 
change points in the bridge state. If the bridge remains in a consistent condition throughout 
the observation period, the algorithm is not expected to detect any change points. This 
method has been validated in [30, 31] using numerical simulations and laboratory experi- 
ments, but it has not yet been tested on full-scale bridges.

identify potential change points in the bridge state.

524  introducing localised added mass, enabling the simulation of damage and evaluation of the 
525  classification capability of the method. In contrast, Case Study II did not include damage 
526 simulation due to the high mass and operational constraints of the structure, which made it 
527  impractical to induce a measurable dynamic change. Instead, the focus was on characterising 
528 the nominal state and minimising false positives. The methodology successfully identified 
529  structural variation in Case Study I and accurately characterised the nominal state in Case 
530  Study II. 
531 
The MP approach was similarly applied to both case studies to detect change points 
532  within the observation sequences. When the bridges remained in their benchmark states, 
533  the corrected arc curve stayed close to one, with no significant minima observed, indicating 
534  that no false detections occurred. 
535 
In summary, this study demonstrates the feasibility of applying unsupervised learning 
536 and change point detection techniques for drive-by bridge inspection using a customisable 
537  inspection vehicle. The findings provide a solid foundation for future work in structural 
538 damage identification. One of the key advantages of the proposed inspection vehicle is its 
539  modular and customisable nature, which opens opportunities to explore the design space and 
540  optimise its configuration for enhanced sensing performance. Future research will also focus 
541  on studying long-term patterns and the effects of environmental and operational variability, 
542  as well as validating the methodology across a broader range of bridge types and conditions.
