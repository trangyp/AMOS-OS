INDECOMPOSABLE KLEIN BOTTLES WITH ORDER-4 MERIDIANS
JEFFREY MEIER
Abstract. We exhibit an infinite family of indecomposable Klein bottles in the 4–sphere with
order-4 meridians.
1. Introduction
A foundational theorem in knot theory states that every knot in S3 can be expressed uniquely as
a connected sum of nontrivial prime knots. It is natural to wonder to what extent such a theorem
might hold in dimension four, where one is concerned with embeddings of surfaces in S4, or surface-
knots. To this day, very little is known about the ways in which a surface-knot might decompose
as the connected sum of simpler surfaces. For example, each of the following questions remains
unanswered:
(1) Can the unknotted 2–sphere be expressed as the connected sum of two knotted 2–spheres?
(2) Is every knotted projective plane the connected sum of a knotted 2–sphere and an unknotted
projective plane?
The first of the above questions is closely related to the famous Unknotting Conjecture for 2–
knots [CKS04, pages 55, 97], while the second is the purview of the Kinoshita Conjecture, which
posits an affirmative answer [KS98, Remark 3.7].
Figure 1. The Klein bottle K
It is known that surface-knots can decompose in
surprising ways: Viro gave an example of a knotted
2–sphere S such that S#P = P, where P is the un-
knotted projective plane [Vir73]; see also [CKS04, page
56]. This illustrates the subtlety of finding an analogue
in dimension four to the prime factorization theorem
for knots in dimension three, especially where nonori-
entable surface-knots are concerned.
A surface-knot S ⊂S4 is called decomposable if we
have S = S1#S2 such that neither S1 nor S2 is a 2–
sphere; otherwise, S is called indecomposable.
The purpose of this paper is to present an infinite
family of indecomposable Klein bottles with order-4
meridians. Let l be an even integer with |l| ≥2, and
let m and n be odd integers with |m|, |n| ≥3. Let
K(l, m, n) be the Klein bottle introduced in Definition 3 below and shown in Figure 1.
Theorem 1. If one of m or n is divisible by 3 and the other is divisible by 3 or 5, then the Klein
bottle K(l, m, n) has order-4 meridians. If m = n, then K(l, m, m) is indecomposable.
The first part of Theorem 1 is Corollary 7, while the second part is Proposition 11. Corollary 9
shows that the K are pairwise non-isotopic whenever their parameters are distinct modulo signs.
Indecomposable Klein bottles have previously been given by Yoshikawa [Yos98] and by Lidman
and Piccirillo [LP25]. In fact, we use the argument of Lidman and Piccirillo to prove Proposition 11.
Interestingly, the double branched cover of our Klein bottle K(2, 3, 7) has the same fundamental
group as the double branched cover of their Klein bottles.

The first example of a Klein bottle with order-4 meridians was given by Yoshikawa, who produced,
for each even (nonnegative) integer, a Klein bottle having meridians of that order [Yos98]. Curiously,
the Klein bottle Y with order-4 meridians produced by Yoshikawa has the same group as our
Klein bottle K(2, 3, 3). Yoshikawa’s Klein bottles with meridians whose order is not 2 or 4 are
automatically indecomposable: The order of a meridian of a projective plane (or any connected
sum of projective planes) is either 2 or 4, since the peripheral subgroup is the quaternion group
Q8 [Pri77].
Surface-knots S and S′ are stably isotopic if there exist unknotted surface-knots U and U′ such
that S#U and S′#U′ are isotopic. A surface-knot S is stably reducible if it is stably isotopic to a
surface-knot S′ with χ(S′) > χ(S); otherwise, it is stably irreducible.
Proposition 2. Let K be any K(l, m, n). Then, either
(1) K is stably irreducible, or
(2) K is stably isotopic to a projective plane with order-4 meridians.
Note that projective planes with order-4 meridians are not known to exist [Yos98, Section 4]; the
existence of one would give a negative answer to the Kinoshita Conjecture, which was discussed at
the outset of this article.
The first examples of stably irreducible surface-knots were orientable and were given by Liv-
ingston [Liv85]; more recently, Lidman and Piccirillo gave examples of stably irreducible non-
orientable surface-knots [LP25]. The Klein bottles of Lidman and Piccirillo are also indecompos-
able, but it is not known whether they have order-4 meridians. The Klein bottles of Lidman and
Piccirillo are ribbon, and it is not clear whether the Klein bottles produced in this note are ribbon.
In Section 5, we give a number of open questions motivated by this work. In particular, Conjec-
ture 12 posits that all of the K(l, m, n) are indecomposable, have order-4 meridians, and are stably
irreducible.

We remark that an alternative calculation of π(K) can be given using the handle-decomposition
of Figure 1. It is not a priori obvious whether a, b, and c have order 2 or 4. We suspect they
always have order 4, but we have only been able to prove the following.
Corollary 7. If one of m or n is divisible by 3 and the other is divisible by 3 or 5, then the
generators a, b, and c of π(K(l, m, n)) have order 4.
Proof. Suppose without loss of generality that 3 | m and δ | n, where δ ∈{3, 5}, and recall that l
is even. In this case, there is a surjection π(K(l, m, n)) ↠π(K(2, 3, δ)). This latter group is finite
and fits into the short exact sequence
1 −→SL2(Zδ) −→π(K(2, 3, δ)) −→Z2 −→1,
which splits if δ = 5. These facts can be verified, for example, using GAP [GAP24]. For either
choice of δ, one finds the image of a has order 4, in π(K(2, 3, δ)). It follows that a has order 4 in
π(K(l, m, n)). Since b and c are conjugate to a, they have order 4, as well.
□

4. Decomposability and stable irreducibility
We can obstruct decomposability of a subfamily of our Klein bottles using the same argument
as Lidman and Piccirillo [LP25, Remark].
Proposition 11. If m = n, then K(l, m, m) is indecomposable.
Proof. Let K = K(l, m, n). In [LP25, Remark], Lidman and Piccirillo prove that their Klein bottle
is indecomposable. Their argument applies to our Klein bottles, with the following three caveats.
First, we need to know that Σ2(K) has signature zero. This is implied by the fact that the
normal Euler number of K is zero, which follows from Proposition 5 in the case that m = n. (Even
without the restriction that m = n, it is possible to verify that the normal Euler number of K is
zero using the technique of [Kam89, Section 3], which involves tracing longitudes for the unlink in
Figure 1 through the band attachments.)
Second, we need to know that π1(Σ2(K)) doesn’t split as the free product of two nontrivial
groups. If (up to signs) (l, m, n) ̸∈{(2, 3, 3), (2, 3, 5)}, then this follows by the argument given by
Lidman and Piccirillo: In this case, π1(Σ2(K)) is generated by hyperbolic rotations of finite order,
so the Cayley graph contains no cut vertex, in contrast to the Cayley graph of a free product. If
(up to signs) (l, m, n) is (2, 3, 3) or (2, 3, 5), then π1(Σ2(K)) is A4 or A5, respectively. Since these
groups are finite, they cannot be nontrivial free products.
Third, we need to know that Σ2(K) admits an orientation-reversing diffeomorphism. This is
implied by the fact that K is amphicheiral when m = n, as indicated by Proposition 5.
□
Finally, we prove the second result from the introduction, offering some restriction on the stable
isotopy class of our Klein bottles.
Proposition 2. Let K be any K(l, m, n). Then, either
(1) K is stably irreducible, or
(2) K is stably isotopic to a projective plane with order-4 meridians.
Proof. If K is stably irreducible, we are done. Suppose instead that K#U = P#U, where P is a
projective plane and U and U′ are unknotted surfaces, which we can assume to be non-orientable
by adding on additional projective plane summands.
In Corollary 8, we found that G = π(K#U) is a Coxeter group. Howlett calculated the second
homology (or Schur multipliers) of such groups [How88, Theorem A]. In our case, the Coxeter group
is described by a triangular graph with edge weights l, m, and n, with l even and m and n odd;
therefore, Howlett’s calculation tells us that H2(G) is an elementary 2–group of rank one, hence
H2(G) ∼= Z2.

5. Future work
As mentioned in the introduction, we conjecture the following.
Conjecture 12. Each K(l, m, n) has order-4 meridians, is indecomposable, and is stably irre-
ducible.
Our Klein bottles shared enough properties with the ribbon Klein bottles of Lidman and Piccirillo
for us to apply their obstruction to decomposability, but we do not expect that our Klein bottles
are ribbon.
Conjecture 13. The K(l, m, n) are not ribbon.
The Klein bottle Y with order-4 meridians constructed by Yoshikawa is built by attaching a
disoriented tube to the 2–twist spin of the pretzel knot P(−2, 3, 3) [Yos98, Section 4]. Since π(Y)
agrees with π(K(−2, 3, 3)), it seems likely that Y is isotopic to K(−2, 3, 3) (up to changing the signs
of the parameters; see Question 15), which motivates the following question.
Question 14. Which of the K(l, m, n) arise as the result of attaching a disoriented tube to a
(twist-spun) 2–knot?
The fact that π(K(l, m, n)) doesn’t depend on the sign of the parameters (see the proof of
Proposition 6) motivates our final question.
Question 15. Does the isotopy class of K(l, m, n) depend on the signs of the parameters?
