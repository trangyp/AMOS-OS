CONVEXITY OF NEURAL CODES WITH FOUR MAXIMAL CODEWORDS
SABER AHMED, NATASHA CREPEAU, GISEL FLORES, OSIANO ISEKENEGBE, DEANNA PEREZ, AND ANNE SHIU
Abstract. Place cells are neurons that act as biological position sensors, associated with and firing in
response to regions of an environment to situate an organism in space.
These associations are recorded
in (combinatorial) neural codes, motivating the following mathematical question: Which neural codes are
generated by a collection of convex open sets in Euclidean space? Giusti and Itskov showed that a necessary
condition for convexity is the absence of “local obstructions.” This necessary condition is, in fact, sufficient
for certain families of codes. One such family consists of all codes with up to three maximal codewords. In
this article, we investigate codes with four maximal codewords, showing that for many such codes, convexity
is characterized by the absence of local obstructions, whereas for other such codes, convexity is characterized
by the absence of local obstructions and a second type of obstruction, a “wheel”. Key to our analysis is a
case-by-case investigation based on the nerve complex of the set of maximal codewords of a neural code. Up
to symmetry, there are 20 possible nerves; and our results fully characterize convexity in 15 of the 20 cases.
Keywords: Neural code, simplicial complex, nerve, convex, local obstruction, wheel
MSC Codes: 13F55, 52A20, 92C20
1. Introduction
In the brain, place cells act as position sensors. Each place cell is associated with a “preferred” region of
the animal’s environment called its place field — when the animal is in a place field, the corresponding place

d = 2 or d = 3), where subset Ui induces neuron i to fire. The collection U = {U1, U2, . . . , Un} then naturally

A collection of such binary strings is called a (combinatorial) neural code, which encodes the animal’s neural

A surprising feature of experimentally observed place fields is the predominance of convex or nearly convex
shapes. Their boundaries are also gradual, rather than strictly delimited, reflecting diminishing firing rates
farther from the field’s center. As such, place fields are well modeled by convex open sets in Euclidean space.
Accordingly, codes that can be realized by a collection of convex open sets – we call such codes convex – are
of particular interest to neuroscientists.
Towards understanding when codes are not convex, Giusti and Itskov introduced the combinatorial local
obstruction and showed that codes containing local obstructions are not convex [7]. Subsequently, Ruys de
Perez et al. introduced the concept of a wheel and showed that codes containing wheels are not convex [16].
Local obstructions and wheels are two primary tools for establishing that a given code is not convex, and
they are a focus of this article. (Additional tools can be found, for instance, in [9, 15].)

not only necessary but also sufficient for convexity [12]. Their result, however, does not extend for codes

2. Preliminaries
This section recalls definitions pertaining to neural codes (Section 2.1), criteria for confirming or precluding
convexity of neural codes (Sections 2.2 and 2.3), and results on the convexity of neural codes with few maximal
codewords (Section 2.4). We begin by recalling the definition of a simplicial complex (and its faces).
Definition 2.1. A simplicial complex ∆on a nonempty finite set V is a nonempty collection of subsets of V
that is closed with respect to containment (that is, if σ′ ⊆σ ∈∆, then σ′ ∈∆). Here, the set V is the vertex
set of ∆. If ∆is a simplicial complex, then
(1) every σ ∈∆is a face of ∆;
(2) if σ is a face of ∆, then σ is a k-simplex, where |σ| = k + 1; and
(3) the faces of ∆that are maximal with respect to inclusion are the facets of ∆.

2.1. Neural codes.
Definition 2.2. A neural code (or, for short, code) C on n neurons is a collection of subsets c ⊆[n], called
codewords. A codeword c ∈C is maximal if c is maximal with respect to set inclusion. The simplicial complex,
∆(C), of a code C on n neurons is defined by ∆(C) := {σ ⊆[n] : σ ⊆c for some c ∈C}. An n-maximal code
is a code with exactly n maximal codewords.
By construction, the maximal codewords of a code C are precisely the facets of the simplicial complex ∆(C).
Notation 2.3. To simplify notation, we write each codeword as a string of integers, rather than in the roster
form of a set. For instance, {1, 2, 3, 4} is written as 1234. Additionally, in our examples we use boldface to
indicate maximal codewords.

Detecting the convexity of a given neural code is NP-hard [13]. Nevertheless, for some families of codes,
there are simple criteria for assessing convexity.
In what follows, we recall such criteria for confirming
convexity (Section 2.2) and for precluding convexity (Section 2.3).
2.2. Criteria for confirming convexity. In this subsection, we recall two results (Lemmas 2.10 and 2.12).
The first is a monotonicity result of Cruz et al., which states that convexity is maintained when non-maximal
codewords are added to a code [5, Theorem 1.3], as follows.
Lemma 2.10 (Monotonicity of convexity). Let C, D be codes such that C ⊆D ⊆∆(C). Then if C is convex,
D is convex.
The next criterion involves the inclusion of “max-intersection” faces, as defined below.
Definition 2.11.
(a) Let ∆be a simplicial complex. A face σ ∈∆is a max-intersection face of ∆if σ is nonempty and is
the intersection of two or more facets of ∆.
(b) Let C be a code with simplicial complex ∆(C). A max-intersection face of C is a max-intersection
face of ∆(C). Next, C is max-intersection-complete if it contains every max-intersection face of C (or,
equivalently, if C contains every intersection of two or more codewords of C).
The following result is due to Cruz et al. [5, Theorem 1.2].
Lemma 2.12 (Max-intersection-complete codes are convex). If C is a code that is max-intersection-complete,
then C is convex.
The converse of Lemma 2.12 is false, as the following example shows.

2.3. Criteria for precluding convexity. In this subsection, we recall two results that can be used to show
that a code is not convex (Lemmas 2.18 and 2.23).
2.3.1. Wheels and sprockets. In this subsection, we recall the definitions of a wheel and a sprocket [16].
Sprockets generate wheels, and the presence of a wheel is an obstruction to convexity (Lemma 2.18 below).
Definition 2.14 (Wheel). Let C be a code on n neurons, and let U = {Ui}n
i=1 be a realization of C. A tuple
W = (σ1, σ2, σ3, τ) ∈(2[n])4 is a wheel of the realization U if it satisfies:
W(i): Uσi ∩Uσj = Uσ1 ∩Uσ2 ∩Uσ3 ̸= ∅for all 1 ≤j < k ≤3,
W(ii): Uσ1 ∩Uσ2 ∩Uσ3 ∩Uτ = ∅,
W(iii): if Uτ and Uσj ∩Uτ are convex for j = 1, 2, 3, then there exists a line segment such that one of the
endpoints lies in Uσ1 ∩Uτ and the other in Uσ3 ∩Uτ, that also meets Uσ2 ∩Uτ.
Finally, W is a wheel of C if it is a wheel of every realization U of C.
Wheels are defined relative to realizations of a code C, but there are “combinatorial wheels” – combinatorial
conditions that can be read from C and ∆(C) – that guarantee the presence of a wheel. One such combinatorial
wheel is a sprocket (Definition 2.16 below). To define sprockets, we need Jeffs’ definition of a “trunk” [9].
Definition 2.15 (Trunk). Let C be a code on n neurons, and let σ ⊆[n]. The trunk of σ in C is
TkC(σ) := {c ∈C : σ ⊆c} .
Definition 2.16 (Partial-wheel and sprocket). Let C be a neural code. A tuple W = (σ1, σ2, σ3, τ) ∈(∆(C))4
is a partial-wheel of C if it satisfies the following conditions:
P(i): σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3 ∈∆(C), and TkC(σj ∪σk) = TkC(σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3) for every 1 ≤j < k ≤3,
P(ii): σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3 ∪τ ̸∈∆(C), and
P(iii)◦: σj ∪τ ∈∆(C) for j ∈{1, 2, 3}.
A sprocket of a C is a partial-wheel W = (σ1, σ2, σ3, τ) of C for which there exist ρ1, ρ3 ∈∆(C) (these sets ρ1
and ρ3 are called witnesses for the spocket) such that the following conditions hold:
S(1): TkC(σj ∪τ) ⊆TkC(ρj) for j ∈{1, 3},
S(2): TkC(τ) ⊆TkC(ρ1) ∪TkC(ρ3), and
S(3): TkC(ρ1 ∪ρ3 ∪τ) ⊆TkC(σ2).
Remark 2.17. Our notation P(i)–P(iii)◦matches that in [16], whereas for simplicity we use S(1)–S(3)
rather than the notation S(iii)(1)–S(iii)(3) used in [16].
The following result is due to Ruys de Perez et al. [16, Theorem 3.4 and Proposition 4.5].
Lemma 2.18 (Sprockets are obstructions to convexity). Let C be a code.
(1) If C has a sprocket, then C has a wheel.
(2) If C has a wheel, then C is non-convex.

2.3.2. Local obstructions to convexity. This subsection recalls “local obstructions” to convexity (Defini-

Definition 2.21 (Link). Let ∆be a simplicial complex on [n], and let σ ∈∆. The link of σ in ∆is the
following simplicial complex:
Lk∆(σ) := {τ ⊆[n] : τ ∩σ = ∅and τ ∪σ ∈∆} .
The following definition of local obstructions is equivalent to the usual one [6, Proposition 4.8].
Definition 2.22 (Local obstruction).
(1) Let ∆be a simplicial complex. A face σ ∈∆is mandatory if σ ̸= ∅and the link Lk∆(σ) is not
contractible.
(2) Let C be a code with simplicial complex ∆(C). A mandatory face of C is a mandatory face of ∆(C).
Next, C has a local obstruction if there exists a mandatory face σ of C such that σ /∈C.
It follows from definitions that a code C has no local obstructions if and only if Cmin(∆(C)) ⊆C, where
the minimal code Cmin(∆) of a simplicial complex ∆is given by
Cmin(∆) := {σ ∈∆: Lk∆(σ) is not contractible} ∪{∅}
= {facets of ∆} ∪{non-facet mandatory faces of ∆} ∪{∅} .

Lemma 2.23 (Local obstruction to convexity). If C is a code with a local obstruction, then C is non-convex.

In light of the above examples, it is natural to ask whether these two types of obstructions to convexity –
wheels and local obstructions – are enough to characterize convexity in 4-maximal codes. Jeffs conjectures
that the answer is “yes” (Conjecture 1.1), and this conjecture is known to hold for all codes on up to 5

Remark 2.33. In light of Conjecture 1.1, it is natural to ask whether – for codes with more than four
maximal codewords – wheels and local obstructions together characterize convexity. This question is open.
A starting point for such an investigation might be a set of 96 5-maximal codes on 6 neurons that are minimal
codes (and thus have no local obstructions), but whose convexity status is unknown (also unknown is whether
they contain wheels) [16, Table 1].

Proof. Convex codes have no local obstructions (Lemma 2.23), so we need only prove the converse. Accord-

Our next result pertains to codes with any number of maximal codewords. Specifically, the result considers
the case of nerves that consist only of vertices and edges, in other words, nerves with no 2-simplices.
Proposition 3.5 (No 2-simplices). Let C be a code. If the nerve of the set of maximal codewords of C
contains no 2-simplices, then the following are equivalent:
(1) C is convex,
(2) C has no local obstructions, and
(3) C is max-intersection-complete.
Proof. Let F(C) = {F1, F2, . . . , Fm} denote the set of maximal codewords of C. Our assumption that the
nerve of F(C) has no 2-simplices, simply means that Fi ∩Fj ∩Fk = ∅, whenever 1 ≤i < j < k ≤m.
The implications (3) ⇒(1) and (1) ⇒(2) are Lemmas 2.12 and 2.23, respectively. Thus, it suffices to
prove the implication (2) ⇒(3).
We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that C is not max-intersection-complete. Then there exists σ ̸∈C
such that σ is a max-intersection face of C. Thus, by definition, σ is the intersection of two or more maximal
codewords. Recall, however, that every triple-wise intersection of maximal codewords is empty. We conclude
that σ = Fi ∩Fj for some 1 ≤i < j ≤m, and σ ̸⊆Fk for all k ∈[m] ∖{i, j}. Thus, Lemma 2.26 implies that
Lk∆(C) (σ) is not contractible. By assumption, σ ̸∈C, so σ is a mandatory face of C, and therefore C has a
local obstruction (by Definition 2.22(2)).
□

readily seen from Figure 14), and F4 is a facet of ∆(C); therefore σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3 is a face of ∆(C), as required.
Next, it is readily seen from the Hasse digram in Figure 15 that TkC(σi ∪σj) = TkC(σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3) = {F4}
for all 1 ≤i < j ≤3. We conclude that P(i) holds.
Next, P(ii) requires that σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3 ∪τ /∈∆(C). From Figure 14, we see that σ1 ∪σ2 ∪σ3 ∪τ is not a
subset of any facet Fi of ∆(C) and therefore is not a face of ∆(C), as desired.
Finally, for P(iii)◦, we must have that σi ∪τ ∈∆(C), for i = 1, 2, 3. Figure 14 shows that σi ∪τ is a subset
of Fi, which is a facet of ∆(C), and so σi ∪τ ∈∆(C). Therefore (σ1, σ2, σ3, τ) is a partial-wheel of C.
We now show that the partial-wheel (σ1, σ2, σ3, τ) is, in fact, a sprocket with witnesses ρ1 and ρ3. Following
Definition 2.16, we must verify three additional conditions:
S(1): TkC(σj ∪τ) ⊆TkC(ρj) for j ∈{1, 3},
S(2): TkC(τ) ⊆TkC(ρ1) ∪TkC(ρ3), and
S(3): TkC(ρ1 ∪ρ3 ∪τ) ⊆TkC(σ2).
Using Figures 14 and 15, we compute the trunks appearing above, and readily verify the three conditions:
• TkC(σj ∪τ) = {Fj}, for j ∈{1, 3},
• TkC(ρj) = {ρj, Fj}, for j ∈{1, 3}
• TkC(τ) = {ρ1, ρ3, F1, F2, F3},
• TkC(ρ1) ∪TkC(ρ3) = {F2}, and
• TkC(σ2) = {σ2, F2, F4}.
Finally, the fact that C has a sprocket implies (by Lemma 2.18) that C cannot be convex.

Remark 3.25. It is known that if a neural code C has a sprocket, then C has a sprocket (σ1, σ2, σ3, τ) such

4. Discussion
We return to the motivation for this work, Conjecture 1.1, which states that 4-maximal codes are convex
if and only if they have no local obstructions and no wheels. Our work made significant progress toward
resolving this conjecture. Specifically, we proved that it holds for all codes from L9 to L23 (Corollary 3.2);
in fact, for such codes, convexity is equivalent to having no local obstructions (there is no need to check for
wheels). We also resolved Conjecture 1.1 for L24 codes that are minimal (Theorem 3.3), and in this case
wheels (specifically, sprockets) are needed. The remaining open cases of Conjecture 1.1 are non-minimal L24
codes (recall Example 3.22) and all cases of L25 through L28. Additionally, we do not know what types of
wheels occur in such codes (for instance, are there any beyond sprockets?).
Our work points the way toward future progress on codes with more than four maximal codewords. We
know, for instance, that Conjecture 1.1 extends to all codes for which all triplewise intersections of maximal
codewords are empty (Proposition 3.5). Additionally, we expect that ideas developed in this work – gluing
realizations together, in particular – will be useful. Finally, we also know that the convex realizations will
no longer always be planar; indeed, there is a 5-maximal convex code – namely, the “sunflower code” S4 [10,
Proposition 2.3] – that has no convex realization even in R3 (but has one in R4).
