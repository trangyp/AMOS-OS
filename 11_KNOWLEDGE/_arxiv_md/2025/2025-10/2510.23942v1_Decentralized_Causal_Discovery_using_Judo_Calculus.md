Abstract
We describe a theory and implementation of an intuitionistic decentralized framework for
causal discovery using “judo calculus", which more formally is defined as j-stable causal
inference using j-do-calculus in a topos of sheaves ShJ(C). Classical causality typically
assumes a single, universal truth: either “X causes Y" everywhere or it does not (Boolean
logic). However, in real-world applications – from biology to medicine and social science
– causal effects depend on regime (age, country, dose, genotype, or lab protocol). Our
proposed judo calculus formalizes this context dependence formally as local truth: a causal
claim is proven true on a cover of regimes, not everywhere at once. The Lawvere-Tierney
modal operator j chooses which regimes are relevant; j-stability means the claim holds
constructively and consistently across that family. Judo calculus extends Pearl’s do-calculus
by requiring that interventions be stable along j-covers, and reduces to the classical case for
the trivial topology.
We describe an algorithmic and implementation framework for judo calculus, combining it
with standard score-based, constraint-based, and gradient-based causal discovery methods.
We describe experimental results on how to (i) form data-driven j-covers (via regime/section
constructions), (ii) compute chartwise conditional independences after graph surgeries, and
(iii) glue them to certify the premises of the j-do rules in practice. We compare the regular
and j-stable variants of popular causal discovery methods, including Greedy Equivalent
Search (GES) [Chickering, 2002], ψ-FCI [Jaber, 2020] and DCDI [Brouillard, 2020] on a range
of domains, from synthetic to real-world datasets from biology and economics.

Introduction
In this paper, we describe an intuitionistic framework for decentralized causal discovery, which builds
on our previous work on Topos Causal Models (TCMs) [Mahadevan, 2025a]. Causal interventions are
modeled formally by the modal operator j as subobjects of a causal model, which yields a poset structure
with intuitionistic Kripke-Joyal semantics [Mac Lane and leke Moerdijk, 1992]. We colloquially refer to
j-do-calculus as “judo" calculus, which enables a highly decentralized framework for causal discovery on
sheaves. Our paper provides a new intuitionistic perspective on causal discovery (see [Zanga and Stella,
2023] for a comprehensive survey). Our approach is influenced by the official mantra of category theory:
look for universal properties. While there has been significant work on categorical approaches to causality
[Fong, 2012, Fritz and Klingler, 2023, Mahadevan, 2023], none of these have focused on causal discovery.
In addition to describing a categorical theory of causal discovery with “judo" calculus, we also give a
detailed algorithmic implementation and analysis of j-stable variants of several causal discovery methods.
We describe preliminary experimental results on how to (i) form data-driven j-covers (via regime/section
constructions), (ii) compute chartwise conditional independences after graph surgeries, and (iii) glue them to
certify the premises of the j-do rules in practice. We compare the regular and j-stable variants of popular
causal discovery methods, including Greedy Equivalent Search (GES) [Chickering, 2002], ψ-FCI [Jaber, 2020]
and DCDI [Brouillard, 2020], showing the efficiency due to the intrinsic parallelism that results from using the
topos of sheaves. There are a wealth of additional real-world applications of causal inference (see [Brouillard,
2025] for a recent survey), and we will produce a more detailed experimental validation of judo calculus in
subsequent papers along with a publicly accessible Python implementation.
Prior to getting into the technical aspects of causal discovery using judo calculus, we begin with an informal
overview of our framework, and contrast it with methods that build on classical do-calculus with Boolean
semantics [Pearl, 2009]. Judo calculus generalizes Pearl’s do-calculus to an intuitionistic setting, where
proofs must be constructive and absolute causal truth is replaced with "local" causal truth. For example, a
conditional independence property may hold only in a particular regime, which is modeled using sheaves in
judo calculus. For ease of readability, we have moved the theory of judo calculus to the appendix.
The categorical framework underlying judo calculus relies on the property that the functor category of Yoneda
embeddings, or (co)presheaves, forms a topos. To ensure consistent extension into a unique global function,
we build on the theory of sheaves [Mac Lane and leke Moerdijk, 1992], which ensures local functions can be
“collated" together to yield a unique global function. In our setting, we will construct sheaves from categories
over causal models through the Yoneda embedding よ(x) : C →SetsCop and impose a Grothendieck topology.
TCMs were originally defined over Grothendieck topologies on categories (known as sites), and we will
generalize that formulation here to Lawvere-Tierney topologies.
Here is a road map to the remainder of this paper. We begin in Section 2 with a high-level overview of judo
calculus. We illustrate two primary advantages: the intrinsic efficiency of sheaf-theoretic causal discovery,
and its richness in modeling complex causal modeling paradigms such as interference. In Section 3 we
describe in detail how to transform a range of previous causal discovery algorithms, including specifically
Greedy Equivalent Search (GES) [Chickering, 2002], ψ-FCI [Jaber, 2020], and DCDI [Brouillard, 2020], into a
decentralized j-stable variant that exploits the power of judo calculus. Section 4 describes how we validate
judo calculus, including experimental design and setup, and experimental protocols. In Section 5, we
describe a series of illustrative experiments that showcase the effectiveness of judo causal discovery. We
stress that this suite of experiments is preliminary, and a further more complete validation of judo calculus,
along with a publicly accessible Python implementation is forthcoming. To simplify the readability of the
paper for those without a deep background in category theory, we relegate the theoretical discussion of
j-stable causal discovery to Section A in the Appendix. Finally, we describe some specific implementation
details in Section B.
2
Informal Introduction to Judo Calculus
We first give a high-level overview of judo calculus in this section, prior to introducing the highly technical
definitions on which it is rigorously based. Judo calculus is intuitionistic, meaning that the law of the excluded
middle p∨¬p does not necessarily hold, and ¬¬p cannot be reduced to p, for any causal proposition p. Rather,
judo calculus requires a constructive proof to determine a statement’s truth. In the context of causality, this
means you can’t assume that a causal claim is either universally true or universally false without a proof for
one or the other.

Judo calculus is based on topos theory [Bell, 1988] a branch of category theory [MacLane, 1971], which
allows modeling causality in a more flexible way than classical methods. For example, in many real-world
applications of causal inference, a particular intervention, such as administering a drug or employing a lunch
program in schools, may not be effective over the entire population, but rather in different “regimes" (e.g.,
senior citizens may respond more favorably than younger recipients, and similarly, students from low-income
backgrounds may benefit nutritionally from a free lunch program than students from high-income regions).

As a concrete example, let us imagine that a city planning a public health initiative to combat childhood
obesity decides to distribute free healthy lunches in public schools. The city policy makers want to determine
if this intervention reduces the students’ body mass index (BMI) by the year’s end. Classical do-calculus
would seek a global average treatment effect over the entire school population. Judo calculus makes it
possible to define individual regimes, such as “low-income" and “high-income" where the causal intervention
may or may not be as effective.
Judo calculus works in the setting of sheaves or sites, which are categories that are equipped with a
Lawvere-Tierney topology defined by a modal operator j on the subobjects. In plain English, this means
that the category has a topology defined by the arrows, and their compositional structure. This j operator
defines a notion of causal “stability", which will be studied extensively in the coming sections. For example,
in the “low income" students, the city’s causal intervention may be j-stable, whereas in the “high income"
category, this intervention may not be as effective. The operator j acts on the subobject classifier Ω: an
object in the category that serves to define “truth", which in general is not Boolean. The j operator acts to
determine “local" truth from “global" truth, and also provides a closure property to determine j-stability. The
j operator on Ωis defined to represent the notion of a “globally valid" causal statement. A j-cover represents
a tesselation of the space of arrows that all have a common co-domain.
2.1
Judo Calculus Model of Causal Inference under Interference
In this section, we want to quickly show how our framework applies to the case when the standard Stable
Treatment Unit Value Assumption (SUTVA) is violated because of “interference". For example, Wikle and
Zigler [2023] report on a study involving power plants that may cause pollution among the residents of a
particular county, where each individual resident may be exposed to the radiation from multiple power
plants. A particular treatment on a power plant, such as imposing pollution controls, may affect many
residents.
Classical do-calculus causal inference often assumes SUTVA (no interference): a unit’s outcome depends only
on its own treatment. In many spatial and networked problems this fails. Consider air pollution: a resident
may be exposed to emissions from multiple plants, with exposure modulated by meteorology. Claims that
ignore this heterogeneity are either false or too brittle to be useful. Our framework captures this by proving
local causal truths on families of comparable regimes (covers) and then gluing them. We illustrate this in a
two-source interference model with overlapping covers; the same pattern scales to real data in §5. Judo
calculus shows how to certify claims locally on covers and glue them via j-stability. This simple example
shows the power of the judo calculus formalism in modeling a range of real-world examples, and we will get
into several real-world datasets in the experiments described later in the paper.
Setting.
Let P = {1, 2} be two sources (plants) and R a population of residents. Time is indexed by t. Each
plant has a binary treatment Zp(t) ∈{0, 1} (e.g., a scrubber on/off). Meteorology (θ(t), M(t)) denotes wind
direction (degrees) and a mixing proxy (e.g., stability or mixing height). Interference arises because multiple
sources contribute to each resident’s exposure.

guards (e.g., forbid edges from the composite to its components).

tions everywhere”), we make a j-stable claim that is provably true on the relevant cover (e.g., west-wind
regimes and their intersections with low mixing). This separation of concerns—choose a cover, prove locally,
glue globally—is the essence of the j-do calculus: the usual identification rules apply, but only after claims
have been made stable along the cover.

Algorithms for j-Stable Causal Discovery
In this section, we present a series of algorithms for causal discovery, based on popular existing methods
that we “sheafify" by constructing their j-stable variant. Specifically, we describe how to (i) form data-driven
j-covers (via regime/section constructions), (ii) compute chartwise conditional independences after graph
surgeries, and (iii) glue them to certify the premises of the j-do rules in practice. We compare the regular
and j-stable variants of popular causal discovery methods, including Greedy Equivalent Search (GES)
[Chickering, 2002], ψ-FCI [Jaber, 2020] and DCDI [Brouillard, 2020], showing the efficiency due to the intrinsic
parallelism that results from using the topos of sheaves.

3.3
TCM Primer: Nerve Invariants on the Skeleton
One simple but elegant categorical trick is to map a causal category into a topological space using the
“nerve" functor. This functor creates a topological object, represented combinatorially as a simplicial set
(see [Mahadevan, 2023] for the details). The intuition here is that a simplicial set is a graded collection of
sets, X[n], n ≥0, where X[0] can be thought of as the vertices (variables) of a causal model, X[1] defines the
directed edges between pairs of vertices, X[2] defines the simplices of order 2, and so on. Here, we simplify
this construction to illustrate how to use it to modify GES. Given a directed graph G, its skeleton S(G) is
the undirected graph obtained by ignoring directions. Write f0(S) = |V|, f1(S) = |edges(S)|, and let f2(S) be
the number of undirected triangles (3-cliques) in S.3 These are entries of the f-vector of the clique (nerve)
complex built on S. Two useful aggregate invariants are:
χ(S) = f0(S) −f1(S) + f2(S)
and
b1(S) = f1(S) −f0(S) + c(S),
where c(S) is the number of connected components in S and b1 is the cyclomatic number of the 1-skeleton. In
the TCM view, controlling f1 and f2 shapes the nerve and stabilizes the induced topology of conditional
independence structures.

3.7
Why the Topology Terms Help (TCM View)
From the TCM perspective, the learned skeleton S(G) induces a clique (nerve) complex whose low-
dimensional f-vector (f0, f1, f2) and Euler characteristic χ capture coarse structure: f1 controls overall

particular, adding u→v that closes many triangles yields a larger ∆f2 penalty via |Γ(u) ∩Γ(v)|, discouraging
overly clustered motifs unless strongly supported by likelihood.

Experimental Results
In this section, we report on a diverse range of experimental domains, from synthetic to real-world, to give
an illustration of the potential of judo calculus in causal discovery. A much wider set of experiments are
currently in progress, and will be reported at a later date.
Why j-stable algorithms outperform their counterparts (intuition).
Pooled fits blur regime idiosyncrasies
and let spurious partial correlations sneak in. j-stable fits each regime separately and keeps only edges
that repeat across regimes. Under perfect interventions, true parents reappear consistently while spurious
links do not. Aggregating into a frequency map F and thresholding at π is a form of stability selection: the
Binomial tail under the true edge rate pT clears π with high probability, while the false edge rate pF almost
never does. A simple net-preference rule (keep i→j if Fi→j −Fj→i ≥δ) resolves directions. Finally, selecting
π/sparsity by validation log-likelihood on held-out regimes lands on a plateau where j-stable dominates
vanilla—especially on denser graphs, where intersecting supports across regimes sheds many fragile edges.

Summary and Future Work
In this paper, we described a theory and implementation of an intuitionistic framework for decentralized
causal discovery, termed j-stable causal inference and a j-do-calculus (informally referred to as “judo
calculus"). Judo calculus overcomes the limitations of classical Boolean logic-based causality, which typically
assumes a single, universal truth: either “X causes Y" everywhere or it does not (Boolean logic). However, as
we illustrated in Section 5, real-world applications from biology to social science, causal effects depend on
regimes (age, country, dose, genotype, or lab protocol). Our proposed judo calculus formalizes this context
dependence formally as local truth: a causal claim is proven true on a cover of regimes, not everywhere
at once. The Lawvere-Tierney modal operator j chooses which regimes are relevant; j-stability means the
claim holds constructively and consistently across that family. Practically, j-stability lets us glue many messy
experiments with potentially conflicting observations into reliable conclusions without assuming the world
is uniform. Judo calculus extends Pearl’s do-calculus by requiring that interventions be stable along j-covers,
and reduces to the classical case for the trivial topology. We described a detailed set of modifications to
existing score-based, constraint-based and gradient based causal discovery methods, and a preliminary
set of experiments showing the effectiveness of judo calculus. A much more detailed set of experimental
validations is currently ongoing, and will be reported at a future date, along with a publicly accessible Python

properties of GES while injecting a TCM-guided regularization that improves stability and parsimony for
categorical and pathway-focused subsets.

B.9
A finite-sample guarantee (informal)
Assumption 3 (Local CI margin and testability). There exists γ > 0 such that for every e, j and every true
J-stable local CI φ at j, the corresponding d-sep (under the true G(e)) differs from any violating graph by at
least γ in a CI score (e.g., CMI or risk gap). Local tests control FWER ≤α uniformly in e, j.

B.10
Implementation notes for the S9 pipeline
• Sections and weights. Each row of umap_sections.csv corresponds to (e, j) with columns reg=e,
child=j, UMAP coordinates (x, y), and sameNN (normalize per j).
• Overlaps. Use mutual k-NN in UMAP among points with the same child j. Typical k ∈[10, 30];

• Local CIs. From G(e)(τ), enumerate local Markov CIs at j: { X ⊥⊥j | Pa(j) \ {X} : X ∈V \ ({j} ∪Pa(j)) }
