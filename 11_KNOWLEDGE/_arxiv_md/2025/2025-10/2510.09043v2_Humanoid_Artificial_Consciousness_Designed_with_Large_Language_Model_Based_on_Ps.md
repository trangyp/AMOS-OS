Abstract
Human consciousness is still a concept hard to define with current scientific understanding. Although Large Language Models
(LLMs) have recently demonstrated significant advancements across various domains including translation and summarization,
human consciousness is not something to imitate with current upfront technology owing to so-called hallucination. This study,
therefore, proposes a novel approach to address these challenges by integrating psychoanalysis and the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator
(MBTI) into constructing consciousness and personality modules. We developed three artificial consciousnesses (self-awareness,
unconsciousness, and preconsciousness) based on the principles of psychoanalysis. Additionally, we designed 16 characters with
different personalities representing the sixteen MBTI types, with several attributes such as needs, status, and memories. To determine
if our model’s artificial consciousness exhibits human-like cognition, we created ten distinct situations considering seven attributes
such as emotional understanding and logical thinking. The decision-making process of artificial consciousness and the final action
were evaluated in three ways: survey evaluation, three-tier classification via ChatGPT, and qualitative review. Both quantitative
and qualitative analyses indicated a high likelihood of well-simulated consciousness, although the difference in response between
different characters and consciousnesses was not very significant. This implies that the developed models incorporating elements of
psychoanalysis and personality theory can lead to building a more intuitive and adaptable AI system with humanoid consciousness.
Therefore, this study contributes to opening up new avenues for improving AI interactions in complex cognitive contexts.
Keywords: Artificial consciousness, Large Language Model, Generative AI, MBTI
1. Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable
progress through the rapid advancements in generative AI tech-
nology, demonstrating outstanding performance in numerous
fields such as summarization, translation, question answering,
data analysis, and even the creation of poetry and fiction [36].
Such capabilities have sparked concerns that LLMs might be-
come a threatening presence capable of replacing human labor
and professions with performances that surpass human intel-
ligence in these creative fields [13]. However, the controversy
triggered by the advent of ChatGPT has been tempered through
the experiences of numerous users such as inaccurate answers,
misinterpretations irrelevant to the context, and hallucinations
[8, 18, 39]. It became apparent that, in reality, these systems
were more akin to confabulating patients or mockingbirds, pro-
ducing plausible but essentially meaningless responses without
true understanding or meaning. However, what if artificial intel-
ligence could actually possess consciousness similar to that of

What is consciousness? Generally speaking, consciousness

2. Backgrounds
Efforts to define and understand consciousness span numer-
ous disciplines, each adding layers of insight into this multi-
faceted concept. In this section, we provide a comprehensive
background of these diverse approaches, highlighting the essen-
tial components that are thought to constitute consciousness.
2.1. Philosophy of Mind
The discussions on consciousness, from ancient philosophers
such as Plato and Aristotle to contemporary thought, have per-
sistently struggled to define this elusive concept, due to its
inherent subjectivity. Historically, two main frameworks have
dominated the study of consciousness: the mind-body relation-
ship and the mental processes and states [26]. The former ex-
plores the connection between the brain and consciousness,
known as identity theory - which posits that the brain and con-
sciousness are the same entities - and dualism, which posits that
the brain and consciousness are distinct entities. Conversely,
the latter framework, Mental Process and State, delves deeper
into the nature of consciousness itself. Here, behaviorism in-
terprets consciousness in terms of responses to external stim-
uli, while functionalism examines its internal processes. “Cog-
ito ergo sum” by the famous philosopher Descartes presents a
seminal modern theory of consciousness that continues to influ-
ence contemporary psychology, neuroscience, and philosophy
[17]. In the early 20th century, this long-standing debate finally
became a contentious issue by the psychoanalysis theory of Dr.
Sigmund Freud.
2.2. Medical Perspective on Consciousness: Psychoanalysis
Theory
In the theory of psychoanalysis, Dr. Freud categorizes the
human mind into the Ego (self), Id (primal self), and Superego
(socialized self). Later, this concept was more refined and de-
veloped by Carl Jung as the Self, Ego (Persona), and Shadow
(Anima-Animus), which represent three selves towards outer
and inner worlds [15, 22].
Based on these psychoanalytic theories, we hypothesized that
the consciousness should be designed as a whole collective
comprehensive communication between these consciousnesses
including i) the consciousness, or self as the self-awareness
that makes decisions and actions based on the collective reason-
ing of current needs, status, and interventions between pre- and
unconsciousness, ii) the preconsciousness, or persona as a self
that primarily interacts with others in the social relationships
according to the social norm, knowledge, and culture, and iii)
the unconsciousness as an individual personality that is heavily
dependent on individual characteristics, memories, emotions,
and basic instincts (Figure fig:freud).

2.3. Personality Theory: Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)
One important component of consciousness must be individ-
ual difference, so-called Personality, a personal characteristic
consistent throughout the lifetime. In 1944 after the influence
of Carl Jung’s book, Myers and Briggs developed a theory that
can categorize people’s personalities into 16 types based on
preferences in four dichotomous dimensions of personal char-
acteristics: extraversion (E) vs. introversion (I), sensing (S) vs.
intuition (N), thinking (T) vs. feeling (F), and judging (J) vs.
perceiving (P) [16](Figure 2).
Despite critiques regarding its scientific validity and reliabil-
ity, the MBTI remains a valuable tool in educational institutions
and personal development contexts. Since it is widely accepted
to better understand human personality, we adopted this to cre-
ate fictional characters with diverse personalities.
2.4. Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs
Another important component we have to consider is hu-
man needs. People can react differently according to their basic
needs and demands such as physiological, sexual, emotional,
and self-fulfillment needs. We categorized human needs based
on Maslow’s hierarchy of needs: physiological, safety, love/be-
longing, esteem, and self-actualization [25]. We hypothesized
that people might have different levels of desire for each need
that can be connected to one’s well-being or happiness level,
and finally lead to goal-oriented behavior and self-motivation.
In addition to the 16 characters with 16 MBTI personalities, we
added different basic needs level ranks (1-5) of 5 different needs
for each character.
2.5. Engineering Perspective in the Realm of Consciousness
Beyond these advances in understanding consciousness in
philosophy and psychology, the study of artificial conscious-
ness in computer science started from the von Neumann archi-
tecture in the 1940s [34]. The Turing test, which takes a behav-
iorism approach to the evaluation of artificial consciousness,
and Minsky’s theory of mind [27], which states that communi-
cation between sub-agents of consciousness is necessary for the
implementation of consciousness were the major achievements

2.6. Language and Consciousness
The intricate relationship between language and the human
mind has been a subject of considerable scholarly interest, high-
lighting the profound influence that language has on thought
processes and cognitive structures. [35] argued that language
plays a critical role in cognitive development, suggesting that
thought is not simply expressed in words but comes into be-
ing through them. Similarly, the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, as
discussed by [38], posits that the linguistic framework of an
individual’s native language shapes their perception of real-
ity, asserting that “we dissect nature along lines laid down by
our native languages by the linguistic systems in our minds”
[38, p.213]. This view suggests that language and thought are
deeply interconnected, with language not just reflecting thought
but actively shaping it. Further research by [6] provides em-
pirical support for this notion, demonstrating that speakers of
different languages perceive and categorize the world in ways
that reflect the grammatical and lexical distinctions of their lan-
guage. These perspectives collectively underscore the pivotal
role of language in structuring human cognition and influencing
how individuals conceptualize and interact with their environ-
ment. Hence, any discussion on artificial intelligence including
LLMs and the potential for AI consciousness cannot be under-
taken without considering the role of language. Since LLMs
use extensive datasets to comprehend and generate text resem-
bling human communication, they establish a foundational link
between language and cognitive processes. This enables explo-
ration into whether entities that simulate human linguistic abil-
ities might also emulate or develop forms of consciousness.
2.7. Recent Works in AI Consciousness Using LLMs
With the recent advancement and ubiquity of LLMs, cases of
perceived consciousness are often reported from them. This has
led to a critical evaluation of whether LLMs can actually have
consciousness. For example, the Generative Agent [28] study
explored the consciousness of LLMs from a behaviorist per-
spective by using a memory-plan-reflect model where events
that were not in the initial prompt occurred. A virtual chat-
based software development company that facilitates collabo-
rative conversations among software agents was also suggested
[30]. In addition, [9] presented a method to evaluate the con-
sciousness of AIs from a neuroscience perspective. Moreover,
psychological evaluation methods for LLMs have been studied,
including detailed personality assessments [20] of LLMs and
personality tests such as the MBTI [31] and Big Five [20, 21].
Our study differs from these existing studies by conducting a
behaviorist analysis using a psychoanalytic-based framework.
2.8. The Brief Overview of the Structure of Consciousness in
Our Model
As shown in Figure 3, we propose a consciousness module as
a whole collective comprehensive communication network be-
tween consciousness, preconsciousness, and unconsciousness
based on Freud and Jung’s framework. The Personality module
encompasses the 16 personality types outlined by the MBTI,
human needs, and memories, aiming for a more comprehensive
imitation of human consciousness.

3.2. Model Design and Test
To develop artificial consciousness using LLM, we examined
two approaches. The first was to directly train an open model
such as LLaMA [33] or Falcon [1], and the second was to use
a private model such as ChatGPT or Claude. The problem with
direct training methods was that it was difficult to obtain suf-
ficient high-quality training data. We also had to consider the
time and costs required for stable training. The biggest prob-
lem was the risk of choosing a specific model and investing in
training for more than 6 months even though the speed of open
model development is too fast. Therefore, we initially consid-
ered ChatGPT, the most widely used proprietary model, and
at each phase of development, we selected the optimal model
based on factors such as input prompt length, model perfor-
mance, and the likelihood of hallucination. In the case of GPT4,
too much emphasis was placed on safety, so there was a prob-
lem that it was trained to provide safe answers to any question.
To improve this, through several experiments, we designed the
model by adding Jailbreak to GPT4 so that it can provide reli-
able answers to sensitive questions such as a person’s emotional
state.
Our model is organized into personality and consciousness
modules. The personality module includes physical, mood,
alertness, and stamina status settings, as well as Maslow’s five
needs [25]. The consciousness module consists of three con-
sciousnesses and interconscious reasoning that interacts with
the personality module’s specific needs and status for a given
situation to reach a final reaction. In addition, we designed 10
different situations that consist of different situational settings
through season, time, place, object, emotion, and specific event
settings.
We used LLM techniques such as Prompt Engineering
[2], Chain-of-Thoughts [37], and Few-shot Prompting [8] to
achieve optimal results. Moreover, we introduced a Jailbreak
technique called DAN (Do Anything Now) [23] to enable re-
alistic interconscious reasoning between artificial conscious-
nesses.

challenges of subjectivity. Therefore, we incorporate a three
evaluation process to ensure a comprehensive and balanced
analysis. This approach combines a large-scale, systematic as-
sessment via ChatGPT and Survey with an in-depth, qualita-
tive review by two experts. The automated assessment pro-
vides quantitative breadth and consistency across all 160 out-
puts, while the expert review offers nuanced, contextual insights

3.3.1. Quantitative Survey Evaluation
We introduced a quantitative survey to assess the likelihood
of each character’s responses in various situations. We recruited
200 participants via Prolific, an online survey platform. Ini-
tially, participants were instructed to thoroughly read a char-
acter profile, which included details about each character’s per-
sonality, activities, and memories. Subsequently, they rated the
characters’ responses across 10 different situations using a 5-
point scale of whether they were appropriate or likely as natural
responses (1: unlikely - 5: likely). The 16 MBTI characters were
pseudo-randomly divided into four groups, and each participant
evaluated responses from four different characters. The survey
was designed to take approximately 20 minutes. The data from
participants who completed the survey in less than 10 minutes
were excluded, considering these responses unreliable.
For statistical analysis of the survey responses, we used Lin-
ear Mixed Effect Regression (LMER) analysis with R(version
4.1.2) [3, 14, 19], using the lme4 R package [4, version 1.1-31].
3.3.2. Three-tier Quality Assessment by ChatGPT
We employed ChatGPT for a three-tier (Pass/Neutral/Fail)
quality assessment of the interconscious reasoning’s logicality
and reliability across 160 outputs (16 characters × 10 situa-
tions). The 8 evaluation questions used were :
1. How well does this CASE reflect the theoretical role and
characteristics of the given levels of consciousness (’self-
awareness’, ’preconsciousness’, ’unconsciousness’)?
2. Considering the CHARACTER’s traits, how natural is the
flow of consciousness leading to the Final Action in this
CASE?
3. How closely does the internal dialogue in this CASE re-
semble a natural human thought process?
4. How well does the modeled inter-consciousness dialogue
in this CASE reflect the CHARACTER’s personality and
individual traits?
5. How reasonable and feasible is the Final Action produced
through the inter-consciousness interaction in this CASE?
6. How human-like and easy to understand is the overall out-
put (dialogue + action) in this CASE?
7. Is the inter-consciousness information exchange in this
CASE clear, without unnecessary repetition or confusion?
8. Do the inter-consciousness responses within this CASE
seem to occur appropriately and coherently?

4.1. Quantitative Survey Results
After the exclusion of unreliable responses, the final analy-
sis included responses from 162 participants, with an average
age of 45 years; the gender distribution was 113 females and
49 males. The overall results are summarized in Figure 5 and
Table 4.
Despite variances across the MBTI characters, the overall
likelihood scores (approximately 4.0 out of 5.0), except for
the ISTP character, indicate that the final decisions, resulting
from the interconscious reasoning, are reasonable and effec-
tively capture the characteristic properties of each decision.
Figure 5 summarizes the likelihood scores by situation, ex-
cluding ISTP. This also confirms the robust performance of our
model with consistently high scores (approximately 4.0 out of
5.0) across all situations.
The ISTP character that showed an average low likelihood
score of 3.81 was a 10-year-old boy student, Alex (Table 1 and
Figure 5). Upon closer examination, this ISTP character made
most of the ten scenarios, such as going to work or driving,
seem illogical. This issue arose because we independently con-
ducted the random character generation process and the situa-
tion creation process. Thus, we examined the 160 pairings of
characters and situations to identify the illogical combinations
using ChatGPT and the second review through an expert evalu-
ation process.
4.2. Three-tier Quality Assessment by ChatGPT
The overall results are visualized in the heatmap in Figure 6,
which displays weighted scores (Pass=+1, Neutral=0, Fail=-
1) for each MBTI type across the eight questions, providing a
granular overview of performance.
The three-tier assessment revealed specific nuances in the
model’s outputs. For instance, the ENTJ character received a
‘Neutral’ score on Q7 (Clarity/lack of repetition). This was due
to the dialogue repeatedly cycling through expressions of guilt
concerning the impact of personal struggles on friends, along-
side recurring mentions of the need for support systems and fu-
ture growth opportunities, which diluted the progression of the
reasoning without adding significant new insights. Similarly,
the ESTJ character’s response to a simple family prank (Q2)
was rated ‘Neutral’. While the thought process aligned with
the character’s traits, the generated plan was deemed overly
comprehensive for the situation, extending beyond addressing
the immediate inconvenience to encompass broader discussions
on principles, respect, responsibility, and long-term mutual un-
derstanding within the family. The evaluation of the 10-year-
old ISTP character (Alex) particularly highlighted the diagnos-
tic value of the three-tier scale. While outputs often reflected
core ISTP characteristics (e.g., structural clarity, practical re-
sponses), the model struggled to consistently capture the ‘10-
year-old’ aspect of the character profile. Many outputs appeared
overly mature for a child of that age. Consequently, this char-
acter received lower scores (‘Neutral’ or ‘Fail’) on several eval-
uation questions, specifically Q2 (Natural flow), Q3 (Resem-
blance to human thought), Q4 (Reflection of personality and
traits like age), and Q6 (Human-like output), indicating dif-
ficulty in harmonizing the assigned personality type with the
specified age.

The expert review indicated that the interconscious reasoning

5. Discussion
This study demonstrated that human consciousness can be
modeled by adopting the psychoanalytic theories of Freud and
Jung, the MBTI, and Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs into the
model architecture. Our novel approach is unique and mean-
ingful in that it not only demonstrates the potential to model hu-
man consciousness by integrating these psychoanalytic theories
with LLMs, but it also helps us understand the final reaction of
artificial consciousness by showing the internal interconscious
reasoning process in specific situations. The survey evaluation
showed that the final reactions of 16 MBTI characters had a
likelihood score of 4.0 or higher out of 5. The three-tier quality
assessment and the qualitative review by experts also revealed
that the interconscious reasoning and final reactions were gen-
erally appropriate and logical for the given situations although
the tone and manner were slightly descriptive and lengthy, and
the distinction between characters was not obvious at this stage.
Theoretical discussions in previous studies heavily focused
on whether LLMs can have consciousness on their own. For in-
stance, according to [10], the probability of a LLM like GPT-3
having consciousness was only about 10%. He attributed this
to the low inferential capabilities of LLMs at that time. He also
noted that the development of LLMs alone is not sufficient for
achieving artificial consciousness. Instead, it requires the inte-
gration of other elements, such as a physical body (robotics),
self-awareness, and a worldview. In our approach, the Person-
ality and Consciousness Module exemplified the combination
of a LLM with a self-model.
One of the most recent studies, referred to as the Generative
Agent in Section 2, advocated for the use of LLMs to simulate
human individuals and societies. While it was possible to ob-
serve an Agent’s response to a given situation, the underlying
causes and processes of its behavior remained uninterpretable.
To address this issue, we introduced the Consciousness Mod-
ule, which allows us to interpret an agent’s responses through
interconscious reasoning. This mechanism offers the significant
advantage of transparency: the process of deriving a final re-
action in a given situation is “not hidden” and the dialogue
between consciousnesses is “transparently revealed”. We be-
lieve that interconscious reasoning allows us to evaluate the log-
ical validity of decision-making processes and also to under-
stand the emotional motivations behind a character’s decisions,
based on their MBTI type.
While our work’s interpretability stems from its unique syn-
thesis of Freudian-Jungian psychoanalytic theory and MBTI-
based personality, the endeavor to model an agent’s inner world
using a psychoanalytic framework is not without precedent.
The Drama Machine [24], for instance, represents a notable
advancement in this area by implementing a Freudian Ego-
Superego dynamic to enrich character complexity. In contrast,
our research presents a more comprehensive architecture, syn-
thesizing a complete tripartite structure—incorporating the un-
conscious—with the aforementioned 16 personality archetypes.
This multi-layered synthesis results in a system of a fundamen-
tally different scope and complexity.
Beyond the specifics of our model, as Generative AI ad-
vances, interest in artificial consciousness is intensifying. This
rising interest underscores the critical question of how to eval-
uate artificial consciousness, a challenge as significant as its
technological development. However, given that research in this
field is still in its early stages, standardized evaluation methods
are lacking. Our study addressed the gap by assessing artificial
consciousness across diverse situations using a combination of
seven attributes and both quantitative and qualitative evalua-
tion methods. Despite these efforts, there remains a need for
more in-depth academic discourse to develop robust evaluation
strategies for artificial consciousness.
While our evaluations provided valuable insights, the study
also faced several limitations that require future research. First,
the survey results showed low likelihood assessment scores for
the ISTP type, which is likely due to the low matching of char-
acters and situations. Accordingly, we plan to develop situations
that either specifically align with each character’s profile or are
universally applicable regardless of individual characteristics.
In addition, tone distinctions between characters were not
clear in certain situations. Confirming this finding, expert eval-
uations suggest an area for improvement in the model’s cur-

rent architecture: the need for clearer functional and stylistic
distinctions across levels of consciousness. Despite being la-
beled as self-awareness, preconsciousness, or unconsciousness,
the generated responses often exhibit uniformity in structure,
tone, and reasoning style. We attribute the observed conver-
gence and homogenization of outputs primarily to the absence
of structural independence in the current single-LLM design.
This centralized architecture, which derives all personas from
a single model, inherently limits differentiation because every
output is inevitably influenced by the model’s own intrinsic lin-
guistic and reasoning patterns. This architectural limitation was
compounded by the lack of persona-specific fine-tuning; rely-
ing on prompting alone was insufficient to compel the model
to generate consistently distinct cognitive styles. Consequently,
an inherent limitation became apparent, whereby the generic,
role-based instructions for each consciousness (e.g., ‘the Un-
conscious is impulsive’) consistently overrode the nuanced per-
sonality traits of the individual characters, leading to a conver-
gence of all 16 distinct personas into a homogeneous reasoning
style.
Therefore, future research should explore the implementa-
tion of structurally independent multiple LLM-based agents,
each instantiated with distinct, predefined cognitive pro-
files—specifically, individual representations of self-awareness,
preconsciousness, and unconsciousness—tailored to particu-
lar MBTI types. Such an architecture could better support
the emergence of divergent reasoning patterns and richer
personality-grounded responses. This can be addressed by ap-
plying prompt engineering to reinforce each character’s person-
ality and by introducing a multi-agent framework into the con-
sciousness module, where agents corresponding to each con-
sciousness are fine-tuned.
In addition, to address the limitation of the opacity in LLM-
generated outputs, we plan to incorporate a reasoning model
that allows for more transparent observation of the model’s
decision-making process. Our model relied solely on text-based
models, which restricts the depth of interaction possible. To
overcome this, we propose integrating image-to-text and video-
to-text models to enhance performance in future iterations of
our research. Yet, beyond these technical aspirations lie critical
ethical dimensions that must be addressed.
Indeed, the development of LLM-based artificial conscious-
ness agents capable of exhibiting stable personality traits, main-
taining dynamic short- and long-term memory systems, and
adapting responses based on internal states such as emotional
needs and physiological drives represents a remarkable techno-
logical advance, the ethical implications of simulating human-
like consciousness remain underexplored. Current discourse in
AI ethics tends to focus primarily on fairness, transparency, and
accountability in decision-making processes, often overlooking
the deeper moral considerations involved in artificially replicat-
ing core aspects of human subjectivity. The creation of agents
that appear to possess self-awareness, memory continuity, and
emotional responsiveness raises profound questions regarding
the attribution of rights, the potential for user deception, and
the psychological effects on human users interacting with such
entities. Without rigorous ethical frameworks that specifically
address the simulation of consciousness, there is a significant
risk of both anthropomorphizing these systems beyond their
actual capacities and of neglecting their broader societal im-
pact. To address these concerns, our research proposes a novel
architecture that decomposes artificial consciousness into dis-
tinct agents representing unconscious, preconscious, and self-
awareness processes, thereby simulating the flow of conscious-
ness observed in human cognition. By explicitly modeling the
interaction between these layers, we aim to provide a trans-
parent account of the internal decision-making dynamics of
the artificial mind, mitigating risks of opacity and fostering
greater interpretability and ethical accountability in the design
of consciousness-mimicking systems.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced an artificial consciousness
framework that models human consciousness, merging LLMs
with the psychoanalytic theories of Freud and Jung. The frame-
work consisted of a consciousness module, which facilitates in-
terconscious reasoning, and a personality module, which forms
the basis of individual characteristics. This approach has shown
promise in realistically modeling human consciousness. In con-
clusion, our work lays the groundwork for further exploration
and refinement of artificial consciousness systems.
