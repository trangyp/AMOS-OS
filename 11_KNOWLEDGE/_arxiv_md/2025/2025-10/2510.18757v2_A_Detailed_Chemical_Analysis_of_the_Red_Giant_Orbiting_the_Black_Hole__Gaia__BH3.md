Thorium*
Zoe Hackshaw1aa, Keith Hawkins1aa, and Catherine Manea2aa
1 Department of Astronomy, The University of Texas at Austin, 2515 Speedway Boulevard, Austin, TX 78712, USA; zoehackshaw@utexas.edu

Abstract
Preliminary astrometric data from the fourth data release of the Gaia mission revealed a 33 M⊙dark companion to
a metal-poor red giant star, deemed Gaia BH3. This system hosts both the most massive known stellar-origin
black hole and the lowest-metallicity star yet discovered in orbit around a black hole. The formation pathway for
this peculiar stellar–black hole binary system has yet to be determined, with possible production mechanisms that
include isolated binary evolution and dynamical capture. The chemical composition of the stellar companion in
Gaia BH3 (hereafter BH3å) can help constrain the potential formation mechanisms of this system. Here, we
conduct the most comprehensive chemical analysis of BH3å to date using high resolution spectra obtained by the
Tull Coudé Spectrograph on the 2.7 m Harlan J. Smith Telescope at McDonald Observatory to constrain potential
formation mechanisms. We derived 29 elemental abundances ranging from lithium to thorium and find that BH3å
is an α-enriched ([α/Fe] = 0.41), r-I neutron-capture star ([Eu/Fe] = 0.57). We conclude that BH3å shows no
chemical peculiarities (defined as deviations from the expected chemical pattern of an r-I halo red giant) in any
elements, which is in alignment with both the dynamical capture and isolated binary evolution formation sce-
narios. With an upper limit detection on Th, we use the Th/Eu chronometer to place limits on the cosmochro-
nometric age of this system. These observations lay the groundwork for heavy-element chemical analysis for
subsequent black hole and low-metallicity stellar binaries that will likely be found in Gaia DR4.

1. Introduction
Stellar-mass black holes serve as key laboratories for probing
both stellar evolution and compact object formation (J. Strader
et al. 2012; M. Morscher et al. 2015; B. P. Abbott et al. 2016;
C. Bambi 2025). Dormant black holes in binaries are especially
valuable because the luminous companion can help constrain
the black hole properties without the observational challenges
introduced by accretion-driven emission. If the companion star
is metal poor, its spectrum preserves the chemical imprint of
early nucleosynthetic events, offering a window into the che-
mical conditions of the early Universe (C. Sneden et al. 2008;
A. Frebel 2018; I. U. Roederer et al. 2018). Studies of such
systems can provide critical constraints on the environments in
which the first generations of stars formed.
Preliminary Gaia DR4 results revealed a 33 M⊙dark com-
panion in an 11.6 yr, eccentric (e = 0.73) orbit with a metal-
poor 0.8 M⊙red giant star (hereafter BH3å; Gaia Collaboration
et al. 2024) identified through Gaia astrometry and confirmed
with the Radial Velocity Spectrometer (RVS; M. Cropper et al.
2018). This system, deemed Gaia BH3, is located ∼590 pc
away in the Galactic halo and shows no detectable emission
from the black hole, with only upper limits observed in the
radio (L. O. Sjouwerman & J. M. Blanchard 2024), infrared
(P. Kervella et al. 2025), ultraviolet (B. Sbarufatti et al. 2025),
and X-ray (N. Cappelluti et al. 2024; M. Gilfanov et al. 2024).
The lack of electromagnetic radiation across all wavelengths
supports the interpretation of the dark companion being a dor-
mant black hole in a binary with a low-metallicity star.
While two other dormant black holes in binaries have been
previously discovered with Gaia data (S. Chakrabarti et al.
2023; K. El-Badry et al. 2023a, 2023b; P. Nagarajan et al.
2023; A. Tanikawa et al. 2023), this system is particularly
unique. It is the most massive stellar-origin black hole known
to date, making it a valuable laboratory for studying hier-
archical black hole formation and the possible seeds of inter-
mediate-mass black holes, especially in the low-metallicity
regime (e.g., K. Belczynski et al. 2016). This is the lowest
metallicity star found in a binary with a black hole, and,
consequently, this system can provide constraints on stellar
evolution in the early Universe (e.g., J. Merritt et al. 2025).
Another unique aspect of the Gaia BH3 system is its asso-
ciation with the halo stream ED-2, making this the first black
hole to be found in a disrupted star cluster (E. Balbinot et al.
2024). ED-2 is a cold (low velocity dispersion) stream cur-
rently passing through the solar neighborhood on a highly
retrograde orbit (E. Balbinot et al. 2023; E. Dodd et al. 2023).
E. Balbinot et al. (2024) proposed that the ED-2 progenitor
was a star cluster due to the low dispersion of metallicities
among ED-2 stars. E. Dodd et al. (2025) reported 22 chemical
abundances of BH3å and other ED-2 stars using the Ultra-
violet and Visual Echelle Spectrograph (H. Dekker et al. 2000)
and supported the conclusion that ED-2 originated as an
ancient star cluster. We expand on this work by reporting more
heavy-element abundances with higher signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) spectra of BH3å, using these elements to place a

This black hole is the first of its kind located in a disrupted
star cluster, and as such the formation pathway of this kind of
system is still an open question. There are two main hypo-
thesized formation pathways for Gaia BH3: isolated binary
evolution or dynamical capture. In the isolated binary evol-
ution scenario, the two stars would have been born together
and remain bound even after the event that created the black
hole. G. Iorio et al. (2024), using models of evolved stellar
binaries, concluded that Gaia BH3 could plausibly have
formed through isolated binary evolution, but only if the natal
kick was mild enough to reproduce the orbital parameters

ejected the system from ED-2. Given Gaia BH3’s association
with a disrupted star cluster, the density of stars could have
caused BH3å to be dynamically captured in the orbit of the
black hole. D. Marín Pina et al. (2024) analysed star-by-star
simulations of globular clusters (GCs) and reproduced BH3-
like systems that dynamically formed with a black hole and
previously unbound star.
In both the isolated binary evolution scenario and the dyna-
mical capture scenario, chemical peculiarities of BH3å are
predicted to be unlikely. In the dynamical capture scenario,
BH3å would have no natal association with the black hole and
would look chemically similar to other ED-2 or halo stars. In the
isolated binary evolution scenario, there is the possibility of
ejecta or mass being accreted onto the secondary star in the
event that the primary star goes supernova (SN; i.e., P. B. Marks
& M. J. Sarna 1998; J. I. González Hernández et al. 2008;
Z.-W. Liu et al. 2015; A. Batta et al. 2017). However,
K. El-Badry (2024) found that the detection of pollution in BH3
is improbable due to the separation of the two objects and the
deep convective envelope of the red giant. Chemical abnorm-
alities may be detectable if the companion star was on the main
sequence, yet the dredge-up that occurs in giant stars would
likely erase all observable signatures of chemical abnormalities
(G. Iorio et al. 2024; A. Rosselli-Calderon et al. 2025).

in the near-UV optical, to derive the heaviest r-process

achieve this, we obtained 44 hr of observations of Gaia DR3

3. Methods
To derive the chemical abundances of BH3å and search for
any peculiarities, we first need to find the stellar parameters of
BH3å to ensure we are using the suitable model atmospheres
in our abundance analysis. One typical way of spectro-
scopically deriving Teff and log g is by using the Fe I and Fe II
excitation potential and ionization balance. However, this
method would likely yield inaccurate results due to the strong
nonlocal thermodynamic equilibrium (NLTE) effects in Fe for
metal-poor red giant stars (e.g, C. I. Short & P. H. Hauschildt
2003; M. Fabrizio et al. 2012; R. Ezzeddine et al. 2013, 2017;
M. Bergemann & T. Nordlander 2014). For completeness,
we report the stellar parameters derived through LTE excita-
tion and ionization balance as Teff = 4904 ± 12 K, log g =
1.89 ± 0.38 dex, and [Fe/H] = −2.69 ± 0.09. In general,
these stellar parameters resulted in systematically higher
[X/Fe] abundances when compared to the abundances derived
with our photometric stellar parameters.
We avoid using the excitation and ionization balance stellar
parameters due to the NLTE effects and instead obtain more
reliable stellar parameters for BH3å using the G −Ks color
and the Ks magnitude. Photometric temperatures and surface
gravities can reduce the discrepancies and provide more
accurate stellar parameters (e.g., L. Casagrande et al. 2010;
K. Lind et al. 2012; A. Frebel et al. 2013). We derived the
effective temperature and surface gravity of BH3å following
the procedures outlined in A. Mucciarelli et al. (2021) and
L. Casagrande & D. A. VandenBerg (2014), respectively. We
derived effective temperatures using the Gaia EDR3-2MASS
color-Teff transformations of A. Mucciarelli et al. (2021).
These relate Teff to Gaia G-band magnitude and 2MASS Ks
colors through metallicity-dependent polynomial fits calibrated
on infrared flux method (IRFM) temperatures. IRFM tem-
peratures are estimated by comparing the ratio of a star’s
bolometric flux to its infrared monochromatic flux with that
predicted by stellar atmosphere models (i.e., M. S. Bessell
et al. 1998). For BH3å, we adopted the G −Ks calibrations,
which have the smallest scatter ( Teff

4.2.1. Light Elements
We determined the abundances of the light elements Li, C,
and Na. These light element abundances for BH3å are plotted
in Figure 3 along with Sculptor, Reticulum II, ED-2, M15, and
other halo stars.
The majority of lithium (Li) in the Universe was created
during Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the Li abundance of
extremely metal-poor unevolved dwarf stars can represent the
primordial value (F. Spite & M. Spite 1982). A smaller fraction
of Li is thought to be synthesized by the ν-process, novae, and
SNe (F. X. Timmes et al. 1995; S. Borisov et al. 2024). The Li
abundance for BH3å was derived from the Li I 6707 Å line,
yielding [Li I/Fe] = 2.57. The absolute lithium abundance of A
(Li) = 1.34 places this star below the Spite plateau (F. Spite &
M. Spite 1982), as expected for a red giant. At this evolutionary
stage, the star has likely begun depleting its surface Li through
convective mixing and internal burning (e.g., A. Skumanich
1972; C. Charbonnel & S. C. Balachandran 2000; L. Sbordone
et al. 2010; R. Singh et al. 2025). The Spite plateau (purple line)
is illustrated in the left panel of Figure 3 by Milky Way halo
dwarf (triangle) and giant (square) stars and plotted alongside
BH3å. We do not see any chemical peculiarities in the Li
abundance of BH3å and it is well predicted by previous studies
of Li in metal-poor giants, such as C. A. Pilachowski et al.
(1993).
Carbon (C) is produced both by nucleosynthesis in massive
(M > 10 M⊙) stars and low- to intermediate- mass asymptotic
giant branch (AGB) stars (C. Kobayashi et al. 2020). The
carbon abundance of this star was derived using mainly the CH
band at ∼4300 Å. We note that there may be complications
with using solely the CH G band for the derivation of carbon
(i.e., P. Santos-Peral et al. 2025), yet no atomic C features
were detected in this star. We find [C I/Fe] = 0.27 ± 0.16,
which is below the threshold to classify this star as a carbon-
enhanced metal-poor star, typically defined as stars with
[C/Fe] > +0.7 (e.g, T. C. Beers et al. 1992; T. C. Beers &
N. Christlieb 2005; N. Christlieb et al. 2008). We see no
chemical peculiarities in the C abundance of BH3å, shown in
the middle panel of Figure 3.
Sodium (Na) is mainly produced during carbon-burning in
massive stars (S. E. Woosley & T. A. Weaver 1995;
C. Kobayashi et al. 2006; K. Nomoto et al. 2013) and enriches
the interstellar medium (ISM) in Type II SNe (F. X. Timmes
et al. 1995; K. A. Venn et al. 2004). Na can also be synthesized
during the third dredge-up in AGB stars (N. Mowlavi 1999).
We derived a sodium abundance of [Na I/Fe] = 0.0 ± 0.18
using two Na I lines at 8183.3 Å and 8194.8 Å. We see no
chemical peculiarities in the Na abundance of BH3å, shown in
the right panel of Figure 3.

4.2.3. Fe-peak Elements
We derived the abundances for the 10 Fe-peak elements Sc,
Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe ,Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn. The Fe-peak abun-
dances for BH3å are plotted in Figure 5 along with Sculptor,
Reticulum II, ED-2, M15, and other halo stars.
Type Ia SNe are the primary production sites for Fe-peak
elements, occurring when a carbon-oxygen white dwarf accretes
matter from a close companion star and exceeds the critical
mass to trigger a thermonuclear explosion (B. M. Tinsley 1980;
F. Matteucci & S. Recchi 2001; T. C. L. Trueman et al. 2025).
There are debates as to whether the companion star is a red giant
that overfills its Roche Lobe, causing the white dwarf to exceed
its Chandrasekhar mass (J. Whelan & I. Iben 1973) or another
white dwarf in which the binary loses angular momentum via
gravitational waves (I. Iben & A. V. Tutukov 1984; Z. Han &
P. Podsiadlowski 2004). Type II Sne also contribute to iron
production (T. Tsujimoto et al. 1995; A. Recio-Blanco et al.
2014), making Fe the prime element for tracing stellar metal-
licities (e.g., A. McWilliam 1997).
Titanium (Ti) has been previously classified as an α element
because it mimics the α elements in the context of Galactic
chemical evolution and can be produced by Type II SNe (i.e.,
K. A. Venn et al. 2004; T. Bensby et al. 2014), yet we classify
Ti as an Fe-peak element due its non-negligible contributions
from Type Ia SNe (J. D. Keegans et al. 2023; T. C. L. Trueman
et al. 2025). The production site of Ti in the Milky Way
remains an open question in both observations of SNe and
Galactic chemical evolution models (e.g., T. Mishenina et al.
2017; N. Prantzos et al. 2018; C. Kobayashi et al. 2020;
T. Sato et al. 2025). We report [Ti I/Fe] = 0.31 ± 0.15 and
[Ti II/Fe] = 0.28 ± 0.07. Similar to J. J. Cowan et al. (2020),
we find that Ti is enhanced relative to the solar abundance,
supporting the notion that Fe-peak synthesis sites produce an
overabundance of Ti at low metallicities. The overall Ti
abundance of BH3å is shown in the top middle panel of
Figure 5, agreeing well with other ED-2 stars and showing no
chemical peculiarities.
The odd-Z Fe-peak elements scandium (Sc), vanadium (V),
manganese (Mn), and cobalt (Co) have similarly debated pro-
duction sites as Ti. Sc typically follows α elements and is mainly
produced in massive stars (F. X. Timmes et al. 1995), while V,
Mn, and Co can be produced in both Type II and Type Ia SNe
(S. E. Woosley & T. A. Weaver 1995). C. Battistini & T. Bensby
(2015) found that Sc, V, and Co loosely track α elements and are
likely produced by Type II SNe, while Mn has significant
contribution from Type Ia SNe. V, Mn, and Co were estimated
each using one line at V II 4875, Mn I 4823, and Co I 5483,
respectively. Our Odd-Z Fe-peak abundances are [Sc II/Fe] =
0.07 ± 0.09, [V II/Fe] = 0.28 ± 0.17, [Mn I/Fe] = −0.52 ± 0.16,
and [Co I/Fe] = 0.12 ± 0.12. It has been shown that Sc, V, and Ti
are positively correlated, especially when pushing to stars with
[Fe/H] < −2 (J. J. Cowan et al. 2020) and our Sc, V, and Ti
abundances for BH3å agree with this finding. We show the Sc, V,
Mn, and Co abundances in the top left, top right, center, and
middle right panels of Figure 5, respectively. There are no obvious
chemical peculiarities or departures from other ED-2 abundances
in any of these elements.

In neutron-dense environments, the heavier elements on the
periodic table are formed through neutron-capture processes,
where neutrons will attach to a seed nucleus and eventually
decay into a stable element. Elements produced with contribu-
tions from the slow neutron capture (s-process) range from Sr to
Pb. Meanwhile, s-process nucleosynthesis typically occurs in
AGB stars, in which thermal pulses create a high neutron flux
which are then captured by seed nuclei to create s-process
elements through radioactive decay (R. Gallino et al. 1998;
M. Busso et al. 1999; A. I. Karakas & J. C. Lattanzio 2014;
G. Contursi et al. 2024). The heaviest elements on the periodic
table are synthesized in the rapid neutron-capture process. The
astrophysical site(s) for the r-process remain uncertain, with
proposed sources including neutron star mergers (B. Abbott
et al. 2017; M. R. Drout et al. 2017), jet-driven magnetorota-
tional SNe (N. Nishimura et al. 2017), and collapsars
(D. M. Siegel et al. 2019). Studies of metal-poor stars enriched
in r-process material can provide valuable insight into this
unresolved question (e.g., D. L. Burris et al. 2000; C. Sneden
et al. 2000; V. Hill et al. 2002; C. Sneden et al. 2003; A. P. Ji
et al. 2016; A. Bandyopadhyay et al. 2024; I. U. Roederer et al.
2024; S. P. Shah et al. 2024).

solar r-process abundance pattern. Stars across a range of
metallicities exhibit a homogeneous pattern in the elemental

A. P. Ji et al. 2016; T. T. Hansen et al. 2017; X.-J. Xie et al.
2025), suggesting that the r-process yields are largely uniform

stars, ultrafaint dwarfs, and classical dSphs). We scale two

r-process abundances of BH3å tend to match the solar pattern,
it is unsurprising that there some offsets. A. Bandyopadhyay

pattern of the GC NGC 2298. This illustrates that while the
r-process pattern could be considered universal, environments
such as GCs can show large r-process dispersions among the
stellar populations, hinting at inhomogeneous mixing or
multiple r-process nucleosynthetic sites.

consistent with those of other ED-2 stars and follow the gen-

15% of halo stars fall into the r-I category, making such stars
relatively rare. Given the strong diagnostic power of r-process
elements in tracing nucleosynthetic pathways and Galactic
enrichment processes (i.e., T. Hansen et al. 2011; T. Tsujimoto
& T. Shigeyama 2014; C. Manea et al. 2024; S. Monty et al.
2024), the r-process signature in BH3å can provide valuable
constraints on its formation history and the broader chemical
evolution of its birth environment.

4.3. Cosmochronometry of BH3å
One of the benefits of characterizing the neutron-capture
elements of BH3å is the possibility of placing a nuclear cos-
mochronometric age on this star. Using the fact that elements
such as Th and U decay on cosmological timescales, we can
adopt heavy-element nuclear chronometers to estimate the age
of the gas from which the star formed (J. J. Cowan et al. 1991;
C. Sneden et al. 2002; V. M. Placco et al. 2017).
If Gaia BH3 formed and evolved through isolated binary
evolution, placing an age on BH3å would place an age on the
black hole. Asteroseismology is widely considered the gold
standard for determining stellar ages (A. Miglio et al. 2013),
but no asteroseismic signal has been detected in the NASA
Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (G. R. Ricker et al.
2014) data for BH3å (D. Hey et al. 2025). Thus, we attempted
to estimate the star’s age using nuclear cosmochronometry.
Heavy elements such as U and Th decay on cosmological
timescales, with half-lives of ∼4.47 Gyr and ∼14 Gyr,
respectively. We can use the detections of these elements in
stars to age the r-process enriched gas from which stars formed
(J. J. Cowan et al. 1991; C. Sneden et al. 2003; A. Frebel et al.
2007; V. Hill et al. 2017; S. P. Shah et al. 2023; Y. Y. Huang
et al. 2025). The most precise chronometer pair would be
U/Th, but we are limited to Th/Eu. For this exercise, we
assumed that our Th upper limit is a fixed Th abundance of
[Th/Fe] = 0.25 and used the following equation from V. Hill
et al. (2002):

5. Conclusions
Gaia BH3 is the third black hole discovered with Gaia
astrometry−a 33 M⊙mass black hole in a binary system with a
red giant star, located in the halo stream ED-2 (E. Balbinot
et al. 2024). This red giant (BH3å) is old, metal-poor
([Fe/H] = −2.27 ± 0.15), α-enhanced ([α/Fe] = 0.41), and
r-process enhanced ([Eu/Fe] = 0.57 ± 0.19). The formation
pathway of this system is still debated, with the two likely
candidates being isolated binary evolution or dynamical capture.
In both cases, chemical peculiarities of BH3å are predicted to be
unlikely. Here, we present the most comprehensive chemical
analysis to date of the red giant in Gaia BH3 and aim to confirm
the lack of chemical peculiarities.
We obtained 43.5 hr of high-resolution spectra of BH3å
from the Tull Coudé spectrograph on the 2.7 m Harlan J. Smith
Telescope at McDonald Observatory to characterize the
abundances of BH3å. We determined the stellar parameters of
BH3å photometrically, yielding Teff = 5416 ± 84 K and log
g = 3.00 ± 0.14 using the G-band magnitude from Gaia
Collaboration et al. (2022) and the Ks magnitude from 2MASS
(R. M. Cutri et al. 2003). Employing the stellar parameter
and chemical abundance code BACCHUS, we derived the

first known black hole binaries with a low-metallicity luminous
