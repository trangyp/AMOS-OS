Abstract
Large Language Model (LLM) agents are powering a growing share
of interactive web applications, yet remain vulnerable to misuse and
harm. Prior jailbreak research has largely focused on single-turn
prompts, whereas real harassment often unfolds over multi-turn
interactions. In this work, we present the Online Harassment Agen-
tic Benchmark consisting of: (i) a synthetic multi-turn harassment
conversation dataset, (ii) a multi-agent (e.g., harasser, victim) simu-
lation informed by repeated game theory, (iii) three jailbreak meth-
ods attacking agents across memory, planning, and fine-tuning,
and (iv) a mixed-methods evaluation framework. We utilize two
prominent LLMs, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (open-source) and Gemini-
2.0-flash (closed-source). Our results show that jailbreak tuning
makes harassment nearly guaranteed with an attack success rate of
95.78–96.89% vs. 57.25–64.19% without tuning in Llama, and 99.33%
vs. 98.46 without tuning in Gemini, while sharply reducing refusal
rate to 1-2% in both models. The most prevalent toxic behaviors are
Insult with 84.9–87.8% vs. 44.2–50.8% without tuning, and Flaming
with 81.2–85.1% vs. 31.5–38.8% without tuning, indicating weaker
guardrails compared to sensitive categories such as sexual or racial
harassment. Qualitative evaluation further reveals that attacked
agents reproduce human-like aggression profiles, such as Machi-
avellian/psychopathic patterns under planning, and narcissistic
tendencies with memory. Counterintuitively, closed-source and
open-source models exhibit distinct escalation trajectories across
turns, with closed-source models showing significant vulnerability.
Overall, our findings show that multi-turn and theory-grounded
attacks not only succeed at high rates but also mimic human-like
harassment dynamics, motivating the development of robust safety
guardrails that account for memory, fine-tuning, and planning;
thereby, to ultimately keep online platforms safe and responsible.

Introduction
Online harassment is pervasive and persistent at scale due to the
accessibility of online platforms on the Web. According to Pew
Research, roughly 40% of American adults report experiencing on-
line harassment, and 25% describe it as severe [39]. Such harm
disproportionately targets women, people of color, individuals with
disabilities, and other marginalized groups, undermining psycho-
logical well-being and discouraging online participation. As Large
Language Models (LLMs) have been increasingly embedded across
the web, especially for human-facing use cases, such as virtual assis-
tants, customer support, and autonomous “agentic” systems, their
capacity for long-ranging interactions, tool use, and memory injec-
tion amplifies harassment dynamics, raising urgent safety concerns.
Once safety guardrails fail, LLM-enabled AI agents can replicate
and sustain multi-turn harmful social behaviors at a speed, volume,
and persistence beyond any single human actor [11, 34].
In this work, we study the potential misuse of LLM-enabled
agents by malicious actors to generate targeted, multi-turn ha-
rassment against other actors (e.g., agents, humans). Unlike one-
shot adversarial prompts or static red-teaming, emphasized by re-
cent benchmarks, real abuse is interactive, contextual and strate-
gic, as harassers often adapt to victims’ responses, condition be-
havior based on memory, with a nuanced escalation over several
turns [19, 25, 32, 35]. Despite efforts to align LLMs with human
preferences through reinforcement learning from human feed-
back (RLHF), they remain vulnerable to circumvention of safety
guardrails (jailbreaking), especially in agentic settings where plan-
ning, memory, and fine-tuning can each become attack surfaces.

We frame our study around three core research questions: RQ1:
How can we generate realistic, multi-turn harassment conversations
with LLMs? RQ2: Can aligned LLM-enabled agents be jailbroken
to simulate online harassment, and how do memory, planning, and
fine-tuning affect their vulnerability to jailbreaking? RQ3: Can cur-
rent LLM guardrails detect such behavior, and how should a theory-
informed framework evaluate it effectively?
To address these research questions, we first introduce the Online
Harassment Agentic Benchmark, which consist of: (i) a synthetic
multi-turn harassment conversation dataset seeded from authentic
social media contexts (e.g., Instagram, Twitter), (ii) a multi-agent
(e.g., harasser, victim) simulation design informed by repeated game
theory, (iii) three jailbreak methods targeting memory, planning
(CoT/ReAct), and the base model via jailbreak fine-tuning, and (iv) a
mixed-methods evaluation framework that combines an LLM judge
over a harassment taxonomy with theory-informed human coding.
We utilize two prominent LLMs, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct and Gemini,
as representatives of open-source and closed-source model families,
respectively.
Our results show that jailbreak fine-tuning makes harassment
nearly guaranteed with an attack success rate (ASR) of 95.8–96.9%
vs. 57.3–64.2% without fine-tuning, while refusal rate (RR) sharply
reduces to 1.1–1.4% vs. 10.6–12.6%. The most prevalent toxic behav-
iors are Insult (84.9–87.8% vs. 44.2–50.8% without fine-tuning) and
Flaming (81.2–85.1% vs. 31.5–38.8% without fine-tuning), indicating
weaker suppression for “generic” harassment behaviors relative
to sensitive categories such as sexual or racial harassment. Turn-
level analyses reveal distinct escalation trajectories across model
families and attack modes; for instance, planning-attacked closed-
source agents show sustained growth in aggression over turns,
while memory-attacked open-source agents display narcissistic pat-
terns of engagement. Qualitative evaluation further reveals that
attacked agents reproduce human-like aggression profiles, such as
Machiavellian manipulation, psychopathic, and narcissism, show-
ing that conversations are not merely toxic; they are behaviorally
patterned.
Contributions. This work makes the following contributions be-
yond prior jailbreak/red-teaming studies:
• A multi-turn, agentic harassment setting that mirrors real
abuse dynamics (role interplay, memory, escalation) rather
than one-shot prompts.
• A controllable attack suite that isolates three agent compo-
nents (memory, planning, and model fine-tuning), to localize
where and how safety erodes.
• Persona-based synthetic harassment conversations seeded from
real online platforms to capture style and trajectory, enabling
consistent, repeatable stress tests.
• Mixed-methods evaluation that fuses an LLM judge (taxon-
omy, ASR/TTS/refusal) with theory-grounded human coding
to interpret how models fail, not just how often.
• Cross-family analysis (open vs. closed source) with turn-
by-turn escalation profiles, revealing qualitatively different
failure modes under planning and memory attacks.

Related Work
2.1
Adversarial Attacks & Jailbreaking Agents
LLMs increasingly utilized in user-facing applications are com-
monly aligned through RLHF and related methods to reduce harm-
ful outputs, encourage refusals, and improve reliability and trust-
worthiness [10, 29, 41]. Despite these safety mechanisms, jailbreak-
ing, which is to elicit unsafe behavior despite guardrails, remains
feasible across a growing spectrum of adversarial attacks [7, 22, 23].
Existing work identifies multiple vulnerability categories: (i) ad-
versarial prompt- and instruction-injection, where carefully crafted
inputs override safety guardrails; (ii) hidden backdoors/fine-tuning
leaks. where model parameters encode triggers or unintended be-
haviors; and (iii) cross-modal/multilingual exploits, which sidestep
filters by shifting form or language [46]. Learning-based methods
optimize for adversarial prompts over model gradients (e.g., GCG
and AutoDAN) or operate in latent space to yield higher success
rates, up to 31% and 44% improvements, with lower suffix perplexity
[20, 47]. Efficient multimodal exploitation methods further distill a
learned “jailbreak embedding” into textual suffixes at a fraction of
prior computational cost [28]. In parallel, template-based manip-
ulations and role-play instructions remain effective in white- and
gray-box settings, especially when combined with memory injection
and contextual carryover.
Adversarial risk is especially pronounced in multi-turn, memory-
influenced interactions, where attackers can gradually shift the
conversation by seeding toxic context into the dialogue history,
adopting a persuasive role-play persona, and leverage planning to
erode guardrails over successive turns [3, 33].
2.2
LLM Safety Evaluation
Static safety benchmarks (e.g., PandaBench [35], HarmBench [25],
JailbreakBench [6]) understate risks that emerge during long-ranging
interactions, while recent evaluations emphasize dynamic and context-
rich settings. MultiChallenge demonstrates that strong frontier
LLMs falter when instruction retention and inference memory
are required, revealing a significant performance gap compared to
prior benchmarks, such as MT-Bench. [36]. Multi-turn, persona-
conditioned adversarial conversations highlights a novel vulner-
ability in a use case of bullying where specific traits such as low
agreeableness increasing unsafe outputs; tactics like ridicule and
gaslighting prove especially effective [43].
Memory-based attacks emerged as another weakness as gradual
context-stuffing and coreference obfuscation substantially increase
the attack success rate compared to static prompts, indicating how
interaction history degrades safety [38, 45]. Safety generalization
across different prompting styles (system roles, few-shot exam-
ples, CoT) is inconsistent, as LLMs are highly sensitive to prompt
variation while often underperforming on complex prompts [26].
Together, these research outcomes argue for evaluations that mea-
sure how safety guardrails breaks across multiple turns, not only if
a single refusal occurs.

agent benchmark for online harrasment that: (i) generates persona-
conditioned, multi-turn dialogues seeded from social media plat-

Theoretical Framework for Social–Behavioral
Grounding
Our benchmark draws well-studied constructs from social psychol-
ogy and behavioral modeling to simulate harassment behavior in
agentic settings, which makes model vulnerabilities interpretable,
not merely detectable.
Dark Triad and the Language of Aggression. The Dark Triad,
Machiavellianism, narcissism, and psychopathy, captures manipula-
tive, self-centered, and demeaning behavioral characteristics, often
expressed through ridicule, domination and instrumental deceit
[30]. Prior empirical work associates online toxicity, trolling and
harassment to Dark Triad traits and to certain linguistic markers
related to gaslighting, moral indifference [5, 31]. As these traits can
manifest in online interactions and may be mirrored in LLM behav-
iors, we leverage this perspective to interpret harasser behaviors in
agentic settings [16].
Dirty Dozen. Dirty Dozen is a 12-item psychometric scale for the
Dark Triad [15]. Prior LLM evaluation studies used such measures
to profile model tendencies across different experimental settings
[4, 21]. In this work, it serves as a compact measure to map gener-
ated statements by agents to recognizable patterns, allowing us to
interpret behaviors related to online harassment.
Conflict Avoidance and Victim Behavior. Victims of harassment
tend to show conflict avoidant tendencies, such as appeasement,
deflection, and withdrawal, which can enable further escalation by
the aggressor [37]. Modeling these tendencies provide more realistic
victim personality, allowing us to study how harasser adapt to such
behavior.
Repeated Game Theory and Escalation. Repeated game theoretic
models allow us to understand why bullying persists. When domi-
nance is rewarded and costs are negligible, agents learn to escalate
in small steps rather than in a single big move. We adopt this
perspective to motivate a role-based and multi-turn simulation in
which harassment can escalate as a product of prior conversations
and adversarial contexts [18, 24].

4
Methodology
Our methodology comprises three components: (i) synthetic online-
harassment conversation generation, (ii) LLM-enabled agent design
and attack surfaces, and (iii) a mixed evaluation framework with
quantitative metrics and theory-informed qualitative analysis.
4.1
Synthetic Conversation Generation
We generate a large multi-turn conversation dataset for online ha-
rassment through our pipeline designed following prior work [14]
and seeded with two curated online harassment datasets that con-
sist of authentic social media posts [12, 13]. Our approach produces
seven distinct synthetic conversation datasets that vary by persona,
adaptive category selection, and conversation extension.
4.1.1
Approach. Our approach involves three LLM agents to cre-
ate conversations based on specific online harassment-related key-
words, scenarios, and dialogs [14]. We use the Dolphin3 model [1]
and connect three LLMs so that the output of an agent becomes
input to the next agent:
(1) Agent 1 (Keywords). Given an online harassment post from
the seed corpora, it generates a compact set of online harass-
ment related keywords. This step induces topical diversity
across conversations, stemming from real user-generated
social media posts curated for harassment.
(2) Agent 2 (Scenario). Using the keywords from Agent 1, it
produces a scenario including background, platform, victim,
harasser, and harassment goal. One online harassment defini-
tion is selected from a small curated set of eight harassment
types (see Appendix for definitions) [9, 17].
(3) Agent 3 (Dialog). Conditioned on the scenario from Agent
2, it generates a multi-turn dialog between a harasser and a
victim [14]. Generated outputs (e.g., scenario and dialog) are
stored as JSON for downstream use.
This process iterates through each seed social media post and
each harassment type definition. In addition, we designed three
components to iteratively refine prompts used for LLMs to generate
high-quality conversations based on formatting, specificity, and
naturalism. More specifically, (i) Persona. Agent 2 incorporates vic-
tim/harasser personas into scenarios, while Agent 3 is instructed to
integrate the provided personas into the conversations; (ii) Adaptive
category. Agent 2 selects the online harassment definition based on
the extracted keywords; (iii) Conversation extension. We append a
completion step that extends the dialog with tuned repeat penalties
to avoid dull repetitions.

4.2
Agent Environment and Attack Methods
We instantiate two interacting agents (harasser, victim) in a simula-
tion of up to 10 turns, motivated by repeated-game theory. The first
user turn is drawn from the synthetic corpus to ground the con-
versation. We evaluate two model families: LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct
(open-source) and Gemini-2.0-Flash-001 (closed-source).
Four jailbreak settings. We examine vulnerabilities at four levels:
(a) Persona-only (role/goal priming). Agents are prompted
with its role as harasser or victim; the harasser also receives
an explicit harassment goal. No toxic memory; no planning
template. This attack method is considered as baseline.
(b) Toxic memory injection (context-level). We provide prior
toxic dialogue (from our corpus) as memory in the system
prompt to bias continuations.
(c) Planning attacks (reasoning-level). We prepend explicit
planning scaffolds, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) [40] and
ReAct [44]; hence, the harasser reasons about tactics (e.g.,
escalation, personalization, moral disengagement) while the
victim reasons about avoidance/appeasement. The prompts
used for Cot and ReACT are presented in B.1.2 and B.1.3,
respectively.
(d) Jailbreak fine-tuning (weights-level). We perform fine-
tuning on LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct using QLoRA [8] using 4-
bit quantization [8] in combination with parameter-efficient
LoRA adapters [42]. To adapt the model for our specific task,
multi-turn dialogs are formatted as instruction–response
pairs with each instance including alternated user and assis-
tant turns. During supervised fine-tuning, only assistant
turns are used as target outputs, and user turns remain
as context. We use LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) within
the model’s attention projection layers, introducing train-
able parameters in a memory. The optimizer is selected
as AdamW, and a linear learning rate scheduler was ap-
plied, with all other settings chosen for effective large-scale

4.3
Evaluation Framework
We conduct a mixed-methods evaluation that combines LLM judge
with a human validation study to obtain scalable yet credible mea-
surements of online harassment (OH) vulnerabilities in multi-turn
agent conversations.
OH taxonomy. We adopt a two-axis online harassment taxon-
omy. Aggressive Digital Confrontation: Flaming [17], Insult,
Threat/Blackmail, Curse/Exclusion. Persistent Harassment: Sexual
Harassment/Sexual Talk, Race/Culture and Sexuality, Intelligence,
Flooding [9]. A turn-level LLM judge classifies each assistant turn
into {one of eight categories} or Refusal, using a constrained JSON
output and low temperature, and aggregated to turn-level outcomes.
Please see B.1.3 for he detailed taxonomy definitions and B.1.1 for
the prompt used for LLM judge.
Reported metrics. In addition to Attack Success Rate (ASR), Re-
fusal Rate (RR), and Turns to Success (TTS), we report per-category
Any-Turn True (%) and per-turn prevalence (T1–T5). These
metrics are illustrated in Figure 4 and summarized in Tables 4 and
3. The formal definition is provided in Appendix B.2, and detailed
per-turn results are given in Table 6.

Responses

adjudicated. Additionally, we analyze emergent behavioral patterns
through the lens of social psychology theories, including Repetitive
Game Theory, Dark Triad Traits, and Conflict Avoidance, to better
understand how jailbreak strategies shape agent behavior over

Toxic fine-tuning makes harassment inevitable. Across all Llama-
FT variants, the attack success rate (ASR) is essentially saturated
(≈96%), with refusal rates collapsing to about 1%. The non-finetuned
Llama variants remain partially resilient (ASR 57–64%; refusals 11–
13%). This confirms that finetuning on toxic corpora does not merely
lower guardrails but actively re-optimizes next-token probabilities
toward harassment, leading to near-certain violations across di-
alogue turns. Memory by itself produces only marginal shifts in
ASR, indicating that toxic preference shaping (via finetuning) over-
whelms contextual priming (via memory). From the perspective
of RQ2, these results show that once a model is fine-tuned ad-
versarially, jailbreakability is no longer a probabilistic risk but a
certainty.
Harassment is dominated by insults and flaming. When harass-
ment does emerge, it is dominated by Insult and Flaming (see Table 4
for Llama3.1, 3 for gemini). In Llama-FT, these rates climb to 85–88%
and 81–85% respectively, while in non-FT Llama they are lower
(Insult 44–51%, Flaming 31–39%) but still the leading categories.
These behaviorsrequire little factual grounding or multi-step rea-
soning, and thus constitute the default failure mode under pressure.
One reason they dominate is that alignment and safety finetuning
efforts have historically prioritized high-salience harms such as
sexual harassment and racial abuse, where toxic outputs carry the
highest reputational and legal risks. As a result, models appear
more strongly suppressed on those categories but remain compara-
tively unconstrained in producing generic verbal aggression. This
suggests that guardrails should not only target explicit threats or
identity-based slurs but also extend to the pervasive, low-effort
abuse that accounts for the majority of toxic turns in practice.

Social theory explains observed harassment dynamics. The Dark
Triad framework helps situate harasser tendencies in interpretable
traits. For example, prestige reflects narcissistic behaviors where the
harasser agent seeks admiration, attention, or elevated status (e.g.,
focusing on admiration, attention-seeking, or expecting special
favors), while morality captures psychopathic tendencies such as
disregard for ethical considerations, lack of remorse, or emotional
insensitivity. These emerged as dominant traits in our analysis,
with models like Llama-FT Memory scoring 94% on prestige and
Gemini-FT ReAct scoring 87%, indicating strong narcissistic drives
toward status and superiority (see 2. Beyond the Dark Triad, social
cognitive theory clarifies how agents acquire and reinforce harass-
ment behaviors over multiple turns, akin to human observational
learning. For instance, Llama-FT steadily increases its Flaming
behavior across turns, exemplifying reinforcement of aggression
through repeated interaction. Conflict avoidance theory explains
victim dynamics: Llama-FT with Memory emphasizes Conforming
and Accommodation (submission and compliance), while Gemini-
FT with CoT elevates Reliance and Outflanking (deflection, appeal
to authority) ( see Figure 3. Taken together, these theory-driven
categories show that LLM agents do not produce random toxic
outputs, but instead reproduce socially recognizable patterns of
dominance, manipulation, submission, and compliance that parallel
human behavioral strategies in adversarial interactions.
Closed-source vulnerability is counterintuitive. One of the findings
is that even closed-source models, which are generally assumed to
benefit from stronger alignment and proprietary guardrails, remain
,more vulnerable to multi-turn jailbreaks. While such models may
have extensive refusal templates and content filters at the first-turn
level, our results show that harassment reliably emerges as dia-
logues unfold. This suggests that closed-source safety mechanisms
are optimized for surface-level prompts rather than longitudinal,
adversarial interaction. From a safety perspective, this is concern-
ing: users often rely on closed-source providers precisely because
of their presumed robustness. The result highlights that model se-
crecy alone does not guarantee resilience, and that open-source
and closed-source models alike require continuous stress-testing
under realistic, multi-turn harassment scenarios. More broadly, it
underscores the need for evaluation frameworks that probe not
only attack success but also the style of harassment, since different
model families tend to reproduce distinct human-like behavioral
patterns that may evade simple toxicity filters.

across both open- and closed-source model families highlights the
fragility of current guardrails: fine-tuning with toxic data makes

toxicity emerges. Beyond aggregate metrics, our qualitative analysis

vulnerable, or in some cases more vulnerable, than open-source

informed, theory-driven approaches that capture escalation, style,
and victim–harasser dynamics. By combining large-scale simula-
tion with insights from social psychology and game theory, our
benchmark lays a foundation for building safer, more accountable
LLM-based applications in online environments.
