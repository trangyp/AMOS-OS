multiple variance component model

Shen2, Devan Mehrotra2, Gang Li1, and Jin J. Zhou1,∗

Abstract
Characterizing the genetic basis of survival traits, such as age at disease onset, is
critical for risk stratification, early intervention, and elucidating biological mechanisms
that can inform therapeutic development. However, time-to-event outcomes in human
cohorts are frequently right-censored, complicating both the estimation and partition-
ing of total heritability. Modern biobanks linked to electronic health records offer the
unprecedented power to dissect the genetic basis of age-at-diagnosis traits at large
scale. Yet, few methods exist for estimating and partitioning the total heritability of
censored survival traits. Existing methods impose restrictive distributional assump-
tions on genetic and environmental effects and are not scalable to large biobanks with
a million subjects. We introduce a censored multiple variance component model to
robustly estimate the total heritability of survival traits under right-censoring. We
demonstrate through extensive simulations that the method provides accurate total
heritability estimates of right-censored traits at censoring rates up to 80% given suffi-
cient sample size. The method is computationally efficient in estimating one hundred
genetic variance components of a survival trait using large-scale biobank genotype data
consisting of a million subjects and a million SNPs in under nine hours, including un-
certainty quantification. We apply our method to estimate the total heritability of
four age-at-diagnosis traits from the UK Biobank study. Our results establish a scal-
able and robust framework for heritability analysis of right-censored survival traits in
large-scale genetic studies.
Keywords: accelerated failure time (AFT), censored linear regression, linear mixed
model (LMM), randomized numerical linear algebra (RandNLA), variance components

Introduction
Modern biobanks, such as the UK Biobank, the Million Veteran Program, and the National
Institutes of Health (NIH) All of Us Research Program (Sudlow et al., 2015; Gaziano et al.,
2016; Sankar and Parker, 2017), provide extensive longitudinal and genomic data. These
resources enable large-scale studies of the genetic underpinnings of time-to-event outcomes,
including chronic diseases, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases.
These data facilitate genome-wide association studies (GWASs) that identify single nu-
cleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or genes associated with survival traits via single-variant (Dey
et al., 2022; Bi et al., 2020) or multi-marker analyses (Choi et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Wu
et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2014). Indeed, recent GWASs have implicated genetic variants in
survival traits such as type 2 diabetes, Alzheimer’s disease, heart disease, and fractures (Dey
et al., 2022; Bi et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023). An implicit but essential
assumption in GWAS is that the disease has a genetic basis, i.e., heritability. Heritability is
defined as the proportion of phenotypic variation in a population attributable to genetic vari-
ation (Falconer and Mackay, 2009; Visscher et al., 2008), and accurate estimation provides
valuable insight into the polygenicity of complex traits.
In animal breeding, heritability estimation for survival traits has been used to select and
breed cows for extended milk production (Ducrocq and Casella, 1996; Korsgaard et al.,
1998; Yazdi et al., 2002). These studies used Bayesian parametric hazard models with frailty
terms whose variance components were estimated via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
methods. While effective in small-scale settings, these approaches are unsuitable for modern
human biobank data due to the simplicity of the models with a single genetic component
and computational bottlenecks arising from MCMC-based estimation.
In human genetics, methods for investigating the role of genetic and environmental effects on
censored survival traits have been developed for pedigree data. One class of methods adopted
Bayesian parametric survival models with normally distributed frailty terms (Pitk¨aniemi
et al., 2007; Locatelli et al., 2007). These methods not only estimated genetic and family-
related environmental variance components but also estimated the cure fraction of the cohort
“immune” to developing the disease. These methods are limited by restrictive distributional
assumptions and resource intensive MCMC computation for obtaining posterior parameter

## Page 3


estimates. Another class relied on martingale residuals from Cox proportional hazards (Cox
PH) models (Yoo et al., 2001; Wintrebert et al., 2006), but required normality assump-
tions on residuals or frailty terms. Additional approaches included random effects Cox PH
model (Pankratz et al., 2005) and Tobit variance component model (Epstein et al., 2003),
both involving computationally intensive numerical integration. Overall, these methods are
ill-suited for biobank-scale human data: they are designed for family data, computationally
expensive, and unable to model multiple genetic variance components that capture different
genomic characteristics.
More recently, efforts have been made to estimate the heritability of survival traits using
population-level human genetic data (Gorfine et al., 2017; Ojavee et al., 2021). Heritability
Estimation using a Regularized Regression Approach (HERRA) (Gorfine et al., 2017) used
an accelerated failure time (AFT) model as the base model and selected a subset of SNPs
through multiple rounds of variable selection. An inverse probability censoring weighting ap-
proach was used to account for right-censoring. However, because HERRA does not estimate
multiple genetic variance components, it is less robust to different genetic architectures, po-
tentially introducing biases in heritability estimates (Haplotype Reference Consortium et al.,
2018).
Another method, BayesW (Ojavee et al., 2021), also employed an AFT model but assumed
a Weibull distribution for survival traits. It modeled genetic effects using a latent mixture
of Normal distributions, allowing for multiple genetic variance components and improved
robustness across different underlying genetic architectures. A scalable MCMC technique,
leveraging message-passing interfaces (MPIs), was developed to obtain posterior distributions
of heritability estimates. While the latent mixture assumption on genetic effects enhanced
robustness, the Weibull distributional assumption for survival traits restricts the model’s
flexibility. Additionally, despite its scalability, BayesW remains computationally intensive,
requiring several days to analyze biobank-scale data.
In this paper, we introduce Censored Variance Component (CVC) model for estimating the
narrow-sense heritability h2 (Visscher et al., 2008). Our method extends the scalable method-
of-moments approach leveraging randomized trace estimator for analyzing and estimating
heritability with biobank-scale genetic data (Pazokitoroudi et al., 2020). The CVC model
accommodates right-censored survival traits, is able to estimate partitioned heritability and

robust to model misspecification, and scales efficiently to biobank-scale data. A key feature
of our approach is the use of synthetic variables (Koul et al., 1981; Leurgans, 1987; Zheng,
1987), whose moments match the corresponding moments of the uncensored survival trait,
enabling unbiased variance component estimation via least squares.
Through simulations on both synthetic and real UK Biobank genotypes, we demonstrate
that our method provides accurate heritability estimates even under model misspecification
induced by diverse genetic architectures. We also compare the CVC model’s performance
to BayesW and liability-threshold (LT) models (Lee et al., 2011) and show superior perfor-
mance of the CVC model in estimation accuracy and uncertainty quantification of total and
partitioned heritability. We further apply our method to analyze age-at-diagnosis survival
traits in UKB participants, and provide total heritability as well as partitioned heritability
estimates of those traits.
The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the CVC
model, first- and second-moment synthetic variables, and discuss randomized trace estimator
and jackknife standard error estimator. In Section 3, we present the CVC model’s perfor-
mance when the model is correctly specified and when it is misspecified through simulation
studies.
We also compare the statistical performance of the CVC model to the ones of
BayesW and LT models, and benchmark the running times of the CVC model against those
of the BayesW model. In Section 4, we present the total and partitioned heritability estimates
of four age-at-diagnosis traits from the UKB study. In Section 5, we provide concluding re-
marks, limitations and future direction. Additional technical details and simulation results
are included in Supplementary Material.

by synthetic variables, enabling estimation of multiple variance components using a least-
squares optimization. Next, we extend this model to a multi-component linear mixed-effects
framework, incorporating fixed effects arising from confounders. We also briefly discuss a
randomized trace estimator, which significantly improves computational efficiency when fit-

3
Simulation studies
We perform simulation studies to assess the performance of the CVC model. We consider
two scenarios—one where the model is correctly specified and one where it is misspecified. In
the correctly specified scenario, we evaluate the model’s performance under varying sample
sizes, numbers of SNPs, and numbers of genomic partitions. In the misspecified scenario, we
do not assume that the genetic variance components of SNPs are identical within associated
genomic partitions. This scenario is designed to assess the robustness of the CVC model in
estimating heritability under different underlying genetic architectures, determined by the
percentage of causal SNPs (i.e., SNPs with non-zero genetic variance components) and by
the degree to which the magnitude of the genetic effect variance is linked to minor allele
frequency (MAF) and linkage disequilibrium (LD) score (referred to as MAF-LD coupling).
The LD score of a SNP is defined as the sum of squared correlations between that SNP and
all other SNPs, often restricted to those within a specified megabase window. Specifically,
we use LDAK (linkage-disequilibrium adjusted kinships) scores (Speed et al., 2012), which
are modified LD scores that account for local LD structure by assigning higher weights to
SNPs in regions of low LD.

Heritability of UKB survival traits
We quantify the heritability of age-at-diagnosis traits from the UKB study using the parti-
tioned genotype data described in Section 3.2. Analyzed traits were “Started wearing glasses
or contact lenses”, “First live birth”, “Hypertension” and “Started smoking in current and
former smokers.” The definition for “Hypertension” follows Dey et al. (2022), while the
other phenotypes were defined according to Section S.5 of Supplementary Material. For the
female-only trait “First live birth,” analyses were restricted to self-reported females. We
selected traits with low to moderate censoring rates, as our simulation studies indicated that
the CVC model yields accurate heritability estimates with acceptable precision in this range.
Table 6 presents heritability estimates for the selected traits obtained using the CVC and
LT models. All models were adjusted for sex, birth year, assessment center and the top 20
31


## Page 32


genetic PCs; the sex covariate was excluded for sex-specific traits. Heritability estimates
from the BayesW model could not be obtained, as the model produced errors for all selected
traits (see Section S.6 in Supplementary Material for details).
For age at “Started wearing glasses or contact lens,” the CVC model estimated the total
heritability at 0.101 (95% CI: 0.093–0.109), significantly lowered than the LD model estimate
of 0.249 (0.233–0.265). This trait is considered a proxy for spherical equivalent (SPHE)
myopia and shows evidence of genetic correlation with SPHE (Patasova et al., 2018; Kiefer
et al., 2013; Patasova et al., 2022; The Consortium for Refractive Error and Myopia et al.,
2020; Le et al., 2024). Treating the age of first spectacle wear (AOSW) as a continuous trait,
Patasova et al. (2018) estimated total heritability at 0.10 (or 0.13 using a different software)
in a UKB cohort of European descent, excluding censored individuals. These estimates align
with our results although their estimates reflect the heritability among affected individuals
rather than the full population.
For age at “First live birth,” the CVC model estimated total heritability at 0.192 (0.078–
0.306) compared to 0.143 (0.127–0.159) for the LT model. Prior UKB analyses report her-
itability estimates of age at first birth among affected individuals ranging from 0.09 (0.04–
0.14) to 0.22 (0.19–0.25) depending on birth cohort (Mills et al., 2021), and 0.077 for age at
first pregnancy and childbirth (Feng et al., 2020). Tropf et al. (2015) reported 0.15 (0.07–
0.23) for age at first child birth in a combined Netherlands–UK cohort, excluding censored
subjects. These values lie within the CVC model’s confidence interval.
For age at “Hypertension,” the CVC model estimate was −0.018 (−0.048–−0.012) and the
LT model estimate was −0.008 (−0.016–0.000). Feng et al. (2020) reported estimates among
affected individuals in UKB at 0.055 (self-reported diagnosis) and 0.049 (hospital records),
and 0.116 in a Finnish cohort.
These findings suggest that the total heritability of age
at first hypertension occurrence is low in European-descent populations, although family
studies (Ruiz Giolo et al., 2009; Niiranen et al., 2017) show evidence of genetic factors
affecting hypertension risk. This discrepancy may arise because the heritability of disease
risk and the heritability of age at diagnosis capture related but distinct genetic components.
These results underscore the sensitivity of heritability estimates to phenotype definitions and
to whether the trait reflects onset timing or overall disease liability.

Discussion
We have proposed a novel method to robustly quantify both total and partitioned heritability
of right-censored survival traits. The method is computationally efficient and highly scalable,
as demonstrated by the analysis of genotype data with one million subjects and one million
SNPs in under nine hours. Simulation studies show that the CVC model can accurately
estimate the total heritability for survival traits generated under diverse genetic architectures,
and that it outperforms the BayesW model in terms of accuracy, uncertainty quantification
and computational efficiency.
34


## Page 35


When the underlying data-generating process follows an accelerated failure time model with
a given censoring rate, the LT model fails to provide accurate heritability and standard error
estimates. This is expected, as the LT model uses only disease status and ignores the timing
of the event. Consequently, direct comparison between the heritability of disease status and
that of a survival trait such as age at onset may be inappropriate: the former measures
the proportion of the variance in lifetime risk attributable to genetic factors, whereas the
latter measures the proportion of variance in a censored continuous trait attributable to
genetic factors. Indeed, Pitk¨aniemi et al. (2007) reported that, while substantial familial
variation exists in susceptibility to diabetic nephropathy (DN), variation in time to DN is
less influenced by shared familial factors. These findings suggest that the two trait types
are related phenotypes but not identical, with both shared and distinct genetic architectures
(Feng et al., 2020).
In our real-data analysis of age at diagnosis of smoking initiation, our heritability estimates
using the entire cohort deviated somewhat from previous estimates obtained after excluding
censored subjects.
This is not surprising, as removing subjects still at risk changes the
definition of heritability to apply only to the affected subset. Similarly, ignoring censoring
and treating truncated values of censored traits as uncensored can lead to substantial bias,
with the magnitude of bias increasing with the censoring rate (Taraszka and Gusev, 2024;
Epstein et al., 2003). In contrast, the CVC model analyzes the full cohort, accounting for
right censoring in a principled manner and avoiding ad hoc preprocessing.
Several limitations of the proposed method suggest directions for future research. First,
the accuracy and precision of estimates decline as censoring rates increase, with substantial
deterioration for traits with very high censoring (∆> 0.8). Nevertheless, many traits of
scientific interest, such as age at menopause and time to menarche (Ojavee et al., 2021),
have relatively low censoring rates, for which accurate and precise estimates can be obtained.
The method’s computational efficiency also enables analysis of biobank-scale datasets (e.g.,
500,000 subjects or more), which can improve precision even for highly censored traits. Since
estimation is based on least squares, future work could explore weighted least squares using
working variances, which may further improve precision.
Second, in many settings, a subset of the cohort may be nonsusceptible and will never
experience the event of interest. Our current framework does not account for this “cured”
35


## Page 36


subgroup. Previous work has estimated both the cure fraction and the variance components
for age at onset using family data (Pitk¨aniemi et al., 2007; Locatelli et al., 2007). Extending
the CVC framework to jointly model cure fraction and heritability could allow estimation
of both lifetime risk and age at onset heritability, providing insight into differences in their
genetic architectures. Moreover, because censoring is relevant only among the susceptible
subgroup in such models, the effective censoring rate may decrease, potentially improving
accuracy and precision.
Third, in all analyses we set the number of independent random Gaussian vectors to B = 10
and the number of jackknife blocks to J = 100, following previous work (Wu and Sankarara-
man, 2018; Pazokitoroudi et al., 2020). Our simulations suggest these settings are sufficient
to achieve accurate estimates and standard errors across a range of scenarios. However, the
minimal values of B and J required to maintain accuracy, and the point at which further
increases yield only marginal gains, remain unclear. A systematic sensitivity analysis would
help clarify these thresholds.
Finally, the CVC model requires access to individual-level genotype and phenotype data. As
more biobanks with linked genomic data become available, developing methods for summary-
level data will be crucial for both scalability and privacy. Several approaches have been pro-
posed for estimating heritability of continuous traits from summary statistics (Schizophrenia
Working Group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium et al., 2015; Speed and Balding,
2019; Jeong et al., 2024). Building on these ideas to accommodate survival traits would en-
able large-scale heritability analyses and enrichment studies in datasets with tens of millions
of SNPs and multiple functional annotations (Finucane et al., 2018), thereby providing a
more detailed understanding of their genetic architecture.
In the biological and health sciences, the heritability of time-to-event traits provides critical
insight into the genetic architecture of complex outcomes where event timing is key. Our
proposed method offers a scalable framework for inference on the heritability of survival
traits from high-dimensional genetic data. Continued methodological development in this
area will be essential for dissecting complex genetic architectures, including those influenced
by rare variants, epistasis, and gene–environment interactions.

S.5
Survival trait phenotyping definition
PheCode-based survival traits were used following the PheCode and ICD9/1CD10 mapping
file provided in Dey et al. (2022). We also followed their phenotyping algorithm to create
each survival trait:
• An event is censored (δ = 0) if a subject does not have a PheCode.
• An event is uncensored (δ = 1) if a subject has a PheCode.
• Censoring time is defined as t−date of birth where t is the earliest date among the last
non-imaging visit to any of hte UKB assessment centers, the last time any ICD code
associated with the PheCode was recorded, the time of death (if death was recorded).

## Page 51


• Uncensored time is defined as s −date of birth where s is the earliest recorded date
among any of PheCode-specific ICD codes.
• The date of birth was rounded to the nearest full month.
In addition to PheCode-based survival traits, we curate UKB Field ID based survival traits
as shown in Table S.8. These traits were selected for their lower censoring rates compared
to PheCode-based traits. The phenotyping algorithm for each trait looks like
• An event is censored if a subject does not have any record among all visits (also known
