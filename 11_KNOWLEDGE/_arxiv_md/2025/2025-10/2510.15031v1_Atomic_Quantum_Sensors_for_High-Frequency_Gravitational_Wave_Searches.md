High-frequency gravitational waves represent an unexplored frontier of gravitational physics.
While pulsar timing arrays reach nHz frequencies and ground-based interferometers probe the audio
band, the regime above the MHz scale remains essentially untested. We propose a hybrid detection
framework in which gravitons convert to photons via the Gertsenshtein effect in strong magnetic
fields; the resulting electromagnetic signal is resonantly enhanced in a cavity and read out by atomic
quantum sensors. Our approach combines multiple channels, including long-coherence Raman in-
terferometry in alkali atoms, microwave Rydberg transitions, photoionization-based detection, and
inner-shell electronic resonances. We derive the graviton–photon conversion probability, compute
the induced photon flux, and estimate the shot-noise-limited spectral strain noise density. Depend-

Introduction — The direct detection of gravitational
waves (GWs) has opened a new observational window on
the Universe and established GW astronomy as a pre-
cision tool for astrophysics and cosmology [1]. Present
observatories, however, probe only a limited frequency
range:
ground-based interferometers access the audio
band from tens to thousands of Hz [2–6], planned space
missions target the mHz regime [7–15], and pulsar tim-
ing arrays access the nHz domain [16–19]. Atom interfer-
ometer proposals aim to bridge the intermediate sub-Hz
to 10 Hz band using quantum superpositions of atomic
states [20–23]. Together, these efforts have revolution-
ized our understanding of black holes, neutron stars, and
multi-messenger astronomy, providing a powerful picture
of the dynamic Universe [24–26].
By contrast, the high-frequency gravitational waves
(HFGWs) domain, spanning frequencies above the MHz
scale, remains essentially unexplored [27, 28]. Conven-
tional astrophysical processes are not expected to pro-
duce detectable signals in this regime [29]. Nonetheless, a
variety of speculative early-Universe mechanisms, includ-
ing violent phase transitions [30–35], decays of topologi-
cal defects [36–38], exotic compact object dynamics [39–
41], or binary mergers of light primordial black holes [42–
45], could in principle contribute to the background at
these frequencies. Detecting HFGWs would test uniquely
high-energy scales and epoch inaccessible by other means.
Even null results yield non-trivial bounds on physics be-
yond the Standard Model.
Detecting HFGWs is exceptionally challenging. Stan-
dard Michelson interferometry fails at short wavelengths
because of arm-length mismatch, motivating alternative
approaches. One long-studied mechanism is the Gertsen-
shtein effect [46], where a GW traversing a static mag-
netic field converts into an electromagnetic (EM) wave.
Although the conversion probability is tiny, it scales with
the square of both the field strength and interaction
length, suggesting that strong fields and resonant struc-
tures could amplify the signal [47–53].
In this Letter, we introduce a hybrid approach in
which GW–EM conversion in a resonant cavity [46] is
interfaced with atomic quantum sensors [54], providing
a new avenue to probe the HFGW band.
A passing
HFGW interacting with a strong static magnetic field
generates a cavity-enhanced EM wave; this signal im-
prints sidebands, frequency shifts, or phase modulations
on a probe laser for Raman schemes, with the high-
Q cavity acting simultaneously as transducer and am-
plifier.
Drawing inspiration from cavity optomechan-
ics [55], where mechanical vibrations modulate optical
fields, this scheme highlights the potential of high-Q res-
onators and quantum sensors [56] to bridge general rel-
ativity, quantum optics, and precision metrology. The
cavity field couples to atoms via the electric dipole in-

Discussion — We first consider microwave/Rydberg
implementations accessing the (1–100) GHz band. In this
regime, superconducting cavities offer ultra-high quality
factors and small mode volumes, while Rydberg atoms
provide large dipole moments scaling as ∼n2.
Ma-
ture readout techniques exist via circuit QED and single-
atom detection. A drawback is that the graviton–photon
conversion power decreases at lower frequencies, reduc-
ing sensitivity relative to optical schemes.
Neverthe-
less, the superior cavity technology and strong atomic
couplings make the microwave approach attractive for
proof-of-principle HFGW searches. Its projected reach
is comparable to other speculative concepts in this band,
such as superconducting rings [80] or enhanced magnetic-
conversion setups [81–83].
Optical Raman schemes, such as those based on the Cs
D2 line at λ ≈852 nm, operate in the frequency range
f ∼(1014–1015) Hz. The larger photon energy at opti-
cal frequencies is advantageous, since the Gertsenshtein
conversion rate scales with frequency, see Eq. (4). Fur-
thermore, optical Raman and clock techniques allow long
interrogation times τ ≫1 ms, with modest spontaneous
scattering for sufficiently large detuning ∆. The main
experimental challenge is to combine several demanding
requirements: high-Q optical cavities, strong static mag-
netic fields, and precise spatial overlap between the cavity
mode and the atomic ensemble.
The sensitivity estimates presented above are derived
under the assumption that quantum-projection noise is
the dominant limitation.
In practice, however, techni-
cal noise sources overwhelm quantum noise many orders
of magnitude earlier, and thus set the true experimental
floor. For optical cavities, mirror-coating thermal noise,
residual absorption, and mechanical vibrations broaden
the linewidth and mask weak signals. In superconduct-
ing microwave cavities, magnetic-flux penetration and
mechanical instabilities introduce additional dissipation
and instability [84]. On the atomic side, laser phase and
amplitude fluctuations, magnetic-field drifts, inhomoge-
neous broadening, atom-number uncertainty, and imper-
fect state preparation degrade coherence and signal con-
trast [58, 85]. Without suppressing such noise channels,
the quoted shot-noise-limited sensitivities cannot be ap-
proached in practice. Moreover, thermal and amplifier

the microwave regime, blackbody photon occupation al-

to sub-kelvin temperatures, while even the best avail-
able amplifiers only approach the quantum limit under
highly optimized conditions. Identifying and mitigating
the dominant noise mechanisms in each frequency band
is crucial for prototype design.
Our estimates so far have neglected cavity–atom hy-
bridization [86, 87]. When the atomic transition is tuned
near the cavity resonance, the two systems form polari-
ton modes with collective vacuum Rabi splitting ΩN =
2g
√
N for N atoms with single-photon coupling g, alter-
ing both signal transduction and noise scaling. In this
strong-coupling regime, the graviton-induced excitation
populates a hybrid polariton mode rather than a bare
cavity photon, changing the effective coupling strength
and its scaling with atom number. The sensitivity formu-
las quoted above thus apply only in the dispersive limit; a
full treatment of the resonant hybridized regime is left for
future work. Furthermore, only a fraction η of the gravi-
ton–photon–converted power couples into the resonant
cavity mode. Realistic values of η < 1 depend sensitively
on magnetic-field homogeneity, spatial overlap with the
cavity mode, and cavity losses, and must be included in
realistic projections. Strong magnetic fields also induce
Zeeman shifts ∝µBB, where µB = 5.8 × 10−5 eV/T. For
B >∼1 T, these shifts approach microwave transition fre-
quencies, causing broadening and decoherence. A practi-
cal solution is to place the atomic ensemble in a magnet-
ically shielded region, spatially separated from the con-
version cavity and coupled via a low-loss waveguide, with
the associated transfer loss absorbed into η.
Overall, the proposed schemes provide strong sensitiv-
ity in two complementary HFGW bands. In the (109–
1011) Hz range, the aggressive microwave configuration
reaches strain sensitivities approaching the BBN limit,
which bounds only stochastic backgrounds with long co-
herence times.
Such sensitivity opens the possibility
of probing stochastic signals sourced by a range of ex-
otic early-Universe phenomena, including evaporation or
mergers of light primordial black holes [42, 45, 88–91],
cosmic string cusp or kink bursts [92], or first-order phase
transitions at energy scales around 1013,GeV [93–96].
Even for cosmic origin, the improved techniques enable
sensitivity to inflation and (p)reheating with h2ΩGW ∼
10−9 [97–105], reaching amplitudes far below current in-
direct limits.
At f ∼1015 Hz, the optical Raman ap-
proach extends sensitivity by several orders of magnitude
beyond LSW experiments, opening a new observational
window onto particle-physics phenomena and exotic com-
pact objects, including axion-like particles and evaporat-
ing primordial black holes [28, 106].
Conclusion — We have presented a theoretical
framework for detecting high-frequency gravitational
waves (HFGWs) via graviton–photon conversion in
strong magnetic fields, followed by readout using atomic
quantum sensors. We analyzed several concrete imple-
mentations, including Raman schemes in alkalis, mi-
crowave Rydberg transitions, photoionization thresholds,
and inner-shell resonances, highlighting the versatility of
atomic platforms across a wide frequency range.

These results are significant in two complementary
ways. First, by approaching the BBN limit in the GHz

background from violent phase transitions, topological
defect dynamics, or primordial black hole evaporation.
Such sensitivities probe energy scales far beyond the
reach of terrestrial colliders, opening a direct observa-
tional window onto high-energy physics.
Second, the
pursuit of HFGW detection drives advances in quantum
sensing, motivating improvements in high-Q cavity engi-
neering, suppression of thermal and technical noise, con-
trol of large atomic ensembles, and long-lived coherence.
These progresses have broad impact across precision mea-
surement, quantum metrology, and fundamental tests of
gravity. In summary, while astrophysical HFGW sources
remain too faint to be detected, hybrid cavity–atomic
platforms provide a concrete path to reach cosmological
benchmarks and to develop quantum-limited sensors ca-
pable of probing unprecedented frequencies.
