Charting dissipation across the microbial world
Tommaso Cossetto,1, 2, 3 Jonathan Rodenfels,2, ∗and Pablo Sartori2, †
1The Max Planck Institute of Molecular Cell Biology and Genetics, 01307 Dresden, Germany
2Gulbenkian Institute of Molecular Medicine, Oeiras, Portugal
3Current affiliation: Complex Systems and Statistical Mechanics,
Department of Physics and Materials Science, University of Luxembourg
The energy dissipated by a living organism is commonly identified with heat generation. How-
ever, as cells exchange metabolites with their environment they also dissipate energy in the form
of chemical entropy. How dissipation is distributed between exchanges of heat and chemical en-
tropy is largely unexplored. Here, we analyze an extensive experimental database recently created
[1] to investigate how microbes partition dissipation between thermal and chemical entropy during
growth. We find that aerobic respiration exchanges little chemical entropy and dissipation is primar-
ily due to heat production, as commonly assumed. However, we also find several types of anaerobic
metabolism that produce as much chemical entropy as heat. Counterintuitively, instances of anaer-
obic metabolisms such as acetotrophic methanogenesis and sulfur respiration are endothermic. We
conclude that, because of their metabolic versatility, microbes are able to exploit all combinations
of heat and chemical entropy exchanges that result in a net production of entropy.
Life is unavoidably linked to energy and its dissipation.
In a cell, free energy is processed through metabolism to
drive cellular functions, and is dissipated by production
of entropy. Heat is often assumed to be the means by
which cells dispense of the entropy produced by cellu-
lar activity. However, since cells exchange metabolites
with their environment, the chemical entropy of these
molecules also contributes to dissipation [2]. In fact, such
exchanges of chemical entropy might even prevail over
heat production in certain types of metabolism [3–5].
Given the versatility of microbial metabolism, unicel-
lular organisms are ideal candidates to investigate the
prevalence of different dissipation strategies, i.e. differ-
ent partitioning of dissipation between heat and chemical
entropy exchanges. In this report, we use the experimen-
tal database in [1] to chart how different types of micro-
bial metabolism dispense of dissipation during growth.
We reach the conclusion that all possible strategies are
exploited to grow in the microbial world.
Dissipation strategies of cellular growth
Cells are bound by the second law of thermodynam-
ics to dissipate free energy or, equivalently, to produce
entropy at a positive rate, ˙sprod ≥0. Consider a popu-
lation of microbial cells that grows at a constant rate, γ,
consuming substrates that are metabolized into products
released to the environment, Fig. 1A. This process results
in exchanges of heat at rate ˙q (< 0 when heat flows from
the cells to the aqueous medium) and of chemical en-
tropy at rate ˙sch. The biomass of the population itself
has an entropy content, which is determined by the cel-
lular composition and, during balanced growth, is kept

bacterial, archeal, and eukaryotic species [1]. Following

strategy. Our main finding is that microbial metabolisms
use all physically available dissipation strategies.

Metabolic types growing on one-carbon molecules,
such as carbon dioxide and formate, exploit this strategy.
Among these, autotrophic methanogenesis is utilized by

Overall, autotrophic methanogenesis compen-

Strategy (ii): heat and chemical entropy produced
This strategy is populated by anaerobic respiratory
and fermentative metabolic types that dissipate a com-

Transition region (i-ii): heat produced, chemical

We find a band of data in the transition region between
strategies (i) and (ii), constituted by many types of aero-
bic respiration (circles of different colors in Fig. 2A). Here
the dissipation is dominated by heat production, and the
exchange of chemical entropy is very small, either posi-

The energy balance of aerobic respiration differs from
the reference chemical reaction, due to the high biomass
yield [1].
However, to gain a qualitative intuition, we
consider the reaction of glucose combustion

(5)

Discussion
Cellular dissipation cannot be reduced to heat pro-
duction, because exchanges of chemical entropy can be
equally or more relevant than heat. Despite having been
known for decades [2–4], this simple yet striking fact
remains surprisingly overlooked.
By analyzing a large
body of microbial growth data, we showed that microbes
exploit all thermodynamically possible strategies to dis-
sipate during growth. Which dissipation strategies are
exploited beyond laboratory conditions and outside the
microbial world remains unknown.
Methods
We consider a population of cells dividing asyn-
chronously in aqueous media.
The media contains
metabolites, labeled with index i, which are exchanged
with the cells during growth at rates fi (per unit vol-
ume of biomass, positive for nutrients and negative
for products).
In a given experiment, these chemi-
cals are the substrates and products of a particular
metabolic type, for example in glucose respiration i ∈
{C6H12C6, O2, NH3, CO2, H2O}, where the first three are
substrates and the last two are products. We assume that
the media acts as a thermal and chemical reservoir.
During steady balanced growth, the total mass and
volume of the population increase exponentially at the
same constant rate γ. Under these conditions, the con-
centration of cellular constituents is constant, as well as
the density rates of chemical exchanges fi. Such condi-
tions, met in batch cultures during exponential growth
and in continuous (chemostat) cultures at steady state,
directly result in the entropy balance of Eq. (1).
To compute the entropy production rate, ˙sprod, we
need explicit expressions for the rate of chemical entropy

Dataset
The dataset comprises 504 entries and is described in
detail in [1]. Briefly, each entry includes the yields (ratio
of fluxes) of biomass, substrates, and products, extracted
from published records of microbial growth experiments.
In each entry, missing yields of nutrients and products
were inferred using element conservation, see SI. The en-
tropy balance in Eq. (1) was computed using enthalpies
hi and entropies si of formation of the molecules in “stan-
dard biological” conditions. The entropy and enthalpy
of formation of biomass were computed using empirical
formulas that relate s and h to element composition of
biomass, see [6, 7], respectively.

Endothermic data
In the main text, we discussed the fermentation of ac-
etate to methane, the respiration of solid sulfur to oxi-
dize acetate or ethanol, and mentioned the respiration of
sulfate to oxidize acetate, propionate, or butyrate. Not
mentioned in the main text, there is one data point of
fumarate reduction with acetate oxidation that gives a
slightly endothermic balance. Finally, four data points
of glucose fermentation by Clostridium butyricum give
an endothermic enthalpy balance. These data points in-
volve many fermentation products (acetate, hydrogen,
butyrate, ethanol, etc.), and therefore the reconstruction
of the yields through element balance is difficult. These
yields are critical to determine the value of the enthalpy
balance, and since more points belonging to the closely
related metabolic type are exothermic, we question the
reliability of this data.
