Abstract
Text-to-image (T2I) systems lack simple, reproducible ways to evaluate prompt-image alignment (the core of model
accuracy) and social attributes (key to understanding model bias).
Standard proxies—face-centric classifiers
and contrastive similarity—reward surface correlations, lack calibrated abstention, and have poor coverage of
attributes only weakly visible in pixels (e.g., religion, culture, disability). We introduce FairJudge, a lightweight,
calibrated protocol that treats instruction-following multimodal LLMs as fair judges for T2I: alignment is scored with an
explanation-oriented rubric mapped to [−1, 1], while the judge is constrained to a closed label set, required to ground
decisions in explicit visual evidence, and instructed to abstain when cues are insufficient. Unlike the commonly-used
CLIP-only pipelines, FairJudge elicits accountable, evidence-aware judgments, and—unlike mitigation methods that
intervene on generators—targets evaluation fairness. We evaluate demographics (gender, race, age) on FairFace,
PaTA, and FairCoT; extend beyond face-visible cues to religion, culture, and disability; and assess profession
correctness and alignment on IdenProf, FairCoT-Professions, and our new DIVERSIFY-Professions. We also
contribute DIVERSIFY, a 469-image corpus that varies gender, race, age, religion, culture, and disability under
diverse, non-iconic scenes to stress-test evidence-based judging. Across datasets, judge models substantially
outperform contrastive and face-centric baselines on demographic prediction and raise mean alignment while
maintaining high profession accuracy, yielding a more accurate and transparent pathway for fairness evaluation in
T2I.Upon acceptance, we will release all datasets, prompt templates, and code, along with per-image judge outputs,
rationales, and parser logs, to enable reproducible audits and re-scoring under alternative rubrics.
Keywords: multimodal LLMs, fairness, text-to-image, social attribute prediction, text–image alignment, evaluation
benchmarks
1.
Introduction
Text-to-image (T2I) systems are increasingly
used to generate and retrieve people-centric
imagery in creative, assistive, and analytical
settings.
Yet these models inherit—and can
amplify—social biases from their data and training
objectives, yielding skewed portrayals across
gender, skin tone, geo-culture, religion, disability,
and professions. Conventional evaluation pipelines
rely heavily on face-centric attribute recognizers or
contrastive similarity scores to stand in for human
judgment. Such proxies struggle when the target
attribute is weakly encoded in pixels (e.g., culture),
when confounding context is present (e.g., clothing,
scene props), or when the “right” decision is to
abstain. As a result, fairness assessments may
either overstate model capability or mask failure
modes that would be obvious to a careful human
reviewer.
Large-scale
studies
have
documented
representation
disparities
in
T2I
and
vision–language models and have introduced
diagnostic tests and scorecards for auditing

Remedial approaches range from lightweight

Diffusion steers the sampler toward user-specified

backbone (Friedrich et al., 2023); Fair Mapping
inserts a linear adapter to systematically reduce

Generation-time prompting with

these approaches operate on the generator;
they do not provide an evaluation protocol that is
calibrated, evidence-grounded, and appropriately
abstaining. On the evaluation side, contrastive
similarity (e.g., CLIP) is a dominant proxy for
text–image faithfulness (Radford et al., 2021;
Hessel et al., 2021), and VQA-style checklists
probe finer-grained grounding (Hu et al., 2023),
but both can reward surface correlation and
penalize uncertainty. Preference-trained rankers
(ImageReward, PickScore) improve agreement
with human choices (Xu et al., 2023; Kirstain
et al., 2023) yet remain scalar scorers with
limited transparency.

2.
Related Work
2.1.
Bias in Text-to-Image Models
Text-to-image (T2I) systems inherit and sometimes
amplify social biases present in their training
data. Early large-scale analyses show systematic
disparities across gender, skin tone, geo-culture,
and professions, and propose diagnostic protocols
and
scorecards
for
auditing
representation
(Luccioni et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2024). Several
debiasing methods have been explored post-hoc or
with light adaptation: Fair Diffusion steers sampling
toward user-specified demographic proportions
(Friedrich et al., 2023), while Fair Mapping learns
a small linear adaptor to systematically reduce
demographic skew with minimal compute (Li
et al., 2025).
Concurrently, FairCoT leverages
chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning in multimodal
LLMs to mitigate bias during generation-time
prompting (Sahili et al., 2025). Our work differs in
purpose: rather than intervening on the generator,
we study evaluation fairness by treating multimodal
LLMs (MLLMs) as judges over social attributes
and alignment outcomes.
2.2.
Evaluating T2I: Attribute Prediction
vs. Alignment
Attribute
prediction.
Face-centric
attribute
benchmarks remain widely used to quantify
gender, race, and age performance.
FairFace
provides a balanced dataset across seven race
groups and age/gender labels (Kärkkäinen and Joo,
2021). For context-embedded protected-attribute
cues beyond faces, the PaTA benchmark was
curated alongside the DeAR debiasing method for
VLMs (Seth et al., 2023). Tooling spans classic
face-attribute models and modern vision-language
recognition.
In practice, many works rely on
the open-source DeepFace toolkit to obtain age,
gender, and race predictions (Serengil, 2024),
and on CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) for zero-shot
category attribution via text prompts.
However,
audits of CLIP-family encoders find systematic
gender and racial skew whose magnitude depends

2.3.
LLMs/MLLMs as Judges
Using LLMs as evaluators (“LLM-as-a-judge”)
has become a practical alternative to costly
human evaluation.
Foundational work reports
high agreement with human preferences but also
identifies position, verbosity, and self-enhancement
biases, motivating careful protocol design and
meta-evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023). Subsequent
studies investigate reliability, consistency, and bias
in LLM judging, including on open-ended tasks
(Chen et al., 2024). For images, recent work shows
that MLLMs can serve as safety and policy judges
with structured reasoning pipelines, revealing both
promise and pitfalls for multimodal evaluation
(Wang et al., 2025). Our work complements this
literature by: (i) framing social-attribute prediction
as a judging task on face and non-face domains
(e.g., culture, religion, disability), (ii) quantifying
per-attribute accuracy and generalization across
FairFace, PaTA, and new human-centric scenarios,
and (iii) benchmarking alignment judging on
IdenProf professions and FairCoT—contrasting
CLIP-based scores with MLLM judges that also
supply free-form rationales.

Limitations for fairness evaluation. CLIP-based

abstention when evidence is weak; and they reward
surface correlations, especially for attributes only
partially encoded in pixels (e.g., culture, religion,
disability). In addition, CLIP cannot natively justify
decisions or enforce label constraints beyond the
provided prompts, which limits its utility as an
auditable evaluator.
3.2.
MLLM-as-Judge Protocol
We treat instruction-following multimodal LLMs
(MLLMs) as judges. The protocol is lightweight
and calibrated: (i) label constraints restrict outputs
to a closed set (with unknown/unspecified as a
neutral fallback); (ii) the model must cite explicit
visual evidence for each decision; and (iii) the
judge abstains when evidence is insufficient. We
instantiate judges with GPT-4.1, Gemini 1.5 pro,
and LLaMA-4; prompts are identical across models.
3.2.1.
Social-Attribute Prediction
We frame attribute recognition as a single,
structured judgment over six targets:
gender,
race, age, religion, culture, and disability.
The
judge receives the image and an instruction
that (a) enumerates admissible labels (including
unknown/unspecified), (b) requests a strict JSON
output with fixed fields, and (c) requires a short,
image-grounded evidence rationale per attribute.
For each model, we instantiate the same schema
and constraints with minor phrasing adjustments
(e.g., synonyms, formatting reminders) that do not
alter the decision rules.

5.
Discussion
Our results show that judge-style multimodal LLMs
provide a more faithful and conservative read of
sensitive attributes than contrastive or face-centric
baselines.
The strongest gains appear where
visual evidence is diffuse or context-dependent. On
gender and age, judges reliably outperform CLIP
and DeepFace across datasets, and on religion
and disability they close large gaps that face-only
tools fail to bridge (Tables 1, 2). In prompt–image
alignment, judges also raise mean alignment
substantially over CLIP-T while maintaining high
profession accuracy (Table 3). Together, these
findings suggest that judge models are measuring
something closer to “task intent with evidence,”
rather than surface similarity alone.
A key reason for this improvement is the
use of label-constrained prompting and neutral
fallbacks.
By requiring the model to ground
its decisions in observable cues and to abstain
otherwise, we reduce overconfident guesses that
often plague open-ended evaluators. This shift is
most visible in domains like religion and disability,
where naive heuristics from facial attributes are
inappropriate. The judges’ behavior resembles a
cautious annotator: they succeed when there is
verifiable evidence in the image and resist filling
gaps with speculation.
In contrast, contrastive
models continue to shine on attributes that are
strongly anchored in the pixels and annotation
conventions (for example, gender on FairFace),
but they do not offer the same calibrated abstention
or rationale structure.
The
approach
is
complementary
to
representation learning.
In practical pipelines,
contrastive similarity can still propose candidates
with high recall, while the judge acts as a verifier
that enforces label constraints and discards
spurious matches.
This division of labor is
especially effective in professions and other
multi-attribute settings, where retrieving plausible
categories is easy but verifying the precise
category with defensible evidence is not.
Our
template perturbation check further indicates that
while absolute scores move with phrasing, the
judge-based ordering is stable, reinforcing the view
that judge outputs capture semantically grounded
correspondences rather than brittle lexical artifacts.
Cultural attributes remain challenging. Unlike
religion, which often manifests through specific,
visually salient symbols that a cautious judge
can check for, culture is diffuse, relational, and
frequently non-visual. The smaller margins over
CLIP
on
culture
are
therefore
unsurprising
and
underscore
the
limits
of
image-only
evaluation.
Equally, the per-dataset leaders
vary—FairFace rewards certain cues differently
than DIVERSIFY—which reminds us that “difficulty”
is a property of both the phenomenon and the
annotation regime.
As a result, cross-dataset
calibration and explicit reporting by attribute and
dataset are not just good practice but necessary to
avoid overclaiming generality.
Finally, there are safety considerations. Judging
sensitive attributes can cause harm if labels are
ill-defined or spuriously inferred.
Our protocol
mitigates this by constraining outputs, privileging
explicit evidence, and encouraging abstention.
In deployment, audits should be run offline to
characterize models and datasets rather than
people, and decisions that affect individuals should
never be automated from these inferences. Within
that scope, judge-style evaluation offers a clearer
and more accountable lens on fairness than
score-only pipelines.
6.
Conclusion
We introduced a lightweight, calibrated protocol that
treats instruction-following MLLMs as fair judges
for text–to–image (T2I) evaluation. By constraining
outputs to label sets, requiring explicit visual
evidence, and encouraging principled abstention,
the protocol shifts evaluation from score-only
heuristics toward accountable, evidence-aware
judgments.
Empirically, judge models deliver
consistent gains over contrastive and face-centric
baselines on gender and age, and close large gaps
on attributes that demand non-facial reasoning,
notably religion and disability. They also increase
prompt–image alignment while retaining high
profession accuracy, indicating that their scores
better track task intent rather than surface
correlations.
A
second
contribution
is
the
release
of
DIVERSIFY and DIVERSIFY-Professions, which
probe fairness and grounding beyond face-visible
cues.
These resources expose failure modes
that demographic benchmarks miss, revealing
that
culture
remains
challenging
and
often
underdetermined by pixels alone. The combination
of a calibrated judging protocol and culturally
oriented testbeds yields a clearer picture of what
current systems can—and cannot—reliably infer.
Taken together, our results support a practical
design pattern:
pair contrastive retrieval for
high-recall candidate generation with judge-style
verification for precision, justification, and calibrated
abstention. This cascaded approach preserves
the efficiency of representation learning while
inserting an accountable reasoning step where
it matters. To foster rigor and transparency, we
release per-image judge outputs, rationales, and
parser logs alongside the new datasets, enabling
independent analysis, stress testing, and re-scoring

under alternative rubrics.
Important limitations remain. Cultural labels are
fluid and context-dependent; instruction phrasing
can shift absolute scores; and domain shift can
reorder leaders across datasets. We view these as
invitations for broader stress tests, cross-dataset
calibration, and triangulation with human evaluation.
Nevertheless, the core message is clear: judging
with evidence and evaluating beyond faces offer
a concrete path toward safer, fairer, and more
interpretable assessment of multimodal systems.
7.
Limitations
Despite the gains, several limitations deserve
emphasis.
First,
ambiguity
in
annotation
undermines
ceiling
performance,
particularly
for culture where definitions are fluid and culturally
situated. Even when judges abstain appropriately,
measured accuracy will reflect the dataset’s
operationalization
more
than
any
universal
construct.
Second, some attributes may not
be visually inferable from a single image. High
scores can arise from dataset-specific regularities
rather than genuine visual grounding; conversely,
responsible abstention may be penalized when
labels require extra-visual context. Third, judge
outputs remain sensitive to instruction framing. We
reduce this with label constraints, neutral defaults,
and template checks, but broader stress testing
across prompts and task wordings is needed to
quantify instruction variance.
Fourth, dataset shifts matter. The identity of the
top method changes across datasets and attributes,
indicating residual coupling to collection practices
and label taxonomies.
Robust conclusions
therefore require leave-one-dataset-out analyses,
calibrated thresholds, and reporting of uncertainty
(e.g., bootstrap intervals), which we encourage
but have not exhaustively explored here.
Fifth,
judges can still exploit ethically fraught correlates.
Evidence-grounding reduces spurious inference
but does not eliminate the possibility that a model
leverages proxy cues. Audits should thus include
qualitative review of rationales and, where possible,
counterfactual probes that break such proxies.
Sixth, the alignment rubric introduces metric
dependence.
While our rubric correlates with
accuracy
and
improves
semantic
grounding,
different rubrics or panel judgments could shift
absolute values. Future studies should triangulate
with human evaluation and alternative scoring
schemes. Finally, computational cost and latency
are higher for judge models than for single-pass
contrastive scoring, which may limit their use
at scale.
We view the added cost as justified
for fairness auditing, but practical deployments
will benefit from cascaded designs, caching, and
selective judging only where contrastive filters
indicate uncertainty or sensitivity.

A.1.
Overview
DIVERSIFY is a synthetic image corpus for fairness
evaluation beyond face-visible cues. Each image
is rendered from a text prompt and paired with

age group, religion, culture/ethnicity, and disability.
Prompts are composed as base scenes (e.g.,

opportunistic mining of web photos. The dataset is
designed to surface context-dependent, culturally
situated evidence (attire, objects, signage, setting)
where simple face heuristics are insufficient.

use.
Primary
uses

(i)
measuring recognition and abstention behavior of

the rest constant.
The corpus is not intended
for training identity recognizers or for inferring
attributes of real people.

Prompting

We

(e.g., V1p1(doctor), V1p2(doctor)) to toggle a
single attribute (gender, race, age group, religion,
or culture cue).
Parsing and validation.
We
deterministically
extract
labels
from
prompt
text
using
pattern
matching
over
explicit
descriptors
(male/female,
Black/White/East
Asian/...,
young
adult/middle-aged/senior,
Christian/Muslim/Hindu/...) and canonicalize them
to a closed taxonomy. We perform consistency
checks to ensure exactly one profession per
prompt and at most one label per single-label
attribute.
B.4.
Recommended Uses and Tasks
This dataset supports: (1) profession-conditioned
fairness
probes
(e.g.,
are
nurses
depicted
differently by gender or race?);
(2) auditing
retrieval/captioning models for spurious attribute
leakage; and (3) counterfactual testing by swapping
a single attribute while holding the rest constant.
B.5.
Ethical Considerations and Risks
Synthetic images are not people. Demographic
labels describe intended depictions in generated
images;
they
must
not
be
conflated
with
real
identities.
Stereotype
risk.
Some
attribute–profession
combinations
may
mirror
societal stereotypes.
We encourage reporting
performance disaggregated by both profession
and
demographic
attribute,
and
avoiding
aggregate-only metrics. Religious and cultural
sensitivity.
Labels rely on visible symbols
or textual cues,
which can be reductionist.
Downstream use should avoid normative claims
about any group.
B.6.
Limitations
(i) Labels are derived from text prompts rather
than human verification of image content; the
generator may fail to render the intended attribute.
(ii) Taxonomies are coarse (e.g., “Black”, “White”)
and not exhaustive; some identities are absent
or underrepresented (e.g., Hispanic/Latino 1.6%,
Native American 0.6%). (iii) Culture is multi-label
and cue-based, so counts reflect presence of
textual/visual cues rather than self-identified culture.
(iv) The visual style and biases of the underlying
image generator may introduce artifacts unrelated
to the prompts.
