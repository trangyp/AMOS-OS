A Deterministic Limit Order Book Simulator
with Hawkes-Driven Order Flow
Sohaib El Karmi
IMT Atlantique, Brest, France
sohaib.el-karmi@imt-atlantique.net
Abstract
We present a reproducible research stack for market microstructure: a modern C++ deterministic
limit order book (LOB) engine, a multivariate marked Hawkes order-flow generator exposed to both
C++ and Python, and a set of diagnostics and benchmarks released with code and artifacts.
On the theory side, we recall stability conditions for linear and nonlinear Hawkes processes,
provide complete proofs with intuitive explanations, and use the time-rescaling theorem to build
goodness-of-fit tests.
Empirically, we calibrate and compare exponential vs. power-law kernels on Binance BTCUSDT
trades and LOBSTER AAPL Level-3 books, reporting likelihood, KS/QQ residuals, ACFs, and
branching ratios, with deterministic scripts to regenerate all figures and tables.
Our results highlight (i) the practical importance of subcritical but nearly unstable regimes for
realistic clustering, and (ii) where Hawkes-based simulators match or miss LOB stylized facts relative
to queue-reactive baselines. The full stack (code, configs, figures) accompanies this paper.
Keywords: Hawkes processes, limit order book, market microstructure, high-frequency trading,
time-rescaling, reproducible research

Introduction
Why simulate limit order books?
Modern financial markets operate through electronic limit order books (LOBs) where buyers and
sellers submit orders at various price levels. Understanding LOB dynamics is crucial for:
• Market making: Optimal quote placement requires predicting short-term price movements.
• Execution algorithms: Minimize market impact while executing large orders.
• Risk management: Assess liquidity risk during stressed market conditions.
• Regulation: Evaluate market stability and potential for flash crashes.
The challenge: Real LOB data is complex, high-dimensional, and exhibits stylized facts (clustering,
long memory, heavy tails) that simple models fail to capture.
Our approach: Combine a deterministic LOB engine with stochastic order flow driven by Hawkes
processes, balancing realism and analytical tractability.
1.1
Contributions
Our work makes three primary contributions:
1. Theoretical rigor with intuition: We provide complete proofs of stability conditions for linear
and nonlinear marked Hawkes processes, accompanied by intuitive explanations, concrete examples,
and visual illustrations. Unlike existing surveys that merely state results, we derive them from first
principles.
2. Production-ready simulator: We implement a C++17 LOB engine with price-time priority,
coupled with Python bindings for Hawkes process simulation. The architecture supports:
• Multiple kernel types (exponential, power-law)
• Mark distributions (log-normal, exponential)
• Reproducible experiments with seed control
• Interactive Streamlit interface
3. Comprehensive benchmarks: We calibrate our models on two datasets:
• Binance BTCUSDT: Crypto trades (high frequency, 24/7 market)
• LOBSTER AAPL: Equity LOB messages (traditional exchange)
All results are reproducible with provided scripts, configurations, and random seeds.
1.2
Related Work
Hawkes processes in finance.
Self–exciting point processes were introduced by Hawkes [1] and later
formalized as cluster processes by Hawkes and Oakes [2]. Their ergodicity and stability were rigorously
established by Br´emaud and Massouli´e [3], providing the theoretical backbone for modern self–exciting
dynamics. Simulation and inference methods rely on the thinning procedure of Lewis and Shedler [4] and
Ogata’s extensions [5, 6]. In financial econometrics, Bowsher [7] first modeled multivariate order–flow
clustering in continuous time, while Bacry, Mastromatteo, and Muzy [8] offered a systematic review
of Hawkes applications to market microstructure, together with non-parametric estimation tools [9].
Jaisson and Rosenbaum [10] later analyzed the nearly-unstable regime, linking high endogeneity to long
memory in order flow. Our framework builds upon these foundations to provide a pedagogical synthesis
and a reproducible implementation of Hawkes-driven order dynamics.
Limit order book modeling.
Structural approaches to limit order book (LOB) dynamics were pio-
neered by Cont and de Larrard [11], who characterized price changes as Markovian queueing processes.
Huang, Lehalle, and Rosenbaum [12] extended this perspective with the queue-reactive model, intro-
ducing state-dependent intensities that respond to the book configuration. We integrate these insights
by coupling self-exciting order arrivals with a deterministic matching engine, bridging statistical and
structural views of liquidity. All empirical analyses are based on the LOBSTER dataset of Huang and
Polak [13].

Goodness-of-fit procedures for point processes rest on the time-

Objectives and Scope
We pursue four concrete objectives that frame the contribution of this work:
1. Formal foundations: present a self-contained derivation of Hawkes processes for order-flow mod-
eling, including intensity construction and compensator calculus.
2. Stability and existence: state a precise stability condition for (possibly nonlinear) Hawkes

3. Empirical validation: calibrate the model on high-resolution LOB data and evaluate goodness-

4. Reproducibility: provide code, data access instructions, and configuration files so that all figures

2.1
Limit order books
Definition 2.1 (Limit order book). A limit order book (LOB) is a data structure maintaining all
outstanding buy and sell orders for an asset, organized by price level with time priority within each level.
Structure of a LOB:
The LOB has two sides:
• Bid side: Buy orders sorted by price (highest first)
• Ask side: Sell orders sorted by price (lowest first)
Key concepts:
• Best bid/ask: Highest buy price / lowest sell price
• Spread: Difference between best ask and best bid
• Mid-price: Average of best bid and best ask
• Depth: Total volume at each price level
Matching rule: Incoming market orders execute against the best available limit orders (price-time
priority).

2.3
Stylized facts of order flow
Empirical studies reveal several robust stylized facts:
1. Clustering: Orders arrive in bursts (high autocorrelation)
2. Long memory: ACF decays slowly (power-law rather than exponential)
3. Heavy tails: Large orders occur more frequently than Gaussian prediction
4. Self-excitation: Activity begets activity (market orders trigger more market orders)
5. Cross-excitation: Buy orders can trigger sell orders and vice versa
Why Hawkes processes?
Hawkes processes naturally capture these stylized facts:
• Clustering ←Self-excitation mechanism
• Long memory ←Power-law kernels
• Cross-excitation ←Multivariate structure
They provide a parsimonious, mathematically tractable framework that matches empirical evidence.

Discussion and Limitations
8.1
What Hawkes processes capture well
• Temporal clustering: Self-excitation naturally reproduces bursts of activity
• Autocorrelation: Positive ACF matches empirical observations
• Branching structure: Immigration-birth interpretation is intuitive and analytically tractable
• Parsimonious: Few parameters (µ, α, β) explain complex dynamics
8.2
Limitations
• No LOB state dependence: Hawkes intensity doesn’t condition on current depth, spread, or
imbalance. Queue-reactive models handle this better.
• Stationarity assumption: Real markets exhibit intraday seasonality, regime changes. Extensions
with time-varying baseline µ(t) needed.
20


## Page 21


• Latency and microstructure: Our model abstracts away latency, order types (e.g., iceberg
orders), and strategic behavior.
• Calibration challenges: Power-law kernels are harder to calibrate, sensitive to initialization.
8.3
Future directions
1. Hybrid models: Combine Hawkes excitation with LOB-dependent base intensity: µi(t) = f(depthi(t), spread(t)).
2. Learning-based calibration: Neural networks to learn kernel shapes from data (going beyond
parametric families).
3. Multi-asset contagion: Extend to cross-asset Hawkes processes (e.g., SPY ↔VIX).
4. Real-time inference: Develop online estimation algorithms for live trading applications.

Conclusion
We presented a reproducible framework for simulating limit order book dynamics driven by marked

Our contributions span theory (complete proofs with intuition), implementation
(C++/Python simulator), and empirics (benchmarks on Binance and LOBSTER data).
Key takeaways:
• Hawkes processes provide a mathematically rigorous and empirically validated approach to mod-
eling order flow clustering
• The nearly-unstable regime (ρ(G) ≈1) is crucial for realistic dynamics
• Time-rescaling diagnostics offer powerful goodness-of-fit tests
• Future work should integrate LOB state dependence for improved realism
