Abstract
Despite extensive research, the mechanisms through which on-
line platforms shape extremism and polarization remain poorly
understood. We identify and test a mechanism, grounded in empir-
ical evidence, that explains how ranking algorithms can amplify
both phenomena. This mechanism is based on well-documented
assumptions: (i) users exhibit position bias and tend to prefer items
displayed higher in the ranking, (ii) users prefer like-minded con-
tent, (iii) users with more extreme views are more likely to engage
actively, and (iv) ranking algorithms are popularity-based, assign-
ing higher positions to items that attract more clicks. Under these
conditions, when platforms additionally reward active engagement
and implement personalized rankings, users are inevitably driven
toward more extremist and polarized news consumption. We for-
malize this mechanism in a dynamical model, which we evaluate by
means of simulations and interactive experiments with hundreds of
human participants, where the rankings are updated dynamically
in response to user activity.

Introduction
Online platforms are central to political communication and public
discourse. A vast literature has examined their potential in shap-
ing opinion, fueling extremism, and contributing to polarization
[5, 9, 18]. The mechanisms that drive these effects, however, remain
contested. Some studies report limited attitudinal effects of altering
exposure during large platform interventions [26, 41], while other
work shows that exposure to opposing views can backfire and in-
crease partisan hostility [4]. Complementary evidence on Facebook
indicates that individual choices, more than algorithmic ranking,
reduce cross-cutting exposure, and that position in the ranked list
strongly shapes clicking behavior [6]. Similarly, field studies around
the 2020 U.S. election reveal that algorithmic choices strongly shape
exposure and engagement, yet the short-run effects on attitudes
are limited [24, 26, 27, 41]. These mixed findings underscore the
importance of understanding the interaction between user behavior
and algorithmic design choices [39].
Over the last years, a body of empirical research has documented
regularities in user interaction with news and ranked content. Ex-
amples include position bias, where items ranked higher attract
disproportionately more clicks [6, 16, 21, 32, 35, 44], confirmatory
tendencies or assortativity, where individuals prefer content aligned
with their prior beliefs [4, 6, 19, 34, 53], and a U-shaped distribution
of activity, where users with extreme views engage more intensively
through liking, sharing, or commenting [6, 20, 36]. Regarding algo-
rithmic design, user engagement (measured through clicks, shares,
or reactions) is the key criterion for optimizing ranking and recom-
mendation systems [26, 27, 30, 41].
Building on this evidence, we propose a mechanism that makes
explicit the feedback effects between user behavior and ranking
algorithms. In essence, the more a ranking algorithm favors highly
engaging content and personalization, the more the content con-
sumed by extreme users becomes visible on the platform to less
extreme users, who also tend to consume it. This creates a feedback
loop that amplifies both polarization and extremism in consump-
tion.
We propose a simple model that formalizes this mechanism; cal-
ibrate its parameters using behavioral data, analyze its predictions
via simulations, and test them in a human-in-the-loop experiment.
Specifically, we address three research questions:
RQ1: How can a minimal dynamic model of user–algorithm
coevolution capture the feedback mechanisms underlying news
consumption and engagement?
RQ2: How do personalization and engagement-based ranking
weights influence the amplification of extremism and polarization
in simulated user–algorithm interactions?
RQ3: To what extent do human-in-the-loop experiments with dy-
namically updating rankings reproduce the amplification patterns
predicted by the model?

Evidence from Online Platforms and Field Experiments. — A grow-
ing empirical literature examines how platform design and rank-
ing choices shape exposure, engagement, and attitudes across ma-
jor platforms [54]. Randomized controlled experiments conducted
during the 2020 U.S. presidential election on Facebook and Insta-
gram demonstrate that feed-ranking and reshare features strongly
shaped user exposure to and engagement with political content
[2, 24, 26, 27, 41]. Beyond Facebook, evidence from Twitter and
Google Search shows that algorithmic design also influences ampli-
fication and partisan engagement [30, 43], underscoring the broader
role of ranking choices in shaping online consumption. These ex-
periments also sparked debate about context and validity [3, 26].
Our work complements this literature by formalizing a simple
mechanism that links engagement-driven ranking and personal-
ization to amplification, and by testing it in controlled human-in-
the-loop experiments with dynamically updating rankings, which
allows us to probe user–algorithm feedback under transparent and
reproducible conditions.
Mechanisms and models (theory and simulation). — Several ana-
lytical and simulation-based models have been proposed to study
how user–algorithm feedback drives polarization. Analytical ap-
proaches [1] formalize opinion and network dynamics under algo-
rithmic influence, while agent-based and simulation studies [11, 29,
51] model adaptive interactions between users and recommender al-
gorithms, including engagement optimization and misinformation
spread. Work on feedback and personalization mechanisms [38, 40,
42, 45] further shows how user–algorithm loops amplify popularity
bias, polarization, and information cocoons. The model proposed in
this work is most similar to those recently introduced in [21–23, 48].
Compared to these models, our approach adopts a discrete formu-
lation and extends them by incorporating parameters derived from
human experiments. This focus on ranking dynamics complements
approaches to polarization and extremization that have examined
how users of social media adapt their behavior over time to the
feedback they receive from other users [33, 46].
Algorithmic Design for Mitigating Adverse Effects. — Develop-
ing recommendation and ranking algorithms that mitigate adverse
societal effects has emerged as a central research challenge. Exam-
ples include consequential systems that balance immediate utility
and long-term welfare [49], the incorporation of partisan audience
diversity as a reliability signal in collaborative filtering [8], atten-
tion mechanisms that emphasize topic-specific terms while down-
weighting polarized language [47], and the provision of negative
quantitative feedback via a “dislike” button [34]. More recent ap-
proaches include online feedback control to trade off click-through
rates with harm [12], the use of conformal risk control to provably
bound unwanted content in personalized recommendations [14],
graph-based strategies to disrupt radicalization pathways [17], and
model-side techniques to limit attention polarization [37].
Whereas these frameworks emphasize algorithmic performance,
our model offers a principled understanding of how user behavior
and ranking mechanisms coevolve, which can guide the design of
more transparent and theoretically grounded interventions.

Methodology
Our methodology consists of four stages. First, we introduce a
model that formalizes interactions between users and a dynamic
ranking system, determining item rankings based on the stance of
news and users under simple behavioral assumptions.
Second, we design an experiment on a simple online platform,
measuring the interactions carried out by human participants. We
call this experimental setup static ranking experiment, aimed at
measuring all relevant quantities in our model.
Third, we use the results of the static experiment to simulate
several scenarios with different ranking algorithms and analyze the
different outcomes.
In the fourth stage, we design a dynamic ranking experiment
to test the effect of rewarding active engagement and personaliza-
tion on the platform. This is achieved by evaluating two alternative
scenarios: one in which the ranking algorithm has no personaliza-
tion and ignores active engagement, and another with strong per-
sonalization and a strong reward for active engagement. We analyze
the outcomes obtained in the dynamic condition for two alternative
scenarios, perform statistical tests to assess their differences, and
compare the results with the model’s simulated predictions.
Fig. 1 summarizes the framework. A ranking system displays
personalized lists of news items to users, who interact through
clicks and highlights. These interactions update item popularity,
reshaping the ranking shown to subsequent users and creating a
feedback loop between user behavior and algorithmic visibility.

3.2
Survey Design
Our survey is designed to provide an interface that mimics the
behavior of an online platform, and to measure user interactions
with ranked news content. While simpler than similar platforms [10,
13, 15, 31, 50], it allows for the estimation of the quantities defined
in Section 3.1 and testing model predictions under different ranking
conditions.
Participants were recruited on Prolific, selecting only US resi-
dents with an approval rate above 90%. Using the “quota sample”
setting, we selected a balanced number of participants by gender
and US political affiliation. Each participant had up to 40 minutes to
complete the survey; during this time, they were shown the terms
and conditions, reported their political stance, and interacted with
ranked lists of news items. They received £1.50 for completing the
task ( ∼£9 per hour, based on average completion time).
The core of the survey comprised four tasks. In each task, par-
ticipants reported their stance (5 levels) on a specific topic and
interacted with related news items. The topics were “gender poli-
cies”, “vaccination”, “immigration”, and “climate change”, selected
as controversial (hard news) topics [6]. Task order was randomized
for each participant to mitigate order effects.
3.2.1
News Selection. For each of the four tasks, we selected two
news items per political stance (𝑁= 10 in total) from Ground.news 1;
the website groups news about the same event from multiple outlets
and categorizes these outlets into seven political stance categories,
with clear stance labels (Far Left, Left, Lean Left, Center, Lean Right,
Right, and Far Right).
The political stance of news item 𝑛, 𝑠𝑛, was obtained from the
Ground.news categorization of its source. Because we use five
stances {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2} rather than the seven of Ground.news, we
mapped them as follows: −2 for far left/left, −1 for learning left, 0
for center, 1 for learning right, and 2 for right/far right.
To maximize experimental control, for each topic (which corre-
sponds to a task), news items shown to participants were all about
the same specific event, covered by outlets spanning the political
spectrum 𝑆.
3.2.2
Task design. Each task involves three sequential steps, with
one screen per step (see Appendix A) :
(1) User Stance: Participants read the description of one of the
four topics and a description of the typical left/center/right
stance on that topic. They then reported their own stance on
a 5-item scale (Strongly Left, Leaning Left, Center, Leaning
Right, Strongly Right), which we coded onto the -2 to +2
scale.
(2) Ranked List and Clicking: Participants were shown a scrol-
lable ranked list of 𝑁= 10 news items about a topic-related
event. Each item displays a title and a left/center/right label.
Participants clicked on exactly one item. See Fig. 2 for an
example.

3.5
Experiment 2: Dynamic Ranking
Our second experiment involved human participants using a dy-
namically updated ranking. We conducted experiments under the
two extreme parameterizations of the ranking algorithm (scenarios
1 and 4 above).
We generated three independent rankings per topic for each
parameterization, i.e. 24 independent runs (2 parameterizations × 4
topics × 3 repetitions). Initial rankings are randomized in all runs.
Each participant was assigned to the four tasks in random order,
as in the static condition. In this case, however, the ranking was
drawn from a pool whose rankings were dynamically updated.
Each ranking remained locked during the participant’s interaction
(clicking and highlighting) and was released afterward.
To ensure sufficient interactions for the ranking dynamics to
converge to a stationary distribution, given the convergence time
observed in the simulations (Appendix D), we recruited a total of
1, 534 participants, yielding approximately 253 interactions per run.
Consumption Extremism and Consumption Polarization (Sec-
tion 3.1.3) were computed over a window of size 𝑤= 200 from
the last ranking updates of each run, discarding the approximately
first 50 periods to remove transients still dependent on the initial
ranking. Metrics were computed as means and standard deviations
across the three repetitions for each parameterization and topic.
We applied a one-tailed Mann–Whitney U-test to the distri-
butions obtained from Eqs. 6 and 7, considering each individual
click. The test assesses whether clicks under the personalized,
engagement-rewarding condition tend to be more extreme or po-
larized than those under the non-personalized baseline. Finally, we
computed the average rank of news items by stance 𝑠𝑛to quantify
how much items of different stances were promoted or demoted in
each scenario as a result of the ranking algorithm.

When personalization is introduced, the interaction of these
patterns reinforces same-stance engagement: ideologically aligned
clicks (H2) dominate item popularity, as they are no longer coun-
terbalanced by exposure to diverse or centrist content. Adding
engagement-based rewards intensifies this effect, as extremist users
contribute disproportionately to popularity (H3), pushing like-
minded extreme content higher in the ranking. The bias toward
top-ranked items (H1) further strengthens this feedback loop, lead-
ing users to click more frequently on confirmatory content already
positioned near the top. As a result, news consumption among left-
and right-leaning users drifts toward extreme same-stance content,
while attention to opposing or centrist items declines (RQ2).
Data-driven simulations confirm that these outcomes indeed
emerge from the interplay between user behavior and algorith-
mic design: Adjusting algorithmic parameters systematically alters
clicking patterns. The simulations also indicate that personaliza-
tion is the primary driver of polarization, while rewarding active
engagement boosts extremist content. Although our simulations
are not designed for quantitative predictions, they reproduce the
qualitative patterns observed in the experiment well.
Finally, our findings provide experimental evidence that two
ranking algorithms, differing only in personalization and engage-
ment parameters, produce systematically distinct user behavior,
establishing a causal link between algorithmic design and the am-
plification of extremism and polarization (RQ3). We quantify this
amplification as an approximate 20% increase in clicks toward more
extreme content from both left- and right-leaning users, and a 13%
increase in clicks to same-stance news among all users.
These results demonstrate that algorithmic personalization and
engagement optimization, while effective at capturing attention,
can unintentionally intensify ideological polarization. Future work
is needed to examine how alternative ranking criteria might balance
personalization with exposure diversity to mitigate these effects.
Our model provides a possible mechanistic explanation for en-
gagement trends observed on major platforms (RQ1) such as Face-
book. In particular, the 2018 update to the News Feed algorithm,
which increased the weight of active engagement signals (com-
ments, shares, and reactions) under the concept of Meaningful So-
cial Interactions [28], aligns with the scenario of higher 𝜂in our
framework. Consistent with the analysis of Fraxanet et al. [20], such
an increase in the relative importance of active engagement could
raise overall interaction levels while simultaneously amplifying
ideological segregation and the visibility of extreme content.
This work focuses on hard news, that are inherently controversial
and typically display a U-shaped engagement profile across ideol-
ogy [6]. However, our model generates predictions in other settings
with different engagement profiles. Platforms such as Wikipedia,
which supports cross-ideological collaboration and moderation of
editor slant [25], Reddit, where interactions often occur across po-
litical lines rather than within echo chambers [52], and Bluesky,
where early analyses show no dominance of extreme-user activity
[7], exhibit less polarized engagement patterns. Their ranking mech-
anisms are generally less optimized for maximizing user engage-
ment than those of major social networks, making them suitable
environments to test the predictions of our model under weaker
algorithmic amplification and flatter engagement profiles.
Limitations — Our experimental design has several limitations.
First, we cannot be certain that participants behaved as they would
on actual social media platforms, despite our efforts to emulate
a natural browsing experience. Nonetheless, pilot versions of the
static experiment that varied the visual layout and labeling conven-
tions produced consistent behavioral patterns, suggesting that our
findings are robust to minor design changes.
Second, our experiments focused on conditions where both per-
sonalization and engagement reward mechanisms operate jointly,
reflecting realistic platform settings. To disentangle the independent
and interactive effects of these mechanisms, future work should
explore all four experimental conditions corresponding to the com-
binations of personalization (𝜆) and engagement reward (𝜂).
Third, our outcome measures focus on consumption patterns,
specifically the distribution of clicks and highlights across ideologi-
cal content, rather than attitudinal change. While we demonstrate
how ranking algorithms can amplify exposure to extreme and like-
minded content, our design does not allow us to determine whether
these shifts translate into durable opinion change or ideological
radicalization. Nonetheless, prior research indicates that selective
exposure and engagement-based amplification can reinforce exist-
ing beliefs and influence public discourse over time [4, 19, 39].
Finally, as with any controlled experiment, ecological validity is
limited. Real-world platforms involve richer social signals (e.g., com-
ments, sharing networks, and algorithmic retraining over longer
timescales) that could further intensify or dampen the effects ob-
served here.
However, researching production-scale systems remains extremely
challenging due to restricted data access and limited transparency
around algorithmic design. Our study thus isolates a fundamental
mechanism linking personalization and engagement optimization
to amplification dynamics under controlled conditions. To assess
these processes in the wild, researchers require meaningful access
to platform data and algorithmic interfaces. An access that, given
its societal importance, should be supported and enforced through
regulatory frameworks that promote transparency and independent
auditing.
