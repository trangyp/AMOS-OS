Abstract. This article defines a partial order structure to study the relationship
between levels and contents of conscious subjective experience in a single mathematical
set-up. We understand phenomenal structure as extrapolated relationships among
experiences, instead of fixed properties of specific experiences.
Our mathematical
account is based on multilayer network theory. Multilayer theory is a generalization
of graph and network theory, widely used in several scientific domains. This structure
is also the underlying conceptual and mathematical structure of most current models
of conscious experience. From our simple set of assumptions, yet rigorous analysis,
we conclude that assuming the comparison and quantification among phenomenal
experiences yield only partial comparison, rather than commonly assumed absolute
comparability. This has implications for evolutionary and animal consciousness: evolution
may encompass diverse modes of experiencing, not necessarily implying larger ones on
an absolute scale. Our characterization elucidates structural constraints on experiential
comparisons imposed by assumptions and choices made by modellers as active participants
in the scientific process.
In summary, in light of our phenomenological intuitions,
it might be right that some experiences carry qualitative aspects that make them
incompatible or non-comparable with other experiences, quantitatively speaking. Some
experiences are comparable (e.g.
at some experiential levels), but others are not.
These results have direct implications for consciousness science, evolution and animal
consciousness.
Keywords: Animal Consciousness, Consciousness, Structuralism, Subjective experience,
Poset, Multilayer network
1. Introduction
In recent years, applications of pure mathematics in consciousness science led us to
several new insights. In particular, different mathematical approaches draw connections
from mathematical structure (i.e. a set of operations and relations satisfying certain
requirements or axioms) to phenomenological structure (i.e.
a set of relationships
characterizing the interactions among subjective experiences). For example, the use of
category theory to unravel the distinctions between levels and content of consciousness
[28], the use of monoidal categories to account for minimal aspects of experience [25, 24],
and the use of a multilayer framework to account for embodied experience [19, 20],

In this article, we extend and combine previous approaches to study the consequences
of our structural assumptions. Here we focus on the combination of levels of consciousness
and phenomenal content in one single structure to investigate experiential comparisons.
Experiential comparison is broadly defined as comparing experiences in different contexts,
either among global states of consciousness (e.g. deep sleep, dream sleep, awake, etc)
or among experiences within a global state (e.g. taste of wine, listing a melody, etc).
In this line, it is commonly assumed that levels of consciousness somehow relate to
global states, corresponding to a quantifiable dimension of conscious experience (i.e.
there are higher and lower levels, etc). Then, phenomenal content relates mainly to the
qualitative and subjective dimension of experience within levels [27]. Both theoretical
distinctions aid us in understanding the variability among subjective experiences within
and across individuals in several conditions. Eventually, these investigations may also
elucidate the nature and evolution of conscious experience across the animal kingdom
[3].
Theoretical research on both, levels and contents, for example, may also help
validate and refine models of consciousness [23].
Some models predict that aspects
of subjective experience (e.g., self-awareness, introspection, cognitive control) become
prominent at higher levels of consciousness. Other models based on a multidimensional
approach postulate changes in some dimensions, or aspects, but not others [2, 1, 30].
Integrating these notions into a single structural framework and studying levels and
phenomenal content together with the intertwined relation between quantitative and
qualitative aspects of experience may provide a better understanding of the conscious
subjective experience and its structural relations.
Here, we exploit the relational aspects of phenomenal experiences within a structural
mathematical account, i.e. we abstract experiential relationships that can be generalized
and modelled by mathematical structures. Our structural account deals with relations
among experiences (e.g. qualitative similarity such as redness being similar, or closer
to orange, more than green, or temporal relations such as one experience preceding
another), instead of focusing on specific experiential structures such as colour, vision,
taste, imagination, etc. In this sense, at this stage, our model focuses on experiential
relationships inferred and instantiated across several experiences and independent of
the experiential specificity (e.g. bigger than, closer, first than, next, etc). Yet, the
specificity (of experiences, modalities and aspects) can also be instantiated in our model
by further defining a concrete set of experiences representing phenomenal content (with
its extra structural constraints given by graph configurations, see below). This set of
experiences is a modeller’s choice and depends on the particularities of the phenomena
to be modelled. This makes the role of the scientist explicit, as an active participant in
the scientific process [9, 6].

2. Layers of Experience
This section introduces a multilayer structure to model experiences, followed by
two compositional operations, ⊗and ⊙, accounting for their combination. With this
structure, the model can scale up from very simple postulates and eventually account
for complex or richer experiences as combinations of a group of minimal ones (see
Figure 1). We then can study how structural constraints limit our efforts to compare
all possible experiences.
2.1. Experiences as layers. To provide the most general structure possible and
potentially implement it under extra structural assumptions, we model experiences
as a network in a multigraph G formed by a set of nodes V (G) (see [19] and Figure 1
for details). V (G) is a multisubset of edges E(G) that corresponds to pairs of elements
of V (G), together with the multiplicity function mG : E(G) →Z+.
The edges can
have different colours, and colours represent different aspects, or modalities, of a given
experience.
Then, C is a finite set of colours representing modalities or aspects of
experience, and colG : E(G) →P(C) a mapping that to each edge assigns a subset
of colours, i.e. aspects of experience. This simple and general network structure can
account for any model of conscious experience assuming relationships among any type
of nodes and interactions (i.e. edges), up to extra structural choices.
For schematic purposes, one could associate each colour with a sensory modality,
such as layers account for internal (organs) and external (sense) minimal unimodal
experiences. In such interpretation (other more general interpretations are also possible),
each colour represents an experiential aspect that may have an associated set of layers
representing specific instances of such modality of experience (Figure 1A). For instance,
let’s say that the blue edge colour represents auditory experiences. Then, particular

The multigraph with colours and nodes in example 2.1 and 2.2, will be called a layer
of experience. A layer corresponds to the pair (G, colG), where G is a multigraph and
colG is the mapping assigning colours/aspects to the edges in the multigraph G. For
simplicity, we identify the layer (G, colG) with G and, for s ∈N, we say that a layer is
s-colored if colG is onto and s = |C|. s denotes the number of colours in the layer. In
the example above, we have two colours s = 2.
Each layer of experience is assumed as a closed unity, a single experience that is
experienced as a unified experience and may constitute further experiences. As such,
we interpret layers as modelling a minimal set of possible experiences [19]. Also, in
this framework, layers are instantiated by the modeller. In other words, the scientist
defines a group of minimal layers and aspects of experience according to what is
expected to be modelled (e.g. this is similar to assigning interpretations to the axes
in a 2D Cartesian coordinate system, to model acceleration we require different axes
than to model position).
Then, graph configurations and the choice of a minimal
set of layers of experience could account for specific phenomenal structures to be
modelled and any further phenomenological restrictions (e.g. experiences that never
occur simultaneously), need to be instantiated in the model at the moment of defining
the minimal set of experiences that can be combined. The reader may note at this point
that our model does not intend to be a complete model of experiences, it is a conceptual
prototype and formalization that requires to be informed by phenomenological and
rigorous considerations about what is expected to be modelled.
2.2. Experiences as multilayers. A minimal phenomenal experience rarely appears
isolated, some precede or follow others and in general, they combine and compose to
give rise to richer configurations (Figure 1B). In our model, the combination of layers of
experience generates new experiential configurations. These configurations are depicted
by merging minimal layers of experience (Figure 1B). The number and forms of these
configurations depend on how much detail we add to the experiential description, i.e.
the definitions of layers of experience. Again, this description is instantiated by the
researcher [19], following phenomenological guidance.

The tensor product ⊗places layers in parallel and gives them a determined order (i.e.
this product is non-commutative, meaning that G ⊗H is not the same as H ⊗G). The
implicit order given by ⊗is a requirement associated with potentialities of experience
and their interactions or combinations (i.e. the potential to interact, being closer or
not). For example, given three layers G, H, K, the configuration G ⊗H ⊗K says
that G have the potentiality to interact with H before K. Contrary, the configuration
G⊗K ⊗H says that G have the potential to interact with K, before H. Therefore, this
operation is non-commutative and imposes an order in the multilayer configuration.
The tensor also formalizes the intuition of layers of experience G and H as experienced
separated or in parallel and represents how the different experiential layers are presented
without interacting between them (for more details see [20, 25, 22]). This could model,
for example, specific elements or contents of our experiences that can be distinguished
as "separated" in a stream of experience.
Finally, to simplify notation, we fix nodes to the finite set V and denote such a
set by G⊗C (without reference to n). We also use the terms layers and multilayers of
experience indistinctly as simply layer.

3. The partial ordered experiential structure
In the previous section, we introduced two ways of composing layers of experiences:
⊗and ⊙. These two operations form a mathematical structure that can model an
increasing phenomenological structure based on a group of minimal experiences informed
by phenomenological data or biological data. As such, the structure can easily scale
up and enable us to compare different aspects of experience in a theory-independent

3.1. Experiential comparison. We start by assuming layers of experiences are comparable,
quantitatively, and see how far we can get with such an assumption.
Indeed, this
assumption may look trivial to some researchers, since, for example in humans, some
experiences look more complex or richer than others. The taste of wine may require
distinctions among several flavours and textures, while the taste of salt does not.
In terms of supposed levels of conscious experience, it seems that minimal states
of consciousness are lower than fully awake subjects.
These two examples, indeed,
correspond to different cases of experiential comparison.
The first corresponds to
the comparison among phenomenal contents of experience, while the second is about
comparing levels of consciousness.
Our model intends to integrate both notions in the same mathematical structure.
For that, we exploit a partial order structure of a given chain of layers. This order is
already present in the two experiential operations ⊙and ⊗. For example, given two
multilayers G⊗H ⊗K and G⊙H ⊗K both ∈G⊗C, our mathematical structure imposes
that G⊙H ⊗K is over G⊗H ⊗K, in the sense of being able to experience more aspects
in a combined manner. In this case, the ⊙operation transfers its priority to any given
chain of layers.

4. Implications and discussion
In this section, we discuss the main results and their consequences. In short, structural
and mathematical choices constrain the comparison of phenomenal experiences.
4.1. Implications for experience comparison. In consciousness science one would
like to know if experiencing the taste of wine entails more complex relationships than
seeing a beautiful landscape, or perhaps being able to distinguish between human

mathematically defining how one particular experience may contribute more subjectively,
i.e.
something experienced more actual and more unified, than others (e.g., using
verbal reports, neural measurements, etc).
In the second case, not having verbal

By extension, something similar happens when assessing whether patients in minimal

According to section 3.1 and 3.2, we must note that under the assumption that
experiences are quantitatively comparable (i.e. the need to introduce a poset structure
for such comparison) and described by layers of experience (i.e. a general structure
to account for phenomenal content and experiential configurations), we reached a
counterintuitive result: an ordered structure to compare experiences leads us only to
partial comparisons of inter-species experiences (section 3.1) and intra-species experiences
(section 3.2).
In other words, assuming: i) a poset structure ii) the definition 3.1 and iii) mappings
fj, then interactions given by the application of fj are the only ones comparable, i.e.
between horizontal lines or levels in example 3.7, but not between configuration within
the same levels. This is because, mathematically, configurations at the same level are
considered different, however, simultaneously, there is no mathematical way, in this
structure, to quantify the difference between, for example, G ⊗H ⊗K and G ⊗K ⊗H.
This mathematical constraint extends to more complex experiential configurations. For
instance, the form G ⊙H ⊗K is less complex than G ⊙H ⊙K. Yet, G ⊙H ⊗K is not
comparable with G ⊗H ⊙K, since these configurations do not have a defined order
within the structure (i.e.
only configurations satisfying the conditions in definition
3.1 are comparable). This structural constraint makes it impossible to quantify and
compare (bigger or smaller than the other) layer configurations at the same level,
i.e. they are mathematically not comparable. Nevertheless, this makes perfect sense
phenomenological speaking, i.e. how could we distinguish a configuration of experiences
having more actuality and unity than another configuration having the same group of
aspects but ordered differently? In other words, experiencing colour relative similarity
in one configuration, for instance, may not be more actual and unified than experiencing
relative similarity in another experiential configuration.
In summary, the main consequence of our assumptions, the multilayer poset structure
and definition 3.1 (exemplified by examples 3.3 and 3.7), is that experiential comparison
requires a distinction between interacting layers that allow comparison within them,
i.e. different levels on the chain of possibilities, and interacting layers that do not, i.e.
configurations at the same level on the chain of possibilities. In other words, levels
of consciousness are comparable (by counting the appearance of ⊙) while phenomenal
contents at the same level are not. This mathematical conclusion imposes a space of
possible experiences and allows flows and trajectories among them. Future works may

4.2. Implications for evolutionary and animal consciousness. The number of
experiential layers (i.e. potential aspects to be experienced) and their combinations
account for experiential systems, allowing partial comparison (section 3.1 and 3.2).
Depending on the layer structure, we can compare some experiential configurations
inter-species (d < e) and intra-species (d = e). The first case allows us to study animal
and evolutionary consciousness from a mathematical perspective (section 3.1), while
the second investigates levels and phenomenal content within individuals of the same
species (section 3.2 and 4.1).
In the most general case (d ≤e), the layer structure assumes that different species
have different numbers of experiential layers, having different potentialities for experiencing.
One can consider, for example, biological layers such as cortical organizations, or more
broadly, biological organizations which may also constrain phenomenal possibilities.
A simple example may be animals with two types of photoreceptors, e.g.
one rod
and one cone, and animals with one rod and three cones, plus additional cortical
structure. As such, a way to study and compare experiential potentialities is through
the instantiation of layers as analogous to biological organization [19], with additional
justified assumptions and inferences that can be mathematised as further structure in
the model.
Then, our mathematical analysis suggests that being upper on the evolutionary scale
does not always entail a bigger consciousness, as something graded on an absolute
and unique scale.
Indeed, having different potentialities for experiencing does not
mean having more, or less, consciousness at the species level (inter-species comparison).
Suppose experiential configurations have the same operations but one has more layers.
In that case, the bigger experiential configuration is the one with more layers being
experienced (i.e. it may have access to more aspects of experience). However, as we
see in the example 3.3, some cases cannot be compared, i.e. we cannot say which one
is bigger in an absolute sense. For instance, the experiential configuration G ⊙H ⊗K,
despite having a ⊙operation, is not bigger than G⊗H ⊗K ⊗L, which has more layers,
in an absolute sense. In other words, G ⊙H ⊗K and G ⊗H ⊗K ⊗L do not have an
order relation: while G ⊙H ⊗K has more experiential actuality than G ⊗H ⊗K ⊗L,
the latter has more experiential potentiality than the former.
In the inter-species case, it means that even though a species may have the potential
for several modes of experience, specific instantiations may not always reach its local
maxima (local for the species, in comparison to all possible layer configurations defining
a global maxima), and therefore not comparable to species that may have reached their
local maxima. In other words, since layers describe and represent the possibility of
experiences, there are local maxima and partial maxima (partial within the layers below

the local maxima of a species) that species may reach independently. Here, evolution
implies different modes of conscious experience associated with the top configurations
of a given layer configuration. Each species may have its maxima, some of them are
comparable and others not. Moreover, for example, experiencing relative similarity in
one species can correspond, structurally, to a subset of layer configuration in another
species (e.g colour similarity), but it can also be the case that some layers are present
only in the "lower" species, but overall have fewer layers (e.g. odour experiences of

This makes some aspects comparable but others not,
and might be suggested, for instance, by extrapolations of behavioural experiments
in rats [14] and monkeys [15]. All this seems coherent with current biological evolution
theories. Evolution is not a linear process, e.g. ants may be considered at the top of
an evolutionary chain without having a straightforward comparison with mammals (at
least as the world’s most successful eusocial insects [31, 10, 5]). As such, assuming that
humans or other animals are upper in the chain of evolution and that this implies always
a higher-graded conscious experience, seems to be a theological bias. Mathematically,
and under the assumptions of our layer structure, evolution seems to entail different
modes of phenomenal experience (i.e. different species might be conscious of different
numbers of experiential aspects), but it does not entail, by itself, more actuality and
unitary character of such experiences.
By extension, animal experiences and perhaps even synthetic experiences might
not always be comparable to human experiences (e.g. the "colour" experience of an
octopus). If one should translate Thomas Nagel’s seminal paper "What Is It Like to
Be a Bat?" [26] into mathematical structures, our layer model might be a reasonable
starting point. Here we obtain similar conclusions using a formal mathematical account.
Finally, it is important to note that our model does not intend to account for all
aspects of conscious subjective experience. Here we gave a structural framework that
can be instantiated to study evolutionary consciousness and animal consciousness from
the perspective of experiential comparison.
We do not claim that our model is a
complete account.
5. Conclusions
We developed a simple layer model to study the structure of experiential comparisons,
which can complement and extend cognitive and philosophical approaches by adding
extra structure (e.g. metrics). Our model has a minimal set of assumptions: experiences
can be accounted for by layers (either abstract representations or biological concrete
implementations), layers can be composed, and composition can be actual (⊙) or
potential (⊗). From these basic assumptions, the poset structure and definitions follow
naturally under the assumption that experiences are comparable. Indeed, the layer
of experiences model introduced through section 2 and 3 can scale to address further

assumptions of experiential comparison (quantitative or qualitative): the modeller only
needs to add extra structure, either in the form of geometries, metrics, etc to study the
consequences of their assumptions regarding experiential comparisons, or phenomenal
structure more broadly.
This approach aligns with an axiomatic and minimal model of explanations for
conscious subjective experience [21], i.e.
minimal assumptions are instantiated to
account for minimal experiential dimensions. This strategy is pragmatic, metaphysically
agnostic, and might eventually better describe relevant aspects of subjective experience.
The goal is to create and develop tools that will assist conscious scientists, in analogy to
how specific tools assist carpenters depending on the job at hand. For that, one might
start by focusing on structural relationships from general to specific relations. Here,
for example, we studied structural aspects of experience that might go beyond content
specificity.
However, we do not assume a complete theory for conscious subjective
experience accounting for all aspects of experience, instead, we start by building upon
assumptions and studying their consequences step by step.
In this article, the consequences of our model and structural assumptions point to:
i) a general enough structure to account for inter-species and intra-species experiential
comparisons based on phenomenal representations or biological instantiations, ii) under
these assumptions, experiences are partially comparable, not always comparable in
absolute terms, iii) the evolution of conscious experience may entail different modes of
phenomenal experiences, not always implying bigger experiences, as commonly assumed.
This characterization accounts for structural restrictions on experiential comparisons
and experiential trajectories imposed by our means of modelling and mathematical
choices. In this sense, mathematics has the power to instantiate assumptions uniquely
and transparently and study its consequences. This makes our minimal approach a
powerful tool to study but also acknowledges the importance of the scientist’s experience,
as an active modeller of the phenomenon intended to be modelled.
We end by remarking that using Category Theory ([13]), posets become categories
and we can prove that fj are idempotent endofunctors corresponding to monads and
modal operators.
In this framework, each new experience can be interpreted as a
modality in a logic system, having a multimodal system where every modality ♢is a
conjunction of possible applications of functions fj satisfying a group of axioms. In
future works, we exploit the particularities of modal logic applied to the analyses of
structural aspects of conscious experience [7, 8].
