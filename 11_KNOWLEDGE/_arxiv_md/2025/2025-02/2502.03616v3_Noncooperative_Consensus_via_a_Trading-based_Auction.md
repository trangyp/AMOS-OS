Abstract— Noncooperative multi-agent systems often
face coordination challenges due to conflicting prefer-
ences among agents. In particular, when agents act in
their own self-interest, they may prefer different choices
among multiple feasible outcomes, leading to suboptimal
results or even safety concerns. We propose an algorithm
named trading auction for consensus (TACo), a decentral-
ized approach that enables noncooperative agents to reach
consensus without communicating directly or disclosing
private valuations. TACo facilitates coordination through a
structured trading-based auction, where agents iteratively
select choices of interest and provably reach an agreement
within an a priori bounded number of steps. A series of nu-
merical experiments validate that the termination guaran-
tees of TACo hold in practice, and show that TACo achieves
a median performance that minimizes the total cost across
all agents, while allocating resources significantly more
fairly than baseline approaches.
Index Terms— Decentralized control, Consensus prob-
lem, Noncooperative games, Multi-agent systems
I. INTRODUCTION
Multi-agent systems often require consensus among agents,
yet such consensus can be challenging due to conflicts and
disagreements. These conflicts stem from differences in the
strategic priorities of the agents, making coordination difficult
without communication or external mediation. A representa-
tive example of such challenges arises when agents must select
one shared outcome from multiple feasible choices that reflect
their individual strategic preferences. Without a mechanism to
enforce a consensus, these disagreements can cause suboptimal
outcomes and even safety risks in critical applications [1], [2]
as illustrated in Figure 1.
Reaching a consensus in multi-agent systems is particu-
larly difficult when agents are noncooperative, and this diffi-
culty arises from several factors. First, the consensus process
must be procedurally rational, meaning noncooperative agents

II. RELATED WORK
Existing multi-agent consensus methods employ leader-
follower frameworks [17], [18], finite-time consensus algo-
rithms [19], [20], robust control techniques [21], auction mech-
anisms [22], and game-theoretic approaches for noncoopera-
tive agents [23]. These have been applied in UAV coordination
[24]–[26], traffic flow optimization [23], and wireless sensor
networks [27]. However, most rely on centralized leaders or
high-bandwidth communication, limiting their applicability in
fully decentralized, noncooperative settings [28].
A related stream of research considers coordination among
agents when multiple feasible outcomes exist, often framed
as multi-choice problems. Most of these approaches adopt
a centralized or cooperative structure, allowing agents to
collectively evaluate all possible outcomes and agree on one
through a common decision rule [3]–[8], [13]–[15]. While
such frameworks can ensure consistency in the final outcome,
they require agents to disclose private cost information or
require agents to follow decision directives issued by a central
authority, making them impractical in decentralized settings
where privacy and autonomy are essential.
Several works tackle multi-choice problems in noncoopera-
tive systems [9]–[12], [29]–[31]. Examples include enforcing
unique joint decisions by adjusting agents’ utilities [29] or
employing structured processes like the linear tracing proce-
dure that rely on common initial beliefs [9], [10]. Although
these methods maintain individual rationality, where agents
have incentives to participate based on self-interest, they still
depend on partial coordination or shared information, and thus
fall short of achieving fully decentralized consensus.
Negotiation algorithms provide another approach to decen-
tralized consensus among noncooperative agents [16]. Com-
mon methods include voting, bargaining, and auction-based
mechanisms. Voting is widely used but often fails to ensure
rationality, meaning outcomes may not be acceptable to all
agents [16]. Bargaining relies on direct communication and
is often problem-specific [32]–[34], limiting its applicability
in general decentralized negotiation environments. In con-
trast, auctions align agents’ incentives with self-interest while
preserving privacy (i.e., agents need not disclose sensitive
private information) [16], [32], [35]–[37]. However, auctions
primarily address resource allocation rather than single-choice
problems, highlighting the need for research that bridges this
gap by adapting auction-based methods for decentralized,
noncooperative equilibrium selection.
III. COORDINATION UNDER CONFLICTING
PREFERENCES
In decentralized multi-agent systems, agents are often re-
quired to reach agreement on one outcome from a finite set of
feasible alternatives, even when their individual preferences
conflict. We refer to this general setting as a multi-choice
consensus problem. Each agent aims to minimize its own cost
or maximize its utility, but the system must converge to a
single shared choice to ensure coherent operation. Such situa-
tions arise naturally in scheduling, task allocation, and traffic
management, where multiple admissible joint configurations
may exist but only one can be implemented in practice.
A prominent example of this class of problems is the
equilibrium selection problem. In many noncooperative games,
several equilibria coexist, each representing a distinct yet
individually stable outcome for all agents. Although every
equilibrium satisfies the best-response condition, the coexis-
tence of multiple equilibria introduces ambiguity: agents must
somehow agree on one equilibrium to realize a consistent joint
outcome. This subsection illustrates this issue using standard
game-theoretic notation.

IV. TRADING AUCTION FOR CONSENSUS
The Trading Auction for Consensus (TACo) algorithm is
designed to address the challenge of decentralized conflicting
choice problems among non-cooperative agents. The primary
goal of TACo is to help agents reach a consensus or agree on
a single choice, even when they have conflicting preferences.
The algorithm accomplishes this without requiring direct ne-
gotiation or communication between agents, and it preserves
each agent’s private information during the process.
A. Key elements of TACo
TACo consists of several key components: a pay matrix,
an offer matrix, a cost matrix, a profit matrix, the private
valuations over secondary assets, the amount of trading units,
and the decrement factor. These components are central to how
agents compute their profit and make decisions during TACo.
Let n P Zě0 denote the number of agents, and m P Zě0 the
number of choices available in the auction. For any matrix X,
Xij refers to its pi, jq entry.
1) Offer matrix (O): The offer matrix O P Rnˆm represents
the units that each agent i can receive if it selects choice
j. Specifically, Oij denotes the number of units that agent
i will receive when it chooses choice j, determined by the
offers made by other agents.
2) Pay matrix (P): The pay matrix P P Rnˆm captures the
cost each agent incurs when selecting a particular choice.
Pij is the number of units that agent i has to pay to others
when it chooses choice j.
3) Cost matrix (C): The cost matrix C P Rnˆm reflects
the intrinsic cost associated with each agent’s choice. Cij
represents the original cost experienced by agent i when it
selects choice j. Each row of C corresponds to the private
cost structure of an agent and need not be shared with
others, preserving privacy in how each agent evaluates the
available options.

valuation, bi, to the units of the asset being traded in the
TACo algorithm. This valuation, represented as the i-th

C. Properties of TACo
There are several properties of the TACo procedure.
1) Procedural rationality: TACo incentivizes agents to act
in their self-interest by enabling them to trade and negotiate
based on their individual valuations of the available options.
This best-response decision rule corresponds to procedural ra-
tionality in the sense that agents make locally optimal choices
under bounded information [39], [40]. As demonstrated in
Example 2, each agent selects the option that maximizes its
profit, naturally driving the auction process toward a consen-
sus. Unlike voting-based methods, which may force majority
outcomes that are not individually beneficial [16], [41], TACo
ensures that the final outcome remains individually rational
for all agents.
2) Privacy preservation: One of TACo’s core strengths is
its ability to preserve the privacy of each agent’s valuations.
The trading mechanism functions without requiring agents
to disclose their private valuations for the available options
(C) or trading assets (b), as only the agents’ current choice
selections are broadcast at each round, and no cost or valuation
information is revealed. This contrasts with bargaining-based
schemes that rely on pairwise exchanges and require disclosure
of utility or preference information [16], [32].
3) Independence from direct communication: TACo operates
under a minimal communication framework, where agents
only need to broadcast their choices. This eliminates the
need for direct, 1-on-1 communication, making the algorithm
straightforward to implement in practical systems. This stands
in contrast to bargaining protocols that rely on repeated direct
exchanges.
4) Termination guarantee: TACo is guaranteed to terminate
with an agreement within a bounded number of rounds. The
proof of termination, as well as an explicit upper bound on
the number of rounds needed to terminate, is provided in the
following section.
5) Consensus over discrete choices: TACo enables single
discrete-choice consensus among noncooperative agents with-
out central coordination. Conventional auction mechanisms,
by contrast, are typically designed for resource allocation [22],
[35], [37] and do not address this problem.
Remark 1 (Notes on rationality of TACo). The update rule in
TACo reflects procedural rationality under bounded and asym-
metric information. Agents update their choices by selecting
the option that maximizes their current profit at each iteration,
which is the rational response given the information available
to them at each iteration. Since reaching a joint decision

V. TERMINATION PROOF OF TACO
The proof of TACo’s termination (with an agreement)
involves three steps. First, we show that TACo always enters
a cycle, where the auction steps repeat in a loop as defined
in Definition 3. Next, we demonstrate that profit differences
between choices within a cycle are bounded, with the bound
proportional to the trading unit per step d. This restricts each
agent’s available choices to options with profit differences no
greater than the bound. Finally, we prove that TACo satisfies
the epsilon-termination condition and hence converges.
The intuition behind the convergence of TACo lies in that,
as soon as a cycle is detected, the trading unit d is reduced by
a fixed decrement factor γ. However, as d decreases, the profit
differences within the cycle shrink and must thus eventually
fall below the threshold ε for all agents. At this point, the
agents reach a consensus by definition, and TACo terminates.

B. Baseline algorithms
We evaluated the effectiveness of the TACo algorithm by
comparing it with widely used negotiation approaches. While
these baseline methods lack key features of TACo, they serve
as intuitive benchmarks for multi-agent coordination. The
baseline algorithms considered in this experiment are:
‚ Voting: Each agent votes for their preferred options, and
the final decision is determined by majority rule [41]. In
the event of a tie, the winner is chosen randomly.
‚ Random dictator: A random agent is selected, and all other
agents must conform to the preferences of this chosen
agent.
‚ Utilitarian: A central coordinator selects the option that
minimizes the total costs across all agents, requiring
compliance from every agent.
‚ Egalitarian: A central coordinator selects the option that
maximizes fairness among agents, and all agents are
required to comply with the decision.
The voting mechanism offers a simple, decentralized ap-
proach but can lead outcomes which are not unilaterally
rational, as agents must accept majority decisions even when
misaligned with their preferences. Moreover, the utilitarian
and egalitarian methods optimize system-level objectives but
impose centralized decisions, making them unsuitable for
noncooperative and decentralized settings. Lastly, the random
dictator mechanism also relies on centralized selection but
ensures fairness over time in repeated interactions by rotating
decision-making power among agents.

F. Scalability test
Although (14) provides a worst-case upper bound on the
number of rounds required for termination, the bound is
conservative. To evaluate the algorithm’s practical efficiency,
we conducted a scalability test by varying both the number
of agents (n) and the number of choices (m). We tested
n P t3, 5, 7, 10u and m P t3, 10, 30, 100u, using parameters
γ “ 0.9, ε “ 10´1, and initial trading unit d0 “ 1. For each
pn, mq pair, we sampled both the intrinsic cost matrix and the
private valuations independently from uniform distributions,
with Cij, bi „ Uniformp0, 1q. Each configuration was run for
100 trials, and we recorded the mean number of rounds to
termination with confidence intervals.
As shown in Figure 7, the number of rounds grows sub-
linearly with respect to the number of choices m, whereas its
dependence on the number of agents n is significantly stronger,
exhibiting growth that exceeds linear scaling for fixed m.
These empirical observations indicate that TACo operates far
more efficiently in practice than the theoretical worst-case
bound (14) suggests, while the bound remains useful for
guaranteeing finite-time termination.
