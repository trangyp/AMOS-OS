Towards Scalable Defense Against Intimate Partner Infiltration
Weisi Yang1, Shinan Liu2, Feng Xiao3, Nick Feamster2, and Stephen Xia1
1Northwestern University, 2University of Chicago, 3Georgia Institute of Technology
Abstract—Intimate Partner Infiltration (IPI)—a type of Inti-
mate Partner Violence (IPV) that typically requires physical
access to a victim’s device—is a pervasive concern around the
world, often manifesting through digital surveillance, control,
and monitoring. Unlike conventional cyberattacks, IPI perpe-
trators leverage close proximity and personal knowledge to
circumvent standard protections, underscoring the need for
targeted interventions. While security clinics and other human-
centered approaches effectively tailor solutions for victims,
their scalability remains constrained by resource limitations
and the need for specialized counseling. We present AID, an
Automated IPI Detection system that continuously monitors for
unauthorized access and suspicious behaviors on smartphones.
AID employs a unified architecture to process multimodal
signals stealthily and preserve user privacy. A brief calibration
phase upon installation enables AID to adapt to each user’s
behavioral patterns, achieving high accuracy with minimal
false alarms. Our 27-participant user study demonstrates that
AID achieves highly accurate detection of non-owner access
and fine-grained IPI-related activities, attaining a false positive
rate of 1.6%, which is 11x lower than existing methods, and
an end-to-end F1 score of 0.981. These findings suggest that
AID can serve as a forensic tool that security clinics can
deploy to scale their ability to identify IPI tactics and deliver
personalized, far-reaching support to victims.
1. Introduction
Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) is a prevalent issue
around the world. In the United States alone, 47% of women
and 44% are affected by IPV during their lifetime [32].
Perpetrators of IPV often monitor, control, and harrass
victims through technology and physical devices that are
prolific in our daily lives [9], [45], [23], [5]. For example,
an IPV abuser might exploit a home router to monitor
victim’s smartphone [55] and Internet activity [23], a GPS-
tracker to give the abuser information about victims’ real-
time location, or a hidden camera to spy on the victim’s
daily activities [9]. Among these tactics, Intimate Partner
Infiltration (IPI) is particularly problematic, as it often re-
quires physical access to the victim’s devices.
We define IPI behavioral intents as the act of covertly
accessing and misusing a victim’s smartphone by an inti-
mate partner, leveraging physical proximity, shared access,
or relationship familiarity (e.g., knowledge of the victim’s
routines or authentication credentials) instead of traditional
technical exploits.
IPI-related intrusions are unique compared to typical
problems in computer systems security because of the vastly
different characteristics of the threat model and perpetrator.
To better contextualize IPI within the broader security land-
scape, we summarize in Table 1 a comparison between IPI
attackers and traditional cyber attackers. This table is derived
from a synthesis of prior security literature [48], [7], [20]
combined with empirical insights from IPV studies [55],
[46], [56], [27]. IPI perpetrators typically arise from the
general population, who may not have the same technical
background as traditional intruders. However, the lack of
technical expertise does not make defending against intru-
sive behaviors easier. On the contrary, intimate relationships
frequently give abusers physical access to victims’ personal
devices, which often bypasses the need for sophisticated
hacking. For instance, these abusers are often already regis-
tered on shared devices (e.g., fingerprints previously added
to the device’s authentication system), or can obtain access
through educated guesses and coercion. This unique com-
bination of physical proximity and intimate knowledge
of the victim’s behaviors allows abusers to exploit trust
rather than technical vulnerabilities, creating challenges
that are inherently different from traditional attacks.
Limitations of Security Clinics. There has been extensive
research in IPV/IPI across psychology, health, engineering,
and computer science. Recently a class of approaches called
clinical computer security [27], [56] has gained traction for
mitigating IPV or IPI. These approaches are administered
through Security Clinics [6], [56], [27] and leverage a com-
bination of clinical interviews, consultations, and technical
support to provide tailor interventions for IPV victims.
However, such clinics are difficult to scale. For instance, it
is challenging to expand the service into rural areas where
limited human resources constrain its availability.
In addition to logistical barriers, human factors also need
careful consideration, further complicating scalability. IPI
victims often arrive at clinics carrying deep emotional scars
and traumas from their experiences [42]. These individ-
ualized needs necessitate detailed interviews and therapy
sessions, making the work labor-intensive for experts and
consultants. The emotional and psychological demands of
these interactions can also lead to burnout among clinic staff,
further limiting capacity.

tional solutions such as anomaly detection, continuous au-
thentication, and human activity recognition (HAR), while
extensively explored in security literature, fall short in
the IPI context. Specifically, anomaly detection risk vic-

• We propose and formalize a novel threat model for

anomaly detection, user authentication, and HAR–by 17x,
11x, and 48x lower FPR respectively. (Section 6).
2. Background and Motivation
In this section, we discuss two categories of work related
to Intimate Partner Infiltration (IPI): (1) human-centered ap-
proaches, such as security clinics developed for IPV victims,
and (2) technical solutions drawn from adjacent fields (e.g.,
anomaly detection, user authentication, and human activity
recognition). While both offer valuable perspectives, we
show why neither adequately addresses the unique require-
ments of IPI detection on smartphones.
2.1. IPV-related Research
Prior research on technology-facilitated IPV has mainly
provided qualitative insights into victims’ experiences and
perceptions of how perpetrators misuse technology, such as
how abusers deploy GPS and audio surveillance tools [24],
[23] or leverage online forums for Intimate Partner Surveil-
lance (IPS) and other technology-driven abuses [5], [55].
Other research has also emphasized the need for legal
frameworks and defensive tools to combat IPV and address
the widespread availability of spy devices [53], [9]. A range
of mitigation solutions has emerged in response to these
findings, which we detail next.
Digital tools. Although basic tools exist to help users re-
move browser histories and delete digital traces to safeguard
their privacy [3], they do not address the wide spectrum
of potential IPI abuses, such as impersonating the victim,
changing account settings, or detecting intrusions.
Security clinics. Security clinics provide in-person consul-
tations for victims, offering tailored support to identify risks
and rebuild a sense of safety [27], [56]. While these clinics
have demonstrated significant promise, scaling their services
beyond local communities presents considerable challenges.
One major limitation is their specific physical location,
which restricts access for victims outside those areas. Fur-
thermore, these clinics require the involvement of techni-
cal experts who possess specialized knowledge to address
the complex and evolving nature of technology-facilitated
abuse. Recruiting and training such experts, as well as en-
suring ongoing support, is resource-intensive and difficult to
sustain at scale. This combination of geographic constraints
and reliance on highly skilled personnel highlights the barri-
ers to replicating models like the Clinics to End Tech Abuse
(CETA) in New York City [13], Madison Tech Clinic (MTC)
in Madison [35], and the UC Berkeley Center for Long-Term
Security (CLTC) [57] in broader contexts. These challenges
underscore the need for scalable, accessible, and resource-
efficient digital approach to combat IPI on a wider scale.

2.2. Related Technical Domains and Their Limita-
tions in IPI Context
Existing mitigation heavily relied on human-intensive
approaches. In contrast, we aim for an automated solution
to address this unique problem. Several well-established
domains – such as anomaly detection, user authentication,
and human activity recognition (HAR) – appear relevant,
our empirical results (Section 6.3) reveal significant short-
comings: high false positive rate of 27.6%, 18.1%, and
78.0%, respectively. These levels of inaccuracy are deeply
concerning as they risk further traumatizing victims. As
summarized in Table 2, a core reason to these failures is
that existing methods do not jointly reason about who is
using the phone (identity) and what they are doing (intent).
The remainder of this subsection explains each domain’s
limitations in the context of IPI; Section 3.3 then introduces
our joint identity–intent formulation that rectifies them.
Anomaly detection produces binary outputs indicating
whether anomalies occur in the input data sequence. Prior
works have explored anomaly detection in domains such
as network intrusion [39], [44], IoT security [47], [43].
However, IPI behavioral intents detection involves additional
human-centered considerations. First, anomaly detection
systems often rely on Personally Identifiable Information
(PII), such as user logs, network traffic, raising privacy
concerns. Second, conventional anomaly detection solutions
for computational systems can notify an operator as soon
as the detection confidence passes a threshold. Such an
approach is not suitable for IPI behavioral intents, because a
sudden alert could escalate abuse and endanger the victim.
In addition, many IPI behavioral intents resemble normal
usage patterns, undermining the core assumption in anomaly
detection that malicious events deviate sharply from benign
or risk-neutral events [11], [36].
User authentication: Smartphone user authentication falls
into two categories: one-time and continuous methods.
While one-time verification (e.g., PINs, facial recognition)
initially prevents casual intruders, it is insufficient in IPV
settings where abusers may know these credentials [55],
[62]. Continuous authentication, drawing on biometric or
behavioral data (e.g., motion sensors [19], [10], [34], touch
interactions [21], [62], or hybrid approaches [16], [1]),
offers stronger security by persistently checking whether the
current user matches the legitimate owner.
On the surface, user authentication is well-suited to this
problem as it can flag intruders on the phone. However, a
critical nuance is that the ”intruder” may not always pose
harm. Benign sharing – such as lending a phone to a friend
or family member—is common in close relationships, yet
existing systems Existing systems are primarily designed
to detect unauthorized access by strangers, without consid-
ering the user’s intent on the device. This mismatch can
cause benign interactions to be misclassified as intrusions,
leading to unnecessary concern or false alarms. Besides,
some authentication methods rely on IPI-sensitive data such
as location [16] and touch coordinates [8], which could
expose private information like location traces or typed pass-
words. Finally, many authentication systems are interactive
by design, requiring explicit user input (e.g. QA prompt [15]
or pop-ups). Such overt mechanisms are unsuitable for the
stealth and safety requirements of IPI detection.
Human activity recognition (HAR) and digital behavior
study: A fundamental distinction between HAR and detect-
ing IPI-intent lies in the objectives. Most HAR research
focuses on physical movements [63] with sensory data such
as inertial measurement units (IMUs). However, physical
movements alone offer limited insight into what the user is
doing within an app. In contrast, detecting IPI behavioral
intents requires much higher-level semantic and contextual
understanding that cannot be solely capture by motion or
vision-based sensing. And this points to a different line of
research – digital behavior study.
Recent studies in contextual and semantic digital behav-
ior understanding on mobile devices have used app metadata
and user-interface-trait data to analyze users’ in-app activ-
ities and intents. For example, [52] proposed a tool that
captures text content displayed on phone screens to analyze
user psychological states. On top of screen content, other
types of data have also been explored for some general
behavior understanding such as location, WIFI SSID and
app usage logs [61], [29], [40].
However, these approaches fall short in IPI context for
two key reasons. First, mostly focus on general-purpose
behavior summarization or contextual sensing and have no
safety considerations to adapt to risky IPI scenario, and
do not incorporate safety-critical considerations required in
IPI scenarios. Second, many of these methods rely on data
sources that seriously raise privacy concerns, such as screen
display record, which are unsafe and impractical to collect
in sensitive environments. These constraints make behavior
understanding in the IPI context uniquely challenging.
To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has at-

3. Problem Formulation
3.1. Threat Model
We define an intimate partner broadly as someone with
a close personal relationship with the victim and shared or
frequent access to the victim’s smartphone. This includes
current or former romantic partners, close friends, family
members, and cohabiting individuals such as roommates.
We focus on smartphone-based IPI behaviors, and define the
victim as the legitimate owner of the smartphone, and the
abuser as the intimate partner who misuses victim’s device
to cause harm.
Adversary’s capabilities. We make following assumptions
on adversary’s capabilities:
1. Authenticated access. As shown in Table 1, unlike tradi-
tional cybersecurity attacks, we assume that the abusers have
authenticated access to victims’ devices, e.g., by registering
their biometrics or having access to passwords.
2. User-interaction level operations. Aligned with previous
research [23], we assume that abusers have limited technical
background, since abusers typically come from the general
population compared to traditional cyber attackers. This
means that abusing behaviors are restricted to UI-bound
interactions, e.g., viewing content on the phone or modifying
the configurations.
3. Abuser awareness of AID. Our baseline assumes that the
abuser is unaware of AID on the phone, which is consistent
with prior assumptions of the adversary’s limited technical
background. Nonetheless, we also analyze stronger and ex-
treme yet rare cases (aware but passive; aware and actively
probing) in Section 5 to demonstrate AID’s resilience to
discovery attempts.
4. Application scope. AID is designed to be app-agnostic;
unlike most prior abuser or intrusion detection studies that
focus on finance [4], [19], [8]. Section 6 evaluates six
everyday apps across six representative categories.
Non-IPI behavior. Prior threat models consider only harm-
ful behaviors. However, a common scenario is that the cur-
rent user is neither the victim nor abuser and is interacting
with the device in a non-harmful way. This is common
among friends, family members, or cultures where sharing
personal devices is common (e.g. watch a video or online
search) [30], [17]. Detecting this case as non-IPI is essential
for keeping AID’s false-positive rate low.

4. AID Design
4.1. Workflow Overview
Figure 1 shows AID’s workflow. It takes in fixed-length
time-series sequences, where signals from multiple modali-
ties are concatenated and min-max normalized per feature.
Pretraining. AID is designed in a dual-branch manner based
on our formulation in Section 3.3: 1) Identity-branch utilizes
an autoencoder (AE) to confirm if the user is the owner
and 2) Intent-branch classifies IPI/NIO intents into our
taxonomy defined in Section 3.2. Finally, 3) AID fuses the
output of these two branches through the context-aware risk
analyzer that leverages T, a learned thresholding parameter.
Before deploying AID, the dual-branch is pretrained on data
collected from a diverse user group.
Adaptation. When clinics decide to deploy AID on a vic-
tim’s phone, AID fine-tunes the decision network in the
identity-branch, with five minutes of the victim’s usage data
collected during an initial calibration phase to enable AID
to distinguish phone usage by the owner and non-owners.
Detection and Reporting. This stage occurs on-device post
adaptation. It uses the adapted system to identify anomalous
users and suspicious intents indicative of IPI events. The
suspicious results, which contain time stamps, app usage,
and contextual evidence, are silently logged and securely
reported to authorized support channels (i.e., clinic experts,
see Section 5.1 for details).
4.2. Pretraining Stage
User identity-branch. There is large variability between
different users, so it is difficult to train a single model that
can distinguish between any user. As such, we adopt a fine-
tuning scheme that adapts to the phone’s owner.
As shown in Figure 2a, we propose a multi-head LSTM-
based Auto-Encoder that extracts latent features which are
analyzed by an SVM-based decision module to determine
the identity of the user. The SVM decision module is
adapted to new users and phones with a small amount of
labeled data, which is discussed in Section 4.3.
On the other hand, the Auto-Encoder is used to learn
general features in behaviors that are common across the
vast majority of users and is trained with unlabeled data in
an unsupervised manner. The encoder consists of H paral-
lel LSTM heads, each receiving the same input sequence
and encoding it into a distinct latent representation. These
embeddings are then concatenated into a single feature
vector, which is then passed to a single LSTM decoder
to reconstruct the original input sequence. This multi-head
structure encourages the encoder to capture diverse signals
in parallel.

4.3. New User Adaptation
Few-Shot learning for efficient adaptation. Model train-
ing is typically resource-intensive and time-consuming. In
our dual-branch architecture, the intent-branch performs in-
ference using a trained model, while the identity-branch
requires fine-tuning for user adaptation. To minimize this
overhead, we implement the few-shot learning scheme that
enables efficient adaptation with minimal data. During pre-
training, the Auto-Encoder is trained on a diverse dataset,
capturing general patterns of mobile interaction. This knowl-
edge enables the model to rapidly adapt to a new user’s
specific interaction patterns through fine-tuning. Our ex-
periment (Appendix C) demonstrates that random sampling
of just 20 samples during the calibration session is suffi-
cient for fine-tuning, achieving high detection accuracy. This
eliminates the need to process the full dataset, significantly
reducing computational and time costs. The resulting system
is both practical and efficient for real-world deployment.
Adapting to different users. When the victim installs the
application for the first time, AID guides the owner through
a short calibration procedure of 5 minutes, where the owner
uses the device naturally. This session provides sufficient
data fine-tuning the classifier to adapt to the owner’s unique
behavioral patterns. The fine-tuning dataset is constructed
by selecting a subset of the data newly collected from the
owner and a subset of data from other users as negative
examples. To ensure balance during classifier training, we
maintain a 1:1 ratio between the selected samples from the
owner and other users.

4.4. IPI Risk Detection
After adaptation is complete, AID begins logging and
inference on logged data for suspicious IPI behaviors.
Temporal Consistency Module (TCM) for stable detec-
tion. To further enhance reliability, we introduce a Tempo-
ral Consistency Module (TCM) as a post-processing stage
applied to both user identification and behavior classifica-
tion before context-aware fusion. The design of TCM is
motivated by the observation that isolated mispredictions
often occur amidst a sequence of correct and consistent
outputs. Leveraging temporal continuity, TCM suppresses
such outliers and ensures stable predictions over time.
1. Identity-branch. We extract temporal features (rolling
mean and standard deviation) from prediction scores, which
are input into a k-means clustering model (k = 2) that
partitions the prediction space into regions corresponding to
true users and potential abusers. A temporal voting mech-
anism aggregates predictions within a sliding window to
reduce variability. This approach significantly reduces false
positives while maintaining high detection sensitivity, which
we will show in our experiments.
2. Intent-branch. Post-processing for intent classification
adopts a simpler rolling window average to smooth fluc-
tuations in predictions and reduce temporal variations.
Asynchronous detection. While deep learning models are
effective for accurate detection, running them continuously
on mobile devices can lead to excessive resource consump-
tion, overheating, and degraded user experience. Unlike
traditional user authentication systems, which prioritize real-
time processing and alerts - potentially notifying abusers -
AID performs detection asynchronously at night when the
device is idle and charging. This design avoids real-time
computational overhead, with the only daytime burden being
lightweight logging of interaction signals, which is power-
efficient (0.12% battery drain over 60 minutes at 20 Hz
sampling rate, 0.30% at 40 Hz, 3.02% at 130 Hz–well
beyond typical operating frequency).
Report generation. Detection results (IPI detection result,
identity and intent states) are compiled into a secure report
with key details such as the time of detection, the app in-
volved, the user’s status (e.g., owner or non-owner), the most
likely behaviors detected (e.g., risk-neutral or suspicious
actions in Table 3), and the overall risk assessment based on
confidence measures output from each branch. For instance,
the report might highlight that a verified user performed risk-
neutral activities on one app. At another point in time, the
report may detect an anomalous user engaging in actions like
”Alter Account Setting,” leading to an ”IPV Risk Detected”
classification. The report remains securely stored on the
device until it is analyzed by security experts.
Interpreting intents with context. Unlike traditional hu-
man activity recognition (HAR), which leverages IMU or
camera data to track physical movements, AID operates
under a more constrained and privacy-preserving setting. It
infers behaviors from indirect signals—such as app usage
and touch interaction—where distinct actions may appear
similar (e.g., typing a password vs. searching YouTube). To
accommodate such ambiguity, AID logs the top-k behavior
predictions for each anomalous user session, offering a
broader context to support cautious and informed interpre-
tation by security professionals.

5. Safety and Stealthiness
5.1. Deployment of AID with Security Clinics
The goal of AID is a digital tool that provides fine-
grained insights into the occurrences of IPI to enable secu-
rity clinic experts to craft personalized intervention strate-
gies for victims. The intended usage of AID is as follows.
A victim consults with a security clinic expert, who
evaluates the victim’s situation. If the security clinic expert
determines that finer granularity monitoring is required, then
the victim installs AID on their smartphone. Reasons for
requiring fine-grained monitoring could range from leak-
age of personal information, discovery of altered settings,
or forensic goals resulting from lack of awareness of an
abuser’s actions.
With victim’s consent, AID is side-loaded by the expert,
who copies a signed APK from a secure machine to victim’s
phone via USB. Then the expert guides the victim through
the accessibility service setting page, granting permissions
to, enabling the service, and a five-minute adaptation process
(Section 4.3). From that point, AID runs quietly in the
background and restarts at boot.
While AID is running on the victim’s smartphone, re-
ports will generated when potential instances of IPI are
detected. Reports are saved to the AID’s app-specific direc-
tories [25], which are not accessible by other applications
or users until the victim returns to the security expert to
avoid at-home escalation. The security expert leverages the
reports from AID to analyze the types, frequency, and time
of IPI events, which can then used to develop a personalized
intervention strategy. For example, if it is discovered that
most intrusions occur in the evening during sleeping hours,
a possible intervention is for the victim to adjust habits to
make the phone physically inaccessible to the abuser during
this time.
AID also employs a variety of strategies to reduce
detection and escalation by abusers, such as UI deception,
local inference, and data self-destroying mechanisms, which
we discuss next.
5.2. Safety Design
Review of risky data sources. AID should be “invisible”
to abusers while also being privacy sensitive towards the
owner of the phone. As such, we discard data streams only
available through APIs that violate these principles. We
identified four such signals, which are not present in AID:
1. Why not touch? Touch is available through the Motion-
Event API and provides information about the coordinates
of a user’s touch, swipe direction, pressure etc. However,
accessing this API in the background places a floating icon
on the screen and triggers mandatory system alerts.
2. Why not camera or face ID? While cameras can accu-
rately identify the current user, they can record highly sensi-
tive information, which can result in severe privacy breaches
if leaked. Also, activating the camera in the background
triggers mandatory notifications, which can alert abusers.


## Page 9


Another potential solution is to directly leverage the
camera or biometric sensors to perform user authentication,
but IPI abusers can often bypass these mechanisms due to
prior authorized registration or intimate knowledge of their
partners. Besides, face ID typically requires the user to look
directly at the device with proper alignment to the sensors,
which is impractical for stealthy detection scenarios where
the device may be used from various angles. Furthermore,
Biometrics and face ID provide no defense, monitoring, or
privacy against abusers who gain unfettered access to the
phone, while AID provides safe and stealthy monitoring of
potential IPI instances after login.
3. Why not audio? Similar to the camera, using audio
through the MediaRecorder API raises significant privacy
concerns and triggers mandatory on-screen notifications that
can alert abusers.
4. Why not location? While location tracking through the
LocationManager API, GPS, and wireless signals could
provide useful information, it can reveal sensitive details
about the user’s habits or frequent locations. Additionally,
like camera and audio recording, system notifications and
battery drain from continuous tracking further make this
option less suitable.
User-interface deception. We crafted our Android applica-
tion to run in the background as a service, without a UI
or application icon, and randomize the name. Moreover, we
leverage the Accessibility Service [26], a built-in function of
Android OS that enables AID to launch without clicking on
the application icon and remain absent from the “Recent
Apps” page to enhance stealthiness and prevent abusers
from terminating AID from here. We believe that AID can
operate with systems like HideMyApp [41] to further hide
its presence, which we leave for future work.
Local inference and secure communication. AID performs
inference and fine-tuning on-device, which eliminates the
need to send logging data to a remote server, reduces the
chance of data leaks, and enhances data privacy. Further-
more, we take advantage of the IPV context to further reduce
AID’s energy footprint. Because we avoid alerting users to
prevent discovery by abusers, AID does not need to provide
insights continuously and in real-time. As such, during the
day, AID collects and logs traces, and analyzes the collected
data at night, when the device is likely idle and connected
to power. Furthermore, considering some IPV abusers may
have access to the home router and could monitor network
traffic, AID ensures that any transmitted messages are en-
crypted at the application layer, providing an additional layer
of protection against network-level surveillance.
Destroying generated reports. While AID remains largely
invisible because it lacks a UI icon and runs as a background
service, an abuser who is aware of AID may attempt to gain
access to the generated reports. Because reports are stored
in AID’s app-specific directories, the only way for a user
to obtain these files is through the android debug bridge
(adb), which involves connecting the victim’s device to a
PC through usb. When AID detects that the victim’s device
is connected in this way, AID deletes all generated reports
in its app-specific directories to prevent detection.
5.3. What if the abuser is aware of AID?
Although the average IPV attacker may not have the
same technical background of a cyber attacker, there is a
chance that abusers may become aware of AID, even with
the safety measures employed. Here we discuss scenarios
reflecting the degree of the abuser’s awareness and technical
knowledge.
1. Abuser is aware of AID’s existence, but is not aware of
how to stop AID. Here, AID is still feasible, as the abuser
cannot prevent AID. However, since AID functions as a
background service, a more informed abuser can kill AID
through the services or Apps menu in Settings. While this
can stop AID, doing so reveals a clear intention IPI, which
can be used to inform victims and security experts.
2. Abuser is aware of the generated reports and attempts
to gain access. An abuser who is aware of the reports being
generated may attempt to gain access to them. As discussed
in Section 5.2, this is only possible through the adb by
connecting the victim’s device to a host PC through usb;
AID slef-destroys all generated reports when it detects the
adb to prevent detection.
3. Abuser is aware of AID’s capabilities and attempts
to mimic victim behavior. If an abuser knows that AID
detects IPI-related behaviors s/he may attempt to mimic
the behavior of his/her partner to bypass detection. While
this may work initially, many works have shown that it is
difficult to consciously mimic the behaviors and biomet-
rics of another person for extended periods of time [31],
[60]. As such over the long run, we anticipate that AID
would still capture unauthorized intrusions and IPI behavior.
Moreover, once mimicry fails, the resulting deviations in
sensor readings may further amplify anomalies, marking the
session as highly suspicious and increasing the likelihood of
recognition.
4. Can AID be used against the victim? Source code
will not be made publicly available and data is only shared
with clinics. Victims and abusers alike cannot access reports
generated by AID unless visiting a security clinic. More-
over, no data is shared or transmitted if no potential IPI is
detected. Given these constraints, it is difficult for an abuser
to manipulate AID to harm the victim.

6.2. Ethical Considerations
IRB approval and participant safety. This study was
approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB). To avoid
psychological or physical harm, we did not involve real IPV
victims or abusers; instead, we recruited couples and friends
in healthy relationships to simulate relevant behaviors.
Deception and debriefing. To minimize behavioral bias,
participants were initially told the study was about “smart-
phone usage”. Full disclosure was provided during post-
study debriefing, including the study’s true purpose and
rationale for deception. Participants were then offered the
option to withdraw their data.
Privacy and data protection. All data was anonymized at
collection, with no personal identifiers stored. Participants
used lab-provided phones and accounts. Data was encrypted
and stored on secure lab servers with restricted access.

We implemented and trained both methods using the same

7. Discussion and Future Work
Expansion of coverage of apps and intent types. In
this work, six apps from six representative categories are
selected for data collection and evaluation, demonstrating
AID’s applicability across diverse usage scenarios; Our IPI
intent taxonomy, with modality combination of Int and App,
enabled effective intent classification. However, there remi-
ans room for improvement in both granularity and accuracy
for intent detection, especially for wider usage cases. This
is due to the low-fidelity nature of the available modalities
we found and the ambiguity observed across certain intent
classed. Future work could expand to a larger-scale study
involving a broader range of apps, leverage more advanced
modalities, and refine the current taxonomy to support more
fine-grained and generalized detection in complex, real-
world scenarios.
Potential generalization to other fields. IPI detection in-
volves identifying anomalous behavior from abusers that are
intimately familiar and often have access to the victim’s de-
vice. These characteristics share similarities with other types
of security problems, such as 1) detecting insider attacks,
which originate from attackers within an organization who
often have authorized access to the organization’s systems,
and 2) misuse detection, which focuses on detecting ab-
normal system patterns and behaviors. We believe that the
methods developed for AID could be beneficial for these
adjacent problems that we plan to explore in future work.
Potential generalization to other platforms. In future
work, we plan to expand the capabilities of AID to other
common devices and explore collaborative detection strate-
gies across multiple devices and platforms (e.g., routers and
PCs) to detect evidence of IPV beyond the victim’s phone.
Collaboration with security clinics to deploy and collect
data in realistic scenarios. Due to the ethics of IPI, it is
difficult obtain data in real scenarios. In future work, we
plan to expand our user studies and iterate the design of
AID based on data collected from more realistic scenarios
by collaborating and deploying AID with security clinics,
who have access to victims of IPV.
8. Conclusion
In this work, we formalized the threat model of Inti-
mate Partner Infiltration (IPI) through a custom-designed
taxonomy, and presented AID, an automated IPI detection
system that jointly analyzes user identity and intent via a
dual-branch architecture processing OS-level and physical
signals on smartphones – under stealth, safety, and privacy
constraints. AID incorporates a wide range of mechanisms
to maintain stealth and adapt to varying levels of awareness
and technical knowledge of the abuser, such as careful selec-
tion of signals to analyze, UI deception, local inference, and
self-destruction of data to prevent access to sensitive infor-
mation. A short calibration phase tailors AID to individual
user behavior patterns, which allows it to distinguish non-
owner access attempts and identify fine-grained IPI intents
accurately. Our evaluation with 27 participants demonstrated
AID ’s effectiveness, achieving up to 0.981 F1 score and
maintaining a low false positive rate of 1.6%. These results
highlight the potential of AID to serve as a forensic tool for
security clinics, enabling scalable assistance to IPV victims.
There are several future directions that we believe AID can
be expanded.

Appendix A.
Evaluation Settings
We train and evaluate the models on a Linux server
running on Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS with an NVIDIA L40 GPU,
using Tensorflow 2.17.0 and Python 3.12.7.
The AutoEncoder for user identification is pretrained on
a self-supervised reconstruction task for a maximum of 100
epochs with a batch size of 512, using the Adam optimizer
and a learning rate of 1e-3. We incorporate early stopping
with a patience of 5 epochs and a minimum improvement
threshold of 0.0001. Mean Squared Error (MSE) is used
as the loss function to reconstruct the input data. Similarly,
the behavior classification model is trained for 50 epochs,
batch size = 512, Adam optimizer, learning rate = 1e-3,
and is optimized with Categorical Crossentropy as the loss
function.
Appendix B.
User identification encoder comparison in em-
bedding space
To understand the superior intimate partner detection
performance of our user identification module, we analyze
the embedding space produced by each model. As shown
in Table 13, AID yields significantly greater Euclidean
distances and lower cosine similarity between owner and
attacker samples—particularly for partners. Notably, our em-
beddings achieve negative cosine similarity (-0.032), indicat-
ing strong directional separation even for behaviorally sim-
ilar partners. In contrast, baseline methods cluster partners
and owners closely (e.g., 0.913 in AuthentiSense), reducing
their ability to detect social insiders. These results confirm
that AID learns more discriminative and generalizable rep-
resentations.
Appendix C.
Effects of pretraining and fine-tuning
Here, we analyze the impact of pretraining and fine-
tuning. As shown in Figure 5, we vary the number of
samples used to fine-tune the base model to the phone’s
owner (x-axis), which is collected from the user during a

user initially installs AID. We also ran experiments, with and
without pretraining. Finally, we also looked at different se-
lection schemes for choosing the windows used during fine-
tuning. Ultimately, AID adopts a random selection scheme
(e.g., choose n random windows of data provided by the
user), which observed had the highest performance. We
compared against selecting fine-tuning windows based on
chronological time and similarity.

use the first n consecutive windows provided by the user.

selection because the windows collected at a specific time is
