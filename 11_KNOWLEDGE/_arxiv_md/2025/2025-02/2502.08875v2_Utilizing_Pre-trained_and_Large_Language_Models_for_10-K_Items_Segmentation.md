Abstract 
Extracting specific items from 10-K reports is challenging due to variations in document 
formats and item presentation. To improve over traditional rule-based approaches, this study 
introduces and compares two advanced item segmentation methods: (1) GPT4ItemSeg, using 
a novel line-ID-based prompting mechanism to utilize a large language model, ChatGPT-4o, 
for item segmentation, and (2) BERT4ItemSeg, combining a pre-trained language model, 
BERT, with a Bi-LSTM model in a hierarchical structure to overcome context window 
constraints. Trained and evaluated on 3,737 annotated 10-K reports, BERT4ItemSeg achieves 
a macro-F1 of 0.9825, surpassing GPT4ItemSeg (0.9567), conditional random field (0.9818), 
and rule-based methods (0.9048) for core items (1, 1A, 3, and 7). These approaches enhance 
item segmentation performance, improving text analytics in accounting and finance. 
BERT4ItemSeg offers satisfactory item segmentation performance, while GPT4ItemSeg can 
easily adapt to regulatory changes. Together, they provide an extensible framework for 10-K 
item segmentation that supports reliable and reproducible results. 
 
Keywords: pre-trained language model, large language model, natural language processing, item 
segmentation, 10-K filing

I. INTRODUCTION 
Form 10-K filings are structured into items to organize firms’ disclosures. Important items 
such as the Management’s Discussion and Analysis of Financial Condition and Results of 
Operations (MD&A; Item 7), Risk Factors (Item 1A), and Legal Proceedings (Item 3) are 
essential for stakeholders in the capital market. Given their importance, prior accounting 
research often focuses on the textual content of specific 10-K items to study topics such as 
information content, market reactions, disclosure quality, and information environment 
(Loughran and McDonald 2016; Cohen et al. 2020).  
Leveraging item-specific text from 10-K filings requires item segmentation, the task of 
identifying item boundaries and extracting item-specific text. Despite its foundational role in 
the text analytics pipelines, item segmentation is typically performed using rule-based methods 
that combine string matching, regular expressions, and HTML parsing (Li 2008; Brown and

4 
 
Tucker 2011; Brown et al. 2020a; Campbell et al. 2014). These methods are brittle and difficult 
to maintain over time because they must account for substantial variation in item titles, ordering, 
and document formatting (Loughran and McDonald 2016). Errors introduced at this stage may 
propagate throughout downstream analytical workflows, degrading model performance and 
limiting reproducibility. 
We argue that two critical gaps remain. First, the literature lacks a standard, large-scale 
annotated dataset for benchmarking item segmentation performance, hindering systematic 
evaluation and comparison of alternative approaches. Second, recent advances in deep learning 
and large language models have not been fully leveraged to address this upstream 
preprocessing problem. The continued reliance on rule-based systems is particularly vulnerable 
to regulatory changes and requires continuous manual maintenance. These limitations motivate 
the development of robust, scalable learning-based item segmentation methods. 
In response, we constructed a manually annotated item segmentation dataset comprising 
3,737 10-K filings randomly sampled from fiscal years 2001 to 2019. To our knowledge, this 
dataset is the first large-scale benchmark for training and evaluating item segmentation 
performance across a wide range of disclosure periods. By enabling systematic comparison of 
alternative methods under a unified evaluation framework, the dataset addresses a critical 
infrastructure gap in the literature. 
Moreover, building on recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly large 
language models (LLMs) and pre-trained language models (PLMs), we develop two novel item 
segmentation approaches: GPT4ItemSeg and BERT4ItemSeg. GPT4ItemSeg leverages 
ChatGPT-4o, a LLM with strong semantic understanding, to identify item boundaries in 10-K 
filings, making it well-suited to handle regulatory changes. To align the model with the item 
segmentation task, GPT4ItemSeg relies on few-shot prompting, requiring only a small number 
of partial, manually curated examples to convey the structure and semantics of 10-K items.

5 
 
This design substantially reduces the need for large labeled training datasets and enables rapid 
adaptation when new disclosure items are introduced. 
At the same time, directly applying LLMs to long regulatory filings presents practical 
challenges related to context length and hallucination. First, 10-K filings often span tens or 
even hundreds of thousands of tokens, sometimes exceeding the maximum context window 
supported by current LLMs. When relevant portions of a filing fall outside the available context, 
the model may fail to identify correct item boundaries. Second, when LLMs are prompted to 
generate or reconstruct item-specific text, they may produce fluent but nonidentical content 
that does not exactly match the original filing, a phenomenon commonly referred to as 
hallucination. In the context of regulatory disclosures, such deviations are particularly 
problematic, as even minor discrepancies between the extracted text and the source document 
can compromise data integrity and downstream empirical analyses. 
To address these issues, we introduce the line-ID-based (LIB) prompting mechanism. The 
LIB approach asks ChatGPT to identify custom-generated line IDs for starting lines of items. 
A complete item is subsequently generated based on the line IDs and original filings. Our 
approach addresses the hallucination problem and ensures that the returned item text matches 
the original filings. 
In contrast, BERT4ItemSeg adopts Bidirectional Encoder Representations from 
Transformers (BERT) (Devlin et al. 2018) to obtain line-by-line embedding and applies 
Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) models (Huang et al. 2015; Hochreiter 
and Schmidhuber 1997) to learn the label sequences. This PLM, BERT, plays an important 
role in providing contextualized embedding for each line. Bi-LSTM, on the other hand, is 
responsible for learning line-level dependencies for accurate item segmentation. The 
BERT4ItemSeg addresses the limited context window (512 tokens) of BERT by incorporating 
a hierarchical structure in which the lower-level BERT encoder considers only the context of


## Page 6


6 
 
each line. The upper-level Bi-LSTM, on the other hand, predicts line labels based on the 
embeddings provided by the lower-level. Since Bi-LSTM has no limits on the sequence length, 
we effectively overcome the limitation of BERT’s short context window. 
Our experimental results show that, for items often adopted in previous studies (i.e., Items 
1, 1A, 3, 7), the BERT4ItemSeg achieves a macro-F1 of 0.9826, outperforming GPT4ItemSeg 
(macro F1=0.9552) and baseline approaches such as conditional random field (CRF) (macro 
F1=0.9788) and rule-based method (0.9048). For other items (excluding Item 15 because of its 
ambiguous ending position), the BERT4ItemSeg achieves a macro-averaged F-1 of 0.9692 
while GPT4ItemSeg achieves a performance level of 0.9385. These results provide high-
quality item segmentation that is usable in downstream tasks.  
Our study makes multiple contributions with important implications. First, unlike the rule-
based approach that relies on manually compiled rules, GPT4ItemSeg and BERT4ItemSeg are 
based on large language models and pre-trained language models that leverage recent 
advancements in deep learning and AI to address issues caused by variation in title wording, 
order, and document formatting, achieving better item segmentation performance. 
Second, the proposed GPT4ItemSeg overcomes hallucinations and the limited context 
window with the novel LIB prompting mechanism. BERT4ItemSeg addresses the limited 
context window problem by incorporating BERT and Bi-LSTM in a hierarchical structure. 
While these innovations can also be applied to other long business documents, they originate 
from the 10-K item segmentation problem, which is fundamental and critical for text analytics 
in accounting and finance.  
Third, GPT4ItemSeg requires fewer than 10 partial examples (i.e., few-shot prompting) to 
align ChatGPT effectively with the item segmentation task. While the fully trained 
BERT4ItemSeg performs better than GPT4ItemSeg, the latter is highly effective in scenarios

where new SEC regulations introduce additional items. GPT4ItemSeg can be utilized to 
generate new training examples for BERT4ItemSeg and other machine learning models, 
enabling efficient operation with smaller models while maintaining flexibility to address

Moreover, our findings have critical implications for empirical research in accounting and 
finance. High-quality and publicly available item segmentation routines directly enhance the 
reliability and reproducibility of textual analyses, enabling researchers to construct cleaner and

auditors, and institutional investors, can also benefit from these advancements by streamlining 
the extraction of relevant disclosures, thereby supporting more timely and accurate decision-
making. 
Finally, the proposed approach offers a generalizable framework for processing other 
filings. The LIB prompting strategy can be readily adapted to new filing types with minimal 
manual examples, enabling high-quality segmentation while mitigating the risk of 
hallucination. In cases where it is economically viable, users can also collect a sufficient 
number of training instances and fine-tune the BERT4ItemSeg model for the target filing type, 
thereby facilitating large-scale deployment without incurring ChatGPT API usage costs.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW 
Text analytics using 10-K filings is an emerging research field in accounting and finance. 
Since there are excellent survey papers (Loughran and McDonald 2016; Loughran and 
McDonald 2020; Das 2014; Kearney and Liu 2014; Li 2010b; Bochkay et al. 2023) on this 
subject, our discussion focuses on firm disclosures and their theoretical foundations, followed 
by the item segmentation pipelines and a discussion on LLMs and their applications in 
accounting. 
Firm Disclosure and 10-K Filings 
Information asymmetry between firms and investors has long been recognized as a central 
issue for an efficient capital market (Healy and Palepu 2001). Public disclosures, such as the 
annual 10-K filings, aim to reduce information asymmetry by providing information on firms’ 
performance, risks, and governance. Past studies have approached this vital issue from various 
perspectives, including readability, disclosure similarity, sentiment, forward-looking 
statements, and risk factors. These studies often leverage either the entire 10-K filing or specific 
items as the data source for textual analysis. Below, we provide a succinct summary of these 
research directions. 
Readability refers to the cognitive load on readers when comprehending text (Martinc et 
al. 2021). Impression management theory (Brennan et al. 2009) hypothesizes that managers 
may obfuscate disclosure information to reduce readability. Arguments based on Agency 
theory, on the other hand, predict that managers may voluntarily disclose information to reduce 
information asymmetry (Baginski et al. 2000). Li (2010a) shows that poor performance firms 
tend to have complicated annual reports, while annual reports with higher readability 
disclosures are associated with higher earnings persistence. Moreover, the relation between 
earnings and the Fog Index mainly stems from the MD&A.


## Page 9


9 
 
Another stream of research focuses on document similarity in disclosures. Hoberg and 
Phillips (2010) leverage the description of businesses and products in Item 1 to compute 
product similarity between two firms with cosine similarity. Using text-based measures based 
on cosine and Jaccard similarity, Cohen et al. (2020) document a trading strategy that shorts 
firms with large changes in 10-K and 10-Q reports and buys firms with small 10-K and 10-Q 
report changes, earning up to 188 basis points of abnormal returns per month. Moreover, a 
trading strategy that focuses on Risk Factors (Item 1A in 10-K) and Legal Proceedings (Item 
2.1 in 10-Q) achieves the highest abnormal returns. Other streams of research examine the 
information content of sentiment (Loughran and McDonald 2011),  forward-looking statements 
(Muslu et al. 2015), and risk factors (Campbell et al. 2014). 
It is worth emphasizing that these studies often rely on the ability to segment the 10-K 
filing into its constituent items accurately. Improved segmentation enables more reliable 
textual features and enhances reproducibility. As such, methodological advancements in 
automated item segmentation represent a foundational contribution to the growing literature on 
text analytics in accounting and finance. 
10-K Item Segmentation 
As discussed in the previous subsection, many studies focus on one or several items in 10-
K filings, such as MD&A (Li 2010a; Kothari et al. 2009; Loughran and McDonald 2016), Risk 
Factors (Campbell et al. 2014; Lyle et al. 2022; Hope et al. 2016), and Business (Hoberg and 
Phillips 2010). While the quality of item segmentation may greatly affect subsequent analysis, 
most accounting and finance studies have adopted basic rule-based approaches to segment 10-
K items. The EDGAR system provides mechanisms for users to collect a comprehensive set of 
files. We provide a brief overview of the data preparation pipeline and a summary of the 10-K 
item segmentation techniques in the literature.

10 
 
The data preparation pipeline starts by identifying target documents. Our study focuses on 
annual reports typically associated with document types 10-K and 10-K405 (Loughran and 
McDonald 2011). The master index (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/full-index/) lists all 
available files and provides a convenient venue to construct a comprehensive list of target files. 
It is straightforward to collect files from the list using modern crawling tools, such as Python’s 
requests library. Raw files downloaded from the EDGAR website are in the Standard 
Generalized Markup Language (SGML) format. The main 10-K filing is wrapped in a 
document session, and it can be in Hyper Text Markup Language (HTML) or pure text (i.e., 
TXT) format. Unwrapping the 10-K filings from the document session provides the main text 
files for subsequent analysis. 
Most studies go through several text pre-processing steps before item segmentation. One 
of the most common is the removal of HTML Tables (Loughran and McDonald 2011; Li 2008). 
The rationale is to remove tables that mostly contain numbers; however, this step may also 
remove text unrelated to tabular data because the <table> tag may be used to format text instead 
of organizing numeric data (Loughran and McDonald 2014). Several studies also remove 
paragraphs with more than 50% non-alphabetic characters and replace HTML tags with blanks 
(Li 2008). 
After transforming 10-K filings into a clean format, the next steps are to segment the text 
and extract the target items. The basic idea is to identify the starting and ending locations of 
the target items. For example, to identify MD&A (Item 7), one can use keywords such as “Item 
7” and “Managements Discussion” to detect the starting locations; keywords such as “Item 7A,” 
“Quantitative and Qualitative Disclosures,” or “Item 8” can identify the ending locations (Li 
2008; Brown and Tucker 2011; Brown et al. 2020a; Hope et al. 2016). Keyword searching 
often results in false positive matches. The order of items provides additional clues for selecting 
more precise locations (Brown and Tucker 2011). An alternative approach operates directly on


## Page 11


11 
 
HTML files and uses visual cues that help human readers recognize boundaries (Campbell et 
al. 2014). 
Keyword-based item segmentation routines typically yield moderate performance. Brown 
and Tucker (2011) use a regular-expression-based approach and extract 73% of the MD&A 
items from 10-K filings. Campbell et al. (2014) combine the regular expression approach with 
the HTML scoring and extract 65% of Items 1A, 7, and 7A from 10-K filings. The precision 
of the extracted items was 98%. 
We believe that extracting items using rules and regular expressions has the following 
three problems. First, the current HTML-to-text conversion suffers from loss of formatting 
information, which may negatively influence the quality of item segmentation. Second, 
complex regular expression rules must be created due to the wide variety of companies’ writing 
styles, typography, and content. Moreover, the title and format of the items change over time 
to meet the SEC’s requirements. Last but not least, the item extraction method using regular 
expressions cannot process parts of the 10-K reports due to unforeseen formatting variations. 
Consequently, we hope to develop advanced approaches that leverage large language models 
and pre-trained language models to perform item segmentation robustly and accurately. 
Applications of Pre-trained Language Models and Large Language Models in Accounting 
The power of AI algorithms gives machines human-like linguistic intelligence (Zhao et 
al. 2023). At the core of this technology, language modeling (LM) involves estimating the 
likelihood of word sequences to predict the probability of subsequent tokens. LM initially 
emerged through statistical language modeling in the 1990s. Subsequent advancements in 
neural networks and deep learning have driven substantial improvements in LM capabilities. 
This progresses from Word2Vec, which utilizes a shallow neural network (Mikolov et al. 2013), 
to BERT, featuring its transformer architecture and self-attention mechanisms (Devlin et al.


## Page 12


12 
 
2018). Over time, neural language models (NLMs) and PLMs have increasingly demonstrated 
exceptional performance in addressing NLP tasks. Among these technical advancements, 
LLMs represent a significant milestone in the field. By incorporating hundreds of billions of 
parameters and optimizing them on extensive text corpora, LLMs expand the capacity of PLMs 
and enhance their effectiveness on various downstream tasks (Zhao et al. 2023). While LLMs 
have performance advantages over PLMs, LLMs either require specialized and expensive 
hardware or can only be accessed through dedicated APIs. As a result, PLMs continue to play 
a crucial role in research projects. 
The development of domain-specific PLMs such as FinBERT has driven recent 
advances in sequential labeling for financial text processing. FinBERT (Huang et al. 2023) is 
a domain-specific adaptation of BERT, pre-trained on financial texts including 10-Ks, 10-Qs, 
financial analyst reports, and earnings conference call transcripts. This specialization enables 
it to capture financial terminology and context more effectively, leading to improved 
performance in sentiment classification and ESG discussion classification. Loukas et al. (2022) 
developed the FiNER dataset for numerical entity recognition. Their dataset contains more than 
one million sentences from 10-Ks and 10-Qs, with ground truth identified by iXBRL tags. They 
have also explored the benefit of continuous pre-training and released SEC-BERT, a BERT 
model pre-trained on 200K financial filings.  
Li et al. (2025) propose a framework to extract accounting data from PDF documents. 
Their framework converts PDFs to plain text and leverages an LLM for understanding tables 
of contents and extracting financial variables. Vakilzadeh and Wood (2025) develop a retrieval-
augmented generation (RAG)‑based AI application to help scholars synthesize accounting 
research papers more efficiently. Other relevant studies include detecting suspicious 
transactions (Wang et al. 2025) and key audit matter sentiment analysis (Liu et al. 2022) using 
deep-learning approaches.

One of the most prominent LLMs is ChatGPT launched by OpenAI in November 2022. 
Specifically, GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters and was pre-trained on general text data 
(Yenduri et al. 2024). Compared to GPT-2, which has 1.5 billion parameters, GPT-3 
demonstrates a significant increase in capacity achieved by scaling its generative pre-training 
architecture. Additionally, GPT-3 introduced the concept of in-context learning (ICL), wherein 
LLMs can be guided to understand new tasks by presenting training examples directly within 
the prompt (Liu et al. 2021). In March 2023, OpenAI released GPT-4, which extended input 
capabilities from text to multimodal signals. GPT-4 demonstrates superior performance in 
addressing complex, human-generated problems and improves safety in responding to 
provocative or sensitive queries.  
ChatGPT’s advanced human interaction and problem-solving capabilities have also drawn 
considerable attention from accounting researchers. Harris (2024) adopts ChatGPT-4 to 
analyze 10-K filings and develop a metric to measure managers’ perception of market 
competition. The empirical results reveal a positive association between this newly constructed 
metric and accruals-based earnings manipulation, highlighting the influence of perceptions of 
market competition on financial reporting practices. Andreou et al. (2021) adopt ChatGPT to 
analyze disclosure in MD&A, revealing that firms providing more forward-looking disclosures 
related to R&D activities exhibit a positive association with future idiosyncratic stock price 
crashes. In addition, the sentiment analysis performance of ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-4o mini 
in the MD&A sections outperforms that of traditional machine learning models and dictionary-
based methods (Lehner 2024).  
Ethical considerations in AI-generated financial text analysis center on ensuring accuracy, 
fairness, transparency, and compliance with legal and professional standards (Siau and Wang 
2020). Given the high stakes of financial communication, AI outputs must be factually correct 
and free from hallucinations to prevent misinformation that could distort market behavior.

Users should be informed when content is AI-generated, and human oversight should remain 
in place for accountability. Privacy and intellectual property rights must be respected, as 
models may process sensitive or proprietary filings.  
 
III. RESEARCH QUESTIONS 
The value of 10-K items has been widely recognized in previous studies. The lack of 
advanced 10-K item segmentation approaches and the advancement of LLMs and PLMs 
provide an opportunity to address this fundamental problem. To systematically investigate this 
research problem, our Research Question 1 (RQ1) aims to develop a research dataset for 
training and evaluating 10-K item segmentation models. 
Both LLMs and PLMs can effectively address the challenge of 10-K item segmentation. 
Although both are based on the transformer architecture (Vaswani et al. 2017) and require 
model pre-training to acquire general linguistic patterns and world knowledge, they differ in 
several ways. First, LLMs are essentially PLMs with a significantly larger number of 
parameters. Second, their task-solving paradigms vary. PLMs are primarily designed as 
transferable task solvers, following a “pre-training and fine-tuning” approach to adapt to 
specific tasks. LLMs, in contrast, function as general-purpose task solvers. They can handle a 
wide range of tasks without additional fine-tuning, relying instead on prompt engineering to 
adapt to the requirements of each task. 
Third, LLMs demonstrate a unique capability for few-shot learning through in-context 
learning. This means they can effectively address tasks with minimal examples provided within 
the input prompt, a feature not typically supported by smaller PLMs. 
While LLMs are promising approaches that lead to state-of-the-art performance on many 
tasks, they have a few critical shortcomings. LLMs typically have more than 10 billion


## Page 15


15 
 
parameters and are expensive to develop and operate. Second, while LLMs excel in many NLP 
tasks, they did not perform well in sequence labeling tasks. Recent studies (Ma et al. 2023; Gao 
et al. 2023) point out that LLMs perform worse or are at best on par with PLMs fine-tuned on 
appropriate training data. Third, LLMs may hallucinate and generate content that sounds 
plausible but is, in fact, incorrect. Finally, both PLMs and LLMs are limited in the length of 
the context window. For example, ChatGPT-4o has a context window of 128 thousand tokens, 
while PLMs such as BERT only have a context window of 512 tokens. Since a word may map 
to more than one token, even a 128-thousand-context window may be insufficient. 
PLMs and LLMs offer distinct advantages but also have their own weaknesses in item 
segmentation. We thus plan to investigate their performance and provide concrete guidelines 
for the most suitable scenarios for using these two approaches. Based on the above discussion, 
our Research Question 2 (RQ2) is: How do we leverage large language models for 10-K item 
segmentation? Similarly, our Research Question 3 (RQ3) is: How do we develop a 10-K item 
segmentation model that leverages pre-trained language models? Finally, Research Question 4 
(RQ4) compares and contrasts these approaches and offers specific recommendations for 
researchers who need to tackle the 10-K item segmentation problem in the future. 
IV. METHODOLOGY 
We develop PLM-based and LLM-based approaches to tackle the 10-K item segmentation 
problem. For the PLM-based approach, we propose BERT4ItemSeg, which leverages BERT 
and a Bi-LSTM to address the issue of long text. The BERT4ItemSeg approach can handle 10-
K reports of arbitrary length and achieve excellent performance. As for the LLM-based 
approach, we opt to leverage ChatGPT-4o and develop the LIB prompting mechanism to 
facilitate effective item segmentation and reduce the potential problem caused by hallucination.

PLM-based and LLM-based 10-K Item Segmentation 
We tackle the 10-K item segmentation problem by leveraging PLMs and LLMs. PLMs 
and LLMs are the bedrock of current NLP techniques and are widely used in many NLP studies 
(Minaee et al. 2024). We propose BERT4ItemSeg and GPT4ItemSeg to leverage PLMs and 
LLMs for 10-K item segmentation. We describe the details in sequence. 
BERT4ItemSeg 
The BERT4ItemSeg leverages the BERT model (Reimers and Gurevych 2019; Devlin et 
al. 2018) to encode the contextual information into fixed-length dense vectors. BERT is an 
encoder-only PLM pre-trained on large corpora using masked language modeling (MLM) and 
next-sentence prediction tasks (NSP). The MLM pre-training randomly replaces words with a 
special token ([MASK]). The model then learns to predict the original words based on the 
surrounding context. In NSP, the model is given two sentences and asked to predict whether 
the second sentence logically follows the first. Both pre-training tasks help BERT understand 
context and learn general linguistic patterns.  
One severe limitation of BERT is its 512-token context window. Since the average length 
of a 10-K report is 37,400.92 words, it is impractical to process a full 10-K report using BERT. 
To address this limitation and accommodate the long document length, we design 
BERT4ItemSeg with a two-layer structure. The first layer encodes the text line-by-line into a


## Page 19


19 
 
fixed-length embedding vector. The second layer is a Bi-LSTM structure that takes the 
embedding vectors from each line and predicts item boundaries as labeled using the modified 
BIO scheme.  
The rationale behind BERT4ItemSeg is as follows. First, item boundaries coincide with 
the beginning of a new line of text. Our preliminary investigation suggests that both HTML- 
and pure-text-formatted filings follow this pattern. Since our HTML-to-text conversion routine 
preserves line and paragraph boundaries in the original file, we can leverage this pattern to 
simplify item segmentation. Second, most lines are shorter than 512 tokens. We can encode 
individual lines using BERT without hitting the context window length limit. Lines longer than 
512 tokens are truncated since the front part of a line should contain enough information for 
the model to predict item boundaries correctly. Third, since BERT4ItemSeg adopts Bi-LSTM 
for item boundary prediction, the model can process 10-K filings of arbitrary length, 
overcoming the limitation imposed by BERT’s short context window. 
GPT4ItemSeg 
The rise of LLMs marks the shift from learning task-specific solvers to general-purpose 
models. A typical approach to utilize LLMs is the in-context learning proposed along with 
GPT-3 (Brown et al. 2020b). In-context learning formats prompt with a task description, 
followed by a few examples as demonstrations. The last part of a prompt is the test instance, 
which, in our case, is the target 10-K report for item segmentation.  
Our preliminary investigations using ChatGPT-4o suggest that directly following current 
in-context learning templates is challenging due to hallucinations and the length of 10-K filings. 
ChatGPT may deviate from the input 10-K report and generate items different from the original 
input, even when the prompt explicitly asks the model not to do so. There is no simple 
workaround for the well-known hallucination problem. Moreover, while the context window 
of GPT-4o is longer than that of most 10-K reports, adding a few demonstrations at the


## Page 20


20 
 
beginning of the prompt can easily consume a large chunk of the prompt space, squeezing 
precious space for the final test instance.  
We develop the LIB prompting mechanism to address the potential hallucination problem 
when generating item segmentation results. Moreover, to save prompt space, we compile 
examples that demonstrate item extraction from partial filings instead of including the full 
filing. These two critical designs allow us to effectively extract 10-K item boundaries while 
minimizing the potential downsides of LLMs.  
Specifically, the LIB prompting mechanism attaches an integer line ID before each line. 
The GPT model is asked to output the line ID corresponding to each item’s beginning line. The 
output-parsing program automatically checks whether the output line IDs are integers 
consistent with the original prompt. Outputs containing inconsistent IDs are rejected and rerun. 
We construct item boundaries and the item’s full text after verifying the line IDs. 
In addition to LIB prompting, we format the demonstrations following the same format 
and provide several examples involving partial 10-K reports. Since text near item boundaries 
is more informative, we provide text chunks around selected item boundaries along with the 
corresponding expected outputs. Note that the model can output “NA” for items not in the text. 
Table 1 shows the example prompt used in GPT4ItemSeg. We truncate part of the text to 
save space. The first part of the prompt is the task description: “I am an excellent financial 
professional. The task is to identify the starting lines of items,” followed by an additional 
explanation regarding possible items in the 10-K. The second part is the few-shot 
demonstration, providing input and output examples. The last part is the target 10-K formatted 
using LIB. To reduce the length of longer 10-Ks and allow a full report to fit into the limited 
prompt space, we truncate the text in a line and keep only the first L words.

Performance Evaluation and Baseline Models 
We adopt the F1 score as the primary performance indicator for all item segmentation 
tasks. Item segmentation is similar to a classification task, and the F1 score is a standard 
performance measure for this type of problem. While we label an item using both the B and I 
tags, we merge these two tags when computing prediction performance. This approach ensures 
that all lines within an item are treated equally. 
For a target item, the precision is the percentage of correctly classified lines among lines 
classified as part of the item. The recall of an item is the percentage of lines in the item that 
have been correctly classified. Since the F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, 
we interpret the F1 score of an item as the performance level at which an item can be correctly 
identified. While our subsequent discussion mainly focuses on the F1 score, we also report 
average precision, recall, and accuracy. Detailed results, including per-item F1 scores, 
precision, recall, and accuracy, are provided in the Appendix.  
In our experiments, we consider three baselines. The first is a rule-based approach. We 
develop this approach using a set of regular expressions that capture common item headings 
and important keywords. The program is implemented as a Python script.  
The second baseline is the traditional conditional random field (CRF) model with 
manually crafted features.3 Similar to GPT4ItemSeg and BERT4ItemSeg, the unit of analysis 
is individual lines, and we develop three types of features: semantic, syntactic, and structural.

Model Training and Parameter Setting 
GPT4ItemSeg leverages the OpenAI API to access ChatGPT-4o. BERT4ItemSeg is 
implemented in PyTorch, using a fine-tuned Sentence-BERT model (Reimers and Gurevych 
2019) to encode each line of text and feed the resulting embedded vectors into a Bi-LSTM 
module. We explore the hidden dimensions (64, 128, 256, or 512) and the number of layers (1 
or 2) of the Bi-LSTM module. To balance training efficiency and performance under 
computational constraints, we select a hidden size of 256, two layers, and a batch size of 32. 
Dropout probability is set to 0.5 after evaluating 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 0.9. Model training is 
performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001. The average length of a 
line was 25.08 words, and more than 90% of lines had fewer than 100 words. Using the BERT 
tokenizer, we truncate a line if it is longer than 512 tokens. The early stopping patience

To address RQ4, we examine local deployment, usage fees, data protection considerations, 
and the challenge of concept drift. This discussion concludes with recommendations for 
researchers and practitioners tackling the item segmentation problem. 
Among the evaluated methods, all except GPT4ItemSeg can be deployed locally without 
requiring access to remote computational resources via APIs. BERT4ItemSeg requires a PC 
equipped with a consumer-grade GPU, such as an Nvidia GTX 3090 or 4090, to execute the 
model efficiently. On the other hand, CRF can run on most x86-based PCs with a capable CPU 
and sufficient memory. The rule-based method has even lower hardware requirements and can 
operate on modest hardware setups. 
Running GPT4ItemSeg incurs token fees for OpenAI API usage, whereas other methods 
operate entirely locally and therefore incur no additional costs. Another important 
consideration is data protection. Using GPT4ItemSeg requires submitting prompts and reports 
to OpenAI’s cloud-based API, which means the transmitted content is accessible to the service 
provider. While 10-K filings are public documents, the specific selection, sequencing, or


## Page 26


26 
 
processing of these filings can reveal nontrivial information, such as analysts’ focus on a 
particular industry, firm, or reporting period. By contrast, running segmentation models locally 
avoids sending any proprietary context outside the controlled computing environment, thereby 
offering greater assurance against unintended information leakage. 
Concept drift poses another challenge in item segmentation, as the SEC periodically 
updates its disclosure requirements. For example, the SEC introduced Item 1A in 2005 to 
mandate risk disclosure and recently announced Item 1C, which requires cybersecurity 
disclosures for 10-Ks filed for fiscal years ending on or after December 15, 2023. Most models, 
including BERT4ItemSeg, CRF, and the rule-based method, struggle to adapt to such changes 
because they cannot recognize new items. Fortunately, GPT4ItemSeg can easily accommodate 
the situation with minor changes in prompts. To include a new item in the LIB prompting, we 
need to list the new item as one of the targets in the few-shot demonstrations. If prompt space 
allows, we can also add a few partial examples highlighting the processing of new items. These 
changes can be made quickly to allow GPT4ItemSeg to recognize and process new items. 
When addressing new items, GPT4ItemSeg is especially valuable for generating 
additional training examples. These examples can augment the original dataset, enabling a 
retraining process for BERT4ItemSeg. This approach ensures that researchers and practitioners 
can maintain access to up-to-date, locally deployable models with robust performance, even as 
disclosure requirements evolve. By leveraging these recommendations, researchers and 
practitioners can effectively manage the challenges of item segmentation while ensuring 
adaptability to changing regulatory landscapes.  
Given the evolving nature of SEC disclosure rules, it is essential to establish a model 
maintenance strategy that balances performance stability with adaptability. Models should be 
continuously monitored to detect shifts in segmentation performance. While not every SEC 
update warrants an immediate retraining, significant reporting changes or persistent accuracy


## Page 27


27 
 
degradation should trigger a retraining cycle using updated labeled data. This proactive 
approach ensures that the segmentation models remain aligned with current reporting standards 
while minimizing the operational risk of outdated classifications. 
Moreover, the proposed approaches can be readily extended to 10-Q reports, which share 
similar structural patterns and item definitions with 10-K reports. Existing annotation scheme, 
LIB prompting mechanism, and BERT4ItemSeg model architecture can be applied with 
minimal modifications. Minor adjustments to the prompt examples in GPT4ItemSeg can 
accommodate 10-Q–specific items. This adaptability makes the methods suitable for 
expanding empirical research and automated disclosure analysis beyond 10-Ks. 
The approaches proposed in this study have been made publicly available for researchers 
and practitioners working on 10-K item segmentation at https://github.com/hsinmin/itemseg. 
BERT4ItemSeg is the preferred choice for users with access to GPU-enabled computers, 
offering the best combination of performance and deployment flexibility. CRF is a viable 
alternative for users without access to GPUs, delivering satisfactory results with minor 
performance trade-offs. We do not recommend the rule-based approach and do not offer this 
approach in the released open-source tool.

show no clear pattern; thus, it is difficult to draw specific recommendations from the model 
development. While different approaches have random errors at different lines, there are no 
clear patterns for model improvement. We do note that reports with fewer than 100 lines 
occasionally underperform. The limited context reduces the amount of information available 
for the model to identify item boundaries accurately. Moreover, a misclassified line contributes 
heavily to performance degradation.

Limitations 
Despite the strong performance of the proposed approaches, several limitations warrant 
consideration. First, while this dataset spans diverse industries and filing styles, it may not fully 
capture rare formatting patterns, industry-specific terminology, or unique disclosure structures


## Page 33


33 
 
found in less common filings. Consequently, the reported performance metrics may overstate 
generalizability when applied to filings outside the sampled period or atypical corporate 
reporting practices. Additionally, the annotation process remains susceptible to variability in 
human judgment, especially in borderline cases. 
Second, both BERT4ItemSeg and GPT4ItemSeg exhibit limitations inherent to their 
underlying architectures. BERT4ItemSeg is constrained by its reliance on pre-trained 
embeddings and supervised learning; it requires additional runs of fine-tuning when SEC 
reporting requirements change substantially. GPT4ItemSeg, while more adaptable to 
regulatory changes, relies on a cloud-based API, raising concerns about data privacy and long-
term service availability, and incurs token fees to use. Furthermore, GPT4ItemSeg’s 
performance may be sensitive to the future revisions of LLMs.  
Finally, the evaluation in this study focused on publicly available 10-K reports and did not 
account for integration with broader downstream tasks, such as sentiment analysis, topic 
modeling, or event extraction. While improved segmentation accuracy should enhance the 
reliability of such tasks, the actual gains in end-to-end analytical pipelines remain to be 
empirically validated. 
VI. CONCLUSIONS 
Items in 10-K filings are essential sources of corporate disclosure information, and 
accurate segmentation of these items is foundational for robust textual analysis in accounting 
and finance. Developing automatic extraction routines for key items, such as Items 1A and 7, 
reduces noise, streamlines research design, and mitigates the risk of misclassification errors 
that can distort empirical findings. In this study, we introduce two novel approaches: 
BERT4ItemSeg, which combines BERT embeddings with a Bi-LSTM in a hierarchical 
architecture, and GPT4ItemSeg, which leverages the LIB prompting mechanism to guide large


## Page 34


34 
 
language model outputs. BERT4ItemSeg achieved a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.9826 for 
core items and 0.9692 for other items, while GPT4ItemSeg also performed strongly despite 
relying on only a handful of few-shot examples. Both approaches outperformed the rule-based 
baseline by a large margin, signifying the benefits of advanced deep learning approaches. To 
the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to leverage PLMs and LLMs in developing 
item segmentation approaches that can be applied to analyze all 10-K reports disclosed by US 
public companies.  
A key contribution of this work is the creation of a large, high-quality, and publicly 
available annotated dataset comprising 3,737 manually segmented 10-K filings from 2001 to 
2019. This dataset spans multiple industries, disclosure styles, and reporting formats, making 
it a valuable benchmark for evaluating segmentation algorithms in accounting and finance 
research. By making both the dataset and our methodologies available, we provide the research 
community with the resources needed to replicate our results, compare against standardized 
baselines, and extend this work in a transparent and reproducible manner. This addresses a 
long-standing challenge in financial text analysis, where undocumented or ad hoc extraction 
routines have hindered comparability across studies. 
The proposed framework is also highly generalizable to other corporate and regulatory 
filings. The LIB prompting mechanism in GPT4ItemSeg can be adapted to new document types, 
such as foreign filers’ 40-F forms, 10-Qs, 8-Ks, or proxy statements, with a minimal number 
of few-shot examples, enabling rapid deployment in response to evolving SEC disclosure 
requirements and concept drift. Similarly, BERT4ItemSeg can be fine-tuned for alternative 
filings to produce robust, locally deployable models without recurring API costs. These 
capabilities make our approaches valuable not only for researchers but also for practitioners, 
such as financial analysts, auditors, and institutional investors, who seek the timely and 
accurate extraction of relevant disclosures.


## Page 35


35 
 
Looking ahead, future research could extend this work by integrating these segmentation 
methods into end-to-end analytical pipelines, such as sentiment analysis, topic modeling, 
tabular data interpretation, and numeric extraction, to evaluate the impact of improved 
segmentation on substantive findings. Additional efforts could explore active learning to 
reduce annotation costs, hybrid approaches that combine rule-based heuristics with AI models 
to further enhance accuracy, and hybrid architectures that leverage the robustness of locally 
deployed PLMs with the flexibility of prompt-driven LLMs. Another promising direction is 
examining model explainability to better understand how AI models make segmentation 
decisions and to improve transparency for end users.
