Abstract
Generalization problems in languages with binders involve computing the most common structure
between expressions while respecting bound variable renaming and freshness constraints. These
problems often lack a least general solution. However, leveraging nominal techniques, we previously
demonstrated that a semantic approach with atom-variables enables the elimination of redundant
solutions and allows for computing unique least general generalizations (LGGs).
In this work, we extend this approach to handle associative (A), commutative (C), and associative-
commutative (AC) equational theories. We present a sound and weak complete algorithm for solving
equational generalization problems, which generates finite weak minimal complete sets of LGGs for
each theory. A key challenge arises from solving equivariance problems while taking into account
these equational theories, as identifying redundant generalizations requires recognizing when one
expression (with binders) is a renaming of another while possibly considering permutations of
sub-expressions. This unexpected interaction between renaming and equational reasoning made this
particularly difficult, necessitating semantic tests within the equivariance algorithm. Given that these
equational theories naturally induce exponentially large LGG sets due to subexpression permutations,
future work could explore restricted theory fragments where the generalization problem remains
unitary. In these fragments, LGGs can be computed efficiently in polynomial time, offering practical
benefits for symbolic computation and automated reasoning tasks.

Introduction
The generalization problem [18] (a.k.a. anti-unification problem) is concerned with finding
a generalization of two given input expressions in a formal language, e.g., finding a more
general program between two different programs, or a more general first-order formula, or a
more general proof. Formally, given two expressions s and t, the generalization problem is
concerned with finding an expression r such that rσ1 = s and rσ2 = t for some substitutions
σ1 and σ2. For instance, it is easy to see that the expressions f(a, b) and f(a, g(x, c)) can
be generalized by the variable z (the initial expressions can be retrieved via substitutions
{z 7→f(a, b)} and {z 7→f(a, g(x, c))}). However, this generalizer gives no information about
the input expressions. Note that f(a, y) is also a generalizer, besides, it gives much more
information about the initial expressions, in fact, it represents all the similarities. The general
approach is to abstract away the particularities of s and t and find a (maximally) common
structure between them (in other words, to find a pattern).
As expected, the problem becomes more complicated in languages with binders (e.g.,
λ-calculus, the Calculus of Constructions [16], the nominal language [17], etc), and more

Among the difficulties are the need of:

This work is about generalization problems for a nominal language with atom-variables
and taking into account equational theories for associativity (A), commutativity (C) and
associativity-commutativity (AC). The choice is motivated by the expressivity of the language,
it can be used to express λ-terms, or first-order formulas, or π-calculus processes, etc, and it
has notions such as α-renaming and freshness embedded in the language, therefore, the usual
implicit renaming and freshness choices adopted in many works can be explicitly implemented.
In addition, reasoning in the nominal syntax has a first-order-like behaviour that is appealing.
We build upon a framework introduced in [19] that uses atom-variables and a semantic
approach to discard redundant solutions and compute unique lggs. As expected, working
with equational theories requires working in equivalence classes of these theories, which
we thought would be a natural extension of the work. Unexpectedly, these equivalence
classes interact with our semantic approach when solving the equivariance matching problems
which appear as sub-problems in our algorithm in a non-trivial way that turned out to be a
challenging problem. We expand on this and other challenges below.
Contributions.We present a rule-based algorithm, NAUE, for solving E-generalization
problems. Our approach refines existing structural rules for abstractions and atom-variables
while introducing new rules to handle function symbols under equational theories and
equivariance. These refinements strengthen the rules, eliminating one of the sources of weak
incompleteness. We propose a method to solve a more general version of the equivariance
problem, which consists of finding a permutation π and a freshness-context ∇such that
∇|= π · s ≈E t. Here, we consider an equational theory E ∈{A,C,AC}, and π, ∇, s, t may
contain atom-variables. It requires the analysis to be semantical, represented using the
symbol ⊨. Note that it is harder to evaluate, e.g., whether the generalized permutation
(A C) acts in the abstraction such that (A C) · (λC.f AC(A, B, C)) ≈AC λA.f AC(A, B, A)),
i.e., makes these two abstractions equal modulo renaming of bound variables and modulo
AC. The difficulty arises because we don’t know how (A C) acts on B. Here, A, B, C are
atom-variables which could be instantiated to the same or to different concrete atoms. The
valuations of the atom-variables have an effect when branching on the possible permutations
of the AC-function symbol f AC. Dealing with the interaction was a challenge, and required
specialized methods, described in Section 6, to guarantee correctness while branching.
Related Work. Generalization problems have several applications, such as, on parallel
recursion scheme detection [5], clone detection [8], analogy making [14], etc. First-order anti-
unification problems modulo equational theories have been extensively investigated [1, 2, 9, 12].
While the techniques presented there are similar to the ones used here, the interaction with
nominal techniques is novel. Nominal techniques have been investigated in the context of
nominal unification [15, 21, 22] including equational theories [3, 4], but not much research
has been done w.r.t. nominal anti-unification.

(lgg) for nominal terms-in-context, i.e., pairs of the form (∇, s), where ∇is a freshness context
and s is a (standard) nominal term. It was observed that if atoms were taken literally, there

Preliminaries
We assume the reader is familiar with the nominal syntax, and only recall the main concepts
and notations that are needed in the paper. For more details we refer the reader to [13, 17].
2.1
NLa: A Ground Nominal Language with Equational Theories.
Fix a countable infinite set of atoms A = {a, b, c, . . .}. Atoms are identified by their name, so
it will be redundant to say that two atoms a and b are different. A signature Σ is a set of
term-formers (disjoint from A) such that to each f ∈Σ is assigned a unique non-negative
integer n, called the arity of f, written as f:n, and constants (a, b, c . . .) are symbols in Σ
with arity zero. A ground permutation π is a bijection A →A which is the identity almost
everywhere, i.e., the set {a ∈A | π(a) ̸= a} is finite. Permutations are represented by
sequences of swappings written as (a b) where a, b ∈A. Id denotes the identity permutation.
The composition of two permutations π and π′ will be denoted as π ◦π′. We may write
permutations omitting the ◦-symbol as (a1 b1)(a2 b2) . . . (an bn).
The nominal language NLa is built by the grammar:
S, Si ::= a | π · S | f(S1, . . . , Sn) | λa.S
π ::= Id | (a b) ◦π
where a, b are atoms, λa.S denotes the abstraction of atom a in the term S, and f(S1, . . . , Sn)
is a function application, where f ∈Σ and f:n.
Since NLa consists of ground terms (sometimes called expressions), every term in NLa
can be simplified by applying permutations such that only terms (and subterms) of the
forms a | f(S1, . . . , Sn) | λa.S remain, i.e. expressions of a lambda-calculus with atoms
and function symbols. The effect of a permutation application is defined recursively on the
structure of a term as usual. The predicate ≈, which stands for α-equivalence of the terms
in NLa, is defined as usual [22, 23]:

not derivable. Finally, a freshness constraint a#S holds (or is valid) iff it is derivable using
the rules. Validity can be established for α-equality constraints in a similar way.

2.2
NLA: A Nominal Language with Variables and Equational Theories.
We recall the basic notions of nominal terms with atom-variables, which were introduced
in [19]. We fix a countable infinite set of term-variables X = {X, Y, Z, . . .} and a countable
infinite set of atom-variables A = {A, B, C, . . .} such that X ∩A = ∅. Variables are distinct
from objects in the signature Σ which is taken from NLa. The NLA language is defined
by the following grammar of terms (also denoted T(Σ, X, A)) and permutations π with
atom-variables instead of atoms:
s, si
::= W | π · X | f(s1, . . . , sn) | λW.s
(nominal terms)
W, Wi
::= π · A
(atom-variable suspensions)
π
::= id | (W1 W2) ◦π
(atom-variable permutations)
Differently from the standard grammar of a nominal language [13], NLA terms do not
contain atoms.
We denote the inverse of a permutation π with π−1.
If π is a list of
swappings (A1 B1) · · · (An Bn), then π−1 = (An Bn) · · · (A1 B1), since (A1 B1) · · · (An Bn) ·
(An Bn) · · · (A1 B1) is the trivial permutation under any interpretation of atom-variables.
There are two kinds of suspensions: π · A and π · X, i.e., one on atom-variables and one on
term-variables, respectively. We write π ·V to denote a suspension over a variable V ∈A∪X,
and AV(F) for the set of atom-variables occurring in a the formula F. Abstractions are more
general here as well: λW.s denotes the abstraction of a (suspended) atom-variable W on
a term s. Function application extends to nominal terms in NLA as expected. As in NLa,
in NLA we permit function symbols that are commutative, associative, or both.

a term variable (A B) · X. Note that the semantics of these terms can only be established
once the atom-variables A, B, C are instantiated to concrete atoms and the term-variable

The Semantics of NLA-Expressions and Freshness Constraints
The semantics of NLA-expressions is based on interpretations ρ to ground expressions in NLa.
▶Definition 2 (Interpretations). An interpretation ρ maps NLA-terms to NLa-terms. It
is determined by a mapping from atom-variables to concrete atoms and term-variables to
(ground) NLa-terms. Interpretations act on NLA-terms homomorphically. It is also extended
to freshness constraints, contexts, and equational theories, as expected.
We define now the semantics, which includes equivalence of expressions, validity of
freshness constraints and contexts. We also assume that an equational theory E is defined
by axioms from A, AC, and C for specific function symbols. Like substitution applications,
applications of interpretations are written in postfix notation.
▶Definition 3 (Semantics in NLA). The semantics of NLA is defined on expressions in NLa:
1. Two NLA-permutations π1, π2 are semantically equivalent iff for all interpretations ρ: π1ρ
is the same permutation as π2ρ. This is denoted as π1 ≡π2
2. Two NLA-terms s, t are semantically equivalent iff for all interpretations ρ: sρ ≈E tρ.
3. An NLA-freshness constraint W#s is valid, iff for all interpretations ρ: Wρ#sρ is valid.
4. A context ∇is valid, if for all interpretations ρ, every constraint in ∇ρ holds.
5. A context ∇is consistent w.r.t. ρ iff every constraint in ∇ρ holds. In this case, we say
that ρ respects ∇.
Note that items 1,3,4,5 are independent of the equational theory.
▶Example 4. Interesting constraints such as A#λB.A or C#λA.λB.C are expressible in
NLA. Note that A#λB.A is consistent w.r.t. ρ iff Aρ = Bρ. Differently, C#λA.λB.C is
consistent w.r.t. ρ iff Cρ = Aρ ∨Cρ = Bρ: in fact, for an interpretation ρ of A, B, C such
that Cρ ̸= Bρ ∧Cρ ̸= Aρ, the constraint would reduce to Cρ#Cρ, which is inconsistent.
We can now consider judgements of the form ∇⊨A#s, ∇⊨s ≈E t and ∇⊨π1 ≡π2,
and their meaning is established next.
▶Definition 5 (Semantics of Judgements). Let ∇be a consistent NLA-freshness context, A#s
a freshness constraint and π1, π2 be NLA-permutations. We say that
∇⊨A#s holds iff for all interpretations ρ: ∇ρ valid =⇒Aρ#sρ valid.
∇⊨s ≈E t holds iff for all interpretations ρ: ∇ρ valid =⇒sρ ≈E tρ valid.
∇⊨π1 ≡π2 holds iff for all interpretations ρ: ∇ρ valid =⇒π1ρ = π2ρ.

Generalization Problems in NLA modulo Equational Theories
We are interested in the following problem:
Given: Two NLA-terms-in-context (∇, s) and (∇, t).
Find: An NLA-term-in-context (Γ, r) that is a generalization modulo E of (∇, s) and
(∇, t) which preserves most of their common structure while taking into account the
equational theory E.
This problem is known as the generalization problem modulo E (also known as the anti-
unification problem modulo E), which we will investigate in the context of the language NLA.
In the following sections, we will introduce the main ingredients to solve this problem, which
we will refer to simply as generalization (or anti-unification), as reasoning modulo E will be
used throughout this work.
4.1
A Semantic Approach to Generalization in NLA
We follow a semantic approach to solve the generalization problem in NLA by investigating
the semantics of the pairs (∇, s) and (∇, t) that we want to generalize. The semantics will
be based on the equivalence classes modulo ≈E (i.e. modulo E and α-renaming) of the
ground instances s and t that respect the corresponding instances of ∇. First, we define the
semantics of a pair (∇, s) w.r.t. interpretations of s on the ground language NLa:
▶Definition 6. The semantics of (∇, s), denoted [[(∇, s)]], consists of the following set:
[[(∇, s)]] = {[sρ]E | ρ is an interpretation and ∇ρ holds},
where [r]E denotes the equivalence class of r modulo ≈E.
Note the semantics of terms-in-context that contain abstractions are always infinite due
to the existence of infinitely many names for α-renaming:
▶Example 7 (Semantics of Abstractions - part 1). The semantics of ({A#B}, λA.f(A, B)) is
the following:
[[({A#B}, λA.f(A, B))]] = {[λAρ.f(Aρ, Bρ)]E | ρ is an interpretation and Aρ#Bρ holds}
= {[λAρ.f(Aρ, Bρ)]E | Aρ ̸= Bρ} = {[λa.f(a, b)]E, [λa.f(a, c)]E, . . .}
The second argument of f can be any concrete atom, as long as it is not captured by the
abstraction.
▶Example 8 (Semantics of Abstractions - part 2). The semantics of the term-in-context
({A#C}, λA.f(A, B)) is the following:
[[({A#C}, λA.f(A, B))]] = {[λAρ.f(Aρ, Bρ)]E | ρ is an interpretation and Aρ#Cρ holds}
= {[λAρ.f(Aρ, Bρ)]E | Aρ ̸= Cρ}
the interpretation Cρ is irrelevant for the semantics since it does not occur in the term.
One can always find ρ such that Aρ ̸= Cρ. Hence, ({A#C}, λA.f(A, B)) is equivalent to
(∅, λA.f(A, B))
The semantics of terms-in-context provides a framework to for comparing terms and
determining their relative generality. Specifically, a pair (∇, t) is more general w.r.t. E

▶Definition 9 (Generalization, lggE). A term-in-context (∇, r) is an generalization of (∇1, s)
and (∇2, t) iff (∇, r) the following holds: (∇, r)⪯E(∇1, s) and (∇, r)⪯E(∇2, t). The pair

generalization, i.e., every other generalization (∇′, r′) of (∇1, s) and (∇2, t), is more general
than (∇, r), that is, (∇′, r′)⪯E(∇, r) holds.
A set G of generalizations for (∇1, s) and (∇2, t) is complete iff for each generalization
(∆, r′) of (∇1, s) and (∇2, t), there exists (∇, r) ∈G such that (∆, r′)⪯E(∇, r). The set G
is called minimal, if for all different (∇′, r1), (∇′′, r2) ∈G, we have (∇′, r1) ̸⪯E(∇′′, r2) and
(∇′′, r2) ̸⪯E(∇′, r1). Such minimal set will be denoted as mcsgE((∇1, s), (∇2, t)).
The existence and cardinality of the minimal complete set of generalizations characterize
the anti-unification type of an equational theory E. Thus, E has nullary (anti-unification) type
if there are terms-in-context T1 and T2 for which mcsgE(T1, T2) does not exist. Otherwise,
E has type unitary (resp. finitary), if mcsgE(T1, T2) is a singleton (resp. finite) for all
terms-in-context T1 and T2; and infinitary, if mcsgE(T1, T2) is infinite for some T1 and T2.

NAUE: An Anti-Unification Algorithm
In this section, we introduce the rule-based algorithm NAUE to solve the generalization
problem. The rules for NAUE are described in two parts: (i) Figure 2 contains the basic rules
that have no explicit interaction with E; (ii) Figure 3 contains the rules dealing with E. In
addition, NAUE relies on a novel algorithm Eqvm for solving the equivariance problem modulo
E, which is detailed in Section 6. Rules in Figure 2 labeled in (gray) remain unchanged
from [19], as they required no modification to accommodate the equational theories in E. In
contrast, rules labeled in (green) are either new or improved versions of existing rules. The
symbol ·∪denotes disjoint union.
The NAUE operates on tuples (called states) of the form P; S; Γ; σ where:
P denotes the set of unsolved anti-unification equations between terms, denoted X : t ≜s.
Here, X is a term-variable that will represent the generalization of t and s.
S denotes the set of solved anti-unification equations (the store). The store S may
contain anti-unification equations between suspended atom-variable permutations, denoted
C : π · A1 ≜π2 · A2, where C is an atom-variable that will represent the generalization of
π · A1 and π2 · A2.
Γ denotes the freshness context computed so far which constrains generalization variables;
σ is a substitution, computed so far, mapping generalization variables to terms in NLA.
Furthermore, there is as a global parameter a freshness context ∇that does not constrain
generalization variables. Note that in P, generalization variables are only term-variables,
while in S they can be term- or atom-variables. X and C are called generalization variables.

In this section, we introduce Eqvm, a novel algorithm for solving equivariance problems in
NLA, specifically for handling equivariant (matching) equations of the form t ⊑s. Unlike
the equivariance algorithm developed for the empty theory in [19], Eqvm accounts for
the equational theories considered in this work. Handling E is nontrivial, as atom-variable
permutations interact with equational theories in unexpected ways (cf. Example 21), requiring
additional tests and refinements.

Conclusion and Future Work
We have investigated the extension of the nominal anti-unification problem to theories A,
C and AC. Our proposed method considers the nominal language with atom-variables NLA and
a semantic approach to find least general generalizers modulo E ∈{A, C, AC}. The extended
algorithm NAUE is obtained by adding specific rules to deal with each particular theory
as well as new non-deterministic equivariance algorithm Eqvm that works, semantically,
modulo the theories considered here. We left as future work the investigation subclasses of
the generalization problem that are computationally tractable and lead to a single lgg, and
other optimization methods to work in wider subclasses of the problem, using for e.g., the
rigidity functions as in [11]. Further equational theories like the considered one with a unit
element are also left for future research.

▶Definition 34 (EQR-fresheness constraints). Let M be a finite set of atom-variables. An

▶Algorithm 7 (C-criterion). Let s = f C(s1, s2) and t = f C(t1, t2) be the two expressions to
