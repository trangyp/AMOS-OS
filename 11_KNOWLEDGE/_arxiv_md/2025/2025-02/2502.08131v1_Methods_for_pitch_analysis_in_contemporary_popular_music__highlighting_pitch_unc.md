Methods for pitch analysis in contemporary popular music:
highlighting pitch uncertainty in Primaal’s commercial works
Emmanuel Deruty∗1,3, Luc Leroy2, Yann Mac´e2, and David Meredith3
1Sony Computer Science Laboratories, 6 rue Amyot, 75005 Paris, France
2Neodrome Entertainment, 5 rue Vernet, 75008 Paris, France
3Department of Architecture, Design and Media Technology, Aalborg University,
Rendsburggade 14, 9000 Aalborg, Denmark
Abstract
We identify characteristic features of how pitch is manipulated for expressive purposes by
Hyper Music, a mainstream commercial music company specialising in advertisement music for
global corporations. The study shows that the use and organisation of pitch in the company’s
‘Primaal’ brand differs from Western classical music. Through interviews with producers and
in-depth analysis of their work, we reveal that their methods centre on a conscious aim to con-
struct a musical discourse based on pitch uncertainty, contrasting with the clear transmission
of well-defined pitches in Western classical traditions.
According to the Primaal producers,
who acknowledge the influence of artists such as Kanye West and Daft Punk and use widely
available technology, pitch uncertainty captures the listener’s attention. We provide analyses of
musical excerpts demonstrating their approach, alongside descriptions of the tools and methods
employed to achieve their expressive goals. These goals and methods are placed in a broader his-
torical context, contrasting with fundamental principles of pitch organisation in Western music.
Techniques used by Hyper Music to introduce and control pitch uncertainty include boosting
upper partials, expressive use of inharmonicity, continuous pitch distributions around ‘poles’
tied to specific ‘modes’, and continuously evolving pitch. We examine these techniques from a
psychoacoustic perspective, and conduct listening tests corroborating some of the observations.
The ultimate goal of the study is to introduce a set of methods suited to the analysis of pitch
in contemporary popular music.
Keywords:
music analysis; pitch; contemporary popular music

Introduction
In this paper, we examine the elements that evoke a sensation of pitch in a small set of commercial
musical works. Our analysis considers both their construction and organisation within the music.
We use phenomenological reduction and summarise our findings in theoretical models. The study
was conducted in collaboration with the producers, providing opportunities for validation.
The music we study is used in mainstream content like advertising, appealing to a wide audience
as major brands acquire it for public representation. We focus on Hyper Music’s brand ‘Primaal’,
the company’s latest successful project. The Hyper Music producers, Luc Leroy and Yann Mac´e,
describe Primaal’s music as electronic with urban elements. We expect that our results will apply
to other music in these genres.
Our analysis shows that Hyper Music’s approach to pitch differs radically from the way in which
pitch is typically organised in Western classical music. In Primaal’s works, tones are inharmonic.
Low frequencies often fail to evoke pitch.
Single complex tones can evoke multiple pitches and
function as chords around which songs are structured. Pitches are distributed around scale degrees,
with the spread of these distributions controlled as a musical parameter. Each song typically focuses
on one or two scale degrees. Traditional chord sequences play only a minor role.
Hyper Music’s producers deliberately introduce and manipulate pitch uncertainty, contrasting
sharply with Western classical music, where tonal organisation and instrument design aim to dis-
ambiguate pitch.

Primaal
Primaal is a project of the Hyper Music production company (Hyper Music, 2024), referred to in
Deruty et al. (2022) and Deruty and Grachten (2022). In 2022 and 2023, Primaal produced music
for brands such as L’Or´eal, Adidas, Vichy, Honda, GoPro, and Chanel. The producers recognise
Trent Reznor, Daft Punk, Kanye West, Skrillex, and Rosalia as influences. Roland TR-808 bass
tracks, common in popular music, especially hip-hop (Lavoie, 2020), are key to Primaal’s music. We
therefore assume that Primaal’s production techniques reflect shared practices in the music industry.

of music to media typically takes ten months to two years, so more songs may have been synchronised
by the time this paper goes to press.

Terminology
In regards to tones, we use the following terminology:
1. The term partial denotes the spectral representation of a single periodic sine wave. The notion
corresponds to what Helmholtz (1885, p. 23) call constitutents or partial tones.
2. A harmonic complex tone designates an ensemble of partials that are integer multiples of a
fundamental frequency.
The notion corresponds to what Helmholtz (1885, p. 23) called a
compound. We denote by f0 the frequency that is the greatest common divisor for the partials’
frequencies.
3. An inharmonic complex tone designates a tone that consists of an ensemble of partials that
are near-integer multiples of a fundamental frequency. Fletcher et al. (1962) and Rasch and
Plomp (1982, p. 9) provide examples of such tones. Following Fletcher et al. (1962, p. 757),
we denote by f0 the greatest common divisor of the harmonic frequencies in the harmonic
complex tone that is most similar to the inharmonic complex tone.
4. In a harmonic or inharmonic complex tone, an overtone can be any of the partials except the
one that corresponds to the fundamental frequency. The notion corresponds to what Helmholtz
(1885, p. 23) called upper partial tones.
5. A harmonic is any sinusoidal component of a harmonic complex tone, with the nth harmonic
having a frequency that is n times the frequency of the fundamental (the fundamental is thus
the first harmonic). The notion corresponds to what Helmholtz (1885, p. 23) called a harmonic
upper partial tone.

Pitch uncertainty
A key feature of Primaal’s music is evoking uncertainty in the perception of pitch. The producers
believe this enhances perceptual richness and boosts listener appeal.
4.1
Aspects of pitch in Primaal’s music
4.1.1
Pitch in the production process
Figure 1 summarises the processes in Primaal’s music that relate to pitch. The production process
involves several musical parameters in McAdams’s (1999) sense:
(1) Origin of perceived pitch. The producers rely on three techniques for producing pitch-
evoking sounds:
(a) Using harmonic complex tones whose perceived pitches correspond to their fundamental
frequencies, relating to the most common way of generating perceived pitches in Western
classical music (Rameau, 1750).
(b) Boosting specific overtones in complex tones so that they are heard as separate ‘tones’
with independent pitches (Section 4.3). In particular, the producers commonly boost the
third and fifth harmonics. The bass often provides such partials.
(c) Generating multiple perceived pitches through distortion, waveshaping (Section 7.2), or
ring modulation (Section 7.3).

The choice of the origin of a pitch (i.e., (a) fundamental, (b) boosted partial, or (c) generated
partial) serves as a musical parameter. Changes in the origins of pitches used in a passage can
be used to articulate a song’s form and structure (Sections 8.6 and 8.7).
The choice of a particular pitch origin determines a set of affordances provided by that selected
method of evoking a sense of pitch. In other words, each method of generating a pitch has its
own ‘idiom’ in a similar sense to that in which a musical instrument has its own idiom—that
is, those techniques and patterns that are more natural and easy to use or perform on that
instrument (Huron and Berec, 2009, p. 103).
The choice of a particular method of generating pitches (origin of pitch) may determine some
finite set of pitches—effectively a ‘scale’ or ‘mode’—from which the producer may select pitches.
If one generates pitches by boosting selected partials, then the decision as to which complex
tone to use as a source also determines the set of pitches (partials) from which one can select
the ones to boost.
This results in passages of the music being based on ‘resultant scales’
that emerge from the choice of pitch origin, as opposed to ‘acting scales’ that are proactively
selected by the composer. This opposition is reminiscent of the shift from resultant harmony
to active harmony mentioned by Cœurdevey (1998, p. 82) in relation to the transition from
modal polyphonic writing to harmonic and tonal writing.
(2) ‘Pole’ choice. Perceived pitches are organised into scales with modes, typically derived from
the twelve-tone chromatic scale (see Section 8.4). Each scale step is realised according to a

deviations can be used to articulate a song’s form and structure (Sections 8.6.3 and 8.7.2).
Primaal’s musical parameters relate to Schneider and Frieler’s (2009) dimensions for the sounds
of musical instruments:
1. ‘Signal’, with values ranging from stationary to transient.
2. ‘Periodicity’, from clear to uncertain.
3. ‘Spectrum’, from harmonic to inharmonic.
4. ‘Frequencies’, from stable to fluctuating.
5. ‘Pitch’, from clear to ambiguous.
6. ‘Typical’, from the sensation of a single pitch to multiple/no pitch.
For instance, our musical parameter (1), ‘Origin of perceived pitch’, relates to Schneider and
Frieler’s dimensions 5 and 6. Technique (a), using the harmonic series as a source of inspiration,
leads to the clear sensation of a single pitch, while techniques (b) (boosting overtones) and (c)
(generating overtones) lead to multiple and/or uncertain pitches.
4.1.2
Consequences on pitch tracking
Pitch trackers extract pitches from monodic sounds using expert features (Drugman et al., 2018)
or deep-learning techniques (Kim et al., 2018; Riou et al., 2023), and may involve virtual pitch
detection (Terhardt, 1979; Meddis and O’Mard, 2006).
The use by the Primaal producers of the seven musical parameters identified above and the
way in which these parameters relate to Schneider and Frieler’s dimensions result in music that is
resistant to automated pitch tracking. In particular, pitch trackers typically assume that one tone
corresponds to one pitch, whereas ‘single’ complex tones often evoke multiple perceived pitches in
Primaal’s music (relating to Schneider and Frieler’s dimension 6). Another problem is that the tones
that evoke pitch sensations in Primaal’s music are typically not harmonic, whereas automatic pitch

4.2
Inharmonicity
4.2.1
Background
In Primaal’s music, tones are almost always inharmonic. Inharmonicity is defined as ‘the deviation
of frequencies from an exact harmonic series’ (Campbell, 2001).
A distinction is made between
inharmonic sounds irrelevant to music (e.g., noise) and ‘coherent’ inharmonic signals, which sound
stable like harmonic signals (De Boer, 1956). We focus on inharmonicity caused by inharmonically-
related partials.
The relationship between inharmonic partials has been studied in piano strings (Young, 1952),
involving an inharmonicity coefficient that indicates the gradual shift of partials from harmonic
positions.
Primaal’s music was produced using electronic tools that allow for the generation of
partials that deviate from harmonic positions.
Inharmonic complex tones may evoke one or more pitches. Thus,
• if partials are close to harmonic positions, the tone evokes a single pitch, approximately the
fundamental of the least-deviating harmonic series (Rasch and Plomp, 1982, p. 9); whereas,
• if partials are further from harmonic positions, both the fundamental and other partials may
dominate (J¨arvel¨ainen et al., 2000). As an example, Bregman and Ahad (1996, p. 41) show
how a partial becomes audible as a separate pitch from the fundamental as inharmonicity
increases.
Section 8.2.4 discusses whether inharmonicity in Primaal’s music results in one or several per-
ceived pitches. We conclude that multiple pitches result from boosted partials (Section 4.3) rather
than inharmonicity. We carried out a listening test demonstrating that high inharmonicity can lead
to listeners perceiving two separate pitches (listening test 1, SM A.1).
4.2.2
Inharmonic tones with low fundamental frequencies
Loudspeakers do not have a flat frequency response, especially in the low frequencies. Expensive
studio monitors like the Genelec 1236A (Genelec, 2024) may have a flat response across the entire
range. In contrast, near-field monitors often have a weaker response below 35Hz (Newell et al.,
2001; Deruty, 2024), and consumer products show variable low-frequency responses (RTings.com,
2024). Headphones like the Sony WH-1000XM5 offer a flatter low-frequency response (Taboada,
2022) while laptop speakers struggle at these frequencies (Dave2D, 2022).
The f0 values in Primaal’s bass parts often lie between 35 and 45Hz (SM C), which some speak-
ers may not reproduce. In harmonic tones, the auditory system may derive pitch through temporal

4.3
Amplification of upper partials
See Section 5.2 for a discussion of the historical context of the debate regarding perception of upper
partials as individual entities.
4.3.1
Process and purpose
In Primaal’s music, a key technique is amplifying upper partials while attenuating the fundamental,
making the upper partials audible as carriers of perceivable pitches. Listening test 3 (SM A.3)
confirms that multiple pitches can be heard in complex tones.
In Primaal’s music, partials do not follow equal temperament and may evolve continuously
(Section 8.3).
The process of boosting selected partials is well-suited to bass parts with rich spectra. Using
low f0 values (e.g., around 35Hz) aids harmonic perception over the fundamental. At equal energy,
lower partials produce lower loudness (ISO, 2023) and may not be transmitted by the playback
system (Deruty, 2024). The producers aim to make the instrument a rich ‘harmonic entity’ with
‘presence’ and ‘personality’. Instead of adding instruments, they amplify the bass’s upper partials,
and attenuate the f0 in order to emphasise these partials. The producers prefer bare arrangements
where the richness of the bass stands out.
An extreme example is the bass track in ‘¡Fire!’ (C.10), where pitch sensation comes from the
amplitude modulation of partials from a low f0. Similar methods are used in ‘Cardinal’ (C.6) and
‘Elevate’ (C.9).
Amplifying upper partials demonstrates a pitch–timbre continuum, where a partial’s amplitude,
traditionally a property relating to timbre (Peeters et al., 2011), can influence perceived pitch if it
is sufficiently large (Yost, 2009, p. 1706).
4.3.2
Technical means
The producers highlight upper partials by either boosting existing partials or generating new ones.
For bass, they use synthesis engines like the ‘808 Woofer Warfare’ (Section 7.1) to create loud
partials. They also use a posteriori equalisation (see Section 7.5) to boost medium frequencies and
reduce or remove the fundamental.
Examples include the bass in ‘Elevate’ (+10dB@1kHz) and
‘Boom’ (+10dB@5kHz), the synth break in ‘Cardinal’ (C.6), and the gimmick in ‘Whomp’ (C.14).

4.3.4
Odd harmonics and pitch uncertainty
As seen in the ‘Danger’ bass track (SM C.7.1), the producers often use odd-harmonic tones. Such
tones promote pitch ambiguity, particularly with low f0. Yost (2009, pp. 1705–1706) notes that
when a harmonic complex tone lacks its lowest partials, and the lowest is an odd harmonic, temporal
analysis may yield two pitches. This also occurs when a tone has odd partials and a low f0 (e.g.,
30Hz). Low-frequency partials result in lower loudness and may not be transmitted (Section 4.2.2),
making the lowest audible partial an odd upper partial. Listening test 4 (SM A.4) confirms that
perceived pitch increasingly differs between low-f0 odd-harmonic tones and all-harmonic tones as f0
decreases.
4.4
Pitched content in drums
4.4.1
Context
Musical signals have been divided into ‘percussive’ and ‘harmonic’ components. Percussion is short
and noisy, while harmonic elements are longer and pitch-concentrated (Rump et al., 2010). These
distinctions guide early source separation methods (Fitzgerald, 2010; Yoo et al., 2010).
Drums, however, may contain pitched content (Richardson, 2010). Though their eigenfrequencies
are often inharmonic, they can be harmonic (Antunes, 2017). Drum stems extracted by SpectraLay-
ers or iZotope RX 8 confirm that pitch is often present (Attack Magazine, 2022, 14’30, 19’41). Thus,
drums can be tuned. In popular music, acoustic drum tuning impacts sound quality (Toulson et al.,
2009), though there does not seem to be any consensus on tuning methods (Drum Magazine, 2010).
4.4.2
Drums in Primaal’s music
The Primaal producers often tune the kick drum, especially for longer samples, but find the snare
harder to tune. They may tune other percussions or leave them out-of-tune to act as ‘punctuation’,
as in ‘Cardinal’, where metallic percussion contrasts with the bass.
They emphasise caution when tuning drums, prioritising timbre and register over pitch—a prac-
tice also noted by hip-hop producer Scott Storch (Deruty, 2024). The next closest ‘pole’ is usually
chosen (see Section 4.1.1), but if transposition alters timbre too much, they leave the sample out of
tune or select another. A semitone or tone shift is acceptable, but not a third. For example, they
detuned the snare in ‘YadaYada’ by one semitone to match the closest ‘pole’.
The producers note that out-of-tune drums may not always clash with a song’s pitches, as drum
pitch perception can change or be lost when played with tracks of higher pitch strength. Listening
test 5 (SM A.5) shows that perceived kick drum pitch changes when played with stronger-pitched
tracks, though no clear rules emerged. See Section 8.5 for more on drum tuning in Primaal songs.

Pitch uncertainty in a historical context
5.1
Acoustic beats and combination tones
Acoustic beats and combination tones result from the superposition of pure tones with different
frequencies. Acoustical interference beats are periodic amplitude fluctuations caused by two pure
tones with slightly different frequencies. Turner (1977, p. 12) notes that when two intense tones
are played together, the ear perceives a third, lower ‘combination’ tone, called a ‘Tartini tone’ after
violinist Giuseppe Tartini (1692–1770).

5.1.1
Acoustic beats, tuning, and unison
Figure 3 shows that pure intervals (small-integer ratios) generate no acoustic beats, while impure
intervals produce beats. Impure intervals have been considered undesirable, leading to the develop-
ment of temperaments that reduce acoustic beating (Lindley, 2001b). Instrument tuning, such as
for organs (Baggaley and Thurlow, 2023), harpsichords (Christensen, 2006, p. 255), pianos (White,
1917, p. 87), and guitars (Klickstein and Keaton, 1993), also seeks to minimise beating. However,
the ‘unison’ effect used by Primaal (Section 7.4) is designed to create beats.
Figure 4 contrasts different attitudes towards beating corresponding to different historical peri-
ods. ‘Just intonation’ refers to harmonic intervals tuned purely so they do not beat (Lindley, 2001a).
Before Rameau (1737) endorsed equal temperament, minimising acoustic beating was a priority. In
modern popular music, techniques like unison and overdubbing (Huber and Runstein, 2013, p. 31)
intentionally produce beats by combining tracks with slightly different pitches.
5.1.2
Combination tones, distortion and ring modulation
Helmholtz observed that the tetrads in Palestrina’s Stabat Mater minimise combination tones, sug-
gesting they are undesirable (Kursell, 2015; Helmholtz, 1885, p. 225). In contrast, 20th century
composer G´erard Grisey, following Hindemith’s ring modulation harmony (Hindemith, 1941), used
sum and difference tones to generate harmonic fields (Anderson, 2000, p. 52). Emmerson (1977)
provides examples of ring modulation harmony, and Costa (2019) explores the link between ring
modulation harmony and temperaments. The producers heavily use ring modulators, distortion,
and waveshaping (Section 7.2).

5.2
‘Hearing out’ individual harmonics
5.2.1
Historical debate
In Section 4.3, we indicated that the producers boost higher formants to evoke pitches independently
of the fundamental frequency, raising the question of how well humans can ‘hear out’ individual
harmonics. This debate has a long history. Mersenne (1636) suggested that a struck string produces
at least five sounds, though only the best ears can hear them. Rameau (1750, pp. 13–14) later used
this idea to form his theory of harmony.
The ear as a frequency analyser was key to the Ohm–Seebeck dispute (Turner, 1977). Ohm
(1843) argued that only the fundamental partial contributes to pitch, while Seebeck (1843) claimed
overtones are involved. Helmholtz (1885, pp. 58–59) supported Ohm, stating overtones affect timbre
but not pitch. For further discussion, see Plomp (1964, 2001) and Turner (1977).
5.2.2
Practical experiments
Experiments have explored the ear’s ability to ‘hear out’ harmonic partials (Moore, 2012, pp. 86–
88). These studies suggest humans can identify the first 5–8 harmonics of a periodic sound, limited
by the ear’s critical bands. Helmholtz (1885, p. 58) argues that the difficulty of hearing harmonics
is not dependent on their energy. Plomp (1964) disagrees, stating that harmonics set at the same
loudness level have ‘equal chances’ of being heard. The Primaal’s producers’ practice of boosting
harmonics to make them individually audible contradicts Helmholtz’s view.
5.2.3
Enlarging the scope of the debate
The validity of the hypothesis that the ear is a frequency analyser has been questioned. Dixon Ward
(1970, p. 438) calls it a ‘quarter-truth’, noting that the ear acts this way only in limited conditions.

may distinguish contemporary popular music from Western classical music.
5.3
Modality: background
The concept of ‘mode’ has been interpreted in various ways in the literature. Powers et al. (2001)
distinguish between its use in European music history and as a modern concept in non-Western
music. Another distinction is whether a mode is a scale or a melodic type. For Primaal’s music, the
early Western definition (900–1000) applies, where a mode is a scale with one important degree, the
‘final’. Later interpretations of ‘mode’ are not considered here.
This interpretation appears in treatises such as Musica Enchiriadis and Scholia Enchiriadis
(Sancti Amandi, 900a,b), translated by Erickson and Palisca (1995). Erickson and Palisca (1995,
p. 50) describe the final as central to the scale. Cœurdevey (1998, p. 17) and Powers et al. (2001)
derive similar definitions from Dialogus de musica (ca. 1000–1100). Cœurdevey states that ‘[a] tone
or mode is the principle which distinguishes any song by its final.’ The final determines the end of
every song (Odo, 2001).
The final’s role remains ambiguous. According to Dialogus de musica, it is both the starting and
ending scale degree. Mee`us (2023) calls it the ‘reference’ note, while Powers (2001) refers to it as the
concluding degree of any melody in a mode. In Primaal’s music, the final serves as the ‘reference’
note.

5.4
Modality or tonality
There is ambiguity between modality and tonality, with debates on whether medieval and Renais-
sance polyphony is ‘modal’ or ‘tonal’. Powers (1981) calls the ‘modal system’ a ‘doctrine’, preferring
to look for ‘tonal relationships’, and questioning whether the concept of mode is real (Powers, 2013).
Powers (2001) notes that the near synonymity of ‘final’ and tonic is a cultural assumption rather than
an inherent connection. Mee`us (2013) challenges this, stating that church modes are diatonic, while
tonality requires a chromatic scale, but also recognises a ‘tonal center’ in Renaissance polyphony
(Mee`us, 2023). Atcherson (1973) argues that ‘key theory’ (tonality) is not a descendant of ‘modal
theory’, disputing that major and minor scales derive from church modes.
Schenkerian analysis (Schenker, 1935) proposes that harmonic progressions reduce to I/V/I struc-
tures, contrasting with the looped ‘shuttles’ of popular music (Tagg, 2009). Cœurdevey (1998, p. 82)
describes the shift from modal polyphony to tonal writing as a move from resultant to active har-
mony. To qualify as ‘tonal’, music must have directional harmonic progressions and active harmony.
Primaal’s music lacks both: its harmony is not directional and results from complex tones, often
from the bass. As a result, Primaal’s music may be considered modal rather than tonal.

Methods of analysis and analytical process
6.1
Analysis and ‘popular’ music
In this section, we address several issues identified in previous work on popular music analysis.
Wicke (2003) considers many of these concerns.
6.1.1
The ‘primary text’
Moore and Martin (2018, p. 1) contrast the ‘primary text’ (the sounds themselves) with the ‘sec-
ondary text’ (commentaries on the sounds). Moore and Martin argue that popular music analysis
has focused too much on the ‘secondary text’ and not enough on the ‘primary text’. According to
Keil (1966, p. 338), if analysis centres on the ‘primary text’, the analyst’s main duty is to explain the
syntax or grammatical rules of the musical system, as ‘[a]ll music has syntax or embodied meaning’.
In this article, we concentrate on the ‘primary text’ to understand the syntax and grammatical rules
related to pitch in Primaal’s music.
6.1.2
Harmony as a bias
Popular music analysis may be biased by the nature of music analysis itself. Brackett (2023, p. 19)
notes that the language of music analysis carries its own ideology, affecting the analysis results.
Middleton (1990, pp. 104–105) argues that ‘mainstream musicology’ from the 19th and 20th centuries
is often not helpful for popular music, as it has a rich vocabulary for areas like harmony and tonality,
but lacks vocabulary for pitch nuance and gradation outside the diatonic/chromatic system. In this
paper, we have focused on pitch nuance and gradation beyond this system, showing for example
that ‘dissonance’ and ‘resolution’ may be produced through non-harmonic means (Section 8.3.4).
6.1.3
The score as a bias
According to Middleton, a key obstacle to analysing the ‘primary text’ in popular music is the score.
Musicologists often adopt a ‘notation-centric’ approach, where the score is seen as ‘the music’, leading
to specific forms of listening that may not apply to all music (Middleton, 1990, p. 105). This bias
13


## Page 14


foregrounds musical parameters that are easily notated, while neglecting those that are not, such as
non-standard pitch and pitch movement (Middleton, 1990, pp. 104–105). Mellers (1974, p. 16) notes
that scores can be misleading, as they cannot capture improvised elements or pitch distortions that
are central to orally conceived music.
Many analysts still rely on Western-style transcriptions for popular music (Tagg, 1982; Everett,
1986; Middleton, 1990; Moore, 2016; Moore and Martin, 2018). In this study, we avoid a ‘notation-
centric’ approach, preferring signal analysis over scores to address ‘the volatility of sounds due to
the lack of a system of notational symbols’ (Aluas, 1996, p. 103) – see Section 6.2.3.
6.1.4
Pertinent pre-analytical approaches
According to Moore and Martin (2018, p. 10), one goal of the popular musicologist is to ‘elucidate
theoretical approaches pertinent to the music [...] This activity is best considered pre-analytical,
since any analysis must be based on theoretical preconceptions’. We have proposed that conjoint
critical listening with the music’s producers backed up by signal analysis (SM C) may be a suitable
‘pre-analytical’ theoretical approach. We have then performed analyses based on this approach.
6.1.5
The producers’ point of view
Everett (2000, p. 271) testifies that ‘[he]’ll demonstrate expressive manipulations of formal construc-
tion, vocal and instrumental colorings, rhythmic relationships, melodic devices, and tonal systems,
without once ever considering whether the composers, arrangers, artists, or engineers might have
been fully conscious of how [he] might hear what they were doing’. We want to avoid such a lack of
communication with those who produce the music. We hope that maintaining strong communication
with the producers addresses this issue.
6.2
Phenomenological reduction and music analysis
6.2.1
Husserl’s phenomenology
Husserl (1983, p. 33) defines the philosophical epoch´e as follows:
‘the philosophical `επoχ´η that we are undertaking shall consist of our completely abstain-
ing from any judgment regarding the doctrinal content of any previous philosophy and
effecting all of our demonstrations within the limits set by this abstention’.
Abstaining from judgement means that ‘[t]he positing is “put out of action”, parenthesized’ (Husserl,
1983, pp. 59–60)—original German: Einklammert, often translated as ‘bracketed’. The phenomeno-
logical epoch´e stands upon the act of bracketing, according to which the observers
‘exclude all sciences relating to this natural world no matter how firmly they stand there
for [them], no matter how much [they] admire them, no matter how little [they] think of
making even the least objection to them’ (Husserl, 1983, p. 61).
Exclusion is not refutation.
The process does not involve formulating a contrary judgment, it
‘shuts [the observer] off from any judgment’ (Husserl, 1983, p. 61). The bracketing process prompts
phenomenological reduction (Husserl, 1983, p. 66).

6.2.2
Phenomenology and music analysis
In music analysis, phenomenological epoch´e involves making naive judgments based on face value
and disregarding everyday knowledge (Serhan, 2022, p. 3). Serhan highlights Schaeffer’s reduced
listening (´ecoute r´eduite) as an application of phenomenology. This involves listening to sounds
purely as sound objects, removing their source and meaning (Chion, 1983, p. 33).
A key difficulty in applying phenomenology to music analysis is the existence of the score (Sec-
tion 6.1.3). Benson (2011) notes that in Ingarden’s (1986) ontology of the musical work, the score
often eclipses the composition, despite acknowledging that the score is an incomplete guide for per-
formance. According to Benson, this problem is intrinsic to notation. Even a superior notational
system would not resolve the indeterminacy.
6.2.3
Phenomenological reduction as the first step of the analysis
At the start of our analysis, we choose to have minimal information about the music. We have the
stems, with the producers noting the importance of some (e.g., bass) over others. In phenomenologi-
cal reduction, we bracket out cultural preconceptions. We do not know if pitch arises from harmonic
tones, if pitches are stable enough to form notes, if their distribution forms a scale, or if simultaneous
pitches form known chords.
Ferrara (1984, p. 359) describes open listening as the first step of phenomenological music anal-
ysis. Our first step is open listening combined with signal analysis, with no scores to bracket out.
Signal analysis addresses ‘the volatility of sounds due to the lack of a system of notational symbols’
(Aluas, 1996, p. 103), paralleling early Western attempts at notation.
Typically, signal analysis of Primaal’s music provides the following observations.
A spectral
transform of a stem reveals partials forming an auditory stream (Bregman, 1994), though their
relations are non-harmonic. We often hear multiple pitches from a single set of partials, which do
not correspond to the lowest partial. Frequency values are rarely stable, and frequency distributions
across tracks rarely coincide, even when perceived pitches are similar.
Such observations then lead to what Ferrara (1984, p. 359) describes as ‘syntactical’ listening—
the next step of phenomenological analysis (Section 6.1.1).
6.3
‘Pitch’, as understood by the machine learning and music information
retrieval (MIR) community
6.3.1
MIDI files and piano rolls as reification of the score
The reification of the score, as noted by Middleton (1990, pp. 104–105) and discussed in Section 6.1.3,
influences methods in the MIR community. Briot et al. (2020, sec. 4.2) argue that the essence of
music lies in the compositional process, revealed through symbolic representations like scores or lead
sheets. A common MIR task is audio-to-MIDI alignment (Raffel and Ellis, 2016), where MIDI files
act as symbolic ‘versions’ of the pitched content (Raffel, 2016; Benetos et al., 2018).
However, in terms of lossy compression, transcribing Primaal’s music to MIDI results in signifi-
cant loss, making the algorithm inadequate. The original content cannot be faithfully reproduced.
As the producers note, ‘if you play this music on a piano, it will be boring and will not sound like
anything’.
6.3.2
AI song contest and Italian secular monodies with basso continuo
Ashworth and O’Dette (2012) define basso continuo, used between 1600 and 1750, as a bass line with

specified or implied harmonies. Giulio Caccini’s Nuove Musiche (1602) helped codify this practice,
widely applied in Italian secular monodies (Caccini, 1602; Williams and Ledbetter, 2001).
The AI song contest (AI song contest, 2024) promotes co-creative composition, where musicians
use AI to blend human and machine creativity. The contest’s focus on melody, harmony, and bass
(Huang et al., 2020) parallels 17th-century monodies with basso continuo (D´eguernel et al., 2022).
However, Primaal’s music diverges from this approach, which may not represent how contemporary
popular music is conceived.
This modular view of popular music may derive from the tradition of teaching composition
via figured bass theory. As Williams and Ledbetter (2001) observe, continuo was fundamental in
the 17th and 18th centuries, forming the basis for composition teaching and remaining relevant for
theoretical analysis into the 19th and 20th centuries.
6.3.3
The harmonic complex tone as the sole origin for the impression of pitch
According to Oxenham (2012), the most common pitch-evoking sound is a harmonic complex tone,
where two tones share the same pitch if they have the same f0. This view is widely adopted in the
MIR and machine learning communities. Pitch detection, or ‘pitch tracking’, is often framed as an
f0 estimation task (Drugman et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2018). Neural network approaches assume the
signal is ‘periodic’ or ‘quasiperiodic’, relying on harmonic audio data for training (Riou et al., 2023).
Such assumptions underpin other tasks in music information retrieval, like harmonic-percussive
source separation (Ono et al., 2008), automatic music transcription (Ewert et al., 2014), and multi-
pitch detection (Christensen et al., 2008). Inharmonic models (Vincent et al., 2008) are rarely used.
Equating ‘pitch’ with ‘f0 of harmonic complex tones’ contrasts with psychoacoustic studies of
inharmonicity (Section 4.2.1) and with our analyses of Primaal’s music, where most instruments are
inharmonic, and pitch is often carried by one or more overtones.
6.4
Analytical process
Our analysis procedure consisted of five steps:
1. Song selection
The songs, produced in Avid Pro Tools, were exported as stems. Song choice
was based on stem availability.
2. Critical listening and signal analysis
We listened to the stems using phenomenological reduc-
tion (Section 6.2.3) and selected sections for signal analysis with the producers, focusing on
the ‘bass’ tracks, key to this music.
3. Interviews
We conducted a six-day interview with the producers, using the analysis results
to discuss the techniques used to manipulate pitch in each song.
4. Further analysis
Additional signal analyses were performed to explore observations and hy-
potheses raised during the interviews.
5. Validation
The producers reviewed and verified the conclusions.
Signal analyses in this study incorporate equal-loudness-contour weighting, recognizing that hu-
mans are not equally sensitive to all frequencies (Fletcher and Munson, 1933). Various models exist
that weight the power spectrum to reflect perception (Fletcher and Munson 1933; Robinson and
Dadson 1956; Skovenborg and Nielsen 2004, p. 7). One such model is ISO226:2023 (ISO, 2023). We
apply the ISO226:2023 50-phon equal-loudness contour before spectral analysis to produce results
that better reflect what listeners actually hear.

6.6
Structure: background
Sections 8.6 and 8.7 describe the large- and small-scale structures in Primaal’s music. Deruty et al.
(2013, p. 13) summarise the distinction between the two time scales.
The large-scale structure, following Bimbot et al. (2012), is the ‘semiotic structure’, or ‘sectional
form’, which describes long-term regularities between musical parts.
Segments at this scale are
‘autonomous and comparable blocks’ (Bimbot et al., 2010), with a typical duration of around 11.5s
(Deruty et al., 2013). In Western classical music, such segments have been referred to as ‘periods’
(Monelle, 2014, pp. 149–150) and classified as ‘sentences’ and ‘periods’ (Schoenberg, 1967; Caplin,
1998). Section 8.6 provides examples of Primaal’s large-scale structure.
The small-scale structure refers to the internal organisation of segments, also known as ‘form’
(Caplin, 1998), or the ‘morpho-syntagmatic level’ (Bimbot et al., 2014). Functions at this level
include contrast (Bimbot et al., 2016) and antecedent and consequent (Caplin, 1998). Section 8.7
offers examples of small-scale structure and determinants of form in Primaal’s music.

Distortion and waveshaping

7.2.1
Distortion and waveshaping generate partials
Distortion and waveshaping apply a non-linear transformation to the signal, causing intermodulation
distortion (Newell, 2017, p. 464). This distortion produces partials from both input partials and their
combinations. If fa and fb are the input frequencies, the output contains frequencies kafa + kbfb,
where (ka, kb) ∈Z2. The generated partials include both lower and higher frequencies than the
input.
7.2.2
Interaction between original and generated partials
Primaal’s preferred distortion plug-in is SoundToys’ Decapitator. Primaal’s favourite settings result
in equal energy between generated harmonics and original inharmonic partials (SM D). In this
case, distortion emulates the ‘Unison’ process (Section 7.4), combining close frequency partials and
modifying the sound’s envelope using the resulting acoustic beats (Section 5.1).
7.2.3
SansAmp PSA-1
Primaal also use the SansAmp PSA-1 plug-in.
SansAmp provides more radical processing than
Decapitator (SM C.1). The generated partials can evolve in the opposite direction to the original
signal, resembling frequency folding during aliasing (Park and Hazra, 1993). The producers note
that these partials help mask pitches and reinforce pitch ambiguity.
7.3
Ring modulation
The producers use Omnisphere’s Ring Modulation9 to boost or create partials. Unlike intermodula-
tion distortion, which generates many partials (kafa + kbfb), ring modulation with the same input
frequencies only produces fa + fb and fa −fb (Parker, 2011). This makes ring modulation more
controllable, allowing producers to target specific partials.
7.4

In Western classical music, ‘unison’ is defined as the simultaneous execution of one part by multiple
performers at the same pitch or an octave apart (Rushton, 2001). The unison audio effect, originally
found in analogue synthesisers, simulates this by superimposing slightly detuned versions of the
original part, creating a ‘thicker’ sound (Daily Analog, 2024). Roederer (2008, p. 34) notes that
superposing two tones livens up the sound. One effect of the unison audio process is the emergence
of acoustic beats (see Section 5.1.1 and SM E). The producers extensively use Omnisphere’s Unison
effect.10

The producers also highlight other intervals. In the ‘Boom’ bass (C.1), they emphasise the 11th
harmonic, which is 49 cents less than a tritone. This harmonic was selected to give the impression
of a mistake resulting in a dissonant interval.
In the upper bass track from ‘Danger’ (C.7.2), one audible partial is a low D, corresponding
to the C♯4 + 16 cent partial, near the position of the 7th harmonic (minor seventh). The tone is
inharmonic and slightly lower than the 7th harmonic.
8.2.3
Consecutive partials
The producers may highlight consecutive partials instead of a single one. In ‘¡Fire!’ (C.10), with
a very low f0 (ca. 37Hz), gain is applied to groups of partials around the 36th harmonic, resulting
in an uncertain pitch around 1320Hz. The complex tone from the first partial (f0) can be seen as
a carrier, with modulation applied via selective gain. A similar approach is used in the ‘Cardinal’
synth break (C.6), where groups of partials are highlighted with a filter’s sliding resonance.
8.2.4
Several perceived pitches: amplified partials or inharmonicity?
Listening test 1 (SM A.1) shows that an inharmonic tone can lead to the perception of more
pitches than a harmonic tone. Listening test 3 (SM A.3) shows how tones in Primaal’s music
result in multiple perceived pitches. Does this perception arise from inharmonicity or from boosted
energy in the upper partials?
We argue that it generally stems from increased energy in upper partials. The producers boost
upper partials to make them audible. The examples in SM C are less inharmonic than the tone in
Listening test 1. In many cases, the f0 is very low, and both the f0 and the fundamental of the
least-deviating harmonic series (Rasch and Plomp, 1982, p. 9) may not be heard on most listening
systems.

8.3
Continuous frequency distributions
As shown in Figure 1 (Section 4.1.1), in Primaal’s music, frequency values that evoke pitch are
continuously distributed around ‘poles’. This can result from (1) continuous frequency evolution
within a single track, or (2) small variations in discrete frequencies occurring simultaneously across
different tracks.
The two aspects are interlaced, as frequency evolutions in different tracks are
generally not parallel.
8.3.1
Continuous frequency values: the Roland TR-808 bass as a model
A key method for producing continuous frequency values in Primaal’s music is the use of the TR-808
bass drum. The producers state that the TR-808 is crucial in urban music, reflecting its influence
on Primaal’s sound (Section 7.1).
The TR-808’s f0 drops by a semitone before stabilizsng (Deruty, 2024), as seen in the bass tracks
of ‘Elevate’ (C.8) and ‘Silver’ (C.12). For longer bass notes, pitch falls through larger intervals, as
in ‘Boom’ (C.1), where it drops half an octave.
This transient-like pitch drop is also used in non-percussive tracks, such as the stems ‘square
synth’ in ‘R U ready’ (0’26-0’40), ‘sampled vocals’ in ‘Elevate’ (2’22-2’36), ‘vocals’ in ‘Silver’ (0’08-
0’40), and ‘synths’ in ‘Yada Yada’ (0’01-0’14).
8.3.2
Continuous frequency values: bends, glides, and screws
Continuous frequency changes may be achieved using bends, glides, or screws (Section 7.5). These
processes are used to confuse pitch perception. To increase this confusion, the producers often set
random pitch bend limits to avoid harmonic intervals like the octave or fifth.
The producers may adjust glide time to the length of the musical element. In the bass tracks
from ‘Elevate’ and ‘Silver’ (C.8, C.12), they use the time between onsets to let the glide fully develop.
This contrasts with tracks like ‘Danger’, where the glide quickly stabilises (C.7). When possible,
they prefer pitch contours that never fully stabilise to enhance pitch ambiguity.
8.3.3
Examples of continuous frequency distribution stemming from continuous fre-
quencies over time
Figure 9 compares f0 distributions in two tracks: the vocal loop in ‘Cardinal’ (C.5) and the flute
loop in ‘Yada Yada’ (C.16), providing real examples of the schematic distributions in Figure 1. The
top distribution is wider than the bottom one, and the producers confirmed they intentionally set
these widths.
Regarding the ‘Yada Yada’ flute, the producers edited the original sample to keep only content
near each attack, making the pitches less stable. However, the overall result remained relatively
stable, which the producers felt made it too easy for listeners to identify the pitch. To address this,
they contrasted this stable part with wide glides, using the width of the distribution as a musical
parameter.
In Primaal’s music, frequency distributions exist around a ‘pole’, and deviations from this ‘pole’
contribute to the music’s interest.
To help listeners identify the ‘pole’, the producers introduce
anchors. An example is the vocal gimmick in ‘Boom’, where the second ‘note’ of the loop serves
as a reference for the ‘pole’ (C.2). However, the ‘pole’ in one track may not match the tuning of
others, as tuning is generally specific to each track. The next section explores the relation between
frequency values across tracks.

‘Boom’ features a kick drum, bass, and looped vocal gimmick. The f0 sequences for these tracks

dissonance, with the bass descending towards the kick drum’s pitch (modulo the octave).
8.3.5
Purpose of a continuous frequency space
The producers use continuous frequencies and distributions to achieve several goals. They describe
Primaal’s music as ‘mono-chordal’, where controlled shifts from ‘poles’ bring ‘perceptual richness’.
Chord progressions would mask such shifts (their terms). For example, in ‘Boom’ (C.2), the pro-
ducers explain that the vocal track ‘hovers around stable pitches’, keeping the listener wondering,
‘when will this tension be resolved’? Yet, the tension is never resolved, keeping listeners engaged.
Another goal is to emphasise rhythm over pitch. During glides, pitches are hard to pinpoint, so
listeners turn to rhythm to find something familiar.
The TR-808 serves as a model for frequency evolution, where partials’ frequencies decrease quickly
and then stabilise, creating a ‘tension-release’ effect that holds interest.
The producers use different tuning patterns for each voice to make them stand out, citing the
‘Silver’ brass theme as an example (‘D D D E♭D’, 0’02, 0’25, 0’33, 0’59, 1’07), where a higher tuning
was chosen so the part stands out.

Modal organisation in Primaal’s music
8.4.1
Constitution of the mode
According to the producers, all pitches in each song originate from a single chosen final. Pole pitches
are then added through the processes shown in Figure 1. The added ‘notes’ may have the following
relationships with the final:
1. They may come from a scale where the final is the first degree.
2. They may be partials of the tone for which the final is the f0.
3. They may be partials of a tone where the final is a result of temporal modeling.
All ‘note’ choices are influenced by overtones, directly (cases 2 and 3) or indirectly (case 1). The
determination of the scale relates to homoph¯onoi intervals (Richter, 2001). Figure 10 isolates these
processes. Relation (1) links arbitrary pitches to harmonic overtones, similar to Western classical
music. Relations (2) and (3) directly derive from upper partials (Section 4.3).

8.4.2
A constraint on the final
Sections 4.2.2, 4.3.1, 8.5, and listening test 6 (SM A.6) discuss how first partials with low f0

they use finals near these notes, including C♯1 in ‘R U Ready’ (C.11), E1 in ‘Danger’ (C.7), D1 in

8.4.3
One or several ‘reference’ notes
Mee`us (2023) uses the term ‘reference’ or ‘nominal’ note, which may not always be the mode’s final.
In some Primaal songs, the final is the sole ‘reference’ note, while in others, the fifth and sometimes
the third (major or minor) also play a key role. The salient notes for each studied song are: ‘Boom’:
B and F♯; ‘Cardinal’: D minor triad; ‘Danger’: B♭; ‘Elevate’: E♭; ‘¡Fire!’: difficult to identify; ‘R U
Ready’: C minor triad; ‘Silver’: D and A; ‘Sweet Money’: E♭; ‘Whomp’: F major triad; and ‘Yada
Yada’: F and C. As noted in Section 8.3.5, the presence of a salient triad in some songs leads the
producers to describe these songs as ‘mono-chordal’. However, as argued in Section 5.4, the presence
of a chord does not render the songs tonal.
8.4.4
Purpose of modality
In Section 8.3.5, we explain that the absence of chord progressions allows the derivation of musical
parameters from continuous frequency distributions. Tonality would require chord changes, so the
Primaal producers prefer a modal organisation. Beyond continuous frequency distributions, they
also focus on highlighting rhythmic patterns and the precise editing of elements to fit short-term
musical structures (Section 8.7).
This approach is common in hip-hop/R&B. Many hip-hop tracks are based on a single mode
with one final or tonic (Section 5.4). The producers aim for music that ‘hovers around one note’ to
emphasise other musical elements. They reference Jamie xx’s ‘Gosh’ (Jamie xx, 2016) as an example.
Other examples include Massive Attack’s ‘Angel’ (Massive Attack, 1998) and Pop Smoke’s ‘Dior’
(Pop Smoke, 1998).
8.4.5
Exceptions to modality
In Section 8.6, we illustrate how one semiotic segment may differ drastically from others, creating
long-term structure. In Section 8.7, we show how ‘contrasts’ contradict implications, creating struc-
ture at short time scales. In both cases, an element can either take on exceptional values within a
dimension or derive from a different system. Sections 8.6.3 and 8.7.2 provide examples of tonality
and cadences being used for such structural patterns.

Summary and conclusion
Primaal is a project under the Hyper Music production company, a branch of Neodrome Enter-
tainment. Currently, Primaal’s music is primarily sold to advertisement companies for use in ads
for global corporations. Influences on Primaal’s music come from urban and electronic genres (Sec-
tion 2).
9.1
Summary of observations
We critically listened to Primaal’s music with the producers (Section 6.4), applying phenomenological
reduction to bracket out musical education (Section 6.2). We examined the production process and
the technologies (Section 7) and analysed representative excerpts (SM C), focusing on pitch.
Our conclusion is that pitch manipulation in this music differs significantly from its use in Western
classical music, specifically the 1650–1875 period. In particular, a central principle in Primaal’s
music is cultivating pitch uncertainty (Section 4). As confirmed by listening test 3 (SM A.3),
resulting from deliberate production techniques, the number of pitches at a given moment is generally
uncertain.
We suggested that Primaal’s music is primarily modal (Sections 4.1.1 and 8.4), with scale de-
grees defined as distributions rather than discrete values. The distributions stem from continuous
frequency evolution over time and the superimposition of slightly different frequencies at a given
moment (Sections 4.1.1 and 8.3).
Most tones in Primaal’s music are inharmonic, sometimes highly so (Sections 4.2 and 8.1).
The frequency derived from temporal modeling is generally different from the f0. The producers
deliberately use inharmonicity to increase pitch uncertainty.
The producers derive pitches from the upper partials of bass tracks (Sections 4.3 and 8.2), pre-
venting pitches from being organised around equal temperament scales. The first partial is often
inaudible (Section 4.2.2), making the overtones even more significant.
Drums are tuned, even though their pitch content is uncertain (Sections 4.4 and 8.5).
The
producers deliberately use this pitch strength continuum as a musical parameter.
9.2
Historical perspective
While Primaal’s manipulation of pitch has little in common with late Baroque and Viennese classical
music, it may relate more closely to earlier Western music concerns. Several aspects of Primaal’s
production support this: their use of homoph¯onoi intervals (Ptolemy, 2nd century, Section 8.4); their
interpretation of mode resembles 11th-century definitions (Section 5.3); and their use of combination
tones and acoustic beating echoes 16th and Renaissance music theory (Sections 5.1.1 and 5.1.2).
Despite differing from Western composers of the Baroque and classical eras (1650–1875), Pri-
maal’s techniques are similar to some 20th-century ‘art’ music. For example, Maurice Ravel blurred
timbre and pitch in Bol´ero (1928) by arranging a melody in parallel thirds, sixths, and fifths, pro-
ducing rich timbral effects, similar to Primaal’s boosting of the fifth harmonic (Section 8.2.1).
Further examples of continuous pitch manipulation in 20th-century music include Ligeti’s At-
mosph`eres (1961), Penderecki’s Threnody (1961), and Stockhausen’s use of ring modulation (Sec-
tion 7.3) in Mixtur (1964), Mantra (1970), and Mikrophonie II (1965). The Primaal producers
acknowledge the influence of Ravel and Stockhausen in their work.

9.3
Summary of contributions
We believe the work presented here contributes to music analysis, music information retrieval, com-
putational creativity, music generation, and psychoacoustics.
Contributions to music analysis include the following.
• We demonstrate the potential of phenomenological reduction in popular music analysis. Ig-
noring score notation highlights under-documented musical dimensions related to pitch.
• This helps us better understand contemporary popular music: there is more to pitch than
musical notes. Figure 1 (Section 4.1.1) outlines dimensions beyond notes.
• Boosting partials to make them audible underlines a timbre–pitch continuum (Section 4.3.1),
bridging the gap between harmony-focused and production-focused literature.
• Our approach challenges the reification of the score (Section 6.1.3). Instead of performing
music analysis from scores, we suggest starting from the production process, the signal, and
what the listener may actually hear.
Contributions to music information retrieval (MIR) include the following.
• We show that one complex tone does not always result in one pitch (Sections 6.3.3 and 4.1.2).
• Pitch tracking should estimate the probability of heard pitches, aligning more with psychoa-
coustics, rather than attempting to reverse-engineer the ‘notes’ that were played in order to
produce the sounds.
• Continuous pitch distributions suggest that representing pitch in terms of discrete notes may
be generally inappropriate for popular music (Section 6.3).
Contributions to computational creativity and music generation include the following.
• Expressive variations in pitch and timbre are crucial for music generation—not just tempo,
timing, and dynamics.
• Improved descriptions of contemporary music, accounting for pitch-manipulation techniques,
could enhance text-to-music interfaces like Google’s MusicLM.
• Understanding pitch manipulation may open new avenues for practical music generation ap-
plications such as the A.I. song contest.
Finally, the contributions of this paper to psychoacoustics include the following.
• Instead of debating whether partials can be heard individually, we propose that partials may
be audible if loud enough and suggest studying audibility thresholds.
• Conditions for hearing individual partials may occur more commonly than was previously
thought (Sections 7.1, 7.2, and 7.5).
• Results from listening tests 2 and 3 (SM A.2 and A.3) suggest probabilistic pitch models
may better suit popular music. Assuming that the ‘number’ of simultaneous pitches is always
a positive integer may lead to an over-simplified representation of what is heard.
• Inharmonicity in Primaal’s music (Sections 4.2 and 8.1) may provide a new perspective on the
assumption that ‘[t]he most commonly considered form of pitch-evoking sound is a harmonic
complex tone’ (Oxenham, 2012) – see Section 6.3.3.
• TR-808 basses, where the first partial is often inaudible, challenge the assumption that tones
share the same pitch if they share the same f0 (Section 4.2.2).
