Read disturbance in modern DRAM chips is a widespread
weakness that is used for breaking memory isolation, one of
the fundamental building blocks of system security and privacy.
RowHammer is a prime example of read disturbance in DRAM
where repeatedly accessing (hammering) a row of DRAM cells
(DRAM row) induces bitflips in physically nearby DRAM rows
(victim rows). Unfortunately, shrinking technology node size
exacerbates RowHammer and as such, significantly fewer ac-
cesses can induce bitflips in newer DRAM chip generations.
To ensure robust DRAM operation, state-of-the-art mitigation
mechanisms restore the charge in potential victim rows (i.e.,
they perform preventive refresh or charge restoration). With
newer DRAM chip generations, these mechanisms perform pre-
ventive refresh more aggressively and cause larger performance,
energy, or area overheads. Therefore, it is essential to develop
a better understanding and in-depth insights into the preventive
refresh to secure real DRAM chips at low cost.
In this paper, our goal is to mitigate RowHammer at low cost
by understanding the preventive refresh latency and the impact
of reduced refresh latency on RowHammer. To this end, we
present the first rigorous experimental study on the interactions
between refresh latency and RowHammer characteristics in
real DRAM chips. Our experimental characterization using
388 real DDR4 DRAM chips from three major manufacturers
demonstrates that a preventive refresh latency can be signifi-
cantly reduced (by 64%) at the expense of requiring slightly
more (by 0.54%) preventive refreshes. To investigate the impact
of reduced preventive refresh latency on system performance
and energy efficiency, we reduce the preventive refresh latency
and adjust the aggressiveness of existing RowHammer solutions
by developing a new mechanism, Partial Charge Restoration
for Aggressive Mitigation (PaCRAM). Our results show that
by reducing the preventive refresh latency, PaCRAM reduces
the performance and energy overheads induced by five state-
of-the-art RowHammer mitigation mechanisms with small ad-
ditional area overhead. Thus, PaCRAM introduces a novel
perspective into addressing RowHammer vulnerability at low
cost by leveraging our experimental observations. To aid fu-
ture research, we open-source our PaCRAM implementation at
https://github.com/CMU-SAFARI/PaCRAM.
1. Introduction
To ensure system robustness (i.e., reliability, security, and
safety), it is critical to maintain memory isolation: accessing
a memory address should not cause unintended side-effects
on data stored on other addresses. Unfortunately, with aggres-
sive technology node scaling, dynamic random access memory
(DRAM) [1], the prevalent main memory technology, suffers
from increased read disturbance: accessing (reading) a DRAM
cell degrades the data integrity of other physically close and un-
accessed DRAM cells [2–6]. This is because reading a DRAM
cell causes physically nearby DRAM cells to lose their charge
(i.e., charge leakage) and exhibit bitflips. RowHammer is a
prime example of such DRAM read disturbance phenomena
where a row of DRAM cells (i.e., a DRAM row) can experi-
ence bitflips when another physically nearby DRAM row (i.e.,
aggressor row) is repeatedly opened (i.e., hammered) [2,4–70].
RowPress is another example of DRAM read disturbance phe-
nomenon where a DRAM row can experience bitflips when
another physically nearby DRAM row is kept open for a long
time period (i.e., pressed) [71,72]. Prior work [73] shows that
combining RowHammer and RowPress significantly exacer-
bates disturbance on a victim row and significantly reduces the
number and duration of row activations needed to induce the
first read disturbance bitflip.
Many prior works demonstrate security attacks on a wide
range of systems that exploit read disturbance to escalate privi-
lege, leak private data, and manipulate critical application out-
puts [2,4,6–57,61–64,71–95]. To make matters worse, recent
experimental studies [2,4–6,38,48,51,56–71,82,90,91,94–108]
have found that newer DRAM chip generations are more suscep-
tible to read disturbance. For example, DRAM chips manufac-
tured in 2018-2020 can experience RowHammer bitflips after
an order of magnitude fewer row activations compared to the
chips manufactured in 2012-2013 [63]. Recent studies [90,91]
also show that modern HBM2 DRAM chips are as vulnera-
ble to both RowHammer and RowPress as modern DDR4 and
LPDDR4 DRAM chips.
To ensure robust DRAM operation, several prior works [2,
38, 44, 80, 109–136] propose to perform an operation called
preventive refresh. Preventive refresh restores the electrical
charge stored in potential victim cells (i.e., performs charge
restoration). This operation is performed before the victim row
gets disturbed significantly enough to experience bitflips due
to RowHammer or RowPress. For precise timing of preventive
refreshes, these works define a threshold called RowHammer
threshold, which represents the minimum hammer count needed
to induce the first bitflip in a victim row. Existing RowHam-
mer mitigation mechanisms can also prevent RowPress bitflips
when they are configured to be more aggressive, which is prac-
tically equivalent to configuring them for smaller RowHammer
thresholds [71–73].
Charge restoration of a DRAM cell has a non-negligible la-
tency, denoted by the (LP)DDRx timing parameter tRAS, which
is typically 32−35ns [137–144]. During charge restoration of
cells in a DRAM row, the DRAM bank that contains the row
becomes unavailable, and thus refreshing a DRAM row can
delay memory accesses and induce performance overheads. As
read disturbance in DRAM chips worsens, RowHammer mitiga-
tion mechanisms face a trade-off between system performance
and area overhead. Some solutions rely on more aggressive

To mitigate read disturbance at both low performance and

48, 51, 56–71, 82, 90, 91, 94–108] study the characteristics of

though charge restoration and read disturbance both greatly
affect charge leakage in DRAM cells, no prior work rigorously

this interaction on RowHammer mitigation mechanisms and

Our goal is to enable RowHammer mitigation at both low
performance and area overheads by understanding RowHam-
mer under reduced refresh latency in real DRAM chips. To
this end, we present i) the first rigorous experimental study on
the interactions between refresh latency and read disturbance
characteristics of real modern DRAM chips and ii) demonstrate
the potential benefits of our empirical observations by develop-
ing a mechanism, Partial Charge Restoration for Aggressive
Mitigation (PaCRAM). The key idea of PaCRAM is to reliably
reduce the latency of the preventive refreshes in both high-
performance-overhead and high-area-overhead RowHammer
mitigation mechanisms, thereby improving system performance
and energy efficiency. By doing so, PaCRAM introduces a
novel perspective into addressing the RowHammer vulnerabil-
ity while incurring small additional area overhead over existing
RowHammer mitigation mechanisms.
Experimental characterization. We present the first rigorous
experimental characterization study that examines the effects
of refresh latency on RowHammer vulnerability, on 388 real
DDR4 DRAM chips. Our experimental characterization demon-
strates that the latency of preventive refreshes can be safely
reduced with either no change or a relatively small reduction in
the RowHammer threshold, and such reduced refresh latency
does not further exacerbate RowHammer vulnerability beyond
the initial degradation, even after thousands of preventive re-
freshes. Therefore, the latency of a vast majority of preventive
refreshes can be reduced reliably without jeopardizing the data
integrity of a DRAM chip.
Potential benefits of reduced refresh latency. We showcase
the benefits of our empirical observations by developing a mech-
anism, Partial Charge Restoration for Aggressive Mitigation
(PaCRAM). PaCRAM is implemented in the memory controller
together with existing RowHammer mitigation mechanisms.
PaCRAM carefully and reliably reduces the latency of preven-
tive refreshes, restoring the charge of the potential victim cells
partially (i.e., partial charge restoration). To ensure secure oper-
ation, PaCRAM adjusts the aggressiveness of a RowHammer
mitigation mechanism (i.e., configures the existing mechanism
with the reduced RowHammer threshold) as some DRAM chips
exhibit an increased vulnerability to RowHammer when they are
refreshed using reduced refresh latency. To configure PaCRAM,
we use our experimental data (i.e., reduced charge restoration
latency, RowHammer threshold) from real DRAM characteriza-
tion. We demonstrate that PaCRAM significantly increases the
system performance (energy efficiency) by reducing the over-
heads induced by five state-of-the-art RowHammer mitigation
mechanisms, PARA [2], RFM [141], PRAC [144], Hydra [124],
and Graphene [113] by 18.95% (14.59%), 12.28% (11.56%),
2.07% (1.15%), 2.56% (2.18%), and 5.37% (4.50%) on aver-
age across 62 workloads while introducing small additional
area overhead in the memory controller (0.09% of the area of a
high-end Intel Xeon processor [146]).
We make the following contributions:
• We present the first rigorous characterization of the effects of
reduced refresh latency on RowHammer vulnerability in real
DRAM chips. Our experimental results on 388 real DRAM
chips from three major manufacturers show that latency of a
vast majority of preventive refreshes can be reduced reliably.
• To demonstrate the potential benefits of our experimental
observations, we propose Partial Charge Restoration for Ag-
gressive Mitigation (PaCRAM), a new mechanism that reli-
ably reduces the latency of preventive refreshes performed
by RowHammer mitigation mechanisms, and accordingly
adjusts the aggressiveness of RowHammer mitigation mecha-
nisms.
• We showcase PaCRAM’s effectiveness by evaluating its im-
pact using five state-of-the-art RowHammer mitigation mech-
anisms. Our results show that PaCRAM significantly reduces
their performance and energy overheads while introducing
small additional area overhead in the memory controller.
2. Background
2.1. DRAM Organization and Operation
DRAM organization. Fig. 1 shows the hierarchical organiza-
tion of a modern DRAM-based main memory. The memory
controller connects to a DRAM module over a memory channel.
A module contains one or multiple DRAM ranks that time-share
the memory channel. A rank consists of multiple DRAM chips.
Each DRAM chip has multiple DRAM banks each of which
contains multiple subarrays. A DRAM bank is organized as a
two-dimensional array of DRAM cells, where a row of cells
is called a DRAM row. A DRAM cell consists of i) a storage
capacitor, which stores one bit of information in the form of
electrical charge, and ii) an access transistor.

DRAM refresh. DRAM is a volatile memory technology: a
DRAM cell leaks charge over time and can lose its data (i.e.,
charge leakage). If charge leakage exceeds a level, the data
of the cell cannot be sensed by the sense amplifier correctly.
These errors are known as data retention failures. To prevent
these failures, the memory controller issues REF commands
to refresh DRAM cells. A REF command internally activates
DRAM rows to sense and restore the charge levels of the cells;
which takes around 32−35ns [137–144]. A single refresh can
be thought of as opening (i.e., activating) a row and closing
(i.e., precharging) the bank, as shown in Fig. 2. To maintain
data integrity, the memory controller periodically refreshes each
row in a time interval called refresh window (tREFW) (32ms for
DDR5 [141] and 64ms for DDR4 [140]). To ensure all rows are
refreshed every tREFW, the memory controller issues REF com-
mands with a time interval called refresh interval (tREFI) (3.9µs
for DDR5 [141] and 7.8µs for DDR4 [140]). Each REF com-
mand blocks the whole DRAM bank or rank for a time window
called refresh latency (tRFC). tRFC is a function of the charge
restoration latency and the number of rows refreshed with a
REF command. tRFC is 195ns and 350ns for 8Gb DDR5 [141]
and 8Gb DDR4 [140] DRAM chips, respectively.
2.2. DRAM Read Disturbance
As DRAM manufacturing technology node size scales down,
DRAM cell sizes and cell-to-cell distances shrink. As a re-
sult, accessing a DRAM cell can more easily disturb data
stored in another physically nearby cell. This phenomenon
is called read disturbance. Two prime examples of read dis-
turbance in modern DRAM chips are RowHammer [2] and
RowPress [71], where repeatedly accessing (hammering) or
keeping active (pressing) a DRAM row induces bitflips in phys-
ically nearby DRAM rows, respectively. In RowHammer and
RowPress terminology, the row that is hammered or pressed is
called the aggressor row, and the row that experiences bitflips
is the victim row. For read disturbance bitflips to occur, the ag-
gressor row needs to be activated more than a certain threshold
value, defined as RowHammer threshold (NRH). Several charac-
terization studies [2,4–6,38,48,51,56–71,82,90,91,94–108]
show that as DRAM technology continues to scale to smaller
nodes, DRAM chips are becoming increasingly vulnerable to
RowHammer (i.e., newer chips have lower NRH values).1 Re-
cent studies [90, 91] also show that modern HBM2 DRAM
chips are as vulnerable to both RowHammer and RowPress as
modern DDR4 and LPDDR4 DRAM chips. To make matters
worse, NRH reduces significantly by crafting access patterns
that exploit both RowHammer and RowPress [71].
DRAM read disturbance mitigation mechanisms. Many
prior works propose mitigation mechanisms [2,5,26,32,38,44,
59,80,84,89,97,98,100,109,111–136,140,145,147–190] to
protect DRAM chips against RowHammer bitflips, leveraging
different approaches. These mechanisms usually perform two
main tasks: i) execute a trigger algorithm and ii) perform a
preventive action. The trigger algorithm observes the memory
access patterns and triggers a preventive action based on the re-
sult of a probabilistic or a deterministic process. The preventive
action is usually one of i) refreshing victim rows (i.e., preven-
tive refresh), ii) dynamically remapping aggressor rows, and
iii) throttling unsafe accesses. The DDR5 standard introduces a
new command called refresh management (RFM) [141] to pro-
tect DRAM chips against RowHammer attacks. The memory
controller tracks the activation count of each DRAM bank and
issues an RFM command when the count exceeds a threshold
value. When the DRAM module receives an RFM command, it
internally takes preventive actions within the given time window.
RFM tracks the row activation counts at bank granularity rather
than row granularity. Therefore, RFM commands are issued
based on the total activation count across thousands of rows in a
bank with no notion of the row-level temporal locality of those
activations. As such, the memory controller can trigger an ex-
cessive number of RFM commands, causing high performance
overheads [133]. To reduce the number of RFM commands, the
latest DDR5 standard introduces a new mechanism called Per
Row Activation Counting (PRAC) [144]. PRAC enables fine-
grained tracking by implementing per-row activation counters
inside the DRAM chip. When a row’s counter exceeds a thresh-
old value, the DRAM chip requests an RFM command from the
memory controller with a new back-off signal. Once the mem-
ory controller receives this signal, it issues an RFM command
and allows the DRAM chip to take preventive actions. Existing
RowHammer mitigations can also be used to prevent RowPress
bitflips when their trigger algorithms are configured to be more
aggressive, which is practically equivalent to configuring them
for sub-1K NRH values [71–73].
RowHammer mitigation mechanisms face an important trade-
off between two sets of metrics: i) system performance (and
energy efficiency) and ii) area cost. We categorize RowHammer
mitigation mechanisms depending on their dominant overheads,
i) high-performance-overhead mitigations and ii) high-area-
overhead mitigations. High-performance-overhead mitigations

Preventive refresh overheads of RowHammer mitigation
mechanisms. Prior works [4–6,44,63,104,113,129,130,132,

all tested RowHammer mitigations (not shown here; see §9.2).

at the expense of increasingly large chip area overhead that
reaches 10.38mm2 (4.45% of the chip area of an Intel Xeon pro-
cessor [146]) when configured with an NRH of 32 for a dual-rank
system with 16 banks per rank. With these four observations,
we demonstrate that existing RowHammer mitigations incur sig-
nificant preventive refresh overheads or area overheads as NRH
decreases. Therefore, we conclude that reducing the overheads
of such mechanisms at low cost is critical.
Reducing the time and energy spent for preventive refreshes.
Prior works already show that DRAM timing constraints include
large guardbands [65, 191–200]. To reduce the performance
overhead of RowHammer mitigation mechanisms, we explore
the idea of reducing the time spent on each preventive refresh.
To this end, we experimentally evaluate the effect of reducing
tRAS on the i) reliability of real DDR4 DRAM chips using the
methodology described in §4.2, and ii) time and energy costs of
preventive refreshes.

Characterizing the Impact of Charge Restoration Latency.
To reliably reduce the charge restoration latency of preventive
refreshes, it is critical to understand the limits of a DRAM chip.
Therefore, it is important to investigate i) how RowHammer
Threshold and the number of RowHammer bitflips change with
reduced charge restoration latency, ii) whether using reduced
charge restoration latency for many consecutive preventive re-
freshes further worsens RowHammer threshold or number of
RowHammer bitflips, and iii) how the data retention time of
a DRAM row changes with the reduced charge restoration la-
tency. Although many prior works [2,4–6,38,48,51,56–71,82,
90, 91, 94–108] study various aspects of the read disturbance
characteristics of DRAM (e.g., access and data patterns, voltage,
temperature), no prior work characterizes the effect of charge
restoration latency on RowHammer.
In this paper, we conduct a rigorous experimental charac-
terization study to understand how reducing charge restoration
latency affects RowHammer vulnerability in real DDR4 DRAM
chips, along with its effects on system performance and energy
efficiency.
4. Characterization Methodology
We describe our DRAM testing infrastructure, the real DDR4
DRAM chips tested, and our testing methodology.
4.1. DRAM Testing Infrastructure
We test commodity DDR4 DRAM chips using an FPGA-
based DRAM testing infrastructure that consists of four
main components (as Fig. 5 illustrates):
i) a host ma-
chine that generates the test program and collects experi-
ment results, ii) an FPGA development board (Xilinx Alveo
U200 [201]), programmed with DRAM Bender [105, 202]
(based on SoftMC [203, 204]), to execute our test programs,
iii) a thermocouple temperature sensor and a pair of heater
pads pressed against the DRAM chips to maintain a target tem-
perature level, and iv) a PID temperature controller (MaxWell
FT200 [205]) that controls the heaters and keeps the temperature
at the desired level with a precision of ±0.5 ◦C.2

Eliminating interference sources. To observe RowHammer
bitflips without any circuit-/system-level interference, we take
three measures to eliminate potential sources of interference,
as done in prior works [44,63–65,71]. First, we disable peri-
odic refresh during the execution of our test programs to pre-
vent any unintended charge restoration by periodic refreshes
or potential on-DRAM-die TRR mechanisms [38,44]. Second,
we make sure the runtime of any program does not exceed
the refresh window such that no data retention failures are ob-
served. Third, we confirm that the tested DRAM modules and
chips have neither rank-level nor on-die ECC. By doing so, we
directly observe and analyze all circuit-level bitflips without
interference from architecture-level correction and mitigation
mechanisms [206,207].

4.3. Testing Methodology
Metrics. To characterize a DRAM row’s RowHammer vulnera-
bility, we examine two metrics: i) NRH, the minimum number of
activations per aggressor row to observe at least one RowHam-
mer bitflip and ii) Bit-Error-Rate (BER), the fraction of DRAM
cells in a row that experience a RowHammer bitflip. A higher
NRH (BER) indicates lower (higher) vulnerability.
Tests. Alg. 1 describes our main test loop and helper func-
tions. Our main test loop (lines 12-36) measures NRH and
BER of each tested row for different charge restoration laten-
cies and different numbers of restorations. Our RowHammer
testing function (lines 6-11) uses the double-sided hammer-
ing pattern [2, 16, 63, 64, 71], where we hammer two phys-
ically adjacent (aggressor) rows to a victim row in an alter-
nating manner. We perform double-sided hammering with
the maximum activation rate possible within DDR4 command
timing specifications [140] as this access pattern is demon-
strated to be the most effective RowHammer access pattern
on DRAM chips when RowHammer mitigation mechanisms
are disabled [2, 16, 38, 39, 63, 64, 90]. After initializing the
aggressor and victim rows (line 7), we perform partial charge
restoration on the sandwiched victim row (i.e., the row between
two aggressor rows) (line 8) using reduced charge restoration
latency (tRAS(Red)) before we perform double-sided hammering
(line 9). To guarantee that reducing charge restoration latency
does not cause any failures, we keep the victim rows unaccessed
for a time window as large as tREFW (i.e., until the partially re-
stored row would be refreshed by periodic refresh) (line 10).
By doing so, we ensure that we can detect if reduced charge
restoration latency causes a data retention failure. An NRH
value of 0 indicates that the victim row experiences a bitflip
without hammering due to data retention failure (lines 21-24).
To analyze the effect of partial charge restoration, we sweep the
charge restoration latency and the number of consecutive charge
restorations (lines 1-5). We run all our experiments for five iter-
ations and record the lowest (highest) observed NRH (BER) in
our analyses similar to prior works [2,63–65,71,90,184,198].

do so, we perform different numbers of consecutive preventive

6. Effect of Charge Restoration Latency on the
Half-Double Access Pattern
An aggressor row can cause bitflips in victim rows that are
not physically adjacent [2,63]. The impact of RowHammer on a
victim row decreases and eventually disappears as the physical
distance between the victim and the aggressor rows increases.
To account for this characteristic, prior works define blast radius
as the distance between an aggressor row and its furthest victim
row [2,38,39,43–45,47,63,64,113,123,125,135,145,220,221].
A recent RowHammer attack, Half-Double [47], introduces an
access pattern (i.e., Half-Double access pattern) that exploits
blast radius to induce bitflips with a significantly lower activa-
tion count. Half-Double demonstrates that hammering a far
aggressor row (i.e., physically two rows away from the victim
row) many times and then hammering the near aggressor row
(i.e., physically adjacent to the victim row) a much smaller num-
ber of times leads to bitflips in some DDR4 DRAM chips. Half-
Double access pattern is based on exploiting two weaknesses of
a poorly designed RowHammer mitigation mechanism i) under-
estimation of blast radius (i.e., the maximum distance between
an aggressor and a victim row) and ii) ignoring the hammer-
ing effect of the victim row preventive refreshes. To mitigate
Half-Double bitflips, existing RowHammer mitigation mecha-
nisms perform preventive refreshes on all four rows in ±2-row
distance of the aggressor row [44,131,133,134,145,190]. For
completeness, we analyze the impact of reducing charge restora-
tion latency on the Half-Double access pattern. To do so, we
modify Alg. 1 such that the hammering function performs the
Half-Double access pattern instead of double-sided RowHam-
mer on randomly selected two modules from each of Mfrs. H
and S (four modules in total).

8. PaCRAM
To demonstrate the potential benefits of our experimental
observations, we propose a new memory controller-based mech-
anism, Partial Charge Restoration for Aggressive Mitigation
(PaCRAM). PaCRAM works with an existing RowHammer mit-
igation mechanism and leverages our experimental observations
to safely reduce the latency of preventive refreshes that the ex-
isting mitigation mechanism performs. By doing so, PaCRAM
introduces a novel perspective into addressing RowHammer
vulnerability at low cost.
8.1. Overview of PaCRAM
Fig. 15 illustrates a high-level overview of PaCRAM. When
a DRAM row is activated 1 , PaCRAM and the existing
RowHammer mitigation mechanism are provided with the acti-
vated row address. The mitigation mechanism decides whether
to perform a preventive refresh and issues a preventive refresh
if it detects a potential aggressor row 2 .
Simultaneously,
PaCRAM determines whether preventive refresh latency can
be reduced and provides the memory controller with reduced
or nominal preventive refresh latency 3 . The memory request
scheduler schedules a preventive refresh (generated by the exist-
ing mitigation mechanism) using the preventive refresh latency
(determined by PaCRAM) 4 .
8.2. Reducing Preventive Refresh Latency

partial charge restoration.5 To safely perform partial charge
restoration, PaCRAM overcomes two challenges. First, re-
ducing preventive refresh latency can reduce the row’s NRH
(Takeaway 1). To address this challenge, PaCRAM configures

state is periodically reset into F.
Security. PaCRAM safely reduces charge restoration latency
of preventive refreshes by applying two modifications to the
existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism, based on experi-
mental characterization data: i) reducing NRH and ii) limiting
the maximum number of consecutive refreshes using partial
charge restoration. Therefore, PaCRAM guarantees that the
existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism is correctly con-
figured based on the DRAM chip’s vulnerability level. An
existing RowHammer mitigation mechanism’s security against
DRAM read disturbance-based exploits is identical to that of
the mechanism integrated with PaCRAM.
8.3. Detailed Implementation and Configuration
Full charge restoration interval (t FCRI). To ensure robust
execution, we calculate the smallest time interval at which a
DRAM row should be refreshed using full charge restoration.
We assume the worst case, where a DRAM row is hammered
as frequently as possible (i.e., activated every tRC). The latency
of a preventive refresh that performs partial charge restoration
is computed as the sum of the reduced charge restoration la-
tency and the nominal precharge latency (tRAS(Red) + tRP). The
RowHammer mitigation mechanism performs a preventive re-
fresh after NRH row activations. Therefore, the smallest interval
of such refreshes is the cumulative latency of performing i) NRH
activations (NRH ×tRC) and ii) the preventive refresh (tRAS(Red) +
tRP), resulting in (NRHtRC + tRAS(Red) + tRP). From experimental
characterization data (Fig. 12), we obtain the maximum number
of consecutive partial charge restorations (NPCR). The smallest
time window that can contain NPCR of such preventive refreshes
is NPCR(NRHtRC +tRAS(Red) +tRP), which we denote as the full
charge restoration interval (tFCRI).6 For example, the tested
module S6 exhibits the NRH and NPCR values of 3.9K and 2K,
respectively, at tRAS(Red) = 0.36tRAS and thus it requires rows to
be refreshed with full charge restoration at an interval of 374ms.
Maintaining row states. To maintain each row’s state at low
cost, PaCRAM implements a bit vector, called fully restored
bit vector (FR). FR stores a single bit for each row in the
DRAM module. Each bit in FR indicates whether a row should
be refreshed using nominal latency within a tFCRI. FR can be
implemented as an SRAM array in the memory controller.
8.4. Hardware Complexity
PaCRAM’s metadata storage (i.e., FR) can be implemented
using SRAM in the memory controller. The metadata size
is independent of NRH, making PaCRAM scalable with the
increasing RowHammer vulnerability. PaCRAM stores one bit
of information per DRAM row on the fully restored bit vector
(FR). We evaluate PaCRAM’s chip area and access latency
overheads using CACTI [222]. Our results show that PaCRAM
has an area cost of 0.0069mm2 per DRAM bank where a DRAM
bank consists of 64K DRAM rows. When configured for a dual
rank system with 16 banks at each rank, PaCRAM requires
storage of 8KB per bank, corresponding to a 0.09% overhead
on a high-end Intel Xeon processor [146] (1.35% of the memory
controller area [223]). The metadata access latency in SRAM
is 0.27ns, which is significantly smaller than the latency of a
DRAM row activation (e.g., 14ns [224]), and can be hidden by
the row activation latency.
8.5. PaCRAM with on-DRAM-die RowHammer
Mitigation Mechanisms
Various prior works propose on-DRAM-die RowHammer
mitigation mechanisms [80, 121, 133–136, 144, 150, 172], in-
cluding the PRAC mechanism (§2.2) in the recent DDR5 stan-
dard [136,144]. To support these mechanisms, PaCRAM can
also be implemented in a DRAM chip with an on-DRAM-die
RowHammer mitigation mechanism. When a preventive refresh
is scheduled, PaCRAM determines whether the refresh latency
can be safely reduced by checking FR and stores the preventive
refresh latency information in the mode registers (MR) [141].
When the memory controller performs a preventive refresh
(e.g., by issuing an RFM command [133]), it uses the latency
specified in the MR. We believe better memory interfaces can
facilitate easier and more efficient integration of on-DRAM-die
RowHammer mitigation mechanisms and PaCRAM-like mech-
anisms that make RowHammer mitigation more efficient and
scalable. For example, Self-Managing DRAM [135] enables
DRAM chips to autonomously perform maintenance operations.
In a memory system that implements Self-Managing DRAM,
PaCRAM can easily be implemented in the DRAM chip with no
modifications to the memory controller or the memory interface.

10. Profiling Overhead
Characterization of DRAM chips. For PaCRAM to work
robustly, one needs to correctly identify the appropriate charge
restoration reduction. Doing so requires profiling the DRAM
chips in use. This profiling can be performed in different ways
at low cost as described in our methodology (§4.3) and several
prior works (e.g., [64,129,192,192,194,198,240–242]). First,
the system can perform profiling the very first time DRAM
is initialized and configure PaCRAM. Second, DRAM ven-
dors can perform profiling at manufacturing time and embed
configuration data in the Serial Presence Detect (SPD) cir-
cuitry [243]. The memory controller can read the configu-
ration data from the SPD circuitry and configure PaCRAM.
Third, the system can perform online profiling to configure
PaCRAM [192,198,211,240,244]. To quantify the profiling
cost, we demonstrate an optimized profiling methodology.
Profiling cost. As §4.3 describes, our profiling methodology
tests a DRAM row for five different tRAS values with ten dif-
ferent consecutive partial charge restorations counts at five dif-
ferent hammer counts for five iterations. Given that each test
includes a wait time of tREFW (64ms), we concurrently test and,
by doing so, overlap the testing time of 1270 different DRAM
rows in a DRAM bank. Therefore, we test 1270 DRAM rows
within an 80s time window (64ms × 5 tRAS values × 10 differ-
ent numbers of partial charge restorations × 5 hammer counts
× 5 iterations). As each DRAM row contains 8KB of data,
our methodology’s profiling throughput is 127 KB/s (1270 ×
8KB / 80s). Following this methodology, profiling a DRAM
bank with 64K rows takes 68.8 minutes. However, this profiling
can be performed in chunks of 80 seconds spread across time
by blocking only 9.9MB of data at a time. We leave further
optimizations of profiling to future work.
System reliability. PaCRAM is limited to systems that em-
ploy DRAM chips that implement guardbands in the tRAS
timing parameter.
We experimentally demonstrate on real
DDR4 DRAM chips that there exist significantly large guard-
bands in the tRAS timing parameter.
Our findings are in
line with prior works [65, 191–200] that show that several
timing parameters (e.g., tRCD, tWR, tRP, tRAS) in commod-
ity DRAM chips implement large guardbands. To improve
robustness and to account for dynamic variability, e.g., ag-
ing, PaCRAM can be combined with error correction mecha-
nisms [89,153,198,211,240,244–253]. We leave the exploration
of such systems for future work.
11. Related Work
To our knowledge, this is the first work that i) experimentally
studies how reducing charge restoration latency affects modern
DRAM chips’ RowHammer vulnerability, ii) investigates impli-
cations on state-of-the-art RowHammer mitigation mechanisms,
and iii) proposes a new technique, called PaCRAM, which
reduces the performance overheads of existing RowHammer
mitigation mechanisms by reducing the time spent on preventive
refreshes. §9 already quantitatively evaluates PaCRAM’s bene-
fits when used with five state-of-the-art RowHammer mitigation
mechanisms: PARA [2], RFM [141], PRAC [144], Hydra [124],
and Graphene [113]. In this section, we discuss related works
in three related directions.
DRAM operation under reduced latency. Many works char-
acterize DRAM under reduced latency [184,191,193,194,196,
198–200,209,210,242,244,254–263] and develop techniques
that reduce the latency of DRAM operations to improve system
throughput and energy efficiency [161,184,191,194,196–200,
244,261–271], generate true random numbers or physical un-
clonable functions using DRAM chips [242,254–257,272–274],
and perform computation using DRAM techniques [105,209,
210, 258–260, 272, 274–279]. None of these works study the
effect of reduced charge restoration latency on RowHammer
vulnerability.
Experimental RowHammer characterization. Prior works
extensively characterize the RowHammer vulnerability in real
DRAM chips [2,4–6,38,48,51,56–71,82,90,91,94–108]. These
works demonstrate (using real DDR3, DDR4, LPDDR4, and
HBM2 DRAM chips) how a DRAM chip’s RowHammer vul-
nerability varies with i) DRAM refresh rate [2,38,44], ii) the
physical distance between aggressor and victim rows [2,63,94],
iii) DRAM generation and technology node [2, 44, 63, 64],
iv) temperature [57, 64], v) the time the aggressor row stays
active [57, 64, 71–73, 91], vi) physical location of the victim
cell [64, 90, 91, 129], vii) reduced wordline voltage [65], and
viii) time [107]. None of these works investigate the effect of
charge restoration latency on RowHammer vulnerability in real
DRAM chips. Our characterization study advances the analyses
in these works by uncovering new insights into RowHammer
under reduced refresh latency.
RowHammer attacks and mitigation mechanisms. Many
prior works [2,4,6–57,61–64,71–95] show that RowHammer
can be exploited to mount system-level attacks to compromise
system security and safety (e.g., to acquire root privileges or
leak private data). To protect against these attacks, many prior
works [2, 5, 26, 32, 38, 44, 59, 80, 84, 89, 97, 98, 100, 109, 111–
136,140,145,147–190] propose RowHammer mitigation mech-
anisms that prevent bitflips from compromising a system. The
observations we make in this work can be leveraged to reduce
the performance overhead of existing mitigation mechanisms
by reducing the execution time and energy spent on preventive
refreshes, as we quantitatively demonstrate in §9.
12. Conclusion
We present the first rigorous experimental study on the ef-
fects of reduced refresh latency on RowHammer vulnerability of
modern DRAM chips. Our analysis of 388 real DDR4 DRAM
chips from three major manufacturers demonstrate that charge
restoration latency of RowHammer-preventive refreshes can
be significantly reduced at the expense of requiring slightly
14


## Page 15


more preventive refreshes. We propose a new mechanism, Par-
tial Charge Restoration for Aggressive Mitigation (PaCRAM),
which robustly reduces the preventive refresh latency in ex-
isting RowHammer mitigation mechanisms. Our evaluation
demonstrates that PaCRAM significantly enhances system per-
formance and energy efficiency when used with five state-of-
the-art RowHammer mitigation mechanisms while introducing
small additional area overhead. By doing so, PaCRAM enables
new trade-offs in RowHammer mitigation. We hope and expect
that the understanding we develop via our rigorous experimental
characterization and the resulting PaCRAM mechanism will in-
spire DRAM manufacturers and system designers to efficiently
and scalably enable robust and efficient operation as DRAM
technology node scaling exacerbates read disturbance.

B. PaCRAM for Other Types of
Charge Restoration
PaCRAM can be extended to also reduce the charge restora-
tion latency for periodic refreshes and dynamic accesses, in
addition to preventive refreshes (as we have done so far). To
exemplify the potential benefits of such extensions, we analyze
the impact of reducing charge restoration latency for periodic
refreshes, which restore every cell’s charge in the module ev-
ery tREFW to prevent data retention failures, similarly to prior
work [200]. To do so, we modify PaCRAM such the latency of
all periodic refreshes is reduced since our experimental charac-
terization demonstrates that charge restoration latency can be
reduced significantly without causing any data retention failures
(Takeaway 6). We use a configuration that does not employ
any RowHammer mitigation mechanism (i.e., no preventive
refreshes are issued) and sweep the charge restoration latency
for periodic refreshes.

efficiency by 23.31%, and 36.49% of a 512Gb DRAM chip,
respectively. Second, performance improvement and energy
efficiency increase as periodic refresh latency decreases. For
example, system performance increases by 8.83% when peri-
odic refresh latency decreases from 64% to 36%. Third, for
all periodic refresh latency values, periodic refresh overheads
increase as DRAM chip capacity increases. Based on these
observations, we conclude that reducing the latency of periodic
refreshes improves PaCRAM’s performance benefits.
To robustly employ this extension of PaCRAM to periodic
refreshes, PaCRAM needs to guarantee that a DRAM row
does not receive NPCR consecutive periodic refreshes using
reduced charge restoration latency. As each DRAM row is
refreshed with periodic refresh once every refresh window
(tREFW), PaCRAM can safely use reduced charge restoration
latency for NPCR refresh windows. After periodically refreshing
each DRAM row using reduced charge restoration latency for
NPCR times, PaCRAM uses nominal charge restoration latency
for the next refresh window to fully restore each cell’s charge.
To do so, PaCRAM implements a single counter that counts the
number of refresh windows using reduced charge restoration
latency and increments the counter every tREFW. When the
counter reaches NPCR, PaCRAM uses nominal charge restora-
tion latency for that refresh window and resets the counter to
zero.
