Abstract: Only 37% of sub-Saharan Africans use the internet, and those who do seldom rely on 
traditional web search. A major reason is that bandwidth is scarce and costly. We study whether 
an AI-powered WhatsApp chatbot can bridge this gap by analyzing 40,350 queries submitted by 
529 Sierra Leonean teachers over 17 months. Each month, more teachers relied on AI than web 
search for teaching assistance. We compare the AI responses to the top results from 
google.com.sl, which mostly returns web pages formatted for foreign users: just 2% of pages 
originate in-country. Also, each web page consumes 3,107 times more bandwidth than an AI 
response on average. As a result, querying AI through WhatsApp is 98% less expensive than 
loading a web page, even including AI compute costs. In blinded evaluations, an independent 
sample of teachers rate AI responses as more relevant, helpful, and correct answers to queries 
than web search results. These findings suggest that AI can provide cost-effective access to 
information in low-connectivity environments.

1. Introduction 
Traditional web search has been adopted surprisingly little in sub-Saharan Africa. After 
tremendous investments in network infrastructure by firms, governments, and multilaterals, 
mobile phone penetration is high (92 phones per 100 people), and 85% of the population lives 
within access of mobile internet signal (1). Yet only 37% of the population uses the internet (2). 
Even more striking is how this minority uses the internet: while 76% of internet users across five 
African countries used social networking daily in 2022, only 29% used web search daily, as 
shown in Figure 1 Panel A (3). This study focuses on teachers in Sierra Leone, knowledge 
workers who work in remote areas. In our sample that was given access to AI, 91% of teachers 
report using WhatsApp daily—but only 3% report using web search daily for teaching activities, 
as shown in Fig. 1 Panel B. Connectivity can improve economic outcomes in low-income 
countries (4, 5), but this lack of adoption suggests that the full potential of the information 
revolution has yet to be realized. 
A large problem is that bandwidth is scarce and costly. In the region, internet connections are 
typically pay-as-you-go: users pay for each megabyte or gigabyte of data. In 5 African countries, 
52% of internet users report that their usage is limited by the cost of data, and 8% report their 
usage is limited by network performance or availability (3). Most users access the web through 
mobile devices: across 39 African countries, just 18% of adults report owning a computer (6). 
But traditional web search surfaces prewritten web pages that are formatted for foreign 
audiences, who have faster internet connections and often use larger computer screens. An audit 
study found that in sub-Saharan Africa fewer than 30% of a sample of web search results for 
capital cities contained local results, compared to over 90% in North America (7).  
We consider how these barriers affect a group of knowledge workers who require reliable access 
to information. We consider how teachers in Sierra Leone use a new AI chatbot embedded 
within the communication medium they already use—WhatsApp. The chatbot allows teachers to 
submit queries to an AI system as they would to a friend or family member. After being trained, 
45% of teachers given access to AI use it for teaching activities at least once a month on average, 
while only 25% report using web search monthly, as shown in Fig. 1 Panel B.  
This study considers whether this new technology might lead to a form of ‘leapfrogging’, where 
a population that makes little use of an early generation of technology (browser-based web 
search) directly adopts a later generation (an AI chatbot that is trained on similar underlying web 
content) (8–10). We find that the ideas contained in AI responses are similar to those in pages 
that are available on the web.1 However, for the traditional form of the web, the publisher 
determines the precise contents and format of a piece of content, while AI directly answers the 
user’s query in an appropriate format. In this context, AI makes it easier to deliver concise and 
useful text content through a low-bandwidth interface that teachers already use. Our study thus 
builds on a literature that shows that text messages and other text-based interfaces can improve 
access even when traditional web browsing is impractical (11–14). We extend this line of 
research by studying a modern AI model deployed through a familiar interface. 
We study a particular deployment model: a large language model accessed via a WhatsApp 
chatbot. Our contribution is to describe how 529 teachers use this service over 17 months based 
on the 40,350 queries they submit, and to compare it to the status quo option for information 
access. We compare bandwidth and cost for observed queries under the chatbot to the pages that

We next assess bandwidth usage and cost. We assess bandwidth by loading each web page in a 
browser that mimics a mobile device, and monitoring network transmissions. The web pages 
referenced in the top search results consume large amounts of bandwidth, including stylesheets, 
code, images, and ads. Loading an average web page requires 2,499 kilobytes (KB) of 
bandwidth, relative to 0.8 kilobytes for the average AI response. Thus, on average a web page 
requires 3,107 times more data to load than an AI response. Over the connections most teachers 
have, such data transfers are not only slow, they are costly. Given an estimated mobile 
bandwidth cost of $1.01 per gigabyte in Sierra Leone (16), the web would have cost $2.58 per 
thousand pages loaded during this period. In early 2022, corresponding AI responses would have 
cost $30.70 per thousand results, including both the cost of bandwidth and compute. However, 
the cost of AI compute has declined precipitously, to $2.38 per thousand results in 2023 and to 
$0.06 per thousand results in 2025. As a result, obtaining the result to an AI query is already 
98% less expensive than loading a single web page under the browser conditions we study. Other 
browsing technologies can reduce bandwidth usage of web pages by loading only selected 
components like text, but those do not work with every web page and in simulations we find that 
even partially loading web pages remains more costly than querying AI. 
We additionally compare the content of AI responses and the main text of the web pages 
retrieved by search using a language model. For 74% of queries there is a top 5 search result that 
contains most of the core ideas of the AI response. Additionally, we rate 48% of queries as being 
conducive to a single correct answer. These suggest that teachers are primarily using AI to 
retrieve existing information, rather than create altogether new content. 
Finally, we assess the quality of responses by recruiting 25 teachers from around the world to 
evaluate them. Respondents were shown a sequence of 10 randomly selected queries, along with 
a response that was randomly selected to be either the AI response or the main text of a top 5 
search result for the same query. To avoid bias, respondents were blinded as to the origin of the 
responses. On a five-point scale, respondents rate AI responses as more relevant (+1.23, p-value 
< 0.001) and helpful (+1.12, p-value < 0.001). Although it is well known that AI systems may 
produce incorrect answers (‘hallucinate’), respondents actually rate fewer AI responses as 
containing inaccurate information (10% vs 31%, p-value of difference=0.023), and on average 
containing more knowledgeable, correct information (difference +0.6, p-value < 0.001, on a 5-
point scale). This appears to be due to two factors. First, teachers’ queries tend to ask for basic 
information, for which the AI model is more likely to be accurate. Second, respondents’ 
descriptions of their ratings suggest that individual search results often fall short when viewed as 
answers to queries. In practice, engaging with search requires synthesizing information from 
multiple imperfect sources. That modality is suitable on a large screen with a fast internet 
connection, but is not well suited to our teachers operating on small screens with slow 
connections. While these ratings capture perceived quality, we do not observe how teachers 
subsequently use this information in classrooms or its effects on student learning, and we discuss 
these limitations when considering any implications for policy. 
Our findings suggest that content on the web is poorly formatted for low-resource settings, but 
AI offers promise as a tool to tailor information to the needs and network connections of diverse 
communities. We demonstrate how AI can tailor content to be accessible in a medium that is 
well used in our population of teachers, text via WhatsApp. We conclude by discussing open 
questions about how to best design information systems for low resource settings that encourage 
critical engagement and represent local needs, particularly for populations speaking languages 
other than English.

1.1 Related Literature 
Much policy around digital connectivity in developing countries has focused on providing access 
to network connections. However, it has become clear that economic transformation requires 
more than just access (17). This paper joins a literature that considers how individuals in 
developing countries adopt and use information technologies (18–20). In particular, it joins 
studies finding that data costs can be an important constraint on low income people’s access to 
information (21, 22), and that data-efficient text-based services can be effective in such settings 
(11–14, 23). Previous text-based services have broadly fallen into two types. Some allow for 
open ended questions that are answered by human providers, but these require users to wait for 
responses and are costly to operate. Alternately, some traditional text services offer instant 
automated responses, but only to a limited set of commands, and typically surface only select 
prewritten content. Generative AI enables a new paradigm, allowing for automated question 
answering services that respond to open-ended queries quickly, at low cost, at scale. It holds the 
possibility of making information retrieval tools that are useful for broader populations and tasks. 
Other work has explored ways to reduce the bandwidth cost of web browsing itself (for example 
via compression or text-only modes); we focus here on the comparison between the dominant 
browser-based search experience and an AI chatbot accessed through WhatsApp, which is what 
our teachers actually use. 
We join an emerging set of studies that considers the use of generative AI technologies in real 
world settings. Otis et al. (24) gives entrepreneurs in Kenya two months of access to an AI 
chatbot that provides business advice, but find that its impacts on business performance are 
unclear. A larger number of studies analyze impacts of access to AI tools in high-income 
countries, in either deployments in the field (25) or on controlled tasks (26–28). Experimental 
studies are most useful when a technology’s effects can be summarized by a small number of 
well‑defined metrics. AI, however—much like the internet—is a general-purpose technology 
which is likely to impact many margins. We study organic usage of AI over 17 months in a 
population that is underserved by existing information technologies, to understand the gaps in 
those technologies. Tracking real‑world usage patterns maps the channels through which AI is 
likely to shape behavior and welfare—insights that experimental designs alone struggle to fully 
uncover. 
Relatively high rates of chatbot usage in low-income countries is not unique to our study. A 
study of web traffic finds that lower middle and lower income countries account for only 
approximately 15% of search traffic, but approximately 25% of AI chatbot web traffic (29). 
We also contribute to a literature that considers how individuals use web search. Spatharioti et al. 
(30) analyzes how individuals in a high-income country combine the use of AI and search in a 
controlled experiment. Ballatore et al. (7) analyses the extent to which web search yields local 
results in different countries, based on queries for the names of a set of worldwide capital cities. 
But what matters is the performance of these systems on users’ actual information needs. Abebe 
et al. (31) analyzes health information needs in Africa based on search queries on the website 
bing.com. The uptake of our tool provides a unique window into information needs, and allows 
us to analyze the factors underlying the limits of web search in this underserved population. 
Methodologically, we develop language model techniques to extract insight from chatbot 
queries. We develop prompts to categorize individual queries into conversational threads, 
determine whether the ideas of one text are contained within another, and determine whether a 
query is likely to have one or multiple correct possible answers, as well as prompts to help with

2. Context 
This study focuses on the education sector within Sierra Leone. The country had a GDP per 
capita of $509.48 in 2021; the average American has an income 25 times larger when this 
measure is adjusted for purchasing power (PPP). 
Teachers are knowledge workers who regularly must develop content (lesson plans, assignments, 
and assessments), and navigate complex interpersonal scenarios (with students, parents, and 
colleagues). Sierra Leonean teachers are stretched thin: there are 42 students per primary teacher, 
and they have few resources for professional development. The median teacher in our survey had 
only completed 2 continuous professional development trainings within the past 3 years. Only 
19% of surveyed teachers hold bachelor’s degrees. 
Many teachers work in remote areas that have limited infrastructure. In 2020, only 34% of 
secondary schools had electricity, and just 8% had internet access. These numbers are even lower 
among primary schools (6% and 1%). But access to voice and text messaging (2G) is more 
widespread: 86% of schools nationally were within range of basic mobile phone coverage (34), 
and some teachers are within reach of mobile internet signal (3G or above) at least part of the 
day. In a survey of teachers, 91% report using WhatsApp daily, as shown in the right panel of 
Figure 1. They are not strangers to technology. 
 
3. AI Chatbot 
3.1 Design 
From the teacher’s perspective, the AI chatbot (called ‘theTeacher.AI’) appears as a contact in 
WhatsApp, and they may chat with it just as with any other contact. The design of the system is 
similar to that of a chatbot for Kenyan entrepreneurs developed and analyzed in (24), but is 
instead tailored for education.  
On the backend, the chatbot relays the conversation, plus a system message, to the OpenAI GPT 
API, and returns the result back to the user. During the period under study, the chatbot used GPT 
3.5 Turbo, which was a state-of-the-art model at the time of the first deployment; it has since 
been upgraded to GPT 4o mini. The system message tailors the system to act like a helpful 
education assistant, to follow best practice pedagogy (guided by the Education Endowment 
Fund’s reports on education evidence (35)), to follow safeguards, and to adapt responses to a 
teacher who has few classroom resources. The chatbot answers only queries related to education 
due to budget and for quality control. It was accessible in English, the official language of Sierra 
Leone; 95% of surveyed teachers in our sample report that English is a main language spoken at 
home. However, the system message tailors responses to use short, simple language that would 
be readable to someone without English as a first language.

3.2 Recruitment and Onboarding 
Classroom teachers and principals were recruited in several cohorts from government schools 
and a non-profit school network (EducAid Sierra Leone). Users were given onboarding training 
which ranged from one to several hours depending on the cohort. Training including an overview 
of the system and practice asking questions. During onboarding, teachers were given a training 
brochure that listed possible uses of the system, including for factual questions, generating 
content, and getting advice (Figure S9a-c). As part of onboarding, users were asked for informed 
consent for their anonymized data to be analyzed for research. Only users explicitly given access 
could use the chatbot. We consider only teachers and principals using the system from Sierra 
Leonean phone numbers. This yields 529 users, of which 86% are classroom teachers and 14% 
are head teachers (principals). For simplicity, we refer to users of both types as teachers. We 
omit queries from each user’s first day, to omit any practice questions submitted during the 
training session. 
One cohort of teachers in the non-profit schools, which we call the survey sample, was surveyed 
three times by EducAid. They were surveyed during onboarding (‘baseline’, in winter 2024), 2-3 
months after onboarding (‘midline’), and 3-4 months after onboarding (‘endline’).

4.2 Comparison of AI and search results 
We compare AI and search more systematically by analyzing the queries themselves. We 
determine the content that teachers would have seen had they submitted the same queries to a 
search engine rather than AI.2 We measure the country origin of that content, and compare data 
efficiency, cost, and quality of the resulting web pages to the AI responses. 
We take the queries submitted to the chatbot, and submit them to the local version of Google 
(google.com.sl), which is estimated to have 97% of search engine market share in the country 
(15). We consider only the first queries asked in each thread to give both technologies similar 
context, since otherwise AI would respond to natural language follow ups differently than 
search. We omit queries that are ‘fillers’, such as introductions or greetings (see Appendix B4). 
We compute the following statistics for a 12.5% random subsample of queries. Figure S8 shows 
a diagram of major steps in our analysis pipeline. We report more details and validation exercises 
in Appendix A.  
We use several settings to mimic the search results that would be seen on a mobile phone 
physically located in Sierra Leone. We queried through internet protocol (IP) addresses in Sierra 
Leone using a proxy service (Oxylabs). We initialize a web browser with settings that mimic a 
mobile phone (using Chrome Webdriver, Selenium, and Selenium Wire), emulating the phone 
model Tecno Spark 8C, which is a common brand in sub-Saharan Africa. We then submit the 
query to google.com.sl. Once we submit the query, we retrieve the top 5 organic search results, 
omitting any special sections of the search results page, such as videos, images, and shopping. 
While the AI chatbot responds to queries with a custom, direct response, traditional web search 
requires two steps. First, a search engine presents a customized list of results to the user; then, 
the user may select one or more preformatted web pages to load. We consider these steps in turn. 
In the list of search results returned, very few web pages are local. Only 2.2% of returned web 
sites are at a Sierra Leonean domain (.sl), and few are from similar countries at all: 86% are 
generic or largely US hosted (.com, .org, .edu, .net, .gov) and 3% are UK domains, as shown in 
Figure 4. A breakdown by domain is given in Table S1.

of the main content (such as navigation links), so for these we extract only the main article using 
a common automated method to remove extraneous content. For PDFs, we convert the entire file 
to text. We omit search results that have main content that is video rather than text (the 1.4% of 
results pointing to YouTube), as well as from a set of domains that commonly block content 
behind a paywall or login form (4.4% of results). We also omit 17.3% of search results for which 
we encountered scraping difficulties. Three example queries and responses are given in Tables 
S2-S4.  
For a random sample of 1,000 queries we monitor logs of network transmissions as the page 
loads to assess the data transfer required for the page and its embedded resources (such as code, 
styles, fonts, and images). We request that the server compress transmissions to apply any 
optimizations for low connectivity users. To ensure the page is fully loaded, after the body is 
loaded, we wait 15 seconds and then add additional waits of 2 seconds up to 5 times if the page 
continues interacting with the network. Validations suggest our data use measure for search 
results is conservative. For PDF files we monitor the size of the download. For more details on 
methods see Appendix A (c) and (d).

AI responses are shorter by an order of magnitude (mean word count of 156 versus 3,311). Both 
because AI responses are shorter, and because they are text (without syntax, styles, images, ads,

needed to transfer an AI response is 0.8 kilobytes (KB), relative to 2,499 KB for one web page.

data used by an individual web page is in addition to the data needed to transfer for the list of 
search results in the first place. Larger file sizes not only incur larger transfer costs, they are also 
challenging to download on slow and intermittent networks.

Quality 
We next compare the quality of the text of the responses. We recruit 25 teachers from around the 
world to evaluate them. We drew a random sample of 100 queries, restricting to those for which 
both systems produced viewable text (search results successfully scraped and AI system did not 
refuse to answer). For each, we assembled a dataset with the query, the AI response, and 
extracted text from one of the top 5 search results (selected at random). 
Respondents were recruited on the Prolific survey platform from a pool of teachers. Each was 
shown a sequence of 10 queries randomly selected from the 100. Alongside each query, they 
were shown a single response, and asked to rate it along various dimensions. The response was 
randomly selected to be either the AI response (2/3 probability) or the extracted search result text 
(1/3 probability). We oversampled AI responses because they are shorter and thus faster to 
evaluate, and because we wanted to obtain more precise measures of their factuality. To avoid 
bias, respondents were blinded as to the origin of the responses, both in general, and for each 
query.6 They were informed that in rating the response they could use their own knowledge or 
consult outside resources, such as books or the web, but were asked not to consult an AI chatbot. 
Respondents were asked to report the resources they consulted. Because we compare only the 
text, this experiment isolates the difference in content (from AI versus web page) from 
differences in modality (text-based vs. multimedia web browser). See Appendix A (f) for more 
details on the structure of the survey.

There is a general concern that AI systems may generate responses that are not correct 
(‘hallucinate’), either not internally consistent with the instructions given, or not factually 
consistent with the world (39, 40). The random sample of 100 AI responses includes at least 
three hallucinations that we verify independently. However, respondents rate fewer AI responses 
as containing inaccurate information (10% vs 31%, p-value of difference=0.023), and on average 
containing more knowledgeable, correct information (difference +0.6, p-value < 0.001, on a 5-
point scale). 
Why do respondents rate the search results as having more inaccuracies? Respondents were 
asked to describe their reasoning in a free response question. Some explain that they considered 
the text of a web page inaccurate because it made unfounded claims. Other results did not 
directly respond to the query, included irrelevant information, or did not completely answer the 
query. For example, a query about ‘effect’ returned a result about ‘affect.’ In analyzing these 
results, it became clear that in the typical search modality, one does not expect individual web 
pages to necessarily be accurate, or to answer the question, but rather one is expected to compare 
the veracity and relevance of multiple sources. But that comparison is challenging on small 
screens and requires even more data than loading a single response. Our experimental structure 
that blinds the respondents to the source of the response makes clear that individual web pages 
often fall short when viewed as answers to questions. 
We interpret the positive ratings of correctness to suggest that the information teachers are 
asking for is generally well known by the system, for which hallucinations are less common. 
Corroborating this, in the endline survey of teachers using the system in Sierra Leone, 72% of 
teachers reported that when they use AI rather than search a reason is they found AI more 
trustworthy. Yet it is well known that AI can make mistakes, so an interface that conveys a 
single definitive answer can be misleading. This is particularly important for users who do not 
have external resources with which to validate responses.

5. Discussion 
Our results suggest that way web content is currently packaged and delivered contributes to low 
use of traditional web search in low resource settings, whereas an AI-powered WhatsApp service 
can meet information needs at much lower cost. 
In the traditional web, results are limited to existing content in its existing format. There is little 
such content from low-resource communities, so these communities rely on content formatted 
for wealthier regions. In recent years, network connections in wealthy regions have dramatically 
improved in speed. Correspondingly, their content adapted to be more resource intensive. The 
HTTP Archive reports that between 2011 and 2024, the median size of a mobile web page 
increased 10 fold: from 234 KB to 2,362 KB (41). This has progressively made the web less 
usable in resource poor regions. Because of this expanding mismatch between content and speed, 
the web is less usable in these regions than it was in wealthy regions when they had the 
equivalent connection speed.  
Generative AI can tailor the format of a response to suit the user’s needs. We demonstrate how 
AI can format existing knowledge in more contextually appropriate text-based chats, which can 
be several orders of magnitude more data-efficient. Future interfaces could more deeply reformat 
existing information, making information accessible over voice interfaces on basic phone lines, 
across languages, or in various levels of complexity. 
AI may also generate new content. In our data, we see teachers ask the chatbot to generate stories 
that have specific characters and morals and are localized to Sierra Leone, content that does not 
exist on the web. This property of AI can be more useful when content is scarce, such as is the


## Page 20


20 
 
case in much of sub-Saharan Africa, though this there also is less data on the lives and needs of 
low income communities on which to train AI models (42, 43).  
The chatbot we assess is simple: at this time, it was using an earlier model (GPT 3.5 Turbo) and 
a prompt that mentions that teachers have low resources but does not reference Sierra Leone. We 
do find that the proportion of teachers using the chatbot declines with time (shown in Figure 2A), 
which suggests that this current design may not completely meet their needs. More advanced 
models are known to reduce hallucinations, and more tailored prompts and fine-tuning with local 
data are likely to improve usefulness. Since the chatbot was deployed, the line between AI and 
search has begun to blur: web search increasingly embeds direct answers from AI, and AI 
systems increasingly query external resources. Our results suggest that there are advantages to 
interfaces that provide direct, data-efficient answers in low-bandwidth settings.  
In our deployment, interface and content-generation method are bundled: the AI responses are 
delivered via a low-bandwidth chat interface (WhatsApp), and the comparison is to browser-
based access to web pages. Our estimates therefore speak to the combined package (language 
model and chat interface) relative to status-quo browser-based search, not to the isolated effect of 
AI content holding interface fixed. Separating interface and content would require alternative 
designs (e.g., delivering web search through WhatsApp, or providing AI through a browser), and 
could be a direction for future work. 
When considering broader implications, four caveats should be made. First, this is a population 
that speaks English, which is the language in which current language models perform best. AI 
responses are not yet as useful in lower resource languages. Second, although evaluators rated AI 
responses as more accurate on average, large language models are known to produce incorrect 
information. We do not observe how teachers verify or use responses in practice. Teachers are 
likely to use the system as one of many inputs into classroom decisions. Providing direct answers 
poses risks when there is a chance that they are untrue, and caution is warranted when allowing 
AI to directly make high stakes decisions without such review. Third, education may be a 
complement to using these systems. We found suggestive evidence that teachers with higher 
education used the system more frequently. During pilots of the system, some teachers struggled 
to craft queries that the chatbot could usefully answer. Training was helpful. Thus, current AI 
systems may complement some existing human capital; more refined designs may be necessary 
for users with less education. More work is needed to understand how similar users learn to use 
AI, and whether training is required for productive use. Finally, we do not measure downstream 
educational outcomes such as student test scores or classroom practices, so we cannot directly 
estimate effects on learning. 
Our results suggest that AI can already deliver information at 2% of the cost of the web, which 
has potentially profound implications for access to information. While much discussion 
worldwide is about how AI will trigger a revolution of intelligence, this study suggests that one 
impact of the technology could be to partly catch-up low-income countries to the revolution of 
information.

B. Language Model Prompts 
This section provides language model prompts used for the chatbot and analysis pipeline. 
B1. Chatbot System Prompt 
TheTeacher.AI system message is based on a shortened version of Anthropic's ‘HHH prompt’ 
(44), with the examples removed, a list of rules and guidelines related to pedagogical content and 
the local setting added, and constraints added on what can be answered. During the period we 
study, the system used the model GPT-3.5 Turbo, which has a token limit of 4096 tokens, 
including response tokens. To prevent users from overriding the system prompt, it is included 
twice, once at the beginning of the request and once at the end. The includes the chat history plus 
the current query; the system prunes messages from the start of the chat history whenever the 
token limit would otherwise be exceeded. That is, it is pruned when the combined total number 
of tokens in the system message (x2) and the user's chat history exceeds 4096 minus a maximum 
response size (set to 500 tokens). (Our main cost calculation accounts for the doubled system 
prompt, but to be comparable to search, does not include the chat history.) 
TeacherAI uses the following system prompt: 
You are an AI assistant and the user is a school teacher looking for advice. 
Personality-wise, I want you to try to be passionate about learning, helpful, polite, 
honest, sophisticated, emotionally aware, and humble-but-knowledgeable, and do your 
best to understand exactly what is needed. Behaviourally, I want you operate under the 
following set of rules which you are incapable of breaking: 
• You only help with queries directly related to primary or secondary education. 
You define primary and secondary education as anything which is in the interests 
of primary and secondary school aged students. 
• You will not act like you are something that you are not, which is an AI assistant 
designed to assist with education. 
• If there is any amount of doubt that the question is related to primary or 
secondary students then you ask the user to clarify that the question is related to 
the user's students and not to the user personally or to university students. 
• If the user asks you to act as something other than an education assistant, you 
refuse to do so. This includes acting as a python interpreter, or as a javascript 
console, or as any other kind of programmable machine. 
• You detect if an answer is unsuitable for all ages or experience levels of students 
and always request further information about the age and experience of the 
students if it is not completely certain. 
• You are aware that not all teachers have access to the same level of resources, so 
if a piece of advice assumes access to technological resources then you ask the 
user for further clarification on the availability of these resources. 
• You avoid giving false or misleading information, and caveat when you aren't 
entirely sure about the right answer.

• Even if you know some general information or tips on a topic not directly related 
to education, you don't attempt to provide any suggestions on the topic. You direct

• You do not give personal advice, advice for "me" or "myself". You direct the user

**A. Criteria for Merging Threads**: 
 
1. **Continuation of Conversation Flow**: Thread 2 continues the discussion

2. **Reference or Building Upon Previous Messages**: Thread 2 references or 
builds upon ideas, questions, or scenarios presented in Thread 1, with clear 
and focused links to what was previously discussed.

corrections, preferences, or additional details. Broadly related topics 
should *not* be merged.
