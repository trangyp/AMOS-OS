Abstract
Mixed-Integer Quadratically Constrained Quadratic Programs arise in a variety of appli-
cations, particularly in energy, water, and gas systems, where discrete decisions interact with
nonconvex quadratic constraints. These problems are computationally challenging due to the
combination of combinatorial complexity and nonconvexity, often rendering traditional exact
methods ineffective for large-scale instances. In this paper, we propose a solution framework
for sparse MIQCQPs that integrates semidefinite programming relaxations with chordal de-
composition techniques to exploit both term and correlative sparsity. By leveraging problem
structure, we significantly reduce the size of the semidefinite constraints into smaller, tractable
blocks, improving the scalability of the relaxation and the overall branch-and-bound procedure.
We evaluate our framework on the Unit Commitment problem with AC Optimal Power Flow
constraints to show that our method produces strong bounds and high-quality solutions on stan-
dard IEEE test cases up to 118 buses, demonstrating its effectiveness and scalability in solving
MIQCQPs.
Keywords. Nonlinear programming, mixed-integer programming, semidefinite programming,
global optimization, optimal power flow, unit commitment.

Introduction
Mixed-Integer Quadratically Constrained Quadratic Programming (MIQCQP) represents a class
of optimization problems characterized by the presence of both continuous and discrete decision
variables, where the objective function and constraints are quadratic. MIQCQP problems are gen-
erally NP-hard due to the combination of nonconvex quadratic terms and integrality restrictions.
The integration of integer variables within a QCQP framework significantly increases computational
complexity, requiring advanced solution techniques. MIQCQPs arise naturally in numerous real-
world applications, particularly in energy systems (Kocuk et al., 2017; Bingane et al., 2019), water
distribution networks (Bragalli et al., 2012; Faria & Bagajewicz, 2011), gas networks (Li et al., 2024;

Liers et al., 2021), and engineering design (Misener & Floudas, 2009). In these domains, many prob-
lems involve discrete decision variables alongside quadratic constraints, making MIQCQPs difficult
to solve at scale.
The primary challenge in solving MIQCQPs stems from the interplay between combinatorial
decisions and the nonconvexity introduced by quadratic constraints. Traditional exact methods
such as branch-and-bound struggle with scalability as the problem size increases. Consequently,
researchers have explored convex relaxations, including Semidefinite Programming (SDP), as in
Anjos and Lasserre (2011), Second-Order Cone Programming (SOCP), as in Kim and Kojima (2003)
and Kuang et al. (2019), and Reformulation-Linearization Techniques(RLT), as in Sherali (2002),
to provide tractable approximations while maintaining strong bounds on optimality. While recent
years have seen progress in convex relaxations and global optimization techniques for quadratic
programming, the computational burden remains substantial, especially for large-scale MIQCQPs.
One promising direction is to use SDP relaxations to handle the nonconvex constraints, leading
to Mixed-Integer Semidefinite Programs (MISDP). Despite their modeling power, MISDPs pose
significant computational challenges due to their high dimensionality and the presence of both
discrete and positive semidefinite constraints. A promising direction to reduce the size of the SDP
matrices is to exploit the sparsity and structure inherent in many real-world instances to design
more scalable and efficient relaxations. In particular, many problems that are modeled as MIQCQPs
exhibit different types of sparsity. For instance, they can exhibit term sparsity where each quadratic
constraint or objective term involves only a small subset of variables, and correlative sparsity,
where variable interactions are localized and can be represented as sparse graphs. By leveraging
these structures, we employ chordal decomposition techniques to break down large semidefinite
constraints into smaller ones. This significantly reduces both the computational burden of solving
SDP relaxations and the complexity of branch-and-bound procedures that embed them.

is quadratic and the constraints are represented by a set of quadratic inequalities. The general

3
Methodology
As highlighted in the previous section, one of the most successful paradigms for solving mixed-integer
programs is the use of branch-and-bound algorithms. We consider a branch-and-bound framework
in which branching decisions are applied to the binary variables y, while the continuous variables x
are handled within each node through efficient relaxations. This approach leverages the fact that
fixing subsets of y leads to tractable subproblems where decisions x can be optimized efficiently,
either through problem-specific relaxations and/or exploiting the problem structure. This strategy
is particularly well-suited to problems where the binary decisions drive structural or combinatorial
configurations, and the associated continuous variables represent operational or flow quantities that
can be optimized given a fixed binary configuration. By branching on y, the algorithm incrementally
explores the discrete decision space and, at each node, the resulting subproblem, now a quadratic
continuous optimization problem in x, can often be solved using efficient convex relaxations. This
approach enables the exploitation of problem decomposability and structure, especially in appli-
cations such as energy networks, transportation design, and facility planning, where the binary
decisions encode dispatching or routing, and the continuous variables determine optimal operations
conditioned on those decisions.
The success and the computational efficiency of the branch-and-bound procedure strongly de-
pend on the quality of the relaxation, the early generation of feasible solutions to get good upper
bounds, and the branching rules used to define the ensuing subproblems. Exploiting the structure
of the relaxation can help speed up the branch-and-bound process by reducing the computational
cost at each node. The following sections describe the components of the branch-and-bound algo-
rithm proposed to solve mixed-integer quadratically constrained quadratic problems formulated as
[MIQCQP].
3.1
Lower bounds through conic relaxations
To solve [MIQCQP] efficiently, one would require strong relaxations, particularly in large-scale set-
tings. One common approach to solve such problems is to use MISOCP relaxations, which provide
convex approximations of the nonconvex quadratic constraints. However, while MISOCP relax-
ations can offer good solution bounds, they become computationally prohibitive as the problem size
increases due to the large number of conic constraints and their complexity (Benson & Sağlam,
2013). On the other hand, mixed-integer semidefinite programming (MISDP) though also challeng-
ing in general, can be highly effective for MIQCQPs that arise from network-structured problems,
where they can lead to a more favorable trade-off between solution quality and computational time
(Buchheim & Wiegele, 2013). Network problems typically exhibit strong sparsity and structural
properties that can be exploited using correlative and term sparsity. As we discuss in Section 3.2, we
leverage this sparsity structure to focus on MISDP relaxations, since they provide a good balance
between solution quality and scalability for large-scale optimization problems. Next, we present the
SDP relaxation with binary variables of [MIQCQP], which builds upon matrix W = xxT:

3.2
Exploiting structure: Sparsity
Many real-world problems such as those that arise in energy, gas, and water networks are represented
by sparse graphs because the degree of most nodes in these networks is small. We consider two
strategies to leverage sparsity based on the definitions of correlative and term sparsity, as described
in Wang et al. (2021, 2022), and implement the general approaches from those papers for [SDP-R].
This allows us to take advantage of any correlative and term sparsity patterns that might be present.
Next, we provide more details about the above two types of sparsity.
There has been a lot of research in the literature on how to reduce the size of SDP constraints,
for example by decomposing the matrix ˆW into smaller submatrices Wk in a way that ˆW ⪰0
if and only if all Wk are PSD. In Ghaddar et al. (2016, 2017), the so-called correlative sparsity
(subsets of variables that appear separately in constraints) was considered. Exploiting it allowed
solving large-scale water and energy networks. Another form of sparsity is term sparsity, which is
particularly useful when there are few monomials present in the problem. Term sparsity has not
yet been applied extensively and, to the best of our knowledge, the only network problem to which
it has been applied is the optimal power flow problem (Wang et al., 2022). Thus, investigating
the term sparsity for [SDP-R] is a relevant question as different networks often exhibit a similar
structure. The authors in Wang et al. (2022) combine the two approaches to decompose the SDP
matrix of the optimal power flow problem, exploiting the correlative sparsity (Waki et al., 2006)
and the term sparsity (Wang et al., 2021) at the same time.
Correlative sparsity, CS, was introduced in Lasserre (2006) and Waki et al. (2006, 2008) and
exploits the fact that there are few variables per constraint. The CS approach preemptively omits
monomials that are unlikely to contribute to the solution.
To implement the CS approach, we
introduce the correlative sparsity graph of [MIQCQP]. The vertices of this graph consist of all the
continuous variables in the given optimization problem, and there is an edge between two vertices
if the corresponding x variables appear together in the same monomial in the constraints or the
objective function. To exploit correlative sparsity, one proceeds in three steps. First, one needs to
perform a chordal extension of the CS graph if this graph is not originally chordal. The second step
is to decompose the (possibly chordally extended) CS graph into cliques corresponding to the subsets
of variables. A clique in this context is a subset of the graph where every two distinct vertices (i.e.,
variables) are adjacent. In Waki et al. (2006), maximal cliques are used. The final step is to construct
6


## Page 7


a sparse SDP relaxation where, for each clique identified in the previous step, we formulate SDP
matrices that are smaller and pertain only to the variables within that clique. Additional equality
constraints are added by exploiting the clique graph to ensure there is consistency in the resulting
SDP matrices representation.
As follows from its name, term sparsity, TS, exploits having few terms, or monomials, per
constraint. As a result, each SDP matrix is defined on a subset of monomials. The concept and
its numerical implementations have been introduced in Wang et al. (2021, 2022).
In order to
formally introduce term sparsity, it is convenient to describe with the help of the term sparsity
graph, TS graph, whose vertices correspond to standard monomial basis for the objective function
and constraints and two nodes are joined if the product of the two basis elements is a monomial in
the problem. Unlike correlative sparsity, which is based on variable connections, term sparsity is
based on monomial dependencies.

3.3
Upper bounds from local solver
In a branch-and-bound algorithm, it is of particular importance to find good feasible solutions as
early as possible to increase the fathoming of nodes, reducing the search space. Unfortunately,
obtaining feasible solutions for general MIQCQP problems is not an easy task, since it is often
already challenging to find feasible solutions for general binary linear programs. In our solution
framework for MIQCQP problems, we integrate a MINLP local solver to generate feasible solutions
during the branch-and-bound process. Given that calls to such solvers are also computationally
demanding, the frequency and strategy for invoking this local solver can significantly affect both
solution quality and overall computational performance. In general, since we want to find good
feasible solutions early on, the local solver is called more often in the initial iterations. The trade-
off is clear here: calling the local solver too frequently adds computational overhead, and calling
it too infrequently may result in delayed discovery of good, feasible solutions.
To balance this
trade-off, the local solver is initiated whenever the number of iterations, iter, satisfies the following
condition:
iter ≥run_local(nlocal+3)
or
iter = 1,
where nlocal is the number of times the local solver has been previously called and run_local is a
fixed parameter that controls the frequency of the calls: the larger run_local is, the less frequently
the local solver is invoked.
In this framework, when the local solver is invoked, we provide it with an initial solution derived
from the optimal solution of the relaxation at the last solved node. This initial point includes a
combination of fixed binary values, enforced by prior branching decisions, and initial values for other
binary and continuous variables. Specifically, the binary variables that have not yet been fixed are
rounded to the nearest integer, while those constrained by the branching rules are explicitly fixed
within the solver. Calling the local solver with different initial solutions at different nodes enables
8


## Page 9


the discovery of different feasible solutions, increasing the likelihood of finding better upper bounds
for [MIQCQP] early in the search.
Apart from the above calls, the local solver is also called at some other points of the algorithm.
Recall that the underlying SDP relaxation, [SDP-R], is obtained by relaxing the integrality con-
straints of the [MISDP] formulation. [SDP-R] is used within the branch-and-bound algorithm to
compute a lower bound for [MISDP] and, by extension, for [MIQCQP]. As the branch-and-bound
algorithm unfolds, whenever an optimal solution for [SDP-R] is also feasible for [MISDP], if this
solution leads to an improved upper bound, the local solver is called, hoping to get a close solution
for [MIQCQP]; nlocal is not updated after these calls. The procedure remains similar: we pass this
solution to the local solver, where all y variables initially take binary values. Yet, these variables
are not fixed and can be modified by the local solver. Conversely, whenever a call to the local
solver finds a feasible solution, there is an additional call to solve [SDP-R] with all binary variables
fixed to the values of this solution. This should deliver a solution to [MISDP] with an objective
function equal or better than the one obtained by the local solver; ensuring that the upper bound
for [MISDP] is always equal or better than the upper bound for [MIQCQP].

4
Use Case on Unit Commitment with Optimal Power Flow Con-
straints
The integration of renewable energy sources and increasing demand for resilient and efficient power
systems have intensified the need for advanced optimization techniques capable of modeling the
inherent complexity of modern electricity markets. In this context, the Unit Commitment (UC)
problem, which involves determining the optimal scheduling of generating units, plays a critical
role in ensuring the reliability and economic operation of power systems. When augmented with
AC Optimal Power Flow (ACOPF) constraints, the UC problem captures both operational and
network-level physics, resulting in a highly nonconvex and large-scale MIQCQP formulation. To
address the nonconvexity of ACOPF constraints, recent advances have explored convex relaxations,
notably SDP relaxations, which offer tight bounds for AC power flow models. When coupled with
integer decisions from the UC problem, this leads to an MISDP problem. The goal of this section
is to analyze the potential of the algorithmic approach described in Section 3 by applying it to
the aforementioned challenging problem, also called UC-OPF. In order to do so, we first need to
formally define the two problems involved in UC-OPF: the optimal power flow problem and the
unit commitment problem. Both of these problems have been extensively studied in the literature
each on its own, however very few work consider the joint problem due to the complexity and the
challenges behind its solution.
4.1
AC optimal power flow problem
The AC optimal power flow problem, hereafter OPF, was introduced in Carpentier (1962). Several
formulations exist for this class of problems, but the common objective of all of them is to decide
how to optimally operate a power distribution network given the nonconvex constraints imposed
by physics’ laws and other limits related to the operations. OPF can be adapted to be used for
long-term or short-term planning, but regardless of the formulation employed, the use of the power
flow equations is what characterizes this problem and also what makes it hard to solve. It is a
nonlinear, nonconvex optimization problem and, as mentioned in Lehmann et al. (2016), it is NP-
hard. There is abundant literature devoted to proposing different approaches to solve this problem:
from simplifications of the model using DC power (Frank et al., 2012) to specific algorithms designed
for this particular problem as well as conic based relaxations (Lavaei & Low, 2012; Kocuk et al.,
2018; Bienstock & Villagra, 2025; Chowdhury et al., 2025).
In Lavaei and Low (2012) a formulation using rectangular coordinates is introduced. The prob-
lem is modeled as a network of electrical buses (nodes) connected by branches (arcs or edges).
The branches, i.e., the connections between the buses, are the representation of transmission lines,
cables, transformers, and other equipment. The objective of the system is to transfer electrical
energy from the generation buses (supply) to the load buses (demand). Since we are dealing with a
network, each bus is an element of the set of nodes, denoted by N, and each branch belongs to the

4.3
Security-constrained unit commitment problem
UC has been used successfully to obtain significant savings in the operation of a particular power
network as can be seen in Carlson et al. (2012). However, the UC problem might deliver solutions
that can’t be used in practice, as they might compromise the stability of the power network. This
is the reason behind the interest in adding the OPF constraints to the UC problem, resulting in
problem UC-OPF. This new problem has the scheduling capability of UC but, at the same time,
since it has the power flow equations included, the physical properties in the network are guaranteed
for the obtained solution.
The resulting optimization problem combines the constraints from UC and OPF problems, the
former being a combinatorial problem with binary variables and the latter a nonconvex nonlinear
problem. Therefore, the resulting UC-OPF optimization problem is a challenging problem to solve,
as it combines the complexity of both underlying problems. Incorporating AC network constraints
into the UC problem can provide significant merit in terms of feasibility and economic value. There
has been substantial effort in developing different approaches to obtain feasible solutions for the
UC-OPF problem, see for instance the surveys Padhy (2004) and Bhardwaj et al. (2012). Most
of the effort has focused on using decomposition methods such as Benders decomposition (Fu et
al., 2006; Sifuentes & Vargas, 2007; Nasri et al., 2016) or outer approximations and Lagrangian
relaxations (Murillo-Sanchez & Thomas, 1999; Ma & Shahidehpour, 1999). Recently, high-quality
solutions to realistic industry-scale UC-OPF problems were presented by Brun et al. (2025) as
part of the Grid Optimization Competition (Elbert et al., 2024), who decompose the problem and
apply a penalty-based alternating direction method to solve it. Similarly, the work of Zhang et al.
(2023) employs an alternating direction algorithm to efficiently obtain good feasible solutions for
the UC-OPF problem. Yet, in general, most of the proposed solution methods do not guarantee
the optimality of the obtained solutions, not even local optimality in some cases. Building upon the
SDP relaxation for the OPF problem, we introduce a new framework to solve the UC-OPF problem
with optimality guarantees. We employ the SDP relaxation, adapting it to the UC-OPF case, and
then embed the resulting mixed-integer SDP problem into the branch-and-bound scheme described
in Section 3.4 to find an optimal solution to the relaxation. This optimal solution is then used to
certify the optimality of a solution to the UC-OPF problem obtained by the use of a local solver
for MINLP problems or, at least, to provide a tight optimality gap for it.

5.1.3
Algorithm configuration
Our framework consists of four main components: the local solver, the SDP solver, the sparsity
exploitation, and the branch-and-bound algorithm in charge of managing the binary variables.
Below we describe in detail how each of these elements has been set up for the numerical analysis.
Local solver. We begin by describing the local solver. We use Knitro (Byrd et al., 2006) as the
nonlinear programming solver, interfaced through Pyomo (Bynum et al., 2021; Hart et al.,
2011). We rely primarily on Knitro’s default settings, with a few modifications to better suit
our iterative node-based strategy. Specifically, we set mip_multistart to 1, convex to 0, and
mip_terminate to 1. The last setting instructs Knitro to return a feasible solution promptly,
without spending additional time trying to refine it. To safeguard against excessive runtimes
in difficult nodes, we also cap the solver time limit using the mip_maxtime_real option.
SDP solver. We use conic programming solver MOSEK (MOSEK ApS, 2019). We formulate the
SDP relaxation using MOSEK’s Python-based Fusion API, which allows flexible modeling of
conic problems and provides direct access to advanced solver features (MOSEK ApS, 2019).
Sparsity exploitation We follow the approach described in Wang et al. (2021, 2022), to maintain
the theoretical properties of the decompositions. We use their Julia (Bezanson et al., 2017)
package to obtain the decomposition of the Wt matrices. Their approach is called CS-TSSOS
and, as discussed in Section 4.5, it combines correlative sparsity (Waki et al., 2006) and term
sparsity (Wang et al., 2021).
Branch-and-bound algorithm. As mentioned above, we have implemented from scratch a branch-
and-bound algorithm in Python, tailored for solving mixed-integer QCQP problems.
The
parameters of Algorithm 1 are set up as follows:
• tol. Stopping tolerance is set to 0.02, i.e., 2% optimality gap.

• timelimit. Time limit is set to 3600 seconds (1 hour).
• run_local. All instances are solved 4 times, each with a different value of run_local
parameter: 1.1, 1.25, 1.5, and 2. The larger the value of run_local, the smaller the
number of calls to the local solver.

Conclusions
Our primary focus in this work has been finding optimal solutions to MIQCQPs, under the general
assumption that the quadratic objective and constraints are nonconvex, i.e., not all quadratic forms
involved have positive semidefinite Hessian. This class of problems combines two types of noncon-
vexity: discreteness of a subset of variables and nonconvex nonlinear constraints and/or objective
function. While branch-and-bound is the algorithm of choice to find optimal solutions of nonconvex
optimization problems, plain branch-and-bound implementations that use simple McCormick re-
laxations of the quadratic constraints show poor performance due to the loose relaxation that only
considers single bilinear terms. The SDP relaxation of a nonconvex QCQP provides much tighter
bounds, but suffers from scalability problems and the lack of warm-starting mechanisms, the latter
preventing its effective use in a branch-and-bound framework.
We propose a solution algorithm that exploits a key fact about several practical applications:
their MIQCQP formulation has a sparse quadratic structure. Specifically, we use term and correla-
tive sparsity in these quadratic forms to reformulate a large-scale SDP relaxation such that it can
be solved more efficiently by enforcing positive semidefiniteness of much smaller submatrices via
chordal extensions. Our solution approach embeds such sparse SDP solver, which uses the chordal
extension tool CS-TSSOS, within a branch-and-bound algorithm, thus obtaining tight bounds and
faster node solves. We add calls to an external local solver that helps find feasible solutions.
We test our approach on one of the toughest classes of MIQCQP, namely the Unit Commitment
problem under ACOPF constraints. While Unit Commitment introduces binary variables and logical
constraints for the operation of a power grid across multiple time periods, ACOPF constraints are
quadratic, nonconvex, and sparse due to the sparse topology of the power grid. This is perhaps a
quintessential hard MIQCQP in that it combines two problems that are NP-hard in their own right:
optimization under ACOPF constraints and Unit Commitment. Our approach can tackle problems
with up to 118 nodes and 186 edges and for 24 time periods with a small optimality gap.
These results attest to the effectiveness of our proposed algorithm.
Due to the complexity
of the proposed approach, in our discussion of the computational results, we provide a detailed
account of the influence of its components: tightness of the SDP relaxation, performance when
using correlative/term sparsity, frequency of the calls to a local solver, as well as the sensitivity of
our solver to variants of the original instances.
The results we have obtained suggest possible research directions for this method. First, our
approach is based on a simple branch-and-bound implementation that does not incorporate all
routines that are critical to its effectiveness, such as presolving and cutting. Moreover, it uses an
external SDP solver (although very efficient) and a separate tool to handle term and correlative
sparsity. It would be interesting to know whether a more tightly integrated solver, based on a state-
of-the-art branch-and-cut solver, can further improve the performance on nonconvex MIQCQP.

Another possible research direction consists of replacing the full resolution of the reduced-size
SDP relaxation by adding SDP cuts (Sherali & Fraticelli, 2002) based on the same decomposition.
This would take advantage of warm-starting capabilities in solving SDP relaxations that are crucial
in branch-and-cut algorithms. Finally, while our approach restricts branching rules to integer vari-
ables, it would be interesting to extend branching to continuous variables that appear in bilinear
terms, similar to spatial branching mechanisms that are typical of nonconvex MINLO solvers.
