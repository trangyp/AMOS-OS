On the Smart Coordination of Flexibility Scheduling
in Multi-carrier Integrated Energy Systems
Christian Doh Dinga , Student Member, IEEE, Sander van Rijn , Laurens J. de Vries , Milos Cvetkovic
Abstract—Coordinating the interactions between flexibility
assets in multi-carrier integrated energy systems (MIES) can
lead to an efficient integration of variable renewable energy
resources, and a cost-efficient energy transition. However, the
proliferation of flexibility assets and their participation in active
demand response increases the complexity of coordinating these
interactions. This paper introduces different approaches to model
the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES. We propose a
market auction-inspired model coupling approach to address the
challenges of preserving the autonomy and privacy of flexibility
providers, and the issue of scalability. We benchmark our
approach against co-optimization and an iterative price-response
method by conducting experiments with varying problem sizes
and computing infrastructure. We show that our approach scales
well and is suitable for modeling flexibility in large-scale energy
systems in a more realistic way. From an optimality standpoint,
the flexibility dispatch schedules and electricity prices are “near-
optimal”. Our methodology is implemented as a new open-
source software, which offers several practical applications. For
example, flexibility providers and network operators can couple
their models to simulate the interaction between their systems
without disclosing confidential information; policy regulators
can use it to investigate new market design and regulations to
optimize the utilization of flexibility in MIES.
Index Terms—Multi-energy systems, Distributed energy re-
sources, Demand response, Electricity markets, Model coupling.

I. INTRODUCTION
E
LECTRIFICATION of energy demand increases the
interactions and interdependencies between electricity,
(hydrogen) gas, and heat systems, resulting in multi-carrier
integrated energy systems (MIES). While energy system in-
tegration offers new sources of flexibility, the inclusion of
many different types of distributed energy resources (herein,
referred to as flexibility assets) increases the complexity of

the energy system [1]. This is because it becomes challenging
to coordinate the interactions between demand-side flexibility
assets (e.g., electric vehicles, electrolyzers, etc.) [2]. Unlike
traditional flexibility providers such as conventional generators
that are (partially) controlled by the system operator, demand-
side flexibility assets are controlled by prosumers who have
full autonomy over their systems. This implies that prosumers
can participate in active demand response where they schedule
their power consumption and/or generation predominantly
based on price signals [3]. As shown in [4], excluding the
autonomy of prosumers in energy system models can result
in lower operational profits, sending misleading investment
signals to flexibility providers. Moreover, disregarding the
autonomy of prosumers fails to capture the potential conse-
quences of demand response. For example, uncoordinated re-
sponse from a large pool of demand-side flexibility assets can
aggravate congestion in the electricity grid if prosumers simul-
taneously ramp up consumption in response to low electricity
prices. Therefore, energy system integration brings along a
methodological challenge—the challenge of coordinating the
interactions among an arbitrarily large number of different
types of flexibility assets (owned and operated by prosumers
with full autonomy) to optimally schedule flexibility.
A. Conventional modeling approach vs. model coupling
The conventional approach to model the coordination of
flexibility scheduling in MIES is based on combined opti-
mization (co-optimization). Co-optimization is a monolithic
approach that models all subsystems using a standalone com-
putational tool to coordinate their interactions by optimizing
a global utility function, e.g., system costs. Li et al. [5] used
co-optimization to optimize flexibility scheduling in a MIES
under uncertainty. In [6], a co-optimization model is developed
to quantify flexibility from electrifying heat demand. Many
similar studies can be found in the literature, for example,
see [7]–[9]. Although widely used, co-optimization has several
drawbacks: (1) its top-down control strategy assumes that
agents give up control over their assets and that local control
(dispatch) decisions are made centrally. This assumption takes
away the autonomy of prosumers, which, as we already
argued, is a key characteristic that reflects the operational real-
ities of flexibility providers. Therefore, while co-optimization
could be a valid approach for modeling a single agent’s
problem, it is, however, not suitable for studying flexibility in a
MIES where many agents (with full autonomy) interact; (2) to
centrally compute dispatch decisions, co-optimization requires
complete information of all agents, including the techno-
economic parameters and internal states of their assets. This
raises a privacy issue as in reality, agents will not be willing
to share such information due to confidentiality reasons; (3)
co-optimization scales poorly with problem size (number of
agents) since all dispatch decisions are computed centrally;
(4) similar to all monolithic methods, co-optimization strug-
gles to accurately represent all dimensions of energy system
integration—the market interactions of agents and physical
interactions of assets. This is because monolithic tools are
generally developed with a particular dimension of interest
in mind, making them either only economic or technical in
nature [10]. So, in order to squeeze “everything” into a single
modeling tool, they make simplifying assumptions that can
result in misleading conclusions [11].
These drawbacks of co-optimization not only limit its ap-
plication for the analysis of flexibility, but also limit its capa-
bility to investigate market design and policy regulations that
influence the behavior of flexibility providers. This is because
the lack of autonomy in agents’ local decision-making makes
it impossible for co-optimization to fully represent prosumer
behavior since agents are modeled as passive market partici-
pants. Also, the impracticality of sharing confidential data due
to privacy concerns makes it difficult for co-optimization to
model the interactions between flexibility providers in a more
realistic way. Therefore, from the perspective of regulators and
policymakers who seek to achieve efficient utilization of flexi-
bility resources, such models are inadequate to investigate the
impact of heterogeneous policy instruments (e.g., electricity
grid tariffs) on prosumer behavior, system-wide social welfare,
and their spillover effects on other energy sectors [12].
To address the limitations of co-optimization, we propose
to use a model coupling-based approach. Model coupling
combines different models developed in various computational
tools by leveraging software techniques and algorithms to
manage data exchange and time progression [13]. Our mo-
tivation for model coupling is twofold: autonomy and privacy.
First, model coupling can preserve autonomy since it enables
agents to locally optimize their dispatch decisions. This makes
it possible to represent prosumer behavior and model their
interactions in a more realistic way. Moreover, preserving
agents’ autonomy makes it possible to simulate each agent
(subsystem) in its native tool with dedicated solvers. From
a computational point of view, this not only increases ac-
curacy, but also increases scalability due to the potential
for parallelization [11]. Second, since coupled models only
need to share interface variables, model coupling offers a
way to include confidential information in simulations without
violating privacy. Another added benefit of model coupling is
that it allows for the reuse of existing software, avoiding the
need to make rudimentary assumptions to develop overarching,
all-encompassing models [14].
In the literature, the alternating direction method of multipli-
ers (ADMM) has been proposed as a possible model coupling
strategy [15]. This model coupling approach, herein referred
to as price-response model coupling, uses a distributed coor-
dination architecture. Agents locally optimize their dispatch
and interact to cooperatively optimize a global utility. The
required information exchange here includes: (1) point-wise
demand-supply timeseries computed locally by each agent as
a response to prices broadcast by the coordinator agent; (2) the
system imbalance computed as the difference between total de-
mand and supply. Given that agents’ dispatch must satisfy the
system power balance constraint that couples their decisions,
prices and dispatch decisions are iteratively updated based
on the imbalance until a consensus price is reached where
the imbalance is below the specified threshold. Generally,
provided an equilibrium exists, this iterative process converges
to a solution (almost) equal to that of co-optimization [16].

However, although optimal, this approach has several draw-
backs. First, the autonomy of agents is somewhat partial since
their dispatch decisions are penalized over iterations to align
them towards cooperatively optimizing a global utility. Second,
in its basic form, the ADMM algorithm is not fully privacy-
preserving [17]. This is because it repeatedly shares the system
imbalance over iterations—a signal that contains the aggregate
dispatch of agents. This iterative process exposes the price-
response method to potential privacy leakage since adversarial
models can reconstruct agents’ subproblems through iterations
[17]. Third, this approach often requires many iterations before
converging, which is computationally expensive since deci-
sions in each iteration are computed over the full simulation
horizon. Hence, price-response scales poorly with complex
systems that have long timeseries. Moreover, there is generally
no guarantee of convergence for such iterative procedures if
the problem involves integer decision variables [18].
B. The proposed model coupling approach
To address the challenges of preserving the full autonomy
and privacy of flexibility providers, and the issue of scala-
bility, we propose a market auction-inspired model coupling
approach that uses a decentralized coordination architecture
instead. This approach is characterized by the following:
(1) it allows for full autonomy in agents’ local dispatch
decisions in a more realistic way. This is because it relaxes the
assumption of a central agent optimizing dispatch decisions in
a top-down control manner, or a coordinator agent penalizing
agents’ local dispatch decisions to align them towards a global
optimum. This, however, comes with a sacrifice in optimality
compared to co-optimization and the price-response method;
(2) the coordination mechanism simulates market auctions by
matching agents’ bids through social welfare maximization,
requiring that information is exchanged within the resolution
and horizon of the market clearing. This approach promises
significant gains in scalability since the full horizon is par-
titioned into smaller windows and simulated in a rolling
manner; (3) it only requires agents to share their demand and
supply bid functions that internalize their techno-economic
and intertemporal constraints. How they construct these bids is
their confidential trade strategy encapsulated in their respective
models. Interestingly, exchanging bid functions rather than
point-wise timeseries eliminates the need for iterations, which
is usually prone to non-convergence.

coordination models as described above is provided in Table I.
As shown, these approaches differ mainly in the coordination
architecture, coordination mechanism, required information

C. Contribution
The main contributions of this paper with respect to the
scientific literature include:
1) We propose a market auction-inspired model cou-
pling approach to model the coordination of flexibility
scheduling in MIES. The software-coupled framework
of our approach makes it possible to model different
subsystems in their domain-specific software, and ex-
ploit parallelism to increase computational efficiency. Its
information exchange requirements eliminate the need
for iterations, which, together with parallelism, make
our approach highly scalable. Finally, its interface de-
sign provides a way to include confidential information
about flexibility into system-wide energy models without
violating privacy.
2) We benchmark our proposed model coupling approach
against co-optimization and price-response methods by
conducting several experimental runs with varying prob-
lem sizes and computing infrastructure. This benchmark
provides insights into the trade-off that energy system
modelers need to make between accuracy (preserving
agents’ autonomy), optimality, and scalability.
3) Our model coupling framework is openly available on
GitLab and archived on Zenodo [19] as a new modeling
software, which the energy system modeling commu-
nity can freely use to explore an alternative approach
to model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in
MIES. The code for running our experiments is also
made open-source [20] to ensure that the results pre-
sented in this paper are reproducible by third parties.

a modeling environment where prosumers, flexibility aggre-
gators, and network operators can couple their models to
simulate the interactions between their systems—to avoid
myopic operational or investment decisions. For example, for
electricity and heat network operators, coupling their models

B. Price-response model coupling
In this coordination model, the problem is formalized as a
set of optimization problems, each representing the dispatch
optimization of a generator or prosumer. A visualization of
the interactions in this approach is illustrated in Fig 3. As
shown, it is an iterative process that starts with a coordinator
agent broadcasting a price timeseries and system imbalance
to agents. Given this information, agents run their local opti-
mization to determine their optimal dispatch. Next, they report
back their electric power generation or consumption timeseries
to the coordinator agent, who then calculates the new system
imbalance. If the stopping criteria is not below the predefined
threshold, a new price timeseries is computed and broadcast,
and the process repeats again. In what follows, we define the
optimization problem of each agent type in iteration k.

III. EXPERIMENT DESIGN AND CASE STUDY
A. Experiment design
To evaluate the viability of our proposed approach, we
benchmark it against co-optimization and price-response by
comparing the generator and prosumer dispatch schedules, sys-
tem costs, electricity prices, and scaling performance of each
method. This benchmark provides insights into the trade-off
between accuracy (preserving agents’ autonomy), optimality,
and scalability. For scalability, we compare how the runtime
of each method scales by varying the following parameters.
1) Problem size: Problem size can be varied along two
dimensions: time (simulation horizon) and space (number of
agents). As a first experiment, we vary problem size while
maintaining the same computing infrastructure. We increase
problem size by increasing the simulation horizon from 1 day
to a week (with hourly resolution) and the number of agents
from 30 to 90. Note that while experiments with larger prob-
lem sizes could be performed, the selected problem sizes are
sufficient to capture scaling trends along temporal and spatial
dimensions, without exhausting the available computational
resources. Yet, our work provides a baseline for future studies
to evaluate the scalability of different energy system models.
2) Computing infrastructure: The different coordination
models make use of different architectures, meaning they also
differ in how much they can benefit from parallelism in the
problem definition. Knowing how much a modeling approach
can benefit from parallelism will help inform which trade-off
the user can make. Naturally, there will also be a limit to
how much benefit can be obtained from parallelism, beyond
which it will not make sense to deploy the modeling approach
on a system with more parallel CPU cores. To give an
initial indication of this trade-off, we vary the computational
resources used to run the benchmark between the authors’
modern laptop with 16 CPU cores, and a more capable 48-
core and 128-core server machine—Snellius [23], as a second
experiment. This will indicate some actual possible speed-up
from parallelism in the modeling approaches.
B. Case study and data source
To use real energy system data for our experiments, we ran
PyPSA-Eur model [22] with Germany only for the year 2013.
The collected data include asset capacities (generators, heat
pumps, etc.), generator marginal costs and hourly availability
profiles for renewables; hourly demand profiles for electricity,
heat, hydrogen, and transport; hourly price profiles for energy
carriers such as methane, hydrogen, and biomass. We increase
the capacity of storage to reflect a system with much flexibility.
A typical node in a PyPSA-Eur model looks like the MIES
in Fig 1, and nodes are interconnected through electricity
transmission lines to form a network. To mimic electricity
market zonal pricing, we wrote Python scripts to flatten the
original network, resulting in a copper plate model in which
all prosumers and generators are connected to a single electric
bus. A prosumer is assumed to own all assets (excluding
generators) that feed into the bus to which it is attached. If
there is more than one demand on a bus, the first is assumed to
be the base demand (inflexible), while the others are assumed
to be flexible. To vary the number of agents for our scaling
experiments, we use PyPSA-Eur clustering functionality to
increase the spatial granularity of the network to generate
the required number of agents. The smallest network instance
(with a spatial granularity of 1) results in about 30 agents, of
which 16 are generators and 14 are prosumers. Increasing the
spatial granularity by a step size of 1 increases the number
of agents by roughly 10, and the proportion of distribution
between agent types is constant; for every 10 agents added,
about 6 are generators and 4 are prosumers.
We used Gurobi solver with default parameters for all
experiments. For the price-response method, we set the primal
and dual residual convergence tolerances to 0.1 and employed
a dynamic penalty parameter update strategy to accelerate
convergence [18]. For the market auction method, satellite
models were assigned a look-ahead horizon of 24 hours, while
the market clearing horizon was set to 1 hour.
IV. RESULTS
A. Accuracy vs. Optimality
This section shows the performance of the three coordi-
nation models in terms of optimality and shows the impact
of preserving agents’ autonomy (accuracy) on the “ideal”
optimum computed by co-optimization.
1) Generator and battery storage dispatch: The dispatch
of generators and battery storage is shown in Fig. 5. As
expected, the generation schedules from co-optimization and
price-response are almost identical. The main difference with
market auction is in the dispatch of gas, CHP, and oil
generators, which are used to satisfy peak demand. In the
market auction method, less power is produced by these three
generators in earlier periods. However, there is a steep ramp-up
in power from these generators thereafter. This difference can
be explained through the battery storage dispatch in Fig. 5(b).
Due to its perfect foresight, full knowledge, and controllability
of the MIES, co-optimization can anticipate high prices in the
future, and therefore dispatch more production from CHP and
gas at an earlier time to charge the battery. The battery is then
used to supply peak demand later, thereby preventing the more
expensive oil generator from being dispatched at all. The same
trend can be observed for the price-response method.
Conversely, the battery schedule from market auction is
completely different. This difference arises from the autonomy
of battery operators, who optimize dispatch based on local
objectives—revenue maximization. As described in algorithm
1, a battery operator looks for the cheapest price in the
forecast and places buy bids there so it can sell later at a
higher price. Since the cheapest price in the forecast appears
at a later time, the battery operator does not charge earlier,
causing the earlier peak demand to be supplied by gas, CHP,
and oil. Such strategic behaviors from flexibility providers,
along with their interactions under imperfect knowledge, are
features that cannot be fully represented in the other two
methods. Therefore, both co-optimization and price-response
are less accurate (with respect to modeling flexibility under
imperfect competition) since both methods disregard features
that are inherent in real-world settings. Our approach can fully

by the way battery storage is operated, triggering the dispatch
of the oil generator. In any case, the price from co-optimization
is not meant to be a strict metric of performance for evaluating
our approach since it results from an ”ideal” world, whereas
actual market outcomes are almost never optimal. The goal
of our approach is not to optimize prices, but to provide a
modeling environment to simulate the behavior of flexibility
providers and their interactions, which can be influenced
through policy regulations to optimize system-wide benefits.

bidding strategy. Such a bidding strategy will require the use
of block bids [24] to handle intertemporal dependencies in a

of satellite models on a per-hour basis after the market has
cleared. It is worth mentioning that our rationale is that the
bidding strategy of agents is to be confidential. This implies
it is the responsibility of agents to come up with their bids
so that their privacy is preserved. Therefore, while it is out
of scope to provide a “perfect” bidding algorithm, yet, our
limited implementation serves as a proof-of-concept that our
approach can be used to couple energy models at scale.

In sum, our results show that the market auction approach
offers superior scalability compared to co-optimization and
price-response, making it particularly well-suited for large-

modeling the coordination of demand-side flexibility assets,
as it offers the benefits of preserving the full autonomy and
privacy of flexibility providers, and is highly scalable.
V. CONCLUSION
This paper introduces and compares several approaches to
model the coordination of flexibility scheduling in MIES. We
propose a market auction-inspired model coupling approach
to deal with the issue of autonomy and privacy of flexibility
providers, since it allows agents to locally optimize their sys-
tems and interact without disclosing confidential information.
We benchmark our proposed approach against co-optimization
and price-response to provide insights into the trade-offs
between accuracy, optimality, and scalability. Results show
that preserving the autonomy and privacy of agents allows for
a more realistic way of modeling flexibility since real-world
features such as strategic behavior and imperfect knowledge
can be captured. We empirically show that our approach
offers superior scalability, making it particularly well-suited
for large-scale implementations.
The proposed methodology is implemented as an open-
source software, which is freely available for the energy
modeling community to explore. A practical contribution of
this software is that market parties, flexibility aggregators,
and network operators can use it to simulate the interaction
between their systems and avoid myopic operational decisions.
Policy regulators can use it as a ”test bed” to investigate new
market design questions and policy instruments to optimize the
integration and utilization of flexibility resources in MIES.
This work can be extended in multiple directions. First, the
bidding strategy of agents can be extended to use block bids
to encapsulate intertemporal dependencies spanning multiple
hours. This will make it possible for the central model to run
with a horizon of arbitrary length. Finally, while copper plate
models are used for all subsystems, these can be extended to
include spatially resolved networks. These are current subjects
of research and will be addressed in our future work.
