Abstract
We improve the status of the problem of determining minimum-sized percolating
sets in a × b × c grids under the 3-neighbour process. Using several new constructions,
we show that optimal percolating sets exist whenever min(a, b, c) ≥7. As an important
step toward this, we also show that all grids with min(a, b, c) ≥4 have a percolating
set whose size exactly achieves the lower bound (ab + ac + bc)/3 whenever this value is
an integer.

Introduction
Let G be a graph and r a positive integer. The r-neighbour bootstrap percolation process on
G begins with an initial set A0 ⊆V (G) of ‘infected’ vertices; at each time step, a new vertex
becomes infected if it has at least r infected neighbours. That is, for t ≥1, the set of vertices
infected at time step t is
At := At−1 ∪{v ∈V (G) : |NG(v) ∩At−1| ≥r}.
We say that A0 percolates under the r-neighbour process on G if ∪∞
t=0At = V (G). An early
application of bootstrap percolation was in modelling the dynamics of ferromagnetism [2].

For finite graphs, it is natural to ask for the smallest size of an initial set which percolates.
This paper builds on some recent work on the case r = 3 for a special class of graphs.
For positive integers a1, . . . , ad, the d-dimensional grid graph with side lengths a1, . . . , ad
has vertex set [a1] × · · · × [ad], with two vertices u = (u1, . . . , ud) and v = (v1, . . . , vd)
adjacent if and only if, for some i ∈[d] we have |ui −vi| = 1 and uj = vj for all j ̸= i.
Alternatively, this grid is the Cartesian product of paths Pa1□. . . □Pad. A focal point of
research on bootstrap percolation assumes G is a grid graph; see for instance [1, 3, 4, 5, 6]
and the references therein. The first two of these references make significant progress on
the extremal problem with r = d = 3; in other words, they investigated the smallest size of
a percolating set under the 3-neighbour process on 3-dimensional grids. In particular, this
question was answered in [3] for a × b × c grids whenever min(a, b, c) ≥11. One of our main
results, Theorem 4.3, is to extend this to min(a, b, c) ≥7.
It turns out (see Lemma 2.1) that an initial set A0 which percolates on the a × b × c grid
must satisfy the lower bound |A0| ≥(ab + ac + bc)/3. When equality holds, the right side
is an integer and the 3-neighbour process always infects each new vertex in At with exactly
three neighbours in At−1. We say that a grid is perfect if it admits a percolating set A0
exactly meeting this condition.
For convenience, we abbreviate the grid graph Pa□Pb□Pc with the ordered triple (a, b, c)
and say that it has thickness equal to min(a, b, c). It was shown in [1] that grids (1, 2k −
1, 2k −1) are perfect under the 3-neighbour process for each positive integer k. Later, it was
shown in [3] that these are the only perfect grids of thickness 1. Of course, grids of thickness
1 are equivalent to 2-dimensional grids, so the problem is fully settled for d = 2.
Using various nicely structured and computer generated initial sets A0, it was also shown
in [3] that all grids (a, b, c) with 3 | ab+ac+bc and thickness at least 5 are perfect. Here, we
reduce this minimum thickness to 4. This is helpful for our above-mentioned result answering
the overall extremal problem for thickness at least 7.
In the next section, we give the lower bound on |A0| for r = d = 3. Proofs also appear
in earlier work on the topic, but we sketch a proof here for completeness. We also review
a useful recursive construction from [3] that assembles a minimum-sized percolating set in
terms of those for four smaller grids. In Section 3, we give several new constructions of
perfect grids having thickness 2 or 4. Some of these are infinite families and analyzed in
more detail; illustrations showing the percolation process are also given in the appendix. In
Sections 3 and 4, we feed these into the recursive construction to prove our main results. We
conclude in Section 5 with a summary of the status of the problem.

percolates under the 3-neighbour process. Then
|A0| ≥ab + ac + bc

Proof. Let G denote the grid and consider a set A ⊆V (G). For x ∈A, let n(A, x) :=

Now, suppose A0, A1, . . . , At = V (G) is a sequence of steps in the 3-neighbour process
on G. The key observation is that the quantity 6|Ai| −n(Ai) is nonincreasing over time
steps i = 0, 1, . . . , t. This is because each newly infected vertex x ∈Ai+1 \ Ai adds six but
subtracts two for every neighbour of x in Ai.
Suppose min(a, b, c) ≥2. There are (a−2)(b−2)(c−2) vertices of degree 6, 2(a−2)(b−
2)+2(a−2)(c−2)+2(b−2)(c−2) vertices of degree 5, 4(a−2)+4(b−2)+4(c−2) vertices
of degree 4, and 8 vertices of degree 3. So, after simplification,
n(G) = 6abc −2ab −2ac −2bc.
(2.3)
Suppose, on the other hand, that min(a, b, c) = 1. If a = 1 and b, c ≥2, there are (b−2)(c−2)
vertices of degree 4, 2(b −2) + 2(c −2) vertices of degree 3, and 4 vertices of degree 2. This
gives n(G) = 4bc −2b −2c, agreeing with (2.3). The case a = b = 1 (G = Pc) is trivial, with
n(G) = 2c. This also matches (2.3).
It follows that
6|A0| ≥6|A0| −n(A0) ≥6|At| −n(At) = 2ab + 2ac + 2bc,
which implies the bound (2.2).
A grid (a, b, c) is perfect if it has a percolating set A0 achieving the lower bound in (2.2);
it is optimal if it has a percolating set A0 of size equal to the ceiling of the fraction on the
right of (2.2).
It is easy to see that a grid (a, b, c) can be perfect only if either two of the integers a, b, c
are divisible by three, or all three of a, b, c are in the same congruence class (mod 3). Further
details, including a more general lower bound for higher-dimensional grids, can be found in
[3].
Next is an important recursive construction used in earlier work, and also useful for our
main results to follow.
Lemma 2.4 ([3]). Let ai, bi, ci be positive integers for i ∈{1, 2}. Suppose that each of the
grids (a2, b2, c1), (a2, b1, c2) and (a1, b2, c2) is perfect.
(a) If (a1, b1, c1) is perfect, then so is (a1 + a2, b1 + b2, c1 + c2).
(b) If (a1, b1, c1) is optimal, then so is (a1 + a2, b1 + b2, c1 + c2).

Perfect grids of thickness 2 and 4
Recall that the grid (1, b, c) is perfect only if b = c = 2k −1 for some positive integer
k.
Therefore, it is of limited use to apply Lemma 2.4(a) with grids of thickness one as
ingredients. In more detail, if (say) a1 = 1, then the construction would require b1 = c1 and
b2 = c2. This would result in perfect grids (a, b, c) only when b = c, and indeed only for a
sparse set of such values.
The construction becomes more versatile using grids of thickness 2 as ingredients. In [3],
it was shown that grids of the form (2, b, c) are perfect if b, c ≥3 have different parity and
3 | 2(b + c) + bc. This is summarized below for later reference.
Lemma 3.1 ([3]). The grid (2, b, c) is perfect if b, c ≥3, b ≡c ≡0 or 2 (mod 3), and
b ≡c + 3 (mod 6).
To supplement these constructions, the next lemma gives some perfect constructions for
thickness 2 which do not have the parity restriction on b, c.
Lemma 3.2. The following grids are perfect:
(a) (2, 3, c) and (2, 6, c) for all c ≡0 (mod 3), c ≥6.
(b) (2, 5, c) and (2, 8, c) for all c ≡2 (mod 3), c ≥5.
Proof. We focus on the cases not already covered by Lemma 3.1. The cases (2, 3, c) for
c ≡3 (mod 6) were shown to be perfect in Proposition 5.4 of [3]. For the remaining cases,
the reader is referred to Appendix A.2, which gives constructions based on periodic initial
sets.
Although Lemma 3.2 suffices for our purposes, we remark that certain cases admit easy
generalizations to two parameter families (2, b, c) with general values b ≡c (mod 6).
Shortly, it will be shown that Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 combine via the recursive construction
to settle most cases with thickness 4. However, we need a few special constructions when
one or both of the other sides is small. The next two lemmas cover these cases.
Lemma 3.3. The following grids are perfect: (4, 6, 6), (4, 6, 9), and (4, 9, 9).
Proof. For (4, 6, 6), we can apply Lemma 2.4 with (a1, a2) = (1, 3) and (b1, b2) = (c1, c2) =
(3, 3). For (4, 6, 9) and (4, 9, 9), explicit constructions meeting the bound (2.2) were found by
computer. These are given in the appendix as Constructions A.1 and A.2, respectively.
Lemma 3.4. The following grids are perfect:
(a) (4, 4, c) for all c ≡1 (mod 3), c ≥4.
(b) (4, 7, c) for all c ≡1 (mod 3), c ≥4.
Proof. We divide each case into slightly separate constructions according to the parity of c.
Similar to the thickness 2 families, the reader is referred to Appendix A.2 for constructions
based on periodic initial sets.

We are now ready for the first of our main results, which characterizes all perfect grids
of the form (4, b, c).
Theorem 3.5. The grid (4, b, c) is perfect for all b ≡c ≡0 or 1 (mod 3), b, c ≥4.
Proof. Suppose without loss of generality that b ≤c. For b ∈{4, 7}, we make direct use of
Lemma 3.4. If both b, c ∈{6, 9}, we apply Lemma 3.3. Therefore, in what follows we may
assume c ≥10. We divide the remainder of the proof according to cases on the congruence
classes of b and c (mod 6). In each of these cases, we use Lemma 2.4(a) with a1 = a2 = 2,
and choose b1, b2, c1, c2 such that b1 + b2 = b and c1 + c2 = c.
Case 1: b ≡c ≡0 (mod 3).
First, if b = 6 and c ≥12, we can take (b1, c1) =
(3, 6).
Perfect grids exist for (2, 3, 6) and (2, 3, c −6) by Lemma 3.2(a).
Suppose next
that b ≡c (mod 2) with b ≥9 and c ≥15.
We can again take (b1, c1) = (3, 6).
The
grids (2, 3, 6), (2, 3, c −6), (2, b −3, 6) are perfect by Lemma 3.2(a), and (2, b −3, c −6) is
perfect by Lemma 3.1. Suppose b ̸≡c (mod 2) with b ≥9 and c ≥12. This time, we take
(b1, c1) = (6, 6). The grids (2, 6, 6), (2, 6, c−6) and (2, b−6, 6) are perfect by Lemma 3.2 and
(2, b −6, c −6) is perfect by Lemma 3.1. In each of these cases, the recursive construction
Lemma 2.4(a) with the indicated ingredients gives us that (4, b, c) is perfect.
Case 2: b ≡c ≡1 (mod 3). If b = c = 10, we can apply the recursive construction with
four copies of (2, 5, 5), which is perfect by Lemma 3.2. So assume c ≥13 in the remainder of
the argument. If b ≡c (mod 2), use (b1, c1) = (5, 8). If b ̸≡c (mod 2), use (b1, c1) = (5, 5).
In either case, b2, c2 are each at least 5 and have different parity. Thus all required perfect
grids exist by Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2(b).
The recursive construction shows that (4, b, c) is
perfect.

Optimal grids of thickness 7 to 10
In this section, we use Theorem 3.5 to lower the known minimum side length of optimal
3-dimensional grids under the 3-neighbour process. First, we set up some helpful specific
constructions, beginning with a series of small optimal grids found by computer search.
Lemma 4.1. The grid (a, b, c) is optimal whenever a ∈{4, 5} and b, c ∈{2, . . . , 7}.
Proof. Perfect constructions for min(a, b, c) ∈{2, 3} follow from various small cases found in
[3], and for min(a, b, c) ∈{4, 5} from Theorem 3.5. Otherwise, explicit optimal (non-perfect)
constructions for the remaining cases are given in Appendix A.3.
The next lemma summarizes various results in Section 5 of [3].
Lemma 4.2. The grid (3, u, v) is perfect whenever u ∈{2, . . . , 7} and v ≡3 (mod 6), with
the exception of (u, v) = (2, 3).
Proof. These follow directly from the following four results in [3]: Proposition 5.4 for u =
2, Proposition 5.9 for u = 4, Proposition 5.11 for u = 6, and Proposition 5.13 for u ∈
{3, 5, 7}.

With these tools in place, we are ready for our main result on optimal 3-dimensional
grids.
Theorem 4.3. The grid (a, b, c) is optimal whenever min(a, b, c) ≥7.
Proof. Suppose without loss of generality that 7 ≤a ≤b ≤c. Theorem 1.6 of [3] handles
all cases with a ≥11. The proof of this uses the recursive construction, stated here as our
Lemma 2.4(b), combining one optimal and three perfect grids of thickness at least 5. With
our Theorem 3.5 and Lemma 4.1, we have access to optimal and perfect grids of thickness
at least 4. The same proof as in [3] now works for a = 10. We give a minor modification of
the argument for each of the cases a ∈{7, 8, 9}.
Suppose a = 9. If either 3 | b or 3 | c, then (a, b, c) is perfect by Theorem 6.3 of [3].
Otherwise, let a1 = 6 and choose b1, c1 ∈{2, 4, 5, 7} such that b −b1 ≡c −c1 ≡3 (mod 6).
Put a2 = 3, b2 = b −b1, and c2 = c −c1. The grids (a2, b1, c2) and (a2, b2, c1) are perfect
by Lemma 4.2. The grid (a1, b2, c2) is perfect, either by Lemma 4.2 if min(b2, c2) = 3 or by
Theorem 6.3 of [3] when min(b2, c2) ≥9. The grid (a1, b1, c1) is optimal, either by Lemma 4.1
if max(b1, c1) ≤5 or in the case (6, 7, 7) via optimal (3, 4, 4) and perfect grids (3, 3, 3) and
(3, 3, 4). With these choices of ai, bi, ci, Lemma 2.4(b) implies that (a, b, c) is optimal.
Suppose a = 8. If b ≡c ≡0 (mod 3) or 2 (mod 3), then (8, b, c) is perfect by Theorem
6.3 of [3]. Otherwise, let a1 = 5 and choose b1, c1 ∈{2, . . . , 7} such that b −b1 ≡c −c1 ≡3
(mod 6). Put a2 = 3, b2 = b −b1, and c2 = c −c1. Since b2, c2 ≡3 (mod 6), we know that
(a1, b2, c2) = (5, b2, c2) is perfect, either by Theorem 6.3 of [3] if b2, c2 ≥9, or by Lemma 4.2
if b2 or c2 = 3. Suppose, for the moment, that neither (b1, c2) nor (c1, b2) equals (2, 3). Then
each of (a2, b1, c2) = (3, b1, c2) and (a2, b2, c1) = (3, b2, c1) is perfect by Lemma 4.2.
The case (b1, c2) = (2, 3) implies c = c1 + c2 ≤7 + 3 = 10 while b = b1 + b2 ≥2 + 9 = 11,
a contradiction to our assumption that a ≤b ≤c. The case (b2, c1) = (3, 2) implies b =
b1 + b2 ≤7 + 3 = 10, and hence b ∈{8, 9, 10}. Since perfect grids of thickness 8 are already
settled and c ≡c2 = 2 (mod 3), we may assume b ̸= 8.
For b = 9 or 10, we use the minor modification (b1, b2) = (3, 6) or (4, 6), respectively. We
again have that (a1, b2, c2) = (5, 6, c2) is perfect, this time using Lemma 4.1 if c2 = 3. The
grids (a2, b1, c2) = (3, b1, c2) and (a2, b2, c1) = (3, 6, 2) are perfect by Lemma 4.2.
The grid (a1, b1, c1) = (5, b1, c1) is optimal for each choice of b1 and c1, by Lemma 4.1. It
follows by Lemma 2.4(b) that (a, b, c) is optimal.
Finally, suppose a = 7. The proof in this case is similar to that for a = 8, except for the
following differences. Perfect cases occur when b ≡c ≡0 (mod 3) or 1 (mod 3). We use
(a1, a2) = (4, 3). When b2 ≡c2 ≡3 (mod 6), the grid (a1, b2, c2) is perfect by Theorem 3.5.
Grids (a2, b1, c2) and (a2, b2, c1) are again perfect by Lemma 4.2 unless (b2, c1) = (3, 2). This
only occurs for b ≤10 and c ≡5 (mod 6). The same patch taking b2 = 6 works except
when b = 7. In that case, we have (a, b, c) = (7, 7, c). Here, we can use (a1, a2) = (b1, b2) =
(4, 3). When c = 11, the choice c1 = 5 yields one optimal and three perfect ingredients
by Lemma 4.1. When c ≥17, the recursive construction with c1 = 8 works assuming an
optimal grid (4, 4, 8) and perfect grids (3, 3, 8) and (3, 4, c −8). The first of these is given as
Construction A.30 in the Appendix and the other two are shown to exist in Propositions 5.8
and 5.9, respectively, of [3].

Conclusion
In this paper, we have studied the 3-neighbour bootstrap percolation process on a×b×c grids.
We have extended the near-classification of perfect grids via several new constructions. In
particular, it is now known that (a, b, c) is perfect whenever min(a, b, c) ≥4 and 3 | ab+ac+bc.
As a consequence, we have also shown that (a, b, c) is optimal whenever min(a, b, c) ≥7.
Perfect two-dimensional grids were fully classified in [3].
To complete the classifica-
tion of perfect three-dimensional grids, all that remains is to find certain still outstanding
two-parameter perfect constructions for grids of thickness 2 and 3. Following this, a full
classification of optimal 3-dimensional grids should be within reach. The non-optimal grids
are almost surely limited to (2, 3, 3), (2, 2, c) for c ≥8, and (1, b, c) for certain cases of b
and c. In the first case, the minimum size of a percolating set is 8. In the second family
of cases, a straightforward argument gives ⌈3c+1
2 ⌉as the minimum size. Finally, according
to a very recent seminar announcement, Neal Bushaw has completed the determination of
minimum-sized percolating sets in two-dimensional grids.
