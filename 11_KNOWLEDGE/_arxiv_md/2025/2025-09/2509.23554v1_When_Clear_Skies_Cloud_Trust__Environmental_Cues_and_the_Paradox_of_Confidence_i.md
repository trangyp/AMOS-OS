When Clear Skies Cloud Trust: Environmental Cues and the
Paradox of Confidence in Government
Xiangzhe Xua,∗, Ran Wub
aSchool of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing, 100084, China
bSchool of Energy and Environment, Southeast University, Nanjing, 210096, China
Abstract
Government trust, as a core concept in political economy and public policy research, serves
as a fundamental cornerstone of democratic legitimacy and state capacity. This paper exam-
ines how environmental conditions, particularly sunlight efficiency, influence reported gov-
ernment trust through both affective and cognitive mechanisms. Leveraging World Values
Survey Wave 7 data merged with NASA POWER high-frequency weather data, we propose
and validate a novel “salience and attribution” mechanism: clearer skies may paradoxically
reduce government trust by heightening environmental awareness and triggering negative
attributions. We further identify potential mediating pathways, including subjective well-
being, political interest, political discussion, and health perception, and demonstrate that
environmental conditions introduce measurement error in survey-based trust indicators. Our
findings provide theoretical contributions to environmental psychology, behavioral political
economy, and survey methodology, and yield practical implications for governance, policy
design, and survey implementation.
Keywords:
Institutional Confidence, Measurement Error, Environmental Psychology,
Sunlight Efficiency, Political Attitudes, Survey Methodology, Behavioral Political Economy
1. Introduction
Government trust, as a core concept in political economy and public policy research,
serves as a fundamental cornerstone of democratic legitimacy and state capacity [25]. It not
only predicts public support for redistributive policies, tax compliance, and policy imple-
mentation effectiveness, but also directly relates to political system stability and governance
efficacy [4]. Therefore, developing a comprehensive understanding of the determinants of gov-
ernment trust holds significant implications for both theoretical research and policy practice.
Traditional research has widely recognized institutional performance and political culture as
the primary driving forces behind government trust [26, 10, 18].
However, survey-based
measurement approaches may be contaminated by contextually dependent behavioral noise,
raising a more nuanced question: do seemingly irrelevant external factors, such as sunlight
duration or sky clarity, influence respondents’ reported levels of government trust?

In recent years, environmental psychology research has demonstrated that both short-
term and long-term environmental conditions can significantly influence human cognition,
emotion, and decision-making processes [29, 28]. Weather conditions’ impact on mood and
judgment, in particular, provides a novel theoretical perspective for understanding political
attitude formation. The “affect-as-information” theory posits that individuals often utilize
their current emotional states as sources of evaluative information, potentially misattributing
environmentally induced emotional changes to political objects. While previous research
has primarily focused on how positive weather conditions improve mood and subsequently
enhance political evaluations, this literature remains divided and has largely overlooked
potential pathways through which environmental cues may produce negative effects.
Concurrently, government trust faces significant fluctuations globally, particularly in the
context of the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting the urgency of understanding how external
shocks shape political assessments [31]. However, existing research has paid insufficient at-
tention to the political implications of short-term, transient weather conditions, particularly
lacking in-depth exploration of how these factors might influence political attitudes through
non-traditional mechanisms.
This study challenges traditional theoretical assumptions regarding environmental influ-
ences on political attitudes. Previous research has primarily relied on the “positive affect” hy-
pothesis, suggesting that favorable weather conditions enhance political evaluations through
mood improvement. However, we propose and validate a counterintuitive “salience and attri-
bution” mechanism: clearer skies may paradoxically reduce government trust by heightening
awareness of environmental quality, thereby triggering negative attributions regarding gov-
ernmental failures in environmental governance. This finding provides a novel theoretical
framework for interdisciplinary research at the intersection of environmental psychology and
political behavior.
From a policy practice perspective, the findings of this study offer direct implications for
government governance and public policy formulation. The research demonstrates that pub-
lic assessments of government trust may be influenced by environmental factors unrelated
to institutional fundamentals, cautioning policymakers to consider environmental contextual
factors when interpreting public opinion poll results. Meanwhile, the mediation pathways
revealed by the study—through subjective well-being, political interest, political discussion,
and health perception—suggest that governments should account for how environmental con-
ditions shape civic engagement and public perceptions. Furthermore, this research provides
important guidance for survey organizations and public opinion polling practices. The study
recommends avoiding interviews on exceptionally clear days in highly polluted areas, or
adopting weighting schemes to adjust for environmental condition effects, thereby enhancing
the accuracy and reliability of survey data.
This study makes three important theoretical contributions. First, it develops and val-
idates a "cognitive-salience" model of environmental influences on political attitudes, chal-
lenging traditional affective mechanism assumptions and introducing a paradox whereby
improved environmental conditions reduce government trust. This theoretical innovation
provides a new analytical framework for understanding how environmental cues shape po-
litical attitudes. Second, this study reveals the specific mechanisms through which environ-
mental conditions influence political assessments, including subjective well-being, political
interest, political discussion, and health perception. Third, this study enriches theoretical

2. Literature Review and Theoretical Framework
2.1. Literature Review
Government trust, as a core concept in political science and public administration, has
consistently drawn scholarly attention regarding its formation mechanisms and determinants.
Through cross-national comparative analysis of six panel studies, Devine and Valgarðsson [7]
found that political trust remains relatively stable in the medium to long term, showing weak
responsiveness to individual environmental changes but demonstrating certain responsiveness
to political environmental changes in the short to medium term.
From an institutional
perspective, Besley and Dray [2] elucidated the bidirectional relationship between trust and
state effectiveness, proposing the ”trust-compliance” mechanism.
However, the vulnerability of government trust when facing external shocks has also
attracted scholarly attention. For instance, in the context of climate change, Rhein and
Jansesberger [30] found that drought exposure erodes public confidence in political systems
across sub-Saharan Africa.
This indicates that extreme environmental events may alter
political trust by affecting public assessments of government response capabilities.
Extensive research in environmental psychology has established a solid foundation for
understanding how weather conditions influence human psychological states. Denissen et al.
[6] examined the effects of six weather parameters on mood and found significant relationships
between temperature, wind, sunlight, and negative emotions. Klimstra et al. [19] identified
distinct weather response types, revealing significant heterogeneity in weather sensitivity.
Neuroscience research by Gillihan et al. [9] further confirmed neural mechanisms linking
weather and emotional changes. Social media evidence by Baylis et al. [1] reinforced large-
scale effects of weather on expressed sentiment.
The “affect-as-information” theory provides a theoretical framework for connecting weather
conditions to political attitudes [33, 32]. The theory posits that people misattribute weather-
induced emotions to political evaluations, but increased attribution awareness can reduce
this bias. Further work suggests that affective states shape not only judgments but also
information processing strategies.
Recent political behavior studies have begun to examine weather effects. For example,
Liao and Junco [23] showed that extreme weather events influence campaign financing and
elections. Wu et al. [36] analyzed nonlinear socioeconomic impacts of environmental gover-
nance uncertainty. Other studies [39, 34] emphasize the complexity of political consequences
from climate events. Cologna and Siegrist [5] highlight differentiated roles of trust in envi-
ronmental behavior.
Overall, mainstream literature often assumes that positive weather leads to favorable
political evaluations. Yet this overlooks cognitive assessment and attribution processes. Our
study challenges this “positive weather–positive evaluation” hypothesis, proposing instead a
“salience and attribution” mechanism, whereby clear skies may reduce government trust by
raising awareness of environmental governance failures.

2.2. Theoretical Background and Hypotheses
2.2.1. Environmental Psychology and Political Cognition
The relationship between environmental conditions and political attitudes operates through
multiple psychological pathways that have been extensively documented in environmen-
tal psychology and political behavior research.
Traditional approaches have emphasized
the affect-as-information mechanism, where environmental conditions influence mood
states that subsequently contaminate political evaluations through affective transfer pro-
cesses [33, 15]. Under this framework, pleasant environmental conditions such as sunshine
and clear skies enhance positive affect, which individuals then misattribute to their evalua-
tions of political objects, leading to more favorable assessments of government performance.
However, environmental cues may also operate through cognitive salience mecha-
nisms that have received less attention in political behavior research. Drawing from dual-
process theories of cognition [8], environmental stimuli can influence political judgments
through deliberative processes rather than purely affective pathways. When environmental
conditions become cognitively salient, they may trigger systematic consideration of gov-
ernment performance in related policy domains, particularly environmental governance and
regulation.
2.2.2. The Salience and Attribution Framework
We propose a novel salience and attribution mechanism that operates through two
sequential cognitive processes. First, environmental salience: clearer atmospheric con-
ditions increase the visibility and cognitive accessibility of environmental quality, making
citizens more likely to consider environmental factors when evaluating government perfor-
mance [14, 38]. This process aligns with research on issue salience in political behavior,
which demonstrates that the accessibility of issue considerations significantly influences po-
litical attitudes [20, 16].
Second, attribution processes: when environmental quality becomes cognitively salient,
citizens engage in causal reasoning about government responsibility for environmental out-
comes. Attribution theory suggests that individuals systematically assign causal responsi-
bility for observed outcomes to various actors, including government institutions [13, 35]. In
contexts where environmental governance is a recognized government function, clear skies
may paradoxically heighten awareness of environmental challenges and governmental ac-
countability, leading to more critical evaluations of government performance.
This mechanism is theoretically distinct from mood-based explanations because it oper-
ates through deliberative rather than affective pathways. While clear skies might simultane-
ously improve mood (supporting the positive affect hypothesis), the salience of environmental
quality may trigger cognitive evaluations that work in the opposite direction, producing a
net negative effect on government trust.

Building on the literature in behavioral political economy and environmental psychology,
we model institutional confidence as a signal influenced by transient psychological states.
Formally, let Cic denote the latent institutional confidence of individual i in country c,
reflecting evaluations of institutional performance and norms. The observed survey response

Based on these competing theoretical frameworks, we formulate three hypotheses:
• H1 (Positive Affect Hypothesis): Following established research on weather and
mood [6, 19], higher ambient sunlight on the interview day promotes positive affect and
well-being through physiological mechanisms such as increased serotonin production
and circadian rhythm regulation [22]. This positive emotional state is then heuristically
transferred to evaluations of government performance through the affect-as-information
mechanism [32]. Under this hypothesis, Sict primarily represents **mood**. We expect
a positive effect (θβ > 0).
• H2 (Salience and Attribution Hypothesis): Conversely, clearer atmospheric con-
ditions increase the cognitive salience of environmental quality and government respon-
sibility for environmental outcomes. This heightened salience triggers deliberative eval-
uation processes focused on government performance in environmental domains, po-
tentially revealing perceived shortcomings in environmental governance, pollution con-
trol, or climate action. Under this mechanism, environmental visibility paradoxically
reduces confidence in government by drawing attention to environmental challenges
and governmental accountability. Under this hypothesis, Sict represents a **cognitive
evaluation state** characterized by attribution of responsibility and critical thinking
about government performance. We expect a negative effect (θβ < 0).
• H3 (Institutional Resilience Hypothesis): Drawing from research on institutional
trust stability [7] [4], government confidence reflects deep-seated evaluations of in-
stitutional performance and legitimacy that are resistant to transient environmental
shocks. Under this view, short-term atmospheric conditions represent irrelevant con-
textual noise that should not systematically influence political attitudes among rational
citizens. We expect no significant effect (θβ = 0).
We further hypothesize that if a significant effect emerges, it operates through two pri-
mary channels: (i) a physiological/affective channel, where sunlight influences perceived
health, comfort, and emotional states that subsequently affect political evaluations; and (ii)
a cognitive/salience channel, where atmospheric clarity heightens political attention and
attribution processes related to government performance, particularly in environmental do-
mains. Concretely, these effects may be mediated by four pathways: subjective well-being,
political interest, political discussion, and health perception.

likely to hold government accountable for environmental outcomes, including low-income
individuals, urban residents, and those with higher environmental awareness.
3. Data and Methods
3.1. Data and Variable Selection
We draw on individual-level microdata from Wave 7 of the World Values Survey Haerpfer
et al. [12] (2017–2023), covering 67,400 valid respondents across 50 countries (after filtering).
The dependent variable is confidence in the national government, measured from responses
to: “I am going to name a number of organizations. For each one, could you tell me how
much confidence you have in them: is it a great deal of confidence, quite a lot of confidence,
not very much confidence or none at all?”
Our key explanatory variable is the sunlight efficiency index, a constructed variable
defined as the ratio of all-sky surface shortwave radiation (ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DWN)
to clear-sky surface shortwave radiation (CLRSKY_SFC_SW_DWN), sourced from NASA
POWER satellite data (The data were obtained from the National Aeronautics and Space Ad-
ministration (NASA) Langley Research Center’s Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources
(POWER) project funded through the NASA Earth Science Division) NASA Langley Re-
search Center [27] (The weather data were obtained from NASA’s Prediction Of Worldwide
Energy Resources (POWER) project (Hourly 2.7.6 version, accessed 2025/08/15).). This
index captures both the atmospheric transparency and the perceived warmth and clarity of
sunlight. The numerator, all-sky radiation, represents the actual solar energy reaching the
Earth’s surface under prevailing atmospheric conditions, while the denominator, clear-sky
radiation, denotes the theoretical maximum under ideal cloudless and aerosol-free conditions.
Compared to conventional measures such as the all-sky insolation clearness index (ALL-
SKYKT), which solely reflects transparency, our sunlight efficiency index offers a more com-
prehensive proxy for perceived sky conditions. Values closer to one indicate clear, sunny
skies with minimal atmospheric interference, whereas lower values signify increased cloud
cover, pollution, or other attenuating factors that reduce solar irradiance and diminish the
perceptible warmth and brightness of sunlight. This enhanced measure not only improves
accuracy in capturing subjective experiences of weather but also strengthens identification
by more fully accounting for the psychological and physiological effects of sunlight expo-
sure. The two measures are highly correlated (r = 0.9626), and we employ ALLSKYKT in
robustness checks to verify that our results are not driven by measurement choice.
To ensure credible identification, we link WVS responses to exact interview dates and
locations, applying a clustering approach (K-means within countries) to aggregate survey
coordinates into about 130 survey-region cells. We further construct two instrumental vari-
ables: (i) sunlight efficiency from the nearest adjacent grid cell, and (ii) the five-year average
of sunlight efficiency. Both provide plausibly exogenous variation and satisfy the exclusion
restriction. For robustness, we also consider fixed effects based on the original WVS regional
classification; however, with approximately 880 categories, this approach is overly granular
for our main specification and is reserved for supplementary analyses.

3.1.1. Measurement of Government Confidence
Our main dependent variable, confidence_gov, is derived from item Q71 of the World
Values Survey Wave 7, which states:
“I am going to name a number of organizations. For each one, could you tell me
how much confidence you have in them: is it a great deal of confidence, quite a
lot of confidence, not very much confidence, or none at all?”
Specifically, Q71 asks about confidence in “the government”, with responses recorded
on a four-point scale: 1 (A great deal), 2 (Quite a lot), 3 (Not very much), and 4 (None at
all).
For analytical consistency and interpretability, we reverse-coded this variable to ensure
that higher values correspond to greater confidence. The recoded scale is as follows: 1 (None
at all), 2 (Not very much), 3 (Quite a lot), and 4 (A great deal). This transformation allows
regression coefficients to be directly interpreted as the marginal effect of sunlight efficiency
on the level of government confidence.
3.1.2. Control Variables
For individual-level variables, prior studies have demonstrated that demographic and
socioeconomic factors significantly predict levels of institutional trust, helping to control
for compositional differences across respondents . For weather-related variables, we include
meteorological factors that have been shown to influence mood independently of sunlight,
allowing us to isolate the unique effect of sky clarity . We incorporate two sets of controls:
(i) Individual-level controls: age, gender, education, income, marital status, subjective social
class, household size, religious affiliation, and urban/rural residence. These variables capture
socioeconomic and demographic heterogeneity that prior research has shown to significantly
influence political attitudes and trust in government [3, 11, 37].
(ii) Weather-related controls: daily precipitation (PRECTOTCORR), temperature at 2 me-
ters (T2M), wind speed (WS2M), and relative humidity (RH2M). These meteorological fac-
tors are included to isolate the specific effect of sunlight efficiency, as they may independently
affect mood and emotional states, potentially confounding the relationship with reported
government trust [5, 6, 21, 24].
3.2. Empirical Strategy
3.2.1. The Need for Fixed Effects: Addressing Heterogeneity
Our identification strategy leverages quasi-experimental variation in day-to-day atmo-
spheric clarity within narrowly defined geographic areas. To credibly isolate this short-term
variation, we must account for substantial time-invariant heterogeneity at both country and
regional levels that could confound the relationship between sunlight efficiency and political
trust.
Preliminary graphical analysis (see Appendix Figure A1) reveals two key patterns that
motivate our empirical approach. First, cross-country plots show a wide dispersion in mean
levels of both government trust and sunlight efficiency, reflecting fundamental differences in
political systems, economic development, and climatic conditions. Second, within-country
regional analysis for representative cases like China and the United States shows significant
7


## Page 8


subnational variation, likely driven by local institutional quality, economic structure, and
environmental factors. Crucially, both cross-country and within-country analyses suggest a
negative bivariate relationship between sunlight efficiency and government trust, providing
preliminary support for our salience and attribution hypothesis (H2).
This observed heterogeneity presents a clear empirical challenge: simple pooled OLS
estimates would confound these deep-seated cross-sectional differences with the short-term
temporal variation we aim to capture, potentially producing biased results. Our preferred
specification therefore incorporates high-dimensional fixed effects to absorb all time-invariant
characteristics at the region level.
3.2.2. Regression Specification
Our empirical framework follows recent advances in high-dimensional fixed effects mod-
eling. Specifically, we estimate:
confidence_govirt = α + β · SunlightEfficiencyirt + Xirtγ + Wirtδ + µr + λm + λw + εirt, (3)
where Xirt is a vector of individual-level controls (age, gender, education, income, marital
status, subjective social class, household size, religious affiliation, and urban/rural residence);
Wirt are weather controls (daily precipitation, temperature, wind speed, and relative humid-
ity); µr are region fixed effects constructed from clustered survey locations; λm are month
fixed effects; and λw are weekday fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the region-cell
and country levels to account for correlated shocks within these dimensions.
This design leverages quasi-experimental variation in atmospheric clarity, isolates short-
term effects, and minimizes omitted variable bias by comparing individuals interviewed in
the same region but on days with different weather conditions, while controlling for seasonal
and weekly patterns. This approach aligns with best practices in political economy and
environmental psychology for identifying the causal effect of environmental cues on political
attitudes.

4.3. Robustness Checks
We subject our main finding to a battery of robustness tests to assess its sensitivity to
alternative model specifications, measurement strategies, and potential confounding factors.
The core result—a statistically significant negative effect of sunlight efficiency on govern-
ment confidence proves to be exceptionally robust. The complete results of these tests are
presented in Appendix Table B1.
1. Alternative Estimators: Our result is not an artifact of the linear functional form.
Estimates from an ordered logit model and a linear mixed-effects model yield coeffi-
cients that are both statistically significant and qualitatively identical to our baseline
OLS estimate.
2. Temporal Dynamics: We find no evidence that the effect of sunlight efficiency per-
sists beyond the interview day or that individuals anticipate future weather conditions.
The effect is exclusively contemporaneous, as coefficients on one- to three-day lags are
insignificant.
3. Measurement and Specification:
• The effect is robust to using an alternative measure of sky clarity (the clearness
index).
• Winsorizing extreme weather values to mitigate the influence of outliers leaves
our conclusion unchanged.

## Page 11


• We find no evidence of a nonlinear relationship between sunlight efficiency and
government confidence.
• A placebo test confirms that sunlight efficiency does not predict confidence in
other, unrelated institutions (e.g., the armed forces), bolstering the claim that
the effect is specific to evaluations of government performance.
4. Inference: The significance of our result is not sensitive to the choice of clustering
level for standard errors, remaining robust to various alternative clustering schemes.
In sum, the negative relationship between sunlight efficiency and government confidence
is not driven by model specification, measurement, outliers, or inference choices. It represents
a stable and specific behavioral phenomenon.
4.4. Endogeneity Tests and Instrumental Variable Strategy
To address potential endogeneity concerns in the relationship between sunlight efficiency
and government trust, we employ an instrumental variable (IV) strategy. Although reverse
causality is implausible—individual-level government trust cannot influence daily sunlight
efficiency, which is determined by exogenous meteorological factors—omitted variable bias
remains a concern. Regional pollution shocks (e.g., industrial emissions or wildfires) or policy
interventions (e.g., emission regulations) could simultaneously affect atmospheric clarity and
public confidence in government, potentially attenuating the OLS estimate.
We construct an instrument to address this: using the deviation of daily sunlight efficiency
from its five-day historical average (sunlight_efficiency_bias), capturing short-term weather
shocks purged of seasonal trends. The use of historical averages to construct weather devi-
ations follows established methodologies, such as Jayachandran (2006), who employs local
rainfall deviations from long-run district means as an exogenous shock to identify produc-
tivity impacts on wages [17]. First-stage regression confirms that the instrument is strongly
correlated with sunlight efficiency (F-statistic = 5772.97), ruling out weak instrument con-
cerns.
Table 2 presents the IV estimation result alongside our baseline OLS specification. The
OLS coefficient for sunlight efficiency is -0.086 (p=0.009), indicating a statistically significant
negative relationship. The IV estimate using the bias instrument produces a coefficient of
-0.055 (p=0.112).
Formal endogeneity tests provide limited evidence of endogeneity: the Durbin-Wu-Hausman
test fails to reject the null hypothesis of exogeneity (p=0.103). Despite the strong first-stage
relationship, the similarity between the OLS and IV estimates, coupled with the theoretical
implausibility of reverse causality, suggests that omitted variables do not substantially bias
our baseline results. We therefore maintain that the OLS estimates provide the most reliable
evidence for the relationship between sunlight efficiency and government confidence.

To further address potential selection bias and strengthen causal inference, we implement
a propensity score matching (PSM) approach. We classify respondents into treatment and
control groups based on whether their sunlight efficiency reading falls above the median
(quartiles 3-4 vs. 1-2). Using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1, we achieve
good covariate balance across 12 demographic and meteorological variables, with mean bias
reduced from 21.7
The PSM estimates reveal a statistically significant negative treatment effect: individ-
uals experiencing higher sunlight efficiency levels exhibit lower government confidence by
approximately 0.05 points on the 4-point scale (p<0.05).
This effect remains robust in
doubly robust estimation that incorporates covariates alongside matching weights (-0.048,

evidence that the relationship between atmospheric conditions and political attitudes is not
driven by selective exposure or compositional differences across weather conditions.

Government trust, as a core concept in political economy and public policy research,
serves as a fundamental cornerstone of democratic legitimacy and state capacity. This paper
examines how environmental conditions, particularly sunlight efficiency, influence reported
government trust through both affective and cognitive mechanisms. Leveraging World Values
Survey Wave 7 data merged with NASA POWER high-frequency weather data, we propose
and validate a novel “salience and attribution” mechanism: clearer skies may paradoxically
reduce government trust by heightening environmental awareness and triggering negative
attributions. We further identify potential mediating pathways, including subjective well-
being, political interest, political discussion, and health perception, and demonstrate that
environmental conditions introduce measurement error in survey-based trust indicators. Our
findings provide theoretical contributions to environmental psychology, behavioral political
economy, and survey methodology, and yield practical implications for governance, policy
design, and survey implementation.
5.1.4. Interpretation of the Mediation Effect
Before examining behavioral pathways, we tested traditional affective mediators, includ-
ing self-reported health, happiness, and life satisfaction.
None of these channels
produced statistically significant indirect effects, suggesting that the link between sunlight
efficiency and government trust is unlikely to operate through general well-being or affective
states.
The mediation analysis for Q211 reveals that lower sunlight efficiency reduces overall
government trust (total effect = -0.086).
At the same time, it increases the likelihood
that individuals consider participating in peaceful demonstrations (path a > 0). Greater
willingness to protest is associated with lower government trust (path b > 0 but operating
through reduced trust levels). As a result, the indirect pathway reinforces the negative main
effect, yielding a small but statistically significant mediation share of −2.02%.

In substantive terms, this mechanism can be summarized as follows: when sunlight effi-
ciency decreases, citizens become more inclined to consider collective protest. Rather than
strengthening confidence in institutions, this heightened protest orientation reflects discon-
tent, thereby slightly amplifying the negative relationship between environmental conditions
and political trust. In other words, low sunlight efficiency →higher protest inclina-
tion →lower government trust.
This interpretation highlights the importance of distinguishing between civic engage-
ment as constructive participation and protest as a signal of dissatisfaction. In this case,
the protest pathway functions as a channel of grievance expression, aligning with our attri-
bution mechanism: unfavorable environmental conditions increase scrutiny of government
performance and reduce institutional trust.
5.2. Heterogeneity Analysis
While our baseline result establishes a significant average effect, we explore whether the
relationship between sunlight efficiency and government confidence varies across subpopula-
tions. Such analysis can offer valuable, albeit suggestive, insights into potential mechanisms
and distributional consequences, even in the absence of statistically significant interaction
effects.
We conducted extensive subgroup analyses across socioeconomic, demographic, infor-
mational, and environmental dimensions (see Appendix C for full results). We estimated
our main specification separately for each subgroup, maintaining all individual and weather
controls, as well as high-dimensional fixed effects.
The results, summarized in Appendix Figure B1, reveal patterns consistent with sev-
eral theoretical frameworks: (1) Economic Vulnerability: The negative effect appears
more pronounced among lower-income individuals and those self-identifying with a lower
social class; (2) Information Sophistication: Respondents with higher education, fre-
quent internet use, and urban residence show stronger negative responses; (3) Institutional
Buffering: The effect is attenuated among individuals reporting higher levels of religious
participation, suggesting religion may provide an alternative source of legitimacy and social
support; (4) Environmental Context: The effect is stronger in regions experiencing higher
climatic stress (e.g., higher temperatures).
Important Note on Interpretation: It is crucial to emphasize that formal statistical
tests of these subgroup differences (via interaction terms) were generally not significant.
Therefore, these patterns should be interpreted as descriptive and suggestive rather than as
evidence of definitive causal heterogeneity. They represent intriguing gradients that align
with theoretical expectations but require further investigation in future research.
Despite this caution, the observed patterns hold value. They suggest that the political
impact of environmental cues may not be uniform across society, with economically vul-
nerable and information-rich groups potentially being more responsive. This underscores
the importance of considering distributional effects in environmental policy design and com-
munication. Future research with larger samples or different methodological approaches is
needed to robustly test these hypotheses and explore the underlying mechanisms in greater
depth.

6. Conclusion and Discussion
This study investigates how environmental conditions—in particular, sunlight efficiency
shape citizens’ political trust. Leveraging large-scale individual survey data merged with
high-resolution meteorological information, we provide robust evidence that lower levels of
sunlight efficiency significantly reduce confidence in government institutions. The finding
holds across alternative specifications, placebo tests, and a wide array of robustness checks.
Our mediation analysis sheds light on the underlying pathways. Contrary to affect-based
explanations, we find no evidence that subjective health, happiness, or life satisfaction me-
diate the effect of weather conditions on political trust. Instead, the evidence highlights
a behavioral attribution pathway through willingness to participate in peaceful demonstra-
tions (Q211). Specifically, when sunlight efficiency decreases, individuals report a greater
inclination to protest, which is associated with lower government trust. This indirect effect,
though modest in size, operates in the same negative direction as the direct attribution effect,
reinforcing the overall decline in trust.
These findings carry several theoretical implications. First, they show that transient en-
vironmental conditions can shape political trust primarily through cognitive attribution and
grievance expression, rather than mood enhancement. Second, they underscore the need to
differentiate between forms of political engagement: whereas constructive civic participation
may strengthen institutional legitimacy, protest-oriented behavior reflects dissatisfaction and
weakens trust. Third, the results confirm our theoretical framework by lending support to
the salience and attribution hypothesis (H2) over the traditional affect-as-information
hypothesis (H1) and the institutional resilience hypothesis (H3). The empirical evidence
suggests that environmental cues trigger attribution processes and protest intentions that
jointly reduce confidence in government institutions.
In sum, our analysis demonstrates that low sunlight efficiency diminishes political trust
both directly, through heightened attribution of responsibility, and indirectly, through in-
creased protest orientation. This dual pathway provides compelling evidence that environ-
mental shocks can systematically erode confidence in political institutions, reinforcing the
salience and attribution mechanism as the dominant explanation of weather’s impact on
political trust.
