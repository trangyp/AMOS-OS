The Interplay between Utility and Risk in Portfolio Selection∗
Leonardo Baggiani†
Martin Herdegen‡
Nazem Khan§
Abstract
We revisit the problem of portfolio selection, where an investor maximizes utility subject
to a risk constraint. Our framework is very general and accommodates a wide range of
utility and risk functionals, including non-concave utilities such as S-shaped utilities from
prospect theory and non-convex risk measures such as Value at Risk.
Our main contribution is a novel and complete characterization of well-posedness for
utility-risk portfolio selection in one period that takes the interplay between the utility and
the risk objectives fully into account. We show that under mild regularity conditions the
minimal necessary and sufficient condition for well-posedness is given by a very simple either-
or criterion: either the utility functional or the risk functional need to satisfy the axiom of
sensitivity to large losses. This allows to easily describe well-posedness or ill-posedness for
many utility/risk pairs, which we illustrate by a large number of examples.
In the special case of expected utility maximization without a risk constraint (but in-
cluding non-concave utilities), we show that well-posedness is fully characterised by the
asymptotic loss-gain ratio, a simple and interpretable quantity that describes the investor’s
asymptotic relative weighting of large losses versus large gains.
Keywords: portfolio selection, utility maximization, risk measures, sensitivity to large losses,
asymptotic loss-gain ratio

this sense, the risk constraint is genuinely effective, ensuring that utility cannot be driven to its
supremum except in the presence of arbitrage.
From a theoretical perspective, it is both mathematically and economically interesting to iden-

perspective, the existence of an optimizer is crucial: one can meaningfully attempt to compute or
approximate solutions, rather than face an ill-posed problem in which utility diverges to U(∞).
Literature Review
The literature on utility-risk portfolio selection spans several decades and covers many different
settings. We focus on papers that study some form of market-independent well-posedness. For
clarity, we organize the discussion into two strands: (i) one-period markets and (ii) multi-period
and continuous-time models. Within each, we first review the unconstrained utility maximization
problem (that is, with R ≡0), before turning to the risk-constrained case.
As our main interest lies in one-period markets, this part of the review is more comprehensive,
while the discussion of multi-period and continuous-time results is included for completeness.

One-Period Markets
Despite its practical relevance, the theory of utility-risk portfolio selection in one-period mar-
kets is surprisingly incomplete. To the best of our knowledge, the most general result concerning
unconstrained utility maximization is Föllmer and Schied [2016, Theorem 3.3] (originally pub-
lished in 2002), which shows that (1.1) admits a unique optimizer for U(Y ) = E[u(Y )] when
u : R →[−∞, ∞) is continuous, strictly increasing, strictly concave, and either bounded above
or satisfies u(a) = −∞for some a < 0. These conditions, however, are far from necessary and
in particular exclude the non-concave S-shaped utility functions of prospect theory introduced
by Kahneman and Tversky [1979].
Bernard and Ghossoub [2010] and He and Zhou [2011a] study portfolio selection under cu-
mulative prospect theory without a risk constraint in a one-period setting with one risk-free and
one risky asset. Unlike ordinary prospect theory, cumulative prospect theory incorporates a
distortion function that perturbs the probability measure by overweighting small probabilities,
consistent with behavioural biases. In particular, He and Zhou [2011a, Section 3.2] introduce
the notion of a large-loss aversion degree (LLAD) for strictly increasing and twice differentiable
utilities u that are convex on (−∞, 0) and concave on (0, ∞), defined as

In summary, the results in the extant literature cover only particular cases: unconstrained

case of mean-risk optimization. A general theory that characterizes precisely when utility-risk
portfolio selection admits solutions across all one-period markets has not been addressed in the
literature before.
Multi-Period and Continuous-Time Markets
The theory is more complete in multi-period and continuous-time settings. Unconstrained util-
ity maximization has been studied extensively, most notably by Kramkov and Schachermayer
[1999] and Schachermayer [2001] in continuous time, and by Rásonyi and Stettner [2005] in
multiple periods. Kramkov and Schachermayer [1999] and Schachermayer [2001] showed that,
under standard no-arbitrage assumptions, market-independent well-posedness is characterized
by growth restrictions on the utility function, expressed via asymptotic elasticity. These con-
ditions are both necessary and sufficient for the existence of an optimal portfolio. In discrete
time, Rásonyi and Stettner [2005] obtained related sufficiency results, providing growth bounds
that ensure existence under no-arbitrage, though without a corresponding necessity result. For
a more detailed review, see Remark 5.7(b).
Portfolio selection under cumulative prospect theory without a risk constraint is also more
developed in multi-period and continuous-time markets than in the one-period case. In complete

continuous-time markets, Jin and Zhou [2008] analyze optimal investment for behavioural agents
with S-shaped preferences and probability distortion, identifying sufficient market-independent

incomplete discrete-time multi-period markets, Carassus and Rásonyi [2015] study the optimal
investment problem under cumulative prospect theory and provide sufficient market-independent
conditions for well-posedness. In both cases, however, necessity results are lacking.
Risk-constrained utility maximization has also been analyzed in greater detail than in the
one-period framework. The first paper to directly embed risk management objectives into a
utility-maximizing framework is due to Basak and Shapiro [2001] who work in a continuous-time
complete market with a Value at Risk constraint. They show in particular that the constraint
may induce larger losses than in the unconstrained problem. A more theoretical analysis is
provided by Gundel and Weber [2008], who investigate when the risk constraint is binding
for expected utility maximizing agents in general continuous-time models under a utility-based
shortfall risk constraint. Further works on risk-constrained utility maximization include Gabih
et al. [2009], He and Zhou [2011b], He et al. [2015], Wei [2018], and more recently Ghossoub and
Zhu [2025].
Overall, while multi-period and continuous-time utility-risk portfolio selection is now rela-
tively well understood, the corresponding one-period theory remains fragmented. Our goal in
this paper is to close this gap by identifying the exact market independent conditions on U and
R that ensure the existence of an optimizer in (1.1).

The Problem
Throughout the paper, we consider a one-period economy in which uncertainty about the ter-
minal state of the world is modelled by an atomless probability space (Ω, F, P). Denote by L1
the space of integrable random variables, and by L∞the space of essentially bounded random
variables. Let L be a Riesz space such that L∞⊂L ⊂L1 and assume it is closed under weak
tail domination, i.e., for every non-negative random variable Y , we have Y ∈L whenever there
exists Z ∈L such that P[Y > y] ≤P[Z > y] for all y ≥0. This implies in particular that a
non-negative random variable Y lies in L if there is Z ∈L with Y ≤Z P-a.s. This is a very
mild condition that is satisfied by all Lp spaces for p ∈[1, ∞], and more generally, by all Orlicz
spaces and Orlicz hearts.
Elements of L represent payoffs of financial positions at the terminal date, with the conven-
tion that positive values correspond to profits.
2.1
Utility and Risk
The reward of a financial position is captured by a utility functional U, while its riskiness is
evaluated using a risk functional R. The numerical values assigned by these functionals are not
directly important; only the induced ordering of positions, reflecting preferences, is relevant. We
adopt an axiomatic framework. To that end, a functional H : L →[−∞, ∞] is said to be:
• normalized if H(0) = 0;
• decreasing if H(Y ) ≥H(Z) for all Y, Z ∈L with Y ≤Z P-a.s.;
• increasing if H(Y ) ≤H(Z) for all Y, Z ∈L with Y ≤Z P-a.s.
Moreover, if H is increasing or decreasing, then we set H(∞) := lim
y→∞H(y) ∈[−∞, ∞].
Definition 2.1. A utility functional is any increasing functional U : L →[−∞, ∞) that is
normalized and satisfies U(Y ) < U(∞) for all Y ∈L.
A risk functional is any decreasing
functional R : L →(−∞, ∞] that is normalized.
A few comments regarding the above definition are in order. The assumption that no position
is assigned infinite utility or negative infinite risk is completely standard in the literature. The
condition U(Y ) < U(∞) reflects non-satiation and expresses that no position Y ∈L is as
desirable as receiving unbounded deterministic wealth. It will play an important technical role
in some of our proofs. By constrast, we do not impose the condition R(Y ) > R(∞) as this
would exclude the important case of no risk constraint, i.e., R = 0. Finally, the normalization
assumption on U and R is for notational convenience and without loss of generality, provided that

Each axiom introduced above has a natural economic interpretation and plays a role in our
analysis. Concavity of a utility functional reflects standard risk aversion, while convexity of a
risk functional encodes preference for diversification. Both are foundational in utility and risk

theory; cf. Föllmer and Schied [2016, Chapters 2 and 4] and the references therein. Quasi-
concavity and quasi-convexity are weaker shape restrictions that still encode diversification.
Negative star-shapedness and positive star-shapedness are also weaker conditions. They impose
diminishing returns to scale, and are more behaviourally plausible than positive homogeneity,
which assumes linear scaling. We refer to Landsberger and Meilijson [1990] for examples of
negatively star-shaped utility functionals and to Castagnoli et al. [2022] for a recent presentation
of positively star-shaped risk functionals.
Cash-concavity and cash-convexity, though less common in the literature, are weaker than
full concavity or convexity, and imply star-shapedness by taking constants equal to zero. Cash-
additivity generalizes the familiar cash-invariance property used in coherent risk measures Artzner
et al. [1999] (see Proposition B.1), but is less common for utility functionals, except in specific
frameworks such as Yaari’s dual theory Yaari [1987] and variational preferences Maccheroni
et al. [2006]. Note that cash-additivity together with negative/positive star-shapedness implies
cash-concavity/convexity; cf. Proposition B.2.
Finally, the upper and lower Fatou properties are mild regularity conditions that ensure
stability under limits. In particular, the lower Fatou property is standard in the literature on risk
functionals, where it is often referred to simply as the Fatou property. Law-invariance expresses
the idea that only the distribution of outcomes matters, which is a standard assumption in models
of preference. In certain settings, law-invariance implies the upper/lower Fatou property; see,
for instance, Jouini et al. [2006].

2.3

The concept of sensitivity to large losses was introduced and studied in detail in Herdegen

(a) Uw,r is weakly sensitive to large losses.
(b) Rw,r satisfies the lower Fatou property, positive star-shapedness and sensitivity to large

the investor’s preferences must exhibit sufficient aversion to downside risk, or the imposed risk
constraint must be strong enough to curb excessive risk-taking. The criterion is minimal and
robust, relying only on sensitivity to large losses and mild regularity properties.
4.3

In most practical investment problems, the agent seeks to maximize utility while remaining
within acceptable risk levels, as in the (U, R) portfolio selection problem. However, in certain
settings, it is more appropriate to consider problems in which the investor directly minimizes risk,
subject to a target utility constraint. Although the literature on risk minimization is relatively
limited compared to that on utility maximization, several notable contributions exist.

5.1
Expected Utility
A utility function u captures an agent’s preferences over monetary outcomes.
Definition 5.1. A function u : R →[−∞, ∞) is called a utility function if it is increasing,
u(0) = 0, u(∞) := limy→∞u(y) > u(z) for all z ∈R and lim supy→∞u(y)/y < ∞.
It is natural to assume that u is increasing, reflecting a preference for more wealth, and
normalized so that u(0) = 0. The non-satiation condition, u(∞) > u(y) for all y ∈R, means
that utility grows strictly at infinity, i.e., no finite outcome is ever as desirable as becoming
arbitrarily wealthy. Finally, the asymptotic condition lim supx→∞u(y)/y < ∞ensures that u
grows at most linearly at infinity, thereby ruling out excessively risk-seeking behaviour. This

Conclusion and Outlook
In this paper, we have developed a new perspective on the interplay between utility and risk in
one-period portfolio selection. For a utility functional U and a risk functional R, we showed that
(U, R)-portfolio selection is well posed in every arbitrage-free market provided that at least one
of the two functionals satisfies the axiom of sensitivity to large losses. This reveals that, despite
their different roles, U and R are in a precise sense two sides of the same coin. Furthermore,
if either U or R is law-invariant, then sensitivity to large losses is not only sufficient but also
necessary for market-independent well posedness; see Theorem 4.7.
The classical case of unconstrained expected-utility maximization, encompassing both tradi-
tional concave and non-concave S-shaped utilities, emerges as a special case of our framework.
In this setting, well posedness is governed by whether the asymptotic loss-gain ratio of u is
infinite; see Corollary 5.4.
A key strength of our theory is that it yields simple, verifiable necessary and sufficient condi-
tions for market-independent well posedness in one-period models, under only mild assumptions
on the utility and risk functionals. This stands in contrast to much of the existing literature,
where well-posedness typically requires strong regularity assumptions on the utility function or
restrictive conditions on the market models.
Our results also open several avenues for further exploration, particularly on the computa-
tional and numerical side. For instance:
