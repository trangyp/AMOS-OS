OnlineMate: An LLM-Based Multi-Agent Companion System for
Cognitive Support in Online Learning
Xian Gao1, Zongyun Zhang1, Ting Liu1,†, Yuzhuo Fu1,†
1Shanghai Jiao Tong University
gaoxian@sjtu.edu.cn
Abstract
In online learning environments, students of-
ten lack personalized peer interactions, which
are crucial for cognitive development and learn-
ing engagement. Although previous studies
have employed large language models (LLMs)
to simulate interactive learning environments,
these interactions are limited to conversational
exchanges, failing to adapt to learners’ individ-
ualized cognitive and psychological states. As
a result, students’ engagement is low and they
struggle to gain inspiration. To address this
challenge, we propose OnlineMate, a multi-
agent learning companion system driven by
LLMs integrated with Theory of Mind (ToM).
OnlineMate simulates peer-like roles, infers
learners’ psychological states such as misunder-
standings and confusion during collaborative
discussions, and dynamically adjusts interac-
tion strategies to support higher-order think-
ing.
Comprehensive evaluations, including
simulation-based experiments, human assess-
ments, and real classroom trials, demonstrate
that OnlineMate significantly promotes deep
learning and cognitive engagement by elevating
students’ average cognitive level while substan-
tially improving emotional engagement scores.

Introduction
The use of artificial intelligence to provide immedi-
ate and personalized online instruction originated
with Intelligent Tutoring Systems (Nwana, 1990).
With the advancement of LLMs, LLM-driven AI
teachers (Markel et al., 2023) and teaching assis-
tants (Tu et al., 2023) have been widely adopted.
However, unlike learning in traditional classrooms,
students engaging in online learning through these
AI technologies often face the challenge of lim-
ited interaction, which results in a weakened sense
of classroom participation (Muilenburg and Berge,
2005; Ferri et al., 2020; Akpen et al., 2024).
†Corresponding Authors
To address these gaps, recent studies (Yu et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2025c) have leveraged LLMs’
multi-agent collaboration capabilities to simulate
peer companions, aiming to stimulate discussions.
However, these methods only mimic conversational
styles without aligning with students’ needs or cog-
nitive levels, failing to initiate targeted, personal-
ized discussions that would stimulate deeper reflec-
tion and enhance cognitive development.
To address these limitations, we integrate LLMs’
Theory of Mind (ToM) capabilities, which is the ca-
pacity to infer others’ thoughts and psychological
states (Apperly, 2010; Zhu et al., 2024; Nguyen,
2025; Zhang et al., 2025b), and propose Online-
Mate, a ToM-enhanced multi-agent learning com-
panion system. Unlike existing multi-agent online
learning systems (Yu et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2025c) that only simulate peer-like agent roles for
dialogue, OnlineMate infers and adapts to learners’
cognitive and psychological states during collab-
orative discussions. Specifically, we decompose
ToM reasoning during student classroom discus-
sions into three stages, i.e., hypothesis generation,
refinement, and response validation, in alignment
with metacognitive theory (Flavell, 1979; Zhang
et al., 2025b), and integrate classroom context man-
agement and behavior control. This design enables
the system to not only simulate peer interactions
but also dynamically adjust its interaction strategies
to cater to learners’ interests and needs, thereby en-
hancing their engagement and fostering cognitive
development.
We conducted rigorous evaluations, including
simulation-based experiments with an LLM-driven
Evaluation Agent, human assessments using stan-
dardized rubrics, and real classroom trials in a QS
top-50 university. Results confirm that OnlineMate
effectively improves students’ cognitive levels and
learning outcomes. We also investigated key fac-
tors influencing learning effects.

Related Work
2.1
LLMs in Education
LLMs’ pretraining on extensive corpora enables
expert-level proficiency across domains. In edu-
cation, LLMs have been applied to teaching assis-
tance (JeonJaeho and LeeSeongyong, 2023), lesson
planning (HuBihao et al., 2024), instructional re-
port generation (Gao et al., 2025a), lesson deliver-
ing (Tu et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2025), and role simu-
lation (Lee et al., 2023; Markel et al., 2023). Some
studies use multi-agent systems to orchestrate class-
room discussions (Yue et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024;
Zhang et al., 2025c). However, these approaches
lack personalized adaptation mechanisms and fail
to dynamically tailor content to individual learners’
cognitive states. In this work, we integrate ToM rea-
soning and cognitive scaffolding, which is aligned
with Bloom’s Taxonomy, to achieve personalized
interaction, rather than merely simulating roles or
conversations.
2.2
LLMs for Human Simulation
LLM-driven agents exhibit human-like decision-
making due to extensive training data on human
behaviors. They have been used in social simula-
tions (Park et al., 2023; Aher et al., 2023; Li et al.,
2023; Guo et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024), scien-
tific inquiry (M. Bran et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b;
Gao et al., 2025b), collaborative workflows (Hong
et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Kulkarni, 2025), and
educational role-play (Yu et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2025c). However, existing educational simulations
only mimic conversational styles, lacking align-
ment with authentic learning processes.
2.3
Theory of Mind (ToM) in LLMs
ToM, the capacity to attribute mental states to oth-
ers and anticipate behavior (Premack and Woodruff,
1978; Apperly, 2012), is a core component of social
intelligence. Recent studies show that LLMs ex-
hibit notable ToM abilities (van Duijn et al., 2023;
Zhou et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024),
with internal representations of self and others’ be-
liefs (Zhu et al., 2024). To enhance ToM, some
studies decompose reasoning into explicit chains
(Gu et al., 2024b; Lin et al., 2025) or multi-agent
workflows (Zhang et al., 2025b). In this work,
we similarly decompose ToM reasoning into three
stages and integrate agent persona constraints and
Bloom’s Taxonomy, balancing role consistency and
cognitive enhancement in classroom discussion sce-
narios.
3
OnlineMate Framework
The design of OnlineMate must ensure two key
requirements: (1) each agent’s behavior aligns with
its persona and accesses accurate context, and (2)
interactions unfold naturally. To address these, we
propose the Classroom Context Manager to govern
the flow of information accessible and the Class-
room Behavior Controller to determine the actions
undertaken by each agent. Furthermore, to facil-
itate a more rigorous evaluation of system func-
tionality, we incorporate an evaluation agent tasked
with simulating student roles, thereby enabling sys-
tematic evaluation of OnlineMate Agents’ pedagog-
ical effectiveness. Figure 1 shows the framework
and workflow.
3.1
Classroom Context Manager
As a complex and information-rich communica-
tion environment, LLMs may experience halluci-
nations or role confusion during role-playing in
the classroom teaching process (Park et al., 2023;
Qian et al., 2024). Moreover, each OnlineMate
Agent requires independent contextual information,
including contextual memory, reasoning, and be-
liefs, in addition to the shared dialogue history. The
Classroom Context Manager stores contextual ele-
ments (e.g., dialogue, memory), visible scope (e.g.,
specific agents, discussion groups), and agent role
configurations to enhance the consistency of role-
playing within LLMs. During response generation,
agents query their designated role and the relevant
visible context from the context database, ensur-
ing independent and efficient context management.

3.2
Classroom Behavior Controller
We utilize the Classroom Behavior Controller to
govern the OnlineMate Agents’ actions within the
classroom to ensure persona-consistent and au-
tonomous behavior. During response generation,
the Classroom Behavior Controller assigns a score
(ranging from 0 to 10) to each agent’s likelihood
of speaking based on the current context and the
agent’s persona. Subsequently, it randomly selects
a speaker from the top 2 scoring agents to pre-
vent any single agent from overly dominating the
conversation. The Controller then determines the
actions (e.g., posing questions, making analogies)
and knowledge points to be included in the agent’s
response, drawing from the agent’s persona and the
knowledge retrieved by the context manager. If a
specific agent is directly addressed in the context,
such as when a student follows up with a question
directed at a particular agent, that targeted agent
becomes the next speaker.

4
OnlineMate Agent
Each OnlineMate Agent embodies a specific per-
sona, receives student utterances as input, infers
learners’ cognitive and psychological states via
ToM, and generates persona-consistent responses.
Based on the classification of different behavioral
types exhibited by students in classroom discus-
sions in educational psychology (Cesareni et al.,
2016; Wang and Li, 2021), we set the role types
of the OnlineMate Agent to four types: Facilita-
tor, Follower, Integrator, and Questioner & Orga-
nizer, named respectively as Insight Sparker, Fun-
damentals Checker, Synthesis Expert, and Critical
Challenger. The detailed prompts are presented in
Appendix C.1.
Inspired by prior research, which has concep-
tualized such ToM reasoning process as a struc-
tured sequence of inference (Flavell, 1979; Frith
and Frith, 2006; Grave de Peralta Menendez et al.,
2008; Cross et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b), we
decompose the agent’s workflow into three stages:
ToM hypothesis generation, hypothesis refinement
and filtering, and response generation and valida-
tion. Figure 3 illustrates the complete workflow of
OnlineMate Agents from obtaining input to gener-
ating responses.
4.1
ToM Hypothesis Generation
ToM hypothesis generation serves as the foun-
dation for inferring students’ cognitive and
psychological states. In this stage, OnlineMate
Agent infers students’ cognitive and psychological
states based on their current utterance, dialogue
history, and memory that reflects the agent’s infer-
ences about the students’ preferences, emotions,
and other traits over time. The agent generates
diverse candidate hypotheses accompanied by a
natural language explanation and labeled with ToM
categories from Belief, Desire, Intention, Emotion,
and Thought. To ensure pedagogical relevance
and prevent an overemphasis on psychological
inference at the expense of instructional goals,
we further incorporate reasoning about the
student’s cognitive level, following the six-tier
structure of Bloom’s Taxonomy (Muilenburg
and Berge, 2005; Apperly, 2012), including
Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate,
and Create.
Specifically, the ToM reasoning mechanism of
the OnlineMate Agent unfolds in four stages: (1)
generating initial hypotheses from dialogue context
by LLM-based reasoning; (2) filtering out implau-
sible ones via memory; (3) assigning each remain-
ing hypothesis a corresponding ToM label; and
(4) inferring the student’s current cognitive level
based on the utterance. This ensures diverse, peda-
gogically relevant hypotheses. We design prompts
that align with both psychological definitions of
ToM and the levels of Bloom’s Taxonomy, guid-
ing the LLM to reason about students’ mental and
cognitive states in a manner analogous to human
instructors. The resulting k diverse and plausible
hypotheses enable flexible downstream response
generation, avoiding premature commitment to a
single rigid response outline.
4.2
Hypothesis Refinement and Filtering
This stage takes the set of generated mental state hy-
potheses, OnlineMate Agents’ personas, and con-
straint rules as input and refines the hypotheses
to align with the persona and classroom context.

The constraints serve as the basis for determin-
ing whether a given hypothesis should be retained,
revised, or discarded. For example, if the initial
hypothesis suggests that the student intends to play
a game during a classroom dialogue, the personas
and rule-based constraints guide the OnlineMate
Agent to reinterpret this intention in a contextu-
ally appropriate manner, such as treating it as an
application scenario or interpreting it as a play-
ful remark, thus ensuring alignment with both the
agent’s persona and the instructional setting.
The Hypothesis Refinement and Filtering pro-
cess is carried out in two sequential steps. First, the
agent revises each hypothesis to incorporate con-
straints. Then, it scores revised hypotheses based
on plausibility, context, and memory, selecting the
most appropriate one for response generation.
4.3
Response Generation and Validation
In the final stage, the OnlineMate Agent generates
an appropriate response and verifies its alignment
with the inferred cognitive and psychological states
of the student by transforming the refined hypothe-
sis from the preceding stages into a concrete natural
language output. This stage takes the selected hy-
pothesis as input and incorporates memory and role
constraints to ensure consistency with the student’s
cognitive profile, prior emotional states, and the
agent’s predefined persona. Moreover, to steer re-
sponses toward enhancing the student’s cognitive
level, which is a process termed "cognitive scaffold-
ing", the model is explicitly prompted to guide the
student’s thinking through its answers. The relevant
knowledge graph content from Context Manager is
also provided to ensure knowledge correctness.
To further guarantee that the generated response
aligns with the student’s inferred needs and inten-
tions, a self-reflection mechanism is integrated to
assign a utility score to the output, evaluating its
alignment with student needs and context. If the
utility score falls below a certain threshold, the
system triggers a regeneration of the response to
improve alignment and pedagogical effectiveness.

Human Evaluation
We adopted discussion as-
sessment scales developed by Carnegie Mellon
University1 and Northwestern University2 to eval-
uate the simulated classroom discussions by hu-
man experts. These scales cover various dimen-
sions, including frequency of participation, quality
of comments, listening and co-construction, crit-
ical analysis, participation, and dialogue quality.
Both the course instructor and educational experts
were invited to independently rate each discussion
segment, with a minimum of two experts assigned
to each. The experts first assessed the plausibil-
ity and human-likeness of the Evaluation Agent’s
simulated dialogue, and subsequently rated the
dialogues that conformed to human student be-
haviour based on the rubric criteria. All scores
were mapped onto a 0–3 scale, and the average was
then computed.

We evaluated the effec-
tiveness of the OnlineMate system in promoting
student participation in online discussions by ob-
serving students’ verbal contributions and behav-

lized is an online collaborative learning knowledge

ucational theory (Dixson, 2015; Sinha et al., 2015;
Olakanmi, 2016). Initially, we classified the col-
laborative learning behaviors exhibited by students,
AI companions, and teaching assistants during the
discussions (Gunawardena et al., 1997), then em-
ployed a Likert five-point scale for rating, with

behavior was not observed. The behaviors assessed
included Expression (EX), Listening (LI), Coordi-

the course instructor, teaching assistants not in-
volved in the discussions, and doctoral students
with a background in both education and the sub-
ject matter of the course. Each discussion was
scored by no fewer than two individuals, and the
average score was recorded. The specific content
of the scale is presented in Appendix D.1.
Participant Evaluation
To assess participants’
authentic experiences while using OnlineMate, we
developed a student evaluation scale (Appendix
D.2) to collect feedback. The student evaluation
scale examines response quality, the functional ef-
fectiveness of each role, a comparison with human
teaching assistants, overall usability, and several
open-ended questions. A Likert five-point scale
was utilized, and student volunteers participating
in the experiment were asked to rate each item on
a scale from 1 to 5.

We presents OnlineMate, a multi-agent learning
companion system based on LLMs and enhanced
by ToM capabilities. The system not only simu-
lates peer-like interactions but also dynamically
adapts its interaction strategies based on learn-
ers’ cognitive and psychological states. This per-
sonalized pedagogical approach fosters a more
engaging and reflective learning experience, pro-
moting the development of higher-order thinking
skills and emotional investment. Our evaluation
results demonstrate that OnlineMate significantly
enhances students’ cognitive engagement and fa-
cilitates more meaningful interactions, ultimately
improving learning outcomes. The experimental
findings further provide deeper insights into the

mechanisms of AI-mediated learning. We envision
that OnlineMate holds promise for broader educa-
tional applications, serving as a powerful enabler
of personalized learning experiences.
Limitations
Despite the promising results of OnlineMate, this
study has several limitations that point to avenues
for future improvement:
Concerns about Generalizability
The real classroom experiment was conducted in
a QS top-50 university with a focus on two techni-
cal courses (Digital Integrated Circuit Design and
Comprehensive Practice in Artificial Intelligence).
The participating students likely had relatively high
academic backgrounds and self-directed learning
abilities, which may limit the generalizability of
the findings to diverse educational contexts—such
as community colleges, K-12 education, or non-
technical disciplines. The system’s role design
and knowledge alignment are tailored to technical
coursework, and adapting it to humanities, social
sciences, or vocational training would require ad-
justments to role personas, knowledge graphs, and
interaction strategies.
ToM Reasoning Boundaries
While OnlineMate decomposes ToM reasoning into
three stages and integrates Bloom’s Taxonomy, its
inference of students’ cognitive and psychological
states remains dependent on textual dialogue data.
It may struggle to capture non-verbal cues (e.g.,
hesitation, frustration) or implicit misunderstand-
ings that are not explicitly articulated in text.
Long-Term Efficacy and Dependence Risks
The real classroom experiment lasted for 10 weeks.
While short-term gains in cognitive engagement
and learning outcomes were observed, the long-
term impact of OnlineMate, including whether stu-
dents maintain deep learning habits, avoid over-
reliance on AI companions, or develop sustained
higher-order thinking skills—remains unaddressed.
Over-reliance on the system’s cognitive scaffold-
ing could potentially weaken students’ independent
problem-solving abilities if not properly guided.
Technical and Scalability Constraints
OnlineMate’s performance relies on a structured
framework (Classroom Context Manager, Behavior
Controller, and knowledge graphs), which requires
significant upfront effort to adapt to new courses or
educational settings. The system’s current design
also assumes stable access to high-quality course
knowledge graphs and LLM resources, which may
be inaccessible in resource-constrained educational
environments.
Additionally, while the Context
Manager mitigates hallucinations, LLM-generated
errors (e.g., incorrect technical analogies or mis-
aligned cognitive guidance) could still occur, es-
pecially in rapidly evolving fields with emerging
knowledge.
Role and Interaction Limitations
The four predefined agent roles (Insight Sparker,
Fundamentals Checker, Synthesis Expert, Critical
Challenger) are based on common classroom inter-
action patterns but may not cover the full spectrum
of student learning styles or supportive roles.
Ethical Statements
Students and educators were explicitly informed
that they were interacting with AI agents (not hu-
mans), and the system’s purpose, capabilities, and
limitations were clearly communicated. All the
volunteers were paid appropriate compensation.
Participants were notified of how their dialogue
data, cognitive assessments, and feedback would
be stored, analyzed, and anonymized to protect pri-
vacy. Strict data governance protocols are enforced
to safeguard sensitive student data (e.g., learn-
ing gaps, cognitive levels, emotional responses)
against unauthorized access, breaches, or misuse.
Anonymization is mandatory throughout data stor-
age and analysis, and data retention periods are
limited to the duration of the study or educational
purpose.
OnlineMate is intended to complement, rather
than replace, human instructors and peer relation-
ships. We told the participating educators to guide
students to use the system as a scaffolding tool
for deep learning, not a substitute for independent
thinking or human collaboration. In the future we
will provide development to help educators inte-
grate the system effectively.
There are potential risks that, despite defenses
against prompt injection attacks, emerging risks
(e.g., using the system for plagiarism, avoiding crit-
ical thinking) require ongoing vigilance. Biases in
role interactions or knowledge explanations could
disadvantage specific student groups if not audited
and corrected. Educational inequalities may be ex-
