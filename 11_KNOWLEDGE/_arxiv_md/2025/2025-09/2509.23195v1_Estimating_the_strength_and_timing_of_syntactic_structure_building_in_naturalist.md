Estimating the strength and timing of syntactic structure building in 
naturalistic reading 
Nan Wang1, Jiaxuan Li2  
1 Department of Linguistics, the Chinese University of Hong Kong;  
2 Department of Language Science, University of California Irvine; 
 
Corresponding author: nanwang@cuhk.edu.hk 
 
Abstract 
A central question in psycholinguistics is the timing of syntax in sentence processing. Much of 
the existing evidence comes from violation paradigms, which conflate two separable processes—
syntactic category detection and phrase structure construction—and implicitly assume that 
phrase structure follows category detection. In this study, we use co-registered EEG and eye-
tracking data from the ZuCo corpus to disentangle these processes and test their temporal order 
under naturalistic reading conditions. Analyses of gaze transitions showed that readers 
preferentially moved between syntactic heads, suggesting that phrase structures, rather than 
serial word order, organize scanpaths. Bayesian network modeling further revealed that 
structural depth was the strongest driver of deviations from linear reading, outweighing lexical 
familiarity and surprisal. Finally, fixation-related potentials demonstrated that syntactic surprisal 
influences neural activity before word onset (–184 – –10 ms) and during early integration (48 –
300 ms). These findings extend current models of syntactic timing by showing that phrase 
structure construction can precede category detection and dominate lexical influences, 
supporting a predictive “tree-scaffolding” account of comprehension. 
 
Introduction 
The understanding of how the brain processes language, particularly the syntactic structures that 
underlie sentence comprehension, has long been a topic of debate in psycholinguistics and 
cognitive neuroscience. A prominent framework for explaining this process is Friederici's  
(2002) three-phase model of syntactic processing, which provides a neurocognitive perspective 
on how the brain builds syntactic structures in real time. According to this model, language 
comprehension unfolds in discrete phases. In Phase I (100–300 ms), syntactic category cues 
trigger the autonomous construction of a skeletal phrase structure, indexed by the early left-
anterior negativity (ELAN). In Phase II (300–500 ms), lexical-semantic and morphosyntactic 
information refine thematic role assignments, reflected in Left Anterior Negativity (LAN) and 
N400 activity. In Phase III (500–1000 ms), structural and semantic information are integrated or 
revised, producing the P600. This framework implies two separable processes—syntactic 
category detection and phrase-structure construction—assumed to unfold simultaneously in 
Phase I, in order to build up an autonomous syntactic structure independent of meaning in the 
first place. Another influential model, the Extended Argument Dependency Model (Bornkessel 
& Schlesewsky, 2006), also posits a three-phase processing structure. In this model, Phase 1 is 
responsible for phrase structure construction based on word category information, without 
involving relational interpretations such as assigning thematic roles. Here, syntactic category

2 
detection (e.g., distinguishing nouns, verbs, etc.) is considered an integral part of phrase structure 
construction (e.g., building the basic framework of a sentence) in Phase I. In both models, 
syntactic category detection and phrase-structure construction are not conceptually dissociated; 
instead, they are assumed to support and interact with each other in the process of building the 
syntactic structure.  
The conceptual overlap between the two processes can create ambiguity about how these 
processes are functioning in the model. It’s unclear whether they are distinct processes that 
happen at the same time (but remain separate) or combined processes that cannot be separated at 
the cognitive level. A lack of distinction on the empirical level has also made things worse. 
Violation paradigms, for instance, probe phrase-structural processing by inserting unexpected 
word classes (e.g., a determiner where a noun is expected). While these manipulations elicit 
robust ELAN or P600 responses, they simultaneously implicate failed category recognition and 
the collapse of hierarchical parsing (Ad et al., 1993; Hahne & Friederici, 2002; Hinojosa et al., 
2003; Neville et al., 1991; Steinhauer & Drury, 2012a). Similarly, minimal-composition studies 
show effects of phrase composition (Bemis & Pylkkänen, 2011; Maran et al., 2022; Zaccarella et 
al., 2017), but such effects often co-occur with lexical category constraints, making it difficult to 
disentangle the two processes. Visual-world experiments also fail to dissociate the two factors: 
fixation patterns are shaped both by category-driven expectations and by higher-order structural 
commitments (Kamide & Kukona, 2018; Koring et al., 2012; Mitsugi, 2017). Consequently, the 
relative timing of category and structure effects remains unresolved. 
Electrophysiological studies provide partial but inconclusive answers. Research using MEG in 
continuous speech processing demonstrates that word-class information (e.g., noun, verb) is 
identified as early as 100 ms post-word onset, with similar findings observed during reading at 
approximately 160 ms (Arana et al., 2021a; Gwilliams et al., 2024; Sarrett et al., 2022). In 
comparison, structure-sensitive signals related to syntactic composition emerge near 200 ms, 
specifically in the posterior temporal regions (Flick & Pylkkänena, 2018; Matchin et al., 2019). 
This contrasts with evidence showing that phase structure effects can sometimes dominate or 
precede lexical category detection. In rapid parallel visual presentation paradigms, structural 
frame cues such as sentence type (declarative vs. interrogative) exerted strong effects in both 
early (231–407 ms) and later (506–622 ms) windows, whereas verb subcategorization showed 
null effects (Krogh & Pylkkänen, 2025). Fallon and Pylkkänen (2024) reported a temporal 
precedence of structural over lexical constraints. Cross-linguistic evidence strengthens this 
conclusion: Mandarin EEG studies indicate that apparent part-of-speech tracking is in fact better 
explained as tracking of hierarchical configuration and head–complement relations, with 
sensitivity to sequential statistics emerging only under artificial conditions (Y. Lu et al., 2022, 
2023; Zhao et al., 2025). Relatedly, neural tracking studies show that phrase-rate rhythms can be 
dissociated from lexical predictors, further suggesting that structure building can be an 
independent process (Brennan & Hale, 2019a; Coopmans et al., 2022). 
Taken together, these findings challenge the assumption that phrase-structure construction is 
strictly downstream of category detection. Instead, they point toward a model in which structural 
expectations are generated early, potentially in parallel with or even prior to lexical 
identification. The critical unresolved issue is whether such precedence extends beyond 
controlled paradigms and is observable in naturalistic reading, where comprehension unfolds 
continuously, without violations or artificial constraints. Addressing this gap requires

disentangling the two processes under ecologically valid conditions and establishing their

The present study 
The present study directly addresses this issue by separately examining phrase structure effects 
and syntactic category effects under naturalistic reading conditions, using the co-registered 
EEG–eye-tracking dataset ZuCo (see Figure 1). We first test whether pre-defined phrase 
structures could guide readers’ eye movements. From the eye-movement data, we computed 
transitional probabilities between words. If readers construct a phrase structure in advance, we 
predict that transitions will be stronger between syntactic heads—nodes central to structure 
building—relative to a baseline of serial, word-by-word reading. Next, we evaluate how well 
phrase structure-based predictions explain reading trajectories compared with alternative 
linguistic factors (e.g., lexical familiarity, surprisal). We compare the explanatory power of 
syntactic tree-based predictions against other linguistic factors in shaping reading trajectories. 
Finally, we examine the neural time course of structural influences by regressing syntactic 
surprisal against fixation-related potentials (FRPs). This approach allows us to test the existence 
of pre-defined syntactic trees and its comparative power and time course of structural influences 
in real-time comprehension. Together, these three steps allow us to disentangle phrase structure 
effects and syntactic category effects, providing the first naturalistic test of their relative timing, 
strength, and interaction.

Methods 
Materials and experiment design 
We used an openly available dataset, Zurich Cognitive Language Processing Corpus, for analysis 
(Hollenstein et al., 2018). It comprises simultaneous eye-tracking and EEG recordings of 12


## Page 4


4 
native English participants (5 female; mean age = 37.5 years; SD = 10.3 years) during reading. In 
this task, participants were presented with 400 positive, negative or neutral sentences and rated 
the emotion valence after reading each sentence. The sentences are movie reviews extracted from 
Stanford Sentiment Treebank and participants were allowed to read at their own pace. We used 
data from the Task 1 rather than from Task 2 for three reasons: firstly, the emotional valence task 
is more likely to elicit holistic holistic sentence processing compared to Task 2 which involves 
factual questions and might bias participants to focus on semantic content rather than syntactic 
structure; secondly, the stimuli in Task 1, which are from movie reviews, tend to use more 
diverse syntactic constructions than the biographical Wikipedia sentences in Task 2; thirdly, the 
omission rates on sentence level are lowest across the three tasks. Eye-tracking data and EEG 
data were recorded simultaneously during their reading.  
 
Data acquisition and preprocessing 
Eye movements were recorded with EyeLink 1000 Plus infrared video-based eye tracker (SR 
Research) at a sampling rate of 500 H, while EEG data were recorded with a 128-channel EEG 
Geodesic Hydrocel system (Electrical Geodesics, Eugene, Oregon), at a sampling rate of 500 Hz 
with a bandpass of 0.1 to 100 Hz. The preprocessing of EEG and eye movements followed the 
same pipeline of Hollenstein (2018). For the eye movements, fixations were identified with a 
Gaussian mixture model for better cluster gaze data and fixation allocation, and those shorter 
than 100ms were excluded from further analysis as they are unlikely to reflect meaningful 
cognitive activities. EEG data underwent high-pass filtering at 0.5Hz and notch filtering at 49-
51Hz. Channels with less than 0.85 correlation with other channels, with line noise 4 standard 
deviations more than other channels, with a flatline duration longer than 5 seconds were marked 
as bad. Artifacts were removed by regressing out the nine EOG (electrooculography) channels 
from the 105 scalp EEG channels and MARA (Multiple Artifact Rejection Algorithm). Bad 
channels that could not be cleaned were interpolated using spherical spline interpolation.   
 
Experiment 1: Transitional probability 
Our first experiment examines the role of pre-defined syntactic structures in eye movements 
using eye movement transition probabilities. We define words in sentences as head or non-head 
in syntactic dependency trees and test whether there’s an observable preference in fixating on 
words that belongs to syntactic heads over non-heads. We focus on the movement of eyes instead 
of durations of fixations as syntactic analysis related strategies are often not visible in duration-
only measures but in scan paths (Dempsey et al., 2023; Malsburg & Vasishth, 2013).  
Method 
We define the head v.s. non-head role of words in the dependency tree building with parsing 
depth values, which were obtained for each word using dependency parsing module of Standard 
Parser (de Marneffe et al., 2006; Nivre et al., 2016; Schuster & Manning, 2016). The root of a 
sentence was considered the base node and was assigned zero and the depths of the rest words 
were determined by counting the number of dependency links traversed from the word to the 
root.  These words were categorized into two groups based on their syntactic centrality: head 
(depth = 0 or 1, corresponding to syntactic heads) and non-head (depth ≥ 2). Those with a 
parsing depth of 1 or 0 are defined as head words as they are most syntactically close to the root,


## Page 5


5 
thus playing a prominent role in the sentence. Conversely, words with a higher depth (i.e., those 
farther from the root) often serve secondary syntactic roles, such as modifiers or adjuncts and 
were labeled as non-head.  
After labeling each word in each sentence as “head” or “non-head”, we obtained a baseline 
measure of transitional probabilities by tracking the frequency of four transition types: head to 
head, head to non-head, non-head to head, and non-head to non-head. The transitional 
probability of each type is given by dividing the frequency of that transition type by the total 
number of transitions. This baseline measure reflects transition probabilities expected under the 
assumption that readers read sentences in a word-by-word, serial manner.  
To evaluate actual transitional probabilities from eye-tracking data, we repeated the analysis on 
word sequences reconstructed based on the order of fixations that reflected participants’ gaze-
driven reading paths. The resulting gaze-driven transitional probabilities were averaged per 
sentence across participants. Finally, to test whether readers exhibited a stronger head to head 
transition tendency during naturalistic reading than expected from syntactic structure alone, we 
conducted an independent samples t-test comparing the head-to-head transition rates between the 
syntactic baseline and the gaze-derived condition. We also compared other transition types (head 
to non-head, non-head to head, and non-head to non-head) across conditions to examine whether 
the effects were specific to syntactic heads or reflected broader changes in gaze behavior. 
Our analysis focused on comparing head to head transitional probabilities between the syntactic 
baseline and the gaze-derived (actual) reading condition, rather than comparing different 
transition types (i.e., head to head vs. head to non-head) within a single condition. This is to test 
whether naturalistic reading behavior reflects stronger syntactic structuring than would be 
expected from serial word order alone. A higher frequency of head to head transitions in the 
gaze-derived condition, relative to the syntactic baseline, would indicate that readers are more 
likely to move between syntactic heads than would occur by chance or linear progression. In 
contrast, comparing head to head versus head to non-head transitions within a single condition 
would capture local preferences for specific transition types but would not reveal whether such 
preferences were due to a pre-existing syntactic tree or due to original syntactic ordering. 
 
Results  
To test whether readers are guided by syntactic structure during naturalistic reading, we 
compared transitional probabilities between syntactic heads and non-heads across the gaze-
derived and text baseline conditions (Figure 1). Transitional probabilities were analyzed 
separately for four transition types: head-to-head, head-to-non-head, non-head-to-head, and non-
head-to-non-head. 
Figure 2 shows the mean transitional probabilities for four transition types across the gaze-
derived and text baseline conditions. For head-to-head transitions, the gaze-derived condition (M 
= 0.22, SD = 0.20) showed a higher mean probability than the text baseline (M = 0.17, SD = 
0.18). A Welch’s t-test confirmed that this difference was significant  t = 3.63, p = .003). This 
pattern suggests that during natural reading, gaze shifts are more likely to connect syntactic 
heads compared to what would be expected from a purely serial reading baseline. 
For head-to-non-head transitions, there was no reliable difference between the gaze-derived and 
baseline conditions (p > .3). A similar null result was observed for non head-to-head transitions

(p > .4). These non-significant differences indicate that readers did not preferentially transition 
from heads to dependents or vice versa across conditions. 
In contrast, non-head-to-non-head transitions exhibited the opposite pattern of head-to-head 
transitions: the gaze-derived condition (M = 0.47, SD = 0.22) showed lower probabilities than 
the text baseline (M = 0.51, SD = 0.23), t = –2.72, p = .0067. This reduction suggests that gaze

Overall, these results reveal a selective shift in transitional probabilities. Specifically, gaze 
trajectories in natural reading show an increase in head-to-head transitions and a decrease in non-
head-to-non-head transitions, while other transition types remain unchanged. This pattern is 
consistent with the idea that syntactic heads serve as anchors of structural connectivity, while 
non-head elements are less likely to be directly linked by eye movements.

Experiment 2: Bayesian networks 
However, it’s possible that significant head-to-head transitions are attributed to non-syntactic-
related factors. In the next step, to dissociate the weight of syntactic structures, as opposed to 
other factors, in influencing the deviation of eye movements from actual word sequences, we 
employed a data-driven, non-causal modeling approach using Bayesian network. Unlike standard 
regression, which typically assumes fixed model structure and largely independent variables, 
Bayesian networks can model conditional dependencies and learn the dependency structure 
directly from the data.  
Method 
A total of six variables were included. One variable was the edit distance between two sequences 
collected in the previous analysis, i.e., each sentence’s observed gaze-derived word sequence and 
the original left-to-right textual word order. Edit distance is used to measure the similarity of two 
sequences (gaze-derived sequence, word sequence) and it’s the minimal number of edit 
operations that have to be performed on one of these sequences in order to transform one into the


## Page 7


7 
other. The minimal number of edits was computed using the Needleman–Wunsch algorithm 
which has been used in bioinformatics for analyzing DNA sequences (Needleman & Wunsch, 
1970). In this study, we calculated the distance between two sequences: one sequence is a 
sequence of words that are fixated by the readers, in the order of time and the other sequence is 
simply word sequence presented in the original text.  This measure quantifies the extent to which 
a reader's eye movement pattern deviates from the linear progression of the sentence, serving as a 
proxy for the impact of structural and lexical factors on reading behavior. Two structural 
variables were derived from dependency parsing trees: maximum parsing depth, which captures 
the depth of the most deeply nested dependency relation in a sentence (Weissbart & Martin, 
2024), and the number of clauses, which reflects sentence-level structural complexity (X. Lu, 
2010). Two other variables were drawn from the E-Z Reader model of eye movement control 
during reading (Reichle et al., 1998, 2003), including familiarity and lexical surprisal. 
Familiarity was defined as a lexical-level estimate of word retrieval ease based on frequency 
norms. Lexical surprisal was computed using the GPT-2 language model (Radford et al., 2019), 
operationalized as the negative log probability of a word given its preceding context. We chose 
the two variables as they represent core components driving saccadic programming, with word 
familiarity influencing the early stages of lexical access (L1), and predictability/surprisal 
influencing later integration (L2), according to the updated E-Z Reader model (Reichle et al., 
2003).  
For each sentence, word-level surprisal values were averaged to produce a sentence-level 
estimate. All variables were z-scored and subsequently discretized into four bins of equal width 
to allow for categorical Bayesian modeling. Prior to modeling, the distributions and 
interdependencies among variables were examined using correlation matrices. Visualization of 
the correlation structure was performed using the “corrplot” package in R (version 4.2.2). 
We employed a data-driven structure learning approach using the hill-climbing algorithm 
implemented in the “bnlearn” package (Scutari, 2010, version 5.0.1). This algorithm identifies a 
network structure that optimizes the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) based on the observed 
data. The resulting structures were visualized using graphviz.plot. 
The final network was fitted using maximum likelihood estimation, and the strength of 
connections between nodes was assessed via arc strength and mutual information, using the 
“infotheo” package (version 1.2.0.1). Arc strength, computed through bootstrapped resampling, 
reflects the stability of each connection in the learned network structure; while mutual 
information quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about the target variable (edit distance) 
provided by each predictor. The two variables allowed us to assess both the robustness and 
explanatory power of each factor in explaining scanpath deviations. 
Results  
We next examined how structural and lexical factors jointly influence scanpath deviations, 
operationalized as edit distance between observed gaze sequences and baseline reading order. A 
correlation matrix (Figure 2a) revealed moderate-to-strong associations among the five 
predictors: number of clauses, maximum tree depth, edit distance, familiarity, and surprisal. 
Structural measures were especially interrelated: maximum depth correlated strongly with edit 
distance (r = .67) and number of clauses (r = .64). Lexical factors showed weaker correlations 
with edit distance (familiarity: r = .48; surprisal: r = .32).

Experiment 3: Surprisal-Based Regression Analysis 
To further probe the timing of syntactic influence on reading, we further identified syntactic 
surprisal as the variable to index the anticipatory processing of syntactic structure. We regressed 
this variable against fixation-related potentials (FRPs), which are event-related EEG responses 
time-locked to eye fixations during reading, in order to formally characterize when syntactic 
expectations begin to influence neural activity.  
Method 
Syntactic surprisal, different from lexical surprisal, can quantify the change in syntactic 
uncertainty as each word is integrated into an evolving parse structure, best capturing how well 
the unfolding sentence matches a predicted syntactic frame, if there’s any. In our study, syntactic 
surprisal is calculated from the unpredictability of the next word’s syntactic role, as indicated by 
the supertag, following the practice of Arehalli (2022). A supertag is a syntactic label assigned to 
a word that encodes how it combines with neighboring words to form larger syntactic structures 
according to Combinatory Categorial Grammar (CCG). To calculate syntactic surprisal, the 
researchers used a two-layer LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory network) as the encoder and it is 
trained on a combination of minimizing language modeling loss and CCG supertagging loss. 
During training, the model learns to predict both the next word and its supertag based on the 
preceding words in the sentence. 
FRPs were extracted by segmenting the continuous EEG signal into epochs time-locked to the 
onset of each eye fixation, using “EYE EEG extension” (Winkler et al., 2014). To ensure precise 
temporal alignment between EEG and eye-tracking data, shared events recorded in both data 
streams were identified, and a linear transformation was applied to correct for minor timing 
discrepancies between the two modalities. The resulting synchronization error was minimal, not 
exceeding one sample (2 ms). Following synchronization, the EEG was segmented into epochs 
ranging from –600 ms to +1000 ms relative to fixation onset, allowing for analysis of neural 
activity both before and after each fixation.

To statistically assess the reliability of these effects, we implemented a non-parametric cluster-

effect was consistent across participants. To control for multiple comparisons across time, we 
used 1,000 permutations to generate a null distribution of cluster-level statistics, and clusters 
were considered significant if their cluster-level p-value, corrected via permutation, was below 
0.05.

In addition to the cluster-based permutation approach, we implemented a 
complementary bootstrap-based analysis to assess the temporal reliability of regression 
coefficients linking surprisal to FRPs. This method provided robust estimates of confidence 
intervals for effect sizes while also correcting for multiple comparisons across timepoints. 
For each subject, we obtained time-resolved beta coefficients from the linear regression of 
surprisal onto EEG amplitudes. These subject-level beta time courses were then entered into 
a non-parametric bootstrap procedure (Efron & Tibshirani, 1994). At each timepoint, we drew 
1,000 bootstrap resamples with replacement from the set of participants and recomputed the 
mean beta coefficient for each resample. This generated a bootstrapped distribution of mean 
effects, from which we extracted bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals by taking the 2.5th and 
97.5th percentiles. 
To statistically assess whether coefficients at each timepoint were reliably greater than zero, we 
applied a Wilcoxon signed-rank test across participants. To control for the family-wise error rate 
across time, we corrected raw p-values using the false discovery rate (FDR) method (Benjamini 
& Hochberg, 1995) with α = .05. Timepoints were considered significant if their FDR-corrected 
p-values fell below threshold. 
For visualization, mean beta values were plotted across the time course, with shaded bands 
representing 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals. Timepoints surviving FDR correction were 
highlighted with scatter points (see Figure 3b). To directly compare predictors such as syntactic 
surprisal and lexical surprisal, this analysis was repeated for each predictor, and the resulting 
time courses were overlaid on the same axis. 
This bootstrap-based procedure complements the cluster-based permutation analysis in two 
ways. First, it provides time-resolved confidence intervals around regression coefficients, giving 
a more precise estimate of effect magnitude. Second, while the permutation test 
identifies contiguous clusters of significance, the bootstrap approach allows us to track pointwise 
reliability across time. Together, these methods offer converging evidence for the robustness and 
temporal profile of surprisal effects on neural activity during reading. 
Results  
Finally, we investigated the time course of syntactic surprisal effects on fixation-related 
potentials (FRPs) using regression-based analyses (Figure 4a). The time-resolved regression 
revealed two significant temporal clusters, as identified by cluster-based permutation testing 
(p < .05, two-tailed, corrected). 
The first cluster occurred in the late pre-fixation window (−184 – −10 ms), where syntactic 
surprisal positively modulated EEG activity. This effect indicates that neural activity was already 
sensitive to the syntactic predictability of the upcoming word before fixation, consistent with 
anticipatory processing. The second cluster emerged in the early post-fixation window (48–300 
ms), again showing a positive-going effect of syntactic surprisal. This suggests that once the 
word was fixated, surprisal continued to influence neural activity during the earliest stages of 
lexical access and syntactic integration. Notably, the effect dissipated after ~300 ms, with no 
significant clusters detected in later windows overlapping with canonical N400 or P600 
components.

Consistent with the cluster-based permutation results (Figure 3a), the bootstrap method 
revealed early and sustained regression effects of syntactic surprisal. Beta values rose above zero 
starting around –184 ms relative to fixation onset, remained reliably positive throughout the pre-
fixation window, and extended into the early post-fixation period. The dense cluster of 
significant timepoints between ~–184 and +300 ms indicates that surprisal systematically 
predicted EEG amplitude both before and shortly after the eyes landed on the word. Importantly, 
the non-parametric confidence intervals confirm that this effect was robust across participants 
rather than driven by outliers. 
Beyond ~400 ms, the bootstrapped intervals increasingly overlapped with zero, and fewer 
timepoints reached significance. This suggests that surprisal exerted its strongest influence in 
the anticipatory and early integration windows, with reduced impact during later stages of 
processing. 
Together, these findings provide evidence for an early, dual-stage effect of syntactic surprisal: 
one anticipatory, occurring before the eyes land on the word, and one rapid, unfolding 
immediately after fixation. These temporal dynamics indicate that syntactic expectations shape 
neural activity both proactively and within the first few hundred milliseconds of word 
processing.

Discussion 
The present study examines how syntactic structure influences naturalistic reading by combining 
eye movement data with electrophysiological measures of neural activity. Across three 
converging analyses, we found robust evidence that syntactic structure exerts an early and 
measurable influence on both gaze trajectories and neural responses. Specifically, (i) transitional 
probability analyses revealed that readers’ gaze shifts disproportionately linked syntactic heads, 
while bypassing non-heads; (ii) Bayesian network modeling demonstrated that the gaze 
trajectories are largely explained by structural measures, particularly maximum parse depth, in 
comparison with lexical surprisal and familiarity; and (iii) regression-based EEG analyses 
identified two early clusters of syntactic surprisal effects, one pre-fixation and one shortly after 
fixation, pointing to both anticipatory and rapid integration mechanisms. Together, these findings 
support the view that readers engage in tree-guided processing, where an internalized syntactic 
scaffold constrains eye movements and neural dynamics from the earliest stages of sentence 
comprehension. 
 
Syntactic heads as anchors in reading 
Our analysis of transitional probabilities provides new evidence that readers’ gaze trajectories are 
not uniformly distributed across words, but rather show a selective preference for syntactic 
heads. Head-to-head transitions were significantly more frequent in the gaze-derived condition 
than in the serial text baseline, suggesting that heads serve as organizational anchors in guiding 
visual attention. Conversely, non-head-to-non-head transitions were suppressed in natural 
reading, implying that dependents receive reduced direct linkage. This finding echoes with a 
previous study linking eye-movement transitions to dependency structure, which reports that 
backward saccades coincide with dependency relations, supporting structure-guided regressions 
in naturalistic reading (Lopopolo et al., 2019).  
Previous frequency-tagging and TRF studies, by separating structural metrics (unlabeled node 
counts, phrase-rate rhythms) from POS/lexical predictors, have also shown in many cases, 
distinct neural signatures for structural building don’t depend on (and can precede) category 
confirmation (Brennan & Hale, 2019a; Coopmans et al., 2022, 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). 
However, in these studies, structural operations are often derived from model regressors. Our 
study contributes to this line of evidence by more directly hypothesizing the format of phrase 
structures and testing the precedence of it by analyzing eye movement trajectories.  
 
Impact of syntactic factors on reading paths 
Despite the significant transitions between syntactically important words, it’s possible that the 
scan path preference is dominated by other lexical factors, instead of syntax itself.  The Bayesian 
network analysis offers further insight into the strength of different factors in shaping deviations 
from baseline serial reading. Among the candidate predictors, maximum parse depth emerged as 
the strongest determinant of edit distance, followed by lexical familiarity, with surprisal exerting


## Page 13


13 
only a weak effect. This hierarchy suggests that readers’ scanpaths are most sensitive to 
structural complexity—specifically, the depth of hierarchical embedding—and less sensitive to 
the distributional predictability of upcoming words. 
Word frequency was included as it is a foundational determinant of reading efficiency, exerting a 
strong and early influence on word recognition processes. High-frequency words are identified 
more rapidly, receive shorter fixations, and are more likely to be skipped compared to low-
frequency words, making frequency a key predictor of visual attention during reading (Inhoff & 
Rayner, 1986; Rayner & Duffy, 1986). ERP studies similarly show that high-frequency words 
elicit reduced N400 amplitudes, reflecting more efficient semantic retrieval (Kutas & 
Federmeier, 2011). Predictability, often quantified by cloze probability, likewise impacts 
integration, with highly predictable words being processed faster, skipped more often, and 
generating smaller N400s (Ehrlich & Rayner, 1981; Kutas & Hillyard, 1980; Rayner et al., 
2004). Together, frequency and predictability act as lexical-level facilitators, streamlining word 
recognition and integration in fluent reading. 
However, both familiarity and surprisal operate primarily at the word level, whereas edit distance 
is a sentence-level structural measure. Thus, although lexical frequency and predictability 
contribute to local processing differences, their impact on global scan path deviations is more 
limited. This explains why familiarity had a moderate influence and surprisal a weaker effect, 
and why surprisal had much lower mutual information with edit distance than parsing depth in 
the Bayesian model. Parsing depth captures the degree of hierarchical embedding within a 
sentence—such as center-embedded or object-relative clauses—long associated with processing 
difficulty and re-reading behavior (Gibson, 1998; Grodner & Gibson, 2005; Lewis & Vasishth, 
2005). Eye-tracking studies confirm that deeper structures elicit longer fixations, more 
regressions, and disrupted forward progress (Frazier & Rayner, 1982; Staub, 2015). These 
nonlinear scan paths translate directly into greater divergence from serial reading order, which 
the edit distance metric captures. 
Combined, the evidence seems to suggestion a complementary role for lexical and syntactic 
factors in shaping fixation trajectories: lexical factors modulate processing load locally, while 
syntactic trees provide the backbone guiding global navigation. Interestingly, lexical familiarity 
exerted a substantial secondary effect, nearly rivaling parse depth, suggesting that when readers 
encounter less familiar words, they may be more likely to deviate from a serial scan path—either 
because of integration demands or regressions to retrieve supporting context.  
 
Timing of syntactic factor  
Much of the prior literature on the timing of syntactic processing has relied on violation 
paradigms. Two classical ERP findings dominate this field: (i) a large posterior positive-going 
wave peaking around 600 ms after the critical word, typically associated with syntactic 
reanalysis or repair (the P600), and (ii) an early left anterior negativity (ELAN) around 100–200 
ms, elicited by word-category violations (Friederici, 2002; Neville et al., 1991). These violation-
elicited responses have been critical in establishing that syntax is processed rapidly and 
automatically. However, as many scholars have noted, such responses can be lagging indicators 
of downstream repair processes rather than direct reflections of the earliest syntactic 
computations (Steinhauer & Drury, 2012b). Moreover, violation paradigms raise questions about


## Page 14


14 
ecological validity: they rely on rare or artificially constructed anomalies that may not generalize 
to naturalistic reading. 
In the present study, we used syntactic surprisal—a graded, context-sensitive measure of 
structural expectation—as a predictor of neural activity during naturalistic reading. This 
approach revealed three significant temporal clusters: one beginning approximately –184 ms 
(pre-fixation), one emerging at ~ 48 ms, and a later cluster around 300 ms after word onset. 
The pre-fixation cluster (–184– –10 ms) is particularly striking, as it indicates that neural activity 
was modulated by syntactic expectations even before the eyes landed on the word. This 
anticipatory effect is consistent with predictive processing accounts of language comprehension 
(Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Heilbron et al., 2022), which posit that readers continuously 
generate expectations about upcoming syntactic categories. Similar anticipatory negativities have 
been observed in EEG and MEG studies of speech comprehension, where pre-onset activity 
reflects predictive coding of syntactic features (Bonhage et al., 2015; Brennan & Hale, 2019b; 
Goldstein et al., 2022). Our findings extend these results to naturalistic, self-paced reading, 
providing strong evidence that structural prediction is not an artifact of controlled laboratory 
paradigms but a central feature of everyday sentence processing. 
The second cluster (48–300 ms post-onset) likely reflects the rapid matching of expectations 
with incoming input. Neurophysiological studies have shown that word-class violations can elicit 
neural responses within 100 ms—earlier than responses to semantic mismatches (Hasting & 
Kotz, 2008; Neville et al., 1991). Our finding of a surprisal-driven modulation beginning around 
50 ms dovetails with this work, suggesting that syntactic category information is extracted 
almost immediately upon visual uptake and checked against pre-existing expectations. 
These findings refine the traditional temporal sequence of parsing. Previous studies have 
generally identified three time windows for the processing of syntax. Firstly, an early time 
window(≈100–250 ms after an informative cue), where syntactic category information is 
decodable very quickly: ~100 ms after word onset in continuous speech and ~160–190 ms after 
within‑word disambiguation—while early posterior‑temporal signals of composition emerge 
around ~200 ms (Arana et al., 2021b; Gwilliams et al., 2024; Matchin et al., 2019; Sarrett et al., 
2022). Secondly, a mid time window  (~250–400 ms), which is dominated by reliable 
structure‑engagement effects across left fronto‑temporal regions. Paradigms that minimize 
semantic or temporal confounds (e.g., lists embedded in sentences; simultaneous two‑word 
presentations) consistently report boosts in LIFG/LATL/PTL around ~250–400 ms and 
frame‑sensitive effects beginning ~231 ms, pointing to active phrase‑structural integration in this 
band (Krogh & Pylkkänen, 2025; Law & Pylkkänen, 2021). Auditory MEG with representational 
analyses also finds ongoing lexico‑syntactic sensitivity in posterior temporal cortex during this 
period, reinforcing that category‑linked and phrase‑structure computations cohabit these 
latencies rather than occupying distinct slots in time (Matchin et al., 2019; Tyler et al., 2013). 
Thirdly, a late time window (≥500–900+ ms, extending further) that encompass reanalysis, 
repair, closure, and reinstatement processes triggered by both category/subcategorization 
conflicts and phrase‑structural demands (Osterhout & Holcomb, 1993).  
Our study pushes the “early window” backwards into a genuinely anticipatory phase, by showing 
that syntactic surprisal modulates neural activity before the eyes land on the word. This extends 
prior onset‑locked evidence for rapid, context‑driven class/structure signals in MEG/EEG to a 
pre‑fixational timescale, tying syntactic prediction directly to the oculomotor timeline—a gap in


## Page 15


15 
the current corpus, which lacks eye‑movement–aligned neural data (Gwilliams et al., 2024; 
Sarrett et al., 2022). It supports the view that top‑down structural expectations are in place before 
foveal uptake, complementing early context‑driven word‑class decoding around ~100 ms after 
onset in continuous speech.  
Also, the study goes beyond classical syntax-first models by clarifying temporal order of 
different components of syntax (category detection versus phrase structure construction). These 
three strands of evidence support what we might term a tree-spotting hypothesis of reading: 
readers activates an internalized syntactic scaffold that (i) privileges head-to-head connections in 
guiding eye movements, (ii) modulates scanpath deviations primarily through structural depth 
rather than lexical probability, and (iii) shapes neural activity in both anticipatory and rapid-
integration phases. Our findings therefore lend support to hybrid models (Staub & Clifton, 2006; 
Kuperberg, 2007), in which both syntactic structure and probabilistic expectations jointly shape 
comprehension, but with syntactic scaffolds providing the backbone of early processing. 
Furthermore, a methodological contribution of this work is the use of the ZuCo dataset, which 
co-registers eye movements and EEG during naturalistic reading. Unlike rapid serial visual 
presentation paradigms, our approach captures reading in conditions closer to everyday 
experience. This allowed us to test not just whether syntactic processing occurs early — already 
well-established — but whether such processing measurably shapes the pattern of eye 
movements and the time course of neural activity in continuous text. By linking transitional 
probability analyses with Bayesian modeling and regression-based EEG, we triangulate 
converging evidence across multiple analytic levels. 
 
Conclusion 
This study distinguishes two types of syntactic process: 1) syntactic category detection, 2) phrase 
structure construction and provides direct evidence of the temporal order of the two processes.  
Across analyses of transitional probabilities, Bayesian network modeling, and time-resolved 
EEG regression, we found that phrase structure exerts a dominant influence on comprehension: 
readers preferentially link syntactic heads through gaze transitions, structural depth strongly 
predicts deviations from linear scanpaths, and syntactic surprisal reliably modulates neural 
activity in anticipatory and early post-fixation windows. 
Importantly, these effects were observed under ecologically valid conditions, extending beyond 
the violation paradigms that have dominated prior neurolinguistic research. Our results show that 
syntactic processing begins well before word onset, consistent with predictive coding 
frameworks, and that structural complexity exerts a stronger influence on global reading 
trajectories than lexical frequency or surprisal. Together, these findings support a tree-
scaffolding account of sentence comprehension, in which readers maintain and update syntactic 
structures that constrain both visual attention and neural processing in real time. 
By integrating eye-tracking and EEG evidence with modern statistical methods, this work 
advances our understanding of the timing, strength, and mechanisms of syntactic processing. 
More broadly, it underscores the importance of moving beyond isolated anomalies to study how 
predictive syntactic coding operates in everyday reading. Future research should extend these 
methods to developmental and clinical populations and across languages, to determine whether

reading experience and linguistic structure.
