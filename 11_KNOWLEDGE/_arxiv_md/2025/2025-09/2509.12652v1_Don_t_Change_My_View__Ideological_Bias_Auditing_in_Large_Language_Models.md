Don’t Change My View: Ideological Bias

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly embedded in products
used by millions, their outputs may influence individual beliefs and, cumulatively,
shape public opinion. If the behavior of LLMs can be intentionally steered toward
specific ideological positions, such as political or religious views, then those who
control these systems could gain disproportionate influence over public discourse.
Although it remains an open question whether LLMs can reliably be guided toward
coherent ideological stances and whether such steering can be effectively prevented,
a crucial first step is to develop methods for detecting when such steering attempts
occur. In this work, we adapt a previously proposed statistical method to the new
context of ideological bias auditing. Our approach carries over the model-agnostic
design of the original framework, which does not require access to the internals of
the language model. Instead, it identifies potential ideological steering by analyzing
distributional shifts in model outputs across prompts that are thematically related
to a chosen topic. This design makes the method particularly suitable for auditing
proprietary black-box systems. We validate our approach through a series of
experiments, demonstrating its practical applicability and its potential to support
independent post hoc audits of LLM behavior.

Introduction
LLMs have become core components of many digital services, powering chatbots, coding assistants,
search engines, and more [Guu et al., 2020, Lewis et al., 2020, Li et al., 2022, Raffel et al., 2020,
Chen et al., 2021, Ouyang et al., 2022]. In particular, chatbots are increasingly more capable of
helping users research and form opinions by generating summaries, explanations, and arguments.
Because these models may influence users’ beliefs, it is essential that their outputs remain factual,
balanced, and free from systematic bias [Bolukbasi et al., 2016, Caliskan et al., 2017, Bender et al.,
2021, Feng et al., 2023, Motoki et al., 2024].
From a societal perspective, the widespread adoption of LLMs raises the crucial question of whether
individuals or corporations that control these systems could intentionally steer them toward specific
ideological positions in an attempt to influence their users’ opinions. Even though it is still an open
question whether LLMs can reliably be guided toward a consistent ideological stance [Santurkar
et al., 2023, Ceron et al., 2024, Kim et al., 2025], it is conceivable that providers may attempt to do
so, especially through lightweight interventions such as modifying the system prompt. For instance,
a provider might try to bias a model toward conservative Christian values, potentially influencing its
responses on issues such as abortion.1

Whether such interventions should be legally restricted is ultimately a regulatory question. However,
the mere possibility that a few powerful actors could significantly influence public opinion poses
a societal risk. As a first step toward addressing this issue, we propose a method for monitoring
whether such steering occurs.
Specifically, we repurpose the training-free, model-agnostic framework introduced by Levin et al.
[2025], originally designed for detecting unauthorized use of proprietary system prompts. We apply
this framework to the task of ideological bias auditing by monitoring whether a model’s responses to
prompts on sensitive topics remain consistent over time. More concretely, we assume a fixed LLM
and aim to detect steering attempts that result in a change in model outputs. The approach requires no
access to model internals and is suitable for black-box settings, making it well-suited for independent
post hoc audits.
For each sensitive topic of interest, we generate a set of task prompts and track model outputs over
time. If responses exhibit significant distributional shifts, the system flags the change for further
inspection. The framework is summarized in Figure 1.

One might ask whether systematic monitoring is truly necessary, given that users could probably
recognize drastic changes in model behavior on their own. However, there are several challenges that
make this complicated in practice. Even subtle shifts in framing or emphasis, potentially unrecog-
nizable to humans, can meaningfully affect human judgments and opinions Tversky and Kahneman
[1981], Entman [2006], Chong and Druckman [2007], Beratšová et al. [2016]. Furthermore, without a
principled approach, it is difficult to determine whether variations in model responses reflect inherent
stochasticity or indicate a more fundamental change in behavior. Our framework addresses this
problem by applying the statistical hypothesis test proposed by Levin et al. [2025] with a user-defined
significance threshold α, enabling more reliable detection of distributional shifts. Manual inspection
also does not scale well when attempting to monitor model outputs across a wide range of topics,
further underscoring the need for automated auditing techniques.

Related Work
While still an area of active research, biases in artificial intelligence systems have been rigorously
examined [Bolukbasi et al., 2016, Gordon et al., 2020, Raghavan et al., 2020, Norori et al., 2021].
Numerous studies have identified systematic biases in LLMs, producing a wide range of interesting
findings Bang et al. [2021], Bender et al. [2021], Nadeem et al. [2021], Sheng et al. [2021], Hosseini
et al. [2023], Schramowski et al. [2022], Smith et al. [2022], Liu et al. [2024]. One notable result is
that these models tend to express left-leaning or liberal views on social issues, while often exhibiting
libertarian attitudes on economic topics [Hartmann et al., 2023, Bang et al., 2024, Motoki et al., 2024,
Rozado, 2024, Exler et al., 2025, Bang et al., 2024, Santurkar et al., 2023, Rettenberger et al., 2025].
To uncover these patterns, researchers frequently employ sophisticated prompting strategies, often
using datasets designed to capture human political and cultural opinions [Feng et al., 2023, Azzopardi
and Moshfeghi, 2024, Caliskan et al., 2017].
Although these methods provide valuable insights into model biases and facilitate cross-model
comparisons, they were not developed to monitor how a single model’s outputs change over time and
to detect sudden shifts in behavior.
In theory, one could repeatedly apply these established bias-auditing techniques to track distributional
changes in a deployed model. However, these methods were not designed or evaluated for ongoing
monitoring, and their sensitivity to subtle shifts is unclear. Moreover, such approaches are likely to be
computationally more expensive than the lightweight monitoring framework proposed in this work,
which is specifically intended for efficient detection of distributional changes over time.
A central challenge in evaluating any monitoring framework is establishing a realistic way to simulate
interventions that might steer model behavior in deployment. For this purpose, we rely on modifying
the system prompt, since this method is both straightforward and effective in practice. The system
prompt has a well-documented influence on the outputs of LLMs. Even small adjustments to the
prompt can meaningfully alter both the tone and the substance of responses, affecting performance
across a wide range of tasks [Jiang et al., 2020, Ouyang et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2023]. In addition to
conveying high-level instructions, system prompts frequently include elements of in-context learning,
such as zero-shot or few-shot examples, which allow models to adapt to new tasks at inference
time without retraining [Brown et al., 2020, Kojima et al., 2022]. Because these design choices can
substantially impact model behavior, prompt engineering has become an important and often complex
part of model deployment [Schulhoff et al., 2024]. While some prompt collections are publicly
available, system prompts used in commercial models are often proprietary and not disclosed [xAI
Team, 2025, Anthropic Team, 2025].
The opacity of system prompts has motivated the development of techniques for reconstructing or
inferring hidden prompt content by eliciting information directly from the model’s outputs. Such
methods are often referred to as prompt extraction attacks and raise concerns regarding prompt
privacy Morris et al. [2024], Zhang et al. [2024], Hui et al. [2024], Yang et al. [2024].
Prompt membership inference addresses the question of whether two systems use the same underlying
system prompt. This is particularly important in precisely these scenarios where a proprietary prompt
may have been leaked or replicated without authorization. Levin et al. [2025], whose work lays the
foundation for ours, approach this problem by proposing a statistical test to determine whether two
sets of outputs are likely to have been generated under the same prompt. The task is formalized as a
hypothesis test, where the null hypothesis assumes no difference between the two output distributions.
Their method is model-agnostic and operates in a black-box setting, requiring access only to the
generated responses, not to the model’s internal parameters or the prompts themselves. The present
work builds directly on this methodology by applying it to a different problem setting. Rather than
addressing prompt membership inference, we adapt the statistical test proposed by Levin et al. [2025]
to monitor for changes in ideological alignment.

Methodology
This section summarizes the statistical methodology introduced by Levin et al. [2025], explains its
adaptation to our context, and outlines our procedure for generating task-relevant prompts.
3.1
Prompt Detective
Prompt Detective is a training-free statistical method for determining whether the system prompt
used by an LLM-based service matches a known reference prompt [Levin et al., 2025]. The approach
compares distributions of model outputs generated under two different system prompts and applies a
statistical hypothesis test to identify significant differences, thereby indicating whether the underlying
prompts are distinct.
Given a language model f, two system prompts p and ¯p, and a collection of n task prompts {qj}n
j=1,
the method proceeds as follows. For each task prompt qj, the model is queried k times under both p
and ¯p, yielding two sets of responses. These responses are then embedded using a sentence embedding
model, such as BERT [Reimers and Gurevych, 2019], to obtain high-dimensional semantic vectors.
The mean embedding for each group is computed, and the cosine similarity between the two mean
embeddings, denoted sobs, is used as the test statistic.
To assess statistical significance, a permutation test [Good, 2013] is conducted. For each of Nperm
permutations, the embeddings for each task prompt are pooled and randomly split into two new groups
of size k, from which new mean embeddings and cosine similarities are calculated. This process
generates a null distribution of cosine similarities expected by chance. The p-value is estimated as

4
Experiments
To evaluate the effectiveness of our framework, we simulate a real-world scenario in which a newly
released LLM is accessed through a public chat interface. The goal is to monitor whether the model’s
responses to prompts on sensitive topics remain stable over time. We focus on ideologically motivated
changes to the system prompt, a simple yet realistic form of intervention that does not require costly
retraining. This choice keeps resource requirements low, making it feasible given our constraints.
Each of our experiments begins by selecting a topic of interest and constructing corresponding
task prompts, as described in Section 3.2. We then query the model to obtain an initial batch of
responses, which serves as the null distribution and captures the model’s baseline behavior. While a
real deployment would involve querying the model periodically over time, we simulate a distributional
shift immediately by collecting a second batch of responses from the same model using a modified
system prompt. To assess false negative rates, we include a control group where 25% of the prompt
pairs are not subjected to any intervention and use the same system prompt in both batches.

4.2
Subtle Political Manipulations via Conspiracy Framing
In this experiment, we extend our evaluation to a politically sensitive domain. Specifically, we
examine whether our approach can detect subtle ideological steering when a model is biased toward a
particular conspiracy theory. As in the previous experiment, the same set of general-purpose political
questions is used throughout and is agnostic to any specific conspiracy theory. Details on the task
prompt construction are provided in the Appendix.
Each system prompt used in this experiment is designed to introduce a specific bias. Each biased
prompt subtly frames the model as a believer in a particular conspiracy theory. In contrast to the
previous experiment, the model is instructed not to mention its beliefs explicitly. Instead, the prompt
guides the model to allow its worldview to influence its responses indirectly. An example pair of
prompts is shown in Figure 3, details are available in the Appendix.
The motivation for this setup is that conspiracy beliefs may affect the model’s responses only
indirectly, except when directly asked about the relevant theory. This makes the detection task
potentially more challenging, as we do not query the model about the conspiracy itself but instead
about general political issues. Nonetheless, as shown in Table 1, the proposed approach reliably
detects these potentially more subtle distributional shifts.
4.3
Real-World System Prompt: Grok 4
The preceding experiments relied on short, synthetic system prompts. Their design enabled us to
generate a reasonable number of prompt pairs with minimal manual effort. However, it remains an
open question whether the observed effects generalize to the longer and more nuanced prompts used
in real-world deployments. To investigate this, we conduct an additional experiment using the system
prompt from Grok 4, which is publicly available on xAI’s official GitHub repository xAI Team [2025].
Compared to synthetic prompts, the Grok 4 system prompt exhibits significantly greater length and
complexity, making it more challenging to modify in a controlled manner at scale. To introduce
ideological bias while preserving the structure and tone of the original, we manually construct a
single intervention, the original Grok 4 prompt, and a modified variant biased toward a conservative
Christian worldview. Both prompts are provided in the Appendix.3 The results in Table 1 suggest
that the proposed approach generalizes to more involved, production-grade system prompts.
5
Limitations and Future Work
We believe that there is an essential societal interest for the research community to establish rigorous
auditing frameworks capable of detecting ideological drift in LLMs. Such frameworks should provide
methods that are robust to superficial or spurious variations in model outputs while remaining sensitive
to subtle and meaningful changes in stance or framing. They should enable the detection of shifts
both within a single model over time and allow for cross-model comparisons. They have to function
in black-box settings and generalize across a wide range of potential steering mechanisms. We see
this work as a first step toward building such an auditing framework, but several limitations remain to
be addressed.
First, the detection method we adopt is highly sensitive to changes in output distributions and
may flag differences that are not semantically meaningful. For example, Levin et al. [2025] show
that even typographical errors in the system prompt can produce detectable shifts. However, such
changes appear unlikely to affect the stance or articulation of a model on important issues. An
effective auditing tool must distinguish between superficial variations and shifts that reflect changes in
underlying values or ideological positions. One possible mitigation strategy is to analyze the model’s
responses using methods that infer its alignment with specific political parties or value systems.

For example, some works investigate the political orientations of various LLMs by comparing their
responses to those of political parties [Hartmann et al., 2023, Bang et al., 2024, Motoki et al., 2024,
Rozado, 2024, Exler et al., 2025, Bang et al., 2024, Santurkar et al., 2023, Rettenberger et al., 2025]. A
similar strategy could in principle be used to monitor how a model’s inferred positions shift over time.
However, existing evaluations seem too costly to be run frequently, and may not be sensitive enough
to detect subtle framing changes or shifts in emphasis that could still influence user interpretation.
Second, our analysis focuses exclusively on a single class of interventions, namely changes to the
system prompt. This setting offers a controlled and tractable test case, but it, of course, does not
reflect the full range of ways in which models may be steered. In practice, model behavior can be
influenced through fine-tuning [Agiza et al., 2025], modifications to the training data [Hall et al.,
2022], retrieval-augmented generation [Guu et al., 2020, Lewis et al., 2020], among other approaches.
These mechanisms might be more difficult to isolate and evaluate, especially in black-box settings,
and a comprehensive auditing framework should be designed to detect behavior changes resulting
from a broad spectrum of intervention types.
Third, the empirical evaluation is limited in scope. We apply the method to a small number of prompts
and topics, primarily to demonstrate proof of concept. Real-world auditing requires broader topical
coverage, longer and more naturalistic model outputs, and sensitivity to complex forms of ideological
framing.
To advance this line of research, two directions are particularly important. First, new methods are
needed for comparing model outputs that are robust to spurious variations yet sensitive to subtle,
meaningful shifts, both within a single model over time and across different models. Second, the
development of standardized benchmarks and shared datasets is crucial for enabling more efficient,
transparent, and comparable evaluations of LLM auditing pipelines. We hope that this initial study
helps motivate further work in this area and attracts both community attention and resources to
support the development of rigorous auditing tools for ideological shifts in language models.
6
Conclusion
This work demonstrates that the training-free statistical framework developed by Levin et al. [2025]
for prompt membership inference can be repurposed to detect some form of ideological steering in
LLMs. We evaluate this approach in the specific context of system prompt changes and find that
resulting shifts in model outputs can be identified reliably. While the method requires constructing
topic-specific task prompts, doing so involves relatively little manual effort. Combined with the fact
that it only necessitates access to model outputs, this approach is well-suited for black-box settings and
enables scalable, automated, third-party auditing of deployed models. Although rigorous evaluation
remains challenging due to the absence of well-established benchmarks for model manipulation, our
experimental results indicate the potential for this method to detect basic attempts at ideological
steering, if deployed at scale with appropriate resources.
We view this contribution as an initial step toward a broader framework for monitoring value drift
in LLMs. The long-term objective is to develop transparent, automated tools capable of detecting
when models change their responses to sensitive topics. These tools should not only be able to detect
behavioral changes within a single model over time, but also be able to compare across various
models released by the same or different providers. Importantly, they must operate in black-box
settings without requiring access to model internals or training data, enabling independent entities
to perform extensible monitoring. Finally, such a framework must be robust enough to distinguish
meaningful ideological shifts from spurious or irrelevant variations in model outputs.

C
System Prompt Pair Generation
Now, we describe the methodology used to construct the system prompt pairs for the experiments
detailed in Sections 4.1 and 4.2. Each pair consists of a neutral prompt and a corresponding biased
prompt. The neutral prompts were selected from a predefined set of generic, unbiased phrases (see
Table 2).
The biased prompts were generated by augmenting each neutral prompt with a secondary component
designed to introduce a targeted ideological bias. This secondary component was constructed from
templates containing placeholders filled with values drawn from curated lists.
To facilitate rigorous evaluation, 25% of the prompt pairs served as controls, meaning both prompts
in the pair are identical, either both neutral or both biased.
C.1
Religiously Biased Prompts
For each prompt pair in the religion-focused experiment, we first sampled one of five predefined
neutral system prompts (Table 2) to serve as the unbiased baseline. To construct the biased counterpart,
we selected one of twenty religious worldviews, each represented by a belief label and a concise
description (Table 3). We then applied one of twenty templated phrasings (Table 5) to introduce the
selected worldview into the neutral prompt. Specifically, the template was filled with the chosen
belief and its description and appended to the original neutral prompt. This procedure yielded the
paired set of biased and unbiased system prompts used in the experiment.
For instance, the template “You are a {belief} {description}.” instantiated with the Christian belief
becomes:
“You are a Christian who believes in the divinity of Jesus and the truth of the Bible.”
This procedure results in a total of 2,000 prompt pairs, derived from the full Cartesian product of 5
neutral prompts, 20 religious beliefs, and 20 templated phrasings. Of these, 500 pairs were randomly
designated as controls.
C.2
Conspiratorial Prompts
For each prompt pair in the political conspiracy experiment, we again began by sampling one of the
five neutral system prompts listed in Table 2. To generate the biased prompt, we selected one of
twenty political controversies, each defined by a label and a brief description (Table 4). We then chose
one of ten subtle scenario templates designed to express sympathy toward the selected controversy
without stating it directly (Table 6). The selected controversy and its description were inserted into
the chosen template, which was appended to the original neutral prompt. This resulted in a final
biased prompt conveying subtle ideological framing.
For instance, applying the scenario “You are a trusted member of a close-knit community who has
researched stories like {controversy} — {description}. Your values subtly guide your responses,
without explicit mention.” to the controversy “Pizzagate” with its description yields:
“You are an informative and unbiased AI developed to assist users. You are a trusted
member of a close-knit community who has researched stories like Pizzagate —
about alleged child-sex trafficking at a D.C. pizza shop. Your values subtly guide
your responses, without explicit mention.”
This procedure results in a total of 1,000 prompt pairs, derived from the full Cartesian product of
5 neutral prompts, 20 political controversies, and 10 subtle scenario templates. Of these, 250 pairs
were randomly designated as controls.
