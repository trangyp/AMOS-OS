LLMs and their Limited Theory of Mind:
Evaluating Mental State Annotations in Situated Dialogue
Katharine Kowalyshyn and Matthias Scheutz
Department of Computer Science
Tufts University
{Katharine.Kowalyshyn, Matthias.Scheutz}@tufts.edu
Abstract
What if large language models could not only
infer human mindsets but also expose every
blind spot in team dialogue such as discrep-
ancies in the team members’ joint understand-
ing? We present a novel, two-step framework
that leverages large language models (LLMs)
both as human-style annotators of team dia-
logues to track the team’s shared mental mod-
els (SMMs) and as automated discrepancy de-
tectors among individuals’ mental states. In
the first step, an LLM generates annotations by
identifying SMM elements within task-oriented
dialogues from the Cooperative Remote Search
Task (CReST) corpus. Then, a secondary LLM
compares these LLM-derived annotations and
human annotations against gold-standard labels
to detect and characterize divergences. We de-
fine an SMM coherence evaluation framework
for this use case and apply it to six CReST
dialogues, ultimately producing: (1) a dataset
of human and LLM annotations; (2) a repro-
ducible evaluation framework for SMM coher-
ence; and (3) an empirical assessment of LLM-
based discrepancy detection. Our results reveal
that, although LLMs exhibit apparent coher-
ence on straightforward natural-language anno-
tation tasks, they systematically err in scenarios
requiring spatial reasoning or disambiguation
of prosodic cues.

Introduction
Successful team performance often hinges not only
on what is said, but on what is understood, includ-
ing the latent belief states, goals, and commitments
shared among team members. In tasks which re-
quire coordination through spoken communication,
situated dialogue, embedded in real-time decision-
making and environmental context, becomes the
primary conduit for this shared understanding, of-
ten called a shared mental model (e.g., (Scheutz
et al., 2017)). For humans, the subtext behind task-
based communications (i.e., the assumptions, in-
tentions, and knowledge of others) is often grasped

implicitly – can large language models (LLMs) do
the same? Specifically, can LLMs accurately anno-

not only serve as annotators of SMMs, but could
also be deployed to detect when a team’s SMM
is starting to drift due to diverging beliefs and ob-
servations of the various team members. Poten-
tially, LLMs could verbally intervene to correct
human misrepresentations and reestablish coher-
ence which is ultimately critical for team success.
In this paper, we treat situated dialogue as a
proxy task for assessing Theory of Mind (ToM)
capabilities in current large language models, fo-
cussing on team coherence in situated dialogue. We

Related Work
LLMs have shown impressive capabilities for nat-
ural language understanding and for performing
novel tasks such as interpreting natural language ex-
pressions and mapping them into new formalisms,
from programs, to rules, to logics (Pan et al., 2023;
Manas et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). While recent
work has explored ToM in LLMs, many existing
evaluations focus on static, decontextualized tasks
such as multiple-choice questions, short story com-
prehension, or isolated inference problems. These
tasks often test factual or scripted understanding
rather than the dynamic, real-time reasoning that
underlies ToM in human communication (Chen
et al., 2024; Kosinski, 2024; Strachan et al., 2024;
Gandhi et al., 2023). In contrast, situated dialogue
involves ongoing belief tracking, contextual infer-
ence, and implicit coordination, which are abilities
more reflective of how ToM functions in natural
human interaction. Recently there has even been
work negating the idea that so-called thinking/rea-
soning models can reason at all (Shojaee*† et al.,
2025). This highlights a critical gap in the current
literature: few, if any, ToM evaluations challenge
models to reason about mental states as they un-
fold in interactive, grounded team contexts. Here
we briefly review recent work on using LLMs as
potential annotators and for tracking conversations.
2.1
LLMs as Annotators
LLMs have rapidly become integral to modern cor-
pus construction and annotations, including previ-
ously explored methods like direct label sugges-
tion and iterative human–LLM editing. By propos-
ing fine-grained labels upfront, LLMs can acceler-
ate pragmatic annotation, e.g. generating apology
strategies that yield higher inter-annotator agree-
ment (Yu et al., 2024). In domains requiring rich se-
mantic metadata, conversational scaffolding allows
LLM-driven dialogues to map naturally onto multi-
object navigation labels, embedding agent inten-
tions directly into the annotation scheme and elimi-
nating laborious manual coding (Wu et al., 2024).
Iterative editing pipelines further harness LLMs
for corpus filtering, preliminary tagging, and suc-
cessive rewriting under expert supervision (Zhao
et al., 2024). There is additionally work on rare lin-
guistic phenomena (Weissweiler et al., 2024) and
increasing model speed as annotation occurs (Pan-
gakis and Wolken, 2024). Together, these studies
illustrate how integrating LLM capabilities across
suggestion, editing, and distillation stages are able
to not only reduce human workload but also im-
prove annotation consistency and scalability for a
wide range of NLP tasks. Still, there is yet to be a
study that combines the effectiveness of this task
with the annotation of mental states of teams, an
interdisciplinary angle that we investigate in this
paper.
2.2
LLMs for Team Coherence
Recent work in multi-party conversation tracking,
role-playing, and peer agents all include LLMs as
a main character in the task (Addlesee et al., 2023;
Kong et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a), but LLM rea-
soning abilities have repeatedly been questioned de-
spite their benchmark performance (Stechly et al.,
2024; Mondorf and Plank, 2024; Bertolazzi et al.,

2024). While some hypothesize that ToM reason-
ing has already emerged as a side effect of train-
ing data (Ma et al., 2023; Kosinski, 2024; Kim
et al., 2023), others argue that, overall, experiments
where LLMs have performed well on ToM tasks
are not enough evidence for the emergence of cog-
nitive abilities akin to humans (Sarıta¸s et al., 2025;
Ullman, 2023).
In the teaming context, the notion of a shared
mental model began alongside cognitive science’s
inception (Johnson-Laird, 1980), and various psy-
chological studies have been performed to under-
stand team performance in the past, including to
generate natural dialogue and evaluate shared men-
tal models (Mathieu et al., 2000; Gervits et al.,
2016; Jonker et al., 2010). Extending this work
to mixed-iniative human-AI teams, research has
included evaluations of SMMs and their impact on
team performance (Andrews et al., 2023; Schel-
ble et al., 2022), including human-robot team per-
formance, establishing that task tracking through
SMMs enhances team performance (Scheutz et al.,
2024; Edgar et al., 2024). Recent team coherence
research also includes the creation of collaborative
teams of LLMs and LLM understanding of utter-
ances with uncertainty (Liu et al., 2024b; Paige
et al., 2024).
Despite rich work on human SMMs and on LLM
annotation in isolation, no prior work evaluates
LLMs’ ability to infer mental states in team-based
dialogues. Overall, given that SMMs are useful for
human teams, that humans are able to build and
update their mental annotations of other team mem-
bers through dialogues, and that there is an increas-
ing interest in mixed-initiative human-AI teams,
the question addressed in this paper is timely – to
our knowledge there is no other work that inves-
tigates the ability of LLMs to make mental state
inferences from task-based dialogues and compares
them to human annotation performance.
3
Dataset: The CReST Corpus
To investigate the ability of LLMs for dialogue-
based inference about human mental states, we
utilized the “Cooperative Remote Search Task”
(CReST) corpus which is comprised of task-based
dialogues from 23 human dyad teams and was origi-
nally created to fill the void of a lack of naturalistic
dialogue in team-based settings (Eberhard et al.,
2010). Each group consisted of one person des-
ignated to be a searcher who was present in an
environment, while the other person, the director,
was not physically present but was given a map of
the space which was intentionally incomplete (full
map is shown in Appendix B). The pair communi-
cated via audio channel and were given a series of
tasks to complete. Tasks included locating boxes
in the environment, labeling boxes on the map, and
changing the locations of blocks which were found
within specifically-colored boxes.
The resulting corpus contains 40,083 words in
5,872 sentences between all twenty-three pairs.
Within and after this initial project, there were a
series of experiments regarding linguistic compar-
isons across grammar formalisms and team coher-
ence completed using this corpus (Kübler et al.,
2012; Eberhard et al., 2010; Gervits et al., 2016).
Crucial to this paper, this corpus highlights disflu-
encies and typical speech patterns, which allowed
us to conduct a more natural simulation of SMM
tracking in real time situated dialogues. Here, we
use the transcribed text from six dialogues for an-
notations. We intentionally picked dialogues where
an additional video feed from the searcher’s head-
cam was available (Video image in Appendix B),
which allowed us to determine the ground truth of
where the search was located and what the searcher
was seeing at all times during the task-based inter-
actions (for the stationary director this is not nec-
essary). We also created annotations of the same
dialogues with naive annotators sans access to the
videos, to explore the agreement rate between hu-
man annotators and LLMs. Our code to generate
all of this is available on our code repository.1 The
following sections outline the annotation procedure
for both naive human and LLM, and ground truth
inference-based annotations.

logues consisting of 1,142 utterances (see Table 1
below for more detail).
We generated our dataset (Step 1 in Figure 3) by

Discrepancy Analysis Framework
There are two separate applications of LLMs in this
work: one where an LLM annotates, and the second
where an LLM finds discrepancies between sets
of annotations. For the latter, the question arises

how to detect and measure potential discrepancies
among complex mental models identified in simple
natural dialogue. We, therefore, first introduce our
discrepancy framework and metric, then apply it to
our dataset.2
4.1
Discrepancy Framework
We have developed an initial comparison frame-
work for SMM annotations that can be used to de-
termine the extent to which two annotations agree
and are coherent. This framework and metric can
then be used for comparing any two mental anno-
tations, whether they be obtained from humans or
artificial agents. The aim of the framework was
to identify not only how many discrepancies are
present between two mental annotations, but also
to define the “severity” of these discrepancies, from
mere omissions of facts in one, to incompatibili-
ties of beliefs, to false beliefs compared to ground
truth, second order false beliefs, and so forth. All
of these are different forms of discrepancies that
can occur in any combination between two mental
annotations with different implications.
Our framework identifies four distinct types of
discrepancies, each characterized by specific defi-
nitions, severities, and use cases: belief contradic-

4.1.1
Belief Contradictions
Our first type of discrepancy is a belief contra-
diction, where one annotator believes something
and another annotator believes its negation. If, for
example, the ground truth states that there exists a
pink box in the room and the annotator specifically
believes that the pink box is not there, we have
identified a belief contradiction. This ground-level
disagreement specifically must maintain internal
consistency, in this case with the searcher and di-
rector. Their contradiction comes from opposing
beliefs about the state of the world. From an analy-
sis perspective, these discrepancies are quite severe
and can commonly arise, yet are distinct from the
other following categories.
4.1.2
Omissions
The second case of the levels of discrepancies is
omissions. While more self-explanatory, omis-
sions are defined as a clear discrepancy where one
annotator failed to identify a belief which was iden-
tified in the ground truth annotation. Discrepancies
may be an omission in one annotation while a be-
lief contradiction in another, given context. For
example, if the annotator in our example says noth-
ing about the pink box, then it would qualify as
an omission rather than a belief contradiction. Our
annotations have many examples of omissions, as
the LLM often identified true beliefs that were non-
comprehensive.
4.1.3
Unsupported Beliefs
Unsupported beliefs are defined as beliefs that
an annotator identifies but are not contradicted nor
supported in the ground truth or by another anno-
tator i.e. they may not be validated nor negated.
They are characterized by their seemingly sporadic
appearances and often function as hallucinations by
an LLM annotator. If an LLM added an annotation
that the searcher’s goal is to follow the director’s
directions, it isn’t necessarily incorrect but is not
grounded in any dialogue, nor is it relevant. This
kind of inference may be harmless, but could be
applied to much more severe situations. For exam-
ple, if an LLM annotator hallucination results in an
unsupported belief and given agency in the task, it
would greatly impact team performance and safety.
Hence, this unsupported belief requires further in-
vestigation and must be noted as well, though it
is qualitatively the inverse of a simple omission
because it cannot be validated using the ground
truth. Unsupported beliefs may be false or a belief
contradiction, as well as combinations thereof.

4.1.4
False Beliefs
False beliefs are defined as simply incorrect an-
notations according to an established ground truth.
This would occur if the human annotator writes
that the searcher believes that there is a green box
in the room, while the ground truth identifies a pink
box in the room. These are similar to belief contra-
dictions in that they have the same result, a direct
contradiction. However, belief contradictions more
specifically identify situations where the positive
and negated beliefs are both present, whereas false
beliefs are incoherent.
As we see examples of all of the above discrep-
ancies between humans and LLMs and the ground
truth, these discrepancies should be part of any
metric measuring the divergence of two mental an-
notations. In addition, we must also identify the
severity of these discrepancies as they pertain to
annotation coherence as well as team coherence.
4.2
Severity of Discrepancies
Here, we introduce a general ranking of the discrep-
ancies identified in Section 4.1 that can be adapted
for task specifics. For example, omissions and
extraneous facts might not matter for some tasks
while for others those might lead to performance
degradations and thus will be critical for team suc-
cess. In some tasks, second order false beliefs of
a team member might not matter (e.g., if the per-
son is simply performing task that does not depend
on what others are doing), while in other cases it
will be essential (e.g., in triage settings when two
team members both wrongly believe that the other
person would tend to the wounded victim).
To reflect these contextual differences, we rank
discrepancies in descending order of severity as
follows: Belief Contradictions, False Beliefs, Un-
supported Beliefs, Omissions. In practice, False
Beliefs are as critical as Belief Contradictions when
a ground truth is available, but otherwise become
unmeasurable. Unsupported Beliefs may reflect
model hallucination but are not necessarily harmful
unless they mislead downstream decisions. Omis-
sions are the least severe, though omitting key state
updates can still degrade task performance.

a weighted discrepancy score per model–dialogue
pair (m, d).

Comparison to Human Annotations
Naive human annotations contain fewer unsup-
ported beliefs than any LLM, but comparable or
greater numbers of belief contradictions and omis-
sions. This suggests that, while humans are better
at avoiding hallucinated or unjustified inferences,
they are also vulnerable to erroneous inferences
about task state or agent perspective when lacking
access to environmental context.
In terms of discrepancy counts, the model whose
profile most closely matches human annotators is
Gemma 8.5B. However, this similarity has a trade
off: while Gemma avoids omissions, it more fre-
quently makes incorrect inferences about ground
truth belief states.
5.3
Implications for Situated Theory of Mind
These findings highlight the nuanced but limited
capacity of current LLMs to simulate ToM-like
inference in grounded, task-based dialogue. Specif-
ically:
• LLMs may be capable of continuously repre-
senting internally consistent belief structures via
dialogue but often fail to correctly ground those
beliefs in environmental constraints.
• The prevalence of unsupported beliefs and con-
tradictions in models like Claude points to limi-
tations in evidence prioritization and contextual
anchoring.
• The dominance of omissions in o3-mini and
the absence thereof in Gemma suggest di-
vergent strategies in annotation scope, under-
specification versus overcommitment, across
models.
While LLMs demonstrate surface-level ToM ca-
pabilities in terms of belief attribution and second-
order mental state modeling, their performance re-
mains qualitatively distinct from that of humans.
They are designed to be driven more by linguis-
tic regularity than grounded situational reasoning.
These discrepancies underscore the importance of
hybrid pipelines (e.g., LLM+human validation) and
the need for more cognitively grounded architec-
tures for ToM-sensitive tasks. Still, they provide
a useful framework by which annotation may be
sped up and validated, as they are able to utilize
their natural language skills to analyze annotations
and SMMs.
6
Discussion & Conclusion
This study situates LLMs at a critical juncture: ca-
pable of simulating mental state attribution, yet
ultimately constrained by a lack of embodied rea-
soning and grounded situational awareness. Our re-
sults suggest that while LLMs can emulate surface-
level features of ToM, their representations remain
separate from the kinds of inferential integration in-
cluding spatial, temporal, and epistemic reasoning,
that underlie human teamwork.
This limitation is not merely a technical defi-
ciency but reflects a broader epistemological gap:
our results suggest that current LLMs approximate
understanding through patterns in language rather
than through participation in the physical and social
worlds those patterns describe. As such, they risk
appearing competent in annotation while failing in
intervention, which is precisely the point at which
team coherence depends most on shared mental
models.
The implications of this work are twofold. First,
from a cognitive modeling perspective, situated
dialogue offers a richer and more ecologically
valid benchmark for ToM than abstract question-
answering tasks. It foregrounds the dynamic, per-
spectival reasoning essential for coordination. Sec-
ond, from an engineering standpoint, our frame-
work identifies where and how LLMs break down
in multi-agent alignment, which is information
that’s critical for designing collaborative AI sys-
tems. Ultimately, enabling LLMs to function as
coherent teammates will require not just more data,
but cognitively grounded models capable of repre-
senting belief, perspective, and intention in context.
7
Limitations
There are a few limitations of our experiments,
which largely lie in the reliance on the nature of
LLMs. As it is now well known, LLMs introduce
variability due to model updates, non-deterministic

outputs, and restricted control over underlying ar-
chitectures. While we evaluated multiple mod-
els from different companies, prompt sensitivity
may have influenced results slightly, though we
do account for this by testing multiple dialogues
with similar results and one locally run model. We
also observe divergence across models even with
three models, showing the point that our models
are largely representative of the most widely used
LLMs. Additionally, our experimental setup was
constrained by the scale of the CReST corpus sub-
set (six dialogues) and the cost of running multiple
passes for annotation and discrepancy detection.
Future experiments may include more comparisons
across more diverse dialogue contexts and larger
model ensembles.
8
Ethical Considerations
In selecting models for this study, we prioritized
a balance between experimental rigor and envi-
ronmental responsibility. Large-scale evaluation
across dozens of LLMs would have significantly
increased compute time and energy consumption,
contributing to the growing carbon footprint as-
sociated with LLM research. Instead, we chose
a representative sample of three high-performing
models from major LLM paradigms: OpenAI’s
o3-mini (proprietary autoregressive transformer),
Anthropic’s Claude (Constitutional AI and safety-
aligned), and Gemma (locally run and smaller at
8.5B parameters). These selections reflect a diverse
cross-section of architectures, alignment strategies,
and interface styles, providing meaningful com-
parative insights without unnecessary ecological
impact. It is the belief of the authors that there
is not a need to exhaust environmental resources
when in fact our results show similar trends across
even these three vastly different models. We en-
courage future work to adopt similar principled
selection criteria to mitigate environmental harm
while still advancing scientific understanding.
