ABSTRACT
15
Background and Objective: Cardiotocography (CTG) is a low-cost, non-invasive fetal health
16
assessment technique used globally, especially in underdeveloped countries. However, it is
17
currently mainly used to identify the fetus’s current status (e.g., fetal acidosis or hypoxia), and the
18
potential of CTG in predicting future adverse pregnancy outcomes has not been fully explored.
19
We aim to develop an AI-based model that predicts biological age from CTG time series (named
20
CTGage), then calculate the age gap between CTGage and actual age (named CTGage-gap),
21
and use this gap as a new digital biomarker for future adverse pregnancy outcomes.
22
Methods: The CTGage model is developed using 61,140 records from 11,385 pregnant
23
women, collected at Peking University People’s Hospital (Beijing, China) between January 1,
24
2018, and December 31, 2022. For model training, a structurally designed 1D convolutional
25
neural network (named Net1D) is used, incorporating distribution-aligned augmented regression
26
technology. The CTGage-gap is categorized into five groups: < –21 days (underestimation group),
27
–21 to –7 days, –7 to 7 days (normal group), 7 to 21 days, and > 21 days (overestimation group).
28
We further defined the underestimation group and overestimation group together as the high-risk
29
group. We then compare the incidence of adverse outcomes (including premature infants, low
30
birth weight infants, neonatal asphyxia, fetal distress, fetal malformations and congenital heart
31
disease) and maternal diseases (including gestational diabetes mellitus, anaemia, maternal
32
congenital disease, umbilical cord problems and placental lesions) across these groups.
33
Results: The average absolute error of the CTGage model is 10.91 days. When comparing
34
the overestimation group with the normal group, premature infants incidence is 5.33% vs. 1.42%
35
(p < 0.05) and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) incidence is 31.93% vs. 20.86% (p < 0.05).
36
When comparing the underestimation group with the normal group, low birth weight incidence is
37
0.17% vs. 0.15% (p < 0.05) and anaemia incidence is 37.51% vs. 34.74% (p < 0.05).
38
Conclusion: Artificial intelligence-derived CTGage can predict the future risk of adverse
39
pregnancy outcomes and holds potential as a novel, non-invasive, and easily accessible digital
40
biomarker.

INTRODUCTION
45
Abnormal fetal growth and development increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes1, which
46
can have a serious impact on the safety of both the mother and the fetus2. According to the
47
World Health Organization (WHO)’s report on trends in maternal mortality, the number of maternal
48
deaths worldwide in 2020 was approximately 28.7 thousand cases3. In 2022, the global stillbirth
49
rate was 13.9 per 1,000 births, meaning that for every 72 births, there was one stillbirth4. Fetal
50
growth restriction (FGR) is a common pregnancy complication associated with various adverse
51
pregnancy outcomes. It is a leading cause of stillbirth, neonatal death, and short- and long-term
52
neonatal morbidity worldwide5 6 7. Despite improvements in neonatal care, FGR continues to
53
increase the rate of neonatal mortality and short-term morbidity, and growth-restricted infants
54
are at risk of long-term complications8 9 10. Fetal growth depends not only on genetic factors, but
55
also on various maternal, fetal and placental variables11. Children of women with gestational
56
diabetes mellitus (GDM) are often overweight12 13, particularly at the ages of 5 to 914, which
57
increases the risk of lifestyle diseases15. Therefore, the health of pregnant women affects fetal
58
development, and fetal development determines the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.
59
Early detection of fetal growth abnormalities can effectively reduce the risk of stillbirth16 17 and
60
ensure a positive pregnancy outcome18.
61
Currently, the standard practice for identifying fetal growth abnormalities is to use the Hadlock
62
formula to estimate fetal biometry based on ultrasound. However, due to differences in patient
63
characteristics and ultrasound equipment quality, there is still considerable variation in the ac-
64
curacy of scans19. Furthermore, this method is complex and costly20 21, requiring specialised
65
equipment and technicians, and is difficult to implement in resource-poor areas22 3. For example,
66
in underdeveloped countries, due to the lack of integration in the healthcare system to facilitate
67
information exchange, most pregnant women cannot undergo frequent prenatal check-ups during
68
the early stages of pregnancy, increasing the risk of maternal and fetal mortality in these regions.
69
Additionally, monitoring pregnant women’s vital signs without medical supervision is impractical,
70
and this situation is jeopardising the health of both mothers and fetuses23. Furthermore, this
71
traditional method cannot provide real-time fetal health monitoring and fails to meet pregnant
72
women’s demand for real-time fetal health monitoring.
73
CTG is an external electronic monitoring of fetal heart rate (FHR) and uterine activity performed
74
using an abdominal monitor, providing critical information about fetal accelerations, decelerations,
75
baseline heart rate, and heart rate variability24 25. Since the 1970s, this technology has been
76
clinically used to assess fetal health status26 27. Additionally, CTG signals obtained through
77
monitors offer the advantages of convenience, home monitoring, and low cost. However, most
78
current research focuses on using CTG signals to monitor current fetal health status, with insuffi-
79
cient research on predicting future adverse outcomes. Existing studies have almost exclusively
80
simplified CTG signals into a binary classification task for a specific adverse pregnancy outcome,
81
neglecting the clinically more relevant multi-outcome scenarios28, and lacking a generalized
82
metric for predicting adverse pregnancy outcomes using CTG signals.
83

84
(LMP) to the present, and it serves as a crucial indicator for assessing fetal developmental stages.
85
Meanwhile, biological age (of the fetus), typically estimated from fetal ultrasound examinations,
86
reflects the actual developmental maturity of the fetus. The discrepancy between these two ages,

DISCUSSION
189
This study proposes and validates a new AI-based CTG analysis method, using AI-derived
190
CTGage as a digital biomarker to predict adverse pregnancy outcomes. The results show that the
191
larger the absolute value of CTGage-gap, the higher the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes
192
for the fetus. This suggests that AI-derived CTGage could serve as a potential indicator for early
193
identification of high-risk pregnancies.
194
This study found that fetuses with CTGage-gap exceeding ±21 days had a significantly higher
195
incidence of adverse outcomes. This is consistent with previous studies, which found that fetal
196
growth deviation from the actual CTGage (such as FGR or excessive growth) is itself an important
197
predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes29. In addition, maternal diseases such as GDM,
198
anaemia, and placental lesions can also cause fetal growth to deviate from the normal trajectory
199
by affecting placental perfusion or the metabolic environment, resulting in abnormal CTGage-gap8.
200
This study uses growth curves to confirm that there is a certain pattern in the changes in adverse
201
pregnancy outcomes in CTGage-gap, namely, the greater the absolute value of CTGage-gap, the
202
greater the risk of disease. And this study also confirms significant differences in CTGage-gap
203
distribution under different maternal disease conditions through box plots. Maternal disease
204
may cause CTGage-gap abnormalities by affecting the fetal growth environment, indicating
205
that CTGage-gap abnormalities can be a potential signal of the impact of maternal disease on
206
the fetus. At the same time, this study further reveals significant differences between different
207
CTGage-gap groups in the CTG feature space through t-SNE visualization analysis, indicating
208
that the AI-derived CTGage outputted by the model can be used as an effective indicator to
209
distinguish whether fetal development deviates from the actual CTGage.
210
This study adopts a Net1D architecture combined with distribution-aligned augmented regres-
211
sion to effectively mitigate the bias caused by sample distribution imbalance in CTGage prediction.
212
Compared to traditional regression models, this method demonstrates stronger robustness in tail
213
samples, significantly improving prediction accuracy. Additionally, the sliding window and random
214
perturbation data augmentation strategies significantly improve the model’s generalization ability
215
across different CTGage segments. More importantly, we use gradient visualization methods to
216
identify the critical time periods the model focuses on in CTG sequences, providing new insights
217
for enhancing model interpretability and offering potential solutions to the ’black box problem’ in
218
future clinical deployments.
219
Traditional fetal health assessments rely on ultrasound and biochemical indicators, which are
220
highly accurate but costly and difficult to access, especially in resource-limited areas. In contrast,
221
CTG devices are portable, low-cost, and can be used at home. Combined with AI models, they
222
can achieve continuous, non-invasive, and scalable fetal health monitoring. This study marks
223
the first time CTG data has been expanded from ‘real-time status assessment’ to ‘long-term risk
224
prediction,’ breaking through its traditional application scenarios and holding significant public
225
health implications.
226
Although this study has a large sample size and good model performance, there are still
227
limitations. Firstly, the data source is single, and there may be population bias issues, which
228
will require multi-central and multi-ethnic verification in the future. Secondly, the definition of
229
adverse outcomes is limited. This study only included six types of adverse fetal outcomes and
230
several maternal complications, and did not cover more serious outcomes. Thirdly, although the
231
CTGage-gap threshold of ±21 days is set based on clinical experience, further prospective studies
232
are needed to validate the optimal cutoff point. Finally, while the model’s interpretability has been
233
11

Future research will focus on the following directions. Firstly, validate the model’s generalization

METHODS
242
Dataset and Preprocessing
243
Dataset The dataset of this study covers detailed obstetric information collected from Peking
244
University People’s Hospital between 2018 and 2022, aiming to provide comprehensive data
245
support for the assessment of fetal health and the study of adverse pregnancy outcomes. The
246
dataset contains 61,140 CTG signals from 11,385 subjects, and data are collected at a frequency
247
of 2 Hz, with each data point representing a 1-minute fetal heartbeat value. The dataset is
248
collected without any pre-processing steps, preserving the integrity of the original data. Each
249
record also contains detailed information about the pregnant woman, pregnancy monitoring data,
250
labor and delivery, and newborn health indicators. This study was approved by the Institutional
251
Review Board of Peking University People’s Hospital (approval number 2022PHB169-001). This
252
study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.
253
The basic information in the dataset includes maternal age, gestational age, monitoring time,
254
medical record number, and discharge diagnosis, among others. The maternal age range is 15
255
to 54 years old, with an average age of approximately 33 years. This information provides the
256
foundation for patient identification and timeline references for the study. In addition, the dataset
257
also records information such as fetal malformations, fetal distress, neonatal asphyxia, and low
258
birth weight infants, which can help evaluate the health status of newborns and the medical
259
interventions they may need. Finally, the dataset also covers the mother’s health status, including
260
gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), maternal congenital diseases, and thyroid function indicators.
261
These data help assess the impact of maternal health status on pregnancy outcomes.
262
Outcomes and disease definitions We extract multiple definitions of adverse pregnancy
263
outcomes related to the fetus and maternal diseases from the dataset. Among these, adverse
264
pregnancy outcomes related to the fetus include premature infants, low birth weight infants,
265
neonatal asphyxia, fetal distress, fetal malformations, and neonatal congenital heart disease.
266
Maternal-related conditions include gestational diabetes mellitus, anaemia, maternal congenital
267
disease, placental lesions, and umbilical cord-related problems such as umbilical cord prolapse
268
and umbilical cord entanglement.
269

270

274

275
process, we select several specific adverse pregnancy outcomes and maternal diseases and
276
then segment the dataset based on the presence or absence of these. The normal dataset is
277
divided into training, validation, and test sets in a ratio of 8:1:1. This segmentation ensures that

Model Architecture
289
The CTGage prediction model is based on Net1D30, a one-dimensional convolutional neural
290
network (CNN) constructed on a variant of ResNet31. It is chosen because of its ability to
291
efficiently extract complex features from CTG signals and map them to the CTGage prediction
292
task. Net1D employs sequential convolutional blocks with residual connectivity31, which allows
293
signals in the network to be passed directly bypassing some of the layers, helping to alleviate
294

295
addition, Net1D introduces Squeeze-and-Excitation Modules32, which enhance the network’s
296
focus on important features by adaptively recalibrating the channel weights, and can dynamically
297
adjust the importance of the feature channels, thus improving the feature extraction capability of
298
the model. This architecture ensures robust and efficient processing of CTG signals for CTGage
299
prediction.

Distribution-Aligned Augmented Regression in CTGage Prediction
301
To address the issue of data imbalance, we propose a distribution-aligned augmented regression
302
technique. The core idea of this technique is to address prediction bias caused by the sparse
303
distribution of samples under certain labels and sample distribution shifts by enhancing samples
304
with sparse label distributions and aligning the predicted label distribution with the theoretical
305
label distribution. This approach also further enhances data diversity.
306
We first perform data augmentation on non-diseased samples using sliding window technology,
307
and then further augment samples with sparse label distributions. Each original data point is
308
subjected to random temporal distortion (stretching or compression) and random noise addition,
309
followed by sliding window augmentation.
310
Enhancing samples with sparse label distributions can only moderately alleviate the data
311
imbalance issue, and in reality, prediction values still tend to cluster within the range of labels with
312
larger sample sizes. To address this, we adopt the DistLoss33 and made improvements. The core
313
of this loss function is to enable the network not only to perform point-to-point label regression but
314
also to align the distribution shape of batch predictions with the prior theoretical label distribution.
315

316

317

318

319

320

321

347
The model is implemented using the PyTorch 2.0 framework and trained on a high-performance
348

Statistical Analysis Methods for Clinical Validation
365
In the clinical validation of this study, we use a series of statistical analysis methods to evaluate
366
the relationship between CTGage-gap and adverse pregnancy outcomes. We also analyze the
367
impact of maternal disease on CTGage-gap.
368
15


## Page 16


AI CTGage reflects the current development of the fetus, while CTGage-gap reflects whether
369
the current fetal development is consistent with the actual CTGage. We divide the CTGage-
370
gap into five groups according to ±7 and ±21 days: less than -21 days, -21 to -7 days, -7 to 7
371
days, 7 to 21 days, greater than 21 days. This is because a CTGage-gap within ±7 days is a
372
normal physiological fluctuation range of developmental deviation that does not require special
373
intervention, but if the fetal developmental deviation is greater than three weeks (21 days), this is
374
usually beyond the normal range of variation in clinical practice, and there may be pathological
375
developmental abnormalities that require timely investigation of potential pathological factors.
376
Therefore, the first and fifth groups are more likely to be directly associated with adverse outcomes
377
and are considered high-risk groups, with stronger analytical necessity and clinical significance.
378
The third group represents normal development and serves as the normal reference group. The
379
second and fourth groups fall into the ‘grey zone’ of developmental deviation, which can typically
380
be managed through enhanced monitoring with lower short-term risks, and thus are not the
381
primary focus of analysis. Therefore, we will focus on analyzing the experimental results of the
382
first, third, and fifth groups.
383
We analyze the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in each group to understand the
384
impact of different CTGage-gap on adverse pregnancy outcomes. To further verify the statistical
385
significance of these differences, we use the T-test to compare the impact of CTGage-gap on
386
adverse pregnancy outcomes and use the Kruskal-Wallis H test to compare the differences in
387
adverse pregnancy outcomes between different CTGage-gap groups. In addition, we plot a curve
388
to quantify the relationship between CTGage-gap and adverse pregnancy outcomes. At the same
389
time, we also plot a box plot to compare the effect of maternal disease on CTGage-gap. All
390
statistical test results are expressed as P values, where P values less than 0.05 are considered
391
statistically significant. Together, these analyses provide comprehensive statistical support for
392
evaluating the effectiveness of CTGage-gap as a potential numerical biomarker.
