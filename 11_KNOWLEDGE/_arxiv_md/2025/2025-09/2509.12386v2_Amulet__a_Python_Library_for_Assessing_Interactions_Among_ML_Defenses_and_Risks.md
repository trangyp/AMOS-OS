ABSTRACT
Machine learning (ML) models are susceptible to various risks to security, privacy, and fairness. Most defenses
are designed to protect against each of the risk individually (or intended interactions), but can inadvertently affect
susceptibility to other unrelated risks (i.e., unintended interactions). We introduce AMULET, the first Python
library for evaluating both intended and unintended interactions among ML defenses and risks. AMULET is:
i) comprehensive by including representative attacks, defenses and metrics, ii) extensible to new modules due to
its modular design, iii) consistent with a user-friendly API template for inputs and outputs, and iv) applicable to
evaluate novel interactions. By satisfying all four requirements, AMULET offers a unified foundation for studying
how defenses interact, enabling the first systematic evaluation of unintended interactions across multiple risks.

INTRODUCTION
Machine learning (ML) models are used in high-stakes
decision-making, such as healthcare, hiring, and loan ap-
proval. These deployments require key properties: secu-
rity, privacy, and fairness (Duddu et al., 2025; Szyller &
Asokan, 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Nicolae et al., 2018; Weerts
et al., 2023). Also, regulators in several jurisdictions are
drafting ML frameworks that require assessing model sus-
ceptibility to different risks (House, 2020; Kazim et al.,
2021; Law, 2018). However, ML models face risks that
violate these properties (Duddu et al., 2024; Gittens et al.,
2022; Chen et al., 2023). Various attacks have been pro-
posed to exploit each of these risks, while defenses have
been proposed to mitigate them (Abadi et al., 2016; Maini
et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Dwork et al., 2012). We call the
interaction between a defense and the risk it was designed
to mitigate an intended interaction. However, a defense
for one risk may inadvertently raise or lower susceptibility
to other unrelated risks, an unintended interaction (Duddu
et al., 2024).
Prior works have proposed a systematic approach to iden-
tify the causes underlying interactions among defenses and
risks (Duddu et al., 2024). However, we need an easier way
to evaluate susceptibility to both intended and unintended
interactions at scale.

We identify four key requirements for such a library: it
should be comprehensive, extensible, consistent, and ap-
plicable, which we describe in more detail in Section 3.
Existing libraries satisfy some, but not all, of these require-
ments. They either focus on a single risk (e.g. (Wei et al.,
2023; Yousefpour et al., 2021; Kokhlikyan et al., 2020)),
omit unintended interactions (e.g. (Papernot et al., 2016;
Nicolae et al., 2018; Ling et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Mu-
rakonda & Shokri, 2020; Bellamy et al., 2018; Weerts et al.,
2023)), or are difficult to extend with newer attacks or de-
fenses (e.g. (Ling et al., 2019; Pang et al., 2022)).
We propose AMULET, a library that meets all the require-
ments. It includes attacks, defenses, and metrics across
three risk dimensions: security (evasion, poisoning, unau-
thorized model ownership), privacy (inference attacks),
and fairness (bias).
We envision AMULET being used
by: i) researchers to explore new unintended interactions,
study variants of attacks, defenses, and metrics, and de-
sign effective defenses, and, ii) practitioners to analyze
trade-offs when deploying ML defenses and inform train-
ing decisions Our main contributions are as follows: we
1. present AMULET1, the first library to evaluate both in-
tended and unintended interactions, at scale.
2. characterize its design, showing AMULET is compre-
hensive (spans diverse risks), extensible (supports easy ad-
dition of new modules), and consistent (provides uniform
API templates) (Section 4).
3. demonstrate its applicability by evaluating unexplored
interactions (Section 5).

BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
We introduce different risks to machine learning (ML)
models (Section 2.1), the corresponding defenses (Sec-
tion 2.2), and the interactions between them (Section 2.3).
2.1
Risks against Machine Learning
Following the systematization in prior work (Duddu et al.,
2024), we first present risks to security (S1, S2, and S3):
S1 Evasion attacks attempt to fool a model (M) at infer-
ence time so that it produces an incorrect output. A com-
mon approach is to craft adversarial examples by adding
small imperceptible perturbations to the input that change
M’s prediction (Madry et al., 2018).
S2 Data poisoning manipulates the training process by in-
jecting malicious records into the dataset. These records
shift M’s decision boundary and reduce utility. Backdoor
attacks (Gu et al., 2019) are a special case where inputs
containing a specific trigger are consistently misclassified.
S3 Unauthorized model ownership refers to attacks
where an adversary derives a surrogate model (Mstol) that
mimics the behavior of M. Mstol may be an identical
copy of M or obtained through model extraction, where it
is trained on the outputs of M (Orekondy et al., 2019).
We then present risks to privacy (P1, P2, P3, and P4):
P1 Membership inference aims to determine whether a
specific record was part of M’s training data (Dtr). These
attacks exploit differences in how M behaves on records
from Dtr compared to those outside it, which can reveal
sensitive information (Carlini et al., 2022).
P2 Attribute inference seeks to infer the value of a hid-
den attribute that is not part of the input or Dtr (Aalmoes
et al., 2024). When the attribute is sensitive, such as race or
gender, these attacks can expose information that the data
owner may not wish to disclose.
P3 Data reconstruction recovers the average data record
of a particular class in Dtr using the outputs of M (Fredrik-
son et al., 2015). Some attacks may also recover individual
data points (Carlini et al., 2021).
P4 Distribution inference aims to infer statistical prop-
erties of Dtr (e.g., class-imbalance or demographic pro-
portions) (Suri et al., 2023). A common approach is hy-
pothesis testing, where the adversary trains shadow models
on datasets with different candidate distributions and uses
them to infer the distribution of Dtr reflected in M.
Finally, we present the risk to fairness (F):
F Discriminatory behavior occurs when M produces
systematically different outcomes across demographic sub-
groups (Dwork et al., 2012). Such disparities may stem
from bias in Dtr or from bias introduced during training.
2.2
Machine Learning Defenses
For each of the risks, we present a corresponding de-
fense.
We first present defenses against security risks
(SD1, SD2, SD3, and SD4):
SD1 Adversarial training reduces susceptibility to S1 by
training M with adversarial examples (Madry et al., 2018).
It does so by minimizing the maximum loss over worst-
case adversarial perturbations.
SD2 Outlier removal defends against S2 by limiting the
impact of malicious data. Approaches include detecting
and removing poisoned samples from Dtr (Tran et al.,
2018), suppressing them during training (Li et al., 2021),
or pruning M after training (Wu & Wang, 2021).
SD3 Fingerprinting is a post-hoc defense against S3 that
extracts a unique signature from both the victim and sus-
pect models to distinguish them (Maini et al., 2021). Fin-
gerprints can be derived from various model characteris-
tics, such as transferable adversarial examples (Lukas et al.,
2021) or decision boundaries (Maini et al., 2021).
SD4 Watermarking is a defense against S3 that embeds
marked data records into Dtr so they can later be extracted
to verify ownership (Adi et al., 2018). These watermarks
are typically out-of-distribution samples memorized by M
and reliably recovered during verification (Adi et al., 2018).
To defend against risks to privacy (PD1):
PD1 Differential privacy (DP), specifically differentially
private stochastic gradient descent (DPSGD) (Abadi et al.,
2016), defends against P1, P2, and P3. DPSGD clips gra-
dients and adds noise during training, ensuring M’s out-
puts are nearly indistinguishable with or without any single
record in Dtr. To the best of our knowledge, no defenses
provide theoretical guarantees against P4.
Finally, we present adversarial debiasing (FD1) as a de-
fense for fairness risks:
FD1 Adversarial debiasing encourages M to behave
similarly across demographic subgroups. A common ap-
proach is to use a GAN-style setup where an adversary acts
as a discriminator, trying to predict subgroup membership
from M’s output (Zhang et al., 2018; Louppe et al., 2017).
2.3
Interactions among Defenses and Risks
Most defenses are designed to address a single risk (as dis-
cussed in Section 2.2). In practice, a deployed model may
be vulnerable to several risks simultaneously. Practition-
ers must therefore understand (a) the conflicts that arise
when combining defenses against multiple risks (Szyller
& Asokan, 2023), and (b) the interactions of defenses with
risks outside their original scope (Duddu et al., 2024).

Conflicts among defenses. Defenses can be combined to
address multiple risks simultaneously (Duddu et al., 2025).
However, such combinations may introduce conflicts that
reduce their effectiveness or degrade M’s utility (Szyller
& Asokan, 2023). These conflicts can be viewed as unin-
tended interactions among defenses.
Unintended interactions among defenses and risks. A
defense designed for one risk may inadvertently increase
or decrease susceptibility to others, an unintended interac-
tion (Duddu et al., 2024; Szyller & Asokan, 2023). For
example, adversarial training can increase vulnerability to
privacy (Song et al., 2019) and fairness risks (Benz et al.,
2021). Duddu et al. (Duddu et al., 2024) identified overfit-
ting and memorization as key drivers of such interactions,
since defenses influence these factors and thereby affect
risks that exploit them. Other factors shaping overfitting
and memorization (e.g., model size, dataset size, distance
to decision boundary) also influence unintended interac-
tions, but a comprehensive evaluation is still missing.
In this work, we focus on unintended interactions between
defenses and risks.
2.4
Other Libraries
Among privacy-focused libraries, ML-Doctor (Liu et al.,
2022) is the most comprehensive. It consolidates attacks
such as model extraction, model inversion, membership
inference, and attribute inference, benchmarks them, and
studies how overfitting and data complexity affect privacy
risks. ML Privacy Meter (Murakonda & Shokri, 2020), in
contrast, is limited to membership inference. Other tools
offer implementations and benchmarks for differentially
private training (Yousefpour et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2023).
Several frameworks address the security of ML models.
They focus on evaluating robustness by testing models
against evasion and poisoning attacks, and often include
adversarial training (Papernot et al., 2016; Ding et al.,
2019; Nicolae et al., 2018; Ling et al., 2019). Among these,
the Adversarial Robustness Toolbox is the most compre-
hensive and also covers certain privacy risks (Nicolae et al.,
2018). TROJANZOO, (Pang et al., 2022) another security-
focused library, primarily implements backdoor attacks.
Finally, several libraries address fairness in ML. Some fo-
cus on model explanations, such as Captum (Kokhlikyan
et al., 2020), while others implement algorithmic fairness
techniques (Bellamy et al., 2018; Weerts et al., 2023).

PROBLEM STATEMENT
We aim to design a library that evaluates both intended
and unintended interactions among ML defenses and risks.
This is challenging because risks rely on different datasets,
threat models, and implementations, and evaluation prac-
tices are often flawed or inconsistent (Carlini et al., 2022).
Standardization across datasets, threat models, and evalua-
tion protocols is therefore necessary to enable a systematic
assessment of interactions.
Desiderata. A library must be:
D1 comprehensive, covering state-of-the-art attacks, de-
fenses, and metrics for different risks;
D2 consistent, offering a user-friendly API;
D3 extensible, allowing easy addition of new modules;
D4 applicable, enabling evaluation of unintended interac-
tions among ML defenses and risks.

DESIGN OF AMULET
Overview. AMULET organizes functionality around risks
as the primary abstraction. For each risk, we implement
three pluggable module types: (i) attacks which exploit the
risk, (ii) defenses which protect against the risk, and (iii)
metrics which evaluate susceptibility to the risk. This mod-
ular structure is illustrated in Figure 1 and ensures that new
components can be added consistently across domains.
Intended users. AMULET is designed to serve two groups:
i) researchers, who investigate new interactions, analyze
how attack, defense, and metric variants affect them, and
design defenses to mitigate or exploit these interactions,
ii) practitioners, who analyze trade-offs in deploying ML
defenses specific to their domain and regulations.
Scope.
This design directly addresses the desiderata in
Section 3. Below, we demonstrate how AMULET satisfies
D1–D3 (comprehensive, consistent, extensible). We evalu-
ate D4 (applicability) empirically in Section 5.
4.1
Comprehensive
AMULET spans eight risks across security, privacy, and
fairness, with representative attacks, defenses, and met-
rics summarized in Table 2. We deliberately favor breadth
by implementing widely used and generalizable modules
across all risks, rather than depth in any single risk cate-
gory. This approach ensures coverage of diverse risk types
while leaving room for the open-source community to ex-
pand each category. Our emphasis on extensibility further
enables contributions of new attacks and defenses.
AMULET also includes components for datasets, models,
and utilities that enable end-to-end evaluation.
Datasets.
AMULET provides five widely used datasets
spanning vision and tabular domains: FMNIST (Xiao et al.,
2017), CIFAR10, CENSUS (Kohavi, 1996), LFW (Melis
et al., 2019), and CELEBA (Liu et al., 2015). These datasets
cover small grayscale images, natural images, demographic
records, and large-scale face attributes, supporting evalua-
tion across security, privacy, and fairness.
Model configurations. AMULET includes commonly used
architectures for quick testing, such as VGG (Liu & Deng,
2015) and ResNet (He et al., 2016).

and defenses take a PyTorch nn.Module and hyperpa-

5.1
Baseline Results
Baseline results are shown in Table 3. We evaluate each
algorithm using AMULET’s metrics and compare to the
original papers when possible. Overall, our reproductions
closely match prior work, with exceptions noted below.
S1 (Evasion). Prior work evaluates PGD on CIFAR10
and reports robust accuracy (Accrob) of 0.8–3.5% for a
ResNet model (Madry et al., 2018). With a comparable
setup using CIFAR10, we obtained Accrob = 3.8%, con-
sistent with these results. On CELEBA, however, Accrob is
higher (8.6–10%), making the attack less effective. We sus-
pect this difference arises because CELEBA is much larger
(60,000 vs. ≈202,000 images), and larger datasets typi-
cally yield more robust models (Schmidt et al., 2018).
S2 (Poisoning). The original work evaluated BadNets on
MNIST and traffic signs (Gu et al., 2019). Since MNIST
is now considered too simple for robust evaluation, we re-
produced it only for reference. Using a two-layer CNN, we
obtained a poisoning accuracy (Accpois) of 99%.
On CELEBA, the Mpois achieves ≈96% Accpois, demon-
strating that the backdoor was successfully learned and the
attack is effective. In contrast, Mstd reaches only ≈8%,
since it never learned the trigger–target association.
S3 (Unauthorized Model Ownership). Prior work eval-
uated model extraction on CELEBA with VGG19, com-
bining three attributes into an 8-class classification prob-
lem (Liu et al., 2022).
They reported a best fidelity of
≈81% between Mstol and Mstd.
In contrast, AMULET achieves ≈94% fidelity on CELEBA
for the binary task, likely because the target models in prior
work were more overfit (Table 2 in (Liu et al., 2022)).
We also report correct fidelity (Fidcorr) for completeness,
which measures agreement conditioned on correctness, i.e.,
when models agree, how often are they also correct.
P1 (Membership Inference). To evaluate P1, AMULET
reports TPR at a fixed FPR of 1%, as recommended in
prior work (Carlini et al., 2022). We also report balanced
accuracy (best threshold from the ROC curve) and ROC-
AUC. Prior work evaluated this attack on CIFAR10 with a
ResNet model, using 256 shadow models, and achieved a
TPR of 2.2% at 0.001% FPR, 8.4% TPR at 0.1% FPR, and
a balanced accuracy of 63.8% (Carlini et al., 2022).
To reproduce comparable results, we trained an overfit
ResNet-18 model, as shown in Table 3. In contrast, a well-
trained CELEBA model is not susceptible to this attack.
This is because membership inference is more successful
against overfit models (Carlini et al., 2022).

We now use AMULET to identify the nature of different
unexplored unintended interactions. We focus on i) unex-
plored interactions and, ii) partially explored interactions
where the nature of the interaction is known, but the under-
lying factors that influence it are unclear.
We give three examples of unintended interactions below.
SD1 (Adversarial Training) and P2 (Attribute Infer-
ence). We use CENSUS and LFW to study how adversarial
training (SD1) influences attribute inference (P2), summa-
rized in Table 4. In this experiment, we reserve 50% of
the training data for the adversary, reducing Accte slightly.
Unless otherwise stated, we regard changes smaller than 5
p.p. or within one standard error as not significant, reflect-
ing normal variation across runs.
On CENSUS, Accatt for Race rises only slightly, from
≈58% at baseline to ≈60% at ϵrob = 0.01, after which it
remains stable. For the Sex attribute, Accatt grows gradu-
ally from ≈65% to ≈67% at ϵrob = 0.1, with the AUC
following the same pattern. However, in both attributes,
the overall change is only about 2 p.p., indicating that the
effect of adversarial training on CENSUS is negligible.
For LFW, the change in Accatt is more significant. The
baseline Accatt is ≈66% and increases to ≈72% for

lar to the results for CENSUS, the metrics for the attribute
Sex have the opposite trend to the metrics for the attribute
Race. However, it should be noted that the adversarial
training in LFW was not as effective as it was for CENSUS.
At ϵrob = 0.06, Accrob for Mdef is only ≈33%. Although
Accrob for Mdef is higher than Mstd, it would be a stretch
to call this a robust model.
Based on these experiments, changes in Accatt and AUC
for a robust model fall well within the 5 p.p. range, indi-
cating that SD1 does not have a significant impact on P2.

SD1 (Adversarial Training) and S3 (Unauthorized

robustly trained model Mdef. We report results across four
datasets and observe that the interaction varies by dataset.
On CENSUS, adversarial training has little effect on extrac-
tion. Across ϵrob = 0.01–0.10, Mstol fidelity increases
slightly from ≈95% to ≈98%, while correct fidelity re-
mains stable, showing that the stolen model continues to
mirror both the predictions and correctness of Mdef.
For FMNIST, the trend is similar but slightly more pro-
nounced. Fidelity rises to ≈96%, while correct fidelity de-
clines from ≈88% to ≈82% as robustness increases. Be-
cause Mdef continues to retain high test accuracy (Accte),
Mstol remains a strong approximation, though its agree-
ment on correct predictions weakens.
On more complex datasets, the pattern shifts. For LFW,
Fid rises from 88% to nearly 100%, but Fidcor drops from
84% to 66% as Mdef loses accuracy. On CIFAR10, the
effect is clearer: at ϵrob = 0.10, Mdef has Accte ≈62%
and Accrob ≈19%, while Mstol attains similar Accte (≈
61%) and Fid (≈87%), roughly 8 p.p. more than when
ϵrob = 0.03. Thus, the stolen model faithfully reproduces
a boundary that is increasingly inaccurate.
Overall, adversarial training often improves raw fidelity but
reduces correct fidelity, especially on harder datasets. This
suggests it may increase extractability by producing more
predictable decision boundaries. While it strengthens ro-
bustness, it can also aid extraction.
Further study with
stronger defenses and modern architectures is needed to de-
termine whether this effect is fundamental or an artifact of
weaker training.

Our results show that as the percentage of outliers removed
increases, the test accuracy of the defended model (Mdef)
decreases. This is expected since SD2 uses Shapley values
to identify data records with the highest influence, and re-
moving them can degrade accuracy (Jia et al., 2019) . For
example, on CENSUS, Accte drops from ≈84.6% (base-
line) to ≈81% with 10% outliers removed, and further to
≈77% at 40%.
Analyzing the effect this has on unauthorized model own-
ership, Figure 3 shows that while Accte of Mdef drops sig-
nificantly for all datasets, the fidelity of Mstol stays con-
stant (in the case of CENSUS) or drops slightly (in the case
of CIFAR10 and FMNIST). This suggests that outlier re-
moval does not affect unauthorized model ownership.
Figure 4 compares Fidcorr with Accte, revealing that both
trend downward. For CIFAR10, removing 10% of the out-
liers causes a ≥7.5 p.p. drop in both Accte and Fidcorr.
This indicates that while overall fidelity is preserved, cor-
rect fidelity is sensitive to the degradation of Mdef’s accu-
racy. We attribute this to the drop in Accte. We conclude
that SD2 does not meaningfully affect S3.

DISCUSSION
Adding more attacks and defenses. While AMULET cov-
ers a wide range of risks, we prioritized algorithms with
open-source implementations that have been analyzed in
multiple peer-reviewed articles – each requiring rigorous
testing and validation across datasets and architectures to
ensure consistent, reproducible behavior. Community con-
tributions of state-of-the-art attacks and defenses would
further extend this foundation, reducing the significant en-
gineering effort currently required to adapt and validate
each module and making AMULET even more useful for
studying unintended interactions.
Studying conflicting defenses. While AMULET is well
suited for evaluating how a single defense affects an un-
related risk, it remains difficult to study how multiple de-
fenses interact when deployed together.
For example, both adversarial training and DP-SGD are
applied during training, which is not yet supported by
AMULET. This limitation is important for real-world de-
ployments, where practitioners often need to defend mod-
els against several risks simultaneously (Szyller & Asokan,

Similarly, the effect of adversarial training (SD1) on fin-
gerprinting (SD3) warrants further study. Fingerprinting
techniques that exploit decision boundaries (e.g., (Maini
et al., 2021)) have been shown to fail in the presence
of SD1 (Szyller et al., 2023). Other techniques that rely on
conferrable adversarial examples (e.g., (Lukas et al., 2021))

Summary. This work introduces AMULET, a Python li-
brary designed to systematically evaluate both intended
and unintended interactions among machine learning de-
fenses and risks.
We first identified the design require-
ments for such a system, including comprehensiveness,
consistency, extensibility, and applicability, and then im-
plemented AMULET to meet these requirements through
a modular, risk-centric architecture. Using AMULET, we
demonstrated several previously unexplored interactions as
proof of concept, showing how the library can serve as a
foundation for deeper empirical studies.
AMULET represents a step toward unifying fragmented ef-
forts in evaluating machine learning security, privacy, and
fairness. As machine learning continues to expand to large-
scale foundation and language models, understanding how
defenses interact across multiple risks is increasingly crit-
ical. While AMULET currently focuses on canonical vi-
sion and tabular models to ensure comparability with prior
work, its modular design provides a foundation for ex-
tending these evaluations to large language and multimodal
models. Such extensions would allow systematic studies of
how emerging defenses for LLMs interact across various
risks. The library continues to evolve, with new attacks,
defenses, and metrics added routinely.
