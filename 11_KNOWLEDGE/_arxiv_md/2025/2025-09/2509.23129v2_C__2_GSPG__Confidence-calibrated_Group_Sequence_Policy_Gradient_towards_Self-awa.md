Abstract
Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods, exemplified by Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
and its variants, play a central role in developing reasoning models. However, these methods often
suffer from a critical overconfidence issue, which prevents them from achieving self-aware reasoning
models. In this study, we propose a simple yet effective confidence-calibration group sequence policy
gradient method, called C2GSPG, which simultaneously enhances reasoning performance while
suppressing overconfidence. In principle, we propose a Group Sequence Policy Gradient (GSPG)
framework for learning reasoning models, which eliminates the token-level bias commonly appearing
in GRPO and its variants. In this framework, we define the model confidence for each reasoning
problem using the normalized sequence-level probability, and then apply a cross-entropy regularizer
to calibrate the model confidence to the sequence’s reward. We demonstrate that the confidence
calibration regularizer and GSPG are collaborative for binary rewards, as their objectives always share
the same gradient direction. For non-binary rewards, we apply nonlinear reward normalization and
adaptive regularizer clipping, mitigating the potential conflict between the two objectives. Applying
C2GSPG to post-train large language models in logical and mathematical reasoning tasks, we show
its superiority over state-of-the-art methods in both reasoning accuracy and confidence calibration.
The code of C2GSPG is available at https://github.com/HaotianLiu123/CCGSPG.

Introduction
Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a pivotal post-training technique for enhancing the advanced
reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). Unlike traditional Supervised Fine-Tuning
(SFT), RL-based methods utilize verifiable rewards to enable models to explore and refine their reasoning
pathways autonomously. Among prominent RL algorithms, the recent Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) has garnered significant attention. Unlike PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) and
DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), GRPO eliminates the need for a value model, thereby simplifying model design
and improving computational efficiency. Along this direction, many variants of GRPO have been proposed,
e.g., DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), GPG (Chu et al., 2025), and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025), and demonstrated
superior performance in logical and mathematical reasoning tasks.
While GRPO and its variants can improve the reasoning ability of LLMs, they often induce an overconfidence
issue — the post-trained models cannot be aware of their mistakes (Bereket and Leskovec, 2025; Chhikara,
2025). In the paradigm of RL, this issue is reflected by the mismatch between the probabilities of generated
sequences and the rewards associated with the sequences, i.e., the high-probability sequences may lead to
low rewards, as shown in Figure 1(a). To mitigate the overconfidence issue, some methods first modulate
reward functions based on empirical or learnable model confidences, and then reduce the significance of high-

model confidence and the corresponding reward, resulting in the proposed C2GSPG method. We theoretically
demonstrate a collaborative relationship between policy optimization and our regularizer in binary-reward
scenarios — these two objectives share the same gradient direction in all situations. This insight motivates us
to propose nonlinear reward normalization and adaptive regularizer clipping to mitigate the potential conflict
between the two objectives in the more complex, non-binary reward cases.
We compare C2GSPG with GRPO and its variants on logical and mathematical reasoning tasks. The results
demonstrate that our method effectively mitigates the model overconfidence problem while also improving
reasoning ability. Moreover, Figure 1(a) validates that, unlike baselines which often exhibit poorly separated
distributions, C2GSPG successfully cultivates a bimodal confidence profile: high confidence is predominantly
assigned to correct answers, while incorrect answers are given low confidence. Furthermore, Figure 1(b)
illustrates that C2GSPG consistently achieves a higher accuracy on the validation set while simultaneously
driving down the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) more effectively than competing approaches.

2.2
The Overconfidence Issue and Current Solutions
As aforementioned, GRPO and its variants often result in overconfident models (Leng et al., 2025; Bereket
and Leskovec, 2025), which necessitates confidence calibration to ensure their reliability. The practical
risks posed by such overconfidence have been well-studied (Melotti et al., 2022; Omar et al., 2024; Griot
et al., 2025). In risk-sensitive applications such as disease diagnosis and auto-driving, confidence scores are
key signals for introducing human auditing or fallback mechanisms. Overconfident models are unreliable
(and inapplicable) in such applications because their mistakes are hard to predict. An LLM’s confidence
is essentially its own prediction of how likely its answer is to be right, while calibration is the test of how
reliable the prediction is — it checks whether the model’s stated confidence levels match its true success
rate (Yoon et al., 2025b). Several methods have been developed to estimate this confidence. Gupta et al.
(2024) uses the token-level probability of the answer to form a confidence score. Kadavath et al. (2022)
employs other models to predict correctness. Xu et al. (2024) leverages labeled confidence scores datasets
3


## Page 4


to fine-tune models. The methods in (Yoon et al., 2025b; Damani et al., 2025) elicit verbalized confidence,
which prompts the model to articulate its own confidence estimate directly within its output.
Traditional confidence calibration approaches often operate at inference time by prompting the model to
self-assess its understanding (Wen et al., 2024) or by introducing distractors (Chhikara, 2025). However, they
often focus on scenarios of verbalized confidence, with unverified applicability to token-probability-based
confidence. Recent works have sought to integrate calibration directly through regularization. For example,
RLCR (Damani et al., 2025) designs a hybrid reward combining accuracy with a Brier calibration score.
Taming LLMs (Leng et al., 2025) dynamically modifies reward values during PPO training based on recent
performance. Similarly, CoDaPO (Zhou et al., 2025) modulates the advantage function with confidence
and difficulty weights. Despite their contributions, these training-based methods often struggle to achieve
a trade-off between confidence and accuracy, sometimes sacrificing performance or yielding suboptimal
calibration.

Confidence-calibrated Group Sequence Policy Gradient
To mitigate the model overconfidence issue while maintaining high reasoning performance, we propose a
method, called Confidence-Calibration Group Sequence Policy Gradient (C2GSPG), that introduces a novel
objective function to explicitly align a model’s self-assessed confidence with its task performance. Our
method is motivated by a desideratum commonly used in confidence calibration (Grabinski et al., 2022;
Wen et al., 2024; Yoon et al., 2025a): the probability of generated sequence should reflect the model
confidence corresponding to the prompt/question. Therefore, our method defines model confidence directly
from the model’s sequence-level probabilities, rather than using verbalized confidence statements (Bereket
and Leskovec, 2025). This is universally applicable and provides a more direct measure of the model’s
internal certainty (Gupta et al., 2024). Formally, for the i-th sequence oi corresponding to the prompt q, we
compute the model confidence of oi as

Experiments
To validate the effectiveness of C2GSPG, we conduct experiments on logical and mathematical reasoning
tasks. In each task, we compare C2GSPG with state-of-the-art methods, including GRPO (Shao et al., 2024),
GPG (Chu et al., 2025), GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025), and AR-Lopti (Yang et al., 2025b). Representative

4.1
Implementation Details
For the logical reasoning task, we employ the K&K dataset (Xie et al., 2024), stratified by character count (3 to
7) to represent varying difficulty. Following the methodology of (Yang et al., 2025b), we select the Qwen2.5-
3B-Instruct model2 as our backbone and train it on a composite dataset encompassing all difficulty levels. Our
training process is guided by a reward function adapted from Logic-RL (Xie et al., 2025), which combines
structural and correctness signals. The raw discrete reward signal of {−3, −1, −0.5, 3} is transformed
through the proposed sigmoid mapping: boundary scores −3 and 3 are mapped to 0 and 1, while intermediate
values are converted via (10) (with α = 3), yielding a final reward set of {0, 0.047426, 0.182426, 1}. The
hyperparameter β = 0.03, and we pass it through the proposed clipping function I(·).
For the mathematical reasoning task, we utilize the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model3, which we train on the
DeepScaleR-Uniform dataset (Shi et al., 2025), a curated subset of the DeepScaleR benchmark (Luo et al.,
2025). Our training methodology employs a straightforward binary reward mechanism: a reward of +1 is
assigned if the model’s final answer is correct, and 0 otherwise. In this case, we set β ∈{0.01, 0.1, 0.5}. To as-
sess generalization, we evaluate performance across a diverse suite of benchmarks, including GSM8K (Cobbe
et al., 2021), Olympiad (He et al., 2024), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), MATH 500 (Lightman et al.,
2024), AMC2023, and AIME2024.

4.4
Further Analysis
The results in Tables 3 and 5 indicate that our method achieves significant improvements in logical reasoning
but incremental gains in mathematical reasoning. In addition, when changing β, the reasoning accuracy and
confidence calibration metrics (BS and ECE) show a trade-off on the math datasets, whereas improvements
in these metrics are consistent across the K&K dataset, as shown in Table 10 in the Appendix. Note that these
two phenomena are common (Zhang et al., 2025; Lacombe et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025), whose reasons are
the difficulties of the reasoning tasks and the capabilities of the pre-trained LLMs w.r.t. the tasks.
• When the pre-trained LLM is strong while the task is relatively easy (e.g., K&K), the LLM possesses
sufficient latent capacity for the task. Thus, fine-tuning with different methods can yield substantial
performance gains. Notably, since K&K does not appear in the pre-training corpora of mainstream
LLMs, these performance improvements primarily stem from genuine learning rather than data mem-
orization. Consequently, the performance improvement of our method compared to other baselines
further validates its effectiveness.
• When the pre-trained LLM is not strong enough while the task is challenging (e.g., the mathematical
datasets), the work in (Yue et al., 2025) points out that although RL methods can improve sample
10


## Page 11


efficiency, they remain unable to exceed the upper bound of their capabilities, thus resulting in limited
performance gains. In such a situation, an overconfident model is encouraged by RL methods (including
ours) because overconfident guessing may yield higher rewards, whereas an honest response often leads
to failure. To maximize rewards, the model resorts to strategic guessing, leading to improved accuracy
but degraded confidence calibration.
To address the above issue, we will further explore how to improve the calibration of models whose capabilities
are currently insufficient for handling challenging tasks.
5
Conclusion
In this work, we propose C2GSPG, a confidence-calibrated group sequence policy gradient method designed
to suppress the overconfidence issue in reinforcement learning-based reasoning models. Within the group
sequence policy gradient framework, C2GSPG introduces a confidence-modulated advantage and confidence
calibration regularizer, which effectively eliminates token-level bias and enforces consistency between model
confidence and rewards. Experimental results on logical and mathematical reasoning tasks demonstrate that
C2GSPG significantly improves both reasoning accuracy and confidence calibration, outperforming existing
state-of-the-art methods.
Limitations and Future Work. On the mathematical reasoning task, we observe that the gap in confidence
calibration between the training and test sets remains relatively large, which may stem from the distribution
discrepancy between the two datasets. Additionally, the model performance is significantly impacted by the
choice of β. Addressing these issues, we plan to explore methods to enhance out-of-distribution robustness
and calibration, as well as develop more effective regularization strategies to suppress overconfidence in
erroneous generations.

B.3
Reward Formulation for Logical Reasoning
The K&K logic puzzles dataset is based on the classic “Knights and Knaves" scenario. In this setting, each
inhabitant of an island is either a Knight (who always tells the truth) or a Knave (who always lies). The
model’s objective is to deduce the true identity of each character from their statements.
The complexity of this task is reflected in its unique reward structure, which generates a composite score
from the set {−3, −1, −0.5, 3}. This score is calculated based on two distinct criteria:
• Formatting Correctness: The model is required to produce a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rationale
enclosed within <think> tags, followed by the final solution in <answer> tags. Adherence to this
format yields +1 point, while any deviation results in a penalty of -1 point.
• Answer Accuracy: The final answer is graded on its correctness. A completely correct solution receives
17


## Page 18


+2 points. A partially correct answer incurs a penalty of -1.5 points, and a fully incorrect answer is
penalized by -2 points.
The final reward is the sum of the scores from these two criteria. This multi-faceted reward signal is designed
to evaluate both the model’s reasoning process and the accuracy of its conclusions.
