WHEN RISK DEFIES ORDER: ON THE LIMITS OF FRACTIONAL
STOCHASTIC DOMINANCE
CHRISTIAN LAUDAG´E AND FELIX-BENEDIKT LIEBRICH
Abstract. Motivated by recent work on monotone additive statistics and questions regarding op-
timal risk sharing for return-based risk measures, we investigate the existence, structure, and ap-
plications of Meyer risk measures.
Those are monetary risk measures consistent with fractional
stochastic orders suggested by Meyer (1977a,b) as refinement of second-order stochastic dominance
(SSD). These so-called v-SD orders are based on a threshold utility function v. The test utilities
defining the associated order are those at least as risk averse in absolute terms as v. The generality
of v allows to subsume SSD and other examples from the literature. The structure of risk measures
respecting the v-SD order is clarified by two types of representations. The existence of nontrivial
examples is more subtle: for many choices of v outside the exponential (CARA) class, they do not
exist. Additional properties like convexity or positive homogeneity further restrict admissible exam-
ples, even within the CARA class. We present impossibility theorems that demonstrate a deeper link
between the axiomatic structure of monetary risk measures and SSD than previously acknowledged.
The study concludes with two applications: portfolio optimisation under a Meyer risk measure as
objective, and risk assessment of financial time series data.
Keywords: Fractional stochastic order, monetary risk measure, return risk measure, risk aversion,
second-order stochastic dominance.
1. Introduction
How should one decide between two random prospects?
This question is central to decision
theory, risk management, and financial investment. A classical approach is comparing the prospects
in second-order stochastic dominance (SSD) pioneered by Hadar and Russell (1969), Hanoch and
Levy (1969), checking if there is consensual preference for one payoff over the other among all
expected utility (EU) agents who share a probabilistic view of the future; prefer more to less
(nondecreasing utility); and are risk averse (concave utility).
It has been observed, e.g., by Leshno and Levy (2002) that SSD-based decisions can be hijacked by
unrealistically risk-averse EU agents. Even when the preference between two payoffs is intuitively
clear, SSD may not provide a ranking. They illustrate this with the choice between two payoff
lotteries: the first delivers $1 with certainty; the second offers 1 million dollars with 99% chance, and
nothing otherwise. While virtually all decision makers would prefer the second lottery over the first,
this preference is not reflected by an SSD ordering. Another drawback of using SSD comparisons is
that they do not always yield a decision—it is an incomplete order of random prospects.
Two alternatives exist. The first one, suggested by Leshno and Levy (2002) and revisited later by
Tzeng et al. (2013), Tsetlin et al. (2015), M¨uller et al. (2017), and Mao et al. (2021), modifies the set
of relevant utility functions, excludes agents whose risk aversion is deemed extreme, and adds locally
risk-loving EU agents. These contributions have been preceded by Meyer (1977a,b), where the risk
aversion of the decision-making EU collective is controlled directly. This approach is crucial for the
present paper and has been revisited, e.g., by Huang et al. (2020), Liu and Meyer (2025). Several

(1) Why should one care about Meyer risk measures?
(2) Do nontrivial Meyer risk measures exist, and if yes, what do they look like?
(3) Which properties do Meyer risk measures have, and which common risk measure properties
can they have?
(4) How do they behave in real-world applications?

While these base risk measures appear innocuous, they pose problems that lead to research
question (3), which we address in Sections 4.2 and 5. First, base risk measures are only invariably
v-SD-consistent themselves if v is CARA. Conversely, if v displays monotonically increasing or
decreasing absolute risk aversion in the limit, most of them are not v-SD-consistent. Paradoxically,
this means that v-SD-consistent risk measures are most naturally represented in terms of simpler
risk measures that are not v-SD-consistent. Secondly, risk measures are often additionally assumed
to be positively homogeneous (like Expected Shortfall and Value-at-Risk) or to express a positive
attitude to diversification. Apart from the worst-case risk measure, positive homogeneity and v-SD-
consistency often prove to be irreconcilable. Similarly, properties like convexity and star-shapedness
require the risk to grow at maximal speed when leverage is increased. Hence, many convex risk
measures like the ES from above are not v-SD-consistent for nontrivial v. In conclusion, our findings
indicate that the axiomatic framework for monetary risk measures is more profoundly and intricately
linked to SSD than acknowledged before.
Finally, we approach question (4) in Section 6 by means of two applications. The first one is
inspired by the risk minimisation problem with an SSD-consistent risk measure in (Mao and Wang,
2020, Section 5). In our case, the objective evaluates payoffs with an SSD-consistent return risk
measure, obtained as a transformation of a Meyer risk measure consistent with an exponential
threshold utility. We prove existence and uniqueness of the optimal solution, which, in contrast to
the original problem in Mao and Wang (2020), turns out as a product of different model ingredients.
In the second example, we test the performance of Meyer risk measures based on exponential
threshold utilities for real-world time series data. These considerations illustrate, how the decision
making depends on the CARA-risk aversion parameter of the investor.

2.1. Risk measures. A (monetary) risk measure is a functional ρ: X →R satisfying
antitonicity: ρ(X) ≥ρ(Y ) holds for all X, Y ∈X with X ≤Y ; and
cash-additivity: ρ(X + c) = ρ(X) −c holds for all X ∈X and c ∈R.
For a thorough introduction to monetary risk measures, see (F¨ollmer and Schied, 2016, Chapter 4).
While risk measures have been studied on spaces different from X containing unbounded random
variables, the present setting is convenient and sufficient for our purposes.
The quantity ρ(X) is interpreted as the capital that needs to be held in combination with the
financial position X to limit risk to an acceptable level. This can be seen from the representation
ρ(X) = inf{m ∈R | X + m ∈Aρ},
where the acceptance set Aρ := {X ∈X | ρ(X) ≤0} collects all payoffs that are already adequately
capitalised and do not require additional capital injections.
Financial positions X ∈X model
payoffs, i.e., net gains instead of net losses. Antitonicity reflects that an a.s. larger payoff should
not require more risk capital to reach acceptability. Cash-additivity means that an additional sure
payoff reduces the required capital by precisely that amount.
The interpretation of risk measures as capital requirements makes also other properties econom-
ically meaningful. A monetary risk measure ρ is called:
(a) law invariant if payoffs with the same distribution under P are mapped to the same value;
(b) positively homogeneous if ρ(tX) = tρ(X) for all X ∈X and t > 0;
(c) convex if ρ(λX + (1 −λ)Y ) ≤λρ(X) + (1 −λ)ρ(Y ) for all X, Y ∈X and λ ∈[0, 1];
(d) normalised if ρ(0) = 0;
(e) star-shaped if it is normalised and ρ(λX) ≥λρ(X) holds for all X ∈X and λ ≥1.
Positive homogeneity reflects the absence of liquidity risk: the risk associated with a larger
exposure to a given payoff increases proportionally with that exposure. Convexity means that di-
versification does not increase the capital requirement and expresses favour for strategies that simul-
taneously merge and downsize positions. Convexity is a stronger property than star-shapedness,
which promotes downsizing, but does not necessarily encourage merging. This further indicates
that—unlike positive homogeneity—star-shapedness does not rule out the occurrence of liquidity
risk. A comprehensive treatment of star-shaped risk measures is Castagnoli et al. (2022).

3. Why v-SD consistency matters in risk analysis
In this section, we provide context for our analysis of v-SD consistent risk measures. While the
literature on the interaction between law-invariant risk measures and stochastic orders is a key
motivation, the monotone additive statistics of Mu et al. (2024) also naturally lead to Meyer risk
measures, for which exponential utilities play a central role. In Section 3.3, we discuss the benefits
of keeping v flexible a priori. Finally, we note the natural occurrence of Meyer risk measures when
measuring the risk of returns, as in the approach of Bellini et al. (2018).
3.1. Classical results on consistency. The literature contains numerous results about consis-
tency of functionals designed to assess risk or utility with stochastic orders. These are not only
theoretically significant, but also underpin the study of optimal investment decisions that minimise
risk (see Section 6.1 below) and enable finding optimal risk sharing schemes (see Section 3.4).
Crucially, it is often not consistency itself that is presumed, but law invariance of the risk measure
in question. If an agent uses a lower-semicontinuous, convex and law-invariant risk measure as
objective—as in the risk sharing study of Filipovi´c and Svindland (2008)—the conjunction of these
properties implies SSD-consistency. This implication has, e.g., been observed for risk measures with
domain X— the case of interest to the present paper—in (Cerreia-Vioglio et al., 2011, Theorem
5.1), and for domain L1 in (Dana, 2005, Theorem 4.1) and (B¨auerle and M¨uller, 2006, Theorem
4.4). Not least, (Bellini et al., 2021, Theorem 3.6) extend the result to risk measures defined on
general spaces of random variables.4
Given the prominence and applications of these results, several logical questions arise. Which risk
measures are consistent with stochastic orders beyond SSD? What specific properties are required
to achieve this consistency? And how can one construct new types of such consistent risk measures?
In this study, we address all these questions for the broad class of v-SD orders, which includes SSD
as a special case, but extends significantly beyond it and leads to stronger or weaker consistency
concepts, as already illustrated by the implications in (2.6) and (2.7). As a byproduct of this level
of generality, one may hope to uncover unifying principles.
This motivation is also supported by the existing literature. Yamai and Yoshiba (2002) define
risk measures consistent with higher-order stochastic dominance relations. The case of the so-called
3-convex order (3CO) is treated in Bellini and Caperdoni (2007). Their Theorem 14—extending
(H¨urlimann, 2004, Theorem 6.3)—demonstrates that, under a mild condition on the underlying
probability space, only a trivial 3CO-consistent distortion risk measure exists, the expectation
itself. This negative result shares the spirit of some of our main results here. Hence, an additional
question that requires attention and care is if non-trivial Meyer risk measures exist at all. As we
shall see momentarily, the answer to the latter question is affirmative.

7. Conclusion and outlook
We study Meyer risk measures, defined by their consistency with a stochastic order from the v-SD
class, v being a threshold utility function. This generalises SSD-consistent risk measures and allows
to grapple with risk sharing problems for the recently introduced return risk measures. Among
the possible choices for v, the family (ec)c∈R of exponential utilities, displaying constant absolute
risk aversion, is crucial. With this choice, formulae for general risk measures are readily available,
demonstrating the abundance of nontrivial examples.
However, this is not the case for general
threshold utilities v. While v-SD-consistent risk measures can be represented as the lower envelope
of a class of so-called base risk measures, the latter are typically not v-SD consistent if risk aversion
of v is not constant. These observations reveal a clear distinction between Meyer risk measures and
the subclass of SSD-consistent ones, which becomes more pronounced when additional properties
are imposed. Notably, only the worst-case risk measure achieves both positive homogeneity and
v-SD consistency. Similarly, the classes of convex or star-shaped Meyer risk measures are quite
limited.
Finally, we apply Meyer risk measures in practice. We use an e−1-Meyer risk measure—corre-
sponding to an SSD-consistent RRM—as the objective in a risk minimisation problem. Unlike SSD-
consistent risk objectives, which yield an optimal solution with an additive structure, our problem
yields a multiplicative structure. We then empirically evaluate the performance of ec-SD-consistent
risk measures using real-world financial data.
Open questions persist and present opportunities for future research. Firstly, in our study we
always fix a threshold utility function v and inspect if ρ is v-Meyer. The inverse route, identifying
for a given risk measure ρ all threshold utilities v for which it is consistent, remains open. Secondly,
the risk minimisation problem (6.4), studied for e−1-Meyer risk measures, could be extended to
the more general class of ec-SD-consistent risk measures with c ̸= −1. Thirdly, while v-SD orders
exclude EU agents with risk aversion incomparable to that of v, they still allow arbitrarily high risk

to exclude excessively risk-averse investors by imposing a maximal relative or absolute risk aversion
via a second threshold utility. Can risk measures respect such refined stochastic orders? Finally,
investigating the risk-sharing problem for RRMs is beyond the scope of this paper.
