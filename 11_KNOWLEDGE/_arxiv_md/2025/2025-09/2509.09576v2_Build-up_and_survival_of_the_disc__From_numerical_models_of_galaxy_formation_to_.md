ABSTRACT
We study the build-up and survival of angular momentum in the stellar disc using a statistical suite of cosmological simulations
of Milky Way-mass galaxies. Our results show that stellar kinematics at 𝑧= 0 rarely recover the true times of disc spin-up,
due to the disruptive impact of massive radial merger events. The proto-disc (i.e. Aurora) and kicked-up disc stars (the Splash)
become indistinguishable at low metallicities, and the local fraction of kicked-up disc stars remains < 20 per cent even after major
mergers. In contrast, observations from Gaia and legacy surveys reveal that Galactic 𝛼-rich populations as old as 𝜏= 13.5Gyr
show significant rotation, with median 𝜂> 0.75. This places strong constraints on the total merger ratio between the proto-Milky
Way and its last significant merger (Gaia-Sausage Enceladus, GSE), favouring minor mergers with mass ratios < 1 ∶4. We present
the age–metallicity relation for the stellar halo and estimate the interaction epoch at 𝜏spin-up ≃𝜏GSE ∼11Gyr. We note an abrupt
dearth of halo and Splash stars after a lookback time of 10Gyr, marking the end of the merger interaction. Finally, we show that
Globular Clusters in the metallicity range −0.8 < [Fe/H] < −0.3 share a formation time of 𝜏starburst ∼11Gyr, which we interpret
as a signature of a starburst triggered by the first pericentric interaction of the GSE. This is remarkable corroboration between
our GSE interaction and starburst times of 𝜏GSE = 𝜏starburst ∼11Gyr.

1 INTRODUCTION
Our Milky Way Galaxy (MW) can be considered to be a relatively
typical galaxy, especially among large galaxies in under-dense en-
vironments (Kormendy et al. 2010). It lies near the characteristic
luminosity 𝐿∗at the peak of the stellar luminosity function (e.g.,
Bell et al. 2003) and is a disc galaxy that occupies the green valley
(Zasowski et al. 2025, the underpopulated transitional region be-
tween star-forming blue-cloud galaxies and quiescent red-sequence
galaxies, see Bell et al. 2004; Mendez et al. 2011; Mutch et al. 2011).
Therefore, the MW is an invaluable laboratory for studying galaxy
formation in general.
In the current ΛCDM cosmological model (Planck Collaboration
et al. 2014), galaxies like our own grow hierarchically (Efstathiou
et al. 1985) through the condensation of gas at the centre of DM
haloes (White & Rees 1978; White & Frenk 1991). This hierarchical
growth is evidenced through extragalactic studies of large statistical
galaxy samples using HST and JWST, which have helped to describe
how disc galaxies grow inside-out from 𝑧= 3 to the present-day (van
Dokkum et al. 2013; Patel et al. 2013; Tan et al. 2024). Whilst these

studies are predominantly focused on photometry, similar spectro-
scopic efforts have found signatures of rotation in many star-forming
massive disc galaxies (e.g. Förster Schreiber et al. 2006; Wisnioski
et al. 2019). Studies with ALMA detect rotating disc galaxies out to
redshift 𝑧∼4 (e.g. Rizzo et al. 2020, and see also Lelli et al. 2021;
Roman-Oliveira et al. 2023; Pope et al. 2023; Rowland et al. 2024;
Scholtz et al. 2025), although those systems have roughly an order of
magnitude higher stellar masses and star formation rates than those
expected for the infant MW (Snaith et al. 2015). Fortunately, studies
of our own Galaxy can still serve as a direct probe to bridge this gap
at high-redshift.
Stellar populations in the MW allow us to examine the structure,
kinematics, and chemistry of the Galaxy as a function of time (Free-
man & Bland-Hawthorn 2002; Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016),
which acts as a probe of the formation of disc galaxies more generally.
These efforts have revealed several notable stellar populations. For
instance, Gaia observations of abundant anisotropic halo stars near
the Solar neighbourhood has confirmed earlier signs that the MW
underwent an important merger event some 10 Gyr ago (e.g. Chiba
& Beers 2000; Freeman & Bland-Hawthorn 2002; Brook et al. 2004;
Meza et al. 2005). This merger event is more commonly known as
the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus (GSE), and must have had a number of

Another example is the Splash (Di Matteo et al. 2019a; Belokurov
et al. 2020), believed to represent ancient disc stars that were kicked-

predictions that such populations should arise through interactions

in Myeong et al. (2022). This population, with intermediate chem-

Then there is Aurora, an in-situ, spheroidal and metal-poor pop-

Kravtsov 2022) investigated the emergence of ordered circular mo-
tion through the study of tangential velocities in Aurora stars as
a function of metallicity. This showed a rapid rise in the range
−1.3 < [Fe/H] < −0.9 (e.g. Belokurov & Kravtsov 2022), support-
ing arguments for a rapid disc formation over a period of ∼2 Gyr.
Subsequent studies have corroborated these findings with a few mi-
nor variations (Chandra et al. 2024; Viswanathan et al. 2025). This
topic is of special interest, as it relates to the growing detections of
rotating disc galaxies at high redshifts. However, these works rely on
metallicity as a proxy for stellar age, which is known to be unreliable
because the MW stellar disc and halo (comprised of accreted dwarf
galaxies) inherently evolve on different enrichment timescales.
Given the apparently tumultuous history of the early MW accretion
history, and the emergence of these new classes of stellar populations
within the MW, there are many open questions: what are the, Splash,
Eos and Aurora stars, what exactly is their relation to the GSE merger
event, and can they be easily disentangled or separated?
In this work, we are interested in the build-up of angular momen-
tum in the stellar disc, and the subsequent survival of various stellar
populations. We use insights from fully cosmological simulations
of MW-mass galaxies together with the interpretation of observa-
tional data from the Gaia satellite. We make use of complementary
datasets in the space of metallicity, alpha abundances and stellar ages
(Li et al. 2024; Xiang & Rix 2022) in order to bring new insights into
the formation history of the MW and the impact of the last significant
merger on the Galaxy.
We describe our methods and definitions of emerging stellar pop-
ulations in the simulations in Section 2. Our results are presented in
Section 3, including comparison with observational MW data from
Section 3.7 and onwards. We discuss the implications of our results
for the MW in Section 4, and provide our interpretation for the early
formation of the stellar disc. We conclude in Section 5.

which the fiducial suite includes thirty magneto-hydrodynamic simu-
lations of isolated MW-mass galaxies inside a full cosmological con-
text of 100 cMpc (Grand et al. 2017, 2024). Each galaxy was selected

they transition to evenly spaced intervals in scalefactor. We use the
commonly employed nomenclature of Au-𝑖, where 𝑖indicates the

2.2 Post-processing
2.2.1 Property definitions
We calculate group and subhalo properties with the subfind halo
finder (Springel et al. 2001). Virial masses and radii are defined
with a spherical region enclosing a mean density equal to 200× the
critical density of the universe at that time (𝑀200crit and 𝑅200crit).
Haloes are tracked between snapshots with the lhalotree merger
tree algorithm (Springel et al. 2005). To calculate properties such
as satellite pericentre times, we improve precision by interpolating
between snapshots using fitted splines. In the case of 3-D orbits, we
use make_interp_spline from the scipy.interpolate python
package with an order of 3. In the case of other properties, for which
shape must be preserved, we use the PchipInterpolator function.
We orient our reference frame on the stellar disc of the host pro-
genitor galaxy over all snapshots. This is achieved by aligning on
the angular momentum of star particles within ten per cent of the
virial radius 𝑅200crit, including only in-situ stars that are at maxi-
mum 3 Gyrs old. These selection criteria ensure a reduced bias from
merger interactions and/or tilted outer discs.
Throughout this work, we make use of the orbital circularity pa-
rameter (𝜂). This is a measure of orbital shape, where 𝜂= −1 corre-
sponds to circular and retrograde orbits, 𝜂= 0 corresponds to plung-
ing radial orbits, and 𝜂= +1 corresponds to circular and prograde
orbits. We follow the definition in Abadi et al. (2003):
𝜂= 𝐿𝑧/𝐿circ,

and 𝐿circ is the corresponding angular momentum for a particle on a
circular orbit at this same energy. In practical terms, we approximate
𝐿circ by computing the maximum 𝐿𝑧for star particles at similar total
orbital energies.

We assume that the stellar halo is a non-rotating population with
a symmetric distribution in 𝜂, and that its prograde and retrograde
components share the same metallicity distribution function (MDF).
Based on this assumption, we compute the joint probability density
function of 𝜂and metallicity for the retrograde stars, and use this
to randomly sample a corresponding set of prograde stars. The ret-
rograde and sampled prograde stars are classified as halo members,
while the remaining prograde stars are assigned to the disc. This
approach is essentially the same as that used by Grand et al. (2017),
but with the added inclusion of the stellar metallicity.
Where we quote merger mass ratios, these are defined as the total
mass ratio between the main satellite and parent haloes in subfind
(excluding the masses of any substructure in each object). The ratio
is determined at the moment when the merging halo joins the same
subfind group as the parent halo, at which time it will have expe-
rienced only minor tidal stripping. This definition allows for some
edge cases where the merging halo is momentarily of a greater mass
than the parent halo (see examples in our Table 1). We stress that the
merger ratio determinations can vary substantially along the merger
infall path, and should be viewed as approximate rather than exact.
Auriga follows the abundances of nine chemical species, which
are normalised to Solar values using Asplund et al. (2009). Further
corrections are applied to the iron abundances in order to lower the
metallicity ratio ([Fe/H]) by 0.4 dex, in accordance with prior auriga
works.
2.2.2 Merger selections
In order to compare with the MW, we target a selection of realisations
with mergers that have the following key properties:
●An ancient first orbital pericentre in the range 11 < 𝜏/Gyr < 7.
●A highly radial infall trajectory, where the first orbital pericentre is
within 30 kpc of the galactic centre and the infall angle 𝜃is less than
30 degrees (where 𝜃is the angle between the satellite velocity vector
and the vector pointing directly toward the galactic centre at the time
of infall).
●A sharply raised star formation rate that correlates with the merger
pericentre, of approximately twice the pre-merger level or higher.
●The impact can be clearly correlated with this single merger event,
rather than some kind of group infall scenario. If more than one
merger fulfils all criteria, then the selected merger must represent
> 50 per cent of the total mass. We also exclude two realisations
where there is a coincident major merger.
We place no direct restriction on the merger mass ratio, which is
intended to allow for examples both lesser and greater than the range
of plausible merger ratios predicted for the GSE (pre-infall mass
around the current-day mass of the SMC, see for e.g. Belokurov
et al. 2018; Helmi et al. 2018; Mackereth et al. 2019; Vincenzo
et al. 2019; Das et al. 2020; Feuillet et al. 2020a; Naidu et al. 2020;
Kruijssen et al. 2020; Han et al. 2022; Lane et al. 2023; Plevne
& Akbaba 2025; Lane & Bovy 2025). Using these constraints, we
identify suitable merger events in 16 out of 30 Auriga simulations,
and these are described in Table 1. The lowest mass-ratio merger in
our sample occurs in Au-22, though this is in fact part of a double
merger, where two systems of nearly equal mass reach pericentre at
roughly the same time.
Previous studies have shown that 10 of the Auriga galaxies un-
dergo ancient mergers that give rise to a substantial fraction of
anisotropic, GSE-like stellar halo debris (Fattahi et al. 2019; Orkney
et al. 2023, defined as having a velocity anisotropy parameter of
𝛽> 0.8 and a contribution fraction to the halo of > 0.5). Whilst sev-
eral of the same simulations are examined in this work, the mergers
we target are not necessarily the same as those responsible for those
GSE-like debris features.
2.2.3 Population selections
In this work, we will consider four ancient stellar populations:
●The merger-induced starburst.
●The Splash, which is the population of disc stars that are scattered
onto more halo-like orbits by the merger.
●The Aurora stars, defined as the pre-disc spheroid.
●Eos, as first identified for the MW in Myeong et al. (2022).
The starburst population is selected using a mass-weighted his-
togram of all star particles within the host galaxy at 𝑧= 0. The time
period of elevated star formation that coincides with the target merger
event is selected manually. These starbursts are clearly distinct from
the background star formation rate, so a more elaborate selection
criterion was deemed unnecessary. The onset of this epoch typically
begins close to the time of the merger pericentre passage, and has a
duration of a few hundred Myrs.
The Splash population is identified with a more rigorous scheme
that we describe fully in Appendix A, and can include both in-situ
and ex-situ member stars.
Aurora stars are ideally defined as the in-situ population that
formed prior to the disc spin-up. We identify the spin-up as the time
when stars begin forming with a median orbital circularity of 𝜂> 0.3,
considering only stars within the radial range 3 < 𝑅G/kpc < 20. Cir-
cularity is computed in 50 Myr bins and smoothed over seven bin
widths to mitigate stochastic fluctuations. However, we also deter-
mine an equivalent spin-up using stellar metallicity as a proxy for
age, allowing for closer comparison with observational methods.
The Aurora population is then defined as stars with metallicities be-
low this characteristic spin-up metallicity. Throughout this work, we
adopt Aurora selections based on the spin-up metallicity inferred
from 𝑧= 0 stellar kinematics, as this provides the most direct ana-
logue to observational data.
One possible formation scenario for Eos is that it formed from a
cocktail of gases from the proto-MW and the infalling GSE dwarf
galaxy, acquiring chemical abundances intermediate between those
of the in-situ proto-MW and the GSE (Myeong et al. 2022). For
the purposes of our investigation, we identify this population as the
earliest (the first 50 Myr) in-situ stars which formed out of the gas
donated by our target mergers. This definition requires the use of gas
tracers which can follow the flow of Lagrangian gas cells through
time. The level-4 Auriga simulations do not include gas tracers, and
so we investigate Eos for just one sample simulation that we have
re-run with gas tracers enabled as part of our “genetically modified”
simulations campaign (Orkney et al. in prep.).

In most cases, the splashed fraction is far lower for minor mergers
(an average of 𝑓splash = 0.41 for minor mergers and 𝑓splash = 0.58 for
major mergers). There are two exceptions, those being Au-5 and Au-
22, which have unusually high splashed fractions. In those cases, the
actual mass in splashed stars is not particularly high, and the disc itself
was only just beginning to form. This can be seen from the very low
𝐷/𝑇fractions of 0.10 and 0.22. Therefore, the high Splash fractions
do not necessarily reflect especially disruptive merger events, but an
increased vulnerability of the host. There are prior works which link
this to the ability of gas-rich discs to absorb the kinematic energy of
a disruptive merger impact (e.g. Hopkins et al. 2009; Moster et al.
2010), meaning gas-poor discs would be more vulnerable.
The spin-up times are in most cases delayed when based upon the
stellar kinematics at 𝑧= 0, and this is a consequence of cumulative
kinematic heating and, more importantly, the disruptive impact of
the merger event itself. The kinematic signature of any pre-merger
disc population is violently erased, either completely or partially.
The magnitude of this delay is dependant on many circumstances
including the merger mass ratio, the pre-merger disc mass, and the
merger infall trajectory. In some cases, there are later significant
mergers which delay the spin-up by an even greater amount of time
(see Au-5, Au-10, Au-7 and Au-30). If one considers the spin-up time
as determined from the birth kinematics, they range from lookback
times of between 11–12.6 Gyrs, indicating that Auriga galaxies be-
gin to form rotating discs even earlier than the most likely infall time
of the GSE in our Galaxy (8-11 Gyr ago, see for e.g. Helmi et al.

From these plots, it is clear how massive merger events can impact
the determination of the disc spin-up time. Firstly, the transition
from low-to-high circularities has shifted to later times. In the case
of the major mergers, it has shifted to after the merger interaction
time. Despite the substantial impact to the early disc stars, the disc
is rapidly reformed with a higher median circularity following the
coalescence of the merging subhalo.
Secondly, the merger impact has altered the apparent duration
of the disc spin-up. In Au-18, the disc spins up rapidly over the
range 12.5 > 𝜏/Gyr > 11.5. Following the merger interaction, this
sharp spin-up transition has been blurred and appears to take from
12 > 𝜏/Gyr > 10.5. In Au-27, the disc spins up very gradually
during its violent assembly from many major mergers. After the
merger interaction, the spin-up transition appears to occur within
just a few hundred Myrs. These effects depend on the mass ratio of
the merger and other properties as well, such as the satellite infall
trajectory (similar to how Splash fractions depend on the merger
orbital eccentricity in Grand et al. 2020) and the resilience of the
disc to perturbation (e.g. Hopkins et al. 2009; Moster et al. 2010).
Finally, we draw attention to the starbursts associated with the first
pericentric passages of each merger, which can be seen as a dark
blue vertical stripe in the histogram data. In all cases, these popula-
tions extend to both highly prograde and highly retrograde median
orbital circularities, and occur predominantly within the inner galaxy
(𝑅G < 6 kpc). This is particularly evident in Au-18. Whilst the merger
interaction itself has only a modest impact on pre-existing stellar or-
bital circularities, the starburst stars have a mass-weighted average
circularity of 𝜂= 0.46 and extend far into the retrograde portion of
the histogram. This signature feature is preserved in the kinematics
at 𝑧= 0, though the distribution of circularities has broadened as the
stellar orbits have dynamically relaxed.

The spin-up occurs over roughly 0.8 dex for Au-18, 0.2 dex for Au-27
and 0.4 dex for Au-26. We will show in Section 3.7 that the stars in
the MW transition over a range of ∼0.2 dex, in rough agreement with
prior studies.
Whilst this may suggest that the GSE was a major merger similar
to that in Au-27, we caution that the metallicity range associated with
the spin-up in these simulations is sensitive to the unique chemical
enrichment history of the host galaxy. The birth circularities in Au-18
show that the spin-up occurs a very broad range of 1.5 dex, whereas
the birth circularities in Au-27 exhibit narrower spin-up ranges of
only 0.5 dex. This wide metal range for the spin-up in Au-18 is
incongruent with the rapid and early time range of the spin-up. We
have also considered Au-24, which has a GSE-like massive merger of
similar merger mass ratio to that in Au-18, but without such a broad
metallicity range in the spin-up of the birth circularities. There, the
post-interaction spin-up occurs over a narrow range of ∼0.2 dex, just
like in Au-27.
For both Au-18 and Au-27, there is a steep downturn in the cir-
cularities of the most metal-rich stars. This is an effect of sampling
stars that form in the metal-rich bulge region, which naturally have
lower orbital circularities and easily dominate the fraction of stars at
high metallicities. This feature can be removed with more stringent
cuts on orbital radius.
A key question is whether the dense and low-circularity starburst
features, easily distinguished in terms of stellar age in Figure 1, can
also be seen in terms of stellar metallicity in Figure 2. We find that
whilst there are corresponding features, they are smeared over a wide
metallicity range such that they are difficult to perceive, especially
when viewed in the 𝜂(𝑧= 0) histograms. We advise caution when
interpreting such features, because they can also be produced through
other mechanisms. For example, in Au-18 there is a population of
stars extending to lower circularities at [Fe/H] ≈−0.8, and this owes
mostly to the starburst population. However, there is another similar
feature at [Fe/H] ≈−0.6, and this corresponds to stars that formed
from inflowing CGM gas over a long period of time during the
formation of the low-𝛼thin disc (see Orkney et al. 2026 for more).
Merger impacts do not only influence orbital circularities and ve-
locities, but also the distribution of stars above and below the disc
plane. Stars born before the merger interaction are scattered onto
orbits with greater ∣𝑧∣-heights and greater vertical velocities, and this
can give the appearance of a highly-dichotomous formation of thick
and thin disc components (see Grand et al. 2020). This behaviour
holds true even for Au-1, for which the pre-interaction stars gain an
increased azimuthal velocity. We do not examine this phenomena
further in this work, but remind the reader that many of our reported
results have similar implications for these other kinematic or geomet-
ric properties. Furthermore, Funakoshi et al. (2025) recently provided
observational evidence for this behaviour in the Milky Way, identify-
ing a rapid transition from a thick to a thin disc geometry among red
giant stars in APOGEE DR17 and Gaia DR3. This transition, dated
to ∼10 Gyr ago, coincides with the accretion epoch of the GSE.
In Figure 3, we compare starburst stars at their birth locations with
stars that formed in a stable epoch before the merger (the pre-burst
epoch). The pre-burst stars form in a highly symmetric thin disc
configuration with 93 per cent born at orbital circularities initially
above 𝜂= 0.8. In contrast, the starburst stars are forming in a thicker
and more compact disc configuration, with some stars belonging to
tendrils that extend to higher radii. This is a sign of the dense asym-
metric gas filaments that arose during the merger interaction. This
asymmetrical gas distribution and raised star formation rate is also
a fertile environment for the formation of Globular Clusters (GCs)
(Li et al. 2017; Forbes et al. 2018; Bekki 2019). Whilst the auriga
simulations do not resolve cluster formation, this is a predictor of a
period of rapid GC formation in the MW Galaxy that coincides with
the GSE-induced starburst (and see Whitmore & Schweizer 1995;
Larsen et al. 2001; Kruijssen et al. 2012). We will return to this point
in Section 3.7.

upper metallicity limit used to define Aurora.

Now that we have investigated starburst, Splash and Aurora analogue
stellar populations within Auriga, we seek to address how much
mass they contribute to the stellar haloes of their respective galaxies.
We show decompositions of the stellar haloes in Figure 5. We
determine which stars belong to the halo using a probabilistic selec-
tion based on the orbital circularity, as described in Section 2.2. The
total masses of each population are found following their definitions
given in Section 2.2, though we do not account for Eos because this
would require tracer information that is not available for most of these
simulations. However, Eos can be considered as subgroup of the star-
burst population (as is also suggested in Myeong et al. 2022). Our
definition of Aurora stars is non-exclusive, such that they can also
be starburst or Splash stars. Therefore, we indicate the overlapping
fractions with a hatched fill-style.
Our results reveal that there is a large variation between individual
realisation, with a few trends that hold in most cases. The starburst
populations never contribute more than ∼20 per cent of halo stars,
despite the starburst itself being a very massive population. This is
because a significant fraction of starburst stars have disc-like orbits
which are retained at 𝑧= 0. Even so, the starburst halo fraction is
usually greater than for the Splash, with Au-18 being the greatest
exception. The Aurora fraction is higher around Solar radii, despite
being the most ancient population. This is a selection effect owing
to the early development of negative radial metallicity gradients,
in which low-metallicity stars are favoured to form from the more
metal-poor gases at the peripherals of the proto-galaxy.

merger interaction. In fact, all of the four halo components shown

counter-balance each other such that their fractional contributions

rapidly mixes with that of the host galaxy and becomes inseparable in
terms of its chemistry, and so we do not consider those as “Eos-like”.

much lower than implied by the detection in Myeong et al. (2022),
and the portion of this mass that is chemically distinct from other
contemporaneous in-situ stars is even smaller, which prompts con-
sideration that a truly pure Eos population may not be detectable. We
consider this further in the discussion section.
We take this opportunity to note that massive mergers will also
trigger the inflow of surrounding metal-poor CGM gases, and this
would undoubtedly contribute to the starburst (see Di Matteo et al.
2007; Bustamante et al. 2018; Moreno et al. 2021; Renaud et al.
2021; Orkney et al. 2026). However, here we focus specifically on
stars which originate from a mixing of merger gas rather than gas
from the peripherals of the host galaxy.
It is also worth noting that stars with Eos-like chemical abundances
(i.e., intermediate between those expected for in-situ and ex-situ pop-
ulations) can originate from more than one single merger event. The
analogous stellar populations from even earlier mergers are typically
deposited near the very bottom of the potential well, but exhibit a
more symmetric distribution in 𝐿𝑧at 𝑧= 0, owing in part to orbital
scattering after the last significant merger.

3.7 Build-up of angular momentum in the Milky Way
In this section we use the insight we have gained from numerical
simulations of MW-mass galaxies to interpret observations of our
own Galaxy, mainly focusing on the build-up and survival of angular
momentum in the Galactic disc. This topic has been explored by a
number of studies which analyse variations in the stellar circularity
(or azimuthal velocity) as a function of metallicity (e.g. Belokurov
& Kravtsov 2022; Chandra et al. 2024; Viswanathan et al. 2025).
Whilst metallicity can be used as a chemical clock (to a zeroth
order approximation), different regions of the Galaxy will evolve and
enrich on different timescales, depending on the details of galaxy
formation such as the CGM cycle and galactic chemical evolution
(e.g. Tinsley 1980; White & Frenk 1991). This is particularly evident
in the central region of the Galaxy, which hosts both ancient metal-
poor stars and ancient super-solar metal-rich stars (Rix et al. 2024). A
more direct approach would be to consider the evolution of circularity
as a function of stellar age, which has not yet been done although
such datasets are becoming more readily available.
Here, we make use of the dataset provided in Xiang & Rix (2022),
which contains a sample of ∼270, 000 subgiant stars with pre-
cise photo-spectroscopically determined ages using the Yale-Yonsei
isochrones (Yi et al. 2001, 2003). An interesting feature of this sam-
ple is that it probes a wide range of guiding radii from the inner region
of the Galaxy, the solar neighbourhood, and out into the stellar halo.
We calculate orbital parameters within the same assumed potential
of Belokurov et al. (2023) that was used in Section 3.6. In this study,
we only consider stars whose ages are determined within a precision
of 𝜏/𝜎𝜏≤10 per cent. Xiang et al. (2025) already used this data to
determine the age and mass of the oldest high-𝛼thick disc (which
was forming as far back as 13–14 Gyr ago) through the study of its
structural properties. Here, we take a kinematics approach to study
the stellar halo and high-𝛼disc properties in relation to the 𝜂–[Fe/H]
diagram.

4 DISCUSSION
4.1 Satellite influence on the spin-up
Early idealised simulations demonstrated that interactions with satel-
lites can profoundly perturb stellar discs, generating transient warps,
flares, asymmetries, vertical and radial heating, and even the ero-
sion of disc-like orbits (e.g. Quinn et al. 1993; Velazquez & White
1999; Kazantzidis et al. 2009; Martig et al. 2014). Already in these
early studies, the formation of thick discs was attributed to the ac-
tion of satellite encounters. The degree of disc heating was shown
to depend on the properties of the infalling system, with massive,
singular mergers being more efficient at disrupting discs than a se-
ries of smaller ones, and with prograde orbits typically imparting the
strongest effects (see Kazantzidis et al. 2009). In particular, Martig
et al. (2014) linked even relatively minor mergers (relative stellar
mass ratios as low as 1 ∶15, which is even lower than the equivalent
total mass ratios shown in this work) to sharp step-like changes in
the age-velocity dispersion relation. These behaviours are now con-
firmed in fully cosmological simulations (e.g. House et al. 2011; Hu
& Sijacki 2018; Grand et al. 2020; McCluskey et al. 2024; Chandra
et al. 2024; Semenov et al. 2025).

The impact of the GSE is expected to have been at least partially
responsible for creating the hotter kinematics and vertically extended
geometry of the thick disc (Helmi et al. 2018; Grand et al. 2020;
Ciucă et al. 2024). Then, it stands to reason that the GSE would
have also impacted the rotational velocities and orbits of those stars.
This would affect the inferred spin-up of the disc as measured by
the 𝑧= 0 kinematics, as we have shown in Section 3.2. Therefore,
any interpretation of the spin-up should be mindful that it does not
necessarily represent the original timing.
This has repercussions for the proto-MW (Aurora, identified in
Belokurov & Kravtsov 2022). This population is described as a
purely in-situ and mildy rotating group of stars that formed before
the formation of the MW disc, present only at lower metallicities of
−1.5 < [Fe/H] < −0.9. Then, this Aurora population should have
undergone scattering as a result of the GSE interaction, in a similar
manner as the Splash population. Depending on how early Aurora
was formed, it may have been impacted by even earlier mergers such
as the speculative Heracles/Kraken. Therefore, it stands to reason
that Aurora may have once been more disc-like than it is today.
In contrast to our results, an analysis of 14 galaxies in the fire sim-
ulations finds significant stellar rotation even among stars that formed
before merger interactions (McCluskey et al. 2024). Comparing this
with our own auriga selection, we attribute the discrepancy to differ-
ences in the types of merger events considered. Our sample focuses
on highly radial mergers, which impart little angular momentum to
pre-existing stars, whereas the realisations in McCluskey et al. (2024)
include more tangential in-spirals that torque the pre-existing stel-
lar population (see also Santistevan et al. 2021). Thus, the apparent
preservation of pre-interaction disc kinematics likely reflects the in-
heritance of angular momentum from certain merger events rather
than the survival of a primordial disc. Such a scenario is improbable
for the MW, where the GSE merger is thought to have followed a
highly radial and retrograde infall trajectory.
4.2 The composition of the in-situ stellar halo
In Myeong et al. (2022), the authors identify a variety of components
in the local stellar halo through the use of an unsupervised Gaussian
mixture modelling approach, enabling them to determine the rela-
tive contributions of populations including Aurora, Splash and Eos.
They exploit a number of chemical abundances and orbital energies
as taken from both APOGEE-Gaia and GALAH-Gaia survey data.
Since this is an unsupervised approach directed only by the data,
and it does not account for kinematics other than orbital energy, the
results should not be considered as absolute. Nonetheless, here we
compare with our own findings.
The Aurora populations in Auriga represent a much greater frac-
tion of stars than is implied for the MW by Myeong et al. (2022).
Another estimate of Aurora stars comes from Kurbatov et al. (2024),
who model the distribution of RGB stars from the Galactic cen-
tre out to 18 kpc, accounting for the Gaia selection function. Using
this, they report an Aurora mass of 2.01 × 108 M⊙over the range
−3 > [Fe/H] > −1.3 (approximately up to the metallicity where the
spin-up occurs). From our Table 1, this value is again far lower than
the Aurora mass amongst the auriga simulations. This tension may
stem from the early mass growth in the auriga galaxy-formation
models, as discussed in section 5.3 of Grand et al. (2017). To sum-
marise, the Auriga galaxies lie above the 1𝜎scatter of the Moster13
stellar mass-halo mass relation at 𝑧= 3 (Moster et al. 2013), indi-
cating an overproduction of stars at the earliest times. This has been
attributed to a deficiency in AGN feedback.
The Splash population recovered by Myeong et al. (2022) includes
stars with abundances lying along the high-𝛼sequence of the thick
disc, occupying a range of abundances given by [Fe/H] = −0.61 ±
0.16 and [Mg/Fe] = 0.29 ± 0.02 for their APOGEE-Gaia sample.
This range is far narrower than what we find in Figure 4, implying
that Splash should contain member stars well beyond the confines
in Myeong et al. (2022). Yet, their Splash fraction is very high at
25 per cent for the APOGEE-Gaia sample and 31 per cent for the
APOGEE-Gaia sample. This is closest to the local halo fraction
in Au-18, for which the post-merger accretion history is quiescent
and so the stellar halo around the Solar neighbourhood contains
proportionally fewer accreted stars. Di Matteo et al. (2019b) use
APOGEE and Gaia data to quantify the total mass of heated proto-
disc stars in the inner stellar halo (roughly within the Solar circle),
estimating 2–5×109 M⊙. This is broadly comparable to the numbers
seen in our Table 1, although we recognise that the overall stellar
halo masses in auriga are much greater than those of the MW and
most external galaxies (see Monachesi et al. 2019).
Finally, Myeong et al. (2022) discover a population of stars which
they call Eos, where one possible origin is that they formed with gas
accreted from the GSE merger. This population was later found via
independent methods in Matsuno et al. (2024). Eos is estimated to
contribute 10-15 per cent of halo stars in the solar neighbourhood.
Whilst our analysis supports the existence of this population owing
to merger-accreted gases, characterised by tightly bound orbits and
intermediate chemical abundances, the mass fraction is far lower than
suggested by Myeong et al. (2022). This is because the merger gas
rapidly mixes with the in-situ interstellar medium, leaving only a few
tens of Myr for the formation of stars with intermediate chemistry.
In An et al. (2025), parallels are drawn between Eos and a popu-
lation of low-𝛼, relatively high-Na stars (termed LAHN stars) from
a sample of high proper-motion stars in a collection of spectroscopic
surveys and with Gaia kinematics. This population is estimated to
include one star for every ten accreted GSE stars, from which they in-
fer a total mass of 𝑀𝐸𝑜𝑠= 5×107 M⊙. This is lower than predictions
in Myeong et al. (2022) and is more compatible with our numerical
estimate. Notably, our Eos analogue population favours prograde or-
bits as the donated gas begins to join the rotating gaseous disc of the
host galaxy. This is analogous to the twin LAHN populations found
in An et al. (2025), of which one is concentrated at 𝐿𝑧= 0 and the
other is more disc-like.
One population not found by Myeong et al. (2022) is a GSE-
induced starburst. Starbursts are a common fixture in cosmological
simulations of galaxy formation (e.g. Sparre & Springel 2016; Davis
et al. 2019; Rodríguez Montero et al. 2019; Hani et al. 2020; Renaud
et al. 2022; Orkney et al. 2022), and there are claims of a starburst in
the MW at around the time of the GSE accretion (Ciucă et al. 2024,
and see also evidence of a star formation peak from modelling the
colour magnitude diagram of local stars in Fernández-Alvar et al.
2025). Our Figure 5 shows that this population is a significant con-
tributor to the ancient stellar mass budget, with a stellar halo fraction
similar to that of the Splash, and a further contribution to the stellar
disc. In this work and in Grand et al. (2020), it is clear that many
starburst stars have orbital and chemical properties coincident with
that of the Splash. Then, the MW starburst population may be hiding
in plain sight, a possibility also highlighted by An et al. (2025).
4.3 A timeline of MW disc formation
Observations of the Solar neighbourhood have revealed that the star
formation rate was highest at early times (𝜏= 11 Gyr, e.g. Haywood
et al. 2013; Snaith et al. 2015; Haywood et al. 2018), indicating
an early rapid mass growth for the Galaxy. This result is further

reinforced by analysis of the star formation rate at the Galactic centre
in Lian et al. (2020). Likewise, cosmological simulations in a ΛCDM
cosmology increasingly suggest that the MW underwent a front-
loaded assembly followed by a relatively quiescent growth up until
the present day, with the transition coinciding with the expected
arrival time of the GSE merger (Carlesi et al. 2020; Santistevan et al.
2020; Semenov et al. 2025; Woody et al. 2025; Wempe et al. 2025).
Following our results in Section 3.7, there are a large number of
high-𝛼disc stars already in place at 𝜏≥13 Gyr, indicating that the
MW acquired its rotation relatively early when compared to other 𝐿∗
galaxies. This is not without precedent, spectroscopic observations
of early galaxies (2 > 𝑧> 1) have discovered surprisingly high rates
of ordered gas rotation among the main-sequence galaxy population
(e.g. Stott et al. 2016; Wisnioski et al. 2019; Lelli et al. 2023; Birkin
et al. 2024). The fraction of galaxies that are rotation dominated
(i.e., 𝑣/𝜎> 1) increases with galactic mass and time, yet remains
relatively high even as far back as 𝑧= 2. Furthermore, MW analogues
from JWST have signatures of an ordered photometric disc as early
as 𝑧= 5 (e.g. Tan et al. 2024). Additionally, cosmological galaxy
simulations in the artemis simulations tend to exhibit an earlier disc
spin-up if they had a higher-than-average virial mass at early times,
or if they experienced a GSE-like merger (Dillamore et al. 2024,
which is itself a proxy for an earlier mass assembly). Constrained
cosmological simulations of the Local Group in Wempe et al. (2025)
systematically predict that the MW formed earlier than M31, with a
quiet accretion history due to tides exerted by M31. Therefore, there
is direct observational and inferred theoretical evidence favouring an
early disc formation in the MW.
In Section 3.2, we showed that the time of the spin-up in three
auriga galaxies occurs even earlier than it appears based on the
𝑧= 0 stellar kinematics. This is a behaviour that is consistent across
the auriga suite, and is caused by satellite interactions acting to
scatter and kinematically heat the early disc stars. Tt becomes even
more difficult to accurately determine the timing of the spin-up when
using metallicity as an analogue for stellar age, as is common for
observational analyses.
This result is consistent with examination of MW-analogues from
the TNG-50 cosmological simulation in Chandra et al. (2024), where
it is shown that major mergers consistently act to ‘delay’ the disc spin-
up time by kinematically heating the pre-existing disc stars. Similarly,
the authors in (Semenov et al. 2025) perform re-simulations of MW
analogues from TNG-50, and discover exceptionally early disc for-
mation. This can begin as early as 𝑧∼6–7 with a spin-up time period
of just 0.2 Gyr. They describe how a major merger at 𝑧= 3 erases
the kinematic signatures of this early disc formation, shifting the
apparent spin-up time to higher metallicities.
Critically, if the GSE merger also impacted the kinematics of
pre-existing disc stars, then this would undermine the utility of the
observed spin-up time as a predictor of MW disc formation. Even
earlier mergers, such as the speculated Heracles/Kraken merger event
(Kruijssen et al. 2019; Massari et al. 2019; Horta et al. 2021), may
have played their own roles in degrading the kinematic imprints of the
MW’s initial disc formation. Nonetheless, neither of these mergers
were massive enough to completely erase signatures of early stellar
rotation in the MW, as we showed in Section 3.7. This effectively
places an upper limit on the merger mass ratio of the GSE (and
indeed, any other early mergers): it must have been a minor merger
if pre-existing disc kinematics survived the encounter.
This points to a remarkably quiescent environment and growth for
the MW during its early formation history. Nevertheless, this does
not contradict the claim that the GSE was a significant merger, in the
sense that it might have contributed to a central starburst in the Galaxy
(e.g. Grand et al. 2020; Orkney et al. 2022, 2026), kinematically
affected the orbits of stars (Bonaca et al. 2017; Gallart et al. 2019; Di
Matteo et al. 2019b; Amarante et al. 2020a; Belokurov et al. 2020),
or dominate the mass budget of the stellar halo (e.g. Belokurov et al.
2018; Naidu et al. 2020)
We can also constrain the epoch of the GSE interaction with
the ancient MW by using the age–metallicity relation of the stel-
lar halo. From this, we have estimated an initial interaction at 𝑧> 2
(or 𝜏= 11 Gyr) and a completion of the interaction at 𝑧∼2 (or
𝜏= 10 Gyr). This is aligned with the peak of the MW SFR at
𝜏starburst = 11 Gyr, consistent with the idea that the GSE triggered
a starburst. Our interpretation of the starburst is supported by the
instantaneous formation of GCs at 𝜏starburst ∼11 Gyr, as are expected
following a starburst (e.g. Whitmore & Schweizer 1995; Larsen et al.
2001; Kruijssen et al. 2012), which have metallicities consistent with
the ambient ISM of the Galaxy at that time. The absence of metal-rich
GCs at earlier times implies that there was no major starburst asso-
ciated with earlier significant merger events, such as the speculative
Kraken/Heracles, even though many ex-situ GCs are thought to have
been donated by this merger (Massari et al. 2019). This would indi-
cate that Kraken is a less significant merger event than, for example,
the 1 ∶3 mass ratio merger shown in Orkney et al. (2022).
A similar concept has recently been put forward by Valenzuela
et al. (2024), who suggest that the MW GC population is bi-modal.
They argue that the GSE merger not only brought in a population
of ex-situ GCs, but also triggered the formation of a secondary in-
situ group around 10–11 Gyr ago. Although they do not pinpoint a
specific interaction time, their results highlight GC formation as a
valuable tracer of wet merger events.
5 CONCLUSIONS
We have performed an investigation into the kinematic consequences
of ancient, massive mergers in the auriga simulation suite of Milky
Way-mass (MW) galaxies, and used these to interpret the GSE merger
in our own Galaxy. Our focus has been on the early build-up of angular
momentum in the disc, often termed the spin-up, and ancient stellar
populations including the Splash, Aurora, Eos and merger-induced
starbursts. We summarise our key conclusions here.
●In agreement with prior work, we find that the spin-up transition
(as defined based on the stellar kinematics recovered at 𝑧= 0) is not
necessarily an accurate reflection of the true spin-up time because
it is heavily influenced by the impact of the last significant radial
merger. Furthermore, ancient disc-like orbits can partially survive
after a minor merger interaction (merger mass ratio < 1 ∶4), but are
almost entirely erased after major mergers (> 1 ∶4).
●In auriga galaxies, distinguishing between the identified starburst
and Splash populations is difficult based on the stellar property dis-
tributions alone. Moreover, there is substantial overlap between stars
classified as belonging to the Splash and Aurora populations. We
therefore caution that analyses of these populations in the MW, such
as efforts to infer properties of the GSE merger event, must account
for these degeneracies. In addition, the MW starburst population may
be concealed within the Splash distribution.
●The MW high-𝛼disc was becoming established some 13 Gyr ago,
and the kinematic signature of this has survived to 𝑧= 0. This means
the GSE, which accreted between 𝜏GSE ∼11–9 Gyr ago, could not
have been a major merger. By the same argument, even more ancient
mergers such as the speculative Kraken/Heracles must also have
been relatively minor interactions. Similarly, from comparison of the

energy footprint of GSE debris with merger debris in auriga (see
Figure 7), we conclude that the GSE is most consistent with minor
mergers of total mass ratio < 1 ∶4.
●If we assume that the spin-up marks the timing of the last significant
merger, it can be used to date the epoch of the GSE interaction in the
MW. With the spin-up metallicity measured at [Fe/H]spin-up ∼−1.2,
we can place this event on the age–metallicity relation of the ex-situ
stellar halo. This comparison shows that it began with a pericen-
tre passage around 𝜏GSE-peri ∼11 Gyr ago and then the interaction
concluded between 𝜏GSE-coalescence ∼10–9 Gyr ago.
●We highlight a surge of MW Globular Cluster formation at
𝜏starburst ∼11 Gyr ago, consistent with having formed during a star-
burst triggered by the first pericentric passage of the last signifi-
cant merger. It is remarkable that this time satisfies the criterion
𝜏starburst = 𝜏GSE-peri ∼11 Gyr, and this lends further credibility to the
value of both timescales and to our interpretation that these both owe
to the GSE interaction. This is, to our knowledge, the first time that
this association has been made.
Altogether, our results paint a portrait of a calm and quiescent
MW during the first few Gyrs. With the advent of next-generation
surveys such as PLATO (Rauer et al. 2025), stellar age estimates will
achieve unprecedented precision. Nevertheless, they will remain far
from the sub-per cent level required to fully resolve conditions in
the early proto-Galaxy, and this will prove to be a major bottleneck
inhibiting further discovery. Moreover, stellar kinematics can only
ever be measured at 𝑧= 0, limiting the scientific possibilities.
This, then, provides strong motivation to look beyond the MW
to analogues of the MW progenitor in the high-redshift Universe.
This can already be achieved up to 𝑧∼4 (e.g. Jain et al. 2025), and
upcoming observations with JWST and ALMA can probe further
beyond these limits, potentially supplemented by results from strong-
lensing observations (e.g. Rizzo et al. 2020; Pope et al. 2023; Lee
et al. 2025). This era can also be studied locally by analysing the
Galactic centre, where there are direct constraints on the stellar disc
mass, star formation rate, and the various stellar populations that
built the proto-MW during the epoch of reionisation. By bridging
studies of the MW with observations of high-redshift progenitors,
we can directly link local stellar populations to their early Universe
counterparts and use this to constrain Galactic assembly.

APPENDIX A: SPLASH SELECTION
In Figure A1, we show how the Splash population is identified for the
example of Au-18. Firstly, we calculate the circularity distribution of
all stars in the main progenitor galaxy at a time 100 Myrs before the
first pericentre of the merger. The particle IDs of all disc stars are
then stored for later, where the disc is defined following the method
described in Section 2.2. By definition, these stars will lie in the
range 0 < 𝜂(before) < 1.
Next, we recalculate the circularity distribution of the main pro-
genitor galaxy at a time after the merger event. This is judged to be
1 Gyr after the end of the merger-induced starburst epoch. By this
time, the merger remnant has completed multiple pericentric pas-
sages, each of which has contributed to scattering disc stars onto
more radial orbits, and the disc has then resettled.
The Splash population is defined as all stars from the pre-
interaction disc that fulfill the following criteria:

From Figure A1, one can see that the Splash population (pink

mass has grown after the interaction despite this loss of Splash stars,
and that is due to the ongoing and high levels of star formation.
APPENDIX B: STELLAR AGE PRECISIONS
Stellar spectroscopy can currently achieve precisions in stellar age
estimates to around the 10 per cent level in some cases (e.g. Xiang &
Rix 2022; Anders et al. 2023). Whilst this is sufficient for analysing
younger populations, it becomes prohibitive for investigating events
in the first few Gyrs of the Universe. In particular, the starburst and
spin-up features we highlight for auriga in Figure 1 often occur over
sub-Gyr intervals, which would be below the stellar age uncertainties
at those times (𝜏≃10 Gyr).
In Figure A2, we replot Figure 1 using a Gaussian filter on the
time-axis with a variable kernel smoothing of 𝜎(smooth) = 0.1𝜏.
The filter has a drastic effect on the appearance of starbursts at ancient
times, even the major starbursts corresponding to our target mergers
(represented with black coloured lines in the top row), smearing them
together such that they can no longer be recovered. The apparent
duration of the spin-up transitions has increased, too.
APPENDIX C: SURVEY COMPARISON
In this work, we use three different observational datasets: those
provided by Li et al. (2024), Xiang & Rix (2022), and GALAH DR4
(Buder et al. 2025). These surveys cover different regions of the
sky. For example, the Xiang & Rix (2022) sample is based on Gaia
DR3 and LAMOST DR7 data, which primarily target the Northern
hemisphere, whereas GALAH DR4 focuses mainly on the Southern
hemisphere. In Section 3.7, we adopt the Xiang & Rix (2022) dataset
for our stellar age analysis because it uses the luminosity of subgiant
stars as a precise tracer of their age. In contrast, age estimates in
GALAH DR4 are derived through Bayesian isochrone fitting and are
generally less reliable. However, the GALAH DR4 dataset includes
a broader set of elemental abundances, including Aluminium, which
we use to infer whether a star originated within the Galaxy or was
accreted from a dwarf.
