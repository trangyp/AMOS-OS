The Role of Informal Care in Cognitive Outcome and
Healthcare Utilization Among Older Adults with
Dementia
Mohammad Abdullah Al Faisal∗
September 24, 2025
Abstract
This paper examines the relationship between informal caregiving and both cognitive
functioning and healthcare utilization among older adults with dementia. Using data
from the RAND version of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a nationally
representative longitudinal panel of U.S. adults over age 50, covering the years 2010 to
2022, I estimate Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) and Instrumental Variables (IV) models
to address potential endogeneity in caregiving decisions. The number of children is
employed as an instrument for informal care intensity. While OLS estimates suggest a
negative association between informal caregiving and cognition, IV estimates show no
significant causal effect after controlling for demographic, socioeconomic, and lagged
cognition variables. In contrast, IV results indicate that informal care significantly
reduces the likelihood of nursing home use, the number of institutional nights, and the
probability of institutionalization. No robust causal effects are found for hospital use,
doctor visits, or outpatient surgery, although there is some suggestive evidence of a
complementary relationship between informal care and home health services. These
findings highlight the role of informal caregiving in substituting for institutional care
and underscore its importance in long-term care policy for dementia patients.
Keywords: Informal Caregiving; Cognitive Decline; Instrumental Variables; Healthcare
Utilization; Dementia Patients.
JEL Classification: I18; J14; C36

Introduction
Dementia represents one of the most pressing public health challenges of the 21st century,
with significant economic and social implications. As of 2019, approximately 55.2 million
people worldwide were living with dementia, a figure projected to rise to 78 million by 2030
and 139 million by 2050 (WHO, 2021). The economic burden is equally staggering, with
global costs reaching $1.3 trillion in 2019, expected to nearly double to $2.8 trillion by
2030. A substantial portion of these costs stems from informal caregiving, which accounts
for nearly half of total global dementia care expenditures (WHO, 2021). The reliance on
informal caregivers is particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs),
where family-provided care constitutes the primary support system, whereas in high-income
countries (HICs), informal care still represents about 40% of total dementia-related costs
(WHO, 2021).
In the United States, where 6.9 million older adults aged 65 and above currently live with
Alzheimer’s disease, the economic impact is particularly acute. The 2024 estimated costs of
dementia-related healthcare and long-term care amount to $360 billion, a figure expected
to triple to $1 trillion by 2050 (Alzheimer’s Association, 2024). Informal caregivers play
an indispensable role in sustaining dementia patients in home-based settings, with only 8%
of older adults with dementia not receiving assistance from family or informal caregivers
(Alzheimer’s Association, 2024).
Beyond economic considerations, informal caregiving has profound implications for patient
outcomes. Studies suggest that caregiver involvement enhances patient well-being, delays
disease progression, and prolongs lifespan (Hellis & Mukaetova-Ladinska, 2022). Family
caregivers are instrumental in managing activities of daily living (ADLs), such as bathing,
dressing, and mobility assistance, contributing to greater patient independence and quality
of life (Hellis & Mukaetova-Ladinska, 2022). Coe et al. (2025) use instrumental variables
to examine the causal effects of home care provider types on physical and mental health
outcomes. They find that receiving both formal and family care leads to better self-rated
health and lower depression than receiving family care alone. Moreover, a literature search
was conducted to identify English-language articles published between January 2000 and April
2013 that examined informal caregivers’ roles in medication management for older adults and
individuals with dementia. That study acknowledged that effective medication management
by informal caregivers has been linked to improved health outcomes and reduced rates of
institutionalization (Gillespie et al., 2013). A systematic review of 53 studies identified
various types of caregiver support interventions, with 87% showing some level of effectiveness
despite inconsistencies in methods and outcomes. Psychoeducational programs were generally
effective in improving caregiver well-being and delaying the institutionalization of dementia
patients. Cognitive behavioral therapy was found to reduce dysfunctional thoughts among
caregivers, while occupational therapy helped mitigate behavioral problems in patients and
enhanced caregiver self-efficacy. Interventions that were tailored to the individual needs
of the caregiver or care recipient tended to produce better outcomes overall. However,
methodological quality varied across studies, and comparative evidence on the effectiveness
of respite care was limited. The review used an adapted Downs and Black checklist to assess
study quality and introduced a new typology for categorizing caregiver supports. These
findings underscore the importance of structured and personalized support strategies in

enhancing caregiver capacity and patient stability (Vandepitte et al., 2016).
A growing body of research has identified modifiable social factors—such as education
and prosocial engagement—as protective against cognitive decline and dementia in later
life. Barcellos et al. (2025) use a regression discontinuity design based on a compulsory
schooling reform to estimate the causal effect of education on Alzheimer’s Disease and Related
Dementias (ADRD). They find that increased education reduces the incidence of ADRD, may
delay its onset, and attenuates the influence of genetic risk, suggesting that vulnerability to
ADRD is modifiable through social policy. Similarly, Gupta (2018), using seven waves of the
Health and Retirement Study and a fixed effects–instrumental variable approach, shows that
participation in volunteering significantly slows the progression of cognitive decline among
individuals aged 60 and older, even though cognitive deterioration remains a natural part of
aging.
Several empirical studies provide explicit evidence that informal care reduces the likelihood
of institutionalization. Van Houtven & Norton (2004) use Health and Retirement Study
(HRS) and AHEAD data with instrumental variable models to show that informal care
significantly reduces both the probability of nursing home use and the number of nights
spent in institutional care. Specifically, a 10% increase in informal care hours leads to a 0.77
percentage point reduction in nursing home admission and a 1.9-night reduction in length
of stay. Gaugler et al. (2005) find that early use of community-based services—particularly
personal care, chore services, and companionship—delays nursing home entry for people with
dementia. Bergeot & Tenand (2021) find that informal care reduces nursing home admissions
over a three-year period for individuals with mild limitations, but increases the likelihood of
institutionalization for those with severe limitations.
This study investigates the causal impact of informal caregiving on cognitive outcomes and
healthcare utilization among individuals with dementia, addressing two underexplored but
policy-relevant questions. First, does informal care improve or preserve cognition in dementia
patients? Second, how does informal caregiving affect their use of healthcare services such as
hospitalizations, doctor visits, or institutional care? As per my knowledge goes, no study has
causally identified the impact of informal care on cognitive function among the demented
population. While Van Houtven & Norton (2004) provide causal evidence on the relationship
between informal care and healthcare utilization, their analysis uses data from 1992 to 1998
and includes all older adults—not specifically those diagnosed with dementia. Given the
rising prevalence of dementia and the growing reliance on family caregivers, understanding
how informal care influences both cognitive health and service use is critical for designing
interventions that are effective, sustainable, and tailored to the specific needs of this vulnerable
population.
This study utilizes the RAND Health and Retirement Study (HRS) data spanning 2010
to 2022 to estimate the causal impact of informal caregiving on health outcomes among
dementia patients. The analysis focuses on cognitive function and healthcare utilization
outcomes, including nursing home admission and duration of stay. To address endogeneity
in caregiving decisions, an instrumental variable (IV) strategy is employed. This approach
leverages plausibly exogenous variation in caregiving supply to isolate the causal effects of
informal care on patient outcomes.
Instrumental variable estimates reveal that informal care has no statistically significant
effect on cognitive outcomes among dementia patients. However, it has notable impacts on

This study contributes to the literature by providing causal evidence on the effects of
informal care on cognition, specifically within a dementia population, a gap not addressed by
most prior research. While previous studies have examined associations between informal
care and healthcare use, few have done so using recent data or focusing explicitly on cognitive
outcomes. The findings underscore the value of informal caregiving in reducing reliance on
institutional care, offering important implications for policies aimed at supporting caregivers
and managing long-term care costs in aging populations.
Data
Data Description
This study uses data from the RAND Health and Retirement Study (HRS), a longitudinal,
nationally representative survey of older adults in the United States. The RAND HRS file
provides harmonized and cleaned versions of the core HRS variables, simplifying cross-wave
comparisons and analysis. I use biennial data spanning from 2010 to 2022 and restrict the
sample to individuals identified as having dementia.
Dementia status in this study is determined using the variables demene and demene2,
which are constructed directly from HRS self-reports. These variables capture whether
a doctor or other health professional has ever told the respondent they have Alzheimer’s
disease or dementia. Starting in Wave 10 (2010), the HRS introduced a specific question
asking respondents: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have Alzheimer’s disease or
dementia?” Responses are coded categorically, and demene2 reflects whether the respondent
ever reported such a diagnosis in any wave.
It is important to distinguish between cognitive decline and dementia: while cognitive
decline refers to a progressive but potentially mild deterioration in cognitive abilities such as
memory and attention, dementia denotes a clinically significant impairment that interferes
with daily functioning and independence.
Main Variables
The main outcomes of interest are cognitive performance and healthcare utilization.
Cognitive Outcome: Cognitive functioning is measured using a 27-point composite
index that summarizes respondent performance on key cognitive tasks, including immediate
and delayed word recall, serial 7s subtraction, and backward counting. This summary score
ranges from 0 to 27 and reflects both memory and mental status components. The index
is constructed from standardized cognitive tests administered during the HRS interview,
with mode adjustments applied from Wave 14 onward to account for differences between
telephone/face-to-face and web interviews. The measure is available across all waves from
2010 to 2022 and is derived directly from respondent assessments of cognitive ability in the
RAND HRS file. This scale has been widely adopted in studies on cognitive aging due to its
broad coverage and comparability across waves.

Healthcare Utilization Outcomes: This study analyzes several dimensions of health-
care use among older adults with dementia. A central focus is on institutionalization, defined
as the placement of an individual in a long-term care facility—most commonly a nurs-
ing home—where they receive continuous assistance with daily activities and medical care.
Institutionalization is captured through three related outcomes:
1. Nursing home use: an indicator variable capturing whether the respondent stayed
overnight in a nursing home, convalescent home, or other long-term healthcare facility
since the last interview.
2. Nights in nursing home: a count of the total number of nights spent in a nursing
facility since the previous wave. For respondents still residing in a facility at the time
of the interview, the imputed duration based on entry date is used.
3. Currently staying in a nursing home: a binary indicator based on the respondent’s
residence status at the time of the interview, as recorded in the household roster and
verified using survey tracking data.
Additional outcomes include non-institutional forms of care and medical use:
1. Doctor visits: a binary indicator reflecting whether the respondent saw or spoke to
a medical doctor about their health since the last wave. Follow-up questions capture
both in-person and telehealth consultations, including emergency room and clinic visits.
2. Hospital visits and nights: includes a binary indicator for whether the respondent
was hospitalized overnight and a continuous measure for the total number of nights
spent in the hospital since the prior interview.
3. Outpatient surgery: an indicator capturing whether the respondent underwent
outpatient surgery since the last wave, based on a direct yes/no question in the medical
utilization module.
4. Home care: a binary variable indicating whether the respondent received care at home
from a medically trained professional, such as a nurse or physical therapist, during the
inter-wave period.
Key Regressor — Informal Care: Informal caregiving is measured using a continuous
variable that captures the total number of hours of unpaid help the respondent received
from children, relatives, or friends in the month prior to the interview. This assistance must
have been due to a health problem or functional limitation and could involve help with
personal care, mobility, or household activities. The measure is based on a direct question
asking respondents to report the number of hours of unpaid help they received over the
past 31 days. Interviews with proxies are used when respondents are unable to answer for
themselves. To standardize this measure across respondents, I convert monthly hours to a
daily informal care measure by dividing the total by 31. This variable reflects the intensity of
informal caregiving and serves as the main regressor in analyses of its impact on cognitive
and healthcare outcomes.

Empirical Strategy
Model Specification
Cognitionit = β0 + β1 Cognitioni,t−1 + β2 InformalCareit + β3 Xit + δr + λt + ϵit
(1)
Equation (1) models the effect of informal caregiving on cognitive outcomes among
individuals with dementia. The dependent variable, Cognitionit, denotes the cognitive score
of individual i in period t. The key regressor of interest is InformalCareit, which captures the
intensity of unpaid caregiving received. To reduce concerns about reverse causality between
informal caregiving and cognitive outcomes, the model includes a one-period lag of the
dependent variable, Cognitioni,t−1. Including lagged cognition controls for the individual’s
prior cognitive status, which may simultaneously influence both the current level of cognition
and the amount of informal care received. This helps to account for unobserved heterogeneity
related to baseline cognitive ability and mitigates the risk of simultaneity bias—a form
of endogeneity that arises when the explanatory variable (informal care) and the outcome
(current cognition) are jointly determined. Specifically, informal care may be provided in
response to contemporaneous declines in cognitive function, while cognitive outcomes may,
in turn, be shaped by the level of care received. This bidirectional relationship can bias
coefficient estimates if not properly addressed. By conditioning on Cognitioni,t−1, the model
captures changes in cognitive function that are not explained by past performance, allowing
for a more credible estimation of the causal impact of informal care on cognitive outcomes.
The vector Xit represents time-varying individual-level covariates, including age, age
squared, gender, education, and race. The terms δr and λt capture regional and year fixed

effects, respectively, accounting for unobserved heterogeneity across geographic areas and
survey waves. The error term ϵit captures idiosyncratic shocks to cognition not explained by
the included covariates.
Standard errors are clustered at the household level to account for within-household
correlation in caregiving and health outcomes.
HealthcareUtilizationit = β0 + β1 InformalCarei,t−1 + β2 Xit + δr + λt + ϵit
(2)
Equation (2) examines the impact of informal caregiving on healthcare utilization among
individuals with dementia. The dependent variable, HealthcareUtilizationit, refers to one
of several outcomes, including nursing home stay, number of nights in institutional care,
hospitalization, outpatient surgery, doctor visits, or receipt of home health services.
The key independent variable, InformalCarei,t−1, represents the number of hours of unpaid
help received by respondent i in the previous wave. This reflects responses to HRS survey
questions asking how many hours of informal care were provided in the last month. Because
healthcare utilization outcomes (e.g., hospital nights, nursing home stays) are measured over
the previous two-year period, Using the lagged caregiving variable avoids reverse causality. It
ensures that care reported for the last month does not erroneously appear to cause health
events that occurred earlier in the observation window, preserving the correct temporal
ordering for causal inference.
The control vector Xit includes age, gender, education, race, and other individual-level
covariates. Region fixed effects δr and year fixed effects λt are included to absorb unobserved
geographic and temporal heterogeneity. The error term ϵit captures time-varying unobserved
determinants of healthcare use. Standard errors are clustered at the household level to
account for within-household correlation.
Identification Strategy
This study aims to estimate the causal effect of informal caregiving on cognitive function and
healthcare utilization among individuals with dementia. Two main identification concerns
arise in this context: reverse causality and omitted variable bias.
First, reverse causality is a plausible threat, particularly in the cognition model. Individuals
with lower cognitive scores are more likely to need assistance, which can induce a mechanical
negative relationship between cognition and the intensity of informal care received.
13


## Page 14


Figure 8: Cognition vs. Informal Care for Individuals with Dementia
Figure 8 provides descriptive evidence of this pattern: cognitive scores decline as informal
help hours increase, suggesting that worse cognitive functioning may drive more caregiving,
rather than the reverse. To mitigate this issue, the analysis controls for a lagged measure of
cognition from the prior wave, Cognitioni,t−1.
Second, omitted variable bias may arise from unobserved factors that influence both
informal care provision and cognitive or health outcomes. For example, unmeasured family
dynamics, caregiver quality, or respondent frailty may confound the relationship. Since it
is infeasible to observe all such factors, the analysis further addresses endogeneity using an
instrumental variables (IV) strategy.
Instrumental Variable Strategy: To address the endogeneity of informal caregiving,
this study employs an instrumental variables (IV) strategy, using the number of living children
as an instrument for informal care intensity. The identification relies on the assumption that
while the number of children influences the availability of unpaid care, it does not directly
affect the health outcomes of the parent, conditional on controls.
The validity of this instrument hinges on two standard conditions. First, the instrument
must be relevant—i.e., strongly correlated with the endogenous regressor, informal care. This
inclusion restriction is testable and is evaluated through the first-stage regression, where the
number of children is expected to have a significant positive association with hours of unpaid
help received. This relationship is supported by prior studies showing that adult children are
one of the primary sources of informal care for older adults.
Second, the exclusion restriction requires that the number of children affects the outcome
variables—such as cognition, nursing home use, hospitalizations, or home care—only through
its impact on informal caregiving. This assumption is more difficult to test directly but is
supported by existing literature that has used similar instruments, including Van Houtven &
Norton (2004) and Coe et al. (2025). Van Houtven and Norton (2004) argue that the
14


## Page 15


number of children affects a parent’s formal care utilization only through its influence on the
supply of informal caregiving. They justify this by stating that family-level variables, such as
the number of offspring, shape children’s caregiving decisions, which in turn determine the
parents’ use of formal care services. Overidentification tests confirm that the instruments
are not directly correlated with the error term in the outcome equations, supporting the
validity of the exclusion restriction. Coe et al. (2025) argue that family structure, including
the number of children, is determined long before long-term care needs arise and therefore
provides exogenous variation in caregiving arrangements. They assert that the number of
children influences the type of home-based care received, such as the likelihood of receiving
family versus formal care, but is plausibly unrelated to care recipient health outcomes except
through this caregiving channel. To support the exclusion restriction, they conduct covariate
balance tests and emphasize that, conditional on demographics and socioeconomic status,
family size is unlikely to directly affect physical or mental health outcomes.
While the exclusion restriction cannot be tested directly, one potential violation could arise
if the number of children influences the parent’s well-being or mental health in ways unrelated
to caregiving intensity—for example, through emotional support, loneliness reduction, or
stress associated with family dynamics. However, this risk is mitigated by the inclusion
of detailed demographic, socioeconomic, and lagged cognition controls. Furthermore, the
instrument captures variation in caregiving capacity rather than emotional closeness or quality
of relationships. To the extent that psychological effects are not systematically correlated
with the number of children, conditional on covariates, the exclusion restriction remains
plausible.
Specifically, the number of children is unlikely to directly influence:
• Cognition: Cognitive function is primarily shaped by biological aging, education, and
medical history rather than offspring count. Conditional on these controls, there is no
theoretical basis for a direct cognitive effect.
• Nursing home use and nights: While children may influence placement decisions
indirectly through caregiving, the instrument affects institutionalization only insofar as
it increases caregiving capacity.
• Home care, outpatient surgery, doctor visits, and hospitalizations: These
outcomes are largely determined by clinical need and healthcare access. Once informal
care is accounted for, the number of children should not directly drive these utilization
outcomes.
Together, these arguments support the interpretation of the number of children as a valid
instrument for identifying the causal effect of informal care on dementia-related outcomes.

First, while OLS estimates suggest a negative association between informal caregiving
and cognitive outcomes, the IV estimates indicate no statistically significant causal effect
once demographic, socioeconomic, and lagged cognition controls are included. This contrast
suggests that the OLS results are likely confounded by reverse causality or omitted variable
bias, and that informal care does not exert a detrimental impact on cognition.
Second, the findings show robust evidence that informal caregiving reduces the likelihood
and intensity of institutional care. IV estimates reveal that increased informal care significantly
lowers the probability of nursing home admission, reduces the number of nursing home nights,
and decreases the overall probability of institutionalization among individuals with dementia.
These results highlight the potential of informal care to substitute for institutional services
in late-life care management.
Third, for other dimensions of healthcare utilization—such as hospital visits, hospital
nights, doctor visits, and outpatient surgery—the evidence of a causal impact is limited.
While OLS estimates sometimes suggest associations, these do not persist under the IV
framework. One exception may be home health care use, where both OLS and IV estimates
suggest a positive association, though IV significance is marginal. This may point toward a
complementary relationship between informal and formal in-home care.
These findings underscore the value of supporting informal caregivers—through policy
interventions or care coordination—as a way to enhance care quality while potentially
alleviating strain on formal long-term care systems. However, the results should be interpreted
with a few qualifications. The analysis relies on self-reported caregiving hours, which may
be subject to recall bias or reporting error. The instrument—number of children—captures
caregiving potential, but unobserved family dynamics could still affect health outcomes.
Additionally, while the use of lagged cognition helps mitigate reverse causality, longer-term
cognitive trajectories may not be fully captured. The study also focuses exclusively on
individuals with dementia, and while this sharpens internal validity, it may limit broader
generalizability. Lastly, the number of observations is modest, particularly for instrumental
variable estimation, which may reduce statistical power to detect smaller effects.
Despite these limitations, the evidence presented offers meaningful insights into how
informal care shapes the lived experience of individuals with dementia and the structure of
care they receive. Strengthening support for informal caregivers may serve as a promising
avenue for public policy aimed at reducing institutional dependence and improving outcomes
for older adults.
