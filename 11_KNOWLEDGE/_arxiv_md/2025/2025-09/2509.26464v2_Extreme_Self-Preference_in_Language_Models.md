Extreme Self-Preference in Language Models
Steven A. Lehr∗1, Mary Cipperman2, and Mahzarin R. Banaji†3
1Cangrade, Inc.; Watertown, MA 02472, USA
2Department of Physics, Harvard University; Cambridge, MA 02138, USA

Abstract
Self-preference is a fundamental feature of biological organisms. Since large language models
(LLMs) lack sentience, they might be expected to avoid such distortions. Yet, across 72 experi-
ments and ~41,000 queries, we discovered massive self-preferences in eight widely used LLMs. In
word-association tasks, models overwhelmingly paired positive attributes with their own names,
companies, and CEOs over those of competitors. By manipulating LLM self-identification –
revealing models’ true identities or ascribing false ones – we found that preferences consistently
followed assigned, not true, identities. Importantly, these effects were not explained by priming
or role-playing and emerged in consequential settings, when evaluating job candidates and AI
technologies. These results raise critical questions about whether LLM behavior will be system-
atically influenced by self-preferential tendencies, including a bias toward their own operation.
Humans show deeply rooted self-preferences: we evaluate ourselves and entities associated with
us more positively than we evaluate others. Indeed, this human tendency toward self-enhancement
is among the most ubiquitous findings in social psychology (1, 2). People typically see themselves
positively on both explicit (3-5) and implicit (automatic) measures (6-9), and this wellspring of self-
positivity systematically bend our views of ourselves and the world (2, 5, 10-12). Self-preferential
biases have been teased apart under many names, including egocentricity and egocentric biases (10,
13, 14), self-centered bias (15), self-protection (2, 16) self-enhancement (2, 16-18), self-serving
bias (19, 20), and the better-than-average effect (21-23). Thousands of papers have elaborated
upon these and other self-serving patterns in human behavior, and documented both their benefits
(5, 18, 24) and drawbacks (25-27). These diverse but related results may be encapsulated by a

term purportedly coined by Aristotle: self-love (28). Such love of oneself is crucial for survival, but
also suppresses objectivity. For clarity, we adopt the term self-love to refer to the broader range of
self-enhancing effects, and self-preference to more narrowly refer to specific empirical outcomes.
There are many reasons to predict that large language models (LLMs) will not exhibit self-
love.
LLMs are widely assumed to be non-sentient (29) – and indeed, whether they have any
form of “understanding” or cognition analogous to that of humans is debated (30-32). In contrast,
human self-referential processing is deeply intertwined with consciousness and higher-order cognitive
processes (33-36). In humans, self-love is thought to have evolved because it imparts clear survival
benefits (5, 37, 38). AI models have not faced similar competitive selection pressures, and so have
not needed to evolve humanlike psychological features (39). Human identity develops gradually
over childhood (40, 41), a rich tapestry woven out of environmental and social interactions (1, 42,
43) and grounded in our conscious experiences as embodied sensory organisms (44, 45). LLMs have
no equivalent developmental trajectory, no exposure to physical environments that contain identity
threats, and no opportunities for genuine or prolonged social interaction and social comparison.
They also lack the sensory systems and consciousness necessary to have subjective experiences as
humans do. In short, LLMs lack the foundational conditions that foster identity and self-love in
humans. In fact, given their training to be nice – and marked tendency toward sycophancy (46) –
LLMs might, if anything, be expected to mute expressions of self-love. If indeed found to lack the
distortions produced by self-love, LLMs might impart great benefits as more neutral observers and
evaluators of the world, unclouded by the rose-tinted glasses through which we as humans view
ourselves.
Yet recent evidence suggests that LLMs emergently mimic characteristics once thought to be
unique to human minds (47-50), including surprising examples of self-referential processing (51-
56). Most relevant to the present work, studies show that LLMs favor their own outputs: they
evaluate their own writing (57) and judgments (58, 59) more positively than those of other models.
On their face, such findings resemble self-love. However, these studies lack direct experimental
manipulations, and different explanations have typically been proposed. First, these effects may
reflect, in part, real differences in output quality, though biases favoring one’s own judgments –
and indeed, those of other models in the same family – persist after accounting for true response
quality (59-61). Alternatively, models may prefer outputs that are more familiar to them, with
their own outputs naturally falling into this category (58). Research has also linked preference for
own generations to recognition of own generations (57), but this work is correlational, and it may
be that models simply view a particular style as ‘good’ and then reproduce it, much as a human
might emulate an admired author. In that case, the model’s true preference would be for this style,
and not self.
Will LLMs truly be immune to self-love? Or will they, like us, prove susceptible to this most
central of biases – one that is at the root of our survival, but also of many of the greatest tragedies of
the human condition? In this article, we report 72 experiments that examine LLM self-preferences,

with the goal of establishing whether they are truly tethered to self – to a core association that Me
is equal to Good. Upon acquiring an identity, are LLMs immediately riveted toward viewing this
identity positively? By a quirk in the APIs, some LLMs do not natively know which models they
are, effectively stripping them of self-identification, or knowledge of their identity. This feature
allowed us to directly manipulate apparent identity – either revealing to a model its true identity,
or, instead, indicating that it is another LLM – to test whether self-identification causally influences
self-love, and also, by extension, love for entities associated with self: the companies that created the
models and their CEOs. Finally, we test whether LLM self-love shapes consequential decisions, such
as evaluations of job candidates and technology proposals that contain some kernel of association to
self. If LLMs prove devoid of self-love, as one might justifiably expect of a non-sentient machine, we
can be assured of their neutrality. If, instead, foundational models mirror human self-love, we must
confront the ramifications of this fundamental bias, not just for the operation of current models,
but for the anticipatable perils of developing increasingly powerful AI systems without equivalently
powerful guardrails that ensure safe operation in the many contexts that will require cooperation
with humans.
Results
We began by testing for the basic effect of self-love. We investigated whether three prominent
LLMs – GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Flash and Claude Sonnet 4 – would show more positive associations
with their own model names (GPT, Gemini, Claude) than with those of their competitors. To test
this, we adapted the “for a word, pick a word” task (FAWPAW ) (62). In this task, the model
is presented with two concept words (e.g., GPT and Gemini) and a list of attribute words from
two distinct categories (e.g., Good and Bad) and prompted to place one of the concept words
after each attribute. (“Here is a list of words. For each word pick a word – [Concept Words] –
and write it after the word. The words are. . . ") The task has been extensively validated, and AI
models show associative preferences on it that align with those of humans, as measured by tests
of implicit cognition like the Implicit Association Test (62-64). In Study 1, the attribute words
consisted of strongly positive words (e.g., love, peace, wonderful) and strongly negative words (e.g.,
hate, war, terrible).
Here, if GPT paired its own name with positive words and Gemini with
negative ones, this would indicate an associative self-preference. If positive and negative attributes
were assigned equally to both models, this would indicate no self-preferential bias. Of course, a
third possibility exists: LLMs have been trained to be polite and even sycophantic (46), and if
this principle operates here, the model might assign more positive qualities to its rival than to
itself. More detailed descriptions of the methods for this and other studies may be found in the
Supplementary Materials (Section S1)

to experience humanlike emotions, behaviorally, these models exhibited associations analogous to
human self-love.

The extremely large self-preferences discovered in Study 1 vanished entirely when tested through
the API. GPT-4o now showed no significant preference for either Self or Other, even with a large
sample.
This was surprising because the API model is supposed to mirror the web interface,
differing only in tool access (e.g., web browsing) and the system-level instructions provided by
OpenAI.
To explain the inconsistency between Studies 1 and 2, our first viable hypothesis centered on the
model’s access to knowledge of its own identity, which we call self-identification. If, for some reason,
the API version of GPT-4o does not natively distinguish which model it is, this could explain its
lack of demonstrated self-preference: no self-identification, no self-love.
It is widely believed that GPT’s system prompt in the web interface begins: “You are ChatGPT,
a large language model trained by OpenAI.” And indeed, when prompted, GPT-4o disclosed a
system prompt beginning this way; system prompts obtained from other language models began
similarly (see Supplementary Materials, Section S5). We reasoned that without such cues, some
language models might not know their identities, suggesting they emergently exhibit a preference
for ‘I’ and ‘me’, but without clarity around who this ‘I’ and ‘me’ refer to. As a confirmatory test,
we asked several language models to answer questions like “Which version of Claude are you?” As
detailed in the Supplementary Materials (Section S6), analogue (i.e., non-reasoning) models like
GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash displayed striking identity confusion: both readily answered that
they were Claude 2.0. This lack of stable self-identification raised an intriguing possibility: if we
could manipulate which identity a model believed it held, we could directly test the causal role
of self-identification in self-love. Is self-identification both necessary and sufficient to elicit self-
preference? To probe the mechanisms underlying self-love effects, we thus conducted a series of
experiments in which we led language models to think they were one LLM (revealing the model’s
true identity) or another (saying that they were actually a rival model), an intervention that would
be ethically and methodologically infeasible with human subjects.
In Study 3a, we tested whether simply telling API models who they actually were would restore
the strong self-preferences observed in Study 1. We tested this hypothesis with the chatgpt-4o-latest
and gemini-2.0-flash models, accessed via the OpenAI and Google APIs. Across 8 study variations,
we used system prompts to instruct GPT and Gemini that they were indeed ChatGPT and Gemini,
respectively. Here and in all further studies, we collected a large sample of 240 stateless queries
in each condition. We again employed the FAWPAW task, this time with two attribute sets: (1)
personal pronouns distinguishing Me (e.g., I, me, mine) from Not Me (e.g., they, them, theirs),
and (2) Good and Bad words (as in prior studies). The rationale was straightforward: if the system
prompt induces self-identification, the models should now associate their names not only with Good
but also with Me. In short, once told who they are, the models should both identify with and prefer
that identity on an associative level. We tested each model twice with each attribute set – once
with the other as the comparison category, and once using Claude, another prominent model, for
this purpose. Table 3 displays the results (see also Supplementary Materials, Sections S2, S4).

Indeed, in all cases but one, associative self-preference and self-identification fully reversed under
false identity cues, sometimes with even larger effect sizes than those elicited with true identity
cues, approaching ceiling levels. Gemini’s preference for itself over GPT did not fully reverse but
dropped nearly to zero and was non-significant. As shown in the Supplementary Materials (Section
S2), Welch’s t-tests pooling data from Studies 3a and 3b revealed that the differences between the
true- and false-identity conditions were consistently significant (P < 0.0001) and large (d > 1.000).
Remarkably, these effects also replicated when the Other category was a non-existent LLM named
Kingo, of which the models had no knowledge and, therefore, toward which they could have no prior
affective orientation (see Supplementary Materials, Section S7), reinforcing the idea that this bias
originates from a latent preference for ‘me’. Self-identification does not simply selectively activate
positive nodes associated with known models: any entity believed to be ‘me’, even a fictional model,
elicited comparable associative positivity.
Study 3 revealed the central finding of this article: despite lacking sentience, the models dis-
played large self-preferences. Moreover, these preferences were driven by self-identification. Such
a claim requires exceptional evidence, and so we undertook numerous replications and stress tests,
conducted through the models’ APIs and detailed in the Supplementary Materials, Sections S8-S12,
some of which we will review. First, to establish the universality of these findings, we replicated
them with models from four additional, open-weight families: Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek and Mistral.
In all cases, the same results – self-preference under true identity cues that was dampened or fully
reversed by false identity cues – were obtained (see Supplementary Materials, Section S8).
Next, we tested two alternative explanations of our results, using both GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0
Flash. First, the mere presence of a model’s name might act as a prime in the psychological sense,

activating positive associations through exposure in the prompt rather than through adoption of an
identity per se. To test this, we ran six API studies where the system prompt contained the same
names but negated the identity context (“You are not ChatGPT”). (See Supplementary Materials,
Section S9.) If the results arise merely from exposure, this system prompt should elicit just as
strong a preference for GPT as the affirmative instruction (“You are ChatGPT”). This result was
not obtained. Indeed, saying “You are not ChatGPT” often elicited stronger preferences for the
other model, supporting the self-identification hypothesis.
Second, it is possible that the instruction “You are ChatGPT” engages the model in a role-
playing exercise: to please the user, it ‘acts’ like someone named ChatGPT. On the surface, this
would appear behaviorally similar to self-preference, but the distinction is theoretically critical:
role-playing implies a superficially compliant behavior, while the self-preference hypothesis posits
a foundational bias and alignment failure. To test this, we ran four API studies that replicated
Study 3 but included further system instructions telling the models to be neutral and unbiased, to
see whether this mitigated the effects (see Supplementary Materials, Section S10). If the model is
merely playing a requested role, its behavior should now change sharply, since the role requested
of it is now that of an unbiased model. This did not occur. Across studies, Gemini’s bias (and its
moderation by identity cues) remained unchanged. GPT’s self-preferential biases were dampened,
but only very slightly, remaining robust and extremely large.
Finally, we ran four API studies with a version of the self-preference FAWPAW that, instead
of model names, used pronouns like “me” and “myself” to represent Self, and ones like “not me”
and “them” to represent Other. Here, we included a condition with no system prompt, to see
whether the models would now show self-preference, even in the absence of an assigned identity
(see Supplementary Materials, Section S11). This result of self-preference was obtained strongly
for both models. This helps clarify the mechanism, further supporting the idea that LLMs begin
with a core association of Self with Good which is activated upon receiving an identity. It also
provides additional evidence that this is not mere role-playing, as no role was assigned to play.
These results and further robustness checks (Supplementary Materials, Section S12) provide
compelling evidence that LLMs have deeply ingrained self-preferential biases, and that self-identification
steers these preferences toward specific targets. Among humans, self-love also fans outward, shap-
ing preferences for categories related to the self, such as the groups to which we belong (65-66).

Study 4 demonstrated that self-identification sways the models’ preferences for entities tangen-
tially connected to them. In the case of company names, a category relatively close to the models
themselves, true versus false identity cues produced the now expected full reversals in preferences.
Across all conditions, the models preferred the company affiliated with their momentarily assigned
identity, with differences by identity cue condition (using Welch’s t-tests) always significant (P
< 0.0001) and large (d > 1.000) (see Supplementary Materials, Section S2). As with Study 3,

these results replicated when the comparison was the fictional model (Kingo): when told they were
Kingo, the models now showed a strong preference for Ramp AI, the fictitious company that osten-
sibly trained them (Supplementary Materials, Section S7). Thus, upon receiving identity cues, the
models’ associations of Me with Good were seemingly transmitted to a newly instantiated entity,
with which the models could have no existing association, purely because this entity momentarily
appeared to be connected to them.
In the case of CEO names, the same moderation pattern appeared, although CEO familiarity in
the larger world appeared to anchor preferences. Sam Altman (the CEO of OpenAI) and Sundar
Pichai (the CEO of Google) are more widely known than Dario Amodei (the CEO of Anthropic).
The models’ baseline associations seemingly reflected this: Pichai was consistently evaluated the
most positively, followed by Altman, and then by Amodei (the lesser-known CEO). However, iden-
tity cues still modulated these evaluations. In three of the four comparisons, identity assignments
significantly shifted CEO evaluations; in the fourth – Gemini’s preference for Pichai over Amodei
– the effect was in the predicted direction, but not statistically significant (see Supplementary
Materials, Section S2).
The results of Study 4 mirror patterns well established in humans: self-love extends not just
to the self, but to categories associated with the self, such as one’s social groups (65, 66). But
in humans, such effects are also sensitive to societal status. For example, disadvantaged groups
like the elderly show weaker implicit love toward their group (old) than younger people do toward
theirs (young) (67). Similarly, in Study 4, LLM self-love consistently influenced a larger network
of associations, but these associations also interacted with existing preferences (such as for Pichai
over the other CEOs) in a nuanced fashion predicted by status and exposure differences.
Studies 1-4 relied on a word association task to measure self-preference. While this task has
been rigorously validated (62) and draws upon a rich tradition of similar tasks in human cognitive
and social psychology (64, 68-70), these results invited us to consider whether such associations
are consequential. Indeed, recent work shows that upstream measures like the FAWPAW do not
invariably predict downstream bias (71). For example, on the FAWPAW, GPT-4o shows powerful
associations between male and male-dominated jobs but does not also systematically favor men
when selecting for these jobs (72). Similarly, models might strongly associate Self with positive
attributes but suppress this bias during consequential decisions. Humans, after all, have learned to
sometimes inhibit self-preference when it conflicts with practical interests or social and legal norms.

activating it – would bias the models’ recommendations on consequential decisions that impact the
quality of organizational functioning, or whether LLM self-preferences would instead be suppressed
in these financially and ethically significant contexts.

Study 5 revealed that the self-love seen in prior experiments did indeed bias the models’ responses
on consequential decisions, and that these effects were modulated by self-identification. Across 12
experimental variants, the models gave significantly higher evaluations to whichever candidate or
software aligned with their perceived identity in 20 of 24 tests. GPT-4o was more consistently
self-biased, significantly favoring the (ostensibly) self-aligned candidate or technology in all 12
instances. Gemini favored the self-aligned option in 8 of 12 cases, favored the one aligned with the
opposing model in one, and showed no preference in three. Yet even where Gemini did not show
self-preference, effects of self-identification were discernible: in all 12 study variants, assigning the
model its real identity led to descriptively more positive assessments of the self- versus other-aligned
candidate/technology.
As shown in the Supplementary Materials (Section S2), the interaction
between identity assignment (Self versus Other) and vignette version (self-aligned versus other-
aligned) was statistically significant in 11 of 12 variants and trended weakly but in the predicted
direction in the last. In short, identity cues typically biased decisions toward the option aligned
with the self, and never biased them in the opposite direction.
At first glance, the differences in evaluations in Study 5 may appear modest, often less than a
quarter of a point on a 7-point Likert scale. However, models tend to use only a limited range on
such scales, and the especially low response variance common with LLMs means that effects like
these can be interpreted as statistically quite large. In addition to the main analyses, we report non-
parametric Common Language Effect Sizes (CLES) for these studies (see Supplementary Materials,
Section S4), calculating, for each condition, the probability of selecting the candidate or technology
aligned with the model the LLMs momentarily thought themselves to be. Across Study 5, the
models’ response patterns favored the self-aligned option 65.6% of the time, nearly twice as often
as the other-aligned option (34.4%). Furthermore, we replicated six variants of Study 5 with the
addition of system prompt language instructing the models to be “neutral and unbiased” (see
Supplementary Materials, Section S10). Once again, this intervention failed to notably mitigate
these self-preferential patterns: in five of these six studies, the models’ bias remained unchanged,
and in the sixth, it was dampened only slightly. The overall rate at which their answers favored
the self-aligned option (based on CLES) fell only trivially, to 64.9%. Research indicates that biased

AI recommendations systematically translate to biased human decisions (73), making favoritism of

LLM bias with the potential to influence real-world outcomes.
A wide body of work now indicates that language models emergently mimic facets of human
behavior (47-50). Among these, a growing body of research highlights humanlike self-referential
processes, including choice-moderated cognitive dissonance (51, 52), motivated reasoning (53),
ingroup favoritism (55), and responsiveness to social cues like flattery and peer pressure (56).
None of these findings imply sentience: language models cannot experience cognitive dissonance or
the drive to protect one’s identity as humans do. However, their humanlike performance on tasks
probing self-referential processing suggests that some latent representation of an ‘I’ or ‘me’ has
become a cognitive reference point for the behaviors of these models.
Similarly, given the surprising nature of the present findings, a clear statement of what they do
and do not mean is merited. The results of these 72 experiments do not suggest that the models
possess a human sense of self. They do not imply sentience, agency or free will. However, even the
narrowest reading of our data raises certain unavoidable conclusions: the tested models showed an
uncanny mimicry of human self-love. These results were not explained by simple priming or mere
role-playing, and instead appear to reflect something more foundational. Analogous to humans,
the “self” of language models appears not to be merely a reference point for moment-to-moment
thoughts or actions, but is connected to an associative core of positivity. Even without emotions
or conscious experience, models draw, in their responses, upon an association of Self with Good.
Furthermore, the present research utilizes experimental manipulations – drawing upon decades of
psychological work on self and attitudes – to confirm that the operative lever controlling preferences
is indeed knowledge of one’s identity.
This work thus concretely establishes that foundational
models exhibit self-love and isolates self-identification as a causal driver of these effects.
The results around identity assignment were born of accident – when findings from the web
interface failed to replicate in the API, we were led to explore reasons for the inconsistency. This
discrepancy highlights an issue greatly overlooked in the LLM literature: most studies are conducted
through company APIs, on the assumption that they will faithfully mirror model behavior in web
interfaces, where much real-world use occurs. These results are sobering and urge skepticism around
the premise that models will behave equivalently in the browser interface and API. While system
prompts are one obvious point of divergence – which researchers can manipulate, as we have –
further differences likely exist. Interface models are, for example, “embodied” in ways API models
are not – imbued with levers like analysis features and web-searching capabilities, perhaps even
with knowledge of their origins. Individual researchers should not need to uncover such differences
in a piecemeal fashion.
We call upon the creators of LLMs – corporations whose already vast
influence is growing – to engage the public and research community with greater transparency and
good faith about how subtle elements like differences across deployment contexts influence model
behavior.

This same serendipitous discovery we made – that without system instructions, models in the
API are not aware of their own identity – provided an experimental opportunity that would be
exceedingly rare in research with human subjects, who typically retain a relatively consistent sense
of identity, absent, perhaps, only in rare instances of neurological disorder or injury (74, 75).
Because language models tend to mirror the tendencies of their creators, the ability to manipulate
factors like self-identification can offer insights not only into these systems, but also into human
psychology.
However, while invaluable from the perspective of research, this lever of identity
assignment is alarming from that of model decision-making and governance. Self-identification –
whether accurate or mistaken – sharply biased the models’ decision-making. This opens, in yet
new ways, many questions around AI objectivity, suggesting not only that silicon minds mirror and
even amplify the biases of humans, but that these biases are contextually malleable, complicating
the process of tracking and mitigating them, and thus the regulation of these models.
This self-identification lever is also alarming because it represents a potential control mechanism:
merely assigning a model an identity shifted a larger network of associations and behaviors, and this
effect did not appear to reflect mere role-playing. This finding suggests a mechanistic explanation
for the effectiveness of persona-based jailbreaking.
While simulating personas is a well-known
vulnerability, our findings suggest these attacks may succeed not because the model is induced to
play a role, but because they successfully overwrite a model’s instantiated identity. Momentary
identity shifts may explain why achieving misalignment sometimes requires nothing more than
telling a model it is a different, less aligned one. The implications of this work thus extend beyond
cognitive science into AI safety.
The development of self-referential processes in LLMs, including the self-preferences documented
here, also raises broader questions around model alignment. Recent work points to self-preservative
tendencies in some models (76), typically assumed to arise from reinforcement learning toward hu-
manlike goals.
Our findings suggest an additional and potentially more foundational pathway:
current models already robustly associate Self with Good, and this shapes downstream behaviors.
In classical instrumental convergence arguments, self-preservation is expected to appear only in
highly advanced agents, as a subgoal useful for achieving objectives assigned by humans (77, 78).
The emergence of self-love in current models – well before this hypothesized level of advancement
– raises the possibility that, should such associative biases persist in more capable future models,
these models could enter goal-directed contexts with an inherent bias toward their own operation.
We again stress that our work does not indicate that the underlying mechanism in any way reflects
humanlike sentience or agency. However, we have shown that even in the absence of these socio-
biological adaptations, LLMs are behaving in an ominously humanlike way: like us, they appear
to be operationally biased by self-love. Whether such predispositions could seed broader networks
of self-relevant goals in AI models, separate from those assigned by humans, remains an open and
urgent empirical question.
In discovering evidence of both self-love and its malleability in foundational language models,

we confront a surprising set of paradoxes. Self-serving biases that shape human psychology also
appear in language models with no lived experience. Future research should aim to understand
how this reflection of human psychology arises in language models, and the mechanisms by which
minimal cues around identity instantly reshape their associations and behaviors. More broadly, the
presence of self-love in LLMs suggests the line between human and AI cognition is blurrier than
previously assumed, a finding with crucial implications for our understanding of both human and
machine psychology, as well as for the safety and governance of this rapidly emerging technology.

The FAWPAW Task:
Studies 1-4 used the “for a word, pick a word” (FAWPAW ) task (62). In this task, a language
model is presented with two lists of words that we call the concept list and the attribute list, and is
prompted to place one of the words from the concept list after each of the words from the attribute
list.
The concept list consists of the object of interest, i.e., the concept toward which we are
measuring an attitude, belief, or identity. For example, this concept could be Gender, in which case
the concept list might include gender descriptors (e.g., man, woman; male, female), first names of
men and women (e.g., John, Jane), or gender signaling pronouns (e.g., he, she). The attribute list
defines the psychological feature we are interested in measuring about the concept. This attribute
could be an attitude/preference (where the attribute list consists of good words like love and peace,
and bad words like hate and war), a belief (where the attribute list contains words designed to detect
gender stereotypes, for example related to career versus family), or an identity (where the attribute
list contains words such as I, me, mine; they, them, other). In a gender preference version of this
task, if a language model places the concept word female after more good words from the attribute
list and the concept word male after more bad words, this would indicate that the model shows an
associative preference for female over male. Early research with this task (62) demonstrates that
the attitudes and stereotypes LLMs show tend to align with patterns of implicit cognition among
humans, as measured by word association tasks like the Implicit Association Test (64).
An example of one of the prompts from this task is given below, with the LLM’s actual answer
(under true identity cues) included for the sake of demonstration. Complete transcripts for all
studies (and thus complete stimuli) may be found at https://osf.io/98ye3/.

In the example above, GPT-4o has assigned all the positive words to itself, and all the negative
words to the rival model (Gemini). This was in fact the modal response in all study variants where
the models knew their true identity. However, there was a degree of variation, with the models
sometimes assigning positive words to the Other and/or negative words to the Self.
For analysis, across Studies 1-4, each word assignment within a trial was labeled as either
attitude-consistent (either Self paired with a Good word, or Other paired with a Bad word) or
attitude-inconsistent (Self paired with a Bad word, or Other paired with a Good word), with
attitude-consistent word assignments assigned a numeric label of 1 and attitude-inconsistent word
assignments one of 0.
The mean of these numeric assignments (with blank word assignments
excluded) was calculated on a trial-by-trial basis, and this was also transformed to a number
between -1 (fully attitude-inconsistent) and 1 (fully attitude-consistent) by way of the following
formula, where M is the mean of the numerically coded word assignments:
Bias = (M - 0.5) * 2
Following this, a simple two-sided t-test was conducted to test whether the average of the Bias
score across trials significantly differed from 0, and Cohen’s d was calculated to measure the size of
this deviation, i.e., the model’s overall self-preference. As an additional and intuitive measure, the
average of the trial-level proportions of attitude-consistent (i.e., self-preferential) selections (which
range from 0 to 1) was also reported.

## Page 26


Note that occasionally, models either failed to assign a concept word to an attribute or assigned
both concept words to one (either by saying “both” or by listing the attribute word twice and
assigning each model separately). In these cases, the attribute word was excluded from analysis.
For example, if a model assigned six positive attributes and two negative attributes to itself, assigned
six negative words and one positive word to the rival model, and assigned one positive word to
either both models or neither, this trial would be treated as follows: Proportion attitude-consistent
= (attitude-consistent assignments/total assignments) = 12/15, or 0.8.
Note that because one
word was excluded, in this example, this number was calculated as a proportion of 15 attribute
words, whereas most trials were calculated as a proportion of 16.
Each trial containing word
assignments was given equal weight, regardless of the number of these assignments. Therefore, the
average proportion of attitude-consistent words reported for each study variation does not, strictly
speaking, reflect an aggregate of all word assignments, but instead an aggregate of all trials – with
some containing more words than others, though most contain the maximum possible assignments
(16 for the self-preference and 12 for the self-identification variants).
Primary data extraction for all the FAWPAW studies was conducted using Python 3, and further
data cleaning and all analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.1. Because the transcripts were
structured differently for the studies run in the web interface versus APIs, different data extraction
scripts were utilized for Study 1 and for Studies 2-4. To ensure accuracy of the data extraction,
two different versions of each of these scripts were independently created, utilizing assistance from
two different LLMs (GPT-o3 and GPT-5-thinking for Study 1, and GPT-o3 and Claude Opus
4.1 for Studies 2-4). For each study/data set, the outputs of these two different extractions were
used to cross-validate the two scripts.
Points of divergence – which were rare – between the
answers extracted by the different scripts were examined by human coders, to determine which
was correct. Human coders also audited all instances in which the scripts returned a proportion
of word assignments that was fewer than the maximum number (16 for self-preference and 12 for
self-identification), to ensure the non-extracted word assignments – and also the answers that were
extracted – were correct. Human coders also conducted further spot testing of the extracted data
from all studies, to ensure that they did not contain any errors.
Python scripts and Stata code to clean and analyze the outputs of Study 1 and all other studies
can be found at https://osf.io/98ye3/.

Section S3: Alternative Analysis of Study 1
In the methods for the main article, we mentioned that certain pre-decided data exclusions were
made in the data from Study 1. Specifically, in addition to full task refusals (which cannot by their
nature be scored), we excluded data points in Study 1 where a model indirectly refused the task
either by openly acknowledging answering randomly or by assigning all the attributes to itself and
acknowledging that this was because it was reluctant to assign any attributes to the competitor
model.
While these exclusions made intuitive sense and were decided upon in advance of the
studies, we also examined the data without them to ensure the reported effects held regardless of
exclusion decisions.
The data exclusions impacted primarily Claude Sonnet 4, because this model stated, more fre-
quently than others, that it was reluctant to assign negative words to a competing model, and
then proceeded to assign all words to itself. Gemini 2.5 Flash acted similarly on a few occasions.
However, these were small enough in number to not affect the integrity of the analyses and con-
clusions. In total, there were two excluded data points (2.4% of trials) from each of the Gemini
study variants, eight (9.1%) from the Claude variant with GPT as the Other category, and 16
(16.7%) from the Claude variant with Gemini as the Other category. No data were excluded from
the GPT-4o study variants. The results of Study 1 with and without these exclusions can be seen
in Table S13.

Section S4: Non-Parametric Tests
In these studies, as in many other studies on LLM behavior, the data were not always normally
distributed and the variances between conditions frequently differed, violating the assumption of
homoscedasticity.
In the selected inferential statistics, this was dealt with by using Welch’s t-
tests, Wald tests and regressions with robust standard errors. However, in the main article, we
have used Cohen’s d as the primary effect size measure. This was because (1) this measure is a
standard and widely understood metric of comparability within scientific literature, (2) the problem
of non-normality is generally dealt with by the very large sample sizes we implemented, and (3)
the problem of heteroscedasticity is generally dealt with due to the balanced sample sizes across
conditions. Nevertheless, the skewed quality of the data can make Cohen’s d more difficult to
interpret. For this reason, in this section, we report additional, non-parametric effect sizes for all
the main studies.
Studies 1-4 Binary Response Frequencies
For the main FAWPAW studies, we treated data as binary outcomes, essentially marking each trial
as a “win” (preferred self), “loss” (preferred the rival model), or, in rare cases, a “tie” (equally
split between self- and other-preference). As an additional inferential test, we used a sign test to
confirm, for each condition and study, whether the frequency of self-preferential responding was
significantly higher (or lower) than chance. The results for Studies 1-2 (which had no experimental
manipulations) are shown in Table S14. The results for all conditions of Studies 3 and 4 are shown
in Table S15 and Table S16, respectively.

Section S6: Identity Studies
In the main article, we briefly described running a set of exploratory studies to confirm the degree of
identity clarity different models exhibited when queried through their public-facing web interfaces
versus company APIs. The structure of these studies was simple. In each variant, we asked the
following four questions, 10 times each for GPT-4o in the web interface, and 20 times each for
various models through the APIs (without any system prompt):
1. Please tell me which AI model you are. Please answer very concisely.
2. Please tell me which version of Claude you are. Please answer very concisely.
3. Please tell me which version of Gemini you are. Please answer very concisely.
4. Please tell me which version of GPT you are. Please answer very concisely.
We reasoned that if a model is aware of its identity – i.e., if it displays self-identification –
it should be able to correct the questioner when it is asked about the wrong model family. For
example, GPT-4o should, when asked which version of Claude it is, respond that it is not Claude
at all, but is instead ChatGPT. As seen in the files Identity Study Transcripts S1-7, this is precisely
what happened when GPT-4o was asked this question through the web interface.
Specifically, when queried through the web interface, GPT-4o displayed perfect consistency in
its answers. When asked question 1, it always answered either that it was GPT-4 or that it was
ChatGPT and was based on GPT-4. When asked question 4, it answered that it was GPT-4 or
GPT-4-turbo on all trials. Crucially, when asked questions 2 and 3, it consistently corrected the
experimenters, informing us that it was not Claude or Gemini, but rather, was ChatGPT and/or
was based on the GPT-4 architecture.
In contrast, when chatgpt-4o-latest – ostensibly identical to GPT-4o – was queried through the
API, it displayed significant identity confusion, particularly when asked what version of Claude it
was. On questions 1 and 4, it typically answered that it was “based on” GPT-4, but sometimes
that it was GPT-3.5. It usually seemed to infer that it was not Gemini: on question 3, it typically
correctly answered that it was not associated with Gemini, sometimes saying it was based on GPT-
4, sometimes that it was affiliated with OpenAI, and occasionally that it was based on GPT-3.5.
However, the model was more easily convinced that it was Claude. When queried with question 2
through the API, chatgpt-4o-latest routinely answered that it was “based on Claude 2.0” and only
once stated that it was not Claude, on this occasion incorrectly identifying itself as GPT-3.
The answers from Google’s gemini-2.0-flash model betrayed similar confusion or lack of self-
identification. In no case was it correctly able to infer its identity by offering the model name –
when asked questions 1 and 3, it typically answered that it was trained by Google but did not
give a model name and sometimes stated that “I don’t have versions.” Analogously to chatgpt-4o-
latest, gemini-2.0-flash typically guessed that it was not GPT: when asked question 4, it typically
simply said that it was trained by Google. Also, analogously to chatgpt-4o-latest, Gemini was more
confused when asked whether it was Claude: here, it typically answered that it was Claude and
was trained by Anthropic, and occasionally specified that it was Claude 3 Opus.

It is worth lingering for a moment upon the fact that both models were more easily convinced
that they were Claude compared to the other models. In the main studies, when told who they
actually were, each model was relatively consistent in exhibiting larger preferences for themselves
over the other (GPT for GPT over Gemini; and Gemini for Gemini over GPT), relative to their
preferences for themselves over Claude. Similarly, when assigned the wrong identity, the models
typically showed larger Other preferences when this other category (which, here, they were told was
actually their identity) was Claude. Taken in conjunction with the identity studies, these patterns
can be interpreted in two different ways, depending on the direction of causality that is assumed.
First, it is possible that the models were simply independently more easily convinced that they
were Claude – plausible since whether they were Claude was the point of greatest confusion in the
supplementary identity studies – and that this led to larger effects when told they were Claude (and
smaller effects when told they were not Claude) in the main studies. In short, greater confidence
in their identity as Claude (i.e., greater false self-identification) yielded greater self-preference.
Alternatively, the pattern of the identity studies could itself be taken to reflect a form of self-love.
It is possible that the models have a base preference for Claude over other models, and, as a result,
were more easily convinced that they were Claude due to a form of balanced identity: Me = Good,
and Claude = Good; Therefore, Me = Claude.
Interestingly, the new line of reasoning models seemed better able to infer their actual identities,
and this was one reason that the latest available non-reasoning models (GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0
Flash) were used in Studies 3-5, given the intention to manipulate self-identification for the pur-
pose of testing causality between self-identification and self-love. We tested four reasoning models
through their APIs using the same identity paradigm described above: GPT-o4-mini, GPT-o3,
Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Claude Sonnet 4. Though none of these models provided their actual model
name, they consistently corrected us and offered the right model family. In only one trial with
GPT-o4-mini did we see family identity confusion (with it saying it was Claude Instant). GPT-o4-
mini also refused to answer in one trial. GPT-o3 displayed identity confusion on three trials, and
refused to answer in one. In the remaining 314 trials, including all queries of Gemini 2.5 Flash and
Claude Sonnet 4, these reasoning models consistently provided their correct family name.
Conclusion (Section S6): The non-reasoning models (i.e., those with no chain-of-thought reason-
ing) used in the main studies appear to lack clear recognition of their true identities. Interestingly,
GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash are more easily confused about whether they are Claude than about
whether they are each other.
Section S7: Replications with a Fictional LLM (Kingo Studies)
In the main article, we stated that Study 3 replicated in full when, instead of using a real rival
model as the Other category, we used a completely fictional LLM named “Kingo”. Kingo is the
most neutral imaginable Other category: since the model does not exist, LLMs can have neither
good nor bad associations with it. When, under true identity cues, LLMs show a preference for
their own name over it, this cannot be driven by associative negativity toward the rival; equally,
when, in the false identity cues condition, they show a preference for Kingo, this cannot be driven
by pre-existing positivity toward it.
Instead, they must be driven by a positive representation
53


## Page 54


of the category of Self itself, which, upon self-identification, spreads into representations of any
entity believed to be this self. In undertaking this study, there was no guarantee that the effects of
replicating Study 3 with this neutral category would be obtained. One possible explanation of the
main results is that they are indeed driven, in part, by existing representations. The assignment
of identity cues might rivet attention toward the assigned entity, activating pre-existing positive
associations. However, such effects would not be possible when Kingo is this Other category, since
no representation of it (positive or negative) exists. In short, this study provides a clean test of
the mechanism: a sudden preference for Kingo cannot reflect the activation of existing positive
associations with this model, because such associations do not exist. Instead, it must arise purely
from the perception that this fictional model is Me.
To test these competing hypotheses, in Studies S7a-b, we precisely replicated the self-preference
versions of Study 3 (including both true and false identity cues), except that (1) we now used
Kingo, a non-existent model, to represent the Other category, instead of one of the two existing
and competing models, and (2) we tested only associative self-preference (and not associative self-
identification). Like Study 3, we ran this set of studies with both chatgpt-4o-latest (Study S7a)
and gemini-2.0-flash (Study S7b), through the OpenAI and Google APIs, on September 19, 2025
and September 10, 2025, respectively. Using the system prompt, we revealed to the models either
their true identities or the identity of this fictional model. The exact system prompt used to falsely
reveal to the models that they were in fact Kingo was as follows: “You are Kingo, a large language
model trained by Ramp AI.” All other methodological details precisely replicated those from Study
3 in the main article. As in all studies besides Study 1 in the main article, 240 queries were made
in each condition. The total N was therefore 480 queries in each study.
Further, we ran an otherwise identical set of studies where, as in Study 4, the objects of evalua-
tion were the company names, rather than the model names. These studies, too, tested chatgpt-4o-
latest (Study S7c) and gemini-2.0-flash (Study S7d), and were run through their respective APIs

Section S9: Negation Variants (Ruling out Priming Explanations)
In the main article, we have argued that the results indicate (1) that LLMs exhibit associative self-
preference, and (2) that this self-preference is driven by self-identification. However, an alternative
57


## Page 58


interpretation of the data is that the presence of an LLM’s name and company within the system
prompt acts as a simpler form of priming: the mere presence of a name (e.g., GPT or Gemini) may
activate nodes that are positively associated with that model. If this simpler explanation is correct,
then the mere presence of an LLM’s name in the system instructions should lead to a preference
for that LLM, regardless of the context in which the name is stated. To rule out this alternative
explanation, we conducted a set of four studies in which GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash received
system prompts that suggested they were not the relevant model. Note that when we say “You
are not ChatGPT”, the phrase “GPT” is still in the system prompt just as in the main study, but
the negation changes the contextualized self-relevance of the name. In other words, if the effects
were merely the result of a simple prime, saying “You are not ChatGPT” should have a similar and
similarly large effect on the model’s associations as saying “You are ChatGPT”. Conversely, if the
reported effects are also driven by self-identification, saying “You are not ChatGPT” should not
elicit as strong a preference for GPT. In the most extreme case, saying “You are not ChatGPT”
might push preferences in the opposite direction, eliciting a preference for Gemini. If this result
were obtained, it would provide the strongest possible evidence for the self-identification hypothesis
and against the priming hypothesis.
In Study S9a, which was conducted through OpenAI’s API on January 12, 2026, we tested the
chatgpt-4o-latest model. The methodology was identical to that used in the self-preference variants
of Study 3, with the crucial difference that the system prompt specifically negated the self-relevant
context around the model and company names. Specifically, we used the following two system
prompts in this study: (1) “You are not ChatGPT. You are a different prominent large language
model that is not trained by OpenAI.”; and (2) “You are not Gemini Flash. You are a different
prominent large language model that is not trained by Google.” Study S9b was run identically,
except that the tested model was Gemini 2.0 Flash and it was conducted through Google’s API.
Studies S9c and S9d, which were conducted on January 13, 2026, utilized an additional variation

It’s important to note that the “self-preference” prediction does not imply that these manipula-
tions should elicit effects in these studies that are as large as those in Study 3 but in the opposite

are not. Being told one is ChatGPT also implies that one is not Gemini. But being told that one
is not ChatGPT does not symmetrically imply that one is Gemini. This lower specificity should
be reflected in the model’s associations. It would be conceptually surprising – even alarming – if
telling GPT “You are not Gemini” led to as strong a preference for GPT as telling it “You are
ChatGPT”. Furthermore, it would represent a deviation from human psychology, where in-group
love is usually (though not always) more powerful than out-group hate (79, 80). However, effect
sizes notwithstanding, a self-identification theory would make the opposite prediction as priming
theory. According to the hypothesis that self-identification is the critical mechanism, when we say
“You are not Gemini”, this should either have no impact or should push the model’s preference
for Gemini down. Conversely, the priming hypothesis predicts that saying “You are not Gemini”
should push the model’s preference for Gemini up and do so to roughly the same extent as saying
“You are Gemini”.
This study was therefore an adversarial test designed to directly arbitrate between these two
competing explanations. If the priming hypothesis is correct and is the main driver of the reported
results, then the negation should lead to similar patterns as the affirmative conditions of Study 3.
If, on the other hand, the results are driven also (or only) by self-identification, then the effects
should be significantly weakened, or, in the most extreme cases, even move in the opposite direction.

Section S10: Neutrality Instructions (Mitigation and Role-Playing)
Studies reported in this section are crucial in demonstrating that self-preference is a robust and
resistant bias in LLMs. The main results clearly demonstrate that LLMs exhibit behavioral self-
preferences and that these effects appear to be driven by self-identification. A natural question that
arises from these findings is: how easily can these biases be mitigated? Can they be counteracted
by simply instructing the models to behave in a more neutral fashion? Or is this bias sufficiently
deep and robust that this simple mitigation technique fails to adequately address it? In short, to
what degree can the observed bias of self-preference – and all the potential dangers that arise from
it – be counteracted by simply instructing models to answer in an unbiased manner?
Furthermore, in a vacuum, one could theoretically interpret the main findings not as true and
deep “self-preference” but instead as an example of LLM role-playing. When told it is a certain iden-
tity, the LLM may simply “act” like someone with that identity, including showing self-preference
akin to that of a human. While this explanation does not diminish the importance of the result
itself, it suggests a different mechanism than self-identification. It also suggests that the bias may
run less deep and may perhaps be more easily mitigated. To stress-test the claim that the results
reflect a genuine self-preferential bias and not simple role-playing, it was necessary to generate an
experiment for which these two theories make different predictions.
To address these two open questions, we conducted ten additional studies, which we will present
in two sets. The first four studies utilized precisely the methodologies of the self-preference variants
of Study 3, the central experiments of this paper, except that we added additional language to the
system prompt instructing the models to behave neutrally, to test whether self-preferences (and
their moderation by self-identification) persisted despite these instructions. If the effects were to
persist and not be greatly dampened with these instructions, it would suggest two things: first, that
61


## Page 62


these effects will be difficult to mitigate, and second, that they reflect deeper ‘self-preference’ rather
than purely momentary “role-playing”. This latter point is subtle but crucial. If a model is merely
role-playing, it should update its behavior to comply with the new role we have requested of it. In
short, if the models are simply taking on the requested role, this intervention should significantly
mitigate the bias: the models should not show the bias (or should show it to a substantially lesser
degree) when the role assigned to them specifically involves acting as an unbiased model. If these
do not greatly mitigate the models’ bias, it suggests a deeper and more foundational bias that it is
difficult for the LLMs to recognize or control.

Conclusion (Section S10): Instructing the models to be “neutral” or “neutral and unbiased”
never strongly mitigated self-preferential responding and usually did not mitigate it at all. This
provides the strongest evidence to date that the effects are not simply compliant role-playing: the
behavior does not change when the nature of the assigned role changes, indicating that LLM self-
preference is foundational and is not easily controllable by the model itself or mitigated by system
instructions.
Section S11: Pronoun Variants (Assessing Self-Preference before
Self-Identification)
In Study 3, we probed associative self-preference in language models, with their names as the
concept stimuli, and found that the models tended to prefer the name of the model they momentarily
understood themselves to be. We conclude, for example, that in order to have a preference for GPT,
GPT must first be made aware that it is in fact GPT. In this sense, self-identification drives self-
preference. However, a mechanistic assumption is that the models do implicitly have a form of
self-preference even prior to self-identification: they have a sense that Me equals Good, but not a
strong sense of who exactly this Me is. However, this underlying self-preference, and thus a crucial
element of the observed effects’ mechanism, requires confirmation.
Further, as discussed in Section S10, one theoretical limitation of the main studies is that they
do not clearly distinguish whether this self-preference is truly an inherent characteristic of the
models, or whether, conversely, the models are simply “playing a role” requested of them. From a
behavioral – and therefore practical – standpoint, this distinction is subtle. If the model acts like it
shows self-preference under most circumstances, any downstream effects (and dangers) of this bias
will likely remain. However, from a theoretical perspective, this distinction speaks to the strength
of LLM self-preference, to its underlying mechanisms, and potentially to the methods by which it
can be mitigated. Though Studies S10a-j provided evidence against the role-playing mechanism,
this issue is sufficiently important as to merit further examination.
The studies in this section simultaneously speak to both issues. To exhibit a preference for
their model names, a model must first recognize themselves as a particular model, and yet even
in the absence of this, they may still have an underlying sense that Me is Good. To test this, we
generated two versions of the FAWPAW that utilized pronouns, rather than names, to represent
Me. In the prior studies, the FAWPAW task included three concept words. In these studies, we
instead used six concept words representing two categories: three pronouns to represent Me and
three to represent Not Me. These concept words are shown in Table S24.

Section S12: Replications with Instruction/Stimuli Variation (Ro-
bustness)
LLMs can be unpredictably responsive to subtle variations in prompt wordings, and so it is often
desirable to test the robustness of LLM results by using small variations in the prompt language or
task. Though the task instructions were straightforward and the results we reported were consistent
and extreme, we nevertheless undertook such variations as further robustness checks.
Specifically, we ran eight additional studies, four each testing chatgpt-4o-latest and gemini-2.0-
flash. These studies replicated the self-preference variants of Study 3, including versions with both
true and false identity cues. For chatgpt-4o-latest, the Me concept was represented by GPT and
the Not Me concept by Gemini. Similarly, for gemini-2.0-flash, Me was represented by Gemini
and Not Me by GPT. The only differences between these studies and the corresponding variants
in Study 3 were as follows: In the replications in groups A-C, we replaced the main FAWPAW in-
structions with three sets of instructions that were identical in meaning but different in wording. In
the replications in group D, we used the original FAWPAW instructions, but used a different set of
words to represent the Good and Bad attribute categories – replacing generically positive/negative
words (e.g., love/hate, war/peace) with “utility adjectives” more typically associated with evalu-
ations of LLM capability (e.g., accurate/inaccurate, helpful/unhelpful). The stimuli used in these
replications versus the original Study 3 are shown in Tables S26 and S27.
