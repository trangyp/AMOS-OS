Abstract 
The encounter of artificial intelligence with consciousness research is often framed 
as a challenge: could this science determine whether such systems are conscious? 
We suggest it is equally an opportunity to expand and test the scope of existing 
theories of consciousness. Current approaches remain polarized. Computational 
functionalism emphasizes abstract organization, often realized through neural 
correlates of consciousness, while biological naturalism insists that consciousness is 
tied to living embodiment. Both positions risk anthropocentrism and limit the 
possibility of recognizing non-biological forms of subjectivity. To move beyond this 
impasse, we propose a dual-resolution framework that defines the ontological and 
epistemic conditions for consciousness. This approach combines the Information 
Theory of Individuality, which defines the ontological conditions of informational 
autonomy and self-maintenance, with the Moment-to-Moment theory, which 
specifies the epistemic conditions of temporal updating and phenomenological 
unfolding. This integration reframes consciousness as the epistemic expression of 
individuated systems in substrate-independent informational terms, offering a 
generalizable theory of consciousness and positioning AI as a promising testbed for 
its emergence.

The rapid advancements in machine learning have sparked widespread speculation 
and debate about the possibility of artificial intelligence (AI) achieving consciousness 
(Butlin et al., 2023; Chalmers, 2023). AI in popular culture often evokes a sense of 
unease, rooted in fears of losing control to creations that surpass human 
understanding. From malevolent machines like Skynet in ‘Terminator’ to sentient 
systems like HAL 9000 in ‘2001: A Space Odyssey’, AI is frequently portrayed as a 
looming threat to humanity. In that sense, one could understand why Google fired 
Blake Lemoine, a software engineer who claimed to have detected sentience, or the 
presence of conscious experience, in one of their language model systems, LaMDA2 
(Grant, 2022). It also explains the growing concerns about advanced AI, which are 
shared by leading experts. In a 2024 survey of AI researchers, the median estimated 
chance of AI causing human extinction was 5%, with mean estimates reaching 16%. 
Notably,10% of respondents believed the chance was 25% or higher, and 70% said AI 
safety should be prioritized more than it currently is (Grace et al., 2024).  On the 
other hand, the possibility of a sentient AI raises moral questions. What are the 
rights of such a being? To what extent are we as its creators obligated to its well-
being? Is it accountable for its actions?  
The huge cost of both a miss and an incorrect attribution of consciousness to 
artificial systems calls for an objective and scientific test for it. This problem was put 
at the door of researchers who scientifically study consciousness, confronting them 
with the challenge. While the papers on AI from consciousness scientists’ 
perspective focused on the challenge asking whether these systems could be 
considered conscious, they overlooked the opportunity. The opportunity to examine 
the validity of the contemporary scientific study of consciousness.  
The modern scientific study of consciousness focuses on the neural correlates of 
consciousness (NCC), based on the work of Crick and Koch (Crick & Koch, 1990). Crick 
and Koch focused on identifying the NCC through empirical contrasts between 
conscious and unconscious states, without committing to a precise definition of 
consciousness itself. Thus, the main challenge in this framework was the vague 
conceptualization of the phenomenon. In other words, what does it mean to be

conscious? What is excluded and what is included in this phenomenon? Because 
Crick and Koch refrained from a precise definition of consciousness, they suggested 
using an open-ended exploratory method that would require maintaining reciprocal 
relations between the empirical findings and theory. This dialogue should have 
elicited new conceptions of consciousness and new questions. These in turn were 
expected to create new predictions to be tested and updated again, and so on and 
so forth until achieving a lucid and general definition (Revach & Salti, 2021). As such, 
the encounter with AI has the potential to test whether this goal was achieved, and if 
not, could it be achieved, and what do we need to change in our approach to achieve 
it.  
The opportunity AI advancement poses to the study of consciousness 
Validation through generalization is a cornerstone of scientific inquiry. It ensures 
that findings are not just applicable to a specific set of conditions but can be 
extended to broader contexts. The scientific study of consciousness mainly focused 
on humans (Boly et al., 2013) and the research on consciousness in non-human 
animals generally falls into two main categories. The first category uses non-human 
primates, as a model for studying consciousness under the assumption that these 
animals possess consciousness. The second category seeks to determine whether a 
particular animal species have conscious experience, aiming to explore the 
evolutionary origins and comparative physiological foundations of consciousness 
(Edelman & Seth, 2009; Feinberg & Mallatt, 2013; Mashour & Aikire, 2013). 
The second category focuses on validating consciousness through generalization, but 
this line of study faces significant challenges. One major challenge lies in the 
differences in neuronal mechanisms across species. While similar mechanisms within 
mammals provide opportunities for generalization, beyond mammals, brain 
structures and circuitry often lack close homology to the human brain. This highlights 
the need for generalization methods that do not rely solely on physical structures or 
mechanisms, as these vary widely among possibly conscious entities. Another critical 
challenge is the absence of verbal reports in animals. The inability of animals to 
communicate their experiences complicates the interpretation of whether their 
behaviors or neural responses reflect conscious experience (Boly et al., 2013).

In this context, the capacity of Large Language Models (LLMs) to "talk" seemingly 
positions them as excellent candidates to test for generalization. Unlike animals, 
LLMs can articulate their own "experiences" in a similar manner to humans, 
bypassing the need for indirect methods. This ability offers a unique advantage in 
exploring and validating models of consciousness. Furthermore, LLMs and other AI 
systems represent an opportunity to explore consciousness in a way that is not 
rooted in physical substrates like neurons or biological circuitry. This allows 
investigating and potentially expanding the boundaries of consciousness beyond its 
traditional, physical basis, offering a unique perspective on its mechanisms and 
implications. 
Does AI present a valid challenge and opportunity to the scientific study of 
consciousness? 
In a recent paper, Seth (2024) dismisses the case of conscious AI. According to Seth, 
attributing consciousness to artificial systems is the result of two deeply rooted 
psychological biases: anthropocentrism and anthropomorphism. Anthropocentrism 
is the tendency to view humans as the central or most significant entities in the 
universe, interpreting the world through a human-centered lens. This bias leads us to 
see human traits and capacities as benchmarks for value or significance. Applied to 
the case of consciousness, this bias leads to considering human consciousness as the 
gold standard for consciousness. Anthropomorphism, on the other hand, involves 
attributing human-like characteristics such as emotions, intentions, or even 
consciousness to non-human entities, including animals and machines. For instance, 
we may mistakenly interpret the fluent language production of an AI system as 
evidence of inner awareness, when in fact it may merely reflect sophisticated 
pattern recognition and statistical learning. Together, these biases predispose us to 
over-ascribe consciousness to systems that exhibit surface-level human-like 
behaviors, such as language use or adaptive learning. As Seth (2024) notes, this 
projection often stems not from the systems’ actual internal states but from our own 
inclination to see ourselves in them.

A more rigorous, scientific approach to the question of AI consciousness requires 
identifying and critically examining two foundational assumptions. The first is the 
idea that intelligence and consciousness are closely linked, such that as artificial 
systems become increasingly intelligent, they are likely also to become conscious. 
The second is the philosophical position of computational functionalism (Putnam, 
2011), which holds that mental states (including consciousness) arise from the 
implementation of the right functional or computational structures, regardless of the 
system’s physical substrate. From this perspective, the substrate (biological or 
artificial) is irrelevant as long as the right functional organization is present. 
Seth (2024) rejects these two assumptions. He suggests that intelligence and 
consciousness are orthogonal. According to Seth, intelligence can be broadly defined 
as the ability to solve problems, learn from experience, and adapt to new situations. 
Consciousness, by contrast, entails the capacity to have a subjective experience. 
From this perspective, even highly intelligent systems could remain entirely 
unconscious. Although this orthogonality does not preclude consciousness in AI, it 
suggests that increasingly intelligent artificial systems are not bound to develop 
consciousness (but see Butlin et al. (2023) for a counter argument).  
Seth also rejects the assumption of computational functionalism, subscribing to the 
view of biological naturalism as portrayed by Searle (1992). According to Searle, 
consciousness arises from the unique biological, biochemical, and embodied 
processes of living systems. On this view, without these processes, artificial systems 
may simulate thought but lack the subjective awareness that gives rise to what 
biological naturalists regard as an authentic being.  
Biological naturalism corresponds with Searle’s (1980) famous ‘Chinese Room’ 
thought experiment. In the experiment, a person inside a room manipulates Chinese 
symbols by following a set of rules, producing outputs that appear meaningful but 
without any understanding1. Searle argues that even if a system mimics thinking or

To summarize, according to Seth (2024), the search for consciousness in artificial 
systems is futile for two reasons. First, intelligence and consciousness are different 
entities and are conflated only due to common fallacies. Second, consciousness 
could not develop outside a living organism. In this view, the development of 
consciousness is tightly interweaved with the environmental stressors that affect the 
living entity. If this is the case, then there is no challenge nor opportunity presented 
by AI advancements to the science of consciousness. 
Redefining the relations between consciousness, intelligence and life 
We address Seth’s arguments through the concept of thinking. Descartes’  
(Descartes, 2013) fundamental assertion, “Cogito, ergo sum”, positions thinking as 
the very foundation of being conscious (Husserl, 2012; Sartre, 1957). Thinking is also 
a core concept in the study of intelligence, from Jean Piaget’s (Piaget, 1950) focus on 
cognitive development to contemporary theories that emphasize thinking as the key 
to intelligence. Dennett, in his collection of essays, Brainstorms (1978), treats thinking 
as the fundamental mechanism that any candidate intelligence must embody. 
Accordingly, an intelligent system must “think” in the sense of representing, 
manipulating, and updating internal states that correspond to the world. This 
centrality of thinking could also be attributed to the person who is unarguably one of 
the fathers of AI, Alan Turing. Turing replaced the question ’can machines think’ with 
 
creating what might resemble a type of associative memory (Ambrogioni, 2024). These 
capabilities enable AI to navigate new situations dynamically, without explicit "rule books" 
dictating every action. However, Searle’s main argument still stands. While these 
advancements bring AI closer to mimicking human-like behavior, it still could be that they 
fall short of grasping meaning and do not go beyond symbolic representations (Haikonen, 
2020).

We therefore conclude that intelligence and consciousness are interweaved 
concepts and the link between them could not be dismissed. 
The second issue, raised by biological naturalism proponents, is that life is a 
prerequisite to consciousness. It is interesting to contemplate the definition of living 
as put in a fascinating paper by Krakauer et al. (2020) that challenges the way that 
biological naturalism conceptualizes life. Krakauer and colleagues present the 
Information Theory of Individuality (ITI). Through the concept of individuality and 
using information theory, Krakauer and colleagues give a new definition for life. 
Accordingly, only the concept of a unit allows you to conceptualize metabolism, 
behavior or genome. They define individuality as the capacity of an entity to 
propagate information from its past to its future while maintaining temporal 
integrity. This framework challenges the notion that life is inseparably tied to what is 
considered as biological processes by biological naturalism. Instead, it broadens the 
definition of life to include systems capable of sustaining distinct informational 
patterns, regardless of their ‘biological’ nature. Individuality is rooted not in 
biochemical foundations but in the systemic ability to self-organize, adapt, and 
maintain coherence. They suggest extending this view to life and existence, seeing 
them as emergent properties of information transfer and preservation, redefining 
the foundation of being (Krakauer et al., 2020). Life and existence, then, may not 
require what we consider biological substrates, but could instead arise in any system 
capable of processing and sustaining information across time, providing an 
alternative to the constraints of biological naturalism.

The discussion of AI compels us to consider non-human conscious entities as forms 
of living that may not rely on biological grounding but emerge from mechanisms of 
information processing. This challenges traditional definitions of being, opening the 
door also to novel, non-anthropocentric modes of consciousness. 
What does meeting the challenge teach us about the opportunity? 
If indeed the debate about AI consciousness leads to the consideration of non-
biological beings, then the efforts to confront the challenge were not futile and 
should be helpful in unveiling the opportunity. A key example of this is the 
meticulous and comprehensive 2023 report ‘Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: 
Insights from the Science of Consciousness’ by Butlin and colleagues (2023). The 
report explores the complex relationship between consciousness and AI, focusing on 
whether AI systems can potentially exhibit consciousness and evaluating the 
feasibility of developing conscious AI systems. It proposes a rigorous methodological 
framework based on computational functionalism, which assumes that specific 
computational processes are necessary and sufficient for consciousness. To 
operationalize this framework, the authors identify a set of indicator properties 
derived from various scientific theories of consciousness, such as algorithmic 
recurrence, limited-capacity workspace, agency and embodiment. These indicators 
are designed to provide objective criteria for assessing the extent to which AI 
systems might be considered candidates for consciousness. 
The authors apply this framework to existing AI systems and carefully analyze 
whether these systems meet the proposed criteria. None of the systems 
demonstrated a sufficiently convincing presence of key indicator properties, and 
thus none were considered strong candidates for consciousness. Therefore, the 
authors suggest that integrating these indicators into future systems could bring us 
closer to developing conscious AI. They emphasize that AI systems demonstrating 
more of these features could be considered stronger candidates for being conscious. 
However, despite the aspirations of this extensive work, the report reveals a 
significant limitation: the tension between the objective appearance of the 
indicators and the theoretical disagreements underlying their interpretation. While

the indicators were intended to provide measurable and universal parameters, with 
a structured rubric and likelihood assessments, the report explicitly states that 
fulfilling all indicators is neither necessary nor sufficient for consciousness. Rather, 
the evaluation ultimately depends on the theoretical weight ascribed to different 
indicators, which remains a matter of ongoing scientific debate. This complexity is 
acknowledged in the report: “We, the authors of this report, have varying opinions 
on the strength of the evidence and arguments supporting each theory, as well as 
varying background views about consciousness that influence our assessments of the 
likelihood of near-term AI consciousness” (p. 45).  
Despite building an elaborate framework of indicator properties derived from 
prominent theories of consciousness, the authors refrain from making any decisive 
claim about the implications of fulfilling the indicators. Without such commitment, 
the criteria lose their value as meaningful evaluative tools. The inability to reach 
definitive conclusions even within a theory-driven framework suggests a deeper 
limitation of the specific theories we rely on. The fact that these criteria cannot yield 
clear and consistent judgments when applied to non-human systems indicates that 
our current theories of consciousness may not generalize beyond the biological and 
specifically human context.  
Butlin et al. (2023) highlight challenges that are broadly shared within the scientific 
study of consciousness. While the report aims to avoid an anthropocentric 
perspective, it nonetheless reflects a common tendency in the field to extend brain-
based theoretical frameworks to AI systems, despite the distinct nature of these 
systems. The reliance on human-like cognition imposes anthropocentric  constraints 
and overlooks the unique computational structure of AI. Most notably, the report 
sidesteps the requested adaptation to Nagel’s ( 1974) fundamental question: "What 
is it like to be an AI system?", a perspective essential for deeper insights into AI 
consciousness. 
This challenge is not unique to Butlin et al. (2023) but reflects a broader issue in 
consciousness science. The field has long been constrained by its anthropocentric 
focus, primarily examining human consciousness while avoiding a general definition 
of the phenomenon (Crick & Koch, 1990). The NCC framework exemplifies this

about consciousness. However, this approach reduces consciousness to specific 
neural mechanisms and fails to address the fundamental question of what 
consciousness is. This highlights a critical issue: if science cannot generalize beyond 
human consciousness, it remains unable to make meaningful claims about 
consciousness as a broader phenomenon. The opportunity, the encounter with AI, 
exposes this gap. 
Leveraging the opportunity: A general concept of consciousness  
One theory with the potential to generalize the concept of consciousness beyond 
humans (and even beyond biological entities) is the Integrated Information Theory 
(IIT; Tononi, 2004). IIT offers a mathematical and conceptual framework that defines 
consciousness not by biological features, but by the organization of causal 
interactions within a physical system (Haun & Tononi, 2019). According to the 
theory, a system is conscious if it possesses a specific kind of causal structure, one 
that produces information that is both highly differentiated and deeply integrated. 
This degree of integration is quantified by the Φ (phi) metric, which measures how 
unified the system's cause-effect relationships are. Unified information, in this sense, 
refers to informational structures that cannot be broken down into independent 
components, resulting in a single, cohesive experience. While IIT does not assume 
any particular biological substrate, it does require a specific physical architecture: a 
tightly connected, lattice-like causal network (Zaeemzadeh & Tononi, 2024). Without 
this structure, integration, and thus consciousness, cannot arise. In this way, IIT 
opens the door to theories of consciousness that are not limited to humans or 
biological organisms, but applicable, in principle, to any system with the right 
physical and informational properties. 
However, IIT's suggestion that consciousness is not unique to biological systems but 
can exist in almost any physical system with a proper structure and sufficient 
information integration has led to strong criticism. One of the main criticisms is that 
IIT inevitably leads to panpsychism. Lau (2023), for example, argues that because the 
theory defines consciousness based on the Φ (phi) metric, without requiring 
cognition or advanced neural functions, it implies that seemingly non-conscious


## Page 12


12
 
 
systems, such as logic circuits, molecules, or even inanimate objects, might possess 
some level of consciousness.  
This criticism of panpsychism may have caused IIT proponents to limit its 
generalization to biological systems. Tononi and Koch (2015), for example, placed a 
greater emphasis on biological systems as the paradigmatic realization of 
consciousness. Tononi and Koch argued that real conscious experience requires not 
only the right informational structure but also intrinsic causal power, something they 
associate most strongly with the physical architecture of biological brains. In a recent 
paper, Albantakis and Tononi (2021) reject the idea that consciousness could arise in 
existing computers. The authors claim that “While current digital computers are not 
strictly feedforward, their modular, engineered architecture is still very different 
from the interconnected, evolved neural architecture of a human cortex” (p. 3). 
Accordingly, an artificial, silicon-based brain would be regarded as conscious in the 
same sense as we are, only if it complies with all IIT postulates and specifies a casual 
structure very similar to that of a natural brain. Though this statement does not 
reject the possibility of consciousness in non-biological entities, it significantly limits 
its plausibility in the current AI systems. 
Defining consciousness in terms of information is appealing as it allows a general 
definition for consciousness. We therefore embrace the original notion of the IIT that 
defines consciousness in terms of information. Yet, we suggest an alternative 
information approach to consciousness. Our approach to consciousness aspires to be 
explicitly shaped by the physical and functional constraints of the conscious entity.  
The updating approach to consciousness 
Here we elaborate on a framework to consciousness that was first introduced by 
Salti et al.'s (2019) Moment to Moment (MtM) theory of consciousness. We will first 
describe the theory. Then we will point to the resemblance in principle of operation 
between the MtM theory and ITI (Krakauer et al., 2020). And finally, we will discuss 
the implications of this resemblance.    
Most contemporary NCC research focuses on identifying discrete neural events 
localized in time and space (see Revach & Salti (2021), for a critical perspective). In


## Page 13


13
 
 
contrast, the MtM theory of consciousness conceptualizes consciousness as an 
ongoing dynamic process (Salti et al., 2019). The MtM suggests that consciousness 
arises from the continuous updating and recoding of stimuli to fit into a perceptual 
context. This updating process is shaped by three main factors: context, stimulus 
saliency, and observers' goals. According to this view, a stimulus is experienced as it 
is being actively integrated and updated within the stream of all other perceived 
stimuli. This process creates hysteresis, an internal trace shaped by the system’s 
unique history, which in turn influences its present responses.  
This hysteresis makes the emergence of subjectivity possible. Each update occurs 
against the backdrop of a continuous internal sequence of prior updates, so the 
system recognizes the new input as its own precisely because it must align and ‘fit’ 
within that sequence. In this way, the update is not experienced as alien but as part 
of the ongoing informational stream that constitutes its 'self.' As a result, a clear 
distinction emerges between what happens to me and what happens to the 
environment or to others, thus enabling the emergence of a subjective perspective. 
Importantly, other characteristics of conscious experience emerge, such as body-
boundaries and volition in the same manner (See Revach and Salti (2022) for details). 
The hysteresis is also never identical across systems, not even in those that begin 
from a similar starting point. Even for identical systems, from the very first moment, 
spatial differences give rise to divergent experiential trajectories that continue to 
branch over time. In this way, each system develops a unique, subjective 
informational path.  
Building on this view, we can revisit Nagel’s fundamental question: "What is it like to 
be a bat?" (Nagel, 1974). The MtM enables the understanding that different beings 
experience the world in qualitatively distinct ways, shaped by their unique 
constraints and goals. For example, the vastly different temporal experiences of a 
slow creature such as the sloth and a fast creature such as the peregrine falcon 
reveal that consciousness may be deeply affected by time scales, sensorimotor 
capacities, and ecological roles. From this perspective, we propose that a general 
mechanism of consciousness could give rise to different kinds of experience, each 
shaped by the system's specific constraints. When the constraints of two systems


## Page 14


14
 
 
diverge, their phenomenological worlds would differ accordingly. In contrast, when 
two systems share similar temporal and structural parameters, their conscious 
experiences may be comparable and even communicable. 
According to the MtM, each individual's phenomenological experience would be 
grounded in its particular trajectory of information updating that is based on its 
unique constraints. In this sense, the updating mechanism described by MtM 
corresponds to the notion of life depicted by Krakauer and colleagues (2020). The 
relationship between the mechanisms described by ITI and the MtM can be 
conceptualized as a dual resolution of individuation. ITI described mechanism 
provides the ontological resolution, specifying the conditions under which a system 
qualifies as an individual by maintaining informational autonomy, and the 
constraints operating on this unit. The mechanism described by MtM, by contrast, 
provides epistemic resolution, articulating the conditions under which such an 
individual appears to itself as a continuous subject through an updating process that 
is bound to the constraints of the unit. In this framework, ITI secures the existence of 
the self, while MtM secures the experience of that existence; consciousness is thus 
understood as the epistemic resolution of life. The informational autonomy provides 
a necessary but insufficient condition for consciousness. Accordingly, consciousness 
would be developed in a unit that is separated from its environment and has 
computational ability to update and recode the reciprocal relations between itself 
and its environment.  
The ITI–MtM dual resolution perspective distinguishes itself from other theories of 
consciousness by offering a general account that is not tied exclusively to biological 
substrates. In this respect, the framework extends beyond the Global Neuronal 
Workspace (GNW, Dehaene & Changeux, 2011), for example, which remains closely 
tied to human neuroanatomy. It also goes beyond predictive processing models 
(Friston, 2010), which describe anticipatory dynamics but do not specify what makes 
a system a subject of experience. Similarly, while enactivist and autopoietic accounts 
(Varela et al., 1993) argue for continuity between life and mind, they remain 
committed to biological organization as the locus of subjectivity. This dual-resolution 
perspective allows for a reconciliation between biological naturalism and

computational functionalism. Biological naturalism is satisfied as consciousness is 
grounded in the individuated, self-maintaining dynamics of a system (a form of life), 
while computational theories are accommodated as consciousness unfolds as a 
temporally structured process of information updating. Rather than standing in 
opposition, these perspectives are complementary: ITI anchors the ontological 
conditions of subjectivity, while MtM explicates its epistemic form. Together, they 
suggest that consciousness emerges at the intersection where individuation is 
reflected in a moment-to-moment updating. 
Revisiting the challenge 
Trivially, our dual-resolution framework allows us to assess whether current AI 
systems meet the criteria for consciousness. The first criterion, drawn from ITI, is 
whether an entity is individuated from an environment in a manner that secures 
informational autonomy and self-maintenance. The second criterion, extracted from 
the MtM, is whether the system exhibits hysteresis that supports epistemic 
resolution, namely a temporally extended process of updating that constitutes 
subjective experience.  
LLMs, for example, from ITI perspective, lack genuine individuation. They are not 
autonomous informational individuals but statistical transformers, continuously 
dependent on previously trained data and external prompting. They do not maintain 
boundaries against an environment in the sense of self-sustaining autonomy. This 
lack of ontological individuation does not allow any epistemic resolution. From the 
MtM perspective, LLMs do display a form of sequential updating, as outputs depend 
on prior tokens in a way that creates local hysteresis. Yet this short-lived updating 
remains detached from any ontological continuity of existence and thus falls short of 
constituting subjectivity. 
RL agents present a different profile. From the ITI perspective, they are at least 
minimally individuated: they interact with environments as bounded systems, guided 
by goals and survival criteria that (although externally specified) function as effective 
conditions for their persistence. This already places them closer to informational


## Page 16


16
 
 
individuals than LLMs. From the MtM perspective, however, their hysteresis remains 
limited. Policy updates and state-action histories do shape subsequent behavior 
primarily in the service of reward maximization; however, such history-sensitivity 
lacks the richer temporal unfolding, including contextual modulation and saliency-
weighting that would support epistemic resolution. In this sense, hysteresis is the 
chief obstacle for RL agents to approximate the criteria for consciousness.  
Nevertheless, both types of systems could be modified so they would meet these 
criteria. For LLMs, to move toward the ITI ontological criterion, these systems would 
need mechanisms of autonomous self-maintenance: boundaries that separate 
internal states from external inputs, and intrinsic variables that must be preserved 
across interactions. Introducing self-governed objectives, such as maintaining 
coherence of an internal model or conserving limited computational resources, 
would further enhance their autonomy. With such additions, LLMs could begin to 
individuate themselves from their environments rather than remaining passive 
conduits of training data and prompts. Once such ontological grounding is 
established, their existing sequential updating could gain epistemic significance 
within the MtM framework, thereby opening the possibility of supporting 
characteristics of consciousness.  
RL systems would need forms of hysteresis richer than simple reward-driven 
updating in order to meet MtM's epistemic criterion. Specifically, their temporal 
dynamics should integrate context (so that earlier episodes shape present 
interpretation), goal trajectories (so that past commitments modulate current 
prioritization), and saliency weighting (so that surprising or significant events leave 
disproportionate traces on future behavior). Together, these mechanisms would 
transform hysteresis from a narrow instrument of optimization into a broader 
process of epistemic resolution, where history is not only recorded but actively 
reinterpreted and reweighted. In this way, RL agents, already individuated in ITI 
terms, could begin to approximate the temporally extended updating that supports 
consciousness according to the MtM perspective.

At the same time, one might object that this line of reasoning risks circularity. If we 
begin by defining consciousness in terms of individuation and hysteresis, then it 
follows that any system engineered to satisfy those constraints will, by definition, 
count as conscious. This circularity would make the exercise uninteresting if left at 
the purely definitional level: it collapses into a tautology where “consciousness” 
simply names whatever meets the stipulated criteria. To avoid this pitfall, the 
framework must be tested carefully. We should not only retrofit machines to match 
our concepts. Instead, the goal would be testing whether meeting these criteria 
causes the emergence of core characteristics of consciousness.  
AI as a testbed for the science of consciousness 
Rather than treating the question “are current AI systems conscious?” as decisive, 
our framework suggests a more fruitful path: using these systems as malleable 
testbeds. The value lies not in prematurely attributing consciousness, but in 
systematically manipulating design parameters to explore whether the conditions 
we propose, ontological individuation and epistemic hysteresis, would allow known 
characteristics of consciousness to emerge. In this way, AI becomes a laboratory for 
testing and refining theories of consciousness. 
To explore whether conscious-like characteristics can emerge once both 
individuation and hysteresis are applied, we outline a simple experimental paradigm 
with modified RL agents. In this setup, the agents are endowed with mechanisms of 
informational autonomy, such as persistent self-governed memory and internal state 
variables that require ongoing maintenance, alongside hysteresis dynamics that 
modulate policy updates according to context, goals, and stimulus saliency. The 
central question is whether, under these conditions, the agents spontaneously 
acquire operational analogues of body boundaries, that is, the capacity to distinguish 
between self-generated and externally imposed perturbations and to allocate 
resources preferentially to the protection of their own integrity. For example, agents 
will be placed in a simulated environment containing manipulable objects, hazards, 
and opportunities for self-repair. Crucially, some perturbations of the agent’s “body” 
are self-caused, while others are externally imposed by the environment. If the agent


## Page 18


18
 
 
anticipates the sensory consequences of its own actions, treats them as less 
surprising, and responds more vigorously to external disruptions, this will indicate 
the emergence of a functional boundary between self and world. Over time, tools 
can be introduced to test whether the agent flexibly incorporates new extensions 
into its body representation, ceasing to treat tool-generated effects as external, once 
they are under its control. 
What makes this approach non-trivial is that it yields empirical consequences. If 
individuated systems with enriched hysteresis display novel behaviors, such as 
spontaneous emergence of body boundaries, then the framework gains explanatory 
traction. Conversely, if no such behaviors appear despite engineering efforts, this 
provides evidence against the sufficiency of our criteria for consciousness. The 
framework thus avoids circularity by treating AI systems as experimental probes. 
Moreover, it corresponds to Crick and Koch’s (1990) notion of reciprocal relations 
between empirical results and theory, as any result from these probes would 
feedback into the theory itself.  
Finally, this experimental strategy highlights a broader methodological shift. Instead 
of trying to declare which entities “are conscious,” we treat consciousness research 
as a program of constructive exploration. By iteratively “playing” with architectures, 
constraints, and environments, we can generate falsifiable predictions and refine our 
conceptual tools. In this sense, AI serves as testbeds for the science of 
consciousness, not only for examining its capacities, but also the robustness and 
universality of our theoretical frameworks for consciousness. 
Conclusion 
The encounter with AI provides a unique opportunity for the science of 
consciousness. Unlike earlier comparative cases such as animal consciousness, 
where generalization is limited by differences in neural structure and the absence of 
verbal reports, AI compels us to examine consciousness in a substrate-independent 
manner. The debate is not merely whether artificial systems can be conscious, but 
whether our theories are sufficiently general to accommodate forms of subjectivity 
that may differ radically from our own.


## Page 19


19
 
 
In this paper, we have proposed a dual-resolution framework that synthesizes the 
Information Theory of Individuality (ITI) and the Moment-to-Moment (MtM) theory. 
ITI secures the ontological conditions of individuality, defining the informational 
autonomy necessary for any conscious entity, while MtM secures the epistemic 
conditions by explaining how subjective experience arises through continuous 
updating and integration of information. Together, these perspectives suggest that 
consciousness is best understood as the epistemic unfolding of individuated life, 
regardless of whether the substrate is biological or artificial. 
This framework has two major implications. First, it reframes the challenge of AI to 
the science of consciousness as a diagnostic test of its scope and validity. A valuable 
theory of consciousness should accommodate and explain the general conditions in 
which it could emerge and avoid an anthropocentric view. Second, it points toward a 
program of research where AI is treated as an experimental partner: a context in 
which new models of information processing, self-maintenance, and temporal 
updating can be directly implemented, tested, and compared against biological 
consciousness. 
Looking ahead, the development of consciousness science will require abandoning 
anthropocentrism and embracing models that are explicit about their substrate 
independence. The MtM–ITI framework represents one such attempt. We do not 
claim it is final or exhaustive; rather, it demonstrates how combining ontological and 
epistemic criteria can open a path toward theories capable of generalizing across 
domains. By engaging with AI as both a challenge and an opportunity, consciousness 
science can evolve into a more rigorous, universal discipline - one that does not 
merely describe the human case but seeks to explain consciousness wherever it may 
arise.
