Yuhan Cheng ∗Yanchu Liu † Heyang Zhou ‡
Abstract
We leverage the capacity of large language models such as Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT)
in constructing factor models for Chinese futures markets. We successfully obtain 40 factors to design
single-factor and multi-factor portfolios through long-short and long-only strategies, conducting back-
tests during the in-sample and out-of-sample period.
Comprehensive empirical analysis reveals that
GPT-generated factors deliver remarkable Sharpe ratios and annualized returns while maintaining ac-
ceptable maximum drawdowns. Notably, the GPT-based factor models also achieve signiﬁcant alphas
over the IPCA benchmark. Moreover, these factors demonstrate signiﬁcant performance across extensive
robustness tests, particularly excelling after the cutoﬀdate of GPT’s training data.
Keywords: Large language models, Chinese futures markets, factor analysis, zero-shot learning

Introduction
Risk factor models are fundamental tools in ﬁnance, designed to explain investment returns and evaluate
risk.
These models distill the complexities of capital markets into key factors that drive asset returns
across various regions (Sharpe, 1964; Fama and French, 1992; Carhart, 1997; Fama and French, 2015; Fama
and French, 2018; Hou et al., 2019). Over the years, technological advancements—particularly in machine
learning and artiﬁcial intelligence—have transformed factor modeling by uncovering deep, non-linear patterns
in vast datasets that were previously inaccessible (Kelly and Xiu, 2023). These breakthroughs have enhanced
the agility and predictive accuracy of models. A cutting-edge development in ﬁnance is the application
of large language models (LLMs), such as GPT series model, which are increasingly adept at analyzing
unstructured data sources like news articles and social media. This capability enables more granular insights
into market sentiment, contributing to the development of more powerful and predictive factor models. The
adoption of LLMs underscores the growing convergence of ﬁnance and advanced computational techniques,
continually reshaping investment strategies and risk management.
Despite these advancements, the Chinese futures market—a key representative of emerging markets—faces
unique challenges in factor research. These challenges stem from two main facts: ﬁrst, the potential diﬀer-
ences in pricing rules and factor systems between the Chinese and U.S. markets, as validated by research
in equity markets showing diﬀerent factor relevances (Liu et al.,2019); and second, the limited availability
of data in China, which makes it diﬃcult for data-driven models like deep learning to perform eﬀectively.

Literature Review
2.1
Financial Factor Models
The use of risk factors in ﬁnancial markets traces back to the 1960s, originating from the groundbreaking
contributions of the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) introduced by Sharpe (1964), where risk was
exclusively linked to the market factor. This concept was further developed by Fama and French (1992),
who added two more factors to the model, establishing the renowned Fama-French three-factor model, a
foundational pillar in the ﬁeld of quantitative ﬁnance. Carhart (1997) added momentum as a fourth factor
to the Fama-French model, which captures the tendency of stocks that have performed well in the past
to continue performing well in the near future. Continuing to the Fama-French model, Fama and French
(2015) further reﬁned their approach by introducing two additional factors, thus creating the Fama-French
ﬁve-factor model. Meanwhile, Hou et al. (2015) proposed the q-factor model, which incorporates market,
size, investment, and proﬁtability factors. In a related eﬀort to assess and rank asset pricing models, Fama
and French (2018) explored the maximum squared Sharpe ratio as a metric for evaluating factor models,
considering both nested and non-nested frameworks, including a six-factor extension of the Fama-French
model that incorporates momentum. Building on these advancements, Barillas and Shanken (2018) compared
multiple asset pricing models, demonstrating the importance of momentum, value, and proﬁtability factors
updated monthly.
Stambaugh and Yuan (2016) introduced mispricing factors that aggregate anomalies
to improve explanatory power, while Daniel et al. (2019) proposed behavioral factors capturing investor
psychology at short and long horizons, outperforming traditional models in explaining return anomalies.
More recently, Hou et al. (2020) enhanced the q-factor model with an expected growth factor, improving its
explanatory power and outperforming the Fama–French six-factor model.

Machine Learning in Financial Markets
This rapidly developing approach in asset pricing heavily utilizes machine learning to analyze complex,
high-dimensional data, signiﬁcantly expanding beyond traditional revenue metrics. The method, as explored
by Gu et al. (2020), employs sophisticated algorithms such as random forests and deep learning, addressing
challenges like multicollinearity and enhancing the modeling of stock returns using diverse company charac-
teristics. In a comprehensive review, Kelly and Xiu (2023) highlight key advancements and futures directions
in machine learning applications within ﬁnancial markets. Focusing on conditional factor models, Light et al.
(2017) and Freyberger et al. (2020) utilized sophisticated techniques like Partial Least Squares (PLS) and
LASSO to address statistical complexities involving numerous predictors. Kozak et al. (2018) demonstrated
that a select group of principal components from 15 unique portfolios could eﬀectively assess these portfolios’
values, though resulting in negligible alpha. Moreover, Murray et al. (2024) applied machine learning to val-
idate the eﬃcient market hypothesis by predicting stock returns based on historical data, which accurately
forecasted future stock returns across a variety of markets, yet the model showed limitations with certain
asset types. The recent introduction of a novel Feature Set Data-Driven Search framework (FETCH) by Li
et al. (2023b) and a novel automated feature generation method (OpenFE) by Zhang et al. (2023b) further
exempliﬁes the ongoing innovation in machine learning within the ﬁnancial sector. These tools extend the
capabilities of machine learning systems in feature generation and selection, achieving performance on par
with expert-level ﬁnancial analysts. Additionally, the reinforcement learning-based model proposed by Yu
et al. (2023) represents a signiﬁcant shift towards more adaptive and performance-optimized factor creation
strategies, moving away from traditional methods to a more dynamic and tailored approach.

2.4
Factors in Futures Markets
In the realm of commodity futures markets, factor models have been instrumental in understanding and
predicting price behaviors. Jagannathan (1985) extended the ICAPM to test futures prices for corn, wheat,
and soybeans, providing valuable parameter estimates despite rejecting the model. Cortazar and Naranjo
(2006) employed an N-factor Gaussian model using all available price information, demonstrating strong
performance across oil and copper futures. Initial models, such as those proposed by Cortazar and Schwartz
(2003), have been extended to incorporate features like convenience yields and spot price volatility, tailoring
models to speciﬁc commodities, as seen in studies like Shiraya and Takahashi (2012) on oil. Westerlund et al.
(2015) supported the Eﬃcient Futures Market Hypothesis through a factor analytical approach, enhancing
test power with panel data. Chong et al. (2017) identiﬁed liquidity as a critical pricing factor in commodity
futures, while Heath (2019) highlighted the dynamic relationship between oil futures and economic activity.
In recent years, researchers have explored various extensions and applications of these models. Kang and
Kwon (2017) investigated the predictability of commodity futures momentum, while Han and Kong (2022)
identiﬁed a trend factor that outperformed traditional momentum factors. Recent work has compared two-
factor and three-factor models, reﬁning them to better reﬂect market realities and the interplay of market

Methodology
This section outlines our methodology for generating and evaluating investment factors derived from
GPT. We ﬁrst describe the process of auto-generating novel factors using GPT and then assess their ﬁnancial
performance through a series of backtests. To evaluate the practical utility of these factors, we construct
portfolios based on each generated factor and measure key performance indicators including annualized
return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. These metrics serve as the foundation for assessing risk-
adjusted proﬁtability.
For benchmarking, we also compare the alpha generation ability of these factors
against the IPCA model proposed by Kelly (2017), thereby situating our ﬁndings within a broader empirical
ﬁnance context.
3.1
GPT’s Auto-factor Generation
We utilize GPT as an artiﬁcial ﬁnancial analyst to generate novel, multi-dimensional ﬁnancial factors
to predict future stock returns, balancing generalizability and computational eﬃciency. To avoid misin-
terpretations from GPT’s text-based outputs or mathematical notations, we restrict its access to our data
architecture framework, excluding substantive data, and extract the computational algorithm directly from
the model Cheng and Tang (2024). GPT is tasked with deﬁning a computational framework based on its
understanding of dataset column labels and their meanings, comprising sequential cross-sectional data. It
proposes a complex function, f, to dynamically model the factor magnitude vector across all futures at time
t, leveraging market nuances at and before t. A thorough manual review ensures the factor’s uniqueness and
validity, preventing duplication of existing models and advancing ﬁnancial research with innovative AI-driven
analytical tools.
Our methodological approach involves guiding GPT step-by-step to systematically develop the compu-
tational blueprint for an innovative ﬁnancial factor. This structured process begins with careful prompt
engineering, explicitly instructing GPT to assume the role of a ﬁnancial expert, thus aligning its analytical
perspective closely with the intricacies of ﬁnancial analysis. Next, we comprehensively brief GPT on the
speciﬁc task of factor generation, clearly communicating the nature of the problem, the desired outcome,

3.2
Construction of Diﬀerent Factor Portfolios
In constructing and evaluating factor-based portfolios, we begin with a single-factor model employing two
investment strategies: a long-short and a long-only approach. Futures are ranked daily by their factor values;
the long-short strategy takes long positions in the top 10% and short positions in the bottom 10%, while the
long-only strategy focuses solely on the top decile. Building upon this, we extend to multi-factor portfolio
construction using both static and dynamic strategies as outlined in Cheng and Tang (2024). The static
approach determines the polarity of each factor—positive or negative—based on its historical long-short
returns. These polarities guide the aggregation of standardized factor signals across multiple dimensions,
yielding a composite score for each asset. This methodology enables a ﬂexible yet principled integration of
multiple predictive signals in a uniﬁed portfolio framework.
To validate the robustness and eﬀectiveness of factors, we divide the time series data of each factor into two
segments. The ﬁrst segment is used to construct the long-short portfolio, and its performance, particularly
the annualized return rate, is employed to determine the sign (positive or negative) of the factor. This
sign assignment is critical as it indicates whether the factor predicts futures performance in alignment with
market movements. The second segment of the data is then used to build the actual investment portfolios
to be evaluated, which focuses on generating performance metrics. By separating the dataset into two parts
for diﬀerent purposes—model building and performance evaluation—we ensure that our analysis is robust
and that the factor’s eﬀectiveness in real-world scenarios is accurately assessed. This methodology not only
highlights the predictability of the generated factors but also establishes a systematic approach to testing
their practical applicability in real market.
On the other hand, the dynamic method involves daily adjustments to the polarity of factor values
based on cumulative historical returns. This approach requires recalculating the daily factor values with the
historical return-based sign, incorporating real-time data to reﬁne the portfolio continuously. Subsequently,

In this section, we delve into the intricacies of the factors generated by GPT and the datasets utilized in
our study. By elucidating the underlying mechanisms and the breadth of data employed, we aim to provide
a comprehensive understanding of the parameters that inﬂuence GPT’s outputs. This analysis will lay the
groundwork for a nuanced exploration of the model’s performance and capabilities.
Harnessing the advanced computational capabilities of GPT, we generated a diverse array of factors,
ultimately identifying 40 that seamlessly ﬁt into our coding architecture. Each factor underwent a thorough
evaluation process to verify its compatibility and relevance within our analytical framework, which ensured
that the factors were robust and applicable across a range of market scenarios.
For the following empirical analysis, we collected daily market data for 104 leading and secondary con-
tinuous contracts, including all stock index futures and liquid commodity futures in China, from the Wind
ﬁnancial database. The data included variables such as basis, spot, futures premium and discount, open, high,

the models’ predictive capabilities under various market conditions. Elementary information for all futures

(5) Analytical Objectivity: While developing factors, maintaining analytical objectivity is crucial, which
means testing for Statistical Signiﬁcance of the indicators and validation against historical data to check the

(6) Continuous Evaluation and Iteration: Market conditions evolve, and so should the factors. Continuous

dynamics.
