Introduction
1.1
To Write or Not To Write
With the awakening of AI, writing an article like the current one has become a challenge. For
example, Google Gemini or Microsoft Copilot can now compose a reasonable version quite swiftly,
and virtually on any subject.
The challenge is to genuinely convince ourselves, the Human Intelligence (HI), that we can write
something truly special, more exquisite, better predictive, and hence more rewarding to the audience
than AI could offer.
Such confidence starts to drain supersonically when we face reality, especially when AI has
become the dearest darling on the red carpet of capitalism. It is heart-wrenching to acknowledge
even the slightest hint that AI has been gradually winning over HI, and to forfeit the very pride of
HI that has been meticulously sowed since the Stone Ages by our caveman ancestors.
After wrestling with our own intelligence this way back and forth, however, we do come to
rediscover a precious jewel almost completely lost in this social-media era. That is, “Aha, at least
we are able to harvest joy through writing!”
To develop a paper or article is a conscious effort of organizing and browsing through one’s ex-
periences, linking disconnected observations, playing with various thought experiments, and finally
arriving at a coherent piece of creation. Once composed with sufficient devotion, such an article
is no different from a decent pop song or a piece of classical music, when it comes to the joy and
satisfaction that reward the inner world of the author(s).
An AI agent can also respond with a text string like “Aha, I am very proud of my answer.” But
only God knows whether it truly tastes the sweetness of that “pride,” or it is merely an algorithmic
parrot that is able to replicate an emotional phrase.
Once this unique article is written and published on any archive site, it will be instantly fed
into the incessantly crawling AI and absorbed into AI’s next response. In this sense, it is hopeless
that HI will ever be able to retain our superiority over AI. But for now, we should at least happily
pacify ourselves - “Thank you, Mr. AI. The joy is still mine, though.”
1.2
The Yin and Yang of GenAI
Unlike AI answering, HI writing is always based on the limited and often specialized personal
exposure to a phenomenon or a field of concern.
Therefore, we should first seriously warn the
readers that biases, flaws or limitations are inevitable in this writing.
Most content here results from the author’s limited working experience in both TradFi (i.e.,
traditional finance) and DeFi (i.e., decentralized finance - mainly on cryptos), as well as via various
meetings, conferences, and thematic dialogues with colleagues of similar interests, in both industry
and academia.
We will cover several emerging applications of generative AI (GenAI) in the financial industry,
and mainly focus on the tremendous potential of large language models (LLM) in modern corporate
and office environments. These applications may signify a dawning era of AI, as well as a profound
shake-up to the inertia of conventional working styles since the very beginning of the industrial
revolutions.
Numerous use cases or thought experiments will be constructed and discussed in
Section 2.
From internal project management, trading, sales to more general client services, all such emerg-
ing applications open new doors to unprecedented efficiency and productivity in modern corpora-
tions. Meanwhile, the universal entanglement of Yin and Yang (i.e., risks and rewards) commands
us to also stay vigilant for the associated risks and controls. This constitutes the second main theme

Opportunities of LLM-Based GenAI
In this section, we explore several major use cases where LLM-based GenAI could profoundly impact
the financial industry. Some of them are universal in the office working environment, though.
2.1
Summarization and Consolidation
2.1.1
GenAI-Assisted Meeting Minutes
Before COVID-19, most corporate meetings were conducted over desk phones. Ever since, they
have been hosted over multimedia platforms such as the Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet,
just to name a few.
Unlike the physical desk phones, which cannot be moved arbitrarily, these applications (Apps)
allow participation from anywhere using the Internet. Paid corporate accounts are often equipped
with even more meeting features. As a result, these Apps have substantially boosted the meeting
culture in both industry and academia.
Corporate meetings differ significantly from academic ones. In academia, there are mainly two
major types: small-scale meetings like a department seminar and large-scale ones such as the annual
events of Joint Mathematical Meeting (JMM) or Joint Statistical Meeting (JSM). Novel research
discoveries are disseminated through these meetings, and participants are inspired to make potential
advancements in their own projects.
For academic meetings, there are no hard deadlines or follow-up requirements, e.g., nothing
like “Dr.
Jackie Shen - please advance your project “ABCDEFG” by utilizing this methodology
“UVWXYZ” as revealed in this meeting by September 4th, 2026.” Indeed, most scientific advance-
ments could not be possibly prescheduled. This is the key difference from corporate meetings.
On corporate floors, meetings are forums where projects are formally kicked off, stakeholders
and responsibilities clearly assigned, tasks properly divided and to be conquered, progress tightly
tracked and deadlines constantly monitored. Many meetings may also involve stakeholders from all
the three Lines of Defense, e.g., Traders, Sales, Analysts, Engineers, Compliance, Legal, Risks, and
Internal Auditors.
As a result, meeting minutes become critical to track the progress, identify next immediate
deliverables, highlight short-term obstacles and solutions, and keep all teams on the same page.
They are important for both meeting participants and those who are required but have missed it.
In some occasions, they even serve as auditable evidence for regulators and auditors.
A meeting mediator or mediating team typically takes quick notes during a meeting, and after-
ward reorganizes them into meaningful meeting minutes or memos. Team members also crosscheck
notes to identify any noteworthy discussion points. In the post-COVID online meeting rooms, a
mediator also needs to demonstrate the PPTs, PDFs or other materials. As a result, it could be a
daunting task for a mediator to track all major talking points in real time, esp. in a multi-team
and fast-paced environment.
4


## Page 5


Most modern meeting Apps allow automated transcribing, esp. in professional or corporate
versions. Once this function is enabled, an entire meeting rolls out like text streaming, which is the
most ideal feed for LLM-based GenAI Apps such as OpenAI ChatGPT, Google Gemini or Microsoft
Copilot, only to name a few. A meeting minutes memo can then be generated in just seconds.
The first raw draft by such an AI assistant may not read perfectly, e.g., containing hallucinations
or missing critical points that may have appeared statistically insignificant. But more often, it is a
reasonable version and a meeting mediator can easily finalize it with minimum retouches.
Even when a meeting cannot be recorded or automatically transcribed for some regulatory or
legal reason, LLM-based GenAI can still consolidate the handwritten notes of a single mediator or
several members of a mediating team into a reasonable meeting memo.
This use case may not be the most impressive application of GenAI. It does help illustrate
that many indispensable office activities can be handed over to AI. One after another, such novel
applications can profoundly impact floor operations and cultures, and quietly revamp all modern
corporations.
2.1.2
Thematic Learning and Consolidation
A team is the smallest functional unit on corporate floors. Analogous to the cells and tissues in
multicellular biology, members and their teams are created for specific tasks and deliverables. Col-
lectively, they weave the very business texture of a corporation, and their efficiency and productivity
directly define its reputation and prestige.
For a given team, its cumulative knowledge could be scattered among multiple sources, e.g.,
• various working documents (e.g., in PDF, Word, or PPT) stored in shared team folders,
• internal corporate Wikipages, or the popular Confluence pages (owned by Atlassian),
• emails and other communication channels such as Microsoft Teams or Slack chats,
• standardized policies and procedures that are owned by a team, and
• internally public business information and documentation within the entire firm.
Team members may come and go, but this knowledge reservoir keeps accumulating throughout the
years thanks to business continuity.
Herein, LLM-based conversational GenAI may find two major use cases:
(A) generic thematic knowledge extraction for the purpose of learning, and
(B) specific thematic consolidation for the purpose of uplifting existing processes.
Case (A) is more for the new joiners of a team, or incumbents who want to learn in-depth about
a specific area within the team’s mandates.
Teams often have some formal onboarding packages prepared for new joiners, e.g., information
on where and how to get various authorizations or accesses, or best practices on working with
shared code libraries. Beyond these sources, a new joiner has to bug their teammates for guidance
on day-to-day floor works. Awkward moments may arise when the incumbents are under immense
pressure to resolve much more urgent trading issues.
LLM-based GenAI can then become a most comforting mentor to new joiners. For example, in
order to onboard her first client properly, a new joiner may type in a prompt like:

“Please summarize all the key approval steps required to engage a new client,”
or in another occasion when a new joiner seeks a more private guidance:
“Based on the published HR guidance, please summarize the best practices for ap-
proaching my manager for the annual performance review.”
Such knowledge seeking efforts are usually not limited to just new joiners. When a team member
named John leaves a firm and an incumbent named Alice is asked to fill in John’s coverage, Alice
has to learn some novel subjects. Thanks to all the documents and materials previously deposited in
the team’s shared folders by John, as well as all the group emails shared via the team’s distribution
list, Alice could send a prompt like this one to a GenAI App:
“Summarize the stakeholders and steps involved to report a trading incident.”
Case (B) is similar but has a distinct mission in mind - to uplift or overhaul a specific process
that has already been implemented over the years.
Consider for example, the “Kill Switch” procedure (KSP) existing at all electronic or algorithmic
trading desks. It is the most critical control for modern automated trading. Whenever some erratic
trading behaviors emerge and defy any localized correcting efforts, the KSP is likely to be activated.
Unsurprisingly, it is also dubbed the “Panic Button.”
Within a typical trading or investment firm, when manual trading is gradually converted to
more efficient automated trading, the associated KSPs are often implemented on demand desk by
desk. Over the years, we may be able to piece together the following key components or activities.
(i) Initially, the KSPs may have been implemented solely by the trading desks as best practices,
without any independent supervision or validation from other Lines of Defenses (LoD) such
as Risk, Compliance or Internal Audit.
(ii) Due to variations in coding languages (e.g., C++, Java or Python) and order management
systems (OMS), the KSPs may take different forms, e.g., with a consolidated single button on
an OMS GUI or by logging into a Unix/Linux box and typing in specific commands, etc.
(iii) Since a KSP materially impacts a trading desk, it is critical to specify who can authorize.
(iv) After a KSP is activated and trading is halted, it is critical to immediately investigate and
remediate the root issues. Proper assignment of these responsibilities becomes pressing.
(v) For robustness, there also should be a designated person or team who can verify the effective-
ness of the remediation actions and officially close the incident.
Over the years and across numerous asset classes and desks, a firm may end up with several
dozen KS Procedures. Each of them may be implemented and documented differently by individual
desks to cover the aforementioned key elements.
At some point, both regulators (e.g., UK PRA via SS5/18 and European ESMA via MiFID II
- RTS 6) and internal policies may require these voluntary KSPs to be implemented more formally
according to a firmwide standard. This is where we can potentially benefit from LLM-based GenAI.
For example, one could prompt a GenAI App as follows:

“Based on all 42 Kill-Switch Procedures (KSP) stored in folder “\etrading\KSP-Docs\”, please
create a more standardized template file for documenting KSP.”
Or less ambitiously compared with requesting a full template,
“Based on all 42 Kill-Switch Procedures (KSP) stored in folder “\etrading\KSP-Docs\”, please
summarize the key components of a typical KSP in no more than 10 bullet points.”

Activate a KSP, (b) Activating Steps of a KSP, (c) Issue Investigation, (d) Issue Remediation,
(e) Trading Resuming and Authorization, and (f) Roles and Responsibilities.”
In this hypothetical use case, an LLM-based GenAI App (e.g., Microsoft Copilot or Google
Gemini) could substantially alleviate the pain of digesting all 42 KSP documents. For example,
when a compliance officer attempts to create a firmwide policy for implementing a uniform KSP
standard, she would only need a very light retouch on the raw outputs from GenAI.
This gives a taste of the sweetness of GenAI - a task that traditionally requires a week or longer
can be completed in just a few hours or less.
2.2
Structuring the Unstructured
Throughout history, human intelligence (HI) has minted other imaginary forms of intelligence to
partially alleviate her own loneliness and limitations, among which are the Greek Gods and God-
desses from ancient times, and Master Yoda in the Hollywood blockbuster “Star Wars,” for example.
Although endowed with supernatural powers that defy the laws of space and time, they are still
handcuffed by the basic frameworks of HI. They are fake and not very helpful in terms of HI’s
self-examination and improvement.
AI is different. It suddenly becomes real after decades of persistent trials, although still in its
infancy. For the first time in human history, HI finds a tangible companion and a much-needed
mirror. AI helps HI identify her blind spots and enlighten many previously subconscious realms.
One blind spot in a modern society is the nature of “office work” that most people have taken for
granted every morning. A major chunk of a typical employee’s work is to gather and process different
sources of information and data, digest and organize them and take necessary actions. Processing
and digestion could consume a significant amount of time, mainly due to the unstructured nature
of most files and data.
Communication or documentation is traditionally carried out via natural languages, which are
sequential flows of symbols and thoughts. These flows are not conveniently structured, as contrary
to the modern protocol of JSON formatting, i.e., key-value pairing and nesting.
To be concrete, here are several common examples in investment banks, in which employees have
to devote a substantial amount of their “office work” to correctly deciphering various unstructured
information.
(i) A multi-billion family office investor may approach the Delta-One or Index-Swap desk with
an email or Bloomberg chat like the following:

2.3
Unstructuring the Structured
In the opposite direction, LLM-based GenAI could potentially unleash the true power of various
structured products or services that investment banks have been offering for decades. They can now
be connected to their clients in a more human-friendly and unstructured manner.
Since the full dawning of the internet era about three decades ago, two universal structured
services have become the industrial norm to effectuate successful interactions with clients and cus-
tomers - GUIs on the front end and relational databases on the back end.
(A) GUIs - Graphic User Interfaces. From a dashboard interface on a website (after login) to
a modern App handy on mobile devices, GUIs are designed by engineering teams for specific
needs in business or customer/client services.

Figure 1: LLM-based GenAI can substantially shorten the parsing and processing time (from days
or hours to minutes or seconds) for most unstructured financial documents. This can drastically
increase both efficiency and capacity of various departments and desks, e.g., loans, credits, legal,
equity research, etc. It ushers in the newest industrial revolution for the financial industry.
(B) Relational Databases. These backend databases store both static information of customers
as well as their dynamic business interactions with a bank or service provider.
They are
the equivalents of time and space or worldlines in modern physics, and hence fundamental in
understanding the evolving business landscapes and dynamics. For decades, they have been
recorded in the form of tables and interconnected within relational databases such as MySQL.
While the combination of the two has successfully boosted the internet-based economy in the
past few decades, they have also handcuffed their clients and customers in the following sense.
(a) A GUI is pre-designed by a service provider, and cannot handle any other customer requests
that have not yet been structured into the GUI functionalities. Many novel customer requests
cannot be expressed via a “canned” GUI.
(b) Relational Databases are highly structured via pre-formatted tables and not conversational.
They must rely on GUIs or pre-designed software to be processed and exposed.
Consider a more tangible example using Yahoo! Finance. (Throughout the current work, I have
intentionally avoided the specific settings of any particular investment banks.) Both the website
GUI and the App GUI (in Android or Apple) have a fixed format of revealing stock information
or analytics. They are pre-designed by the engineering teams at Yahoo! Finance, using the market
data amassed on the back end.
Suppose a creative retail investor is curious about how the stock “TSLA” fairs just before its
earnings days. Instead of all the information passively fed to her by the Yahoo! Finance GUIs, she
actually wants to know the following.
“Tell me the average 10-day return of TSLA from 10 business days before, to the day just before
its earnings day, for the most recent 6 earnings days.”
Another trader may be interested in the 5-day returns instead, or an observation window right after
the earning days, etc. No conventional and static GUIs can handle all such unstructured requests on

So far, we have been focusing on some of the key functionalities or capabilities that LLM-based

to various functional agents and more sophisticated agentic networks.
We could identify each agent with a persona. On the corporate floors, for example, an agent
could assume the character of
(a) a mailman - handling incoming or outgoing emails to specific customers or clients,
(b) a junior team member - organizing and documenting a team’s daily floor activities,
(c) an HR recruiter- coordinating and communicating with teams and their candidates, or

(d) a customer supporting member - addressing all incoming requests via emails or chats.
The potential is almost unbounded and has also stirred up nervous apprehension among Main Street
folks - “Please show mercy and keep some roles to humans!” (We will leave this sensitive subject to
economists, social scientists or even philosophers, who should be able to illuminate this very karma.)
Like human employees, these agents have to be orchestrated and integrated in order to execute
complex objectives. This gives rise to agentic networks or workflows. An automated workflow can
consist of
• either purely AI agents
• or a hybrid of AI agents and more conventional APIs or software components.
Communication and coordination among agents and data contexts can be facilitated by emerging
frameworks such as the Model Context Protocol (MCP). Like the HTTP protocol, such standards
make possible seamless interactions among different components, much like the client-server duality
so that client-side requests are always picked up and responded by server-side “listeners.”
Such agentic networks may be represented by graphs with nodes and directed edges. Then the
following structure naturally emerges, as inspired by the notion of parent and child nodes.
• Parent Agents. A parent agent is an upstream agent that orchestrates a number of child
agents to achieve a specific task.
• Child Agents. A child agent is relative to a given parent agent, and receives and acts on
instructions from the parent.
Each child agent can also become a parent of some grandchild agents, and vice versa. Furthermore,
the connectivity does not need to be 1-to-N, resulting in Super Networks (of agents) as compared
with the more rudimentary networks of simple neurons.
Inspired by human employees, the parent and child agents are often referred to as the “manager”
agents and “worker” agents in some radio/TV programs or podcasts.
Let us explore two use cases to better understand these concepts.

risks of LLM-based GenAI and also propose the corresponding remediation measures.
3.1
Hallucination - Risks of Fabricated Outputs
Hallucination refers to LLM-based GenAI generating fabricated statements that do not reflect the
reality of the world. When acted upon by users, they may lead to erroneous consequences.

First, we point out that hallucination is not so pathological as it may have sound.
• Hallucination is somewhat innate to LLM models that are based on statistics and distributions.
LLM models are not just passive organizers of existing facts or statements. Their impressive
power lies exactly in the generalization capabilities so that new narratives can be created.
Once a model is learned, it samples the next possible word or token given what has been
presented. A sample could happen to be real or have been observed, but more often it is
novel.
The financial world is not unfamiliar with hallucination. For instance, in Monte-Carlo sim-
ulations, hundreds of thousands of daily stock price paths are simulated for pricing complex
path-dependent derivatives using stochastic differential equations (SDEs). Almost none of
these paths will turn out to be real (say, in the coming 12 months). They are all hallucinated
fabrications, but good enough to serve their purpose.
• For human intelligence, hallucination also represents a major source of creativity. When an
imaginative piece is presented as real, it often stimulates ingenuity and innovations. From
tele-speaking, tele-listening, to even tele-transporting in ancient mythologies, the hallucina-
tory stories have transcended space and time, and inspired modern technologies of wireless
networks, cell phones, quantum communications, etc.
Let us observe perhaps a more palpable and heartening example in life. A young mom, Jacklyn
King, asks her kindergarten daughter Emily -
Mom: “Emily, tell me who is the greatest of the world: Newton, Einstein, or Elon Musk?”
Daughter: “Mama, it’s YOU!”
What a hallucination to the rest of the world ! But does anyone dare to challenge Emily?!
This alleviates the common anxiety about GenAI’s hallucination. For business, however, mis-
taken or false statements do lead to erroneous and even disastrous consequences, and must be
properly reined in. Below are some risky examples due to inadvertent hallucinations.
(a) Via Bloomberg chat, a client requests to buy the stock with ticker “APLE” (for the public
firm “Apple Hospitality REIT, Inc”). But the GenAI agent hallucinates it as to buy “Apple
Inc.” (that manufactures iPhones) whose ticker is “AAPL.”

(C) Regression Tests. For formatted or structured outputs as discussed in Subsection 2.2,

(e.g., actual trading requests of all the recent years) and their expected outputs.

whether the concern over family connection is valid or not, this GenAI agent is accessing
the private chat channel between ABCDEF and UVWXYZ (e.g., within Slack or Microsoft
Teams), and inadvertently exposing such confidential discussion to a generic team member.
These may seem to be thought experiments, but really contain the common grains of inadvertent
breaching of data privacy or information barriers. The following control measures may mitigate this
specific risk.
(A) Localized Data/Files Access for Users. For 3rd-party commercial GenAI Apps such
as Microsoft Copilot, always identify a user’s identity via Microsoft account, company ID
or Kerberos, etc, and then strictly limit the GenAI App to only accessing the data, files,
emails, or chats that this specific user is entitled to within the firm.
Similarly, GenAI Apps developed by a firm for their external customers must be able to
identify a customer via her username or account ID, and strictly limit the backend data
or context access to what this user is authorized for.
(B) Information Walls for Divisions. When strict information barriers already exist per
regulatory or compliance requirements among different divisions or departments, e.g., Pub-
lic vs. Private Markets, it is advisable to deploy different versions of an LLM-based internal
25


## Page 26


GenAI App for different divisions or departments. Then such an App can simply follow
the existing information walls for context restriction on the backend.
(C) Due Diligence by Internal GenAI Developers. When an engineering team receives
a request to develop an LLM-based GenAI App, the project must clearly identify who the
intended users are. From there, the developers should assess if the required data or files
access for backend context generation is legitimate for the given user group.
More importantly, in the Firm’s GenAI inventory, all such details should be clearly doc-
umented for each App. Any material changes (e.g., substantially expanding the intended
user group) must seek new approvals from designated 1st and 2nd LoD stakeholders.
(D) Systems for User Feedback and Incident Reporting. Given the inherently dynamic
nature of GenAI, it is strongly recommended that a firm deploys designated reporting
systems that allow users to report various incidents or potential issues. Such a system
should be similar to the Operational Incidents Reporting System that most firms have
already developed. But it deserves its own identity.
Ideally, a taxonomy of incident types should be defined, and the main remediation parties
should also be clearly assigned. Breaching information barriers is certainly a major type.
Such centralized reporting systems are vital for GenAI to evolve and stabilize.
From
neural networks to decision trees, it has been clearly demonstrated in this AI era that
error correction is a universal mechanism for improved learning.

Over-generalization refers to a user overstretching and misusing GenAI responses.
There is one specific area worthy of attention - relying on LLM-based GenAI to evaluate the
performance of employees or teammates. It is a very tempting application of GenAI, but must be
conducted with caution.

Such use cases may include the following scenarios, for example.
(a) For the annual performance review of a team member ABCLMN, a manager asks an internal
LLM-based GenAI App: “Based on all my Outlook emails, Microsoft Teams chats, and
various shared documents in the team’s shared folders, summarize for me by several bullet

(b) Similarly, for the specific category of “Communication Effectiveness” in performance review,
a manager may ask the GenAI App: “Based on all my Outlook emails and Microsoft Teams
chats since Jan 1, 2026, please rank from 1 to 10 (with 10 being the most effective) the
communication-effectiveness score for my team member ABCLMN.”
On one hand, we must acknowledge that these tempting inquiries are NOT baseless at all.

(a) In contemporary workplaces, most activities of our work are indeed digitized. From long-
term strategic projects to short-term requests from internal/external clients or teams, every
event has footprints represented by emails, chats, shared files, or Confluence/Sharepoint
documentation, etc.
(b) When we discuss “communication skills,” for example, it indeed sounds natural to ask a
GenAI App to summarize such “skills” or “effectiveness” from all available digital commu-
nication channels like emails and chats.
On the other hand, a few caveats should not be ignored if managers become fully dependent on
GenAIs for performance evaluation.
(i) Not all our works have digital or language footprints. For example, in investment
banks, general phone or video calls are prohibited from recording, except for specifically
approved categories. When external or internal supporting is delivered via such channels,
there will be no detailed records other than probably a simple chat note like “Thank you
so much for the help, Dave.” A teammate may routinely help traders resolve very urgent
technical issues via phone calls and screen sharing, but there is simply not much digital
or language representation of these important contributions.
(ii) Many workers may prefer in-person chats or direct desk-side support. The
choice is always personal - via phones, emails, chats, video calls, or simply walking to
a desk and solving a problem right on the spot.
For the last approach, a teammate
may spend two long sessions before totally figuring out the root cause of an IT glitch
and having it beautifully fixed.
Such on-site contributions are generally not digitally
recognizable unless there have been formal issue tickets.
(iii) Cultural contributions may not have digital or language footprints at all. To
build a culture full of positive energy or inspiration is vital for a company to thrive, though
more often such activities may not have digital or language footprints.
• Mentorship. A senior team member consciously gives daily technical mentoring as
well as emotional support to a new junior hire, who is a bit nervous and always lacks
confidence in the first couple of months.
• Mutual Support. After observing the abnormally low spirit of another team mem-
ber for several days, Sally Miller invites him for lunch and the conversation reveals
some major family issues bothering him. In the following weeks, Sally gives maximal
accommodation to this member and allows him to gradually step out of the gloomy
shadow of these issues and refocus on an assigned project. The member later on tells
Sally that he would have already resigned if it were not for her kind support.
• Quality Assurance.
On the evening just before issuing a finalized multi-team
report, Joanne Moore notices that one key data table from her team is actually
outdated and seriously misleading to senior stakeholders. Her team member Jack
London was responsible earlier for generating the table, but has been out of the

A firm should also publish new policies or design training sessions to formalize the guidance on
this front, esp. for managers or senior executives.
3.5
Chain Reaction - Risks of Erroneous Agents in Agentic Workflows
In automated agentic workflows, the errors made by any AI agents could pollute the accuracy of an
entire task chain. It is critical to ensure that each agent delivers correct solutions and integrates
seamlessly into the entire workflow.

Listed below are some examples of such failures.
(a) The final language response of a workflow to a client contains socially offensive phrases or
tones, such as discrimination against race, age, or gender, etc.
(b) The final response to a client contains information that the client is not supposed to receive
or clearly breaches established information barriers.
(c) Due to errors made by a parsing agent, a downstream database agent fetches wrong data
from databases, such as mistakenly pulling historical data of ticker “APLE” (Apple Hospi-
tality REIT, Inc.) for a client whose real intention is to trade “AAPL” (Apple Inc.).
(d) Due to errors made by a parsing agent, a downstream computing engine managed by an
analytics agent generates wrong quantitative outputs, such as calculating and outputting
the average historical volume instead of volatility when a client requests a “VOL”-weighted
trading strategy.
Therefore, proper control measures must be taken to maintain the accuracy and stability of
automated agentic workflows. The following list of measures is by no means exhaustive.
28


## Page 29


(A) SDLC Standards.
Agentic workflows are fundamentally also packages of software.
Therefore, it is crucial to adhere to the well-established Software Development Life Cy-
cle (SDLC) protocols, which cover well-established industrial standards to properly design,
implement, test, and deploy such workflows or networks. The following measures are some-
what all under this framework, though they may contain more specific details due to the
involvement of LLMs.
(B) Control Agents and Human Copilots. Embedded within an agentic network, there
could also be some agents that are exclusively devoted to control functions. We call them
“Control Agents.” For example, before the final natural-language response of a workflow
is released to a client, it could be evaluated by such control agents on potential offensiveness
(e.g., on racial or gender discrimination).
For instance, a control agent could prompt to an LLM-based GenAI: “From 1 to 10 with
10 being the most offensive, please rate the racial discrimination score of this text response:
(copying naked network final response here).” Engineers could then set up thresholds, e.g.,
5.0, above which the final responses of an agentic network require reviewing by a human
supervisor (i.e., copiloted by humans).
(C) Documentation and Approvals. The initiation of an agentic-network project should
be properly documented and approved by relevant stakeholders, including both first-line
business teams and second-line control functions such as Risk or Compliance.
At a minimum, such documentation should clearly explain: (1) what is the targeted work-
flow to be automated, (2) what are the traditional bottlenecks and why GenAI can offer
better solutions, (3) what is the preliminary evidence of success with GenAI, (4) what
data or file sources will be required, and what are the associated policies on data privacy
or information barriers, (5) the major stakeholders of the automated workflow, including
owners, users, developers et al., (6) what are the targeted LLM models in mind for different
agents and their tokens costs, and (7) what are the control measures to be developed, etc.
(D) Data Access and Privacy.
For the data or file sources of each agent in an agentic
workflow, evaluation must be done on whether the access (1) is legitimate for the proposed
tasks and workflows, (2) is compliant with a firm’s established policies on data privacy and
restrictions, and (3) adheres to established information barriers.
If a user’s conversational prompts are to be saved for performance improvement, one should
also ask whether (a) such practice has been properly disclosed to the users, or (b) options
have been provided so that a user can decide if the prompts or questions should be saved
beyond a live session, etc.
(E) Pre-Deployment Testing. Once the development of an agentic workflow is completed,
assurance and quality tests must be conducted before it can be formally released to external
clients or internal users. Unit tests should be thoroughly designed to ensure predictable
responses from individual components (or units) up to individual agents. Regression tests
should also be provided whenever possible and run independently and periodically.
29


## Page 30


Due to the statistical nature of LLM components, a comprehensive ensemble of represen-
tative requests or prompts should be tested for an agentic network to ensure stability and
accuracy. Results should be compared with those previously produced by human manual
processes or by conventional benchmark software.
(F) Post-Deployment Monitoring and Issue Tracking. After deployment, developers or
engineers should monitor logs or alert messages produced by an agentic workflow, which
themselves could be summarized by a separately designed agent called “Alert Agent.”
Furthermore, there should be created a user feedback system or issue tracker, so that any
user with concerns or complaints can help log detailed information. Analyzing and acting
on these issues can facilitate error correction and improve the stability and accuracy of
agentic networks.
Due to the statistical nature of LLMs, offering such issue trackers is critical since not all
issues can be predicted or simulated in advance.
(G) Change Management. The landscapes of LLMs and GenAI are constantly morphing.
So are the design and usage of agentic networks. The core LLM models used by agents
can be updated or switched from one vendor to another. The network topology can be
reconfigured. The user group can be expanded from the original one, and so can be the
initial scope.
Any material changes to an existing and approved agentic workflow must be properly
designed and tested by the developers, and also controlled and approved by relevant stake-
holders such as Risk and Compliance. In particular, such material changes must be prop-
erly documented and versioned.
Any key issues previously identified from network monitoring or reported by users should
also be analyzed and tested under the proposed new changes.
In general, these risk management and control measures should be seamlessly integrated into a
firm’s existing internal policies or regulatory rules/laws, including the frameworks of RCSA - Risk
Control Self Assessment, Operational Risk Management, and Model Risk Management,
for example.
4
Conclusion
In this article, we have explored both emerging opportunities and associated risks for GenAI in the
financial industry.
The Yin and Yang arise from the very same kernel of “intelligence” within GenAI. On one hand,
because of this new dimension of “intelligence,” many conventional tasks and workflows can turn
more friendly, versatile, and streamlined in terms of services and solutions. On the other hand, also
because of “intelligence” and its inherent “creativity,” there emerges an unsettling feeling somewhat
similar to the control of running dogs or horses, mankind’s best pals - what if a dog or horse acts
out of its own will instead of according to the expectations of HI?
At least for the current stage, we have demonstrated that many risk control measures can
still work effectively for LLM-based GenAI, including especially those from Operational Risk Man-
agement (OpRisk), Model Risk Management (MRM), Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)
Standards, etc. Testing and monitoring, for instance, the two core pillars of SDLC, are still critical

vital roles, including the Risk Division, Compliance, and Internal Audit, for example.
