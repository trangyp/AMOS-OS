Summary 
 
This review highlights brain health as a dynamic process shaped by both 
genetic and environmental influences throughout development. Critical 
periods provide unique windows of heightened neural plasticity, during 
which genetic–environmental interactions and parental influences 
profoundly impact brain maturation. Frameworks such as DOHaD, ACEs, 
and neurosocial plasticity elucidate how early-life experiences modulate 
long-term cognitive and emotional outcomes.  Brain health science is 
emerging as a field integrating neuroscience, public health, and social 
context. Resilience-oriented approaches and predictive processing, offer 
renewed 
perspectives 
on 
adaptive 
brain 
function. 
Clinically, 
understanding critical periods and plasticity spanning from fetal life to old 
age, has implications for early detection, targeted interventions, and 
resilience-oriented strategies, emphasizing the potential for lifelong 
optimization of mental health.

1. Brain Health as Dynamic Adaptation 
 
The notion of a "healthy brain" is increasingly understood not as a static 
endpoint but as a dynamic process of adaptation, regulation, and 
integration across the lifespan1. Brain health reflects the brain’s capacity 
to maintain functional integrity and behavioral flexibility in response to 
internal and external demands. This perspective highlights the brain as 
self-organizing system marked by complexity, emergent properties, and 
adaptability2. It encompasses cognitive, emotional, and sensorimotor 
functions, which depend on the integrity of neural circuits and are 
continuously shaped by psychosocial, environmental and physiological 
influences3–8. From early development onward9, brain health results from 
reciprocal interactions between biological factors and context, requiring 
neuroscience, social behavior, endocrinology, and developmental 
biology to offer a more integrative understanding of brain adaptability  
framework that spans lifespan4,10–13.  
2. Development: genetic and environment influences 
Human brain development results from the interplay of genetic and 
environmental factors across both prenatal and postnatal stages. Early 
embryonic and fetal development is mainly driven by genetic programs 
that regulate molecular and cellular events14,15, though intrauterine

conditions such as maternal stress or nutritional deficits can disrupt this 
process. After birth, while genes still provide basic instructions, 
experience and environmental influences increasingly shape brain 
architecture, cognition, emotion, and behavior; allowing the brain to 
remain responsive to social and contextual inputs during all lifespan2,16–
22.  
3. Critical periods in brain development: a window into plasticity 
Critical periods are developmental windows during which neural circuits 
display 
heightened 
plasticity 
and 
are 
particularly 
sensitive 
to 
environmental input. Their onset, duration, and specificity vary across 
brain regions, critical periods are essential for the maturation of sensory, 
cognitive, emotional, and social functions.  Experience during these 
periods can leave profound and often lasting effects on brain structure 
and function. Brain health thus emerges from the ongoing interplay 
between biology and experience, with adaptive capacity and plasticity as 
defining features. 
Two complementary forms of experience-driven plasticity guide brain 
development. Experience-expectant plasticity depends on species-
typical stimuli that are anticipated during specific windows to refine 
preconfigured neural circuits. Any deviation from expected inputs risks 
compromising development. Classic examples include the development 
of binocular vision in humans and barrel cortex organization in rodents.

In non-human primates, rearing in deprived environments can produce 
lasting cortical and social abnormalities23,24. By contrast, experience-
dependent plasticity reflects the brain’s lifelong capacity to remodel 
circuits in response to unique, individual experiences. Though less 
pronounced outside critical periods, this form supports learning and 
adaptation across the lifespan—for instance, skill acquisition or 
enrichment-induced 
plasticity 
and 
neurogenesis 
in 
the 
adult 
hippocampus25.  
The existence of critical periods represents an evolutionary strategy that 
balances flexibility with stability. Early plasticity allows the brain to 
calibrate circuits to the sensory, social, and emotional environment into 
which an organism is born26. The regulated opening and closure of 
critical periods ensures that foundational skills are hardwired in early life, 
while subsequent stabilization safeguards efficient processing and 
memory in adulthood and senescence27,28. 
Importantly, these windows are not passive. Their opening and closure 
are orchestrated by a tightly regulated interplay of molecular and cellular 
mechanisms. Recent research has shed light on the neurobiological 
"triggers" and "brakes" that gate these windows of heightened plasticity 
(Figure 2).

Maturation is governed by both intrinsic genetic programs and activity-

accelerate interneuron development. Perturbations in these processes 
have clinical relevance: for example, abnormalities in PV interneuron

The termination of critical periods is mediated by structural and 
molecular “brakes.” A central mechanism is the formation of perineuronal

7 
 
nets (PNNs)—specialized extracellular matrix structures that surround 
PV interneurons. PNNs stabilize synaptic contacts and restrict further 
plasticity, thereby consolidating mature circuits. Strikingly, the same PV 
interneurons that initiate heightened plasticity later become the primary 
targets of PNN encapsulation, illustrating a coordinated program in which 
the triggers of plasticity are also linked to its closure. This dual role 
prevents the brain from remaining indefinitely malleable, ensuring the 
consolidation of essential abilities33–35.  
At a mechanistic level, several pathways fine-tune the timing and 
regulation of critical periods. Neuregulin-1/ErbB4 signaling modulates 
excitatory input onto PV interneurons, directly influencing the onset and 
duration of plasticity. Manipulating this pathway can either constrain or 
restore plasticity in adulthood, highlighting its therapeutic potential36,37. 
Synaptic adhesion molecules, including L1 and NCAM, contribute to 
pruning and stabilization, acting in concert with the extracellular matrix to 
regulate closure38. Beyond neurons, glial cells are pivotal in energy 
management and active participants of this process. Astrocytic mGluR5 
signaling shapes inhibitory synapse development, while microglia 
mediate synaptic pruning in an activity-dependent manner, underscoring 
the importance of neuron–glia interactions in sculpting developmental 
trajectories39,40.

4. Effects of Interference during critical periods 
4.1. Developmental Frameworks: DOHaD, ACEs and plasticity 
Rooted in the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) 
framework53–56, brain health is conceptualized as context-sensitive 
flexibility driven by adaptive plasticity. Early environmental exposures 
during critical and sensitive periods, particularly the prenatal and early 
postnatal windows57,  calibrate  stress-regulatory systems (HPA axis, 
autonomic nervous system (ANS), and the central nervous system 
(CNS), shaping neural circuits involved in emotion, cognition and social 
behavior through epigenetic reprogramming. Exposure to suboptimal 
nutritional conditions, maternal stress, infections, or substance abuse 
can negatively impact neurodevelopment and increase mental and 
behavioral disorder risk and disease risk in subsequent life48,58. 
Conversely, favorable early environments promote adaptive processes 
that support survival and thriving in future life contexts59.  For instance, 
Lähdepuro et al. (2024)60 found that higher maternal perceived social 
support during pregnancy—a key aspect of a supportive psychosocial


## Page 10


10 
 
environment—was associated with better cognitive performance in 
children at age eight, highlighting pregnancy as a sensitive period for 
developmental influence. Similarly, Lähdepuro et al. (2023)61 showed 
that greater positive maternal mental health during pregnancy was linked 
to a reduced risk of mental and behavioral disorders in children up to age 
12, even among those whose mothers experienced depressive 
symptoms. Although these studies do not disentangle genetics from 
environmental contributions, both are likely important.  
While DOHaD research focuses largely on prenatal and early postnatal 
exposures, adversity in  childhood and adolescence—termed adverse 
childhood 
experiences 
(ACEs)—also 
exerts 
lasting 
effects 
on 
neurodevelopment62,63. Childhood history of abuse, neglect, and 
household dysfunction as assessed by the Adverse Childhood

concerns, including perinatal mood and anxiety disorders during 
adulthood64,65. Teicher and colleagues19,66 show that different types, 
timings, and chronicities of maltreatment uniquely alter brain maturation 
and stress responsivity. They caution against oversimplified scoring 
systems for ACEs19. These changes may carry adaptive value in high-
risk environments but increase vulnerability when contexts change67,68.

Sex-specific responses have been described after ACE, at different 
levels. Studies in animal models arise from differences in brain 
maturation timing and neurobiological pathways affected by stress in a 
sex-specific manner. For example, early stress accelerates certain 
neurodevelopmental processes in male rodents resulting in specific 
cognitive impairments, while in females it results in a depressive-like 
phenotype69. Additionally, males exposed to ACE often show greater 
impairments in cognitive functions such as working and spatial memory, 
linked to smaller hippocampal volumes, whereas females may 
experience more emotional disturbances such as depression symptoms 
without the same degree of cognitive impairment70. In humans, females 
tend to show more complex and varied profiles of childhood adversities, 
with higher prevalence of sexual abuse and affective disorders like PTSD 
compared to males, who might report higher exposure to other types of 
trauma71. At brain activity level,  Bürger and colleagues72 describe 
differences on resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) involved in 
stress reactivity and coping between healthy males and females. 
Females showed higher amygdala–vACC and amygdala–mPFC rsFC for 
social exclusion than for achievement stress, and compared to males. 
For females, feeling socially affiliated might be more fundamental than 
for males. This  evidence highlights the necessity to evaluate sex specific 
strategies for mental health interventions and treatments.

4.2 Impacts of Neurosocial Plasticity on Brain Development  
The social environment and interactions with the living world represents 
the most powerful determinants of brain development. According to the 
neurosocial plasticity concept, social experiences become biologically 
embedded, with lasting consequences for neural structure and function. 
The “social brain”, comprising regions such as the prefrontal cortex, 
amygdala, hippocampus, and insula, continuously changes in response


## Page 14


14 
 
to dynamic interpersonal interactions, attachment, and social stress99,100. 
The quality of these environments, ranging from supportive relationships 
to toxic experiences, shapes brain development, behavioral outcomes, 
and healthy aging101–103. 
Faraji and Metz2 introduced neurosocial plasticity to describe how the 
brain dynamically adapts to social experiences through the coordinated 
action of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and oxytocin (OT). 
Reconceptualizing the brain as a self-organizing system, this framework 
highlights the co-modulation of neural circuitry and social behavior by 
these molecular pathways. Central to this process is the BDNF–OT 
interaction, which operates via co-localization, co-expression, and co-
regulation. BDNF, a neurotrophin essential for synaptic plasticity, 
learning, and neurogenesis2,  exerts its effects primarily through the TrkB 
receptor, widely distributed across the developing and adult brain. 
Remarkably, TrkB can also be transactivated by OT, revealing a 
molecular convergence of trophic and hormonal signaling104,105. The 
synergy between BDNF and OT is evident across brain regions like the 
hippocampus, prefrontal cortex and amygdala, each critical for emotion, 
memory, and social behavior2,21,106. Both molecules are upregulated 
during key social experiences88,107, and early-life exposures induce 
region- and sex-specific expression patterns, particularly in the


## Page 15


15 
 
hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex106. OT further enhances 
BDNF transcription and TrkB activation, creating a positive feedback 
loop that 
supports neurogenesis 
and resilience105. Conversely, 
dysregulation of either system—such as reduced BDNF in depression or 
diminished 
OT 
signaling—is 
linked 
to 
impaired 
socioemotional 
functioning108,109. Emerging evidence suggests that cooperative BDNF–
OT signaling may counteract stress effects and promote recovery 
following neural injury or chronic inflammation110. 
Importantly, this molecular synergy of BDNF and OT is age- and sex-
dependent. During aging, BDNF and OT levels naturally decline, 
contributing to reduced plasticity and increased vulnerability to cognitive 
and emotional disorders111. However, their mutual regulation may buffer 
against aging-related decline, for instance by preserving telomere length 
via upregulation of telomerase activity and anti-inflammatory signaling112. 
Moreover, sex hormones modulate BDNF–OT interactions, with estrogen 
enhancing both OT receptor sensitivity and BDNF expression, potentially 
conferring greater resilience in females under certain conditions89,107.  
Chronic or severe stress, particularly during sensitive developmental 
periods, triggers sustained inflammation. Stress upregulates pro-
inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6113–115 , which 
not only drive systemic inflammation but also accelerate biological aging


## Page 16


16 
 
(“inflammaging”)116. Elevated cytokines have been associated with 
telomere shortening117, cognitive decline118, and impaired immune 
regulation. Stress responses are sex-specific and moderated by social 
context and coping capacity, underscoring the need for individualized, 
sociocultural perspectives. 
The heightened pro-inflammatory milieu induced by early-life stress can 
profoundly disrupt neurodevelopmental trajectories119,120. Cytokines such 
as IL-1β and IL-6 readily cross the blood-brain barrier or act via the 
vagus nerve to influence neuroimmune interactions, leading to glial 
activation, synaptic pruning abnormalities, and impaired neurogenesis118. 
Elevated inflammatory markers have been associated with structural and 
functional alterations in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, which 
are regions critical for memory, executive function, and mood 
regulation121. Longitudinal studies have further shown that prenatal 
inflammation predicts increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, 
including anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment later in life. 
Notably, these neuroimmune effects are shaped by early social 
interactions, maternal care, and hormonal milieu, which can either 
exacerbate or buffer against the neurotoxic effects of inflammation.

4.3 Parental influence on development 
4.3.1 Parental mental health influence  
 
In addition to the vulnerability for perinatal mental health disorders from a 
preexisting genetic vulnerability (e.g., mother with history of postpartum 
depression), a preexisting mental health disorder (e.g., parental history 
of mood and anxiety disorders), and pre-pregnancy sensitivity to 
hormone changes (e.g., premenstrual dysphoric disorder)122–124, the 
childhood psychosocial environment affects brain health and sets the 
stage for vulnerability to perinatal mood and anxiety disorders alone and 
in conjunction with perinatal stressors.  For example, maternal childhood 
adversity creates a cognitive vulnerability to perinatal stress and 
postpartum depression through repetitive negative thinking125. ACEs 
affecting future birthing parent mental health creates the potential for 
perpetuating intergenerational cognitive vulnerability to poor emotional 
health. McDonnell & Valentino (2016)126 found that maternal childhood 
maltreatment predicted increased maladaptive infant socioemotional 
symptoms as measured by the Ages and Stages Questionnaire-Socio 
Emotional (ASQ-SE), and maternal household dysfunction indirectly 
predicted maladaptive infant socioemotional symptoms126.  
Emerging evidence points to a possible intergenerational impact of 
paternal sexual abuse exposure on neural mechanisms underlying facial


## Page 18


18 
 
expression recognition103. Although the relation between perinatal mental 
health and child development has primarily focused on maternal mental 
health, paternal mental health has been found to have a substantial 
impact on child development. A recent meta-analysis by Le Bas et al.47 
found that paternal perinatal distress (depression, anxiety, and stress) 
was associated with poorer socio-emotional, cognitive, language, 
physical, and global development in their children, with postpartum 
paternal distress having the strongest association with child outcomes. 
Fathers involvement is associated with positive child developmental 
outcomes, but perinatal paternal mental illness can lessen father 
engagement and worsen child developmental outcomes127. This is 
notable because clinical prevention and treatment interventions that 
solely focus on maternal perinatal mental health leave the child at risk for 
poor developmental outcomes because they do not address paternal 
mental health.  
4.3.2 Parent–Infant Synchrony  
Parenthood triggers profound hormonal, neural, and behavioral 
adaptations in both mothers and fathers, tuning them to their infants. 
While research has focused on human mother–infant dyads, evidence 
shows fathers also exhibit synchrony and co-regulation with their 
infants128.


## Page 19


19 
 
Biobehavioral synchrony refers to coordinated timing of signals (gaze, 
touch, vocalizations) and physiological states (heart rate, hormones) 
between parent and infant. This dyadic system involves mutual 
regulation and rhythmic adaptation, with each partner’s behavior attuned 
to the other’s. These processes occur across hormonal (oxytocin, 
cortisol), autonomic (heart rate/RSA), and neural (brain activity) levels 
and are foundational for developing self-regulation. Synchrony is not 
unique to humans; mammalian parent–offspring interactions often show 
coordinated neural oscillations during contact129–131. 
Maternal adaptation includes dramatic hormonal surges during 
pregnancy and birth (estrogen, progesterone, oxytocin, prolactin) that 
remodel the brain132. Imaging studies reveal extensive postpartum gray-
matter changes in the amygdala, hippocampus, insula, and prefrontal 
cortex during the first weeks after birth, persisting for months132,133. 
These neuroplastic changes enhance maternal sensitivity and caregiving 
by tuning emotion and reward circuits. 
Fathers, though not experiencing pregnancy, also undergo hormonal 
shifts—often lower testosterone and higher prolactin/oxytocin—which 
correlate with increased caregiving. Longitudinal data link decreased 
testosterone and elevated oxytocin in new fathers to greater childcare 
involvement134. Reviews report significant endocrine (testosterone,

Parents’ hormones can become synchronized in the perinatal period. 
Oxytocin and cortisol levels in both mothers and fathers often covary in 
late pregnancy and postpartum, correlating with adaptive parenting and 
bonding138. Infant–parent interactions such as calm face-to-face play can 
elicit matched hormonal and autonomic responses—oxytocin release, 
vagal activation—in both partners139. Mothers’ co-regulatory effects are 
often evident even in low-demand contexts like soothing or feeding, 
whereas fathers may require more active engagement to achieve similar 
effects. 
Both mothers and fathers use multimodal cues—touch, voice, affect—to 
engage infants and modulate arousal. Neuroimaging shows both sexes 
activate common “parenting circuits” in prefrontal and limbic areas in 
response to infant cues135. Both can experience hormonal coupling with 
infants, and close bonds synchronize stress systems among family 
members138. 
Differences emerge in style, context, and physiology. Mothers often 
maintain continuous engagement, rapidly scanning cues and modulating 
affect to match infants, even in quiet contexts. Fathers tend toward more 
physical, play-oriented engagement, sometimes less attuned to subtle 
distress128. Neurobiologically, maternal brain–infant synchrony correlates 
strongly with oxytocin and amygdala activity, while fathers’ synchrony

A significant body of research indicates early life adversity, like maternal 
adverse childhood experiences and sociodemographic risks, disrupts 
parent-child synchrony across behavioral, physiological, and neural 
aspects141. Meta-analyses show reduced mother-child respiratory sinus 
arrhythmia (RSA) synchrony in high-risk pairs, yet quality parent-child 
interactions can buffer these effects142. Neural synchrony, assessed via 
fNIRS, also decreases with adversity, affecting emotional processing and 
regulation143.  
In summary, parent–infant synchrony is fundamental biobehavioral 
processes supporting infant development. Both mothers and fathers can 
synchronously attune to their infants, but mothers generally show 
stronger, more immediate synchrony across contexts, supported by 
prenatal neuroendocrine adaptations. Fathers’ synchrony emerges 
postnatally, often relying on active engagement and contextual cues. 
Together, these complementary styles contribute to infants’ regulation, 
resilience, and social development. Notably, prenatal stress impacts 
dyadic interactions, underscoring intergenerational stress transmission. 
Positive relational experiences can foster synchrony, promoting healthy

5.1 Hormesis 
Hormesis—defined as an adaptive response to low or intermittent 
stressors—also plays a key role. It shows  how mild stressors—like 
fasting, exercise, or cognitive challenge—can enhance adaptive 
resilience. It involves a ‘dose-response revolution’ i.e. the changing 
perception that the fundamental nature of the dose response is neither 
linear nor threshold, but U-shaped149.  Mild physical or cognitive 
challenges during development may upregulate protective molecular 
systems, including mitochondrial resilience, neurotrophic signaling, and

24 
 
anti-inflammatory pathways150–155. These mechanisms highlight the 
potential for environmental enrichment to promote resilience and 
adaptive capacity.  
5.2. Predictive processing model  
The predictive processing framework views the brain as a hierarchical 
inference system that continuously generates and updates models of the 
world to minimize prediction error156,157. A healthy brain achieves this 
through accurate generative models and appropriate precision-weighting 
of sensory inputs, while failures in this process contribute to conditions 
such as anxiety, depression, psychosis, and attention disorders157. 
Recent extensions incorporate interoceptive and affective signals, linking 
predictive processing to emotion regulation, decision-making, and 
therapy158. This perspective positions brain health as a dual challenge of 
energy optimization and uncertainty reduction, shaped by early-life 
adversities, especially those experienced in utero159,160. 
This 
novel 
perspective 
integrates 
insights 
from 
developmental 
neuroscience, particularly regarding energy constraints on neural 
architecture and the impact of early-life experiences on neuro-
immunometabolic processes: 
• Energy Optimization (Emin) in Biological Brains:


## Page 25


25 
 
Unlike artificial intelligence (AI), which typically maximizes information 
(Imax) without metabolic constraints, biological brains develop under 
strict energy limits. Neural architecture appears to follow a metabolically 
optimized search strategy, where glial cells play fundamental roles 
alongside neuron161,162. Glia not only support neurons but adapt to 
intrauterine adversity, shaping postnatal developmental trajectories163. 
Dysfunctions in these energy-dependent processes are implicated in 
Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and neurodegenerative conditions 
such as Alzheimer’s164. 
This energy-centric view fosters an iterative AI–neuroscience exchange: 
computational models can help simulate developmental processes, while 
biologically inspired principles guide the design of more efficient neural 
architectures. Dynamic coordination theory links energy consumption to 
network dynamics165,166, suggesting that predictive performance in brain-
inspired AI should optimize both Emin and Imax. This approach enables 
the development of deep glial-neural networks (DGNNs) capable of 
multistability and behavioral flexibility167, offering powerful tools for 
modeling disease-specific neurodevelopmental trajectories168.

6.1 Critical periods: 
 
Recent advances in elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing 
critical periods, which represents vulnerability and also opportunity, have 
opened new therapeutic avenues to mitigate the consequences of early 
adversity, restore function after injury, and treat neurodevelopmental and 
psychiatric disorders222. Pharmacological or behavioral interventions 
capable of reopening or extending critical periods in adulthood hold 
promise for conditions previously viewed as irreversible. For instance,

inhibitory 
neurotransmission, 
chromatin

or 
perineuronal net digestion has been shown to restore juvenile-like 
plasticity in the adult brain223,224. 
The concept of gene–environment interactions also challenge traditional 
categorical diagnoses in psychiatry, suggesting that disorders may 
emerge from complex developmental systems rather than purely genetic 
or environmental causes. This perspective supports individualized 
treatment approaches that integrate biological and environmental 
contexts. 
6.2 Rejuvenating Adult Plasticity: Bypassing the Brakes 
The discovery of molecular mechanisms controlling critical periods has 
overturned the belief that their effects are irreversible, revealing new 
therapeutic possibilities. A landmark study showed that digesting 
perineuronal nets (PNNs) with bacterial Chondroitinase ABC in adult rat 
visual cortex reopened plasticity, enabling ocular dominance shifts long 
after the critical period had closed27,225.  
Beyond these cell-intrinsic and synaptic mechanisms, epigenetic 
regulation also governs plasticity. DNA methylation levels increase 
following the closure of critical periods, stabilizing inhibitory synapses 
and reducing the potential for circuit reorganization. Interestingly,


## Page 31


31 
 
pharmacological demethylation can reopen critical period-like plasticity in 
adulthood, highlighting a reversible molecular control point226. 
These findings reframed critical periods as regulated rather than fixed, 
highlighting that adult plasticity is suppressed but not lost227,228. Future 
work must therefore address not only the refinement of molecular 
interventions but also strategies to harness reopened plasticity toward 
functional and beneficial outcomes. 
6.3 Health implications of neurosocial plasticity  
Dysregulation of BDNF and OT systems has been implicated in 
neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression, anxiety, autism 
spectrum disorder, and Alzheimer's disease21,229,230. Thus, targeting this 
interaction may open new therapeutic avenues. Interventions aimed at 
enhancing neurosocial plasticity, such as social enrichment, may 
improve cognitive resilience, emotional health, and social functioning 
across the lifespan. Thus, the BDNF–OT interaction acts as a central 
regulatory mechanism linking social experience, neural plasticity, and 
behavioral resilience making this axis a promising target for future 
interventions in neurodevelopment, aging, and lifetime mental health.

6.4 Pregnancy and Environmental Enrichment 
Pregnancy is a period of opportunity that allows the impact of positive 
experiences, with beneficial effects on maternal mental health, 
physiological regulation, and fetal development. Positive psychosocial 
and sensory stimulation during pregnancy can shape neuroendocrine 
balance, reduce stress hormone exposure, and promote optimal 
placental function231. Such influences extend beyond the immediate 
pregnancy, as favorable maternal emotional states have been linked to 
longer gestational length, higher birth weight, and reduced risk of 
preterm birth232. These effects may operate through interacting pathways 
(resumed on Box1) involving stress physiology, immune modulation, 
metabolic regulation, and epigenetic mechanisms that regulate gene 
expression in both mother and fetus82,233,234. 
Integrating environmental enrichment into prenatal care provides a 
biologically grounded, evidence-based pathway to enhance maternal and 
fetal health with lasting benefits235. Targeted approaches such as 
mindfulness, structured social support, music, or nature exposure may 
strengthen resilience and improve perinatal outcomes. Practical 
applications include incorporating enrichment through art, partner 
involvement, and psychosocial counseling to support maternal mental 
health and birth outcomes. Early initiatives, like the Early Care Network

collaboration among healthcare providers236. Future studies should 
assess long-term impacts of prenatal enrichment on maternal recovery, 
child development, and intergenerational health.  
6.5.Psychosocial, Biological, and Computational Pathways to Resilience 
As previously mentioned, the emerging  brain health science increasingly 
emphasize positive determinants— rather than focusing solely on risk or 
pathology237,238. For instance, in the salutogenic model239,240  individuals 
maintain health through effective stress regulation and meaning-oriented 
coping. A ‘sense of coherence’ and ‘generalized and specific constraints’ 
are  key concepts in this framework241. 
Resilience-oriented theories emphasize, environmental and systemic 
supports that strengthens individuals’ ability to cope with stress (Betthell 
etal, 2017). Haynes et al (2023)242 propose frameworks rooted in social 
justice and public policy to reduce structural inequalities that undermine 
resilience . Creating stable, predictable environments can further protect 
against the harmful effects of uncertainty on mental health243.   
Immunopsychiatry highlights immune–brain dynamics as foundational to 
mental health. Together they show resilience as a multi-level

phenomenon, shaped by both psychosocial context and biological 
regulation.  
An important extension is the meaning-making perspective, which 
highlights the brain’s role in constructing coherent and emotionally 
integrated interpretations of experience. Meaning-making supports 
identity development, emotion regulation, and coping with adversity 
across the lifespan68,244–249. Neuroscientific research links meaning-
making processes to activation in the default mode network (DMN), 
ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), and autobiographical memory 
systems—regions implicated in self-reflection, valuation, and narrative 
construction250–253. While meaning-making can become maladaptive 
when shaped by rigid or negative beliefs, constructive narratives promote 
psychological flexibility, post-traumatic growth, and integrated identity. As 
such, this perspective complements developmental, salutogenic, and 
predictive frameworks, offering insights for preventive and therapeutic 
interventions targeting mental mental health254–256. 
Recent attention has turned to immunopsychiatry, a field that emerged 
from psychoneuroimmunology, which investigates how immune system 
activity influences brain function and behavior. Chronic inflammation, 
immune priming, and perinatal immune activation are now understood to 
be core contributors to depression, schizophrenia, and cognitive

decline257,258. Together with other approaches described in this section 
they show resilience as a multi-level phenomenon, shaped by both 
psychosocial context and biological regulation. Hormesis can prime 
adaptive processes across systems, but is yet conditional: stress 
exposures can misfire when thresholds are exceeded, as illustrated by 
mindfulness-based interventions that worsen distress or insomnia in 
vulnerable groups259,260.  
Computational Positive Psychology (CPP) advances this field by 
integrating multimodal biological, psychological, and social data to build 
predictive models of flourishing, tailoring interventions to individual and 
systemic contexts, and linking resilience science to public health and 
policy- ultimately linking to hormesis by modeling non-linear, dose-
sensitive dynamics.  Thakkar et al261 explores how AI can support 
emotional regulation and improve care for conditions like schizophrenia, 
autism, and mood disorders; it also stresses the need for culturally 
sensitive and bias-aware approaches, highlighting collaboration between 
experts as key to responsibly integrating AI into mental health care. In 
this way, CPP serves as a meta-framework that not only integrates 
resilience-oriented and immunopsychiatric insights but also translates 
them into evidence-based, scalable strategies for wellbeing in the digital 
era261,262.

7. Future of brain health science and mental health  
Brain health science is emerging as a field integrating neuroscience, 
public health, and social context to enhance mental health across the 
lifespan. The brain functions as a dynamic system, balancing information 
processing and metabolic efficiency while adapting to energy availability, 
stress, and early-life experiences (Figure 3). Experience-dependent and 
experience-expectant processes guide neural circuit development, 
though many mechanisms remain to be discovered, offering fertile 
ground for research. Genetic variation, epigenetic modifications, and 
early-life experiences together shape developmental trajectories, 
indicating that effective therapies should be tailored to each individual’s 
unique profile.  Neurosocial plasticity, particularly via the BDNF–OT axis, 
links social experience to neural adaptability and resilience, supporting 
interventions such as social enrichment. Predictive processing has


## Page 37


37 
 
clarified aspects of mental distress264 and psychiatric disorders265, though 
its explanatory power for complex cognition is debated266,267, with 
dynamic, ecological, and embodied approaches offering complementary 
perspectives. Clinical translation now includes re-opening critical periods, 
pregnancy and environmental enrichment, and resilience-oriented 
strategies. Accounting for sex/ gender and age in research is essential to 
improve the precision and applicability of findings to individualized 
mental health outcomes. The integration of big data approaches, 
including genomics, transcriptomics, and environmental exposure 
measures, combined with machine learning methods, is beginning to 
reveal complex interaction patterns not detectable by conventional 
analyses, offering new pathways for precision psychiatry268,269.  In 
addition, multisystemic resilience, by emphasizing the dynamic interplay 
between individual, social, cultural, and institutional systems, offers a 
comprehensive framework for improving mental health, highlighting the 
importance of equitable access to resources, adaptive strategies, and 
transformative changes in environments to support thriving under 
adversity270. Together, these insights mark an era where harnessing 
lifelong adaptability may transform mental health across the lifespan.
