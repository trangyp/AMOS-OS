Harnessing Information in Incentive Design
Raj Kiriti Velicheti

Abstract
Incentive design deals with interaction between a principal and an agent where the former can
shape the latter’s utility through a policy commitment. It is well known that the principal faces an
information rent when dealing with an agent that has informational advantage. In this work, we em-
bark on a systematic study of the effect of information asymmetry in incentive design games. Specifi-
cally, we first demonstrate that it is in principal’s interest to decrease this information asymmetry. To
mitigate this uncertainty, we let the principal gather information either by letting the agent shape her
belief (aka Information Design), or by paying to acquire it. Providing solutions to all these cases we
show that while introduction of uncertainty increases the principal’s cost, letting the agent shape its
belief can be advantageous. We study information asymmetry and information acquisition in both
matrix games and quadratic Gaussian game setups.

Introduction
In a variety of principal-agent interactions, a principal (P) seeks to induce a desired response from an
agent (A), where the two parties may have different strategic objectives, e.g., see [Bas¸24]. Incentive design
is a widely studied mechanism for inducement. It defines a hierarchical decision-making problem, where
P announces how she would react to A’s action. A then acts, after which P implements her announced
strategy. These interactions can be studied via feedback Stackelberg game setups. The feedback structure
allows the principal to commit to a reaction policy to A’s action instead of an open-loop action policy at
the start of the game.
A long line of work has studied stochastic incentive design games where the two parties may have
different information about cost-relevant random variables, e.g., see [Bas¸84, CB82, ZB82, SCJ81, Bas¸82,
CB85]. However, a common assumption in these setups has been that P knows more than A. Indeed,
if the goal is to influence A to act exactly how P would have preferred A to act to minimize the former’s
cost, it is conceivable that P must know what A knows. However, in a variety of contexts, such an as-
sumption does not hold. Consider for instance the interaction between an insurance provider and a pol-
icyholder. Although risk-sharing is established upon signing a contract (the incentive mechanism) where

ing a strategy to first act, P commits to a policy to react to an action taken by A. The committed policy
can be thought of as a rule-book that P proposes to induce a desired response from A. Such games have
applications in the design of tax codes and environmental taxes, among others. See [Gro73,BO98,FT91]
for a survey.

advantage for P, we give informational advantage to A. Not knowing θ, P can only solve a Bayesian
version of a feedback-Stackelberg equilibrium. As one would expect, P will incur a higher (expected)
cost in an equilibrium of the Bayesian game compared to that in G1. The difference is indeed the price P
pays for not knowing θ in this game of asymmetric information.
Can P acquire information about θ and garner better costs on an equilibrium path? We address this
question in this paper, by considering two different mechanisms of information acquisition in G3 and
G4. In G3, we allow A to report a signal that is correlated with θ and let P update her belief about θ.
However, A can act strategically in how it designs the signal to persuade P. In a sense, G3 embodies
an example where Bayesian persuasion from [KG11] meets incentive design games in [Bas¸84]. Here, the
information-rich player (A) sends a signal and seeks to influence how the information-poor player (P)
would design its incentive policy. As will become clear from our examples, A acting purely out of self-
interest can choose to reveal information about θ to P, allowing P to incur a cost lower than in G2.

Our setup is more complex than usual Bayesian persuasion problems considered in [KG11] because we
accommodate incentive design as the reaction of the receiver, which in our problem is P.
In G4, we consider a setup where P invests in devising an experiment or a signaling mechanism.
This mechanism allows P to refine its knowledge about θ prior to interacting with A. However, the
experiment is not free and P must pay more for an experiment that reveals more information (suitably
defined) about θ. While our analysis of G4 bears similarity to the costly Bayesian persuasion problem
studied in [GK14], our setup is fundamentally different in that the information-poor player (P) invests
in an experiment design and not the other way around.
Our key contribution is the formulation and study of information acquisition in a principal-agent
interaction, where P enjoys the incentive design advantage, while A possesses information advantage.
Before delving into our analysis, we further contextualize this study within the long literature on games
of asymmetric information that has a long history dating back to [AMS95]. Within the context of in-
centive design, the role of asymmetric information in open-loop Stackelberg games has been addressed
in [XFC+16]. To our knowledge, this is the first work that studies information asymmetry and infor-
mation acquisition in feedback Stackelberg matrix games–a type of interaction that more accurately
models rule-book design. While Bayesian persuasion itself has been studied in matrix game contexts
in [KG11, DX16], this work attempts to merge two different types of inducements–belief shaping and
incentive design. In addition, the last game G4 borrows tools and techniques from the persuasion litera-
ture to address a question where P chooses to invest in shaping her own belief before she interacts with
A.
Departing from the world of finitely many actions and states, we consider the same set of questions
G1–G4 in quadratic Gaussian (QG) games. In such games, direct computation of a Stackelberg equi-
librium in stochastic incentive design problems have proven challenging. An indirect solution method
leveraging a suitable team problem has been studied in [Bas¸84]. However, due to the non-classical infor-
mation structure in problems we consider, this route is rendered inapplicable. As a result, our study of
Bayesian variants with information acquisition techniques in QG games with a suitable restricted class of
incentive policies stands out as an important contribution of this work. Again, while Bayesian persuasion
in QG games have received recent attention, e.g., in [Tam18, SB20, VBB25], the combination of persua-
sion and incentive design in QG games remains unexplored. These games provide a powerful modeling
framework for inducement with continuous state/action spaces. Furthermore, the incentive policies we
consider are soft in that they depend continuously on A’s response [Bas¸24]. In practical contexts, such
soft policies are typically more defensible than threat policies.
Within the context of Principal-agent interactions, the effect of asymmetric information was studied
under the name of adverse selection starting from [MR78, Ake78, MR90] with follow-up works advo-
cating reduction of such information asymmetry through phenomena such as signaling [Spe78]. For a
more detailed exposition of this literature see, [BD04]. However, in contrast to these works we deal with
a different kind of information disclosure. In our work A can commit to a disclosure mechanism similar
to Bayesian Persuasion [KG11]. Recently, [RS17, BBM15, ALV23] have addressed such verifiable disclo-
sure in principal-agent interactions similar to our work, concentrating on specific cost structures such as
monopolistic pricing. [BHM22] study the setting where P has both information advantage and can can
design quality differentiated products.

by P.
Finally, we consider game G4, where P invests in designing an information channel π that generates
a signal s ∈S that carries information about θ. Then, the state is realized and observed by A, but not
P. However, P uses π to generate a signal s and uses s to update her belief about θ. Akin to the setup
in G3, P updates her belief from µ0 to the posterior distribution µs(s, π) and proceeds with the design
of an incentive mechanism for A with this updated belief. Thus, G4 is similar to G3, except that P must
now design the signaling experiment to her advantage instead of leaving it to A, whose incentives might
not be aligned with P.

Conclusions
In this paper, we have formulated and studied an incentive design problem between a principal (P) and
an agent (A), where we have endowed A with informational advantage. Specifically, we have allowed P
to know only the prior distribution of a cost-relevant random variable (the state), while letting A know
the realization of the state. We have studied two types of information acquisition mechanisms. Namely,
in one variant, P allowed A to reveal information about the state prior to the interaction, and in the
other, invested in a channel to gain information about the state. We have studied these games within two
setups–matrix games and quadratic Gaussian games. Our results reveal the trade-offs between these two
types of information acquisition techniques. One direction for future work is to consider more general
classes of policies for P and study a broader class of QG games. Another direction for future research
concerns studying the same question in the context of repeated and dynamic games, where the state itself
might evolve, P can update beliefs over time, and A can shape these beliefs.
