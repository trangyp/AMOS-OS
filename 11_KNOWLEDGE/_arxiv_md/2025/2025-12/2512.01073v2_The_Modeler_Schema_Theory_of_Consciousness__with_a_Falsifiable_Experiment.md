The Modeler Schema Theory of Consciousness,  
with a Falsiﬁable Experiment 
Frank Heile, Ph.D. 
May 5, 2026 
Abstract 
We propose that consciousness arises from a single control agent, the Modelerschema. It monitors the 
brain’s Modeler as that system constructs and updates the internal World Model. As part of that 
monitoring, the Modelerschema generates experience by applying a qualia-based consistency check to 
the Modeler’s output. The Human Agent comprises three cooperating agents: Modeler, Controller, and 
Targeter, each paired with an associated regulatory “schema” agent. We also describe fast-Modelers 
and fast-Controllers, evolutionary shortcuts whose rapid actions precede awareness. Our core 
prediction is that the Modelerschema performs a qualia-based consistency check during saccades and 
issues a bottom-up attention request when a discrepancy is found. To test this prediction, we propose a 
saccadic change-detection experiment that distinguishes Modeler-generated from Modelerschema-
generated bottom-up attention requests. Locating qualia in the Modelerschema ties experience to the 
regulation and reﬁnement of internal representations, clariﬁes how awareness arises from model 
control, and suggests a path toward empirical falsiﬁcation, thereby oﬀering a concrete, testable 
proposal toward solving the Hard Problem of consciousness.

Introduction 
In the Modelerschema Theory of Consciousness, an agent is deﬁned as a system that takes input, pursues 
goals, and interacts with the world or other agents. Here, goals refer to internal representations that 
guide action selection; they may be evolutionarily shaped, learned, or context-dependent. 
In this framework, the brain, the Human Agent,1 is a multi-agent system composed of three 
cooperating agents, each regulated by its own schema agent (for the deﬁnition of a schema agent, see 
“Introduction to Cybernetic Regulation”): 
• Modeler: builds and updates the internal World Model of body and environment; supplies Focal 
Target Information to the Controller; and generates candidate bottom-up attention requests 
based on current World Model contents, salience, and goal relevance.

The World Model consists of three interrelated components—the Concrete World Model built from 
current sensory inputs, the Memories of the Concrete World Model, and the Abstract World Model 
(see Figure 1). It also includes the associations linking objects to related concepts and memories. 
Our central thesis is that conscious experience originates in the Modelerschema, which regulates the 
Modeler. The Modelerschema monitors the Modeler’s updates to the World Model and performs a 
qualia-based consistency check; when a discrepancy is detected, the Modeler is prompted to modify its 
processing accordingly, and a bottom-up attention request may also be generated to bring the relevant 
content into focal attention for the Controller as a precaution. 
Generic cybernetic regulation is not suﬃcient for consciousness. If it were, any feedback system would 
qualify, making the theory too permissive to distinguish conscious from nonconscious systems. We 
avoid this by tying experience to the Modelerschema’s continuous checks for representational consistency 
in the Modeler’s construction of the World Model, rather than to generic agent regulation. While many 
control systems update internal models through feedback, these updates typically operate on task-
speciﬁc variables to guide particular actions, whereas the Modelerschema evaluates consistency across 
integrated representations of the perceived world and the self, enabling reﬁnement of those 
representations. 
The Modeler constructs and maintains the World Model, while in parallel, the Modelerschema constructs 
an internal Quale World Model consisting of qualia representations derived from the Modeler’s 
outputs. These two models are distinct but tightly coupled: the World Model supports prediction and 
action, while the Quale World Model supports consistency checking and model reﬁnement, as will be 
described later. We also describe fast-Modelers and fast-Controllers as evolutionary shortcuts that 
enable rapid actions which precede awareness. 
The structures and functions of these agents and of the World Model are described in more detail in 
“The Modeler, Controller, and Targeter Agents” and “The Three-Part Structure of the World Model.” In 
“Solving the Hard Problem of Consciousness,” we use this framework to explain how locating qualia in 
the Modelerschema, and distinguishing it from the nonconscious Controller, addresses the Hard Problem 
and clariﬁes why consciousness appears so mysterious from the Controller’s perspective—that is, from 
the perspective of the reporting Human Agent.

Introduction to Cybernetic Regulation 
Cybernetics, a foundational inﬂuence on cognitive science, provides the principles of control, feedback, 
and regulation that underlie the Modelerschema framework. The term “cybernetics” was coined by 
Norbert Wiener in his 1948 book. Although the term does not appear in their publications, the Good 
Regulator Theorem (Conant & Ashby, 1970) and Friston’s Free Energy Principle (Friston, 2010) are 
widely considered part of the cybernetic tradition. Ashby is recognized as a co-founder of the ﬁeld, and 
both works formalize the idea that eﬀective regulation requires modeling: a regulator must contain an 
internal model of the system it seeks to control. In Conant & Ashby, this typically refers to the regulator 
modeling its own system. For example, the Controller would regulate itself by containing a model of 
itself. Friston also frames a regulator as modeling its external environment (i.e., the world) to minimize 
prediction errors. Thus, if an agent contains an explicit model of the world, the regulating agent must 
contain a model of that world model. 
In our theory, internal modeling is delegated to a dedicated control or schema agent paired with, but 
distinct from each agent. Thus, each “agent” is paired with a unique cybernetic control agent, its 
schema2 agent, which monitors and optimizes3 its functions. For the Human, Controller, Modeler, and 
Targeter agents, the corresponding cybernetic control agents are Humanschema, Controllerschema, 
Modelerschema, and Targeterschema. To situate our theory within the broader cybernetic and cognitive 
science context, we now compare our architecture with Friston’s predictive coding framework. 
The World Model is dynamic: the Modeler uses it to predict changes in the environment and sensory 
input. This predictive role parallels Friston’s generative model of bodily dynamics, which anticipates 
future states of the body. Because the body is part of the world, the generative model must encompass 
both external and internal states. In our architecture, Friston’s framework maps onto the Modeler, the 
World Model, and the Controller. Since the Controller controls the body, integrating the model of the 
body with the Controller and its control agent (the Controllerschema) yields the Controllerwhole, which

While we are awake, the Modeler constructs and continuously reﬁnes the World Model from sensory

modiﬁes its assumptions accordingly. To support this role, the Modelerschema maintains internal models 
of the relatively static Modeler and the rapidly changing sensory portion of the World Model. 
Friston’s Free Energy Principle explains how the brain minimizes prediction error and guides behavior, 
and our Modeler–World Model–Controller architecture aligns with this framework. However, the Free 
Energy Principle does not specify what subjective experience should be like. Taken literally, lower 
peripheral precision might be expected to produce a blurry percept, yet ordinary experience feels 
uniformly crisp, and saccades yield continuity rather than surprise because the Modeler normally 
compensates for the lower resolution of peripheral vision before the Modelerschema generates the 
corresponding qualia. That is why peripheral vision ordinarily feels more accurate than its lower 
receptor density alone would predict. Only when the pre- and post-saccadic representations diﬀer 
more than expected would the Modelerschema detect a mismatch. We therefore accept the Free Energy 
account for behavior while proposing that conscious experience–the feeling of vision–requires an 
additional mechanism: the Modelerschema’s generation and use of qualia for consistency checking and 
model reﬁnement. 
The Three-Part Structure of the World Model 
Any animal-like agent requires a World Model to navigate its environment. This World Model contains: 
• A dynamic Concrete World Model that serves as short-term memory, continuously updated 
from all current sensory inputs. Rather than storing raw data, it maintains a structured 
representation of recognized objects and their spatial relationships. The Modeler creates it to 
enable predictions of probable future states and to support the Controller’s interactions with 
the environment. Objects in this model become focal experiences when selected as attention 
targets. Emotions generated by the Controller are treated as an internal (interoceptive) sense; 
their contents are also represented in the Concrete World Model. The entire Concrete World 
Model is continuously available to “diﬀuse awareness” (deﬁned later). 
• Memories of the Concrete World Model store medium-, long-, and permanent records of past 
objects and newly imagined ones. These are experienced only when they become focal targets. 
Once a concrete object or associated emotion is no longer actively sensed, it is reclassiﬁed as 
part of the Memories of the Concrete World Model and retained through long-term changes in 
internal representations. When a sensory memory is recalled, these internal representations are

used to recreate a Focal Target Information stream approximating the one generated by the 
original concrete target. 
• The Abstract World Model is relatively rudimentary in most animals, but extensive in humans. It 
stores concepts and relations across levels of abstraction and time scales, from names and 
words to mathematics, representing generalizations over objects and their relations in the 
Concrete World Model and its associated Memories. Like the Memories of the Concrete World 
Model, it includes short-, medium-, and long-term representations: long-term structure is 
encoded in persistent internal representations that can be reactivated when needed, while 
current abstract focal targets are maintained as transient activity patterns. 
Although predictive coding frameworks encode memory in learned parameters or internal 
representations, this theory distinguishes three functional manifestations of this encoding. The 
Concrete World Model corresponds to the current, actively updated state that reﬂects short-term 
integration of sensory input. The Memories of the Concrete World Model represent the persistence of 
past states over longer time scales. The Abstract World Model extends this principle further, storing 
highly compressed and symbolic regularities acquired through learning. In all cases, what predictive 
coding would treat as diﬀerent timescales of updating is here treated as distinct but interrelated 
components of a single representational system. 
Therefore, we group the Concrete World Model, Memories of the Concrete World Model, and the 
Abstract World Model under “World Model.” Each embodies the same underlying principle: the storage 
of structured representations of the body and environment at diﬀerent time horizons and levels of 
abstraction. 
The World Model, in this sense, is not a separate anatomical entity but a functional designation for the 
ensemble of representations that the Modeler constructs and maintains, and that the Controller relies 
on to plan and act. It encodes an implicit understanding of physical principles (e.g., motion, object 
interactions) and basic psychological principles (e.g., body language, social expectations). This 
knowledge enables the Controller to predict the physical dynamics of objects and infer the behavior 
and intentions of agents, since the Concrete World Model provides the structured representations that 
enable those predictions. By integrating predictive models of physical and social dynamics, the 
Concrete World Model helps the Controller achieve its goals. 
In this theory, the World Model encodes the physical world through a continuum of increasingly 
abstract representations. At one end are concrete concepts that correspond to tangible objects and 
events in the current or past Concrete World Model. Further along the spectrum are abstract concepts, 
beginning with names and words tied to concrete objects, and culminating in fully abstract ideas, such 
as those in mathematics. In all three components, the continuity and stability of these representations 
ultimately depend on persistent internal representations, with shorter timescales sustaining the 
Concrete World Model and longer timescales supporting memories and abstract concepts.

Whole Agents and Consciousness Attribution 
Having introduced the Controllerwhole informally, we now deﬁne a Whole Agent and formalize it with 
explicit body and symbolic representations. A Whole Agent is an integrated structure comprising a 
functional agent, its cybernetic control agent (“agentschema”), and any symbolic representations of that 
agent in the World Model. This deﬁnition applies to all agents and provides the basis for attributing 
consciousness within our framework. 
The Controllerwhole comprises the Controller, its cybernetic agent (Controllerschema), and two symbolic 
representations:  
• The Bodymodel encodes bodily state and dynamics in the Concrete World Model and its 
Memories. It may also incorporate frequently used tools as part of the body representation. 
• The I/Me/Mymodel represents abstract identity, personal history, and conceptual self in the 
Abstract World Model. When it is linked to the Bodymodel in the Concrete World Model and its 
Memories, together they represent the entire human within the World Model.

In this theory, diﬀuse awareness plays a vital role: recognizing that the Controllerwhole cannot access it is 
key to unlocking the Hard Problem of consciousness.

Ninety years later, (Treisman & Gelade, 1980) began with:

predictive model of physical and social environments.

Assessment evaluates how the current world state aﬀects the Human Agent’s goals. If an update

bottom-up attention request. However, assessments are limited: they detect single features or simple

The Concrete World Model is updated continuously from current sensory input while we are awake; the 
Focal Target Information stream is updated as needed to ensure the Controller receives current 
information about its tracked targets. These updates are stored in short-, medium-, or long-term 
memory for both concrete and abstract representations.12 
These three processes—understanding, assessing and updating—are continuous, often overlap, and 
may together produce a distinct class of Appraisable Emotions. 
Appraisable Emotions 
We propose that this ongoing cycle, especially the Modeler’s understanding and assessment, gives rise 
to Appraisable Emotions.13 
Appraisable Emotions, adjectival or adverbial, reﬂect how a situation is internally processed. Unlike 
Controller-generated emotions such as anger or fear (typically expressed as nouns), Appraisable 
Emotions arise in the Modeler as it processes input and updates the World Model. They function as 
internal senses that track progress in understanding, assessing, or updating, and are naturally 
expressed adjectivally or adverbially. These states become conscious only when represented in the 
Quale World Model.

The Four Types of Qualia 
In this theory, qualia are internally represented experiences generated and “experienced” only within 
the Modelerschema. They do not constitute an additional non-physical substance, but arise from the 
system’s internal representations and are used for consistency checking. These qualia fall into four 
types: 
• Sensory Qualia encompass perceptual experiences and emotions, with emotions treated as 
internal senses reﬂecting the Controller’s current state (e.g., fear, sadness, joy). These qualia

explanatory gap by locating experience within a single, self-contained mechanism (the Modelerschema). 
In this theory, sensory input produces the Concrete World Model, a structured, multisensory map of 
the external environment. The Modelerschema applies the quale-conversion process to transform this

Dynamics of the Concrete World Model 
A stable, coherent model of the world is essential for the Controllerwhole to plan and execute goal-
directed actions. The brain constructs the Concrete World Model by integrating multimodal sensory 
input; however, vision requires special handling due to frequent saccadic eye movements. These rapid 
shifts occur several times per second, yet the visual world is experienced as remarkably stable and 
continuous.

To account for this apparent stability, prior work has proposed complementary mechanisms underlying 
visual processing. According to (Burr & Morrone, 2012), vision relies on two complementary systems, 
which we describe as: 
• Transient saccadic process:21 a fast visual mechanism in eye-centered (retinotopic) coordinates 
(≈200 ms) that preserves continuity across saccades and supports rapid visual judgments.22 
• Stable spatial map: A 3D multisensory, spatiotopic body/world-centered representation 
constructed more slowly in dorsal pathways (≈500 ms). 
These systems cooperate to ensure seamless perception. The transient process holds the focal pre-
saccadic image constant during the saccade, preventing conscious awareness of the eye’s movement. 
At the end of the saccade, it aligns pre- and post-saccadic focal visual input to seamlessly continue 
updating the focal visual portion of the Concrete World Model.23 When not saccading, visual input 
ﬂows continuously into the stable spatial map. 
We treat Burr and Morrone’s “stable spatial map” as functionally equivalent to our multisensory 
Concrete World Model, which supports the Controllerwhole’s interaction with the sensory world. It 
integrates spatial and object-level information, enabling the Controllerwhole to plan and execute actions 
to achieve its goals.  
“This transient system cooperates with the spatiotopic mechanism to provide a useful map to 
guide interactions with our environment: one rapid and transitory… the other slow, long-lasting, 
and low-resolution…” (Burr & Morrone, 2012) 
Ordinary interaction relies on the stable map despite its ≈500 ms delay. During this interval, the 
Modelerwhole updates the Concrete World Model, and the Controllerwhole begins a response; muscle 
movement does not start until ≈500 ms. When faster action is needed, humans can respond in ≈200 ms 
via the transient process, bypassing slower updates. These fast responses, distinct from spinal cord and 
brainstem reﬂexes, are driven by simpliﬁed systems we call fast-Modelers and fast-Controllers. Here, 
the distinction is functional: fast responses do not depend on full World Model construction and 
updating, but instead rely on limited, task-speciﬁc information passed from the fast-Modeler to the 
fast-Controller. Thus, the ≈200 ms delay is the interval required for the fast-Modeler and the initial fast-
Controller systems to process the input and initiate a motor action. 
The transient fast-Modeler provides immediate high-resolution input to the fast-Controller for urgent 
actions, bypassing the stable Concrete World Model. Meanwhile, the stable model supports goal-
directed reasoning by the Controllerwhole. Though slower, its spatiotopic multisensory integration makes 
it essential for deliberate interaction.

The Proposed Experiment 
The proposed visual experiment tests whether the Modelerschema performs a consistency check on 
qualia across saccades during Step 5. This consistency check should produce a bottom-up attention 
request if a peripheral, non-focal visual object changes during a saccade, likely accompanied by a 
“surprised” adjectival emotion. In principle, such a bottom-up attention request could arise from Steps 
1, 3c, 4, or 5. Step 2 is excluded because it handles only non-visual data. We present arguments and 
supporting evidence to rule out Step 4. If the predicted results are observed, they would rule out Steps 
1 and 3c, leaving Step 5 as the sole source of bottom-up attention requests generated by this 
experiment during saccades. 
Participants may report that something drew attention, yet may be unable to identify the speciﬁc 
feature that changed. Feature identiﬁcation requires the Controllerwhole to compare the pre-saccadic 
and post-saccadic focal targets of the same object. If the object becomes a target only post-saccade, 
the Controllerwhole lacks a pre-saccadic object representation and can report only that something has 
changed. Nevertheless, the instructions will ask participants to indicate what changed or to note that it 
was only a hunch if they are uncertain. 
Intuitively, Step 1 detection is diﬃcult because the image sweeps rapidly across the retina during a 
saccade. Accordingly, we ensure that object changes occur at a relatively slow rate compared with the 
instantaneous eye velocity. The eye’s velocity during a saccade follows a bell-shaped curve. We give the 
size, position, and color changes the same smooth temporal curve, scaling their rates to a small, ﬁxed 
fraction of the eye’s instantaneous velocity, so they remain slow relative to ocular motion. 
Independent of these measures to limit Step 1 detections, Step 3c is responsible for aligning the 
intended saccade target with the actual landing point. When there is a misalignment, a corrective 
microsaccade may follow, or the Modeler may shift the image data to compensate. We propose 
additional experimental methods to rule out Steps 1 and 3c as sources of bottom-up attention 
requests. Together with the arguments excluding Step 4, successful results would thereby identify Step 
5 as the source of such attention requests and support the theory.

Diﬀerentiating Step 1 Target Requests from Step 5 Target Requests 
Here we concentrate on Steps 1 and 5. Other intermediate steps are considered later. This theory 
predicts that during saccades, relatively small, permanent changes in the size, position, or color of 
objects will be detected by Step 5 and generate a bottom-up attention request. Only Step 1 can detect 
temporary visual changes because Step 3 holds the pre-saccadic image constant until the end of the 
saccade. Therefore, only Step 1 can, in principle, detect both temporary and permanent visual changes.

velocity for size or position and color-change rate for color. Therefore, if Step 1 alone were responsible 
for generating a bottom-up attention request, temporary changes of the same magnitude would be 
more detectable than permanent ones. The strongest evidence for Step 5 would be the detection of 
permanent changes when temporary changes of the same magnitude are not detected. This result

intended goal and aligns post-saccadic input with the pre-saccadic preview at that location. When the 
target is shifted slightly during a saccade, observers typically do not report the displacement, so no

Schneider, 1998). In addition, landmarks near the saccade target act as spatial references that bias 
perceived stability and displacement after the eye lands, further linking post-saccadic localization to 
objects found at the landing site (Deubel, Koch, & Bridgeman, 2010).

Why Step 4 Cannot be the Source of the Bottom-Up Attention Request 
During Step 4, the Modeler understands and assesses multisensory input, while updating the focal-
target portion of the Concrete World Model. The literature cited in this Proposed Experiment section 
suggests that the Concrete World Model has two functionally distinct components: 
• Focal Attention Information: For each focal target, one or more sensory systems contribute data 
that are integrated into a uniﬁed object representation. For example, during manual 
manipulation, visual, tactile, and sometimes auditory information are combined to support 
precise control. Even when only a single sense contributes, that input is processed to yield the 
best available task-relevant model. This Focal Attention Information feeds the Focal Target 
Information stream available to the Controller (see Figure 2). 
• Diﬀuse Attention Information: All incoming sensory data are understood and assessed at an 
initial level, including scene gist extraction and coarse object categorization, to determine 
whether any item merits promotion to a focal target. Regions that are not current targets, and 
for which no bottom-up attention requests are sent to the Targeter, receive only this preliminary 
analysis. However, these diﬀuse-awareness data must remain accessible to the Modelerschema so

recalled visual targets often lack the vivid qualities of direct perception and vary widely across 
individuals, from aphantasia to hyperphantasia. Therefore, the Quale World Model’s concrete 
representation cannot be identical to the Concrete World Model,28 and the Modelerschema component

representation diﬀerent from the Concrete World Model (i.e., an abstracted model), and there must be 
distinct quale-conversion processes for the three parts of the World Model. 
Therefore, if the experiment rules out Step 1 and Step 3c, and the foregoing arguments and evidence 
rule out Step 4, the remaining candidate is Step 5, the Modelerschema’s qualia-consistency check. This 
process performs the pre-saccadic versus post-saccadic comparison and issues a bottom-up attention 
request for a permanently changed, non-focal object. A positive result on these tests would support the 
prediction that Step 5 is the sole source of such requests and would strengthen our proposed answer to 
the Hard Problem.

We now turn to the implications of our theory for the Hard Problem of consciousness. 
Solving the Hard Problem of Consciousness 
Suppose science has precisely determined all the functions the brain performs to produce behavior. In 
this case, Chalmers deﬁnes the Hard Problem of consciousness as the question, “Why is the 
performance of these functions accompanied by experience?” [italics in the original] (Chalmers, 1995). 
This appears impossible to solve: if experience aﬀects behavior, then some subset of those functions 
must produce consciousness; yet phenomenal consciousness does not seem reducible to the logical 
transformations and functions that neurons implement.  
On the other hand, if experiences do not aﬀect behavior, then why do they exist at all? The 
Modelerschema Theory addresses this by granting that experiences do not directly aﬀect behavior on 
short timescales but instead assigns them a long-term functional role.  
Indeed, if a stroke selectively disabled the Modelerschema while leaving all other brain functions intact, 
the person would behave essentially normally yet lack experiences; about as close as one can get to a 
philosophical “zombie.” However, this “zombie” would be detectable: without the Modelerschema, some 
appraisable-emotion signals that depend on qualia would be missing. The Modeler would still supply its 
own appraisable-emotion signals; the absent signals are those tied speciﬁcally to qualia computed by 
the Modelerschema. Consequently, the Controllerwhole would not furnish the same range of experiential 
reports—descriptions would lack some experiential adjectives and adverbs, yet it could still support 
ﬁne discriminations. For example, such a person could judge a shade of red as intermediate between 
two others by its RGB values and could name colors by those values. 
On the other hand, if the Modelerschema were disabled in infancy, the brain would not develop normally 
because the Modeler’s algorithms would not be monitored or improved. The resulting adult would be 
severely impaired. We propose that avoiding this outcome is the evolutionary function of qualia within 
the Modelerschema. 
In the short term, this theory holds that consciousness does not directly aﬀect behavior and is 
therefore epiphenomenal with respect to immediate action. Over developmental timescales, however, 
consciousness calibrates and optimizes the Modeler, yielding adaptive advantages; accordingly, it is not 
epiphenomenal in the broader evolutionary and developmental sense.

First, addressing the “what” question, Experiential Unity speciﬁes a single mechanism in which the

Why We Misattribute Consciousness: The Control Hierarchy Illusion 
Given this architecture, a natural puzzle arises: why does the Humanwhole claim to be conscious, even 
though only the Modelerschema experiences qualia? The answer lies in the control hierarchy. The 
Modelerschema is embedded within the Humanschema, which functions as the cybernetic regulator of the 
Humanwhole. Because the conscious agent is nested inside the agent that governs the entire system, the 
Humanwhole, as a system, reports consciousness. However, in everyday thinking, the Humanwhole tends 
to identify with the Controllerwhole—the agent that selects actions, produces language, controls the 
Bodymodel, and identiﬁes with the I/Me/Mymodel. This misidentiﬁcation is the root of the “problem” in 
the Hard Problem of consciousness. For the Controllerwhole, which is entirely nonconscious, 
consciousness is indeed an insoluble mystery. It can comment on experience only through appraisable 
emotion signals supplied by the Modelerschema. Although it uses words like “experience” and 
“consciousness,” it lacks phenomenality. 
Diﬀuse awareness provides evidence that the Controllerwhole is not the locus of consciousness since it 
can only report focal target content. This supports locating consciousness outside the Controllerwhole, 
most plausibly in the Modelerschema. Moreover, because the Controllerwhole can report on experience 
without directly accessing qualia, the architecture requires an intermediary evaluative signal. We

machinery that acts and the machinery that experiences creates the illusion that consciousness is not

processes (Concrete, Memories of the Concrete, Abstract), with strong variability outside the 
evolutionarily constrained sensory Concrete World Model channel (see “Distinct Qualia from 
Distinct World Model Components” and Table 1). 
• Saccadic stability as a training signal (why qualia are needed): Visual stability across saccades 
(Burr & Morrone, 2012), (Wells-Gray, Choi, & Bries, 2016) motivates a qualia-based comparison 
within the Modelerschema that detects mismatches and trains the Modeler, yielding optimized 
qualia30 rather than the task-oriented object encodings used by the Concrete World Model. The 
proposed experiment, if conﬁrmed, would provide evidence that these qualia are computed 
within the Modelerschema. 
• Peripheral visual data remapping (where remapping occurs): As discussed in “Diﬀerentiating 
Step 3c Target Requests from Step 5 Target Requests” in “The Proposed Experiment,” the 
Modeler remaps focal target data so focal images appear stable to the Controllerwhole during a 
saccade. However, evidence indicates that the Modeler does not remap peripheral, non-focal 
data (Melcher & Colby, 2008). Since diﬀuse visual awareness is stable both centrally and 
peripherally, the periphery must be remapped within the Modelerschema. Because this

Although we have mainly discussed vision, similar considerations apply to all sensory representations in 
the Concrete World Model. In each sensory modality, a corresponding quale-conversion process would 
allow the Modelerschema to optimize that sensory channel. Including all sensory modalities is especially 
important for multisensory events in which qualia from diﬀerent senses are attributed to a single

perceived location of the voice, biasing it toward the dummy’s mouth despite audition’s lower spatial 
precision. Thus, the Modelerschema generates the experience of the voice as coming from the dummy, 
even though the Controllerwhole knows the dummy cannot speak.

model reﬁnement. In this framework, the Hard Problem becomes a design problem: identify the qualia-
generating agent (the Modelerschema), specify its quale-conversion processes and consistency checks, 
and test the behavioral consequences of these mechanisms, such as the proposed experiment. 
Possible Objections to this Theory 
One possible objection is that the Modelerschema and its associated components are not clearly 
identiﬁable as distinct anatomical structures in the brain. However, this theory is formulated at a 
functional cognitive science level of description. The Modeler, Controller, Targeter, and their associated 
schema agents are deﬁned in terms of roles, information ﬂows, and interactions within a cognitive 
architecture, not in terms of ﬁxed neural substrates. As such, the absence of a one-to-one mapping to 
speciﬁc brain regions does not count against the theory. Many successful frameworks in cognitive 
science and control theory operate at this level of abstraction, with neural implementation treated as a 
separate question. 
A second objection is that the theory introduces multiple interacting components, which may appear 
unnecessarily complex. However, the proposed architecture is motivated by the need to explain a 
speciﬁc set of phenomena—most notably the stability and coherence of conscious experience across 
disruptions such as saccades. Each component plays a distinct functional role in generating and 
maintaining this stability. This theory is therefore not intended to minimize the number of components, 
but to include the components needed to account for the phenomena it targets. 
A third objection is that the Modelerschema, as a central element of the theory, may seem diﬃcult to test 
empirically. In response, the theory generates a concrete, falsiﬁable prediction: that changes to 
peripheral stimuli during saccades can be detected under speciﬁc conditions that distinguish between 
focal-target information and the contents of the Quale World Model. The proposed experiment is 
designed to test this prediction directly. If the predicted pattern of detection does not occur, the theory 
would be undermined. 
Finally, one might object that the theory relies on abstract constructs such as the Quale World Model 
and internal representations, which are not directly observable. This is a common feature of theories in

Conclusion 
The Modelerschema Theory of Consciousness identiﬁes a speciﬁc, abstract control agent implemented in 
the brain as the sole generative source of conscious experience. By integrating subject, process, and 
content within a single cybernetic agent, it oﬀers a path toward closing the explanatory gap. Qualia are 
not mysterious byproducts but functional internal constructs that calibrate and optimize the World 
Model. Unlike accounts that merely describe awareness, this theory speciﬁes testable mechanisms and 
explicit falsiﬁcation criteria. 
If conﬁrmed, the proposed saccadic-consistency experiment would support the hypothesis that qualia 
serve a crucial developmental function in maintaining perceptual stability and accuracy. Conversely, if 
temporary changes (of matched magnitude) are reliably more detectable than permanent ones in 
saccade trials with approximate isoluminance controls, or if permanent peripheral changes are not 
detected at all, this would falsify the Modelerschema Theory. 
Taken together, the arguments and evidence presented here—along with the experiment’s predicted 
results—support considering the Modelerschema as a plausible source of human consciousness. We oﬀer 
this theory as a starting point and welcome feedback.
