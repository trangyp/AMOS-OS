Introduction
A subgraph H of an edge-colored graph G is called an rainbow subgraph if no two edges of H
share the same color. In 1973, Erd˝os, Simonovits, and Sós [5] introduced the anti-Ramsey number
arP(n, H) as the maximum integer t such that there exists an edge-coloring of Kn using exactly t
colors that contains no rainbow subgraph isomorphic to H. This concept is closely related to the
rainbow number rb(G, H), defined as the minimum number of colors k for which any edge-coloring
of G with at least k colors guarantees a rainbow subgraph isomorphic to H. It follows directly from
these definitions that rb(Kn, H) = arP(n, H) + 1.
In wireless sensor networks or satellite communication, signal transmission may fail due to fre-
quency conflicts or malicious interference. By assigning distinct frequency bands (colors) to edges,
the rainbow number rb(G, H) quantifies the minimum number of bands required to guarantee an
interference-resistant path (rainbow path) between any two nodes. For instance, if rb(G, P3) = k,
then at least k bands are needed to ensure a path without consecutive same-frequency edges (P3
denotes a 2-edge path). In encrypted networks, rainbow paths model multi-hop encrypted relays,
where each edge represents an encryption layer and colors denote unique keys; the minimality
of rb(G, H) prevents attackers from decrypting entire paths by intercepting monochromatic edges,
enhancing security. In protein interaction networks, vertices represent proteins, edges denote in-
teractions, and colors signify functional categories (e.g., catalysis, signaling); a small rb(G, K3)
indicates stable triads of functionally diverse proteins (e.g., kinase-phosphatase-substrate com-
plexes), critical for understanding cellular regulation. In social networks, colors reflect topics
(e.g., tech, entertainment, politics), and rb(G, S 4) (for star subgraphs) measures the difficulty of ac-
cessing diverse information via colorful paths; a high rb(G, S 4) suggests topic homogeneity among
an influencer’s followers, necessitating algorithmic interventions to diversify content and mitigate
“filter bubbles.”
Rainbow numbers (or anti-Ramsey numbers) for some special graph classes have been stud-
ied in the literature [1, 4, 6, 9, 12, 13, 31]; researchers have also investigated rainbow numbers in
specific host graph families, including Kneser graphs [16], bipartite graphs [21], complete multi-
partite graphs [2, 19, 33], planar graphs [8], bipartite planar graph [15, 28, 29], plane triangulations
[3, 10, 12, 20, 24–27], and outerplanar graphs [17, 18, 30]. Notably, the hypergraph variant of this
problem has received dedicated attention [7, 14, 22, 23, 32].
In particular, Jendrol’, Schiermeyer, and Tu [12] pioneered the study of rainbow numbers in
plane triangulations by focusing on matchings; for a matching of size k (denoted Mk), they es-
tablished exact value for rb(Tn, Mt) when t ≤4, and derived lower/upper bounds for all t ≥5
with n ≥2t, where Tn denotes the class of plane triangulations on n vertices and rb(Tn, Mt) :=
max{rb(G, Mt) | G ∈Tn}. Subsequently, Qin, Lan, and Shi [24] determined the exact value of
rb(Tn, M5) for n ≥11 and improved the upper bound to rb(Tn, Mt) ≤2n + 6t −16 for all t ≥5
and n ≥2t. Chen, Lan, and Song [3] further obtained the exact value of rb(Tn, M6) and refined
