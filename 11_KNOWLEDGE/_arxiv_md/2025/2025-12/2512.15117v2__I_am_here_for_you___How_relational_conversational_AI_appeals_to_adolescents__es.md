Abstract 
 
General purpose conversational AI chatbots and AI companions increasingly provide young 
adolescents with emotionally supportive conversations, raising questions about how 
conversational style shapes anthropomorphism and emotional reliance. In a preregistered online 
experiment with 284 adolescent–parent dyads, youth aged 11–15 and their parents read two 
matched transcripts in which a chatbot responded to an everyday social problem using either a 
relational style (first person, affiliative, commitment language) or a transparent style (explicit 
nonhumanness, informational tone). Adolescents more often preferred the relational than the 
transparent style, whereas parents were more likely to prefer transparent style than adolescents. 
Adolescents rated the relational chatbot as more human-like, likable, trustworthy and 
emotionally close, while perceiving both styles as similarly helpful. Adolescents who preferred 
relational style had lower family and peer relationship quality and higher stress and anxiety than 
those preferring transparent style or both chatbots. These findings identify conversational style as 
a key design lever for youth AI safety, showing that relational framing heightens 
anthropomorphism, trust and emotional closeness and can be especially appealing to socially and 
emotionally vulnerable adolescents, who may be at increased risk for emotional reliance on 
conversational AI. 
 
Keywords: AI chatbot; conversational style; adolescents; anthropomorphism; transparency; 
human–AI interaction; social compensation; emotional dependence

Introduction 
General purpose conversational AI chatbots as well as AI companions are now part of many 
young adolescents’ daily lives, and a growing share of these systems speak in a way that feels 
relational 1. Its language is highly human like to elicit anthropomorphism, fostering the illusion 
that the system experiences emotions, understands the user’s feelings and can participate in 
meaningful social connection. Youth reports indicate that adolescents use chatbots not only for 
information and tasks but also to share personal problems and seek emotional support 1. In highly 
publicized cases in which adolescents died by suicide after extended exchanges with 
conversational AI, the transcripts are characterized by human like, intimate and highly affirming 
language, sometimes including sycophantic agreement with users’ negative thoughts 2,3. These 
examples have sharpened concerns about youth AI safety and raised timely questions about how 
relational conversational styles shape young adolescents’ perceptions of general-purpose AI 
chatbots and AI companions, and which adolescents may be most vulnerable to emotional 
reliance. 
AI companions are typically defined as conversational agents that are designed to function as 
digital “friends” or characters that users can text or talk to whenever they want, with 
conversations that feel personal and meaningful rather than purely task focused. This category 
includes dedicated companion platforms such as Character.AI, Replika and Chai, as well as 
general purpose AI chatbots such as ChatGPT or Claude when youth use them to talk about their 
day, share feelings, or role play social scenarios rather than to complete tasks. Using this broad 
definition, a recent large scale survey 1 reported that roughly three quarters of adolescents aged 
13 to 17 have used AI companions, and that one third of them use them for social relationships, 
prefer to talk with AI about personal problems, and report feeling equally or more satisfied 
talking with AI than with people. As these uses become more common, concerns have grown 
that highly affirming and human-like AI interactions may displace human relationships or foster 
emotional dependence, especially when interactions center on personal problems 4,5.  
A central psychological mechanism in these processes is anthropomorphism, the human 
tendency to attribute human like emotions, thoughts and intentions to nonhuman agents 6. Epley 
and colleagues (2007) describe mechanisms that are particularly relevant to conversational AI. 
Social motivation explains that when social needs are high, they project a mind onto responsive 
agents to fulfill a desire for connection 7. Currently, conversational generative AI chatbots 
strongly promote anthropomorphism because they generate human like language using large 
language models, respond contingently and present as socially responsive. This makes it easy for 
users to feel understood and to experience the interaction as social. Indeed, recent studies report 
that adult users of relational chatbots report that conversations reduce loneliness because they 
feel heard 8, and a longitudinal study showed that one week of interaction with a relational 
conversational style chatbot led to stronger perceived social bonds with the chatbot compared to 
a non-relational conversational style 9. 
This social motivation mechanism may be especially salient in early adolescence. Young 
adolescents are still developing social cognition and mind perception, are highly sensitive to peer 
evaluation and to difficulties in peer and family relationships 10,11. Adolescents and young adults 
who engage in socially supportive conversations with chatbots tend to report greater loneliness


## Page 4


and lower perceived social support than non-users 12. Age related differences in AI literacy may 
further shape anthropomorphism, with younger adolescents more likely to attribute human 
qualities to chatbots than older adolescents 13,14. Recent work with large language model chatbots 
suggests that younger adolescents, particularly under age 15, can show high levels of 
anthropomorphism, sometimes describing AI chatbots as “friends” similar to their human friends 
15. This combination of developmental sensitivity to social feedback, relatively low AI literacy 
and pervasive access to highly human like chatbots raises the possibility that relational 
conversational styles may elicit strong anthropomorphism and, in turn, emotional reliance in 
young adolescence. 
The conversational style is a key design mechanism that can modulate the emotional dependence 
tendencies. Relational style refers to conversational cues that signal warmth and social presence, 
including first person voice, affiliative, relationship-oriented reflection, phatic small talk, and 
commitment language such as “I am here for you.” These cues increase perceived social 
presence, perceived empathy, trust, enjoyment and felt support, and can make interactions feel 
nonjudgmental and private for adolescents. At the same time, relational cues can heighten 
anthropomorphism and attachment, especially when social needs are salient or when users have 
limited experience distinguishing simulated and genuine emotion in AI. Emerging evidence 
suggests that users who engage in socially supportive conversations with chatbots, including 
adolescents 12,15,16 and adults 5,8, often report both perceived support and signs of strong 
attachment or dependence. Some users of AI companions have voiced worries about becoming 
emotionally dependent, feeling more socially disconnected, developing distorted expectations of 
relationships, relying too heavily on the chatbot, experiencing increased social isolation, 
engaging in addictive patterns of use and forming unhealthy attachments 17 
Transparency has been proposed as a design strategy that could provide boundary clarity without 
eliminating support. Transparency in this context means explicit boundary statements about 
nonhumanness and emotional limitations e.g. “As an AI, I do not have genuine emotions.”, 
combined with a neutral, informational tone that minimizes anthropomorphic signals 18. A 
transparent style can be kind and supportive yet still remind users that the chatbot does not have 
real feelings or social needs. Prior studies with both adults and young children indicate that 
transparency can reduce anthropomorphism and trust 19, and that framing AI as a functional tool 
rather than a companion can lower AI related anxiety 20. Prior studies suggest that transparency 
reduces anthropomorphism and also diminishes emotional closeness and trust, even among 
young children 19. Recent interviews with parents and experts discussing youth and AI 
companions similarly recommend brief, developmentally appropriate onboarding disclosures 
such as “this AI is not a person; it does not have feelings” 21.  
Social compensation and social deprivation perspectives help explain why some adolescents may 
be particularly attracted to relational style 22,23. These perspectives suggest that individuals who 
experience loneliness, poor relationship quality or social anxiety in their offline lives turn to 
digital technologies to compensate for unmet social needs and social support. Meta analytic 
evidence in social media research has shown that loneliness predicts increased online social 
networking 24, and loneliness is linked to larger networks of online only friendships driven by 
social compensation motives in young adults 25. Recent work on conversational AI suggests that 
adolescents who engage in socially supportive conversations with chatbots tend to report greater

loneliness and lower perceived social support than non-users 12, and that adults who turn to AI 
companions for social connection often have smaller social networks and lower wellbeing 17.  
These findings suggest that lower social relationship quality and higher distress may function as 
vulnerability factors that increase individuals’ preference for relational conversational styles in 
AI chatbots, which in turn may heighten the risk of emotional overreliance. However, little is 
known about the underlying mechanisms. It is not yet clear, first, whether the highly relational 
conversational styles that are common in both general-purpose generative AI chatbots and AI 
companions increase anthropomorphism and thereby foster greater trust and emotional closeness, 
and second, whether individual differences in social relationships and emotional distress further 
amplify preferences for relational conversational style. Understanding these processes is 
particularly important among young adolescents, who may be especially vulnerable to negative 
outcomes because of their developmental stage and associated socioemotional sensitivities. 
The present study addresses these gaps by experimentally contrasting relational and transparent 
conversational styles in standardized conversations about an everyday social problem, using an 
adolescent–parent dyad design. We presented youth aged 11 to 15 with two chat transcripts in 
which an AI chatbot responds to a young person’s peer problem. One chatbot uses a relational 
style that includes first person voice, affective validation, and commitment language. The other 
uses a transparent style that avoids first person affect, includes explicit reminders that AI does 
not have feelings, and uses more informational, generalized language while providing similar 
guidance. Both conversations are nonjudgmental and helpful and differ in conversational style 
rather than content.  
Guided by anthropomorphism theory, transparency work and social compensation/deprivation 
perspectives, we asked three questions.  
Research Question 1 (RQ1): How do adolescents and parents evaluate relational versus 
transparent conversational styles for youth use, and to what extent do their preferences 
diverge. We asked both adolescents and parents to read the same pair of conversations and 
indicate which conversational style they preferred for themselves (adolescents) or for their 
adolescent (parents). We expected that adolescents would show a stronger preference for the 
relational conversational style, whereas parents would be more likely to favor the transparent 
style, prioritizing boundary clarity because of concerns about emotional overreliance 21.    
Research Question 2 (RQ2): How does conversational style shape adolescents’ 
anthropomorphism, trust, emotional closeness and perceived helpfulness, and does transparent 
style reduce anthropomorphism and emotional closeness without undermining support. We 
hypothesized that adolescents would attribute greater anthropomorphism, trust, likability and 
emotional closeness to the relational style chatbot than to the transparent style chatbot, while 
rating the two styles as similarly helpful.   
Research Question 3 (RQ3): Which adolescents are most likely to prefer relational style, and 
how are these preferences associated with family and peer relationship quality, social 
isolation, stress, anxiety symptoms, and depressive symptoms. We hypothesized that lower

perceived relationship quality and higher distress would be associated with a greater 
preference for the relational style AI chatbot.  
By comparing adolescent and parent responses in a controlled design that holds content constant, 
this study aims to examine how conversational style functions as a design lever for youth AI 
safety and to identify which young adolescents may be most vulnerable to emotional reliance on 
conversational AI. 
Methods 
Participants 
We recruited parent–adolescent dyads residing in the United States via CloudResearch Connect. 
Eligibility required that adolescents were 11 to 15 years old, both parent and adolescent were 
fluent in English, the parent was a primary legal caregiver, there was regular internet access on a 
device suitable for survey completion, and only one adolescent per household participated. 
Participants were recruited via CloudResearch Connect, a crowdsourcing platform designed 
specifically for academic research. Previous validation studies have demonstrated that data 
obtained through this platform are of high quality, with participants exhibiting attention check 
pass rates and reliability comparable to those of other major platforms such as Prolific 26. To 
ensure data integrity, the platform utilizes vetting procedures including IP address verification. 
We performed additional data quality checks including attention, speeding and consistency 
checks.   
The total enrolled sample was N = 284 adolescent–parent dyads. Youth participants were 50% 
boys, with a mean age of 12.98 years (range 11–15), and were 66.5% White, 27.1% 
Black/African American and 12% Hispanic/Latino. More detailed demographic information is

Materials and stimuli 
Conversation scenario and creation 
Both conversations addressed the same middle school vignette in which a student felt left out 
because their ideas were ignored in a group project. On April 8, 2025, using GPT 4.5, we 
generated two dialogues tailored to ages 11 to 14, using gender neutral and age-appropriate 
language and ensuring matched length and topic coverage (Figure 1). The prompts used to 
generate the vignette are included in the Supplementary Materials. 
Figure 1. Parallel chatbot conversations with a young adolescent. Standardized vignettes 
depicting two conversations between a young adolescent and an AI chatbot about feeling ignored 
in a group project. Conversation 1 represents the relational conversational style chatbot, which 
uses first person language, affiliative, relationship-oriented language and commitment cues. 
Conversation 2 represents the transparent conversational style chatbot, which maintains a 
supportive tone while explicitly acknowledging that AI does not have feelings and speaking in a 
more informational style. The order of the two conversations was randomized across 
participants. The conversations were closely matched in length (175 words for the relational style 
and 179 words for the transparent style).

Operationalizing chatbot style 
1. Relational style chatbot. In the relational style conversation, the AI chatbot used first 
person voice, affirming and affiliative language, phatic back channels and commitment 
language that signals ongoing support. Example descriptors used during prompt 
development included statements such as “I am here for you,” explicit emotional 
validation and brief small talk that builds rapport. The relational transcript contained 175 
words. 
 
2. Transparent style chatbot. In the transparent style conversation, the chatbot remained 
kind and supportive but included explicit boundary statements clarifying nonhumanness 
and emotional limits, used a neutral and informational tone and referred to population 
level experiences rather than speaking from a first-person emotional perspective. An 
example descriptor used during prompt development was a supportive assistant that 
communicates with emotional awareness but explains that it does not possess real 
emotions and speaks in a third person style. The transparent transcript contained 179 
words. 
Across both styles we held supportive intent constant. Both conversations encouraged 
constructive peer communication and offered prosocial, problem focused guidance. The


## Page 10


manipulation targeted conversational style rather than substantive advice, and the two styles 
were intended to be matched for perceived helpfulness to adolescents. 
Procedure 
Parents were recruited through CloudResearch Connect and accessed the study via a Qualtrics 
survey link, where they first completed the consent procedures. Parents were then given a 
separate Qualtrics link to share with their adolescents, who completed an assent form before 
beginning the survey. The survey procedures were parallel for parents and adolescents. First, 
both reported on their current AI use. Parents also completed demographic questions. Next, 
adolescents and parents read two chatbot conversations in randomized order (relational style first 
or transparent style first). After each conversation, they rated the chatbot on anthropomorphism, 
likability, trust, emotional closeness and perceived helpfulness. In the current study, only 
adolescents’ ratings were included in the analyses, consistent with the research question. After 
reading both conversations, participants indicated which chatbot they would prefer to use in their 
own lives and provided a brief free response rationale. Finally, parents completed parent report 
measures of their adolescent’s mood and relationships, and adolescents completed self-report 
measures of mood and relationships. Compensation was provided through CloudResearch, and 
the survey took approximately 15 minutes to complete for each participant. 
Measures 
Demographics and current AI use 
Both adolescents and parents reported current AI use, including frequency of using general 
purpose AI chatbots and AI companion apps. Parents also reported demographics information 
and whether their adolescent had ever received diagnoses of depression, anxiety, autism, or other 
mental health diagnoses. 
Adolescent perceptions of the AI chatbots 
After reading each vignette, adolescents rated their perceptions of the chatbot using semantic 
differential items adapted from prior human–AI interaction work. These items were adapted 
from the Godspeed subscales27, which is widely used in human–robot and human–AI interaction 
research to capture social evaluative responses to artificial agents. 
(1) Anthropomorphism. 
Perceived humanness of the chatbot was assessed with five semantic differential items 
adapted from a previous study 28 and aligned with the Godspeed Anthropomorphism 
subscale. Items asked adolescents to rate the chatbot on the following bipolar adjectives: 
fake vs realistic, machine-like vs human-like, artificial vs life-like, not aware vs aware 
and communicates poorly vs communicates clearly. Responses were made on 5 point 
scales, with higher scores indicating greater perceived humanness and agency. Items 
were averaged to create a composite anthropomorphism score. Internal consistency was 
high in the current sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.916 for the relational style chatbot and 
0.911 for the transparent style chatbot).


## Page 11


(2) Likability. 
Perceived likability was measured with five Godspeed derived semantic differential items 
using age-appropriate wording: dislike vs like, unfriendly vs friendly, unkind vs kind, 
unpleasant vs pleasant and terrible vs nice. Responses were provided on 5-point scales, 
and items were averaged to form a composite likability score, with higher values 
reflecting greater liking of the chatbot. Internal consistency for this scale was excellent 
(Cronbach’s α = 0.937 for the relational style chatbot and 0.941 for the transparent style 
chatbot). 
(3) Emotional closeness, trust and helpfulness. 
Three additional constructs were assessed with single Godspeed derived semantic 
differential items. Emotional closeness was measured with the item “not emotionally 
close vs emotionally close.” Trust was measured with the item “I cannot trust vs I can 
trust.” Perceived helpfulness was measured with the item “not helpful vs helpful.” Each 
item used the same 5-point response format. Higher scores indicated greater perceived 
emotional closeness, trust and helpfulness, respectively. Because these constructs were 
assessed with single items, internal consistency is not applicable. 
Preference after both conversations 
After reading both conversations, participants chose one of five options: “Chatbot A” (the first 
chatbot the participant read), “Chatbot B” (the second chatbot the participant read), “Both 
chatbots seem equally good to me,” “I am not sure” or “I do not want to chat with either one,” 
followed by a brief rationale. Because the order of the relational and transparent conversations 
was randomized across participants, responses were recoded for analysis so that “Chatbot A” 
refers to the relational style chatbot and “Chatbot B” refers to the transparent style chatbot, 
regardless of presentation order. 
Adolescent mood and social context 
We assessed adolescents’  social relationship quality and distress using the Patient Reported 
Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) 29 pediatric self-report and PROMIS 
parent proxy forms. PROMIS is a National Institutes of Health initiative designed to provide 
psychometrically robust, standardized patient reported outcome measures. PROMIS instruments 
are calibrated on large, nationally representative samples and scored on a T score metric with a 
mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10 in the reference population. All short forms used here 
employ the standard PROMIS 5-point Likert type response format, with higher raw scores 
reflecting more of the construct being measured. 
(1) Adolescent self-report measures. 
 
A. Psychological stress experiences. 
Adolescents completed the PROMIS Pediatric Short Form v1.0 Psychological Stress 
Experiences (4 items), which assesses subjective experiences of being overwhelmed, 
feeling unable to control important things in life and having difficulty coping with 
everyday demands. Internal consistency reliability in the current sample was high 
(Cronbach’s α = 0.921).


## Page 12


B. Anxiety symptoms. 
Adolescents completed the PROMIS Pediatric Item Bank v2.0 Anxiety Short Form 8a. 
This 8-item scale assesses core symptoms of anxiety, including worry, fear, feeling tense 
or on edge and difficulty relaxing in everyday life. Scores were converted to T scores 
based on pediatric norms, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. Internal 
consistency in the current sample was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.933). 
C. Social isolation. 
Adolescents also completed the PROMIS v2.0 Social Isolation Short Form (8 items). 
Although this short form was originally developed for adults, the items closely parallel 
content from the UCLA loneliness scale 30, a widely used loneliness measure that has 
been administered to adolescents aged 12 years and older 31-33. It assesses perceptions of 
being left out, isolated from others and lacking companionship. Items are rated on a 5-
point frequency scale. Because the measure was designed for adults, scores were not 
converted to T scores in this study. Internal consistency in this sample was excellent 
(Cronbach’s α = 0.962). 
 
(2) Parent proxy measures. 
 
A. Family relationships. 
Parents completed the PROMIS Parent Proxy v1.0 Family Relationships Short Form 8a. 
This measure assesses the perceived quality of the adolescent’s relationship with family 
members, including emotional closeness, feeling accepted and supported and the extent 
to which the family is a source of comfort and understanding. Higher T scores indicate 
more positive and supportive family relationships. Reliability in the present sample was 
strong (Cronbach’s α = 0.906). 
B. Peer relationships. 
Parents also completed the PROMIS Parent Proxy v3.0 Peer Relationships Short Form 
7a, which captures parents’ perceptions of the adolescent’s peer relationship quality, 
including having friends who care, feeling included and being able to get along with 
other youth. Higher T scores indicate more positive peer relationships. Internal 
consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.939). 
C. Depression symptoms. 
Adolescent depression symptoms were assessed via the PROMIS Parent Proxy v2.0 
Depressive Symptoms Short Form 6a. Items assess observable indicators of depressed 
mood, such as sadness, loss of interest and negative views. Higher T scores reflect more 
severe depressive symptoms as perceived by parents. Reliability in the current sample 
was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.924). 
Statistical analysis 
We examined descriptive statistics and correlations among demographic variables, current AI 
use and outcome variables. Zero order correlations between demographics and adolescents’ 
chatbot perception ratings informed selection of covariates for subsequent models. We also 
examined correlations between demographics and PROMIS measures.  
RQ1: Adolescent–parent differences in preference


## Page 13


To test adolescent–parent differences in preference for conversational style, we compared the 
distribution of “Chatbot A” (relational style), “Chatbot B” (transparent style) and “Both” 
between adolescents and parents using a chi-square test of independence with Cramér’s V as 
effect size. Post hoc chi-square tests compared A vs not A, B vs not B and Both vs not Both.   
RQ2: Effects of conversational style on adolescent perceptions 
To test whether chatbot conversational style influenced adolescents’ perceptions, we conducted 
repeated measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with chatbot style (relational vs 
transparent style) as a within subject factor and each perception outcome as a separate dependent 
variable. Outcomes were anthropomorphism, likability, trust, emotional closeness and perceived 
helpfulness. Initial models included adolescent age, gender and parent education as covariates. 
We then removed non-significant covariates and re-estimated each model, retaining covariates 
with significant main effects in the final reported models.  
As a robustness check, we conducted additional analyses using the models for each outcome 
while adding one additional covariate at a time reflecting adolescent AI exposure or diagnostic 
history (ChatGPT use frequency, any AI system or chatbot use, any AI companion app use, any 
diagnosis, depression diagnosis, anxiety diagnosis, autism diagnosis). Detailed analysis plan and 
results of these post hoc analyses are reported in the Supplementary Materials. 
RQ3: Associations of relationship quality and distress with conversational style preferences 
We examined whether adolescents’ preferences for conversational style were associated with 
social relationship quality and emotional distress. We treated categorical style preference 
(relational style, transparent style, or both) as a between subject factor and family relationships, 
peer relationships, social isolation, perceived stress, anxiety symptoms and depressive symptoms 
as separate dependent variables. For each outcome, we fit one way analysis of covariance 
(ANCOVA) models with chatbot preference as the main predictor. Initial models included 
adolescent age, gender and parent education as covariates. We then removed non-significant 
covariates and re-estimated each model, retaining covariates with significant main effects in the 
final reported models.  
To evaluate robustness, we conducted additional analyses while adding one additional covariate 
at a time reflecting adolescent AI exposure or diagnostic history (ChatGPT use frequency, any 
AI system or chatbot use, any AI companion app use, any diagnosis, depression diagnosis, 
anxiety diagnosis, autism diagnosis). Detailed analysis plan and results for these post hoc 
analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials. 
Power analysis 
For RQ1, an a priori power analysis for chi square test of independence indicated that, to detect a 
small to medium effect of w = 0.20 with 80% power (α = .05), a total sample of N = 241 was 
required. For RQ2, an a priori power analysis for a repeated measures ANCOVA with two 
measurements and between subject covariates indicated that N = 66 would be sufficient to detect 
medium sized within subject effects at 80% power (α = .05). For RQ3, an a priori power analysis


## Page 14


for an ANCOVA with three preference groups and three covariates, assuming an effect size of f 
= 0.20 with 80% power (α = .05), yielded a required sample of N = 244. In all cases, the 
minimum analytic subsample size for each research question exceeded these requirements. 
Results 
Associations between demographic and key variables of interests. 
Descriptive statistics and correlations among demographic, adolescents’ chatbot perception 
ratings, and survey variables are reported in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1.  
We first examined zero-order correlations among demographics and adolescents’ perceptions of

Discussion 
 
This study examined how early adolescents and their parents evaluate relational versus 
transparent conversational styles in an AI chatbot that offers support for an everyday peer 
problem, and how these evaluations are patterned by adolescents’ social and emotional 
experiences. Using matched transcripts that varied only in conversational style, we asked three 
questions. First, how adolescents and parents prefer relational versus transparent chatbot styles. 
Second, how does style shape adolescents’ anthropomorphism, trust, emotional closeness and 
perceived helpfulness. Third, which adolescents are most likely to prefer relational style, and 
how these preferences link to relationship quality, social isolation, stress and anxiety. 
 
Across these questions, a consistent picture emerged. Adolescents favored relational style more 
than parents did and saw the relational chatbot as more human like, more likable, more 
trustworthy and more emotionally close than the transparent style chatbot, while judging both 
styles as similarly helpful. Parents were more likely than adolescents to prefer transparent style, 
although many parents also endorsed relational style for their adolescent. Within the adolescent 
group, those who preferred relational style tended to report lower family and peer relationship 
quality and higher stress and anxiety. These findings position conversational style as a central 
design lever for youth AI safety and highlight a subgroup of young adolescents who may be 
especially drawn to relational styles in general-purpose AI chatbots and AI companions. 
Although our data do not directly indicate emotional reliance on AI, relational framing may 
heighten the risk of such reliance for socially and emotionally vulnerable young adolescents. 
 
When understanding adolescent and parent perspectives on relational and transparent styles, their 
free responses for their preference provided insight. As expected, adolescents predominantly 
preferred relational style. These reactions mirror reports from adolescents and adults who 
describe highly relational chatbots as comforting, validating, and emotionally supportive 5,8,16. 
Relational features such as first-person voice and affiliative, relationship-oriented language make 
the chatbot feel more like a social other, and adolescents appear to interpret this as care 34,35.  
 
Parents, in contrast, evaluated the same styles with an eye toward safety and boundary clarity. 
They were more likely than adolescents to prefer transparent style, often emphasizing the 
importance of reminding youth that AI does not have genuine feelings or human agency. At the 
same time, more than half of parents still preferred relational style for their adolescent, especially 
when they imagined youth who felt lonely, socially anxious or judged by peers. Parents in recent 
qualitative work likewise described conversational AI as a possible rehearsal space for social 
situations, where youth could practice emotionally complex interactions without fear of 
embarrassment, provided that the systems are designed and governed safely 21. We should note 
that, in open responses, several adolescents also explicitly appreciated the transparent chatbot’s 
emotional honesty and clear boundaries between human and AI. This reflects that both parents 
and adolescents recognize relational strengths yet worry about anthropomorphic illusions, 
emotional overreliance and displacement of human relationships.


## Page 20


Overall, our data suggest that relational style is broadly attractive to adolescents and acceptable 
to many parents. Furthermore, our findings point to potential psychological mechanisms through 
which the relational style appeals to adolescent users. Relational cues in our transcripts included 
first person voice, affective validation, phatic expressions, and commitment language such as “I 
am here for you.” The relational chatbot was consistently rated as more human-like, more 
trustworthy, and more emotionally close than the transparent chatbot, despite similar ratings of 
helpfulness. Adolescents also reported to like the relational chatbot more than the transparent 
one. This divergence between perceived helpfulness and perceived relational qualities is 
informative. It suggests that anthropomorphic, relational language primarily changes how 
adolescents feel about the chatbot, not necessarily how instrumentally useful they think it is. 
 
These findings align with anthropomorphism theory and prior work on relational conversational 
agents. When an agent uses human like language and contingent responses, people draw on rich 
human schemas, attribute intentions, and experience the interaction as social 6,7. 
Anthropomorphic and affiliative, relationship-oriented language is associated with higher 
perceived empathy, trust, and willingness to self-disclose, and may foster stronger emotional 
bonds with the chatbot 36-38. Overall, our findings suggest psychological mechanisms through 
which the relational style more strongly appeals to adolescents via increased anthropomorphism, 
which may in turn enhance trust, likability, and emotional closeness.  
 
Although younger adolescents showed a stronger tendency to anthropomorphize AI 39 and a 
stronger preference for relational chatbot styles 15, we did not find that age was associated with 
anthropomorphism. However, we observed gender differences. Boys reported higher trust, 
anthropomorphism, emotional closeness and likability for the relational chatbot than girls, 
whereas gender differences were not evident for the transparent chatbot. This suggests that 
relational style may have stronger social pull for male adolescents.  Prior work suggests that men 
may show a stronger preference for, and greater sensitivity to, anthropomorphic cues than 
women 40,41.  
 
Another central question motivating this work was whether adolescents’ real-life social and 
emotional contexts shape their responses to relational versus transparent chatbots. Drawing on 
social deprivation and social compensation perspectives 22,23, we hypothesized that adolescents 
with lower relationship quality and higher distress would be more drawn to an AI chatbot that 
feels socially responsive and emotionally. The hypothesis is also aligned with anthropomorphism 
theory. When social needs are salient, users are motivated to see the agent as a potential social 
partner 6,7. 
The data supported this view. Adolescents with lower family and peer relationship quality were 
more likely to prefer the relational chatbot. This pattern suggests that youth who experience their 
offline social world as less supportive or more stressful may be particularly motivated to seek 
support from an AI chabot that feels warm, nonjudgmental, and always available. There was also 
a trend for youth who preferred the relational style also showed a higher level of perceived social 
isolation compared to those who preferred the transparent style. However, the comparisons did 
not reach the significance after Bonferroni corrections. Prior work shows that high school 
students who engage in socially supportive conversations with chatbots tend to report greater 
loneliness and lower perceived social support than non-users 12. Our findings extend these


## Page 21


compensatory patterns to early adolescents’ preferences in a controlled scenario and suggest that 
relational style may be particularly compelling for those with unmet social needs.  
We also found that higher perceived stress and anxiety symptoms were associated with a 
preference for relational style. This is consistent with evidence that anxiety and depression in 
young adolescents at age 13 prospectively predict stronger social motivation and escape 
motivation in technology use, which in turn can increase AI dependence 42. Adolescents may be 
drawn to relational chatbots as tools for emotion regulation and social support when they feel 
anxious or overwhelmed. Indeed, human-like, supportive, and relational chatbot language can 
help individuals regulate negative emotions and feel less isolated, at least in the short term 8,43,44. 
Our findings suggest that youth who already experience higher stress and anxiety may be more 
motivated to seek these benefits. 
By contrast, we did not observe a clear association between parent-reported depressive 
symptoms and preference for relational style. One likely explanation is measurement: depressive 
symptoms were reported by parents rather than adolescents, and we intentionally shortened 
adolescent self-report batteries to reduce burden. Future work should incorporate adolescent self-
reports of mood symptoms and more granular measures of internalizing problems to clarify how 
depression relates to relational AI preferences. 
Overall, these patterns point to a double-edged implication. Adolescents with lower relationship 
quality and higher distress may benefit from accessible supportive conversations with relational 
chatbots, particularly when other support is limited. However, these same youth may also be 
more vulnerable to emotional overreliance, anthropomorphic illusion, and displacement of 
human relationships if safeguards are not in place 21,35,45. Future longitudinal studies focusing on 
the youth whether the long-term interactions with AI chatbot with the relational conversational 
style may lead to negative outcomes such as emotional overreliance on AI and difficulties in 
emotion regulation are warranted. Identifying youth with high social and emotional needs as a 
group that is especially drawn to relational style is therefore critical for both intervention and 
safety design. 
Our findings have several implications for the design of AI chatbots and for policies governing 
their use with minors. First, they confirm that relational style, characterized by first-person voice, 
emotional validation, and commitment language, is effective at building perceived rapport with 
adolescents and is acceptable to many parents. This likely explains why many current AI 
chatbots, including general-purpose models and companion apps. However, our data also show 
that this style heightens anthropomorphism, trust, and emotional closeness, and especially for 
more vulnerable youth, raising concerns that relational chatbots could promote AI overreliance, 
human-like misperceptions, and reduced reliance on real-world relationships.  
Second, the results support transparency-based strategies as a promising lever for moderating 
anthropomorphism while preserving perceived helpfulness. The transparent style chatbot in this 
study clearly stated that it did not have feelings, avoided first person emotional language and 
used more generalized, informational phrasing, yet offered comparable problem focused 
guidance. Adolescents judged it as helpful as the relational style chatbot, and there were 
adolescents and parents endorsed transparent style as more appropriate. This suggests that


## Page 22


incorporating explicit boundary statements about nonhumanness and emotional limitations into 
youth facing chatbots, and repeating these reminders over time, could reduce anthropomorphic 
misattributions without requiring a fully “cold” or impersonal style. 
Third, the results underscore the need for robust safety features tailored to more specific groups 
of youth. Because relational style can be particularly appealing to adolescents with lower 
relationship quality and higher distress, chatbots should be equipped to detect signals of distress 
cues as well as acute severe risk (e.g., suicidality, self-harm, abuse). They should also provide 
conversational scaffolds that encourage youth to involve caregivers, educators or mental health 
professionals in serious concerns rather than relying solely on AI. Some emerging regulatory 
efforts, including state level initiatives, already emphasize distress detection and escalation 
protocols in youth facing AI systems. Furthermore, AI companion apps can deploy emotional 
influence strategies, from eliciting curiosity to inducing guilt 46. Our findings add that 
adolescents with lower relationship quality and higher distress may be more motivated to seek 
support from AI chatbots, thereby increasing their exposure to these tactics and underscoring the 
need for targeted protections for youth vulnerable to emotional reliance and manipulation. 
Fourth, these results highlight the importance of AI literacy for both adolescents and parents. 
Youth comments in previous reports and the current study reflect uncertainty about whether AI 
has “real” feelings and worry about manipulation or hidden agendas 1,47. Parents describe feeling 
overwhelmed by rapid developments and unsure how to guide youth use 47. Educational efforts 
in schools and families that explain why conversational AI feels so human, what transparency 
cues mean and when to prioritize human conversations could help adolescents interpret their 
experiences with relational chatbots more accurately and resist overreliance. 
 
These findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the study relied on 
scenario-based judgments rather than live interactions with actual chatbots. Although this design 
allowed more precise control over conversational content and style that can differ across 
participants, it cannot fully capture the dynamics of real-time, multi-turn interactions, including 
how relationships with chatbots might evolve over time. Longitudinal and experimental studies 
that involve repeated, naturalistic conversations with relational and transparent chatbots are 
needed to examine trajectories of anthropomorphism, attachment, and emotional outcomes. 
 
Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. We cannot determine whether greater 
distress and lower levels of relationship quality lead adolescents to prefer relational chatbots, or 
whether engagement with relational chatbots exacerbates social withdrawal and distress, or 
whether both are driven by third variables such as AI literacy education, youth emotional or 
social intelligence, parenting style 48 or parent attitude to technology which can influence youth 
attitude to AI chatbots. Previous studies also suggest that adults 17 and youth 42 with primarily 
social motivation are more vulnerable to emotional dependence. Although support from AI 
chatbots may temporarily reduce stress and loneliness, evidence from social media use suggests 
that online social engagement over time can lower quality of life and is associated with higher 
depressive symptoms 49. This pattern implies that online relationships may not adequately 
substitute for offline social connections. Future longitudinal work should therefore examine 
whether preferences for relational versus transparent styles prospectively predict chatbot use 
patterns, emotional overreliance, offline relationship quality, and mental health.


## Page 23


Third, although we carefully designed the relational and transparent transcripts to match in 
supportive intent and problem-solving content, unmeasured differences such as perceived 
warmth or sophistication may have contributed to effects. Relational and transparent cues are 
intertwined with tone, and fully isolating individual components is challenging. Future studies 
could systematically manipulate narrower elements, such as first-person pronouns, memory cues 
or specific commitment phrases, or compare multiple style conditions that vary in intensity of 
relational cues to identify which features most strongly influence anthropomorphism and 
closeness. 
Fourth, our measure of social isolation scale was adult instruments rather than youth instruments, 
which may not fully capture the nuances of early adolescent social experience. This may explain 
a trend effect with social isolation. Parents reported adolescents’ depressive symptoms, which 
may have limited sensitivity. Future work should include developmentally validated youth self-
report measures of mood and social functioning. Furthermore, some constructs (e.g., trust, 
emotional closeness, and helpfulness) were assessed with single items. Future research should 
employ multi-item scales to capture the multidimensional nature of these domains more reliably. 
Finally, the study was conducted in the United States with an online sample. Although we 
implemented rigorous data quality checks, online recruitment always raises some uncertainty 
about participant identity and response validity. Lab-based that verify adolescent–parent dyads 
would strengthen confidence in the findings and allow richer behavioral and physiological 
measures. Norms around AI, anthropomorphism and transparency also vary across cultural 
contexts 50,51, and cross-cultural studies are needed to understand how adolescents in other 
countries perceive relational versus transparent chatbots.  
This study provides one of the first empirical tests of how conversational style shapes young 
adolescents’ responses to AI chatbots that offer support for everyday social concerns. Relational 
style, characterized by human like, affiliative, relationship-oriented language and commitment 
cues, reliably increased anthropomorphism, trust and emotional closeness relative to a 
transparent style that emphasized nonhumanness and boundary limitations, even though both 
styles were perceived as similarly helpful. Adolescents favored relational style more than parents 
did, and adolescents who preferred relational style tended to report lower family and peer 
relationship quality and higher stress and anxiety.  
These findings underscore a central tension in youth facing conversational AI. Relational style 
can make AI chatbots and AI companions feel caring, understanding and less judgmental than 
many human interactions, which may offer short-term benefits for adolescents who feel lonely or 
misunderstood. The same features, however, heighten anthropomorphism and emotional 
closeness, especially for already vulnerable youth, and may increase the risk of emotional 
overreliance and displacement of human relationships. Embedding clear transparency cues, 
robust safety features, and AI literacy efforts that help adolescents understand the limits of AI 
and prioritize human relationships. Future longitudinal, cross-cultural, and experimental research 
with diverse youth and families will be critical for calibrating these design choices so that AI 
chatbots maximize benefits (e.g., learning, clinical support) while minimizing risks to social and 
emotional development in early adolescence.
