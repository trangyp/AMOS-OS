Two-dimensional (2D) materials with frustrated crystal ge-
ometries can host strongly correlated electrons, potentially
leading to a range of exotic many-body quantum phases
such as Mott insulators, quantum spin-liquids, and Kondo
lattices. The ability to control exchange-coupling within
these systems is therefore highly desirable. Here, we use
an atomically sharp scanning tunneling microscope probe
to vertically manipulate a 2D Mott insulating kagome metal-
organic framework (MOF) featuring Kondo-screened local
magnetic moments on Ag(111). We show that by control-
ling the adsorption height of the MOF, we can also con-
trollably and reversibly change the strength of Kondo cou-
pling between the MOF’s local spins and the substrate’s
conduction electrons. This mechanical control of Kondo
coupling could be extended to other forms of interlayer ex-
change coupling, potentially allowing for atomic-scale de-
sign or control of spintronics technologies.

INTRODUCTION
The Kondo eﬀect is a many-body phenomenon in which the spin of a magnetic impurity in a metal host is screened
by the metal’s itinerant electrons via scattering processes below a critical temperature, TK. This eﬀect was found to
be the origin of experimentally observed increases in the resistivity of metals at low temperatures.[1;2] The advent of
the scanning tunneling microscope (STM) brought renewed interest in the Kondo eﬀect.[3] The ability to probe both
structure and local density of states at the atomic-scale via STM has allowed for the precise study of the Kondo eﬀect
in individual magnetic impurities, such as single transition metal atoms or magnetic molecules, on metal surfaces at
low temperatures. [4]
The other great advantage provided by STM is the ability to manipulate single atoms and molecules using the STM
tip with atomic-scale precision. Lateral manipulation of single magnetic impurities has allowed for precise studies
on the inﬂuence of the environment on the Kondo eﬀect - such as diﬀerent adsorption positions of the surface
and diﬀerent conﬁgurations of neighboring atoms/molecules. [5–7] Lateral manipulation has also allowed for precise
construction of nanostructures such as quantum corrals.[8] The inﬂuence of the modiﬁed electronic environment
due to quantum conﬁnement of substrate surface states within these nanostructures on the Kondo eﬀect has been
investigated, [9] and quantum mirage eﬀects also observed. [10] Vertical manipulation has also been used to study the
evolution of the Kondo eﬀect at varying impurity-surface distances.[11–13] This has allowed for precise tuning of
Kondo coupling strength, allowing for precise measurement of parameters such asTK or contributions from spin-orbit
coupling. [14;15]
Recently, the Kondo eﬀect has also been observed in a number of two-dimensional (2D) materials on metal
substrates. These materials include several transition-metal dichalcogenide (TMD) materials such as 1T-TaSe2, [16]
1T-TaS2, [17;18] and 1T-NbSe2, [19] and one metal-organic framework (MOF). [20] The Kondo eﬀect is not typically ob-
served within periodic structures, as electrons (and their spins) are usually too delocalized within bands to scatter
itinerant conduction electrons of the metal substrate. What unites this group of materials is that they all host a Mott
insulating phase - a Coulomb repulsion-driven insulating phase in which electrons become highly localized within half-
ﬁlled bands. [21–24] The resultant highly localized lattice of spin 1/2 electrons can couple with substrate conduction
electrons to give rise to Kondo singlets. These highly correlated materials can host diverse many-body phases such
as heavy-fermion Kondo lattices, [17;18] or quantum spin-liquids. [16;19;25] The ability to control the Kondo coupling
strength within these systems is therefore highly desirable, and has not yet been demonstrated.
Here, we examine variations in Kondo coupling strength of a 2D honeycomb-kagome MOF consisting of 9,10-
dicyanoanthracene (DCA) molecules coordinated to copper (Cu) atoms on a Ag(111) surface. We show that local
variations in MOF adsorption height lead to varying Kondo coupling strengths (measured byTK) as a result of diﬀerent
substrate-MOF hybridization. Using an STM tip to contact the MOF, we are able to controllably and reversibly alter
the MOF’s adsorption height via vertical manipulation, thus achieving control over the associated Kondo coupling.

Kondo temperature, MOF adsorption height, and MOF-substrate hybridization
We synthesized the single-layer DCA3Cu2 MOF on Ag(111) following the method outlined in the literature. [20] DCA
molecules were sublimated onto the clean Ag(111) surface at room temperature, followed by deposition of Cu atoms
while the sample was cooled to ∼100 K. Finally, the sample was gently annealed to ∼200 K (see Methods for more
details). This growth method maximized the yield of the well ordered DCA3Cu2 MOF.
In this 2D MOF, DCA molecules are arranged in a kagome pattern and Cu atoms in a honeycomb pattern, with
each DCA molecule coordinated (via the cyano group nitrogen atoms) to two single Cu atoms, and each Cu atom
coordinated to three DCA molecules in a trigonal geometry (see Fig. 1a,b). Despite the hybrid honeycomb-kagome
physical structure, the MOF has been shown to exhibit three isolated kagome bands near the Fermi energy.[24;26–28]
Characterization of the 2D MOF on Ag(111) via STM revealed diﬀerences in the apparent heights of Cu atoms
within the MOF. Figure 1a shows an STM image of a region of the MOF, with distinct bright (labeled CuA) and dark
(CuB) Cu sites clearly visible. This diﬀerence in apparent height is also clear in a non-contact atomic force microscopy
(ncAFM) image of the same region, shown in Fig. 1b, indicating that the diﬀerence between Cu sites is structural
in nature (and not purely electronic). To quantify the structural diﬀerence between Cu sites, we measured ncAFM
frequency shift, ∆f , as a function of tip-sample distance, ∆z, at both CuA and CuB sites. These measurements, shown
in Fig. 1c, reveal a 0.2 Å adsorption height diﬀerence between these sites. The distribution of these distinct Cu sites
within the MOF which diﬀer by their adsorption height does not follow a crystalline order and arises due to a lattice
mismatch between the DCA3Cu2 MOF and the Ag(111) substrate. [20]
Strong electron correlations within the DCA3Cu2 MOF give rise to localized electrons. [24] When the MOF is ad-
sorbed on Ag(111), the spins of these local electrons become Kondo-screened by the substrate conduction electrons,
resulting in a characteristic zero-bias peak in diﬀerential conductance (dI /dV ) scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS)
measurements. [20] Because of the adsorption height diﬀerence between CuA and CuB sites, the Kondo screening at
these sites also exhibits diﬀerences. Figure 1d,e show temperature-dependent dI /dV spectra acquired on CuA and
CuB sites, respectively, with the characteristic zero-bias Kondo peak decreasing in magnitude and broadening as the
temperatureT increases. Following the same method outlined previously, [20] we used ﬁts of the experimental dI /dV
spectra, with the zero-bias Kondo peak ﬁt to a Fano function (see Methods) with half-width at half-maximum Γ. The
resulting temperature-dependent evolution of Γ for each Cu site is shown in Fig. 1f. By ﬁtting Γ(T ) to the equation:

with a characteristic resonance in the density of states at the Fermi level (detectable via dI /dV STS).[4] Within this

The diﬀerence in TK between the CuA and CuB sites in the DCA3Cu2 MOF can be understood within the frame-

the STM tip closer to the surface, in an eﬀort to manipulate the MOF atomic-scale structure. Figure 3a shows an STM
image of a DCA3Cu2 MOF area, where one CuA and three CuB sites are highlighted by red and blue dashed circles,

circle in Fig. 3b), while monitoring It (and hence the conductance G = It/VB; see blue path in Fig. 3b). For most of
this approach path, G scales exponentially with changing tip-sample distance ∆z. At ∆z ≈−3.3 Å, however, a sharp
increase in conductance was observed (upward pointing black arrow in Fig. 3b). This is evidence of a snap-to-contact

observed signiﬁcant deviations of G from the typical exponential ∆z dependence. For a ∆z range between ∼−2 and

tip and MOF, which we interpret as lifting of the MOF from the surface with the STM tip.[31;32] This is supported by
the observation that upon returning to the original tip position (∆z = 0), G was found to be two orders of magnitude
larger than the original value (red arrow in Fig. 3b). From ∆z ≈−1 to 1.5 Å, relatively normal exponential ∆z scaling
of G is observed, with some small deviations likely as a result of structural changes in the tip-MOF-Ag(111) junction.
At ∆z ≈1.5 Å, a sharp drop in conductance was observed, which we interpret as breaking of the tip-MOF connection,
returning to the regular tunneling regime (downward pointing black arrow in Fig. 3b). After observing this, we then
re-approached the tip towards the surface (blue curve), from ∆z ≈2 Å to the original tip position of ∆z = 0. The
conductance at this original tip position was found to be smaller than its original value after this vertical manipulation
(from Ginitial ≈3.2 × 10−5 to Gﬁnal≈1.5 × 10−5 2e2/h; double-headed black arrow in Fig. 3b), indicating a change in the
tunneling junction as a result of the manipulation.
An STM image of the same sample region following the manipulation procedure is shown in Fig. 3c. The MOF
appears to be relatively unchanged compared to Fig. 3a, with no sign of damage to the overall structure (and also no
suggestion of changes at the tip apex). Strikingly, however, the Cu site at which manipulation was performed (black
cross in Fig. 3a,c) appears to have switched from a CuA appearance to a CuB appearance. Similarly, the neighboring Cu
atom to the left of the site at which manipulation was performed (white arrow in Fig. 3a,c) appears to have switched
from a CuB to a CuA site. The other two CuB neighbors of the site indicated by the black cross remain unchanged by
the manipulation procedure. As illustrated schematically in Fig. 3d,e, the result of the manipulation is a “see-saw”-like
ﬂip of one pair of neighboring CuA and CuB sites connected by a DCA molecule.
To understand the eﬀect of the manipulation procedure on the local electronic properties of the DCA3Cu2 MOF,
we performed dI /dV measurements at aﬀected Cu sites before and after manipulation. The upper panel of Fig. 4c
(solid curves) shows dI /dV spectra acquired at CuA (black) and CuB (orange) sites indicated in Fig. 4a, prior to manipula-
tion, with zero-bias Kondo peaks associated with diﬀerent characteristic Kondo temperaturesTK (and hence diﬀerent
Kondo screening strength), as outlined in Fig. 1. The lower panel of Fig. 4c (dashed curves) shows dI /dV spectra at
the same locations, following manipulation. The dI /dV spectrum (black solid curve) at the initial CuA manipulation
site is associated with a large-magnitude, narrow zero-bias Kondo peak. This peak becomes weaker in magnitude and
wider after manipulation (black dashed curve). The dI /dV spectrum (orange solid curve) at the initial CuB, adjacent to
the manipulation location, is associated with a small-magnitude, wide zero-bias Kondo peak. This peak becomes larger
in magnitude and narrower following the manipulation (orange dashed curve). These spectra are compared directly
in Fig. 4d. The spectrum at the initial CuA manipulation site is nearly identical to the spectrum of the adjacent site
following manipulation. Similarly, the spectrum at the initial CuB site (adjacent to the manipulation location; orange
circle in Fig. 4a) is almost identical to the spectrum at the manipulation site (black cross) after manipulation. This is
strong evidence that the observed structural changes in the DCA3Cu2 MOF as a result of the manipulation procedure
are accompanied by switches of the eﬀective Kondo coupling strength at the aﬀected Cu sites.

Reversible and extended structural and Kondo coupling switching
The switching of CuA and neighboring CuB sites was reproducible across several MOF unit cells, provided a sharp
metal tip was used. In some cases, we observed that the manipulation aﬀected a substantial region of the sample, and
that the process was reversible.
A region of the DCA3Cu2 MOF is shown in Fig. 5a, in which four pairs of CuA and CuB sites are highlighted by light
blue ovals. At the CuA site indicated by the black cross, we performed a manipulation as in Fig. 3. The evolution of G
as a function of ∆z during this manipulation procedure (Fig. 5b) is almost identical to that in Fig. 3b. This manipulation
results in a switch of the black cross site from CuA to CuB, and of its lower left neighbor from CuB to CuA (Fig. 5c). In
this case, a larger region of the sample is also aﬀected by the manipulation, with four further pairs of CuA and CuB
switching.
We then repeated the manipulation procedure at the same initial black cross site (now in a CuB state). As the tip
was approached, G followed the typical exponential evolution relative to ∆z, until a sharp increase in G was observed
at ∆z ≈−3 Å (upward black arrow), indicating a snap-to-contact with the MOF (Fig. 5d). The tip was then retracted,
with G ﬁrst increasing within a small ∆z range from ∼−3.3 to ∼−2.8 Å, indicating lifting of the MOF. At ∆z ≈−2.8
Å, a sharp drop in G was observed, indicating breaking of the tip-MOF connection. From there, typical exponential
decay of G with increasing ∆z was observed again until returning to the original tip position ∆z = 0. In this case,
we observed an increase in G relative to the pre-manipulation value. An STM image after this second manipulation
procedure is shown in Fig. 5e, where the black cross site switched back to its original CuA state. Notably, all four pairs
of adjacent Cu sites aﬀected by the original manipulation also switched back to their original conﬁguration, with the
STM image in Fig. 5a almost identical to that in Fig. 5e. The vertical manipulation of the sample at a speciﬁc location
can aﬀect an extended region of the MOF, and is a reversible process.
It has been suggested that the DCA3Cu2 MOF is a metastable phase on Ag(111) due to the lattice mismatch
between MOF and substrate. [20] The inﬂuence of local manipulation on an extended region of the MOF shown in Fig.
5 is evidence of its instability, with the slightly buckled, frustrated system forced to ﬁnd a new local energy minimum
after being perturbed by the manipulation. This leads to a collective rearrangement and switching of several DCA
molecules and pairs of CuA and CuB sites.
In this work, we addressed the properties of diﬀerent Cu sites of the 2D DCA3Cu2 MOF, which is where diﬀer-
ences in MOF adsorption height are most pronounced. It is important to note that previous work has demonstrated
that the near-Fermi electronic states of the DCA3Cu2 MOF – which give rise to a Mott insulating phase at speciﬁc
eﬀective MOF electron populations, and to spins that produce Kondo features on Ag(111) – have predominantly
molecular character (with some weak Cu character due to hybridization).[20;24;26] The zero-bias dI /dV Kondo res-
onances are observed at locations following the spatial distribution of these near-Fermi states with predominant
molecular character (including at the extremities of the DCA anthracene backbone).[20] Due to the spatial extent of
these molecular states, the Cu sites – each of which is coordinated to three DCA cyano groups – also exhibit such
zero-bias Kondo resonances in the dI /dV spectra. [20]
The DCA3Cu2 MOF hosts a periodic array of spins, each of which exhibits a zero-bias Kondo resonance with a
high characteristic TK value (> 100 K) on Ag(111). This hybrid metal-organic system therefore meets all the criteria
to host a 2D Kondo lattice. [33] In our experiments, however, we did not ﬁnd any evidence for the emergence of
Kondo lattice behavior. [17;18] We hypothesize that the slight disorder arising from MOF-substrate lattice mismatch
within the system may disrupt the fragile Kondo lattice state, reducing the critical temperature of its onset below the
temperatures achievable in our experiments.
Importantly, our work links diﬀerences in experimentally measured TK with changes in MOF-substrate electronic

CONCLUSION
We have demonstrated that variations in Kondo coupling strength of local spins in a 2D DCA3Cu2 MOF on Ag(111)
are due to diﬀerent MOF adsorption heights and, as shown by the Anderson impurity model, varying MOF-substrate
electronic hybridization. We have been able to exploit this by vertically manipulating the MOF with an STM tip to
switch the adsorption height of Cu sites within the MOF, hence also switching their Kondo coupling strength. The
ability to mechanically control exchange coupling without requiring a magnetic ﬁeld could be translated to other 2D or
layered magnetic systems, with potential applications in spintronics. The demonstrated ability to contact a 2D MOF
could also be exploited for future transport measurements using an STM tip as one electrode.
Methods
|
Sample preparation
The DCA3Cu2 MOF was prepared on Ag(111) following a method we outlined previously. [20] The Ag(111) crystal
was cleaned by repeated cycles of sputtering with Ar+ ions and annealing to ∼500 oC in ultrahigh vacuum (UHV).
We ﬁrst deposited DCA molecules (Tokyo Chemical Industry; >95%) onto the clean Ag(111) substrate held at room
temperature. Then, we cooled the Ag(111) surface to ∼100 K and deposited the Cu atoms. A balance of MOF domain
crystallinity and size was achieved by then subsequently gently annealing the sample to ∼200 K. Base pressure during
molecular and metal depositions was < 5 × 10−10 mbar.
|
Scanning probe microscopy measurements
All scanning probe microscopy measurements were performed at a base temperature T = 4.4 K (unless otherwise
stated) in UHV conditions (∼1 × 10−10 mbar) in a Createc LT-STM using a metallic Ag-terminated Pt/Ir tip. All STM
images presented were acquired in constant-current mode at the speciﬁed setpoints (with bias voltage applied to
the sample and tip grounded). The dI /dV STS measurements in Fig. 4 were performed using a lock-in method with
a bias voltage modulation amplitude Vmod = 1 mV (zero-to-peak) and frequency fmod = 971 Hz. The temperature-
dependent dI /dV measurements in Fig. 1 were performed by numerically diﬀerentiating I (V ) datasets. In these
temperature-dependent measurements, the base temperature of the system was kept at 4.4 K, with the STM then
heated to a speciﬁed temperature with a built-in Zener diode. We waited for the system to stabilize and the thermal
drift to settle prior to dI /dV data acquisition. For STS measurements performed above 77 K, the STM was heated
from a base temperature of 77 K. The experimental dI /dV spectra in Fig. 1d, e were ﬁtted following a procedure
outlined, [20] with the zero-bias Kondo resonance accounted for by a Fano function (see below), and eﬀects of trivial
thermal broadening included. NcAFM measurements were performed using a qPlus sensor (resonance frequency

Anderson impurity model and zero-bias Kondo resonance simulation
Using the Anderson impurity model, we plottedTK as a function of |Vhyb| using Eq. (2) in Fig. 2b. The quoted values of
|Vhyb|A and |Vhyb|B represent the values of |Vhyb| which resulted in TK(|Vhyb|) values that matched the experimental
TK values for CuA (125±7 K) and CuB sites (162±16 K), respectively. The percentage diﬀerence between |Vhyb| values
was calculated using ∆|Vhyb| = (|Vhyb|B −|Vhyb|A)/|Vhyb|A.
Within Eq. (2), we chose the value of ρ0 based on the known density of states of Ag(111) at EF of ∼0.27 eV−1
atom−1. [35] We assumed that half of the atoms in a 7x7 Ag(111) supercell (corresponding to approximately one MOF
unit cell) [20] contributed to the Kondo coupling, to reﬂect the presence of two Cu sites per MOF unit cell. As discussed
in detail in SI Section 1, however, diﬀerent ρ0 values rescale both |Vhyb|A and |Vhyb|B equally, such that there is no
eﬀect on the percentage diﬀerence between these two quantities if ρ0 varies.
The values of U and ϵMOF are typically determined by the energy positions of the upper (UHB) and lower (LHB)
Hubbard bands. However, we were not able to unambiguously detect these features for the MOF experimentally
on Ag(111). [20] Therefore, we chose U = 0.65 eV and ϵMOF = −0.2 eV based on measurements of the same MOF
on hBN/Cu(111). [24] Note that these values possibly represent overestimates, as we expect greater screening on
Ag(111) – resulting in smaller U – and the formation of a Kondo resonance is known to renormalize the UHB and
LHB – resulting in a reduction of both U and ϵMOF. [16;17;19] The potential inﬂuence of this possible overestimate is
discussed in SI Section 1.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT
Spin
A slightly buckled 2D kagome metal-organic framework (MOF) adsorbed on
Ag(111) hosts localized spins which are Kondo-screened by the underlying
metal substrate. Using the tip of a scanning tunneling microscope, the MOF
can be vertically manipulated to controllably change its adsorption height
and with it, the Kondo coupling strength.

ANDERSON IMPURITY MODEL - PARAMETER EXPLORATION
As discussed in the main text, the values for ϵMOF and U were chosen based on measurements [1] of the DCA3Cu2
MOF on hBN/Cu(111) measurements. Therefore, these values may have been overestimates for the same MOF on
Ag(111).
Also, the value of ρ0 is known to be ∼0.27 eV−1 atom−1 for Ag(111). [2] The number of Ag atoms of the surface
to consider as coupling with the MOF spins (which mostly have molecular character) was less clear, however. We
considered half of the Ag atoms of the 7x7 Ag(111) supercell – corresponding to one MOF unit cell – since there are
two Cu atoms within the MOF unit cell. [3]

As can be seen in Fig. S1a-c, variations of U, ϵMOF and ρ0 aﬀect the evolution of TK relative to |Vhyb|. Smaller
values of both U and |ϵMOF|, potentially more realistic on Ag(111) due to screening and renormalization of the MOF

rescale both |Vhyb|A and |Vhyb|B equally by a factor of √ρ0. Varying ρ0 within the model, therefore, does not aﬀect
the percentage diﬀerence ∆|Vhyb| = (|Vhyb|B −|Vhyb|A)/|Vhyb|A. Overall, we conclude that the main text estimate
of ∆|Vhyb| ≈4 % is likely a lower bound estimate, and that diﬀerences in MOF-substrate electronic hybridization
between CuA and CuB sites caused by adsorption height diﬀerences and resulting in Kondo temperature diﬀerences
may potentially be slightly larger.
