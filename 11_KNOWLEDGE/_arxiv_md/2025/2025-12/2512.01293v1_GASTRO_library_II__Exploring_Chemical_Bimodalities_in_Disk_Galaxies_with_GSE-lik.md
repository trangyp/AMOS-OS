ABSTRACT
We use several smoothed particle hydrodynamics+N-body models as part of the GASTRO library to
study the role of high-density star-forming clumpy regions and a single merger on the formation of the
α-rich and α-poor populations in the disk galaxies. These experiments are tailored to mimic what is
expected to be the Gaia-Sausage/Enceladus (GSE) accretion event, which occurred circa 10 Gyr ago in
the Milky Way (MW). We find that either an early clumpy phase or a retrograde merger significantly
reduces the star formation rate (SFR) of the disk, giving rise to a chemical bimodality qualitatively
similar to the MW’s. The decrease of the SFR as the cause of the chemical bimodality is consistent
with previous idealized and cosmological simulations. On the other hand, a prograde radial merger
does not significantly modify the SFR of the disk, resulting in no clear chemical bimodality. We further
show that stars originating from the inner regions (Rform < 4 kpc) do not create the disk’s chemical
bimodality, although they can enhance it. Finally, only the models with an early clumpy phase can
produce a significant fraction of old, age > 11 Gyr, α−poor stars with disk-like orbits, similar to
what has been recently observed in the MW. Our results strengthen the case of clumpy disky galaxies
observed at redshift z ≈1 −2 as likely progenitors of our Galaxy.

1. INTRODUCTION
In the current cosmological Λ-Cold Dark Matter
model (Planck Collaboration et al. 2020), galaxies grow
hierarchically (Searle & Zinn 1978; Efstathiou et al.
1985) through the condensation of gas at the centre
of dark matter (DM) halos (White & Rees 1978) and
forming stars, the entire process being regulated by
feedback mechanisms (e.g. Dekel & Silk 1986; White &
Frenk 1991). As such, the Milky Way (MW), being a
disk galaxy, must have had a fairly quiescent merger
history (e.g. Freeman & Bland-Hawthorn 2002; Bland-
Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016) in order to form and main-
tain its disk morphology over several Gyr (see e.g. Xiang
et al. 2025).
When decomposed in terms of chemistry, the Galaxy
appears, to first order, to be composed of two disk chem-
ical sequences: an α−rich disk with large scale height
and short scale length, and an α−poor sequence with
a short scale height and larger scale length (Bovy et al.
2012). Dissections of the disk as a function of height and
radius (e.g. Hayden et al. 2015; Queiroz et al. 2020a)
reveal a number of other subtleties pointing to poten-
tially different chemical dichotomies with separate ori-
gins. Particularly, in the inner region of the MW, at
galactocentric radius Rgc < 4 kpc, the chemical se-
quence appears as a single track with a high−α disk and
a metal-rich α−poor bimodal distribution separated by
a dearth of stars between them (Queiroz et al. 2021).
This has been suggested as the outcome of an episode of
significantly reduced star formation in the central region
of the Galaxy, potentially caused by the bar, a merger-
induced starburst, or AGN activity (see e.g. Haywood
et al. 2024; Grand et al. 2020; Beane 2025; Beane et al.
2025; Orkney et al. 2025).
Alternatively, Debattista
et al. (2023) found that an increased star formation
caused by massive clumps reproduces the MW’s trend.
In the solar neighborhood, on the other hand, the
chemical dichotomy takes a different form with the
α−poor sequence extending to the metal-poor end of
the high−α sequence. This has been suggested as ev-
idence for inflow of pristine gas from the outer region
of the Milky Way (e.g. Haywood et al. 2013). But see
Khoperskov et al. (2025a) who recently found a less
pronounced metal-poor tail for the low−α disk based
on an orbit-superposition approach (Khoperskov et al.
2025b).
Both idealized and cosmological simulations
find that this portion of the chemical bi-modality is typ-
ically formed through gas condensation from the hot-
CGM halo (e.g. Khoperskov et al. 2021; Renaud et al.
2021; Parul et al. 2025; Orkney et al. 2025).
A generic result across all cosmological simulations is
that the formation of the high−and low−α sequences
is sequential (e.g. Orkney et al. 2025, but see also, e.g.
Agertz et al. 2021; Renaud et al. 2021 for a coeval forma-
tion scenario) with the high−α sequence forming at high
redshift inheriting its high velocity dispersion from the
more turbulent state of the interstellar medium (ISM)
and the low−α sequence forming during the more qui-
escent phase of galaxy growth after z ∼2 and inheriting
the lower velocity dispersion for its stars at birth (Bird
et al. 2013; Minchev et al. 2015; Navarro et al. 2018).
Whether a galaxy develops two chemical sequences or
just a single track seems to also depend mostly on the
gas reservoir of the galaxy and star formation history
(Orkney et al. 2025), as such chemical bimodalities may
not be a generic feature of every galaxy, with M31 being
an example of a disk galaxy with currently no apparent
evidence for a chemical bimodality according to the lat-
est JWST data (Nidever et al. 2024, but see Kobayashi
et al. 2023).
Old low−α disk stars have been observed in the so-
lar neighborhood (Silva Aguirre et al. 2018) and in the
outer edge of the Galaxy (Laporte et al. 2020). In recent
years, there has been growing evidence that these stars
make up a significant population of the early MW disk
(Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021; Gent et al. 2024; Nepal
et al. 2024; Borbolato et al. 2025) and thus suggest
an almost co-evolutionary formation for the high and
low−α sequence.
Observations of high-redshift galax-
ies often show evidence for massive star-forming clumps
(e.g. Elmegreen & Elmegreen 2005a; Guo et al. 2018;
Sok et al. 2025). Idealized simulations of hot halos cool-
ing within DM halos find that these clumps, although
having life times of order of only a few hundred Myr
(Garver et al. 2023), can self-enrich and also dynami-
cally heat their surroundings, such as to produce the
high−α sequence, while the low−α sequence starts form-
ing more extended, out of the clumps, and with a lower
SFR (Clarke et al. 2019; Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021).
Moreover, our Galaxy is known to have undergone a
significant merger around z ∼2 with a galaxy dubbed
Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus (GSE, Belokurov et al. 2018;
Helmi et al. 2018; Haywood et al. 2018) which may
have triggered subsequent star formation (e.g. Bignone
et al. 2019; Grand et al. 2020; Ciuc˘a et al. 2022) and/or
excited the formation of the low−α sequence through

The initial experiments of Clarke et al. (2019) consid-

only the efficiency of supernova feedback, which resulted
in an evolution with a clumpy and non-clumpy mode of
star formation at low feedback efficiency, and only a non-

absence of clumpy star formation, no chemical bimodal-
ity forms. However, given the unambiguous evidence for
the GSE merger (Chiba & Beers 2000; Brook et al. 2004;
Meza et al. 2005; Nissen & Schuster 2010; Leaman et al.
2013; Deason et al. 2013; Belokurov et al. 2018; Helmi
et al. 2018; Haywood et al. 2018; Myeong et al. 2018;
Limberg et al. 2022), one would also need to consider
its impact on the later formation and evolution of the
MW and its chemical sequences, under scenarios with
and without clumpy star formation. This is the goal of
this contribution, which aims to study the formation of
chemical bimodalities and differences between different
star formation models in the presence of a GSE merger
through a library of hydrodynamical simulations: the
GASTRO library.
This work is organized as follows: in Section 2 we
present the GASTRO library and discuss the evolution of
the host and GSE-like galaxy for each model considered
in Section 3. In Section 4, we contrast the various chem-
ical sequences resulting from each experiment and how
the different chemical dichotomies arise.
We then re-
visit when the α−poor sequence starts to dominate the
disk in numerical experiments in the presence of clumpy
and non-clumpy star formation with a GSE-like merger
in Section 5. In Section 6, we focus our attention on
the emergence of old (age greater 10 Gyr), metal-poor
low−α disk stars for each model. Finally, we discuss the
implications of our results in Section 7 and present our
conclusions in Section 8.
2. GASTRO LIBRARY
As introduced in the previous section, isolated disk-
galaxy models with an early clumpy phase reproduce
several key aspects of the MW: the chemical bi-modality
in the disk (Clarke et al. 2019), the chemo-geometric cor-
relations of α−poor/α−rich and thin/thick disks (Be-
raldo e Silva et al. 2020), the heated thick disk (Ama-
rante et al. 2020), the old, age > 10 Gyr, α−poor popu-
lation (Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021), and the bulge’s chem-
istry (Debattista et al. 2023). Motivated by these, we
developed the Gaia-EncelAdus-Sausage Timing, chem-
istRy and Orbit library, GASTRO1(Amarante et al. 2022),
which includes a single merger event mimicking the GSE
dwarf merger of the MW (Belokurov et al. 2018; Helmi
et al. 2018; Haywood et al. 2018). GASTRO comprises a
number of N-body + Smoothed Particle Hydrodynam-
ics (SPH) simulations, evolved with GASOLINE (Wadsley
et al. 2004), exploring the effects of clumps and merg-
ers in a disk-like galaxy. A subset of these realizations
was used to study the chemodynamical signatures of a
GSE-like merger in the MW’s halo, allowing us to study
the rich chemodynamical space created by the GSE de-
bris and show that some of the known substructures of
the MW stellar halo may share a common origin with it
(Amarante et al. 2022).
2.1. Input physics
We refer to Amarante et al. (2022) for a detailed de-
scription of the input physics in the galaxy formation
model used to run the simulations. The current work ex-
plores models that couple either 20% or 80% of the 1051
erg per supernova as thermal energy injected into the
ISM following the blastwave implementation (Stinson
et al. 2006). In these tailored numerical experiments,
the lower feedback regime prevents the gas from being
expelled from the clumps, thus allowing a clumpy star
formation mode. Hereafter, we will call the simulations
with 20% and 80% feedback as clumpy and non-clumpy,
respectively.
2.2. Galaxies initial condition
The initial conditions (ICs) for the MW-like galaxy
consist of a dark matter (DM) halo following a Navarro-
Frenk-White (NFW, Navarro et al. 1997) density profile
with virial radius r200 ≈200 kpc, mass 1012 M⊙, and
scale radius 30 kpc. There are a total of 106 DM parti-
cles with two distinct masses: 106 M⊙and 3.5 × 106 M⊙
inside and outside 200 kpc, respectively. The gas corona
has a total mass of 1.4×1011 M⊙and follows the same ra-
dial profile as the DM halo. There are 106 gas particles,
each starting with an initial mass of 1.4 × 105 M⊙. The
spin parameter of the gas is set to λ = 0.065 (Bullock
et al. 2001), so that as it cools, via metal-line cooling
(Shen et al. 2010), it settles into a disk.
The soften-
ing length for the gas and DM particles is 50 pc and
100 pc, respectively. Star particles inherit the soften-
ing length and chemistry from their parent gas particle.
They form with probability 5% directly from the gas
particles whenever the temperature drops below 15,000

2.3. Dwarf initial orbit setup
For all the models, the dwarf galaxy is set at a start-
ing distance of rgc = 200 kpc from the MW host. To
understand the effect of the orbit impact parameter on
the evolution of an MW-like galaxy disk, we focus on
a set of models that includes both prograde and retro-
grade mergers of different orbit circularities. A merger
is retrograde (prograde) when its initial orbital angular
momentum, Lz,0, is negative (positive) with respect to
the MW-like galaxy stellar vertical angular momentum,
Lz, component. We consider models with orbit circular-
ity, η ≡Lz,0/Lc(E), set to 0.3 and 0.5, where Lc(E) is
the angular momentum of a circular orbit with energy
E in the disk plane. All of the models have their or-
bit inclination relative to the host galaxy’s disk set to
θ = 15◦. Our orbital setups were motivated by Naidu
et al. (2021), who used N-body-only models to explore
a grid of orbital parameters of a GSE-like merger that
best reproduces the present-day MW’s stellar halo kine-
matics.

4. CREATING A CHEMICAL BIMODALITY
The bimodality in the [Fe/H]-[α/Fe] plane is a key fea-
ture of the MW’s disc (e.g. Fuhrmann 1998; Venn et al.
2004; Reddy et al. 2006; Bensby et al. 2014; Anders et al.
2014; Hayden et al. 2015; Queiroz et al. 2020b; Imig
et al. 2023) and sensitive to its mass assembly, chemical
enrichment, and stellar nucleosynthesis; thus, we take
advantage of these tailored models to understand the
mechanisms that may have produced it in the MW. We
use oxygen as a tracer of α−abundance and, through-
out this work, we use the ratio [O/Fe] to distinguish
α−rich and α−poor stars. Despite this ratio not being
quantitatively similar to what is observed in the MW,
it can still be used qualitatively to distinguish the two
populations. Finally, we note that, in this work, we are
interested in the shape of the chemical bimodality at
the disc, encompassing the Solar Neighbourhood region
and excluding the central parts of the galaxy, with two
overlapping α−sequences in metallicity. This region is
defined by 4 < R/kpc < 12, |z| < 3 kpc.

5. THE TRANSITION TO AN α−POOR DISK
We showed, in the previous section, how a single
merger and massive star-forming clumps can modulate
the SFR of both the α−rich and α−poor populations,
affecting the development of any chemical bimodality.
In this section, we study how this modulation can im-
print distinct signatures in the age distribution for both
populations and set the time at which the fraction of
α−poor stars is higher than the α−rich stars in the disk.
We refer to this time as the transition time.
A key imprint of the clumpy models is the presence
of a low SFR for the α−poor population as early as
t ≈1 Gyr, which promotes the formation of the old (age
> 10 Gyr) α−poor population. This contrasts with the
non-clumpy models where the α−poor SFR starts at
t > 2 Gyr, and can be delayed up to t ≈3 Gyr in
the retrograde merger scenario (Figure 5, second row,
first and second columns). The early formation of the
α−poor disc has been discussed in Beraldo e Silva et al.
(2021) with isolated models, and we will further explore
it in the next section.
Figure 6 shows the fraction of α−poor and α−rich
stars at a given age bin (similar to figure 4 in Borbolato
et al. 2025 for the MW). The isolated models are shown
as the dotted lines. The clumpy isolated model has a
significant fraction, > 10%, of α−poor stars as old as
11.5 Gyr, which is a direct result of its SFR discussed
in Section 4.2. In fact, model iso.fb10 (colored dashed
lines), which has a stronger clumpy phase, has a fraction
of low−α disk stars older than 11 Gyr as high as 20%.
As can be seen in Figure 6, the time of transition de-
pends in a non-trivial way on the orbit of the merger
and the clumpy phase of the main galaxy.
Neverthe-
less, some important qualitative insights are apparent.
Firstly, a retrograde merger in a non-clumpy galaxy, be-
sides creating a chemical bimodality (see Section 4), also
delays the transition to an α−poor dominated disk, i.e.
when its fraction is greater than 50%, which occurs ≈0.5
Gyr after the last pericenter of the dwarf. On the other
hand, in the clumpy models with a retrograde merger,
the transition happens earlier compared to the clumpy
isolated models, although coincidentally, still at a simi-
lar time.
The delay and anticipation of the transition in non-
clumpy and clumpy models, respectively, also occur in
the prograde merger models when the circularity is low,
i.e. η = 0.3. However, in the non-clumpy model, the
merger does not affect the transition time to an α−poor
disk, while for the clumpy model, a prograde merger on
a more circular orbit advances the transition compared

6. THE OLD α−POOR DISK
The properties of the α−poor and α−rich disks have
been extensively explored in the literature (see e.g. Imig
et al. 2023 for a recent overview). For instance, their
chemical abundances (e.g. Bensby et al. 2003; Prant-
zos et al. 2023), kinematics (e.g. Yan et al. 2019; Hu &
Shao 2022; Hu et al. 2023), ages (e.g. Wu et al. 2023;
Fern´andez-Alvar et al. 2025; Cerqui et al. 2025), and
spatial distribution (e.g. Bovy et al. 2012; Lian et al.
2022) highlight distinct formation processes. The cur-
rent view of the formation timeline of the MW disk can
be summarized as follows:
i. An early turbulent phase of the MW’s history, dur-
ing which metal-poor stars dominated the proto-
Galaxy in a pressure-supported system (Belokurov
& Kravtsov 2022; Conroy et al. 2022; Rix et al.
2022);
ii. This is followed by the rise of angular momentum
as the Galaxy enriches in [Fe/H], a process termed
the spin-up phase, turning it into a rotationally
supported system (Belokurov & Kravtsov 2022;
Conroy et al. 2022; Xiang & Rix 2022; Chandra
et al. 2024; Viswanathan et al. 2024);
iii. The merger with the GSE dwarf, which quenched
the α−rich disk and sets the start of the SFR for
the α−poor disk (Gallart et al. 2019; Bonaca et al.
2020).
This sequential scheme also aligns qualitatively with
several MW analogues in cosmological simulations (e.g.
Buck 2020). However, it fails to explain the presence of a
significant fraction of old α−poor stars on circular orbits
(Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Laporte et al. 2020; Beraldo
e Silva et al. 2021; Nepal et al. 2024; Gent et al. 2024;
Borbolato et al. 2025). Recently, Borbolato et al. (2025)
analyzed ≈60 thousand stars with spectroscopic infor-
mation from LAMOST and APOGEE data and reliable
age measurements from StarHorse (Santiago et al. 2016;
Queiroz et al. 2018). They found a relatively large num-
ber of old (age > 11 Gyr) α−poor disk stars. This rein-
forces that this population, although a small fraction of
the entire α−poor disk, represents a non-negligible frac-
tion of old disk stars. Thus, its presence needs to be bet-
ter understood theoretically and reproducible through
Galactic chemical evolution and galaxy formation mod-
els.
Beraldo e Silva et al. (2021) concluded that the clumpy
scenario is a plausible formation mechanism for the old
α−poor population. They also showed that an isolated
MW-like galaxy with an early clumpy phase could re-
produce the peaks of the observed pericenter distribu-
tion of RR Lyrae stars, which are typically old (but see
e.g. Iorio & Belokurov 2021; Bobrick et al. 2024; Zhang
et al. 2025; Mateu et al. 2025, on the possibility of rela-
tively young RR Lyrae stars), on disk orbits in the MW
(Prudil et al. 2020). Now, with the models presented
here, we can further explore whether a GSE-like merger
can impact the formation of the old α−poor disk.
Similar to their isolated counterparts, a clumpy galaxy
which underwent an early merger also has a non-
negligible number of old α−poor stars. From Figure 6
we can see that there are between 5-20% α−poor stars
in the oldest age bins, i.e. age>10 Gyr4.
Interestingly, the merger affects the presence of old
α−poor stars in the solar cylinder for the non-clumpy
models. The isolated non-clumpy model and its counter-
parts with the prograde mergers on a more circular orbit
have a significant fraction of old α−poor stars. However,
these models do not have a chemical bimodality (see
Fig. 4); thus, they are not strictly similar to the MW.
The non-clumpy models with a retrograde merger, which
have a chemical bimodality, do not have an old α−poor
population at the solar cylinder. In fact, a merger in a
non-clumpy model can either delay the transition from
high−to low−α disk, or conversely, completely prevent
the formation of an old low-α disk by transitioning very
early.
We can conclude that, in these tailored models, al-
though a merger can induce the formation of chemical
bimodality, an early clumpy formation is necessary to
form a significant fraction of old α−poor stars at the so-
lar cylinder at the same time that it produces a chemical
bimodality. In fact, a stronger clumpy phase produces a
relatively larger fraction of old α−poor stars in the old-
est age intervals, i.e. age > 11 Gyr, as can be seen by

7. DISCUSSION
In this section, we discuss our results in the context
of previous work using tailored and fully cosmological
models (Sections 7.1 and 7.2, respectively).
Finally,
we explore the connection between our models with
clumpy galaxies observed at redshift z ≈2 in Section

7.1. Comparison with Beane et al. (2025)
Tailored merger simulations that include star forma-
tion, such as those presented here, enable us to explore
a larger parameter space, such as the orbit configura-
tion of a merger and different subgrid physics, and their
effect on a MW-like galaxy. This comes at a relatively
lower computational cost compared to full cosmological
simulations. In the context of the impact of a GSE-like
merger on the chemistry of the MW disk, Beane (2025)
presented a suite of tailored N-body models, which in-
cluded gas and star formation, of MW-like galaxies that
went through a GSE-like merger during their first Gyr of
evolution. They propose that a brief, t < 300 Myr, halt
in star formation can lead to a bimodal [α/Fe] distri-
bution at a given metallicity interval. Previously, pure
analytical models have also suggested a brief halt in the
SFR in the MW for a chemical bimodality to emerge
(e.g. Chiappini et al. 1997). In Beane (2025), the GSE
merger event can trigger this halt, but they noted that
non-merger scenarios can also present an SFR quench-
ing.
Contrary to Beane (2025), our models with a chemical
bimodality in the disk do not completely quench. This is
in line with current observational measurements show-
ing that the MW disk had a continuous star formation
history, with a concomitant period of formation of the
α−poor and α−rich sequences (Ratcliffe et al. 2025). As
discussed in Section 4.2, our models have a significant
decrease in the total SFR in the disk either due to the
end of the clumpy phase (model c.iso) or induced by
the retrograde merger in both clumpy and non-clumpy
models, but the disk is always forming stars.
Another difference is the time of the first pericenter
passage of the satellite: Beane (2025) tune it to happen
at an earlier time, t ≈0.8 Gyr, compared to the ones
presented here, t ≈1.6 Gyr. Nonetheless, we argue that
the decrease, or in their case the halt, in the SFR hap-
pens at the time of complete disruption of the satellite,
in both suites of models.

7.2. Comparison with cosmological simulations
Our experiments model a MW-like galaxy with a fixed
DM halo mass of 1012 M⊙, making them idealized rela-
tive to the ΛCDM hierarchical galaxy assembly frame-
work. Although DM halos are expected to grow over
time, their masses are defined relative to the critical
density of the universe, which decreases towards lower
redshift.
This effect leads to a pseudo-growth of the
DM halo and dominates their apparent evolution from
z = 1 to z = 0 for halos of 1012 M⊙(e.g. Diemand
et al. 2007; Diemer et al. 2013; Zemp 2014).
There-
fore, a MW-sized galaxy will likely not experience sub-
stantial physical growth in its inner regions over this
period. Furthermore, Semenov et al. (2024) used Illus-
tris TNG50 (Springel et al. 2018; Nelson et al. 2019) to
show that MW-like galaxies that went through a rapid
increase (timescale ≲1 Gyr) of their rotational sup-
port, as in the MW (e.g. Xiang & Rix 2022; Belokurov
& Kravtsov 2024), typically accreted their total mass in
the first 2 Gyr of evolution. Therefore, in terms of total
DM halo growth, the models presented here capture key
aspects of MW-like evolution within a fully cosmological
context.
Although our experiments are idealized, it is encour-
aging to see that we can obtain similar conclusions on
the formation of the chemical bimodality in non-clumpy
star formation models as in some cosmological realiza-
tions through the mediation of a merger event (e.g. Buck
2020; Renaud et al. 2021; Orkney et al. 2025). How-
ever, we note that having a GSE-like merger may not
be a necessary requirement to form a chemical bimodal-
ity (Orkney et al. 2025).
A common feature across several current cosmolog-
ical simulations of MW-like galaxies forming chemical
bimodalities (e.g. Grand et al. 2018; Buck 2020; Parul
et al. 2025; Orkney et al. 2025) is that the high−α and
low−α tracks form sequentially. Alternatively, cosmo-
logical models showing a phase of co-formation do not
produce a significant amount of stars older than > 10
Gyr in the α−poor sequence (Agertz et al. 2021; Renaud
et al. 2021). Observations of the MW stars, on the other
hand, seem to suggest some level of coevalness between
the low−and high−α sequences during the first ∼3
Gyr of evolution (e.g. Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Prudil
et al. 2020; Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021; Gent et al. 2024;
D’Orazi et al. 2024; Borbolato et al. 2025) which is re-
produced in our idealised models with a clumpy star
formation mode.
Currently, the effect of clumpy star
formation in the chemodynamical evolution of MW-like
galaxies has not been considered in a full cosmological
context.
7.3. Linking the MW with clumpy galaxies at z ≈2
Clumpy galaxies at redshift z ≈2 were first observed
at rest-frame UV/optical with the Hubble Space Tele-
scope (HST) (e.g. Cowie et al. 1995; van den Bergh et al.
1996; Elmegreen & Elmegreen 2005b). More recently,
with the JWST, it has been possible to confirm that a
large fraction of these clumps, up to ≈85%, observed
in the rest-frame near-IR at z > 1 overlap spatially
with their UV counterpart (Kalita et al. 2024, 2025a).
Several key properties of the clumps have been identi-
fied with HST and JWST: their sizes and mass rang-
ing from 100 −500 pc and ≈107 −108.5, respectively
(e.g. Dessauges-Zavadsky et al. 2017; Cava et al. 2018;
Ambachew et al. 2022; Kalita et al. 2025b), their life-
time have been estimated at a few hundred Myr (e.g.
Claeyssens et al. 2023; Sok et al. 2025), and they signifi-
cantly contribute to the total star formation of a galaxy
(e.g. Wuyts et al. 2012; Guo et al. 2018). Finally, due
to the relatively high fraction, ≈30 −55%, of clumpy
galaxies across a range of redshifts (e.g. Guo et al. 2015;
Sattari et al. 2023; de la Vega et al. 2025), it is reasonable
to ask what the effects of clumps are in the formation of
a MW-like galaxy.
Clumps were proposed as a possible formation mech-
anism of galactic bulges (e.g. Noguchi 1999; Dekel et al.
2009) and geometrical thick disks (Bournaud et al.
2009). A series of works directly linked the clumpy for-
mation in an isolated disk galaxy to several present-day
observational properties of the MW, such as the emer-
gence of the chemical bimodality of the disk (Clarke
et al. 2019, and this work), the chemo-geometrical di-
chotomy of the disk (Beraldo e Silva et al. 2020), the
presence of a low-angular momentum, vϕ < 100 km/s,
metal-rich, −0.7 < [Fe/H] < −0.2, in-situ halo popu-
lation (Amarante et al. 2020), the presence of an old,
age< 10 Gyr, α−poor disk (Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021,
and this work), and the chemistry of the bulge (Debat-
tista et al. 2023). Moreover, the clumps in these tailored
experiments have sizes, masses, and lifetimes similar to
what is observed in high redshift galaxies (Garver et al.
2023).
Most of the aforementioned properties of the MW
have several other formation mechanisms other than

throughout the text, although the merger can induce the
formation of the chemical thick disk, only models with
clumps have a significant fraction of old α−poor disk
stars. Therefore, in light of the presence of the early
spin-up phase of the MW (e.g. Belokurov & Kravtsov
2022; Conroy et al. 2022; Rix et al. 2022) and the pres-
ence of the old α−poor population, we argue that the
significant fraction of MW-mass clumpy galaxies at red-
shift z ≳2 (Ferreira et al. 2022; Robertson et al. 2023;
Ferreira et al. 2023) are potential progenitor analogues
of our Galaxy.
8. CONCLUSION
In this contribution, we have studied the formation
of the chemical bimodality in the [α/Fe]-[Fe/H] plane
of several MW-like hosts in the presence of a GSE-like
merger.
We have explored different orbital setups of
the merging satellite and the mode of star formation
that regulates the formation or absence of massive star-
forming clumps in the disk during the first few Gyr of
evolution. Our main conclusions are the following:
• A chemical bimodality in the disk, 4 < R/kpc <
12, naturally occurs when there is a significant de-
crease in the SFR for a brief period of time, typ-
ically ≲300 Myr (Section 4.2, Figure 5). In our
experiments, this is caused by:
– A retrograde merger, as it significantly de-
creases the SFR of the α-rich population, al-
lowing type Ia SNe to enrich the interstellar
medium enough for the low−α sequence to
emerge;
– Or, massive star-forming clumps, which have
a high star formation rate in the first ≈3 Gyr
of evolution, creating a significant fraction of
α−rich stars. Once the clumps cease to form,
there is an abrupt decline in the SFR, allow-
ing the formation of the bimodality.
• In the disks with the chemical bimodality, the
SFR of the α−rich population is higher in the
first Gyr of evolution compared to the SFR of
the α−poor population at later times, in agree-
ment with Clarke et al. (2019) based on an isolated
clumpy model;
• A prograde merger does not seem to significantly
decrease the SFR in the disk and is not able to
produce the chemical bimodality in the clumpy
and non-clumpy disks (bottom panels of Figure
4). This confirms, using full hydrodynamic mod-
els with star formation, predictions from chemical
analytical models (Johnson et al. 2025);
• A GSE-like merger can delay the transition from
an α−rich to an α−poor dominated disk (Figure
6);
• In our models, the chemical bimodality in the disk
is created from a combination of stars born in the
outer, Rform > 4 kpc, and inner, Rform < 4 kpc,
regions (Section 4.3).
Contrary to expectations
from pure analytical models, stars originated from
the inner parts fail to produce the disk’s chemical
bimodality, but can enhance it;
• Only in the clumpy scenario does a significant frac-
tion, ≈10 −20% of old, age > 10 Gyr, α−poor
stars form in the disk (Section 6, Figure 6);
• In the clumpy models,
the α-poor/thin disk
emerges concomitant with the GSE merger, there-
fore it is not the GSE that induces its formation.

APPENDIX
A. ROTATION CURVES
Figure B1 shows the rotational curve of the models presented in this work. They are calculated using the profile class
of the pynbody library (Pontzen et al. 2013), which estimates the circular velocity, vc, from the total gravitational force
generated by the particles. Besides showing the rotational curve from the total mass (black), we show the contributions
of DM (green), stars (blue), and gas (red).
B. ORBIT CIRCULARITY OF STARS FORMED IN THE INNER GALAXY
As in Section 4, Figure B2 presents the [O/Fe] distribution for stars with −0.7 < [Fe/H] < −0.2, 4 < R/kpc < 12,
and |z| < 3, kpc, now distinguishing stars formed in the inner galaxy, Rform < 4, kpc, by their orbital circularity
ηs. Stars on more circular orbits, ηs > 0.9, predominantly increased their angular momentum Lz since formation
while preserving orbital circularity, indicative of churning (Sellwood & Binney 2002). These stars underwent radial
migration, with their guiding radii shifting from formation to the present day. In contrast, stars on more radial orbits,
ηs < 0.9, experienced blurring (Sellwood & Binney 2002) or rapid scattering during clump formation. These stars are
temporarily present in the outer disk regions due to their eccentric orbits.
In the clumpy models, ∼62−74% of stars formed in the inner galaxy (Rform < 4, kpc) that contribute to the α−rich
disk component have dynamically hot orbits (ηs < 0.9). This fraction is reduced in non-clumpy models, though only
the isolated non-clumpy model – which lacks a chemical bimodality – shows a significant difference.
