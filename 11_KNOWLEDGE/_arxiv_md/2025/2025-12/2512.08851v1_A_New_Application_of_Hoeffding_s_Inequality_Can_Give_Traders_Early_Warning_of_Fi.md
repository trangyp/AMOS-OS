Introduction
Hoeffding’s Inequality defines the maximum probability that an observed mean ¯
X on N samples
of an independent random variable (RV) varies from the RV’s true long-term mean µ by at least
a specified tolerance t. It applies to all RVs that are bounded; that is to say, RVs for which any
measurement Xi of the RV is confined to the interval a ≤Xi ≤b, a, b ∈R. For the specific case
that a = 0, b = 1:
P[ ¯X −µ ≥t] ≤e−2t2N
(1.1)
Hoeffding’s Inequality holds true regardless of the underlying distribution of the RV. It holds whether
successive observations of the RV are independent or serially correlated. Hoeffding’s Inequality
serves as a useful “worst-case” metric in applications where the modeler avoids assumptions about
variance, autocorrelation, or the underlying distribution, as the bounds in 1.1 become tighter only
when such assumptions are included (Azuma, 1967; Talagrand, 1995; Zhang & Chen, 2021).
In statistical learning theory, the true value of a predictive model’s expected future performance
µ on out-of-sample data cannot be known, but out-of-sample data are assumed to be drawn from
the same distribution as test data, for which average performance is observed to be ¯
X, so the
assumption is made that E( ¯X) = µ. In this context, Hoeffding’s Inequality demonstrates why
learning is feasible: it constrains the risk that µ will exceed ¯
X by at least tolerance t by defining
the worst-case, maximum value for the probability P[| ¯X −µ| ≥t]. This probability maximum falls
exponentially as N and t2 increase.
Here we propose an alternative, trader’s application of Hoeffding’s Inequality.
We define a financial regime as the complex of largely unknown causal relationships between
the distributions of obtainable signals and future asset prices that support repeated successful
application of a given trading strategy. The often-unspoken underlying hypothesis, H0, needed to
justify continued application of a given strategy is that the same financial regime under which the
strategy was found to work remains available.
Traditionally, financial regimes have been thought of in terms of structural shifts in basic characteristic
variables such as volatility (Hamilton, 1989), stock returns (Schaller & van Norden, 1997), or interest
rates (Ang & Bekaert, 2002). This formulation has enabled the development of forward-looking
regime-switching models that attempt to forecast changes in regimes in macroeconomic indices such
as the S&P 500 and the VIX (Xu et al., 2024).
Our definition of “regime change” departs from the traditional view in that it places a practical
emphasis on the efficacy of a trading strategy as realized by the trader. Financial regime change is
defined here to mean that the underlying distributions and causal patterns have changed enough to
render a trading strategy ineffective. This is the alternative hypothesis H1.
The trader’s application uses Hoeffding’s Inequality as a regime change indicator; in the trader’s
application, µi,j a trader’s expected future performance of a trading strategy during a specified
forward-looking time interval [i, j], conditional on the hypothesis H0 that the favorable financial
regime persists.
Applying Hoeffding’s Inequality requires only that a trader commit to a specific belief, µi,j that
quantifies performance sufficient to offset the opportunity cost or risk of a given strategy. The
belief can be expressed either in negative terms, as a loss percentage, or in positive terms as a win

Statistical Learning Theory Application of Hoeffding’s Inequality
The widely known application of Hoeffding’s Inequality is motivated by the need to quantify whether
a given machine learning model has been trained and tuned sufficiently for its output to be trusted
when applied to new data in a production environment. Hoeffding’s Inequality is adopted to quantify
the mathematical relationship between the empirical error rate of a machine learning classifier Ein,
and that classifier’s unknown future error probability Eout:
P[|Ein −Eout| > ε] ≤2e−2ε2N
(1.2)
Hoeffding’s Inequality, replacing ≥with > and tolerance t with estimation error ε.
See (Abu-Mostafa et al., 2012, p. 22).
This application is based on the insight that the error rate of a classifier is itself a bounded random
variable described by Inequality 1.1; by definition, an error rate cannot fall below 0 or exceed 1.
Inequality 1.2 helps to quantify the risks of shipping a new predictive model by providing an
upper bound on the probability that the difference between the empirical error rate Ein on a set of
N observed examples and Eout the true error probability, exceeds a tolerance ε.
Limiting the inequality to those cases where Eout > Ein (because in practice the concern is primarily
that a classifier may in future performs worse than it did on a test sample) reduces the probability
of exceeding the tolerance by half, so Inequality 1.2 can be restated:
P[|Ein −Eout| > ε] ≤e−2ε2N
(1.3)
Note the difference in kind between Ein and Eout. Ein is an observed mean, ¯
X, of bounded random
variables based on a sample of N independent draws, while Eout is an expectation µ of an unknown
stationary distribution from which all draws Xi must be made. Although µ is the cause of ¯
X, in
this learning theory application of the Inequality it is only through measurement of ¯
X that one
infers an estimate for unknown µ.
In the statistical learning theory context, Hoeffding’s Inequality is always applied under the
assumption that the Ein = ¯
X is calculated from N observations drawn from an unknown stationary
distribution with expectation Eout = µ . The true value of µ is then inferred as approximately
equal to the in-sample mean ¯
X, and combining the assumption with the inference, a maximum
probability of µ meeting or exceeding any given tolerance ε can be calculated.
However, nothing in the original formulation of Hoeffding’s Inequality requires this relationship
between ¯
X and µ.

Trading Application of Hoeffding’s Inequality
Most traders will choose to base their expected performance µi,j on back-tested historical data,
perhaps in tandem with a reasonable forecasting model if any trends are expected. However, because
µi,j represents what the trader expects for the strategy’s performance, there is no formal limitation
on how a trader calculates µi,j: only that the trader commits to µi,j as the strategy’s expected
performance under the hypothesis H0 of the market regime assumed extant in time interval [i,j].
In the trading application, instead of inferring the value of µ from ¯
X as in statistical learning theory,
one can start with any assumption about the value of µ from any source whatsoever, and continually
observe the value of t as ti,j = µi,j −¯
Xi,j. Here, ¯
Xi,j is the observed average performance of a
series of N round-trip trades and ti,j is the continually-measured difference between µi,j and ¯
Xi,j.

hypothesis of regime change, H1. Note that this is not a claim that e−2t2N is the maximum
probability the trader’s assumption of H0, namely that E(X) = µ, is correct.
Setting up a Prospective Trading Strategy with Hoeffding Signals.
Part 1: Performance Metrics of Bounded Random Variables With Ranges Bounded by (0,1)
Any metric about the expected trade performance that is a rate between 0 and 1 can be treated as
the random variable. For example, a back-tested strategy consists of a set of trades with known
entry and exit prices. These can be characterized by the following rates:
W −Win percentage: what portion of trades achieved a notional gross profit, ignoring time value
of money, transaction costs, etc.
P −Profit percentage: what portion of trades achieved a net profit, taking into account time value
of money, transaction costs, and any other etc.
U−Target upside exit percentage: assuming the trading rules for the strategy are constructed
so as to close out the position at a target percentage gain, what percentage of trades reached the
target exit price? (In the absence of an automatic close-out target, the percent of all exits above a
threshold upside return on equity can be tracked).
D−Stop loss percentage: assuming the trading rules for the strategy are constructed so as to close
out a position automatically at a maximum percentage loss, what percentage of trades reached the
stop loss price? (In the absence of an automatic stop-loss target, the percent of all exits worse than
a threshold equity loss can be tracked).
M (for Misc.) - Any rate over the range (0,1) and derived from the back-tested trade data can be
tracked.
The trader defines the trading strategy in terms of entry rules, exit rules, and expected values for the
above rates. After each round-trip trade is complete, the Hoeffding probabilities can be recalculated.
In one application, traders might use 50%, 25% and 10% as indicators. These probabilities are the
maximum probability that the difference between the actual and the target metric is due to chance
alone.
When a maximum probability drops below 50%, it is more likely than not that the trader’s current
beliefs about the market regime are not completely correct. If a maximum probability drops below
25%, there is a significant risk that the trader’s beliefs about the current market regime are, when
taken as a whole, no longer justified.

And if a maximum probability drops below 10%, it is almost certain that market regime change has
occurred and it is time to halt the current trading program and rethink the trader’s entire strategy
in light of fundamentally changed market conditions.
Of course, traders are also free to use values other than 50%, 25%, and 10%, and to automate their
response to Hoeffding probability signals, as well as to use them to inform margin and leverage
levels and overall stake management.

Summary of Assumptions and Definitions:
X = [X1, X2, · · · Xn] symbolizes a series of observations of n random variables. The mean and
variance of each random variable may differ; all that is required is that Xi – the value of the ith
observation of the RV – is constrained to the interval defined by upper and lower bounds b and a;
in other words, a ≤Xi ≤b in which b, a ∈R and b > a.
If a variable is random, then there is more than one possible observation for the value of that
variable. If a random variable is bounded, then all observed values Xi for the RV lie within [a,b].
Therefore, the expected value of an RV thus defined exists and lies in [a,b].
The assumption of boundedness also places limitations on the possible values for variance of the RV;
namely, a max value for the variance such that σ2 ≤(µ −a)(b −µ); or, for the special case that
a = 0, b = 1 : σ2 ≤µ(1 −µ).
No distribution is required or assumed for any of the random variables. The Hoeffding bounds hold
regardless of distribution.
