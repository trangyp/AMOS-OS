Abstract
Short-form video moderation increasingly needs learning pipelines
that protect user privacy without paying the full bandwidth and la-
tency cost of cloud-centralized inference. We present FedVideoMAE,
an on-device federated framework for video violence detection that
combines self-supervised VideoMAE representations, LoRA-based
parameter-efficient adaptation, client-side DP-SGD, and server-side
secure aggregation. By updating only 5.5M parameters (≈3.5% of a
156M backbone), FedVideoMAE reduces communication by 28.3×
relative to full-model federated updates while keeping raw videos
on device throughout training. On RWF-2000 with 40 clients, the
method reaches 77.25% accuracy without privacy protection and
65∼66% under strong differential privacy. We further show that this
privacy gap is consistent with an effective-SNR analysis tailored to
the small-data, parameter-efficient federated regime, which indi-
cates roughly 8.5 ∼12× DP-noise amplification in our setting. To
situate these results more clearly, we also compare against archived
full-model federated baselines and summarize auxiliary transfer
behavior on RLVS and binary UCF-Crime. Taken together, these
findings position FedVideoMAE as a practical operating point for
privacy-preserving video moderation on edge devices. Our code
can be found at: https://github.com/zyt-599/FedVideoMAE.

Introduction
Short-form video platforms have become a dominant medium for
online communication [27], with billions of daily uploads across Tik-
Tok, YouTube Shorts, and Instagram Reels. This scale has made con-
tent moderation both more important and more difficult. Current
cloud-centralized moderation pipelines rely on perceptual hashing,
multimodal deep learning models [29], and manual review, but they
introduce three persistent limitations.
First, transmitting raw or lightly processed videos to cloud servers
creates privacy concerns, especially for restricted-visibility con-
tent such as private albums, friend-only videos, or sensitive personal
footage. Recent work shows that even uploaded but unpublished
videos, or user data reused for training, can expose sensitive in-
formation [5, 15]. Second, short-form videos are bandwidth-heavy:
platforms may take in large files that are later discarded due to
policy violations, creating substantial wasted bandwidth and op-
erating cost [5, 9]. Third, remote inference introduces moderation
latency through upload and server round-trips, which is particu-
larly undesirable in interactive settings [9, 30]. Despite progress in
violence detection benchmarks [4, 23, 25], most deployed pipelines
still inherit these cloud-centralized privacy and communication
burdens.
Federated learning (FL) [12, 16] offers a natural alternative for
privacy preservation because it keeps private videos on device
while still allowing collaborative training. As illustrated in Fig. 1,
however, standard FL is not sufficient on its own: although raw
videos stay local, unprotected model updates can still reveal content
through gradient inversion attacks [6, 14, 28]. This motivates a
defense-in-depth design for federated video moderation. Existing
FL methods for video understanding still face three open challenges:
high communication cost from large transformer backbones, limited
support for end-to-end privacy protection through DP and secure
aggregation, and little analysis of how DP noise interacts with small
client datasets and parameter-efficient tuning.

Related Work
Federated Video Learning. Federated learning has recently been
extended to video understanding tasks. Rehman et al. [21] propose
FedVSSL for self-supervised video representation learning, showing
that useful spatiotemporal features can be learned without central-
izing raw videos. Yan et al. [31] introduce FedMAE with one-block
masked autoencoders to reduce communication cost by 20–30×.
They also show that video FL is a harsher systems problem than
image FL because the models are larger, the inputs are temporally
redundant but high-dimensional, and client distributions can differ
sharply in scene dynamics and motion statistics. Their primary
focus, however, is representation quality and communication reduc-
tion rather than formal privacy guarantees or analysis of privacy
noise in small-client video regimes.
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning. He and Wang [10] present
pMAE for parameter-efficient federated continual learning, combin-
ing reconstructive prompts with LoRA adapters [11] to reduce com-
munication overhead. More broadly, low-rank adaptation has be-
come a standard PEFT tool because it preserves pretrained weights,
introduces no additional inference-time branch, and sharply re-
duces the number of trainable parameters required for task adapta-
tion. This idea transfers naturally to vision and video transformers,
where federated cost is dominated by large projection matrices.
Existing PEFT-based federated approaches, however, do not study
differential privacy or secure aggregation, and they do not examine
privacy-preserving adaptation for video transformer models such
as VideoMAE.
Differential Privacy in Federated Settings. Differentially
private federated learning has been studied extensively [12, 17].
DP-SGD [1] introduces per-sample clipping and Gaussian noise,
while Rényi Differential Privacy [18] improves privacy accounting
under repeated composition. Much of this literature, however, is
grounded in image or language benchmarks where the number of
clients or the amount of local data is substantially larger than in
realistic on-device video moderation. Prior work has also explored
optimization, clipping, and aggregation strategies to mitigate DP-
induced utility loss [3, 20, 24]. Yet these findings mostly come from

larger-scale settings and do not directly address PEFT-based video
transformers with very limited local data. For video tasks, Luo
et al. [15] show that DP-SGD can substantially degrade activity-
recognition accuracy, highlighting the difficulty of applying DP to
high-dimensional spatiotemporal models. This challenge becomes
even sharper in our setting, where privacy noise acts on a small
trainable subspace and each client holds very little data. Prior work
does not explicitly analyze this combined regime, which motivates
our effective-SNR perspective.
Gradient Leakage and Defense-in-Depth. A separate line of
work shows that FL alone is not a privacy guarantee. Early gradient
inversion attacks demonstrated that raw training examples can be
reconstructed from shared updates [8, 33], and subsequent studies
show that this threat persists under stronger attacks and broader
settings [6, 28]. These findings motivate combining protocol-level
protection with formal privacy guarantees rather than relying only
on local data retention. In particular, secure aggregation and differ-
ential privacy mitigate different privacy risks: secure aggregation
prevents an honest-but-curious server from directly inspecting
individual client updates during aggregation [2], whereas differen-
tial privacy bounds what can be inferred about protected training
data from the released aggregate or final model. Most of this lit-
erature, however, studies image or language models rather than
parameter-efficient video transformers, leaving open how such
defense-in-depth mechanisms behave in low-data federated video
moderation.
3
Methodology
3.1
Federated Training Pipeline
Figure 2 summarizes the FedVideoMAE training pipeline. Multiple
client devices collaboratively train a shared moderation model with
a central server in a round-based FL loop. At the beginning of each
round, the server broadcasts the current global trainable parameters
to participating clients; at the end of the round, it aggregates their
protected updates using FedAvg to form the next global state.
On each client, the feature extractor is a self-supervised Video-
MAE encoder [26], pretrained via masked video auto-encoding and
kept frozen during downstream training. VideoMAE learns generic
spatiotemporal representations by masking a large fraction of video
patches and reconstructing them, yielding label-efficient features
that transfer well to downstream video tasks. In the client branch
of Fig. 2, local short-form videos are split into fixed-length clips,
encoded by the frozen VideoMAE backbone, and then processed by
trainable LoRA and prompt adapters plus a lightweight violence
classifier head. Local losses are computed only on the adapter and
head parameters. This separation is important: the frozen encoder
preserves a stable pretrained representation across clients, while
the compact adaptation layers absorb task-specific updates and
privacy noise.
The server branch in Fig. 2 shows how these local updates are in-
tegrated under privacy constraints. Each client converts its adapter
gradients into a private update using the DP-SGD and secure aggre-
gation mechanisms described next, and sends only the protected
adapter state to the server. The server aggregates these updates
into a new global adapter state and broadcasts it for the next round.
After federated pretraining, the learned representation is evaluated
through centralized linear probing or small-head fine-tuning, sepa-
rating representation quality from the cost of end-to-end full-model
federated supervision. Throughout this pipeline, raw videos, inter-
mediate features, and frozen backbone parameters stay on device;
only compact privacy-preserving adapter updates are communi-
cated.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. Our main federated study is conducted on RWF-2000 [4],
a widely used surveillance violence detection benchmark contain-
ing 2,000 videos (1,000 violent and 1,000 non-violent). We adopt
the standard split of 1,600 development videos and 400 test videos,
and further partition the development split into train/validation
subsets as summarized in Table 1. Videos are uniformly shuffled
and assigned to 40 clients with 40 videos per client, reflecting a low-
sample federated setting of the kind often encountered in on-device
moderation. For reference, Table 2 reports the archived 40-client
partition statistics used by our full-model RWF-2000 baselines: the
positive-video ratio varies widely across clients and 5 of the 40
clients are single-class. This makes the benchmark useful not only
for evaluating privacy-preserving learning, but also for analyzing
how DP noise interacts with parameter-efficient updates under
severe local data scarcity.

Federated Pretraining Configuration. All experiments use
the same federated training setup unless otherwise noted. We per-
form 200 communication rounds with all 40 clients participating in
every round, which removes client-sampling variance and simpli-
fies interpretation of the privacy results. Each client trains locally
for 4 epochs per round using batch size 32. We adopt AdamW with
learning rate 2×10−4, weight decay 0.01, and mixed-precision train-
ing. Video inputs consist of 16-frame clips at 224 × 224 resolution.
This configuration deliberately targets a low-data federated regime:
in FedVideoMAE pretraining, each client optimizes on its full local
partition rather than a separate client-side validation split, so op-
timization stability and privacy robustness must still be achieved
under limited per-client data.

Our goal is not to outperform full-model supervised federated
learning in raw accuracy. Instead, FedVideoMAE operates at a
different point in the design space, trading some utility for a sub-

protections. For edge-based video moderation, this trade-off is often
more practical.
DP Robustness under Parameter-Efficient Training. A no-
table finding is that relaxing the privacy budget from 𝜀= 1 to 𝜀= 5
does not improve accuracy. This suggests that utility is governed
mainly by clipping and noise magnitude rather than by the nom-

effective-SNR analysis in Sec. 3.4: Eqs. (8) and (9) indicate that small
per-client datasets, large noise multipliers, and a 3.5% trainable-
parameter budget jointly place training in a noise-dominated regime
in which differences within 𝜀∈[1, 5] have limited practical effect.
Even in this regime, all DP settings converge stably and retain
meaningful representational quality.
Training Dynamics. Across all privacy settings, federated pre-
training exhibits stable optimization behavior, with DP-protected
runs following broadly similar trends. The close similarity across
privacy levels indicates that clipping and noise dominate the op-
timization trend, which supports our SNR-based interpretation of
the privacy–utility relationship.
Downstream Metrics. All DP-protected configurations yield
ROC-AUC values within a narrow 71–72% band, showing that the
learned representations remain discriminative even under strong
privacy. The small spread across 𝜀∈{1, 5, 10} is itself informative:
once the system enters a noise-dominated regime, relaxing the nom-

Interpretation and Implications. Rather than treating these
observations as an isolated limitation, we interpret them as evi-
dence that DP-SGD in video FL must be understood through signal
strength. Our effective-SNR metric helps explain why DP-SGD
behaves differently in video-based federated settings than in large-
scale image or text datasets. It also suggests concrete design di-
rections for future privacy-preserving video learning systems: in-
crease local dataset size (𝑛> 100 videos per client), allocate a larger
trainable parameter budget (5–10%), start from stronger pretrained
backbones, or employ adaptive clipping and noise scheduling. In
other words, the path to better private video FL is not simply to
choose a larger 𝜀; it is to increase the usable signal available to the
trainable parameters.
Even in this challenging small-scale regime, the system main-
tains stable convergence and produces meaningful representations,
demonstrating that parameter-efficient FL remains viable under
strict privacy constraints.

Conclusion
We presented FedVideoMAE, a defense-in-depth federated video
learning framework that combines self-supervised VideoMAE rep-
resentations, parameter-efficient LoRA adaptation, differential pri-
vacy, and secure aggregation for on-device violence detection. By
updating only a small trainable subset, the framework reduces com-
munication by 28.3× while still maintaining useful performance
under strong privacy budgets on RWF-2000. We further situate
these main results using archived full-model federated baselines
and auxiliary transfer summaries on RLVS and binary UCF-Crime,
which together clarify the method’s operating point in terms of
privacy, utility, and efficiency. Beyond the empirical results, we in-
troduced an effective-SNR perspective that explains how DP noise
interacts with per-client data scarcity and parameter-efficient up-
dates in this regime. Taken together, the framework and analysis
provide a practical foundation for privacy-preserving video analyt-
ics on edge devices and point toward future improvements through
stronger signal regimes, adaptive privacy mechanisms, and larger
federated video benchmarks.
Future work will investigate stronger privacy mechanisms, in-
cluding adaptive clipping, noise scheduling, client-level DP account-
ing, and optimization strategies better suited to PEFT-based trans-
formers, to reduce utility loss under a fixed privacy budget. Beyond
the honest-but-curious server assumed here, it will also be impor-
tant to extend the framework to stronger threat models involving
malicious clients, poisoning and backdoor attacks, and Byzantine
robustness, toward a unified treatment of privacy and robustness.
