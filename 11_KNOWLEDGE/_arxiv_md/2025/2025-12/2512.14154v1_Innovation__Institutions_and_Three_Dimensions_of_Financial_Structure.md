Abstract
This paper studies the response of stock markets relative to the banking sector to in-
novation by using a panel of 75 countries from 1982 to 2021. We find that innovation
increases the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative to the banking sec-
tor, moderated by proximity to technological frontier and institutional quality. The
moderating effect of institutional quality is positive for activity and efficiency but neg-
ative for size. Moreover, the moderating effect can be nonlinear depending on specific
indicators. The marginal effect of innovation on the activity is persistent over many
years, but the moderating effect of institutional quality gradually fades away.
Keywords: innovation; financial structure; institutional quality; proximity to techno-
logical frontier

Introduction
Most studies on the relationship between innovation and financial systems share two perspec-
tives. First, stock markets are better positioned to fund innovation than banks and second,
innovation is a dependent variable, an outcome shaped by financial markets (Holmstr¨om and
Tirole, 1993; Allen and Gale, 1999; Hsu et al., 2014; Brown et al., 2009, 2013; Rajan and
Zingales, 1998; Kortum and Lerner, 2000). However, plotting the number of patent applica-
tions, a proxy for innovation, against the three dimensions of financial structure shows that
it depends on the specific dimension of financial structure whether stock markets are more
relevant than banks.

and size (Beck et al., 1999; Levine, 2002). Higher ratios mean that stock markets are more
active, more efficient, or larger in size than the banking sector.
We can see that stock markets are more active and efficient than the banking sector in
countries where innovation is higher, even though there is a significant variation at the low
level of innovation. On the other hand, there is almost no relationship between innovation
and the size of stock markets relative to the banking sector and a little variation in the
relative size over innovation.
Those contrasting stylized facts motivate us to study the
relationship between innovation and financial system for the three dimensions of financial
structure separately.
Regarding identification, we do not view a country’s financial structure as causing innovation
in the first place. Stock markets do help companies grow and raise innovative capacity by
financing R&D investments (Brown et al., 2009). However, stock markets do not provide
ideas for innovation. In fact, the literature traditionally views financial development as a
factor mitigating financial constraints for entrepreneurs but not as a determinant causing
innovation (Levine, 1997). We share the idea that innovation expands equity financing by
reducing monitoring and bankruptcy costs (Boyd and Smith, 1998; Bose, 2005). To establish
a causality from innovation to financial structure, we will employ a instrumental variable
(IV) strategy.
Our premise is that patent activity makes innovation outputs observable and verifiable and
reduces information asymmetry, thereby compelling revaluation of firms in capital markets.
We view surges in patent activity as demand shocks for external finance that should raise
the importance of stock markets relative to the banking sector. Framing the relationship
between innovation and financial structure in this way allows us to study how the response
of stock markets to innovation differs compared with the banking sector in terms of he three
dimensions of financial structure. In addition, we analyze how those relationships depend on
a country’s proximity to technological frontier and institutional quality, incorporating views
from the law-finance nexus literature (La Porta et al., 1998, 2000; Levine et al., 2000; Brown
et al., 2013).

We use a panel of 75 advanced and emerging economies over 1982–2021 to show that in-
novation increases the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative to the banking
sector and that those marginal effect are moderated by institutional quality and proximity
to technological frontier. In our view, whether the demand shocks for external finance is
transmitted into stock market activity, efficiency, and size relative to the banking sector
depends a country’s proximity to frontier as market signals might be stronger in more pro-
ductive economies. Moreover, the transmission of the demand shocks depends critically on
institutional quality as markets may fail to process innovation signals with weak investor
protection, poor enforcement, or limited disclosure (La Porta et al., 2000), whereas strong
institutions enhance the ability of investors to interpret market signals and enforce contracts
(La Porta et al., 1998; Levine et al., 2000).
Our main findings are as follows.
First, the number of patent applications is positively
associated with the activity, efficiency, and size of stock markets relative to the banking
sector. To establish causality from innovation to financial structure, we interact a country’s
geographical distance to the regional innovation leader with regional patent growth and define
the interaction terms as an IV, based on the idea that technology spills overs regionally, while
financial structure is determined by country-specific factors (Levine et al., 2023).
Second, the marginal effect of innovation on financial structure is amplified in countries closer
to the world technological frontier, and also moderated by institutional quality indicators
based on Kaufmann and Kraay (2024).1 In particular, we find that the moderating effects
of institutional quality are heterogeneous. While institutional quality indicators, when sig-
nificant, have a positive moderating effect on the activity and efficiency of stock markets
relative to the banking sector, they have, when significant, a negative moderating effect on
the relative size. This hints at the increasing size and role of the banking sector for financing
innovation in high institutional economies.
Third, the moderating effect of institutional quality can be nonlinear. When significant,
most institutional quality indicators show a U-shaped moderating effect. This means that

innovation can lead to higher activity and efficiency of stock markets relative to the banking
sector at low and high levels of institutional quality. Since the banking sector’s response to
innovation does not vary much with institutional quality, stock markets are mostly responsi-
ble for the nonlinear moderating effect. The result of a large mediating effect of institutional
quality at a low level of institutional quality is intriguing and suggests that there might be
alternative financing channels not captured by the literature on law-finance nexus but are
relevant for economies with low institutional quality. One example of such a channel is based
on reputation and relationships explored for China in Allen et al. (2005).
Lastly, we employ the local project (LP) method with an IV approach, following Jord`a
(2005), to show that the effects of innovation on financial structure are persistent, typically
intensifying around the fourth year after an innovation shock. In addition, stronger institu-
tional quality amplifies the marginal effect of innovation in the initial years, with the effect
diminishing over longer horizons. Moreover, we use alternative innovation proxies, finan-
cial structure indicators and instruments to ensure that our findings are not driven by any
single proxy and offer more robust and generalizable evidence on the role of innovation in
influencing financial structure.
Regarding the functional differences between banks and stock markets in financing innova-
tion, our results are in line with theoretical arguments that banks are generally reluctant to
finance high-risk, intangible R&D activities (Holmstr¨om and Tirole, 1993; Allen and Gale,
1999), and consistent with empirical findings that stock markets and venture capital are
better equipped than credit market to support innovation by pooling risk and valuing future
growth potential (Rajan and Zingales, 1998; Kortum and Lerner, 2000; Brown et al., 2009,
2013; Hsu et al., 2014).

the main results. Section 4 examines the dynamic effects of innovation. Section 5 concludes.

Data
We examine the impact of innovation on financial structure using an unbalanced panel of
159 advanced and emerging market economies, with annual data from 1960 to 2021. After
including the necessary instruments and control variables, the estimated sample is reduced
to 75 countries spanning from 1982 to 2021. We measure innovation of each country by the
number of patent applications and the activity, efficiency and size of stock markets relative
to the banking sector. The definition and sources of the variables are given in Table 1.
Table 2 reports the summary statistics for the full sample and for advanced and emerging
economies separately.
The average number of patent applications per year is 2.21, with
a high standard deviation of 8.59 and a maximum of 124.57. This indicates substantial
cross-country heterogeneity, with innovation concentrated in a few technologically advanced
economies. The distribution of patents is more clustered for advanced economies (standard
deviation of 8.23) than emerging markets (8.77). In particular, the maximum number of
patents in emerging economies (124.57) far exceeds that in advanced countries (38.74) despite
a much smaller mean of 1.14 compare to 3.41 in advanced economies, driven by rapidly
industrializing countries such as China.
Regarding financial structure, Table 2 shows that advanced economies have a higher mean of
stock market activity and efficiency relative to the banking sector than emerging economies.
Emerging economies, however, exhibit a higher mean of the size of stock markets relative to
the banking sector than advanced economies, indicating that higher innovation activity is
not necessarily associated with larger stock markets relative to the banking sector.

3.3
Institutional quality channel
The preceding analysis demonstrates that the effect of innovation on the activity, efficiency
and size of stock markets relative to the banking sector are larger as a country approaches the
world technological frontier. However, PTF alone may not be sufficient to fully understand
how innovation shapes the three dimensions of financial structure. Even countries at similar
levels of innovation capability can exhibit different outcomes if institutional quality differs.
Institutions influence how effectively innovation is translated into market activity. Strong
institutions promote legal certainty, regulatory quality, and transparency, all of which are
crucial for fostering investor confidence and enabling financial markets to respond to inno-
vative activities.
Therefore, this section extends our empirical framework to examine whether institutional
quality moderates the marginal effect of innovation on financial structure. We analyze six
key institutional indicators: regulatory quality, voice and accountability, corruption control,
rule of law, political stability, and government effectiveness based on Kaufmann and Kraay
(2024). Our second-stage empirical specification remains consistent with the approach used

not increase relative to banks’ assets when innovation accelerates, because innovative firms
often rely on bank credit and internal funds rather than issuing equity in high institutional
quality economies (Cornaggia et al., 2015; Chava et al., 2017; Spatareanu et al., 2019).

Local projection with the instrumental variable
While the previous analysis establishes the marginal effect of innovation on financial struc-
ture, it remains unclear how quickly or persistently these effects unfold over time. Stock
markets and banks may respond to innovation with delays due to adjustment costs, institu-
tional frictions, or the time required for new technologies to diffuse. Static models capture
only the contemporaneous average impact, potentially overlooking important dynamic ad-
justment paths. In this section, we focus on the dynamic responses of the activity dimension,
as it is most widely used for capturing financial structure in related fields.

Conclusion
This paper revisits the relationship between innovation and financial structure from a new
perspective. We conceptualize innovation as a catalyst that gradually shapes financial struc-
ture of an economy. Drawing on a broad panel of 75 countries over 40 years, we provide
empirical evidence that innovation increases the activity, efficiency, and size of stock markets
relative to the banking sector. By employing instrumental variables, local projections, as
well as various nonlinear specifications, we address endogeneity concerns and explore the
heterogeneous and dynamic nature of those relationships.
Our results suggest that while innovation leads to more active, more efficient and larger
stock markets relative to the banking sector, the banking sector actively finances innovation,
for example, by using patents as collateral for loans, increasing banks’ assets relative to
stock market capitalization, when institutional quality is high. Furthermore, we show that
the moderating effect of institutional quality on the relationship between innovation and
the activity and efficiency of stock market relative to the banking sector can be nonlinear,
strengthening the effect for high institutional quality.
Our local projection estimates further indicate that the influence of innovation on the activity
of stock markets relative to the banking sector is persistent over time. The dynamic effects
tend to intensify around the fourth year after an innovation shock. Furthermore, stronger
institutional environments enhance the initial marginal effect of innovation on the activity of
stock markets relative to the banking sector. However, this influence is not permanent. Over
time, the positive effects diminish, with several institutional indicators showing responses

When estimating dynamic panel data models, endogeneity is a common concern, especially
when the model includes a lagged dependent variable among the regressors. Such a setup may
cause reverse causality, as the dependent variable may influence the independent variable over
time. To address this issue, the GMM estimator developed by Nickell (1981) has been widely
adopted. Early literature laid the foundation by proposing difference GMM estimators to

Arellano and Bover (1995) and Blundell and Bond (1998), combines equations in levels and
differences. This approach enhances efficiency and helps mitigate the bias arising from fixed
effects and short time spans, making it particularly well-suited for our empirical setting,
which involves dynamic relationships and unbalanced panel data.6
