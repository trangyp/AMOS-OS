The Communication Complexity of Combinatorial Auctions with
Additional Succinct Bidders
Frederick V. Qiu
S. Matthew Weinberg
Qianfan Zhang
December 9, 2025
Abstract
We study the communication complexity of welfare maximization in combinatorial auctions
with bidders from either a standard valuation class (which require exponential communication to
explicitly state, such as subadditive or XOS), or arbitrary succinct valuations (which can be fully
described in polynomial communication, such as single-minded). Although succinct valuations
can be efficiently communicated, we show that additional succinct bidders have a nontrivial
impact on communication complexity of classical combinatorial auctions. Specifically:
Let n be the number of subadditive/XOS bidders. We show that for SA ∪Succ (the union of
subadditive and succinct valuations):
• There is a polynomial communication 3-approximation algorithm.
• As n →∞, there is a matching 3-hardness of approximation, which (a) is larger than the
optimal approximation ratio of 2 for SA [Fei09], and (b) holds even for SA ∪SM (the union
of subadditive and single-minded valuations).
• For all n ≥3, there is a constant separation between the optimal approximation ratios for
SA ∪SM and SA (and therefore between SA ∪Succ and SA as well).
Similarly, we show that for XOS ∪Succ:
• There is a polynomial communication 2-approximation algorithm.
• As n →∞, there is a matching 2-hardness of approximation, which (a) is larger than
the optimal approximation ratio of e/(e −1) for XOS [DNS10], and (b) holds even for
XOS ∪SM.
• For all n ≥2, there is a constant separation between the optimal approximation ratios for
XOS ∪SM and XOS (and therefore between XOS ∪Succ and XOS as well).

Introduction
In a combinatorial auction, there are a set of items M := [m] and bidders N := [n]. Each bidder
i ∈N holds a private monotone valuation function vi : 2M →R+. We often restrict the valuations
to a valuation class V depending on the problem setting. An allocation is an n-tuple of pairwise
disjoint subsets of M, and we denote the set of all allocations by Σ. The objective is to find an
allocation A = (A1, . . . , An) ∈Σ that approximately maximizes the social welfare P
i∈N vi(Ai).
Since each bidder i only knows their own valuation vi, two challenges arise:
1. The bidders must communicate in order to find a high welfare allocation.

2. A bidder may attempt to strategically manipulate the protocol by lying about their valuation,
reducing overall welfare in order to achieve higher utility for herself.
Combinatorial auctions are well understood with respect to challenge (1).
For example, with
fully cooperative bidders and polynomial communication, a tight Θ(√m)-approximation is known
for general valuations [LOS02, NS06, BN05], a tight 2-approximation is known for subadditive
valuations [Fei09, EFN+19], a tight e/(e −1)-approximation is known for XOS valuations [Fei09,
DNS10], and the optimal approximation ratio for submodular valuations is known to lie in [2e/(2e−
1), e/(e −1) −10−5] [DV13, FV10]. However, these approximation algorithms fail if bidders may
strategically manipulate the protocol.
Combinatorial auctions are also well understood with respect to challenge (2). The VCG auc-
tion [Vic61, Cla71, Gro73] is a mechanism by which bidders are charged prices p corresponding
to the allocation A, where p is carefully chosen so that bidders seeking to maximize their utility
vi(Ai) −pi are incentivized to cooperate with the protocol. Such mechanisms are called truthful.
However, the VCG auction requires exponential communication to run.
When both challenges are considered together, surprisingly little is known. The best known deter-
ministic truthful mechanisms achieve an ˜O(m)-approximation for general valuations and ˜O(√m)-
approximation for subadditive/XOS/submodular valuations [QW24], nearly a factor of √m worse
than their fully algorithmic (i.e., ignoring issues of incentives) counterparts. Not only that, but
known lower bounds for deterministic truthful mechanisms beyond those already known for non-
truthful algorithms are rare and apply only to restricted settings [AKSW20, RTWZ24]. As such,
understanding the interplay between efficiency and incentive compatibility is a paradigmatic prob-
lem at the intersection of Economics and Computing.
1.1
Combinatorial Auctions with Additional Succinct Bidders
As of now, we have only mentioned the valuation classes submodular, XOS, and subadditive.
These classes are interesting because while they provide additional structure that makes good
approximation tractable, the valuation classes are still rich enough so that bidders cannot just
trivially communicate their entire valuation. However, the recent work of [RTWZ24] proved the first
3-bidder separation between deterministic truthful mechanisms and non-truthful algorithms for the
valuation class subadditive-union-single-minded, initiating the study of what we term combinatorial
auctions with additional succinct bidders. We call a bidder succinct if their valuation can be fully
expressed in polynomial communication.
Although the union of two disparate valuation classes has not received much previous attention,
its study is motivated by the following.
• The approach of the 3-bidder deterministic truthful lower bound for subadditive-union-single-
minded valuations in [RTWZ24] is the most promising avenue towards a non-trivial poly(m)-
bidder deterministic truthful lower bound, as it is the first to leverage the full power of the
taxation complexity framework introduced in [Dob16b]. The 2-bidder lower bound for XOS
valuations in [AKSW20] also uses a consequence of the taxation complexity framework, but
their approach cannot be generalized beyond 2 bidders.
• While [RTWZ24] is the first work to point out the potential usefulness of additional succinct
bidders in proving lower bounds, the concept has shown up previously: [QW24] implicitly
proves that for VCG-based mechanisms (a subclass of deterministic truthful mechanisms),

Technical Highlights
Below, we share some technical highlights for our upper and lower bounds.
XOS ∪SM Lower Bound.
Let there be n XOS bidders and c single-minded bidders, and for
simplicity, let m = nc. Our XOS ∪SM lower bound works by dividing the m items into n rows
and c columns. For some small ε, each XOS bidder i holds a collection Si of subsets of row i of
size εc, and their valuation is the rank function of the downward-closed set family defined by Si.
Each single-minded bidder j just wants the items in column j. We weight the bidders’ valuations
accordingly so that the maximum welfare from the single-minded bidders is 1, and the maximum
welfare from the XOS bidders is 1.
If there exists a set S of εc columns where, slightly abusing notation, S ∈Si for all XOS bidders i,
then the optimal welfare is approximately 2 −ε: we can give the XOS bidders the εc columns S,
and the single-minded bidders everything else.

SA ∪SM Lower Bound.
Our SA ∪SM construction leverages the single-minded bidders like the
XOS ∪SM lower bound, but additionally uses the 2-inapproximability of subadditive valuations.
More generally, let V be a valuation class closed under addition and point-wise maximum (these
are the minimal properties needed to create an XOS ∪SM-style hard instance; indeed, observe that
XOS is the smallest non-trivial valuation class closed under addition and point-wise maximum).
Further, suppose that V cannot be α-approximated in polynomial communication.
Then repeat the construction of the XOS ∪SM hard instance, but instead of each column having
n items, make each column have nc items (and let m = nc2), and instead of each V-type bidder
wanting a single item from each column, the V-type bidders should form a hard instance within each
column. Then each V-type bidder’s overall value adds up across columns in an XOS-like fashion.
Now any efficient algorithm faces two challenges:
1. As in the XOS ∪SM construction, the algorithm cannot hope to find a small column set
satisfying the V-type bidders.
2. Additionally, the algorithm cannot achieve an α-approximation of the V-type bidders within
each column.
Therefore, by letting the V-type bidders contribute welfare α and the single-minded bidders con-
tribute welfare 1, the optimal welfare is approximately 1 + α −ε, but an efficient algorithm cannot
beat welfare 1.

approximate than the simpler XOS ∪SM hard instance.
It turns out that subadditive valuations satisfy a notion of strong 2-inapproximability that
allows for the SA ∪SM hard instance to work as intended. Note that such a property is not a
given; for example, XOS valuations do not satisfy any such strong inapproximability notion, despite
being e/(e −1)-inapproximable in the standard sense. This is why the XOS ∪SM hard instance
cannot be made meaningfully harder with more complexity within each column.
Our key technical contribution here is a notion of strong inapproximability, where a hard instance
remains hard even when all but a not-too-small subset of the bidders are removed. This notion and
the following result for subadditive valuations may be of independent interest for other communi-
cation lower bounds.
We show that subadditive valuations are strongly (2 −ε)-inapproximable. In particular, for all
sub-polynomial n (a slightly weaker result holds when n = poly(m)), there exist n subadditive
valuations whose welfare is 2n, but no polynomial communication algorithm can find an allocation
where (1) no more than t ∈[2, n] bidders receive a non-empty set of items and (2) the welfare is at
least (1+ε)t. The construction is a natural generalization of the 2-bidder construction in [EFN+19]
to n bidders, but the proof approach is qualitatively different; [EFN+19] uses internal information
complexity for their lower bound, but we must use external information complexity because the
internal information complexity can be 0 for any > 2-player communication protocol.
The V ∪SM construction above with V = SA then yields a (3 −ε)-inapproximability result for
SA ∪SM. We remark that our framework allows any future strong inapproximability results for

Algorithms.
For any α-approximable valuation class V, there is a trivial (1 + α)-approximation
for V ∪Succ: optimize the welfare of the succinct bidders and α-approximate the V-type bidders
separately, and choose the higher welfare allocation. It turns out that if V-type valuations are
strongly (α −ε)-inapproximable, this trivial algorithm is asymptotically optimal. Indeed, because
subadditive valuations are both 2-approximable and strongly (2 −ε)-inapproximable, the trivial
3-approximation algorithm for SA ∪Succ is optimal.
The XOS ∪Succ algorithm is more interesting. The optimal e/(e −1)-approximation algorithm
for XOS valuations solves an LP and rounds the solution using (correlated) contention resolution
schemes (CRS) for each item. Omitting details, the e/(e −1)-approximation stems from the fact
that the optimal CRS is (e−1)/e-selectable. Importantly, while the rounding procedure introduces
correlations between the items, approximation guarantees for XOS valuations are unaffected by
such correlations.
On the other hand, succinct bidders can be very affected by correlations. For example, single-
minded bidders get nonzero value only when receiving their entire desired set. To handle this, our
algorithm first learns the full valuations of the succinct bidders, which allows them to be treated
as a single surrogate bidder. Then our algorithm rounds the LP solution using online contention
resolution schemes (OCRS) for each item. The benefit of using an OCRS is that we can correlate
them into all making the same decision for the first bidder. Then by presenting the surrogate
succinct bidder to the OCRSs first, we avoid any unfavorable correlations. We again omit the
details, but the 2-approximation we get for XOS ∪Succ stems from the fact that the optimal
OCRS is 1/2-selectable. Thus, the seemingly unrelated optimal approximation ratios of e/(e −1)
without succinct bidders and 2 with succinct bidders actually stem from a deeper connection to
contention resolution.
One final note is that the XOS ∪Succ (SA ∪Succ, respectively) algorithm works for XOS (sub-
additive, respectively) valuations, plus a single general monotone bidder, which succinct bidders
can be reduced to. On the other hand, our lower bounds only require single-minded bidders, which
are a narrower class than succinct bidders. Thus, there is an interesting equivalence in the optimal
approximation induced by the addition of single-minded bidders, succinct bidders, and a single
general monotone bidder.

Related Work
As discussed previously, there has been extensive study in the communication complexity of algo-
rithms for combinatorial auctions under a variety of valuation classes [LOS02, DNS06, BN05, Fei09,
EFN+19, DNS10, DV13, FV10]. Our work seeks to understand the communication complexity of
algorithms for combinatorial auctions with additional succinct bidders, which is directly motivated
by applications in [RTWZ24] (and to a lesser extent, applications in [QW24]) to lower bounds for
deterministic truthful mechanisms [AKSW20, Dob16b].
There is also significant work in many adjacent settings. For example, while “truthful” is usually
shorthand for “it is an ex-post Nash equilibrium for bidders to behave truthfully,” [DRV22, RST+21]
considers the communication complexity of dominant strategy truthful mechanisms. There is also
a long line of work in developing randomized truthful mechanisms [DNS06, Dob07, KV12, Dob16a,

Preliminaries
Combinatorial Auctions.
In a combinatorial auction, there are a set of items M := [m] and
bidders N := [n]. Each bidder i ∈N holds a private valuation function vi : 2M →R+ that is
monotone, i.e., vi(S) ≤vi(T) for all S ⊆T. We often restrict the valuations to a valuation class V
depending on the problem setting. An allocation is an n-tuple of pairwise disjoint subsets of M, and
we denote the set of all allocations by Σ. The objective is to find an allocation A = (A1, . . . , An) ∈Σ
that approximately maximizes the social welfare P
i∈N vi(Ai). Unless otherwise noted, we take the
number of bidders n ≤poly(m). For notational convenience, we may also define n to be the number
of non-succinct bidders.
Communication Complexity and Protocols.
We study the combinatorial auction problem
in the general communication model.
In particular, we consider the n-player number-in-hand
blackboard communication model, where the input known to each player i ∈N is her valuation
function vi ∈V, and the messages sent are visible to every bidder. The communication cost of a
protocol is the maximum expected number of bits that are written to the blackboard in the worst
case, and the communication complexity of a problem is the minimum communication cost of a
protocol that solves it w.p. at least 2/3.

Succinct Valuations.
We call a valuation class U succinct if |U| ≤2poly(m). In plain terms,
a succinct valuation class is one whose member valuations can be “represented” in polynomial
communication. Note that any succinct valuation class has a polynomial communication welfare
maximizer.
Since representability is always relative to some communication protocol, the notion of “succinct
valuations” is not a well-defined concept, though we will informally use the term “succinct valu-
ation” and refer to Succ as the “class of succinct valuations” for convenience. Formally, for any
valuation class V, we say that
• There exists an α-approximation for V ∪Succ in z communication if for every succinct
valuation class U, there exists an α-approximation algorithm for V ∪U in z communication.
• Any α-approximation for V ∪Succ uses z communication if for some succinct valuation class
U, any α-approximation algorithm for V ∪U uses z communication.

Algorithms
In this section, we design communication-efficient algorithms for SA ∪Succ and XOS ∪Succ.
Theorem 3.1. There exists a (3−2/n)-approximation for SA ∪Succ in poly(m, n) communication.
Theorem 3.2. There exists a 2-approximation for XOS ∪Succ in poly(m, n) communication.
3.1
(3 −2/n)-approximation for SA ∪Succ
The (3−2/n)-approximation for SA ∪Succ is straightforward: since we can optimize the welfare of
just the succinct bidders, and we can 2-approximate the welfare from just the subadditive bidders,

We first outline the main ideas of the proof. For simplicity, let a = n (otherwise, discard the
additional bidders) and let m = rc (otherwise, discard the additional bidders/items). The hard
instance for V ∪SM is constructed as follows:
• Arrange the items into c columns of r items R × C, and let ε > 0 be small.
• Each single-minded bidder j gets value ε for all the items in column j.
• The V-type bidders form (the same) arbitrary instance with welfare λ on the items within
each of the columns.
• Each V-type bidder i holds an exponentially-sized collection of column-sets Si ⊆C, where
every S ∈Si is of size εc. Across columns, each V-type bidder i adds up their values in the
column-set S ∈Si which maximizes their value (this is where we need V to be closed under
addition and point-wise maximum).
• There exists a single set S∗∈S1 ∩· · · ∩Sn.
The optimal allocation gives columns S∗to the V-type bidders and the remaining columns to the
single-minded bidders, which yields welfare (1 + λ −ε)εc.
However, since S1, . . . , Sn are exponentially-sized, an efficient algorithm cannot hope to find S∗.
Further, by (roughly) λ-inapproximability, an efficient algorithm cannot obtain more than 1/λ of
the welfare from the V-type bidders.
So giving everything to the single-minded bidders yields
welfare εc, and giving everything to the V-type bidders (via a λ-approximation algorithm) also
yields welfare εc.

Definition 4.1 (Scarce). An allocation is t-scarce if all but at most t bidders are allocated ∅.
Definition 4.9 (Strong Inapproximability). A valuation class V is strongly α(·)-inapproximable in

Notice the qualitative differences between the two results: the first gives stronger convergence to

However, solving the OR of z problems does require z times the information (for an appropriately

7.3
Information Complexity of FarSets
The final piece of the communication lower bound is a lower bound on the information complexity of
FarSets with respect to a distribution satisfying the requirements of Theorem 7.14. As before, the
proof approach follows that of [EFN+19], but differs in the technical details due to using external
instead of internal information complexity.
The proof sketch looks like a hybrid argument from cryptography:
1. Suppose that the information complexity of a protocol is small on a distribution of 0-instances.
2. There is just enough freedom in the promise of what a 0-instance looks like, so that we can
chain together many 0-instances (call these instances links), with each adjacent instance in
the chain differing only by one player’s input.
3. By a counting argument, we show that if the information complexity is low, there exists some
chain where the protocol reveals little information for each of its links. Fix any such chain.
4. Using standard information theory, little information revealed means the distribution of the
transcripts for every link in the chain must be close to each other.
5. By what is essentially the randomized analogue of a deterministic rectangle argument, we
argue that even if we mix inputs from different links in the chain, the resulting transcript
must still be distributionally close. This step is the main technical component of the proof.
6. If we have constructed the chain such that a 1-instance can be made by mixing inputs from
different links, then there exists a 0-instance and a 1-instance such that their transcripts (and
hence the output of the protocol) are distributionally close, which implies the worst-case error
of the protocol must be high.
7. We conclude that any protocol with low worst-case error must have high information com-
plexity (relatively speaking).
