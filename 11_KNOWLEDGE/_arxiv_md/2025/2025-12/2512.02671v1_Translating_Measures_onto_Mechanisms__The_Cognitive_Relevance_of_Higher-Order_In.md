Abstract
Higher–order information theory has become a rapidly growing toolkit in computational neuroscience,
motivated by the idea that multivariate dependencies can reveal aspects of neural computation
and communication invisible to pairwise analyses. Yet functional interpretations of synergy and
redundancy often outpace principled arguments for how statistical quantities map onto mechanistic
cognitive processes. Here we review the main families of higher-order measures with the explicit goal
of translating mathematical properties into defensible mechanistic inferences. Firstly, we systematize
Shannon-based multivariate metrics and demonstrate that higher-order dependence is parsimoniously
characterized by two largely independent axes: interaction strength and redundancy-synergy balance.
We argue that balanced layering of synergistic integration and redundant broadcasting optimizes
multiscale complexity, formalizing a computation-communication tradeoff. We then examine the par-
tial information decomposition and outline pragmatic considerations for its deployment in neural
data. Equipped with the relevant mathematical essentials, we connect redundancy-synergy balance
to cognitive function by progressively embedding their mathematical properties in real-world con-
straints, starting with small synthetic systems before gradually building up to neuroimaging. We close
by identifying key future directions for mechanistic insight: cross-scale bridging, intervention-based
validation, and thermodynamically grounded unification of information dynamics.

1 Introduction
Higher-order information theory has gained increasing popularity within computational neuroscience to
investigate how the brain’s many components share information to serve cognitive functioning. Informa-
tion theory has been argued to be the lingua franca for the modeling of complex systems [1], such as the
brain. If we grant this, extending the vocabulary beyond its usual domain of bivariate interactions to
three or more variables would represent a valuable step towards capturing higher-order cognitive func-
tioning. Higher-order information theory provides a more general language for describing information
processing beyond linear, bivariate correlations, which still dominate neuroscientific analysis methods,
such as functional connectivity analyses. This renders higher-order information theory uniquely sensi-
tive to emergent multivariate effects supporting cognition, whereas conventional methods are otherwise
insensitive to them. Cognition is often characterized as an emergent property of the system [2], where
the whole is irreducible to the parts in isolation. Stereoscopic vision, for instance, is a canonical exam-
ple of such a synergistic effect [3]: It is to be found in neither eye or hemisphere in isolation but only
through their triangulation with the scene information that they capture complementarily. In contrast,
color information is redundantly processed in each hemisphere. The blind spot of established methods for
such key emergent properties of cognitive function might be filled in by higher-order information theory.
Redundant information is information copied over multiple subsets of elements, while synergistic
information is the information only disclosed when considering them jointly. The alluring promise of
synergy and redundancy to capture inherently higher-order effects, where the information present in
the whole is greater (or smaller) than the sum of information between the parts, has inspired various
functional interpretations in the cognitive neuroscience literature, from their role in multisensory feature
integration [4] to explaining psychedelic effects on consciousness [5] (for a recent overview, see [6]). These
efforts have been mostly facilitated by the partial information decomposition (PID), since it was first
proposed that information contributions of multiple sources about a target variable could be separated
into non-negative atoms of redundant, synergistic and unique information [7]. Behind the PID stands a
decades-old effort in information theory to deal with negative bit values when generalizing the notion of
information sharing to more than two variables, which Claude Shannon had restricted himself to in his
foundational work [8–11].
Since [12] published their guide on how to understand and use higher-order information measurement
tools up to the PID, their application in interpreting empirical data has risen dramatically. Although
more theory has been developed and readily applied since then, questions around fundamental theo-
retical disagreements (What does redundancy mean in mathematically precise terms?) and functional
interpretation have become more immediate. For this reason, we regard it as an appropriate moment to
recapitulate the most essential theory to carefully build towards a well-founded functional interpretation
when measuring higher-order information. To avoid model reification and interpretational pitfalls, we
need to distinguish mathematical ‘map’ of our models and functional ‘territory’ of the cognitive systems
they model [13, 14], yet build mechanistic bridges between them. In other words, the properties of our
statistical models should not be conflated with the properties of the system that the statistics describe.
In this review, we seek to guide the reader through the foundation mathematics of higher-order
information theory to understand how a link between model properties and cognitive function can be
meaningfully established (without over-attributing mechanisms where they do not exist). By starting
with simple models, we emphasize intuitive understanding that can inform sound application. Concretely,
we seek to connect the purely mathematical properties of higher-order information measures to their
capacities and limitations in furnishing mechanistic explanations. We first systematize Shannon-based
multivariate measures in terms of interaction strength and redundancy-synergy balance, showing how
they already constrain functional interpretation. We then review the PID, emphasizing the conceptual
and practical consequences of choosing a redundancy function, and illustrating why this choice deserves
more than a technical footnote. Finally, we step into the territory of empirical data to trace how syn-
ergistic and redundant motifs manifest across temporal, spatial, and contextual scales. The overarching
goal is pragmatic: to equip the experimentalist with a principled understanding of what each measure
can and cannot tell us about neural function, and how to deploy them in ways that genuinely advance
mechanistic insight.

2 From Shannon Entropy to Redundancy-Synergy Balance
Shannon’s original work [8] was concerned with the engineering task of lossless transmission of informa-
tion from a sender A to a receiver B. Still, the metric he created, mutual information, is itself undirected.
Instead of capturing a physical, causally powerful flow of information, it has instead been shown to be
much closer to a generalized notion of correlation between two variables that can subsume various other
linear, bivariate tests of statistical dependence [15]. Ultimately, as cognitive scientists, we are interested
in how function arises from the higher-order interplay among many components, often numbering hun-
dreds or thousands of neurons. This implies a two-fold challenge: (1) using the appropriate higher-order
information metrics to fit the research question, and (2) translating these measures onto mechanistic
inferences about cognitive function. To this end, we will move from mathematical tools, illustrated in
Boolean toy systems, progressively towards biological cognition in the context of real-world demands.
2.1 Diverging Notions of Higher-Order Information Sharing

practical purposes, we will restrict ourselves to discrete probability spaces, starting with a single variable.

information is about the reduction of uncertainty. Roughly speaking, entropy inflates the scope of our
uncertainty about which state a system is in, while information deflates it. Formally, entropy is defined as

Only when considering an additional random variable X2 can we start to talk about mutual infor-
mation, uncertainty shared between two variables. Mutual information is necessarily nonnegative, as the
joint entropy H(X1, X2), the entropy of the composite system (the union of X1 and X2), is smaller than
the sum of the individual entropies. (Joint) entropy is a subadditive quantity, since the joint entropy
H(X1, X2) can be smaller (but never greater) than the sum of their entropies individually considered. If
two variables are always in the same state, for instance, their joint entropy is reduced to H(X1, X2) = 1
bit, while the sum of their individual entropies H(X1) + H(X2) = 2 bits. Since we can learn about
the entropy of one from observing the other, in this case, their entropy is said to be overlapping. This
gives rise to non-zero mutual information. Regardless of which one is measured, MI is symmetric and
undirected, so that
I(X1; X2) = I(X2; X1) = H(X1) + H(X2) −H(X1, X2).
(2)
One bit of information about variable X1, or even a fraction of it, chips away uncertainty about
the state of X2, and vice versa. Let’s assume a neuron can only occupy two possible states: firing and
resting. Then, for two neurons to share one bit of information, both need to be firing with maximal
randomness when individually considered (H(X1) + H(X2) = 2), yet do so in perfect dependence when
jointly considered, collapsing their joint entropy into one mutually shared bit (H(X1, X2) = 1). This
means they show a joint firing pattern that reflects either perfect synchrony or perfect asynchrony (one
always fires when the other does not, and vice versa). Importantly, mutual information will only tell us
about the reliability of this association, but does not distinguish between these polar opposite cases in
terms of correlation.
While shared information is easily interpretable for two variables, the idea of shared information
between three or more variables remains poorly defined. Under a liberal conception, it could be the
information shared by at least two variables (out of possibly hundreds or thousands). In contrast, the
strictest conception could count as mutual only randomness shared among all variables. Capturing these
different definitions of higher-order dependency motivates introducing various ways to account for probing
dependency mathematically. Multiple measures have been introduced to generalize mutual information,
each effectively querying the system in a different way. We may ask:
1. To what extent are the parts coupled? (Total Correlation)
2. To what extent is the whole constrained by relationships among the parts? (Dual Total Correlation)
3. To what extent does redundancy dominate synergy? (O-information & Co-information (for even n))
4. What is the aggregate information sharing strength? (S-information)
5. What is the average information across a cut through the system? (TSE complexity)

ing the prototypes of redundancy and synergy: the COPY (‘giant bit’) and XOR (‘parity’) distributions

dancy and synergy, so it is worth lingering on them. As we can see in Figure 2, the Venn diagram is not

we look at their corresponding truth tables, though, we can see for the former that the state of each indi-
vidual variable is fully determined by knowing the state of any of the other variables. This is redundancy

for the XOR distribution. Although each variable, considered individually, is equally likely to be 0 or 1
for both, the joint state introduces additional uncertainty in the synergistic case. As we will now explore,
different queries to the system account for these intrinsically higher-order dependencies in various ways.

2.4 O-information (& co-information): To what extent does redundancy
dominate synergy?
Although TC and DTC have a bias towards measuring redundancy and synergy, respectively, they tally
their contributions together with the overall information sharing strength, which does not differentiate
between synergy and redundancy and also includes bivariate dependencies (cf. Figure 3). When one

two values that approximately capture (a) how strongly information is shared and (b) whether the
whole or the parts predominantly hold it with minimal computational overhead. Generally, the co-
information, total correlation and dual total correlation do not fall neatly into these two categories. The
TSE complexity scales much more poorly than the S-information, while correlating strongly with it in
neuroscientific data [24].
Overall, these entropy-based measures have established themselves as valuable tools that require
few assumptions beyond the core tenets of information theory and can quickly provide answers to key
questions about large systems, such as large neural ensembles. Since redundancy and synergy seem to
play such a fundamental role in explaining higher-order information sharing, we might ask whether we
can cleanly disentangle them, not just to measure their aggregate balance (O-information), but also to
measure their contributions directly. Additionally, we might ask how they are distributed among many
variables to compose into the purely Shannon-based metrics so far reviewed. These are the objectives
behind the various proposals for decomposing higher-order information into disjoint parts, which turns
out to be much trickier than it might seem.
3 Partial Information Decomposition(s)
At the core of the Partial Information Decomposition (PID) stands the idea that the information multiple
source variables hold about a target variable (separated by a semicolon, such as in I(X1, X2; X3)) can
be decomposed into unique, redundant, and synergistic atoms (Figure 4). The label ‘partial information
atoms’ suggests that the PID is trying to break information down into its smallest constituent parts. In
contrast to the undirected measures previously reviewed, directionality is baked into its approach, so that
we consistently quantify information about the target in particular. If we take X3 to be the target, then:

I(X1, X2; X3) = {1}{2} + {1} + {2} + {12},
(11)
where {1}{2} is (the atom representing) the information in either X1 or X2 (redundancy) about X3,
{12} is the information about X3 only available by considering X1 and X2 jointly (synergy), while {1}
and {2} represent the information unique to each source. Despite the promise of cutting information at
its joints in this way, there are three main issues with the PID that we want to highlight: (1) It is not
clear how to measure the atoms in an uncontroversial way, (2) the inherent distinction between sources
and targets brings additional, contentious assumptions with it, and (3) its computational as well as
interpretational complexity renders its practical usefulness limited.
The decomposition can be algebraically unlocked by defining a function that defines one of the
atoms, usually the redundant one, which then allows assigning bit values to all synergistic and unique
information atoms. This means that the research program around the PID has revolved around finding a
proper mathematical definition of redundancy, although seeking agreement on a particular function has
largely been abandoned in favor of conceptual pluralism.
Being a ‘proper’ redundancy function refers here to the property of satisfying a set of axioms, which
themselves heavily rely on our intuitions about what redundancy means precisely. However, it is already
in the mathematical distillation of these intuitions into axioms where disagreements lurk, which has
effectively led to a pluralism of conceptions about what exactly redundancy refers to. This is reminis-
cent of the disagreements discussed in section 2.1 regarding the mathematical commitments one must
make when generalizing MI to higher dimensions. Instead of reviewing the whole battery of redundancy
functions that have been proposed (see, e.g., [25] for a thorough technical comparison), we focus our dis-
cussion on the general difficulties arising from trying to make the resulting PID satisfy all the properties
we would like to see.
To illustrate issues (1) and (2), we consider the AND gate, where the outcome variable X3 is only in
state 1 if both inputs X1 and X2 are both in state 1 as well (see Figure 4). In this case, we do have a
designated target variable, an asymmetry in the truth table that we did not have for the XOR and COPY
distributions. This might or might not align with the source-target distinction that the PID forces us to
make, effectively yielding one ‘aligned’ and two ‘misaligned’ decompositions. In other words, ‘alignment’
here means that the target in the PID is actually the output variable of the AND gate. Looking at
Table 2, we can see that a subset of redundancy functions disagree in their assignment of bit values (1)
to unique, redundant and synergistic atoms and (2) when comparing ‘aligned’ and ‘misaligned’ target
variable choices.
Besides the divergent (negative) results, which I±, Isx, and Iccs show, the other shown redundancy
functions (of which we have collected seven popular choices) at least show consistency in this simple case
of the AND gate. When applied to neuroscientific data, several among the proposals have been shown to
correlate strongly with each other [26], or at least permit qualitatively similar conclusions [27]. Again,
I± and Isx (I±’s differentiable cousin) stand out as notable oddballs, since they permit negative atom
values (together with Iccs).
As more and more redundancy functions have been proposed [26, 28–34] with, as yet, only meager
convergence of opinions, a central problem at the core of the PID undertaking has become clear: Not
only is redundancy taken to mean different things by different authors in their formalizations, but also
has the inclusion of one set of axioms resulted in tradeoffs with the violation of other axioms deemed
essential by other theorists. Unfortunately, it still remains unclear how one is to make a decision on
which redundancy function to use without getting bogged down in mathematical details. The fact that
most measures correlate strongly for real data, as mentioned above, should at least allow for qualitatively
similar conclusions regardless of the redundancy function chosen. However, one can hardly say that
these conclusions are robust, though, since they do depend on the assumptions made by the redundancy
function.
Even if one were to hypothetically discover the ‘right’ redundancy function, a huge practical limitation
of the PID is the superexponential growth of PID atoms with the number of included variables. The case
with more than eight variables has already become infeasible, even with high-performance computing,
in contrast to the family of entropy-based measures, which can scale easily to hundreds of variables.

The hefty computational price tag arising from the algorithmic mission of the PID is a sweeping one,
aiming for a complete decomposition instead of just a summary metric, like the the co-information or
O-information, which only indicate overall tendencies towards redundancy or synergy. It seems neither
feasible nor necessarily useful from an epistemological viewpoint to aim for complete decompositions of
larger systems, which quickly blow up in their computational as well as semantic complexity. Ultimately,
we want to use the measures to tell us something meaningful about information processing in our system
of interest. Instead of worrying about the prohibitively high costs of a complete PID for neural recordings
from dozens or hundreds of sites, we should take into account that the granularity of our decomposi-
tion should be just fine enough, and not necessarily finer than that, to answer our research questions.
Otherwise, the complexity of the results exceeds our own capacity to draw interpretable inferences from
them.
If one is willing to bite the bullet and commit to a redundancy function, one can ameliorate PID’s
limitations in several ways. Firstly, for the sake of interpretability, restricting oneself to triads (two sources
and one target) is helpful, as is usually done in practice. Then, one should ensure that the inherent source-
target directionality in the PID should align itself with the causal source-target directionality in the
system modeled. The previously mentioned study by [27] did this at a single-neuron level, for instance,
by taking basal and apical dendrites as source and axonal action potential as target variables. Usually,
though, the causal directionality is far from being this clear from the outset. In that case, one may
infer directed connections between variables by using transfer entropy (TE) [35], which quantifies how
much X1’s past can inform us about X2’s future states above X1’s own past. In this way one can subset
significantly interconnected triads at the time scale that reflects underlying cause-effect delays [36]. By
using the fact that causes generally are taken to precede effects in time, one can harness TE to increase
the likelihood of finding proper source-target alignment, while minimizing computational overhead.

4 Functional Interpretations of Synergy and Redundancy
Whether with entropy-based measures or PID, what can we infer from these fundamentally statistical
measures about the underlying functional structure of the system it is applied to? We seek to build
intuitions from the math to the goal-driven functioning of cognition under external constraints, progres-
sively adding layers of complexity. We start off associating redundancy-synergy balance with (1) directed
network motifs, then add (2) temporal evolution to explore functional dynamics, before adding to a
functional interpretation (3) the crucial element of their role in adaptation to demands external to the
system measured.
4.1 Network Motifs of Redundancy-Synergy Balance and the
Computation-Communication Tradeoff
So where does the structure of logical dependencies in maximally redundant / synergistic toy systems
point us towards in terms of functional difference? In order to inch closer towards characterizing functional
purposes of synergy and redundancy, we consider two systems of seven variables with known directed
dependencies that represent dual hierarchies in terms of redundancy and synergy (Figure 5a). For the
synergistic example, we find a feedforward hierarchy of XOR gates that recurrently integrates inputs at
each level to inform the output at the next level. In this case, we find maximal sensitivity to inputs,
because a bit flip in any of the input variables leads to a bit flip at the top of the hierarchy. Crucially,
all the states of the ‘XOR hierarchy’ jointly shape its state, where knowledge of all the other variables
together is necessary to determine the output state.
In contrast, a ‘broadcast hierarchy’ of COPY operations lets all parts move in synchrony so that a
bit flip in any variable (here placed at the top) determines the state of all other variables. In this way,
the maximally redundant system is constrained to only occupying two possible joint states out of 27
allowed ones. The maximally synergistic system has a much higher degree of degeneracy in what joint
states correspond to individual states, retaining half (26) of the allowed joint state repertoire (Figure
5b). Thus, the parts move in synchronous lockstep for maximally redundant systems as the same state
is effectively copied onto other parts. In contrast, the parts move in maximally synergistic systems in
complete context-dependence, where no context short of all inputs considered together defines a part.
Due to their statistical degeneracy, maximally synergistic systems look a lot closer to independent
noise. Synergy requires by definition weak correlations between individual parts. As correlations are
increased, the joint state repertoire becomes so constrained that redundancy starts to dominate. From
an inferential perspective we cannot rely on these here a priori defined dependencies, so that we are
more likely to ascribe synergy to spurious correlations, which can easily appear from undersampling [38].
Coming back to the idealized case, we find an ‘all-onto-one’ motif on the synergistic end of the
spectrum, where all the inputs jointly are needed to determine the state of any one of them. On the
opposite end, all the arrows point outwards from any one variable, allowing for the deduction of all
the other states from a single one. The O-information directly quantifies the balance between these two

The brain needs to balance these two extremes against one another to not just move in unison as
one (extreme redundancy), associated with epileptic [39] or unconscious states [5], nor to fragment into
a brittle, highly context-sensitive regime of runaway integration (extreme synergy). To serve functional
purpose, integration and segregation need to be balanced within the system, precisely what the TSE
complexity measures. TSE complexity been shown to be intimately linked to optimizing various functional
properties of cognition [40], as reflected by measures of perturbational sensitivity [41], criticality [42, 43]
or metastability [44]. In line with this intuition, we find that a mixture of XOR and COPY distributions
peaks in its TSE complexity for a mixture close (Ω(X) = 0.33) to where redundancy and synergy
are balanced (Ω(X) = 0) (Figure 5). Although these simple distributions do not functionally compute
anything, we find here an example of how redundancy-synergy balance in the very structure of a network
can support more complex organization.
The two prototypical directed motifs of information-sharing behind redundancy and synergy may
serve complementary, dual purposes within a complex system: Redundant broadcasting of information
replicates the same information from one part onto the rest for communication, whereas synergistic
integration creates information in one part from the rest for computation. Several authors have noted that
synergy is closely linked to computation, occurring when multiple inputs jointly influence the output of
a third element [12, 45–47]. Although redundancy is most frequently associated with robustness [3, 48],
we find that its duality to synergistic computation can instead be interpreted as a sign of large-scale
broadcasting of information (communication) [49]. This can then underwrite the robust availability of
information in multiple variables (or neurons).
Importantly, we find in synergy-redundancy balance a mirroring of the tradeoff between computa-
tion and communication: Communicating the same piece of information onto many downstream parts
redundantly stands in direct conflict to their capacity to compute their own state from the joint con-
text. If a neuron strongly entrains its neighbors, the communication of its state diminishes their power
to deliver their own contributions towards computation jointly. This is directly supported by a series of
studies that investigated the network motifs associated with synergy and redundancy in brain organoids
at the circuit level [12, 36, 42, 49–53]. One of the main takeaways is that synergy is associated with
recurrent integration of inputs towards a joint upstream output, while feedback from target to sources
decreases synergy [49, 51]. The authors attribute the negative correlation between feedback of the target
neuron and synergy to the top-down signal that reduces the variance of upstream neurons, which they
require to interact synergistically. The upstream feedback signal effectively induces redundancy down-
stream as prediction errors are back-propagated. This echoes the purely mathematical treatment with
a functional explanation at the circuit level: Horizontal modulation of source neurons corresponds to
increased synergy, while the top-down broadcasting of information reduces it.
4.2 Temporal Evolution and Information Dynamics
So far we have only related the logical structure in essentially atemporal probability distributions to
cognitive function. However, cognition, at least in the brain, is primarily taken to unfold across time,
which we have to account for in order to investigate synergy and redundancy’s impact on dynamics.
Once more, we first take recourse to fully transparent Boolean systems to investigate dynamics arising
out of informational properties that are determined a priori. Particularly insightful work in this domain

This adds not only a notion of temporality (the system evolves over time steps) but also locality (only
the local vicinity matters), moving it somewhat closer to real cognitive systems. CAs have been shown
to provide a Turing-complete [55] model of how complex patterns are generated by simple rules and
allow for investigating the dynamics of complex systems in a fully transparent, rule-governed sandbox
environment.
[56] used a genetic algorithm to evolve Boolean networks’ updating rules towards maximal and mini-
mal O-information, before investigating differences in the patterns these networks generate. While highly
redundant networks very quickly settled into a repetitive attractor state, highly synergistic ones took
much longer to detour through state space, with average transient lengths on par with those of randomly
updating networks. From the perspective of an external observer, synergy acts here as a generator of
novelty, or irreducible randomness [57], while redundancy constrains it. Every single-cell update of the
network results from a distributed set of inputs, which themselves are highly context-dependent. Thus,
a single bit flip can set off interpenetrating cascades of bit flips down the line, reminiscent of chaotic
dynamics.

The chaotic dynamics associated with strongly negative O-information can be explained through
highly interdependent contributions of neighboring cells, where single bit flips are much more likely to
also flip the output state than in redundant networks. [58] support the idea that we can understand purely
synergistic networks as highly sensitive to initial conditions and perturbations, setting off an echo of
unpredictable ripples through the network as it evolves through time. The opposite holds for redundancy:
Periodic, oscillatory dynamics, which are hardly sensitive to single-bit flips, robustly prevail. [58] showed,
by translating update rules into logic gates, that higher redundancy corresponds to fewer distributed
logical operations. As updates in one part of the CA do not interdependently factor into the computation
of output states in other parts, redundancy implies a much poorer computational expressivity of the joint
state space. This restriction can be of great advantage, though, when information needs to be reliably
communicated with high fault tolerance [59]. Redundant interactions can serve as perturbation-absorbing
structures that can channel cascades onto stable trajectories [60].
In line with the more repetitive oscillatory patterns found in redundancy-dominated CAs [56], recent
neuroscientific studies of local field potentials (LFPs) [61, 62] showed that narrowband oscillations are
almost exclusively connected with redundancy-dominance. If we consider the fact that oscillations picked
up by LFPs are generally taken to arise from large-scale synchrony at the circuit level [63], this makes
sense in the light of the link we drew in previous sections between synchronous state evolution and redun-
dancy. If, however, broadband signals with no characteristic frequency govern brain dynamics, this is a
strong sign of synergy-dominance. As the brain accommodates surprising information, synergy-dominated
transients give way to redundancy-dominated oscillations through recurrent feedback loops [64]. Although
much work remains to be done on the cross-scale relationship between information decompositions at
different levels of resolution, a clearer picture is emerging: As high-dimensional stimuli impinge on the
sensorium, synergistic computation rapidly integrates unpredicted information within low-level modules.
As the local components converge upon a collective response, gamma oscillations [65] allow for a strong,
redundant signal to be broadcasted [62, 66]. Large-scale narrowband oscillations may therefore index
high-precision regimes in which predictive inference has reduced uncertainty, yielding low-dimensional,
redundant dynamics suitable for broadcasting.
It becomes evident that the brain needs to deploy redundant and synergistic information dynamics in
concert to integrate information about the world without losing global coherence. Boolean networks with
maximal TSE complexity showed this kind of balance: they exhibited longer transients than redundant
networks but showed much clearer attractor dynamics than synergistic ones [56]. Likewise, the brain
nests more irregular transients within slower oscillations. Through highly synergistic processing, the
brain introduces the variability in outputs needed to respond to the richness of external states flexibly,
complying with Ashby’s law of requisite variety [67, 68]. In excess, however, this variability would not
allow the state space to be sufficiently bounded to revolve around a set of homeostatic attractor states
[69]. From a dynamical stability perspective, redundancy can be thought of as ensuring canalization
[70, 71] of trajectories, converting variability in input sequences into coherence in output. What this
output should be steered towards depends, though, entirely on the (task-)context, whose role in shaping
the synergy-redundancy balance will be investigated in the next section.
4.3 The Contextual Dimension: Connecting Measures to External Influences
In this final section, we will turn to embedding the putative functional roles of synergy and redundancy
within the contextual constraints under which cognition operates. Although Boolean systems provide an
excellent ground for honing intuitions about the properties of redundancy and synergy, they are far too
simple to capture the externally constrained functioning that evolution imposes on cognitive systems. We
highlight three factors external to a cognitive system that significantly determine the role that synergy
and redundancy end up playing within it: (1) task context, (2) resource constraints, and (3) exogenous
noise. We first focus on task-induced effects, then show how task goals select synergistic vs redundant
regimes, and finally discuss how resource limits and noise bias this balance.
To understand the functional purposes of redundancy and synergy support, it helps interpret the mea-
sures when they are taken within a task context. Only then, redundancy and synergy can be meaningfully
related to cognitive states, such as done by [73], for instance: Intracranial recordings from macaques’

Artificial neural networks (ANNs), like brains, need to deal with limited resources when solving
task demands. This can effectively induce an informational bottleneck [75], which is instrumental in
learning task-relevant representations. Only by ignoring some task-irrelevant information can patterns
be meaningfully abstracted, while overfitting to irrelevant details is punished. This inductive learning
process can be studied transparently in ANNs. It was shown that increased synergy was associated with
both layer depth and training time [76, 77]. Information that is redundantly represented by many nodes,
dominant during early training, is gradually eliminated as the network learns less complex, more efficient
encoding strategies. The algorithm effectively leverages the computational power of synergistic encoding
with the limited resources it has. Informational bottlenecks force ANNs towards synergistic dynamics,
making them sensitive to information that must be considered jointly to be task-relevant [78].
Beyond characterizing state changes across task epochs, redundancy and synergy have also been
used directly to probe how stimulus and behavior are jointly encoded in neural activity. For audiovisual
speech, [4] decomposed the mutual information between auditory and visual speech envelopes and MEG
responses. It showed that theta-band activity in left posterior STG chiefly carries redundant information
about the two modalities. In contrast, the left motor cortex encodes synergistic information that emerges
only when both streams are considered together. This highlights that although oscillations exhibit redun-
dancy when considered without context, accounting for a task variable can bring more subtle details
about neural representations to light. In a related vein, [79] used redundancy between stimulus, MEG
responses, and behavior to distinguish task-relevant from task-irrelevant coding, demonstrating how mul-
tivariate information can directly index which aspects of neural activity are functionally recruited for
performance.

a noise-robust normalization to counteract fallacious inferences based on spurious synergy/redundancy.
This highlights once more how crucial it is to ensure that our models’ readouts are carefully translated
into functional interpretations.
5 Future Directions
Although the nascent community applying higher-order information theory to neuroscientific data seems
to converge on putative functional interpretations of synergy and redundancy in the brain, many questions
remain. As highlighted in this paper, the mapping of information-theoretic quantities in our models
onto functionally relevant mechanisms is tricky, yet promising due to the general language with which
information theory can describe computation and communication at various scales.
The first issue that needs to be tackled more rigorously is the explicit connection of information
dynamics across scales. Various recent studies on whole-brain dynamics have been conducted using highly
coarse-grained fMRI data [3, 24, 83–85], measuring the activity of 105 −106 neurons per spatial bin
(voxel) [86], a massive difference from the circuit-level studies discussed that come closer to measuring
101 −102 neurons. Too few studies have been conducted at the mesoscale of intracortical recordings
(around 103 neurons) to allow for an explanatory bridge between micro and macro. This can lead to
seemingly conflicting results from the same dataset for the same regions of interest (ROIs), if we choose,
for instance, a finer-grained time resolution or a more microscopic measurement apparatus: Synergy at
the microscopic circuit level is maximal at synaptic time scales [36, 87] but in macroscopically resolved
fMRI recordings at much longer timescales [47]. Similarly, we find different correlations between synergy
and distance across different spatial resolutions. Using sufficiently microscopic measurements from U-
probes in macacques, stronger synergies have been found within cortical columns within V1 than between
them [88], reflecting a decrease of synergy with distance. In contrast, the exact opposite relationship has
been detected with a very different technique [3], using much more coarse-grained fMRI recordings on
resting-state data. This serves as a reminder that inferences about synergy and redundancy emerge from
within the measurement setup we choose, leaving plenty of work for experimentalists and theorists to
design cross-scale studies and renormalization tools (e.g., coarse-graining-consistent decompositions).
The second main obstacle to stronger, valid statements about the mechanistic roles of synergy and
redundancy is the disentangling of their causal contributions from spurious detection during measure-
ments, a schism we hope to have sufficiently highlighted. Intervention seems indispensable here in order
to dissociate mechanism from measurement bias [89]. Further statistical biases induced by undersampling
[38] or the lack of null-models [82] are just starting to be accounted for in this context.
A fruitful direction towards a mechanistic, physically grounded interpretation of information atoms in
brain data is the explicit grounding of neuroscientific research in thermodynamic principles [90]. Although
crucial impetuses have come from researchers in statistical physics (e.g. [11, 68, 91–94], concepts and
tools out of this niche are only starting to enter mainstream computational neuroscience [95–100]. There
are promising avenues to connect the study of how the brain minimises variational free energy [101–103],
a concept that bridges information theory and thermodynamics, focusing on the cross-scale informational
architecture that supports the brain’s adaptive functioning [104]. Crucial mathematical links for this
unification have been developed [24] but still need to be exploited empirically.

6 Conclusion
Synergy and redundancy represent fundamental concepts in extending our understanding of cogni-
tion beyond bivariate interactions. In this review, we tried to provide intuitions and guidance on how
to connect the mathematical tools to valid inferences about their complementary roles in cognitive
functioning. From purely a priori mathematical considerations, it is evident that non-trivial interdepen-
dencies between many elements in a system require the amalgamation of synergy and redundancy. Pure
redundancy collapses a system into rigid, low-dimensional synchrony, while pure synergy yields high-
dimensional sensitivity that becomes functionally brittle. When translated from simple Boolean systems
onto neural circuits, we showed that redundancy-synergy balance mirrors a computation-communication
tradeoff, so that the computational power of synergistic integration and the robustness of redundant
broadcasting can be simultaneously leveraged across scales. The next challenge is to explain through
causal interventions how this balance is realized across neural scales and timescales, and when it is adap-
tively shifted by context. This promises a more rigorous mapping of statistical measures onto physical
mechanisms supporting cognitive functioning.
