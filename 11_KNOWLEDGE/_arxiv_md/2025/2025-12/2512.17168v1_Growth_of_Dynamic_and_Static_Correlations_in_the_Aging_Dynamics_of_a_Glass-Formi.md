Growth of Dynamic and Static Correlations in the Aging Dynamics of a
Glass-Forming Liquid
Santu Nath∗and Smarajit Karmakar†
Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, 36/P, Gopanpally Village,
Serilingampally Mandal, Ranga Reddy District, Hyderabad 500046, Telangana, India
(Dated: December 22, 2025)
Using extensive molecular dynamics simulations, we have performed ﬁnite-size scaling (FSS) in
the aging regime of a model glass-forming liquid to investigate how the length scales associated with
amorphous order (static length) and dynamic heterogeneity (dynamic length) evolve with waiting
time. The α-relaxation time in the aging regime reveals non-monotonic ﬁnite-size eﬀects with a peak
at an intermediate system size, which, as far as we know, are not found in the equilibrium systems,
and the peak position shifts to larger system sizes with decreasing temperature and increasing
waiting time, indicating a growth of a characteristic length scale with waiting time. The extracted
correlation volume associated with amorphous order increases logarithmically with the waiting time.
Detailed analysis of the dependence of the length scale on waiting time allowed us to estimate the
static length scale in the deep supercooled liquid regime. The dynamic length scale, obtained from
FSS and block analysis of the four-point dynamic susceptibility, follows a power-law growth with
waiting time. The values of the length scales obtained agree well with those obtained from diﬀerent
spatial correlation functions.
Introduction:
Quenching a supercooled liquid
well below the glass transition temperature from a
high-temperature equilibrated state makes it out-of-
equilibrium over a long time window. Unlike the situ-
ation in thermal equilibrium, measurements in the out-
of-equilibrium states strongly depend on how much time
has evolved; this phenomenon is known as Aging [1–9].
In an aging state, the physical properties of a glass are
inﬂuenced by its waiting time, tw, which allows us to
tune its mechanical and optical properties by suitably
tuning the preparation history, as demonstrated in a re-
markable experiment on organic glasses prepared by two
diﬀerent ways [10]. The waiting time dependence of the
out-of-equilibrium dynamics has been extensively studied
experimentally [11–13] and numerically [14–18] in var-
ious spin-glass models.
Aging in structural glasses [2]
was ﬁrst time numerically investigated in the well-known
Kob-Andersen model [19]. Traditionally aging in ther-
mal glasses has been studied via temperature quenches;
however, recent studies have shown that aging in active
glasses [20, 21] can be induced by both temperature and
activity quenches [22, 23].
Unlike the equilibrium dynamics, time correlation
functions in aging depend on both the time diﬀerence
t and the waiting time tw. It exhibits a scaling behavior
with the ratio t/tµ
w (µ ≤1) in the long time limit, and the
corresponding relaxation time grows as tµ
w. Similar scal-
ing analysis of the spatial correlation function indicates
slow domain growth with increasing waiting time in spin
glasses [16, 18]. This growing length scale with waiting
time in an out-of-equilibrium dynamics of glass-forming
liquids [24] has received less attention. In Ref. [5], the
growth of the dynamic length scale as a function of wait-
ing time is discussed.
The length scale was extracted
from a two-point spatial correlation function studied us-
ing Monte Carlo simulations. A Mode Coupling Theory
(MCT) approach in the aging regime [9] shows that the
nonstationary version of a three-point correlation func-
tion [25] can capture the volume of cooperatively rear-
ranging regions (CRR), which grows with waiting time.
In equilibrium dynamics, the existence of CRR is widely
regarded as the primary cause of spatial correlations in
inhomogeneous local dynamics, commonly referred to as
dynamic heterogeneity (DH) [26]. The corresponding dy-
namic length scale ξd in equilibrium has been calculated
from the ﬁnite-size scaling [27] and the block analysis [28]
of the four-point dynamic susceptibility χ4, spatial cor-
relations of particle displacement guu [29, 30], etc.
The rapid increase in relaxation time upon cooling a
supercooled liquid is believed to be due to the growth of
an amorphous order [31–33], associated with a concomi-
tant growth of a static length scale ξs, which has not yet
been calculated in the aging regimes of a structural glass.
However, Ref. [5] mentioned that the waiting time depen-
dence of a similar length scale can also be calculated from
the radial distribution function. Methods like point-to-
set correlations [34], FSS of the minimum eigenvalue of
Hessian [35], and FSS of α-relaxation time [27, 36] are
typically used to calculate ξs in equilibrium dynamics.
Finite-size scaling (FSS) [37], involving the computa-
tion of various dynamical and static properties at diﬀer-
ent system sizes, has been well established [27, 36, 38].
In equilibrium dynamics, within a computationally ac-
cessible timescale, τα, the α-relaxation time (see Ap-
pendix A), grows inversely with system size [27, 36]
in three dimensions.
In Ref. [38], “Kac-Fredrickson-
Andersen” (KFA) lattice-gas model [39] was introduced,
which showed that in the long-range interaction limit of
the model, τα can have a non-monotonic growth with
system size.
Despite that, simulations of model glass-

only a weak non-monotonicity [38, 40], particularly below
the MCT temperature [41]. With current computational
power, equilibrium molecular dynamics for various sys-

same time, ﬁnite-size studies of aging dynamics are fea-

to deep glassy regimes across diﬀerent waiting times and
system sizes [3].
In this letter, we present a detailed ﬁnite-size-scaling
analysis of a model glass-forming liquid in an out-of-
equilibrium aging state. We performed molecular dynam-
ics simulations with measurements in diﬀerent waiting
times and across various system sizes. The static length
scale associated with amorphous order is obtained from
the FSS analysis of the dependence of τα on system size.
We show that the correlation volume grows logarithmi-
cally with waiting time in the aging regime, and the esti-
mated size of this volume provides a reliable estimation
of the correlation volume in equilibrium, which is consis-
tent with the Random First Order Transition (RFOT)
theory [44–46] prediction.
The length scale associated
with dynamic heterogeneity, obtained from the FSS and
block analysis of χ4, is found to show a power-law depen-
dence on waiting time.
Simulation Details: We have studied an 80 : 20
binary mixture (A and B-type) of the Kob-Andersen
model [19], in three dimensions (3d). We simulate the
system consisting of N = 200–100 000 particles, while
keeping the number density ﬁxed at ρ = N/L3 = 1.20
(L is the box length) with an integration time-step of
5 × 10−3.
Constant volume and temperature (NVT)
molecular dynamics simulation has been performed us-
ing the Nos´e–Hoover thermostat [47] by utilizing our in-
house code and LAMMPS [48].
An out-of-equilibrium
scenario has been generated by initially equilibrating at
temperature Ti = 5.00 and then rapidly quenching to
the ﬁnal temperatures T = 0.395, 0.385, and 0.370, be-
low the glass-transition temperature (Tg ∼0.40 for this
model). All the measurements have been performed, af-
ter evolving at T, for a waiting time tw ∈{101, 5 × 105}.
Depending on the system size, 16 to 768 independent re-
alizations were used to improve statistical accuracy. All
relevant quantities reported here are computed for A-
type particles unless otherwise mentioned. Further de-
tails of simulations can be found in the Supplementary
Material (SM) [42].

The observed non-monotonic growth of τα(N, tw) with
system size N at the waiting time tw is related to the
growth of the static length scale ξs(tw) with waiting time.
In Ref. [38], it was argued that an upper bound of relax-
ation time exists at equilibrium for a given system size
N, as τ e
α(N, T) ∼exp (kN/T), when the system size is
smaller than the characteristic static length scale. Here
k is a constant and T is the temperature. The argument
is shown to be exact for a system with discrete degrees
of freedom and evolving via stochastic dynamics [49]. If
we extend this argument for the aging system as a func-
tion of waiting time, then we would expect the relaxation
time to grow with increasing N, and the growth will be
stronger as tw increases due to the growth of the un-
derlying length scale with tw. In Fig. 1(a–c), we see that
clearly for various aging temperatures. A detailed numer-
ical analysis of the KFA model [38], where the relative
importance of Mode Coupling dynamics and cooperative
dynamics can be controlled, suggested that the relaxation
time ﬁrst grows with system size and then decreases at
large system size with a peak at an intermediate system
size. We see, probably for the ﬁrst time, a clear indica-
tion of this behavior in our aging study of structural glass.
A similar argument in Ref. [50] argued that, in equilib-
rium dynamics with cooperative relaxation process, one
expects to have a system size dependence of τ e
α(N, T)
that goes as 1/N for a large system size limit. Thus, the
observed ﬁnite-size behavior of the relaxation time with
waiting time in an aging glass-forming liquid indicates
a very clear crossover from Mode Coupling dynamics to
activated dynamics.

Conclusions: In this letter, we performed ﬁnite-size
scaling analysis in the aging regime of a glass-forming liq-
uid, demonstrating that a logarithmic growth with aging
time best describes the time evolution of the static length
scale, while the dynamic length scale follows a power-law
growth. We show how aging results can be used to es-
timate the growth of the static length scale in equilib-
rium, and this estimate agrees well with the predictions
of the RFOT theory. The correlation lengths computed
from spatial correlation functions are consistent with the
ﬁnite-size scaling analysis, suggesting that aging dynam-
ics are governed by growing static and dynamic length
scales. As aging measurements in microgels and synthetic
clays are comparatively easy to perform experimentally,
we hope that this work will motivate experimental studies
to measure length scales from the waiting-time descrip-
tion of the FSS and spatial correlation functions.
