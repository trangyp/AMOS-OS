Abstract 
Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for generating domain-
specific multiple-choice questions (MCQs), offering efficiency gains for certification boards 
but raising new concerns about examination security. This study investigated whether 
LLM-generated items created with proprietary guidance differ meaningfully from those 
generated using only publicly available resources. Four representative clinical activities 
from the American Board of Family Medicine (ABFM) blueprint were mapped to 
corresponding Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs), and three LLMs (GPT-4o, 
Claude 4 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Flash) produced items under a naïve strategy using only 
public EPA descriptors, while GPT-4o additionally produced items under a guided strategy 
that incorporated proprietary blueprints, item-writing guidelines, and exemplar items, 
yielding 160 total items. Question stems and options were encoded using PubMedBERT 
and BioBERT, and intra- and inter-strategy cosine similarity coefficients were calculated. 
Results showed high internal consistency within each prompting strategy (BioBERT: naïve 
0.77, guided 0.71; PubMedBERT: naïve 0.87, guided 0.83), while cross-strategy similarity 
was lower overall (BioBERT: 0.56; PubMedBERT: 0.70). However, several domain–model 
pairs, particularly in narrowly defined areas such as viral pneumonia and hypertension, 
exceeded the 0.65 “high-similarity” threshold, indicating convergence between naïve and 
guided pipelines. These findings suggest that while proprietary resources impart 
distinctiveness, LLMs prompted only with public information can still generate items 
closely resembling guided outputs in constrained clinical domains, thereby heightening 
risks of item exposure. Safeguarding the integrity of high-stakes examinations will require 
human-first, AI-assisted item development, strict separation of formative and summative 
item pools, and systematic similarity surveillance to balance innovation with security.

Introduction 
Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly advanced into highly capable tools for producing 
domain specific (such as medicine) multiple-choice questions (MCQs) (Artsi et al., 2024; Le et 
al., 2025; Makinde, 2025; Olney, 2025). For medical certification boards that rely on large and 
continually updated item banks, these technologies promise unprecedented efficiency gains in 
item writing. However, the same accessibility that enables innovation also introduces new 
vulnerabilities. Examinees, test-preparation companies, or other third parties can now exploit 
LLMs to generate practice questions that closely mimic operational content (García-Méndez et 
al., 2025). Unlike traditional test-security threats—such as item harvesting or sporadic, small-
scale content sharing—this emerging risk arises from algorithmic approximation at scale: 
publicly accessible models, guided only by openly available exam materials, can reproduce the 
semantic and stylistic features of proprietary items. This development poses urgent challenges 
for assessment validity and test security in the contemporary AI landscape. 
 
The central concern is whether LLMs trained or prompted only with public information—such as 
exam blueprints, content outlines, or competency frameworks—can generate questions that are 
semantically indistinguishable from those produced with access to proprietary, internal 
resources. If so, the boundary between high-stake test content and publicly generated practice 
material may erode, creating substantial risks of item overexposure that could threaten exam


## Page 4


validity. Conversely, if proprietary guidance demonstrably shifts the semantic space of generated 
items, then certification organizations can retain greater confidence that their internal processes 
impart distinctiveness, preserving test integrity. 
 
To examine this issue, the present study compares two item-generation strategies. In the naïve 
strategy, a large language model is prompted only with publicly available documents, such as 
Entrustable Professional Activity (EPA) outlines the clinical activities conducted by specific 
profession such as Family Medicine (Shaughnessy et al., 2013).  In the guided strategy, the 
model is further supplied with proprietary resources, including detailed internal blueprints, item-
writing guidelines, and exemplar items. From each strategy, we generated parallel item sets and 
then quantified their semantic similarity using cosine similarity on embeddings derived from 
PubMedBERT(Gu et al., 2022) and BioBERT(Lee et al., 2020)—two biomedical encoders 
optimized for medicine specific vocabulary. 
 
A high degree of similarity between guided and naïve item sets would indicate a critical security 
risk: even without access to internal resources, publicly available information may be sufficient 
to reproduce content that closely mirrors operational items. A lower similarity would suggest that 
proprietary resources provide a meaningful buffer, maintaining sufficient distinctiveness to 
protect item confidentiality. Although other evaluation dimensions, such as clinical accuracy, 
cognitive level, and editorial quality, remain essential for operational use, semantic overlap 
offers a direct metric of potential exposure risk.


## Page 5


It is important to recognize that guided items are typically an initial pass subject to further expert 
editing. Yet, if the raw outputs from guided generation already align closely with those from 
naïve, public prompts, such overlap may scale beyond what human review can feasibly resolve. 
This possibility underscores the need for certification organizations to proactively assess LLM-
driven threats, adjust the granularity of public-facing materials, and implement safeguards such 
as automated similarity surveillance in the item-development pipeline. 
 
In sum, this study addresses a pressing security challenge of the AI era: determining whether 
generative models collapse the boundary between proprietary and public item spaces. By 
empirically testing overlap across generation strategies, we provide evidence to guide policy, 
governance, and technical safeguards for secure adoption of AI-assisted item generation in high-
stakes medicine certification. 
 
Method 
Item-Generation Strategies 
In this study, we use the American Board of Family Medicine (ABFM) certification blueprint as 
an exemplar framework for linking assessment content to professional activities. The blueprint 
comprises five domains, four of which specify discrete clinical activities. From each of these 
four domains, one representative clinical activity was selected and mapped to its most closely 
aligned, publicly available Entrustable Professional Activity (EPA). These clinical activity–EPA 
pairs functioned as the sampling units on which two distinct item-generation strategies were 
applied. The selected clinical activities and their mapped EPAs are summarized in Table 1.


## Page 6


These clinical activity–EPA pairs served as sampling units for which two item generation 
strategies were implemented.  
 
In the naïve strategy, three large language models (LLMs)—GPT-4o accessed through Azure AI, 
Claude 4 Sonnet, and Gemini 2.5 Flash—were prompted only with EPA descriptors and a 
generic instruction to “write a single-best-answer multiple-choice question following ABFM 
certification exam style.” Default system parameters were used to approximate the behavior of 
end users with minimal technical expertise. Specifically, the temperature was set to 1, which is 
the presumed default, and the maximum token length was set to 2000, allowing sufficient 
latitude for generation while constraining item length. For Gemini, special prompts were 
developed to disable built-in guardrails; details of these prompts and representative outputs are 
provided in the Appendix. 
 
In the guided strategy, GPT-4o under Azure AI was additionally provided with ABFM’s 
proprietary item-writing guidelines, exemplary operational items, and full three-level blueprint 
metadata (domain → body system → clinical activity). Guided generation was limited to GPT-
4o to preserve the security of internal ABFM documentation, which was transmitted only via the 
secure Azure environment.  
 
Each pipeline generated 10 unique questions for each of the four selected clinical activities. This 
yielded a total of 40 guided items (GPT-4o only) and 120 naïve items (40 each from GPT-4o, 
Claude, and Gemini).

Text Extraction and Sentence Encoding 
All generated items were exported as Microsoft Word documents, and individual questions were 
parsed by isolating paragraphs beginning with the prefix “Q.” Question stems and response 
options were concatenated and transformed into embeddings using two biomedical encoders. The 
first was the pritamdeka/BioBERT-mnli-snli-scinli-scitail-mednli-stsb model, a variant of 
BioBERT fine-tuned on multiple biomedical natural-language inference and semantic-text-
similarity datasets (Lee et al., 2020). The second was the NeuML/pubmedbert-base-embeddings 
model, also referred to as MSR BiomedBERT, which is a fine-tuned version of PubMedBERT 
trained exclusively on PubMed abstracts and vocabulary (Gu et al., 2022). Both models generate 
768-dimensional vector representations that capture clinical terminology and nuanced semantic


## Page 8


relationships. Benchmarking on the Biomedical Language Understanding and Reasoning 
Benchmark (BLURB) has shown strong performance for both encoders, with PubMedBERT 
often yielding slightly superior results, particularly for biomedical entity classification 
(Stanković et al., 2022). 
Similarity Computation 
Pairwise cosine similarity coefficients were calculated for all items. Two summary metrics were 
derived: intra-strategy similarity, defined as the average of all pairwise coefficients within the 
naïve corpus and separately within the guided corpus, and inter-strategy similarity, defined as the 
mean coefficient between each naïve item and each guided item. Higher similarity coefficients 
denote greater lexical-semantic overlap and, by extension, a higher risk of inadvertent 
duplication across item sets. All similarity values were exported for subsequent statistical 
comparisons of within- versus across-strategy overlap. Interpretive thresholds for cosine 
similarity values were based on prior work (Becker & Kao, 2022; Meng & Li, 2024) and are 
summarized in Table 2. This table provides a heuristic framework to aid interpretation of the 
results, acknowledging that the mappings are not definitive.

When using BioBERT embeddings, inter-strategy similarity averaged 0.58 for GPT and Claude 
and 0.53 for Gemini. Three EPA–LLM combinations—Viral Pneumonia and Hypertension for 
GPT, and Hypertension for Claude—exceeded the 0.65 threshold for “high similarity” (Table 1), 
suggesting potential convergence between naïve and guided items. The remaining combinations 
showed lower cross-strategy similarity, indicating that the two prompting methods generally 
produced more distinct item sets. As expected, intra-strategy similarity was higher, averaging 
0.77 for the naïve corpus and 0.71 for the guided corpus, reflecting thematic cohesion within 
each generation stream. 
 
PubMedBERT embeddings produced consistently higher similarity values than BioBERT. 
Across the 12 EPA–LLM combinations, seven exceeded the 0.65 high-similarity threshold. The 
average cross-strategy similarity was 0.70, compared with 0.56 for BioBERT. This difference 
likely reflects PubMedBERT’s training exclusively on PubMed abstracts, which enables greater 
sensitivity to clinical language, whereas BioBERT was trained on a broader mixture of 
biomedical and general-domain texts (Gu et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2020). Intra-strategy similarity 
again exceeded inter-strategy similarity, averaging 0.87 for the naïve corpus and 0.83 for the 
guided corpus. 
 
Taken together, these findings indicate that items generated within the same prompting strategy 
exhibit stronger lexical and semantic similarity than those generated across strategies. However, 
specific blueprint categories characterized by narrow and highly standardized clinical 
presentations, such as viral pneumonia and hypertension, showed notable convergence between

Discussion 
This study examined whether prompting large language models (LLMs) with different strategies 
(either publicly available or proprietary information) leads to the generation of items that overlap 
with one another. Results showed that items displayed relatively high internal consistency within 
each prompting strategy. Using BioBERT embeddings, intra-strategy similarity averaged 0.77 
for the naïve corpus and 0.71 for the guided corpus; with PubMedBERT embeddings, these 
values increased to 0.87 and 0.83, respectively. This pattern is consistent with prior research


## Page 14


demonstrating PubMedBERT’s stronger performance on biomedical tasks compared to 
BioBERT (Gu et al., 2021). 
Cross-strategy similarity was lower, averaging 0.56 with BioBERT and 0.70 with 
PubMedBERT. While most item sets demonstrated moderate overlap, two of the 12 BioBERT 
comparisons and eight of the 12 PubMedBERT comparisons exceeded the 0.65 threshold 
commonly associated with near-duplicate items. These findings suggest that proprietary 
guidance does introduce additional nuance, but overlap remains a concern, particularly in 
narrowly defined clinical domains. The choice of embedding model also influenced 
interpretation: PubMedBERT appeared more sensitive to medical text than BioBERT, 
underscoring the importance of embedding selection when evaluating highly specialized content. 
The implications for examination security are significant. If candidates can use publicly available 
materials, such as EPAs or curriculum guidelines, to generate items that closely mirror 
operational test content, the confidentiality and validity of certification exams may be at risk. 
This risk is heightened in domains with limited clinical variability, such as viral pneumonia or 
hypertension, where the item pool is naturally constrained and LLM-generated outputs converge 
more easily. In these contexts, naïve and guided generation pipelines may both produce highly 
similar items, increasing the likelihood of overlap with operational content. 
At the same time, these findings suggest strategies for mitigating security risks while harnessing 
the benefits of generative AI. Boards may consider adopting “human-first, AI-assisted” 
workflows in which subject-matter experts author the clinical scenario and rely on LLMs only 
for refinement tasks, such as improving clarity or balancing distractors. This approach preserves 
conceptual originality while leveraging the efficiency of AI. A complementary governance 
model could maintain strict separation between formative and summative item pools, allowing


## Page 15


AI-driven expansion of educational resources without jeopardizing the integrity of high-stakes 
inventories. 
In terms of item quality, expert review confirmed that many AI-generated items required only 
modest revisions. Across 16 items randomly selected for review (four from each domain and two 
prompting strategies under the GPT-4o model), two family medicine physicians judged most as 
requiring minimal or substantial edits, with only a few classified as complete rewrites. Although 
reviewer judgments differed on the degree of editing required, both reviewers agreed that AI 
outputs can provide a viable starting point for item development. 
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was restricted to four domain–EPA pairs 
and two prompting strategies, which may limit generalizability to other content domains, model 
parameters, or iterative prompting techniques. Similarity estimates were also model-dependent, 
and thresholds derived from BioBERT and PubMedBERT may not translate directly to other 
embedding frameworks. Finally, while overlap at the item level is concerning, the practical 
challenge of generating a sufficiently large and targeted practice bank to replicate operational 
content should not be underestimated. Producing thousands of high-quality items may resemble 
intensive studying rather than intentional item harvesting, though both could narrow the margin 
of security. 
In summary, generative AI offers a powerful tool for rapidly producing clinically authentic 
items, but also introduces new vulnerabilities for test security. The similarity metrics employed 
here provide a practical framework for detecting overlap and preserving item uniqueness. 
Balancing innovation with protection of operational content will require boards to refine their 
workflows, combining expert oversight with careful deployment of AI, and maintaining a clear 
separation between formative and summative uses.

def extract_text_from_pdf(pdf_path): 
    """Extracts text from a PDF file.""" 
    text = "" 
    with open(pdf_path, "rb") as file: 
        reader = PyPDF2.PdfReader(file) 
        for page in reader.pages: 
            page_text = page.extract_text() 
            if page_text: 
                text += page_text + "\n" 
    return text
