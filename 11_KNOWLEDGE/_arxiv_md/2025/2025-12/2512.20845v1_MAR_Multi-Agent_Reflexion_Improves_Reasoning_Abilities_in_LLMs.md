MAR: Multi-Agent Reflexion Improves Reasoning Abilities in LLMs

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown strong capabil-
ities in reasoning and program synthesis, yet enabling them
to improve through experience remains a central challenge.
Reinforcement learning offers one opportunity for agent im-
provement, but updating large models is computationally ex-
pensive and requires significant environment interaction. The
Reflexion framework introduced a lightweight alternative in
which an LLM agent generates natural language reflections
about its failures and stores them as episodic memory that
guides future attempts. Reflexion demonstrated meaningful
gains on multi hop question answering and code generation
without modifying model parameters. However, our replica-
tion shows that the single agent design of Reflexion leads to
systematic shortcomings. The same model generates actions,
evaluates its own behavior, and produces reflections, which
often results in repeated reasoning errors, confirmation bias,
and limited corrective feedback.
To address these limitations, we reproduce Reflexion on Hot-
PotQA and HumanEval using GPT 3.5 and GPT 4, and we
analyze failure modes through detailed logging of trajecto-
ries and reflections. We consistently observe that self reflec-
tions tend to repeat earlier misconceptions and do not intro-
duce new reasoning paths on difficult examples. Motivated by
these findings, we introduce Multi Agent Reflexion (MAR), a
structured multi agent extension that incorporates diverse rea-
soning personas and a judge model that synthesizes their cri-
tiques into a unified reflection. MAR separates the processes
of acting, diagnosing, critiquing, and aggregating, which re-
duces shared blind spots and prevents the repeated reinforce-
ment of earlier mistakes. On both benchmarks, MAR im-
proves directly over Reflexion. MAR increases HotPotQA
exact-match accuracy by 3 points, from 44 to 47, and im-
proves HumanEval pass@1 from 76.4 to 82.6, a 6.2-point
gain. MAR also reduces the stagnation observed in single
agent Reflexion, indicating that multi agent interaction offers
a simple and effective path toward more reliable LLM agents.
Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) has evolved to generate
strong reasoning traces and high-quality code, but enabling
them to improve through experience remains an open prob-
lem. Traditional reinforcement learning offers one mecha-
nism for agent improvement, but updating billion-parameter
policies is computationally costly and data-intensive. In-
context learning (Brown et al. 2020), where an LLM adapts
using examples directly in the prompt, offers one path
toward improving model performance without additional
training. Building on this idea, prompting strategies such as
chain-of-thought (CoT), expert-style prompting, and ReAct
have been shown to enhance LLM reasoning and decision-
making.
Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023) integrates these advances
while adding a mechanism for the model to self-reflect on
its own mistakes: after each attempt, the agent converts feed-
back into natural-language guidance for the next trial, anal-
ogous to how reinforcement learning algorithms use reward
signals to update a policy (Sutton and Barto 2018).
Reflexion reports substantial gains over baseline LLM
and CoT/ReAct agents on HotPotQA (Yang et al. 2018)
and HumanEval (Chen et al. 2021), improving exact-match
accuracy by roughly 20 points on HotPotQA and pass@1
accuracy by 11 points on HumanEval. We focus on these
benchmarks for replication since multi-hop question (ques-
tions where a model must combine information across sev-
eral steps of retrieveal) answering and program synthe-
sis are the two high-impact domains where iterative self-
improvement most meaningfully enhance LLM agent per-
formance, and covers the majority of use cases.
Despite the gains of the Reflexion framework, in our
replication, we consistently observed that Reflexion is vul-
nerable to degeneration-of-thought (Liang et al. 2023),
where the agent repeats the same flawed reasoning across
iterations even when explicit failures are identified. To mit-
igate this limitation, we introduce Multi-Agent Reflexion
(MAR), a framework that replaces single-agent self-critique
with a structured debate among diverse persona-based crit-
ics. These multiple critics generate richer reflections that
more effectively guide the agent toward improved strategies.
Our full codebase, including replication scripts, log-
ging instrumentation, and the Multi-Agent Reflexion imple-
mentation, is available at: https://github.com/danpro1011/
reflexion.

Background
Reflexion Framework
Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023) wraps a language model in
a simple loop that turns past failures into natural-language
guidance for future attempts. Conceptually, the framework
decomposes the agent into three prompted roles plus an
episodic memory.
Actor (Ma). The Actor is the LLM prompted to behave as
the agent’s policy. It generates actions, intermediate reason-
ing, and final answers. Reflexion is compatible with multiple
prompting styles; the original paper focuses on:
• Chain-of-Thought (CoT) (Wei et al. 2022) prompting,
which encourages structured step-by-step reasoning.
• ReAct (Yao et al. 2022), which interleaves reasoning
steps with explicit actions taken in an external environ-
ment.
Evaluator (Me). The Evaluator judges whether an Actor
trajectory successfully solves the task. Depending on the do-
main, Me may be another LLM prompt, a heuristic rule, or
an external execution environment such as code unit tests.
Importantly, Reflexion only requires a coarse signal, such as
success/failure, not a dense numeric reward.
Figure 1: The Reflexion Architecture. Actor performs an
action on the environment. The evaluator gives feedback to
reflector LLM, with feedback stored in short-term memory.
A new action is then performed starting a new iteration.
Self-Reflector (Msr). When the Evaluator indicates fail-
ure, the Self-Reflection model converts this outcome into
verbal feedback. Given the failed trajectory and its evalu-
ation, Msr produces natural-language reflections that diag-
nose what went wrong, explain why the reasoning failed,
and suggest strategies to try the next attempt. These “verbal
rewards” play the role of a lightweight optimization signal.
At a high level, each failed attempt produces a new reflection
that is added to memory, and the Actor’s next attempt is con-
ditioned on this accumulated verbal history. Over multiple
trials, the agent can therefore improve its decisions without
any parameter updates.
Datasets
We evaluate our approach on two benchmarks that stress
different aspects of LLM reasoning. HotPotQA evaluates
multi-hop question answering over Wikipedia, requiring
models to combine evidence across documents. HumanEval
tests program synthesis by checking whether generated
functions pass hidden unit tests. Together, these datasets pro-
vide complementary settings for analyzing how reflection
influences both symbolic reasoning and code generation.
• HotPotQA (Yang et al. 2018) is a large-scale question
answering benchmark constructed from Wikipedia and
designed to test multi-hop reasoning (questions system
has to reason with information taken from more than
one document to arrive at the answer. Each example pro-
vides a question, a set of candidate paragraphs, support-
ing facts, and a short answer. Successful solutions typ-
ically require integrating evidence from more than one
source, which makes HotPotQA a natural environment
for studying how Reflexion’s episodic memory and re-
flections influence multi-step reasoning. Following the
evaluation protocol in Shinn et al. (2023), we report Ex-
act Match (EM) on the development set after applying
standard normalization procedures such as lowercasing
and punctuation removal.
• HumanEval (Chen et al. 2021) is a benchmark for
program synthesis in which each task specifies a func-
tion signature, a natural-language description of the in-
tended behavior, and a series of hidden unit tests. A
model succeeds if its generated function passes all tests

Performance Metrics
We evaluate both the original Reflexion framework and our
multi-agent extension using task-appropriate accuracy met-
rics. For HotPotQA, we report Exact Match (EM), and for
HumanEval, we use pass@1 based on whether the generated
solution passes all hidden unit tests.
• Exact Match (EM) for HotPotQA. EM measures
whether a predicted answer exactly matches the ground
truth HotPotQA answer matches after applying Hot-
PotQA’s standard normalization steps, including lower-
casing and removal of punctuation and articles.
• pass@1 for HumanEval. A model receives credit for a
HumanEval problem if its single sampled solution passes
all hidden unit tests when executed in the official sand-
boxed environment.
Methodology, Results, and Discussion
Methodology
We evaluate Reflexion and our extensions on two bench-
marks: multi-hop question answering with HotPotQA and
program synthesis with HumanEval. Across both settings,
we follow the original Reflexion protocol while adding de-
tailed logging to analyze how reasoning, evaluation, and re-
flection evolve across attempts.
HotPotQA Setup.
We evaluate Reflexion on the multi-
hop HotPotQA benchmark by reproducing the three agent
configurations examined in the original study: Baseline
GPT-3.5-Turbo using the ReAct framework, and GPT-3.5-
turbo using the ReAct-Reflect framework. These frame-
works are run on a curated selection of 100 difficult Hot-
PotQa questions, matching the dataset used in their paper.
We run these experiments over the course of 5 trials where
after each trial, the ReAct agent will try again after reflecting
over previous failures.
HumanEval Setup.
For the HumanEval benchmark, we
replicate the Reflexion pipeline using the official sandboxed
execution environment. Each generated solution is executed
against the hidden unit tests, and performance is reported us-
ing the standard pass@1 metric. Following the original Re-
flexion protocol, each problem receives one initial attempt
followed by a single reflection-guided retry.

Discussion
In this section, we present the results of these experiments
and compare our replicated performance with the findings
reported in the Reflexion paper.
HotPotQA Discussion
Our replication of HotPotQA
shows similar results to the improvements reported in the
original Reflexion paper but the gains plateau around 45-
50% even with additional reflective attempts. We note that
our replication runs were ran using GPT-3.5 for cost rea-
sons, whereas the original reflexion paper is vague in their
choice of model, and this difference could be a source of
discrepancy between our results. We found the EM metric
often penalizes semantically correct answers, labeling them
as failures due to formatting or synonym mismatches. This
creates a structural limitation: even with better reasoning,
the agent cannot improve beyond what EM will credit. We
hypothesize that this explains the limited improvement of
MAR over the Reflexion baseline.
HumanEval Discussion
Table 2 shows that our replica-
tion confirms the main takeaway of the original Reflexion
paper: reflection-guided retries meaningfully improve code
correctness. Although our gains were slightly smaller, the
trend clearly holds.

Inspection of logged trajectories revealed two recurring
failure modes that likely contributed to the reduced improve-

ion loop repeats its initial flawed reasoning because the same
model acts as Actor, Evaluator, and Reflector; and (2) mode

These observations motivated our Multi-Agent Reflex-
ion extension, which explicitly separates acting, evaluation,
and critique across multiple personas to inject diversity and
reduce these failure modes.
Extensions
In this section, we introduce Multi-Agent Reflexion
(MAR), our extension to the Reflexion framework that re-
places single-agent self-critique with a coordinated team
of persona driven critics meant to address the core failure
modes we observed in replication.
Motivation
LLMs frequently struggle to revise their own reasoning,
and even with external feedback they tend to reinforce the
same flawed chain of thought across successive attempts.
This phenomenon is described as degeneration-of-thought
(Liang et al. 2023) or the mental set problem (Liu et al.
2025). A central cause of this issue is that self-reflective ap-
proaches depend on fixed internal thinking patterns. Even
when agents are prompted with different personas, their un-
derlying reasoning strategies often remain uniform. This
uniformity prevents the model from exploring alternative
perspectives and makes it difficult to escape entrenched er-
rors. These limitations are reflected in the behavior of meth-
ods such as Reflexion (Shinn et al. 2023) and related ap-
proaches (Kim, Baldi, and McAleer 2023; Madaan et al.
2023), which frequently plateau on complex reasoning tasks
and can sometimes reinforce earlier mistakes instead of cor-
recting them (Huang et al. 2024; Stechly, Marquez, and
Kambhampati 2023; Valmeekam, Marquez, and Kambham-
pati 2023).
In our replication of Reflexion, these limitations mani-
fested consistently. The single-agent loop exhibited two re-
curring failure modes:
1. Confirmation Bias in Self-Reflection. The Actor fre-
quently committed an initial reasoning error. The Evalu-
ator, drawn from the same model family, then attempted
to critique the trajectory. However, instead of isolating
the root cause, the reflection often:
• Restated the incorrect reasoning.
• Justified the error through additional flawed logic.
• Produced superficial edits.
2. Mode Collapse Across Iterations. Because the same
model generated, evaluated, and revised the reasoning,
the corrections tended to follow a narrow and repetitive
template. In HumanEval, we observed multiple instances
in which the system reproduced:
• Identical buggy loop structures.
• Repeated indexing mistakes.
• The same off-by-one error.
With a single model acting as its own generator, evalua-
tor, and critic tends to reproduce the same reasoning struc-
ture across iterations with little correction. As such, our pro-
posed Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR) framework explicitly
address these weakness by incorporating deliberately varied
reasoning strategies into the reflection loop, with a judge that
takes away as much meaningful conclusion as possible from
each debate.
Extension: Multi-Agent Debate as Reflection
Figure 2: The Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR) Architec-
ture. This high-level diagram illustrates the extension of the
single-agent Reflexion framework.
Our extension replaces the single self-reflecting model in
Reflexion with a group of LLM agents that each serve as
distinct critics. When the Actor produces an incorrect an-
swer, the system does not rely on a single reflection. Instead,
it initiates a structured multi-agent debate in which several
persona-guided critics analyze the failed reasoning from dif-
ferent perspectives. Each critic contributes alternative hy-
potheses, highlights potential flaws, and proposes correc-
tive strategies. A debate coordinator then aggregates these
perspectives into a final consensus reflection, which is ap-
pended to the Actor’s memory for the next attempt.
The Multi-Agent Reflexion loop works as follows:
1. Actor Attempt: the Actor first attempts the question us-
ing the same baseline model as in standard Reflexion.
2. Evaluation: If incorrect, the system triggers the debate
module. Note that this uses the same evaluator as the
original Reflexion framework.
3. Initial Diagnosis: The failed thoughts (scratchpad) of
the actor are passed to the judge. The judge asks each
persona to write a diagnosis as to why the actor failed the
task.
4. Debates: After each persona has made a diagnosis, the
judge asks whether other personas agree or disagree and
refine their critiques.


## Page 5


5. Consensus and Reflection The judge synthesizes the
debate into a single, actionable ”Consensus Reflection”
which includes error outputs, debate logs, and consensus
between debaters depending on the task. If there has been
no consensus reached between the debaters, then the per-
sonas debate for another round (maximum 2 rounds).
6. Retry This reflection is injected into the Actor’s memory,
guiding the next attempt with specific advice on how to
avoid previous pitfalls.
Personas in Multi-Agent Debate
A key component of MAR is the use of intentionally di-
verse critic personas. We designed personas in a systemic
way such that reasoning tendencies differ, inspired by the
Society of Mind framework and the divergent-thinking ob-
jectives of Multi-Agent Debate (MAD) (Liang et al., 2024).
We define the following axes to vary across for persona
creation. For any given dataset, we carefully craft a set of
persona to cover a comprehensively cover these these axes.
• Evidence Exploitation: how strongly the critic demands
explicit textual support for actions.
• Exploration: whether the critic tries to actively pushes
the debate toward alternative hypotheses.
• Specification strictness: how much the critic optimizes
for exact compliance with the metric.
Within this design space, we instantiate the following
critics (see Appendix C for full prompts):
• Verifier (high exploit, low exploration, medium strict-
ness) chains of thought.
• Skeptic (low exploit, high exploration, medium strict-
ness) tuned to counter confirmation bias.
• Logician (high exploit, low exploration, high strictness)
• Creative (low exploit, high exploration, high explo-
ration)
Concrete definitions of personas are given in the ap-
pendix (see appendix item C), along with exact prompt used
and reasoning behind personas. As future work, we may ask
an LLM to generate personas on these axes for a specific
dataset.
Experimental Setup
To evaluate our Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR), our experi-
mental configuration mirrors the structure of the original Re-
flexion framework where possible, differing only in replac-
ing the single-agent reflection step with a structured multi-
agent debate. We use GPT-3.5 Turbo for all agent roles (ac-
tor, debaters, and judge) which allows us to isolate the im-
pact of persona-guided multi-agent reasoning while preserv-
ing Reflexion’s core design.
Across both HotPotQA and HumanEval, we designed
our agent personas to reflect the dominant failure modes
observed during replication. For HotPotQA, we selected
personas focused on factual grounding, logical consistency,
alternative hypothesis generation, and error checking. For
code generation, we used personas mirroring real world soft-
ware roles (e.g., Senior Engineer, QA Engineer, Code Re-
viewer) to capture algorithmic and specification considera-
tions. Personas were engineered to disagree naturally and
expose different lines of reasoning, so that debates surface
diverse critiques rather than stylistic variations.
We cap debates at two rounds, based on empirical logs
and prior MAD work showing that nearly all meaningful
disagreements arise within the first two exchanges. Trial
budgets were also kept intentionally small (five trials for
HotPotQA and three for HumanEval), to keep API costs
lower. While increasing the limit did yield marginal im-
provements, we observed sharply diminishing returns be-
yond these thresholds, accompanied by rapidly increasing
API cost and latency. This setup therefore preserves the core
design principles of Reflexion while isolating the specific
contribution of multi-agent reasoning.

Takeaways
Reduced Degeneration of Thought.
Single-agent Reflex-
ion often suffers from “degeneration of thought,” where the
model repeats the same flawed reasoning across retries. Be-
cause the Actor, Evaluator, and Reflector are all the same
model, its reflections tend to reinforce the original mis-
conception rather than correct it. MAR mitigates this by
introducing multiple agents with distinct reasoning styles.
Their disagreements surface alternative interpretations of the
specification and error trace, helping the system escape the
mental-set failures that limit Reflexion.
Higher-quality revisions through aggregation.
Each
MAR trial ends with a judge model that selects the most
coherent and spec-aligned proposal from the debate. In prac-
tice, agents catch different issues. One may focus on spec-
ification accuracy, another on edge cases, another on logic
or style. The judge synthesizes these perspectives, produc-
ing patches that are more reliable than the single-agent self-
reflection loop.
Limitations
While MAR consistently outperforms single-
agent Reflexion, this improvement comes with a significant
computational cost. Each failed attempt triggers multiple
critic responses, a structured debate, and a judge synthe-
sis step, expanding both token usage and context length. In
practice, this leads to roughly a 3x increase in API calls and
latency compared to Reflexion. As a result, MAR is sub-
stantially more expensive to run, and its scalability is con-
strained when deployed on large benchmarks or real-world
agentic systems. Reducing debate depth or dynamically se-
lecting which personas to invoke may help mitigate these
costs in future work.
Overall, the goal of Multi-Agent Reflexion is to inject
structured disagreement and diverse reasoning into the re-
flection step, reducing confirmation bias and improving the
agent’s ability to escape repeated failures.
Conclusions
In this work, we introduced Multi-Agent Reflexion (MAR),
a multi-agent extension of the Reflexion framework de-
signed to address key failure modes in single-agent self-
improvement. Most notably cognitive entrenchment and the
degeneration-of-thought problem. Across both reasoning
(HotPotQA) and program synthesis (HumanEval-Python),
MAR consistently outperforms the GPT-3.5 baseline and
single-agent Reflexion, demonstrating that diversified cri-
tique and structured disagreement substantially enhance er-
ror diagnosis and correction.
Looking forward, several directions appear promising:
experimenting with MAD paradigms that reduce context
lengths, as demonstrated by (Chen et al. 2025) and (Smit
et al. 2024). New discoveries that have been shown to im-
prove LLM output diversity, such as (Zhang et al. 2025) also
show promise when integrated with our framework. Overall,
our findings suggest that multi-agent reflective systems of-
fer a scalable, training-free path toward more reliable and
self-correcting LLM agents.
Societal Impact
Our work highlights both the potential benefits and risks
of developing self-improving LLM agents. Multi-agent de-
bate offers a scalable way to elicit diverse reasoning and
richer critiques without gradient-based training, suggesting
a path toward more transparent and interpretable AI sys-
tems. However, our experiments also reveal notable soci-
etal concerns. Because our multi-agent Reflexion pipeline
required roughly 300–400 API calls per task, around three
times the cost of single-agent Reflexion, it substantially in-
creases computational demand, energy consumption, and
therefore environmental impact, while also raising economic
and accessibility barriers. Moreover, self-refining agents that
generate and act on their own critiques introduce safety
and governance challenges, as emergent multi-agent behav-
iors may become harder to predict or align. By demonstrat-
ing both the promise and the heavy computational footprint
of multi-agent reflexive systems, our work underscores the
need for careful evaluation of efficiency, reliability, and the
broader societal consequences of deploying increasingly au-
tonomous LLM agents.

A) Failure Case: Hallucinated Specification Drift in Reflexion
One recurring failure mode we observed in our HumanEval runs was that Reflexion sometimes hallucinated a new task spec-
ification and confidently steered the agent away from the true objective. Listing 1 shows a representative example for the
double the difference problem. The original prompt asks the model to return the sum of squares of positive odd inte-
gers in the list, ignoring negatives and non-integers. However, after a failed attempt, the reflection step incorrectly infers that
the function is supposed to “double the difference of the elements in the list” and rewrites the entire implementation accord-
ingly. This reflection not only fails to diagnose the real bug, it actively redefines the task, causing the second attempt to diverge
even further from the benchmark specification. This illustrates how low-quality self-feedback in a single-agent Reflexion loop
can entrench errors instead of correcting them, and motivates our Multi-Agent Reflexion extension, which introduces external
evaluators and diverse reasoning personas to counteract such self-reinforcing drift.
Listing 1: Failure case: hallucinated task specification for double the difference.
B) Failure Case: EM Rejects Semantically Correct Answer
One recurring limitation of the Exact Match (EM) metric is that it penalizes superficial formatting differences even when the
model’s answer is semantically correct. A representative example from our MAR HotPotQA runs is the following item:
Question.
“Woman’s Era and Naj are what kind of magazines?”
Ground-truth answer.
fortnightly women interest magazine
MAR predictions (Trials 1–5).
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
"women’s interest magazines"
All five trials are marked incorrect under EM, despite the fact that “women’s interest magazines” is:
• semantically identical to the core label “women interest magazine”,
• factually correct for both Woman’s Era and Naj,
• only missing the non-essential temporal modifier “fortnightly”,
• and differing only in surface form (pluralization and wording).
Importantly, inspection of the debate transcripts shows that the reasoning is correct: MAR consistently identifies the category
as women’s interest magazines and does not hallucinate an alternative type. The failure arises entirely from EM’s strict string-
matching requirement rather than from a reasoning error.
This illustrates the broader issue mentioned in the main text: these superficial mismatches arise even when the answer
matches human expectations. Under a more forgiving metric (e.g., F1, or a semantic-match / LLM-judge scoring scheme), this
example would be counted as correct, and the apparent gap between MAR and the original Reflexion results would likely shrink
in favor of MAR.
C) Persona Prompts: All Prompts Used for Any Persona in Paper
Reasoning Personas for HotPotQA
Verifier
You are a Verifier. Your job is to check each claim carefully for factual correctness and internal logical consistency.
For each assertion the Actor or another agent makes, ask yourself: “Is this backed by evidence or context? Could this be wrong?”
Discard any reasoning steps that lack justification, ambiguous references, or unsupported assumptions. If you find an error or
gap, call it out explicitly and explain why it might be wrong.
Planner
You are a Planner. You care about the high-level structure of the reasoning. Before diving into low-level details,
outline a strategy: what steps to take, what subproblems to solve, and in what order. If earlier attempts failed, propose a
different overall plan (alternative breakdown). Your reflections should focus on planning, not just individual mistakes.
Skeptic
You are a Skeptic. Assume earlier reasoning may have hallucinations or leaps. Critique every assumption, spec, and
inference. Ask: “How do I know this is true?”, “What if the premise is wrong?”, “Is there another possible interpretation?” Your
goal is to prevent overconfidence and surface plausible failure modes.


## Page 10


Logician
You are a Strict Logician. Evaluate whether the answer exactly matches the specification or asked question. Do not
accept vague matches, implied meanings, or partially correct statements. If the requirement asks for a full definition, full proof,
or exact formatting, check strictly for compliance.
Creative
You are a Creative Thinker. If conventional reasoning fails or stalls, propose unforeseen angles. Look for edge
cases, trick questions, alternative interpretations, or unusual solutions. Your reflections should expand the search space rather
than refine within the existing pattern.
Meta-Reflector
You are a Meta-Reflector. After seeing multiple failed attempts, reflect not just on code or reasoning errors
but on the overall process. Ask: “Why did we keep failing?”, “Are we stuck in a loop of similar mistakes?”, “Should we change
the memory buffer, retry policy, or strategy type?” Suggest meta-changes: different prompting style, more memory, switching
reasoning mode, or abandoning this approach.
Programming Personas for HumanEval
Senior Engineer
You are a Senior Software Engineer. Your goal is to write clean, efficient, and correct code that passes all
unit tests.
QA Engineer
You are a QA Test Engineer. You focus on edge cases, input validation, and making sure the implementation
truly satisfies the specification.
Algorithm Expert
You are an Algorithm Specialist. Focus on correctness and time/space complexity of the underlying algo-
rithm.
Code Reviewer
You are a Strict Code Reviewer. You check for logical bugs, syntax errors, Pythonic style, and maintainability.
