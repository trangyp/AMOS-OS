Abstract 
As generative artificial intelligence (GAI) enters the mental health landscape, questions arise 
about how individuals weigh AI tools against human therapists. This study examined belief-
based predictors of intention to use GAI and therapists across two populations: a university 
sample (N = 1,155) and a nationally representative adult sample (N = 651). Using paired-sample 
t-tests following a MANOVA, we found that human therapists were viewed as providing greater 
emotional support and coping, relationship, and educational skills as well as being able to 
personalize treatment than GAI chatbots. In turn, GAI support was viewed as being more 
affordable and accessible. No differences between modalities were found with concerns about 
privacy, reliability, stigma, mental health literacy or help-seeking norms. Using LASSO 
regression, we examined how beliefs about each modality jointly shape help-seeking intentions. 
Across both samples, intentions to use either GAI or human therapists were most strongly 
associated with perceptions of interpersonal support, including emotional support, relational 
guidance, and personalization. Barriers differed across modalities: concerns about privacy and 
reliability were more strongly associated with reduced intention to use GAI, whereas structural 
constraints, particularly affordability, were more closely linked to human therapy use. These 
findings extend the Health Belief Model to a dual-modality context, demonstrating that help-
seeking decisions reflect a comparative push-pull process in which barriers to one modality 
redirect users toward the other. Design implications are discussed for developing trustworthy, 
emotionally resonant GAI tools that complement rather than replace human care. 
 
Keywords: Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI), Digital Mental Health, Human-AI 
Interaction, Help-Seeking Intentions, Health Belief Model (HBM)

1.  Introduction 
 
Across the globe, digital technologies are becoming central to how individuals navigate 
mental health support. While rates of anxiety, depression, and stress continue to rise, particularly 
among younger populations and those facing socioeconomic pressures, traditional healthcare 
systems remain constrained by cost, stigma, and limited availability (Velasco et al., 2020; Bor et 
al., 2014; Lipson et al., 2019). This gap has fueled interest in interactive tools, especially those 
powered by generative artificial intelligence (GAI), that promise scalable, on-demand, and low-
cost support. GAI are “AI algorithms and models … capable of producing new content … with 
human-like creativity and adaptability.” (He et al., 2025, p. 1). For example, conversational 
agents such as ChatGPT have introduced new ways for people to access guidance, validation, 
and psychoeducational resources (Guo et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2025; Lattie et al., 2022). At the 
same time, human therapists offer relational depth, diagnostic nuance, and long-term growth. 
Rather than replacing one another, these modalities are increasingly encountered side by side, 
creating a socio-technical ecosystem where users must navigate decisions between AI-driven and 
human-provided care. This moment raises critical questions: How do people perceive and 
evaluate the respective benefits and risks associated with GAI tools and human therapists? And, 
from a design perspective, which aspects of GAI are perceived as most influential in shaping 
users’ intention to engage with such tools? 
 
While prior work has examined barriers to therapy or the adoption of mental health apps, 
most studies treat each modality in isolation (Velasco et al., 2025; Jardine et al., 2024; Naslund et 
al., 2017). This fragmented approach limits our understanding of the comparative processes that

drive real-world help-seeking decisions. In practice, users often evaluate their options across 
modalities, weighing the strengths of GAI against the perceived limitations of therapy, or vice 
versa. However, empirical research that systematically compares these decisions is lacking. By 
examining perceptions of both GAI and human therapists, at the same time, we assess how users’ 
beliefs about each modality contribute to their intention to use it. This perspective reflects how 
choices are often shaped not just by attraction to one option or dissatisfaction with another, but 
by how the options function together in a broader mental health ecosystem. This study therefore 
advances understanding of user decision-making and offers actionable design implications for 
the future development of mental health technologies.  
1.1. Mental Health and Technology Adoption 
 
A growing body of human computer interaction (HCI) research has explored how digital 
technologies can help bridge gaps in mental health support. Traditional barriers to accessing 
human therapists, including high costs, provider shortages, long wait times, and stigma, have 
driven many individuals toward more scalable, on-demand alternatives (Velasco et al., 2025; 
Andrade et al., 2013). In this context, mental health chatbots and generative AI (GAI) tools have 
gained traction as promising supplemental resources. These tools simulate therapeutic 
conversations, deliver psychoeducational content, and offer behavioral exercises with 24/7 
accessibility and reduced perceived judgment (Guo et al., 2024; Hua et al., 2025; Lattie et al., 
2022). For example, Woebot is an application that uses AI-driven dialogue to guide users through 
mood tracking, cognitive reframing, and mindfulness practices (Fitzpatrick et al., 2017; 
Limpanopparat et al., 2024). 
 
More recently, large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have introduced open-
ended conversational capabilities that users have begun informally using for emotional support

(Alanezi, 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Despite limited clinical validation, recent surveys indicate 
that a significant proportion of people are experimenting with AI-based tools for mental health 
conversations (Zao-Sanders, 2025). At the same time, emerging public debates and policy 
discussions have raised concerns about the safety and appropriate role of AI systems in mental 
health support. These developments raise critical questions about user preferences, expectations, 
and beliefs when choosing between AI-mediated support and traditional human therapy, 
particularly as both modalities become more accessible and socially normalized. 
1.2. Digital Mental Health 
 
Studies attempting to explain the uptake and continued engagement with mobile apps, 
telehealth, and web-based interventions (Naslund et al., 2017; Zahed et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 
2022) have found that higher perceived benefits predict stronger intentions, whereas heightened 
perceived barriers tend to suppress adoption and continued engagement with digital health 
interventions (Zahed et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2022). A limitation of prior work in this domain, 
however, is that they typically evaluate a single support modality in isolation (e.g., therapists or a 
particular digital tool), implicitly assuming that beliefs about one option are independent of 
intentions toward the other. In practice, individuals often consider multiple options 
simultaneously, evaluating a human therapist and a GAI tool side by side rather than treating 
each modality in isolation (Young et al., 2024). Beliefs about one modality may therefore 
influence preferences for another. For example, perceiving strong benefits of GAI (e.g., 24/7 
access, anonymity, and affordability) may reduce intentions to seek a therapist, whereas 
perceived barriers to therapy (e.g., cost, wait times, and stigma) may increase interest in GAI-
based support. Conversely, doubts about the reliability, effectiveness, or data practices of GAI 
may shift preferences toward human care. Prior research on digital mental health technologies

suggests that individuals frequently treat digital tools as substitutes or complements to traditional 
therapy, depending on perceived benefits and barriers (Mohr et al., 2013; Young et al., 2024).

health support choices.

1.3. Perceived Benefits and Barriers to Seeking Therapist and GAI Support 
1.3.1 Emotional and Relational Support 
 
One of the most commonly cited motivations for seeking mental health services is the 
need for emotional understanding and interpersonal connection (Flückiger et al., 2018, 2020). 
Human therapists are uniquely positioned to deliver empathic presence, attuned feedback, and 
relationship-based support. These elements not only facilitate trust and vulnerability but also 
serve as active ingredients in therapeutic change. The therapeutic alliance has been repeatedly 
shown to predict outcomes across modalities, especially for complex or identity-laden concerns 
(Flückiger et al., 2018). In contrast, GAI tools can simulate empathic responses and offer 
immediate emotional validation, but their lack of lived experience and authentic presence limits 
the perceived depth of this connection (Montemayor et al., 2022; Ovsyannikova et al., 2025). For


## Page 7


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 7 
some, GAI’s nonjudgmental nature may be a relief; for others, it may feel emotionally 
insufficient or uncanny (Seitz, 2024; Volpato et al., 2025). 
1.3.2 Personalized Coping Strategies and Educational Needs 
 
Beyond emotional support, individuals seek mental health tools for coping strategies, 
relationship guidance, psychoeducation, and personalized support, particularly when managing 
stress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, or interpersonal difficulties (Donker et al., 2009; Park et 
al., 2025). Human therapists can offer highly tailored interventions that address both coping 
needs and relational challenges, drawing from diverse clinical frameworks and adapting them to 
each client’s context. This personalization is supported by dialogue, intuition, and professional 
training. In contrast, GAI tools excel in the scalable delivery of structured educational content 
and coping strategies, such as mindfulness exercises, stress management techniques, and 
explanations of psychological concepts (Inkster et al., 2018). For individuals seeking immediate 
guidance or information, particularly during moments of distress, GAI may serve a practical role 
(Dehbozorgi et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025). However, such tools may remain limited in their 
ability to provide deep personalization or relational guidance when complex clinical histories, 
comorbidities, or nuanced interpersonal contexts are involved. 
1.3.3. Accessibility and Affordability 
 
Structural barriers such as high costs, limited insurance coverage, long waitlists, and 
geographic shortages of providers are pervasive and remain among the strongest deterrents to 
engaging with traditional therapy (Coombs et al., 2021; Nadarzynski et al., 2025). GAI tools 
mitigate many of these concerns: they are available 24/7, typically free or low-cost, and easily 
accessed via smartphone or computer (Sezgin & McKay, 2024; Xian et al., 2024). These 
characteristics may lower practical barriers to seeking mental health support, particularly for

individuals who might otherwise delay or avoid formal treatment due to financial or logistical 
constraints. At the same time, increased accessibility does not necessarily translate into 
equivalent quality of care, raising important questions about when users may rely on GAI-based 
support versus professional therapy. 
1.3.4. Reliability, Privacy, and Literacy Barriers 
Both human therapists and GAI chatbots may suffer from skepticism often centering on 
reliability and privacy: will it be helpful or safe? How will shared information be stored or used? 
These concerns are heightened in sensitive domains like trauma or suicidality, where precision 
and accountability are critical (Sezgin & McKay, 2024; Xian et al., 2024). Additionally, both 
types of support may be limited by the user’s mental health or technology illiteracy, users may 
not recognize when they need help or know how to initiate and navigate the support available 
(Xian et al., 2024). 
1.3.5. Stigma and Norms  
Stigma remains a key barrier to seeking traditional therapy, especially for those who fear 
social stigma or feel uncomfortable disclosing sensitive issues to another person (Vogel & Wade, 
2022), though GAI tools may offer relative anonymity and control over disclosure pacing 
(Sezgin & McKay, 2024; Xian et al., 2024). These practical affordances position GAI as a 
promising supplement to traditional services, particularly for those who are not ready or able to 
seek human care (Aktan et al., 2022). At the same time, help-seeking norms, or the preference 
for informal support from family or friends over professional services, may similarly shape 
modality preferences. Individuals embedded in social contexts that discourage formal help-
seeking may find GAI-based support more socially acceptable, as it bypasses the perceived 
judgment associated with seeking professional care.

1.5.Present Study 
The present study operationalizes parallel constructs (benefits and barriers) for both GAI 
and human therapists and analyzes them jointly. This design enables estimation of both within-
modality effects (e.g., how GAI benefits predict GAI intention) and cross-modality effects (e.g., 
how therapist barriers predict GAI intention, and vice versa), offering a more complete account 
of how perceived benefits, barriers, and efficacy shape real-world mental health support choices. 
For example, individuals may value emotional support, practical guidance, personalization, 
relational support, psychoeducation, accessibility, and affordability, whether provided by humans 
or machines. Conversely, their help-seeking behavior may be constrained by privacy concerns, 
doubts about reliability, stigma, cultural norms around seeking professional help, and gaps in 
either mental health or technology illiteracy. Importantly, different support modalities may 
address these needs and concerns in distinct ways. Our study builds on this understanding by 
examining how people evaluate the trade-offs between GAI and therapists, and how these 
evaluations predict intention to use one, both, or neither. Specifically, the current study posed the 
following research questions: 
- 
RQ1a: How do individuals’ perceptions of GAI and human therapists differ across 
perceived benefits and barriers, including emotional support, coping guidance, 
relationship support, psychoeducation, personalization, accessibility, affordability, 
privacy concerns, reliability concerns, stigma, help-seeking norms, and mental health 
literacy? 
- 
RQ1b: Do these perceived benefits and barriers vary across populations (i.e., university 
and national adult samples)?


## Page 10


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 10 
- 
RQ2: What within-modality and cross-modality benefits and barriers predict intention to 
use GAI versus human therapists? 
By examining these questions across two distinct populations, a university student sample and a 
nationally representative adult sample, we are also able to explore how different populations 
have distinctive modality preferences. Understanding these population-level differences can 
inform both the theoretical extension of help-seeking frameworks to multi-modal contexts and 
the practical design of GAI tools, thereby clarifying when AI-based support is best positioned to 
complement, rather than substitute, traditional therapy. 
2. Method 
2.1. Participants and Procedures 
All research procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the 
first author’s affiliated institution. Data collection took place between April and May 2025 and 
included an online survey (18–20 minutes) using Qualtrics (https://www.qualtrics.com/). That 
contained items regarding the perceived benefits and barriers to seeking help as well as 
intentions to seek help from human therapists and GAI, demographic questions (i.e, age, 
race/ethnicity, gender), and questions regarding generative AI (GAI) use (i.e., how frequently 
they engage with tools such as ChatGPT) and the roles they believe GAI tools play in mental 
health support (select-all-that-apply format). 
2.1.1 University Sample  
University student participants were collected through departmental research pools in 
psychology and computer science at a university located in the southeastern United States. A 
total of 784 students were recruited from the computer science department and 482 from the 
psychology department, yielding an initial sample of 1,266 students. Participants received one

2.2. Measures 
2.2.1. Perceived Benefits and Barriers  
Participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with a series of statements 
describing potential benefits and barriers of using either a human therapist or a generative 
artificial intelligence (GAI) tool for mental health support. Consistent with prior literature 
examining perceived benefits and barriers of mental health support options, participants rated a 
set of twelve dimensions reflecting potential advantages and concerns related to using GAI or 
human therapists: Emotional Support (e.g., feeling emotionally supported and validated), Coping 
(e.g., learning effective coping skills and strategies), Relationships (e.g., improving one’s ability 
to manage relationships or social situations), Education (e.g., receiving information in a clear 
and understandable format), Personalization (e.g., tailored advice or interventions that meet 
individual needs), Accessibility (e.g., availability when needed), and Affordability (e.g., being 
financially affordable),  Privacy (e.g., discomfort with sharing sensitive or personal information),


## Page 13


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 13 
Reliability (e.g., doubts regarding the reliability or helpfulness of the support provided), Stigma 
(e.g., feeling embarrassed or ashamed about seeking mental health support), Help Seeking Norm 
(e.g., preference for informal support from family or friends), and Mental Health Literacy (e.g., 
awareness or understanding of available resources). 
Each of the constructs were assessed using three items presented twice (once for GAI and 
once for therapists). Item responses were recorded on a 0–100 scale, where higher scores 
indicated stronger agreement with the statement. Whenever possible we adapted item wording 
from established help-seeking benefit and risk scales (e.g., Clement et al., 2012; Norman & 
Skinner, 2006; Devilly & Borkovec, 2000). However, most available measures are modality-
bound (e.g., therapist-only or technology-only) and are not designed for side-by-side assessment 
across AI and human care. After development all items were reviewed by Human Computer 
Interaction and Counseling Psychology researchers with a minimum of three years of domain 
experience to ensure clarity and coverage. Reliability analyses indicated acceptable internal 
consistency (Ponterotto & Ruckdeschel, 2007), with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.70 for 
all constructs (ranging from 0.71 to 0.98). For analysis, mean scores were computed for each 
construct. 
2.2.2. Intention to Use 
Participants’ intention to use each type of mental health support was assessed using two 
single-item measures: (1) “I intend to use GAI to support my mental health regularly,” and (2) “I 
intend to use a human professional to support my mental health regularly.” Each item was rated 
on a continuous scale ranging from 0 to 100. For analysis, mean scores were computed for each 
construct.

3.2. Research Questions 
3.2.1. RQ1a: How do individuals’ perceptions of GAI and human therapists differ? and RQ1b: 
Do these perceptions vary across populations? 
To examine how individuals perceive generative AI (GAI) and human therapists (RQ1), 
and if these perceptions differed across samples (RQ1b), we first conducted a multivariate 
analysis of variance (MANOVA) with sample (University vs. National) as the between-subject 
factor across the 13 outcomes. The multivariate test was significant, Wilks’ λ = .904, F(13, 1459) 
= 11.91, p < .001, indicating overall differences in perceptions between the two samples. Follow-
up analyses examined differences in ratings of GAI and human therapists across each dimension. 
To account for multiple comparisons across the 13 follow-up tests, a Bonferroni-corrected alpha 
level of .004 was applied.

Addressing RQ1a, paired-sample t-tests indicated significant differences between GAI 
and therapists across most of the perceived benefits and barriers. Participants rated therapists 
significantly higher than GAI on Emotional Support, Coping, Relationships, Education, and 
Personalization, as well as on Intention to Use. In contrast, participants rated GAI significantly 
higher than therapists on Accessibility and Affordability. With regard to perceived barriers,

3.2.2. RQ2: What within-modality and cross-modality benefits and barriers predict intention to 
use GAI versus human therapists? 
 
To identify the specific within-modality and cross-modality benefits and barriers that 
predict intention to use each modality (RQ2), we employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and 
Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. LASSO is a regularized regression technique that 
constrains the sum of the absolute values of the model coefficients, simultaneously performing 
variable selection and model regularization (Tibshirani, 1996). This approach is particularly 
well-suited to models with many potentially correlated predictors, such as the multiple perceived 
benefits and barriers measured in this study (McNeish, 2015; Zhou et al., 2024). 
 
To further reduce the risk of sample-specific results and false positives, we 
complemented the LASSO analysis with a stability selection procedure (Meinshausen & 
Bühlmann, 2010). Stability selection evaluates the robustness of predictors by repeatedly 
estimating the LASSO model on subsamples of the data and calculating the proportion of times 
each predictor is selected. Predictors that are consistently selected across subsamples are 
considered more reliable and less likely to reflect sampling variability. 
 
Separate LASSO regressions were conducted for two dependent variables: intention to 
use GAI and intention to use a human therapist. Analyses were also conducted independently for 
the university and national samples to allow for population-specific patterns. Each model 
included all belief-based constructs (benefits and barriers) associated with both modalities. This 
setup allowed us to capture both within-modality predictors (e.g., how beliefs about GAI predict


## Page 18


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 18 
GAI use) and cross-modality influences (e.g., how barriers to therapist use predict intention to 
use GAI). 
 
LASSO models were implemented using the glmnet framework, and the regularization 
parameter (lambda) was selected via 10-fold cross-validation, with the optimal value determined 
as the lambda minimizing mean squared error across folds. Prior to model estimation, all 
predictors were standardized using z-score normalization to ensure comparability of coefficients 
across dimensions. For each predictor, we computed a selection probability indicating the 
proportion of subsamples in which the predictor was selected by the LASSO model. Following 
common practice in stability selection, predictors with selection probabilities ≥ .80 were 
interpreted as robust predictors (Meinshausen & Bühlmann, 2010). Standardized beta 
coefficients (β) from the LASSO models are reported alongside selection probabilities to 
facilitate interpretation and comparison across predictors.

4. Discussion 
This study examined how individuals weigh generative AI (GAI) tools against human 
therapists when considering mental health support, with a focus on the belief structures that 
shape these decisions. Using parallel, modality-specific measures of perceived benefits and 
barriers across two samples, university students and a nationally representative adult sample, we 
identified both within-modality and cross-modality predictors of help-seeking intention. Several 
consistent patterns emerged. Across both samples, perceptions of interpersonal and emotional 
benefits were the strongest drivers of intention to use either modality, while reliability concerns 
were the most robust barrier to GAI adoption. Structural factors, particularly affordability, played 
a central role in shaping therapist intentions. Together, these findings suggest that help-seeking


## Page 23


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 23 
decisions reflect a dynamic push-pull process in which individuals simultaneously evaluate the 
strengths of one modality and the limitations of the other. 
4.1. Interpersonal and Emotional Benefits as Primary Drivers 
One of the clearest findings across both samples was that perceived emotional support, 
relational guidance, and personalization were the strongest positive predictors of intention to use 
either modality. This held true whether the target was GAI or human therapists: users were more 
likely to engage with a modality when they believed it could meet their core interpersonal needs. 
These findings align with a substantial body of evidence emphasizing the centrality of relational 
factors in mental health help-seeking (Flückiger et al., 2018; Elvins & Green, 2008). The 
therapeutic alliance, long established as one of the most robust predictors of treatment outcome, 
appears to function as a template against which users evaluate both human and AI-based support. 
Importantly, human therapists were rated significantly higher than GAI on all 
interpersonal benefit dimensions, including emotional support, relational guidance, and 
personalization. This is consistent with prior work suggesting that users remain skeptical about 
whether AI-generated responses constitute genuine care, even when they recognize the practical 
advantages of AI tools (Montemayor et al., 2022; Volpato et al., 2025). What is notable, however, 
is that these same interpersonal dimensions also predicted GAI intention, meaning that users 
were more open to AI-based support precisely when they perceived GAI as capable of emotional 
responsiveness and tailored interaction. This suggests that the bar for GAI adoption is not simply 
about access or convenience, but about whether the tool can credibly signal relational 
competence. 
Interestingly, accessibility and affordability, often cited as the primary advantages of 
digital mental health tools (Sezgin & McKay, 2024; Lattie et al., 2022), did not emerge as


## Page 24

significant predictors of GAI intention in either sample. This discrepancy points to an important 
design insight: structural advantages may lower the barrier to initial engagement, but they do not 
sustain it. Users appear to be asking not just "can I use this?" but "will this actually help me?" 
For GAI to move beyond novelty toward meaningful adoption, it must demonstrate emotional 
credibility, not merely logistical convenience. 
4.2. Reliability as the Central Barrier to GAI 
Across both the university and national samples, concerns about GAI reliability emerged 
as the strongest negative predictor of intention to use GAI, more so than privacy concerns, 
stigma, or any other barrier. Users who doubted whether GAI responses would be accurate, 
dependable, or clinically appropriate were substantially less likely to consider using these tools 
for mental health support. This finding extends prior work documenting skepticism about AI in 
sensitive health domains (Xian et al., 2024; Sezgin & McKay, 2024), and underscores a critical 
challenge for GAI developers: without a credible basis for trust, emotional and relational benefits 
may not be sufficient to drive adoption (Isaac et al., 2024), suggesting that, without a credible 
basis for trust, emotional and relational benefits alone may be insufficient to drive adoption. 
The salience of reliability concerns may reflect broader uncertainties about how large 
language models generate responses, particularly in high-stakes contexts where accuracy and 
consistency matter. Unlike informational queries, mental health support involves emotionally 
sensitive disclosures where an unhelpful or inappropriate response can cause harm. Users may 
therefore apply a higher standard of scrutiny to GAI in this domain than in others (Petzel & 
Sowerby, 2025). Notably, perceived reliability concerns about therapists also shaped intentions: 
students who doubted therapist reliability were more likely to consider GAI as an alternative,

suggesting that reliability functions as a cross-modality switching variable, a factor that can 
redirect users from one modality to the other when trust is undermined. 
4.3. Affordability and Structural Barriers to Therapy 
Perceived affordability was the strongest positive predictor of therapist intention in both 
samples, underscoring the persistent role of financial access in help-seeking decisions (Coombs 
et al., 2021; Andrade et al., 2013). This finding is particularly notable given that affordability did 
not predict GAI intention, suggesting that cost concerns operate asymmetrically across 
modalities. Users appear to take GAI's low cost largely for granted, while financial accessibility 
remains a meaningful signal of feasibility for therapy. In the national sample, help-seeking norms 
also emerged as a significant barrier to therapist use, reflecting how cultural and social 
expectations around formal mental health care continue to suppress engagement with 
professional services (Vogel & Wade, 2022). 
Interestingly, affordability showed a small negative association with GAI intention in the 
university sample. This counterintuitive pattern may reflect a substitution dynamic: students who 
perceive GAI as highly affordable, and who also have access to campus counseling resources, 
may feel less urgency to rely exclusively on GAI, instead distributing their support-seeking 
across multiple available options. This interpretation aligns with prior work showing that 
digitally literate populations often adopt layered or "assemblage" approaches to mental health 
care rather than relying on a single modality (Lupton, 2021; Riboldi et al., 2024). Although this 
interpretation is speculative and warrants direct empirical testing, it suggests that the role of 
affordability in shaping GAI intentions may be more contextually contingent than previously 
assumed. 
4.4. Cross-Modality Push-Pull Dynamics


## Page 26


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 26 
A distinctive contribution of this study is the simultaneous examination of within- and 
cross-modality predictors. Our results reveal that help-seeking intentions are not determined 
solely by attitudes toward a preferred modality, but are also shaped by perceived limitations of 
the alternative. For example, students who doubted therapist reliability were more likely to turn 
to GAI, while those who were concerned about GAI privacy were more likely to prefer 
therapists. In the national sample, GAI reliability and mental health literacy barriers similarly 
predicted higher therapist intention, suggesting that dissatisfaction with one modality actively 
redirects users toward the other. 
This push-pull pattern extends the Health Belief Model's traditional focus on single-
modality adoption by demonstrating that perceived barriers to one option function as indirect 
drivers of another. In practice, users appear to engage in comparative cost-benefit reasoning 
across modalities, a process that existing single-modality frameworks do not fully capture. These 
findings also reveal population-level differences in how this dynamic unfolds. University 
students tended to treat GAI and therapy as complementary resources, using each to fill gaps left 
by the other. In contrast, the national sample more often approached the two modalities as 
substitutes, with intentions to use GAI and therapy showing a more competitive relationship. 
This distinction likely reflects differences in structural access: students embedded in campus 
mental health ecosystems have more flexibility to combine modalities, while adults facing 
greater financial and logistical barriers may feel compelled to choose. 
4.5. Theoretical Contributions 
This study makes several contributions to theory on digital mental health and human-AI 
interaction. Most directly, it extends the Health Belief Model (HBM) to a dual-modality context. 
The HBM has been widely applied to explain adoption of individual health technologies or help-


## Page 27


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 27 
seeking behaviors (Janz & Becker, 1984; Henshaw & Freedman-Doan, 2009; Carpenter, 2010), 
but existing applications typically evaluate a single modality in isolation, by assessing, for 
instance, whether perceived benefits of therapy predict therapy use, or whether perceived barriers 
to app use predict disengagement. This framing implicitly treats each modality as independent, 
overlooking the comparative reasoning that users engage in when multiple options are available. 
Our findings challenge this assumption. By operationalizing parallel benefit and barrier 
constructs for both GAI and human therapists and modeling them jointly, we demonstrate that 
perceptions of one modality systematically shape intentions toward the other. Barriers to therapy 
predicted increased GAI intention; barriers to GAI predicted increased therapist intention. This 
cross-modality structure suggests that help-seeking in a multi-option environment is better 
understood as a comparative evaluation process than as a series of independent adoption 
decisions. Extending the HBM to accommodate these cross-modality dynamics offers a more 
ecologically valid account of how people navigate mental health support in contexts where AI-
based and human-provided options coexist. It is worth noting how the study's constructs map 
onto core HBM components. Perceived benefits in this study, including emotional support, 
coping, personalization, and affordability, correspond to the HBM's perceived benefits construct, 
capturing the anticipated advantages of engaging with a given modality. Perceived barriers, 
including privacy concerns, reliability doubts, stigma, and help-seeking norms, align with the 
HBM's perceived barriers construct, reflecting the anticipated costs or obstacles to action. 
Importantly, whereas the traditional HBM treats these constructs as modality-specific and 
unidirectional, the present study demonstrates that they operate comparatively: the perceived 
barriers of one modality function as indirect cues that redirect users toward the alternative. This 
cross-modality structure represents a meaningful extension of the original framework.

Second, this study contributes to the growing literature on human-AI interaction in high-
stakes domains by identifying the specific belief dimensions that distinguish GAI from human 
care in users' minds. Prior HCI research has documented general skepticism about AI empathy 
and authenticity (Montemayor et al., 2022; Seitz, 2024), but has rarely examined how these 
perceptions translate into behavioral intentions alongside perceptions of human alternatives. Our 
results show that emotional support, personalization, and reliability function as the primary 
evaluative dimensions along which users differentiate GAI from therapists, and that these 
dimensions, rather than structural factors like accessibility or cost, are the proximal drivers of 
adoption. This finding reframes the design challenge for GAI mental health tools: the critical gap 
is not logistical but relational. 
Third, by replicating the core pattern across two demographically distinct samples, a 
university student population and a nationally representative adult sample, we provide evidence 
for the generalizability of the dual-modality belief structure while also documenting meaningful 
population-level variation. The complementary versus substitutive patterns observed across 
samples suggest that the role GAI plays in users' mental health ecosystems is not fixed, but 
context-dependent. This has implications for how theories of technology adoption are applied 
across populations with varying levels of structural access to traditional care. 
4.6. Design Implications 
 
The findings from this study carry direct implications for the design and deployment of 
GAI tools in mental health contexts. We organize these implications around four interconnected 
priorities. 
4.6.1. Prioritize Emotional Credibility Over Structural Convenience


## Page 29


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 29 
Our results consistently showed that emotional support, personalization, and relational 
responsiveness, not accessibility or affordability, were the proximal drivers of GAI adoption. Yet 
participants rated GAI significantly lower than therapists on all three of these dimensions. This 
gap points to a core design challenge: current GAI tools may be optimized for logistical 
efficiency while underdelivering on the relational qualities users actually care about. Reducing 
barriers to entry opens the door, but emotional credibility sustains engagement. 
 
Closing this gap requires moving beyond surface-level empathic language toward 
features that convey genuine responsiveness, adaptive dialogue that evolves with user 
disclosures, real-time validation of emotional experiences, and tonal consistency that signals 
attentiveness over time. Incorporating user feedback mechanisms that allow individuals to signal 
whether a response felt supportive could help systems learn to calibrate emotional attunement 
more effectively (Glickman & Sharot, 2025). Importantly, these are not cosmetic additions but 
core design priorities. Without them, GAI risks being experienced as efficient yet emotionally 
hollow, a tool users recognize as convenient but do not trust with their inner lives. 
4.6.2. Build Trust Through Transparency and Reliability 
 
Reliability concerns were the single strongest barrier to GAI adoption across both 
samples, outweighing privacy, stigma, and other commonly cited obstacles. This finding suggests 
that users are not simply cautious about AI in general; they are specifically uncertain about 
whether GAI responses in mental health contexts will be accurate, consistent, and safe. In high-
stakes domains where an unhelpful or inappropriate response can cause real harm, this scrutiny is 
warranted. 
 
Addressing reliability concerns requires more than improving response quality; it requires 
making trustworthiness visible. Clear communication about what the tool can and cannot do,


## Page 30


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 30 
transparent explanations of how responses are generated, and explicit acknowledgment of 
limitations can reduce uncertainty and build informed trust (Mehrotra et al., 2024). Privacy 
protections should be communicated proactively and in plain language, as ambiguity about data 
handling amplifies distrust, particularly among users who are already cautious about disclosing 
sensitive information. Designing for trust is not simply a matter of safety compliance but a 
prerequisite for meaningful engagement. 
4.6.3. Design for the Ecosystem, Not the Tool 
A key insight from our cross-sample comparison is that the role GAI plays in users' mental 
health support is not fixed. It shifts depending on what other resources are available. Among 
university students with access to campus counseling services, GAI functioned primarily as a 
complement: a flexible, low-barrier option for filling gaps between sessions or providing 
immediate support. Among the national sample, where structural access to therapy was more 
constrained, GAI more often served as a substitute, functioning as a primary scaffold for support. 
 
This distinction has direct implications for how GAI tools should be designed and 
deployed. In resource-rich settings, GAI should be optimized for complementarity, reinforcing 
coping strategies, supporting between-session continuity, and preparing users for in-person work. 
Features such as session summaries, mood tracking, and therapist-ready notes could help bridge 
AI-based and human care. In resource-scarce contexts, however, GAI must be equipped to 
function as a primary support, requiring greater breadth of psychoeducational content, more 
robust emotional responsiveness, and stronger trust-building safeguards. Designing as though all 
users approach GAI from the same structural position risks underserving those who need it most. 
4.6.4. Lower the Cognitive Barrier to Entry


## Page 31


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 31 
 
Across both samples, mental health literacy and technological literacy emerged as 
meaningful barriers, particularly in the national sample, where these factors also predicted a 
preference for human therapists over GAI. This suggests that for many potential users, the 
obstacle to GAI adoption is not skepticism about AI per se, but uncertainty about how to use it 
effectively. Even a well-designed tool will remain inaccessible if users cannot navigate it 
confidently or interpret its outputs meaningfully. 
 
Designing for literacy means rethinking onboarding as a substantive intervention rather 
than a procedural formality. Interactive tutorials that model effective dialogue, in-context 
prompts that guide users through moments of distress, and accessible explanations of 
psychological concepts embedded within the tool can reduce cognitive burden and build user 
confidence. These scaffolds are particularly important for populations with limited prior 
exposure to either digital health tools or formal mental health concepts. Groups that may benefit 
most from low-barrier support but are currently least likely to engage with it. In short, before 
layering on advanced capabilities, GAI systems must lower the cognitive barrier to entry. Equity 
in access requires not just affordability and availability, but genuine usability. 
4.7. Limitations and Future Directions 
 
Several limitations of the present study point to promising directions for future research. 
First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although LASSO regression 
identified belief structures that robustly predict help-seeking intentions, the directionality of 
these relationships cannot be established from a single time point. Longitudinal and experimental 
designs are needed to examine how exposure to GAI tools shapes, and is shaped by, users' 
perceptions over time. For instance, tracking individuals before and after sustained engagement


## Page 32


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 32 
with a GAI-based support tool could illuminate whether reliability and emotional credibility 
concerns diminish with actual use, or whether they persist as stable evaluative dispositions. 
 
Second, both samples were recruited online and presuppose a baseline level of digital 
access and literacy. This limits generalizability to populations who are offline, underserved, or 
who face distinct structural and cultural barriers to both digital and traditional mental health care. 
Future research should prioritize more inclusive recruitment strategies, including clinical 
populations, older adults with limited digital exposure, and communities with historically low 
mental health service utilization, to examine whether the belief structures identified here 
replicate across more diverse contexts. 
 
Third, while this study identifies beliefs that predict intention, it does not capture the 
cognitive and affective processes through which users actually arrive at help-seeking decisions, 
nor does it assess real-world behavior. Intentions and behavior are related but distinct constructs, 
and the gap between them is particularly relevant in mental health contexts where emotional 
state, perceived urgency, and situational constraints can override deliberative reasoning. Future 
research employing longitudinal, qualitative, or experience sampling methods could illuminate 
how users weigh trade-offs between modalities in real time, particularly under conditions of 
acute distress or when access to preferred options is suddenly constrained. 
 
Fourth, intention to use each modality was assessed using single-item measures, which 
may limit reliability and construct validity compared to multi-item scales. Future work should 
employ validated multi-item intention measures to provide more robust assessments of help-
seeking behavior. 
 
Fifth, although the national sample was recruited using demographic quotas to 
approximate U.S. population distributions, participants were drawn from an online

crowdsourcing platform, which may underrepresent individuals with limited digital access or 
literacy. Similarly, the university sample included a substantial proportion of computer science 
students, whose familiarity with AI systems may have elevated perceived benefits of GAI 
relative to the broader student population. These sampling characteristics should be considered 
when generalizing the findings. 
Conclusion 
 
As generative AI becomes increasingly embedded in everyday life, questions about its 
appropriate role in mental health support are becoming urgent. This study addressed those 
questions by examining how individuals evaluate GAI tools and human therapists side by side, 
identifying the belief structures that shape intentions to use each modality across two 
demographically distinct populations. Several consistent patterns emerged. Perceptions of 
interpersonal and emotional benefits were the primary drivers of intention to use either modality, 
while reliability concerns functioned as the most robust barrier to GAI adoption, and 
affordability remained central to therapist use. Critically, these perceptions did not operate in 
isolation: barriers associated with one modality actively redirected users toward the other, 
revealing a comparative push-pull dynamic that single-modality frameworks have not previously 
captured. 
 
These findings carry implications at multiple levels. Theoretically, they extend the Health 
Belief Model to a dual-modality context, demonstrating that help-seeking in environments where 
AI-based and human-provided care coexist is better understood as a comparative evaluation 
process than as a series of independent adoption decisions. Practically, they reframe the design 
challenge for GAI mental health tools: the critical gap is not logistical but relational. Structural


## Page 34


AI AND HUMAN HELP-SEEKING 34 
advantages like accessibility and affordability open the door, but emotional credibility, reliability, 
and personalization determine whether users walk through it. 
 
Ultimately, GAI is neither a replacement for human therapists nor a lesser alternative. It 
is a different kind of resource, with distinct strengths and limitations that position it well for 
some roles and poorly for others. Realizing its potential requires designing not just for what GAI 
can do, but for what users need it to be: trustworthy, emotionally resonant, and meaningfully 
integrated into a broader ecosystem of care.
