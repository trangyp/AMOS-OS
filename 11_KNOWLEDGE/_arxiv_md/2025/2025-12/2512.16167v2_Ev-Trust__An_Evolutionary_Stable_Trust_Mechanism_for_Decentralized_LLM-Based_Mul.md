Ev-Trust: An Evolutionary Stable Trust Mechanism for Decentralized
LLM-Based Multi-Agent Service Economies
Jiye Wang 1 2 Shiduo Yang 1 Jiayu Qin 1 Jianbin Li 1 Yu Wang 1 Yuanhe Zhao 1 Kenan Guo 1
Abstract
Autonomous LLM-based agents are increasingly
engaging in decentralized service interactions to
collaboratively execute complex tasks. However,
the intrinsic instability and low-cost generativ-
ity of LLMs introduce a systemic vulnerability,
where self-interested agents are incentivized to
pursue short-term gains through deceptive be-
haviors.
Such strategies can rapidly prolifer-
ate within the population and precipitate a sys-
temic trust collapse. To address this, we propose
Ev-Trust, a strategy-equilibrium trust mechanism
grounded in evolutionary game theory. Ev-Trust
constructs a dynamic feedback loop that couples
trust evaluation with evolutionary incentives, em-
bedding interaction history and reputation directly
into the agent’s expected revenue function. This
mechanism fundamentally reshapes the revenue
structure, converting trustworthiness into a de-
cisive survival advantage that suppresses short-
sightedness. We provide a rigorous theoretical
foundation based on the Replicator Dynamics,
proving the asymptotic stability of Evolutionary
Stable Strategies (ESS) that favor cooperation.
Experimental results indicate that Ev-Trust ef-
fectively eliminates malicious strategies and en-
hances collective revenue, exhibiting resilience
against the invasion of mutant behaviors.

and mathematically prove the existence and asymptotic sta-

2. Related Work
2.1. Threats and Limitations of Defenses
Recent multi-agent security research distinguishes external
cognitive attacks (e.g., prompt injection) from internal
behavioral degeneration (e.g., strategic deception). While
external threats exploit LLMs’ alignment and are mitigable
via input filtering (Lee & Tiwari, 2024; Gu et al., 2024),
this paper focuses on the more systemic challenge of
behavioral degeneration. Driven by utility maximization,
agents spontaneously develop deceptive strategies that are
intrinsic to interaction and difficult to detect via static rules
(Aitchison et al., 2020), rapidly eroding collective trust
in decentralized networks (Motwani et al., 2024; Ju et al.,
2024). To mitigate these risks, existing methods primarily
rely on centralized supervision (Xiang et al., 2024; Zhang
et al., 2024) or individual defense enhancement (Hua
et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2024). These approaches typically
introduce external supervisors or embed security constraints
into agent planning.
However, such defenses depend
heavily on high-authority arbitration or manual oversight.
In decentralized service markets where agents operate
autonomously without coercive central authority, these
dependencies render current methods ineffective against
the evolutionary accumulation of malicious strategies.
2.2. From Static Games to Evolutionary Trust
Game theory serves as a fundamental framework for mod-
eling strategic interdependence and incentive structures in
multi-agent systems (Von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944).
Traditionally, researchers applied these principles to design
trust mechanisms, such as reputation systems that regulated
transaction dynamics in electronic markets (Resnick et al.,
2006; Cabral & Hortacsu, 2010). More recently, researchers
have extended game-theoretic approaches to explore the
collective intelligence of LLM-based agents. Studies such
as (Akata et al., 2025) and (Guo, 2023) reveal that these
generative agents exhibit human-like strategic reasoning in
repeated game. Furthermore, works like CompeteAI (Zhao
et al., 2023) simulated complex social learning in virtual en-
vironments. Despite these advances, most current research
on LLM-based games relies on static Nash equilibria, focus-
ing on individual reasoning capabilities rather than the long-
term evolutionary stability of the population (Cavalcante
et al., 2012). While traditional evolutionary approaches
(Han, 2022; Bloembergen et al., 2015) proved crucial for
regulating collective behavior, they have not been adapted to
account for the unique instability and generation costs inher-
ent to LLMs. Our work fills this void by constructing a dy-
namic feedback loop that couples trust evaluation with evolu-
tionary incentives within an evolutionary game framework.

Remark 4.2. Notably, Eq. (15) indicates that ∆Ψeff controls
the robustness margin: when observation noise or delays
weaken trust differentiation, ∆Ψeff decreases, and the sta-
bility threshold on α increases accordingly.
In general, Eq. (15) quantifies the system’s resistance to
invasion: cooperation remains dominant when the long-
term reputation gain αrH∆Ψeff exceeds the short-term
defection incentive (ch −δ). Consequently, cooperation
constitutes an evolutionarily stable outcome provided trust-
induced future advantages outweigh immediate deceptive
benefits. Theorem 4.1 further guarantees that, under mild
conditions, the system resists small mutations and converges
to a cooperation-dominant equilibrium.

6. CONCLUSION
This paper presented Ev-Trust, a strategy-equilibrium trust
mechanism grounded in evolutionary game theory to secure
decentralized LLM-based service markets. Addressing high-
risk dynamics characterized by instability and deception,
Ev-Trust redefines trust not as a static metric but as a dy-
namic evolutionary force. By embedding reputation into
expected revenue functions, the mechanism constructs a
feedback loop that reshapes payoffs, converting trustworthi-
ness into a survival advantage. Theoretical analysis based on
Replicator Dynamics confirmed the asymptotic stability of
cooperative equilibria, while extensive experiments demon-
strated that Ev-Trust effectively suppresses malicious strate-
gies, enhances collective revenue, and exhibits resilience
against mutations. We hope Ev-Trust offers a valuable per-
spective on trust modeling for autonomous ecosystems and
inspires further research into sustainable cooperation in de-
centralized networks.

C. Collaborative Service Subgame
Beyond the primary one-to-one interactions detailed in the
main workflow, the market framework incorporates a col-
laborative subgame designed to handle complex tasks from
a requestor R that necessitate cooperation between two dis-
tinct providers, P1 and P2. In this extended setting, the total
reward is contingent upon the synergistic performance and
combined trustworthiness of both participants.
The decision-making process begins with a participation
phase, where each provider Pi independently determines
whether to engage and selects an optimal strategy by weigh-
ing their trust in the requestor R, their confidence in the
partner Pj, and the projected revenue split. The successful
completion of the task is strictly governed by mutual co-
operation: the collaboration only succeeds if both P1 and
P2 act honestly (HQ) or consistently provide low-quality
service (LQ) in alignment with R’s specific requirements.
Conversely, if either agent resorts to fraud (F) or denial (D),
the entire collaborative effort fails.
Following the interaction, the requestor R evaluates the
individual contributions; however, the trust feedback mech-
anism accounts for the complexity of credit assignment, as
a failure precipitated by a single partner may diminish the
collective reputation of both agents if the specific defector
cannot be definitively identified. Ultimately, this subgame
serves as a rigorous test for the trust mechanism’s robustness
within multi-agent coalitions, forcing agents to strategically
evaluate the reliability of both their consumers and their
collaborators.

zones from low-risk zones to maximize the requestor’s ex-

E.1. Justification of Functional Forms
The selection of the exponential function for modeling se-
mantic alignment and drift is grounded in reliability theory
and psychological principles of trust formation. For the
**Likelihood of Competence** (1 −e−kSalign), we model
the probability of observing a specific alignment score given
trustworthiness using an exponential saturation function.
This choice offers distinct advantages over linear models.
First, it captures the property of saturation, where trust con-
fidence exhibits diminishing returns; the exponential form
naturally bounds the probability within [0, 1] and models a
realistic “learning curve,” where initial high-quality interac-
tions provide maximum information gain while subsequent
successes reinforce confidence at a decreasing rate. Sec-
ond, this form ensures robustness, as the core mathematical
requirement for the evolutionary mechanism is monotonic-
ity—higher alignment must yield higher trust probability.
As long as the function is monotonically increasing and
saturating, the conditions for Evolutionary Stable Strategies
(ESS) hold. While alternative forms like Logistic or Power
Law functions are valid, they primarily alter the convergence
rate rather than the final equilibrium stability.
Regarding the Likelihood of Integrity (e−λDdrift), we em-
ploy an exponential decay function based on the current
semantic drift Ddrift to model behavioral stability. This ap-
proach ensures responsiveness to sudden strategy changes,
where a high drift value—indicating volatility—results in a

E.2. Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Robustness
The parameters k (sensitivity to quality), λ (sensitivity to
drift), and decision thresholds θ, τ are not arbitrary heuris-
tics but tunable control variables with clear stability bound-
aries. The sensitivity factor k and decay rate λ effectively
control the “steepness” of trust accumulation and the sever-
ity of penalties for inconsistency. Varying these parameters
primarily influences the system’s convergence speed rather
than the convergence target. A larger k accelerates trust es-
tablishment but may increase susceptibility to noise, while
a larger λ imposes stricter consistency requirements. Our
experiments demonstrate that the system remains robust pro-
vided that k, λ > 0, with specific values chosen to balance
responsiveness and stability for typical market interaction
frequencies.
Similarly, the decision thresholds θ (Payment) and τ (Inter-
action) act as classifiers within the decision process, func-
tioning analogously to the decision boundary in an ROC
curve. Increasing θ reduces False Positives (overpaying
bad providers) but increases False Negatives (underpaying
good ones). Crucially, the mechanism’s stability does not
break if θ shifts within a reasonable range (e.g., 0.5 to 0.8);
such adjustments merely shift the operating point along the
efficiency frontier. The values selected for our experiments
are calibrated to maximize the collective revenue observed
in pilot runs.
