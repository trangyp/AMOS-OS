Impact of temporary lockdown on disease extinction in assortative networks
Elad Korngut1 and Michael Assaf1
1Racah Institute of Physics, Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Jerusalem 91904, Israel
Changing environmental conditions can significantly affect the dynamics of disease spread. These
changes may arise naturally or result from human interventions; in the latter case, lockdown mea-
sures that lead to abrupt but temporary reductions in transmission rates are used to combat disease
spread. However, the impact of these measures on rare events in realistic populations has not been
studied so far. Here, we analyze the susceptible-infected-susceptible (SIS) model in a stochastic
setting where disease extinction—a sudden clearance of the infection—–occurs via a rare, large fluc-
tuation. We use a semiclassical approximation and extensive numerical simulations to show how
the extinction risk of the disease depends on both the duration and magnitude of the lockdown, in
heterogeneous assortative networks, with degree-degree correlations between neighboring nodes.
I.
INTRODUCTION
The spread of infectious diseases through populations
is largely determined by contact patterns between indi-
viduals and the rate of transmission across those con-
tacts [1–8]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, widespread
quarantine measures, such as lockdowns and mandatory
mask-wearing, were implemented to curb transmission
[9, 10]. These interventions led to abrupt and substantial
changes in both contact patterns and transmission rates.
While many studies explored the impact of periodically
varying or fluctuating environmental conditions, more re-
cently, in response to various large-scale pandemics, at-
tention has shifted toward understanding the effects of
temporary lockdown measures [9–18].
To describe disease spread reliably, epidemiological
models must incorporate both the underlying contact
structure and the dynamics of disease progression. The
latter is typically captured using compartmental mod-
els, which divide the population into distinct compart-
ments, each representing a possible state of an individ-
ual. These states commonly include susceptible individ-
uals, infected individuals who carry the disease, and re-
covered individuals who have acquired immunity. In the
absence of long-term immunity, however, the susceptible-
infected-susceptible (SIS) model, which includes only two
compartments of susceptibles (S) and infected (I), pro-
vides an adequate description of the dynamics [2, 3, 5–7].
Here, susceptible individuals transition to the infected
compartment upon infection, while infected individuals
return to the susceptible compartment upon recovery.
Even in its simplest formulation, the SIS model ex-
hibits nontrivial long-term behavior.
When the infec-
tion rate exceeds a critical threshold (discussed below),
the system approaches a persistent endemic state in the
infinite-population limit, with the disease surviving in-
definitely [8]. However, for large (yet finite) populations,
this state becomes a long-lived metastable state, with
random demographic fluctuations ultimately leading to
disease extinction [19]. In this case, the quantities of in-
terest are the extinction time and extinction probability
(EP) of the disease. Computation of the EP—the proba-
bility of escaping the long-lived endemic state via a large
fluctuation—is simplified under the assumption that for
a large population size, the metastable endemic state is
quasistationary, and slowly decays in time [19–21]. Here,
the quasi-stationary distribution (QSD) around the en-
demic state as well as the EP can be analytically com-
puted, in well-mixed environments, in which each indi-
vidual interacts uniformly with all others [1, 7, 20, 22–25].
Yet,

such cases, networks are used to describe the underlying
topology of contacts, where nodes represent individuals
and links capture interactions between them. In static
networks with a fixed structure, degree heterogeneity
plays a key role in disease dynamics and has a profound
effect on rare events statistics [27–32]. Recently, assorta-
tive networks—where nodes with similar degrees tend to
connect [33, 34]—have been shown to alter the extinction
risk, even when the degree distribution is fixed [35].
In this work we are interested in exploring the ef-
fect of time-varying networks, as a measure of mitigat-
ing and controlling the epidemic.
Such scenarios have
been effectively considered in well-mixed settings by tak-
ing time-varying infection and recovery rates [11–15, 36].
In more complex topologies of heterogeneous population
networks, reducing exposure and mitigating outbreaks
may be achieved by individual link severing or rewiring,
or more global measures such as decreasing the overall
number of links due to lockdowns or seclusions [37–48].
An important example of time-varying networks arises
from the implementation of temporary lockdowns, caus-
ing an abrupt reduction in the infection rate for a pre-
scribed period.
Such lockdowns have been studied in
terms of the trade-off between their cost—in duration
and stringency—and their effectiveness in mitigating dis-
ease spread [47, 48]. Moreover, it has been shown that the
way the lockdown is carried out strongly influences the
epidemic dynamics; for example, intermittent lockdowns,
where distinct fractions of the population are quaran-
tined periodically, have been shown to enhance contain-
ment and lead to disease eradication [47, 49, 50]. How-
ever, the question of how such quarantines or lockdowns

II.
THEORETICAL FORMULATION
We begin by formulating the theoretical SIS model on
a heterogeneous network. In the following we will focus
on assortative networks in which high-degree nodes tend
to connect to other high-degree nodes and vice versa [33].
The complementary scenario of disassortative networks,
in which high-degree nodes tend to connect to low-degree
nodes, will be discussed in Sec. III. In this model, an
isolated population of N individuals is divided into two
compartments: susceptible (S) and infected (I). Tran-
sitions between the two compartments are possible via
infection, S + I →I + I at a rate β per encounter, or
recovery, I →S, at a rate γ per individual [8, 51].

nodes and their interactions as undirected links, encoded
in the adjacency matrix A. By assuming the annealed
network approximation—a mean-field average over an en-
semble of networks [8]—the adjacency matrix A may be
replaced by its expectation value ⟨A⟩. Thus, interactions
between individuals depend on the conditional probabil-
ity P(k′|k) that a node of degree k is connected to a
node of degree k′, and on the degree distribution P(k),
which specifies the fraction of nodes with degree k, such
that the number of degree-k nodes is Nk = NP(k), with

Importantly, when demographic stochasticity is ac-
counted for, the endemic state becomes metastable [19],
and the system ultimately arrives at a disease-free state
with probability one. Here, for a large population (or net-
work) size, N ≫1, the lifetime of the metastable state
is very long, and extinction only occurs after the sys-
tem experiences a sufficiently large fluctuation [20, 25].
In order to measure the extinction risk of the popula-
tion, we define the time-dependent extinction probabil-
ity (EP), P(t), as the probability that the system has
reached extinction by time t.
The EP increases with
time and asymptotically approaches one as t →∞. No-
tably, P(t) can be related to the mean time to extinction
(MTE) τ in simple time-independent scenarios, see be-
low. While analytical expressions for P(t) in the large-
population limit exist for well-mixed populations, com-
puting the EP becomes highly complicated when degree
heterogeneity and assortativity are taken into account.
In these cases, analytical results are available only un-
der simplifying assumptions, such as near the bifurcation
point or for weakly heterogeneous networks with either
directed or undirected links [28, 30, 31, 35]. Naturally,
when the rates are explicitly time dependent, this quan-
tity can only be calculated numerically, see below.

to the original state, with the original infection rate β0.

the results of homogeneous and heterogeneous networks
to explore the lockdown impact under varying network
heterogeneity and assortativity.
It is important to note that, as either the strength
or duration of the intervention increases, the resulting
impact on the EP becomes more substantial. However,
stronger or longer interventions are also more resource-
intensive and harder to implement.
Since, increasing
heterogeneity and assortativity can markedly affect ex-
tinction dynamics, potentially shortening the disease life-
time [29–31, 35], we expect that the lockdown effective-
ness will greatly increase in realistic networks compared
to a well-mixed setting. Our goal is therefore to deter-
mine the level of resources a lockdown must allocate to
sustainably increase the EP in a realistic heterogeneous
setting. In other words, given a desired outcome of the
lockdown—i.e, increasing the extinction risk by a certain
prescribed factor—we wish to explore how the lockdown
duration and strength should vary, as a function of the
network’s heterogeneity and assortativity, and vice versa.
III.
NUMERICAL METHODOLOGY
To study the EP in heterogeneous, assortatively mixed
populations, we constructed synthetic networks to repre-
sent the desired topology and used a kinetic Monte Carlo
(KMC) method to simulate the dynamics. The topology
was determined by a prescribed degree distribution P(k)
and degree-degree correlation function based on Eq. (1).
To generate a structure of an uncorrelated (random)
network with zero assortativity, the configuration model
was used [56]. Here, each node is assigned a predefined
number of stubs based on its degree, and the stubs are
randomly paired to form links. This procedure preserves
the degree distribution while eliminating correlations be-
tween connected nodes.
To generate assortative and disassortative networks we
used the Xulvi-Brunet–Sokolov algorithm [57]. Initially,
the network topology is generated using the configura-
tion model. Then, links are iteratively rewired to adjust
the assortativity. In each step, two links involving four
distinct nodes are randomly selected, and the nodes are
sorted by degree. With probability |α| ≤1, the rewiring
process connects either two high-degree nodes to pro-
mote assortativity or a high-degree node to a low-degree
node to induce disassortativity, whereas, with probabil-
ity 1−|α|, the rewiring is done randomly. If the rewiring
step introduces links that already exist in the network,
the step is repeated to avoid link duplication. This pro-
cess allows for the generation of both assortative and dis-
assortative networks with arbitrary −1 ≤α ≤1.
Regardless of the method used to construct the net-
work, the resulting adjacency matrix can be used to com-
pute its largest eigenvalue, which yields the basic repro-
duction number R0 from Eq. (2). The reason we deter-
mine R0 is that, throughout our calculations we keep R0
constant regardless of the network topology, in order to
keep the distance to bifurcation constant and eliminate

the deterministic effects while studying the dependence
of the EP on the network and lockdown parameters.
Once R0 is computed, each node’s infection and re-
covery rates are computed based on its neighbors. Here,
each node j can be in one of two states, jS (susceptible) or
jI (infected), with transitions between the two simulated
using the Gillespie algorithm [58, 59]. The transitions,
jS →jI and jI →jS occur at exponentially distributed
waiting times. Due to the stochastic nature of these pro-
cesses, the EP may significantly vary across different runs
even for the same network realization.
To estimate the EP, we ran a total of nt simulations,
with multiple iterations performed across different net-
work realizations. In each simulation, the infection rate
was set to β0(1 −ξ) during the interval t0 ≤t ≤t0 + T,
and to β0 otherwise. The onset time t0 was chosen to
be several times the system’s relaxation time to make
sure the system entered the long-lived metastable state
prior to the initiation of the lockdown. The duration T
was selected such that t0 + T remained well below the
MTE. Each simulation was propagated until either ex-
tinction occurred or a final cutoff time tf ≫t0 + T was
reached. We recorded the number of simulations that re-
sulted in extinction, ne, and those that did not, ns, with
nt = ne+ns. The EP was then computed as Pa = ne/nt,
where we henceforth denote the post-lockdown EP by P.
That is, P effectively measures the probability of extinc-
tion in the immediate aftermath of the lockdown.
IV.
RESULTS
Below, we apply our numerical method to explore
networks with homogeneous, bimodal, and gamma de-
gree distributions across a range of parameters: T, ξ,
ϵ, and α. These different network types allow us to ex-
amine distinct topological scenarios: homogeneous net-
works represent cases where topology plays no role; bi-
modal networks capture symmetric degree distributions;
and gamma networks capture more realistic degree het-
erogeneity observed in real-world systems [60, 61]. No-
tably, although the bimodal network is a simplified, toy
model, it provides qualitatively similar results to other,
more complex, networks with a symmetric degree dis-
tribution [30].
Therefore, we will henceforth use the
bimodal network as a prototypical symmetric network,
while the gamma network will be used as a prototypical
model for asymmetric, skewed distributions [32].
As a first step, we examined how the EP depends on
the lockdown parameters T and ξ in homogeneous versus
heterogeneous settings. Figure 1 shows the results of P
for random networks with homogeneous, bimodal, and
gamma degree distributions. We also include a compari-
son with the analytical results of the homogeneous case,
which hold as long as | ln P| ≫1, see Appendix. Panels
(a) and (b) demonstrate that increasing the duration or
strength of the lockdown leads to a higher EP. Yet, in ho-
mogeneous networks, P remains significantly lower than
in heterogeneous ones. This result is encouraging, as it
suggests that realistic heterogeneous networks are more
susceptible to lockdown measures; that is, disease can be
much more easily eradicated in heterogeneous settings.

Apart from the difference between homogeneous and
heterogeneous settings, Fig. 1 also shows that different
networks (bimodal and gamma) yield an almost identi-
cal EP, as long as ϵ is identical. This implies that network
heterogeneity, rather than the specific form of degree dis-
tribution, is the key factor in determining the EP.
This attribute is further illustrated in panel (a) of
Fig 2, where P is plotted as a function of ϵ for both
bimodal and gamma random networks. In both cases,
P increases with heterogeneity, and as long as ϵ < 0.5
the curves remain close.
However, as ϵ is further in-
creased, the EP plateaus for bimodal networks, whereas
for gamma networks, P continues to rise sharply. The
discrepancy at large ϵ arises because in symmetric net-
works, ϵ is bound by 1 as the standard deviation can be
at most the size of the mean. For bimodal networks, as ϵ
approaches 1, the network becomes effectively split: half
the nodes have degree 2k0 −1 degree, while the other
half have a degree of 1. We have checked (not shown)
that the qualitative dependence of the EP on the sys-
tem size N for heterogeneous networks is similar to the
homogeneous case: P ∼e−N∆S(ϵ,α,T,ξ). Here, ∆S(. . . )
is the action barrier, which depends on the network het-
erogeneity, assortativity, lockdown duration and strength
(see Appendix for details). Therefore, as ϵ approaches 1
and the bimodal network becomes practically split, the
effective system size becomes N/2, and the EP should
approach the square root of the value at ϵ = 0, exactly
as seen in Fig. 2(a). In contrast, in asymmetric networks,
such as the gamma distribution, ϵ is not bounded. Here,
for ϵ ≳1, a few high-degree hubs dominate transmis-
sion. These hubs, while efficient in spreading the disease,
also make the network more susceptible to extinction via
large fluctuations [32, 35]. Therefore, as ϵ grows, the EP
continues to grow without saturating.

the lockdown becomes more effective as either ϵ or α

Since real-world networks are rarely random, and of-
ten exhibit either assortative or disassortative mixing,
we wanted to check whether the results in Fig. 3 can be
generalized to realistic networks with positive or nega-
tive degree-degree correlations. As shown in Fig. 2, even
networks with the same level of heterogeneity can dis-
play different EPs due to their assortative structure. To
study this effect, we adopted the same approach as in
the heterogeneous case, and generated multiple gamma-
distributed networks with varying assortativity, mapping
the state space, and identifying all combinations of lock-
down parameters and α values that yield the same EP.
In Fig. 4(a), we show results of equal-EP contour lines
versus ξ and α for fixed ϵ and T, whereas in Fig. 4(b) we
show similar results as function of T and α while keeping
ϵ and ξ fixed. These results indicate that as assortativ-
ity increases, the values of T and ξ required to achieve
a fixed EP decrease. This demonstrates that, much like
heterogeneity, assortativity enhances the likelihood of ex-
tinction under intervention. This similarity is consistent
with previous studies showing a correspondence between
assortativity and heterogeneity in static networks [35].
The results in both Figs. 3 and 4 are encouraging, as
they suggest that in structured, realistic environments,
the lockdown measures needed to eliminate an outbreak

DISCUSSION
We have studied the impact of abrupt environmen-
tal changes on the long-lived endemic state within the
SIS model of epidemics.
Such a temporary environ-
mental switch may dramatically expedite disease extinc-
tion, even though the perturbed state’s duration is short.
While previous works have addressed such effects in peri-
odically varying environments or in deterministic settings
[11, 12, 14, 15, 17, 48], the effect of a temporary lockdown
has not been explored in the context of a lingering disease
in realistic heterogeneous topologies.
In this work we have systematically investigated the
role of a temporary lockdown on the extinction risk of a
long-lived disease, for populations residing on heteroge-
neous and (dis)assortative networks. The lockdown was
modeled as a sharp decrease in the infection rate, with
a given magnitude and for a prescribed duration. The
extinction probability (EP) was measured as the fraction
of realizations that got extinct in the aftermath of the
lockdown out of the total number of realizations.
First, we showed that the likelihood of extinction grows
as either the duration or magnitude of the lockdown
increase, both on homogeneous and heterogeneous net-
works. Next, we showed that for a given lockdown, the
EP can be greatly enhanced by either increasing the net-
work’s heterogeneity, or by increasing its assortativity.
Enhancing the network’s heterogeneity gives rise to an in-
creasing number of hubs, which upon recovery, lower the
reproductive number significantly and thereby, expedite
disease clearance and increase the EP. A similar effect
occurs upon increasing the network’s assortativity, giv-
ing rise to high-degree network cliques, which also greatly
expedite disease clearance. The key consequence of these
results is that, as degree heterogeneity or assortativity
increases, milder lockdowns—with shorter durations or
weaker magnitude—suffice to achieve a desired EP.
We then computed the equal-EP contour lines as func-
tion of the lockdown and network parameters. This al-
lowed us to study the interplay between lockdown magni-
tude and duration, and extent of network heterogeneity
and assortativity. Notably, we showed that in structured,
realistic environments, the lockdown measures required
to eradicate a disease may be far less demanding than
predicted by simpler models. This means that such in-
terventions are much more effective, which may revolu-
tionize health policies in realistic scenarios.
While we focused on lockdowns that affect the gen-
eral population, targeted interventions—where only spe-
cific individuals, such as high-degree nodes, experience a
sharp drop in their infection rate—present an interest-
ing direction for future research [62]. Another promising
avenue is the study of repeated lockdowns in such net-
works, which may influence disease dynamics differently
from a single prolonged one [47].
Lastly, although we
modeled lockdowns as abrupt reductions in the infection
rate, other modeling approaches may be worth exploring;
e.g., scenarios where the infection rate changes gradually
or follows a non-symmetric profile, or when the network
topology itself undergoes sudden structural changes.
