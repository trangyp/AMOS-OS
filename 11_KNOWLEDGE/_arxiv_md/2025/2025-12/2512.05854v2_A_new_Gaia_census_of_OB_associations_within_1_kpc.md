ABSTRACT
OB associations are primordial tracers of star formation and Galactic structure. Originally defined about 80 years ago, their
historical membership lists have been superseded thanks to the precise astrometry from ESA’s Gaia’s satellite. Recent studies have
however been mostly focused on individual OB associations or limited by the coverage of spectroscopic surveys. In this paper,
we exploit a complete census of ∼25,000 O- and B-type stars within 1 kpc of the Sun to produce a highly-reliable catalogue of 56
OB associations using the HDBSCAN clustering algorithm, increasing the number of known OB associations by a factor of two
within this volume. We assess the validity of this catalogue by crossmatching our OB association members with other catalogues
of OB associations, star clusters and young stellar groups, confirming the high-confidence of our census of OB associations.
We characterize these OB associations physically (total initial stellar mass, number of OB stars, ...) and kinematically (velocity
dispersion, linear expansion ages, ...). The majority of the OB associations (38 out of 56) exhibit a significant expansion pattern
in at least one direction, including 12 in both plane-of-the-sky directions, though differences in expansion velocity suggest
anisotropical expansion patterns. We compare the locations of these OB associations with superclouds and features in the local
Milky Way such as the Radcliffe Wave and discuss the implications for star formation in the solar neighbourhood.

solar neighbourhood - Galaxy: structure
1 INTRODUCTION
O- and B-type stars are the most massive and hottest stars according to
the Harvard spectral classification (Payne 1925). It is common to refer
to massive stars as OB stars, with a metallicity-dependent minimum
threshold of ≳8–12 M⊙(e.g., Jones et al. 2013; Ibeling & Heger
2013). These stars bring consequential amounts of feedback into the
interstellar medium (ISM, e.g. Hopkins et al. 2014; Krumholz 2014;
Dale 2015): in particular, the core-collapse supernova explosions
following their deaths enrich the ISM with heavy elements that are
used to create new stellar and planetary systems (e.g., de Rossi et al.
2010). Furthermore, due to their short life, OB stars constitute useful
tracers of star formation (e.g., Crowther 2012), as well as of the
location of the Milky Way spiral arms (e.g., Sparke & Gallagher
2000). As such, the distribution of OB stars is often correlated with
other tracers of Galactic structure such as GMCs, Hii regions and
interstellar dust (e.g., Chen et al. 2019; Zari et al. 2021; Vergely et al.
2022; Pantaleoni González et al. 2025).
OB stars are also the most prominent members of OB associations
(e.g., Morgan et al. 1953; Humphreys 1978; McKee & Williams
1997). Originally defined by Ambartsumian (1947) as low-density

within a few tens of Myrs, and therefore serve as key probes of early
stellar evolution as a transitional phase between star-forming regions
and the Galactic field population of stars (Brown et al. 1997; Wright

easier to determine than individual stars (e.g., Quintana & Wright

(i.e, pre-Gaia) catalogues of OB associations (e.g., Humphreys 1978;
Garmany & Stencel 1992) have been defined only by the photometry
or spectroscopy of their brightest members, and have been found to
lack the kinematic coherence expected for young co-moving groups
of stars (e.g., Wright 2020; Quintana & Wright 2021; Quintana et al.
2023), although some progress was enabled with HIPPARCOS as-
trometry (e.g., de Zeeuw et al. 1999; Bouy & Alves 2015).
Gaia data (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016), and particularly its last
data release DR3 (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023), which contains
∼1.5 billion sources with astrometry, offers an opportunity to create a
new census of OB associations that are more kinematically-coherent
than their historical counterparts, and therefore more likely to be real.
Gaia data has so far been mostly used to perform studies on individ-
ual OB associations (e.g., Wright & Mamajek 2018; Cantat-Gaudin

process to identify kinematically-coherent groups from our sample
of OB stars, and how we filter them to obtain a highly-reliable census
of OB associations within 1 kpc. In Section 3 we analyse physically
and kinematically these OB associations, and notably study their ex-
pansion. In Section 2 we compare their memberships with individual
and general OB associations alongside star clusters and young stellar
groups. In Section 5 we compare them with nearby superclouds such
as the Radcliffe Wave and the Split and discuss the implications for
star formation in the local Milky Way. Finally, we conclude in Section
6.
2 IDENTIFICATION OF OB ASSOCIATIONS WITHIN 1
KPC
In this section, we detail how we identify a reliable list of OB as-
sociations within 1 kpc. This first requires us to apply a clustering
algorithm to our catalogue to identify groups (Section 2.1) with a
careful selection of inputs (justified in Appendix A). We use simula-
tions of the contamination rate of our sample (Appendix B1) and its
completeness (Appendix B2) to perform a final rejection of groups
too extended in positional or velocity space to be genuine OB as-
sociations (Section 2.2), before visualizing them and establishing
nomenclature (Section 2.3).
2.1 Initial clustering with HDBSCAN
HDBSCAN (McInnes et al. 2017) is a suitable clustering algorithm to
identify new OB associations, specifically applied to Gaia data (e.g.,
Santos-Silva et al. 2021; Chemel et al. 2022; Quintana et al. 2023).
Its ability to find groups at various densities gives it a significant
flexibility over other similar algorithms such as DBSCAN (Ester
et al. 1996), though this can be at the cost of a high false positive
rate, that requires users to apply careful filtering criteria.

2.2 Final selection of groups
We have reduced the number of candidate OB associations initially
identified in Section 2.1 from 102 to 62 based on a two-step pro-
cess. Firstly, we have estimated a probability for each group through
bootstrapping and chosen the highly-reliable threshold based on a
simulation of false positive groups that could be potentially detected
from a randomized distribution of OB stars. Secondly, we have esti-
mated our incompleteness by calculating the probability to recover
a test association placed on a randomized field of OB stars. In do-
ing so we added back the small, nearby groups with a low observed
probability back into our list of candidate OB associations. The full
details of these simulations are provided in Appendix B.
Our final step was to discard any groups with positional or velocity
dispersions too high to be genuine OB associations. To help select
the threshold for this, we turned to the catalogue of OB associations
from de Zeeuw et al. (1999), which includes 12 nearby OB associ-
ations identified using HIPPARCOS astrometry. We looked into the
equivalent OB associations in our sample (Sco-Cen, Per OB2, Per
OB3, Vela OB2, Lac OB1...) and calculated their intrinsic disper-
sions, which they typically range 10–50 pc in position and 1–4 km
s−1. Nevertheless, contrasting with the list of Auriga OB associa-
tions identified with Gaia DR3 from Quintana et al. (2023), the new
Aur OB1 and Aur OB2 are characterized by intrinsic positional dis-
persions in the range 50–100 pc in some dimensions, implying that
some OB associations can retain some kinematic coherence despite
being more extended in positional space. Based on these values, we
have compromised between the liberal and conservative thresholds
adopted in Appendix A choose a threshold on 1D positional (𝑋𝑌𝑍)
dispersion of 100 pc and on 1D intrinsic (𝑉𝑙,𝑏) dispersion of 4 km
s−1.

our 56 OB associations. The parameters we have derived for each of
them are presented in a table published electronically and available
at the Vizier archive, whose column names and descriptions are
presented in Table 1, and illustrated with Ori OB1b as an example.
The distributions of the parameters described in Table 1 are also

3.1 Physical properties of the new OB associations
3.1.1 Number of O- and B-type stars
We have followed a similar method to Quintana & Wright (2021)
and Quintana et al. (2023) to estimate the total number of O- and
B-type stars in each OB association. B-type stars are defined as
those with 4 < log(𝑇eff) < 4.3, whereas O-type stars as those with
log(𝑇eff) > 4.3 (using the values estimated from the SED fitting
process in Q25), akin to the approach from Martínez García et al.
(submitted). Because of the high multiplicity of O- and B-ype stars
(Duchêne & Kraus 2013), and because the SED fitter applied in Q25
does not resolve binarity, we will refer to these quantities as "number
of systems containing O/B-type stars" afterwards.
However, compared with Quintana & Wright (2021) and Quintana
et al. (2023), the effective temperature threshold we chose for our OB
stars in Q25 (i.e., Teff > 10,000 K) means that we are not including
cooler evolved massive stars in our census of OB association mem-
bers. Therefore, to account for them, we use the SED-fitted mass
of each OB association member to interpolate the stellar effective
temperature as a function of their age from the stellar evolutionary
models from Ekström et al. (2012). This allows us to estimate which
fraction of their lifetime a star of a given initial mass spends with
log(𝑇eff) < 4: thus, each time we add a star to the total number,
we divided by this fraction to account for high-mass non-OB stars
nomenclature was never used again, and only Gem OB2 was more recently
confirmed as an actual OB association (Khalatyan et al. 2024).

3.1.2 Total initial stellar mass
Our estimation of the total initial stellar mass for each OB association
is based on the method used in Section 4.1 from Q25 to derive
the star formation rates from our census of OB stars. We used the
number of OB stars estimated per OB association in Section 3.1.1,
and generated the full stellar population within the group using the
initial mass function from Maschberger (2013), with masses sampled
between between 0.01 and 100 M⊙and with a high-mass exponent
of 𝛼= 2.3. To account for unresolved binarity, we determined if each
star belonged to a binary system randomly with the mass-dependent
binary fractions from Duchêne & Kraus (2013). Then, for binary
stars, we have generated a random mass ratio 𝑞= 𝑀2/𝑀1, (assuming
a flat distribution between 0 and 1) to estimate the mass of the
secondary star, neglecting binary interaction. The total stellar initial
mass for each OB association corresponds to the sum of the individual
stellar masses for either the single stars or both components of the
binary systems, as the median value of a process repeated 1000
times, to account for the fluctuations of the IMF, and the randomness
in drawing the values of the binary probability and fraction. The
errors are drawn from a MC simulation with 1000 iterations, using
the uncertainties on the estimated number of OB stars.
The median total initial stellar mass of all our associations is ∼2200
M⊙, consistent with the typical mass of OB associations (Wright
2020). For specific OB associations, Wright (2020) placed the value
for Ori OB1 in the range 4000–13,000 M⊙, also using the IMF from
Maschberger (2013), whilst Blaauw (1964) and Briceño et al. (2019)
stated it should be larger than 8000 M⊙. If we sum the 𝑀tot from
our Ori OB1a and Ori OB1b, we obtain a total initial stellar mass of
∼9000 M⊙, consistent with past estimates. Meanwhile, estimations
of the total initial stellar mass of Vela OB2 vary, between ∼1300
M⊙(Armstrong et al. 2018) and 2330 M⊙(Cantat-Gaudin et al.
2019a), while our estimate is equal to∼2360 M⊙and therefore at the
upper limits of this interval. Wright & Mamajek (2018) estimated a
𝑀tot of ∼4000 M⊙from the individual estimates of Mamajek et al.
(2002) and Preibisch & Mamajek (2008) for each of the historical
Sco-Cen subgroups, slightly smaller than our value of ∼5000 M⊙for
Sco-Cen 1 and Sco-Cen 2 combined. Finally, as expected because
of our coverage limit, the total initial stellar mass from Aur OB1
(∼2800 M⊙) is lower than in Quintana et al. (2023) where it was
estimated to be ∼6000 M⊙, which was also likely under-estimated as
the derivation method did not account for unresolved binarity.

3.1.3 Maximum isochronal ages
While deriving an isochronal age for our OB associations can be non-
trivial, given that our membership only covers the upper-left part of
the HR diagram, it is possible to estimate an upper age limit, based
on the most massive members of each association. To that end, we
have interpolated the hottest star from each OB association with the
rotating stellar evolutionary models from Ekström et al. (2012), as
they correspond to the members with the shortest lifetime, allowing
us to calculate a maximum age for each OB association. We however
stress that these should be considered as approximate values, as the
evolutionary models from Ekström et al. (2012) are valid for single
stars and neglect binary interaction.
There are some instances where the maximum age is roughly
consistent with literature values, such as Sco-Cen 1 with an age
of ∼12 Myr (compared with 3–19 Myr in Miret-Roig et al. 2022;
Ratzenböck et al. 2023; Posch et al. 2025) , Ori OB1b with an age
of ∼8 Myr (compared with 1–12 Myr in Bally 2008; Briceño et al.
2019; Sánchez-Sanjuán et al. 2024), Tr. 10b with an age of ∼42 Myr
(compared with 45–50 Myr in Cantat-Gaudin et al. 2019a; Kerr et al.
2023), Per OB2 with ∼10 Myr (compared with 2–7 Myr in Bally
2008; Kounkel et al. 2022), and Cyg OB7 aged ∼11 Myr (compared
with ∼13 Myr in Uyanıker et al. 2001).
In many cases, however, the maximum ages are either slightly over-
estimated, such as Cep OB2 aged 21–24 Myr (compared with 3–16
Myr in Szilágyi et al. 2023) and Cep OB3 aged ∼13 Myr (compared
with ∼3 Myr in Kerr et al. 2023), or even strongly overestimated as
in the cases of Sco-Cen 2 aged ∼50 Myr, and Per OB3 aged ∼131
Myr (compared with ∼58 Myr in Kerr et al. 2023).
Some of these age disagreements, particularly for Vela OB2, could
be due to temporal substructures in that association. Indeed, notewor-
thy is this association which has the lowest maximum age of ∼7 Myr,
younger than the commonly-accepted age of the Vela OB2 complex.
This could be because it includes 𝛾2 Vel, a famous Wolf-Rayet binary
system, part of the 𝛾Velorum cluster and thought to have formed
after the bulk of the lower-mass stars in Vela OB2 (Jeffries et al.
2009).
3.2 Kinematic properties of the new OB associations
3.2.1 Positional and velocity dispersion
We have calculated both the positional and velocity dispersions of
the OB associations. The median dispersion in 𝑋(29.0 pc) and in
𝑌(30.1 pc) are similar, whilst the median dispersion in 𝑍is smaller
(15.8 pc) because of the restoring effect of the Galactic disk reducing
expansion in that direction. Likewise, OB associations typically share
a similar extent in velocities in both directions (2.0 and 1.7 km s−1,
respectively): these are significantly smaller than the value of 4.5 km
s−1 derived for the 28 OB associations in Melnik & Dambis (2020),
but their study relied on historical memberships of OB associations.
Our 1D velocity dispersions in the Sco-Cen associations are in the
range 1.0–1.5 km s−1, which is compatible with the average values
of 1.86 ± 0.21 km s−1, 1.38 ± 0.21 km s−1 and 1.21 ± 0.28 km s−1
calculated by Wright & Mamajek (2018) for Upper Scorpius (US),
Upper Centaurus Lupus (UCL) and LCC, respectively. Estimates on
the 1D velocity dispersions of Ori OB1 are typically within the range
1.5–3 km s−1 depending on the region studied (e.g., van Altena et al.
1988; Fűrész et al. 2008; Da Rio et al. 2017), again compatible with
our results (respectively 1.0–1.6 km s−1 for Ori OB1a and 2.0–2.5
km s−1 for Ori OB1b). Likewise, Jeffries et al. (2014) calculated a RV
dispersion of 1.60±0.37 km s−1 for the surrounding population of 𝛾
Vel, which encompasses part of Vela OB2, and is consistent with our
results for Vela OB2 (1.9–2.0 km s−1 ). Finally, the radial velocity
dispersion of ∼3.9 km s−1 estimated by Steenbrugge et al. (2003)
for the early-type stars of Per OB2 is significantly higher than our
velocity dispersions (1.7–2.7 km s−1) for this association, but Wright
(2020) pointed out this value was likely overestimated as they did not
account for unresolved binary motions in their calculations.
3.2.2 Kinematic ages
OB associations typically dissolve after a few tens of Myrs (Am-
bartsumian 1949), and recent investigations with Gaia-based mem-
berships have revealed significant expansion signatures within
them (e.g., Cantat-Gaudin et al. 2019a; Quintana & Wright 2021;
Großschedl et al. 2025). Conversely, Ward & Kruijssen (2018) did
not detect any expansion in their 18 studied OB associations, whereas
Melnik & Dambis (2020) only succeeded to do so in 6 out of 28.
In both cases, however, their analysis relied upon historical (pre-
Gaia) censuses crossmatched with Gaia data, while we identified
members directly from Gaia data. Our new census contains signif-
icantly more OB associations than Ward & Kruijssen (2018) and
Melnik & Dambis (2020), and therefore represents an opportunity
for a more statistically-significant analysis of expansion signatures in
these groups.
Here we apply a linear fit between the Galactic coordinates of
the OB association members and their proper motions in Galactic
coordinates (corrected for the LSR as in Section 2.1, and also cor-
rected for the projection effects, referred to as "virtual expansion",
as detailed in Appendix C), along each axis, using a weighted least
squares linear regression model,. In doing so we estimate velocity
gradients in the 𝑙and 𝑏directions, that we convert from units of mas
yr−1 deg−1 to units of km s−1 pc−1. If the OB association exhibits
a significant expansion pattern (defined as having a gradient 𝜅>
0.01 km s−1 pc−1), we invert the value to obtain a kinematic age. An
example of this linear fit is displayed in Fig. 5 for Ori OB1b, which
exhibits a significant expansion pattern along the Galactic longitude
direction.
We find that 38 out of our 56 OB associations, hence the majority
of our catalogue (∼68%), exhibit a significant (> 1𝜎) expansion
pattern in at least one direction, to be compared with ∼94 % of the
18 stellar groups in Wright et al. (2024) (that includes star clusters
and OB associations) expanding in at least one direction.
Only 12 of our OB associations exhibit expansion patterns in both
directions (see right panel from Fig. 4), but kinematic ages are only
consistent for Cep OB6 (10.7 ± 3.3 Myr in 𝑙and 17.5 ± 7.6 Myr in
𝑏), Cam OB2a (63.9 ± 27.2 Myr in 𝑙and 34.1 ± 17.3 Myr in 𝑏),
Mon OB5 (37.9 ± 13.0 Myr in 𝑙and 41.8 ± 17.6 Myr in 𝑏) and Cep
OB2a (22.8 ± 10.8 Myr in 𝑙and 15.5 ± 3.7 Myr in 𝑏). For other OB
associations, they are either inconsistent (e.g. 49.4 ± 15.0 Myr in 𝑙
and 13.5 ± 3.6 Myr for Aur OB1), or not significant enough because
of the large error bars in one direction (e.g. 14.6 ± 1.7 Myr in 𝑙and
53.2 ± 33.1 Myr in 𝑏for Ori OB1b and 25.3 ± 7.4 Myr in 𝑙and 46.5
± 35.2 Myr in 𝑏for Sco OB3). Whether these differences can be
attributed to large measurement uncertainties or actual asymmetrical
expansion patterns warrant a more accurate method relying on 3D
kinematics and accounting for the effects of Galactic potential in the
association’s expansion, as in Quintana & Wright (2022).

Myr in Miret-Roig et al. 2022; Ratzenböck et al. 2023; Posch et al.

Briceño et al. 2019; Sánchez-Sanjuán et al. 2024) and Per OB2 (4–8
Myr compared with 2–7 Myr in Bally 2008; Kounkel et al. 2022),
many OB associations have wide age intervals, some of which older
than 100 Myr even though OB associations are thought to dissolve
after a few tens of Myrs (Wright 2020).
5 GALACTIC STRUCTURE
OB associations are important for our understanding of the star for-
mation process (e.g., Wright et al. 2023), and thereby can be served
to trace the position and motion of the spiral arms (e.g., Quintana
et al. 2023). Nevertheless, our current census is limited to the nearest
1 kpc, closer than the known locations of the Perseus and Carina-
Sagittarius arms (e.g., Reid et al. 2019). Recent studies have con-
firmed the potential of using overdensities of O- and B-type stars

star formation in the solar neighborhood, which can themselves be

Simulations suggest the existence of wave-like corrugations in disc
galaxies (e.g., Widrow et al. 2012; Carlin et al. 2013; Williams et al.
2013; Tepper-García et al. 2022). In the Milky Way, they have been
linked to the passage of the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy (e.g., Purcell
et al. 2011; Gómez et al. 2013; Laporte et al. 2018; Antoja et al.
2018; Laporte et al. 2019; Bland-Hawthorn & Tepper-García 2021).
Using Auriga cosmological simulations, Gómez et al. (2017) showed
that these corrugations get imprinted both in the gas and in the stars,
including young stellar populations, which was later corroborated by
Tepper-García et al. (2022).
It is therefore not surprising that such vertical corrugations have
been related to the Radcliffe Wave and its stellar counterpart such as
the Cepheus Spur (Pantaleoni González et al. 2021), and confirmed
with the discovery of a distant corrugation in the Milky Way disk
referred to as ‘The great wave’ (Poggio et al. 2025). In this con-
text, structures similar to the Radcliffe Wave serve as tracers of star
formation in the local Milky Way.
Kormann et al. (2026) exploited the 3D extinction map from Eden-
hofer et al. (2024) to identify elongated, parallel structures in the local
Milky Way referred to as superclouds, a nomenclature originally pro-
posed by Elmegreen & Elmegreen (1983) to designate the regular
distribution of star-forming complexes and their associated neutral
atomic hydrogen (HI) in external galaxies, and subsequently observed
in the inner Milky Way (Dame et al. 1986; Elmegreen & Elmegreen
1987). In addition to the previously-known Radcliffe Wave (Alves
et al. 2020) and Split (Lallement et al. 2019), Kormann et al. (2026)
discovered five new superclouds, labeled as the Sagittarius Spur Ex-
tension (SSE), Malpolon Cloud, Vela Ridge Cloud, Natrix Cloud and
Anguis Cloud, with all but the last one located within

• Vela OB2 and Trumpler 10: Both OB associations form the

this will allow us to determine the mechanisms in play (i.e., whether
OB associations decoupled from the natal gas moved away from them
or blew away their surrounding gas). Only a full kinematical analysis,
akin to the approach from Konietzka et al. (2024) for the Radcliffe

some OB associations and the superclouds.
5.1 The Radcliffe Wave
The Radcliffe Wave is a 3D structure initially identified in Alves et al.
(2020) from the molecular cloud census of Zucker et al. (2020). It
is shaped as a coherent alignment of dense gas that extends from
Canis Major to Cygnus-X and intercepts several major star-forming
regions such as Orion. Since its discovery, the Radcliffe Wave has
been traced by young stars (Tu et al. 2022; Kerr et al. 2023) and OCs
(Konietzka et al. 2024; Bobylev et al. 2025), it is therefore natural to
explore whether our OB associations act as tracers of the Radcliffe
Wave as well.
In Fig. 7, we have plotted the OB associations from Fig. 6 that
are close to the Radcliffe Wave across the X-Y plane, here projected
on the Z/Y’ plane. This picture highlights the necessity of the 3D
view as some OB associations close to the Radcliffe Wave in Fig.

Cep OB7 and Lac OB1, which, in the latter case, is consistent with
the findings of Konietzka et al. (2024). Some spatial correlations
between our OB associations and the Radcliffe Wave are corrborated
here: CMa OB4, Ori OB1a, Ori OB1b, Per OB2, Per OB3, Cep OB6,
Her OB1a, Cyg OB10c, Cep OB8 and Cyg OB7. Some of these
correlations were expected as the Canis Major, Orion, Perseus and
Cepheus massive star-forming complexes are known too be part of
the Radcliffe Wave. This is consistent with OB associations whose
different age estimators from Section 3 and Section 4 confirm their
youth, such as Ori OB1b and Cyg OB7, but other OB associations
will require more accurate age determinations and kinematics to fully
connect them to the Radcliffe Wave.

6 CONCLUSIONS
In this paper we have exploited a complete census of ∼25,000 O-
and B-type stars within 1 kpc, to which we have applied a clustering
algorithm to their 3D positions and 2D velocities. In doing so we
have identified 56 highly-confident OB associations, doubling the
census in this volume compared with the known number of 28 OB
associations within 1 kpc from Wright (2020).
We have characterized these OB associations physically and kine-
matically, and estimated linear velocity gradients compatible with
the majority of them (∼68 %) expanding in at least one direction.
We contrasted our OB association members with other catalogues
of OB associations, young stellar groups and star clusters, as well
as with the superclouds of the local Milky Way, in order to trace
star formation in the solar neighborhood. Because our catalogue of
OB associations match well with existing features, we argue that it is
highly-reliable and supersedes previously-made general catalogues
of OB associations within 1 kpc.
The present study was focused on the current location of the OB
associations. Because we have only identified their upper-mass mem-
bers, the lack of reliable RVs prevented us from analysing their full 3D
kinematics and to estimate reliable isochronal ages. The upcoming
releases of the William Herschel Telescope Enhanced Area Velocity
Explorer (WEAVE, Jin et al. 2024) and the 4-metre Multi-Object
Spectroscopic Telescope (4MOST, de Jong et al. 2019), will allow
us to obtain more reliable RVs for the hottest members of these
OB associations. Complementary to this, we intend to extend the
membership to lower-mass stars, which will enable a more precise
estimation of their isochronal and kinematic ages. In the future we
intend to perform a full 3D orbital analysis of the OB associations
to trace their place of birth and to fully link them to surrounding
features, unveiling a full picture of the star formation history of the
local Milky Way. An accurate catalogue of OB associations with re-
liable isochronal and kinematic ages will be statistically-significant
enough to study the timescale between the formation of stars and the
assembly of stellar groups (e.g. Miret-Roig et al. 2024).
As we are also extending the census of OB stars up to a distance
of 2 kpc (Quintana et al., in prep.), we aim at expanding the census
of OB associations as well, using them as tracers of the position and
motions of the Galactic spiral arms.

APPENDIX A: CHOICE OF PARAMETERS FOR THE
CLUSTERING ALGORITHM
As described in Section 2.1, the HDBSCAN outputs depend critically
on the adopted values of its inputs: in this section, we detail how we
explored the parameter space to find the best configuration that leads
to an identification of stellar groups physically consistent with being
OB associations.
Firstly, the parameter cluster_selection_method could either
be ‘excess of mass (EOM)’ or ‘leaf’. Here we favour the second
method that finds smaller and more homogeneous groups, and has
therefore been consistently used to search for OB associations (e.g.
Santos-Silva et al. 2021; Chemel et al. 2022; Quintana et al. 2023).
Secondly, min_samples is the parameter used to compute the dis-
tance between a point and its nearest neighbors (McInnes et al. 2017).
In a past work (Quintana et al. 2023), we adopted min_samples =
10, as this resulted in groups with a stellar density consistent with OB
associations, we thus use the same value here as our overall density
of O- and B-type stars selected from our SED fitter in Q25 will be
similar.
The choice for the other parameters was less straightforward.
min_cluster_size corresponds to the minimum number of OB
stars (in our case) within an OB association. In Quintana et al.
(2023), we chose a value of 15, following the typical number of
OB stars found in historical OB associations (Humphreys 1978), and
below which there is an increasing risk of noise. However, systems
with fewer OB stars could be T associations (whose name originates
from the T-Tauri stars that are their most prominent members). For
this study, we follow the definition from Wright (2020) and consider
OB associations and T associations to be distinct entities, thereby
adopting a minimum value of 15 OB stars for min_cluster_size.
cluster_selection_epsilon, on the other hand, is defined as
a distance threshold (Malzer & Baum 2019), which allows small
groups with similar properties to be connected and thus merged (as
in e.g. Kerr et al. 2021, 2023).
We have then have varied min_cluster_size between 15 and 30
(with a step size of 5), cluster_selection_epsilon between 20
and 40 (with a step size of 10) and the normalization factor 𝑐between
4 and 6 pc km−1 s−1. This choice of parameter spaces ensured to
obtain groups physically corresponding to OB associations, and led
us to a total of 36 different configurations to explore.
On the one hand, the effects of increasing min_cluster_size
was straightforward, as it resulted in the disappearance of smaller
groups, some of which corresponded to well-known OB associations
such as Sco-Cen, Per OB3 and Cep OB6. We thus rapidly concluded
that a lower value of min_cluster_size increased our complete-
ness whilst still allowing us to identify larger OB associations such as
Ori OB1, a similar trend as in Quintana et al. (2023). Consequently,
we have adopted a value of min_cluster_size of 15.
Likewise, whilst varying cluster_selection_epsilon, we
quickly noticed that it led to the merging of smaller groups and
resulted in groups too extended in positional space to correspond to
OB associations, encouraging us to adopt a smaller value for this
parameter.
Nevertheless, varying the normalization factor 𝑐led to more sub-
tle effects, so to find the best configuration, we carried out a MC
simulation where we randomly sampled the Galactic Cartesian co-
ordinates and the transverse velocities of the individual members of
the groups within their uncertainties. At each iteration, we recorded
the dispersion (defined as one standard deviation) in each of their 5D
parameters (𝑋, 𝑌, 𝑍, 𝑉𝑙, 𝑉𝑏), after which we subtracted this observed
dispersion by the median uncertainties of the group to obtain an in-
trinsic dispersion. We repeated this process 1000 times to obtain a
median dispersion for each parameter and for each group7.
OB associations are kinematically-coherent stellar groups (no
more than a few km s−1 in velocity dispersion) and that typically
extend over a few tens of pc (Wright 2020; Wright et al. 2023). These
properties governed our choices of HDBSCAN inputs. The low den-
sity of OB stars prevents us from identifying groups compact enough
to identify star clusters, therefore the risk is to identify too many
groups that are too extended (in positional or velocity space) to be
OB associations.
To verify this, for each configuration, we have calculated the per-
centage of identified groups with an intrinsic positional dispersion
above 50 pc or an intrinsic velocity dispersion above 4 km s−1 in
at least one dimension (the conservative false positive rate), as well
as the percentage of groups with a positional dispersion above 100
pc or an intrinsic velocity dispersion above 5 km s−1 in at least one
dimension (the liberal false positive rate). We ultimately chose the
configuration that gave us both the smallest liberal and conserva-
tive false positive rates, corresponding to min_cluster_size = 15,
cluster_selection_epsilon = 30 and 𝑐= 6 km s−1).

B1 Quantifying the reliability of each group
We estimate the probability of each group following the method of
Quintana et al. (2023). We perform an MC simulation and at each
iteration of the MC simulation, we randomize the 5D parameters
within their uncertainties and applied HDBSCAN again toall stars in
our sample. We consider the resulting groups (which can therefore
have a different membership and number of stars compared with the
original groups) to match the original groups if all their median 5D
parameters are located within 1𝜎of the median 5D parameters of the

each of the individual group members are found in each iteration of
the MC simulation.

ability of a group is thus defined as the number of times an original
group is recovered, divided by the number of iterations, and the mem-
bership probability of an individual star is calculated as the fraction
of times it appeared in a matching group.
