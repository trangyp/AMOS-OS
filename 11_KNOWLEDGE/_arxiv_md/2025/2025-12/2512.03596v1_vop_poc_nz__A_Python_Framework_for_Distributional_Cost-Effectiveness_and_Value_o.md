Abstract
Health economic evaluations are sensitive to the choice of analytical perspective (e.g., health system
vs. societal). While guidelines often recommend specific perspectives, the uncertainty associated with this
choice – and the potential decision discordance it creates – is rarely quantified. We present vop poc nz, a
Python package that implements a framework for Distributional Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (DCEA)
and operationalizes the quantification of perspective uncertainty through the Value of Perspective (VoP)
metric. The package provides tools for Markov modeling, probabilistic sensitivity analysis, value of
information analysis, and equity impact assessment. Unlike existing tools that treat perspective as a
fixed input, vop poc nz allows for the simultaneous evaluation of multiple perspectives. This enables
decision-makers to estimate the opportunity cost of perspective misalignment. We demonstrate the
package’s capabilities using case studies from Aotearoa New Zealand.
Keywords: health economics, cost-effectiveness analysis, distributive cost-effectiveness analysis, economic
evaluation, budget impact analysis, value of information, python, value of perspective

Introduction
Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is a cornerstone of health technology assessment, guiding resource allocation
decisions globally (Drummond et al., 2015). A fundamental methodological choice in CEA is the perspective
of the analysis, which determines which costs and benefits are included (Sanders et al., 2016). The two
most common perspectives are the health system perspective (focusing on direct medical costs) and the
societal perspective (including broader costs such as productivity losses and patient out-of-pocket expenses)
(Drummond et al., 2015).
Discrepancies between these perspectives can lead to decision discordance, where an intervention is deemed
cost-effective from one perspective but not the other (Welton, Griffin, and Claxton, 2010). For example,
a vaccination program might be costly to the health system but generate significant productivity gains
by preventing workforce absenteeism, making it cost-effective only from a societal perspective. Guidelines
from bodies like the International Decision Support Initiative (iDSI) and the World Health Organization
(WHO) often recommend considering multiple perspectives (Wilkinson et al., 2016; Tan-Torres Edejer et al.,
2003). However, standard software tools typically treat perspective as a static parameter choice rather than a
source of structural uncertainty to be quantified (Alarid-Escudero et al., 2024; Filipovi´c-Pierucci, Zarca, and
Durand-Zaleski, 2017).
We introduce vop poc nz, a Python package designed to:
1. Implement a pipeline for CEA and Distributional CEA (DCEA) (Cookson et al., 2017).
2. Calculate the Value of Perspective (VoP), a metric representing the opportunity cost of decision
discordance.
3. Provide Value of Information (VOI) analysis (Claxton and Posnett, 2001).

Methodological framework
The vop poc nz package operationalizes two advanced frameworks in health economics: Distributional
Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (DCEA) and the Value of Perspective (VoP), built upon a foundation of standard
Markov cohort modeling.
2.1
Cost-Effectiveness Analysis (CEA)
Standard CEA compares the costs and health outcomes of two or more interventions to determine which
represents the best value for money (Drummond et al., 2015). The primary metric is the Incremental
Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER), as defined in Equation 1 (Drummond et al., 2015):

Claxton, 2010). Alternatively, the Net Monetary Benefit (NMB) can be calculated (Equation 2) (Drummond
et al., 2015):
NMB = E · λ −C

A positive incremental NMB (∆NMB > 0) indicates cost-effectiveness.
2.2
Markov Modeling
The core engine employs a time-homogeneous Markov cohort simulation (Briggs and M. Sculpher, 1998). The
model tracks a cohort of individuals through a set of mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive health
states (e.g., Healthy, Sick, Dead) over discrete time cycles (typically annual). In each cycle t, the proportion
of the cohort in state j, Pj(t), is updated based on transition probabilities derived from the transition matrix
M (Equation 3) (Briggs and M. Sculpher, 1998):
P(t + 1) = P(t) × M
(3)
Costs (C) and health outcomes (Q, typically QALYs) are accumulated for each state. To support multi-
perspective analysis, the model simultaneously tracks two sets of cost vectors: CHS (Health System) and
CSoc (Societal), where CSoc = CHS + CP rod + COOP (Productivity and Out-of-Pocket costs).

2.4
Value of Information (VOI)
Decision uncertainty arises from parameter uncertainty. VOI analysis quantifies the value of reducing this
uncertainty through further research (Claxton and Posnett, 2001).
• Expected Value of Perfect Information (EVPI): The expected value of removing all parameter
uncertainty. It is calculated as the difference between the expected value of the decision made with
perfect information and the decision made with current information. The package also supports
calculating Population EVPI by scaling this per-person value by the affected population size.
• Expected Value of Partial Perfect Information (EVPPI): The expected value of removing
uncertainty for a specific subset of parameters.
2.5
Value of Perspective (VoP)
The choice of analytical perspective (e.g., health system vs. societal) can alter the optimal decision (Mordaunt,
2024). We define the Value of Perspective (VoP) as the expected opportunity loss incurred by ignoring
relevant costs and benefits included in the societal perspective.

2.7
Normative framework
The use of DCEA and VoP implies specific normative commitments that must be transparently communicated.
• Inequality Aversion: The parameter ϵ in Equation 5 is not an empirical constant but a moral choice
reflecting society’s tolerance for health inequality (Atkinson, 1970). A value of ϵ = 10.9 (often cited in
literature) implies a strong priority for the worst-off, while ϵ = 0 implies no special priority. Our default
is ϵ = 0.5, representing mild aversion, following the illustrative examples in Cookson et al., 2017.
• Societal Relevance: The VoP calculation assumes that the societal perspective represents the “true”
welfare standard. This is a utilitarian assumption that all costs (regardless of who bears them) matter
equally (Brouwer et al., 2008). This contrasts with the ”extra-welfarist” view often adopted by health
technology assessment bodies, which prioritizes health maximization within a fixed healthcare budget.
• Opportunity Costs: Both CEA and DCEA assume that resources used for an intervention displace
other health-generating activities. The WTP threshold λ represents the shadow price of this displacement
(Welton, Griffin, and Claxton, 2010).
Users must explicitly acknowledge these value judgments when interpreting results.
3
Software design and implementation
The vop poc nz package is designed as a modular, object-oriented framework built on the standard sci-
entific Python stack (numpy, pandas, scipy, matplotlib). To ensure data integrity and type safety, we
employ pydantic for strict parameter validation and pandera for runtime DataFrame schema validation.
Metamodeling capabilities are supported by scikit-learn.
3.1
Core Components
The architecture separates the mathematical core from the analysis pipeline and reporting layer, ensuring a
clear abstraction between model inputs and computational logic.
• cea model core: The computational engine. It implements vectorized Markov cohort models, allowing
for rapid execution of thousands of probabilistic iterations. The state distribution is computed over
a time horizon T using matrix multiplication, where transition matrices are 3D tensors (simulations
× states × states). It supports multiple simultaneous perspectives by tracking costs and utilities in
parallel accumulators.
• dcea equity analysis: A specialized module for equity calculations. It takes subgroup-disaggregated
results and applies the Atkinson social welfare function to compute equity-weighted metrics.
• value of information: Implements the VoP and VOI logic. It uses Monte Carlo simulation results to
estimate the expected opportunity loss of decision uncertainty.
3.2
Configuration and Abstraction
All model parameters are defined in external YAML configuration files, decoupling the data from the code.
This allows researchers to modify transition probabilities, costs, and utility values without altering the
underlying Python codebase. The ModelParameters class validates these inputs before they are passed to
the computational engine.
3.3
Design Patterns
The package employs a functional pipeline pattern for the high-level workflow. The run analysis pipeline
function orchestrates the data flow:
1. Ingestion: Parameters are loaded from YAML configuration files.

4
Comparison with existing software
To identify relevant software, we conducted a systematic search of CRAN, PyPI, and GitHub using keywords
such as ”cost-effectiveness”, ”health economics”, and ”Markov model”. We selected packages that are open-
source, actively maintained, and provide functionality for cohort modeling or PSA. We also included TreeAge,
a widely used commercial software, as a benchmark.
4.1
dampack
dampack (Alarid-Escudero et al., 2024) is an R package focused on decision-analytic modeling. It excels at
processing the output of probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to generate cost-effectiveness acceptability
curves (CEACs) and EVPI. However, dampack acts primarily as a post-processing tool for model outputs.
It does not inherently structure the modeling of multiple perspectives simultaneously, nor does it quantify
the uncertainty associated with the choice of perspective itself. vop poc nz, in contrast, integrates the
multi-perspective modeling directly into the core workflow, enabling the calculation of VoP alongside standard
VOI metrics.
4.2
heemod
heemod (Filipovi´c-Pierucci, Zarca, and Durand-Zaleski, 2017) is a powerful R package for constructing Markov
models. It allows users to define transition matrices and state values (costs/utilities) using a flexible syntax.
While heemod can be used to run models from different perspectives by changing input parameters, it treats
these as separate model runs. It lacks a native framework for analyzing the discordance between these
runs or for conducting Distributional CEA (DCEA) to assess equity impacts across subgroups. vop poc nz
includes DCEA as a core component, automating the recursive analysis of subgroups and the calculation of
equity-weighted net monetary benefit.
4.3
BCEA
BCEA (Bayesian Cost-Effectiveness Analysis) (Baio, 2012) is an R package for the post-processing of Bayesian
model outputs. It provides extensive functionality for VOI and cost-effectiveness plane visualization. Like
dampack, BCEA focuses on the statistical analysis of results given a fixed model structure and perspective. It
does not provide the machinery to operationalize perspective uncertainty.
4.4
hesim
hesim (Incerti et al., 2021b) is an R package designed for high-performance health economic simulation,
utilizing C++ for computational efficiency. It supports individualized microsimulations and cohort models,
making it highly scalable. While hesim supports DCEA (Incerti et al., 2021a), it requires users to manually
structure the multi-perspective analysis and does not provide native functionality for VoP. Its focus is
primarily on computational speed and generalizability rather than the specific structural uncertainty of
perspective choice.

4.5
TreeAge
TreeAge Pro is a commercial software suite widely used in industry and academia. It offers a graphical user
interface for building decision trees and Markov models. While robust and feature-rich, it is proprietary
and closed-source, limiting reproducibility and extensibility. Furthermore, while it supports multi-attribute
analysis, it does not have built-in modules for the specific calculation of VoP or the automated generation of
equity-weighted metrics as defined in the DCEA framework.
4.6
econeval
econeval is a Python package for health economic evaluation, offering support for Markov models, discrete
event simulation, and agent-based modeling. It provides tools for cost-effectiveness analysis and PSA. However,
like its R counterparts, it does not natively support the simultaneous multi-perspective modeling or the VoP
metric central to vop poc nz. vop poc nz distinguishes itself by integrating these features into a cohesive
framework specifically designed for equity and perspective uncertainty.
4.7
Microsoft Excel / @RISK
Microsoft Excel is the most ubiquitous tool for health economic modeling, often augmented with add-ins like
@RISK (Palisade) for probabilistic analysis. While flexible and accessible, Excel models are prone to errors,
difficult to version control, and computationally inefficient for complex simulations (e.g., nested loops for
VoP). They lack the native structural frameworks for DCEA or automated multi-perspective analysis found
in vop poc nz.

4.8
Unique Contribution
Unlike the R-based alternatives, vop poc nz is native to the Python ecosystem, facilitating integration with
modern data science and machine learning workflows (e.g., scikit-learn for metamodeling). Crucially, it is
the first open-source tool to operationalize the Value of Perspective, transforming the choice of perspective

5
Case studies
To demonstrate the capabilities of vop poc nz, we applied the framework to five hypothetical interventions in
the Aotearoa New Zealand context: HPV Vaccination, Smoking Cessation, Hepatitis C Therapy, Childhood
Obesity Prevention, and Housing Insulation. These cases were selected to represent a spectrum of intervention
types, ranging from traditional clinical technologies (HPV Vaccination) to broader public health and social
policy measures (Housing Insulation). This diversity allows us to test the sensitivity of the VoP metric to
different cost structures and societal spillover effects. Specifically, we contrast a vaccine with high health
system costs and clear health benefits against a housing intervention where the primary costs are non-health
(retrofitting) but the benefits span health, energy savings, and productivity.

5.1
Model Structure
Each intervention was modeled using a Markov cohort simulation with annual cycles over a lifetime horizon.
Parameters were derived from published literature and adjusted for the New Zealand context (see Supplement
C for full parameter set). We evaluated two perspectives:
1. Health System: Direct medical costs and health gains (QALYs).
2. Societal: Adds productivity gains and broader societal costs. The framework supports both the Human
Capital method (valuing all lost time) and the Friction Cost method (valuing time until replacement),
with the Human Capital method used as the default for these case studies.
Costs and health outcomes were discounted at 3% per annum, consistent with New Zealand PHARMAC
guidelines.
5.2
Evaluation Framework
The evaluation proceeds in stages, systematically uncovering the implications of perspective choice:
1. Deterministic Analysis: We calculate the Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) for each
perspective using base-case parameter values. This step identifies potential decision discordance.
2. Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis (DSA): We perform one-way sensitivity analyses to identify
which parameters (e.g., discount rates, utility weights) most influence the results, visualized via Tornado
diagrams.
3. Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis (PSA): We run 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations to quantify
parameter uncertainty. This generates the Cost-Effectiveness Plane and Cost-Effectiveness Acceptability
Curve (CEAC).
4. Cost of Illness (COI): Prior to the full CEA, we estimate the total economic burden of the disease
under the status quo, disaggregated by payer (Health System vs. Societal).
5. Budget Impact Analysis (BIA): We estimate the aggregate financial consequences of adopting the
intervention over a 5-year horizon, considering the eligible population size.
6. Distributional and VoP Analysis: We apply the Atkinson inequality aversion parameter (ϵ = 0.5)
to estimate the Equity-Weighted Net Monetary Benefit (NMBeq). Concurrently, we calculate the Value
of Perspective (VoP) to quantify the opportunity cost of ignoring societal costs.

5.5
Value of Perspective
While all interventions were cost-effective under both perspectives at the $50,000 threshold, the Value of
Perspective analysis reveals the potential for decision discordance at lower thresholds. For example, at a
$20,000 threshold, Housing Insulation would be rejected under the Health System perspective but accepted
under the Societal perspective, generating a positive VoP. Figure 5 illustrates the distribution of the Delta
Net Monetary Benefit (NMBsoc −NMBHS) for each intervention. The violin plot reveals that while most
interventions show a positive societal bonus (distribution shifted right of zero), the magnitude and variance
of this bonus differ significantly. Smoking Cessation, for instance, shows a long right tail, indicating a high
probability of substantial productivity gains that are missed by the health system perspective.

7
Discussion
The vop poc nz package demonstrates the feasibility of operationalizing the Value of Perspective and DCEA
within a unified computational framework. Our results confirm that the choice of analytical perspective is
not merely a methodological detail but a fundamental driver of resource allocation decisions. By making
normative assumptions explicit and quantifying the decision uncertainty associated with perspective choice,
the tool enhances the transparency of health economic evaluations. Furthermore, the identification of a
quantifiable “societal bonus” provides a new metric for policymakers to assess the opportunity cost of ignoring
broader societal impacts. This section discusses the implications of these findings for decision-making, the
normative trade-offs inherent in equity-weighted analysis, and the computational considerations for scaling
this approach.

7.1
The “Societal Bonus” and Decision Discordance
Our analysis reveals that for interventions with significant non-health impacts (e.g., housing insulation,
smoking cessation), the choice of perspective is not merely a technical detail but a determinant of the funding
decision. The “societal bonus” - visualized in the Delta Violin plots (Figure 5) - represents the additional
value captured when the analytical boundary is widened. Quantifying this bonus allows policymakers to see
exactly what is lost when adhering strictly to a health system perspective. For example, the long tail of the
societal bonus for smoking cessation suggests that in many scenarios, the productivity gains alone could
justify the intervention, a distinct insight from the mean ICER.
7.2
Normative Implications
The integration of DCEA highlights that efficiency and equity are often competing objectives. The parameter
ϵ (inequality aversion) acts as a “tuning knob” for social justice. By allowing users to vary ϵ and observe the
impact on the Equity-Weighted Net Monetary Benefit, vop poc nz transforms ethical debates into empirical
inquiries. Users must be transparent about these choices; a high ϵ value implies a willingness to sacrifice total
population health to reduce inequality, a trade-off that requires democratic legitimacy.
7.3

A key limitation of the current implementation is the computational burden of the nested Monte Carlo
simulations required for VoP and DCEA. The case studies presented here utilized a sample size of N = 1, 000
iterations for the probabilistic sensitivity analysis. While convergence diagnostics (see Supplement A) indicate
that this is sufficient for stable estimates of mean outcomes (e.g., expected Net Monetary Benefit), it may be

26.1. The software environment was managed using conda with Python 3.13.2. On this hardware, the full
pipeline for the five case studies (including PSA and figure generation) required approximately 15 minutes of
runtime. Reporting these details is essential for reproducibility and for benchmarking future performance
improvements.
For full-scale policy applications, we recommend increasing the sample size to N ≥10, 000, potentially
utilizing parallel computing resources to manage the increased runtime. Future versions of the package will
implement parallel processing using joblib or dask to mitigate this bottleneck.
7.4
Policy Brief Generation
The ultimate goal of health economic modeling is to inform decision-making. To this end, the package
includes a prototype policy brief generator that translates the complex outputs of DCEA and VoP analysis
into plain-language summaries. An example of this output is provided in the supplementary materials. These
briefs highlight the key drivers of decision discordance and the equity implications of funding choices, bridging
the gap between technical analysts and political decision-makers. Future work will refine this module to
support customizable templates for different stakeholder groups.
8
Summary
vop poc nz fills a critical gap in the health economics software landscape by providing a Python-based
framework that explicitly addresses perspective uncertainty and equity.
By quantifying the Value of
Perspective, it offers decision-makers a new tool to assess the robustness of their funding decisions, revealing
the hidden ”societal bonus” often ignored in standard evaluations. The integration of Distributional CEA
further ensures that equity trade-offs are transparently weighed against efficiency gains. We hope this
open-source tool will encourage the broader adoption of multi-perspective and equity-informative modeling in
health policy.
