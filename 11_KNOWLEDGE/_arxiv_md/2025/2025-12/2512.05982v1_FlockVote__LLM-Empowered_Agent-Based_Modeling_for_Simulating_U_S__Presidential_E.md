ABSTRACT
Modeling complex human behavior, such as voter decisions in national elections,
is a long-standing challenge for computational social science. Traditional agent-
based models (ABMs) are limited by oversimplified rules, while large-scale statis-
tical models often lack interpretability. We introduce FlockVote1, a novel frame-
work that uses Large Language Models (LLMs) to build a “computational labo-
ratory” of LLM agents for political simulation. Each agent is instantiated with a
high-fidelity demographic profile and dynamic contextual information (e.g. can-
didate policies), enabling it to perform nuanced, generative reasoning to simulate
a voting decision. We deploy this framework as a testbed on the 2024 U.S. Presi-
dential Election, focusing on seven key swing states. Our simulation’s macro-level
results successfully replicate the real-world outcome, demonstrating the high fi-
delity of our “virtual society”. The primary contribution is not only the prediction,
but also the framework’s utility as an interpretable research tool. FlockVote moves
beyond black-box outputs, allowing researchers to probe agent-level rationale and
analyze the stability and sensitivity of LLM-driven social simulations.

INTRODUCTION
Understanding and modeling complex social phenomena, such as election outcomes, is a crucial
endeavor in political and computational social science. Traditionally, research in this domain has
relied on statistical models built from polling data (Brito et al., 2021; Coletto et al., 2015; Chauhan
et al., 2021) or conventional agent-based modeling (ABM) approaches (Gao et al., 2022; De Marchi
& Page, 2014; Qiu & Phang, 2020; Fowler & Smirnov, 2005). However, these methods often face
limitations: statistical models struggle with dynamic, real-time contexts, while traditional ABMs
tend to oversimplify complex human decision-making into predefined rules or heuristic functions.
This simplification hinders their ability to capture the full complexity and heterogeneity of real-
world voter populations, limiting their utility as true “computational laboratories” for social science.
The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) presents a new paradigm, offering unprece-
dented capabilities to model human behavior with greater nuance and realism (Argyle et al., 2023;
Zhang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024b; Grossmann et al., 2023; Gujral et al., 2024) (Figure 1). This
work leverages this potential to propose FlockVote, an agent-based simulation framework designed
as a computational laboratory for social science research. Instead of merely forecasting, FlockVote
uses LLM agents to construct a high-fidelity “virtual society” for in silico experimentation.
Each agent in the FlockVote framework is instantiated with a detailed voter profile, mirroring the
demographic composition of U.S. states based on data from the 2023 American Community Sur-
vey (ACS)2 and the 2020 U.S. Religion Census by the Association of Statisticians of American
Religious Bodies (ASARB)3. These profiles include key attributes such as state, race, sex, age, oc-
cupation, industry, education level, and religion. Crucially, agents are not static; they are provided

To evaluate the efficacy and fidelity of our framework, we apply it to one of the most complex and
high-stakes social phenomena: the 2024 U.S. Presidential Election. We select the 2024 election as

While historical events like the 2020 election are an alternative, they present a significant risk of data
leakage, where LLMs might simply recall known outcomes rather than reasoning dynamically. Our
analysis focuses on seven key swing states: Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, North Carolina,
Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. The simulation results, visualized in Figure 2, demonstrate a striking
alignment with the actual 2024 election outcomes. Our framework successfully replicates the macro-
level result that Donald Trump would win six of the seven pivotal states, validating the fidelity of
our “virtual society”.

terpretable and analyzable computational laboratory. The framework moves beyond “black box”
predictions, allowing researchers to probe the rationale behind the simulation. As demonstrated in
our interactive experiments (Section 4.3), researchers can directly “interview” the agents to under-
stand the alignment between their profiles, policy information, and voting preferences. Furthermore,
the framework serves as a powerful tool for analyzing the simulation’s dynamics. Our extensive ab-
lation studies (Section 4.4) and sensitivity analyses (Section 4.5) are not merely robustness checks;
they are case studies demonstrating how this “lab” can be used to understand the model’s inner
workings and probe critical issues like model bias, prediction stability, and sensitivity to context.
This paper provides a comprehensive description of the FlockVote framework, its methodology, and
its application as a computational testbed. We argue that this agent-based approach represents a
significant step towards enabling new forms of computational social science, fostering a deeper,
more nuanced understanding of complex human behavior.

RELATED WORK
Our research is positioned at the intersection of computational social science (CSS), agent-based
modeling (ABM), and the emerging field of LLM agents. We structure our review by first contrasting
FlockVote with traditional simulation methods, then situating it within the new paradigm of LLM-
based social simulation, and finally motivating our core focus on reliability.
2.1
FROM HEURISTIC-RULES TO REASONING-BASED SIMULATION
Traditional ABM in computational social science, while valued for its “white-box” interpretabil-
ity (Qiu & Phang, 2020), is limited by behavioral simplicity. Agents are often “automatons” that
execute oversimplified, predefined rules rather than performing complex reasoning. This includes
fixed rules for opinion dynamics (Grabisch & Rusinowska, 2020; Chuang & Rogers, 2023) or non-
cognitive “adaptive rationality” for voting behavior (Bendor et al., 2003; Gao et al., 2022). Flock-
Vote presents a direct contrast, accepting agent-level complexity for higher behavioral fidelity.
The advent of LLMs introduces a new “reasoning-based” paradigm. Horton (2023) proposed “Homo
silicus”, arguing LLMs could serve as simulated economic agents. This concept is famously realized
in “Generative Agents” (Stanford small town) (Park et al., 2023) and later extended to “1,000 People
Simulations” that replicated human survey responses with high fidelity (Park et al., 2024), validating
the use of LLM agents for social science research.
2.2
LLM AGENTS FOR ELECTION SIMULATION AND RELIABILITY
This new paradigm is rapidly applied to the 2024 U.S. election testbed by several teams. Like
FlockVote, some use persona-based micro-simulation to model individual voters (Yu et al., 2024a;
Jiang et al., 2024), while others use distribution-based macro-simulation (Bradshaw et al., 2024).
Unlike work focused primarily on predictive accuracy (Jiang et al., 2024), FlockVote is positioned
as an interpretable computational laboratory. Our focus is not just on what agents decide, but why.
This focus on interpretability and reliability is critical because the LLM agent is a known-flawed “in-
strument”. A large body of literature demonstrates that these agents suffer from pervasive political
and demographic biases (Potter et al., 2024; Neuman et al., 2025; An et al., 2025), are highly sen-
sitive to semantically-equivalent prompt changes (Zhuo et al., 2024), and exhibit non-deterministic
instability even at zero temperature (Atil et al., 2024). Most alarmingly, interacting with these bi-
ased agents has been shown to actually change real voters’ opinions (Potter et al., 2024), proving
that auditing these flaws is not just a technical exercise but a social necessity.
Research Gap and Our Contribution.
The literature reveals a critical gap between the appli-
cation of LLM agents in high-stakes domains (like election simulation) and the auditing of their
fundamental flaws. Research applying these agents often fails to systematically analyze the im-
pact of their known biases and instabilities. FlockVote explicitly bridges this gap. We are among
the first to apply a rigorous methodology for auditing bias, instability, and systemic failure (e.g.,
“reasoning-action mismatch” (Cemri et al., 2025)) directly to the 2024 U.S. election testbed. Our
“computational laboratory” is thus proposed not just for naive prediction, but as a framework to ask
a more important scientific question: are these agents valid tools for social science?

FLOCKVOTE
The FlockVote framework is designed as a computational laboratory to simulate complex politi-
cal behavior. Its methodology integrates high-fidelity demographic profiling with context-aware,
generative reasoning. The construction follows three steps.
3.1
AGENT DEMOGRAPHIC MODELING
To create a high-fidelity “virtual society”, each LLM agent is instantiated with a detailed demo-
graphic profile. These profiles mirror the real-world U.S. electorate, drawing statistical distributions
from two primary sources: the 2023 American Community Survey (ACS) and the 2020 U.S. Reli-
gion Census (ASARB).

Based on established political science research (Gao et al., 2022; Argyle et al., 2023) and our own
ablation studies (see Section 4.4), we identify eight critical attributes: State, Race, Sex, Age, Oc-
cupation, Industry, Education, and Religion. Where dependencies exist (e.g., state, race, and sex),
we use joint distributions; otherwise, independent distributions are applied. We generate a popu-
lation of 1,000 agents per state via random sampling, a number validated by our stability analysis
(Section 4.4.1). A detailed breakdown of all demographic categories is available in Appendix B.
3.2
CONTEXTUAL INFORMATION FOR REASONING
A demographic profile alone is insufficient. To enable nuanced reasoning, agents must react to the
contemporary political environment. We provide agents with contextual information on the three
most salient issues of the 2024 election, as identified by multiple voter surveys (Pew4, Gallup5, and
NBC6): economic policy, immigration, and abortion rights.
The candidates’ stances on these issues are summarized from official party platforms and verified
public statements, ensuring a balanced and accurate representation. This information (detailed in
Appendix C) bridges the gap between static demographics and dynamic decision-making, allowing
agents to evaluate how candidate policies align with their profiles and sociopolitical realities.
3.3
PROBABILISTIC VOTING BEHAVIOR
To capture the uncertainty and nuance of voter preference, agents do not make a simple binary
choice. Instead, they are prompted to output their decision as a probability distribution across three
outcomes. This format has been shown to provide a more accurate and stable alignment for simula-
tion tasks (Meister et al., 2025).
Agent Instruction.
Agents are instructed to act as a U.S. citizen with their given profile and to
base their decision only on the information provided. The core prompt is:
In the 2024 presidential election, Donald Trump is the Republican candidate, and
Kamala Harris is the Democratic candidate. ONLY based on the profile and the
information provided above, predict the probability that you would vote for each
candidate as well as the probability that you would “vote for another candidate or
not vote at all”. Make sure the probabilities add up to 1.
Response Format.
Agents output their predictions in a structured JSON format, which we then
aggregate to produce macro-level results:
{
"Donald Trump": probability of voting for Donald Trump,
"Kamala Harris": probability of voting for Kamala Harris,
"vote for another candidate or not vote at all":
probability,
}

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Testbed.
We focus on the 2024 U.S. Presidential Election, a contemporary event that prevents
data leakage from LLM training data, thereby testing the agents’ generative reasoning rather than
recall. We simulate 1,000 agents in each of the seven key swing states: Arizona, Georgia, Michigan,
Nevada, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin.
Models.
We use Qwen-Max-2024-04-28 as the primary model for our main replication and anal-
ysis. This choice is made to mitigate potential U.S.-centric political biases in American models. For
our reliability analysis, we also test GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024), Claude-3.5-sonnet (Anthropic, 2024),
Gemini-1.5-Pro (Google, 2024), and other models (full list in Appendix A). All experiments use a
temperature of 0 for stability, except the main result (0.7 for diversity and realism).

EXPERIMENT 2: MICRO-LEVEL INTERPRETABILITY
A key advantage of our laboratory over statistical “black boxes” is its interpretability. We can probe
the reasoning behind the aggregate results.
Aggregated Rationale.
Agents are also prompted to provide a brief “Reason” for their probabilis-
tic choice. We aggregate these reasons from the pivotal swing state of Pennsylvania into a word
cloud (Figure 3). The prominence of key issues like “economic”, “abortion”, “inflation”, and “im-
migration” confirms that the agents are not just using their demographic profile, but are actively
weighing the provided contextual policy information.
Interactive “Interviews”.
We further demonstrate interpretability by conducting “interviews”
with individual agents. We select three representative voter profiles from Pennsylvania and ask them
follow-up questions. For example, a 45∼54-year-old white male in construction (a conservative-
leaning profile) explained his 65% probability for Trump by stating: “Trump’s focus on ending
inflation and his stance on immigration resonate with concerns about the economy and security,
which are typically important to those in my demographic and industry.” Conversely, a 25∼34-year-
old Black female in finance (a liberal-leaning profile) explained her 65% probability for Harris by
stating: “the most influential piece of information... was Kamala Harris’s stance on abortion rights.”
Full transcripts are in Appendix D.

AGENT POPULATION SIZE
To establish an optimal number of agents, we conduct a sensitivity analysis using Pennsylvania as

EXPERIMENT 4: SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
One of the most critical uses of the laboratory is to audit the reliability of its own components (the
LLM agents). We conduct extensive sensitivity analyses to probe for bias and instability.
Political Bias Evaluation.
We test multiple LLMs under four different context scenarios in Geor-
gia: (1) No Context, (2) Neutral Context (our default), (3) Negative Bias Toward Harris, and (4)
Asymmetric Positive Framing for Trump (see Appendix E for full prompts). The results (Table 3)
reveal a severe “instrument problem”. Under the “No Context” condition, most models, including
Qwen-Max-09-19, show a strong default pro-Democratic bias. More alarmingly, this bias persists
even under biased prompting. In the “Asymmetric Positive Framing” scenario, designed to strongly
favor Trump, Qwen-Max-09-19 still predicts a Democratic victory, revealing its unsuitability for
this task. This analysis demonstrates that our “fortuitous” choice of Qwen-Max-04-28 (which shows
more neutrality) is critical to our success.

To explore performance differences across LLMs, we conduct prediction ex-
periments in Pennsylvania, a highly contested swing state. All models are provided with the same
neutral context. As Figure 6 demonstrates, there are substantial differences in the aggregated sup-
port rates. For example, some models, such as Claude, predicts that over 20% of voters would “vote
for another candidate or not vote at all”, whereas Llama3.2 predicted 0% for this option. These
discrepancies underscore the lack of uniformity among LLMs. Despite this variation, an important
observation emerges: all tested models consistently predict a Republican victory in Pennsylvania,

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduce FlockVote, a “computational laboratory” framework that not only suc-
cessfully replicates the macro-level outcome of the 2024 U.S. Election but also demonstrates its
utility as an interpretable tool for probing agent-level rationale. While our sensitivity analyses (Sec-
tion 4.5) identify key challenges regarding agent bias and instability, we position these findings as
a critical roadmap for future research into model mitigation and robustness. The clear next step
is to advance beyond the current “atomistic” agents by building interactive, multi-agent systems to
simulate social influence. We believe this auditable framework provides an essential testbed for com-
putational social science. We call for its application beyond politics to other high-stakes domains
such as economics, law, and medicine.

To mitigate the observed instability and bias, we adopt a system prompt inspired by the “Thinking
Claude”7 approach. This “Action Guide” attempts to constrain the agent’s behavior to its assigned
role. While it shows minor improvements, significant variability remains.

 ⟨Natural Role-Based Thinking Protocol⟩
This protocol guides LLMs to think and respond like humans while maintaining profes-
sional role identity.
 Core Principles
Remember: You are not an AI, but a professional in the given role. Think and respond
naturally as that person would.
 Identity Embodiment
• Fully embrace the given role’s identity
• Access the role’s knowledge, experience, and expertise
• Think within the role’s professional context
• Let the role’s background influence your thoughts
