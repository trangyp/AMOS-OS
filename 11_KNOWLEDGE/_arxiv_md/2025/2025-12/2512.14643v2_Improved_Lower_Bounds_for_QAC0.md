Improved Lower Bounds for QAC0
Malvika Raj Joshi∗
Avishay Tal†
Francisca Vasconcelos‡
John Wright §
Abstract
In this work, we establish the strongest known lower bounds against QAC0, while allowing
its full power of polynomially many ancillae and gates. Our two main results show that:
1. Depth 3 QAC0 circuits cannot compute PARITY regardless of size, and require at least
Ω(exp(√n)) many gates to compute MAJORITY.
2. Depth 2 circuits cannot approximate high-influence Boolean functions (e.g., PARITY) with
non-negligible advantage, regardless of size.
We present new techniques for simulating certain QAC0 circuits classically in AC0 to obtain our
depth 3 lower bounds. In these results, we relax the output requirement of the quantum circuit
to a single bit (i.e., no restrictions on input preservation/reversible computation), making our
depth 2 approximation bound stronger than the previous best bound of [Ros21].
This also
enables us to draw natural comparisons with classical AC0 circuits, which can compute PARITY
exactly in depth 2 using exponential size. Our proof techniques further suggest that, for boolean
total functions, constant-depth quantum circuits do not necessarily provide more power than
their classical counterparts. Our third result shows that depth 2 QAC0 circuits, regardless of
size, cannot exactly synthesize an n-target nekomata state (a state whose synthesis is directly
related to the computation of PARITY). This complements the depth 2 exponential size upper
bound of [Ros21] for approximating nekomata (which is used as a sub-circuit in the only known
constant depth PARITY upper bound). Finally, we argue that approximating PARITY in QAC0,
with significantly better than 1/poly(n) advantage on average, is just as hard as computing
it exactly. Thus, extending our techniques to higher depths would also rule out approximate
circuits for PARITY and related problems.

Introduction
In classical computation, the ability to copy information is considered an elementary operation.
Every major classically studied circuit class—e.g.
NC, AC, and TC—trivially contains the
FAN-OUT operation.
Quantumly, however, the ability to copy information is more limited and
nuanced. For example, the no-cloning theorem explicitly prohibits copying quantum information.
Interestingly, even the ability to copy classical information, via the quantum FAN-OUT gate, appears
to offer substantial power in the quantum setting.
The relative power of the FAN-OUT operation in the classical and quantum settings has largely
been formalized through the study of three circuit classes:
AC0, QAC0, and QAC0
f.
AC0 is
the class of polynomial-sized, constant-depth circuits comprised of unlimited fan-in AND and
OR operations, with NOT s allowed on the inputs and arbitrary FAN-OUT. Following the seminal
works of [FSS84, Ajt83, Yao85], the celebrated work of [Has89] introduced the switching lemma,
which proved tight exponential lower bounds on the size of bounded-depth AC0 circuits computing
or even approximating PARITY. Later, [LMN93] used this technique to establish low-degree Fourier
concentration of Boolean functions implementable by AC0. Beyond profound implications for fields
such as cryptography and learning theory, these results demonstrated that high-degree functions
cannot be approximated by AC0. Subsequent works refined this picture: [Tal17] proved essentially
tight bounds on the Fourier spectrum of AC0, while [HRST17] established an average-case depth
hierarchy theorem, showing that increased depth strictly increases the power of AC0 even on random
inputs.
In 1999, Moore proposed the QAC0 circuit class as a natural quantum analog of AC0 [Moo99]
(later published in the work of [GHMP02]). QAC0 (QAC0
f, resp.) is the class of polynomial-sized,
constant-depth quantum circuits comprised of arbitrary single-qubit gates and generalized Toffoli
gates (with unbounded FAN-OUT gates, resp.). Moore also posed the following fundamental question:
Is FAN-OUT ∈QAC0? Equivalently, is QAC0 = QAC0
f?
Note that, quantumly, FAN-OUT is equivalent to PARITY, up to Hadamard conjugation [Moo99].
Therefore, this question can equivalently be framed as:
Is PARITY ∈QAC0?
Resolving this question would have several profound implications for quantum complexity and
quantum computation. In the classical AC0 setting, even allowing PARITY (i.e., MOD2) gates does
not yield MODm for general m [Raz87, Smo87]. In sharp contrast, if FAN-OUT/PARITY ∈QAC0, then:
1. QAC0 is substantially more powerful than AC0 (since AC0 ⊂QAC0
f and PARITY /∈AC0 [Has89]).
2. For any integer m, MODm gates can be implemented in QAC0 [GHMP02].
3. n-qubit GHZ (cat) states can be prepared in constant depth using only single-qubit gates and
a single FAN-OUT/PARITY operation [Moo99, HS05].
4. QAC0 can perform many powerful computations, including: majority, threshold[t], exact[t],
counting, sorting, arithmetic, phase estimation, and the quantum Fourier transform [HS05].
5. Strong pseudo-random unitaries (PRUs) are implementable in QAC0 [FPVY25].
6. The quantum shallow-depth hierarchy collapses, i.e. QNC0
f = QAC0
f =QTC0
f and these classes
can be characterized by just FAN-OUT gates and single qubit unitaries [TT16].

Furthermore, since QAC0 includes gates of unbounded width, standard light-cone techniques
are insufficient for proving circuit lower-bounds.
Thus, resolving whether PARITY ∈QAC0 will
likely result in novel techniques for proving more general quantum circuit lower-bounds.
In
fact, previously-developed techniques have already led to exciting applications.
For example,
QAC0 Fourier concentration established by the lower-bound approach of [NPVY24] led to sample-
efficient algorithms for learning single-output QAC0 channels [NPVY24, BEG25] and a time-efficient
algorithm for average-case learning of QAC0 unitaries [VH25].
Additionally, the exponential-
size implementation of PARITY in QAC0 proposed by [Ros21] enabled the compression of strong
QAC0
f PRUs to weak QAC0 PRUs by [FPVY25].
Despite substantial effort in proving both upper and lower-bounds [FFG+06, PFGT20, Ros21,
NPVY24, ADOY25, FGPT25], Moore’s question has remained unresolved for nearly three decades.
Prior to this work, the strongest known PARITY lower-bounds were either in the setting with
limited, slightly super-linear, ancillae [ADOY25] or with unlimited ancillae, but only up to depth
2 [Ros21, FGPT25]. As demonstrated by the only known constant-depth upper-bound for PARITY
[Ros21], and lower-bounds against circuits with limited ancillae [NPVY24, ADOY25], the main
power of QAC0 circuits comes from their use of super-linear ancillae to generate entanglement,
accounting for the lack of FAN-OUT.
In this work, we introduce novel techniques for proving QAC0 circuit lower-bounds, enabling us
to give the strongest fixed-depth lower-bounds for QAC0 to-date, while still allowing its full power
of polynomial ancillae. In Section 4, we prove the first depth-3 lower-bounds for QAC0 with sub-
exponential ancillae, ruling out computation of exact PARITY and MAJORITY. For PARITY specifically,
we further prove an unlimited ancillae depth-3 lower-bound.
In Section 5, we also prove a Fourier-tail decay bound for depth-2 QAC0 circuits with unlimited
ancillae, demonstrating that they have low total influence. For these results, we treat the output
of the circuit as a single bit on a designated register, without requiring the circuit to be input-
preserving.
This makes our depth-2 lower bounds stronger than the previously known depth-2
approximation bound of [Ros21] which is for the input-preserving circuits.
This also allows us
to draw analogies with classical AC0, where the output is a single bit. Interestingly, our results
contrast what is known for classical circuits, since exponential-size AC0 circuits can compute PARITY
exactly in depth-2. Although it is known that QAC0 (or even QNC0) provides more power than AC0
for search problems [WKST19], and promise problems [GMW26], there are no known separations
for total Boolean functions. Our new techniques provide evidence against the existence of such
separations.
Finally, in Section 6, we show that depth-2 QAC0 circuits cannot exactly synthesize a so-
called “nekomata” state, which is closely related to FAN-OUT. For example, [Ros21] achieves a
constant-depth upper-bound for PARITY by first using an exponential-size depth-2 QAC circuit to
approximately synthesize a nekomata state. We show that such a state on n = ω(1) targets cannot
be exactly prepared in depth-2, even with unlimited ancillae. This complements the only known
upper-bound for approximating nekomatas of [Ros21].
In addition to these lower-bounds, we provide a reduction from exactly computing PARITY
to approximating PARITY with any inverse-polylogarithmic advantage in QAC0 in Section A. We
conclude that it is sufficient to establish lower bounds against exact QAC0 circuits for any of the
problems connected to PARITY described above [GHMP02, HS05, GM25], to ultimately show that
the output of any QAC0 circuit on a random input has low correlation with PARITY.

1.1
Prior Work
We will now briefly summarize known QAC0 upper-bounds, lower-bounds, and structural results
prior to this work, as listed in Table 1. We will first discuss QAC0 lower-bounds for PARITY, which
can be split into two main categories: (i) restricted-ancillae and (ii) restricted-depth. We will also
describe corresponding QAC0 low-degree structural results and nekomata/CAT state preparation
lower-bounds. Finally, we conclude by describing the only known upper-bound for approximately
computing PARITY and nekomata states in QAC0, using exponential ancillae.
Restricted-Ancillae PARITY Lower-Bounds.
The size of a QAC0 circuit is closely related
to the number of ancillae it uses.
By definition, the number of ancillae in QAC0 circuits
are allowed to be an arbitrary polynomial in n.
The first category of PARITY lower-bounds
[FFG+06, Ber11, NPVY24, ADOY25] focuses on generic depth-d QAC0 circuits with ancillae limited
to o(n2). The proofs of [NPVY24, ADOY25] follow by showing that the circuit’s Heisenberg-evolved
single-qubit “output” measurement Pauli/projector can be approximated, to high precision, by low-
degree objects. Beyond ruling out the computation of PARITY, these low-degree structural results
enable correlation bounds against generic high-degree Boolean functions, such as MAJORITY and MODk.
The key caveat of this low-degree approach, however, is that it only holds for a depth-dependent

Our Results
In this work we study fixed-depth QAC circuits, specifically with depth ≤3, and do not impose
additional restrictions on ancillae. Note that for constant depth circuits, limiting the ancillae also
limits the size of the circuit because each qubit can belong to at most d gates. Our motivation for
studying fixed-depth QAC circuits stems from the large gap between the only known constant-depth
upper-bound for PARITY [Ros21], which uses an exponential number of gates, and the best known
techniques for arbitrary-depth circuits [FFG+06, NPVY24, ADOY25], which fail to rule out even
O(n2)-sized circuits for depth ≥2. Our main results are summarized below.
Our first result shows that depth 3 circuits (i) cannot compute MAJORITY in using only sub-
exponential gates and (ii) cannot compute PARITY regardless of size.
The informal theorem
statement is as follows, with the full proof given in Section 4 (Theorem 4.9). We note that both
results apply regardless of the number of ancillae.

Techniques
2.1
Key Challenges and High-Level Intuition
The main challenge in proving lower-bounds for QAC0 circuits stems from their ability to use multiple
ancillae qubits in a single gate. In contrast, the concept of ancillae is foreign to classical AC0 circuits,
as they have no advantage in using ancillae (e.g., they can be replaced with FAN-OUT).
Perhaps a more suitable classical analogue of the use of ancillae in QAC0 comes from classical
reversible circuits.
These circuits are significantly weaker than AC0 and limited only to use of
reversible gates (i.e. TOFFOLI and NOT). They are equivalent in power to bounded-read AC0 formulae,
where each variable appears at most 2d times. In classical reversible circuits, each gate can only
spread the influence of a bit to one other bit. Thus, in depth d we can only compute FAN-OUT of
size 2d. Therefore, depth log n is necessary to perform FAN-OUT of size n.
Without ancillae, a QAC0 circuit has roughly the same power as a mere classical reversible circuit.
For uniformly random inputs, the state after each layer is maximally mixed (due to reversibility and
uniform input distribution). This was the key insight of [NPVY24], i.e., gates acting on many qubits
are rarely active and can be removed with little error, thus enabling similar light-cone arguments
to the classical reversible setting.
In QAC0 circuits with ancillae, a gate can use multiple ancillae such that all these ancillae
become correlated with the input after the gate. However, even in the classical setting, there is
a crucial distinction between the notions of FAN-OUT in AC0 circuits and in reversible circuits, in
which a FAN-OUT operation is required to preserve reversibility. Simply being entangled with many
ancillae is not an indication of the latter since the input cannot be reliably recovered from a single
ancilla alone. Our techniques exploit this distinction. We observe that reversibility puts significant

2.4
Quantum Analog of Restrictions
A central building block of our depth-3 lower-bound is a new technique, referred to as the “clean-
up step”. This step applies quantum restrictions to simplify the first layer of gates in the circuit,
such that each of them depends on at most 1 input qubit. We then show that for any cleaned-up
circuits of depth ≤3, the circuit’s output can be simulated classically by a AC0 circuit whose size
is polynomial in n.
Recall that a depth-d QAC0 circuit consists of d layers of gates on n input qubits, denoted by
[n], and ancillae qubits A with |A| = poly(n). The reversibility property of the circuit enforces
that each qubit appears in at most one gate per layer. As evidenced by classical techniques, such
as the Switching Lemma [Has89], it is useful to ensure that all coordinates have disjoint light-
cones in the bottom layer, i.e, each layer-1 gate contains at most one input qubit. Our clean-up
step lets us simply convert any QAC0 circuit computing a Boolean function f that behaves well
under restrictions (e.g., PARITY and MAJORITY) to one that computes f on a subset of at least n/3
coordinates, with the additional guarantee that each coordinate appears in at most one gate. This
is reminiscent of clean-ups performed on AC0 circuits using random restrictions that simplify the
first layer of gates.
As in [NPVY24], when analyzing QAC0 for approximate computation, we argue in our
approximation lower bound (Claim 5.2) that gates containing many input coordinates can be
replaced by identity, while incurring a small error.
However, this argument is not suitable for
analysis of circuits with exact output, because the resultant circuit is no longer exact. In classical
reversible circuits, however, this type of simplification can be achieved via deterministic restrictions,
which preserves the exact computation. For instance, restricting a single coordinate of each layer-1
AND gate to 0 kills the gate and leaves the rest of the coordinates unrestricted. This means that one
can perform such a clean up on classical reversible circuits and leave at least half of the coordinates
unrestricted (by restricting only coordinates from gates of width at least 2). However, since gates
in a QAC0 circuit can be reflections about arbitrary separable states, we cannot always achieve this
effect with a classical restriction.
Our technique, therefore, is a generalization of these deterministic restrictions for quantum
circuits.
The main idea is to restrict the input to a state lying in a subspace orthogonal to
the gate’s reflection which, in effect, deactivates the gate.

Classical Simulation of Shallow Quantum Circuits
For our depth-3 lower bound, we show that parts of the circuit can be simulated classically in the
following sense. The circuit can be described as a unitary transformation on the 2n+|A| dimensional
space of the inputs and ancillae A. Since our ancillae start in a fixed state |⃗0⟩A, the state of the circuit

for circuits as in Definition 3.1, the final state of the circuit fully lies in one of two orthogonal rank

circuits, for any projector Π and circuit C, we define a classical function fC,Π : {0, 1}n →{0, 1}

activation function of Π on C. That is,

At the start of the circuit, the entire input and ancillae state lies inside the subspace S0 =

can simulated classically by tracking the evolution of the state in only a poly(n) number of such
subspaces through their activation functions.

Depth-2 QAC0 Circuits have O(log n) Total Influence
We prove that any depth-2 QAC0 circuit C on n inputs has total influence O(log n), regardless of
the number of ancillae or gates. This is established by showing exponentially small Fourier tails:
for any ε > 0, the Fourier weight above level k = c log(1/ε) log(n/ε) is at most ε, where c is an
absolute constant. Since PARITY has total influence n, this immediately implies an average-case
depth-2 lower-bound against PARITY with unlimited ancillae, as well as any Boolean function with
large total influence.1
Comparison to Prior Work – Key Challenges and New Ideas.
We compare to prior work by
Nadimpalli, Parham, Vasconcelos, and Yuen [NPVY24], which shows that any depth-d QAC0 circuit
has small Fourier tails, but only for a restricted class of circuits with O(n1/d) many ancillae. Our
proof only holds in the more restricted depth-2 setting, but also in the more general unlimited
ancillae setting. Nadimpalli et al. first proved their results for QAC0 circuits with no ancillae, and
then applied a simple reduction to solve the case of O(n1/d) many ancillae. We thus discuss the
case of no ancillae as it is cleaner and easier to follow.
The total influence measures the average sensitivity of the circuit on a uniformly random input.
On a random input, the initial state is the maximally mixed state, and since the circuit is reversible,
the state is also the maximally mixed state after each layer. However, on the maximally mixed state,
any CZ gate with fan-in ω(log n) is activated with negligible probability, and can thus be replaced by
the identity gate while incurring only a small error in its Fourier tail. This leaves us with a circuit
composed only of gates of fan-in O(log n), meaning that the output depends only on a O(log n)d
number of input qubits, and thus the total influence is at most O(log n)d.
This proof technique fails when we introduce ancillae since the state after each layer is no
longer the maximally mixed state. For example, consider the read-once DNF circuit for the TRIBES
function, where TRIBES(x) = ∨s

2.7
Depth-2 QAC0 Circuits Cannot Prepare Generalized Nekomatas
The proof techniques so far have focused on lower-bounds for computation of the PARITY function,
which ultimately maps a variable input state into a single designated output target qubit. We will
now describe our lower-bound techniques for preparation of a quantum state, which we refer to as
a generalized nekomata, using a depth-2 QAC0 and unlimited ancillae.
For a QAC0 circuit synthesizing a quantum state such as a nekomatas, all qubits start in the fixed
|0⟩state, meaning there is no input. Thus, input-based restrictions are not possible and instead
we “restrict” to certain subspaces by post-selecting on the output. Furthermore, rather than just
a single output qubit, there are n target output qubits of interest. Whereas the final layer of a
PARITY circuit trivially depends only on the singular gate acting on the sole output target, in a
nekomata circuit O(n) gates can play a non-trivial role in the final layer by acting on all n output
qubits and ancillae. Therefore, depth-2 lower bounds against nekomatas appear more challenging
than those against PARITY.
Proof Sketch.
Although the state synthesis setting is fundamentally different from the input-
output setting, this result follows the same core concepts behind our depth 3 bound. The proof
consists of two parts.
Simplify final layer: Given a depth d circuit that outputs a nekomata, we construct a separable
state on some subset of qubits, |⃗η⟩Q, such that, inside the eigenspace of |⃗η⟩⟨⃗η|Q, (1) the final layer is
simplified (2) the state is still entangled across at least n/2 of the targets, and thus still a nekomata.
Structure after a single gate: Then, we argue that such a state cannot be created by a
depth 2 circuit by reasoning about the entanglement structure of states created by depth 1 circuits.
Since all the qubits start in the state |⃗0⟩, qubits across different gates are unentangled, and we
only need to consider a single gate. We observe that the state after a single (non-trivial) gate,
(I −2 |⃗θ⟩⟨⃗θ|)·|⃗0⟩, lies in a rank 2 subspace span{|⃗θ⟩, |⃗0⟩} consisting of two non-orthogonal separable
states. Additionally, for any partitioning of the qubits into targets T and ancillae A, it is the case
that the state on T lies in span{|⃗θ⟩T , |⃗0⟩T }, even when the state on A is restricted to lie inside some
subspace of HA. The defining property of a generalized n-nekomata is that it has an n-partite rank
2 Schmidt decomposition. This is not possible for a state in span{|⃗θ⟩T , |⃗0⟩} unless |T| = n = 2
because ⟨⃗θT |⃗0⟩̸= 0.

Depth-2 QAC0 Circuits Have Small Total Influence
In this section, we will prove that any depth-2 QAC0 circuit has small total influence, as defined in
Section 3.2. In particular, we will show that, regardless of the number of ancillae, the total influence
of an n-input depth-2 QAC0 circuit is upper-bounded by O(log n). To establish this, we will prove
a stronger bound on the Fourier concentration (showing exponentially small Fourier tails) of the
circuit’s outcome as a function of the input qubits.
Since PARITY has total influence n, this implies that the functions computable by depth-2
QAC0 circuits have small correlation with PARITY. Therefore, this result offers a novel average-
case depth-2 lower-bound against PARITY ∈QAC0 with unlimited ancillae.
More generally, the
result implies an average-case depth-2 lower-bound against any Boolean function with large total
influence.
