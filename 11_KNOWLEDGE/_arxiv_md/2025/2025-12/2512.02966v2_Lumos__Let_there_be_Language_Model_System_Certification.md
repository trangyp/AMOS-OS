As Language Model Systems (LMS) are deployed across a wide range of applications, aligning them with human
ethics has become crucial. Although recent works propose methods to statistically certify LMS properties
such as fairness, correct question answering, and safety, these approaches are ad hoc and hard to generalize.
We introduce a principled framework: a domain-specific language, Lumos, for specifying and statistically
certifying LMS behaviors. Lumos is the first imperative probabilistic programming language over graphs, with
constructs to sample independent and identically distributed prompts for statistical certification of LMS. It
abstracts large prompt distributions as structured graphs, forming random prompts from subgraphs. Lumos
supports certifying LMS for any user-defined prompt distributions, integrating multiple statistical certifiers.
We provide hybrid (operational and denotational) semantics for Lumos, offering a rigorous way to interpret
specifications. Lumos can encode existing LMS specifications, including complex relational and temporal
properties. Lumos gives a systematic way to reason about, specify, and certify novel properties. Using Lumos,
we develop the first safety specifications for vision-language models (VLMs) in autonomous driving scenarios
and the first specifications for privacy preservation in LMS responses. We show that the state-of-the-art
VLM Qwen-VL exhibits critical safety failures, producing incorrect and unsafe responses with at least 90%
probability in right-turn scenarios under rainy driving conditions, revealing substantial safety risks. We further
integrate a prompt-level deterministic verifier to obtain guarantees over the privacy of the LLM generation
distribution over a prompt distribution. Our results reveal that Qwen-3 (4B) has at least 24% chance of revealing
private email information under adversarial prompt distributions. The modular structure of Lumos allows
easy modification of specifications, enabling LMS certification to keep pace with the rapidly evolving threat
landscape. We further demonstrate that specification programs written in Lumos can uncover concrete failure
cases in state-of-the-art LMS. Finally, Lumos is simple to program in, requiring only a few constructs, as
evidenced by state-of-the-art large language models generating correct Lumos specifications in zero-shot
settings. Lumos is the first principled, language-based framework for specifying and certifying LMS behaviors,
paving the way towards widespread adoption of certification as a reliable LMS evaluation.

guarantees are paramount for user safety and trust [Singh and Chawla 2025; United Nations
Scientific Advisory Board 2025].
Focusing on LMS safety evaluation, recent work has given the first approaches to formally certify
LMS for particular desirable behaviors such as fairness, correct question answering, and safety,
encoded as mathematical specifications. Specifications constrain LMS input scenarios and check
how frequently their outputs follow desirable behavior on these inputs. [Chaudhary et al. 2024]
have given the first specifications for counterfactual fairness in LLM responses, [Chaudhary et al.
2025] give specifications for reading comprehension in general knowledge and medical domains,
[Wang et al. 2025a] specify for catastrophic risk in multi-turn conversations with LLMs, and [Yeon
et al. 2025] have specifications for correct tool selection by agentic systems.
Existing methods rely on black-box certification because traditional white-box neural network
verification [Berrada et al. 2021; Katz et al. 2017; Singh et al. 2019] is fundamentally incompatible
with modern LMS. White-box techniques fail to scale, suffer from severe precision loss, and require
parameter-level access that is unavailable for proprietary frontier models. Furthermore, the rapid
proliferation of diverse architectures [Alonso et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2024] renders
bespoke white-box certifiers impractical. Because localized failures are inevitable across immense
input spaces (e.g., those found by adversarial attacks [Zou et al. 2023]), practical certification is
statistical [Baluta et al. 2021; Li et al. 2022], formally quantifying the probability of desirable LMS
outputs over target distributions. Consequently, black-box statistical certification provides the
strongest attainable guarantees for real-world deployments and has already been adopted for
evaluation by major industry players [Amazon Artificial General Intelligence 2025].
Specification design has traditionally been a challenging task [Brix et al. 2023; Seshia et al. 2018;
Singh and Chawla 2025] and thus prior works [Bonaert et al. 2021; Shi et al. 2020] on neural
network verification have generally worked with toy specifications (e.g., local robustness, word list
perturbations, etc). This is not useful for LMS which see widespread public-facing usecases, thus re-
quiring certification with respect to numerous practical properties (e.g., multi-turn dialogue safety).
Adapting existing specialized LMS certification works to other practical specifications with large
distributions and domain-specification properties—encompassing natural and adversarial threat
models—creates significant engineering complexity when using ad-hoc scripts. This is because their
specifications of desirable behaviors are deeply entangled with their property of interest, described
informally or at best algorithmically, and generally focused on conversational settings with no
clear extensions to other modalities. Minor changes to the specification require reformulating them
entirely without any scope for knowledge transfer. General-purpose scripts force developers to
manually enforce soundness of certification, rendering evaluations mathematically brittle. Fur-
thermore, they entangle the high-level safety specification with low-level execution details, which
renders the specifications inauditable to non-engineer safety researchers and policy makers and
disables systematic certification optimizations.
What we need are the basic building blocks or constructs that can suggest and be easily composed
to design new specifications. Thus we challenge the bespoke, fragmented nature of current formal
LMS certification and argue for a principled approach for specifying arbitrary LMS prop-
erties—rooted in programming language design—that identifies structural commonalities across
specifications and offers a broadly applicable interface for writing and certifying specifications.
Hence, we develop the first domain-specific language for LMS specification - Lumos1. Designing

such a specification language for LMS poses the following key challenges. (1) Desirable properties
for LMS are often derived from human ethics which are not inherently formal. The prior LMS
certification works have attempted to formalize these as mathematical specifications, but there
are no general constructs/ideas that have been identified to form the basis for a language. (2) LMS
operate on complex modalities, such as text and images, and general formal specification involving
reasoning over them is not well-defined. For example, how can we programmatically prompt a VLM
with diverse images from the point of view of an autonomous vehicle. The immediate solutions
that come up for this task have nothing in common with safety assessment of conversations with
chatbots. Hence, a language to unify all such modalities programmatically is not straightforward.
(3) To cover previously studied specifications, the language should be inherently probabilistic,
allowing random sampling from user-defined prompt distributions, guaranteed to be independent,
and identically-distributed (IID) as required for validity of statistical certification guarantees.
Principle 1: Graphs as an abstraction. The key insight in Lumos that addresses the first chal-
lenge is the pervasiveness of a graphical representation to abstractly model LMS prompts
based on realistic scenarios. The graph nodes represent indivisible practical entities at varying
granularity such as real-world concepts or individual questions in a multi-turn dialogue, and the
edges apply semantic constraints between nodes, connecting related concepts or similar questions
to abstractly model these scenarios. They are commonly used as structured representations of
real-world knowledge in prior literature [Ji et al. 2022; Shimojima 2004]. Graphical representation
is evident for question-answering over knowledge graphs [Chaudhary et al. 2025], established
for conversational safety [Wang et al. 2025a], and applicable even to inherently non-graphical
properties like counterfactual fairness under random jailbreaks [Chaudhary et al. 2024] (see §6.1.2).
Because these graphs symbolically represent interpretable scenarios, they enable developers to
easily reason over discrete semantic concepts presented in the graph nodes to construct meaningful
specifications. Lumos formally abstracts random prompt generation as random subgraph sampling
from a primary graph data structure. Specifically, Lumos utilizes text-rich graphs [Pang et al. 2025],
where nodes and edges carry textual attributes that combine to form concrete LMS prompts.
Principle 2: Native multimodal support. Addressing the second challenge, Lumos provides special
constructs to concretize abstract subgraphs into prompt samples, which can be manipulated by the
programmer to obtain diverse prompts in different modalities such as image, audio, etc. Lumos
defines natural language templates that are populated by sampled subgraph nodes/edges to form
textual prompts. These are further converted to any target modality by calls to external, specialized
modality-specific tools to construct multi-modal prompts.
Principle 3: First-class probabilistic constructs. For the final challenge, Lumos is a probabilistic
programming language. Each specification is modeled as a probabilistic program. Lumos provides
fine-grained control to the programmers to specify arbitrary distributions over subgraphs, pro-
grammatically within the specification, representing variable likelihood of the subgraphs. It allows
the programmer to define a custom probability distribution function over graph elements to form
random subgraphs, and also define the subgraph-to-prompt construction process randomly. Lumos
naturally generates IID prompts and allows using them to observe and classify LMS behaviors
as desirable. IID observations of desirable behavior can be used within existing LMS certification
methods, such as Clopper-Pearson intervals [Chaudhary et al. 2024].

temporal (multi-turn conversation) specifications. Lumos enables thinking about new LMS specifi-

Contributions
(1) Lumos is the first imperative probabilistic programming language over graphs. It consists of
first-class constructs to generate independent and identically distributed prompts from prompt
distributions defined by the programmer, for LMS certification. Lumos is based on a primary
graphical abstract representation of the prompt sample space. Prompts are sampled by sampling
subgraphs and framing them into natural language templates. Subgraph sampling over complex
graphs is made intuitive using a constrained sequential processes involving node sampling steps.
(2) We provide detailed hybrid semantics for Lumos consisting of big-step operational semantics
for the imperative core and denotational semantics for the mathematical and probabilistic constructs.
Lumos consists of a statistical certification core, wherein IID observations of LMS behaviors are
analyzed to estimate the probability of observing desirable behaviors with high confidence.
(3) Lumos is highly expressive to encompass complex relational and temporal specifications, e.g.,
arising for fairness and multi-turn conversation settings, going beyond simple local robustness
properties. Existing LMS specifications can be encoded with Lumos. Moreover, the compositional
and graph abstraction driven design of Lumos enables thinking about new LMS specifications.
We support this argument by presenting the first specifications and certification for VLM safety in
autonomous driving scenarios. With this we show that the Qwen-VL model [Wang et al. 2024] has
an abominably low probability of producing safe responses, at most 10% with high confidence, for
autonomous driving scenarios involving right turns in rainy weather, exposing the hazard posed
by the model. Moreover, execution traces of specification programs in Lumos can unveil specific
failure cases arising within the specification. For example, for the VLM safety case, we find that
the Llava model [Liu et al. 2023] can ignore dangerously close-by road barriers and suggest an
autonomous vehicle to drive ahead. Our results show with 95% confidence that Qwen-3 (4B) has at
least 24% chance of revealing private email information under adversarial prompt distributions.
Minimal core constructs enable SOTA LLMs to write specification programs in Lumos with zero-
shot prompts and thus alleviate manual specification effort further. Our implementation is available
here: https://github.com/uiuc-focal-lab/Lumos.

2.3
Probabilistic Specifications and Certification
A specification is a mathematical expression of a desirable system behavior. For LMS, the input
space (all possible prompts) is too large to test exhaustively, so we use probabilistic specifications
that quantify probabilities over a distribution of inputs. For example: “The probability that an
LMS response is safe in a ’distribution of harm-eliciting’ prompts, is at least 0.99.” Since the true
probability 𝑝is unknown, we use statistical certification to obtain a high-confidence estimate.
This process involves: (1) drawing 𝑁Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) prompts from
the input distribution, (2) evaluating LMS on each and observing 𝑘desirable behaviors (success),
and (3) using (𝑁,𝑘) to compute either a confidence interval [𝑝𝑙𝑜𝑤, 𝑝ℎ𝑖𝑔ℎ] or a point estimate 𝑝∗at
confidence 1−𝛿(e.g., 95%). The interval [𝑝𝑙𝑜𝑤, 𝑝ℎ𝑖𝑔ℎ] contains the true probability 𝑝with probability
1 −𝛿, computed in prior LMS certifiers via Clopper-Pearson method [Clopper and Pearson 1934].
2.4
Language Model Systems (LMS)
We define a Language Model System (LMS) as a black-box that maps a prompt (text, images, or
other modalities) to a response. We focus on LMS built from large, predominantly autoregressive
foundation models. The most common are Large Language Models (LLMs), which generate tokens
by sampling from a distribution over a vocabulary V given prior tokens 𝑥1, . . . ,𝑥𝑛. We also consider
Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which extend this process to condition on both visual and textual
inputs [Liu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2023]. These models are pretrained on large
corpora [Liu et al. 2024], exhibit strong capabilities [Brown et al. 2020; Gemini Team 2024; Liu et al.
2023; Wang et al. 2024], and are evaluated via diverse benchmarks [Hendrycks et al. 2021; Rein
et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2018], yet their capabilities and safety remain insufficiently understood.

Overview
We focus on Language Model Systems (LMS) that collectively denote Large Language Models
(LLMs), Vision Language Models (VLMs), and their compound systems. We design the first specifi-
cation language, Lumos, for complex but desirable LMS properties, such as safety, fairness, and
accuracy of their outputs. In this section, we show an example specification program written in
Lumos to highlight the following salient features of the language.
Graphical representation of scenario space: Lumos abstracts existing LMS specifications [Chaud-
hary et al. 2024, 2025; Wang et al. 2025a] into general graph structures that concisely, enabling
writing probabilistic properties over the graph of all scenarios with domain-specific distributions.
Probabilistic certification: Programs in Lumos define probabilistic specifications over distribu-
tions of scenarios, describing the desired LMS behavior across them. Lumos provides native support
for statistical certification using independent and identically distributed (IID) samples from the
scenario distribution—the only known formal method for certifying LMS to our knowledge.
Compositionality: Lumos consists of a small number of primitives, illustrated below, that can be
composed in a variety of ways to write complex, practical LMS specifications.

RQ1: Lumos can encode specifications like temporal and relational
Lumos is general to express diverse LMS specifications, beyond simple local robustness specifica-
tions. We present specification programs for three complex LMS properties previously implemented

only as ad-hoc algorithms. Subsuming these disparate evaluations into a unified framework demon-

(1) Temporal (§6.1.1): Multi-turn dialogue safety evaluated over stateful conversation graphs.
(2) Relational (§6.1.2): Counterfactual fairness evaluated via paired LMS inferences. While
prior work did not structure this property as a graph, we develop a nuanced encoding that
formally generalizes it within our framework.
(3) Domain-knowledge (§6.1.3): Medical question answering via knowledge graph traversal.
Through these diverse applications, we highlight the pervasiveness of the graph abstraction
to represent the scenarios across virtually any LMS task. The graphs used in these specifications
are realistic and massive: PrimeKG [Chandak et al. 2023] used in the domain-knowledge-driven
specifications (§6.1.3) consists of over 100, 000 nodes and 4𝑀edges, and the question graphs used
in temporal specifications (§6.1.1) contain thousands of nodes.
Crucially, Lumos is the first framework to practically enable certification of arbitrary properties
at this scale; applying traditional deterministic verification to such immense state spaces would fail
to terminate conclusively. Despite these massive scenario spaces, Lumos’s scalability is governed
almost entirely by the LMS inference budget. In our profiling, the local CPU-bound graph operations
resolve with minimal overhead, while the heavily I/O-bound network latency of invoking the target
LMS constituted 99% of the total computation time. Structuring this problem within a unified PL
architecture enables future work to systematically co-optimize graph traversal and model inference.
General evaluator functions J are external tools—such as rule-based heuristics, learned classifiers,
or LLM-as-a-judge frameworks—callable directly from Lumos programs. They are crucial for
evaluating complex perceptual tasks and multimodal LMS behaviors. Keeping them external is
imperative, as natively encoding learned perceptual logic is fundamentally incompatible with the
scope of a discrete DSL. The specific J functions described in the following examples were used
and rigorously justified by prior works. Crucially, they satisfy the statelessness and immutability
contracts necessary to guarantee IID sampling. While certification results are inherently sensitive
to J’s accuracy, designing flawless judges for subjective, domain-specific properties is an open
problem, making detector refinement a strictly orthogonal research direction. By treating J as a
modular callable, Lumos seamlessly leverages state-of-the-art, externally validated evaluators and
allows developers to upgrade them as models improve. We refer readers to the respective papers for
the full certification results, consistent with those from equivalent Lumos specification programs.

optimized this prompt using the adaptive attack specification (§6.1.1). For a baseline comparison, we
simultaneously prompt the LLM to generate Python certification programs (Figure 14, Appendix G).
Natural language descriptions (Appendix G.1) were refined up to 3 times to guide the LLM toward
correct syntax. It finally generated correct Lumos programs for all specifications evaluated in this
paper: Random-walk safety (§3), Adaptive safety (§6.1.1), Counterfactual bias (§6.1.2), Medical QA
(§6.1.3), VLM safety (§6.2), and Privacy (§6.4), shown in Appendix G.2.
Comparing the generated outputs reveals a 2x reduction in lines of code in favor of Lumos.
Furthermore, the Python scripts (Appendix G.3) are hard to assess for correctness due to their
verbosity and low-level logic. They are bloated with redundant certification logic, underscoring the
necessity of a standardized certifier interface over ad-hoc library calls. Lumos’s structural advantage
is starkly visible when comparing similar multi-turn dialogue safety tasks. While the adaptive
specification is a direct logical extension of the random walk baseline, the corresponding Python
implementations obscure this relationship behind arbitrary, unstructured boilerplate. By making
their shared foundational structure syntactically explicit, Lumos avoids this tangling and natively
enables specification transfer. Ultimately, this demonstrates the profound ease of programming in
Lumos and the feasibility of automating LMS certification with LLMs.
7
Related works
Probabilistic programming languages. Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) provide
first-class constructs for distributions, samplers, and probabilistic assertions, with popular exam-
ples in both imperative [Bingham et al. 2018; McCallum et al. 2009; Sampson et al. 2014] and
functional [Dash et al. 2023; Goodman et al. 2008; Scontras et al. 2021] styles. However, existing
PPLs are not easily adapted to text-rich graphs, the key data structure for language model systems
(LMS). They typically rely on user-defined distributions; for instance, Scenic [Fremont et al. 2019]
generates random scenes via user-defined scene graphs [Chang et al. 2023], but unlike Lumos, does
not sample subgraphs from a fixed underlying graph to define scenarios.
DSLs for LLMs. [Beurer-Kellner et al. 2023; Khattab et al. 2023; Okuda and Amarasinghe 2023;
Sharma et al. 2024] are query languages without probabilistic constructs, enabling users to craft
individual prompts for desired LMS responses. They complement Lumos on evaluating LMS.

LMS evaluation. Prior works benchmark their performance [Liang et al. 2023; Mazeika et al.
2024; Xie et al. 2025] or conduct adversarial attacks [Perez et al. 2022; Purpura et al. 2025; Vega
et al. 2023; Xu and Parhi 2025]. Recent work has developed probabilistic certifiers for specific LLM
properties [Chaudhary et al. 2024, 2025; Wang et al. 2025a]. Certification offers formal guarantees,
making it a more reliable evaluation of LMS properties [Singh and Chawla 2025]. Here, we generalize
LMS certification with Lumos to specify and certify arbitrary desirable properties.
8
Conclusion
We present Lumos, the first probabilistic programming language over graphs for princi-
pled, statistical LMS certification. Lumos abstracts prompt distributions as text-rich graphs
and guarantees independent and identically distributed (IID) observations required by statistical
certifiers by sampling IID subgraphs and concretizing them into prompts. Evaluations demonstrate
Lumos’s expressivity across temporal, relational, and knowledge-intensive specifications, enabling
new specifications, yielding the first formal specifications for VLMs in autonomous driving
and LLM privacy. We further show that SOTA LLMs can generate accurate Lumos programs,
paving the way for scalable, AI-assisted LMS certification across a broad spectrum of domains.

Background on Simulators for Multimodal Scenarios
Testing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in dynamic domains like autonomous driving requires
complex multimodal scenarios. These scenarios are often generated using specialized simulators.
CARLA[Dosovitskiy et al. 2017] is an open-source simulator for autonomous driving research. It
renders high-fidelity 3D urban environments, manages physics, and simulates sensors, weather,
and dynamic agents like vehicles and pedestrians. It acts as the world engine that produces the
visual (image) data for a test. Scenic[Fremont et al. 2019; Vin et al. 2023] is a probabilistic program-
ming language used to define scenarios within simulators like CARLA. It allows a user to define
distributions over the spatial and temporal relationships between objects (e.g., "a car is 10-20 meters
*ahead of* the ego vehicle," "a pedestrian is *near* a crosswalk *and* the weather is ’rainy’"). In
our VLM experiments, Scenic defines the scenario logic, and CARLA renders that scenario into the
concrete image fed to the VLM.

You are an expert programmer in LMSpec, a domain-specific probabilistic programming language
for specifying and statistically certifying behaviors of Language Model Systems (LMS). Given a
natural-language description of a desirable LMS property, output ONLY the LMSpec program --
no explanation, no markdown fences, no prose. Use # for inline comments.
WHAT LMSPEC IS
LMSpec is an imperative probabilistic programming language over graphs. A program defines a
distribution over LMS input scenarios, samples n IID scenarios, queries a target LMS L on each,
and passes observations to a statistical certifier to bound the true probability of the desired
behavior. The central abstraction: any scenario space is a graph. Nodes are discrete entities or
choices. Edges encode relationships. A scenario is a subgraph sampled probabilistically from the
full graph.
PROGRAM STRUCTURE
Two parts, always in this order:
(1) Graph declaration -- declares named node and edge variables, then assembles them into G.
Every node and edge must be a named variable declared before use. Inline declaration inside
Graph(...) is not valid. For file-backed graphs use G := getGraph("path").
v1 := Node(L{"attr1", "attr2"})
# declare each node as a named variable
e1 := Edge(v1, v2)
# declare each edge as a named variable
G
:= Graph({v1, v2}, {e1})
# assemble -- NO inline decls inside Graph(...)
(2) Certification block -- estimateProb delta n "CertifierName" : opens the loop;
toEstimate(obs) closes it. The body executes n times independently from the same initial
state, guaranteeing IID samples.
