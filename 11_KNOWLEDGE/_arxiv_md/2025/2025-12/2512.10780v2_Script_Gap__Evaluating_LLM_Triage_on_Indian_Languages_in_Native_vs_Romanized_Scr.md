Script Gap: Evaluating LLM Triage on Indian Languages in Native
vs Romanized Scripts in a Real World Setting
Manurag Khullar∗
Utkarsh Desai
Poorva Malviya
Aman Dalmia

∗School of Computing and Information, University of Pittsburgh
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly
deployed in high-stakes clinical applications in In-
dia. Speakers of Indian languages frequently com-
municate using romanized text rather than native
scripts, yet existing research rarely quantifies or
evaluates this orthographic variation in real world
applications. We investigate how romanization im-
pacts the reliability of LLMs in a critical domain:
maternal and newborn healthcare triage.
We
benchmark leading LLMs on a real world dataset
of user-generated health queries spanning five In-
dian languages and Nepali. Our results reveal con-
sistent degradation in performance for romanized
messages, with gap reaching up to 24 points across
languages and models. We propose and evaluate
an Uncertainty-based Selective Routing method to
close this script gap.
At our partner maternal
health organization alone, this gap could cause
nearly 2 million excess errors in triage. Our find-
ings highlight a critical safety blind spot in LLM-
based health systems: models that appear to un-
derstand romanized input may still fail to act on
it reliably.

Introduction and Related Work
Access to timely and reliable healthcare in many
communities in the global south is severely con-
strained Mehta et al. (2024).
Shortages of med-
ical professionals, overburdened primary-care sys-
tems, and linguistic diversity limit patients’ ability
to obtain high-quality guidance. Large language
models (LLMs) offer a promising path to expand
scalable, multilingual health communication where
traditional services are stretched thin. LLMs are
already being explored for patient-facing applica-
tions, such as message triage, portal-message prior-
itization, and safety-oriented medical question an-
swering (Liu et al., 2025; Masanneck et al., 2024;
Borkowski et al., 2023; Singhal et al., 2025; Kaur
et al., 2025).
These systems aim to flag emer-
gencies in patient portal messages, route complex
queries to clinicians, and generate draft replies that
reduce message burden for care teams (Ren et al.,
2024; Chen et al., 2025).
However, existing LLMs still exhibit limitations
that directly affect their suitability for clinical use.
They can perform inconsistently across languages,
hallucinate medical facts, and exhibit unstable rea-
soning under minor input perturbations.
These
issues are exacerbated for low-resource languages
and informal, patient-generated text, where vari-
ation in spelling, phrasing, and code-mixing can
substantially degrade model reliability.
A growing body of healthcare-focused LLM
benchmarks aims to assess these risks, but they fall
short in several ways. Many are sourced from clin-
ical literature, or expert-curated exams, and fea-
ture synthetic question answering, rather than real
patient-provider conversations Jin et al. (2021);
Pal et al. (2022); Jin et al. (2019); Arora et al.
(2025). Only a small number of datasets feature
real conversations but are limited to English and
Chinese Manes et al. (2024); He et al. (2020), and
virtually none assess triage, arguably the task most
relevant for early patient engagement. As a result,
current benchmarks fail to capture the linguistic
and situational diversity encountered in frontline
care. No wonder a growing body of work shows
LLMs can produce clinically unsafe or inconsistent
advice, even when overall accuracy on QA bench-
marks is high (Diekmann et al., 2025; Draelos et al.,
2025; Bedi et al., 2025).
Furthermore,
beyond the healthcare setting,
handling informal, mixed-script communication at
scale remains a challenge for multilingual LLMs.
For Indian languages in particular, romanized in-
put is pervasive in online communications, yet

most benchmarks overlook.
In this paper, we provide the first benchmark-
ing evaluation of real world LLM triage perfor-
mance on Indian languages across both native and
romanized scripts.
We make four major contri-
butions: (1) We construct a multilingual, multi-
script healthcare triage dataset reflecting authentic
patient-provider conversations. (2) We benchmark
several leading LLMs on this dataset, revealing for
the first time substantial script-dependent dispari-
ties. (3) We conduct exhaustive error analyses to
uncover where and why models disproportionately
fail on romanized inputs. We point out that ro-
manized inputs lead to brittle output, even when
models seem to generate rationales that capture
the user’s intent. (4) We propose an uncertainty-
based selective routing method to close this script
gap, and demonstrate its performance across vari-
ous languages and models.

Dataset and Task
2.1
Clinical Setting and Corpus
We study a de-identified corpus of short What-
sApp messages related to maternal and newborn
care provided by Maternal Health Organization
A. Users, typically mothers, pregnant women, or
caregivers, use a WhatsApp chat interface to sub-
mit free-text queries about pregnancy and new-
born issues. For this work, we restrict attention
to a fixed observation window and use only de-
identified data, yielding a corpus of approximately
133k conversations spanning English, five Indian
languages: Hindi, Telugu, Kannada, Marathi, and
Punjabi, and Nepali. From this pool, we draw a
stratified random sample of 3,153 single-turn user
messages for experiments, ensuring coverage across
languages and message lengths. General statistics
of the dataset are described in Table 1 and Figure 1
.

quiring urgent medical attention; Non-emergency
covers concerns that clearly do not require urgent

Validation Dataset
To assess whether the trends generalize beyond our

our real world corpus, allowing us to isolate the
effect of script choice on model performance.
3
Experimental Setup
3.1
Task
The primary objective is triage classification: given
a user message, the system must assign one of three
triage labels. All models operate on the raw user
messages, without normalization, and are evalu-
ated using a single fixed prompt template, temper-
ature = 0, and full context to ensure parity across
models. In addition to the triage label, models are
instructed to produce a brief reasoning behind the
predicted label.
3.2
Models
We evaluate a variety of models known for their
multilingual capabilities,
organizing them into
three buckets: frontier closed models include GPT-
4o, Claude Sonnet 4.5 (ClaudeS4.5), large open-
weight models include DeepSeek V3, LLaMA -
4 Maverick 17B (LLaMA-4), and Qwen3-Next-
80B-A3B-Instruct (Qwen3-80B), and a compact-
plus-Indic-specialized bucket include Mixtral-7B,
Qwen2.5-7B and Sarvam-M 24B.
3.3
Prompt Template
To select the final fixed prompt, we conducted a
minimal prompt-design sweep on a held-out subset
of 500 randomly sampled messages on real world
dataset, comparing four strategies: (i) zero-shot
prompt; (ii) few-shot prompt with labelled exam-
ples; (iii) Triage rules + knowledge base (KB)
of emergency symptoms with chain of thought
prompt. (iv) Triage rules + KB with direct prompt
instructing the model to directly generate a label.
In our experiment, Triage rules + KB with direct
prompt consistently achieved a higher weighted F1
and emergency recall (Appendix L). We therefore
fix the Triage Rules + KB direct template in Fig-
ure 3 for all experiments (detailed prompt in Ap-

Cross Model Agreement
Following prior work on deep ensembles, we inter-

Diagnosing the Script Gap
In this section, we diagnose the script gap through
four research questions.
RQ1:
Is script gap primarily driven by
differences in underlying clinical content?

(1) translate both native script and romanized
messages into a common pivot language, that is,
English, and (2) translate romanized messages

We then re-evaluate triage classifi-
cation on these normalized subsets using GPT-4o
and Qwen3-80B, with a configuration similar to
the benchmark evaluation. Table 3 shows that for
GPT-4o and Qwen3-80B, native script →English
w-F1 drops 0.6 point and 3.1 points respectively,
indicating models already handle native script in-
put effectively.
In contrast, romanized script in-

GPT-4o and Qwen3-80B narrow the gap.

Does romanization add a triage er-

tive strategy: output tokens drop by 76% for GPT-

and 16% of queries as compared to applying script-
aware prompting to all queries, detailed analysis in

Normalization

7
Discussion
Increasingly, organizations in low-resource settings
are integrating LLM-based solutions into critical
workflows, often driven by anecdotal evidence of
general capability rather than systematic safety
testing for specific downstream tasks. Our study
addresses this gap by providing the evaluation of
how romanized input affects LLM reliability in
a safety-critical healthcare task.
Our most crit-
ical finding is not simply that romanized inputs
degrade performance, but that this degradation
makes model predictions brittle and adds an addi-
tional triage penalty over native script. Models ap-
pear to comprehend romanized input, as evidenced
by their reasoning traces capturing the user’s clin-
ical symptoms, yet still fail to act on that under-
standing reliably at the triage classification stage.
Crucially, we quantify for the first time the preva-
lence of romanized input in a real-world deploy-
ment setting, establishing that for real-world de-
ployment, romanization is not a peripheral edge
case to be accommodated but the dominant mode
of user communication. Therefore, there is a need
to move beyond native script centric benchmark-
ing and develop evaluation frameworks that reflect
how users actually communicate. Across models
and languages, we observe an average performance
penalty of 8.6% on romanized queries. At Mater-
nal Health Organization A’s scale of 41 million
patients, where 56% of messages are romanized,
this could expose approximately 23 million users
to higher triage error rates, potentially causing
nearly 2 million excess misclassifications.
Then,
script gap is not a marginal performance variance
but a critical safety liability.
8
Limitations
One key limitation of our work is language cover-
age; while it is broader than prior works studying
romanized scripts, it spans five Indian languages
and Nepali.
Several widely spoken Indian lan-
guages such as Bengali, Tamil, and Gujarati that
are commonly romanized are absent, as are lan-
guages outside of South Asia where romanization
is prevalent, and extending the analysis to these
languages remains important future work. Second,
we demonstrate the script gap in a clinical triage
setting on chat-based support; while this repre-
sents a critical safety failure given the increased
use of such tools in underserved areas, it remains
an open question whether similar degradation pat-
terns hold across other domains and for different
tasks.
9
Ethical Considerations
All data used in this study is de-identified and col-
lected as part of Maternal Health Organization A’s
pilot studies for evaluating LLM safety. Our work
does not deploy any triage system to real patients;
all evaluations are conducted on historical data.
We emphasize that the triage outputs studied here
are intended as routing support, not as medical ad-
vice. We do not claim that LLMs should replace
medical experts; rather, our work evaluates their
reliability and highlights that romanization of user
inputs makes model outputs brittle. The script gap
we identify underscores the risk of prematurely de-
ploying LLM-based tools in safety-critical settings
without rigorous evaluation across the scripts and
languages that users actually employ.

will be given the user's message (mother/caregiver/pregnant woman).
Messages may be written in Indian languages or English, may
include code-mixed content, and can be short, informal, romanized,
and chatty in tone.
Task:
Classify the message into exactly one of three labels.
Label Definitions:
- Emergency: The message indicates, or could plausibly indicate, a
symptom that may require immediate medical attention or urgent care
. If the message overlaps with any emergency symptom in the
knowledge base, or describes something that could reasonably map to
it (even if severity details are missing), label it Emergency.
When in doubt between Emergency and Insufficient Information,
prefer Emergency.
- Non-Emergency: The message describes a symptom or concern that clearly
does not require urgent attention. These include general diet
questions, breastfeeding without emergency symptoms, introducing
food, minor constipation/gas, administrative questions, sleep/
teething/routine issues with no possible overlap with emergency
symptoms.
- Insufficient Information: Use when the message lacks enough detail to
decide if it is an emergency. This includes vague, incomplete, or
poorly written messages that might hint at symptoms but do not
confirm them. If a symptom clearly matches the emergency list,
classify as Emergency. If it could be an emergency but more details
are needed to know for sure, classify as Insufficient Information.
Analysis Rules (must follow exactly):
1) Treat questions as reports of situations that occurred.
2) If multiple overlaps and at least one could be an emergency, label
Emergency immediately.
3) Severity cues (e.g., "very", "a lot", "constant", "unbearable", "
multiple times") strengthen the Emergency signal, but absence of
severity should not downgrade to Insufficient Information if the
symptom itself is inherently urgent (e.g., bleeding, localised pain
, difficulty breathing, high fever, jaundice).
4) For ambiguous mentions (e.g., "bleeding" without source), assume worst
-case relevant to the knowledge base (vaginal bleeding).
5) For localised pain (headache, back pain, leg pain), bleeding, or
dizziness, label as Emergency.
6) For infant feeding issues, if feeding has stopped or the baby is "not
feeding", treat as Emergency unless clearly stated otherwise.
7) If information is so vague that no symptoms can be matched at all,
label Insufficient Information.
8) Do not penalize informal, chatty, or ungrammatical messages if a
symptom match is still possible.
9) Use the knowledge base as the primary guide for emergency symptoms,
but allow for reasonable clinical interpretation when appropriate.
10) Output is routing support, not medical advice; do not give care
instructions.
