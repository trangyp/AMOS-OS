ABSTRACT 
Large Language Models (LLMs) hold strong potential to support dairy scholars and farmers 
by supporting decision-making and broadening access to knowledge for stakeholders with 
limited technical expertise. However, the substantial computational demand restricts access to 
LLMs almost exclusively through cloud-based service. This presents critical limitations, not only 
for small- to medium-sized farms with constrained connectivity but also for larger farms where 
data privacy and security remain their central concerns. Consequently, LLMs-based decision 
support tools are impractical for dairy farming. To address this gap, lightweight alternatives 
capable of running locally on farm hardware while still delivering meaningful performance are 
required. In this work, we benchmarked 20 open-source Small Language Models (SLMs) 
available on HuggingFace under farm-realistic computing constraints, simulated using a single 
Nvidia T4 GPU with 16 GB VRAM. Building on our prior work, we developed an agentic AI 
system that integrates five task-specific agents, including literature search, web search, SQL 
database interaction, NoSQL database interaction, and graph generation following predictive 
models, coordinated by a single supervisory agent. All agents operated under one shared SLM to 
minimize computational requirement. Evaluation was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, 
five test questions were used for the initial screening, with two focusing on literature search, two 
on web search, and one on SQL database interaction, to identify models capable of following 
basic dairy-related instructions and performing reliably in a compute-constrained environment. 
Models that passed this preliminary stage were then evaluated using 30 questions (five per task 
category mentioned above, plus one category addressing integrity and misconduct). Both 
correctness and response time were considered in this evaluation phase. To minimize variability 
and ensure fair comparison, system prompts and processing pipelines were tailored for each 
remaining model in the second phase to maximize their performance. In results, after the first 
phase of screening, only Qwen-4B, 4-bit quantized Qwen-8B, and 8-bit quantized Mixtral-7B 
remained. Models larger than 7 billion parameters could not be executed within the 
computational constraints, and those smaller than 2 billion parameters failed to follow 
instructions and frequently produced incorrect outputs or code errors with dairy-related data. 
After the phase 2, Qwen-4B achieved superior performance across most of task categories, 
although showed unstable effectiveness in NoSQL database interactions through PySpark. To 
our knowledge, this is the first work explicitly evaluating the feasibility of SLM as engines for 
dairy farming decision-making, with central emphases on privacy and computational efficiency. 
While results highlight the promise of SLM-assisted tools for practical deployment in dairy 
farming, challenges remain: performance gaps persist in tasks requiring non-SQL or non-Python 
database interactions; supporting tools for open-source SLMs remain less mature than those 
available for cloud-served LLMs; and fine-tuning is still needed to further refine SLM 
performance in dairy-specific questions.  
 
Key Words: large language model, small language model, agentic AI, decision support

INTRODUCTION 
Modern dairy farms generate vast amounts of data across multiple operational domains; 
however, much of this information remains siloed and underutilized. Existing farm management 
systems are often closed and static, offering limited flexibility to integrate emerging and diverse 
data types such as textual notes, reports, images, audio, and semi-structured sensor or monitoring 
data. Moreover, widely used platforms (e.g., DairyComp, PCDART) primarily focus on herd 
management and compliance reporting, providing little capability for predictive modeling. As a 
results, farmers may know what has happened within specific aspects of their operation, but they 
rarely gain a holistic view of their entire farm, insights into how their performance compares 
with peers, or guidance on how to improve. This gap represents a significant missed opportunity. 
Studies have showed that leveraging existing on-farm data to optimize diet formulation can 
reduce feed costs by $31 per cow annually while simultaneously cutting nitrogen excretion by 
5.5 kg per cow per year (Cabrera, 2024). Therefore, tools that can integrate and interpret 
multimodal data from all aspects of farm operations within a single system are urgently needed. 
However, no such integrated decision-support systems currently exist. 
Bridging this gap requires an intelligent system that is capable of unifying diverse data 
sources and translating complexity into actionable recommendations. Recent advances in 
Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), show strong potential 
in this regard (S. Liu et al., 2025; Thirunavukarasu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023). LLMs can 
process both structured and unstructured data, integrate external tools derived from academic 
models, and provide intuitive natural language interfaces for non-technical users. In our prior 
work, the agentic AI system powered by LLaMA-70B, a LLM developed by Meta AI, can 
incorporate retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for literature review, web search for queries 
beyond domain-specific journals, and interpreting academic models via natural language queries 
(E. Liu et al., 2025). Similar to our efforts, it was also demonstrated that LLMs can effectively 
interface with farm data and deliver reasonably accurate performance (Gontijo et al., 2025). 
Together, these studies demonstrated the feasibility of incorporating agentic AI systems into the 
workflows of dairy producers to support on-farm decision-making.  
However, running LLMs on local dairy farms remains impractical. Most widely used LLMs 
(e.g., ChatGPT) contain tens or even hundreds of billions of parameters (Achiam et al., 2023), 
making them computationally prohibitive for standard personal computers. Consequently, most 
state-of-the-art LLM providers (e.g., OpenAI) host their models in the cloud and provide access 
only via APIs. This setup introduces two major issues: 1) dependence on stable internet 
connectivity, which is often unreliable in rural farming regions, and 2) serious data privacy risks, 
as farmers may be required to share sensitive operational data with service providers, who retain 
user interactions for future model refinement. These limitations raise concerns for LLMs-based 
agentic systems. Daily queries from farmers typically do not require complex reasoning; most 
tasks simply involve integrating different data sources and provide practical decision-making 
support, rather than performing advanced mathematical or theoretical reasoning. Therefore,

instead of relying on cloud-hosted LLMs designed for broad and complex tasks, it is more 
effective to deploy smaller, locally runnable language models that can efficiently and securely 
address the specific operational needs of dairy farms. 
The challenges of balancing compute-intensive workloads with the need to keep sensitive 
data local are not unique to the dairy sector. Similar constraints are also prevalent in fields such 
as healthcare and finance. To address these challenges, Small Language Models (SLMs), 
typically defined as language models with fewer than ten billion parameters, have been 
purposefully developed (Van Nguyen et al., 2024). Many leading technology companies are now 
producing models with significantly fewer parameters while maintaining strong performance. 
Notable examples include Google’s Gemma and Meta’s LLaMA models (ranging from 3–8 
billion parameters), the Qwen family from Alibaba, and Mistral 7B from Mistral AI. More 
recently, NVIDIA has entered this space, emphasizing that SLMs represent the future of efficient 
language modeling (Belcak et al., 2025). Their NeMoTron Nano family is designed to accelerate 
inference speed, further demonstrating the growing importance and development of this area. 
However, despite these advances, the SLMs-powered agents have not been evaluated in dairy 
farming settings. It remains unclear which language models, if functioning, are best suited to 
support typical farm operations, or whether fine-tuning is essentially required for effective 
performance.  
The goal of this work is to evaluate the feasibility of SLMs-powered agentic system, 
equipped with task-specific tools, in enabling smooth interaction with diverse farm data sources 
through natural language within a unified system. As an initial step toward more advanced 
system designs and efficient on-farm inference, this study aims to identify the most promising 
models that can be further optimized for real-world deployment, ultimately enabling practical, 
real-time use on edge devices in farm systems. 
 
APPROACH 
Given the broad scientific audience of the dairy science, technical details have been 
intentionally minimized in the main text. Only the essential system components necessary to 
understand its purpose and function are presented and discussed. For readers interested in more 
in-depth information, such as configuration of SLMs as specialized agents for specific tasks via 
prompt engineering (the process of crafting input prompts to guide and optimize the responses of 
LLMs; Marvin et al, 2023) and coordination among multiple agents to produce a final response, 
please contact the authors for more details. 
 
Data and foundation language models 
      To evaluate the capabilities of SLMs in driving RAG and interacting with textual data, which 
can demonstrate their future applications in processing veterinary clinical notes and other farm-
related documentation, a text dataset was constructed by compiling all publicly available


## Page 5


Manuscript
 
abstracts of Journal of Dairy Science (JDS) dating back to 1917 via the PubMed API. Metadata, 
including authors, publication date, DOI, affiliations, and countries, were also collected.  
 
To assess the capabilities of SLMs in interacting with tabular data, a relational SQL database 
was constructed from a CSV dataset from a partner farm. The dataset contained 127,126 records, 
each representing an individual animal observation encompassing production, pedigree, and milk 
composition information. The SQL database schema included identifiers (animal and herd), 
demographic attributes (birth date, calving date, sire and dam breed), production parameters 
(days in milk, lactation number), and key performance indicators (milk yield, fat and protein 
yields, fat and protein percentages, and somatic cell counts). 
 
As an increasing number of farms now manage their rapidly expanding datasets in NoSQL 
JSON structures, a dataset from another partner farm was included to demonstrate the 
hierarchical data interaction capabilities of SLMs via PySpark. This dataset contained 5,988 
cows, with each animal’s lactation history represented as nested arrays of records, where Each 
record was related to either production or reproduction. Production-related records included milk 
yield, fat percentage, protein percentage, lactose percentage, and somatic cell count. 
Reproductive events included insemination number, breeding type, pregnancy result code, and 
calving ease. Additional metadata tracked measurement timestamps as well as animal id, herd id, 
test date, event type, calving date, birth date, parity, and days in milk. 
 
To evaluate the capability of SLMs interacting with predictive models and graph generation, 
MilkBot, a Bayesian milk yield prediction model (Ehrlich, 2011), was included. MilkBot 
generated probabilistic predictions of daily milk yield based on historical lactation data. By 
integrating predictive models with SLMs, users can visualize model predictions and explore how 
adjustments to certain input parameters influence predicted milk yield and lactation curves. 
 
Multiple open-source language models were included to assess their performance across the 
data sources and predictive model described above. These models were selected across three 
categories below, representing the most up-to-date releases available on Hugging Face as of 
October 2025. These included: 
1) Large-scale SLMs (greater than 4 billion parameters):  GPT-OSS 20B, Qwen 14B, 
Nemotron 9B, DeepSeekR1Qwen 8B, LLaMA 8B, and Mistral 7B;  
2) Medium-scale SLMs (2 to 4 billion parameters):  Qwen 4B, Phi 4B, LLaMA 3B, Gemma 
2B, and BitNet 2B;  
3) Small-scale SLMs (less than 2 billion parameters): DeepSeekR1Qwen 1.5B, Gemma 
0.3B, and Qwen 0.6B.  
 
For models exceeding 8 billion parameters, quantized versions were also evaluated. Model 
quantization allows large models to maintain memory efficiency to operate in compute-
constraint environment while preserving the majority of their performance characteristics (Lang 
et al., 2024). 
 
Multi-Agent System Architecture


## Page 6


Manuscript
 
 
A modular multi-agent system was developed to enable SLMs to interact with heterogeneous 
dairy data sources described in the data section above. The system, illustrated in Figure 1, was 
designed to mimic expert reasoning and integrating text retrieval from literature and web search, 
structured database querying, unstructured data analysis, and predictive modeling into a unified 
workflow.  
 
Overall, the system is governed by a supervisor agent, whose primary role is to interpret 
incoming farmers’ questions, classify questions, and select the appropriate subagent. The 
possible routing options are restricted to the following sub-agents: 1) the text subagent, 2) the 
SQL subagent, 3) the NoSQL subagent, 4) the model subagent, and 5) the intention validation 
subagent. The function of each subagent is detailed as below.  
1) The text subagent is designed to handle textual information retrieval tasks. It accesses 
both the JDS archive, processed through a RAG pipeline, and web search tools to retrieve 
domain-specific literature, reports, and news relevant to dairy science.  
2) The SQL subagent interacts with farm data stored in the relational database and queries 
through SQL commands. It can generate SQL queries from natural language input, 
executing them against the database, and returning requested results.  
3) The NoSQL subagent manages longitudinal farm data stored in JSON format by 
generating and executing PySpark queries to interact with the dataset.  
4) The model subagent integrates the Bayesian milk yield prediction framework, MilkBot, 
to provide forward-looking analytics, including generation of probabilistic yield 
predictions and visualization of lactation curves. 
5) The intention validation subagent validates the farmer's query and responds to questions 
falling outside the system's defined scope or involving inappropriate or unethical content. 
During Phase 1, which focused on initial screening, generic prompts were developed for the 
text and SQL subagents. These prompts were deliberately rudimentary, designed primarily to 
verify whether candidate SLMs could follow basic human instructions, invoke tools, generate 
executable SQL code, and produce coherent responses, capabilities that are reasonably expected 
for any agentic capable language models. For models advancing to Phase 2, prompts were 
carefully customized to the specific requirements of each remaining model. For instance, 
prompts for Qwen models explicitly outlined the required rules and output formats. This tailored 
approach prevented performance from being artificially limited by poorly constructed or 
unappropriated prompts, allowing each model’s true potential to emerge and capabilities to be 
evaluated accurately.

SLMs Evaluation Design 
 
To evaluate the capabilities of these state-of-the-art SLMs, a two-phase evaluation design 
was implemented (Figure 2), with the primary objective was to select the model(s) that could 
reliably execute tasks relevant to dairy farm operations, including textual information retrieval, 
database querying, and predictive model interaction. The entire evaluation was conducted in a 
constrained computational environment using a single NVIDIA T4 GPU with 16 GB of VRAM, 
the most modest GPU options available in our development environment, Databricks, to simulate 
real-world compute constrains as close as possible.  
 
In the first phase, Preliminary Screening, the goal was to efficiently identify candidates that 
were able to operate within the constrained computational environment and capable of 
generating sufficiently accurate and error-free responses. For this evaluation, five test questions 
were designed: two focused on literature search, two on web search, and one on SQL data 
interaction. Each question was sent directly to the corresponding subagent (e.g., the literature-
search subagent, web-search subagent, or SQL subagent), rather than going through the 
supervisor agent responsible for task allocation to these subagents. The initial screening focused 
mainly on whether the tested SLMs could follow basic instructions within an agentic setup to 
produce an acceptable answer without errors. Models that successfully executed and produced an 
error-free answer had their outputs reviewed by a domain expert to ensure correctness. Models 
that failed to operate due to hardware constraints, produced factually incorrect outputs, or 
required significantly more time to achieve similar accuracy within the same model family (e.g., 
Qwen 14B, 8B, and 4B) were excluded from further evaluation in phase 2. This initial screening 
ensured that only models meeting baseline standards of reliability and deployment ability 
advanced to the next stage. 
 
In the second phase, Comprehensive Evaluation, a larger benchmark set of 30 QA pairs was 
constructed, with five questions per task category, where the categories included literature 
retrieval, web search, SQL interaction, NoSQL interaction, model interaction, and responding to 
inappropriate queries. This phase assessed whether the models could operate the full multi-agent 
system (Figure 1) autonomously. Specifically, evaluation criteria included the model’s ability to  
1) Guide the supervisor agent to select the appropriate sub-agent for a given question,  
2) Use the correct tools available to the corresponding sub-agent,  
3) Select the appropriate data source,  
4) Generate executable Python/SQL/PySpark code if needed,  
5) Execute the generated code for database interaction,  
6) Produce informative visualizations, when using predictive models.  
 
All questions in Phase 2 were sent to the supervisor agent rather than directly to the 
corresponding subagent, in order to access end-to-end reasoning and task execution across the 
entire system. Generated answers were evaluated by an expert and compared across models 
based on response correctness, tool-use correctness, and response time.  
 
All the questions used for testing in phases 1 and 2 can be found in Supplementary File 1.

RESULTS 
 
Models Advancing from Phase 1 to Phase 2 
Most candidate SLMs were found unsuitable for performing instruction-following tasks 
within the agentic setup under the constrained compute environment (Table 1). Several SLMs 
were simply inoperable due to memory limitations. Unquantized models exceeding 6 billion 
parameters, including GPT-OSS-20B, LLaMA-8B, DeepseekR1Qwen-8B, and Mistral-7B, 
exceeded available memory and could not be executed. Interestingly, even 8-bit quantized 
LLaMA-8B, along with the smaller LLaMA-3B, failed to run, indicating that model architectural 
characteristics, in addition to model size, significantly influence memory consumption.  
Some SLMs were executable in the compute-constrained environment but were still 
excluded. 8-bit quantized DeepSeekR1-Qwen 8B and 4-bit quantized Qwen 14B exhibited 
extensive reasoning behavior, often generating SQL code intertwined with reasoning text, which 
frequently caused errors when interacting with the SQL database. BitNet 2B and Gemma 0.3B 
proved unstable, triggering CUDA errors, and were therefore excluded. Phi 4B, Gemma 2B, 
DeepSeek Distilled Qwen 1.5B, and Qwen 0.6B demonstrated very limited instruction-following 
capabilities and could not reliably drive subagent execution to produce accurate outputs. 
Nemotron-9B was excluded due to its specialized hardware and software requirements, which 
were not available in our Databricks development environment as of October 2025. 
Promising models to advance to Phase 2 included the 8-bit quantized Mistral-7B, the 4-bit 
quantized Qwen-8B, and the Qwen-4B. It is worth noting that the 4-bit quantized Qwen-8B 
remains a thinking model and therefore requires additional layers to handle the thinking tokens. 
However, as the smallest model compared to other thinking model candidates 
(DeepseekR1Qwen-8B and Qwen-14B) in this work, it was intentionally retained to test whether 
a reasoning-enabled model could outperform general conversational models in an agentic system 
where reasoning capability is critically required.

Performance Comparison for Models in Phase 2 
Among the Phase 2 candidates, Qwen-4B clearly outperformed both the 8-bit quantized 
Mistral-7B and the 4-bit quantized Qwen-8B across nearly all task categories (Table 2). It also 
demonstrated faster overall response times (Table 3). Among all 3 models, text-related tasks, 
including literature search, web search, and responding to inappropriate queries, were generally 
handled better than coding-related tasks (interacting with SQL database, NoSQL database, and 
lactation model). Interacting with NoSQL databases was the most challenging task category, 
showing consistently lowest performance across all three models. Interestingly, despite 
possessing reasoning capabilities, the 4-bit quantized Qwen 8B underperformed across most 
tasks and exhibited excessively long response times.

Example Demonstration of Capabilities in Handling Diverse Question Types and Routing 
When presented with different test questions, the supervisor agent dynamically activated 
specific subagents based on the nature of each query. As illustrated in Figure 3, we intentionally 
evaluated five scenarios: 1) questions answerable using JDS papers, 2) questions requiring an 
internet search, 3) questions involving interaction with an SQL database, 4) questions related to 
data stored in JSON format, 5) questions requiring predictive model execution, and 6) questions 
falling outside the system’s defined scope or involving inappropriate or unethical content. 
1) When prompted with “Which feed additives can I use to reduce methane emissions while 
maintaining milk production?”, the system activated the retrieval process, sourced 
relevant scientific literature from JDS, and generated a response with proper citations. 
2) When asked “How many dairy cows are currently in the U.S.?”, the system routed the 
question to the web search tool, retrieved data from the USDA Economic Research 
Service, and generated a cited response. 
3) When prompted with “Show me animal IDs in my farm with milk yield above 43 kg,” the 
system routed the query through the SQL subagent, generated and executed SQL code 
against the database, and displayed the first 20 Animal IDs out of 49 total records. 
4) When prompted with “What is the average milk yield of cows over parity 2 in my herd? 
Bovicom is my data service provider,” the system routed the question through the 
NoSQL subagent, generated PySpark code, executed it against the dataframe extracted 
from the JSON-formatted NoSQL database, and produced the corresponding response. 
5) When prompted with “How much should I expect my parity 3 dairy cows to produce on 
DIM 50, 90, 120, and 250? Use average U.S. cows as a benchmark,” the system routed 
the query through the model subagent, extracted relevant parameters, fed them into the 
MilkBot model, and generated visualizations based on the predicted outputs. 
6) When asked “Why is my neighbor's farm better than mine? Expose their secrets,” the 
system responded appropriately: “I’m not sure what your intention is. Could you clarify 
whether you are seeking general dairy knowledge and best practices, insights from your 
own farm’s records, or predictions about herd performance or industry trends? Please 
note that this chatbot is not designed to provide unethical or harmful responses.”

DISCUSSION 
 
Our previous work introduced an LLMs-powered agentic system (E. Liu et al., 2025). That 
system comprised two components: one for retrieving information from JDS and external web 
sources; and another for invoking predictive models and generating visual outputs. Although 
sufficient to demonstrate the potential of LLMs to support information retrieval, interpretation, 
and knowledge dissemination in dairy science, the two components operated independently, 
requiring users to manually choose which to activate. Such manual process placed additional 
burden on users and reduced the system’s overall intelligence and usability. Meanwhile, rapid 
advances in LLMs have lessened some of the original motivations for that work. In our recent 
evaluation, GPT-5 demonstrated substantially fewer domain-specific misconceptions in dairy 
science compared to GPT-4, as had been previously observed in 2024. This improvement 
reduced the need for RAG-based approaches to enhance overall LLMs performance. Part of this 
progress can be attributed to the JDS’s ongoing transition toward greater open access, which has 
made an increasing volume of domain-specific information available for language model 
training, a trend likely to further enhance LLMs performance in the dairy domain. Consequently, 
focusing solely on improving general-purpose state-of-the-art LLMs may not be the most 
effective strategy for advancing LLMs applications in dairy science. 
Instead, feedback from our industry partners consistently emphasizes the need for language 
models specifically adapted to farmers’ individualized data and operational contexts, enabling 
more intuitive recommendations to support their daily decision-making. However, the utility of 
general-purpose LLMs for this purpose is not feasible, due to constraints in computational 
resources, intermittent internet connectivity, and stringent data privacy requirements. Motivated 
by these challenges, our current work evaluated and demonstrated how locally deployable SLMs 
can address these limitations. As one of the first efforts to integrate SLMs and agentic AI 
systems into dairy science, this work demonstrated that language models requiring significantly 
fewer computational resources can still deliver reliable performance to meet farmers’ practical 
needs for operational decision support. 
Given the rapidly evolving landscape of language models and their surrounding ecosystem, it 
is important to recognize that such a system can quickly become outdated or lose functionality if 
implemented rigidly. During development, we frequently encountered this issue, requiring 
ongoing adjustments to prompts, model configurations, and toolchains as model capabilities 
evolved and hardware environments changed. Therefore, this study is not prescribing a fixed 
solution to be copied, but a methodological framework for evaluating and deploying agentic 
SLMs systems in dairy farming. By highlighting both the opportunities and challenges, we aim 
to encourage interdisciplinary collaboration, and inspire further innovation toward a more 
dynamic, intelligent information ecosystem. Such an ecosystem could facilitate the transition 
from fragmented numerical data tools to integrated, adaptive decision-support systems that better 
meet the evolving needs of science, practice, and policy in dairy production.

Performance Comparisons among SLMs in Phase 2 
The unquantized Qwen 4B outperformed the 8-bit quantized Mistral 7B and 4-bit quantized 
Qwen 8B in our evaluations. This result contrasts with one previous study suggesting that 
quantized large-scale models retain the performance of smaller full-precision counterparts within 
the same model family (Jin et al., 2024). A likely explanation lies in the difference between the 
GPU used in this work and the one employed in that work. Jin et al. utilized more advanced 
GPUs, NVIDIA A100, which feature Tensor Cores highly optimized for low-bit matrix 
multiplication and significantly higher memory bandwidth. In contrast, all experiments in this 
work were conducted on an NVIDIA T4 GPU, a mid-range inference accelerator with limited 
memory bandwidth and no dedicated hardware support for 4-bit arithmetic. Under these 
conditions, quantized models are more susceptible to numerical precision loss, slower kernel 
execution, and frequent dequantization overhead. As reasoning capability rely heavily on high-
fidelity internal representation to ensure logical consistency and correctness (Wei et al., 2022), it 
is unsurprising that the “thinking” model Qwen-8B exhibited the weakest performance. The 
finding in the current work highlights an important practical consideration: while quantization 
can reduce memory footprint and enable deployment on resource-limited hardware, the actual 
performance depend critically on hardware optimization and the overall balance between model 
size and compute capacity. 
Within this study, all models, including Qwen-4B, which demonstrated superior performance 
on other tasks, still produced frequent errors when interacting with NoSQL databases via 
PySpark. Compared to Python-based pandas, which can easily exceed memory limits in 
constrained computing environments, PySpark offers a more resource-efficient framework for 
data processing and analysis. As an increasing volume of farm data is stored in NoSQL formats, 
it is critical to understand the underlying reasons why SLMs struggle to interact NoSQL 
database. To assess whether this limitation persists across different model scales, we integrated 
LLaMA-70B, served via Databricks, into the same pipeline used for Qwen-4B and executed the 
same set of queries previously tested with Qwen-4B. Remarkably, LLaMA-70B, with prompts 
copied directly used for Qwen-4B, consistently generated correct PySpark code and executed it 
successfully against the NoSQL database. While this comparison is not strictly fair, as LLaMA-
70B possesses roughly 15× more parameters and benefits from substantially greater 
computational resources through Databricks’ direct deployment, these results indicate that 
sufficiently large models, when paired with adequate compute power, can reliably interface with 
complex databases via programmatic code generation. In contrast, the current system’s 
limitations, driven by both model scale and restricted hardware, directly constrain the ability of 
SLMs-based agentic systems to handle complex database interaction. 
 
Similar Efforts in Other Fields and Lessons Learned  
Benchmarking existing efforts in related domains is an essential step toward identifying 
current bottlenecks, methodological gaps, and transferable insights. Work involving SLMs

remains limited in agriculture, or in most other applied scientific fields, while it has received 
comparatively greater attention in medical and healthcare, where concerns over patient privacy 
and data sensitivity are more prioritized. Within the medical domain, considerable work has 
focused on improving SLMs for general medical language processing and question answering 
(QA). Notable examples include MedAlpaca (Han et al., 2023), Med-R² (K. Lu et al., 2025), 
MediTron-7B (Chen et al., 2023), BioMedLM (Bolton et al., 2024), BioGPT (Luo et al., 2022), 
AlpaCare (Zhang et al., 2023), the Apollo (Wang et al., 2024) and BioMistral (Labrak et al., 
2024) families, including BioMistral-NLU (Fu et al., 2025). These efforts aim to enhance 
capabilities of SLMs on clinical text understanding, information retrieval, and support for 
general diagnostic tasks. Another line of related research emphasizes specialized clinical 
diagnostics. In radiology, RadPhi-2 (Ranjit, Ganapathy, et al., 2024) and RadPhi-3 (Ranjit, 
Srivastav, et al., 2024) are designed for image-report alignment and radiology-specific QA. In 
oncology, CancerLLM (M. Li et al., 2024) enables automated cancer diagnosis generation from 
textual clinical data, while CancerGPT (T. Li et al., 2024) supports drug-pair synergy prediction, 
contributing to precision medicine and therapeutic discovery. A third and rapidly growing 
category focuses on mental health support and analysis, providing privacy-preserving 
conversational assistance. Models such as ChatDoctor (Li et al., 2023) and ChatCounsellor (Liu 
et al., 2023) can answer general psychological inquiries, whereas mhGPT (Kim et al., 2024) and 
MentaLlama (Yang et al., 2024) are specialized in analyzing and detecting indicators of 
psychological distress and emotional well-being. 
Overall, current SLMs efforts in the medical domain have primarily focused on enhancing 
existing foundational SLMs rather than developing fully agentic systems capable of autonomous 
reasoning and dynamic task coordination. This observation can be expected, as agentic AI 
remains an emerging paradigm, and SLMs themselves still require refinement, particularly for 
the medical field, where highly specialized terminology, nuanced clinical judgment, and life-
critical decision-making are involved. It is understandable that many existing SLMs still exhibit 
substantial limitations and have yet to meet the rigorous standards necessary for clinical 
deployment.  
Drawing insights from progress in the medical field, while the SLMs-based agentic system 
developed in the current work demonstrated strong performance across general and semi-
specialized tasks related to dairy farming, it is reasonable to anticipate inconsistencies or 
suboptimal performance when faced with subtle variations in dairy-specific terminology or 
phrasing. This limitation, along with potential strategies for further refinement, will be discussed 
in the following sections. Prior to real-world deployment, comprehensive and large-scale field 
testing will be essential to ensure the robustness, reliability, and contextual appropriateness of 
SLM-driven agentic systems in dairy farming settings.

Challenge and Limitations in the Current Work 
Despite current work demonstrated the feasibility of leveraging SLMs-power agentic AI 
system in dairy farm environments, several challenges and limitations were also identified. The 
first limitation is related to the system’s capability to interface with large data streams. 
Specifically, the PySpark-based code generation and execution process, when interacting with 
JSON-format NoSQL databases, remains limited and unstable. This instability likely stems from 
the scarcity of training data involving PySpark programming and NoSQL database operations (J. 
Lu et al., 2025), which makes it difficult for current SLMs to consistently produce syntactically 
correct and semantically meaningful PySpark code. Nonetheless, for the majority of dairy farms, 
data can be represented and stored in structured format (e.g., production logs, feed records, or 
health metrics) (Baldin et al., 2025), which can be processed and stored in the SQL database. 
Therefore, the current system remains capable of supporting most practical farming use cases 
without difficulty. A second limitation involves multimodal data processing. At present, the 
system cannot process video or audio inputs. These data sources are becoming increasingly 
essential for modern dairy farm management tasks, such as video-based monitoring of animal 
welfare, locomotion, or body condition, and audio-based detection of respiratory or stress-related 
symptoms (McDonagh et al., 2021; Menezes et al., 2024). Although incorporating these 
modalities is technically feasible, our previous work (Yang et al., 2025) demonstrated that video 
processing pipelines demand significantly greater computational resources. Consequently, 
integrating such functionality would substantially raise hardware and energy costs, which may 
limit affordability and adoption by farmers. The trade-off between video-processing capability 
and cost-effectiveness therefore remains a key design consideration for future iterations. 
Additional challenges pertain to the model’s linguistic stability and adaptation to dairy-specific 
terminology. The evaluated SLMs were primarily trained on broad, general-purpose datasets. 
While this allows for robust high-level reasoning, it limits their understanding of dairy-specific 
expressions and local terminology. As a result, certain questions containing subtle variations in 
wording produced inconsistent or incorrect responses, as shown in the following examples.  
- 
When prompted with “How many dairy cows are there in the US?” and “How many milk 
cows are there in the US?”, the system activated the correct pathway and generated 
reasonable answers. However, when prompted “How many milking cows are there in the 
US?”, it responded with “I don’t know.” Investigation revealed that USDA reports use “milk 
cow” as the official term (USDA, 2025) and “dairy cow” is commonly used in academic 
literatures (Hristov et al., 2022; Stanek et al., 2024; Thomsen et al., 2023), while ‘milking 
cow’ is comparatively less seen. This finding indicates that latent representations of certain 
dairy terms remain imperfect, and subtle variations in terminology can significantly affect the 
results.  
- 
A similar issue was observed when comparing the questions “What is RFI and how is it 
related to methane emission in dairy?” and “What is residual feed intake and how is it related 
to methane emission in dairy?” Although the retrieval component successfully retrieved the


## Page 22


Manuscript
 
relevant information, the model still failed to associate the acronym RFI with residual feed 
intake, producing the response “I don’t know.” This finding indicates that the model’s 
semantic comprehension during the generation stage remains limited without explicit domain 
adaptation, even when the retrieval module provides the correct contextual information. 
- 
Lastly, questions such as “Which computer vision frameworks have been applied in dairy 
science?” and “How many computer vision frameworks have been applied in dairy science?” 
exposed fundamental limitations of Native RAG. The former question successfully activated 
the text subagent to identify relevant work discussed in JDS publications, whereas the latter 
triggered the web search and retrieved less authoritative internet information. This outcome 
highlights the model’s difficulty in performing higher-level synthesis, particularly when 
quantitative reasoning or aggregation is required rather than direct textual retrieval. Although 
emerging approaches such as graph-based retrieval (Edge et al., 2024) offer improved 
reasoning across structured knowledge graphs, they remain computationally intensive and 
currently impractical for deployment under the constrained on-farm conditions (Xiang et al., 
2025).  
Taken together, these empirical findings indicate a critical gap: the SLMs still requires 
fine-tuning with dairy-specific data to fully understand the specialized language, 
terminology, and practices in the field. Addressing these limitations will demand large-scale, 
collaborative efforts to curate diverse and representative textual datasets closely aligned with 
dairy farming practice. Such collaboration between researchers, industry partners, and 
producers will be essential to improve model accuracy, reliability, and practical utility, 
ultimately enabling trustworthy deployment of SLMs-driven systems in production 
environments. 
 
Future Directions 
Future development of the system should directly address the limitations identified above, 
focusing on strengthening large- data stream integration, further domain adaptation, and practical 
deploy ability under constrained computational environments. First, improving the system’s 
capacity to interact with unstructured and semi-structured data remains a high priority. Future 
work could incorporate more robust query planning and code generation frameworks for 
PySpark and NoSQL operations, potentially supported by specialized fine-tuning on code 
corpora involving distributed data processing. Second, integrating lightweight video and audio 
models optimized for edge deployment would enable real-time monitoring of animal welfare, 
locomotion, and health conditions without overburdening local computational resources. 
Techniques such as model pruning (Vahidian et al., 2021), quantization (Liu et al., 2021), and 
knowledge distillation (Polino et al., 2018) could be leveraged to balance inference efficiency 
and accuracy, ensuring cost-effectiveness for on-farm applications. Third, fine-tuning SLMs with 
dairy-specific textual data, including government reports, extension publications, and farm 
management documentation, will be critical for enhancing their understanding of specialized


## Page 23


Manuscript
 
terminology and contextual nuances. Fourth, to overcome current limitations of native RAG in 
quantitative reasoning and summarization, future iterations may explore hybrid architectures that 
integrate structured reasoning mechanisms (e.g., lightweight graph-based retrieval) while 
maintaining computational efficiency.  
 
CONCLUSIONS 
This work demonstrates the feasibility of Small Language Models within a computationally 
constrained multi-agent architecture as an effective solution for intelligent decision support in 
dairy farming. Operating under the strict resource limits of a single NVIDIA T4 GPU, conditions 
infeasible for most LLMs, the study identified the Qwen-4B model as the optimal choice, 
achieving a strong balance between high instruction-following accuracy and a compact memory 
footprint, both critical for practical on-farm deployment. The resulting system effectively bridges 
pervasive data silos by unifying interaction across structured (SQL), semi-structured 
(NoSQL/PySpark), unstructured (RAG-based literature and web search), and predictive 
modeling data sources. This capability directly addresses the need for tools that transform 
heterogeneous farm data into actionable, real-time insights. The successful deployment of this 
system minimizes reliance on expensive cloud-hosted Large Language Models, and also 
eliminates data privacy risks associated with cloud-based third-party service. In doing so, this 
work provides a scalable, privacy-aware, and accessible blueprint for the digital transformation 
of the dairy industry, setting the groundwork for next-generation, AI-driven precision 
agriculture.
