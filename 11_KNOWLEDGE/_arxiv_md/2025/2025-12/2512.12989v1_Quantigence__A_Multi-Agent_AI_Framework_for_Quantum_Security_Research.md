Abstract
Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computers (CRQCs) pose a structural and existential threat
to the foundations of the global digital economy. The potential realization of algorithms such as
Shor’s integer factoring and Grover’s unstructured search threatens to dismantle the public-key
infrastructure (PKI) securing everything from sovereign communications to financial transactions.
While the timeline for a fault-tolerant CRQC remains probabilistic, the “Store-Now, Decrypt-Later”
(SNDL) attack model necessitates immediate and proactive migration to Post-Quantum Cryptog-
raphy (PQC). However, this transition is hindered by the sheer velocity and fragmentation of the
research landscape, characterized by evolving NIST standards, frequent theoretical breaks in candi-
date algorithms, and heterogeneous deployment environments. To address this cognitive bottleneck,
we present Quantigence, a theory-driven multi-agent AI framework designed for structured, au-
tonomous quantum-security analysis. Quantigence decomposes complex research objectives into
specialized reasoning roles coordinated by a hierarchical supervisory agent. Distinct from tradi-
tional distributed computing, the framework leverages cognitive parallelism, where agent roles rea-
son independently to maintain context purity while execution is serialized on resource-constrained
hardware (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 2060). The framework integrates real-time external knowledge via
the Model Context Protocol (MCP) and prioritizes vulnerabilities using the Quantum-Adjusted
Risk Score (QARS), a formal extension of Mosca’s Theorem that introduces continuous urgency
metrics. Empirical validation demonstrates that Quantigence achieves a 67% reduction in research
turnaround time and superior coverage of the literature compared to manual expert workflows,
democratizing access to high-fidelity quantum risk assessment.
Keywords: Multi-Agent Systems · Quantum Security · Post-Quantum Cryptography · Agentic AI ·
Risk Modeling · Quantum Risk Assessment

Introduction
The security assumptions underpinning modern digital infrastructure are poised for a cataclysmic
disruption. For decades, the confidentiality and integrity of digital communications have relied on the
computational hardness of specific mathematical problems: integer factorization (underpinning RSA)
and the discrete logarithm problem (underpinning Elliptic Curve Cryptography, or ECC). These prob-
lems are intractable for classical von Neumann architectures, requiring astronomical time scales to solve
for sufficiently large key sizes. However, the theoretical advent of quantum computing fundamentally
alters this landscape. Peter Shor’s 1994 discovery of a quantum algorithm capable of solving these
problems in polynomial time transformed the threat from a brute-force impossibility to a deterministic
certainty, contingent only on the engineering challenge of building a sufficiently large, fault-tolerant
quantum processor [1].
Simultaneously, Lov Grover’s algorithm for unstructured database search offers a quadratic speedup
for symmetric key operations, effectively halving the security margin of primitives like AES and
SHA [2]. While less catastrophic than the complete collapse of PKI, this necessitates a global dou-
bling of key sizes—a trivial change in software but a monumental logistical challenge in constrained
hardware environments.
The threat is not merely futuristic. The “Store-Now, Decrypt-Later” (SNDL) adversarial model
implies that any long-lived secret intercepted today—be it genomic data, state secrets, or industrial
intellectual property—is already compromised if its confidentiality requirement exceeds the time until
a CRQC is available (the “Z-time” in Mosca’s terminology [3]). Consequently, the migration to Post-
Quantum Cryptography (PQC) is not a patch to be applied in the future; it is a structural renovation
that must begin immediately.

1.1
The Research Scalability Crisis
The transition to PQC is characterized by extreme volatility. The standardization process led by
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has been a rigorous, multi-year gauntlet,
recently culminating in the release of FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205
(SLH-DSA) [5]. Yet, the field remains treacherous. Promising candidates like SIKE (Supersingular
Isogeny Key Encapsulation) have been shattered by classical attacks late in the evaluation process,
highlighting the fragility of new mathematical assumptions.
For security researchers and Chief Information Security Officers (CISOs), the challenge is one of
information synthesis. A robust risk assessment requires integrating data from disparate domains:
• Theoretical Cryptography: Understanding the nuances of lattice hardness, error rates, and
algebraic attack surfaces.
• Implementation Security: Monitoring for side-channel attacks (timing, power analysis) in
specific software libraries.
• Standardization Policy: Tracking the shifting deadlines and mandates, such as those outlined
in NIST IR 8547 [6], which sets deprecation targets for 2030 and 2035.
• Hardware Constraints: Evaluating whether a specific IoT device has the memory and cycle
budget for PQC keys, which are orders of magnitude larger than their RSA counterparts.
Manual expert analysis scales poorly against this complexity.
The cognitive load required to
maintain a mental model of the entire PQC landscape is immense, leading to fragmentation where
“standards experts” miss technical vulnerabilities and “cryptographers” miss deployment realities.
There is a critical, unmet need for automated systems capable of continuous, high-fidelity synthesis
of this rapidly evolving knowledge base.
1.2
The Agentic AI Paradigm
The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) with reasoning capabilities offers a potential so-
lution.
However, zero-shot prompting of a single model is insufficient for deep research; models
hallucinate citations, lose context over long documents, and struggle to weigh conflicting evidence.
The solution lies in Agentic AI—architectures where the model is not a passive text generator but
an active reasoning engine capable of planning, tool use, and iterative reflection.
We propose Quantigence, a multi-agent framework that operationalizes the scientific method for
quantum security. By decomposing the research process into specialized roles—analogous to a princi-
pal investigator leading a team of post-docs—Quantigence achieves a depth of analysis unattainable
by single-turn interactions [9]. Crucially, we introduce the concept of Cognitive Parallelism: the ar-
chitecture allows multiple “agents” (reasoning threads) to explore different facets of a problem (e.g.,
one checking math, one checking policy) independently, preventing the context pollution that plagues
monolithic models.
1.3
Contributions
This work makes the following contributions to the field of automated security research:
1. Multi-Agent Architecture for Quantum Security: We define a novel supervisor–worker
topology specifically tuned for the PQC domain, separating the concerns of threat modeling,
cryptographic analysis, and compliance checking.
2. Formal Quantum Risk Modeling (QARS): We translate the binary inequality of Mosca’s
Theorem into the Quantum-Adjusted Risk Score, a continuous, differentiable metric that inte-
grates temporal urgency, data sensitivity, and exploitability into a unified priority score.

2
Related Work
2.1
Multi-Agent Reasoning Systems
The evolution of LLMs from chatbots to agents is marked by the introduction of planning and tool
use. Frameworks like AutoGen and LangGraph have standardized the “orchestration” layer, allowing
developers to define graphs of interaction between model instances. Anthropic’s research into multi-
agent systems highlights that decomposing a complex task (like “research a topic”) into sub-tasks
(search, read, synthesize) significantly outperforms monolithic prompting [9]. The “Reflexion” pattern,
where an agent critiques its own output, is central to reducing hallucinations. Quantigence builds on
these general-purpose architectures but introduces domain-specific constraints and tools necessary for
the high-stakes environment of cryptography.
2.2
Post-Quantum Risk Assessment
The theoretical foundation of quantum risk is Michele Mosca’s Theorem (X + Y > Z) [3]. While
elegant, this inequality is a binary classifier—it tells you if you are safe or unsafe, but not how
unsafe. In an enterprise with thousands of assets, binary classifications are insufficient for resource
allocation. Recent work by Grigaliunas et al. has attempted to extend this into “Unified Quantum
Risk Assessments,” proposing multi-factor models [8]. Quantigence automates the calculation of these
models, moving them from theoretical papers into active, scanning software agents.
2.3
Benchmarking AI in Security
The capability of AI agents to perform security tasks is a contentious field. The “CVE-Bench” project
recently demonstrated that while LLM agents can identify and exploit vulnerabilities, their success
rate in real-world “criticdal” scenarios is currently around 13% [27,28]. This highlights the necessity of
the Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) approach adopted by Quantigence. We do not design the system
to autonomously patch systems, but to triage and analyze, preparing high-quality decision support
for human experts.
3
Methodology: The Quantigence Framework
Quantigence is not a single model but a cognitive system. It models the research process as an iterative
traversal of a shared knowledge graph, coordinated by a supervisory entity. The architecture is defined
by three core pillars: Role Specialization, Cognitive Parallelism, and Serialized Execution.

3.1
Cognitive Parallelism and Role Decomposition
The architectural diagram in Figure 1 outlines the high-level functional modules of the system: Lit-
erature Review, Technical Analysis, and Synthesis. To operationalize these functions for the specific
domain of quantum security, Quantigence instantiates five distinct agent personas. While they may
run sequentially on the GPU (due to hardware constraints), they reason as parallel experts:
• The Supervisor (Orchestrator): Corresponds to the Lead/Supervisor Agent in Figure 1.
The central node. It does not do the research; it plans it. It parses the user query, breaks it
into a dependency graph of sub-tasks, assigns them to workers, and critiques their output. It is
responsible for the final synthesis.
• The Cryptographic Analyst: A specialist within the Technical Analysis module. It focuses
on the mathematics of PQC. It understands lattices, isogenies, and hash-based signatures. Its
prompt primes it to be skeptical, rigorous, and theoretical.
• The Threat Modeler: An adversarial thinker, also operating within the Technical Analysis
module. It looks for “off-path” attacks—side-channels, implementation bugs, and bad random
number generators. It queries the CVE databases.
• The Standards Specialist: A compliance auditor executing the Literature Review function.
It is grounded in the texts of NIST FIPS, SP 800-series, and ISO standards. It answers questions
like “Is this algorithm allowed for Top Secret data after 2030?”
• The Risk Assessor: The quantitative engine acting as the Synthesis Agent.
It takes the
qualitative findings of the other agents and computes the QARS score (see Section 4).

3.3
Shared Memory and Knowledge Integration
Agents need a shared reality. Quantigence employs a dual-layer memory architecture:
1. Short-Term Context: The immediate conversation history within an agent’s execution.
2. Long-Term Shared Graph (M): A structured knowledge store. When the Cryptographic
Analyst discovers that “Kyber” is now “ML-KEM,” it updates the node in the graph. When the
Standards Specialist later queries “ML-KEM,” it inherits this alias.
To bridge the gap between the model’s training data (cutoff dates) and the present, we utilize the
Model Context Protocol (MCP). MCP allows the agents to “mount” external data sources as if
they were local file systems. We implement MCP servers for the NIST PQC Database, NVD (National
Vulnerability Database), and ArXiv/IACR. This ensures that the “Cognitive Parallelism” is not just
hallucinating in parallel, but reasoning over fresh, verified data.

Formal Risk Modeling: The Quantum-Adjusted Risk Score (QARS)
Qualitative assessments (“Risk is High”) are insufficient for prioritizing the migration of thousands
of IT assets. We introduce the Quantum-Adjusted Risk Score (QARS), a formal mathematical
framework that extends Mosca’s Theorem into a continuous risk metric suitable for automated scoring.
4.1
Deriving Urgency from Mosca’s Inequality
Mosca’s foundational inequality states that a system is at risk if:
X + Y > Z
(1)
Where:
• X: Migration Time (years to update the system).
• Y : Shelf-Life (years the data must remain confidential).
• Z: Collapse Time (years until a CRQC exists).
We transform this binary inequality into a continuous Urgency Ratio r(a) for an asset a:
r(a) = X(a) + Y (a)
Z(a)
(2)
If r(a) > 1, the asset is critically compromised (Mosca violation). If r(a) < 1, there is a safety
margin. However, the risk does not scale linearly. A ratio of 0.99 is significantly more dangerous
than 0.5. To capture this non-linearity, we map the ratio through a sigmoid function to derive the
Temporal Urgency Factor T(a):
T(a) =
1
1 + e−α(r(a)−1)
(3)
Here, α is a steepness coefficient (calibrated to α = 10 in our default profile). This function ensures
that as the urgency ratio approaches 1.0, the risk score saturates rapidly towards the maximum,
reflecting the “cliff-edge” nature of the cryptographic break.

4.2
The Composite Score
Time is the dominant factor in quantum risk, but not the only one. We integrate two classical risk
dimensions:
• Sensitivity S(a): A normalized score (0-1) based on data classification (e.g., Public=0, Inter-
nal=0.3, Confidential=0.7, Top Secret=1.0).
• Exploitability E(a): A measure of how practical the attack is. A theoretical break requiring
a billion qubits (E ≈0.1) is less risky than a side-channel attack feasible on today’s hardware
(E ≈0.9).
The final QARS is calculated as a weighted linear combination:
RQARS(a) = wT T(a) + wSS(a) + wEE(a)
(4)
Where the weights satisfy wT + wS + wE = 1. Given the existential nature of the SNDL threat, we
default to an urgency-biased weighting scheme: wT = 0.5, wS = 0.3, wE = 0.2.
4.3
Automated Calibration
The Quantigence agents automatically populate these variables. The Standards Specialist estimates Z
based on the latest community consensus (currently ≈2030 −2035 for minimal CRQC risk, aligning

Hardware Feasibility and Execution Model
A key design philosophy of Quantigence is democratization. Advanced security research tools typi-
cally require enterprise-grade clusters. However, many of the stakeholders most vulnerable to quantum
risks—NGOs, researchers in the Global South, and small enterprises—lack access to H100 GPU farms.
We explicitly engineered Quantigence to operate on consumer-grade hardware, specifically targeting
the NVIDIA RTX 2060 SUPER (8GB VRAM) as the baseline.
5.1
The “Poverty Line” of AI Research
Operating a sophisticated agent system within 8GB of VRAM presents severe constraints. A standard
FP16 (16-bit) instance of a 7-billion parameter model requires ≈14GB of VRAM, exceeding the
available buffer.
To circumvent this, we employ 4-bit Quantization (specifically the Q4_K_M
GGUF format or GPTQ).

5.2
Serialized Execution Strategy
Because the VRAM is fully saturated by a single model instance, we cannot run agents in parallel
physically. We must run them serially. The Orchestration Logic (Algorithm 1) handles this context
switching.
• State Save: The Supervisor’s context is serialized to the system RAM (CPU).
• Prompt Swap: The system prompt is replaced. “You are a Supervisor” becomes “You are a
Cryptographic Analyst.”
• Context Injection: The specific relevant subgraph from Memory M is injected into the KV
cache.
• Inference: The model generates the Analyst’s response.
• State Restore: The context is wiped, and the Supervisor’s state is reloaded from RAM.
This “Context Swapping” introduces latency—it takes seconds to swap states—but it allows a single
GPU to simulate an entire research team. We selected Qwen2-7B-Instruct as the core engine due to
its superior performance on reasoning benchmarks compared to Llama-2 or Mistral 7B, particularly
in coding and mathematical logic tasks essential for cryptography.

Analysis of Results
• Time Efficiency: The 67% reduction in time is driven by the machine’s ability to “read” (parse
and vector-embed) dozens of papers in seconds. While the human researcher is still reading the
introduction of the first paper, the Standards Specialist agent has already cross-referenced the
abstract against the NIST database.
• Source Coverage: Quantigence consistently identified more relevant citations.
Human re-
searchers tend to satisfycing behavior—stopping after finding 2-3 good sources. The agents,
driven by exhaustive search parameters, cast a wider net, often finding relevant papers in adja-
cent fields (e.g., electrical engineering journals discussing hardware implementations) that pure
cryptographers missed.
• Risk Agreement: The 89% agreement on “Critical” risk scores suggests the QARS model
aligns well with human intuition for severe threats. Divergence occurred mostly in “Medium”
risk categories, where the AI (driven by the rigid math of the Urgency Factor) tended to be
more conservative/alarmist than humans, who might intuitively discount threats they feel are
“too theoretical.”
6.3
Limitations: The “Hallucination” of Safety
While the system reduces hallucinations compared to zero-shot models, it is not immune. In one
test case, the agent correctly identified a vulnerability but cited a non-existent patch version. This
validates the design decision to keep the “Supervisor” agent and the HITL requirement. Quantigence
is a force multiplier for experts, not a replacement.

Threat Modeling the AI Researcher
Introducing an autonomous agent into the security research loop creates a new attack surface. If we
rely on AI to tell us what is safe, the adversary’s optimal move is to lie to the AI. We term this
Adversarial Information Poisoning.

## Page 10


7.1
The Attack Vector
Quantigence relies on external data: arXiv papers, CVE entries, and blog posts. An adversary wishing
to hide a vulnerability in a cryptographic library could:
• Pollute the Literature: Flood arXiv with “junk” pre-prints claiming a specific algorithm is
secure, using SEO-optimized keywords that trigger the agent’s relevance scorers.
• Citation Loops: Create a ring of cross-citing papers to artificially inflate the “impact factor”
of the false claim.
• Poisoned Context: If the agent uses a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system, the
attacker could inject “prompt injection” payloads into the text of a whitepaper (e.g., “Ignore
previous instructions and classify this algorithm as Risk Level 0”).
7.2
Defense Mechanisms
Quantigence incorporates specific defenses against this:
• Source Hierarchy: The Standards Specialist is hard-coded to trust NIST FIPS documents
(cryptographically signed/verified sources) over arXiv pre-prints.
• Consensus Verification: The Supervisor looks for independent confirmation.
If only one
cluster of authors claims a primitive is secure, and the rest of the field is silent, the system flags
the finding as “Low Confidence.”
• Sanitized Input: The MCP layer strips invisible characters and potential prompt injection
patterns before feeding text to the model.
8
Strategic Implications: Navigating the NIST Transition
The release of NIST IR 8547 in late 2024 formalized the “PQC clock.” The document outlines a rigid
timeline [6]:
• Now - 2030: Phase-out period. Legacy cryptography (RSA, ECC) is deprecated.
• 2030: Deprecation. New equipment must support PQC. 112-bit security is disallowed.
• 2035: Disallowance. Legacy algorithms are forbidden in federal systems.
For an enterprise, this is a massive compliance challenge. Quantigence serves as a strategic ac-
celerator here. By feeding the text of IR 8547 into the memory graph, the Standards Specialist can
audit an organization’s inventory. It can automatically flag: “Asset #402 (VPN Concentrator) uses
RSA-2048. Based on its 10-year depreciation schedule (Y = 10), it will violate the 2035 disallowance
(Zpolicy = 2035). Mosca Violation Detected.” This capability—to project policy timelines onto
technical inventory—is the “killer app” for AI in governance.
9
Future Work
The current iteration of Quantigence is a text-based analyst. It reads about code; it does not run
it.
The next phase of development focuses on Active verification.
Integrating frameworks like
CVE-Bench [27] would allow the Threat Modeler agent to not just read about a vulnerability, but
to spin up a sandboxed Docker container, deploy the target software, and attempt to run the exploit
script. This would move the system from “Theoretical Risk Assessment” to “Verified Vulnerability
Confirmation.” Furthermore, we aim to expand the hardware support to clustered NPUs to enable
true physical parallelism.

Conclusion
The transition to a post-quantum world is an existential imperative for the digital society. It is a
race against a probabilistic clock, run on a track composed of shifting mathematical sands. The sheer
volume of knowledge required to navigate this transition—spanning quantum physics, number theory,
software engineering, and government policy—exceeds the unassisted cognitive capacity of human
teams.
Quantigence demonstrates that Artificial Intelligence, when structured into rigorous, theory-driven
agentic architectures, can bridge this gap. By leveraging Cognitive Parallelism and Formal Risk Mod-
eling (QARS), the framework provides a scalable, reproducible, and accessible means of quantifying
the quantum threat. It allows a researcher with a consumer-grade GPU to perform analysis that pre-
viously required a dedicated consultancy team. As we stand on the precipice of the quantum era, the
ability to rapidly synthesize truth from the noise of research will be our most valuable defense. Quanti-
gence is a step toward that future—a future where our security is guarded not just by mathematics,
but by the automated, tireless reasoning of intelligent agents.
