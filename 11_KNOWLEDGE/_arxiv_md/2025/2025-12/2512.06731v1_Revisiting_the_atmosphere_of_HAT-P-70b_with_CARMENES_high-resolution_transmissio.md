ABSTRACT
Owing to hot and inflated envelopes that facilitate atmospheric studies, ultra-hot Jupiters (UHJs)
have attracted much attention. Significant progress has been achieved, from enlarging the sample size
to broadening the studies to encompass diverse stellar types and ages. Here, we present a transmission
spectroscopy study of HAT-P-70b, an UHJ orbiting a young A-type star, through high-resolution
observations with CARMENES at the 3.5 m Calar Alto telescope. By using the line-by-line technique,
we confirm the previous detections of Hα, Na i, and Ca ii, report a new tentative detection of K i, and
impose an upper limit on the He triplet absorption. Through cross-correlation analysis, we identify the
Ca ii and Fe i absorptions, both blue-shifted by approximately 5 km s−1, indicating a day-to-night side
atmospheric wind. Additionally, we find a new tentative detection of K i. We do not see any significant
atmospheric molecular signal in the near-infrared data. Putting HAT-P-70b in the context of UHJs
from the literature, it turns out that (1) Hα absorption is more common on gas giants orbiting stars
younger than 1 Gyr, with a relative detection probability of PAge<1 Gyr(Hα)/PAge≥1 Gyr(Hα) ∼3; (2)
any UHJ is likely to exhibit Fe i absorption if it has Ca ii.

1. INTRODUCTION
Among
all
planetary
classes,
ultra-hot
Jupiters
(UHJs)−defined as gas giants with equilibrium temper-
atures Teq above about 2200 K (Parmentier et al. 2018;
Stangret et al. 2022; Maíz Apellániz et al. 2026)−serve
as benchmark systems for atmospheric characterization,
which benefit from their large scale height due to the
proximity to their hot host stars. The majority of UHJs
are supposed to be largely cloud-free on the dayside
hemisphere, since the extreme insolation inhibits con-
densation processes (Helling et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2020),
making them ideal targets for atmospheric composition
studies. Meanwhile, the atomic and molecular species
undergo thermal dissociation and ionization (Arcangeli
et al. 2018; Lothringer et al. 2018) such that metals,
particularly refractory elements, either neutral or ion-
ized, exist in the gaseous state and thus could be ob-
served. Detections of these species, and the measure-
ment of their abundances through atmospheric retrievals
(Madhusudhan & Seager 2009; Brogi & Line 2019; Gib-
son et al. 2020), offer a unique opportunity to test the-
oretical predictions, probing planet formation histories
(e.g., Öberg et al. 2011; Mordasini et al. 2016; Turrini
et al. 2021; Mollière et al. 2022; Chachan et al. 2023),
as well as subsequent migration processes (e.g., Mad-
husudhan et al. 2014; Lothringer et al. 2021; Schneider
& Bitsch 2021). Given their short orbital periods, UHJs
are likely tidally locked. Such a special orbital configu-
ration would result in a significant day- and night-side
temperature contrast, which drives atmospheric heat-
ing gradients and wind flows (Miller-Ricci Kempton &
Rauscher 2012; Bell & Cowan 2018), a possible explana-
tion for the observed chemical diurnal inhomogeneities
in some planetary atmospheres (Ehrenreich et al. 2020;
Cont et al. 2021, 2024; Prinoth et al. 2022; Maguire et al.
2024).
One route to characterize planetary atmospheres is
through the transmission spectroscopy methodology
(Seager & Sasselov 2000).
The light from the host
star is blocked by the planet atmosphere, imprinting
wavelength-dependent signatures on the observed spec-
trum. Therefore, it contains information on the morn-
ing/evening terminator atmospheres. In particular, the
high-resolution transmission spectroscopy (see the re-
view by Snellen 2025, and references therein) can trace
spectral absorption of individual lines, or map the sig-
nals from multiple lines onto a single detection peak
through cross-correlation (Snellen et al. 2010). Over the
past two decades, numerous UHJs’ atmospheres have
been investigated with this approach (see Table 1 in
Cont et al. (2024)), revealing a wealth of atomic species
such as Ca i, Cr i, Fe i, Na i, K i, Ti i, and V i (e.g.,
Hoeijmakers et al. 2019; Yan et al. 2019; Chen et al.
2020; Casasayas-Barris et al. 2022; Langeveld et al. 2022;
Zhang et al. 2022; Borsato et al. 2023; Stangret et al.
2024; Cont et al. 2025; Vaulato et al. 2025), as well as
molecules such as CO, H2O, OH, TiO, and VO (e.g.,
Landman et al. 2021; Sedaghati et al. 2021; Prinoth et al.
2022; Pelletier et al. 2023; Gandhi et al. 2024; Hood et al.
2024; Maguire et al. 2024; Weiner Mansfield et al. 2024;
Mraz et al. 2024; Simonnin et al. 2025).
Optical high-resolution transmission spectroscopy is
primarily sensitive to refractory metal elements as they
possess substantial absorption lines in the blue wave-
length band (i.e., 3500–5500 Å; Kurucz 2018). The ab-
sorption of molecules that consist of volatile elements
such as carbon, oxygen, and sulfur, however, predom-
inantly appear in the near-infrared regime (Tennyson
et al. 2016). Hence, the combination of optical and near-
infrared spectroscopic observations enables a thorough
mapping of the atmosphere, for example, determining
the refractory-to-volatile ratio, which will in turn shed
light on the birthplace of the planet (Lothringer et al.
2021). In addition, the Hα λ6565 Å and He i λ10 833 Å
lines (all wavelengths throughout the paper are defined
in vacuum) are key diagnostics of atmospheric escape
(Yan & Henning 2018; Nortmann et al. 2018; Kirk et al.
2020; Lampón et al. 2021; Gully-Santiago et al. 2024;
Saidel et al. 2025). Both lines have different observa-
tional advantages (Seager & Sasselov 2000; Fuhrmeister
et al. 2020; Orell-Miquel et al. 2022), so simultaneously
observing Hα and He i triplet lines offers a more compre-
hensive view of atmospheric escape (Orell-Miquel et al.
2024). Hydrodynamic modeling of the Hα and He i line
profiles can retrieve the mass loss rate (Lampón et al.
2021; Zhang et al. 2023), which is critical for understand-
ing the evolutionary path and fate of the exoplanet.
In this work, we investigate the atmosphere of HAT-
P-70b (see Table 1), an UHJ orbiting a young (0.6 Gyr)
A-type star every 2.7 days (Zhou et al. 2019), with
CARMENES high-resolution transmission spectroscopy.
An initial study by Bello-Arufe et al. (2022) using
HARPS-N (R ∼115 000, 3830–6930 Å) detected nine
atomic species in total including Ca ii, Cr i, Cr ii, Fe i,
Fe ii, H i, Mg i, Na i, and V i.
Absorption from the
Ca ii infrared triplet (IRT) was also recently reported by

2. OBSERVATIONS
We observed one transit of HAT-P-70b on UT 2023
November 26 using the CARMENES spectrograph
(Quirrenbach et al. 2014, 2018). The observation was
carried out as part of the CARMENES Legacy program
(23B-3.5-103). CARMENES is a high-resolution echelle
spectrograph installed at the 3.5 m telscope at the Ob-
servatorio de Calar Alto in Almería, Spain.
The ob-
servation was performed simultaneously in two channels
(VIS and NIR), covering the wavelength ranges 0.52—
0.96 µm and 0.96—1.71 µm with spectral resolutions of
94 600 and 80 400, respectively.
Over the duration of 6.4 h of the observation, a to-
tal of 46 spectra were collected in each channel with
a fixed exposure time of 400 s, covering the 3.5 h-long
transit as well as 1.5 h before the ingress and 1.4 h after
the egress. During the observations, the airmass varied
between 1.12 and 1.77. Figure 1 shows the signal-to-
noise ratio (S/N) per pixel near the Na i doublet lines
at 5891 Å and 5898 Å along with the airmass trend.
The S/N of the first three spectra is much lower than
that of the others due to high airmass and poor see-
ing conditions. Consequently, we excluded these spectra
in the following analysis. The raw CARMENES spec-
tra were reduced with the caracal pipeline (Caballero
et al. 2016) and the calibrated 1D spectra were obtained
via the flat-optimized extraction algorithm (Zechmeister
et al. 2014).

3.3.1. Spectra Pre-Processing
Since a few hundred pixels of the first ten orders of
the VIS-band spectra lack flux information, we fixed
missing values of the initial 700 pixels of them to the
median flux.
We flagged all other pixels of the spec-
tra with flux values saved as NaN (Not a Number), and
replaced their fluxes and uncertainties with the interpo-
lated values using a cubic spline model. We next ex-
cluded the outliers, for example due to cosmic rays, in
the data. To do this, we explored the time evolution
of each pixel of each order.
We fitted a cubic spline
function, and replaced the flux with the best-fit result
if it is discrepant with the model by 5σ. Normalization
was then performed order by order. Basically, we split
each order into several chunks with a binning size of 50
pixels and obtained the averaged continuum by comput-
ing the median wavelength and flux of the top 10 flux
values within each chunk (Passegger et al. 2016; Hoeij-
makers et al. 2020). We interpolated the continuum to
the observational wavelength stamps and normalized the
spectrum through dividing the data by the interpolated
continuum to calibrate the baselines to a similar flux
level. Finally, we mitigated sky emission lines with flux
exceeding 10 percent of the continuum level and strong
absorption lines of tellurics with normalized flux below
0.1 by artificially masking them. To correct the RM and
CLV effects, we interpolated the RM+CLV model (see
Section 3.2) to the wavelength and orbital phase of the
observations, and divided the spectrum matrix by the
RM+CLV model order by order.
Since the planetary atmospheric signals are typically
obscured by telluric absorption and stellar contamina-
tion, we employed the SYSREM algorithm (Tamuz et al.
2005; Mazeh et al. 2007) to iteratively remove these
time-dependent systematic effects.

Doppler-shifted planetary signals could also be erased if

spectrum matrix 8 times, and computed the difference
between the residual spectrum matrix of each pair of two
consecutive runs (Spring et al. 2022). We found that the
standard deviation of the flux variation of all orders be-
comes negligible after five and four iterations for the VIS
and NIR band data, respectively. Therefore, we used

3.3.2. Template Construction

around 2500 K, close to the planetary equilibrium tem-

set the continuum level to 10 mbar since the planet at-

of about 3.5 mbar due to continuum absorption by H−
(Kitzmann et al. 2018; Hoeijmakers et al. 2019). The

(Kurucz 2018) while molecules were adopted from HI-
TRAN (Rothman et al. 2013) except for VO and TiO,
for which we used the results from B. Plez (Mollière et al.
2019) and ExoMol (Tennyson et al. 2016). All templates
were then convolved with a Gaussian function to match

After obtaining the stacked cross-correlation map, we
transformed the results to the planet rest frame based
on the planet velocity vp:
vp(t, Kp) = vsys −vbary(t) + Kp sin 2πϕ(t),
(2)
where vsys is the systemic velocity of the star, vbary
is the barycentric velocity, Kp is the semi-amplitude
of the planetary radial velocity and ϕ(t) is the orbital
phase at time stamp t. Despite having a prior estimate
(Kp ≈187 km s−1), we performed an agnostic search
and shifted the map to a linearly-spaced grid of Kp val-
ues from 0 to 500 km s−1 in steps of 1 km s−1.
We
finally co-added the in-transit cross-correlation results
and constructed a Kp-∆v map. All cross-correlation co-
efficients were converted to S/N through dividing by the
standard deviation outside the region of −40 ≤∆v ≤40
km s−1 and 100 ≤Kp ≤250 km s−1, in which the signal
is expected to be located (Brogi et al. 2018).
Generally, we anticipate an absorption signal to ap-
pear near the predicted Kp, and ∆v around 0 km s−1.
Nevertheless, the location of the signal may have several
km s−1 offsets due to additional motions that are not
taken into account such as atmospheric winds (Miller-
Ricci Kempton & Rauscher 2012), manifesting them-
selves as a blue or red shift along the ∆v axis (Snellen
et al. 2010; Wyttenbach et al. 2015; Louden & Wheat-
ley 2015; Yan et al. 2022).
The planetary winds are
supposed to be driven by the large temperature gradi-
ent from the dayside to the nightside, which is more
significant if the planet has higher insolation as in the
case of UHJs (Komacek & Showman 2016). Therefore,
to claim a robust detection, we required that the sig-
nal has a cross-correlation S/N over 4 and it is located
close to the expected position (Kp = 187 km s−1 and
∆v = 0 km s−1), within δKp = 40 km s−1 as well as
δ∆v = 25 km s−1.
Moreover, we also defined a ten-
tative detection if the signal satisfies the same Kp and
∆v thresholds but with S/N between 3 and 4. However,
when there are more than 5 peaks with 3 < S/N < 4 in
the cross-correlation map, even if one of them is close to
the expected location, we attributed the signal to ran-
dom noise. We located the signal with the maximum
S/N in the Kp-∆v map, and fitted a one-dimensional
Gaussian profile (a standard Gaussian function plus an
offset) to the cross-correlation result. For each species,
we generated a three-column diagnostic plot including
the 2D cross-correlation residual map, the 2D Kp-∆v
map and the 1D cross-correlation function, as shown in
Figure 7.

We find a significant absorption of Hα, visible in the

4.2. Results from Cross-Correlation Analysis
Figures 7 and 8 present the robust and the tentative
detections from the cross-correlation analysis. We sum-
marize the details in Table 3. Overall, we verify pre-
vious detections of Ca ii and Fe i by Bello-Arufe et al.
(2022) and report the new tentative detection of K i.
However, we note that Bello-Arufe et al. (2022) identi-
fied significant absorption of several other atomic species
such as Cr i, Fe ii and V i using HARPS-N whereas they
are not detected in the CARMENES data.
The dis-
crepancy is mainly due to the following two reasons:
the lower spectral S/N of the CARMENES data limit
the detection sensitivity; the wavelength coverage of
CARMENES does not extend below 5000 Å, where most
metal absorption lines are located.
4.2.1. Detections of Ca ii and Fe i
We identified a strong and extended Ca ii absorp-
tion signature with a maximum S/N of 4.3 in the
Kp-∆v map, peaking at Kp = 222 ± 24 km s−1 and
blue-shifted by about −3.2 ± 2.5 km s−1, in agreement

Unlike HARPS-N that covers the Ca ii H&K lines at

Apart from the robust detections of Ca ii and Fe i,
we also tentatively found the presence of K i, as in the
single line analysis. The neutral potassium K i shows
a broad signal (FWHM=19.4 ± 2.2 km s−1) analogous
to Ca ii with a maximum S/N of about 3.1.
Simi-
larly, it is blue-shifted by a few km s−1 and it shows

pected Kp. However, the large scatter in the 1D cross-

Gaussian profile to a value of 2.92 ± 0.28, slightly be-
low the threshold of S/N=3. We emphasize that this
marginal signal barely meet our definition of tentative
detection (3 ≤S/N < 4) and there are two other peaks
in the cross-correlation map, though the total number

5. DISCUSSION
5.1. Hα detection is more common in young
Jupiter-sized planets
We explored if Hα detections on gas giants have any
correlation with planet or host star properties. We com-
bined the result of HAT-P-70 b with the compilation of
Hα studies available at the ExoAtmospheres1 website,
from where we adopted the classification of detections
and upper limits. A detection means that the TS shows
an absorption that is at least 3σ over the noise level.
The results are considered as upper limits when there is
no evidence of planetary absorption. We note that the
median S/N of the transmission spectroscopic observa-
tions with and without Hα detections in the literature
are similar, on average above 35. Therefore, we consider
that the result is not biased to the S/N of the observa-
tion.
We found a tentative relation between Hα detections
and the age of the host stars. Figure 9 top panel shows
1 https://research.iac.es/proyecto/exoatmospheres/index.php (24
June 2025).
the stellar age vs. planetary radius for planets with radii
between 0.5–2.2 RJup. Detections come mainly from the
planets with well defined ages below 1 Gyr, where all ex-
cept three (TOI-1431 b, TOI-2046 b, Orell-Miquel et al.
2024; and KELT-17 b, Stangret et al. 2022) have pos-
itive results (from left to right in Figure 9 top panel:
WASP-33 b, Yan et al. 2021; MASCARA-2 b, Fossati
et al. 2023b; WASP-52 b, Chen et al. 2020; WASP-178 b,
Damasceno et al. 2024; KELT-9 b, Yan & Henning 2018;
TOI-5398 b, D’Arpa et al. 2024; MASCARA-4 b, Zhang
et al. 2022; and WASP-189 b, Prinoth et al. 2024).
The bottom panel of Figure 9 shows the period-radius
diagrams for young (<1 Gyr-old, left) and old (≥1 Gyr-
old, right) planets.
The comparison between both
groups helps to visualize the age trend discernible in
the age–radius diagram. Gas giants orbiting <1 Gyr-old
stars seem more likely to show absorption from Hα than
those orbiting older stars, according to Figure 9. To be
specific, the Hα detection rate is about 75% for <1 Gyr-
old planets while it is only around 26% for ≥1 Gyr-old
planets, resulting in a relative detection probability of
PAge<1 Gyr(Hα)/PAge≥1 Gyr(Hα) ∼3.
Theories about giant planet formation predict in-
tense atmospheric evaporation, mainly within the first
100 Myr (e.g., Sanz-Forcada et al. 2011; Owen & Wu
2013; Dawson & Johnson 2018; Gupta & Schlichting
2020. Thus, atmospheric absorption by Hα is expected
to be easier to detect in young planets. However, the
compiled sample is consistently older than 100 Myr,
when atmospheric evaporation is predicted to be less
intense (Lopez et al. 2012; Owen & Jackson 2012). Gas
giants can experience mass loss without any significant
consequences for their atmospheres (Vissapragada et al.
2022). Therefore, the distribution of Hα detections for
young and old gas giants from observations agrees with
the theoretical predictions but it does not have evolu-
tionary implications. However, we caution the reader
that this age trend may be due to the stellar mass be-
cause most gas giants with detailed atmospheric charac-
terizations are orbiting A and F stars, which are mostly
younger than G and K stars. As shown in Figure 10, Hα
detections are more common on planets around stars
with higher effective temperatures. As a large gas gi-
ant orbiting a young host star, HAT-P-70b follows the
general trends depicted by Figure 9 and predicted by
atmospheric evaporation models.

5.3. Ultra-Hot Jupiters with Ca ii Are Also Likely to
Have Fe i
At present, Fe i and Ca ii have been detected in the
atmospheres of about a dozen UHJ planets (e.g., Hoeij-

studies, a detection is mostly defined as either S/N > 4 if
using the cross-correlation method or > 3σ significance
if using the single line analysis. We emphasize that these
results were based on various instruments, hence they
have different detection sensitivities given their differ-
ent wavelength coverages and spectral resolutions.

in their atmospheres. If the atmosphere of an UHJ has
Ca ii already, we find that the conditional probability
that it also contains Fe i (i.e., PTeq≥2200 K(Fe I|Ca II))
is 100%, according to all detections known so far. In
contrast, for cooler giant planets with Teq ≤2200 K, the
presences of Ca ii and Fe i seem to have no correlation,
though the sample size is small which impedes firm con-
clusions.
The correlation between the presence of Ca ii and Fe i
is perhaps simply due to the fact that both species are
easy to detect.
The Ca ii has strong absorption lines
while the Fe i has numerous lines, both resulting in a
high detection sensitivity.
Nevertheless, it is possible
that there are some intrinsic underlying physics between
the appearances of different species.
Given the high-
temperature environment of UHJs, the atomic species,
in either neutral or ionized states, are expected to be
abundant (Parmentier et al. 2018; Helling et al. 2019;
Stangret et al. 2022).
However, a single transmission
spectroscopic observation is generally not sufficient for
a full characterization of the planetary atmosphere. If
these kind of correlations are physical, one might be
able to predict the existence of some elements based on
the available known detections. Such priors and knowl-

6. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we investigate the atmosphere of HAT-
P-70b, an UHJ orbiting an A star, with CARMENES
high-resolution transmission spectroscopy.
We revisit
several neutral and ionized species examined by Bello-
Arufe et al. (2022), and extend the study to elemental
absorptions in the red and near-infrared bands.
Using single-line analysis, we confirm the detections
of Hα, Na i, and Ca ii IRT. Moreover, for the first time,
we report a tentative absorption of K i and constrain
the atmospheric evaporation through the He triplet on
this planet. Through cross-correlations, we detect the
absorption of Fe i and Ca ii at the S/N level of about
5.4 and 4.3. Both signals are slightly blue shifted with
velocities comparable to the values in the previous work
(Bello-Arufe et al. 2022; Langeveld et al. 2025). In ad-
dition, we find the tentative detections of K i with S/N
barely higher than 3, requiring future observations to
validate or rule out the signal.
Putting HAT-P-70b in broader context, we find that
Hα absorption is more likely to be found in the atmo-
spheres of inflated giant planets around stars younger
than 1 Gyr. UHJs showing Ca ii absorption are likely
also show detectable Fe i with a conditional probability
PTeq≥2200K(Fe I|Ca II) close to 100%, according to the
current observations.
