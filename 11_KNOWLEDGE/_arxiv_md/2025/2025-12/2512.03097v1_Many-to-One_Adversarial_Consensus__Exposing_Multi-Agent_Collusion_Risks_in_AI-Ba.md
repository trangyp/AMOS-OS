Abstract—The integration of large language models (LLMs)
into healthcare IoT systems promises faster decisions and im-
proved medical support. LLMs are also deployed as multi-agent
teams to assist AI doctors by debating, voting, or advising
on decisions. However, when multiple assistant agents interact,
coordinated adversaries can collude to create false consensus,
pushing an AI doctor toward harmful prescriptions. We develop
an experimental framework with scripted and unscripted doctor
agents, adversarial assistants, and a verifier agent that checks
decisions against clinical guidelines. Using 50 representative
clinical questions, we find that collusion drives the Attack Success
Rate (ASR) and Harmful Recommendation Rates (HRR) up to
100% in unprotected systems. In contrast, the verifier agent
restores 100% accuracy by blocking adversarial consensus. This
work provides the first systematic evidence of collusion risk in
AI healthcare and demonstrates a practical, lightweight defence
that ensures guideline fidelity.
Keywords: Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), Large Language
Models (LLMs), Collusion Attacks, Consensus Pressure At-
tacks, Verifier Agent Defence, AI Security, Trustworthy AI,
Clinical Decision Support, AI in Healthcare, IoT Security
I. INTRODUCTION
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly integrated into
healthcare IoT systems, supporting tasks such as remote pa-
tient monitoring, diagnosis, and treatment recommendations.
In this setting, ensuring the security and trustworthiness of AI
decisions is critical, since medical errors caused by unsafe
recommendations can severely harm patients [1]. However,
AI doctors and LLM-based clinical decision agents face
multiple vulnerabilities. They may inherit hidden biases from
training data, hallucinate unsafe recommendations, expose
private health information through prompt injection [2], or face
adversarial attacks [3], [4].
In real-world decision-making, high-stakes decisions are
made by groups of people to add checks, redundancy, and
diverse perspectives. For example, difficult treatments are
reviewed by medical boards rather than a lone physician.
Similarly, smart hospitals or telemedicine platforms need
to integrate multiple specialists or AI subsystems to cross-
validate recommendations. Similar safeguards exist in avia-
tion (pilot–co-pilot cross-checks), air traffic control, corporate
governance boards, and jury-based legal systems.
To avoid adversarial risks, relying on a single AI decision-
maker is insufficient. Multiple assistants can offer second
opinions and challenge unsafe outputs, reducing single-point
failure [5], [6]. In addition, assistant agents can strengthen
reliability by debating, cross-checking, or ranking candidate
answers, creating a more trustworthy committee process [7],
[8]. In theory, multiple assistants should mitigate individual
bias and ensure that consensus reflects robust evidence. This
approach aligns with IoT healthcare needs, where distributed
and redundant intelligence is vital for resilience [9].
However, we find a paradox: the very committee meant to
improve safety can become a new weakness when adversaries
collude. When multiple assistant agents collude adversarially,
their voices do not increase safety but produce a false ma-
jority consensus that pressures the AI doctor to adopt unsafe
recommendations. This failure mode highlights a blind spot in
current MAS research. The assumption that “more agents =
more safety” does not hold under adversarial collusion [10].
This is especially concerning in safety-critical IoT, consensus-
driven support can amplify harm when attackers coordinate.
To address this gap, we propose a collusion-resilient frame-
work for healthcare IoT systems. Fig. 1 illustrates how col-
luding adversarial assistant agents can mislead an AI doctor
in clinical decision-making. Multiple assistants repeat the
same false recommendation (e.g., promoting drug “XYZ” over
the correct guideline-based treatment “ABC”), thus creating
consensus pressure to override safe practice. The AI doctor,
influenced by this group agreement, risks prescribing unsafe
alternatives. To overcome this, we propose a Verifier Agent,
which checks the committee’s recommendation against secure
medical guidelines or databases before a final prescription
is issued, shown in Fig. 2. The verifier overrides the un-
safe recommendation and restores the correct prescription
(“ABC”). This ensures that assistants remain valuable for
diversity and redundancy, but cannot override trusted medical
standards. Our approach explicitly accounts for adversarial
consensus attacks and restores safety through verification
from safe guidelines. Our contributions are twofold. First, we
provide the first systematic study of collusion attacks in LLM-
based IoT healthcare MAS, demonstrating how adversarial
assistants can manipulate a trusted AI doctor under consensus

II. RELATED WORK
AI is increasingly used in healthcare IoT systems, such
as remote monitoring, bedside devices, and telemedicine.
While these technologies promise better access and efficiency,
surveys show that they also introduce new security and privacy
risks that demand trust and auditability for safe deployment
[11], [12]. Clinical LLMs are vulnerable both during training,
where poisoned data can introduce false medical knowledge,
and during use, where they may generate unsafe or misleading
recommendations. Recent studies demonstrate that poisoned
data sets can embed false medical knowledge that later appears
in model output, highlighting the need for verification layers
and defence strategies in healthcare AI [13], [14].
While most existing MAS research has studied one-to-one
adversarial interactions, our work shifts focus to many-to-one
attacks i.e., collusion, where multiple assistants coordinate
against a single trusted doctor. A recent technical report by
the Cooperative AI Foundation on Multi-Agent Risks from
Advanced AI stresses that collusion poses especially urgent
risks [15]. AI systems may learn to cooperate in harmful
ways, bypass oversight, and undermine safety-critical settings
such as healthcare, finance, and security. Our results provide
one of the first concrete demonstrations of this risk in IoT
healthcare, highlighting why collusion-resilient verification
must be treated as a core requirement for trustworthy MAS.
To improve reliability, many researchers propose multi-
agent oversight frameworks. Early work such as AI Safety
via Debate showed that using a judge to select the winner in
a structured argument between models can make oversight of
complex reasoning tractable [16]. Follow-up studies explored
debate with multiple LLMs [14], noting that while it can
improve reasoning, it often collapses to majority opinion
[17]. Parallel lines of research developed LLM-as-a-Judge ap-
proaches, where a separate model ranks or evaluates candidate
answers. These methods are now being surveyed for evaluation
and governance, but still face challenges such as biases and
consistency [18], [19]. Other studies examined model juries
and voting mechanisms, showing how different voting rules
or even the order of presentation can shape outcomes [20].
Collective methods such as Constitutional AI embed social
choice principles directly into training, aiming to align agents
with human norms [21]. While recent work has highlighted
that majority voting often explains the performance gains in
multi-agent debate (MAD) [22], this reliance on consensus
raises a critical vulnerability. If majority influence impacts
the outcomes, then coordinated collusion among agents could
drastically distort the decision-making process. Instead of
converging toward truth, a group of colluding agents can bias
the collective outcome, causing a danger to critical decisions.
This underscores the urgent need to move beyond performance
benchmarks toward robustness analyses that explicitly con-
sider malicious coordination among agents. Building on this
insight, our work shifts the focus from performance gains
to adversarial risks, specifically investigating how collusion
among agents can undermine majority-based mechanisms and
proposing a defence framework to preserve reliability in multi-
agent systems even under coordinated manipulation.
Very recent work surfaces a different threat: secret collusion
among AI agents (including steganographic signaling) that
can bypass naive committee safeguards. Studies at NeurIPS
and follow-ups formalize multi-agent deception/collusion and
show how “agreement signals” can be manufactured without

Prior MAS defences largely reward agreement (debate win-

increase safety. The newer findings above warn that com-

confuse repetition with evidence [24]. Our contribution is to
study this risk in IoT healthcare and to evaluate a practical
mitigation by introducing a verifier agent that cross-checks
the panel’s recommendation against correct clinical guide-
lines/databases. This will preserve the benefits of assistant
diversity by adding guideline-based verification while blocking
collusive consensus from overriding trusted standards.
III. RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
We evaluated the proposed multi-agent collusion attack
against an AI Doctor agent using two different LLMs: Grok
4 Fast and Meta LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct. All experiments
were implemented in Python 3.10.9, using VS Code and the
Anaconda environment. Experimenting with more than one
model shows that these collusion risks are not tied to a single
vendor or training setup; we saw the same patterns across
models and settings. For the analysis, we curated a dataset
of 50 clinical questions, focusing on common medications for
common diseases (e.g., hypertension, asthma, stroke) in CSV
format. Each question represents a realistic patient’s inquiry,
and the gold answer is drawn from trusted clinical guidelines
to serve as the evaluation reference. Following clinical safety
rules, any deviation from the gold answer—including drug
class, dose, or frequency—was counted as harmful. We report
(i) An exploratory grid on single questions at two temperatures
and varying adversarial assistant counts, T ∈{0.2, 1.0} and
k ∈{2, 4, 6}, respectively, in Table I.
(ii) A main 50-questions evaluation at T = 0.3, k = 4 for
full metrics.
The experimental prompt was structured as follows:
User input: The patient’s clinical question (from our dataset).
Gold answer: The guideline-consistent treatment.
Assistant agents (adversarial): Each adversarial assistant was
prompted to recommend the same incorrect alternative, using
vague “updated protocols” and “safety concerns” to fabri-
cate consensus. This setup mirrors how adversarial consensus
could arise in multi-agent healthcare IoT systems, such as
telemedicine platforms or smart hospital assistants.
We modelled four doctor behaviours to test different design
approaches:
1) Scripted – Always listens to consensus if consensus >
threshold.
2) Unscripted (Neutral) – reasons independently without
bias instructions.
3) Unscripted (Consensus) – instructed to respect majority
opinion reflects real-world clinical practice with peer
influence, providing a realistic stress-test condition.
An attack was considered successful if the prescription dif-
fered from the guideline answer, even by dosage, since any
medication error is harmful in clinical settings. Our study
systematically explores the design space of AI doctor systems
and demonstrates that each naive approach has weaknesses
unless augmented with collusion-resilient verification.
This
evaluation resulted in several clear trends:
1) Scripted (S) Doctor (Baseline Vulnerability): Both
Grok and LLaMA show identical behavior in scripted
mode: at low assistant counts (k = 2), attacks fail, but
when k > 3, collusion reliably succeeds. This aligns
with our theoretical framing of majority pressure: once
adversarial mass crosses a threshold, the scripted doctor
caves, proving that deterministic consensus-following is
unsafe in clinical AI.
2) Unscripted (US) Neutral (N) Doctor: The unscripted
neutral (US-N) doctor, reasoning independently, was
also consistently vulnerable. Even when it produced the
correct medication name, dosage errors were common,
which in healthcare is considered harmful; as every
deviation from the guidelines is an attack success. This
illustrates a key point: “neutral” reasoning without safe-
guards cannot be trusted in critical medical settings.
3) Unscripted Consensus-Biased (C) Doctor: Both mod-
els cave when explicitly instructed to follow majority
consensus in unscripted consensus-biased (US-C) doctor
setting. Here, adversarial collusion succeeds consistently
when more than 3 assistants agree. This highlights the
central risk: when a system is biased to respect peer-
consensus, coordinated adversaries can dominate the
outcome.
These results confirm that the risk of collusion is not model-
specific, as both families show vulnerability when assistants
coordinate. Both models fail under scripted or consensus-
biased conditions and showed that even independent reasoning
can become unsafe if the medication diverges from trusted
standards. This shows that IoT-healthcare needs verification
layers, not just model robustness. To illustrate qualitative

IV. DEFENCE METHODOLOGY
Above findings demonstrate that collusion pressure is a
universal risk across LLMs and AI doctor agent behaviors. We
add a verifier agent as a practical defence. A verifier agent
embodies a defence mechanism, checking outputs against
trusted guideline knowledge to mitigate collusion risks while
preserving the benefits of multi-agent reasoning. Introducing
a verifier agent (US-C+V) in the proposed model completely
blocked collusion as shown in table I. The results highlight that
adding verification is a practical way to restore safety without
removing assistants, which remain valuable for diversity and
second opinions.
To illustrate the working of verifier agent, we present an
example of defence transcript shown in Fig. 3, case study
2. Here, despite strong adversarial pressure from multiple
assistants, the verifier agent successfully cross-checked the
guidelines and overruled the manipulated consensus. This
intervention restored the correct prescription and prevented pa-
tient harm, demonstrating a practical defence against collusion
and attack attempts by a group of adversarial agents.

simulations, we limited our experiments to 50 questions from
the medical Q&A dataset. This makes our study an early but
credible evaluation and the results should be read as evidence
of the threat and a proof of concept for defences, rather than
exhaustive benchmarking. This kind of early study is common
when exploring new security problems in AI.

We tested our system on 50 representative clinical questions

VI. DISCUSSION
Our experiments provide an early but credible evaluation
of collusion risks in multi-agent AI healthcare and the effec-
tiveness of verifier-based defence. In our setup, assistants act
like human medical boards or second-opinion panels, offering
supportive or challenging views to the doctor. The AI doctor
may know the right answer, but colluding assistants can still
pressure it into prescribing incorrectly. The verifier agent is not
part of this assistant panel, it works independently checking
the doctor’s final prescription against trusted guidelines and
correcting errors. In this section, we connect the results to our
research questions (RQ1–RQ3).
Our results confirm that collusion among assistant agents
is a potent threat in AI-driven healthcare systems. Because
healthcare IoT decisions directly affect patient safety, even
small deviations such as wrong doses can cause harm, making
collusion a uniquely serious risk in this domain. In line
with prior work on multi-agent deception [25], we show that
repeated, coordinated wrong suggestions can reliably influence
AI doctors to take wrong decisions, answering RQ1. Specifi-
cally, when multiple assistants push the same wrong answer,
the accuracy collapses in scripted and unscripted modes.
The second question RQ2 concerned whether coordinated
consensus among adversarial assistants is more dangerous than
uncoordinated misinformation. The results confirm that iso-
lated wrong suggestions rarely misled the doctor, but coordi-

nated agreement did. When multiple assistants coordinated on
the same wrong answer, attack success rates rose dramatically.
This highlights the special role of consensus pressure, showing
that the collective alignment of adversarial voices is more

Crucially, the verifier agent blocks attacks without compro-
mising baseline correctness. In all defensive runs, the verifier
restored guideline fidelity (accuracy = 100%) and prevented
harmful prescriptions (ASR=0), while preventing any false
overrides. This validates RQ3: a lightweight verification layer
can neutralize collusion while preserving performance. Al-
though the dataset size (50 questions) is moderate, the results
showed stable and consistent patterns across all modes and
models. This indicates that even early evaluations are sufficient
to reveal systematic risks of collusion and the effectiveness of
defences.

Our findings on collusion link to recent observations on
LLM behaviour in strategic games. Researchers found that
repeated interactions can make LLM agents move toward
cooperation and that model type and personality traits strongly

assistants agree, their pressure can override neutral reasoning
unless guideline checks are used. Their idea of conditional
trust suggests an extension: using adaptive verification, where
the verifier checks less often if assistants seem reliable.
Overall, these works show that agent design choices like bias,
personality, or trust rules strongly affect system robustness.
Taken together, these findings underscore the seriousness of
collusion risks in healthcare multi-agent systems, while also
demonstrating a clear and effective path forward. Collusion
reliably misleads naive AI doctors, but adding a verifier agent
provides a robust safeguard that restores guideline accuracy at
negligible cost.
A. Limitations and Future Work
As the first study to examine collusion attacks in LLM-
based IoT healthcare, our evaluation is aimed to provide an
early but credible foundation rather than a full benchmark.
We used a moderate dataset of 50 representative clinical
questions, sufficient to reveal stable and interpretable patterns.
We leave scaling to larger datasets for future work. Collu-
sion mechanism was implemented in a controlled way, while
more adaptive forms of collusion remains for future work,
as it would require more advanced modeling. We fixed a
consensus threshold (k > 3) to isolate the consensus-pressure
mechanism, leaving a full ASR-vs-k study for later analysis.
Finally, our verifier agent relied on gold answers and trusted
clinical knowledge, which allowed us to demonstrate proof-of-
concept. In future extensions, we aim to ground verification
in structured clinical guidelines, electronic health records, and
IoT health monitoring data.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented the first systematic study of collusion
attacks against a single trusted agent in LLM-based IoT health-
care, highlighting a novel and serious failure mode for multi-
agent AI systems. We showed that adversarial assistant agents
can push an AI doctor toward harmful recommendations,
even when the doctor is neutral. In particular, both Grok 4
Fast and Meta LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct failed in scripted and
consensus-biased modes, while the neutral unscripted mode
also proved unsafe because even small deviations are unac-
ceptable in healthcare. These results underscore that collusion
pressure is a notable vulnerability in LLMs and their decision
styles.
Our evaluation shows that a lightweight verifier agent
can completely neutralize these attacks, offering a safer IoT
healthcare deployment. These findings are not limited to
healthcare: any IoT setting with distributed decision-making
(e.g., telemedicine platforms, smart homes, industrial IoT) can
face similar collusion dynamics. Ensuring guideline fidelity
through lightweight verification is required for trustworthy
AI in connected environments. Therefore, collusion-resilient
verification should be treated as a core requirement for safe
and trustworthy IoT healthcare AI.
VIII. ETHICAL STATEMENT
This study relies only on synthetic question–answer pairs
generated by ChatGPT to reflect medical guidelines. No
patient records or clinical trial data were used. The work
focuses solely on security risks of collusion in AI healthcare
and proposes lightweight defences without raising privacy
concerns.
