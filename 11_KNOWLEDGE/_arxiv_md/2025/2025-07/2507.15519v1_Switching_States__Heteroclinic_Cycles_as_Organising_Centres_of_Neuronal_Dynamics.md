Switching States: Heteroclinic Cycles as Organising Centres of
Neuronal Dynamics
Kateryna Nechyporenko1,2, Peter Ashwin2, and Krasimira Tsaneva-Atanasova1,2
1Living Systems Institute, University of Exeter, Stocker Road, Exeter EX4 4PY, UK
2Department of Mathematics and Statistics, University of Exeter, Harrison Building,

Abstract
Neuronal networks alternate between high- and low-activity regimes, known as up and down
states. They also display rhythmic patterns essential for perception, memory consolidation, and
sensory processing. Despite their importance, the principles behind such state transitions re-
main elusive. We propose necessary conditions for the existence of a novel bifurcation structure
as a universal organising centre governing these transitions. Bifurcation analysis and simula-
tions of canonical mean-field network models, including Wilson–Cowan, Tsodyks–Markram, and
Jansen–Rit frameworks, show that this bifurcation structure emerges robustly across models.
We demonstrate that the interplay between external input and (synaptic) connectivity con-
verges onto this shared mechanism, providing a fundamental principle for understanding how
diverse brain states arise and are regulated. Beyond phenomenological mean-field models, we
show that a shared mathematical structure of nonlinear input-output relationships, rather than
model-specific details, preserves the organising centre across frameworks, revealing a general
mechanism for dynamic state transitions.

Introduction
Neuronal activity often exhibits transitions between distinct, experimentally observable regimes,
each characterised by unique dynamical properties. Understanding how such transitions arise and
persist is central to deciphering brain function. Dynamical systems modelling and theory offer the
tools necessary to investigate and interpret such complex dynamics. In particular, phenomenological
mean-field models of neuronal network activity have proven valuable in exploring the mechanisms
governing transitions between neural states, offering a bridge between observed time series data
and the underlying physiological processes [1, 2, 3, 4].
Neuronal network dynamics arise due to broadly defined excitatory (positive feedback) and in-
hibitory (negative feedback) interactions. Such interactions have inspired the development of vari-
ous phenomenological mean-field models that capture the collective behaviour of neural populations.
The Wilson-Cowan model remains a foundational framework for cortical dynamics, describing in-
teractions between excitatory and inhibitory populations via a reduction to firing rate equations
[5, 6]. In contrast, models based on Tsodyks-Markram framework shift the focus to the synap-
tic level, modelling short-term plasticity through neurotransmitter depletion (synaptic depression)
and recovery (synaptic facilitation) [7, 8]. Extensions such as the Mongillo-Barak-Tsodyks model
incorporate calcium-dependent facilitation [9], Markram-Wang-Tsodyks model refines the original

multiple vesicle pool dynamics [11].
Another widely used class of neural mean-field models includes Jansen-Rit framework, designed
to reproduce features of electroencephalogram (EEG) and local field potential (LFP) recordings [12].
The Jansen-Rit model is structured as a network of pyramidal neurons receiving input from both
inhibitory and excitatory interneuron populations, with model equations representing a realisation
of Wilson-Cowan framework [13].
Other variations of the model include Robinson model [14],
Wendling model[15], Liley model [16, 17] and Freeman model [18].
Across this range of modelling approaches and applications, a central challenge remains: Can
one understand how network activity gives rise to metastable dynamics, transient yet structured
states, without relying on chaotic mechanisms? Recent perspective [19] highlights two non-chaotic
mechanisms underlying metastable dynamics: bistability, where the activity states switch between
two coexisting attractors due to noise or external inputs; and heteroclinic dynamics, where the
system moves along the trajectories connecting unstable attractors (saddles). While bistability
provides robustness via sustained attractors [20], heteroclinic dynamics supports order and timing
through the sequential transitions [21, 22]. Both approaches are, however, not mutually exclusive.
Winnerless competition (WLC) is a dynamical systems concept that bridges bistability and hete-
roclinic dynamics, demonstrating how the system with nonsymmetric inhibition and competition
can switch between winner-takes-all behaviour (bistability) and sequential switching among states
[23, 24].
This duality plays a key role in modelling memory and cognition. Deco and Rolls proposed
a model in which sequential memory arises from attractor dynamics and sequential transitions
between neural states, driven by short-term synaptic depression [25]. In other work, Friston and
Buzs´aki argue that hippocampus-prefrontal system employs complementary stability mechanisms,
namely, bistability, to maintain dynamical stability and sequential dynamics for temporal ordering
[26].
While bistability and heteroclinic dynamics are identified as key mechanisms in brain state
transition, there is no unifying theory of non-chaotic metastability dynamics. To address this gap,
we introduce a novel dynamic mechanism (SNIC2) that bridges bistable and sequential dynamics
and explains transitions between different dynamic states such as the up and down states that are
integral to how the brain processes information. We provide the necessary conditions that give rise
to this phenomenon as well as demonstrate that SNIC2 is present in canonical mean-field models,
such as Wilson-Cowan, Tsodyks-Markram and Jansen-Rit. Our results offer a deeper understanding
of the fundamental structures that shape the dynamics of mean-field neuronal network models.

2.1
Conserved bifurcation structure across phenomenological mean-field mod-
els of neural activity
Phenomenological mean-field models of brain dynamics can exhibit a range of dynamic behaviours,
including high activity state (up state), quiescence (down state), oscillations (limit cycle), and
bistability (co-existence of up and down states). We propose a new dynamical system mechanism,
which we term SNIC2, that serves as an organising centre that gives rise to the transition between
these states (Figure 1A). The SNIC2 is a bifurcation point that features a heteroclinic cycle between
two non-hyperbolic saddles (saddle nodes). The proposed name, SNIC2, reflects that this bifurca-
tion emerges from the intersection of two distinct codimension-two saddle node on invariant circle
(SNIC) curves. Non-hyperbolic heteroclinic cycles are rarely discussed in the literature. However,

To illustrate the idea, assume the system in equations 1-2 is based on Wilson-Cowan (WC)
framework that describes mathematically the temporal dynamics arising from the interactions be-
tween populations of simplified excitatory and inhibitory neurons. Wilson and Cowan demonstrated
that their model is capable of supporting up to five equilibrium points, although these do not neces-
sarily coexist simultaneously [5]. In the WC model the variables x and y correspond to the activity
in the excitatory and inhibitory population, respectively, and are coupled through a sigmoidal inter-
action function. In computational neuroscience, sigmoidal functions are commonly used to model
the population-averaged response of neurons. These functions capture the nonlinear relationship
between presynaptic input and the probability of neuronal firing, effectively translating input into
bounded output [28]. Specifically, the shape of the sigmoidal function allows one to incorporate
neuronal features, such as saturation effects and firing thresholds [29]. Phase space analysis of
phenomenological mean-field models reveals that the nullcline associated with the excitatory pop-
ulation exhibits non-monotonic behaviour due to self-excitation, characterised by a local maximum
and a local minimum. In contrast, the nullcline corresponding to the inhibitory population and
synaptic depression is typically monotonic and retains a sigmoidal profile. The combination of
these two characteristics allows to provide the conditions that give rise to the SNIC2 bifurcation
(Figure 1B(left)).
In the case of Wilson-Cowan model the presence of two saddle node equilibria also provides an
equilibrium in between the two saddle nodes (Figure 1B(left)). Depending on model parameters, the
middle equilibrium can be unstable, as is observed in the case of SNIC2, or it can be stable, leading to
tristablity [5], that will not allow a SNIC2 phenomenon. We also note that the specific arrangement
of nullclines required to produce the SNIC2 bifurcation naturally leads to the emergence of an
additional equilibrium point positioned between the two saddle nodes.

Given the SNIC2 nullcline configuration, the system can also switch into a bistable mode if two
saddle nodes simultaneously split into a saddle and a node (Figure 1B(right)). This transition is
illustrated in Figure 1A top left panel and demonstrates how sequential bistability (SNIC2) can
switch into the noise-driven bistability. In this particular example, achieving the conditions for the
emergence of a SNIC2 point critically depends on the inhibitory nullcline being steeper compared to
the excitatory population nullcline. Mathematically, this corresponds to the inhibitory population’s
response function having a larger maximal derivative compared to that of the excitatory population,
resulting in a steeper input–output relationship and thus greater sensitivity to parameter variations.
This configuration is characteristic of an inhibition-stabilised network (ISN) that is a recurrent
neural circuit where the recurrent excitatory connections are so strong that, by themselves, they
would drive runaway excitation and instability. Stability is then restored by equally strong (or
stronger) feedback inhibition. This is typical in brain regions, such as the primary visual cortex
and hippocampus, where excitation and inhibition dynamically track each other closely (often
referred to as “balanced networks”), and hence the network operates in an ISN regime[30, 31].

Examples of SNIC2 Bifurcations in Neural Population Models

as well as the necessary conditions that give rise to the phenomenon. We now detail three ex-
plicit examples of phenomenological mean-field models where SNIC2 is present, demonstrating its
universality. The bifurcation analysis of the models was performed in AUTO-07p [32], while the
simulations are run in MATLAB.

2.3
Universality of the phenomenon
We have shown that the topology of the transitions between dynamical states that are driven
by variations in connectivity (synaptic) strength and external input is preserved across the three
phenomenological mean-field models (Wilson–Cowan, Tsodyks–Markram, and Jansen–Rit) and is
structured by the underlying SNIC2 bifurcation mechanism. A common feature of these modelling
frameworks is the choice of a sigmoidal-function-type nonlinearity describing the interactions be-
tween network components (in our case neuronal populations). This is sufficient to satisfy the
necessary conditions NC1-NC2 for emergence of the SNIC2 phenomenon, while capturing non-
linear input-output relationships in a biologically plausible way. However, the SNIC2 necessary
conditions are general and could be satisfied by other types of functions and modelling paradigms.
For example, we are able to detect a SNIC2 point in the Morris-Lecar model [38] that describes
the electrical activity of excitable cells, such as neurons or muscle fibres, using a two-dimensional
system of nonlinear differential equations that captures voltage dynamics and ion channel kinetics
to reproduce spiking and bursting behaviours. This model features a hyperbolic tangent function
that is akin to the sigmoid (see section 4 and Figure 6). Here, we likewise obtain a 2-parameter
bifurcation diagram with a topology similar to that of the phenomenological mean-field network
models by varying the external input to the cell and the maximal potassium conductance, which

considered here stands for the average connectivity (synaptic) strength. In the single-cell model,
we vary the strength of the potassium current, which modulates the repolarising influence exerted
on the membrane potential. Therefore, in our analysis, one of the parameters we vary governs the
intrinsic properties of the system, while the other modulates the extrinsic (or external) drive. It is

Discussion
In this paper, we propose a novel organising mechanism (SNIC2) that governs a wide variety of
transitions between up and down states, oscillatory dynamics, and bistability in neuronal networks,
driven by changes in external input and connection (synaptic) strength. We identify the necessary
conditions for its existence in two-dimensional systems and demonstrate its presence in three phe-
nomenological mean-field models (Wilson-Cowan, Tsodyks-Markram, Jansen-Rit). These findings
suggest that SNIC2 is a central, model-independent organising principle in dynamical systems the-
ory, offering a useful framework for state transitions in neuronal networks and beyond through
low-dimensional bifurcation structures.
The introduced phenomenon builds on existing theory on non-chaotic mechanisms of metasta-
bility by proposing a unifying framework that connects noise-driven bistability with sequential tran-
sitions structured by heteroclinic cycles, all within a minimal two-dimensional model. For example,
13


## Page 14


[19] employs different dynamical systems to illustrate bistability vs sequential transitions, notably,
with a heteroclinic cycle realised by a three-dimensional dynamical system. However, SNIC2 phe-
nomenon also captures these transitions in an unifying manner. A related model of sequential
transitions between neuronal network states was previously explored in research on winnerless
competition, where the system exhibits a transition from sequential switching to winner-takes-all
dynamics [23, 24]; we note that winnerless competition phenomena are also of high codimension
unless there are invariant subspaces imposed by modelling considerations.
In the dynamical systems literature, studies of heteroclinic phenomena in planar (two-dimensional)
case have primarily focused on loops involving saddle equilibria, with considerably less attention
given to the heteroclinic cycles between non-hyperbolic (saddle node) equilibria [39, 27], probably
due to the need for extra parameters to make these persist in models. Moreover, many heteroclinic
phenomena are yet to find application in the context of biologically or physically motivated mod-
els. Here, we address this gap by demonstrating the emergence of heteroclinic cycle phenomenon
in two-dimensional phenomenological mean-field models (Wilson-Cowan [5] and reduced Tsodyks-
Markram [7, 20]) as well as in a single-neuron model (Morris-Lecar [38]). Our results highlight a
previously unexplored connection between dynamical systems theory of heteroclinic cycles and its
physiological relevance to understanding switches between activity modes in the brain.
From a dynamical systems perspective, the SNIC2 phenomenon opens a new avenue for study-
ing the interplay between excitability, multistability, and periodic behaviour in higher dimensions.
Our analysis identifies both planar and higher-dimensional neuronal network models where centre
manifolds of saddle nodes are otherwise stable. It is, however, of course possible that one or both
saddle nodes could have unstable non-central eigendirections in which case we expect a much richer
range of possibilities may arise. Previously, we have highlighted a ”SNICeroclinic bifurcation” hete-
roclinic bifurcation involving a saddle and a saddle node in the GTPase model, where the resulting
loop was unstable [34], suggesting that unstable SNIC2 dynamics can play a role, for example,
on basin boundaries. In higher-dimensional systems, SNIC2 allows for even richer configurations.
Here, we have identified a SNIC2 phenomenon in the higher-dimensional Jansen–Rit model [12],
which mirrors the bifurcation topology found in the two-dimensional case, characterised by non-zero
eigenvalues at the saddle nodes.
From an application standpoint, the significance of our findings is potentially far-reaching across
several domains in neuroscience, medicine, and artificial intelligence. Disorders such as epilepsy [40,
41], schizophrenia [42], and depression [43, 44] are often marked by abnormal transitions between
brain network activity states. Identifying an organising centre that governs these transitions could
help in pinpointing what goes wrong in these conditions, potentially leading to biomarkers or targets
for intervention. Since up and down states are associated with slow-wave sleep and memory con-
solidation [45, 46, 47, 48], insights into their control mechanisms could inform treatments for sleep
disorders or cognitive decline (e.g., in Alzheimer’s disease). Understanding the dynamical principles
organising transitions in neuronal networks can inform how deep brain stimulation (DBS), tran-
scranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), or closed-loop neurofeedback protocols are applied, especially
for conditions like Parkinson’s or treatment-resistant depression. Clinicians could tailor stimulation
parameters to push the system toward desired activity states enabled through modelling patient-
specific dynamics and identifying how close a network is to a bifurcation point. Techniques such as
bifurcation analysis and mean-field modelling provide a theoretical framework to decode brain-wide
dynamics from macro scale recordings [1, 49, 50, 51], potentially improving diagnostics or real-time
brain monitoring (e.g., in ICU or surgery). This approach enables the connection of microscopic
mechanisms (synapses) to macroscopic outcomes (cognitive state transitions), therefore helping to
unify models across scales, which is a major challenge in systems neuroscience. Understanding how
real neuronal networks switch states via global dynamic structures (rather than local rules) offers
14


## Page 15


valuable insights for designing adaptive, energy-efficient neuromorphic hardware or AI models that
can switch between exploration and exploitation modes, or between learning and inference. Em-
bedding similar principles in artificial systems could lead to context-aware computation, where the
“brain” switches into different operating modes depending on task demands or sensory context,
just like biological systems.
In summary, we introduce a universally applicable organising principle that governs how the
brain toggles between different modes of operation. This could find concrete applications in:
• Diagnosing and treating brain disorders.
• Developing more effective brain-computer interfaces and neuromodulation tools.
• Enhancing our fundamental understanding of cognition and consciousness.
• Designing smarter and more brain-like AI.
