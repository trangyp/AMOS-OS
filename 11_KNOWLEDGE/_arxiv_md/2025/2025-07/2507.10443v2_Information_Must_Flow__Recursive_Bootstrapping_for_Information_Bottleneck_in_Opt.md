ABSTRACT
We introduce the Context-Content Uncertainty Principle (CCUP) as a unifying framework for un-
derstanding how information flows through cognitive systems by resolving the broken symmetry
between high-entropy context and low-entropy content. Inference is framed as a dynamic cycle
that aligns context with content through selective, bidirectional interactions, enabling information
to propagate efficiently across representational hierarchies. Within this framework, cycle formation
resolves the Information Bottleneck in Optimal Transport (iBOT) by coordinating bottom-up contex-
tual disambiguation with top-down content reconstruction. This dual process is implemented via a
memory-amortized inference (MAI) strategy, formalized as variational entropy minimization, which
steers the system toward minimal joint uncertainty while preserving the directionality of inference.
Importantly, local cycle completion gives rise to temporally extended chain formation (temporal
bootstrapping) and spatially distributed hierarchical composition (spatial bootstrapping), enabling
the system to simulate goals, evaluate counterfactuals, and iteratively refine internal models. To
formalize this bootstrapped memory process, we prove a Delta Convergence Theorem showing that
recursive entropy minimization over time yields structured memory states that converge to delta-like
distributions over latent manifolds. These converged attractors act as fixed points in the flow of
information, stabilizing perceptual schemas and motor plans as compact, generalizable priors. Spatial
bootstrapping through ventral–dorsal functional segregation and temporal bootstrapping via the
perception–action cycle support progressive refinement of sensorimotor models through continuous
interaction with the environment. The sleep–wake cycle extends this dynamic across timescales,
transforming episodic experiences into semantic knowledge via recursive consolidation, allowing the
flow of information to bridge immediate perception with long-term abstraction.
We further propose a representational extension of CCUP in which each level of the hierarchy
performs delta-seeded inference: internally generated low-entropy content seeds diffuse outward
through goal-constrained inference paths shaped by top-down priors and external context. This
structure-adaptive flow of information circumvents the curse of dimensionality by confining inference
to task-relevant manifolds. Building on this foundation, we hypothesize that language emerges as
a symbolic transport system, an operational extension of CCUP, that externalizes latent content,
enabling the transfer of structured inferences across individuals and aligning intersubjective inference
cycles. Together, these results establish iBOT as a foundational principle for the directed flow of
information in both individual cognition and collective intelligence, situating inference itself as a
structured conduit through which minds adapt, align, and extend.

Problem Formulation: What is iBOT and How to Tackle it?
The Information Bottleneck (IB) is not merely a tool for supervised learning or signal compression; it is a pervasive
constraint shaping biological cognition, AI, and the evolution of representational systems. At its core, the IB formalism
captures a fundamental tradeoff: how to preserve task-relevant information while discarding irrelevant detail [75]. This
tension manifests universally wherever a system must distill high-dimensional observations into compact internal states
capable of supporting prediction, control, or generalization. In neural systems, the bottleneck appears in the selective
encoding of sensory inputs [11], the consolidation of memory [31], and the abstraction of structure across cortical
hierarchies [86]. In artificial agents, it underlies deep feature compression, latent variable modeling, and the emergence
of disentangled representations [1, 32]. Even in natural language, perception, and planning, agents continually navigate
the bottleneck between ambiguous context and structured intent [45, 24]. Recognizing the IB as a universal organizing
principle allows us to reinterpret cognition itself as a process of adaptive compression, one that aligns internal structure
with external demand across time, scale, and domain.
While the IB formalism captures the tension between compression and relevance, it leaves open the question of how
relevant content should be transmitted or instantiated within a high-entropic context. Optimal Transport (OT) [81]
provides a natural geometric solution to this question, modeling inference as the movement of probability mass from
structured content Φ to observed or imagined context Ψ under a cost-minimizing transport plan. However, when Ψ
is noisy and entangled, direct transport from context to content becomes ill-posed, mirroring the core bottleneck of
inference [76]. This defines what we term the Information Bottleneck in Optimal Transport (iBOT): the problem of
aligning structure and specificity when transport is constrained by the need to process information. Resolving the IB in
OT requires inverting the classical inference pipeline, starting from compact latent structure Φ, projecting outward to
generate plausible contexts Ψ, and refining this simulation through cycle-consistent inference. This inverted perspective
transforms the bottleneck from a limitation into a guiding principle: structure-first inference allows the system to
sidestep entropic overload, stabilize transport, and converge toward generalizable representations across time and scale.
The iBOT directly motivates the Context-Content Uncertainty Principle (CCUP) [50], which frames cognition as a
dynamic alignment between structured content Φ and ambiguous context Ψ. Whereas classical inference attempts to
extract structure by compressing Ψ, this strategy becomes increasingly unstable in high-dimensional or underconstrained
regimes. CCUP offers a structural inversion: it begins with compact, low-entropy content Φ and projects outward to
simulate or interpret possible contexts Ψ, thereby transforming the bottleneck into an opportunity for generalization.
In this view, the entropy gradient is reversed, structure precedes specificity, and inference proceeds by minimizing
conditional uncertainty H(Ψ|Φ) rather than H(Φ|Ψ). This shift from bottom-up encoding to top-down simulation
defines the principle of inverted inference: a generative process where structure constrains interpretation, enabling stable
and scalable transport. As such, inverted inference provides a powerful mechanism for resolving iBOT by avoiding the
compression of entangled context and instead organizing cognition around reusable, generalizable seeds of content.
Inverted inference gains its full expressive power when extended through recursive bootstrapping across temporal and
spatial hierarchies [49]. In the temporal domain, each content state is updated not in isolation but as part of a dynamic
sequence, where structure learned at time t serves as the scaffold for inference at t + 1. This recursive alignment forms
the basis of bootstrapped learning dynamics [50], where the system iteratively refines its latent structure by minimizing
conditional entropy over successive episodes. In parallel, spatial bootstrapping enables the composition of higher-level
abstractions from structured subcomponents. Here, the structure is built recursively by assembling local regularities into
increasingly abstract representations, satisfying both inference sufficiency and compositional coherence. The unification
of these two bootstrapping processes forms a multiscale inference engine wherein each layer constrains the next, and
structure is not only inferred but grown, recursively and contextually, in space and time.
Recursive bootstrapping induces a natural convergence dynamic between content and context representations. At each
iteration, the content variable Φ(t) is refined to better align with the distribution of observed or simulated contexts
Ψ(t), while simultaneously guiding the generation or interpretation of future contexts. As this process unfolds, the
system iteratively reduces the conditional entropy H(Ψ(t)|Φ(t)), concentrating probability mass around increasingly
predictive latent representations. Under mild assumptions—such as continuity, compactness, and contractivity of the
update operator—this dynamic converges to a fixed point Φ∗, which minimizes the long-term entropy of context and
satisfies the self-consistency condition Φ∗= F(Φ∗, Ψ∗). In the limit, this yields a delta-like representation [62]: a
sharply peaked distribution over latent content that collapses ambiguity while retaining a generalizable structure.

Theoretical Foundation: Context–Content Uncertainty Principle (CCUP)
Cognition, at its core, is a process of resolving uncertainty [64]. Every act of perception, action, or memory retrieval
involves mapping ambiguous sensory or internal signals onto behaviorally relevant interpretations. We propose CCUP
as a foundational framework for understanding this process. CCUP asserts that context and content are informationally
asymmetric [3]: although context variables may occupy a lower-dimensional sensory space, they exhibit high entropy
due to ambiguity and observational limits; in contrast, content variables often span a broader semantic space but
maintain low entropy due to goal-specific selection [23]. CCUP characterizes inference as a directional transport
process: from entropic context to structured content. This asymmetry drives the directional flow of inference - one can
use context to predict content reliably, but not content to infer context unambiguously.
2.1
iBOT Calls for Dynamic Alignment of Content and Context Representation
Broken Symmetry is Ubiquitous in Information-rich Systems.
Let Ψ represent high-entropy contextual variables
(e.g., sensory observations or task goals) and Φ represent low-entropy content variables (e.g., object identity or action
choice). The task of inference is to estimate Φ given Ψ, but due to the ambiguity and variability of Ψ, a direct mapping
often leads to loss of specificity or poor generalization. For any cognitive task, an agent must infer stable latent causes
from aliased sensory observations [14]. This inferential directionality, from uncertainty to specificity, is not arbitrary
but structured by cognitive architecture and task demands. More fundamentally, it reflects an inherent broken symmetry
in the flow of information [16], as characterized by the following lemma.
Lemma 1 (Asymmetry of Inference in Information-Rich Systems). [51] Let Ψ denote a high-entropy contextual
variable, and let Φ denote a low-entropy, stable content variable inferred from Ψ. The CCUP formalizes an inherent
asymmetry in inference between these variables, reflected in their conditional entropies. In the forward direction, the
mapping Ψ →Φ involves disambiguating aliased contextual input to infer a precise latent cause. This process is
underdetermined, yielding nonzero conditional entropy: H(Φ|Ψ) > 0. In the reverse direction, the mapping Φ →Ψ
requires generating/simulating a range of compatible contextual instances from a compact content representation. This
generalization introduces additional uncertainty: H(Ψ|Φ) ≫H(Φ|Ψ) > 0.
This directional loss is not due to suboptimal learning but is structurally unavoidable given the entropy gradient between
context and content. Asymmetry in inference is not merely local (directional), but is bounded globally by the mismatch
in marginal entropies. Since both directions Ψ →Φ and Φ →Ψ carry irreducible uncertainty, the sum of these
uncertainties must respect the minimum divergence in information content. Inference systems should minimize this
joint uncertainty by leveraging the broken symmetry (structure prior) [3]: H(Φ) ≪H(Ψ), implying that Φ should
be used to condition and constrain the interpretation of Ψ [60, 7]. We start our solution to the iBOT problem with the
following lemma of entropy decomposition with a variational formulation [39].
Lemma 2 (Entropy Decomposition Lemma with Variational Formulation). [50] Let Ψ denote a high-entropy contextual
variable and Φ denote a structured, low-entropy content variable such that their joint distribution p(Φ, Ψ) is well-
defined. Then, for any inference system that seeks to minimize the uncertainty over Φ and Ψ, the optimal inference
policy proceeds by: 1) Minimizing the joint entropy via decomposition: H(Φ, Ψ) = H(Φ) + H(Ψ|Φ); 2) Under
the assumption H(Φ) ≪H(Ψ), the conditional entropy H(Ψ|Φ) dominates and becomes the principal target of
minimization; 3) This induces a directional alignment: inference should proceed by first establishing Φ, then using
it to constrain Ψ, such that: Φ ⇝Ψ
(structure precedes specificity). Moreover, the variational inference objective:

How IB in OT Arises from the Broken Symmetry.
Under the framework of OT [81], a high-entropic contextual
distribution µ(Ψ) must be mapped onto a lower-entropic content distribution ν(Φ), often via a latent variable Z. Based
on CCUP, the asymmetry between H(Ψ) and H(Φ) induces directional information loss. Therefore, asymmetry creates
an arrow: the inference process must compensate for this directional loss by cycling- i.e., refining Φ through repeated
bottom-up passes and updating Ψ through top-down reconstruction. Only through such cycle formation can the system
mitigate information loss and stabilize representations. Any latent encoding Ψ →Z that attempts to encode context for
relevance to Φ must confront an IB: Z cannot simultaneously preserve enough information for both content specificity
and context recovery. Thus, the classical IB in OT is not merely a constraint on mutual information [16] but a structural
consequence of broken symmetry in uncertainty, which is summarized by the following lemma.
Lemma 3 (Entropy Asymmetry Implies Bottleneck in OT). [51] Let Ψ and Φ be random variables representing
high-entropy context and low-entropy content, respectively. Suppose the joint distribution p(Ψ, Φ) is transported via a
coupling γ ∈Π(p(Ψ), p(Φ)). Under CCUP, we observe: H(Ψ) ≫H(Φ) ⇒I(Ψ; Φ) < H(Ψ), which implies that
any transport from p(Ψ) to p(Φ) incurs an information bottleneck. Moreover, static transport mappings T : Ψ 7→Φ
cannot preserve mutual information. To recover representational fidelity, the system must employ dynamic alignment:
a cyclic process alternating between bottom-up inference p(Φ|Ψ) and top-down reconstruction p(Ψ|Φ).
The inherent asymmetry in entropy between context and content variables makes information loss during OT [81]
unavoidable, creating an IB [74]. Theorem 1 implies that one-way transport cannot preserve mutual information:
encoding context into content inherently discards information unless explicitly corrected via reconstructive feedback.
The only way to overcome this bottleneck is through dynamic alignment: an inference cycle that co-evolves content
and context representations via top-down and bottom-up passes. We first give equivalent conditions for dynamic
alignment below.
Definition 1 (Equivalent Conditions for Context-Content Alignment). [49] Let Ψ denote a high-entropy context
variable and Φ a low-entropy content variable, with bidirectional inference maps q(Φ|Ψ) (inference) and p(Ψ|Φ)
(generation). The pair (Ψ, Φ) is said to be aligned if the following equivalent conditions hold: 1) Cycle Consistency:
DKL(Ψ ∥ˆΨ) ≤ε, DKL(Φ ∥ˆΦ) ≤ε, where ˆΦ = q(Φ|Ψ) and ˆΨ = p(Ψ|ˆΦ), and ε ≥0 is a small alignment tolerance.
2) Mutual Information Saturation: I(Ψ; Φ) ≈H(Φ) ≈H(Ψ), indicating that context and content share nearly
all their informational degrees of freedom. 3) Entropy Symmetry (CCUP Condition): H(Φ|Ψ) ≈0, H(Ψ|Φ) ≈0,
such that each variable fully determines the other under minimal uncertainty. 4) Optimal Transport Coupling:
γ∗= arg minγ∈Π(PΨ,PΦ) Eγ[c(Ψ, Φ)] −εH(γ), where γ∗is a near-deterministic transport plan with low entropy,
implying functional invertibility between context and content.
The insight underlying dynamic alignment of context and content representations informs both theoretical architectures
(e.g., variational autoencoders [39], Helmholtz machines [17]) and biological systems (e.g., ventral-dorsal loops [80],
sleep-based memory consolidation [70]), where recurrent inference cycles are necessary to counteract irreversible
information loss. However, any cycle completion involves a chicken-and-egg problem, arising from the circular
dependency between structured content (Φ) and contextual specificity (Ψ).
2.2
Dynamic Alignment Requires Inverted Inference and Bootstrapping
Structure-before-Specificity Implies Inverted Inference.
In classical formulations, OT [81] faces the challenge of
mapping a high-entropy contextual distribution µ(Ψ) to a lower-entropy content distribution ν(Φ) via a latent variable Z.
Traditional wisdom frames this as a one-shot encoding problem: how to encode Ψ into Z while preserving relevance to
Φ, which introduces an IB due to the entropy gap H(Ψ) ≫H(Φ). CCUP offers a fundamentally different perspective
on introducing a feedback loop to form an asymmetric cycle based on the following observation. The broken symmetry
in entropy decomposition introduces a natural directionality in inference: rather than treating Φ and Ψ symmetrically,
optimal inference must proceed from low-entropy structure to high-entropy specificity. In other words, the system
must first identify structured content Φ, and then use it to resolve ambiguity in the contextual input Ψ. This directional
constraint gives rise to the Structure-before-Specificity (SbS) principle [50], which we now formalize below.
Principle 1 (Structure-before-Specificity Principle). [50] Let Ψ denote high-entropy context variables and Φ de-
note low-entropy content variables inferred under CCUP. Let D = {Ψi}N
i=1 be a set of observed contexts drawn
from an environment. Then an optimal inference strategy proceeds in two stages: 1) Structure Formation (Gener-
alization): The agent seeks a latent structure Φ that minimizes the expected conditional entropy across contexts:
Φ∗= arg minΦ Ei [H(Ψi|Φ)], which yields a generalizable representation Φ∗that captures invariant structure across
variable examples; 2) Experience Binding (Specification): Once Φ∗is established, the agent performs context-specific
inference by minimizing: H(Φ∗|Ψj) for each new example Ψj ∈Dnew, effectively binding content to context using the
learned structural prior.

2.3
Bootstrapping as a Meta-Strategy for Learning and Inference
The temporal difference update rule inspires the formulation of inverted inference in Q-learning [83]. Both the
half-step update in Q-learning and the generalization–specification loop in inverted inference rely on the strategy of
bootstrapping: using current approximations to improve future estimates in the absence of full information, thereby
enabling convergence toward optimal representations through recursive refinement. The brilliant idea behind celebrated
Q-learning [83] is to perform a one-step bootstrapped approximation, using a partial estimate of future value to update
current action values rather than computing the full trajectory to a reward. This “half-step down” idea bypasses the
need for full expansion of the future, suggesting that forward search can be circumvented by local, structured inference.

5.1
From Spatiotemporal Bootstrapping to Social Bootstrapping
While spatiotemporal bootstrapping enables individual agents to construct coherent internal models by integrating
information across scales of space (e.g., object composition) and time (e.g., episodic memory), such inference remains
fundamentally bounded by the agent’s own sensory history and computational constraints. To transcend these individual
limitations, cognitive systems engage in social bootstrapping [13], a process through which structured latent content is
externalized, shared, and iteratively refined via communicative interaction. In any act of communication, a speaker
must transform a rich internal state, comprising beliefs, intentions, emotions, or memories, into a compressed external
signal such as language, gesture, or expression [77]. This transformation inevitably involves information loss, posing a
fundamental tradeoff: how can the speaker reduce the complexity of the message while still preserving its relevance
to the listener’s interpretive task? This tradeoff is captured by the IB principle, which formalizes the problem of
compressing internal content in a way that maximally preserves task-relevant information. In the context of social
communication, the IB framework models how agents encode and decode signals to align internal models under
constraints of noise, ambiguity, and limited bandwidth. It provides a powerful lens for understanding how language,
emotion, and shared context evolve to mitigate communicative uncertainty and enable intersubjective inference.
Definition 3 (Information Bottleneck in Social Communication). Let Φ ∈S denote the structured internal state (e.g.,

informative under the listener’s reconstruction process.
In social contexts, this mechanism becomes the basis for social bootstrapping, a process whereby intersubjective
constraints recursively anchor individual inference cycles. Through imitation, joint attention, and communicative
feedback, agents co-construct shared latent models, refining their internal representations via socially mediated error
