Abstract
Should households buy their homes? Contrary to popular expert advice, our block-bootstrap life-
cycle simulation provides an affirmative answer. Homeownership generates wealth and welfare
gains relative to rent-for-life benchmarks that invest only in financial assets. It lowers household
portfolio downside risk and improves retirement consumption and bequest outcomes. The gains
reflect risk aversion, intertemporal substitution, leverage-induced trade-offs between consumption
and the timing of home purchases, and the liquidity costs of homeownership. Their magnitude
varies across labor income profiles, house price environments, and mortgage rates. Our findings
suggest that homeownership can build wealth more effectively than common portfolio strategies,
including all-equity portfolios.

Introduction
Homeownership is central to American households’ portfolio allocation. 66% of American house-
holds are homeowners, and 80% of households entering homeownership are willing to finance the
purchase.1 In public perception, real estate has ranked first since 2013 as the best long-run investment,
overtaking stocks, bonds, and savings.2 Housing is the most important asset class and the primary
wealth storage for American households, especially for the bottom 50% of households in wealth dis-
tribution (Campbell, 2006; Diwan et al., 2021). For a typical American homeowner, the home accounts
for close to 80% of total household assets (Campbell and Ramadorai, 2025).
Despite its popularity among the public, housing as an investment instrument has long been criti-
cized by experts. Choi (2022) documents that fourteen out of fifty most popular personal finance books
explicitly state that housing is not a great investment, and Cochrane (2022) describes investments in
housing as “disastrous” investments and advises against them.3 Additionally, the illiquid nature of
homeownership as an asset could reduce household consumption and equity market participation,
reducing life-cycle welfare (Campbell, 2006). Meanwhile, the U.S. homeownership growth has lagged
behind other countries, and many Americans have started questioning whether homeownership should
remain part of the “American Dream” (Goodman and Mayer, 2018).
In light of these conflicting views, understanding the life cycle economic implications of home-
ownership is crucial amid the current affordability crisis in the U.S. Should a household purchase a
home from a long-run perspective? This paper provides an affirmative answer to the question and
challenges the negative view on the potential of homeownership in wealth creation and welfare en-
hancement. Considering portfolio allocation in stocks, long-term government bonds, and discretionary
saving rates, our results suggest that a homeowner over the life cycle would generally enjoy gains in
wealth and welfare at death compared to common renter benchmarks, including the all-equity, 60/40

Specifically, we apply a stationary block bootstrap simulation to the historical data within a life cycle
model that incorporates labor income risk, risky returns from different asset classes, and longevity risk.
We utilize the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database (Macrohistory Database) (Jordà et al.,
2019) and employ the method of Politis and Romano (1994) and Anarkulova et al. (2023). Relative to the
literature, our block bootstrap simulation enjoys the advantage of preserving the long-run multivariate
time-series autocorrelation inherent in the data while circumventing the computational constraints that
lead dynamic programming models to restrict the state space, use coarsely spaced time periods, and
impose parametric assumptions on the joint distribution of asset returns and labor income shocks.4
While it is necessary for tractability in dynamic programming models to limit the state space and
suppress the cross-variable dependencies, doing so may materially distort household balance sheets
over the life cycle. By drawing blocks from history in our simulation, we reduce the necessity of
such restrictive assumptions, and the long-run multivariate interdependence is naturally preserved.
Meanwhile, because our setting effectively allows each household to experience a unique economic
environment, our method produces a panel-level annualized total housing risk of 15.33%, relative to 6%
from Cocco (2005), matching that of Giacoletti (2021) measured with individual houses’ holding-period
return data that retains the idiosyncratic risk. Based on 150 years of data across 16 developed countries,
our bootstrap procedure draws observations from millions of possible life-cycle scenarios and evaluates
household performance. The long-run perspective allows us to avoid making conclusions about life-
cycle investments by relying excessively on short-run panel data, as commonly used panel data barely
cover one generation’s full life cycle.
We draw our main conclusion of homeownership’s investment value based on its life-cycle per-
formance along the dimensions of wealth accumulation, welfare enhancement, downside risks, and
wealth inequality. Our evaluation of life-cycle investment strategies begins with constructing a single
household’s life-cycle economic environment using stationary block bootstrap sampling (Politis and
Romano, 1994). We then backtest the exogenously defined strategies with the same simulated house-
hold life cycle. Specifically, for each investment strategy with pre-specified home purchase rules, a

Homeownership preserves wealth, enhances welfare, protects households from the downside risks
of their wealth portfolios, and mitigates wealth inequality. Given these significant benefits, we explore
the underlying mechanisms through which the benefits are generated. We propose four mechanisms
behind the homeownership premium. First, because of the curvature of the utility function, homeown-
ership creates value by reducing risk as a better bond-like asset. We show that the average consumption
equivalent gain relative to the benchmark at the 15% saving rate increases with risk aversion from ap-
proximately 1.0% at δ = 3 to 3.4% at the baseline value of δ = 5, and reaches 4.6% at δ = 10. In
addition, homeowners in our simulations enjoy smooth maintenance costs in addition to their mort-
gage payments, unlike their renter benchmarks that face the volatility of rent-to-income ratios (Sinai
and Souleles, 2005).

in the working life potentially due to the reduction in financial asset holdings, consistent with the prior
literature (Becker and Shabani, 2010; Cocco et al., 2005; Vestman, 2019; Yao and Zhang, 2004). Similarly,

Related Literature
Our study responds to the call for a long-run evaluation of homeownership as an investment, of-
fers essential advice to life-cycle investors, and challenges the negative view on housing investment
(Campbell, 2006; Cochrane, 2022; Cocco, 2005; Choi, 2022; Duarte et al., 2022). Our contribution is
methodological.
Two strands of research frame the homeownership debate. The first strand examines long-run
aggregate price dynamics. Shiller (2015) documents that real home prices in the United States display
no continuous upward trend from 1980 to 2020, and Eicholtz (1997) shows with more than 350 years
of data that local house prices can grow as slowly as 0.14% per year. Choi (2022) reports that popular
personal finance books broadly advise against housing as an investment, and Cochrane (2022) argues
that housing compares unfavorably to equity over the long run. These findings, however, do not fully
account for the complexity of homeownership from the perspective of household decision making. A
household adopts a specific purchase strategy, owns the home for a period substantially shorter than the
time-series horizon, and benefits from leverage and imputed rental savings that aggregate price indices
do not capture. Interpreting household-level homeownership outcomes directly from aggregate price
trends therefore can be misleading. The other strand takes a household perspective using panel data.
For example, Sinai and Souleles (2005) use Current Population Survey (CPS) data from 1990 to 1999
to document that homeownership rates rise with household tenure horizon and the hedging benefits
of housing, while Yao and Zhang (2004) calibrate their life-cycle model against Panel Study of Income
Dynamics (PSID) Wealth Supplement data gathered in only five irregular waves between 1984 and
2001. The panel data are informative, but short windows and infrequent balance-sheet observations
limit generalizability to broader return environments.
Meanwhile, the canonical dynamic programming approach manages the curse of dimensionality
by restricting the return process in ways that limit long-run inference. Stock return innovations are
independently and identically distributed over time in Cocco et al. (2005) and Gomes and Michaelides
8

(2005), and the binomial process that Yao and Zhang (2004) apply to both stock and housing returns
is i.i.d. by construction. These restrictions rule out serial dependence, multi-year autocorrelation,
and regime propagation across periods. Cross-variable dependence is collapsed further. Cocco et al.
(2005) and Gomes and Michaelides (2005) capture all interaction between returns and income through
a single contemporaneous correlation scalar, and Yao and Zhang (2004) parameterize every pairwise
relationship across stock returns, housing returns, and labor income as a fixed contemporaneous scalar.
In the most extreme case, Cocco (2005) sets the correlation between aggregate house price shocks and
labor income shocks to exactly one, explicitly to eliminate one state variable, collapsing both processes
into a single random variable.
Much of the life-cycle housing literature calibrates housing risk to national, regional, or metropoli-
tan price indices, which capture common price movements but abstract from the full distribution of re-
alized household returns. Cocco (2005) calibrates annual housing return volatility at 6.2%, while Flavin
and Yamashita (2002), Sinai and Souleles (2005), and Yao and Zhang (2004) are similarly informative
about aggregate or local market risk but do not model a household-level return distribution. Recent
granular evidence establishes that this dimension of risk is significant. Using property-level repeat-
sales data, Giacoletti (2021) estimates annualized total housing return risk at approximately 15.6% at
short horizons, with idiosyncratic risk accounting for a large share of total variation. Even at a five-
year horizon, the idiosyncratic component is approximately 8.6% while the local aggregate component
is approximately 9%. Bourassa et al. (2009) also documents substantial within-market heterogeneity in
house price appreciation across otherwise similar properties.
Our stationary block bootstrap simulation sidesteps all of the data and dynamic programming re-
strictions. Following Politis and Romano (1994) and Anarkulova et al. (2023), our method preserves the
serial structure of each variable and their long-run joint comovement without imposing any parametric
constraint on the dependence structure. By simulating the household life cycle using the income struc-
ture from Guvenen et al. (2021) and 150 years of historical data, we provide a long-run perspective on
household investments that accounts for the complexity of household portfolio decisions. Because the
simulation is conducted at the household level, it produces a panel that naturally captures household-
level idiosyncratic variation and yields a total housing return volatility of 15.33%, closely matching the
realized housing return risk documented by Giacoletti (2021).

Their study does not examine homeownership. While Sinai and Souleles (2005) provide the theoretical

risk. Duarte et al. (2022) contributes a solution method for a high-dimensional parametric optimiza-

tion preserves multi-year return dependence rather than imposing a restrictive parametric stochastic
environment. The simulation incorporates household-level idiosyncratic variation beyond aggregate
market risk. Because we draw time blocks from 16 countries over 150 years, we avoid the generaliz-
ability concerns of short panels and lucky market bias (Anarkulova et al., 2022; van Binsbergen et al.,

3.2
Simulation Overview
Through the analysis of households’ life cycles, we assess different life-cycle investment strategies
involving homeownership and financial assets. We consider a discrete-time life-cycle model that ac-
counts for households navigating changing economic environments. We follow Guvenen et al. (2021)
for the labor income before retirement to capture various income risks, and households receive Social
Security benefits after retirement.
To maintain the long-run multivariate time-series autocorrelation of different markets in the eco-
nomic environment, we implement a stationary block bootstrap approach (Anarkulova et al., 2023; Poli-
tis and Romano, 1994). Unlike the dynamic programming approach, which must parameterize the joint

3.4
Strategies
Households all start from zero wealth and make life-cycle investment decisions at each period.
Specifically, we construct strategies from the following four parts.
Saving and Consumption
Before retirement, households save a fixed fraction of net labor income
(labor income minus fixed costs) and consume the rest. We examine saving rates of 10%, 15%, and 20%,
with 15% as the baseline. Our search space of saving rates is motivated by literature. First, the baseline
is anchored to Scholz et al. (2006), who find that a constant saving rate of approximately 14.7% of labor
income replicates the life-cycle optimal wealth path derived from a fully calibrated life cycle dynamic
programming model. The same target is corroborated by Dynan et al. (2004), who document compre-
hensive household saving rates rising from 9% at the bottom of the income distribution to 17–19% for
higher-income households, with the median converging near the 15% target. Because our model con-
tains no employer-sponsored pension plan, the discretionary saving rate is the direct counterpart to this
comprehensive measure, as all saving that Dynan et al. (2004) distribute across active saving, defined
contribution pension contributions, and Social Security saving is consolidated into the single saving
rate in our framework. Meanwhile, Choi (2022) surveys the fifty most widely read personal finance
books and finds that nearly all numerical saving targets fall within the 10–15% range, with a minority
extending the recommendation to 20% for households prioritizing early financial independence. This
19


## Page 21


survey-based evidence independently corroborates 15% as the center of conventionally recommended
saving practice and defines the outer bounds of our grid.
Second, the income eligibility conditions for homeownership in our model screen in households
above a certain income level, who must cover minimum consumption plus mortgage obligations before
qualifying for purchase. Huggett and Ventura (2000) document that U.S. households at approximately
one and a half times mean income save approximately 13% of income in cross-sectional data. The
calibrated life cycle model implies an optimal rate of approximately 18% for this group, as above-median
earners face comparatively low Social Security replacement rates and must finance a larger share of
retirement consumption through private saving. The 15% baseline therefore lies at the center of the
empirically observed and model-implied range for the homeownership-eligible population.
Third, major financial institutions also advocate the same 15% target: Fidelity Investments (2024)
and J.P. Morgan Asset Management (2024) both identify 15% of pre-tax income as the standard retire-
ment saving target, and the Vanguard Group (2024) annual survey of defined contribution plan par-
ticipants reports median 401(k) contribution rates of approximately 11% inclusive of employer match.
Because this figure reflects retirement account contributions alone and excludes other saving vehi-
cles, total household saving would exceed this amount and is consistent with a saving target of 15%
or higher. The saving-rate grid also serves a central economic purpose in the paper. The 10% case
is a conservative boundary design that reveals when mortgage service suppresses working-life con-
sumption enough to overturn the consumption equivalent gain, whereas the 15% and 20% cases show
when housing’s wealth gains translate reliably into consumption equivalent gains against the all-equity
renter benchmark.
We follow the popular 4% rule and assume that retirees consume the greater of 4% of current finan-
cial wealth and the withdrawal from the prior period, so consumption does not fall below its previously
attained level (Bengen, 1994; Choi, 2022). Household savings are invested entirely in equities, with one-
third allocated to domestic equity and two-thirds to an equal-weighted international equity portfolio,
excluding the home market, constructed across all countries in the Macrohistory Database sample.
Home Purchase
The home purchase rules are set to imitate the real world. We assume that a house-
hold would purchase a home when two conditions are simultaneously satisfied. First, the household’s
current-period labor income must exceed the sum of the minimum consumption floor and the required
20

mortgage payment, ensuring the household can service the debt without immediately entering finan-
cial distress. Second, the household’s financial wealth must be sufficient to cover the required down
payment fraction of the home value, the additional purchase threshold expressed as a fraction of home
value, and the transaction costs of purchase.
We consider different combinations of down payment (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 100% of the
home value), purchase thresholds (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% of the home value), and the popular
Payment-to-Income (PTI) ratio of at most 1/3. The PTI rule requires the household to spend at most
1/3 of its labor income on the mortgage payment. For strategies considering access to a second home,
if the household decides to purchase a second home, we require the household to have fully paid off its
mortgage on the first home before taking on additional leverage for a second home.
These settings are consistent with the literature and the real-world practice. The two-condition
eligibility rule reflects the joint income and wealth requirements documented in the household finance
literature. Yao and Zhang (2004) show that households remain renters until their liquid assets are suf-
ficient to meet both the down payment requirement and subsequent home equity constraints, while
Cocco (2005) establishes that the down payment constraint prevents young households from purchas-
ing the house size they desire. The additional buffer of 10%–50% of home value above the down payment
ensures the household retains liquid reserves after closing and delays the home purchase, capturing
the empirical pattern in Flavin and Yamashita (2002) that young homeowners enter with a house-to-
net-worth ratio as high as 3.51 and the ratio declines to 0.65 for oldest homeowners. Our choice of PTI
rule mirrors the standard FHA’s “front-end ratio” of 31% and the corresponding debt-to-income ratio
of 36% by agencies such as Fannie Mae.
Following the literature, we impose an annual maintenance cost of 2% of the home value (Cocco,
2005; Cocco and Lopes, 2020; Nakajima and Telyukova, 2017; Yao and Zhang, 2004). In our mechanism
analysis, we also consider other levels of maintenance cost. We also set a transaction cost of 2% of
home value at both purchase and sale.
Reverse Mortgage
Senior households will earn less labor income and have a higher chance of finan-
cial distress. We consider the reverse mortgage as a way to unlock housing capital and maintain the
consumption level. We assume that a household will take a reverse mortgage when all four of the fol-
lowing conditions are simultaneously satisfied. First, the household’s combined income from SSB and

the liquid asset withdrawal is sufficient to cover ongoing home maintenance costs, so the household
can remain in the property. Second, the reverse mortgage value available under the PLF exceeds any
remaining mortgage balance, ensuring positive net equity is available to borrow against. Third, the
combined retirement income from SSB and the floor withdrawal falls to or below the minimum con-
sumption threshold. Fourth, the household is below age 95. When all conditions are met, the household
borrows the maximum HECM-eligible amount. Households are only eligible to take a reverse mortgage
after retirement and will not repay the reverse mortgage unless they have to liquidate the home. We
assume households will only take a reverse mortgage on the first home, since the reverse mortgage
market generally requires the primary residence to be the collateral.
Liquidation
Due to the illiquidity of the housing investment, households may face financial dif-
ficulties and have to liquidate their homes. We consider two liquidation rules. We assume that the
households are self-interested and economically well-aware such that a household will liquidate its
home when the current loan-to-value (LTV) ratio rises above 1.5 after the origination of the loan.17 A
household will also liquidate its home when the minimum consumption level exceeds total available
liquid resources, that is, when current-period labor income plus prior-period financial wealth plus any
secondary-home rental income, less ongoing maintenance costs, is insufficient to meet the minimum
consumption threshold (De Nardi et al., 2010; Hubbard et al., 1995). If the household owns a second
home, it will liquidate any home with a LTV above 1.5. When in financial difficulties, the household
can liquidate one home first and become a single-home owner. If it is still necessary, the household
will liquidate the other home to ensure the consumption need is met.
Households may liquidate their homes by either selling or defaulting on their mortgage.18 We
assume that households sell when the net sale proceeds are sufficient to cover the outstanding mortgage
balance, that is, when (1 −ch) × Hit ≥Mit where Mit is the remaining mortgage balance. When this
inequality is not satisfied, the household defaults.

Benchmark Strategies
For each homeowner strategy, we construct four benchmark strategies, including an all-equity
strategy, a 60/40 stock/bond strategy, a 100-minus-age strategy, a target-date fund strategy.19 We define
the 60/40 strategy as a constant 60% stock and 40% bond allocation, the 100-minus-age strategy as a
stock allocation that starts at 75% at age 25 and declines linearly to 0% at age 100, the target-date fund
strategy following Vanguard’s glide path, and the bond-only strategy as a pure bond allocation.20 For
the stock sleeve in these strategies, we consider both international diversification and domestic-only
allocation following Anarkulova et al. (2023).
We focus on the all-equity strategy as our main benchmark for three reasons. First, the critique
posed by Cochrane (2022) frames homeownership as an illiquid and highly concentrated household
asset that underperforms equities in wealth accumulation (Campbell, 2006; Cocco, 2005). Evidence
in the literature also shows that homeownership tends to crowd out stock holdings in the household
portfolio, making homeownership and stock holdings natural substitutes in the household portfolio
(Cocco, 2005; Flavin and Yamashita, 2002; Yao and Zhang, 2004). Therefore, the all-equity strategy is
the most relevant and clean benchmark for us to evaluate the life-cycle value of homeownership and
conclude whether stock is better than housing for wealth creation.
Second, the test for homeownership should set a high bar focusing on the maximization of wealth
and welfare. Consistent with the findings of Anarkulova et al. (2023), we show that the internationally
diversified all-equity strategy makes the strongest benchmark at the moderate risk aversion level rel-
ative to other alternative benchmark strategies. Under a standard risk aversion coefficient of 4, Choi
et al. (2025) also show that all-equity allocation achieves a near-optimal consumption equivalent level
that is only 0.56% lower than the best analytical optimal allocation Cocco et al. (2005).

5
Mechanisms
The preceding section establishes that homeownership improves life-cycle wealth and welfare. The
natural question is why. We argue that homeownership is a safer asset that reduces two distinct risks
that renters bear. The first is investment risk. Housing returns are less volatile and less correlated with
income shocks than equity returns, so homeowners face smaller portfolio drawdowns and more equal
wealth outcomes than pure-equity renters. The second is housing cost risk. Sinai and Souleles (2005)
establish that the conventional risk analysis of homeownership focuses on asset price volatility while
ignoring the rent risk borne by renters, and that homeownership acts as a natural hedge against this
risk for long-horizon households. The rent-to-price ratio fluctuates over the business cycle, exposing
renters to adverse cost shocks, whereas owners pay a predictable maintenance expense. Together
these two risk channels account for the consumption equivalent premium of homeownership. We
further document how the intertemporal alignment of resources and the leverage-consumption timing
trade-off shape the magnitude and timing of gains. The liquidity channel connects the working-life
suppression of financial assets to the retirement and bequest outcomes that drive the consumption
equivalent improvement.

The second risk-reduction channel operates on the liability side of the household balance sheet. In
our simulation, the rent-to-price ratio fluctuates substantially. Thus, renters face a stochastic housing
cost. Meanwhile, because the rent can be highly persistent, the cost shocks tend to accumulate into
larger lifetime housing expenditure.24 In contrast to the renters, owners in our simulation pay a mainte-

2020; Nakajima and Telyukova, 2017; Yao and Zhang, 2004). These costs are stable and do not co-vary
with market conditions. In other words, homeownership converts a variable liability into a smooth
one. The welfare gain in homeownership is therefore an insurance gain embedded in the liability side
of the balance sheet.

7
Conclusion
Contrary to popular expert advice, homeownership remains a valuable investment in the life cycle
when the alternatives are the common benchmark investment strategies. Using stationary block boot-
strap simulations, we show that homeowner strategies generally raise life-cycle wealth at a 15% saving
rate, and homeowners realize consumption equivalent gains up to 4.5% relative to the most stringent
benchmark, the all-equity benchmark.
We find that the consumption equivalent gains rise with saving rate as stronger financial buffers
better sustain mortgage service and support retirement consumption. Homeownership also enhances
wealth preservation and reduces wealth inequality. Within groups following the same life-cycle in-
vestment strategy, the economic benefits of homeownership are further influenced by heterogeneous
household profiles and the timing of home purchases.
Four mechanisms generate the homeownership value. First, housing is a return-enhancing, risk-
efficient substitute for bonds, delivering bond-level volatility with higher expected returns and thus
reducing portfolio downside risks. Homeownership converts a stochastic rental liability into a pre-
dictable maintenance expense, providing insurance against persistent rent shocks that accumulate into
large lifetime housing costs for renters (Sinai and Souleles, 2005). Second, the intertemporal substi-
tution channel exchanges early-life consumption for late-life gains. Third, the leverage-consumption-
timing tradeoff governs the terms of that exchange: higher down payments and delayed entry reduce
mortgage-induced consumption suppression and raise lifetime welfare, while cash purchase eliminates
