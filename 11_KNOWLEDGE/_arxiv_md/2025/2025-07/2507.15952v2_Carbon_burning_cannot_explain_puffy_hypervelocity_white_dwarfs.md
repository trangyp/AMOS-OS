Carbon burning cannot explain puffy hypervelocity white dwarfs
Natsuko Yamaguchi,1 Kareem El-Badry,1 Tin Long Sunny Wong,2 and Ken J. Shen3
1Department of Astronomy, California Institute of Technology, 1200 E. California Blvd, Pasadena, CA, 91125, USA
2Department of Physics, University of California, Santa Barbara, CA 93106, USA
3Department of Astronomy and Theoretical Astrophysics Center, University of California, Berkeley, CA, USA
ABSTRACT
Several hypervelocity white dwarfs (HVWDs) with space velocities of ≳1000 kms−1 have recently
been discovered. One possible origin of these stars is the dynamically-driven double-degenerate double-
detonation (D6) scenario, in which an accreting sub-Chandrasekhar mass carbon-oxygen (CO) WD
detonates as a SN Ia. In this scenario, the less massive WD may survive its companion’s detonation
and be ejected as a HVWD. Most of the observed HVWDs are hotter and puffier than normal WDs,
perhaps due to their recent proximity to a SN. In this work, we test whether these properties can be
explained by long-lived stable carbon (C) burning in the interiors of CO WD donors triggered by a
SN shock. We model the long-term evolution of CO WDs following rapid energy injection using 1D
models. We find that stable C burning can be ignited in CO WDs with masses of 0.95 −1.10 M⊙if
SN energy penetrates sufficiently deeply. The resulting born-again stars settle on the C-burning main
sequence while they convert their interiors from C and O to Ne and Mg, where they have temperatures
and radii similar to some of the observed HVWDs. However, the timescale over which C-burning WDs
remain inflated is ≲105 yr, which is at least an order of magnitude shorter than the kinematic ages of
observed hot HVWDs. We conclude that observed HVWDs are unlikely to be inflated by C burning.
The stellar evolution of observed HVWDs remains an open problem.

1. INTRODUCTION
Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) are energetic transients
thought to result from the thermonuclear explosion of
white dwarfs (WDs) in binary systems. Despite play-
ing an important role in both cosmology and astro-
physics, the detailed physics of this process has been
a topic of debate for many years (see Liu et al. 2023
and Ruiter & Seitenzahl 2025 for recent reviews). The
canonical single-degenerate scenario involves a WD ac-
creting mass from a non-degenerate companion until it
reaches the Chandrasekhar limit (Whelan & Iben 1973).
Alternatively, in the double-degenerate scenario, both
components are WDs. One such model that has gained
popularity is the dynamically-driven double-degenerate
double-detonation (D6) scenario. Here, a carbon-oxygen
(CO) WD accretes material from a less massive WD
which leads to a helium shell detonation that triggers
a core carbon (C) detonation (Guillochon et al. 2010;
Fink et al. 2010; Dan et al. 2011, 2012; Pakmor et al.
2013).
In the D6 scenario, there are two possible fates of the
donor. First, it could also detonate due to the impact of
the incoming ejecta from the explosion of its companion
(e.g. Tanikawa et al. 2019; Pakmor et al. 2022; Boos
et al. 2024). If it instead survives, it will be flung out
of the orbit as a hypervelocity WD (HVWD) moving
at roughly the Keplerian orbital velocity prior to the
explosion (e.g. Bauer et al. 2021; Braudo & Soker 2024;
Shen 2025).
Recently, several HVWDs have been discovered us-
ing data from the Gaia mission which are proposed to
originate from the D6 scenario (Shen et al. 2018; El-
Badry et al. 2023; Hollands et al. 2025).
These have
typical 3D space velocities of 2000 kms−1 and are both
hotter and more inflated than typical WDs, with radii
of ∼0.03−0.2 R⊙. They span a wide range of tempera-
tures, from ∼8000 K to ∼100, 000 K. One object (D6-2)
has been traced back to a SN Ia remnant, and the UV
spectrum of another object (J0927-6335) has revealed
likely evidence of pollution from a SN Ia ejecta (Werner
et al. 2024).

If these objects are indeed surviving donors from the
D6 scenario, their hot and puffy nature might be inter-
preted as a result of the recent impact of a SN shock.
However, it has proven challenging to reconcile the ob-
served properties and inferred ages of all the observed
objects with theory.
For example, Bhat et al. (2025)
modeled the long-term evolution of donors powered by
shock heating of their outer layers and nickel decay, but
found this could not inflate the WDs over sufficiently
long timescales. Meanwhile, Shen (2025) found that the
cooler HVWDs may be explained by the cooling of very
low-mass fully convective stars. However, this would re-
quire the donors to have experienced copious amounts
of mass loss pre-explosion, and it is unclear whether this
model can explain the hottest objects, where relatively
massive progenitors must become fully convective. In
addition, the cooling timescales of the WD post-genitors
of Type Iax SNe studied by Zhang et al. (2019) are too
short at the high masses implied from the observed ve-
locities of the D6 HVWDs. Given such challenges, Glanz
et al. (2024) proposed an alternative formation scenario
involving a merger of two hybrid helium-carbon-oxygen
WDs.
In this work, we run 1D models to test whether core C
burning ignited in CO WDs could explain the observed
properties of HVWDs. This possibility is motivated by
the works of Paczy´nski & Koz lowski (1972) and Paczyn-
ski (1973) who modeled a linear series of pure carbon
stars and found branches of thermally stable stars burn-
ing carbon. We build on these early works by using CO
WD models with realistic compositions and exploring
the relevant range of energies for SN shocks to study C
burning stars in the context of the D6 scenario.
This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we
describe how we construct our initial CO WD models
and inject energies into their interiors. In Section 3, we
describe the evolution of these models, and in particular,
whether or not they successfully ignite C. For those that
do, in Section 4, we compare the WD properties pre-
dicted by our models to those of the observed HVWDs.
Finally, in Section 5, we summarize our findings.
2. METHODS
We use the 1D stellar evolution code Modules for
Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics (MESA, version
r24.03.1; Paxton et al. 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, 2019;
Jermyn et al. 2023) to produce CO WD models, inject
energy into their interiors, and study their long-term
evolution.
2.1. CO WD models
Given the velocities of the observed HVWDs and pre-
explosion Roche geometry of the D6 scenario, Bauer
et al. (2021) and El-Badry et al. (2023) estimate masses
between ∼0.8−1.0 M⊙(albeit with large uncertainties,
and with the exception of D6-2, which has a lower in-
ferred mass of ∼0.3 M⊙and may have a helium core).
Therefore, we begin by creating CO WD models within
this mass range.
We use the make co wd test suite, which evolves a star
until the start of the thermally pulsating asymptotic gi-
ant phase and then strips off the outer envelope to leave
behind a CO core. For our fiducial models, we assume
solar metallicity (Z = 0.02) and consider initial stel-
lar masses of Minit = 4 −6 M⊙.
This produces final
CO WD masses of MWD ∼0.8 −0.98 M⊙.
The top
left panel in Figure 1 shows the initial abundance pro-
file for the 0.98 M⊙WD model. Models are terminated
when their luminosity falls below 0.01 L⊙. The nuclear
reaction network used by the test suite, which includes
carbon and oxygen burning, is used for all of our models
(co burn extras, with a few special rate factors which
can be found in the publicly available inlists).
The donors in the D6 scenario may have formed via
multiple episodes of interaction and mass transfer (e.g.
Ruiter et al. 2009) and therefore could be more mas-
sive than the maximum mass of a CO WD produced by
single-star evolution (∼1.05 M⊙, Siess 2007). Thus, we
also construct 1.05 and 1.1 M⊙CO WD models by let-
ting the 0.98 M⊙model accrete a mixture of 65% 16O
and 35% 12C by mass (roughly the initial core composi-
tion) using the mass change option. Nuclear burning is
turned off during the accretion to speed up the compu-
tation and is turned back on afterwards.
2.2. Energy injection
We ignite the core of the model WDs by injecting a
specified amount of total energy Einj, over a time inter-
val, ∆t. For our fiducial model, we inject energy such
that the power injected per unit mass throughout the
WD interior is (Einj/∆t)/MWD. In Appendix A, we test
the effect of varying the energy injection profile such
that it decreases in the innermost region of the WD.
Bhat et al. (2025) found that the shock induced by the
SN explosion travels through the WD at ∼104 kms−1
and injected heat into their WD model over ∆t = 0.01 s.
We find that some of our models fail to converge with
such small values of ∆t, so we instead use larger values,
with a default of ∆t = 1 s. In Appendix B, we show
that the value of ∆t has no effect on the long-term evo-
lution of the WD, even for unphysically long values up
to ∆t = 103 yr.

mass ratios ∼0.2 −0.9, the ratio of the donor radius
to the orbital separation is in the range of ∼0.2 −0.4

a realistic model of the effects of the companion’s explo-

the properties of observed HVWDs.
In reality, a SN
shock from the companion may fail to penetrate deeply
enough to ignite C burning, or it may detonate the donor
WD though a second double detonation (Boos et al.
2024). 3D simulations are required to investigate these
outcomes; this work is focused on the effects of successful
C ignition.

be polluted by heavy elements from the SN ejecta (e.g.
Bhat et al. 2025).

We obtained qualitatively similar outcomes for 0.8 M⊙
WD models. As the WD evolution in these models is not
driven by C burning, we do not study their properties
further and instead point readers to Bhat et al. (2025),
who performed a more detailed study of the evolution of
WDs whose outer layers are shock heated by SN ejecta.
Note that our models do not consider heating by the
decay of 56Ni present in the outer layers of the WD from
accretion of the ejecta, which Bhat et al. (2025) found
to be important in this case.
For a 0.9 M⊙WD, we find that stable C burning oc-
curs if Einj is between ∼4−5×1049 erg. For larger values
(e.g. Einj = 7 × 1049 erg), after the first ∼10−8 yr, the
WD expands dramatically to over 10 R⊙and cools down
to effective temperatures roughly an order of magnitude
below the models with lower energies in the following
year. Even after the WD undergoes Kelvin-Helmholtz
contraction, the central temperature reached is lower by
∼150 million K. Accordingly, the power from nuclear
burning falls below that of the lower Einj models very
rapidly, and no C burning is sustained.
Therefore, a
mass of about 0.9 M⊙may be understood to be a lower
boundary for stable C burning to occur.
3.2. Massive CO WD models
In Figure 6, we compare the evolution of the 0.98 M⊙
model with the 1.05 and 1.1 M⊙models which were cre-
ated via accretion of C/O-rich material (Section 2.1),
with Einj = 5 × 1049 erg.
All three models undergo
a phase of C burning, but the period of burning, and
thus the lifetime of the inflated WD, is shorter at higher
masses. As shown in the rightmost panel, more mas-
sive WDs burn more of the available fuel, with the frac-
tion of the total mass of carbon that burns increasing as
51%, 82%, and 92% by the end of the run for the 0.98,
1.05 and 1.1 M⊙models. The final compositions of the
latter two models are dominated by neon.
There are
rapid fluctuations in the C burning power taking place
between ∼3 −5 × 104 yr in the massive models. This
feature is insensitive to changes in the spatial resolution.
During this time, off-center C burning is ignited but is
unable to propagate back to the center where most of the
initial carbon has already been depleted. We see both
regions of thermohaline convection and convective over-
shooting developing near the flame, which may also aid
with the quenching (e.g. Siess 2009; Denissenkov et al.
2013). This process is repeated several times, leading to
the sharp spikes in burning luminosity.
The trend in the duration of stable C burning with
mass holds for lower mass models down to 0.9 M⊙cre-
ated via single star evolution, meaning it is robust to the
choices made in the engineering of the 1.05 and 1.1 M⊙
WDs.
4. COMPARISON TO OBSERVED HVWDS
From the left panel of Figure 2, we see that most of
the observed HVWDs are more luminous at their tem-
peratures than predicted by the models, reflecting their
larger radii and cooler effective temperatures. This is
similarly true for the more massive models described in
Section 3.2. Furthermore, the observationally inferred
inferred ages are 0.6 −4.5 Myr (excluding D6-2), which
range from a factor of a few to more than an order of
magnitude longer than the ∼2 × 105 yr taken for the
WD radii in our models to return to the WD cooling
track.
While J0546 and J1332 approach the tracks on the
HR diagram, as seen on the right panel of Figure 2, this
occurs when the models are only ∼104 yr old, while the
observed kinematic ages are ∼0.6 Myr. Although these
kinematic age estimates are imprecise, an age of order
104 years is very unlikely, given that the stars are not
found within SN remnants. This tension persists across
all models that burn C, even at lower masses down to
0.9 M⊙. While it might be possible to further increase
the energy injected, given that there is minimal differ-
ence between the Einj = 5×1049 and 7×1049 erg models,

5. CONCLUSION
In this work, we ran 1D models of the long-term evolu-
tion of CO WDs in which C burning is ignited following
a companion’s explosion. We summarize the key find-
ings below:
• We used the 1D stellar evolution code MESA to
evolve 4 −6 M⊙stars to obtain CO WD models
with masses 0.8 −0.98 M⊙. We also constructed
massive 1.05 and 1.1 M⊙WDs via accretion onto
the 0.98 M⊙model (Section 2.1). For our fiducial
model, we uniformly deposited energy into the in-
teriors of these models, varying the total amount
of energy injected (Section 2.2).
• WDs with masses ≲0.85 M⊙do not undergo C
burning after the initial energy injection (Figure
5).
• Given sufficiently large amounts of injected en-
ergy ≳5 × 1049 erg (within the expected range
of SN energy intercepted by the donor), WD mod-
els with masses ≳0.9 M⊙can stably burn C for
∼5×104 years, leading to them being significantly
inflated for ∼105 years. Once C burning is suc-
cessfully ignited, the evolution is relatively insen-
sitive to the initial amount of energy injected (Sec-
tion 3.1, Figure 3).
• More massive WDs also undergo C burning, burn-
ing a larger fraction of their fuel, but the duration
decreases with increasing mass (Section 3.2, Fig-
ure 6).
• The majority of the observed HVWDs are too lu-
minous at their temperatures to be consistent with
any of the models. Furthermore, while the hottest
HVWDs briefly approach the models on the HR
diagram, their estimated ages of ∼0.6 Myrs are
over an order of magnitude longer than the time at
which this occurs in the models (Section 4). This
suggests that C burning is unlikely to explain the
observed properties of these systems.

B. SENSITIVITY TO ∆t
In Figure 8, we plot the time evolution of WD radii and luminosities from several nuclear reactions for 0.98 M⊙
models with varying ∆t from 0.1 s to 103 yr and fixed Einj = 5 × 1049 erg. We find that models with ∆t ≤103 yr all
ignite C and the evolution of stellar properties are nearly identical. Their initial response to the energy injection in the
first ≲1000 yr can differ slightly, with an expected delay in the peak of the C burning luminosity at longer ∆t, but we
see that this has almost no impact on the long-term evolution. However, above this, ∆t becomes comparable to the
Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale of the WD and the energy is able to be transported out of the star efficiently, preventing
sustained C burning.
For some models, we find that if ∆t is too short, the solver struggles to converge. Based on the results of this test,
in these cases, we allow ∆t to be increased. For all models presented in the rest of this paper, we keep it significantly
below the maximum value tested, below 1 yr. Thus, our results are very likely insensitive to the precise value.
C. INCREASING OPACITY
We test the effect of increased opacity resulting from the accretion of heavy elements by artificially increasing the
opacity outside of the CO core by an order of magnitude using the extra opacity factor subroutine in MESA. The
results are shown for the 0.98 M⊙and Einj = 5 × 1049 erg model in Figure 9. We see that the lifetime of C burning,
and thus the inflated WD, is slightly longer. However, the difference is insufficient to correct for the discrepancy with
the observed systems. We note the small peak in radii (and luminosity) that occur at ∼6.3×104 yr which corresponds
to the peak in the power from the α-capture of carbon (c alpha).
