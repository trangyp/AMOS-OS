Fair sharing ratios of Profit and Loss sharing contracts
ABASS SAGNA *
Abstract
We consider islamic Profit and Loss (PL) sharing contract, possibly combined with an
agency contract, and introduce the notion of c-fair profit sharing ratios (c = (c1, . . . , cd) ∈
(R⋆)d, where d is the number of partners) which aims to determining both the profit shar-
ing ratios and the induced expected maturity payoffs of each partner ℓaccording to its
contribution, determined by the rate component cℓof the vector c, to the global success of
the project. We show several new results that elucidate the relation between these profit
sharing ratios and various important economic factors as the investment risk, the labor and
the capital, giving accordingly a way of choosing them in connection with the real econ-
omy. The design of our approach allows the use of all the range of econometrics models
or more general stochastic diffusion models to compute or approximate the quantities of
interest.

Introduction
The aim of this work is to determine both the c-fair profit sharing ratios and the induced ex-
pected payoffs at maturity for the partners of the islamic profit and loss (PL) sharing - mud-
harabah and musharakah contracts, possibly combined with a wakalah contract (an agency
contract). A PL-mudharabah contract is a partnership contract where one of the partner brings
the capital and the other, his expertise or labour to manage the funds. A PL-musharakah con-
tract is a contractual partnership where all the partners participate to the capital and in which
profits and losses are shared between the partners.
In fact, the notion of fair profit sharing ratios may be find in [8] for a PL-mudharabah contract.
It consists of finding the profit sharing ratios that equalise the expected payoffs of the partners
of the contract at maturity. The c-fair profit sharing ratios principal extends the fair concept
to more general contracts that may be a combination of a PL-mudharabah contract, a PL-
musharakah contract, and a Wakalah contract (an agency contract) and that may involve several
partners. It is a rating allocation system that affects a rating coefficient cℓto each partners ℓ
according to his contribution to the global success of the project and aims to determine the
profit sharing ratios that equalise the associated rated contributing payoffs at maturity: means
the cℓPayℓ, for ℓ= 1, . . . , d, where Payℓis the payoff of the partner ℓ. We then show several
new results that elucidate the relation between the profit ratios and several important economic

Let us consider a corn farmer who sowed a quantity L of corn seed for a harvesting time
horizon T. The picked quantity RT (we suppose that RT is not identically equal to L: RT ̸≡L)

operation more than its failure, that is, when

Any T horizon and R-asset based investment with initial capital (which could be physical,

investment.
Now, introduce an investor who brings the quantity L of seed that the farmer (who cannot get
hold of it) needs in order to receive back a corn. Here’s what we may line up on the behaviour
of the investor.
1. If the investor suspected strongly that the farmer operation will fail, means, RT < L, he
would likely not take part in it. This naturally imposes the viability constrain (1) that is the
framework we consider in this paper.
2. Now, if he expects a success of the operation (as does the farmer, by the way, if we suppose
that they share the same informations), means, E(RT ) ≥L, and takes part in the (physical)
capital of the corn farmer, two situations may happen at time T:
⋄the operation succeeds RT ≥L: the farmer will get the reward from his labor he must
share with the investor, in proportion to his contribution to the (physical) capital,
⋄the operation failed RT < L: the farmer loses the reward of his labor and is reasonably
not entitled to retain the harvest RT that should belong to the investor. Moreover, except
if the failure of the operation results from a mismanagement of the farmer, in which case
the investor may claim a refund of the damage L−RT , it is not justifiable that the investor
claims more than (the wholeness of) the crop RT . In fact, any additional advantage, in
cash or in any other kind, in favour of the investor in this context is a ribâ (which precept
includes the interest-bearing loans principle, but, not only), prohibited by the Qur’an.
The islamic financing rests on the application of the Shariah (the Islamic law) and operates in
the framework of ribâ-free business transactions. Its major basic principles include (see e.g. [1,
2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]): the gharar-free (and the maysir-free) financing principle, seeking to avoid
excessive contractual uncertainty (gharar) and speculative, or gambling transactions (maysir),
the asset-backed financial principal (the principal that any financial transaction must be linked
to tangible assets, promoting real economic activities and discouraging speculative practices),
the risk sharing principle in sale transactions (avoiding for example the sale of an item that

3
The PL sharing - mudharabah contract
A mudharabah contract between two entities (which may be, for example, a bank and a com-
pany) is a partnership contract where one partner provide the capital (the owner of the capital is
called the rabb al-maal) and the other partner (the mudarib) brings his labor or his expertise to
manage the funds in order to make a profit. The profit, if any, is shared between the partners ac-
cording to an allocation key that must be known at the conclusion of the contract. Each partner
may terminate the contract at any time as long as a maturity is not fixed or before the contract
execution. After contract completion and when the maturity is contractual, the contract will
still be irreversible up to execution of book value of cash assets or up to the maturity. However,
with a mutual agreement between the partners, the profit sharing ratios may be updated at any
time before the maturity.
It is very important to notice that this contract is a participative contract on the fact that since
the investor does not participate to the work, the counterpart of his capital raising is the labour
or the expertise of the worker. Then, as a basic principle, the capital owner is compensated
7


## Page 8


only on the profit (in the same way as the manager) of the investment and has, in the same way
as the manager who loses the reward of his labor in case of loss, to support the losses, if any,
except in the event of mismanagement (a risk that the funding owner may cover by requiring
guarantees from the worker).
Finally, the mudharabah may be absolute or restrictive. It is restrictive when the funding
owner makes restrictions on the domain of activity, for example, or on any other aspect he
consider appropriated. Otherwise, the mudharabah is said to be absolute.

Conclusion remark. The present paper introduces and studies the notion of c-fair profit sharing
ratios for islamic profit and loss sharing contracts, possibly combined with an agency contract.
We show that the c-fair profit sharing ratio is decomposed as a reword from the funding of the
capital and a reward from the labor or from the management of the project. Although the profit
sharing ratios is determined with a common agreement between the partners of PL contracts,
this work may help the islamic finance institutions, first, understanding its link with several key
economic factors as: the expected investment profit, the investment risk, the investment oppor-
tunity, etc, and, secondly, the present results may also help them taking informed decisions
about the profit sharing ratios and the related parameters. Furthermore, the work open new
perspectives in the (dynamic) stochastic modelling and their applications to islamic finance
engineering.
