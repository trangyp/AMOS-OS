Abstract
Building on the task-based approach to labour markets, we develop the Generative
AI Susceptibility Index (GAISI), a job-level measure of UK exposure to large language
models (LLMs). Drawing on Eloundou et al. (2024), we use LLMs as probabilistic raters to
classify task exposure, linking ratings to worker-reported task data from the British Skills and
Employment Surveys. GAISI measures the share of job activities where LLMs can reduce task
completion time by at least 25% beyond existing tools. Systematic validations demonstrate
high reliability, strong validity, and predictive power over existing exposure measures. By
2023/24, nearly all UK jobs (94%) exhibited some LLM exposure, yet only 13% were heavily
exposed (GAISI > 0.5), with the highest concentration in scientific and technical professions.
Aggregate exposure rose 16% of one standard deviation since 2017, driven by occupational
shifts rather than within-occupation task changes. The wage premium for AI-exposed tasks
declined 12% between 2017 and 2023/24, while the period since ChatGPT’s release has
coincided with a relative contraction of job postings in more AI-exposed occupations. These
findings are consistent with generative AI beginning to affect hiring and pay in exposed
occupations, though causal attribution requires further research. GAISI offers policymakers
and researchers a validated, replicable tool for monitoring AI exposure at the job level as
this technology diffuses.
Keywords: large language models, task-based approach, labour market exposure, technological
change, wage effects, job vacancies

Introduction
Recent advances in generative artificial intelligence, particularly large language models (LLMs),
have renewed urgency in measuring which segments of the economy and workforce face labour
market disruptions (Acemoglu, 2025; Korinek, 2024). By Q2 2024, 24%—or 7.4 million—of
UK workers reported using AI-enabled software in their jobs (Henseke, Davies et al., 2025), a
diffusion rate that substantially exceeds historical patterns of workplace technology adoption.
Yet robust measurement of job-level exposure remains limited, hampering both research and
policy responses.
Existing measurement approaches face three fundamental limitations. First, most rely on
occupation-level classifications that mask substantial within-occupation variation in task content
(Arntz et al., 2017; Filippi et al., 2023). Second, many indices measure technological feasibility
rather than economic viability, overlooking the costs and integration challenges that determine
whether feasible automation actually occurs (Melián-González & Bulchand-Gidumal, 2025).
Third, most measures focus on pre-LLM AI capabilities—machine learning, robotics—rather
than the specific affordances of LLMs, which have distinct strengths in language generation,
knowledge synthesis, and analytical tasks that differentiate them from previous automation
technologies.
Eloundou et al. (2024) (hereafter EMMR) addresses this gap by developing a task-based
framework to measure LLM exposure and demonstrating that LLMs themselves can serve
as automated raters. However, their validation relies primarily on inter-rater agreement and
correlations with existing indices, rather than actual AI adoption or labour market outcomes.
Whether LLM-generated exposure ratings contain sufficient signal to track real economic
behaviour remains open.
This paper takes up that question. We adapt EMMR’s framework to UK data, constructing the
Generative AI Susceptibility Index (GAISI) for British jobs, incorporating probabilistic ratings,
occupation-specific task classification, a marginal productivity threshold, and worker-reported
task importance weights from the British Skills and Employment Surveys (SES). The central
contribution is the validation and application. Specifically, we evaluate GAISI against Messick
(1995)’s framework for construct validity, testing it against self-reported workplace AI adoption,
and against wage and job posting data not filtered through the same discourse that trains LLM
raters, directly addressing the epistemic circularity inherent in using LLMs to rate their own
impact.
Our analysis suggests: By 2023/24, nearly all UK jobs (94%) exhibited some LLM exposure, yet
only 13% were highly exposed (GAISI > 0.5), with the highest exposure concentrated in scientific
and technical professions, which presents a clear departure from earlier automation waves that
primarily targeted routine manual and clerical work. The wage premium for AI-exposed tasks
declined between 2017 and 2023/24, and job postings in AI-exposed occupations fell relative to
less-exposed occupations following ChatGPT’s release.

Previous Literature
2.1
The Task-Based Approach and Why Generative AI Differs
The task-based approach to labour markets, pioneered by Autor et al. (2003) and extended by
Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019, 2022), provides the conceptual foundation for assessing
the impact of technology on earnings and employment and the motivation for measuring
technology exposure. Technologies rarely automate entire occupations; instead, they alter jobs
by selectively automating or augmenting specific tasks. This insight shifts attention away from
occupation-level predictions, which implicitly assume uniform task content within occupations,
to task-level analysis that recognises jobs as bundles of heterogeneous activities with varying
susceptibility to automation or augmentation.
Generative AI, particularly LLMs, may represent a departure from previous automation waves in
two respects (Korinek, 2024). First, it targets knowledge-intensive cognitive tasks, such as writing,
analysis, idea generation, or knowledge synthesis, that have historically defined high-skilled
work and resisted automation (Autor, 2024). Second, its low-cost, platform-based deployment
enables rapid, worker-driven adoption without requiring substantial capital investments or
organisational restructuring (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025). Experimental evidence confirms
immediate productivity gains: LLM access increased call-centre agents’ productivity by 14%
(Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2025), reduced writing task completion time while improving
quality (Noy & Zhang, 2023), accelerated software development tasks (Peng et al., 2023) and a
range of typical consultancy activities (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023). Crucially, productivity gains
consistently concentrated among less-experienced workers, suggesting LLMs may compress
productivity distributions within occupations; a pattern distinct from skill-biased technical
change.
The critical question for labour markets hinges on whether productivity gains translate into
employment growth or displacement. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2022) distinguish between
three channels through which technology can disrupt work: displacement (technology substi-
tutes for labour in newly automatable tasks), productivity effects (cost savings expand output),
and reinstatement (new tasks are created that require human labour). The employment impact
depends on their relative magnitudes and timing. Generative AI’s ease of adoption suggests
displacement could materialise rapidly, while productivity-driven reinstatement depends on
uncertain factors: demand-side elasticities for affected outputs, the pace of complementary

innovation, and organisational capacity to redesign work around AI capabilities. This temporal
mismatch underscores the need for granular, task-based exposure measurement to identify
where risks of disruptions are greatest.
2.2
The Evolution and Limitations of Exposure Measures
Efforts to measure jobs’ exposure to technologies have evolved in three waves. Frey and
Osborne (2017) pioneering occupation-level index sparked widespread concern but suffered
from methodological issues. Evans (2025) demonstrated that their results were driven by a weak
feature set and biased training data: despite "engineering bottlenecks" emphasising perception
and manipulation, only social and creative intelligence meaningfully discriminated between
automatable and non-automatable occupations, producing implausible results where nearly
identical occupations received vastly different automation probabilities. Subsequent approaches,
including machine learning suitability indices (Brynjolfsson & Mitchell, 2017; Brynjolfsson
et al., 2018), patent-derived measures (Webb, 2020), and AI capability ratings (Felten et al., 2021,
2023), addressed specific limitations but shared two persistent limitations: occupation-level
aggregation masking within-occupation heterogeneity, and limited validation against actual
technology adoption or employment outcomes.
Recent reviews highlight fundamental measurement challenges. Across 102 studies, Filippi et al.
(2023) document estimates of automation risks ranging from under 10% to over 50%, driven
by differences in analytical granularity, data sources, and methods. Evans (2025) identifies
a pervasive absence of validation against actual labour market outcomes across automation
studies: indices are validated through inter-rater agreement or statistical performance against
training sets, but rarely tested against realised technology adoption or labour market outcomes.
Melián-González and Bulchand-Gidumal (2025) further note that prediction reliability depends
critically on the scale of technology analysed and work descriptors used. Together, these
methodological limitations point to three desiderata for measuring job exposure to generative
AI: task-level rather than occupation-level granularity, to reduce aggregation bias; validation
against actual adoption and market outcomes rather than inter-rater agreement alone; and
specificity to the technology under examination, since measures designed for robotics or routine
software perform poorly when applied to LLM capabilities.
2.3
Measures of LLM Exposure and Remaining Gaps
Eloundou et al. (2024) (EMMR) developed the first framework explicitly designed to measure
LLM exposure rather than general AI or automation potential. Two contributions distinguish
their approach. First, they distinguished exposure channels: direct chatbot use (E1), LLM
integration with other software (E2), and image capabilities (E3). This recognised that labour
market effects depend on implementation requirements and complementary investments, not
just technological feasibility. Second, they demonstrated that LLMs themselves can serve as
automated raters, classifying 2,087 O*NET work activities plus a subset of O*NET tasks with
4


## Page 5


66% human-rater agreement. This scalability is relevant for tracking exposure as AI capabilities
and job content evolve.
Despite these advances, EMMR acknowledge limitations in two areas. As a measurement
instrument, their categorical classification (E0/E1/E2) does not capture gradations of exposure
within categories: tasks are either fully exposed or not, with no intermediate values to reflect
partial or context-dependent effects. Their occupation-level implementation aggregates across
O*NET work activities, masking within-occupation heterogeneity in how workers actually
perform tasks. And while their human raters were experienced with LLMs, they lacked
occupational diversity, limiting "their understanding of O*NET work tasks, occupational
workflows, and occupation-specific software tools." (Eloundou et al., 2024, page 1307). Moreover,
they acknowledge that ratings generated by GPT-4 were sensitive to small prompt changes.
EMMR’s own validation relies on inter-rater agreement, face validity, and correlations with
existing exposure metrics. Subsequent studies have linked EMMR’s scores to occupational
labour market dynamics (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, 2025; Klein Teeselink, 2025), but no
study has yet subjected an LLM-generated exposure index to systematic validation against
actual adoption and labour market outcomes within the same measurement framework.
More broadly, a growing body of work has leveraged LLMs for occupational classification
and task annotation, often yielding results that align closely with expert human judgment
(Gilardi et al., 2023; Gmyrek et al., 2024; Manning et al., 2024). However, their use introduces
distinctive challenges, such as sensitivity to prompt design, potential biases from training data,
or reasoning processes that produce human-like outputs through non-human logic (Anthis
et al., 2025; Hajikhani & Cole, 2024), underscoring the need for rigorous validation when LLMs
serve as research instruments.
The gaps identified in measurement granularity, validation against economic outcomes, ro-
bustness to design choices, and scalability of automated exposure ratings motivate the present
study. We adapt EMMR’s framework to worker-level data from the British Skills and Employ-
ment Surveys. We apply their classification approach with practical refinements, including
probabilistic ratings, occupation-specific task contexts, a marginal productivity threshold, and
worker-reported task importance weights, detailed in Section 4. The resulting Generative AI
Susceptibility Index (GAISI) produces job-level exposure scores that vary both across and within
occupations. The core of the paper is whether this exercise is worth it: whether LLM-generated
ratings produce a measure with sufficient information content to predict actual AI adoption
and track observable labour market outcomes. Section 5 applies it to wages and hiring patterns.
Section 7 evaluates the measure against Messick (1995)’s framework for construct validity.

Data
This paper uses the survey data and questionnaires from the British Skills and Employment
Surveys (SES) to derive, validate, and analyse a new index of generative AI exposure (Butt
et al., 2025). The SES is a series of nationally representative, repeated cross-sectional surveys of
5


## Page 6


people in paid work in Britain, conducted approximately every five years since the mid-1980s
(Felstead et al., 2019). It uniquely collects detailed and consistently measured information on job
tasks, skills use, and workplace technologies, making it exceptionally well-suited for evaluating
technological impacts at the task level (Henseke, Felstead et al., 2025).
Since 1997, SES has asked workers to rate the importance of a consistent set of work activities or
job tasks, enabling within-occupation comparisons across nearly three decades. Importance is
reported on a 5-point scale from essential to not at all important. The instrument aims to collect
information on the tasks performed in jobs across the economy to measure the demand for
generic job skills rather than to provide a complete account of activities workers carry out in their
role (Green, 2012). Its scope and scale are similar to O*NET’s inventory of generalised work
activities, which describes general job behaviours (Hansen et al., 2014).
In SES 2017 and 2023-24, respondents rated up to 44 work activities, with some variation
depending on managerial status and the importance of foundational literacy and numeracy
tasks. To aid exposition, these 44 tasks were organised into 12 task categories (Table 1). This
grouping has no analytical significance; all LLM classifications operate at the individual task
level.
How these general job behaviours map into concrete job activities likely differs by occupation.
For example, selling or problem-solving may require distinct tools and involve different work
environments and processes in a software development role compared to a skilled trades
occupation. Recognising this, the classification process incorporated tailored occupational
vignettes developed from occupation unit group (4-digit) descriptions to account for how
occupational context, such as available tools, workflows, and task requirements, may modify AI
exposure. We concentrate on the 25 sub-major occupation groups (2-digit level) defined by the
UK Standard Occupational Classification 2010 (ONS, 2010), a comprehensive taxonomy covering
all jobs in the UK economy. This yielded 1,100 occupation-task combinations (25 sub-major
groups × 44 tasks).
Task-level scores are aggregated to indiviudal jobs using worker-reported importance ratings (1
= essential, 0.75 = very important, 0.5 = fairly important, 0.25 = not very important, 0 = not at all
important). Workers performed an average of 26 essential-equivalent tasks, with Collaboration

To assess AI’s impact on labour demand, we merge GAISI scores into monthly job vacancy
counts summed to calendar quarters by 3-digit occupation and local authority district from
April 2017 to June 2025, provided by the Office for National Statistics (ONS, 2025). We use
event-study specifications to estimate changes in hiring patterns before and after ChatGPT’s
release in November 2022, comparing changes within occupation-area aggregates. To test
whether GAISI generalises beyond the UK context and SES task battery, we apply the same
classification approach to the OECD Survey of Adult Skills (SAS) 2023 (OECD, 2024). Like SES,
SAS collects worker-reported task data, but across multiple countries and with a different set of
task items and response scales. A clear correlation between SES-based and SAS-based GAISI
scores at the industry-occupation level would provide evidence that the measurement approach
is robust to variation in task measures.

Construction of GAISI
4.1
Task-Level Scoring
Building on EMMR, this section describes the process of scoring tasks within their occupational
context and steps taken to aggregate those scores to the level of jobs. We classify job tasks’
exposure to generative AI using commercial LLMs. Our objective was to estimate the probability
that generative AI could meaningfully augment each SES task within a specific occupational
setting.
The classification distinguishes four levels of exposure:
• E0: No meaningful exposure. LLMs do not reduce task time by ≥25%
• E1: Direct exposure. LLMs alone (e.g., via a chatbot) reduce task time by ≥25%.
• E2: Latent exposure. LLMs require integration with other tools or systems to achieve
≥25% time savings.
• E3: Exposure via image capabilities, e.g., image recognition supports significant task
efficiency.
We define the threshold of 25%-time savings, rather than 50% as used in EMMR, as our criterion
for meaningful exposure, consistent with assumptions of average labour cost savings (Acemoglu,
2025). While this threshold is necessarily arbitrary, even smaller efficiency gains from LLM
use may be meaningful in high-intensity work environments. In sensitivity checks, we assess
how well the resulting index compares to versions using the original assessment rubric and
cutpoints.
Unlike EMMR, which assigned tasks to mutually exclusive exposure categories, we prompted the
LLMs to return a probability distribution across all four exposure levels (E0-E3). This decision
reflects the high-level nature of SES work activities, which would typically comprise subtasks
with varying susceptibility to AI. For example, a task like “problem solving” may include both
elements amenable to generative AI augmentation or automation and others that are not. The
probabilistic format is designed to accommodate cases where exposure-level distinctions are
blurred: when a task could plausibly be augmented via direct chatbot use or via integration
with other software, the model distributes probability mass across categories rather than forcing
a categorical assignment. Section 7.2.2 shows that this distribution is systematic: integration
cues independently predict higher ratings over and above direct affordances (Table C.2). The
prompt required that the probabilities sum to unity across exposure levels; ex-post verification
confirmed compliance aside from minimal rounding errors across all 5,500 ratings (mean = 1.000,
SD ≈0.000). Following EMMR, E2 and E3 were treated as latent exposure when constructing a
summative index of generative AI exposure.
To capture the occupational context of tasks, we classified each of the 44 SES tasks separately for
each of the 25 sub-major (2-digit) groups in the UK Standard Occupational Classification 2010,

resulting in 1,100 occupation-task combinations. These were primarily assessed by Google’s
Gemini 1.5 Pro, with OpenAI’s GPT-4o as an independent robustness check, two leading
commercial LLM families at the time of classification. Gemini 1.5 Pro’s release (May 2024 with
a knowledge cut-off in November 2023) aligns with the SES 2023–24 data collection window,
ensuring that the rater’s knowledge of AI capabilities corresponds to the period in which
respondents reported their task performance and AI use. Gemini 2.5 Pro was released after the
main analysis was completed and is included in further extension; Table C.6 reports rank-order
correlations across models and prompt variants, confirming that substantive results are not
sensitive to model choice.
Each model was prompted using a structured rubric that guided the model to consider:
1. The SES task’s purpose and function within a UK-specific occupational vignette.
2. The tools typically used to perform the task.
3. The incremental contribution of generative AI beyond existing technologies.
4. A step-by-step justification of a probability distribution over exposure levels E0 to E3.
As discussed above, tasks were grouped into thematic categories (e.g., reading, collaboration)
to reduce fragmentation and the number of API calls. Prompts also included classification
examples and followed a chain-of-thought structure to encourage systematic reasoning. The
LLM rater temperature was set to 0.2 rather than 0. This deliberate choice permits estimation
of rating uncertainty at the task-occupation level: at temperature 0, the model returns a single
deterministic output, precluding any assessment of how sensitive individual ratings are to
stochastic variation in the generation process. Each of the 1,100 occupation-task cells was
classified five times, yielding 5,500 total ratings. Section 7.1 reports the distribution of within-cell
standard deviations across these runs and shows that classification uncertainty is informatively
concentrated in tasks at the boundary of LLM capability.
The LLM raters’ knowledge cutoffs were October 2023 (GPT-4o), November 2023 (Gemini 1.5 Pro),
and January 2025 (Gemini 2.5 Pro). Their training corpora included academic and policy
literature on AI capabilities, raising the possibility of epistemic circularity in their ratings; a
concern we address formally in Section 7.3.3. Appendix A reproduces the rater prompt.

5
Assessing the Reach of Generative AI in Britain’s Workforce
This section applies the Generative AI Susceptibility Index (GAISI), derived from Gemini 1.5 Pro
evaluations of task exposure, to worker-level data from SES and online job vacancies at occupation
× area level. The aim is to assess which job tasks and occupations are most susceptible to
augmentation by generative AI, who performs them, and how this exposure is associated with
wages and labour demand. We start by mapping exposure across tasks and jobs, highlighting
the extent and variation of AI susceptibility across the occupational structure, before turning
to differences in exposure across worker groups and the connection between exposure and
reported AI use at work. Finally, we examine how average exposure has evolved between 2017
and 2023/24 and present findings on its correlation with pay and the stock of job postings.
Together, the findings offer a snapshot of where, for whom, and how generative AI is associated
with labour market outcomes in Britain.
5.1
Mapping AI Exposure in Work Tasks and Jobs
Task-level exposure probabilities, averaged across five independent classification runs, show 25%
of tasks rated as directly susceptible to LLM assistance (E1), another 24% with latent exposure
(E2), and negligible multi-modal exposure (E3< 1.5%). In total, half of job tasks were directly or
indirectly susceptible to generative AI (Table B.2). As discussed earlier, subsequent analyses
combine E3 with E2 as latent exposure.
Table 2 aggregates these probabilities to the job level in the SES 2023-24 sample. Taken literally,
the figures suggest that LLMs alone can reduce task time by a quarter or more for 26% of tasks in
a typical job. Another 27% were indirectly exposed through integration with software, databases,
and other tools, or through image recognition. The mean GAISI is 0.40 (= 0.26 + 0.5 × 0.27),
with values at the 10th and 90th percentile ranging from 0.23 to 0.51. Interpreted literally
as an estimate of time savings, GAISI indicates the average job could realise time savings of
approximately 6–13% under current LLM capabilities, depending on how much of the latent
integration potential is realised.1

6
Evaluation Principles
As demonstrated, the Generative AI Susceptibility Index (GAISI) offers insights into how large
language model (LLM) technologies are correlating with ongoing labour market disruptions.
The following sections evaluate the measurement characteristics of this novel index. This section
sets out (i) the conceptual foundation for judging the index, (ii) the reliability and validity criteria
we adopt, (iii) the statistical tests that operationalise those criteria, and (iv) the assumptions
baked into the underlying classification exercise.
6.1
Conceptual Foundation
Exposure to generative AI is not directly observable; it is a latent construct inferred from the mix
of tasks that constitute each job. We view GAISI as an outcome of interaction between (a) a job’s
task content, derived from the SES, and (b) the capability frontier of LLMs. The question we
pose in the following is how well the derived exposure index is fit for purpose and how it adds
value beyond existing measures. Building on Messick (1995)’s unified theory of test validity, we
distinguish six sources of evidence: content, substantive, structural, external, generalisability
and consequential validity, which jointly establish whether GAISI measures what it purports to
measure and whether it does so with sufficient precision for policy analysis.
6.2
Reliability and Validity Criteria
Reliability and validity are treated as multifaceted:
• Reliability or structural validity is interrogated through consistency of probabilistic ratings
across alternative model runs, particularly focusing on meaningful uncertainty in the
ratings across classification runs.
• Content validity asks i) whether the SES task battery spans work activities plausibly
affected by current LLM capabilities, and ii) how well the measured tasks cover the work
people do.
• Substantive validity depends on a convincing theoretical link between the cognitive process
a task requires and the functional competencies that LLMs exhibit, including knowledge
engagement, language generation, analytical support, code generation, image captioning,
direct vs integrated application, and so forth.
• External validity is gauged by correlating GAISI with existing technology exposure indices,
clustered AI-human conversations, and self-reported use of AI tools in the 2023-24 SES,
illustrating the value added of the measure over existing technology exposure indices.
• Generalisability reflects whether estimates remain stable when we vary core design choices,
such as model family, prompts or the relative importance of exposure components.
19


## Page 20


• Consequential validity concerns the interpretive risks of using GAISI to guide public
intervention; here we evaluate whether measurement error is systematically related to
observable worker characteristics, and discuss concerns of circularity whereby LLM-rated
exposure measures may reflect discourse more than technical capabilities.
6.3
Empirical Strategy and Statistical Tests
To operationalise the foregoing criteria, we employ several complementary tests:
Inter- and intra-model reliability: Two-way random-effects Intraclass Correlation Coefficients
(ICCs) are used to compare agreement in task ratings across five classification draws.
Content and substantive validity: To address content validity, we compared AI-affected and
resilient SES job tasks and estimated the amount of work content SES tasks cover. For substantive
validity, we coded 5,500 LLM explanations for affordances, constraints, and integration cues,
checked reliability, and then regressed GAISI scores on those tags.
External validity:
• Convergent and discriminant validity: Spearman correlations place GAISI against earlier
automation-exposure indices. Positive but imperfect correlations are expected with other
measures of generative AI.
• Prediction-based validation: A logistic model tests whether GAISI anticipates actual
workplace adoption of generative AI tools at the worker level; an area-under-curve (AUC)
above 0.70 serves as the pragmatic benchmark for “useful” predictive power. Additional
tests assess whether GAISI remains a significant predictor of work-level AI use after
accounting for existing indices of automation exposure. A conditional effect suggests
information content beyond existing measures.
• Alignment with observed AI-assistant use: 630 conversation clusters from Anthropic’s
Claude assistant are mapped to SES tasks using an LLM, interpreting each mapping as the
tasks the AI assistant performed in the human-AI exchange. Crucially, the conversation
database was released after the GAISI rater model’s knowledge cutoff, ensuring the model
could not have drawn on these data when producing its task-exposure ratings (Handa
et al., 2025). Weighting tasks by the share of conversation records yields a measure of
“AI-conversation task share.” This is compared to GAISI’s direct- and latent-exposure
components to test whether tasks more prevalent in real-world AI conversations align
more strongly with direct (E1) than integration-dependent (E2) exposure.
• Robustness and sensitivity analysis: We recompute exposure scores under alternative
weighting schemes for the latent-exposure component and different time-saving thresholds
(50%) to evaluate how fragile substantive conclusions are to design choices. We also
modify the rating prompt and rater model and apply the approach to task information in
the OECD SAS 2023 sample to evaluate how well the approach generalises.
20


## Page 21


Consequential validity: To test for demographic bias in LLM rater scores, we report whether
residuals from worker-level GAISI regressions correlate with worker demographic characteristics,
once occupation-fixed effects and job-task profiles are partialled out. Such patterns cannot
be attributed to LLM bias unambiguously, but they provide a first-pass diagnostic and an
assessment of consequential validity.
6.4
Core Assumptions Embedded in Classification
The classification pipeline rests on three substantive assumptions that readers should keep in
mind when interpreting analytical results:
Threshold of interest: 25% time saving. A task is deemed “exposed” if model responses indicate
that an LLM could trim completion time by at least one quarter for a typical competent worker.
Probabilistic rather than categorical task ratings. Instead of forcing a yes/no verdict, classifica-
tion prompts instruct the model to return a probability that exposure exceeds the threshold. This
accommodates within-task heterogeneity and rating ambiguity. The probability of exposure via
multimodal capabilities is combined with exposure via LLM integration into latent exposure.
Hybrid aggregation weight (𝜔= 0.50) for latent exposure. Final worker-level scores combine
direct exposure (E1) with latent exposure to generative AI (E2+E3). Assuming an 𝜔of 0.50
reflects a mid-range scenario in which approximately half of the technically feasible integrations
are expected to diffuse within the near future. Subsequent sensitivity tests compare this
assumption with a data-driven weighting of latent exposure.
The framework elaborated here clarifies how we assess the credibility of GAISI, the statistical
evidence that supports this judgment, and where caution remains warranted. The following
section applies the outlined principles to evaluate the reliability, validity, and robustness of
exposure estimates for the 1,100 occupation-task cells in the 2023-24 or combined 2017 & 2023-24
SES samples, depending on data availability.

7.3.1
Relationship with Existing Measures: What Does GAISI Add?
A central question for any new exposure index is what it contributes beyond existing meas-
ures. Table 6 presents pairwise Spearman rank correlations between worker-level GAISI and
established occupation-level measures of automation and AI exposure. Because LLM training
corpora include prior exposure literature, these correlations must be interpreted alongside the
measurement error analysis in Section 7.3.3.
GAISI correlates strongly with contemporary AI-specific indices: Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin,
and Rock (EMMR)’s human-rated generative AI exposure measure (𝜌= 0.78), Felten, Raj, and
Seamans (FRS)’s general AI Occupational Exposure score (𝜌= 0.83) and their LLM-specific
score (𝜌= 0.77). It is weakly or negatively correlated with measures targeting earlier automation
technologies: Webb’s robot exposure (𝜌= −0.49), Frey and Osborne’s automation probability
(𝜌= −0.62), and Webb’s software score (𝜌= −0.034). This pattern establishes technology
specificity. GAISI targets a fundamentally different segment of the task space from robotics- or
routine-based indices.
The strong convergence with EMMR is informative in a second respect. EMMR’s ratings
were produced by expert human annotators classifying 2,087 O*NET work activities — a
resource-intensive process that is difficult to update or extend to other national contexts. GAISI
recovers a comparable signal through structured LLM classification at negligible marginal cost.
It can be updated as capabilities evolve and applied to any national task battery, as the PIAAC
cross-validation demonstrates (𝜌= 0.94).

7.3.3
Epistemic Circularity and Measurement Error
A legitimate concern with using LLMs as raters of their own labour market impact is epistemic
circularity: LLM training corpora include academic and policy discourse that defines AI
exposure, so GAISI ratings may partly encode prevailing beliefs about AI capabilities rather
than independently assessing the technological frontier. We address this concern by framing it
as a measurement problem and showing that the empirical evidence disciplines it.
Let 𝐺𝐴𝐼𝑆𝐼∗denote the true, latent AI susceptibility of a given job, and let 𝐺𝐴𝐼𝑆𝐼= 𝐺𝐴𝐼𝑆𝐼∗+ 𝑒1,
where 𝑒1 represents judgment error that may include discourse-driven noise absorbed from
training data. Similarly, let 𝐴𝐼∗denote true workplace AI adoption, and let the self-reported
measure be 𝐴𝐼= 𝐴𝐼∗+ 𝑒2, where 𝑒2 is reporting error potentially inflated by the same public
discourse. The circularity critique, stated formally, is that 𝑐𝑜𝑣(𝑒1, 𝑒2) > 0: both the LLM rater
and the worker are embedded in shared narratives about what AI can do, inflating the observed
correlation between GAISI and self-reported AI adoption. The relevant question for the current
study is not whether 𝑒1 exists, it surely does, but whether the signal-to-noise ratio is sufficient
for the index to be useful.
Three lines of evidence indicate that GAISI’s signal-to-noise ratio may be sufficient to track
economically meaningful variation. First, the horse-race regressions in Table 7 show that
GAISI predicts worker-level AI adoption even after controlling for the full suite of existing
exposure indices. If GAISI’s signal were primarily discourse-driven noise shared with these
other indices—all of which draw on overlapping academic and policy literatures—it should
add no incremental predictive power. Empirically, it does: the within-occupation, task-level
variation unique to GAISI is what drives the result.
Second, the vacancy and wage evidence in Section 5.3 provides validation that is not subject to
the 𝑐𝑜𝑣(𝑒1, 𝑒2) > 0 critique. ONS vacancy counts are administrative records of employer hiring
decisions, not filtered through worker perceptions of AI. Wage premia are equilibrium prices in
a labour market. The only way circularity contaminates these results is if LLM judgment error
correlates directly with employer behaviour. That is, if employers reduce hiring specifically in

occupations perceived as AI-exposed rather than those actually AI-exposed, and do so after
November 2022, while maintaining flat pre-trends before. This is a considerably stronger claim
than correlated self-reports.
Third, the timing pattern provides further discipline. If GAISI merely encoded pre-existing
narratives about AI susceptibility, there is no reason those narratives should predict a structural
break in vacancy trends, specifically at the public release of ChatGPT. Beliefs about AI exposure
existed before November 2022; the event study in Figure 6 shows a differential response that
begins only afterwards.
The measurement error concern is not unique to LLM-based ratings. Any expert-coded index
draws on accumulated knowledge and prevailing beliefs rather than deriving assessments
from first principles. The convergence of predictive validity (Table 7), labour market evidence
(Section 5.3), and temporal specificity suggests that it is.3
7.4
Generalisability & Robustness
We subjected GAISI to several tests examining sensitivity to design choices, using reported
AI use as a benchmark. We assess discrimination using the area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUC), where 0.5 indicates chance-level discrimination and 1.0 represents
perfect discrimination.
Weighting and rating robustness. Headline GAISI assigns half the weight of direct exposure
(E1) to latent exposure (E2 + E3). Five-fold cross-validation estimated the optimal weight at 0.24
(std. error = 0.05), yielding an out-of-sample AUC indistinguishable from the 0.5 specification
(Table C.5). We recomputed GAISI using GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Pro with pared-down prompts
(Table C.6). Mean exposure varied between 0.35–0.43, standard deviations 0.07–0.13, and
Spearman 𝜌with the benchmark exceeded 0.88. The sole outlier was EMMR’s original binary
rubric: it raised mean exposure to 0.54, reduced correlation to 0.72, and weakened prediction
(AUC = 0.68 vs 0.71–0.75). Classification without occupational contexts may thus inflate exposure
while reducing external validity.
Task coverage sensitivity. SES measures 44 generic job behaviours capturing approximately
one-third of working hours (see Section 7.2.1). We benchmarked extremes for unmeasured time:
assuming all unseen task are AI-resilient (lower bound) yielded an AUC of 0.71, statistically
indistinguishable from the headline index (𝑝= 0.282). Assuming full exposure (upper bound)
barely exceeded chance (AUC = 0.55), significantly underperforming the main index (𝑝< 0.001).
This validates the conditional measure and SES’s adequacy for distinguishing AI-exposed from
AI-resilient work.

8
Conclusion
This study presents GAISI, a task-based index of generative AI exposure in UK jobs derived
from large language model (LLM) assessments. By applying probabilistic LLM ratings to 1,100
occupation-task combinations and weighting them by worker-reported task importance, GAISI
offers methodological advances over existing exposure measures. It demonstrates desirable
measurement properties across several validation criteria: high reliability, strong content and
substantive validity, convergent validity with established AI exposure measures, and predictive
validity. Specifically, GAISI outperforms existing AI exposure metrics in predicting worker-level
AI use. The index reveals widespread but moderate exposure: 94% of UK jobs show some
exposure, but only 13% exceed 50% exposure, with highest levels in high-skill occupations—a
clear break from earlier automation waves affecting routine work.
The presented explorative evidence is consistent with AI beginning to affect the UK labour
market through multiple channels. Aggregate AI exposure rose 1.8 percentage points (16% of
one standard deviation) between 2017 and 2024, driven by shifts in occupational structure rather
than changes in task content within occupations. The wage premium for highly AI-susceptible
tasks declined significantly over this period, while the period since ChatGPT’s release in late
2022 coincides with an overproportional contraction of online job postings in more susceptible
occupations.
These patterns are consistent with a declining labour market, though their
correlational nature precludes definitive causal inference. Within-occupation variation in AI
exposure, persisting even after controlling for 3-digit occupational codes, underscores the value
of task-level measurement.
Policy Implications.
First, substantial within-occupation implies occupation-level policy
interventions may be insufficient; programmes should consider task profile within jobs as
31

well. Second, the decline in wage premia for AI-susceptible tasks between 2017 and 2023-
24, combined with lower labour demand post-ChatGPT, is consistent with labour market
adjustment in AI-exposed occupations. Third, unlike previous automation waves, generative AI
disproportionately impacts high-skill occupations, requiring rethinking which workers may
require support during technological transitions. Finally, GAISI provides a validated, replicable
tool for monitoring AI diffusion and labour market impacts, enabling early warning system for
before displacement effects become entrenched.
Methodological contribution. The study demonstrates that LLMs, when deployed as struc-
tured raters with explicit evaluation rubrics and systematic validation, can yield reliable,
generalisable, and policy-relevant measures. The combination of automated classification
with multi-dimensional validation—reliability, content validity, substantive validity, external
validity, and generalisability—offers a transferable template for measuring exposure to emerging
technologies. By operationalising Messick (1995)’s framework for construct validation and
addressing the measurement validation gaps identified in recent automation research (Evans,
2025), this approach potentially extends the social science measurement toolkit beyond tradi-
tional expert-coded indices. Replicability across LLM families (Gemini, GPT-4o) and design
choices (weights, thresholds, prompts) reinforces the robustness of this method. Successful
application to OECD PIAAC data demonstrates cross-survey transferability.
Limitations. First, the SES task battery covers approximately one-third of working hours on
average, ranging from 17% (machine operators) to 46% (sales workers). While sensitivity analyses
suggest robustness to unmeasured assumptions about unmeasured tasks, incomplete task
coverage may overstate exposure where unmeasured activities are AI-resilient (and vice versa).
Second, focusing on the UK, labour market responses may vary across institutional contexts
and development levels. Although the OECD PIAAC validation demonstrates methodological
generalisability, substantive findings may not transfer to other national contexts. Third, as
discussed in Section 7.3.3, LLM ratings may incorporate discourse-driven noise from training
data. While the evidence suggests a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio for the applications pursued
here, the degree of circularity cannot be definitively quantified. Fourth, GAISI measures
technological susceptibility rather than adoption; the gap depends on economic incentives,
organisational capabilities, regulatory constraints, ethical concerns, and worker objections not
observable in our data. Fifth, reported temporal patterns are correlational and cannot definitively
establish causality; unaccounted labour market adjustments or macroeconomic shocks may have
contributed to these patterns. Sixth, the within-cell rating variation documented in Section 7.1 is
not propagated into downstream standard errors; precision-weighted aggregation could address
this in future work. Finally, our measure is inherently static and cannot capture emerging tasks
or evolving task definitions as AI capabilities advance.
Future Research.Panel data linking AI exposure to individual worker outcomes, including AI
use, would support causal identification of AI’s labour market impacts. Extending GAISI to
additional countries using OECD PIAAC would permit cross-national comparative analyses of
how institutional contexts mediate these effects. Firm-level studies examining heterogeneity
in AI adoption decisions would clarify why some firms invest while others with similar task
