Abstract
Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is associated with heightened physio-
logical arousal in social-evaluative contexts, but it remains unclear
whether such autonomic reactivity extends to non-evaluative cogni-
tive stressors. This study investigated electrodermal activity (EDA)
patterns in socially anxious (SA) and non-socially anxious (NSA)
individuals during an emotionally salient 2-back working memory
task using facial expressions. 50 participants (25 SA, 25 NSA) com-
pleted both a baseline rest period and the task while EDA data were
collected via the Shimmer3 GSR+ sensor. A range of EDA features,
such as tonic and phasic components, number and amplitude of
skin conductance responses, and sympathetic activation estimates,
were analyzed using a standardized, interval-based approach. Re-
sults revealed significant increases in EDA across all participants
from baseline to task, indicating elevated autonomic arousal dur-
ing cognitive load. However, no significant group differences were
found between SA and NSA individuals. These findings suggest
that cognitive-emotional stress, in the absence of social-evaluative
threat, elicits comparable physiological responses regardless of so-
cial anxiety status. The results underscore the context-dependent
nature of anxiety-related autonomic reactivity and advocate for the
inclusion of social-evaluative or recovery phases in future research
to detect more nuanced group effects.

Introduction
Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is characterized by an intense fear
of social scrutiny and negative evaluation, which often gives rise
to compromised cognitive, emotional, and physiological responses
during social situations [43, 57]. This disorder affecting 4.7–17%
of the global population [47] can significantly impair their daily
social functioning [25]. Psychological signs of SAD, such as intense
worry about being embarrassed or humiliated, anticipatory anxi-
ety before attending social gatherings, and post-event rumination
about perceived faults [53] are also accompanied by physiologi-
cal arousal that results in increased autonomic activity [39]. This
physiological arousal takes physical manifestation in the form of
sweating, trembling, rapid heartbeat, nausea, muscle tension, and
dizziness. Therefore, a valid way to quantify the arousal, such as
through electrodermal activity (EDA) can be very informative.
EDA, which measures how well your skin conducts electricity at
a given moment, is a well-established physiological marker of auto-
nomic nervous system (ANS) activity. It is measured by placing two
electrodes in a dense position of the eccrine sweat gland, typically
at the ends of fingers. It primarily reflects sympathetic nervous
system (SNS) activity [5]. When a person experiences emotional
arousal, such as stress, excitement, or fear, the brain activates the
SNS as part of the body’s “fight or flight" response. This activation
causes the sweat glands to produce small amounts of sweat. Al-
though often imperceptible, this affects the skin’s ability to conduct
electricity. It enhances the skin’s electrical conductivity. EDA sen-
sors detect these changes by measuring how easily a small electrical
current passes between electrodes placed on the skin.
EDA is composed of tonic and phasic components, with tonic
skin conductance level (SCL) providing a measure of general au-
tonomic arousal and phasic skin conductance responses (SCRs)
capturing transient changes in response to stimuli [45]. This pro-
vides a window into a person’s physiological response to emotional
or cognitive stimuli. Despite growing interest in the physiological
correlates of SAD, research comparing EDA responses between
Socially Anxious (SA) and Non Socially Anxious (NSA) individuals
under varying task demands remains ambiguous. There are studies
which show correlation between increased skin conductance and
anxiety levels [32, 36, 59], while others present inconsistent findings
[20, 24, 46]. If we specifically focus on social anxiety, Moscovitch
et al. observed that individuals with higher anxiety levels showed

Cognitive accounts of social anxiety suggest that individuals
with SAD often struggle with processing emotional and interper-
sonal information, which can interfere with their daily functioning
[33]. These difficulties may become more pronounced when so-
cially anxious individuals are required to perform tasks involving
emotional or social cues. One such task commonly used in research
is the n-back task [6, 51], a working memory task where partici-
pants must monitor a sequence of stimuli (often facial expressions)
[29, 48] and indicate when the current stimulus/facial expression
matches one presented “n” steps earlier (see Figure 1). Previous
research using the n-back task has shown that individuals with
higher anxiety levels show reduced activation in the dorsolateral
prefrontal cortex, an area important for attention and cognitive
control [3]. However, while there is some understanding of the
neural correlates of anxiety during cognitive tasks, relatively few
studies have explored how these cognitive demands translate into
physiological responses, such as those measured by EDA. Given
that state anxiety often rises during cognitively and emotionally
demanding tasks, it is important to investigate whether individuals
with SAD show distinct physiological arousal patterns compared
to those without SAD. This study explores this gap by examining
EDA in socially anxious and non-anxious participants during the
n-back task.
In this paper, we address the following research question: Do
socially anxious individuals (SA) and non-socially anxious
individuals (NSA) exhibit differential electrodermal activ-
ity (EDA) responses during an emotionally salient cognitive
task? To answer this question, we conducted a controlled study in-
volving 50 participants (25 SA and 25 NSA). EDA data was collected
using the Shimmer wearable sensor during a baseline resting period
and a n-back task period. The data was processed and analyzed
using a standardized analysis pipeline. Our results indicate that
both SA and NSA participants show increased EDA from baseline
to task, but no significant group differences are observed. This sug-
gests that cognitive-emotional stress induces similar autonomic
activation regardless of social anxiety status. Our key contributions
are outlined below:
• We compare EDA responses between SA and NSA individu-
als under the same emotionally salient cognitive load con-
dition. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first
studies to examine autonomic arousal differences using a
socially relevant but non-evaluative cognitive paradigm.

Related Work
2.1
Wearable Sensors and EDA
The advancement of wearable technologies has enabled non-invasive
and real-time monitoring of physiological signals, including EDA,
in both laboratory and ecological settings. Among these, the Shim-
mer3 GSR+ unit has emerged as a gold-standard research-grade
device used in clinical and cognitive studies [9]. Other commer-
cially available wearables have also integrated EDA sensors. For
example, the Empatica E4 and Fitbit Sense offer user-friendly EDA
monitoring features for stress detection [10] and understanding
physiological responses in various contexts [1, 2]. Picard et al. have
notably used the Empatica line for detecting stress and even seizure
prediction [17, 44]. In addition, researchers have experimented
with novel wearable formats, such as EDA-enabled gloves [28] and
hydration-monitoring systems based on EDA signals [26].
Designed for high-resolution EDA acquisition, these devices
offer reliable signal quality and flexible integration with custom
experimental setups. The devices record at high sampling rates,
and its use of dry electrodes ensures consistent skin conductance
data over time, making it ideal for interval-based comparisons in
controlled experiments. These developments collectively point to
the growing role of wearable EDA sensors in understanding human
physiology, with Shimmer devices providing a robust platform for

2.2
EDA in Emotionally Salient Cognitive Tasks
EDA is a widely used physiological measure of sympathetic arousal
and has been shown to reliably reflect changes in emotional and
attentional states during cognitively demanding tasks. A growing
body of work explores how EDA responds to emotionally and cogni-
tively salient situations, providing a window into internal affective
and autonomic processes. Haapalainen et al. [21] demonstrated that
EDA features increase in response to complex visual and mental
arithmetic tasks, suggesting heightened arousal in demanding en-
vironments. Similarly, Nourbakhsh et al. [37] reported increases in
skin conductance during dual-task scenarios requiring sustained
attention and emotional regulation.
Posada-Quintero et al. [39, 41] further refined this understanding
by showing that EDA is sensitive to emotional stress in challenging
tasks such as the Stroop paradigm. This suggests that both emo-
tional salience and cognitive effort interact to modulate autonomic
responses. Additionally, large-scale datasets like CLARE [4] have
enabled the use of EDA in multimodal machine learning pipelines
to classify arousal states across a spectrum of emotionally engaging
cognitive tasks. These findings support the idea that EDA serves
as a robust physiological marker for tracking state-level changes
during complex affective-cognitive interactions.
In summary, while prior research has established EDA as a re-
liable marker of autonomic arousal during emotionally engaging
cognitive tasks and has demonstrated the feasibility of wearable
sensors for physiological monitoring, our work uniquely investigates
whether such arousal patterns differ between socially anxious and
non-anxious individuals during a controlled emotional working mem-
ory task. By using a validated research-grade wearable (Shimmer3
GSR+) and a standardized feature extraction pipeline, we contribute
a rigorous, comparative perspective on the interplay between social
anxiety and physiological reactivity.

Methodology
3.1
Participants
Before proceeding with the study, ethics approval was obtained
from the Institutional Review Board. The participant pool, within a
age range of 18-30, consists of two groups, both recruited through
advertisement within the university campus. One group consists of
sub-clinical population who met the diagnostic criteria for SAD as
assessed by the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS) and the other
group is of healthy individuals [7]. SIAS is a 20-item self-report
questionnaire that measures social interaction anxiety. Each item
in the questionnaire is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from
“not at all characteristic of me” to “extremely characteristic of me”
making the total score range from 0 to 80. Participants scoring ≥43
were classified as SA, while those scoring ≤33 were classified as
NSA, based on clinically accepted cut-offs. The sample consisted of
equal numbers of SA and NSA individuals to facilitate comparative
analysis. Demographic information was collected, including age
and gender can be found in Table 1.To compensate them for their
time, participants received either bonus points for their course
credit or a refreshment box containing snacks.

3.2
Physiological Measure
EDA data were collected using Shimmer3 GSR+ unit sensors1
specifically designed for clinical trials [9]. Two EDA electrodes
were placed near the base of the fingers on the participant’s non-
dominant hand to measure skin conductance. (as shown in figure
2). Participants’ fingers were cleaned with alcohol wipes prior to
sensor placement to remove residues and ensure optimal electrode-
skin contact and reducing signal noise in the EDA recordings. Data
acquisition was performed at a sampling rate of 512 Hz during the
time points as shown in figure 3. After each study session, time-
stamped EDA data were extracted from the Shimmer devices and
securely saved on the study computer for further analysis.
3.3
The Cognitive Task: 2-back Task
The cognitive task used in this study is called n-back task, which
is designed to test how well people can update and manage infor-
mation in their mind. In this type of task, participants are shown
a series of stimuli (such as images, letters, or sounds) and must
decide whether the current item matches the one shown n steps
earlier. In our study, we used a specific version called the 2-back
task (refer to figure 1). Participants were shown a series of facial
expressions—happy, neutral, or disgusted—and asked to press the ‘S’
key if the current facial expression matched the one from two trials
before (Same or “match”), or the ‘D’ key if it didn’t (Different or

3.4
Procedure
Each session began with informed consent after the participant was
briefed though a standard information sheet. This was followed by
baseline EDA measurement for 2 minutes. They were then given
a brief overview of the task with a practice session of 25 trials to
get them accustomed to the task. Afterwards, they performed the
main task that lasted up to 13 minutes. While EDA was obtained
throughout the whole study, the data was later segmented to obtain
EDA signals during baseline and during the 2-back task.
3.5
Data Analysis Pipeline
Our preprocessing and feature extraction pipeline for EDA encom-
passed data segmentation, signal filtering, decomposition, and fea-
ture extraction. We employed an interval-based analysis approach,
comparing EDA responses across two experimental conditions:
baseline and the 2-back task. Although the 2-back task spanned
approximately 13 minutes, only the initial 2-minute segment was
selected for analysis to match the duration of the baseline period.
This methodological decision was made to facilitate a standardized
comparison across conditions. Given the high sensitivity of EDA
measures to the length of the recording, equating the durations of
the baseline and task segments was essential to minimize confound-
ing effects related to segment length. By maintaining consistent
time windows, it was ensured that observed differences in EDA fea-
tures could be attributed to the experimental manipulation rather
than temporal discrepancies [23]. The selection of the initial two
minutes of the 2-back task was informed by evidence suggesting
that EDA responses are most pronounced during the early stages of
cognitive tasks [8, 42]. Additionally, focusing on this early segment
helps mitigate potential confounding factors such as habituation
or fatigue that may influence EDA responses over prolonged task
durations.
Preprocessing of our EDA signal started with the application of
a fifth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of
1 Hz, following Moscato, S., et al [34]. This step was undertaken to
remove high-frequency noise and physiological artifacts. Following
filtration, the signal was decomposed into its tonic and phasic com-
ponents [31] using the cvxEDA method [19] provided in NeuroKit2
[30]. Feature extraction was then performed separately for each of
the two experimental conditions: baseline and task. From the tonic
component, we computed two summary statistics—mean and stan-
dard deviation—capturing the overall level and temporal variability
of sustained electrodermal activity. From the phasic component,
transient responses were analyzed by detecting and counting the
number and average amplitude of skin conductance response (SCR)
peaks. In addition, we calculated an autocorrelation metric to as-
sess the temporal dependency of the EDA signal. Together, these
features enabled a comprehensive, interval-based characterization
of EDA dynamics across conditions. The following are detailed
descriptions of the extracted EDA features:
EDA_Tonic_Mean: The Tonic EDA, also referred to as SCL,
captures the sustained electrodermal activity. Higher SCL values in-
dicate increased sympathetic tone, which is associated with height-
ened arousal. It was computed by averaging the EDA tonic compo-
nent extracted via signal decomposition.
EDA_Tonic_SD: The standard deviation of tonic EDA reflects
variability in baseline sympathetic activity over time. Higher values
suggest more fluctuation in baseline arousal, while lower values
indicate stability. It was calculated as the standard deviation of the
tonic EDA component within the interval.
SCR_Peak_Count: The number of SCR peaks indicates the
frequency of transient sympathetic responses within a given time
window. A higher peak count suggests increased reactivity.
SCR_Peak_Amplitude_Mean: Mean SCR peak amplitude quan-
tifies the average magnitude of detected SCR peaks. It was computed
by averaging the peak amplitudes identified in the phasic EDA sig-
nal. Higher amplitudes indicate stronger sympathetic responses to
stimuli.
EDA_Autocorrelation: It is a quantitative feature representing
the similarity of the EDA signal to a time-lagged version of itself.
It is computed as the Pearson correlation coefficient between the
EDA signal and a version of the signal shifted by a specified lag (4
seconds). This feature captures the temporal dependency or per-
sistence of EDA patterns over time. Higher autocorrelation values
suggest more sustained sympathetic activation, while lower values
indicate rapid fluctuations.

5
Discussion & Conclusion
Our study investigated differences in EDA patterns in SA and NSA
during an emotionally salient cognitive task, specifically, a 2-back
working memory updating task involving facial expressions. Our
results showed that both SA and NSA participants exhibited a
significant increase in EDA from baseline to task across multiple
physiological features (e.g., mean tonic EDA, SCR peaks, SCR am-
plitude, and so on). However, no significant differences were found
between the SA and NSA groups in any of these features. This sug-
gests that while the task successfully increased autonomic arousal,
social anxiety status did not moderate this effect.
The lack of group differences can be supported by previous find-
ings that shows EDA does not show any distinguishing signatures
between SA and NSA cohorts [12, 32, 40]. Christian et al. did not
find any statistically significant difference on the basis of social
anxious when participants were imagining cognitively and emo-

including task context, individual cognitive appraisals, and the in-
terplay with other physiological systems. We wanted to see if EDA
as a standalone measure can capture any differences in experienced
cognitive stress in SA and NSA. Needless to say, EDA is a sensitive
measure of sympathetic nervous system activity. However, it did
not capture any differences in the physiological profiles of SA and
NSA while experiencing cognitive stress.
Attentional Control Theory (ACT) posits that anxiety impairs
top-down attentional control mechanisms under high cognitive
load, potentially manifesting in physiological dysregulation [15].
While ACT predicts decreased performance efficiency and increased
autonomic arousal under emotionally interfering conditions, our
findings did not reveal heightened EDA in the SA group during a
working memory task. Therefore, the predicted physiological dys-
regulation by ACT is not supported by EDA data in a non-evaluative
task setting such as this. The overall increase in EDA from base-
line to task is consistent with prior findings linking cognitive load
and emotional salience to heightened sympathetic activity [13, 49].
This supports the sensitivity and validity of the 2-back paradigm
as a tool to elicit measurable autonomic responses. Yet, our results
highlight that these autonomic changes appear to occur similarly
across SA and NSA individuals.
One limitation of this study is that although participants were
classified based on their SIAS scores, clinical interviews were not
conducted to confirm formal diagnoses, and subclinical variability
within groups may have diluted group-level effects. However, this
is standard practice in social anxiety research, where the use of
sub-clinical populations identified through validated self-report
measures [18, 27, 50, 55, 56] is common and widely accepted. De-
spite the limitation, the study contributes to the growing literature
on the contextual sensitivity of EDA and its utility in assessing
cognitive and emotional states. The absence of group differences
underscores that trait-level anxiety alone may not be sufficient to
drive physiological divergence unless accompanied by contextually
relevant triggers.
Since, the given context is social anxiety one may consider in-
corporating post-task rumination probes to assess delayed effects
of social anxiety on EDA. However, our goal was very straightfor-
ward, we wanted to see differences in physiological arousal, if any,
during the task. As this question is answered now, as a way forward
one can examine temporal dynamics of recovery or habituation
post-task, especially in SAD populations. Prior work suggests im-
paired autonomic recovery in socially anxious individuals, which
could manifest in the post-task period rather than during the task
itself [32]. Inclusion of a recovery phase in future experimental
designs may offer more insights into the difference in physiological
patterns during recovery, a delayed or difficulty of socially anxious
people to come back to baseline after experiencing an arousal state.
Further research can also check for individual differences in coping
strategies which can blunt their physiological responses. Factors
