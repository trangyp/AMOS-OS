The advent of large language models (LLMs) has led to significant achievements in various domains, including legal text processing.
Leveraging LLMs for legal tasks is a natural evolution and an increasingly compelling choice. However, their capabilities are often
portrayed as greater than they truly are. Despite the progress, we are still far from the ultimate goal of fully automating legal tasks
using artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing (NLP). Moreover, legal systems are deeply domain-specific and exhibit
substantial variation across different countries and languages. The need for building legal text processing applications for different
natural languages is, therefore, large and urgent. However, there is a big challenge for legal NLP in low-resource languages such as
Vietnamese due to the scarcity of resources and annotated data. The need for labeled legal corpora for supervised training, validation,
and supervised fine-tuning is critical. In this paper, we introduce the VLQA dataset, a comprehensive and high-quality resource tailored
for the Vietnamese legal domain. We also conduct a comprehensive statistical analysis of the dataset and evaluate its effectiveness
through experiments with state-of-the-art models on legal information retrieval and question-answering tasks.

Introduction
The legal system regulates a broad spectrum of everyday human activities such as finance, taxation, employment,
marriage, etc., thereby exerting influence on both legal practitioners and the general population alike [30]. In recent years,
advances in AI, particularly in NLP, have opened new avenues for enhancing access to legal information. The growing
digitization of legal texts, combined with powerful language models, offers a compelling foundation for reducing the
gap between legal knowledge and lay understanding [46]. For instance, legal text summarization [1, 13] and automated
contract analysis [12, 21] can aid non-experts in navigating complex and often opaque legal language. Similarly, legal
information retrieval (LIR) [22, 28, 37] streamlines the process for practitioners to identify relevant statutory or case
law, reducing the reliance on time-intensive manual efforts. Legal question answering (LQA) systems [5, 23, 47] further
hold promise for assisting individuals in obtaining preliminary legal insights without direct consultation. However,
existing research on LQA and LAR remains limited in scope, often focusing on narrow domains such as tax law or civil
rights, and producing restricted forms of output like binary (yes/no) answers or short phrase spans.
To support the development of practical and scalable LQA systems, we introduce VLQA, the first comprehensive,
large-scale, and expert-annotated dataset for the Vietnamese legal domain. VLQA comprises over 3,000 real-world
legal questions raised by Vietnamese citizens, each meticulously annotated by legal professionals with references to
relevant statutory articles drawn from a corpus of approximately 59,000 legal provisions, as summarized in Table 1. To
the best of our knowledge, VLQA represents the largest real-world expert-verified LQA dataset covering any statutory
domain. VLQA offers four key contributions. First, the questions are sourced from public legal consultation platforms,
ensuring that the dataset reflects genuine legal concerns encountered by everyday citizens. Second, the underlying
legal corpus spans the entirety of Vietnamese statutory law, presenting a realistic and challenging information retrieval
scenario for evaluating LIR and LQA systems. Third, the annotation process is conducted by senior law students under
the supervision of an experienced legal expert. This process involves validating and refining the relevant legal articles
and corresponding answers to ensure the overall quality, accuracy, and reliability of the dataset. Finally, VLQA provides
both relevant articles and long-form answers, addressing two fundamental LIR and LQA tasks. By making the dataset
publicly available, we aim to advance research in legal NLP and promote the development of accessible, trustworthy
legal assistance tools that help bridge the gap between legal systems and the public.
We establish strong baselines on the VLQA dataset by evaluating various state-of-the-art retrieval and LQA methods.
Our retrieval experiments cover both traditional lexical models (e.g., BM25, fastText) and dense neural retrieval
approaches (i.e., BERT, RoBERTa), offering valuable insights into their relative effectiveness in the statutory legal
domain. For LQA, we benchmark extractive models based on transformer encoders (such as BERT and RoBERTa),
generative models (e.g., BARTPho and ViT5), and recent LLMs, including open-weight systems (Qwen2.5, Llama 3.1,
DeepSeek-V3) and commercial offerings (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini). The performance of these systems is assessed through
both automated metrics and manual qualitative analysis. While the LLMs demonstrate high alignment with gold answers
under automated evaluation, human evaluation reveals that their outputs, despite being fluent and well-structured,
might contain factual inaccuracies or hallucinated elements. These findings highlight a significant disparity between
superficial language generation and robust legal reasoning, indicating opportunities for advancement in both evaluative
frameworks and methodological approaches. The dataset and source code will be publicly released soon. In summary,
our main contributions are:

Related works
To support the development of reliable models for various legal NLP tasks, the research community has introduced
several benchmark datasets in recent years. For example, Duan et al. [10] proposed CJRC, a Chinese judicial reading
comprehension dataset comprising over 50,000 pairs of (question, answer) generated from the fact descriptions of
10,000 legal cases. Xiao et al. [41] presented CAIL2018, the first large-scale dataset for legal judgment prediction (LJP)
in Chinese, comprising more than 2.6 million criminal cases. These cases were sourced from the official website of the
Supreme People’s Court of China and are associated with approximately 400 legislative articles and criminal charges.

Similarly, Medvedeva et al. [25] developed a legal judgment prediction dataset consisting of 11,532 admissible cases
from the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) as of September 2017. Meanwhile, Semo et al. [36] introduced the
first class action LJP dataset, comprising 5,459 lost cases and 5,290 won cases. Xiao et al. [42] released CAIL2019-SCM,

summarization dataset of legal texts in Portuguese. contains about 10,000 rulings from the Brazilian Federal Supreme
Court. Likewise, Manor and Li [24] introduced the benchmark of summarization of legal contracts in plain English,
including 446 sets of contract actions and corresponding reference summaries.
Statutory article retrieval remains an underexplored area in legal NLP due to the scarcity of large, high-quality
datasets. Existing resources include the COLIEE Statute Law corpus [11], BSARD [22], and ALQAC [26]. The COLIEE
dataset comprises 1,206 questions from the Japanese bar examination, annotated with references to articles in the
Japanese Civil Code. Similarly, ALQAC consists of 100 question–article pairs curated by jurists and legal experts. Both
datasets primarily target bar exam question-answering, which differs from real-world legal queries posed by non-
experts. Bar exam questions are typically domain-specific, technical, and designed for legal professionals or students,
while everyday legal inquiries from citizens are more general and straightforward. Recently, Louis and Spanakis [22]
introduced BSARD, a statutory article retrieval dataset in France with 1,108 legal questions and annotations referring to
22,633 articles, collected from legal consultant organizations. Similarly, VLQA also sources its questions from actual
citizen inquiries. However, VLQA distinguishes itself by its broader scale and coverage, encompassing 3,129 annotated
questions and a comprehensive corpus of nearly 60,000 Vietnamese statutory articles, offering a foundation for building
practical, data-driven legal information retrieval systems.
Addressing LQA has long presented a significant challenge in legal NLP due to the intricate nature of legal language,
rigid document structures, and the necessity for nuanced reasoning across temporal and logical dimensions. To advance
this field, a variety of datasets have been developed, each reflecting different subdomains and tasks. For example, Zhong
et al. [47] introduced a large-scale multiple-choice QA dataset from China’s National Judicial Examination, comprising
26,365 questions designed to evaluate the legal proficiency of professionals. Similarly, the COLIEE competition [11]
focuses on binary (“Yes”/“No”) answers to bar exam questions in Japanese civil law, emphasizing short-form, high-
precision reasoning. In contrast, long-form LQA has garnered growing attention, where systems must generate complete,
comprehensive answers. PrivacyQA [33], for instance, includes 1,750 questions over 335 privacy policy documents,
with answer spans averaging 140 words. ViRHE4QA, a Vietnamese dataset, targets university training regulations
and contains 9,758 annotator-generated questions across 294 legal articles. Most recently, LLeQA [23] introduced a
long-form dataset from Belgian legal consulting platforms, comprising 1,868 real-world questions and answers covering
diverse statutory domains, with average question and answer lengths of 15 and 264 words, respectively. Following this
trajectory, VLQA presents a great leap forward. It comprises 3,129 high-quality legal questions posed by Vietnamese
citizens, annotated and verified by legal professionals. Unlike prior Vietnamese datasets, which focus on narrow domains
or professional questions, VLQA is grounded in real-world, multi-domain inquiries. We envision VLQA as a foundational
resource for advancing reliable and practically applicable legal question-answering systems, thereby contributing to
narrowing the gap between legal knowledge and public accessibility.

3.1.1
Expansive article corpus collection. The Vietnamese legal system exhibits all the key characteristics of a civil law
jurisdiction, including the use of legal documents that comprehensively regulate specific areas of law, such as civil
rights or education. These documents serve as valuable resources for collecting laws across a wide range of subjects. In
our study, we consider 2,162 legal documents spanning 27 common domains within the Vietnamese legal framework, as
detailed in Table 5. In addition to extracting individual legal articles, we also capture the structural hierarchy of each
document (i.e., document, part, section, chapter), which helps provide context and an overview of article content. During
the collection process, we exclude documents with identical titles but different subjects to avoid ambiguity. During
pre-processing, we apply regular expressions to correct typographical errors and remove misplaced characters (e.g,
newlines, tabs). We also remove articles that were repealed by past laws but are still present in the source documents.
Finally, we construct a legal corpus consisting of 59,636 articles, organized according to a hierarchical structure. These
articles serve as the basic unit for the retrieval task.
3.1.2
Question-answer-article triplets collection. With the growing accessibility of online legal consultation platforms,
an increasing number of individuals are turning to these services for professional advice on legal matters. In this
study, we collect approximately 430,000 raw legal questions posted by laypersons across various domains, including
marriage, taxation, and employment, sourced from well-known legal advice websites in Vietnam. During the question

collection process, we identify and remove questions that reference entire legal documents, repealed articles, or refer to
articles not included in the corpus. We further group duplicated questions that share the same content but differ in
categorization tags. The question-answer pairs contain explicit textual mentions of legal provisions (e.g., “Article 5 of
the Civil Law 2015”, “Article 2 of this code”). We employ regular expression-based parsing to extract these references
and align them with their corresponding articles in the legal corpus. As the collected answers are human-generated,
there is a lack of a unified format in these references. Hence, we filter out questions that cannot extract relevant law
articles. Responses on legal consultation forums follow a recognizable structure, typically beginning with an analysis of
the question, followed by references to relevant legal articles, and concluding with a summarized answer. To exploit
these structured responses, we employ regular expressions to identify and extract relevant segments while filtering out
irrelevant content. To improve the consistency of the question-answer pairs, we use regular expressions and the SpaCy1
language detection package to filter out informal phrases, non-Vietnamese words, icons, and irrelevant characters from
the text. Misspelled and typographical errors are corrected using regular expressions.
3.1.3
Expert validation. Data annotation plays a critical role in improving the quality and readability of the dataset.
Thus, we employ a professional annotation team with five senior law students as annotators and an experienced legal
expert as a reviewer and supervisor. The data annotation process is carried out by law students, who are responsible
for validating and refining the collected answers as needed. Before the official annotation phase, they receive training
and detailed guidance from a legal reviewer to ensure consistency and accuracy. The final stage of quality assurance
is conducted by a legal expert, who reviews the annotated data, performs thorough proofreading, and rectifies any
remaining errors.
We develop a user-friendly and systematic annotation website for annotators using Streamlit API2, as presented
in Figure 2. Annotators are provided with the question, answer, and associated relevant law. They are required to
verify whether the current answer and relevant law are correct and complete. Each question-answer pair is annotated
independently by two annotators to reduce bias from an individual. Annotators are instructed to provide the following
information for each sample:
• Verify answer: Label whether the answer is True or False.
• Modify answer: Provide feedback on the answer. The annotator should provide the correct answer and specify
whether the raw answer is incomplete, excessive, etc.
• Verify relevant law: Label whether the relevant laws are True or False.
• Modify relevant law: This annotator should select the correct relevant laws.
• Feedback: The annotator should provide a detailed explanation or comment about the dataset if necessary. We
gather comments from annotators and the reviewer for further data processing.
Once the annotation task is completed, the quality of the annotated data is evaluated by the expert reviewer, as
presented in Table 3. During the review process, the reviewer will assess the data according to three standard aspects.
• Clearness: Whether the question is specific and well-formed to retrieve relevant laws.
• Validity: Whether the provided answer cites valid and current statutory articles. Any sample referencing repealed
or outdated legal documents is discarded.
• Fluency: Whether the answer is coherent and interpretable for laypersons, with grammatically correct and
logically connected sentences.

Experiments
We now benchmark state-of-the-art methods on the VLQA test set, focusing on three key aspects: retrieval perfor-
mance, answer quality, and factual accuracy. We employ both automatic metrics and human evaluations to provide a
comprehensive assessment of the baseline models’ performance across these dimensions.
5.1
Evaluation Measures
5.1.1
Automatic evaluation: We examine the performance of retrieval models on six automatic metrics, including
recall@𝑘, precision@𝑘, F2@𝑘, mean average precision (MAP@𝑘), and mean reciprocal rank (MRR@𝑘), normalized
discounted cumulative gain (NDCG@𝑘). The objective of legal document retrievers is to retrieve as many relevant

articles as possible within the top-𝑘results. To evaluate retrieval effectiveness, we primarily use the recall score, defined
as the ratio of correctly retrieved articles to the total number of relevant articles for a given question. However, recall
alone may not fully capture retrieval quality, as increasing 𝑘inherently boosts recall. To address this limitation, we also
report the precision score, which measures the proportion of correctly retrieved articles among all retrieved articles.
Finally, we include the F-score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, to provide a balanced evaluation of retrieval
performance. The F-score is calculated as follows:
𝐹𝛽= (1 + 𝛽2) ×
precision × recall
(𝛽2 × precision) + 𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑎𝑙𝑙

In this work, we utilize the F2 score, which means 𝛽= 2, assigning weights to recall higher than precision.
To evaluate our question-answering system automatically, we employ both ROUGE [19] and BERTScore [45]. ROUGE
measures the overlap of 𝑛-grams between the generated and reference texts; in this study, we report ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2,
and ROUGE-L to capture unigram recall, bigram recall, and longest-common-subsequence performance, respectively.
While these lexical overlap metrics are widely used in summarization, they often underperform on open-ended QA tasks
in the legal domain, where semantically equivalent but lexically divergent spans abound [15, 43]. To address this, we
also report BERTScore, which computes the similarity between candidate and reference sentences in a high-dimensional
embedding space of a pre-trained transformer. Prior work has demonstrated that BERTScore correlates more closely
with human judgments at the sentence level, making it especially suitable for assessing the nuanced, context-sensitive
answers typical of legal QA [45].
Human evaluation: Evaluating legal question-answering systems is inherently multifaceted, demanding not only
automatic metrics but also expert judgment to assess factual accuracy and answer completeness. Accordingly, beyond
reporting ROUGE and BERTScore, we engaged qualified legal professionals to review model outputs, flagging errors
of fact, omission, and misinterpretation. Their assessments enabled us to categorize common failure modes, such as
misapplication of legal terms and redundant pieces of information, to derive meaningful insights into the strengths and
limitations of LLMs in legal QA.

We now report the detailed results of state-of-the-art retrieval and legal question-answering methods on the VLQA

6.1
Legal Article Retrieval
Table 8 presents the recall performance of both sparse and dense retrieval models under zero-shot settings. Among
all models, BGE-m3 demonstrates a clear advantage, significantly outperforming other baselines. This performance

show precision scores smaller than 0.40, indicating that less than half of the retrieved articles are relevant, on average.
These results suggest a substantial room for enhancing the performance of retrievers in terms of accuracy.

Qualitative Analysis
To better understand the strengths and limitations of QA methods, including an extractive model and LLMs, we conduct
a detailed manual analysis of 100 randomly selected samples from the test set, as shown in Table 13. Each output
is carefully evaluated across two key dimensions: syntactic errors, referring to formatting issues or the presence of
non-Unicode characters; and semantic errors, where the answer is fluent and natural but logically incorrect or lacks
critical information. Overall, answers generated by LLMs tend to be shorter than the gold answers. Specifically, the
average lengths of responses from Qwen2.5 and GPT-4o-mini are 159 and 172 words, respectively, compared to an
average of 198 words for gold answers. This discrepancy helps explain why many of the semantic errors observed in
LLM outputs come from incomplete answers. Regarding the performance of GPT-4o-mini, it demonstrates notable
strengths in producing coherent, human-like answers. However, most of the generated outputs are incomplete or
logically incorrect, particularly in handling logical inference questions. We also observe that GPT-4o-mini tends to
replicate entire article texts instead of effectively addressing the given legal question. Another noteworthy finding is
that, despite achieving stronger performance on automatic metrics, Qwen2.5-14B shows lower accuracy than PhoBERT
in human evaluations. Among its incorrect outputs, 77% come from syntactic errors, including nonsensical phrasing
or non-Unicode characters. This highlights the limitations of smaller LLMs in handling complex, long-form legal QA
tasks. Finally, while LLMs can generate detailed answers based on provided facts and conditions, they might contain
inaccurate or hallucinated elements, ultimately leading to unreliable conclusions, as reported in Appendix B.

Conclusions
In this work, we introduce VLQA, a comprehensive and high-quality dataset designed for the Vietnamese legal domain.
As part of a broader effort to bridge the gap between the public and legal knowledge, VLQA provides a solid foundation
for the development and evaluation of reliable models in legal downstream tasks. We benchmark a range of state-of-
the-art retrieval and question-answering approaches, evaluated through both automated metrics and human review, to
demonstrate the dataset’s utility and highlight areas for future improvement. Looking ahead, we aim to develop a robust
end-to-end framework capable of handling lengthy statutory texts, complex legal queries, and real-world application
needs. We hope that VLQA will inspire further research into practical and trustworthy legal question-answering systems,
ultimately contributing to equal access to the law for all.
