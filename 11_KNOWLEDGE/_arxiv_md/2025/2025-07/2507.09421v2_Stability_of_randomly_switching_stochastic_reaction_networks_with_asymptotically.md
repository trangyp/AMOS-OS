Stability of randomly switching stochastic reaction networks with
asymptotically linear transition rates
Daniele Cappelletti∗, Aidan S. Howells†, and Chuang Xu‡
August 13, 2025
Abstract
Stochastic reaction networks are mathematical models frequently used in, but not limited to, biochemistry.
These models are continuous-time Markov chains whose transition rates depend on certain parameters called
rate constants, which despite the name may not be constant in real-world applications. In this paper we study
how random switching between different stochastic reaction networks with asymptotically linear rate functions
affects the stability of the process. We give matrix conditions for both positive recurrence (indeed, exponentially
ergodicity) and transience (indeed, evanescence) in both the regime with high switching rates and the regime
with low switching rates. We then make use of these conditions to provide examples of processes whose stability
behavior changes as the switching rate varies. We also explore what happens in the middle regime where the
switching rates are neither high nor low and our theorems do not apply. Specifically, we show by examples
that there can be arbitrarily many phase transitions between exponentially ergodicity and evanescence as the
switching rate increases.

Introduction
A Chemical Reaction Network (CRN) is a set of possible chemical species, along with a finite set of chemical trans-
formations that the species can undergo. These models are frequently used in biochemistry both for investigating
existing systems and for designing new synthetic biological circuits. Their use also extends to epidemiology, ecol-
ogy, genetics. It is impossible to cite all the applications present in the scientific literature; we only suggest a few
works in the aforementioned directions and some comprehensive books: [4,9,12,13,16,17,23,32]. We also note that
dynamical models of CRNs can be used as an alternative expression for families of polynomial differential equations
or for stochastic models such as instances of continuous-time branching processes and Markovian queuing systems.
Deterministic and stochastic mass-action models of CRNs have been studied for over 50 years [18, 26]. These
models are not fully determined by the set of possible reactions, but require an additional “rate constant” for
each reaction, whose chemical interpretation is the propensity of a collection of the reactants to actually undergo
the reaction. These rate constants are not a property solely of the reactions themselves, as they are also affected
by factors such as temperature [11, Section 1.6], and the availability of input species which are not modeled and
assumed to be in abundance [15].
In regimes where the temperature or other relevant factors vary, it may be tempting to assume that the qualitative
behavior of the system is well approximated by ignoring the variability of the rate constants, either by taking an
average or by separately studying the system in the different regimes. However, recent work suggests that this
assumption is dangerous. For example, consider piecewise-deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs) that take the
form of models switching between two linear 2-dimensional ODEs. In [6] they demonstrated that such PDMPs
could potentially be stable or unstable, depending only on the transition rates between the two ODEs. Notably,
Example 1.3 in [6] consists of a PDMP that switches between two linear ODEs, each of which can be realized as a
deterministic mass-action CRN. Thus, though this was not the point of the paper, it demonstrated that deterministic
mass-action CRNs whose rate constants vary stochastically can be either stable or unstable depending only on the
rate at which the rate constants vary, and thus the variability of the rate constants cannot be neglected. Similar
conclusions could be drawn for deterministically switching models from the previous paper [5], that [6] builds upon.
A survey of results on models with deterministic switching between linear ODEs can be found in [28]. PDMPs
which switch between nonlinear ODEs have also been studied [7], as have diffusions in random environments [31].

In the present paper, we investigate the stability of a stochastic process that randomly switches between a
finite number of stochastic CRNs. We denote a choice of stochastic CRN as an “environment,” and we assume the
choice changes in time according to a finite continuous-time Markov chain. We further assume that the transition
rates of the environment Markov chain are multiplied by a common factor κ > 0 (the bigger κ is, the faster the
environment switches occur). See Figure 1 for an example of this. Our four main theorems give matrix conditions
for stability and instability for κ large (Theorems 4 and 10, respectively) and similarly for κ small (Theorems 8
and 16, respectively). In a sense, our results address the question above of when is it safe to approximate the
stability of the system by ignoring the variability of the rate constants. We prove that when the rate constants are
changing rapidly enough, the system with averaged rate constants is qualitatively similar, whereas when the rate
constants are changing slowly enough (subject to some exceptions; see Example 32), the correct approximation is
by the constant rate processes rather than the average.
These results naturally open up the possibility of phase transitions, in terms of the stability of the model, as
the parameter κ varies. We capture this phenomenon in Examples 21 and 25.
The assumptions in our results match the conditions given in [6] in the PDMP case, although that paper only
addresses two of the four possibilities (namely, stability for small κ and instability for large κ; see their Theorem
1.6). Consistently, in their examples they show that (to use our terminology) the system can be stable for small κ
but unstable for large κ, whereas we have examples both of this and of the reverse behavior (instability for small
κ paired with stability for large κ). There is a further difference with [6] that is worth emphasizing, in addition
to their notable restriction to dimension 2: their proof is based on a polar decomposition technique, which allows
them to reduce the dimension of the problem by exploiting the fact that the dynamics of the angle is autonomous.
This technique does not apply in the context of this paper. Instead, we prove our results from scratch using a
novel Lyapunov function construction, which has the added benefit of working in higher dimensions. In fact, our
technique could potentially be adapted back to their setting, though we do not address that possibility in this work.
A Lyapunov function approach is also used in [31], however, the Lyapunov functions we construct in this paper
rely on minor while indispensable changes caused by the switching environments, while their Lyapunov function
constructions are based on a state space decomposition.
Above, we have discussed our results in the cases where κ is sufficiently large or sufficiently small. We also study
the case where κ takes some intermediate value. It turns out that in that case, regardless of the behavior at the
extremes, the process can undergo an arbitrarily large number of phase transitions between stable and unstable.
While similar counterintuitive behavior has been noticed in [27] in the context of PDMPs switching between linear
ODEs, the models in that paper do not arise from CRNs. Moreover, though both here and in [27] the key first step
for showing arbitrarily many phase transitions is to construct a model which is stable for large and small κ but is
unstable for some intermediate κ, both the construction and verification are fundamentally different.
We would be remiss not to mention that we are not the first to look at specifically stochastic mass-action CRNs
with stochastically varying coefficients. In [10], they give conditions for such networks to be positive recurrent
regardless of κ. The monomolecular networks from that paper are a subclass of the networks studied in this one,

The intended interpretation of the SAL Setting is that there are n CRNs with a combined d species, and which

Intermediate Switching
The purpose of this section is to explore possible dynamics for intermediate values of κ. First, we will show that
there are models in which the behavior at sufficiently small and sufficiently large values of κ is the same, but at
some intermediate κ the behavior changes. Specifically, the model in Example 36 will turn out to be transient
for both small and large κ but exponentially ergodic for intermediate κ, whereas the model in Example 40 will
be exponentially ergodic for both small and large κ but transient for intermediate κ. Second, building on these
examples, in Proposition 42 we will show that there exists models with an arbitrarily large number of phase
transitions between exponentially ergodic and transient as the parameter κ is varied, with our preferred behavior
at extreme values of κ.
Recall that Examples 21 and 25 show that there exists models which are transient for small κ and exponentially
ergodic for large κ, and vice versa. If these networks are combined into one big network with two non-interacting
sets of species, one from the first example and one from the second, the combined network will be transient whenever
either model is transient, and exponentially ergodic when both are exponentially ergodic. Thus to construct an
example which is transient for κ both small and large but exponentially ergodic for intermediate κ, we just have
to combine these two previous examples in such a way that regimes where both are exponentially ergodic overlap.

Lastly, a matrix N is said to be non-negative if all entries of N are non-negative. By the Perron–Frobenius
theorem, any non-negative matrix has a non-negative real eigenvalue which is at least as large as the modulus of
each other eigenvalue of N. This eigenvalue will necessarily have the largest real part of any eigenvalue of N. We
will abuse terminology slightly and refer to this (not necessarily simple) eigenvalue as the largest eigenvalue of N.
Notice that if M ∈Rd×d is a Metzler matrix, then for any large enough α ∈R we have that N := M + αId is
non-negative, where Id denotes the d × d identity matrix. Since the eigenvalues of M + αId are the eigenvalues of
M shifted by α, we see that M, like N, will have an a real eigenvalue with the largest real part of any eigenvalue
of M. As above, we will refer to this eigenvalue as the largest eigenvalue of M.

Lemma 53: Suppose that M is a d × d Metzler matrix. Let r denote the largest eigenvalue of M. Let r′ denote
the maximum over ∅̸= I ⊊[d] of the largest eigenvalue of the (Metzler) matrix M I. Then r′ ≤r.
Proof. This lemma, but for specifically non-negative matrices rather than general Metzler matrices, is Proposition
4 in [14, Chapter XIII, Section 3]. To extend to Metzler matrices M, let N := M + αId for some α ∈R>0 no
smaller in absolute value than all diagonal entries of M, so that N is non-negative. Then the largest eigenvalue of
N is r + α. Moreover, for each ∅̸= I ⊆[d] the largest eigenvalue of N I is α plus the largest eigenvalue of M I. It
follows that the maximum over I of the largest eigenvalue of N I is r′ + α. Since the lemma holds for non-negative
matrices we have r′ + α ≤r + α; the lemma in full generality follows.
