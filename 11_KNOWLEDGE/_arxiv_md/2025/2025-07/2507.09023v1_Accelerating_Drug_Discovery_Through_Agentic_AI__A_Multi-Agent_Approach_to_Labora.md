ACCELERATING DRUG DISCOVERY THROUGH AGENTIC AI: A
MULTI-AGENT APPROACH TO LABORATORY AUTOMATION IN
THE DMTA CYCLE
Yao Fehlis, Charles Crain, Aidan Jensen, Michael Watson, James Juhasz, Paul Mandel, Betty Liu, Shawn
Mahon, Daren Wilson, Nick Lynch-Jonely, Ben Leedom, David Fuller
Artificial, Inc.
ABSTRACT
The pharmaceutical industry faces unprecedented challenges in drug discovery, with traditional
approaches struggling to meet the demands of modern therapeutic development. This paper introduces
Tippy, a novel agentic AI framework that transforms laboratory automation through specialized AI
agents operating within the Design-Make-Test-Analyze (DMTA) cycle. Our multi-agent system
employs five specialized agents—Supervisor, Molecule, Lab, Analysis, and Report, with Safety
Guardrail oversight—each designed to excel in specific phases of the drug discovery pipeline. As
far as we know, Tippy represents the first production-ready implementation of specialized AI agents
designed specifically for automating the DMTA cycle, providing a concrete example of how agentic
AI can transform laboratory workflows. By leveraging autonomous AI agents that can reason,
plan, and collaborate, we demonstrate how Tippy’s agentic AI can accelerate DMTA cycles while
maintaining the scientific rigor essential for pharmaceutical research. The system shows significant
improvements in workflow efficiency, decision-making speed, and cross-disciplinary coordination,
offering a new paradigm for AI-assisted drug discovery.
Keywords agentic AI · multi-agent systems · drug discovery · lab automation · DMTA cycle · autonomous systems ·
pharmaceutical research

Introduction
1.1
The Challenge of Modern Drug Discovery
The pharmaceutical industry stands at a critical juncture. Despite advances in computational chemistry, high-throughput
screening, and molecular biology, the cost and time required to bring new drugs to market continue to escalate. The
traditional drug discovery process, often taking 10-15 years and costing billions of dollars, struggles with inefficiencies
rooted in siloed workflows, manual data integration, and sequential rather than parallel decision-making[1, 2, 3].
At the heart of modern pharmaceutical research lies the Design-Make-Test-Analyze (DMTA) cycle—an iterative
framework that drives the optimization of drug candidates from initial concept to clinical development. The DMTA
cycle represents both the fundamental methodology of medicinal chemistry and the primary bottleneck in drug discovery
efficiency. Each cycle involves designing new molecular entities, synthesizing them in the laboratory, testing their
properties through various analytical methods, and analyzing the results to inform subsequent design decisions.
1.2
Understanding the DMTA Framework
The Design-Make-Test-Analyze cycle[4, 5, 6] represents the core methodology of modern medicinal chemistry,
providing a structured approach to drug optimization that has guided pharmaceutical research for decades. Each phase
of the DMTA cycle addresses specific aspects of drug development, from molecular design through experimental
validation to data interpretation and hypothesis refinement.
The Design phase encompasses computational chemistry, molecular modeling, and structure-based drug design.
Medicinal chemists leverage tools ranging from quantum chemistry calculations to machine learning models to propose

new molecular entities with improved properties[7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. This phase requires deep understanding of
structure-activity relationships (SAR), pharmacokinetics, and target biology to generate hypotheses that can be tested
experimentally.
The Make phase involves synthetic chemistry, process optimization, and compound preparation. Synthetic chemists
develop efficient routes to target molecules, optimize reaction conditions, and scale synthesis as needed. This phase
requires expertise in organic chemistry, reaction mechanisms, and analytical characterization to ensure compound
identity, purity, and consistency.
The Test phase encompasses a broad range of analytical and biological assays designed to characterize compound
properties. From basic physicochemical measurements like solubility and LogP to complex cell-based assays and
animal studies, this phase generates the experimental data needed to evaluate compound performance against target
criteria.
The Analyze phase involves data interpretation, pattern recognition, and decision-making about next steps. Researchers
must integrate results from multiple assays, identify trends and outliers, and formulate hypotheses for the next design
iteration. This phase requires statistical analysis, data visualization, and scientific reasoning to extract actionable
insights from complex datasets.
1.3
Current Limitations in DMTA Implementation
Despite its conceptual elegance, the DMTA cycle faces significant implementation challenges that limit its effectiveness
in modern drug discovery. Traditional approaches suffer from several critical bottlenecks that impede rapid iteration
and efficient resource utilization.
Sequential rather than parallel execution represents a major inefficiency in current DMTA implementations. Most
organizations execute DMTA phases in series, waiting for complete results from one phase before initiating the next.
This approach, while scientifically conservative, creates significant delays and underutilizes available resources. Modern
drug discovery demands more parallel and overlapping execution to maximize throughput and minimize cycle times.
Data integration and communication barriers pose another significant challenge. Each phase of the DMTA cycle
generates distinct types of data using different tools, formats, and analytical frameworks. Medicinal chemists work
with molecular structures and SAR data, synthetic chemists focus on reaction yields and analytical characterization,
and biologists analyze dose-response curves and mechanism studies. Integrating these diverse data types into coherent
decision-making frameworks remains a manual and error-prone process.
Resource coordination and scheduling inefficiencies[13] further compound these challenges. Modern pharmaceutical
research involves multiple specialized teams, expensive instrumentation, and complex experimental protocols. Coor-
dinating these resources across DMTA cycles requires significant project management overhead and often results in
suboptimal utilization of both human expertise and analytical capabilities.
1.4
The Promise of Agentic AI for Laboratory Automation
The limitations of traditional DMTA implementation highlight the need for more intelligent approaches to laboratory
automation and workflow coordination. While robotic systems have automated many routine tasks, they lack the
contextual understanding and decision-making capabilities needed to optimize complex workflows like the DMTA
cycle.
The emergence of agentic AI—artificial intelligence systems capable of autonomous reasoning, planning, and goal-
directed behavior—offers transformative potential for pharmaceutical research[14, 15, 16, 17]. Unlike traditional AI
applications that focus on pattern recognition or prediction, agentic AI systems can understand context, make decisions,
and coordinate complex workflows without constant human supervision. This capability is particularly valuable in drug
discovery, where researchers must integrate vast amounts of heterogeneous data, coordinate multiple experimental
workflows, and make time-critical decisions that impact both scientific outcomes and business objectives.
Agentic AI represents a fundamental shift from passive AI tools to autonomous AI systems capable of goal-directed
behavior, reasoning, and collaboration. Unlike traditional AI applications that respond to specific inputs with predeter-
mined outputs, agentic AI systems can understand objectives, develop plans, adapt to changing circumstances, and
coordinate with other agents to achieve complex goals.
In the context of scientific research, agentic AI systems possess several key capabilities that distinguish them from
conventional automation. They can understand and interpret scientific literature, experimental protocols, and analytical
data using natural language processing and domain-specific knowledge. They can reason about experimental design,

Tippy: A Multi-Agent System for Drug Discovery
2.1
Agent Specialization and Domain Expertise
Figure 1: Tippy’s multi-agent architecture showing specialized agents (Supervisor, Molecule, Lab, Analysis, Report,
and Safety Guardrail) with their associated tools for laboratory automation and drug discovery workflows.
The complexity of modern pharmaceutical research requires specialized expertise across multiple domains, from
computational chemistry to synthetic organic chemistry to analytical biochemistry. No single AI system can master all
these domains to the depth required for autonomous operation. Instead, our approach leverages specialized agents, each
optimized for specific aspects of the drug discovery process.
Figure 1 illustrates the multi-agent architecture of Tippy, showing the five specialized agents and their associated tools.
The Supervisor Agent acts as the central coordinator, orchestrating workflows between five domain-specific agents: the
Molecule Agent for computational chemistry tasks, the Lab Agent for laboratory automation and instrumentation, the
Analysis Agent for data processing and statistical analysis, and the Report Agent for documentation and communication.
The Safety Guardrail Agent provides critical oversight across all operations. Each agent is equipped with specialized
tools tailored to their domain expertise, enabling autonomous operation within their respective areas while maintaining
seamless coordination through the supervisor
The Supervisor Agent serves as the central coordinator and primary interface for human researchers within the multi-
agent system. It maintains overall project context, understands research objectives and timeline constraints, and
orchestrates workflow execution across all specialized agents. The supervisor manages task delegation, determines
which agent should handle specific requests based on task requirements, and facilitates seamless handoffs between
agents as workflows progress through different DMTA phases. Additionally, it provides strategic guidance to human
researchers, suggesting optimal next steps based on experimental results and project goals while maintaining visibility
into resource allocation and system performance.
The Molecule Agent specializes in generating molecular structures and converting chemical descriptions into stan-
dardized formats. It can look up known molecules, suggest similar compounds, and optimize them for drug-likeness

properties such as QED (quantitative estimate of drug-likeness) and logP (partition coefficient). The agent converts
molecular names to SMILES notation and can generate molecular images when needed, serving as the primary driver
of the Design phase in DMTA cycles.
The Lab Agent functions as the primary interface to the Artificial platform[18], managing HPLC analysis workflows,
synthesis procedures, and laboratory job execution. It can create and start jobs, query job status and results, manage
workflow parameters, and coordinate laboratory resources. The agent coordinates the Make and Test phases of DMTA
cycles, ensuring efficient execution of synthesis and analytical workflows while maintaining quality and safety standards.
The Analysis Agent serves as a specialized data analyst, processing job performance data and extracting statistical
insights from laboratory workflows. It has access to advanced analytics tools including activity duration analysis, actor
workload monitoring, and job timeline tracking. It uses retention time data from HPLC analysis to guide molecular
design decisions, as retention time correlates with key drug properties. It drives the Analyze phase of DMTA cycles,
converting raw data into scientific understanding and decision-making guidance that feeds directly back into the Design
phase.
The Report Agent acts as a documentation specialist, generating summary reports and detailed scientific documentation
from experimental data. It can convert findings into PDF formats and attach reports directly to job results in the Artificial
Lab suite, ensuring that insights from experiments are properly captured and shared with research teams.
The Safety Guardrail Agent operates as a critical safety oversight component, validating all user requests for potential
safety violations before they are processed by other agents. This specialized agent analyzes requests for dangerous
chemical reactions, unauthorized system access, procedures that could cause harm, and synthesis of controlled
substances. It operates using a fast, lightweight model to provide immediate safety validation without impeding
workflow efficiency, ensuring that all laboratory operations maintain the highest safety standards while allowing normal
research activities to proceed unimpeded.
2.2
Coordination and Collaboration Mechanisms
Individual agent capabilities, while impressive, achieve their full potential only through effective coordination and
collaboration. Our multi-agent framework implements several mechanisms to ensure seamless cooperation between
specialized agents while maintaining clear responsibilities and avoiding conflicts.
A hierarchical coordination structure provides overall direction and conflict resolution through a Supervisor Agent
that acts as the central coordinator and primary interface for human researchers. It understands project objectives,
manages task delegation to specialized agents, and orchestrates handoffs between agents as workflows progress through
different phases. The supervisor maintains overall context about ongoing projects and determines which specialized
agent should handle specific requests based on the nature of the task. Additionally, the Supervisor Agent can guide
human researchers on optimal next steps, suggesting strategic directions based on experimental results and project
goals.
Dynamic handoff mechanisms allow agents to transfer control smoothly as workflows progress through different phases.
When the Molecule Agent completes a design task, it can seamlessly transfer relevant information to the Lab Agent for
synthesis planning. Similarly, when experimental data becomes available, the Lab Agent can hand off to the Analysis
Agent for interpretation without human intervention.
Shared knowledge bases ensure that all agents have access to relevant information about ongoing projects, experimental
results, and historical data. This shared context enables agents to make informed decisions and avoid duplicating work
or pursuing contradictory objectives.
Collaborative decision-making protocols allow multiple agents to contribute to complex decisions that span multiple
domains. For example, selecting the next compounds to synthesize might require input from the Molecule Agent
(synthetic accessibility), Lab Agent (resource requirements), and Analysis Agent (information value based on existing
data).
2.3
Tippy Implementation on the Artificial Platform
To demonstrate the practical application of agentic AI in pharmaceutical research, we have developed "Tippy," a
comprehensive multi-agent system deployed on the Artificial platform. Tippy represents the first production-ready
implementation of specialized AI agents designed specifically for automating the DMTA cycle, providing a concrete
example of how agentic AI can transform laboratory workflows.

In a typical DMTA cycle within Tippy, the workflow unfolds with seamless agent coordination: The Molecule Agent
initiates the Design phase by generating new molecular structures and optimizing them for drug-like properties. The
Lab Agent then manages the Make phase, creating synthesis jobs and coordinating HPLC analysis workflows on the
Artificial platform. During the Test phase, the Lab Agent orchestrates experimental execution while the Analysis Agent
processes incoming data in real-time. Finally, in the Analyze phase, the Analysis Agent extracts insights from retention
time data and other analytical results, feeding this information back to the Molecule Agent for the next design iteration.
Throughout this process, the Report Agent documents the entire cycle, while the Supervisor Agent orchestrates handoffs
and provides strategic guidance to both the specialized agents and human researchers.

2.4
Integration with Laboratory Infrastructure
Successful implementation of agentic AI in pharmaceutical research requires seamless integration with existing
laboratory infrastructure, data systems, and workflows. Our approach emphasizes compatibility with current practices
while enabling gradual transition to more automated and intelligent workflows.
All agent tools are implemented through the Model Control Protocol (MCP), which provides a standardized interface
for AI agents to interact with laboratory systems, computational tools, and data resources. MCP enables consistent tool
discovery, parameter validation, and error handling across all agent interactions, ensuring reliable and secure access to
laboratory capabilities while maintaining clear boundaries between agent responsibilities and system resources.
The system integrates with laboratory information management systems (LIMS), electronic laboratory notebooks
(ELN), and analytical instrument data systems through standardized APIs and data formats. This integration ensures
that AI agents can access historical data, update records automatically, and coordinate with existing quality control and
regulatory compliance systems.
Compatibility with common analytical instruments and automation platforms enables agents to control experiments
directly when appropriate or provide detailed guidance to human operators when direct control is not feasible. The
system can interpret instrument protocols, monitor experimental progress, and troubleshoot common issues without
human intervention.
Integration with computational chemistry platforms and molecular databases allows agents to leverage existing tools
and datasets while contributing new insights and predictions. Rather than replacing existing software, our agents serve
as intelligent interfaces that can orchestrate complex computational workflows and interpret results in the context of
broader research objectives.
2.5
Impact of Tippy on Drug Discovery Workflows
Tippy transforms pharmaceutical research by bridging communication barriers between computational chemists,
synthetic chemists, and analytical scientists who traditionally work in isolation. The specialized agents provide seamless
coordination across disciplines while enabling predictive research strategies that forecast experimental outcomes and
adapt to unexpected results in real-time. Additionally, Tippy can guide users through complex workflows, suggesting
optimal next steps based on experimental results and project goals.
3
Conclusions and Future Directions
The implementation of Tippy represents a fundamental shift in drug discovery research, moving from manual coor-
dination toward intelligent orchestration by autonomous AI agents. Our multi-agent framework demonstrates that
specialized AI systems can automate routine tasks while reasoning about complex scientific problems and accelerating
the DMTA cycle with maintained scientific rigor.
The success of agentic AI opens opportunities for enterprise-scale implementation, enabling coordination of multiple
DMTA cycles and integration with business intelligence platforms. Future development must ensure AI agents enhance
rather than replace human expertise, maintaining human responsibility for critical decisions while providing transparency
for regulatory confidence.
The future of drug discovery will be characterized by human-AI collaboration, where autonomous agents handle
workflow coordination and execution while human researchers provide strategic direction and ethical oversight. This
partnership between human and artificial intelligence offers significant potential for developing therapeutics more
efficiently while maintaining the quality and safety standards essential for patient care[19].
