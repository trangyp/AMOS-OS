Maximal subgroups of free projection- and idempotent-generated
semigroups with applications to partition monoids
James East,1 Robert D. Gray,2 P.A. Azeef Muhammed,1 Nik Ruˇskuc3
Abstract
This paper investigates the maximal subgroups of a free projection-generated regular
∗-semigroup PG(P) over a projection algebra P, and their relationship to the maximal sub-
groups of the free idempotent-generated semigroup IG(E) over the corresponding biordered
set E = E(P). In the first part of the paper we obtain a number of general presentations
by generators and defining relations, in each case reflecting salient combinatorial/topological
properties of the groups. In the second part we apply these to explicitly compute the groups
when P = P(Pn) and E = E(Pn) arise from the partition monoid Pn. Specifically, we show
that the maximal subgroup of PG(P(Pn)) corresponding to a projection of rank r ≤n −2
is (isomorphic to) the symmetric group Sr. In IG(E(Pn)), the corresponding subgroup is
the direct product Z × Sr. The appearance of the infinite cyclic group Z is explained by
a connection to a certain twisted partition monoid PΦ
n , which has the same biordered set
as Pn.
Keywords: Regular ∗-semigroup, projection algebra, free projection-generated regular
∗-semigroup, biordered set, free idempotent-generated semigroup, singular square, maximal
subgroup, presentation, partition monoid, twisted partition monoid.
MSC: 20M05, 20M10, 20M17, 20M20.

Introduction
Among the properties of elements vis-`a-vis algebraic operations, idempotency is certainly one of
the most ubiquitous ones. As it refers to a single operation, semigroup theory is a natural context
in which to study this property in full generality.
The most general theoretical concept for
capturing the idempotent structure of a semigroup is that of a biordered set. To every biordered
set E one can associate a free semigroup object IG(E), which is the free-est semigroup with the
biordered set E – intuitively, IG(E) possesses only the properties that are necessarily implied
by E. Since it is well known that idempotents in any semigroup are in one-one correspondence
with maximal subgroups, the problem of studying maximal subgroups of IG(E) naturally comes
to the fore.
Regular ∗-semigroups are a class of semigroups with involution, which prominently include
various diagram monoids. In such a semigroup one can identify certain special idempotents,
called projections, which typically have simpler structure than generic idempotents, but are still
sufficient to determine the entire biordered set. The framework for describing the structure
formed by these elements is that of a projection algebra. To every such algebra P one can again
associate a free object PG(P), the free-est regular ∗-semigroup with projections P. And again,
analogous to the case of IG(E), the question of their maximal subgroups arises naturally.
The purpose of this paper is to study maximal subgroups of PG(P) and compare them
with those of IG(E). This will first be done on a general level, by establishing a number of
presentations (by generators and defining relations) for the maximal subgroups of PG(P), and
comparing them to the known presentations for the maximal subgroups of IG(E). In particular,
presentations will be obtained that reflect the fact that the former are homomorphic images of
the latter. Then we will apply this theory to compute the maximal subgroups in the case where
the biordered set E and projection algebra P arise from the partition monoid. The determination
of the maximal subgroups of IG(E) in this case has been an important open problem for some
time.
There are many examples of interactions between semigroup theory and other areas of math-
ematics revolving around idempotents.
Some examples worth mentioning are: the work on
the idempotents of Stone–ˇCech compactifications [88, 89], idempotent-generated Banach/C*-
algebras [5,43,44,59,60,82,83], idempotent tropical matrices [54], the fundamental role played
by idempotents in Putcha–Renner theory of reductive algebraic monoids [76–79], as well as the
intrinsic importance of idempotents in the study of rings and representation theory.
Of course, the idempotents of a particular semigroup S need not form a subsemigroup, and,
in many cases, may generate the entire S. These idempotent-generated semigroups are of par-
ticular importance in our context, and possess many features that confirm their significance.
For example, they have the universal property that every semigroup embeds into an idempotent
generated semigroup [48], and if the semigroup is countable, it can be embedded in a semi-
group generated by three idempotents [8]. Also, many naturally occurring semigroups have the
property that they are idempotent-generated. Important examples include semigroups of trans-
formations [3, 48, 49, 51], matrix semigroups [29, 61], endomorphism monoids of independence
algebras [32,36], diagram semigroups [4,15,20,22,24,64], and certain reductive linear algebraic
monoids [76,78].
The concept of the free idempotent-generated semigroup IG(E) over a biordered set E goes
back to Clifford [9] and the seminal work of Nambooripad on the structure theory of regular
semigroups [68]; see also [73]. Biordered sets are somewhat complicated algebraic/order-theoretic
structures whose axioms were given in [68]. It was later proved in [19] that every (abstract)
biordered set is in fact the biordered set of a semigroup S, i.e. the set
E = E(S) := {e ∈S : e2 = e}
of idempotents of S, with a partial multiplication that is defined by restricting the multiplication

Following their discovery, it turned out that the free idempotent-generated semigroups are a
rich source of interesting questions in combinatorial semigroup theory, as well as an intriguing
interface to many other areas, notably combinatorial/geometric group theory and algebraic
topology. The above-mentioned link between subgroups and idempotents immediately points
to the study of maximal subgroups of IG(E), which has turned out to be a key direction. On
the basis of early results [66,69,72], it seemed plausible for a while that the maximal subgroups
may turn out to always be free. This was refuted in [7], where a concrete example was exhibited
having the free abelian group of rank 2 among its maximal subgroups. The methods of [7]
were topological in nature, and involved realising subgroups of IG(E) as fundamental groups of
natural 2-complexes built from E. Using Reidemeister–Schreier rewriting techniques, a general
presentation for the maximal subgroups of IG(E) was established in [40], and was deployed in
the same paper to show that in fact any group is the maximal subgroup of some IG(E). A
number of variations and refinements followed, e.g. [10,12,13,17,18,37].
In the course of this development it became clear that actually identifying the maximal
subgroups of IG(E) for a specific given E can be a tricky business. In [41] it was shown that
when E = E(Tn) is the biordered set of the full transformation semigroup Tn, the maximal
subgroup of IG(E) containing an idempotent of rank r ≤n −2 is isomorphic to the symmetric
group Sr, coinciding exactly with the corresponding maximal subgroup of Tn itself. Analogous
results were proved for the partial transformation monoid [11] and for endomorphism monoids
of free G-acts (G a group) [37]. Furthermore, it was shown in [16] that if one starts with the
general linear monoid of dimension n over a division ring Q, the resulting maximal subgroup of
IG(E) is the general linear group of dimension r over Q, but only for r < n/3. The nature of
the maximal subgroups for higher ranks is an intriguing open problem.
One important class of semigroups that have completely eluded computations of this kind
are the so-called diagram monoids, such as the partition monoid Pn, Brauer monoid Bn and
Temperley–Lieb monoid TLn. Diagram monoids play an important role in many fields, including
algebra, topology, category theory, physics and biology [6, 33, 45, 56, 57, 65, 84]. Beyond their
intrinsic interest, diagram monoids have important structural parallels with transformation and
linear monoids. Of particular relevance to the topic of the current paper is the fact that Pn, Bn
and TLn are ‘almost idempotent-generated’ in the sense that every non-unit is a product of
idempotents [20,24,64]. Thus, it is natural to ask whether maximal subgroups of these monoids
are isomorphic to those of the associated free idempotent-generated semigroups IG(E), as is
the case for (linear) transformation monoids. In fact, it follows from one of our main results,
Theorem 1.2, that this is not the case for Pn. Specifically, maximal subgroups corresponding to
an idempotent of rank r ≤n −2 are (isomorphic to) symmetric groups Sr in Pn, but are direct
products Z × Sr in IG(E).
However, diagram monoids come equipped with some additional structure, in the form of
an involution, making them into regular ∗-semigroups. These are defined as semigroups with a

By way of motivation, let us dwell a bit more on the findings of [26].
Given a regular
∗-semigroup S, an idempotent p ∈E(S) satisfying p = p∗is called a projection.
The set
P = P(S) of all projections can be given the (unary) structure of a projection algebra; these were
introduced first in [52], and subsequently by several different authors in a variety of equivalent
forms (under varying names), a development that is explained in detail in [21, Section 4]. So
every regular ∗-semigroup gives rise to its projection algebra P(S). One of the main findings
of [52] is that, conversely, for every projection algebra P there exists a regular ∗-semigroup S
such that P(S) = P. An alternative proof of this is given in [26], where it was shown that every
projection algebra P gives rise to a free projection-generated regular ∗-semigroup PG(P). This
can be viewed as the analogue of IG(E) for the category of regular ∗-semigroups, as reflected in
the following presentation for PG(P), established in [26, Theorem 7.2]:

So, paralleling the development for IG(E), the following natural general question arises:
• Given a (projection-generated) regular ∗-semigroup S, with P = P(S), how is PG(P) related
to S?
Certainly, S is a natural homomorphic image of PG(P) by construction. Furthermore, since
the projection algebras of S and PG(P) coincide, it follows from the basic theory of regular
∗-semigroups that there are natural bijections between their R-, L -, D- and H -classes (see
Subsections 2.6–2.8 below for more detail).
Informally speaking, this means that the only
potential difference between these two semigroups is in their maximal subgroups. The paper [26]
gives examples of situations where these are different from each other, and some where they
are the same. A striking example of the latter situation is provided by the Temperley–Lieb
monoids TLn, which turn out to be isomorphic to their own free semigroups PG(P(TLn)); see [26,
Theorem 9.1].
There is a strong connection between the projection structure of a regular ∗-semigroup and its
idempotent structure, which we briefly outline; for details and references see [21, Subsection 3.1]
and [26, Section 6]. Let S be a regular ∗-semigroup, let P := P(S) be its projection algebra, and
let E := E(S) be its biordered set of idempotents. Every e ∈E is a product of two projections.
More precisely, letting

homomorphic image of IG(E).

regular ∗-semigroups PG(P), and apply it to compare these with the corresponding subgroups
of free idempotent-generated semigroups IG(E). We do this on both a general level, and also

Then, in the second part of the paper, we apply our theory to determine the

Theorem 1.1. The maximal subgroup of PG(P(Pn)) corresponding to any idempotent of rank
0 ≤r ≤n −2 is isomorphic to the symmetric group Sr.
Theorem 1.2. The maximal subgroup of IG(E(Pn)) corresponding to any idempotent of rank
0 ≤r ≤n −2 is isomorphic to Z × Sr.
The extra direct factor of Z in Theorem 1.2 may appear mysterious at this stage, but we
will see that it arises from a tight connection with a certain twisted monoid PΦ
n , which has the
same biordered set as Pn. The subgroups corresponding to idempotents with r ∈{n −1, n} in
both theorems are actually easy to determine: when r = n −1 they are free with ranks that can
be explicitly computed (see Remark 4.15 and Corollary 6.45), and when r = n they are trivial.
Thus, we know precisely all maximal subgroups in both PG(P(Pn)) and IG(E(Pn)).
The knowledge of the maximal subgroups of PG(Pn) translates into a complete description
of the semigroup PG(P(Pn)). It is ‘almost equal’ to Pn itself: the only differences are that the
D-class consisting of partitions of rank n −1 is ‘inflated’ so that the maximal subgroups are
copies of the appropriate free group, and that non-identity bijections are absent. This may be
interesting to compare to the case of the free projection-generated semigroup arising from the
Temperley–Lieb monoid TLn, which is proved to be exactly TLn itself in [26, Theorem 9.1].
The situation is different for IG(E(Pn)): even though we know all its maximal subgroups, and
hence its regular D-classes, this monoid contains many non-regular elements (see Subsection 2.9),
and hence has a more complicated structure.
However, for every free idempotent-generated
semigroup IG(E), there exists a distinguished (maximal) regular homomorphic image RIG(E),
whose structure is again completely determined by its maximal subgroups. Thus, Theorem 1.2
and the above discussion do entail a complete structural description of RIG(E(Pn)). However,
this semigroup is no longer ‘almost equal’ to Pn but to its twisted counterpart PΦ
n . The authors
find this quite remarkable: starting from the well-known semigroup Pn, and applying the well-
known construction IG to it, leads to another well-known semigroup PΦ
n , which originally arose
via a very different, combinatorial-geometric, route.
Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 can be viewed as analogues of the main result from [40], which asserts
that every maximal subgroup of IG(E(Tn)) corresponding to an idempotent of rank 1 ≤r ≤n −2
is the symmetric group Sr. In fact, the key idea behind the proofs of both our theorems is to
establish links with maximal subgroups of IG(E(Tn)). However, several conceptual and technical
difficulties present themselves in this reduction, especially in the case of IG(E(Pn)). The most
notable is the fact that the ‘target group’ is no longer Sr, but Z × Sr. Another is that while
in Tn every 2×2 rectangular band is a singular square, this is not true in Pn; see Subsection 8.3.

Preliminaries
2.1
General notation
We will use Z to denote both the set of integers and the corresponding additive group. We will
also use standard notation to denote intervals of integers, e.g. [m, n] := {m, m + 1, . . . , n} and
(m, n] := [m + 1, n]. For an integer n ≥0 we will write [n] to denote the interval [1, n].
2.2
Presentations
We will use two kinds of presentations in this paper: semigroup and group. Let A be an alphabet,
and denote by A+ the free semigroup over A, consisting of all non-empty (finite) words over A,
under concatenation. Denote by 1 the empty word, and let A∗:= A+ ∪{1} denote the free
monoid over A.
7


## Page 8


A semigroup presentation is a pair ⟨A | R⟩, where R ⊆A+×A+. The elements of A are called
generators and the elements of R (defining) relations; a relation (u, v) ∈R is typically displayed
as an equality, u = v. The presentation ⟨A | R⟩defines the semigroup S := A+/ρ, where ρ is
the congruence generated by R. The mapping π: A+ →S, w 7→w/ρ is a surmorphism. The
semigroup defined by ⟨A | R⟩is the free-est semigroup generated by an image of A, and satisfying
the relations in R. More formally, if T is a semigroup, and ϕ: A+ →T a homomorphism such
that uϕ = vϕ for all (u, v) ∈R, then there exists a unique homomorphism ψ: S →T such that
ϕ = πψ.
We will typically identify a word u ∈A+ and the element u/ρ of S = A+/ρ it represents.
Thus we will often write u = v to mean that u/ρ = v/ρ in S. When there is a danger of confusion,
we will emphasise that the equality holds in S, or we will say that u = v is a consequence of R.

Green’s relations (pre-orders and equivalences) are the standard tool for working with the ideal
structure of a semigroup. Here we give the basic definitions and facts, and refer the reader to

J -, H - and D-classes, respectively. If K is any of L , R or J , the set S/K = {Ka : a ∈S}

for all a, b ∈S.

Graham–Houghton graphs
The idempotents of a regular D-class of a semigroup lead to the following important combina-
torial structure:
Definition 2.4 (Graham–Houghton graph). Let D be a D-class in a semigroup S, and
let Ri (i ∈I) and Lj (j ∈J) be the R- and L -classes of S contained in D, respectively. The
Graham–Houghton graph GH(D) of D is the bipartite graph with vertices I ⊔J, the disjoint
union of I and J, and with an edge connecting i ∈I and j ∈J if and only if the H -class
Hi,j := Ri ∩Lj is a group. We identify such an edge i −j with the unique idempotent ei,j
contained in Hi,j, which is the identity of this group.
The following observation will play a crucial role in our study. It goes back at least to [38] in
the finite case; the argument of [38] works essentially unchanged in the case that the principal
factor of the D-class is completely 0-simple; see also [80, Theorem 2.1] for a slightly different
formulation.
Proposition 2.5. If S is idempotent-generated, then GH(D) is connected for every regular D-
class D of S.
Proof. Keeping the notation of Definition 2.4, it suffices to show that arbitrary vertices i ∈I
and j ∈J are connected by a path in GH(D). To do so, fix some such i ∈I and j ∈J, and also
fix an element a ∈Hi,j = Ri ∩Lj. Now write a = e1 · · · ek as in Proposition 2.3. Identifying
each idempotent with an edge of GH(D) as explained in Definition 2.4, the sequence e1, . . . , ek
is the desired path from i to j.

Further applications, concluding remarks and open problems
Now that we have completed the proofs of our results, we once again take a broader view, and
discuss some implications. In particular we suggest some natural questions, the answers to which
would advance our understanding of the free objects that we have been considering – PG(P),
IG(E) and RIG(E) – and their mutual relationships.
For a projection-generated regular ∗-semigroup S with P = P(S), we saw in Subsection 2.8
that the Green’s R-, L - and D-structure of PG(P) is the same as the corresponding structure
for S. So, once we also understand the maximal subgroups of PG(P), its structure is completely
determined. Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 6.45 complete this programme when S is the partition
monoid Pn. These results can be interpreted as saying that PG(P) is ‘almost equal’ to Pn, in
the sense that the former is obtained from the latter by only changing groups in the top two
D-classes (to trivial or free as appropriate), and leaving the rest of Pn intact. From this it
follows quickly that PG(P) has a solvable word problem (see [81, Remark 4.5]), and in fact even
admits a presentation by a finite complete rewriting system (using [39, Theorem 1]), and also
that it is residually finite (using [35]).
We hope that the results of this paper will initiate a systematic study of maximal subgroups
of the semigroups PG(P) where P is the projection algebra of other natural regular ∗-semigroups.
We recall that for the Temperley–Lieb monoid we have PG(P(TLn)) ∼= TLn [26, Theorem 9.1],
68


## Page 69


but the following are open:
Problem 12.1. Determine the maximal subgroups of the semigroups PG(P(S)), where S is a
diagram monoid such as the Brauer monoid Bn or Motzkin monoid Mn.
One difficulty that immediately presents itself in the case of Bn is that there is no natural
copy of Tn to work towards, as we did in this paper. As for Mn, it is known that all of its
maximal subgroups are trivial. However in [26, Example 10.12] it is shown that the maximal
subgroups of PG(P(M4)) corresponding to idempotents of rank 1 are infinite.
Like PG(P(Pn)), the free idempotent-generated regular semigroup RIG(E(Pn)) is entirely
determined by its maximal subgroups. The latter are identical to those of IG(E(Pn)) by (RIG3),
and hence determined by Theorem 1.2 and Remark 4.15. Thus, as above for PG(P(Pn)), we
quickly deduce that RIG(E(Pn)) has a solvable word problem, a finite complete rewriting system
and is residually finite.
So, in the same way as PG(P(Pn)) was ‘almost equal’ to Pn, here
RIG(E(Pn)) is ‘almost equal’ to PΦ
n . This seems quite remarkable, in that it implies that the
twisting map Φ, which counts the number of floating components in the product graphs of pairs
of partitions, is somehow intrinsically ‘encoded’ in the idempotent structure of the partition
monoid Pn, whose multiplication expressly ‘forgets’ this information. We believe that it would
be enlightening to find an intuitive explanation/interpretation of this observation.
For the free idempotent-generated semigroup IG(E(Pn)) the knowledge of its maximal sub-
groups only entails precise understanding of the structure of its regular D-classes. As already
pointed out, IG(E(Pn)) does have non-regular D-classes, and therefore its overall structure re-
mains opaque. In particular, it does not readily follow that it possesses any of the above nice
properties.
Problem 12.2. Does IG(E(Pn)) have a solvable word problem? Does it have a finite complete
rewriting system? Is it residually finite?
The word problem in free idempotent-generated semigroups in general is the subject of
[10,12,17], but none of the results there give an immediate answer to the above questions.
On a more general level, we may ask which groups arise as maximal subgroups of different
free objects. For example, it is known that every group arises as a maximal subgroup of some
IG(E) [18,40,87] and hence also of some RIG(E).
Problem 12.3. Is it true that every group is the maximal subgroup of some free projection-
generated regular ∗-semigroup PG(P)?
None of the constructions deployed in [18,40,87] yield regular ∗-semigroups, so the following
seems of interest:
Problem 12.4. Which groups arise as maximal subgroups of IG(E), where E is the biordered
set of a regular ∗-semigroup?
Finally, one could consider maximal subgroups of PG(P) and IG(E) simultaneously:
Problem 12.5. Which pairs of groups arise as maximal subgroups of PG(P) and IG(E) arising
from the same regular ∗-semigroup, and corresponding to the same idempotent?
From general theory, we know that the maximal subgroups of PG(P) are quotients of their
IG(E) counterparts, but at this stage it is not clear to us whether there may be further con-
straints.
