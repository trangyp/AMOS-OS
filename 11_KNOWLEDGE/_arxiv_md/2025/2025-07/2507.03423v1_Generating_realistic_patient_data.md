Abstract
Developing algorithms for real-life problems that perform well in practice highly
depends on the availability of realistic data for testing. Obtaining real-life data
for optimization problems in health care, however, is often difficult. This is
especially true for any patient related optimization problems, e.g., for patient-
to-room assignment, due to data privacy policies. Furthermore, obtained real-
life data usually cannot be published which prohibits reproducibility of results
by other researchers. Therefore, often artificially generated instances are used.
In this paper, we present combinatorial insights about the feasibility of in-
stances for the patient-to-room assignment problem (PRA). We use these in-
sights to develop a configurable instance generator for PRA with an easy-to-use
graphical user interface. Configurability is in this case especially important as
we observed in an extensive analysis of real-life data that, e.g., the probability
distribution for patients’ age and length of stay depends on the respective ward.
Keywords:
combinatorial optimization, hospital bed management,
patient-to-room assignment, data generation
1. Introduction
The development of algorithms for real-world optimization problems that
perform well in practice heavily relies on the availability of realistic data for
testing.
However, obtaining such data is often challenging, particularly for
optimization problems in the health care sector. This difficulty especially holds
true for patient related problems, such as patient-to-room assignment, where
strict data privacy regulations often limit access to necessary information [7].
Moreover, even when real-life data is obtained, it typically cannot be pub-

To address this gap, we have developed an instance generator for patient
data that utilizes explicit probability distributions derived from a large real-
world data set and that is able to create realistic instances. By conducting an
extensive data analysis, we identified significant differences between the wards,
for example regarding patients’ age and length of stay, and successfully clustered
them into distinct types. Using this information, the generator features an easy-
to-use graphical user interface that allows users to choose between the different
options for the input parameters. These choices allow effective generation of
various ward types and creation of personal dataset that mimic the users own
data.
The required patient data of the generator is oriented towards the patient-
to-room assignment problem (PRA).
In the PRA, we consider a ward with
rooms R and cr ∈N beds in room r ∈R, as well as a discrete planning horizon
T = {1, . . . , T}. Further, we are given a set of patients P, for each of whom
we know the registration period, arrival period, discharge period, sex, whether
they are entitled to a single room, and whether they bring an accompanying
person. We denote the set of female patients with Pf ⊆P and the set of male
patients with Pm ⊆P. We assume Pf ∩Pm = ∅and P = Pf ∪Pm. The
task in PRA is to assign every patient to a room for every time period of their
stay. We assume that all patients stay in hospital on consecutive time periods
from arrival to discharge and that they are discharged at the beginning of a
time period. Furthermore, we assume that every patient can be assigned to any
room. The assignment of patients to rooms has to fulfill two conditions for every
room and time period in order to be feasible: (i) rooms capacities are respected;
(ii) female and male patients are assigned to separate rooms.
Checking feasibility for an instance of the PRA is in general NP-complete
but can be solved in polynomial time both for constant room capacities and
for special cases of mixed room capacities [3]. In this paper, we extend the set
of scenarios in which the feasibility problem can be solved in polynomial time,
cf. Section 3. Our instance generator uses these results to construct feasible
instances even in case of a high load factor.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we provide
an overview of the currently existing instance sets for PRA. In Section 3, we
extend the set of scenarios in which the feasibility problem of PRA can be solved
in polynomial time. In Section 4, we describe the instance generator, detailing
what information in the process is used and how it can be utilized. Finally, in
Section 5, we present some conclusions and further outlook.

2. Literature
To our knowledge, there exist currently three instance sets that provide
patient data suitable for PRA. For two of them also the generator code is pub-
lished. The first known benchmark set for PRA was proposed by Demeester et
al. and consists of 13 instances in total that are available at https://people.
cs.kuleuven.be/~tony.wauters/wim.vancroonenburg/pas/. They consider
a centralized bed-management system for the entire hospital where each patient
has to be assigned to a most fitting room regarding department specialty and
room equipment. Demeester et al state that their data is generated based on a
realistic hospital situation, and on interviews with experienced ‘human’ patient
admission schedulers. Each provided instance models the patient arrivals and
room requirements for a planning horizon of two weeks and a hospital with 6
departments with 20 to 30 rooms each. Patients’ length-of-stays are generated
randomly based on a normal distribution with mean 5 and variance 3 and they
generate 60 new patients per considered planning period. No probability dis-
tributions for other patient attributes like sex, age, or length-of-registration are
provided.
Second, Ceschia and Schaerf provide an instance generator for the inte-
grated problem of PRA and operating-room scheduling which can be found at
https://bitbucket.org/satt/or-pas/src/master/. In their setting, a pa-
tient’s admission date is not fixed by the instance but hast to be determined
within a specific time frame as part of the problem. This instance generator
takes the number of rooms and beds as well as the total number of patients as
input. Hence, determining a sensible number of patients for the desired room
configuration is left to the user. After the total number of patients and the
length of the planning horizon is specified, they compute for each day the num-
ber of patients who register on this date based on a Poisson distribution of the
average number of patients arrivals per day. 10% of the generated patients are
urgent patients and have to be treated immediately, 45% have to be admitted
within one week, and the remaining 45% withing two weeks. A patient’s length
of stay (LOS) is computed using a log-normal distribution with parameters spe-
cific to the patient’s treatment. A patient’s gender is randomly chosen and their
age is chosen based on a uniform distribution.
Third, Brandt et al. provide an instance generator for the integrated problem
of PRA and nurse-to-patient assignment [5]. We report here only the details
of the PRA related part of the instance generator. The input parameters of
this instance generator are the number of instances that will be generated,
the length of the planning horizon, the number of rooms per capacity (single,
double, triple, or quadruple rooms), the occupancy rate, and possible types of
room equipment. The number of patients that will be generated depends on the
room configuration and the occupancy rate. Each room is assigned a random
subset of room equipment. Each patient is assigned an admission shift, an LOS
that is randomly chosen between one and five, a randomly chosen gender, and
eventually a subset of the possible room equipment as required equipment. For
the integration with the nurse-to-patient assignment, also the workload caused

In our setting, all specified equipment is rolling stock
and, hence, independent of the rooms. The instances in the set of Demeester
et al. cover multiple departments and a patient has to be assigned to a room
in a suitable department. Unfortunately, we could not find data about other
suitable wards/departments other than the one to which a patient was assigned.
Hence, we do not (yet) integrate the generation of such multi-ward instances.

3.1. Related Problems
In addition to results explicitly derived for PRA, further polynomial cases
can be identified by examining related problems. The first problem is the subset
sum problem (SSP). Given a multiset A := {a1, . . . , an} with ai ∈N for i =
1, . . . , n, and a natural number b ∈N. SSP asks if there exists a subset B ⊆
{1, . . . , n} with P
i∈B ai = b, cf. [9]. The goal of SSP is to partition A such that
the elements of both subsets sum up to a specific value. Similarly, PRA asks
for a partition of the rooms into two subsets. However, PRA differs from SSP
as the total capacity of one subset does not generally need to equal a specific
value, but must rather lie in {F, . . . , P
r∈R cr −M}.

The SSP is in general NP-complete [8], but polynomially solvable cases
exist. If the corresponding SSP instance is solvable in polynomial time, we can
decide it’s feasibility for each b ∈{F, . . . , P
r∈R cr −M}. Since the number of
possible values for b is polynomial in the size of the PRA instance, Corollary 1
implies that we can determine in polynomial time whether the PRA instance is
feasible.
One polynomial case arises if A forms a superincreasing sequence. A se-
quence a1, . . . , an is called superincreasing if Pj
i=1 ai ≤aj+1 for j = 1, . . . , n−1.
In this case, SSP can be solved by a greedy algorithm [1]. Furthermore, SSP is
solvable in polynomial time if A is an arithmetic sequence, i.e., if ai = a1+(i−1)j
for i = 2, . . . , n and j ∈N, cf. [1]. Therefore, the feasibility problem of PRA is
polynomially solvable if the room capacities form a superincreasing or arithmetic
sequence.

The BKP asks if there exists a multiset S ⊆{1, . . . , n} such that the capacity
constraint P

4. Generator
We use the combinatorial insights presented in the previous chapter to gen-
erate feasible instances w.r.t. gender separation - even for instances with a high
load. The code for the instance generator is publicly available at [4]. In this sec-
tion, we first present the data basis used for the instance generator. Second, we
show how to use the generator, and third, we explain the technical generation
process.
4.1. Data
For our instance generator we had access to one year of the data covering an
entire hospital with over 60 wards and 50000 patient stays. For some patients,
the data reports stays on multiple wards. In order to obtain ward-specific data
in our analysis, we treat each stay per ward as an independent patient stay and
use the term patient and patient stay synonymously.
For each patient (stay), we know the registration date, admission date, dis-
charge date, as well as the patient’s age, gender, single-room entitlement, and
whether they bring an accompanying person or come alone. For all dates, also
the exact time stamp is provided. We use the time stamp to identify emergency
patients as those with an admission at most 30 minutes after registration.
During the analysis of our data, we first observe that all attributes of a
patient stay correlate with the patient’s age. Therefore, we first compute for
each ward the probability that a patient has a certain age (between 18 and 100
years). Although there are over 60 different wards in our data set, they can be
partitioned into four types of age distributions, cf. Fig. 5. We then determine
for each type a specific age distribution based on the combined data of all
corresponding wards. Additionally, we determine the general age distribution
based on the entire data set which is most accurately modeled by a log-normal
distribution with mean µ = 61, 5594489311164 and standard deviation σ =
17, 495923251794.
Second, we compute for each ward the probability for a certain LOS. Anal-
ogously to the age distribution, we determined again five different types of
LOS distribution that occur on the different wards, cf. Fig. 6, and one gen-
eral LOS distribution based on the entire data set.

tribution is most accurately modeled by a log-normal distribution with mean
µ = 4, 02136785471962 and standard deviation σ = 1, 24578691452702.

4.2. Using the generator
The instance generator can be used either via the graphical user interface
or via the python API. In this paper, we describe the use of the graphical user
interface as this highlights all parameter choices. An example demonstrating
the use of the python API is provided with the code.
The graphical interface guides the user step by step through all parameter
choices. An overview of this process is provided in Fig. 9. First, the user decides
whether to load a previous parameter setting (called template) or to save a new
one. If an old template is loaded, all parameters are set to the respective values,
but modifications are still possible. In this step, also the time horizon and the
desired load factor are set. The user can also decide if they want to choose
one of our combined distribution for the patients’ LOS and age or pick them
independently. Moreover, the user can specify whether feasibility with respect
to gender-separated rooms should be ensured. If this feasibility is not ensured
in the generation process, instances with a high load and long time horizon will
very likely be infeasible w.r.t. gender separation. However, in some countries,
such as Germany, gender-mixed rooms are generally not allowed in hospitals.
So, in order to generate instances corresponding to hospitals in these countries,
we offer to ensure this feasibility in the generation process.
In the second step, the number of rooms and their bed capacities are speci-
fied. If feasibility w.r.t. gender separation is ensured, the choices are limited in
this step to the cases presented in Section 3.2. In our experience, however, this
covers all common room capacities found in real-world hospitals.
In the third step, either one of the five combined age and LOS distribu-

tion can be chosen or the user can choose independent probability distributions,
depending on the choice made in step 1. The five combined distributions are
depicted in Fig. 7 where darker spots in the heat-maps correspond to a higher
probability. For independent distributions the user can specify a custom uni-
form distribution, a custom log-normal distribution or choose one of the ward-
specific distributions that we extracted from the data Figs. 5 and 6. The default
setting of the log-normal distribution corresponds to the general log-normal dis-

Additionally, it is possible to define a custom minimum and maximum age. Re-
mark however, that our data set and analytical results are not representative
for pediatric wards.
In the forth step, the user can specify a probability distribution for the length
of registration (LOR). This can either be a uniform distribution or a log-normal
distribution. The default setting of the log-normal distribution is, again, the
LOR-distribution that is based on our entire data set.
In the fifth step, the user can specify the percentage of patients that are
female, emergency patients, entitled to a private (single) room, or bring an
accompanying person. All rates depend on the patient’s age and can either be
specified per age class or directly as a polynomial function. The default values
correspond again to the setting we observed in our data set. Remark that also
rates that are specified per age class are internally transformed to a polynomial
function of degree 3.
In the last step, the user can specify the number of instances that should be
generated and where to store them.

5. Conclusion and future work
Motivated by the importance of realistic data for evaluating algorithms de-
signed for patient related problems and the lack thereof, we have developed a
new instance generator to create realistic data. The required input for the gen-
erator is oriented towards the PRA with gender separation, for which multiple
results regarding the complexity of the feasibility problem are already known.
We extended these results by introducing new scenarios in which the feasibility
problem can be solved in polynomial time. All scenarios can be incorporated
into the generator to guarantee the feasibility of the instances, if desired. This is
especially important as gender separation is mandatory in Germany and with-
out this guarantee, randomly generated instances with a long time horizon or a
high load will most likely be infeasible. In addition to guaranteeing feasibility,
the generator allows users to choose in an easy-to-use graphical user interface
from various options for input parameters. We derived these options of explicit
probability distributions for different ward types by conducting an extensive
data analysis of our large real-life dataset. Beyond selecting from recommended
options, users can also personalize instances in detail to mimic specific wards,
provided they have access to relevant data. The proposed generator is made
freely available to other researchers and an earlier version has already been
utilized by Brandt et al. [6].
Looking ahead, future versions of the generator will feature several enhance-
ments such as the inclusion of daily fluctuations in load to more accurately
model patient arrivals, especially considering weekly rhythms and seasonal fluc-
tuations.
Also allowing the simultaneous generation of wards along with a
shared set of emergency patients would enable decisions regarding which ward
to assign each emergency patient. Another interesting extension might be to
expand the applicability of the instance generator for other patient-related prob-
lem by integrating additional parameters, such as patient-induced workload for
the the nurse-to-patient assignment problem.
