Abstract—This full research paper investigates the impact of
generative AI (GenAI) on the learner experience, with a focus on
how learners engage with and utilize the information it provides.
In e-learning environments, learners often need to navigate a
complex information space on their own. This challenge is further
compounded in interdisciplinary fields like bioinformatics, due to
the varied prior knowledge and backgrounds. In this paper, we
studied how GenAI influences information search in bioinfor-
matics research: (1) How do interactions with a GenAI chatbot
influence learners’ orienteering behaviors? ; and (2) How do
learners identify information scent in GenAI chatbot responses?
We adopted an autoethnographic approach to investigate these
questions. GenAI was found to support orienteering once a
learning plan was established, but it was counterproductive prior
to that. Moreover, traditionally value-rich information sources
such as bullet points and related terms proved less effective when
applied to GenAI responses. Information scents were primarily
recognized through the presence or absence of prior knowledge
of the domain. These findings suggest that GenAI should be
adopted into e-learning environments with caution, particularly
in interdisciplinary learning contexts.

Students are increasingly turning to AI tools like ChatGPT
for support when they encounter challenges [1], [2]. When
used with appropriate guardrails, these tools provide many
learning benefits [3]. As students continue to shift away from
traditional help-resources [4], it is important to understand how
GenAI impacts their ability to find relevant information. In this
paper, we investigate how learners’ approaches to information
seeking change when moving from traditional e-tutorials to
GenAI chatbots, particularly in interdisciplinary contexts.
Prior studies have compared GenAI chatbots to traditional
information retrieval systems. While both traditional web
search and GenAI chatbots share a query-box interface, the
conversational nature of GenAI changes how users formu-
late queries [5]. Traditional retrieval-based chatbots resemble
GenAI chatbots in being conversational and ever-present;
however, GenAI’s capability to generate novel, human-like text
with high performance without relying solely on given inputs
makes it more versatile [6]. The potential educational value
of conversation-simulating agents has been recognized since
the late 1960s, but it is GenAI’s performance, versatility, and
affordability that have enabled its large-scale adoption in edu-
cation today [7]. Accordingly, a common shift in educational
technology learners are currently experiencing is the transition
from traditional e-tutorials to GenAI chatbots (See Figure 1).
What makes the dramatic transition from traditional e-
tutorials to GenAI logistically practical is the resemblance
of GenAI responses–probabilistically generated summaries–
to traditional e-tutorials; after all, a tutorial is a summary of
knowledge [8], [9] accompanied by guiding structures [10].
However, several key differences between these formats are of-
ten overlooked. The dominant user behavior with traditional e-
tutorials (Fig. 1A) is scrolling, whereas interaction with GenAI
chatbots (Fig. 1B) centers on chatting. This difference changes
how content is presented to learners: traditional e-tutorials
follow a pre-defined order designed by a teacher, while GenAI
delivers content dynamically in response to learner queries
and model conditions, often at the expense of the teacher-
intended instructional structure. Furthermore, GenAI chatbots
provide instant, human-like feedback as a core feature unlike
traditional e-tutorials. This conversational responsiveness can
lend undue credibility to GenAI content. Although some of
the challenges posed by these differences may be addressed
through curricular integration of AI literacy [11], [12], they
remain underexplored. These concerns are especially relevant
in interdisciplinary learning contexts, where the perceived
benefits of GenAI, such as personalization and support for
self-directed learning [7], are particularly compelling.
We investigate the impact of GenAI on e-learning in an
interdisciplinary context, using information seeking as the
theoretical framework. Information seeking is a purposive
human information behavior centered on the acquisition of
information [13], making it well-suited for understanding

human–AI interactions, since each exchange is initiated by
a user query reflecting an underlying purpose. Concepts such
as orienteering and information scent build upon this foun-
dation. Orienteering is an information search theory inspired
by a sport of the same name, in which participants navigate
from a shared starting point to a common destination via
multiple potential intermediate locations [14]. In the context
of information seeking, orienteering theory suggests that users
incrementally transition between diverse search modes (e.g.,
monitoring, following a plan, and exploring a topic), adapting
their approach based on the results encountered until the task
is complete [14]. Information foraging theory (IFT) can be
used to examine each transition in detail. It models how users
allocate limited attention, drawing on evolutionary-ecological
models of food foraging [15]. In IFT, a patch of information
perceived as valuable is said to have information scent. Be-
cause this value is inferred from contextual cues, information
scent is inherently proximal and often imperfect [15], [16].
The information diet refers to the set of information sources
perceived as most profitable and prevalent in value based on
their information scent [15]. To explore how GenAI chatbots
shape knowledge acquisition, we pose the following questions:
RQ 1: How do interactions with a GenAI chatbot influence
learners’ orienteering behaviors?
RQ 2: How do learners identify information scent in GenAI
chatbot responses?
To address these questions, we conducted an autoethno-
graphic study of GenAI use (See Figure 2). Autoethnography
enables researchers to reflect on their own experiences within a
cultural or technological context [17]. Given the complexity of
our research setting, this method allowed us to introspectively
analyze the experience, providing a richer understanding of the
nuances in human-AI interactions. Our team was particularly
well-positioned for this work due to its diverse interdisci-
plinary expertise and academic hierarchy, which helped miti-
gate potential biases. Hence, we provided detailed snapshots
of interactions with GenAI, revealing experiential insights that
may be generalizable to similar learning contexts.
Our contributions are empirical and opinion-based [18]:
1) We examine how a learner-practitioner interacts with
GenAI in an interdisciplinary context. We identify ways in
which GenAI supports (e.g., code debugging and document
formatting) and distracts (e.g., a heavy verification load,
misaligned goals and misplaced anthropomorphic credibil-
ity) from orienteering. We also observe a decrease in the
learner’s reliance on traditional contextual cues, marked
by changes in information diet and increased dependence
on the presence or absence of prior knowledge to detect
information scent in GenAI responses.
2) We discuss GenAI-induced distraction (e.g., AI “nonsense”
or hallucinations [19], [20]) and argue that AI literacy,
while effective, is not a foolproof safeguard.
3) We explore how changes in information diet challenge the
GenAI-assisted information-seeking process and propose
leveraging prior knowledge–based information scent as a
guiding theoretical framework.
II. METHODS
We adopted an autoethnographic approach [17] to conduct
a formative study on interdisciplinary learning and human-AI
interaction. We explored our own experiences engaging with
a GenAI tool in the context of bioinformatics. The following
sections describe the research team, the GenAI system, the
experimental design, and the types of interactions analyzed.
A. Team Position
In this study, we positioned ourselves not only as researchers
but also as stakeholders in bioinformatics. This positioning
was made possible by our team’s integration of expertise–
an intellectual introgression [21]–across bioinformatics and
human-computer interaction (HCI). The first author is a
bioinformatics and HCI researcher pursuing a PhD. They
routinely build bioinformatics pipelines and refer to tool
documentations. The second author is in bioinformatics, and
the last author is in HCI. Both are experienced researchers
and teachers at the same university who serve as the first
author’s academic advisors. The team was formed around
the first author’s interest in interdisciplinary learning. This
dynamic reflects the hierarchical structure commonly observed
in academic research and education. It is important to note
that the first author also assumed the role of a bioinformatics
practitioner in the study, due to their intersectional position
across both domains. In summary, the second and last authors
functioned as researchers and teachers, while the first author
occupied a hybrid role as researcher, learner, and practitioner.
The first author is AI-literate: they can critically assess AI,
are proficient at communicating with AI, and are frequent
users of AI tools [11]. They have taken multiple AI-related
courses and have read papers on protein structure prediction
models such as AlphaFold [22] and ESMFold [23]. They
also lead the development of an in-house GenAI chatbot and
frequently use ChatGPT for tasks such as language translation,
grammar checking, and code debugging. Despite this familiar-
ity, the first author had not used GenAI for research purposes
prior to the study and instead primarily turned to traditional e-
tutorials, motivated by a preference for independent effort and
a sense of pride in self-reliance. Throughout the study, they
approached the chatbot’s responses with considerable caution,
understanding that its training data had an earlier knowledge
cutoff date and that the most recent advancements are typically
found in academic literature.
B. GenAI System Conditions
To avoid the potential influence of additional system com-
ponents present in platforms like ChatGPT, we conducted
the study using an in-house GenAI system. This system was
implemented using a pre-trained and instruction-fine-tuned
foundation model llama3-70b-8192 (knowledge cutoff:
December 2023) [24] accessed via the Groq client [25]. The
model supports a limited memory of message exchanges
per session, constrained by its context length [24]. It was
initialized with minimal task-specific information using the
following prompt:

C. Study Protocol
This autoethnographic study was designed to capture the
lived experience of using GenAI for learning, as the first author
attempted to learn a bioinformatics topic with support from a
GenAI system while the other authors provided feedback in the
role of teachers (See Figure 2). To mitigate bias, the second
author independently developed a task scenario for the first
author. The selected scenario centered on Evolutionary Scale
Modeling (ESM) 3, a novel protein language model published
in early 2025 that is capable of engineering functional proteins
not found in nature [26]. This topic was outside the first
author’s expertise or routine, but was closely aligned with the
second author’s research interest.
Task Scenario
“Learn how to use ESM-type models in comparative sequence
analysis, know how to run them, and find key applications.”
Along with the task, the first author was provided with two
online references on protein language models [27], [28]. They
had access to all necessary resources to complete the task,
including the GenAI system and external tools such as search
engines [29], [30]. Task completion status was determined
by the second author. Both the second and last authors later
offered feedback on the strategies employed by the first author.
D. Data Collection and Analysis
Data was collected between March 21 and April 3, 2025.
The first author interacted with the GenAI system and col-
lected reflective journals [31], chat transcripts, and memos
from meetings with the co-authors [32]. Additionally, a report
was prepared for the second author to evaluate the outcome
and confirm task completion. We used mixed-methods ap-
proach to analyze the data. For the reflective journals, we
systematically synthesized themes [33] and applied in vivo
coding to preserve nuanced expressions and emotional tone
[34]. For the chat transcripts, we performed content analysis
[35] to identify both quantitative and qualitative patterns. As
a team, we engaged in an iterative process of reflection on the
findings [36].
III. RESULTS
The first author explored the task through interactions with
the GenAI chatbot and subsequently devised and followed an
action plan to produce a task report. Potential bias due to
prior topic familiarity was effectively mitigated: the first author
found the complexity of the assigned task entirely unexpected
but accepted it as a natural aspect of bioinformatics research.
The open-ended nature of the protocol left GenAI use entirely
to the first author’s discretion, so they made a deliberate
effort to incorporate it frequently (See Table I). The resulting
task report was favorably reviewed and marked complete by
the second author who is a strong proponent of learn-by-
doing approaches. The last author also acknowledged that
they would have similarly invested time upfront to understand
the task. The process was documented through six reflective
journals, six chat transcripts, and two meeting memos. The
reflective journals and chat transcripts were the most dominant
form of records. The journals totaled 6,127 words, and the
chat transcripts recorded 92 queries submitted to the GenAI
chatbot. Figure 3 presents a quantitative summary of the query-
response exchanges extracted from the chat transcripts. It
uses words rather than tokens as the unit of measurement
for improved human readability. The number of exchanges
demonstrates that the interaction was sufficiently extensive to
support meaningful analysis.

reflective journals (See Table II). In summary, while they held
negative views about the chatbot’s style and content, they
found its attitude pleasing.
B. RQ1: Distraction for Orienteering
The GenAI chatbot was helpful for orienteering with narrow
focuses after a structured plan established; however, it proved
less effective for exploration prior to that point. A recurring
theme in the reflective journals was the distress caused by
distractions during pre-planning exploration. Although the
original orienteering theory does not inherently account for
adversarial situations resulting from low-performing search
assistants, this study surfaced several forms of distractions
that were antithetic to effective orienteering. While quantitative
measures revealed the prevalence of certain patterns, the nu-
ances of relying on GenAI for orienteering were more clearly
illuminated through qualitative analysis. Codes 1-3 capture
user responses to GenAI-induced distractions.
The theme of distress emerged repeatedly throughout the
study. One contributing factor was the heavy load of verifi-
cation. As an AI-literate bioinformatician aware of GenAI’s
limitations (See Section IIA), the first author was cautious
about accepting information from the GenAI chatbot at face

value, so they frequently verified GenAI responses with al-
ternative sources. However, when not explicitly prompted to
summarize and unconstrained by context length, the chatbot’s
responses tended to be verbose. Across 74 responses that were
neither length-limited nor empty, the average response con-
tained 207.68 words, compared to an average of 32.11 words
per corresponding query. Thus, each instance of exchange-
based information retrieval required the first author to verify a
“wall of text” six times longer than the original query. More-
over, each response functioned as a singular, self-contained
summary, offering no alternative. This was frustrating for the
first author, as it contrasted sharply with the dynamic nature
of human conversation–something GenAI ostensibly aims to
replicate. Due to these characteristics, the first author experi-
enced verification fatigue (See Code 1). The sheer frequency
with which information had to be validated was unhumanly
rapid and dehumanizing.
Code 1. Verification fatigue in evaluating GenAI content
“When [GenAI] gave me something more specific, I was busy
verifying facts. I was correcting and testing the GenAI so much
that the entire conversation was moving away from the main task
at one point. It was a fight between me and the GenAI without
necessarily getting the task done. ... However, it overall felt
very forced to ask followup questions because it was generating
information at a superficial level, and as of now, the most frequent
follow up has been about information verification.”
Another source of distress during orienteering with the
GenAI chatbot was the goal misalignment. Establishing mutual
understanding is critical for progressing in a coherent direction
and pursuing in-depth knowledge. However, the chatbot’s
tendency to “generat[e] information at a superficial level”
while “fight[ing the user] ... without necessarily getting the
task done” (See Code 1) signaled a lack of agreement. The first
author encountered multiple instances of such misalignment
and attempted to mitigate the issue by providing additional
prompts and context (See Code 2). Despite these efforts,
repeated failures to reach an agreement left them feeling
disoriented. As the first author struggled to steer the chatbot
toward task-relevant directions, they frequently found them-
selves a “passive recipient” with limited agency over the
direction of the exchange.
Code 2. Unsuccessful attempts to refine GenAI responses
“[I am the one who] forced the GenAI to re-word the task, so
it is not like I did not know our task was worded imprecisely,
but there was an odd satisfaction in the re-wording process in
the style of discussion. ‘Hopefully the GenAI has enough context
for what we are about to do’, I thought. ... There was one brief
moment of excitement about what it generated for a question, but
that did not really lead to anything interesting on google. [The
information] felt like empty calories.”
The first author was distracted from orienteering as the
chatbot interface made it easy to misplace anthropomorphic
credibility onto GenAI. Because GenAI mimics patterns of
human behavior learned from its training data, the first author
struggled to describe its behavioral features without relying on
person-oriented terms (See Table II), despite conscious efforts
to avoid doing so. We identified an instance in which the first
author’s early misplacement of anthropomorphic credibility
onto the GenAI chatbot resulted in misknowledge that per-
sisted and propagated throughout the journaling process. Code
3 illustrates the moment this occurred. The chatbot response
exhibited common signals for competency and accountability
(e.g., strong action words like “ensure” or “rest assured”) and
humor (e.g., “neigh Oh, hello there!”), a common rhetorical
strategy used for improving persuasion. However, the informa-
tion it provided was inaccurate: the actual knowledge cutoff
date for the model was December 2023 [24], not “December
2022”. As a probabilistic system trained on distributions from
its data, the GenAI chatbot generated response that was a
proximate but incorrect. The first author only recognized this
error during the later stage of data analysis. In the retrospect,
they were able to recall subtle hints pointing to the truth. For
example, while GenAI responses predominantly contained in-
formation from 2019 to 2021 (See Code 5), certain fragments
included information that could only have originated in 2023.
The realization caused the first author considerable distress, as
the chatbot had subtly diverted their focus from distinguishing
reliable information without their awareness.

C. RQ2: Traditional Information Diet Declining in Value
In traditional e-tutorials, the information diet is composed of
instructional structures implemented by teachers to highlight
the value of knowledge. These structures serve as landmarks
where information scent appears with sufficient frequency to
become an integral part of learners’ information diet. Common
examples include bullet points and emphasized related terms.
However, in GenAI responses, the typical information diet
diminished in value (See Codes 4-6).
The presence of bullet points in GenAI responses did not
improve information seeking. The first author found the GenAI
chatbot’s overall content either overly obvious (See Table II) or
demonstrably inaccurate. For instance, in Code 4, the GenAI
response offered a simple four-step plan for completing the
task scenario within a maximum of 33 hours. However, the
plan lacked realistic time estimates and sufficient detail to
effectively support task completion. In Code 5, the information
conveyed through the bullet points was only partially valuable–
presumably because software names were individually treated
as tokens–but the details were largely nonsensical.

IV. INTERPRETATION
This formative study revealed key user behaviors during
interaction with GenAI in the context of interdisciplinary
learning. Using an autoethnographic approach allowed us to
capture fine-grained details through first-hand experiences. As
a team of researchers, teachers, learners, and practitioners, we
designed a study based on a realistic task scenario from the
field of bioinformatics. Notably, the first author’s AI literacy
was not treated as a bias, but as a user trait expected to
become more prevalent in the near future. In the remainder
of the section, we leverage concepts from prior research to
contextualize our findings and outline future directions.
A. RQ1: How do interactions with a GenAI chatbot influence
learners’ orienteering behaviors?
Orienteering with GenAI proceeded smoothly for narrowly
focused information needs, where the cost of verification was
low; however, the cognitive effort required for verification in-
creased significantly in exploratory search. While the need for
information verification has long existed [38], the generative
nature of AI presents a new challenge by inflating the volume
of content to be validated, driven by its tendency to produce
plausible-sounding nonsense [19] and excessive verbosity.
Verification fatigue [39] emerged as a distinct outcome
of bearing cognitive load [40] in this study, characterized
by the cumulative stress and eventual exhaustion caused by
the continuous need to verify information. This cognitive
load stemmed from the first author’s awareness of GenAI
limitations and its potential to generate misleading or incorrect
content. Although information verification is often framed
to be user responsibility [41], our analyses indicated that
verifying every step in the orienteering process was not only
impractical but also unsustainable.
Using a tutorial is inherently a cost-saving [15] strategy,
an attempt to obtain a condensed summary of knowledge
rather than search through an overwhelmingly large knowledge
space. If a well-established traditional e-tutorial meeting an
information need is present, even conducting a web search
across the vast online knowledge space may be less cost-
efficient by comparison. Although GenAI can produce similar-
looking, readily available e-tutorials, the cost may quickly
become exorbitant if users must constantly verify the infor-
mation. However, because users have the convenient, no-cost
option of skipping verification, the heavy verification load does
not always fall on GenAI’s immediate users.
The GenAI verification burden that learners do not carry
eventually shifts to teachers or evaluators, who are relatively
fewer in number. In fact, those who are more likely to propa-
gate the burden are the ones who lack the resources (i.e., time
or ability) to sensitively detect issues in problematic content.
Alongside individual setbacks, this dynamic may undermine
trust in the educational system, which can be detrimental; once
trust is lost, it is difficult to restore [42]. Opinions are divided
on the adoption of AI in classrooms, and perhaps the extra
burden GenAI places not only on learners but also on teachers
should be carefully considered in making such decisions.
GenAI can further disrupt orienteering behaviors in both ex-
plicit and subtle ways, particularly through goal misalignment
and misplaced anthropomorphic credibility. Misalignment of
goals in users and GenAI is a well-documented issue in
GenAI applications [43]. In this study, integrating GenAI into
the orienteering process required considerable patience and
focus on the first author’s part. Despite frequent digressions
introduced by the model, they made deliberate efforts to make
use of its capabilities. Verification fatigue only exacerbated
these challenges, increasing their susceptibility to distraction.
While such issues were at least tangible and detectable,
more insidious forms of distraction bypassing the conscious
detection altogether also emerged. Misplaced credibility in
GenAI was observed despite the first author’s internal resis-
tance to anthropomorphism. An illusion of truth emerged and
was later betrayed, illustrating how anthropomorphic features
of chatbots [6] can work against users. This has twofold
cost implications: learning-wise, unlearning [44] is difficult,
especially when errors go undetected; and cognition-wise,
users may equate human behaviors with GenAI’s human-
like responses, potentially leading to misjudgments due to
fundamental differences in their underlying processes.
The consistent presence of distractions is not accounted for
in the original theory of orienteering. While GenAI shows
promise as an information retrieval tool capable of supporting
self-directed learning [7], the various ways it introduces dis-
traction render self-direction illusory. As a learner-practitioner,
the first author was particularly well-positioned for a mode of
learning where users take greater control over their curricular
design. Yet individual ability alone proved insufficient to fully
capitalize on the potential benefits of GenAI.
AI literacy is widely promoted as a promising strategy for
navigating the AI era, and our findings support this perspec-
tive. The AI-literate first author successfully avoided many
instances of AI-generated nonsense. Nevertheless, individual
competency alone was not foolproof against the subtle effects
of anthropomorphism baked into chatbots. These findings
underscore the need for system-level interventions in GenAI
to mitigate its disruptive influences.

B. RQ2: How do learners identify information scent in GenAI
chatbot responses?
Generally, information scent conveyed through structural
cues is the among the most readily recognized by learners.
Such structures typically follow both contextual and visual
[45] organization. Reliance on these cues is also habitual;
learners taught in traditional settings are familiar to them.
Consequently, structured content serves as a reasonable in-
formation diet. In traditional e-tutorials, the order of content
reflects a teacher-designed curriculum maintained through
linear navigation such as scrolling. However, in the GenAI
chatbot responses observed in our study, such information
scent was significantly weaker, as the order of appearance was
no longer guaranteed. Although GenAI simulated structural
cues like bullet points and related terms, these provided only
an illusion of value; the true density of useful information in
these structures had diminished.
The frequency with which the first author failed to forage
value-rich information in GenAI responses was alarmingly
high. Overall, the first author found GenAI responses to be
bland and overly obvious (See Table II). Much of the text
they consumed lacked informational value, being either self-
evident, inaccurate, or off-topic. The quote “[the information]
felt like empty calories” from Code 2 directly invokes the
concept of empty calories in nutrition; both cases give the
impression of meaningful consumption, but provide little in
terms of actual value. Prather et al.’s recent work on the “illu-
sion of competence” induced by GenAI chatbots [46] offers a
glimpse into how misleading representations of informational
value may influence learners. The traditional information diet,
rooted in traditional e-tutorial experiences, was unsustainable
in the context of GenAI responses.
Most information scents detected in this study were based
on prior knowledge of the learner. These are contextual cues
shaped by each individual’s existing knowledge landscape.
They rely heavily on the learner’s subjective perception of in-
formational value. We identified two types of prior knowledge-
based information scents, shaped by the presence or absence
of prior knowledge. Most information scents emerged to fill
an information gap when content piqued the first author’s
interest. Information scents shaped by the presence of prior
knowledge were rarer; they emerged when the first author, as
a learner-practitioner, recognized a familiar concept in a less
familiar context. The first author leveraged this knowledge to
further request examples related to that concept. This latter
approach was naturally more suitable for advanced learners or
practitioners, as it required some domain knowledge. Although
information scents were generally scarce in GenAI responses,
prior knowledge–based scents hold potential to improve infor-
mation seeking if increased in the information diet.
Because GenAI offers unique opportunities, various perfor-
mance enhancement methods have been proposed, including
reinforcement learning from human feedback [47] at the model
level, retrieval-augmented generation [48] at the data level, and
guardrail implementation [3] at the system level. These are all
active areas of research aimed at aligning AI goals with human
preferences. We bring attention to individual knowledge and
experience with our work. We propose considering prior
knowledge–based information scent as a guiding theoretical
framework at the user level. This approach confers several
advantages. It is well suited for understanding subjective
interest. It also provides knowledge-level context that can help
circumvent goal misalignment. Moreover, it suggests ways
to expand GenAI responses beyond narrowly framed, self-
contained summaries. Finally, it is particularly significant in
interdisciplinary learning contexts, where each learner’s prior
knowledge is unique and personal.
Our work offers directions for personalization in GenAI
from the perspective of diverse stakeholders in bioinformatics-
HCI. We hope it invites continued exploration of interdisci-
plinary learning to better reflect complex viewpoints.
V. CONCLUSION
Introduction of GenAI brought a paradigm shift in both
industry and academia. It is understandably a subject of excite-
ment. In this autoethnography, we systematically conducted
a formative study of human-AI interactions. We identified
several challenges in orienteering with a simple GenAI system.
Notably, we observed that issues such as misplaced anthropo-
morphic credibility can arise even for an AI-literate researcher
working at the intersection of bioinformatics and HCI. Our
study shows that information scents in GenAI responses differ
significantly from those in traditional e-tutorials. We anticipate
these findings will become increasingly relevant as GenAI
adoption expands and AI literacy continues to grow. In the
future, we aim to apply insights from this study to investigate
how information-seeking behavior is influenced when addi-
tional context is made available to the GenAI system.
VI. LIMITATIONS
While we have captured many interesting human-AI in-
teractions in this study, we are aware that some of the
inconveniences listed above is not universal. Some models
may have longer context lengths, may have been retrieval-
augmented beyond knowledge cutoff date, or may have a
higher number of parameters. However, we believe that much
of our experience will remain relevant in the GenAI landscape
as long as there is information outside of the model vocabulary.
