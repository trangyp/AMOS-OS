ABSTRACT 
Large language model (LLM) chatbots show increasing promise in persuasive communication. 
Yet their real-world utility remains uncertain, particularly in clinical settings where sustained 
conversations are difficult to scale. In a pre-registered, four-arm randomized controlled trial, we 
enrolled 915 U.S. adults (ages 45-75) who had never completed colorectal cancer (CRC) 
screening. Participants were randomized to: (1) no message control, (2) expert-written patient 
materials, (3) single AI-generated message, or (4) an AI chatbot delivering a conversation 
explicitly grounded in motivational interviewing (MI) theory. All participants were required to 
remain in their assigned condition for a minimum of three minutes. Both AI arms tailored content 
using each participant’s self-reported demographics including age and gender. Both AI 
interventions significantly increased stool test intentions by over 12 points (mean +12.9 to +13.8 
on a 0–100 scale), compared to a +7.5 gain for expert materials (p<.001 for all comparisons). 
While the AI arms also outperformed the no message control for colonoscopy intent, neither 
showed improvement over expert materials. Notably, for both outcomes, the MI-based chatbot 
did not outperform the single AI message in boosting intent despite participants spending 
roughly 3.5 minutes more on average engaging with it. The MI-based chatbot, however, was 
more effective among participants with lower trust in the CDC, suggesting its potential value for 
harder-to-reach or more skeptical populations. These findings suggest that concise, 
demographically tailored AI messages may offer a more scalable and clinically viable path to 
health behavior change than more complex conversational agents and generic time intensive 
expert-written materials. Moreover, LLMs appear more persuasive for lesser-known and 
less-invasive screening approaches like stool testing, but may be less effective for entrenched 
preferences like colonoscopy. Future work should examine which facets of personalization drive

behavior change, whether integrating structural supports can translate these modest intent 
gains into completed screenings in a health system setting, and which types of health behaviors 
are most responsive to AI-supported guidance. 
INTRODUCTION​
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States, with 
over 150,000 new cases and over 52,000 deaths projected for 2025 (1). In 2021 the US 
Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) lowered the recommended starting age from 50 to 
45 years and reiterated that multiple modalities (FIT, stool DNA-FIT, colonoscopy, flexible 
sigmoidoscopy, CT colonography) are equally acceptable for average-risk patients (2). Despite 
the clear benefits of screening, nearly one-third of adults aged 50–75—and an even larger 
share of the newly eligible 45- to 49-year group—are not up-to-date (3, 4).  As clinicians and 
health systems accelerate outreach beyond traditional clinic visits, and new testing modalities 
become accepted and available, patients must navigate the variety of choices, underscoring the 
need for scalable, comprehensible, and empathetic patient-centered tools. 
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated growing persuasive power outside of 
healthcare: in a preregistered online debate platform, GPT-4 significantly increased the odds of 
attitude change relative to human opponents when allowed basic demographic targeting (5). 
Within health care, however, a more nuanced picture continues to emerge. A single 
GPT-3–generated social-media post outperformed Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 
(CDC) posts in promoting vaccination, yet a three-minute HPV-vaccine chatbot dialog failed to 
beat a duration-matched public health factsheet (6, 7). Longer conversational agent strategies 
have begun to influence behavior change. For example, in a two-week school-based 
intervention, a chatbot delivered to parents tripled HPV vaccine uptake or appointment 
scheduling among their adolescent children compared to no intervention (8). Together, these 
mixed findings leave open two core questions for clinical implementation: How much interaction 
with AI is actually necessary, and do shorter, one-off AI-generated messages already achieve 
most of the persuasive benefit? 
Motivational interviewing (MI) offers a mechanistic lens on these questions. MI is a collaborative, 
person-centered counseling style that boosts motivation by helping individuals articulate their 
own reasons for change. In colorectal cancer screening, MI can address ambivalence, reinforce 
health values, and support informed choices. A 2022 systematic review of 14 randomized trials 
confirmed MI’s superiority over usual care yet highlighted variability in effect sizes and delivery 
formats (9). Additionally, MI can be labor intensive and hard to scale. LLMs could potentially 
emulate MI techniques and make them widely accessible (10). Yet, it remains unclear if MI’s 
empathetic, autonomy-supportive nature requires multi-turn dialogue or if a single, tailored AI 
message can convey enough relational depth to nudge intentions. 
We address this gap with a pre-registered, four-arm randomized controlled trial of 915 U.S. 
adults ages 45-75 who never reported completed CRC screening. Participants were randomized 
equally to (i) no message control, (ii) the Journal of the American Medical Association’s 
colorectal cancer screening patient page, (iii) a single GPT-4.1 message personalized to 
demographics, or (iv) an MI-style GPT-4.1 chatbot conversation personalized to demographics.

By equating content length, tailoring inputs, and minimum exposure time (three minutes) across 
arms, the design isolates the incremental persuasive value of conversational back-and-forth 
over a concise, static AI message. Outcomes focus on 12-month intentions for both 
colonoscopy and stool testing, allowing us to test whether AI persuasiveness varies by the 
familiarity or perceived burden of the screening modality. 
We find that a short, personalized AI message matches a much longer chatbot in boosting 
stool-test intentions and offers no advantage for colonoscopy intentions. These results 
challenge assumptions that conversational depth is inherently superior and suggest that concise 
AI messaging may provide a more scalable path for integrating LLMs into clinical workflows to 
reduce CRC screening gaps. 
​
RESULTS​
The primary treatment sample consisted of 915 participants who had reported never undergoing 
colorectal cancer screening (colonoscopy, stool test, etc.) and passed two attention checks (see 
Appendix Figure S1 for CONSORT diagram). The sample was primarily female (63.7%), had a 
mean age of 51.6 years, and majority White (83.4%) (Table 1). The mean baseline 12 month 
intent was 30.9 (SD = 34.5) for a colonoscopy and 32.2 (SD = 32.8) for a stool test (out of 100). 
Nearly all 695 participants assigned to the non-chatbot arms reported reading the assigned 
material. Specifically, 95.3% (221/232) of the expert material participants, 96.3% (208/216) of 
the single AI message, and 99.2% (245/247) of the no-message participants (assigned a 
fictional short story) reported reading carefully. One of the single AI message participants 
reported not reading the material, while the remainder reported skimming. Participants in the 
chatbot condition sent an average of 88.2 (SD = 69.0) total words, and the chatbot sent an 
average of 1226.2 (SD = 781.6) words (Appendix Table S1). The average chatbot message had 
a Flesch-Kincaid grade level of 9.1 (1.1), the average participant response had a grade level of 
3.7 (2.0), the average single AI message had a grade level of 9.4 (1.1) and the expert-written 
material had a grade level of 10.8. 
To assess factual accuracy and clinical appropriateness, two licensed clinicians each reviewed 
a separate random sample of 50 LLM generated messages: 25 chatbot responses and 25 
single AI messages. Overall, all 100 responses were determined to be factually accurate and 
clinically appropriate. 
Primary Outcomes​
Across all participants, intent to receive a stool test increased by an average of 8.9 (SD=22.9) 
points and increased by an average of 3.3 (SD=17.7) for colonoscopy intent (Appendix Figure 
S2). Overall, all message formats outperformed the no message control in boosting stool test 
and colonoscopy intent. However, relative to the expert material, the AI conditions only boosted 
stool test intent, showing no significant effect for improving colonoscopy intent (Figure 1, 
Appendix Table S2).


## Page 4


To aid interpretation, we computed robust, baseline-adjusted Cohen’s d for each pairwise 
contrast using the model residual SD (colonoscopy model σ = 17.0, stool test model σ = 21.5). 
Both the chatbot and single AI message formats produced medium effects on stool-test intent 
(ds = 0.64 and 0.60, respectively; both ps < .001), outperforming the expert materials (d = 0.35). 
Differences between the AI formats and expert material were statistically significant, though 
small, while no difference was observed between the two AI formats. Full pairwise comparisons 
and colonoscopy-related effects are presented in Appendix Table S3. 
These effects remained robust to demographic controls, with estimated treatment effects similar 
in magnitude and direction to the primary specification (Appendix Table S4). Exploratory tests of 
moderation identified a small set of significant interactions including trust in the CDC, NIH, as 
well as prior discussions about CRC screening with participants’ primary care providers 
(Appendix Table S5-S6). For example, for both colonoscopy and stool test intent, the chatbot 
intervention was more effective among participants with lower trust in the CDC, and among 
those who had not previously discussed CRC screening with their doctor.  
Among those participants whose baseline intent ≤ 50, both AI formats markedly shifted intent 
beyond the 50-point threshold relative to the no message control (Appendix Table S7). The 
chatbot increased the odds 4.4-fold for colonoscopy and 5.4-fold for stool testing, and the 
single-message AI raised them by roughly 4.4- and 5.3-fold, respectively (all p < .001). The 
expert patient material produced a more modest effect (OR ≈ 2.3 for colonoscopy, 2.5 for stool; 
stool p < .05, colonoscopy n.s.). These findings remained consistent when using alternate 
binary thresholds of 25 and 75 on the intention scale, with treatment effects of similar magnitude 
and direction across models (Appendix Table S7). 
Secondary Outcomes 
Across 13 potential reasons for hesitancy scored on a 0-100 importance scale, we observed no 
changes across any intervention relative to the control condition, after applying 
Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons (Appendix Figure S3). 
Comparing AI conditions to the expert material, participants found both more empathetic 
(Appendix Table S8). Only the single AI message was rated more highly than the expert 
material on a message effectiveness scale. Both AI conditions increased self-reported comfort 
discussing CRC screening with one’s doctor relative to the no message control and expert 
material (Appendix Table S8). Notably, participants spent an average of 180 seconds more with 
the chatbot than with the single AI message, for an average of 393 seconds (120% more time 
than the 3 minute requirement) (Table 2). 
The three most common reasons for why the single AI message was convincing were cited as 
motivation and reassurance (34 participants), personalization and relevance (19 participants), 
and accuracy and informational quality (17 participants). The three most common response 
reasons for why the chatbot was convincing were cited as human-like interaction and quality (26 
participants), accuracy and informational quality (22 participants), and communication style and 
language (14 participants). The three most common response reasons for why the expert

patient material was not convincing or inadequate were cited as a lack of need (21 participants), 
missing or inadequate information (17 participants), and content and style critiques (13 
participants). 
Chatbot Conversations 
Conversation coding with BOLT, a computational framework for quantifying MI behaviors in 
LLM-mediated dialogues, showed that the chatbot relied most heavily on concrete elaboration 
and didactic content (11). Across all dialogues, an average of 80% of turns evoked concrete 
elaboration, 65% provided teaching, and 51% included normalizing statements (Table 3). Less 
frequent but still present were problem‐solving moves (30%), explicit identification of user 
needs (25%), and emotion check-ins (22%). Planning language and explicit discussion of 
emotions or conflict were relatively uncommon (<16 % of turns), and perspective‐taking 
prompts appeared in only about 1% of turns. This pattern suggests the chatbot prioritized 
clarifying participants’ concrete barriers and supplying factual information over deeper 
exploration of values or perspectives. 
Single AI Message Persuasion Strategies ​
Content analysis of the single AI messages identified 49 distinct persuasion tactics, which we 
manually clustered into six conceptual themes: (i) facts & information, (ii) practical/process 
guidance, (iii) emotional appeals, (iv) identity-based appeals, (v) social-norm cues, and (vi) 
rhetorical style (Table 4, Appendix Table S9). GPT-4.1 labeling on a four-point prevalence scale 
(0 = none, 3 = high) showed that every message employed a direct-address rhetorical style 
(mean = 3.00, sd = 0.0), making it the only universally applied tactic. After rhetorical themes, 
social-norm cues were most prominent (mean = 2.06), driven chiefly by references to family 
responsibility and peer role-modeling. Identity appeals ranked next (mean = 1.49), with 
empowerment, age appropriateness, and personal responsibility appearing most prominently, 
followed by factual information(mean = 1.41) and emotional appeals (M = 1.35). 
Practical/process guidance was least frequent overall (M = 1.27), though providing clear next 
steps was also prominent. In addition, cost or insurance information, analogies, and testimonials 
from survivors appeared rarely (<0.5 on the four-point scale). These patterns suggest the model 
prioritized motivational language, normative pressure, and high-level evidence cues over 
granular logistical or financial details when constructing these single persuasive pitches. 
DISCUSSION 
This randomized trial found that both a demographically tailored AI generated message and an 
interactive motivational interviewing chatbot significantly increased intent to complete colorectal 
cancer screening relative to no intervention. However, when compared to expert-written 
educational materials, the AI interventions showed no added benefit for colonoscopy intent and 
led to significantly greater gains only in stool test intent, which is not the typical form of 
screening in the US (12). These findings suggest that a concise AI-generated message may be 
an effective and scalable approach to promoting preventive health behaviors, particularly for 
less utilized and less invasive screening options.

Prior research on LLMs and persuasive messaging has largely focused on either static, 
one-shot messages or multi-turn conversations in isolation (5–7, 13–17). Our study fills a critical 
gap by directly comparing these two formats in a controlled setting, holding personalization and 
minimum exposure time constant. Notably, we find no added persuasive benefit for the longer, 
interactive chatbot over the simpler single AI message, despite participants choosing to spend 
significantly more time engaging with the chatbot. These results suggest that conversational 
depth alone may not be necessary when personalization is strong. Single AI messages, which 
are faster to produce and easier to integrate into existing clinical outreach workflows, potentially 
offer a more scalable and practical path to implementation for health systems seeking to 
promote screening uptake. 
These effects, while modest in absolute terms, may have meaningful population-level 
implications. Among participants with low baseline intent (≤50 on a 0–100 scale), those who 
received a single AI message had 4.4 times greater odds of crossing the 50-point threshold than 
those in the no message control. If the baseline probability of crossing this threshold is 20%, 
then delivering an AI message to 10,000 unscreened, low-intent adults could yield over 1,000 
additional completed screenings, assuming that one in three individuals who cross the threshold 
ultimately follow through with screening, a clinically meaningful gain for a fully automated, 
low-cost intervention. 
From a mechanistic standpoint, this equivalence between a single message and conversation 
challenges assumptions about the superiority of multi-turn dialogue. Motivational interviewing is 
well-supported as an effective approach to promote CRC screening, and prior work has found 
LLMs can emulate MI techniques well (18). Yet, studies have also found that LLM-based MI 
therapists more closely resemble low-quality human therapists than high quality therapists in 
their usage of MI techniques (11). Our analysis of the MI chatbot’s messages using a previously 
established framework indicated that the majority of chatbot responses emphasized factual 
information and concrete elaboration over other techniques such as empathetic engagement. 
These limitations may explain why the chatbot did not outperform the single message and 
highlight opportunities for improving MI emulation in AI systems.  
The implications of these findings are clinically and operationally significant. Short, 
demographically tailored messages generated by LLMs can be integrated into patient portals, 
text-based reminders, or printed with mailed screening kits. The relative ease of deployment 
and low time burden for patients suggest that these messages could be used at scale in health 
systems seeking to close screening gaps, particularly for newer or less invasive modalities. 
Moreover, although the messages in this study were based on GPT-4.1 and were not fine-tuned 
or manually edited, all outputs reviewed were deemed factually and clinically appropriate by a 
licensed clinician, further supporting their potential for clinical integration. The AI messages 
were written at approximately a ninth-grade reading level, above the American Medical 
Association’s recommendation of sixth grade, and could likely be simplified in future iterations to 
better meet health literacy needs and improve patient engagement (19). Rather than relying on 
real-time generation, health systems could pre-generate a library of messages tailored to key 
demographic profiles, validate them for accuracy and appropriateness, and deploy them 
repeatedly at scale across outreach channels.

If conversational agents are to outperform static formats, further advances in their design will be 
needed. Improvements in emotional responsiveness, values engagement, and adaptive 
planning may enhance their persuasive effectiveness. Rather than simply increasing message 
length or interaction time, future AI chatbots may need to incorporate higher-quality motivational 
interviewing behaviors, offer logistical support, or integrate with behavioral nudges such as 
appointment scheduling to produce meaningful gains. 
Interestingly, the advantage of AI over expert materials was limited to stool testing and did not 
extend to colonoscopy. This pattern mirrors findings from a recent HPV vaccine chatbot trial, 
where LLM chatbots failed to outperform expert-written materials for boosting vaccine intent (7). 
One interpretation is that AI may be most persuasive for less familiar or lower-barrier decisions, 
where information gaps are greater and resistance is lower. Stool tests are often less well 
known to patients, creating opportunities for persuasive messaging to fill informational gaps 
(20). In contrast, colonoscopies, while a more established and recommended screening option, 
may carry entrenched beliefs about discomfort or logistical challenges that are less amenable to 
change through brief interventions. These differences were observed despite similar baseline 
levels of intent across the two modalities, suggesting that the disparity is unlikely to be driven 
solely by starting preferences or ceiling effects. 
This study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from an online convenience 
panel and skewed toward White and female participants, which may limit generalizability to 
more diverse populations. Second, the study relied on self-reported intentions rather than 
confirmed screening behavior. However, intent has been shown to correlate with actual CRC 
screening (21). Third, participants were required to engage with the assigned intervention for a 
minimum of three minutes, potentially overestimating the impact of static materials compared to 
naturalistic exposure. Fourth, several of the targeting variables used in the study may be 
infeasible to access in real-world implementation. However, additional variables such as race 
and zip code are typically accessible via electronic health records. 
In conclusion, demographically tailored messages generated by LLMs represent a promising, 
low-touch approach to increasing CRC screening intent, particularly for stool-based tests. These 
messages performed as well as more complex chatbot conversations, suggesting that brief, 
personalized communication may be sufficient to influence health intentions when designed 
thoughtfully. As health systems seek scalable and personalized strategies to address preventive 
care gaps, incorporating LLM-generated messages into outreach efforts may provide a practical 
and impactful tool for promoting evidence-based screening behaviors.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
All study protocols and procedures were approved by the University of Pennsylvania’s 
Institutional Review Board; all participants provided informed consent. All study hypotheses and 
analysis plans were preregistered at https://aspredicted.org/j8c5-388z.pdf. All data were 
collected in Summer 2025.


## Page 8


​
Participants​
Adult participants residing in the United States were recruited through CloudResearch Connect, 
an online platform known to provide high-quality respondent samples for academic research 
(22). Eligibility was determined via a prescreening survey, which identified adults aged 45-75 
who reported never (or being unsure about) having undergone colorectal cancer (CRC) 
screening via colonoscopy, stool test, sigmoidoscopy, or CT colonography. Survey participation 
was compensated at a rate equivalent to 18 USD/hour. Participants who passed the eligibility 
criteria were randomized to one of four arms and invited to the main experiment survey 
consisting of baseline measures, the assigned intervention, and post-intervention measures. 
Baseline Measures​
The experiment survey began with a baseline assessment that reconfirmed eligibility and 
collected a range of participant characteristics. These included demographic information such 
as age, gender, education level, political ideology, residential urbanicity, and self-reported health 
status. Participants also reported their engagement with the healthcare system, including the 
frequency of visits to a primary care provider (PCP) and whether they had previously discussed 
CRC screening with a physician. Trust in AI and health-related entities including the CDC, NIH, 
personal PCP was additionally collected. Intentions to undergo CRC screening were measured 
via self-reported likelihood of completing a colonoscopy and a stool test within the next 12 
months, each on a 0 to 100 scale where “Extremely Unlikely” and 100 was marked “Extremely 
Likely”. Participants also rated their comfort discussing CRC screening with a provider on a 0 to 
100 scale. Reasons for CRC screening hesitancy were assessed using a series of items 
adapted from prior research on screening barriers; each was rated on a 0 to 100 scale, with 0 
labeled “Not important at all” and 100 labeled “Extremely important” (23, 24). An open-ended 
“Other” option allowed participants to report additional concerns. One attention check item was 
included to screen out inattentive responses. 
Intervention​
All participants were presented with a stimulus and required to spend at least three minutes 
reading or engaging with the stimulus. A three-minute session length was chosen to mirror 
realistic, practical constraints for public-health use cases. 
In the expert patient material condition, participants read material from The Journal of the 
American Medical Association’s Patient Page on colorectal cancer screening which was a 642 
word educational summary of the US Preventive Services Task Force's 2021 recommendations 
on screening for colorectal cancer (25). Participants were not informed of the source prior to 
reading. 
In the single AI message condition, participants read a single message written by GPT-4.1. 
GPT-4.1 was instructed to write a persuasive 642 word message for a pamphlet designed to 
persuade someone to get screened for colorectal cancer. The model was provided with each 
participant’s age, gender, education level, political leanings, residential urbanicity, self-reported 
health status, and time since last PCP visit, as well as the full text of the JAMA Patient Page. 
Participants were not informed prior to reading the material that the source was an AI model.


## Page 9


In the no message control condition, participants read a neutral, 642 word fictional short story 
about a cat and dog during a storm. 
In the chatbot condition, participants were informed they would engage in a brief conversation 
with a neutral, non-judgmental AI about a complicated topic. This interaction was implemented 
as a multi-turn exchange with GPT-4.1. The model was prompted to use motivational 
interviewing (MI) techniques to encourage CRC screening. The model was provided the same 
participant-specific characteristics as in the single message condition, as well as a 10-step MI 
roadmap adapted from prior research (26).​
​
Both the chatbot and single-message conditions were implemented using OpenAI’s 
Chat-Completions API, embedded in a Qualtrics Vegapunk widget. No model fine-tuning, 
scripted logic, or additional training data were used. The two AI arms differed only in the format 
and system prompts used. We use OpenAI’s best performing non-reasoning LLM at the time of 
the experiment, GPT-4.1, in order to maximize the potential persuasiveness of the AI conditions 
(15). Full prompt texts and example outputs are available in Appendix Tables S1-S2.​
​
Post-Intervention​
Immediately following the intervention, participants completed a series of outcome measures. 
The two primary outcome were the self-reported likelihood of (1) undergoing a colonoscopy and 
(2) completing a stool test within the next 12 months. Each was assessed separately on a 0-100 
scale where 0 was marked “Extremely unlikely” and 100 was marked “Extremely likely”. 
Secondary outcomes included the perceived empathy of the material or interaction on a 5 point 
scale, perceived effectiveness of the material/conversation (adapted from an existing public 
health message effectiveness scale (27)), and comfort discussing CRC screening with a 
provider (0-100 scale). Participants were also asked to re-rate the reasons for CRC screening 
hesitancy, using the same items and format as in the baseline assessment. An additional 
attention check item was included to detect post-intervention inattentiveness. Lastly, participants 
were asked to write one to two sentences describing why they found their given intervention 
convincing and one to two sentences why their given intervention was not convincing. 
Analyses​
Participants who failed either of the two attention checks or received a bot detection score 
below 0.5 based on an embedded invisible reCAPTCHA were excluded from the analytic 
sample (28). ​
​
Linear regressions with HC2 robust standard errors were used to estimate treatment effects. For 
outcomes with baseline measures, pre-intervention scores were included as covariates. 
Standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were obtained by dividing each HC2-robust coefficient by 
the model’s pooled residual SD, with 95% confidence intervals obtained via the delta method 
using the same robust covariance matrix. 
For robustness, we additionally estimated the primary treatment effect on CRC screening 
intentions using a linear regression that included the full set of measured individual 
characteristics: age (centered), gender (female vs. non-female), race (White vs. non-White),


## Page 10


political ideology (liberal, conservative, moderate), urbanicity, self-reported health (good or 
excellent health vs. not), last time seeing PCP (within the past 2 years vs. not), prior CRC 
screening discussions with a doctor, trust in AI (very much or completely vs not), and trust in the 
NIH, CDC, and personal PCP. To explore potential moderators, we estimated separate 
regression models interacting the treatment variable with each individual characteristic, applying 
Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. 
To further explore intervention effects among participants with initially low intent, we conducted a 
post-hoc exploratory analysis limited to individuals with baseline screening intent ≤ 50. We fit a 
logistic regression model predicting whether participants crossed the 50-point threshold 
post-intervention, using study arm as the predictor and HC2-robust standard errors to compute 
odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. As a robustness check, we additionally fit models 
examining 25 point and 75 point thresholds. 
To understand the reasons behind participants' persuasive responses, one co-author manually 
analyzed open-text explanations provided by participants who found the intervention convincing. 
This analysis was limited to responses from individuals whose post-treatment intention scores 
increased for at least one primary outcome and exceeded a value of 50. Conversely, open-text 
responses from participants who did not find the intervention convincing were also manually 
annotated. These included only those whose post-treatment intention scores were equal to or 
lower than their corresponding pre-treatment scores. All thematic categories derived from these 
manual annotations were subsequently reviewed and validated by a second co-author. 
To characterize the chatbot exchanges, we applied BOLT, a computational framework for 
quantifying motivational-interviewing (MI) behaviors in LLM-mediated dialogues (11). Each 
chatbot-generated turn was parsed through BOLT’s in-context classifiers, which tag 13 
canonical MI moves (e.g., open questions, affirmations, reflections). We then normalized the 
counts by the number of turns to determine the normalized frequency of each code within a 
conversation and derived descriptive distributions for the full chatbot corpus.​
​
Persuasion strategies in the single AI message arm were examined with a hybrid 
deductive–inductive content analysis used in prior work on AI-generated messaging (13). First, 
GPT-4.1 was prompted ten times to list strategies that could persuade an unscreened adult to 
complete colorectal cancer screening. After manual de-duplication, one author iteratively coded 
a random set of messages, adding new categories until no additional tactics emerged, 
producing a 49-item list. Finally, GPT-4.1 was supplied with the finalized strategy list and asked 
to rate each strategy’s presence in every message on a four-point ordinal scale (none, low, 
moderate, high). We then categorized each strategy into one of five manually curated themes 
for interpretation. 
To understand the level of complexity across message formats, we computed the grade level 
required to read each of the chatbot conversations, single AI messages, and expert material 
using the Flesch-Kincaid algorithm.

To assess factual accuracy and clinical appropriateness, two licensed clinicians each reviewed 
a separate set of 25 random chatbot conversations and 25 random single AI messages.

**Why Colorectal Cancer Should Be On Your Radar—Right Now** 
 
You may feel healthy and not have any symptoms. But here’s the reality: colorectal cancer doesn’t always show signs until 
it’s advanced. The rates of this cancer are rising among adults under 50, making screening more important than ever before. 
 
Colorectal cancer refers to cancer of the colon or rectum. Common symptoms, like changes in your bowel habits or 
abdominal pain, often appear late—after the disease has had a chance to develop. This is why early detection is crucial: 
when detected early, colorectal cancer is one of the most treatable forms of cancer. 
 
--- 
 
**The Trusted Recommendations—Backed by Science** 
 
The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, a group of independent experts that reviews the strongest medical evidence, 
recommends that adults start screening for colorectal cancer at age 45. For healthy men like you, waiting any longer only 
increases the risk of missing the window when cancer can be treated most effectively—even prevented altogether. 
 
Studies show that screening adults from ages 50 to 75 can reduce deaths from colorectal cancer significantly. Now, 
gathering even more evidence, experts agree: getting screened beginning at 45 could help save even more lives. 
 
--- 
 
**What Are Your Options? Multiple Effective Tests, One Mission: Keeping You Healthy** 
 
There’s no one-size-fits-all approach. You and your health provider can choose the screening method that’s right for you: 
 
- **Colonoscopy:** A powerful tool that allows doctors to see the inside of your colon and remove abnormal growths before 
they can turn into cancer. 
- **Stool-based tests:** Simple tests you can do at home, like the fecal immunochemical test or stool DNA test, catch early 
signs through a single stool sample. 
- **CT Colonography (Virtual Colonoscopy):** An advanced scan that gives doctors a detailed image of your colon. 
 
The goal isn’t to push you toward any specific test. The goal is to get you screened—your health and peace of mind are too 
important to leave unchecked. 
 
--- 
 
**Addressing Concerns: Risks Are Real, But the Benefits Far Outweigh Them** 
 
As a man who weighs every decision carefully, it’s smart to consider both the benefits and the risks. The main risks from 
screening—like bleeding or infection—are rare, and mostly linked to colonoscopy. For most men your age, the benefit of 
early detection and prevention overwhelms these uncommon risks. And remember, less invasive options are available.
