Automation, AI, and the
Intergenerational Transmission of Knowledge
Enrique Ide*
April 3, 2026
Abstract
Motivated by concerns that AI-driven entry-level automation may deprive new generations
of valuable work experience, this paper studies how technological change affects the intergener-
ational transmission of tacit knowledge—practical, hard-to-codify skills acquired through work-
place interaction. I develop a task-based overlapping-generations model in which novices acquire
tacit knowledge by working alongside experts. Knowledge-transfer contracts are incomplete be-
cause tacit knowledge is embodied and non-veriﬁable. In equilibrium, endogenous growth arises
because only the most knowledgeable experts manage production and transmit their expertise to
multiple novices, diffusing best practices. I show that improvements in entry-level automation
increase output on impact but can reduce growth and welfare, even without reducing entry-level
employment. This occurs when such improvements reallocate novices away from the most pro-
ductive experts, weakening the diffusion of best practices. By contrast, technological improve-
ments that increase the span of control of the most productive experts—such as those that create
new labor-intensive tasks—strengthen knowledge transmission and raise growth.

Introduction
Recent advances in Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) have fueled optimistic forecasts of dramatic increases
in productivity and economic growth. Unlike earlier technological innovations—such as robots or
conventional computers—AI systems can learn and adapt from examples, enabling them to perform
work once considered inherently human (Autor, 2024; Brynjolfsson et al., 2025b; Ide and Talam`as,
2025). As a result, many technologists foresee an economic transformation that could rival or even
surpass that of the Industrial Revolution (Amodei, 2024; Levy, 2025; Altman, 2025).
However, the widespread adoption of AI may carry unintended long-term costs. By allowing se-
nior workers to accomplish more tasks independently, AI-driven automation may reduce entry-level
opportunities and threaten the implicit contract in which younger workers exchange their labor for
training and the prospect of future promotion (Roose, The New York Times, 2025; The Journal Podcast,
The Wall Street Journal, 2025; BloombergTV, Bloomberg.com, 2025). Such arrangements have histori-
cally been crucial for transmitting tacit knowledge—practical insights that cannot be fully articulated
yet remain indispensable for carrying out complex work—raising concerns about how future gener-
ations will acquire essential expertise (Beane, 2024a,b; Garicano, 2025).
Motivated by these concerns, this paper develops a framework for analyzing how different forms
of technological change—automation, task creation, and labor- and capital-augmenting technologies—
reshape the intergenerational transmission of tacit knowledge and, through that channel, long-run
growth. The central premise is that technological change affects current and future productivity not
only by changing how tasks are performed, but also by changing who works with whom and, there-
fore, who learns from whom.
Existing research on automation and AI has mainly focused on how new technologies directly af-
fect productivity, labor-market outcomes, and the distribution of income and wealth (e.g. Autor et al.,
2003; Acemoglu and Autor, 2011; Moll et al., 2022; Acemoglu et al., 2024; Autor and Thompson, 2025;
Ide and Talam`as, 2025). However, much less attention has been paid to how these innovations re-
shape the interpersonal interactions through which valuable workplace skills are transmitted. This
paper addresses that gap by embedding technological change within a growth model of knowledge
diffusion and learning (e.g., Lucas, 2009; Lucas and Moll, 2014; Perla and Tonetti, 2014; De la Croix et al.,
2018). In doing so, it provides a uniﬁed framework for analyzing how new technologies jointly affect
current output, tacit knowledge transmission, and long-run growth.
The baseline model features overlapping generations, with individuals living for two periods:
ﬁrst as novices, then as experts. Novices are identical at birth, have no wealth, and supply one
unit of labor inelastically. Experts, by contrast, differ in skill. This skill level is observable, but the
knowledge underlying it is tacit. Outsiders can therefore assess an expert’s productivity, but the
insights and processes through which it is achieved are accessible only through close interaction.
Production combines expert skill with routine tasks that can be performed either by novices or by
machines. Machines are more productive than novices, but can perform only a subset of tasks; the
rest must be carried out by novices. Because each task requires a novice’s full unit of labor, production
gives rise to many-to-one matching: each novice works for exactly one expert, while each expert hires

multiple novices. By working alongside an expert, a novice gains intimate access to that expert’s tacit
knowledge and enters the next period with the same skill level as her mentor.
Since future earnings rise with mentor skill, novices are willing to accept lower wages to work
alongside more skilled experts. However, because expert knowledge is tacit, it cannot be fully artic-
ulated and is therefore non-veriﬁable. Moreover, because it is tied to personal experience and thus
embodied in the individual, it cannot be seized or repossessed. Consequently, novices cannot borrow
against future human capital to pay for access to better mentors. Lacking initial wealth, they face a
zero lower bound on wages. Concurrent labor thus becomes the only way to compensate experts for
the opportunity to learn, giving rise to an apprenticeship-like arrangement.
In equilibrium, all automatable tasks are assigned to machines, and each expert cohort is par-
titioned endogenously by skill. The lowest-skilled experts are inactive because hiring novices at
equilibrium wages is unproﬁtable.1 Above them lies an active region, which itself splits into two:
a price-cleared tier in which experts hire novices at strictly positive wages, and an oversubscribed tier
at the top, where the zero lower bound binds and wages can no longer clear the market for those
positions.2 This endogenous partition then governs the evolution of expertise over time: the next
generation’s skill distribution is obtained by truncating the current distribution at the marginal active
expert and reweighting the surviving upper tail by experts’ span of control—the number of novices
each active expert employs.
When the initial distribution of expert skill is fat-tailed,3 this process generates endogenous asymp-
totic growth. The most productive experts replicate their skills by passing them on to multiple
novices, while the fat upper tail prevents the pool of mentors from becoming too sparse as the econ-
omy moves to higher skill levels. The asymptotic growth rate of output is therefore determined by
experts’ span of control and the tail thickness of the initial skill distribution. Furthermore, once skills
are normalized relative to the least-skilled expert in each cohort, the cross-sectional distribution of
skills converges asymptotically to a Pareto law.
I then use the framework to study several technological shocks, beginning with automation. I ﬁrst
show that expanding the set of automatable tasks can raise output on impact, but can also—through
the displacement of novices from high-skill mentors—reduce subsequent output growth. The imme-
diate output gain reﬂects the reallocation of routine tasks from novices to more productive machines.
At the same time, as active experts hire fewer novices, displaced novices are reallocated toward
lower-skill experts, allowing a new tier of inactive experts to become active. Some novices are there-
fore matched with worse mentors, acquire lower-quality tacit knowledge, and later become worse
mentors themselves. The best practices of the leading experts thus diffuse more slowly, imposing a

Through this displacement channel, the automation shock can reduce aggregate welfare, deﬁned
as the discounted sum of the income of current and future generations. This inefﬁciency stems from
the zero lower bound on wages. In a frictionless environment, novices would borrow against fu-

shock makes expertise more valuable. Instead, novices cannot directly pay oversubscribed experts
for access, so those experts fail to internalize the full social value of mentorship, and automation be-
comes socially excessive. The zero lower bound thus forces novices to ﬁnance elite mentorship solely
through their labor, the very currency that automation devalues.
Displacement, however, operates alongside a countervailing force. Improvements in automation
can also expand experts’ scale of operation by reducing the supervision time required to complete a
given production process. If this scale effect dominates, the overall span of control of the very best
experts rises even if each production process requires fewer novices. The automation shock then
accelerates the diffusion of best practices and raises long-run growth. Even so, at least in these early
stages of adoption, the preliminary evidence on generative AI discussed below seems more consistent
with displacement than with offsetting scale effects. This may be because current AI automation
is still “so-so” (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019), generating limited time savings, or because expert
supervision is constrained by hard cognitive or attentional limits.
Beyond automation, the same logic applies to other forms of technological change. Technologies
that create new labor-intensive tasks raise output on impact and increase long-run growth, even
without scale effects. They do so by inducing the most skilled experts to hire more novices, thereby
accelerating the diffusion of best practices. By contrast, technologies that raise the productivity of
novices or machines in the tasks they already perform increase long-run growth only if they expand
experts’ scale of operation.
Taken together, these results imply that the net effect of new technologies—especially general-
purpose ones—on knowledge transmission is theoretically ambiguous. The same technology can set
multiple, often competing, forces in motion: it can automate some tasks, create new ones, augment
labor and capital in existing tasks, and, under some conditions, expand experts’ scale of operation.
The model nevertheless yields a simple organizing principle: what matters for knowledge transmis-
sion is whether a technology expands or contracts the span of control of the most skilled experts.
Admittedly, this result rests on stark simplifying assumptions, most notably perfect skill inheritance.
Still, the central idea remains: the span of control of leading experts is a useful summary measure of
a technology’s effect on the diffusion of best practices across generations.
While applicable to many technologies, these results are especially relevant to the recent advent
of generative AI. Early evidence points to a weakening of entry-level hiring pipelines in AI-exposed,
knowledge-intensive occupations, precisely where the transmission of tacit skill is most important
(Berger et al., 2024; Hosseini and Lichtinger, 2025; Brynjolfsson et al., 2025a; Klein Teeselink, 2025;

taking positions at smaller, mid-tier ﬁrms that previously struggled to attract such talent (Ellis, 2026;

In this sense, the evidence so far suggests that current iterations of generative AI may be a form

stitutes for entry-level execution while complementing expert judgment. Holding expertise ﬁxed,
such a change is unambiguously beneﬁcial. But expertise is endogenous, and its intergenerational
transmission is governed by incomplete contracts. As a result, seniority-biased technological change
can ultimately erode the future supply of the very expertise it complements. Later in the paper, I
offer tentative back-of-the-envelope calculations to assess whether this erosion may be quantitatively
relevant, and in the Final Remarks, I discuss several policy implications.
1.1
Related Literature
This paper bridges three strands of literature. It connects the task-based literature on technology and
labor demand with the literature on idea ﬂows and economic growth. It does so by examining how
new technologies reshape the workplace interactions through which tacit skills are transmitted across
generations. It also contributes to the literature on the economics of apprenticeships. In particular, it
shows how technological change can reshape early-career learning and, through that channel, affect
aggregate productivity and long-run growth.
The task-based approach to technology and labor demand was developed by Zeira (1998), Autor et al.
(2003), and Acemoglu and Autor (2011). In this framework, production requires the completion of a
range of tasks allocated across factors of production according to comparative advantage and avail-
able technologies. Technological change therefore affects labor demand, wages, inequality, and pro-
ductivity by altering who performs which tasks and at what cost (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2018,
2019, 2022; Acemoglu et al., 2024). Building on this approach, Autor and Thompson (2025) model
occupations as bundles of tasks and shows that automation can be expertise-leveling, reducing occu-
pational expertise requirements in a way standard task-based models do not capture.5
More recently, Ide and Talam`as (2024, 2025, 2026) build on the knowledge-hierarchies literature of
Garicano (2000) and Garicano and Rossi-Hansberg (2004, 2006) to study AI’s distinctive implications.

I contribute to the broader task-based literature on technological change by studying how dif-
ferent forms of technological change reshape the intergenerational transmission of tacit knowledge
and, thus, future productivity and growth. My model builds more directly on the canonical task-
based treatment of technological change. However, it also draws inspiration from the knowledge-
hierarchies literature in emphasizing the economic importance of tacit knowledge and the role of
organizations in determining who works with whom.
A related emerging literature examines the effects of AI on learning and knowledge formation.
Kosmyna et al. (2025) provide neuroscientiﬁc evidence that reliance on AI for essay writing reduces
cognitive engagement and attenuates neural connectivity. More closely related, Acemoglu et al. (2026)
develop a dynamic model in which agentic AI substitutes for human learning effort and can erode the
stock of public knowledge, potentially generating a knowledge-collapse steady state. While these pa-
pers focus on how AI affects learning through cognition or learning effort, I study how new technolo-
gies reshape who learns from whom in production, thereby altering the diffusion of tacit practices
across workers and over time. My focus is therefore on mentor composition, incomplete contracts,
and long-run growth.
The literature on idea ﬂows and economic growth emphasizes how knowledge diffuses through
interactions among individuals and ﬁrms, shaping productivity and growth (Kortum, 1997; Eaton and Kortum,
1999, 2002; Lucas, 2009; Lucas and Moll, 2014).6 My paper contributes to this literature by studying
how technological change reshapes who learns from whom in production, and thereby weakens or
accelerates the diffusion of tacit practices, a margin absent from existing models.
In the context of idea ﬂows and economic growth, the papers closest to mine are Perla and Tonetti
(2014), De la Croix et al. (2018), and Caicedo et al. (2019). In both my paper and Perla and Tonetti
(2014), long-run growth arises from the endogenous evolution of a fat-tailed productivity distribution
as better practices diffuse from high- to low-productivity types. In Perla and Tonetti (2014), however,
diffusion comes through anonymous imitation: low-productivity ﬁrms upgrade by drawing a new
productivity level from the distribution of producing ﬁrms. In my model, in contrast, diffusion is
workplace-based: novices learn by working with experts. This distinction introduces a scarce-mentor
bottleneck and gives technological change a central role in reshaping who learns from whom.

Conceptual Foundations
This section outlines the conceptual foundations of the analysis: the tacit dimension of expertise, the
inherent constraints on its transfer, and the mechanics of its transmission across generations. Section
3 formalizes these concepts to analyze how technological shocks impact long-term productivity and
economic growth.
The Tacit Dimension of Expertise.— Codiﬁable knowledge refers to explicit rules and procedures that
can be easily transmitted via manuals, books, and databases. Tacit knowledge, in contrast, encom-
passes intuitive skills and insights that are difﬁcult to articulate precisely (Polanyi, 1966; Foray, 2004).
These two forms of knowledge are interdependent: codiﬁed instructions usually require tacit knowl-
edge to be properly interpreted and adapted to real-world complexities. As Mokyr (2002, pp. 14-15)
explains:
Tacit knowledge is needed to obtain inexpensive, reliable access to codiﬁed instructions
[. . . ] no set of instructions [...] can ever be complete. It would be too expensive to write
7


## Page 8


a complete set of instructions for every technique. Judgement, dexterity, experience, and
other forms of tacit knowledge inevitably come into play when technique is executed.
While professional capability relies on both forms of knowledge, codiﬁable elements can be stan-
dardized and scaled. This paper therefore focuses on the tacit dimension. Hereafter, I use “exper-
tise” and “tacit knowledge” interchangeably to refer to the unwritten insights required to execute
and orchestrate well-deﬁned tasks. In investment banking, for instance, populating ﬁnancial models
and verifying due diligence documents are largely standardized procedures. However, transforming
their completion into ﬁnancial advice requires the judgment to select appropriate models, identify
anomalies that standard procedures might overlook, and adjust methods when unforeseen contin-
gencies arise.
Because tacit knowledge is tied to personal experience, it is embodied in the individual (Polanyi,
1966; Garicano, 2000). Thus, while tacit insights can be transferred, their transmission requires the
expert’s direct involvement. This makes the expert’s time a key bottleneck to the use and diffusion
of this knowledge (Foray, 2004; Garicano and Rossi-Hansberg, 2015; Ide and Talam`as, 2025).
Labor as a Mechanism and Currency for Acquiring Tacit Knowledge.— Lacking a codiﬁable structure, tacit
knowledge must be acquired experientially through active execution and collaboration with more
experienced individuals. As Polanyi (1962, p. 55) observes:
An art which cannot be speciﬁed in detail cannot be transmitted by prescription [...] It can
be passed on only by example from master to apprentice [...] By watching the master and
emulating his efforts, the apprentice unconsciously picks up the rules of the art, including
those which are not explicitly known to the master himself.
Thus, labor supplied alongside an expert serves a dual role: it is both a productive input and
a mechanism for assimilating tacit knowledge (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Brown and Duguid, 1991;
Beane, 2019). This apprenticeship model is pervasive in expertise-intensive professions (e.g., medicine,
law, ﬁnance), where novices perform routine tasks under expert supervision, observing how the
expert applies judgment to integrate these inputs into a cohesive whole (Garicano and Rayo, 2017;
Beane, 2024b).
Independent trial-and-error can also help acquire these tacit skills, but remains an inefﬁcient sub-
stitute for mentorship. This is evident in white-collar entry-level labor markets, where novices rou-
tinely accept demanding hours and low effective pay to work alongside top practitioners.7 While
this phenomenon could also reﬂect other mechanisms—such as novices trying to acquire a presti-
gious signal of high ability—Oyer (2006) and Arellano-Bover (2024) support knowledge acquisition
as the primary driver. Both studies provide causal evidence that initial placement at elite institutions

The contracts governing the transfer of tacit knowledge, however, are inherently incomplete (Mokyr,
2019). Because such expertise resists precise articulation, it is non-veriﬁable. Moreover, because it is
embodied within the individual, it cannot be seized or repossessed. Consequently, novices cannot
credibly pledge future human capital to ﬁnance this type of training (Becker, 1964; Garicano and Rayo,
2017). Concurrent labor thus remains the only viable means of payment.
Intergenerational Transmission of Tacit Knowledge.— Because tacit knowledge is embodied in individu-
als and shared primarily through joint production, its preservation requires the continual renewal of
practitioners (Foray, 2004). The transmission of tacit knowledge thus operates as an intergenerational
cycle: novices acquire this experiential knowledge by working alongside experts and subsequently
train the next cohort. When novices learn from the most productive experts, this mechanism diffuses
best practices over time, driving aggregate productivity growth.
As the historical embodiment of this dynamic, apprenticeship systems have functioned as cen-
tral engines for economic growth.
Humphries (2006), for example, identiﬁes England’s appren-
ticeship system as a “neglected factor” underpinning the British Industrial Revolution. Similarly,
De la Croix et al. (2018) argue that European apprenticeship institutions signiﬁcantly contributed to
the continent’s relative rise in technological creativity, population growth, and per-capita income in
the centuries preceding the Industrial Revolution.
Together, the features outlined above characterize a distinctive environment that is central to
macroeconomic outcomes. Moreover, by altering the conditions in which learning occurs—and the
value of the labor used to pay for it—technological advances can affect skill accumulation, impacting
both current and future productivity. Section 3 develops a model to study these issues.

First, the production process features a strict division of labor between execution and judgment:
novices and machines perform routine tasks, while experts apply tacit skills. This structure mirrors
the reality of knowledge-intensive industries, where early-career professionals (and, more recently,
AI) execute well-structured tasks, and senior practitioners apply judgment to integrate these inputs
into a cohesive whole. While imposing this exogenous division of labor simpliﬁes the baseline anal-

orchestrate production or supply routine labor.
Second, the assumption that tasks are non-scalable reﬂects experts’ limited time and attention:
each expert can oversee only the measure N of tasks required for a single production process.10
This formalizes a core principle of Section 2: because expertise is embodied in the individual, its
application and transmission cannot be easily scaled. In investment banking, for instance, a senior
partner can oversee only a ﬁnite number of concurrent transactions, regardless of skill. Admittedly,
this ﬁxed-capacity assumption is stark: it rules out scale-of-operation effects. Section 6 relaxes this
restriction and examines the implications of the resulting scale effects.
Third, the model assumes that joint production is necessary for skill acquisition. By executing
routine tasks, a novice gains intimate insight into how the expert transforms these inputs into output,
unconsciously learning “the rules of the art.” As a ﬁrst approximation, I abstract from the ﬁner details
of this process, assuming that all routine tasks provide uniform insight into the expert’s methods
and that novices perfectly inherit their mentor’s skill. To capture skill development beyond direct
mentorship, Online Appendix V also extends the framework to incorporate independent innovation.

for training (wt(q) ≥0). Rather than explicitly microfounding the contractual frictions that preclude

Proposition 3 further implies that the asymptotic distribution of normalized skills depends solely
on the initial tail parameter θ, independent of preferences and technology. Consequently, while
changes in technology or preferences alter the long-run growth rate (Proposition 2) and asymptotic
price schedules (as shown below), the long-run relative distribution of talent remains unchanged.

As m →h, the scale effect vanishes (∂M/∂I →0), and displacement entirely drives the impact on

Second, the displacement effect dominates when task supervision is highly interdependent (high
η). As η →∞, the CES time aggregator converges to a Leontief technology. In this limit, tasks
are supervised in parallel, so total project time is dictated entirely by the slowest inputs—the human
novices. Consequently, even if machines are substantially more productive than novices (m ≫h), the
expert cannot reallocate the time saved by the automation improvement. The scale effect is therefore
negligible, and the automation shock unambiguously reduces the expert’s span of control.
By contrast, when the scale effect dominates, improvements in entry-level automation accelerate
long-run growth. In this case, the expert’s expanded scale of operation outweighs the displacement
of novices per project. Top experts hire a larger aggregate measure of novices, accelerating the dif-
fusion of best practices. The macroeconomic effects of the shock thus mirror those of a task-creating
technology, even without introducing new labor-intensive tasks. As the evidence reviewed in Section
5.1 suggests, however, the empirically salient margin so far appears to be displacement rather than
expert scaling.
Labor- and Capital-Augmenting Technologies.— Beyond automating and creating tasks, AI tools can
increase the productivity of novices and machines at existing tasks. Consistent with labor augmen-
tation, Brynjolfsson et al. (2025b) document that AI assistants lead to a 15% increase in the resolution
rate of customer-support agents by providing real-time response suggestions. For capital augmen-
tation, de Souza (2025) reports that AI-powered maintenance can reduce unplanned machine down-
time by 50% through analysis of real-time sensor data.
Formally, labor- and capital-augmenting technologies correspond to exogenous increases in h and
m, respectively. In the baseline model without scale effects, these improvements generate a one-time
level effect on aggregate output but leave the experts’ span of control—and thus long-run growth—
unchanged. Scale effects alter this result. By reducing task execution time, both technologies lower
total supervision time per project. Experts therefore oversee more concurrent projects, accelerating
knowledge transmission and output growth.24
Span of Control as a Sufﬁcient Statistic.— As the preceding discussion shows, new technologies can
set multiple, often competing, forces in motion: they can automate tasks, create new labor-intensive
ones, and augment labor and capital in existing tasks. Furthermore, even pure automation can ac-
celerate the diffusion of tacit knowledge if the resulting time savings enable experts to scale their
operations. Consequently, the net effect of a new technology on knowledge transmission is theoreti-
cally ambiguous.
The model nevertheless yields a simple organizing principle: what matters for knowledge trans-
mission is whether a technology expands or contracts the span of control of the most skilled experts.

for a technology’s net effect on the intergenerational transmission of tacit knowledge.

A Quantitative Illustration
Intertemporal Tradeoff and the Break-Even Change in the Span of Control.— The preliminary evidence in
Section 5.1 suggests generative AI is displacing novices in AI-exposed sectors. According to Proposi-
tion 4, this displacement yields immediate output gains but compromises future output by disrupting
the transmission of tacit knowledge.
A full structural estimation of this effect is beyond the scope of this paper. It is nevertheless in-
structive to ask whether this intertemporal tradeoff operates at a quantitatively relevant scale. To this
end, I carry out a simple back-of-the-envelope calculation. Taking recent macroeconomic projections
of the one-off output gains from AI adoption as given, I compute the reduction in the experts’ net
span of control required to completely dissipate these gains after exactly one generation.
Importantly, this exercise does not take a stance on whether generative AI will ultimately acceler-

competing forces in motion. Rather, the calculation is conditional: assuming AI creates displacement,
how severe must the contraction in mentorship be to completely offset the projected adoption gains
in just one generation, and is this required reduction empirically plausible?

An External Validity Check.—The derived break-even thresholds depend on projected AI adoption
gains and the tail parameter θ of the skill distribution. While adoption gains are hypothetical, θ can
be empirically disciplined. Standard calibrations set θ = 0.5 to match the cross-sectional dispersion
of earnings. To verify whether this imported parameter yields reasonable predictions in the present
setting, I substitute empirical estimates of the span of control into the model’s growth equation and
compare the implied growth rates with historical U.S. data.
In the model, the generational growth factor of output is ℓθ. Letting T denote the number of years
an individual spends in each stage—ﬁrst as a novice, then as an expert—the annualized growth rate
of output is ℓθ/T −1. To evaluate this rate, I draw on Caicedo et al. (2019), setting T = 22 to match

(for ℓ= 1.31). For an empirical benchmark, FRED data indicate that U.S. real GDP per capita grew

average of 2% U.S. per-capita growth—the benchmark used by Lucas (2009), Lucas and Moll (2014),
and Caicedo et al. (2019)—the model accounts for 31% to 51% of aggregate growth.

The Threat of Stagnation.— In the baseline model, strictly positive asymptotic growth requires experts’
span of control to exceed unity (N −I > 1). Should a technological shock drive this span below one,
the intergenerational transmission of knowledge breaks down, and long-run growth disappears.
Additional back-of-the-envelope calculations yield a rough estimate of the shock strength required
to induce stagnation. Given the empirical span of control estimated by Caicedo et al. (2019), driving
the post-shock span to the stagnation boundary in the model without innovation (˜ℓ≤1) requires a
proportional reduction of 1−1/ℓ. This implies a 36% reduction from the 1990 estimate (ℓ= 1.56) and
a 24% reduction from the 2010 estimate (ℓ= 1.31).
As shown in the Online Appendix, allowing growth to stem from both the diffusion of tacit knowl-
edge and the generation of new tacit ideas makes the economy even more resilient to stagnation:
reaching the stagnation boundary requires a 49.4% contraction relative to the 1990 estimate and a
39.7% contraction relative to the 2010 estimate. These calculations suggest that completely halting
long-run growth requires an exceptionally severe disruption.
6.3
Further Considerations
Relaxing the Strict Division of Labor.— The baseline framework imposes a strict division of labor: ex-
perts exclusively apply tacit knowledge as orchestrators, while novices execute routine tasks. This
assumption forces the least-skilled experts in any cohort into inactivity, yielding zero second-period
income. Suppose instead that experts face an endogenous occupational choice between acting as or-
chestrators and supplying one unit of routine labor.25 Although this ﬂexibility changes within-cohort
allocations, the core macroeconomic results remain unchanged.
Experts’ endogenous occupational choice induces sorting by age. Facing a zero continuation value,
older low-skilled experts (who would otherwise be inactive) maximize static payoffs by supplying
routine labor to the lowest-skilled orchestrators, who pay the highest upfront wages to compensate
for inferior mentorship. Forward-looking novices, in contrast, value skill acquisition and outbid
these older workers for elite orchestrators. The intergenerational transmission of the upper tail is
thus preserved.
With novices still matching with top experts, the aggregate effects of technological shocks parallel
the baseline, but now operate through a different extensive margin: occupational switching between
orchestrating and supplying routine labor. An automation shock shrinks the span of control of elite

orchestrators, displacing novices toward lower-skill experts. To clear the market, marginal experts
who would otherwise supply routine labor become orchestrators. This worsens mentor composition
and permanently slows growth. Conversely, a task-creating technology expands the span of control
of elite orchestrators and reallocates novices toward higher-skill orchestrators. This improves mentor
composition and accelerates long-run growth.
More on Contract Incompleteness.— As discussed in Section 3.1, the zero lower bound on wages serves
as a reduced-form proxy for the contract incompleteness inherent in the transfer of tacit knowledge.
This formulation avoids taking a stance on the speciﬁc friction at work and naturally generates the
rationing of entry-level positions observed at elite professional services ﬁrms (Section 4.3).
At the same time, treating this bound as exogenous abstracts from the possibility that technological
change itself alters the severity of the underlying contractual frictions. A richer framework would
endogenize that interaction. Whether it attenuates or ampliﬁes the forces emphasized here remains
an important question for future research.26
More on Knowledge Transmission.— The baseline model assumes that all routine tasks provide uniform
insight into the expert’s tacit knowledge. I abstract from task heterogeneity because its equilibrium
implications are straightforward. Automating menial tasks that offer no meaningful interaction with
the mentor increases production efﬁciency without hindering knowledge transmission—and may
even enhance it via the scale effect discussed above.
The framework further assumes that novices perfectly acquire their mentor’s skill. Given its un-
codiﬁable nature, the transmission of tacit knowledge is probably noisy: apprentices may fall short of
or surpass their mentor’s expertise by drawing on their own insights. Moreover, changes in commu-
nication technologies and other workplace trends—such as remote work—may endogenously alter
the extent of this transmission. Extending the model along these lines may be a fruitful direction for
future research.
7
Final Remarks
I conclude by summarizing the paper’s key insights, discussing their policy implications, and point-
ing to additional technological possibilities that the framework can help analyze.
The Main Takeaways.— The analysis rests on two premises. First, early-career work serves not only
as an input into current production but also as a central channel for transmitting tacit knowledge
across generations. Second, because tacit knowledge is embodied and non-veriﬁable, the contracts
governing its transfer are inherently incomplete.

compresses the training window and limits the surplus experts can extract, a concurrent increase in the future value of
expertise can offset this reduction by relaxing the novice’s no-quit constraint.

Together, these premises imply that technological change affects the intergenerational transmis-
sion of tacit knowledge by reshaping who works with whom and, therefore, who learns from whom.
They also imply that, because knowledge-transfer contracts are incomplete, technological improve-
ments that displace novices from the most-skilled experts weaken the diffusion of best practices and
can reduce aggregate welfare.
Policy Implications.— The contractual incompleteness inherent in the transmission of tacit knowledge
implies that technological improvements need not be socially efﬁcient, creating scope for policy in-
tervention. At the same time, the analysis shows that new technologies can set multiple, often com-
peting, forces in motion. The policy challenge is to harness the gains from technological progress
without undermining the intergenerational transmission of tacit knowledge.
The model offers three broad insights for addressing this challenge. First, tools that automate
entry-level tasks deserve particular scrutiny, since they are most likely to disrupt the diffusion of
tacit knowledge—a prerequisite for welfare losses in the model. Second, whether that risk materi-
alizes depends on the extent to which automation also generates scale effects. A useful indicator is
therefore whether the span of control of top experts expands or contracts following the technology’s
introduction. Third, when a technology is deemed inefﬁcient, policy should focus on expanding
novices’ access to high-quality mentorship. This may warrant policies that lower the cost of hiring
young workers into entry-level roles—such as hiring subsidies or greater minimum-wage ﬂexibility
for young workers27—as well as taxes on entry-level automation.
Additional Technological Possibilities.— The analysis has focused on more conventional technological
improvements—such as automation, task creation, and factor augmentation—that affect the creation
and execution of tasks. AI, however, may differ from earlier technological changes in its ability to
encode and apply tacit knowledge at scale (Ide and Talam`as, 2025). This raises the possibility that
AI could democratize expertise either by democratizing performance—narrowing the productivity gap
between experts—or by democratizing learning—facilitating novices’ acquisition of tacit knowledge
through exposure to a broad range of examples and on-demand interactive guidance.28
These possibilities point to additional forms of technological change that may shape the intergen-
erational transmission of tacit knowledge and the path of aggregate productivity. Analyzing them is
a natural direction for future research, and the framework developed here provides a useful starting
point.

III
Unobservable Experts’ Skills
The baseline model assumes an expert’s skill level q is perfectly observable. To evaluate the role of this
assumption, consider the opposite scenario where skill levels are completely unobservable. Unable
to distinguish highly productive mentors from marginal ones, novices can no longer strategically
direct their search, rendering the matching process random. Their optimization problem becomes
trivial: they simply supply their single unit of labor inelastically. Because novices cannot target elite
experts, all active experts must therefore pay a single, uniform wage wt.
Experts continue to observe their own skills. Under Assumption 2, they fully automate, producing
if and only if S(q) ≥wt(N −I). Because S(q) is strictly increasing, the active set remains an upper
interval [at, ∞), with the marginal expert’s zero-proﬁt condition yielding the equilibrium wage wt =
S(at)/(N −I). Since every active expert continues to hire exactly N −I novices, the aggregate market-
clearing condition is unchanged. Thus, the unobservability of skill alters the distribution of rents
but leaves the sequence of cutoffs {at}t≥0, the evolution of the skill distribution, and the aggregate
output and welfare paths unaffected. The macroeconomic implications of the technological advances
analyzed in Section 5 of the main text therefore remain exactly the same.
At the microeconomic level, however, the unobservability of skill changes fundamental equilib-
rium properties. First, the zero lower bound on wages never binds. Because novices cannot direct
their search toward high-skill mentors, top experts can no longer extract the value of their training
through lower wages. This same reason explains why the rationing and oversubscription of top

Second, although the equilibrium remains equally inefﬁcient, the root cause of this inefﬁciency
shifts from a ﬁnancial friction to an informational friction. In the baseline model, elite experts cannot
fully appropriate the value of the tacit knowledge they transmit because wages cannot adjust below
zero. Under unobservability, they fail to capture this value because the informational friction entirely
eliminates the ability to implicitly price training through a lower wage.

The baseline model assumes the initial skill distribution F0 has an unbounded upper tail and that
joint production is the sole mechanism for skill acquisition. I relax both assumptions by introducing
exogenous expert innovation. This shows that the core results do not require an initial distribution
with unbounded support, nor joint production as the sole source of skill growth.
I modify the baseline environment in two ways. First, F0 now has bounded support. Second, prior

V
Innovation-Driven Growth
In the “Plato’s Realm of Forms” speciﬁcation of Online Appendix IV, the latent knowledge pool
determines the shape of the skill distribution, but long-run growth is driven entirely by the diffusion
of this knowledge. New ideas fail to generate growth because the latent distribution Ωdoes not
change over time. Thus, as the active skill threshold diverges, draws from Ωbecome asymptotically
irrelevant.
For innovation to contribute to asymptotic growth, the latent pool of ideas must evolve in tandem
with experts’ skills, allowing successive cohorts to draw superior ideas by “standing on the shoulders
of giants.” One way to operationalize this is to assume that, while the initial pool Ω0(q) is exogenous
(and 1 −Ω0 ∈RV−1/θ), the pool available to subsequent cohorts, Ωt, matches the distribution of
effective practices used by the active experts of the preceding generation. This appendix formalizes
this extension.
Unlike in the baseline model, however, this speciﬁcation makes the law of motion for the skill
distribution nonlinear in the distribution itself. For that reason, I do not attempt here to establish
the existence of an asymptotic balanced growth path, nor convergence to it from arbitrary initial
conditions. Instead, I characterize the properties of any such path, if it exists.
