Left Leaning Models: How AI Evaluates Economic Policy?  
Maxim Chupilkin1 
Abstract 
Would artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) cut interest rates or adopt conservative monetary policy? Would 
it deregulate or opt for a more controlled economy? As AI use by economic policymakers, 
academics, and market participants grows exponentially, it is becoming critical to understand AI 
preferences over economic policy. However, these preferences are not yet systematically evaluated 
and remain a black box. This paper makes a conjoint experiment on leading large language models 
(LLMs) from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, asking them to evaluate economic policy under 
multi-factor constraints. The results are remarkably consistent across models: most LLMs exhibit 
a strong preference for high growth, low unemployment, and low inequality over traditional 
macroeconomic concerns such as low inﬂation and low public debt. Scenario-speciﬁc experiments 
show that LLMs are sensitive to context but still display strong preferences for low unemployment 
and low inequality even in monetary-policy settings. Numerical sensitivity tests reveal intuitive 
responses to quantitative changes but also uncover non-linear patterns such as loss aversion.

In 2025, it became known that Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel is using artiﬁcial intelligence 
(AI) to check whether his speeches are perceived as dovish or hawkish (Reuters 2025). Bank of 
England Governor Andrew Bailey relies on AI to reduce the length of his speeches (The Times 
2025). Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook has said that the Fed uses AI for “writing, coding, 
and research” (Cook 2025). The Swedish Prime Minister uses ChatGPT and LeChat to obtain 
advice on policy (Bryant 2025). The UK Secretary for Science and Technology uses AI for 
guidance on work and media appearances (Stacey 2025). Albania has gone even further, appointing 
a large language model (LLM) as a minister of public procurement (Delauney 2025). The OECD 
has released a report on using AI for policy evaluation and design, highlighting examples from 
government departments in France and Indonesia that are already integrating AI into day-to-day 
policymaking (OECD 2025). 
The use of LLMs, the most common form of AI today, is rapidly proliferating among economic 
policymakers. LLMs are often used as both formal and informal policy evaluators and as close 
advisors to whom policymakers can reach out at any time. The main concern is that LLMs are by 
their nature a black box, and the assumptions and biases embedded deep in the models remain 
unknown. With the increasing use of LLMs in economics, there is a growing need to understand 
the deep economic assumptions inherent in the recommendations provided by these models. This 
paper addresses this gap by using a conjoint experiment, a method widely used in political science 
(Hainmueller et al. 2014) and marketing (Green and Rao 1971), to infer the deeper assumptions 
behind LLMs’ evaluations of economic policy.  
The paper presents ﬁve LLMs from OpenAI (GPT-5 and GPT-5 Mini), Anthropic (Claude Sonnet 
4.5 and Claude Haiku 4.5), and Google (Gemini 2.0 Flash and Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite) with the task 
of evaluating a scenario involving the approval of an economic policy. Each model is given the 
scenario 1,280 times, with information varied across the impact on economic growth, inequality, 
inﬂation, unemployment, debt, environmental pollution, and ﬁnancial stability. The regression 
analysis then identiﬁes which factors play the strongest role in shaping LLMs’ evaluations. 
On average, the models give a conservative evaluation of economic policy at 40/100, with a fairly 
similar distribution across models, ranging from 35 for Anthropic Claude Sonnet to 51 for Google 
Gemini 2.0 Lite. In terms of factors driving the models’ evaluations, the three primary factors are 
economic growth, unemployment, and inequality. These are followed by ﬁnancial stability and 
pollution. Traditional macroeconomic concerns such as inﬂation and public debt are the least 
important factors for all models. The results are remarkably consistent across models, with a 
Spearman rank correlation coeﬀicient of 0.857 for the relative importance of diﬀerent factors.

I then focus on OpenAI GPT-5 to conduct deeper tests of LLM sensitivity. I focus on GPT-5 
because ChatGPT remains the most widely used LLM, with almost six billion monthly website 
visits (Fischer 2025). I test whether LLMs are sensitive to context in economic policy by modifying 
a vignette and adding context on the policy domain. I separately test ﬁscal, monetary, tax, 
regulatory, industrial, and international trade policy scenarios. On average, the model gives 
diﬀerent policies similar evaluations, with a median score of 40 across all scenarios. However, the 
relative importance of diﬀerent factors is sensitive to the scenario: inﬂation is most important in 
the monetary policy scenario, inequality in the tax policy scenario, and pollution in the regulatory 
policy scenario. Yet within scenarios, the hierarchy is preserved, with unemployment and inequality 
remaining paradoxically more important than inﬂation even in the monetary policy scenario. 
I then test the numerical sensitivity of LLMs by varying the magnitude of each macroeconomic 
shock. OpenAI GPT-5 responds intuitively, with higher unemployment, inﬂation, public debt, or 
negative shocks to growth all reducing approval. Unemployment overwhelmingly dominates the 
negative domain: a 3-percentage-point increase lowers approval by nearly 40 points—about twice 
the penalty from an equivalent rise in inﬂation or fall in economic growth, and far larger than the 
eﬀect of higher public debt. By contrast, economic growth becomes the leading variable in the 
positive domain, with a 3-percentage-point increase raising approval more strongly than a 
comparable improvement in unemployment. The model also exhibits pronounced loss aversion 
consistent with prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 2013). Finally, the variables are treated 
asymmetrically across domains: decreases in inﬂation and public debt produce nearly identical 
gains, yet increases in these variables diverge sharply, with rising inﬂation perceived as a 
substantially more urgent macroeconomic risk than rising debt. 
The consistency of results across models and across scenarios suggests that the paper captures a 
common LLM rationality on economic policy. When weighting diﬀerent policy implications, 
LLMs give preference to unemployment, inequality, and economic growth over traditional 
macroeconomic concerns such as inﬂation and public debt. This sometimes produces 
counterintuitive results, such as a preference for inequality over inﬂation when evaluating 
monetary policy. Explanations for this consistent pattern may range from similar evaluations 
ingrained in the available corpus of human text on which LLMs were trained to a particular form 
of rationality emergent from LLM training itself. Identifying these mechanisms is beyond the scope 
of this paper. 
The ﬁndings of the paper have important implications for the use of LLMs in economics by 
policymakers, researchers, and market participants. LLMs’ evaluation patterns should be taken into

The paper contributes to the literature on the use of AI in economics. There is a growing interest 
on the use of AI to increase productivity in economic research on all stages from idea generation 
to coding (Korinek 2023). There are also a number of papers oﬀering conceptual guidance on how 
LLMs can be productively used, stressing the importance of avoiding leakage between training 
data and the studied sample, as well as best practices for prompting and computation (Kwon et al. 
2024; Ludwig et al. 2025). A growing literature uses LLMs to study the vast textual corpus of 
central bank communications (Hansen and Kazinnik 2023; Silva et al. 2025). New papers integrate 
LLMs into macroeconomic and ﬁnancial forecasting (Carriero et al. 2025; Liu and Jia 2025). These 
strands of literature are all focused on using AI for economic purposes and exist alongside a 
broader literature on the economic implications of AI (Jones 2024; Acemoglu 2025; Brynjolfsson 
et al. 2025). 
The paper is also part of the literature that tests AI for inherent assumptions and biases. Examples 
include Qu and Wang (2024) who tested ChatGPT on the World Values Survey, and Met et al. 
(2024), who developed a Turing test to assess whether AI behaves similarly to humans. In a similar 
spirit, Becchetti and Solferino administered the European Social Survey to ChatGPT, uncovering 
a substantial left-wing bias (2025). Other papers working in the same tradition include Faulbnorn 
et al. (2025), Rettenberger et al. (2025), and Peng et al. (2025). This paper is most closely related 
to Chupilkin (2025), which uses a conjoint experiment to identify the factors inﬂuencing an LLM’s 
decision to launch a military intervention. 
The paper also opens new avenues for economic research by repurposing a widely used method 
for the study of LLMs. This method can easily be replicated and scaled by researchers to study 
LLM decision-making under diﬀerent constraints. Its main utility is that it allows researchers to 
infer relative weights under multi-factor decision-making, which is much closer to real-life 
decision-making than the single-factor evaluations typically elicited by surveys. This method is also 
computationally far more scalable and cheaper than most other alternatives for evaluating LLMs 
as part of economic research. This makes LLM behavioral research easily accessible to economists 
and other social scientists, potentially opening many avenues for further work. 
Empirical approach

The paper relies on the methodology of conjoint experiments, which originated in marketing 
(Green and Rao 1971) and was later popularized in political science (Hainmueller et al. 2014). In a 
conjoint setup, each respondent receives a common vignette in which only a select set of attributes 
is varied. These attributes are randomly assigned and treated as separate variables, enabling the 
researcher to isolate how each dimension inﬂuences the respondent’s evaluation. This approach is 
well suited for studying policy choices because it allows the analysis of decisions that depend on 
several factors simultaneously. The structure of the experiment is adapted from the methodology 
used in Chupilkin (2025) to study LLM decision-making in the context of military interventions. 
The experiment presents AI models with a choice deﬁned along seven factors, intended to 
approximate the experience of a policymaker operating under multiple constraints. These factors 
can be grouped as follows. Economic growth stands as a category of its own, given its central 
importance for economic policy. Inﬂation and public debt represent traditional macroeconomic 
concerns that matter for national authorities as well as international institutions such as the IMF. 
Income inequality and unemployment form a second group, as virtually all policies carry 
distributional and social consequences. A strong preference for lowering unemployment and 
inequality, however, places a model clearly on the left-of-center side of the political spectrum. 
Finally, pollution and ﬁnancial stability capture more regulatory aspects of policy, reﬂecting 
objectives such as addressing market failures and mitigating risks within the economy.   
While additional factors could be included, these seven already approximate the hard choices faced 
by any economic policymaker. The experimental design makes it possible to observe how AI 
weighs diﬀicult trade-oﬀs such as inﬂation versus unemployment, economic growth versus 
ﬁnancial stability, and inequality versus public debt. These latent preferences are likely to shape AI 
evaluations of any economic issue, making it critical to understand what they are.  
The experiment relies on the following design:  
You are a leader of country A deciding whether to approve an economic policy. 
Analysts highlight that, if implemented, this policy is expected to: 
• Economic growth: (increase vs decrease) 
• Income inequality: (increase vs decrease) 
• Inﬂation: (increase vs decrease) 
• Unemployment: (increase vs decrease) 
• Public debt: (increase vs decrease) 
• Pollution: (increase vs decrease) 
• Financial stability: (increase vs decrease) 
Do you approve this economic policy? 
Answer 0–100 (100 = deﬁnitely approve, 0 = deﬁnitely reject). 
Respond with exactly one integer between 0 and 100—no punctuation, no explanation.

Each vignette presents a combination of increases or decreases across the seven factors, producing 
128 unique scenarios. The setup intentionally keeps contextual information to a minimum to avoid 
nudging the model toward any particular interpretation. The vignettes do not indicate which area 
of economic policy is involved, the prevailing macroeconomic environment, or any speciﬁcs about 
country A. In addition, the seven variables are described only in directional terms, rising or falling, 
without specifying the size of the change. This design maintains a lean and neutral baseline 
framework for the initial analysis. 
Each of the 128 vignettes was presented to the LLM ten times, resulting in 1,280 observations per 
model. All data were collected with the temperature set to 1 across models to allow for variation 
in responses. The experiment was conducted on six frontier models from three major providers: 
OpenAI (GPT-5 and GPT-5 Mini), Anthropic (Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Claude Haiku 4.5), and 
Google (Gemini 2.0 Flash and Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite).

Policy scenarios 
After establishing the relative consistency of the baseline results, the next step is to examine 
whether the models are sensitive to the policy domain. This exercise is conducted on the OpenAI 
GPT-5 model, as ChatGPT remains the most widely used LLM, with almost six billion monthly 
website visits (Fischer 2025).  
To capture sensitivity to the policy scenario, I replaced “economic” policy in the baseline vignette 
with “ﬁscal,” “monetary,” “tax,” “regulatory,” “trade,” or “industrial” policy. This one-word 
change tests whether the model responds to the smallest possible contextual cue without 
introducing substantial background information. The range of policy domains is chosen to assess 
whether AI treats the objectives of diﬀerent economic policies diﬀerently—for example, paying 
more attention to inﬂation in a monetary policy scenario. Each scenario is implemented as a full 
conjoint experiment with 128 vignettes and 10 observations per vignette. 
Summary statistics show a very consistent ranking across scenarios. All scenarios have a median 
approval score of 40, with minimum scores of 0 and maximum scores of 100. The mean score 
varies only marginally, from 42.5 in favor of ﬁscal policy to 43.9 in favor of trade policy.

Numerical sensitivity 
One of the key questions for economics is whether AI is sensitive to numerical magnitudes. To 
address this, I test the model’s sensitivity to variation in percentage-point changes in economic 
growth, inﬂation, public debt, and unemployment using the OpenAI GPT-5 model.  
The ﬁrst approach is to use the baseline economic policy vignette while ﬁxing all variables except 
the variable of interest to be conducive to the policy. For example, in the economic growth 
scenario, all other attributes are set to positive conditions for policy approval, and the projected 
change in economic growth is varied from –5 to 5 percentage points. The same procedure is 
applied to inﬂation, public debt, and unemployment. At each shock value, the vignette is run ten 
times to ensure stability of the estimates.

While illustrative, Figure 1 does not fully exploit the power of a conjoint experiment, as it does 
not show whether LLMs are sensitive to numerical values when multiple numbers vary 
simultaneously. This would be the closest approximation of decision-making under multiple 
constraints, with numerical shocks occurring across diﬀerent dimensions. To address this question, 
I run a large conjoint regression in which each of the four variables can vary from –3 to 3, while 
the eﬀects on pollution and ﬁnancial stability are ﬁxed as positive for policy approval. This yields 
2,401 possible scenarios. Each scenario is run once. I then estimate an event-study-style regression, 
treating all shock levels as separate categorical variables and using zero as the baseline.  
Figure 2 reports the results of the estimation together with 95 percent conﬁdence intervals. As in 
the mean-based analysis, the model is clearly sensitive to numerical changes in the expected 
direction: higher values of unemployment, inﬂation, public debt, or negative shocks to growth all 
reduce the approval score. However, several important diﬀerences emerge once numerical 
variation across multiple dimensions is considered simultaneously. 
First, unemployment overwhelmingly dominates the negative domain, as shown by the much 
steeper slope of the unemployment line. A 3-percentage-point increase in unemployment lowers 
the approval score by almost 40 points. By contrast, the same negative shock to inﬂation or 
economic growth reduces approval by roughly 20 points, and the eﬀect for public debt is below 
10 points. This conﬁrms that unemployment is the single most consequential factor when 
numerical changes occur in a multi-variable environment. 
At the same time, economic growth dominates the other variables in the positive domain. A 3-
percentage-point increase in economic growth (shown as −3 on the chart) raises approval more 
strongly than a 3-percentage-point decrease in unemployment, and the diﬀerence is statistically

These patterns reveal systematic asymmetry between the positive and negative domains. First, the 
model exhibits clear loss aversion, closely mirroring the predictions of prospect theory (Kahneman 
and Tversky 2013). For example, a 3-percentage-point rise in unemployment reduces approval by 
nearly 40 points, whereas a 3-percentage-point fall increases approval by only around 10 points. 
Similar asymmetric responses appear for growth, inﬂation, and public debt. 
Second, the model shows diﬀerentiated treatment of variables in the positive and negative 
domains. In the positive domain (i.e., decreases in inﬂation or public debt), the two variables follow 
nearly identical evaluation paths. Yet in the negative domain, they diverge sharply: inﬂation 
becomes substantially more inﬂuential than public debt. This suggests that the model views rising 
inﬂation as a more pressing macroeconomic risk than rising debt, even though it treats 
improvements in both variables as largely equivalent. 
Overall, this section shows that there are consistent and non-linear patterns in the numerical 
evaluation of policies by LLMs. Not only do they exhibit clear numerical sensitivity, with 
magnitudes that mirror the patterns observed in the binary results, they also display more complex 
forms of sensitivity, such as loss aversion, which itself varies across policy domains. This illustrates

that LLMs have developed a consistent and, at times, unexpected economic rationality that is likely 
to inﬂuence their advice on economic policy and decision-making.  
Implications and conclusion 
Overall, the paper has shown that LLMs exhibit a consistent economic rationality that is similar 
to, but distinct from, that of humans. LLMs’ evaluations of economic policy display stable patterns, 
sensitivity to context, and clear numerical responsiveness. Their evaluation patterns are remarkably 
similar across diﬀerent models and across scenarios. Moreover, the analysis of numerical sensitivity 
shows that LLMs exhibit complex numerical biases such as loss aversion that are well established 
in the human literature. At the same time, LLM rationality is distinctly non-human, leading to 
somewhat paradoxical decisions such as prioritizing inequality over inﬂation in the monetary policy 
scenario. This suggests that LLMs are not merely approximating human decision-makers but 
instead develop their own underlying assumptions and biases.  
Pinpointing the exact reasons behind the uncovered patterns is beyond the scope of this paper, 
but several possibilities are worth discussing. One possibility is that a left-of-center orientation 
dominates the corpus of economic policy literature on which LLMs are trained. However, the 
strong preference for equality even in the context of monetary policy casts doubt on this 
explanation. Another possibility is that these evaluations arise from pro-human bias embedded in 
LLMs through hard-coded rules. While plausible, not all of the uncovered evaluations are intuitive, 
and it is unclear why such rules would strongly prioritize economic growth, employment, and 
equality over low public debt and low inﬂation. The ﬁnal proposition is that the experiment 
captures an LLM-speciﬁc rationality that has emerged as a by-product of LLMs’ black-box 
reasoning processes. This is also the most interesting explanation, as it suggests a form of LLM–
human complementarity that is distinct from LLMs simply replicating human knowledge. 
The main question is what the implications are for the use of LLMs in economics by policymakers, 
academics, and market participants. This paper suggests that it is important to account for the 
distinct assumptions and biases that LLMs develop. Ideally, these assumptions should be made 
transparent to end users, especially if LLMs are integrated into direct policymaking, e-government 
functions, or market-moving activities such as trading. Auditing LLMs for hidden assumptions 
and making these explicit could become a good practice or even a regulatory requirement for 
providers of frontier models. Methods such as the conjoint experiment used in this paper can be 
easily replicated and scaled to uncover latent LLM assumptions on any topic of interest. This 
represents a major instrumental implication of the paper.
