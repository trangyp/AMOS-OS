Abstract
This position paper examines the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) in social
simulation, analyzing their potential and limitations from a computational social science
perspective. We first review recent findings on LLMs’ ability to replicate key aspects of
human cognition, including Theory of Mind reasoning and social inference, while
identifying persistent limitations such as cognitive biases, lack of grounded
understanding, and behavioral inconsistencies. We then survey emerging applications of
LLMs in multi-agent simulation frameworks, examining system architectures, scalability,
and validation strategies. Projects such as Generative Agents (Smallville) and
AgentSociety are analyzed with respect to their empirical grounding and methodological
design. Particular attention is given to the challenges of behavioral fidelity, calibration,
and reproducibility in large-scale LLM-driven simulations. Finally, we distinguish
between contexts where LLM-based agents provide operational value—such as
interactive simulations and serious games—and contexts where their use raises epistemic
concerns, particularly in explanatory or predictive modeling. We argue that hybrid
approaches integrating LLMs into established agent-based modeling platforms such as
GAMA and NetLogo may offer a promising compromise between expressive flexibility
and analytical transparency. Building on this analysis, we outline a conceptual research
direction termed Hybrid Constitutional Architectures, which proposes a stratified
integration of classical agent-based models (ABMs), small language models (SLMs), and
LLMs within established platforms such as GAMA and NetLogo.

Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a transformative class of deep learning
architectures capable of generating human-like text across a wide range of domains.
Built upon the transformer architecture, which employs parallelized attention
mechanisms rather than sequential processing, these models are pre-trained on massive
corpora comprising web data, encyclopedic content, and code repositories [1–3].
Through unsupervised or self-supervised learning, LLMs acquire syntactic, semantic,
and pragmatic language competencies, enabling them to perform a broad array of
downstream tasks such as question answering, summarization, translation, and code

that LLMs have effectively absorbed an immense statistical imprint of human language

decisions — all embedded in the linguistic fabric of their training data. Subsequently,

Given this capability to emulate human-like reasoning patterns, LLMs have
naturally attracted significant attention within the Agent-Based Modeling (ABM)
community. However, as their integration into this field accelerates, it is crucial to
distinguish between two emerging uses of Large Language Models in computational
social science. The first involves using LLMs as coding assistants to facilitate the
technical implementation of simulation models (e.g., generating NetLogo or GAMA
code). The second, which is the sole focus of this paper, is the use of LLMs as the
cognitive architecture of the agents themselves—often termed ’Generative Agents.’ We
examine how LLMs can replace or augment traditional rule-based cognition to simulate
human-like decision-making, social interaction, and reasoning, rather than how they
assist in the software engineering process.
Focusing specifically on this role as a cognitive engine, a critical area of current
investigation concerns the capacity of LLMs to authentically simulate human behavior.
One classical benchmark in this domain is the Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing in
1950 as the imitation game, which evaluates whether a machine can produce
conversational behavior indistinguishable from that of a human interlocutor [8]. Recent
empirical findings suggest that state-of-the-art LLMs, notably LLaMa-3.1 and GPT-4.5,
have demonstrated convincing performance on a three-party version of this test. In
these evaluations, LLaMa-3.1 was perceived as human in 56% of cases, while GPT-4.5
achieved a 73% success rate [8]. This represents a notable advancement over previous
models, which had only succeeded under less rigorous two-party test conditions. These
results underscore the impressive ability of modern LLMs to adopt complex linguistic
styles and behavioral nuances, particularly when guided by carefully crafted persona
prompts — a form of cognitive prompt engineering that remains a highly precise and
non-trivial technique requiring expert design and task sensitivity.
The apparent success of LLMs in the Turing Test has naturally prompted
speculation about their suitability for simulating human agents within computational
social systems [9–11]. However, the capacity of LLMs to produce believable human-like
dialogue is not indicative of genuine understanding or consciousness. Rather, their
performance reflects advanced statistical pattern recognition — the ability to mimic
linguistic structures convincingly — without necessarily modeling the underlying
cognitive or emotional states of a human agent [8]. The tendency of human evaluators
to anthropomorphize fluent language exacerbates this illusion, as observers may
attribute intentionality or mental states to outputs that are, in essence, probabilistically
generated [8], something well-known in psychology as the intentional stance [12]. This
distinction between functional imitation and authentic comprehension is particularly
salient for social simulation. If LLMs merely replicate surface-level features of human
behavior, their utility for generating novel insights into social processes — rather than
reproducing known patterns — is fundamentally limited. Moreover, their use as proxies
for human agents in scientific studies raises questions about external validity,
representational fidelity, and cognitive realism. These concerns constitute a central
theme of this paper and inform our critical assessment of LLMs as generative agents in
artificial societies.

2
LLMs in Simulating Human Behavior: Capabilities
and Cognitive Limitations
A critical question in assessing the suitability of LLMs for social simulation lies in
evaluating how accurately they capture human psychological, emotional, and behavioral
traits. Trained on massive corpora encompassing encyclopedic texts, social media
exchanges, literature, forums, and dialogic interactions, LLMs have absorbed an
unprecedentedly broad cross-section of human communicative behavior. As a result,
they encode statistical traces of how people speak, reason, interact, and respond across
a wide range of contexts — from mundane routines to emotionally charged situations.
These models are thus not merely text generators, but synthesizers of large-scale
sociolinguistic regularities, capable of emulating human-like behavior patterns precisely
because such patterns are deeply embedded in their training data. Crucially, this
capacity does not stem from intentional modeling or experiential understanding, but
from the ability to compress and recompose behavioral signals at scale. When prompted
appropriately, LLMs can reconstruct belief systems, emotional tones, and social scripts
that mirror those present in the corpus — not through explicit design, but as emergent
outcomes of large-scale pattern recognition. This gives rise to a powerful illusion: the
model appears to “understand,” when in fact it is statistically interpolating between

2.1
Theory of Mind and Cognitive Reasoning Tasks
Theory of Mind (ToM) refers to the human capacity to attribute beliefs, intentions,
desires, and knowledge to others. It is considered a foundational element of social
cognition and typically emerges in early childhood. One of the canonical tests to assess
ToM in developmental psychology is the Sally-Anne test [14]. In this task, a child
observes a scene in which Sally places a marble in her basket and leaves the room. Anne
then moves the marble to a box. Upon Sally’s return, the child is asked where she will
look for her marble. A correct response—indicating Sally will look in the
basket—demonstrates an understanding that Sally holds a false belief, which differs
from the child’s own knowledge.
Recent studies have assessed the capacity of LLMs to pass such false-belief
tasks. [15] tested multiple LLMs, including GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, on 40 ToM problems
designed to mirror psychological benchmarks. While early models performed near
chance levels, GPT-4 achieved over 75% accuracy, comparable to that of a 6 or
7-year-old child. Furthermore, GPT-4 demonstrated competence in second and third
order ToM reasoning (e.g., “I believe that she thinks that he knows...”), with occasional
success even at fifth and sixth-order levels, surpassing typical adult human performance
in structured tasks.
The robustness of these findings has been explored in follow-up work. [16] tested
LLMs on modified ToM tasks involving less prototypical narratives—such as a cat
hiding a key or a user renaming folders offline—and found a significant drop in model
performance. Performance was highly sensitive to phrasing; LLMs responded more
accurately to questions using cognitive verbs (e.g., “think”) than to those phrased with
action-oriented verbs (e.g., “look”), suggesting shallow linguistic pattern recognition
rather than deep inference.
In addition to false-belief tasks, LLMs have been evaluated on their ability to
interpret irony, indirect speech acts, and social narratives. [17] found that GPT-4
matched or exceeded average human performance in recognizing indirect requests,
detecting sarcasm, and attributing communicative intent in narrative contexts.
However, these results vary significantly with prompt design and context framing,
raising questions about generalizability.
2.2
Emotion Representation and Affective Simulation
Emotions play a crucial role in human decision-making and social interaction. Unlike
rule-based agents or earlier AI systems, LLMs can generate emotionally appropriate
language in many contexts [18]. For example, given a prompt describing a tragic event,
GPT-4 can produce empathetic responses and plausible emotional narratives.
Nonetheless, this capacity is fundamentally linguistic. LLMs do not experience emotions
nor maintain internal emotional states. Their affective responses are generated through
statistical associations learned from text corpora containing human emotional
expression.

2.3
Anthropomorphic and Statistical Biases
2.3.1
Societal and Representational Biases
Because LLMs are trained on large-scale human-generated data, they inevitably inherit
and can perpetuate societal biases embedded in those texts. These include explicit and
implicit stereotypes related to race, gender, religion, and culture. Studies have shown
that LLMs reproduce associations consistent with biased representations: for instance,
associating certain professions with male pronouns or producing racially skewed
descriptions of criminal behavior [20]. In addition, structural imbalances in data
availability mean that voices from underrepresented linguistic or social groups are
poorly modeled, resulting in inequitable performance across different populations [21].
This ”black-box” inheritance of bias persists despite instruction tuning, and differences
between model versions can lead to inconsistent user experiences and hidden harms in
downstream applications [22].
2.3.2
Cognitive Biases and the Reasoning Paradox
Beyond representational biases, LLMs also exhibit patterns consistent with cognitive
biases observed in humans—defined as systematic deviations from rationality in
judgment. [23] found that LLMs replicate anchoring effects, status quo bias, framing
effects, and recency bias when posed with structured reasoning tasks. While debiasing
efforts using prompt engineering have shown promise, they remain partial solutions [24].
Alarmingly, the tendency to apply ”chain-of-thought” prompting—a method designed
to enhance logical reasoning—does not consistently prevent these biases. In some
instances, reasoning chains actually amplify them, indicating that LLM ”reasoning”
may reinforce pre-trained associations rather than correct them [23]. [24] argues that
mitigating these issues requires upstream control over training data composition rather
than just refined prompting.
2.3.3
Convergence towards the ”Average Persona”
Another bias that raises significant ethical issues is the convergence towards an “average
persona.” LLMs fundamentally operate on statistical probabilities, predicting the ”next
likely word” based on patterns in their training data rather than deep cognitive
understanding [25,26]. Consequently, while human decisions are shaped by a rich
tapestry of lived experiences and future goals, an LLM merely calculates the most
mathematically expected response. This results in a flattening of diversity that can
inadvertently erase the characteristics of minority subgroups in simulated
populations [22,27]. Empirical evidence highlights this homogeneity in economic games,
where LLMs often produce highly uniform responses compared to the wider range of
choices observed in humans [27]. This trend extends to the political sphere, where
models tend to produce narrower distributions of opinions that overrepresent the values
of wealthy, young, and politically liberal individuals—the so-called ”WEIRD”
demographic bias [28,29].

2.3.4
Artifactual Biases from Safety Alignment
Beyond this statistical convergence, the deliberate ”safety alignment” of
LLMs—achieved through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
(RLHF)—introduces a further critical risk: the generation of ”false negatives” in
exploratory research. While training models to be ”helpful and harmless” is essential for
commercial products, this normative alignment acts as a systematic filter that
”sanitizes” agent behaviors. In the context of social simulation, an over-aligned model
may exhibit a ”sycophancy bias” [30], refusing to manifest conflictual, aggressive, or
”dark” social dynamics even when such behaviors are realistic and essential for
understanding phenomena like polarization or radicalization. Consequently, researchers
risk exploring a truncated solution space where catastrophic but plausible social
outcomes are suppressed, leading to a dangerous underestimation of systemic risks.
While context-rich prompts can mitigate this convergence, this variability remains
contingent on the user’s intervention rather than an inherent capacity of the model for
autonomous diversity [31].
2.3.5
Bounded Rationality vs. Artifactual Bias
Finally, a critical theoretical distinction must be made regarding the very definition of
’bias’ in generative social science. Following Herbert Simon’s theory of Bounded
Rationality [32], human decision-making is inherently constrained by cognitive
limitations and heuristics. In social simulation, these human deviations from pure logic
are not defects to be removed, but constitutive features necessary for realism. Therefore,
we must rigorously distinguish between ’Desirable Cognitive Biases’—such as loss
aversion or emotional reactivity, which reflect authentic human irrationality—and
’Artifactual Model Biases’ stemming from the LLM’s training process. The latter
include phenomena like ’Sycophancy’ (the tendency to agree with the user to be
’helpful’) [30] or ’Safety Sanitization’ (the refusal to simulate conflictual interactions).
While Simon’s bounded rationality is a target to be reached, these artifactual biases are
’machine-like’ distortions that flatten social dynamics and must be actively mitigated
through architectural constraints.
2.4
Intrinsic Instability: Hallucinations and Inconsistency
A pervasive challenge in LLM behavior is the issue of hallucinations, where the model
generates outputs that are baseless, untruthful, or diverge from factual reality, even
while maintaining a coherent linguistic structure [4]. These errors often stem from
flawed training data or specific architectural biases [33–35]. Crucially, when LLMs
hallucinate, they typically maintain an ’aura of authority’, generating responses that
appear coherent and well-formed. This deceptive coherence creates a ’validity illusion’
that can mislead users—especially those lacking domain expertise—and introduces
substantial risks in scientific applications where factual accuracy is paramount [36,37].
Compounding this issue is the problem of stochastic inconsistency. Identical or
nearly identical inputs can elicit divergent responses, even within the same session,
which hinders reproducibility and challenges the interpretability of agent behavior [38].
This instability can leave lasting impressions of unreliability, limiting the
trustworthiness of the simulation [39]. Furthermore, while LLMs demonstrate superior
performance in unrealistic, ’omniscient’ settings where all agents possess full
information [40], they struggle significantly in scenarios characterizing real-world
conditions, such as information asymmetry or the management of private
knowledge [41]. Paradoxically, despite this individual instability, Wu et al. (2025)
emphasize that LLM agents, when evaluated across multiple instantiations, often

2.5
Summary and Implications
The current landscape presents a paradox. LLMs exhibit impressive capabilities in tasks
traditionally considered strong indicators of social cognition. They can perform
multi-level mental state inference, recognize irony, and produce emotionally plausible
responses. However, these abilities are brittle, highly sensitive to phrasing, and
grounded in probabilistic mimicry rather than experiential or embodied cognition. Their
outputs reflect statistical associations from training corpora, not inferential reasoning
rooted in a coherent world model.
In light of these findings, LLMs offer valuable opportunities for simulating linguistic
and behavioral patterns within constrained contexts. Yet, their application in scientific
investigations of human psychology or complex social behavior should be approached
with caution. Their limitations—opacity, hallucination, inconsistency, bias, and shallow
understanding—necessitate robust validation, controlled environments, and clear
demarcation between imitation and cognition. In the following section, we turn to how
these capabilities are being harnessed in practical social simulation frameworks, and
how model architecture and evaluation are being addressed in emerging systems.
3
State of the Art: LLM-Based Social Simulation
The use of LLMs in social simulations builds upon years of research that have seen the
application of machine learning techniques in multi-agent systems and, by extension, in
agent-based models [42]. It is therefore essential to understand whether LLMs represent
a major breakthrough or, more modestly, a logical evolution of this partnership, and to
determine if they share the same methodological challenges as previous approaches or
introduce new ones. Building on the previous discussion regarding the representational
capacities of LLMs in psychological and behavioral contexts, this section examines their
application in the field of social simulation. We focus on how they are currently used to
instantiate artificial societies, the architectural strategies adopted for agent based
modeling, and the methodological challenges related to validation and scaling.
3.1
From Generative Agents to Population-Scale Simulation
A foundational illustration of LLM-based social simulation is the Generative Agents
framework introduced by [9], widely known as the “Smallville” project. In this system,
twenty-five agents inhabit a sandbox environment reminiscent of The Sims, where they
autonomously engage in daily routines, form interpersonal relationships, and generate
emergent collective behaviors. Each agent maintains a natural language memory stream,
allowing it to reflect on past events, summarize experiences, and formulate plans.
Remarkably, the simple assignment of a goal—such as organizing a party—was sufficient
to initiate a chain of spontaneous, coordinated interactions among agents. Evaluation
efforts focused primarily on assessing the believability and internal coherence of these
emergent dynamics, rather than on their correspondence to empirical data.
More recent efforts have extended this line of research to address questions of scale,
complexity, and empirical grounding. One prominent example is AgentSociety, a
large-scale platform designed to simulate the dynamics of human societies with over
10,000 LLM-based agents [43]. Each agent is instantiated using GPT-4 and equipped
with cognitive modules for memory, goal management, decision-making, and social
relationship tracking. These agents interact within rich environments encompassing

Complementing these large-scale efforts, Simulate Anything offers a flexible platform
for generating demographically diverse agent populations using LLMs, leveraging
real-world user data for initialization and sampling [45]. The system supports
scenario-specific tuning and hypothesis testing across domains such as politics,
journalism, and economics. Its principal contribution lies in enabling heterogeneous role
configurations and behavioral variability, though its validation methodologies are
comparatively underdeveloped.
Another significant contribution in this domain is S3 (Social-network Simulation
System), which specifically focuses on social network dynamics through
LLM-empowered agents [46]. S3 demonstrates particular strengths in simulating three
pivotal aspects of human social behavior: emotion, attitude, and interaction behaviors.
It leverages advanced prompt engineering and prompt tuning techniques to ensure
authentic human-like agent behavior within social networks.
Several more recent platforms have further advanced the field in terms of
architectural modularity and methodological rigor. GenSim, for instance, is a
general-purpose simulation engine that supports tens of thousands of LLM agents [47].
It introduces structured abstractions for social routines, long-term memory
management, and adaptive behavior correction, all orchestrated by a dynamic
scheduling system. Validation combines empirical replication, face validity assessments,
and anomaly detection to ensure both plausibility and statistical consistency.
Another emerging contribution is AgentTorch, a modular and scalable framework
that unifies large-scale agent-based modeling with differentiable programming and
LLM-powered behavior synthesis [48]. Instead of relying on expensive real-time
prompting for each agent, AgentTorch uses a small number of archetypal LLM agents to
generate compact, reusable policy representations. These are then deployed across
massive populations of lightweight agents in GPU-accelerated simulations. Whereas
narrative-first systems emphasize fine-grained agent individuality, AgentTorch focuses
on experimental control, statistical robustness, and scientific reproducibility, making it
particularly suited for large-scale hypothesis testing and parameter exploration in
computational social science.
SALLMA (Scalable Architecture for LLM Multi-Agent Applications) adopts a
layered architecture, separating operational processes (intent formation, task execution,
communication) from knowledge-level components (agent profiles, shared memory,
workflows) [49]. This modular design facilitates agent specialization and integration
with external tools via APIs. Its validation pipeline combines automated benchmarking
with expert-in-the-loop review, aided by interactive dashboards and simulation playback
for iterative refinement.

3.2
Architecture Patterns for Cognitive Agents
LLM-based multi-agent models generally instantiate each agent as a distinct LLM
instance or session using prompt engineering. Architectures typically involve three
interconnected modules: a memory system that encodes and retrieves past experiences;
a reflection and summarization layer that condenses observations into mental models;
and a planning component that uses these reflections to generate actions [9,52]. These
modules are often implemented as chained or nested prompts, with each step leveraging
the LLM’s generative capacity to simulate cognitive processes such as recall, reasoning,
and intention formation. For instance, the Humanized Agent-Based Models (h-ABM)
framework, proposed by [53], outlines a modular architecture for integrating LLMs into
ABMs to achieve more cognitively and emotionally realistic agents.
3.2.1
Memory Systems and Psychological Foundations
Many LLM-based agent architectures draw inspiration from cognitive psychology,
particularly theories of episodic and semantic memory. For example, the memory
systems in Generative Agents and AgentSociety are designed to mimic human-like recall,
using vector databases or embedding-based retrieval to surface relevant past experiences.
Some frameworks explicitly reference psychological models, such as the
Atkinson-Shiffrin multi-store model or the concept of “theory of mind,” to inform the
design of agent cognition and social reasoning [52,54]. Reflection modules often prompt
the LLM to summarize recent events, extract intentions, or infer the mental states of
other agents, thus operationalizing constructs from social and developmental psychology.
Established cognitive architectures such as SOAR [55] and ACT-R [56] have
provided theoretical foundations for agent design. SOAR, developed by John Laird,
Allen Newell, and Paul Rosenbloom at Carnegie Mellon University [55], embodies the
problem space hypothesis, suggesting that all goal-oriented behavior can be
characterized as a search in a problem space defined by states, operators, and goals.
This cognitive architecture divides knowledge into procedural memory (skills and
know-how), semantic memory (facts about the world), and episodic memory (memories
of experiences). Similarly, ACT-R (Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational) [56] posits
that human cognition can be modeled through interconnected cognitive and perceptual
modules that operate according to rational principles. These architectures inform the
contemporary design of LLM agents by providing structured frameworks for organizing
memory, decision-making, and learning.

3.2.2
Planning and Decision-Making
Planning modules in LLM-based multi-agent systems typically exploit the model’s
capacity to generate contextually appropriate actions by conditioning outputs on
agent-specific goals, environmental affordances, and social context. While a subset of
frameworks integrates reinforcement learning or utility-based decision mechanisms to
refine agent strategies, the majority rely on prompt engineering and scenario-specific
instructions to elicit desired planning behavior. This approach emphasizes the
structured design of inputs to steer agents’ reasoning, allowing for complex
decision-making without the need for explicit reward modeling.
A hybrid approach is exemplified by the LGC-MARL framework proposed by [57],
which combines an LLM-based planner with graph-structured multi-agent reinforcement
learning. In this architecture, the LLM is tasked with decomposing high-level
instructions into executable subtasks, evaluating their coherence and feasibility, and
formulating collaborative strategies among agents before final action selection. In
contrast, more conventional social simulation systems rely predominantly on
prompt-engineering strategies to define agent roles, goals, and reasoning pathways,
typically without integrating reinforcement signals or dynamic adaptation
mechanisms [58]. These approaches underscore the current design trade-off between
architectural complexity and ease of interpretability in the planning components of
LLM-driven simulations.
3.2.3
Orchestration and Communication
In LLM-based multi-agent systems, a centralized orchestration layer is commonly
adopted to manage simulation time-stepping and regulate agent interaction flows. Such
architectures typically support inter-agent communication via various message-passing
mechanisms, including direct messaging, simulated surveys, and public broadcasts.
Approaches to orchestration and communication vary considerably depending on the
complexity and goals of the simulation. For example, platforms like Generative Agents
and AgentSociety utilize orchestration layers to manage complex interactions among
thousands of agents, often based on sequential flows or perception-reflection-action
loops. Others, such as GenSim, integrate structured abstractions for social routines and
a dynamic scheduling system. More advanced frameworks, like AgentTorch, move away
from costly sequential dependencies by generating reusable policy representations from
a small number of archetypal LLM agents, which are then deployed at scale on
lightweight agents for GPU-accelerated simulations. SALLMA proposes a layered
architecture, separating operational processes (intention formation, task execution) from
knowledge-level components (agent profiles, shared memory, workflows), thereby
facilitating agent specialization and integration with external tools via APIs. These
platforms often support modular ”blocks,” which are reusable behavioral units that
allow for systematic experimentation and increased extensibility. Integration with
external APIs (e.g., web search, databases, calculators) is also crucial, as it enables
agents to access additional information and tools, thereby augmenting their reasoning
and decision-making capabilities [59].

3.3
Current Validation Methodologies
3.3.1
Empirical Validation and Benchmarking
The validation of LLM-based social simulations remains an active and evolving area of
research. Among the most rigorous efforts, frameworks like AgentSociety adopt
multi-faceted validation strategies that compare simulated outputs with real-world data.
Empirical benchmarking typically involves aligning simulation results with social
indicators such as voter turnout, economic behavior, or survey-derived attitudinal
distributions from established instruments like the General Social Survey or the World
Values Survey [43]. In addition, some studies replicate classical experimental
paradigms—such as public goods games or rumor propagation scenarios—within the
simulation environment, and evaluate whether the emergent outcomes converge toward
established empirical findings.
Longitudinal consistency is also a critical focus, particularly in simulations involving
extended time horizons. Researchers analyze whether agent behavior remains stable and
coherent across simulation runs, and whether emergent patterns are reproducible under
minor changes to initial conditions or prompt phrasing [27]. These approaches aim to
assess not only face plausibility but also the robustness and generalizability of the
simulated social dynamics.
3.3.2
Human-in-the-Loop Evaluation
Despite advances in automation, human judgment continues to play a crucial role in
validating LLM-driven simulations. Expert evaluation by domain specialists is
frequently used to assess the believability, internal coherence, and sociological
plausibility of agent behaviors. Structured rating protocols are often employed to ensure
inter-rater reliability, although such methods remain sensitive to subjective bias.
Crowdsourced evaluation has also been adopted in some frameworks, wherein large
groups of non-expert raters assess the realism or diversity of agent interactions.
Calibration against real-world survey data constitutes another widely used strategy.
Here, demographic or attitudinal profiles from empirical sources are used to condition
LLM agents, and human evaluators subsequently assess the extent to which the
simulated agents reflect the intended population characteristics [59]. Nevertheless,
recent findings suggest that human raters may be influenced by non-epistemic
factors—such as assertiveness, linguistic fluency, or the perceived authority of LLM
outputs—leading to potential overestimation of their factual or analytical accuracy [63].

The development of standardized benchmarks has been instrumental in advancing the
empirical evaluation of LLM agents in social simulations. For instance, the SocialIQA
dataset [64] evaluates an LLM’s capacity for common sense social reasoning by
presenting situations involving physical, social, and emotional causality and testing
whether the model can infer likely consequences. Another example is CrowS-Pairs [65],
which assesses social bias by contrasting sentence pairs that differ only by protected
attributes (e.g., gender or race), allowing systematic measurement of discriminatory
tendencies. Additionally, benchmarks like BiosBias [66] evaluate demographic fairness
by examining whether the model’s predictions or representations vary unjustly
depending on demographic factors such as gender or ethnicity. While these datasets do
not address agent consistency across sessions directly, related work such as [67]
examines the consistency of language models by testing response variability to
semantically equivalent paraphrased inputs, providing useful insights into
reproducibility concerns in agent behavior.
Comparative studies between human and LLM behavior further inform the
validation landscape. The EPITOME battery [68] includes a series of experimental
tasks designed to test Theory of Mind capabilities in both humans and LLMs. Results
suggest that while LLMs can often match or even exceed human-level performance on
certain tasks, they also exhibit systematic limitations, particularly in pragmatic
reasoning and inference under uncertainty.
Together, these empirical, human-centered, and dataset-driven approaches constitute
an emerging ecosystem of validation methodologies. Nevertheless, their integration into
a cohesive and principled framework remains an open area of research—one that is
essential for ensuring the scientific credibility and social relevance of LLM-based
simulations.

Integrating LLMs into social simulation offers compelling opportunities: simulations are
faster to build, more flexible in scope, and capable of generating richly expressive agent
behavior. However, they also introduce epistemic risks. LLMs, trained to imitate

4
Methodological Challenges and Epistemological
Risks
The transition from the technical frameworks described in the previous section to
rigorous scientific inquiry reveals a distinct set of hurdles. While the platforms reviewed
above demonstrate that large-scale LLM simulations are technically feasible, feasibility
does not equate to scientific validity. The very features that make LLMs
attractive—their ability to generate fluent, varied, and human-like text at scale—also
introduce profound methodological barriers when moving from exploratory modeling to
causal explanation. This section critically examines these barriers, focusing on the gap
between micro-level believability and macro-level veridicality, the loss of epistemic
control due to model opacity, and the systemic risks of bias and cost. To guide this
analysis, Table 1 provides a synthetic overview of these cognitive, methodological, and
technical hurdles, alongside the architectural solutions proposed to overcome them.
4.1
The Validity Gap: From Micro-Believability to
Macro-Veridicality
While frameworks for large-scale simulation have emerged, a critical methodological
barrier remains regarding empirical validation. As argued by [71], validation is currently
the central challenge for generative social simulation, as the field lacks standardized
protocols to move beyond ’surface realism’. Most current validation strategies rely on
’micro-level believability’—assessing whether individual agents respond realistically in
isolated contexts [9]. However, establishing micro-validity does not guarantee
’macro-level veridicality’.
This gap between individual plausibility and collective accuracy is widened by three
systemic failures that only manifest at scale. First, the convergence toward an ”average
persona” [27] suppresses the behavioral heterogeneity necessary for modeling complex
social phenomena. In many domains, this results in a mode collapse where diverse
agents regress to a uniform mean. Second, temporal instability—often exacerbated by
context window limitations—compromises longitudinal coherence. Agents tend to
’forget’ early instructions or exhibit behavioral drift over long simulation runs, making
reproducibility—a cornerstone of scientific inquiry—notoriously difficult [27]. Third,
many simulations rely on omniscient settings where agents access complete information
to maintain coherence. While this enhances performance, it fundamentally contradicts
real-world social dynamics characterized by information asymmetry and private
knowledge [72].
Relying on human evaluation to bridge this gap is perilous. Recent findings suggest
that human raters are influenced by non-epistemic factors—such as the assertiveness

13/26


## Page 14


and linguistic fluency of LLM outputs—leading to a potential overestimation of their
factual accuracy [70]. This creates a ’validity illusion’: the simulation looks realistic
because the text is fluent, but the stochastic and ”black-box” nature of LLMs makes
calibration—the standard process of tuning ABM parameters to match observed
outputs—notoriously difficult. Consequently, until quantitative calibration becomes a
primary focus, large-scale LLM simulations remain in the realm of ’plausible
storytelling’ rather than predictive science [71].
In light of these challenges, [27] proposes a pragmatic framework for determining the
conditions under which LLM-based social simulations can meaningfully advance
scientific understanding. They argue that these simulations are most defensible when
used to explore collective patterns rather than individual decision trajectories, provided
that agent outputs align with population-level averages and that validation methods are
carefully matched to the research objectives and simulation scale.
4.2
The ”Black-Box” Problem and Interpretability
The ”black-box” nature of Large Language Models, stemming from architectures with
billions of parameters and complex non-linear transformations, creates significant
challenges for understanding their decision-making processes [73]. This opacity does not
merely hinder interpretability; it actively contributes to ”automation bias.” This
phenomenon manifests when modelers, seduced by the sophistication and apparent
fluency of LLM outputs, over-attribute accuracy and authority to the model, even when
its underlying reasoning is obscure or flawed [74].
This combination of technical opacity and misplaced human trust becomes
dangerous in high-stakes social simulations. Because the model’s internal processing
does not consistently mitigate biases [23], outputs can be uninterpretable, inaccurate, or
discriminatory. When such simulations are used for critical applications—like policy
testing, resource allocation, or urban planning—where direct human validation is often
impossible [27], there is a substantial risk of propagating flawed conclusions. As noted
by [73], regulators require assurances that decision-support tools are robust and
accountable—requirements that the ”black-box” nature of LLMs directly contravenes.
Without robust interpretability tools, the unexamined nature of these models could lead
to misinformed policy decisions or exacerbate societal inequalities, even when the
researchers’ intentions are positive.
Beyond these ethical concerns, using an LLM to generate agent behavior
fundamentally alters the scientific modeling process itself. Unlike traditional
Agent-Based Modeling (ABM), where the designer explicitly constructs behavioral rules
based on theory or empirical evidence, LLM-driven agents bypass this stage. The
behavioral model becomes implicit, embedded within billions of opaque parameters
learned from massive datasets. As a result, the modeller relinquishes the ability to
clearly define or justify the assumptions underlying agent behavior. This lack of
epistemic control is especially problematic in scientific contexts, where the rationale for
modeling choices must be transparent and subject to scrutiny. While LLMs may, in
theory, reflect statistical patterns closer to real-world data, this advantage is
undermined if the underlying mechanisms remain inaccessible and unvalidated.
4.3
Systemic Bias and the ”Average Persona” Risk
While Section 2.3 highlighted the intrinsic biases of LLMs, their impact scales
non-linearly when applied to multi-agent populations. The most critical risk for social
simulation is systemic homogenization. As noted by [27], the statistical nature of LLMs
drives them to converge toward a generic, safe, and ”WEIRD” behavioral mean. In a
simulation context, this creates a ”Replicant Effect”: even when agents are assigned

distinct demographic prompts, their actual decision-making logic collapses into a
uniform mode, effectively erasing minority behaviors and extreme viewpoints. Moreover,
this homogenization is paradoxically accompanied by ”Generative Exaggeration”. As
demonstrated by [69], when LLMs are prompted to adopt specific personas to escape
the ”average,” they often drift into caricature, amplifying stereotypical traits to the
point of toxicity rather than maintaining behavioral nuance. This suggests that current
models struggle to find a middle ground between the ”flatness” of the average persona
and the ”parody” of specific roles.
This ”Average Persona” problem is exacerbated by the ”Data Scarcity Wall.” As
discussed regarding the limitations of fine-tuning, simulating specific, under-represented
populations—such as smallholder farmers in the Global South—is often impossible
because the massive, digitized behavioral logs required for training simply do not exist.
Consequently, relying on generic LLMs to simulate these groups risks replacing missing
data with hallucinated stereotypes.
To mitigate this, future research must move beyond mere prompt engineering.
Developing more diverse and personalized training datasets is crucial, specifically
incorporating first-person narratives, oral histories, and qualitative psychological
insights to capture the ”long tail” of human behavior. Furthermore, incorporating
external motivation structures (e.g., BDI architectures) can help force agents to deviate
from the statistical mean by driving decisions through explicit goals and fears rather
than mere text prediction. Without these structural interventions, simulations will
remain valuable for exploring collective patterns of the ”average” human [27], but will
fail to capture the diversity essential for understanding complex societal dynamics.
4.4
Computational Constraints and Scalability
While LLMs offer cost-efficiency compared to human experiments, they introduce a
drastic computational bottleneck compared to traditional Agent-Based Models (ABMs).
The resource intensity of LLMs—both in fine-tuning and real-time inference—limits
practical scalability [75]. Crucially, this high cost is not merely a logistical hurdle but a
methodological barrier: it hinders the application of classical sensitivity analyses, which
require running simulations thousands of times to assess parameter influence and model
robustness [27,43]. Unlike lightweight rule-based agents that permit exhaustive
parameter sweeps on standard hardware, the computational latency of LLMs currently
forces researchers to trade statistical rigor for behavioral complexity, often preventing
the extensive replications necessary to validate stochastic models.
4.5
Ethics in Application: Serious Games vs. Predictive Science
Given the methodological barriers outlined above, it is crucial to delineate the
appropriate domains of application for LLM-based agents.
4.5.1
Interactive Environments and Serious Games
In contexts such as educational games and training simulations [76], LLMs offer direct
and significant benefits. Here, the primary goal is often to create a ”believable” and
”engaging” experience rather than a perfectly verifiable replication of social reality [75].
LLMs excel at simulating dynamic, personalized interactions, enhancing user
engagement through adaptive learning pathways and gamified narratives [77].
However, as highlighted by recent critiques, this application requires a shift from
cautious optimism to critical vigilance. The primary risk lies in ’negative training
transfer’: unlike traditional rule-based simulators where every outcome is verified, an
LLM-based agent can hallucinate factual errors with high confidence. Recent studies

highlight that this ’authoritative misinformation’ can lead to superficial learning and
cognitive over-reliance [78]. Furthermore, instructional quality is threatened by inherent
biases; as demonstrated by [79], models often perpetuate harmful stereotypes in
personalized content. Consequently, the deployment of LLMs in training necessitates
rigorous ’red-teaming’ and grounded architectures (RAG) to ensure the simulation
remains a tool for learning, not confusion.
4.5.2
From Prediction to Exploratory Modeling
For ”pure social simulation” aimed at scientific inquiry, the implications of the ”validity
gap” and ”epistemic opacity” (discussed in Sections 4.1 and 4.2) are decisive. Because
of the risks of hallucination and the ”average persona” bias, we argue that LLMs are
currently ill-suited for confirmatory empirical research or precise forecasting (e.g.,
predicting specific election outcomes or policy impacts).
Instead, their most scientifically robust application lies in Exploratory Modeling [59].
In this paradigm, LLMs serve not as oracles of truth, but as engines for hypothesis
generation. They allow researchers to rapidly explore a vast space of possible social
interactions and identify emergent patterns that—while not necessarily predictive of the
real world—reveal the logical consequences of specific behavioral assumptions. As [27]
suggests, these simulations should be viewed as ”reasoning pumps” to test collective
dynamics under diverse narrative constraints, rather than as digital twins of reality.
5
Towards Hybrid Constitutional Architectures
The current landscape suggests a fundamental shift in the approach to social simulation.
While LLMs offer compelling opportunities—enabling the rapid simulation of thousands
of interactions and uncovering emergent behaviors [10,43]—the limitations of
”off-the-shelf” models underscore the enduring value of traditional Agent-Based Models
(ABMs).
5.1
Integrating LLMs into Classical Frameworks
The future of social simulation does not lie in ”off-the-world” AI agents, but in the
seamless integration of LLMs within structured platforms such as GAMA [80] and
NetLogo [81]. This approach, which we define as LLM-augmented ABM, addresses the
fundamental problem of groundedness: while LLMs manipulate abstract concepts, the
ABM provides the ”world model”—the physical, spatial, and resource-based
constraints—that dictates the actual viability of AI-proposed actions.
This synergy allows researchers to move beyond purely narrative modeling. Recent
work, such as Gao et al. (2025) on the S3 system, demonstrates how individual behavior
alignment can be synchronized with complex social network structures, ensuring that
LLM expressiveness does not sacrifice macroscopic coherence [46]. Simultaneously,
frameworks like AgentTorch [48] open the door to massive scalability through GPU
acceleration, enabling simulations of entire populations where the ABM manages the
physics of interaction while the LLM simulates context-sensitive psychology.
For instance, in an epidemiological model like COMOKIT [82,83], an LLM could
interpret evolving social norms or disinformation, while the ABM maintains the
structural rigor of disease spread. This hybrid necessity is even more acute in
high-stakes environments. [84] recently demonstrated this in disaster evacuation
simulations: while the ABM manages the physical constraints of crowd density and
velocity to prevent unrealistic movements, the LLM-enhanced agents simulate the
cognitive panic and decision-making under uncertainty. This ”Physics-Cognition” split

5.2
Cognitive Alignment: Fine-Tuning and its Limits
To correct the instability of generic models, a promising path lies in behavioral
alignment. Binz et al. (2025) have demonstrated that it is possible to transform an
LLM into a cognitively aligned agent (the Centaur model) by training it on massive
datasets derived from human psychological experiments. This process allows the AI to
replicate human learning curves and decision-making biases correlated with human
neural signals [85].
However, this data-driven path hits a critical ”Data Scarcity Wall” as soon as one
moves beyond Western (WEIRD) contexts. As highlighted by [20], when representative
training data is absent, LLMs tend to fall back on generalized stereotypes to fill the
gaps. Consequently, in social simulation where identity is relational and cultural,
modeling the decisions of smallholder farmers in Vietnam is impossible through simple
fine-tuning: the necessary digitized behavioral logs do not exist, leaving the model to
rely on hallucinated or westernized proxies.
Pursuing a strategy exclusively based on data risks reinforcing the ”Average Avatar
Bias”: an AI that perfectly mimics a mean Western human but fails entirely to represent
the specificities of marginalized or culturally distinct groups. To break this impasse,
alignment must shift from being purely statistical (data-driven) to being structural
(theory-driven), justifying the transition toward ”constitutional” architectures.
5.3
Proposed Paradigm: Hybrid Constitutional Architectures
We argue that cognitive alignment in social simulation cannot remain purely
data-driven; it must be structurally anchored in psychological theory. We propose the
adoption of Hybrid Constitutional Architectures, a paradigm shift where the LLM’s
generative freedom is bounded by a theoretical ’scaffold.’
In this stratified cognitive process, we move beyond the monolithic agent:
• Heuristic Stratum (SLMs): High-frequency, routine behaviors (e.g., daily
navigation, basic consumption) are delegated to Small Language Models (SLMs).
This ensures computational scalability [86] and allows for ’local alignment’—where
smaller models are fine-tuned on specific ethnographic data that would be diluted
in larger architectures.
• Reasoning Stratum (LLM as Proposal Engine): Larger models are invoked
only for complex, non-routine social reasoning. Crucially, the LLM does not
execute the final decision; it acts as a ’Proposal Engine’, generating multiple
plausible behavioral trajectories based on the current social context.
• The Theoretical Constitution: Every proposal is passed through a validation
filter grounded in established frameworks, such as the Theory of Planned Behavior
(TPB) [87] or Bounded Rationality principles [32]. For instance, a proposal that
contradicts an agent’s established ’Subjective Norms’ or ’Perceived Behavioral
Control’ is rejected or penalized. The constitutional layer must itself be treated as
a model component subject to audit, since poorly specified normative constraints
may reduce harmful outputs while simultaneously suppressing legitimate social
heterogeneity.
• External Memory Modules (ABM State): To resolve the Temporal

6
Conclusion
LLMs have demonstrated impressive capabilities in mimicking human-like conversation
and passing complex psychological tests [8,15]. Yet, as this paper has explored, these
successes are often rooted in sophisticated pattern recognition rather than genuine
understanding. This leads to the ”Fluency Fallacy”: a state where models maintain high
linguistic coherence even when hallucinating social facts, creating a ”surface realism”
that can mislead researchers into mistaking narrative plausibility for causal validity.
The transition from individual generative agents to robust social simulations requires
moving beyond the Turing Test toward stricter criteria: Behavioral Diversity (to avoid
mode collapse towards an average persona) and Counterfactual Robustness (the ability
to react realistically to unseen shocks) [27,71]. While LLMs show direct utility in
interactive simulations and serious games for engagement [75], their application in pure
social science demands a critical re-evaluation of epistemic control.
Ultimately, the future of social simulation does not lie in a choice between the
data-driven flexibility of LLMs and the theory-driven rigor of ABM, but in their synergy.
By coupling the narrative transparency of Generative AI with the structural rigor of
Hybrid Constitutional Architectures, we can achieve simulations that are not only
plausible but causally valid and intuitively explainable. This hybrid paradigm ensures
that even in data-scarce environments, artificial societies can serve as meaningful
laboratories for understanding the deep-seated nuances of human decision-making.
