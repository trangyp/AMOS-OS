Abstract
Asynchronous Distributed Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
can suffer from degraded convergence when model updates
become stale, often the result of network congestion and
packet loss during large-scale training. This work introduces
a network data-plane acceleration architecture that mitigates
such staleness by enabling inline processing of DRL model
updates as they traverse the accelerator engine. To this end,
we design and prototype a novel queueing mechanism that
opportunistically combines compatible updates sharing a net-
work element, reducing redundant traffic and preserving up-
date utility. Complementing this we provide a lightweight
transmission control mechanism at the worker nodes that is
guided by feedback from the in-network accelerator. To as-
sess model utility at line rate, we introduce the Age-of-Model
(AoM) metric as a proxy for staleness and verify global fair-
ness and responsiveness properties using a formal verification
method. Our evaluations demonstrate that this architecture
significantly reduces update staleness and congestion, ulti-
mately improving the convergence rate in asynchronous DRL
workloads.

Introduction
Distributed machine learning (DML) tasks dominate the com-
putational load in modern AI data centers. The increasing size
of datasets and model architectures has led to the development
of highly distributed training systems, where workers that are
organized in clusters compute model updates and transmit
them for aggregation and redistribution to a central parameter
server (PS) or to all other workers in the cluster [30,33].
One observes two main paradigms in large-scale DML
training. First, in synchronous DML training, periodic global
synchronization barriers align model updates across all work-
ers [16,29,31,37,51]. In contrast, we focus here on the second
paradigm, namely large-scale asynchronous training, where
workers continuously send updates without coordination, aim-
ing to reduce idle time and improve hardware utilization,
specifically given heterogeneous workers [18,36].

Large-scale DML training can overwhelm bottlenecks, e.g.,
at the Top-of-Rack (ToR) switch, due to the volume of up-
dates generated by a large number of workers in a short time
span [10,14,53]. This may be aggravated in multi-tenant envi-
ronments where DML clusters share the same network fabric.
This congestion leads to sporadic packet drops at saturated
queues, causing model staleness in asynchronous training or
delayed model aggregation in its synchronous counterpart.
In this work, we explore how in-network support tech-
niques can alleviate the network-specific challenges asso-
ciated, specifically, with large-scale asynchronous Distributed
Reinforcement Learning (DRL) training [19,42,48,55]. To
this end, we propose an in-network DRL accelerator en-
gine called Olaf, which opportunistically aggregates asyn-
chronously generated model updates on the fly while they
traverse the queue. When compatible model updates from the
same worker or other workers of the same cluster are present

in the accelerator engine queue, Olaf combines them into

A major benefit of Olaf’s opportunistic aggregation is pre-
venting update staleness. Stale updates, those generated long
before they reach the PS, are known to degrade convergence
performance in asynchronous settings [14,15,40,45,49,61].
This issue is particularly harmful in DRL, where delayed pol-
icy updates can misrepresent the latest behavior of the worker
(agent) and negatively affect training efficiency. Specifically,
workers produce model updates by evaluating the outcome of
simulations using the model-at-hand, where staleness can be
particularly detrimental as the collected experience and the
generated update are based on an old model.
To quantify model staleness, we introduce the term Age-
of-Model (AoM), which is a proxy metric that quantifies the
utility, specifically, freshness of a model update. The average
AoM of a cluster can be related to the average convergence
time, in the case of DRL, the time required for the distributed
training to achieve a certain reward.
While in-network aggregation has been explored in prior
work for accelerating DML training [32,47,59], it is not ap-
plicable to the considered case. In a nutshell, in prior work,
the aggregation keeps the information in the switch registers
until a certain criteria is met, whereas we aim for in-flight op-
portunistic aggregation for asynchronous DRL updates. Other
prior works employ asynchronous training with periodic ag-
gregation [32,57], which introduces different criteria for syn-
chronization barriers, departing from the fully asynchronous
training procedure and risking staleness.
As congestion may still arise in the Olaf accelerator engine
with an increasing number of clusters, we introduce a worker-
side transmission control algorithm that takes advantage of
the feedback provided by the accelerator on the reverse path.
The transmission control algorithm regulates the rate at which
workers transmit updates into the network. The combination
of a transmission control algorithm and asynchronous training
is particularly efficient, as the workers may keep training until
the next transmission opportunity.
Finally, we provide a formal model for the AoM based on
the Olaf accelerator configuration and sample paths of worker-
side model updates. Using a Satisfiability Modulo Theories
(SMT) framework together with a solver, e.g., Z3 [17], we
provide a formal verification module that allows to statically
reason about the AoM of different clusters. The framework
can then be used to relate properties such as the maximum
update transmission rate to a system-wide AoM objective. For
example, we explore the case of an AoM fairness objective
such that concurrent clusters with individual maximum update
transmission rates result in comparable average AoM, i.e.,
comparable training convergence time.
We summarize the contributions of this paper as follows:
• We propose Olaf, a programmable in-network accelera-
tor that performs on the fly opportunistic aggregation of
asynchronous DRL model updates.
• We develop Olaf on a hybrid FPGA-P4 pipeline to sup-
port real-time opportunistic aggregation using an alter-
native queue design, while preserving high throughput
and reducing update losses under congestion events.
• We implement a worker-side transmission control al-
gorithm guided by in-network feedback, and provide a
quantification of model staleness that uses the Age-of-
Model as a utility metric allowing to formally verify
system-wide AoM objectives.
• We evaluate Olaf on a hardware testbed and using ns3
simulations, showing increased speedup in model up-
date delivery, significant staleness reduction, and system-
wide AoM fairness given multi-tenant, and asymmetric
network scenarios.
This paper is structured as follows, we begin with the mo-
tivation for opportunistic in-network aggregation (§ 2) and
Olaf’s design overview (§ 3). Then we detail its accelerator
engine (§ 4), describe the worker-side transmission protocol
(§ 5) and the AoM formal model (§ 6). We then cover imple-
mentation details (§ 7), evaluation setup and results (§ 8). We
follow this up with related work (§ 9), before discussing open

The Case for Asynchronous Training
Reinforcement Learning (RL) iteratively solves optimal deci-
sion problems such as robot navigation by finding policies that
map observations to actions [25,27,34,56]. Current methods
to compute optimal policies alternate between simulations
to obtain samples, also denoted collected experience, of the
currently given policy and gradient-based optimization using
objective functions to improve that policy.

with synchronous and asynchronous modes of operation, with

denote model updates, are collected, merged, and distributed

DRL. In synchronous training, workers operate in rounds
where each worker sends its update to the PS, which waits for
all updates, aggregates them, and broadcasts the global model
before the next round can begin. In asynchronous training
with periodic aggregation, workers continue training without
waiting for the global model. The PS performs aggregation in
fixed intervals or after collecting a batch of updates. The sim-
ulation and the optimization processes can run individually
on different workers or together on the same worker.
Synchronous training suffers from fundamental drawbacks:
(i) coordinating heterogeneous workers is challenging as it
involves tuning cutoff timers to close the computation round,
(ii) network incast occurs as many workers transmit updates
simultaneously, and (iii) stagglers delay global convergence.
Asynchronous training with periodic aggregation mitigates
straggler bottlenecks, but it introduces model staleness, as
workers often continue training on outdated models.
To address these limitations, we adopt an asynchronous
training approach where workers transmit updates indepen-
dently and the PS immediately responds with an updated
global model, eliminating the need for global synchronization.
For consistency, we refer to this immediate-response protocol
as asynchronous training in the remainder of the paper.

2.2
Quantifying Staleness with Age-of-Model
Assuming a large-scale fully asynchronous DRL training
mode, model staleness at the aggregation function at the PS
describes the passage of time since the latest update from
any worker in the cluster. As this global model at the PS is
3


## Page 4


distributed to the workers upon every update aggregation, the
staleness of this model is also the staleness of the model used
for asynchronous training at the workers. Network congestion
leading to update drops and retransmissions leads to a lower
update rate at the PS and to workers asynchronously training
with old models. The underpinnings of this concept stem from
the notion of Age and Value of Information (AoI/VoI), which
describe the staleness of information or the timeliness value
of updates at a receiver, respectively [60].
AoM defines the staleness1 of a model update as the time
that has passed at the PS since the reception of the latest
model update. Note that when a model update from a worker
is generated, it obsoletes any previous update from the same
worker in the sense that it subsumes the experience collected
by the previous model update. The AoM is a semi-continuous
"sawtooth" function growing linearly with time and jumping
on the event of receiving a model update to the age of that
update, i.e., how much time has passed since this update was
generated (See §6 for an example sketch). The AoM not only
depends on the delay between the worker and the PS but also
on the generation and transmission times at the worker.
Why AoM?. As the AoM provides the age of the updates,
we can use it as a proxy metric, first, for the average time
required to achieve a specific reward (given the behavior in
Fig. 3, i.e., given a fixed number of required model updates to
achieve a certain reward a lower average age implies that these
updates were received in an overall shorter time span. Second,
beyond its average, the AoM function reveals through its peak
values the times at which the large-scale training application
is not performing well, either due to network congestion or
slow worker-side update generation, or a combination of both.
Finally, Olaf uses the AoM as a proxy metric for fairness in
the multi-tenant case, where a formal verification module (cf.
Fig. 1) computes whether the worker-side update transmission
parameters lead to large AoM disparities between clusters.

A special case is updates from the worker, who only finds
an older update from the same worker waiting. In some asyn-
chronous modes, such as Multi-agent distributed RL in [32],
workers can inject multiple updates before receiving a new
aggregate model/policy. A key observation is that the more
recent update from the same worker, with a smaller AoM, sub-
sumes the older update as it has collected more experience.
Hence, the acceleration engine replaces the older update in
the queue by the new one. Note that a model aggregation
resets the subsuming property of subsequent updates from
the same worker, i.e., replacement occurs if and only if two
unaggregated models of the same worker meet in the queue.
As Olaf is designed for asynchronous DRL training, it does
not keep the updated model on the accelerator engine waiting
to collect a certain number of updates, but only compresses
updates opportunistically whenever multiple updates of the
same cluster are arriving/waiting in the queue. In case many
workers simultaneously send their updates, Olaf mitigates
the occurring incast by compressing all updates of the same
cluster in the queue into a single model update.
As Olaf aims for convergence-preserving aggregation of
asynchronous updates, it needs to filter low-reward updates.
When a new update arrives, Olaf compares its reward value
with an existing update from the same cluster already in the
queue. If the rewards are comparable, i.e., the difference is
within a predefined threshold, the gradients are aggregated. If
the incoming update has a higher reward surpassing the thresh-
old, it replaces the existing one. Conversely, if the reward
is significantly lower, Olaf drops the update. This adaptive
mechanism ensures the queue retains convergence-preserving
updates, which we have empirically observed to lead to more
stable and efficient convergence at the PS.
Transmission Control Function. When workers transmit
updates to the PS immediately after generating a fresh update,
simultaneous transmissions from a large number of different
clusters can cause network congestion [10,53]. Olaf workers
base the transmission decisions on the network’s current sta-
tus. Here, ACKs traversing the accelerator engine retrieve the
status of the bottleneck queue as they travel back to the work-
ers. Olaf assumes symmetric forward and backward paths
as usual if deployed in a ToR switch. This mechanism en-
ables the transmission control function at the worker to make
informed decisions on when to fetch and transmit a model up-
date based on the latest queue status, helping to avoid conges-
tion and minimize the average AoM. Specifically, the worker
can keep collecting experience and send model updates while
avoiding network congestion.

In-Network Opportunistic Aggregation
This section presents how Olaf performs opportunistic aggre-
gation of DRL updates from asynchronous workers in the
data plane. To perform model aggregation or replacement, the
Acceleration engine (see Fig. 4a) must distinguish between
updates based on their source of origin. Therefore, in Olaf, we
assign each worker a Worker_ID and Cluster_ID, and their
combination uniquely identifies updates from each worker.
The comparison between the IDs of the incoming updates and
those already in the queue determines whether the update is
aggregated with an existing one, replaces an older one, or is
appended to the tail of the queue. The update identifier (cf.
Fig. 4a) extracts the Worker_ID and Cluster_ID from the
incoming updates and forwards them, along with the update
itself, to the OlafQueue for processing.
OlafQueue. The task of the queue is to perform enqueuing
and dequeuing operations after receiving the update. To aggre-
gate or replace the updates during enqueuing, the accelerator
engine must search for and locate any existing update in the
queue that matches the Cluster_ID or the Worker_ID of
the incoming update. To enable this functionality, we design
the OlafQueue to track and modify updates as they traverse
through it. Update enqueue logic depends on the current queue
content, as discussed earlier, while dequeuing occurs sequen-
tially from the queue head.
Memory Organization. We strictly define the address bound-
aries in the queue memory to precisely locate the queued up-
dates. The total memory available in the queue is divided into
fixed-size memory segments. In this work, we consider mod-
els that fit into one frame and set the memory segment size
accordingly. In the discussion section (§10), we explore the
implications of model fragmentation. The memory segments,
each capable of storing one update, form the fundamental
blocks for handling updates in the queue.
We define four data structures to track the posi-
tion and process the queued updates.
The structure
available_mem_addrs stores the empty segment ad-
dresses where an incoming update can be stored, while
out_mem_addrs, maintains segment addresses of outgoing
updates in sequential order. The structure cluster_status
stores the pointer to the update in the queue per cluster. Com-
plementarily, replace_status tracks whether a cluster holds
a replaceable update from a worker. It contains a one-bit
replace_flag indicating replaceability (1 if replaceable, 0
otherwise) and a field storing the corresponding Worker_ID
to enable update replacement from the same worker.
We use four pointers to coordinate the enqueue and de-
queue processes in the queue. The pointer write_ptr selects
the next segment address from available_mem_addrs to
store incoming updates, while read_ptr retrieves the next
segment address from out_mem_addrs to fetch outgoing up-
dates. The pointer append_out_addr places the address of
each incoming update at the tail of out_mem_addrs, and
append_available_addr stores the next free segment ad-
dress at the tail of available_mem_addrs once the update
in that segment is sent out. These four cyclic registers drive
the queue operations.

the

While enqueuing, the queue adds the pointer pointing

By leveraging cluster_status, and out_mem_addrs, the

5
Worker-side Transmission Control
The data plane acceleration engine described in §4 is indepen-
dent of the worker-side update transmission control algorithm.
Next, we describe the rationale behind the worker-side update
transmission rate control that arises through the alternative
queue design in the Olaf queue. We then describe a feedback
mechanism from the accelerator engine to the workers and
finally worker-side transmission control to handle congestion
in the queue.
How should the workers pick a transmission rate? Given
that the goal of the large-scale DRL training application is
to minimize staleness, i.e., AoM, the workers need to pick a
transmission rate that keeps congestion low. The alternative
queue design of Olaf, specifically, the opportunistic aggrega-
tion per cluster, implies that congestion only arises with Olaf
when the number of active clusters exceeds the number of
queue memory slots (queue size). This is due to the queue
keeping at most one update at a time from every cluster, and
that each update exactly requires one memory segment. As
long as the number of clusters is less than the available mem-
ory segments in the queue the workers can transmit updates
at the highest possible rate which also minimizes the AoM.
If the number of active clusters exceeds the queue size the
workers have to pick a transmission rate that collectively re-
duces congestion, hence, reducing the update losses affecting
the AoM. Next, we propose a feedback-based transmission
control algorithm that acts as an arbitration scheme.
Reverse-path Signaling. Workers can make informed deci-
sions about whether to transmit model updates, if they have
information about the recent status of the queue. Specifically,
knowing if there is congestion or not (the number of active
clusters being higher or less than the queue size) can guide
the workers to control their transmission rate. Inspired by
Reverse-path Congestion Marking [6,20], we leverage ACKs
on their way back to workers to additionally carry queue status
updates (see Fig. 4a). This approach provides workers with re-
cent OlafQueue congestion status while avoiding additional
status update traffic in the system. We discuss the implications
of reverse path signaling for the switch architecture in §10.
Worker-side Protocol. For now, we consider the topology in

Verifying AoM Objectives
As staleness reduction is a major driver behind Olaf, we need
to explore staleness within different clusters that concurrently
use the same Olaf queue. To this end, we consider the final
component of Olaf (cf. § 3) that is a static formal verifier
that allows reasoning about staleness, i.e., AoM objectives
across clusters. This component consists of (i) a set of formal
constraints, i.e., a formal model, describing the AoM, together
with (ii) parameters of the clusters, and (iii) a query on an ob-
jective function of the resulting AoM functions of all clusters.
The static verifier takes the formal model and the parameters
together with the query to produce a binary result, either that
the parameterized model satisfies the objective function or
not. We will show how this is used to reason about a specific
objective function related to AoM fairness between clusters.
We envision that this can be used in admission control for
new clusters or for constraining the cluster parameter ranges
to adhere to given AoM objectives. This component draws
inspiration from CCAC [5].

Implementation
We implement Olaf’s accelerator engine on an FPGA-based
accelerator card [2]. We envision that such prototypical imple-
mentation can be integrated into future ToR switches that com-
bine switching processors and different hardware acceleration
platforms [13]. OlafQueue, which consists of all queuing-
related operations, is written in Verilog. The Update Identi-
fier (Figure 4a), which extracts Cluster_ID and Worker_ID
from the incoming updates and embeds the queue status in the
ACKs, is implemented in P4 [7]. This combination of Verilog
and P4 works harmoniously, leveraging the strengths of both
languages. P4’s parsing, deparsing, and lookup engines make
it a natural choice for identifying updates and embedding
queue status in ACKs, as these tasks involve header parsing,
using match-action tables to extract IDs, and deparsing the
update to embed queue status in ACKs. Meanwhile, Verilog
simplifies queue management, making it more efficient to
handle queuing operations.
Packet Format. End hosts use UDP packets to transmit model
updates. The packet includes 304 bits of header fields (Ether-
net, IPv4, and UDP), followed by the mean reward of local
iteration and the gradients. ACKs on their way back to the
workers contain the aggregated gradients. The reverse-path
signaling adds the queue status fields, up to 24-bit queue
utilization, and 16-bit # active clusters.
Olaf Packet Processing Pipeline. We prototype Olaf on
Alveo U55C accelerator cards [2], which contains the Vir-
tex XCU55 UltraScale+ FPGA. The Olaf accelerator engine
utilizes only 3.52% of the available LUTs, 1.5% of the regis-
ters as flip-flops, and 1% of the multiplexers (MUXes) on the
FPGA, demonstrating its lightweight hardware footprint. For
prototyping Olaf, we used the AMD OpenNIC shell [58], an
FPGA-based NIC framework that provides a high-speed, mod-
ular networking platform with well-defined data and control
interfaces for seamless integration of custom user logic. Open-
NIC includes core components that implement host and Ether-
net interfaces, along with two user logic blocks for integrating
RTL plugins. We place Olaf’s Acceleration Engine in one of
these user blocks, operating at 250 MHz. We implement the
OlafQueue design from §3 using hardware registers (FPGA
Flip-Flops) to support various packet operations/aggregations,
running as fast as 50 Gbps.
To convert the P4 design into an FPGA-compatible module,
we use Vitis Networking P4 (VNP4) [3], which enables the
integration with other IPs over the AXI4-stream protocol.
We design OlafQueue to use the AXI4-stream protocol [4]
as an interface to communicate with VNP4 modules without
any bridge in between. The Olaf pipeline within the User
Logic block of the OpenNIC shell consists of three stages:
the first P4 block (VNP41), then Verilog-based OlafQueue,
and finally the second P4 block (VNP42). These components
communicate using the AXI4-Stream protocol.
Uplink (Workers to PS). VNP41 receives updates and first
checks whether they originate from known worker nodes by
matching the source IP. Packets not matching any known
worker or the parameter server are treated as non-DRL traf-
fic and bypass all model-related processing. For valid model
updates, VNP41 extracts the Worker_ID (computed as a five-
tuple hash of the UDP packet) and assigns a Cluster_ID by
mapping the worker to a multicast group. The extracted meta-
data (Cluster_ID and Worker_ID) is forwarded (as AXIS
user signal) along with the update to the OlafQueue, which
performs opportunistic aggregation. The outgoing update is
forwarded unmodified by VNP42 to the parameter server.
Downlink (PS to Workers). On the downlink, VNP41 pro-
cesses ACKs identical to the uplink by extracting meta-
data Worker_ID and Cluster_ID) and forwarding them to
the OlafQueue. These packets are identified as non-DML
packets, and Olaf forwards them without queue interaction.
For Reverse-path signaling, VNP42 embeds queue status re-
ceived by OlafQueue (as AXIS user signal) in the ACK and
multicasts the ACKs to all workers in the corresponding
Cluster_ID using preconfigured multicast groups. This en-
8


## Page 9


sures that all workers contributing to an aggregated update
receive the latest global model and queue status.
P4 Control Plane. The P4 control plane interfaces with the ac-
celerator engine to dynamically configure the match-action ta-
bles of the P4 blocks. This capability allows for flexible adap-
tation to evolving training workloads, such as re-grouping
workers into existing clusters or forming new clusters on the
fly, thereby supporting multi-tenant distributed training jobs.

Evaluation
First, we describe the testbed setup and the evaluation method-
ology before presenting microbenchmark results comparing
Olaf’s performance against a traditional FIFO queue on the
data plane. We show then how Olaf improves the training
speed before finally analyzing the impact of Olaf’s worker-
side transmission control in a multi-hop network scenario.
Testbed Setup. Our testbed consists of two Alveo U55C ac-
celerator cards [2] and a 32×100 Gbps Intel Tofino switch [22]
in addition to a number of end-host servers. Each Alveo card
is equipped with 2×100 Gbps QSFP ports. We use scaled
real-world distributed RL traces from our own cluster (cf. the
experimental underpinning in § 2.1) to emulate 27 workers
into 9 separate clusters2, each transmitting model updates to
the parameter server. We implement Olaf’s accelerator side
on the U55C using the OpenNIC framework, as described in
§ 7. To ensure that all packets and their corresponding ACKs
traverse through Olaf’s accelerator side pipeline, we configure

Olaf aggregates model updates. An important advantage
of Olaf lies in its ability to perform in-network model update
aggregation, which significantly enhances resource efficiency
during congestion. When the queue is full, Olaf does not im-
mediately drop incoming updates. Instead, if a newly arriving
update originates from the same cluster as one of the updates
that are already in the queue, it is aggregated with the existing
update rather than discarded. This behavior reduces the vol-
ume of traffic sent to the PS, and it alleviates incast congestion
without sacrificing the information richness of the updates.
Particularly in the context of DRL, where each model update
encapsulates experience derived from the agent’s interaction
with the environment, such aggregation results in outgoing
models that carry higher cumulative experience. In contrast to
a FIFO queue, which drops these updates under congestion,
Olaf enables the delivery of richer models while maintaining
a lower transmission load. This ensures that the PS continues
to receive updates that are both fresh and meaningful, even
under network congestion.

a worker-side transmission control algorithm can improve
AoM fairness, Olaf’s acceleration engine alone is sufficient
to support AoM-reducing opportunistic aggregation without
requiring any modifications at the end host.

Discussion and Future Work
In this section, we outline the trade-offs of Olaf’s design and
highlight potential directions for future improvements.
Update Fragmentation. Unlike other in-network aggregation
frameworks such as ATP [28], SwitchML [47], and iSW [32],
Olaf’s current implementation does not support update frag-
mentation, requiring that the entire model gradient fits within
a single network packet. This imposes constraints on the size
of the neural networks that can be used. Note that the im-
provements shown in the testbed results in §8 are all given
already for model sizes that fit into a Jumbo frame. This
design decision stems from Olaf’s commitment to support
asynchronous training at line rate, which avoids stalling the
queueing system to reassemble fragmented updates, a process
incompatible with low-latency, low-staleness model updates.
Fig. 3 highlights that ideal asynchronous training (employed
in Olaf) significantly outperforms both synchronous and asyn-
chronous training with periodic aggregation methods, such as
those used in SwitchML and iSW, thereby motivating Olaf’s
architecture choices. Although this limitation may seem re-
strictive, recent work in gradient compression and efficient
model design demonstrates that smaller neural networks can
often achieve convergence performance comparable to that of
larger models in reinforcement learning and federated learn-
ing settings [39,43,46,54]. Such strategies can be integrated
into Olaf as future extensions, enabling it to maintain per-
formance while operating within predefined model size con-
straints.
Increased Downlink Traffic. Asynchronous training, where
the PS responds to each model update, lead to increased down-
link traffic towards the worker clusters. To address this, Olaf
reduces the number of updates reaching the PS through per-
cluster in-network aggregation, limiting how often responses
must be sent. Moreover, the PS sends ACKs addressed to each
cluster, not individual workers, and these ACKs are broadcast
only at the final hop using multicast in the VNP42 block. This
minimizes redundant downlink traffic.
Future Work. The presented alternative queue design of
Olaf is applicable beyond asynchronous DRL, e.g. for in-
network support in live video streaming, where segment re-
placements may avoid stalling events at the client side. Fur-
ther, the worker-side transmission control may utilize the
queue feedback beyond the binary states of congestion to
predict congestion dynamics and adopt early on.

In this paper, we present Olaf, a programmable in-network
accelerator tailored for large-scale, asynchronous Distributed
Reinforcement Learning. Olaf addresses model staleness

12.1
Additional Details on the Hardware
Implementation
Handling concurrency. In Olaf, we ensure that no more than
one update belonging to a given Cluster_ID is in the queue
at any given time. In the following we provide the corner
cases where an incoming update cannot replace or be aggre-
gated with an existing one. This situation arises when the
existing update is locked as it is at the queue head and already
scheduled for departure or when parts of it have already ex-
ited the queue. To track such cases and manage cluster-level
queuing, OlafQueue maintains per-cluster metadata using
cluster_head and cluster_tail circular pointers. These
pointers identify the positions of the first and second updates
in the queue per cluster. The associated cluster_status
register maps each cluster_head to its queue index, which
is then linked to the out_mem_addr used to dequeue the cor-
responding update. Since cluster_head and cluster_tail
are circular and may wrap around, Olaf uses a three-column
cluster_status. This design ensures that, at any given time,
the OlafQueue can hold and distinguish up to two active up-
dates per cluster, while also detecting when the cluster has
no updates in the queue (i.e., when cluster_head equals
cluster_tail).
OlafQueue Latency Breakdown. The OpenNIC shell’s inter-
face supports data movement of 512 bits per clock cycle, and
therefore, we restrict each memory block in the OlafQueue
to be 512 bits (64 bytes) in size. To store a standard Eth-
ernet packet of 1500 bytes, we combine 24 memory blocks
into a memory segment. Given that Olaf operates at a clock
frequency of 250MHz (i.e., 4ns per cycle), transmitting a
1500 byte packet requires 96ns. For a model update, which
we encode within a Jumbo frame of 9036 bytes (as in § 8),
Olaf requires 1.15µs (1152ns) to transmit the complete packet
through the pipeline.

SMT framework can be used to ask queries in form of logical
first-order formula with predicates over the AoM processes
and deduce corresponding admissible regions for the configu-
ration of the worker-side transmission control functions.
