ABSTRACT
The discovery of the third interstellar object (ISO), 3I/ATLAS (‘3I’), provides a rare chance to
directly observe a small body from another Solar System. Studying its chemistry and dynamics will
add to our understanding of how the processes of planetesimal formation and evolution happen across
the Milky Way’s disk, and how such objects respond to the Milky Way’s potential. In this Letter, we
present a first assessment of 3I in the context of the ¯Otautahi–Oxford model, which uses data from
Gaia in conjunction with models of protoplanetary disk chemistry and Galactic dynamics to predict
the properties of the ISO population. The model shows that both the velocity and radiant of 3I are
within the expected range. Its velocity predicts an age of over 7.6 Gyr and a high water mass fraction,
which may become observable shortly. We also conclude that it is very unlikely that 3I shares an origin
with either of the previous two interstellar object detections.

1. INTRODUCTION
The discovery by the ATLAS survey (Tonry et al.
2018) of the third interstellar object (ISO), 3I/ATLAS
(hereafter ‘3I’)1, allows us a rare chance to closely
study the product of processes of planetesimal formation
around another star. Though estimates of the popula-
tion size (∼1026, see e.g. Do et al. 2018) suggest that
such interstellar objects are the most common macro-
scopic objects in the Milky Way, their small size and
rapid motion has meant that few have yet been observed.
The discovery of 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov (here-
after ‘1I’ and ‘2I’ respectively) inspired the development
of a new field of ISO studies. While it had been expected
that the ISO population would be primarily cometary

2I, discovered nearly two

of ISOs—from scattering in the outer regions of proto-

to more exotic possibilities, such as fragmentation of
exo-Pluto surfaces (Jackson & Desch 2021) or hydro-

by Pfalzner & Bannister (2019) that ISOs incorporated

the upcoming Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Sur-

2. METHODS
Being products of stars and their planetary systems,
the properties of the ISO population of the Milky Way
can be predicted from the stellar population. We do this
using the method of Hopkins et al. (2025), which we out-
line briefly here. Using the subset of stars within 200 pc
of the Sun with measured velocity, metallicity and age in
Gaia DR3 (Gaia Collaboration et al. 2023), we estimate
the solar neighbourhood sine morte stellar population.
This is what the stellar population would be if stars did
not die, and is needed to predict the ISO population
because, unlike stars, ISOs are not removed from the
Galactic population at a significant rate. Using a proto-
planetary disk model, we then reweight this sine morte
stellar distribution in velocity, metallicity and age into a
predicted ISO distribution in velocity, composition and
age.
The key assumptions of the model, justified in de-
tail in Hopkins et al. (2025), are that: (i) the number
of ISOs released by a star is proportional to its mass
and metallicity, (ii) that scattering of ISOs happens ef-
ficiently early in each planetary system’s lifetime at rel-
atively low speeds (≲10 km s−1) and from beyond the
water ice line, and (iii) that ISOs are long-lived, outlast-
ing their parent stars which may have undergone stellar
death. Finally, the model assumes (iv) that the current
solar neighbourhood sine morte stellar population, with
appropriate reweighting, can be used as a proxy for the
population of ISOs currently passing through the So-
lar System. This does not assume that these currently-
nearby stars are the sources of the ISOs we observe in the
Solar System, but instead that the stars and the ISOs
trace the same Galactic potential and source popula-
tions. Indeed, most observed ISOs will be from distant
stars (Forbes et al. 2024)
The resulting ¯Otautahi–Oxford (¯O–O) model predicts
correlations between ISO velocity and properties such
as composition and age, mostly inherited from simi-
lar correlations in the stellar populations. These cor-
relations mean that inferences can be made about the
physical properties of a discovered ISO from its veloc-
ity alone. The ISO velocity distribution is highly non-
Gaussian and correlated with ISOs’ physical properties,
and so to predict the properties of a single ISO relative to
the model population, only the properties of ISOs from
stars with velocities close to the ISO’s velocity (within
5 km s−1) are considered when drawing distributions.

4. AGE ESTIMATES FOR 3I/ATLAS USING THE
AGE–VELOCITY DISPERSION RELATION
Though the correlation between the velocity of a single
object and its age is weak, there is a well-established re-
sult that the velocity dispersion of populations of objects
in the Milky Way disk increases with age. This so-called
age–velocity dispersion relation (AVR) allows the age of
an object to be estimated. The AVR is thought to be
caused by repeated scatterings over time by large-scale
structures, such as the spiral arms and giant molecu-
lar clouds (Gustafsson et al. 2016). It should be noted
that the contribution from scattering of close passages
with other stars is negligible, due to the long scattering
timescale of 107 Gyr (Forbes et al. 2024), so the large
velocity of 3I is not a indication that it has undergone
a close encounter with a star before the Sun.
Here, we use a simplified version of the method of
Almeida-Fernandes & Rocha-Pinto (2018) to provide
confidence intervals on the age of an ISO, showing the
calculated age posterior distribution for 3I in Fig. 3, and
1I and 2I in Fig. 5. Almeida-Fernandes & Rocha-Pinto
(2018)’s method models the 3D velocity distribution of
ISOs as a Gaussian with dispersion increasing with age.
However, as shown in the U–V plot of Fig. 1, the actual
stellar or ISO velocity distribution is not well approxi-
mated by a Gaussian distribution4: there are large gaps
and over-densities, and the LSR neither lies near the cen-
tre of the velocity distribution nor at a peak. In order

examine the possibility that 3I shares a common parent
system with either 1I or 2I.

intrinsically low probability of seeing an ISO from the
same stream, given the large number of streams through
which the Sun is likely currently passing (Forbes et al.

6. UNUSUAL ASPECTS OF 3I/ATLAS
Early reports show that at its current heliocentric dis-
tance rh ∼4.4 au, 3I is a quietly active comet with a
compact coma (Seligman et al. 2025; Jewitt & Luu 2025;
Alarcon et al. 2025); hence the absolute magnitude of 3I
from the earliest photometry (HV = 11.9±0.6) is an up-
per limit on the true nucleus size.7 The larger inferred
size of 3I (on the order of ≲10 km) compared to 1I
and 2I (∼0.1–1 km) may constrain the size-frequency
distribution (SFD) of the intrinsic galactic ISO popu-
lation in interesting ways. Estimation of the intrinsic
SFD slope based on the detections of 1I and 2I suggests
a steep value (q ∼3-4, Jewitt et al. 2020), i.e. small
ISOs are much more common than large ISOs in the
Galaxy. Additionally, simulations of future ISO discov-
eries within LSST show that their expected median size

This also aligns with tentative number density es-
timates for objects larger than 3I, n (H ≤12)

Their value ig-

order-of-magnitude estimate until full survey simula-

out (Dorsey et al., in prep); however, it can be used
to roughly estimate the slope of the ISO SFD. Using
the number density estimates for objects larger than 1I
(n (H ≤22) ∼10−1 au−3; Do et al. 2018) and 3I, the
ISO SFD can be modelled empirically by a power law
in H with slope of α ∼0.2, equivalent at fixed albedo
to a slope in diameter of q ∼2. While Dorsey et al.
(2025) demonstrated that a single-slope power law with
q < 2.5 is not physically feasible for ISOs (indicating
that a multi-slope power law is necessary instead), this
extrapolation does suggest that the SFD of ISOs is likely
shallower than was previously predicted using 1I and 2I,
or perhaps wavy like other Solar System populations
(Bottke et al. 2023).
The orbital orientation of 3I is also unanticipated.
Due to the Galactic motion of the Sun, ISOs preferen-
tially infall to the Solar System from the direction of the
Solar Apex in the North Celestial Hemisphere (McGlynn
& Chapman 1989; Seligman & Laughlin 2018). Obser-
vatories based in the Southern Hemisphere are therefore
observationally biased to discover objects on orbits with
perihelia in the Southern Celestial Hemisphere, i.e. ar-
guments of perihelia 180◦≤ω ≤360◦.
The ATLAS
Chile observatory and the Rubin Observatory are lo-
cated at similar latitudes (30.47◦and 30.24◦S respec-
tively) and can be assumed to have rather similar point-
ing biases. Dorsey et al. (2025) used the ¯O–O model to
determine that while all orbital ω of ISOs are discover-
able with non-zero probability in LSST, orbits with per-
ihelia in the Southern Hemisphere dominate the discov-
ered population (∼70% of objects). Of the orbits with
perihelia in the Northern Hemisphere (0◦≤ω ≤180◦),
those with argument of perihelia 60◦≤ω ≤150◦were
most strongly biased against discovery. Specifically, the
likelihood that an ISO discovered in LSST will have an
orbit similar to 3I (125◦≤ω ≤130◦) is ∼1%. By com-
parison, the arguments of perihelia8 for 1I and 2I are
a factor of ∼3–4 times more discoverable in the same
survey. The discovery of an ISO on an orbit like that
of 3I by a Southern Hemisphere telescope could simply
be a rare happenstance (an unlikely but not impossible
random draw from the ¯O–O model), a yet-unmodelled
ISO stream encountering the Solar System, or perhaps
evidence toward a different model of Galactic ISO orbits.
7. CONCLUSION
The third known ISO, 3I/ATLAS, has physical differ-
ences to the first two ISOs detected, but its orbit and
incoming velocity place it as a member of the Galactic
population of ISOs. We find that though 3I/ATLAS is
entering the Solar System at a higher speed than 1I or 2I,
this is still well within the expected distribution of ISO
speeds inferred from the ¯Otautahi–Oxford model. The
model predicts that ISOs with 3I’s velocity are generally
more water-rich. While at 3I’s present rh water would
be challenging to detect, this should be observable in
its coma once 3I crosses into water-ice sublimation dis-
tances within ∼3 au.
3I/ATLAS’s direction of approach is more southerly
than the bulk of expected ISOs the Sun will encounter,
due to its very large vertical motion out of the Galactic
plane. From its kinematics, we rule out the possibility
that 3I/ATLAS comes from the same star, or same clus-
ter, as either 1I or 2I, but the velocity does tell us about
its origin: it shares its Galactic orbit with older and
lower-metallicity stars. Though the constraints are not
tight and an exact determination of age is not possible,
our models suggest 3I’s age is likely to be over 7.6 Gyr,
older than 1I, 2I, and all Solar System objects.
The rich evidence that an ISO’s velocity contains in its
chemodynamics demonstrate the benefit of rapid orbital
arc determination by the community. ISOs provide the
opportunity to gain evidence of the process of planetesi-
mal formation and evolution from a host of Galactic en-
vironments, and further observations of 3I/ATLAS will
allow us to constrain and test the assumptions made in
the ¯Otautahi–Oxford model.
