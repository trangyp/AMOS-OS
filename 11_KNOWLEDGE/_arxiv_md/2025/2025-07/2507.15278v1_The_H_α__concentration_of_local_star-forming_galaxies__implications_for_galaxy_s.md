ABSTRACT
In this work, we present a study on the Hα emission line ﬂux concentration of 3098 low-redshift star-forming
galaxies (SFGs) using the MaNGA data available in the Data Release 17 from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey.
We deﬁne the Hα ﬂux concentration index (CHα) as CHα = FHα,0.8 Re/FHα,1.5 Re, where FHα,0.8 Re and FHα,1.5 Re
are the cumulative Hα ﬂux inside 0.8 and 1.5 r−band effective radius, respectively. We ﬁnd that CHα is strongly
correlated with the luminosity weighted stellar age gradient. CHα is also sensitive to environmental effects, in
the sense that low-mass satellite galaxies below the star formation main sequence tend to have higher CHα. For
central galaxies, we ﬁnd that massive disk galaxies with enhanced star formation rate tend to have higher CHα,
while such a phenomenon is not seen in the low-mass regime. We interpret this as evidence that compaction
events more frequently occur in the high-mass regime, which eventually resulting in the buildup of prominent
bulges in massive SFGs. Implications of these ﬁndings on galaxy structure formation are discussed.
Keywords: galaxies: evolution
1. INTRODUCTION
The spatially resolved star formation properties of galax-
ies play a crucial role in understanding how galaxies grow
and evolve.
In the literature, rest-frame ultraviolet (UV)
and Hα ﬂux are two most frequently used star formation in-
dicators, tracing star formation rates (SFRs) on timescales
of < 100 Myrs and < 20 Myrs, respectively (Kennicutt
1998).
Observationally, interstellar dust absorbs starlight
and introduce large uncertainties in SFR measurements,
especially in the UV band (∼1300 −2500 Å). To obtain
attenuation-corrected SFRs, infrared (IR) data are needed,
as starlight removed at short wavelengths by the inter-
stellar dust is re-radiated in the IR (Calzetti et al. 2007;
Kennicutt et al. 2009; Hao et al. 2011). Based on spatially re-
solved UV-optical-IR photometric data, various topics such
as the star formation law (Bigiel et al. 2008; Wyder et al.
2009; Sun et al. 2023), star formation quenching (Pan et al.
2014, 2016), spatially resolved star formation main se-
quence (Abdurro’uf & Akiyama 2017; Enia et al. 2020) are
explored.
panzz@pmo.ac.cn
In the past decade, the advent of large integral ﬁeld spec-
troscopy (IFS) surveys [such as CALIFA, Sánchez et al.
(2012), MaNGA, Bundy et al. (2015), SAMI, Bryant et al.
(2015) ] enables more in depth studies on the resolved star
formation properties of galaxies. With spectroscopic data,
dust correction can be more accurately estimated based
on emission line diagnostics such as the Balmer decre-
ment. There have been many studies focusing on the spa-
tially resolved star formation properties at z < 0.2 (e.g.,
Pérez et al. 2013; Cano-Díaz et al. 2016; Ellison et al. 2018;
Bluck et al. 2020). Among these works, the SFR (or spe-
ciﬁc star formation rate, sSFR) radial proﬁles are widely
used to characterize the star formation distribution of galax-
ies (González Delgado et al. 2016; Schaefer et al. 2017;
Belﬁore et al. 2018; Medling et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2018,
2019; Tang et al. 2020).
Some studies presented the SFR
radial gradients derived from modeling the SFR radial pro-
ﬁles with linear ﬁt (Schaefer et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019;
Sánchez et al. 2022). Physically, star formation is triggered
through the collapse of giant molecular clumps. Thus star-
forming regions often exhibit clumpy structures in galaxies
(also see Figure 1). In this regard, a linear ﬁt to the SFR
radial proﬁles, as done in some previous works, may provide
a poor description to the SFR spatial distribution.

The spatial distribution of star formation properties also
provide valuable insights to understand the structure for-
mation of galaxies. At z = 2 −3, previous works have re-
vealed that a signiﬁcant population of disk galaxies host
highly dust obscured cores, indicating that these galaxies are
rapidly building up dense bulges via central starburst events
(Tadaki et al. 2017; Fujimoto et al. 2018).
Recently, the
spatially-resolved Hα studies on normal SFGs near the star
formation main sequence have greatly improved our under-
standing of galaxy structure formation at z > 2 (Wilman et al.
2020; Matharu et al. 2024; Shen et al. 2024). Using a sample
of ∼3600 SFGs from MaNGA, Pan et al. (2023) showed that
the fraction of SFGs with positive stellar age gradient peak
near log(M∗/M⊙) = 10, indicating that local SFGs build up
central mass most actively in this mass regime.
In this work, we aim to investigate the star formation dis-
tribution of galaxies through their Hα concentrations, and
link the Hα concentration to the on-going structure forma-
tion of SFGs based on the SFG sample compiled by Pan et al.
(2023).
Using the SAMI IFS data, Schaefer et al. (2017)
and Wang et al. (2022) have also investigated the star forma-
tion concentration index of SFGs, with the index deﬁned as
Cindex = log(r50,Hα/r50,cont), where r50,Hα and r50,cont are the
half light radius of Hα ﬂux and r−band continuum, respec-
tively. In this work, we will deﬁne the star formation concen-
tration index with a simpler approach. Throughout this paper,
we adopt a concordance ΛCDM cosmology with Ωm = 0.3,
ΩΛ = 0.7, H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1 and a Chabrier (2003) initial
mass function (IMF).
2. THE SAMPLE
MaNGA is the largest existing IFS survey for nearby
galaxies, which has gathered spatially resolved spectroscopic
information for ∼10,000 galaxies at z < 0.15 (Bundy et al.
2015). MaNGA uses a series of hexagonal optical ﬁber bun-
dles with different sizes (varying from 19 to 127 ﬁbers) to
cover the target galaxies, which corresponds to a ﬁeld of
view (FoV) of 12′′.5 −32′′.5 in diameter (Law et al. 2015).
The MaNGA sample is composed of the "Primary" and the
"Secondary" sample, for which the radial coverage reaches
at least out to ∼1.5 Re and ∼2.5 Re 1, respectively. In this

These selection criteria yield 3590 SFGs. To ensure that
the Hα concentration can be robustly measured, we visually
inspect the Hα map of each source via the map ﬁle produced
by the MaNGA data analysis pipeline (DAP) (Belﬁore et al.
2019; Westfall et al. 2019). When a source has a signiﬁcant
number of masked bad spaxels in its Hα map, we reject the
source from further analysis. This procedure removes 492
galaxies. Our ﬁnal sample contains 3098 SFGs.
One may consider whether the selected MaNGA SFG
sample is representative for the local SFG population. In
Pan et al. (2023), we have showed that at log(M∗/M⊙) >
10.0, our selected MaNGA SFG sample has a similar dis-
tribution as the SFG sample selected at z = [0.02,0.05] in
the Re −M∗plane. At log(M∗/M⊙) < 10.0, however, the
MaNGA SFG sample is biased against compact galaxies (see
ﬁgure 5 of Pan et al. 2023). This is mainly because low-mass
compact galaxies are difﬁcult to spatially resolved due to
their small physical sizes, thus such sources are less selected
for the MaNGA observation. In addition, the exclusion of
galaxies with small IFU sizes (the fourth bullet point) some-
what contributes to this bias. However, the main conclusion
of this work should be insensitive to this sample selection ef-
fect since we focus on disk galaxies, which have relatively
extended morphologies.
3. MEASURING THE Hα CONCENTRATION
In this work, we aim to measure the Hα ﬂux concentration
index (CHα) for the MaNGA SFG sample. In most cases, the
FoV does not cover the whole body of the target galaxy. We
thus do not deﬁne the concentration as that used in imag-
ing surveys. In the CAS system (concentration, asymme-
try, clumpiness; Conselice 2003), the concentration index
is deﬁned as C = R80/R20, where R80 and R20 is the radius
2 https://www.sdss4.org/dr17/manga/
that enclosing 80% and 20% of the total ﬂux, respectively.
In the SDSS studies, the concentration index is deﬁned as
R90/R50, where R90 and R50 is the radius that enclosing 90%
and 50% of the petrosian ﬂux, respectively (Strateva et al.
2001; Kauffmann et al. 2003). In the MaNGA survey, the
"Primary" and "Secondary" sample have different spatial res-
olutions. To enable a uniform CHα measurement for both
samples, we deﬁne the Hα concentration index based on the
Hα ﬂux distribution within 1.5 Re.
We use the growth curve method to deﬁne CHα. Before
measuring the Hα growth curve, dust attenuation correc-
tion must be applied to the observed Hα ﬂux. We use the
Balmer decrement method to quantify the dust attenuation in
star-forming (SF) spaxels. For SF regions, the Hα emission
is produced by the recombination of gas ionized by young
stars.
First, we employ the BPT diagram (Baldwin et al.
1981; Kauffmann et al. 2003) to classify the MaNGA spax-
els in to SF, composite and syfert classes. For the SF class,
we use the equation of Domínguez et al. (2013) to derive the
color excess E(B−V):
E(B−V) = 1.97log10[(Hα/Hβ)obs

For SF spaxels, the intrinsic Hα ﬂux fint,Hα is determined

We notice that we do not apply dust attenuation correc-

massive SFGs (M∗> 1010M⊙), the central regions of many
galaxies fall in these two classes (also see Figure 4). Given
this, we classiﬁed our sample into 3 categories based on the
line ratios of the central 2′′.5-diameter aperture as measured
by the Pipe3D pipleline using the SII−BPT diagram. The
conclusions of this work are based on the cSF sample, for
which the central 2′′.5-aperture regions are also classiﬁed as
the SF class. Thus our conclusions should be robust against
dust correction applied.

emission of DIG can be powered by the leaking radiation
from H II regions, or by the hot evolved stars. In a recent
work, Belﬁore et al. (2022) suggest that in z ∼0 SFGs, the

4. RESULTS
4.1. Comparisons among Hα concentration, Hα ﬂux
gradient and stellar age gradient
The Hα ﬂux radial gradient is widely used to characterize
the spatial distribution of Hα in IFS studies. In this section
we ﬁrst compare the Hα concentration with the Hα ﬂux gra-
dient drawn from the MaNGA Pipe3D catalog. In Pipe3D,
the Hα ﬂux gradient is computed as the slope of the linear ﬁt
to the Hα ﬂux radial proﬁle. The gradient is derived based on
a linear regression, and the ﬁtting is restricted at 0.5−2.0 Re.
When the FoV does not reach 2.0 Re, the regression is re-
stricted to the largest distance covered by the FoV.
Figure 3 shows the comparison between Hα concentration
and Hα gradient. Overall, galaxies with low CHα have shal-
low Hα ﬂux gradients, but the correlation between these two
quantities exhibits a large dispersion, especially in the high
CHα regime. A Spearman coefﬁcient of r = −0.57 is found
between these two quantities. We attribute the relatively poor
correlation between these two quantities to two aspects. (1)
The Hα ﬂux gradient is poorly constrained for many sources.
As can be seen in Figure 3, the Hα ﬂux gradient has a large
uncertainty for a signiﬁcant fraction of sources. This is natu-
ral since the distribution of Hα is quite clumpy, as illustrated
in Figure 1. (2) The radius range adopted in the Hα gradi-
ent ﬁtting is 0.5 −2.0 Re. As shown in previous studies, the
radial proﬁle of a physical property in the central region of a

4.2. The distribution of Hα concentration across the
star-forming population
The left panel of Figure 4 presents Hα concentration as a
function of stellar mass M∗. Based on the line ratios of the
central 2′′.5-diameter aperture provided by the Pipe3D cat-
alog, we ﬁrst classify our SFG sample into "cSF", "cLIER"
and "cSyfert" types using the SII−BPT diagram. SFGs of
different types are shown in different symbols in the ﬁgure.
As shown in Figure 4, the majority of cSF galaxies have
CHα ∼0.4. At log(M∗/M⊙) > 10.3, a signiﬁcant fraction
of SFGs are classiﬁed as cLIERs. A cLIER galaxy is typi-
cally composed by an old bulge+a star-forming disk compo-
nent, which is considered to be quenching from the inside-
out (Belﬁore et al. 2016, 2017). As can be seen, at ﬁxed M∗,
cLIERs generally have lower CHα than the cSF ones, consis-
tent with the "inside-out" star formation quenching scenario.
Some cLIERs also exhibit high CHα. Such sources are likely
low-luminosity AGNs. Syfert galaxies distribute similarly as
cLIERs in this diagram. Since we do not apply dust cor-
rection to the non-SF spaxels, in the following sections, we
focus our analysis on the cSF class.
In the right panel of Figure 4, we show the sSFR−M∗
relation for the cSF class.
The star formation main se-
quence (SFMS) of local SFGs is shown in the dashed line
(Renzini & Peng 2015).
Symbols are color-coded by Hα
concentration. To show the Hα concentration distribution
more clearly, the data are smoothed using the LOESS method
developed by Cleveland & Devlin (1988), with the PYTHON
code provided by Cappellari et al. (2013). As can be seen,
galaxies near the SFMS ridge line exhibit the lowest Hα con-
centration, while those in the upper and lower envelopes of
the SFMS show enhanced CHα. Such a trend is quite similar
to the galaxy Sersic index distribution across the SFMS, as
reported in early studies (Wuyts et al. 2011). In the follow-
ing sections, we will come back to this issue and discuss its
physical origin.
4.3. The dependence of Hα concentration on environment
The environmental effects have signiﬁcant inﬂuences in
shaping the star formation properties of galaxies. In this sec-
tion, we explore the dependence of Hα concentration upon
environment.
We classify the cSF class galaxies into central and satel-
lite galaxies based on the SDSS group catalog of Yang et al.
(2007).
As shown in previous studies, the star formation
properties of low-mass satellite galaxies are sensitive to en-
vironmental effects (Peng et al. 2012). In observations, en-
vironmental effects can cause "outside-in" star formation
quenching in low-mass galaxies. Based on the SAMI sample,
Schaefer et al. (2017) showed that galaxies in high density
environment have steepened Hα slopes, i.e., their star for-
mation distributions are more centrally concentrated. In Fig-

4.5. The dependence of Hα concentration upon SFR for
disk galaxies
The correlation between galaxy structure and CHα ex-
plains why SFGs in the upper and lower envelopes of the
SFMS have enhanced CHα (see Figure 4), since in these
regions galaxies typically have bulge-dominated morpholo-
gies (Wuyts et al. 2011). In this section, we focus on disk-
dominated SFGs. As shown in early works, disk-dominated
galaxies likely form their stellar mass in a more elon-
gated time scale and have younger stellar population age
(González Delgado et al. 2015; Méndez-Abreu et al. 2021).
Investigating the CHα distribution of these galaxies thus can
provide insights in understanding their recent structure for-
mation histories.
We ﬁrst focus on pure disk galaxies (with n < 1.5). From
Figure 6 and Figure 7, it is clear that galaxies with n < 1.5
primarily have 0.2 < CHα < 0.6, with no signiﬁcant depen-
dence on M∗. In Figure 8, we investigate the CHα depen-
dence upon ∆MS, where ∆MS is offset from the SFMS ridge
line (deﬁned as ∆MS = log(SFR/SFRMS)). We calculate the
Spearman correlation coefﬁcient between the two quantities
in 4 stellar mass bins. As shown in Figure 8, CHα shows
no clear dependence on ∆MS at log(M∗/M⊙) < 9.5. How-
ever, this trend does not hold for massive galaxies.
At
10.0 <log(M∗/M⊙) < 10.5 and log(M∗/M⊙) > 10.5, the
Spearman coefﬁcients are r = 0.36 and r = 0.24, indicating
that galaxies with higher SFRs tend to have higher CHα.
Figure 9 shows the results for disk galaxies with 1.5 < n <
2.2. Overall, the trends are similar to those shown in Fig-
ure 8. A slight difference is that the strongest correlation is
found in the log(M∗/M⊙) > 10.5 bin, with r = 0.31. This
may be partially resulted from the different ∆MS range for
galaxies with different n in the log(M∗/M⊙) > 10.5 bin. As
can be seen, in the log(M∗/M⊙) > 10.5 bin, galaxies with
1.5 < n < 2.2 span a wider ∆MS range, thus resulting in a
more pronounced relation between CHα and ∆MS.
Combined together, Figure 8 and Figure 9 suggest that the
enhancement of star formation in massive disk galaxies is
accompanied by a high star formation concentration. We will
discuss this ﬁnding in the context of galaxy evolution in the
next section.
5. DISCUSSION


## Page 9


9
Figure 9. Similar to Figure 8, but for galaxies with 1.5 < n < 2.2.
In this work, we deﬁne a parameter, the concentration of
Hα, to characterize the star formation spatial distribution of
low−z SFGs. This parameter is model free and easy to mea-
sure. We investigate the CHα distribution among low−z SFGs
and study its correlations to galaxy structure and SFRs. Be-
low we discuss the implications of our ﬁndings.
We show that CHα is quite well correlated with the lumi-
nosity weighted stellar age gradient. As stellar age gradient
is shaped by the mass assembly history of the galaxy in the
past, we interpret this as that CHα is a parameter not only
tracing the star formation spatial distribution in the past ∼10
Myrs as Hα does, but likely also reﬂecting a more longer star
formation pattern. For example, when a galaxy encounters a
compaction event, it will maintain a highCHα for a quite long
period. Thus we considerCHα as a useful indicator for study-
ing the structure formation of galaxies.
At z ∼0, SFGs near the SFMS ridge line appear to have
similar Hα concentrations, with CHα ∼0.4, as shown in Fig-
ure 4. This is because SFGs near the SFMS ridge are domi-
nated by disk galaxies with n ∼1 (Wuyts et al. 2011), thus
having similar star formation spatial distributions. At the
cosmic noon, there have been studies on the star formation
spatial distribution (Jain et al. 2024). At z ∼0, Wang et al.
(2019) have studied the star formation radial proﬁles of
MaNGA SFGs with a stacking method. In the stellar mass
range they probed (M∗> 108.5M⊙), SFGs near the SFMS
ridge have similar SFR radial proﬁles. Our results are in line
with those of Wang et al. (2019).
For central galaxies, the positive correlation between CHα
and n at low-mass regime is expected. As already well estab-
lished, the cessation of star formation in the low-mass regime
primarily acts on satellite galaxies via environmental effects,
such as starvation and ram pressure stripping (Peng et al.
2010, 2012). In low-mass central galaxies, the stellar mass
surface density (Σ∗) and star formation rate surface den-
sity (ΣSFR) follow a relatively tight positive correlation, i.e.,
the resolved star formation main sequence (Cano-Díaz et al.
2016). Thus the star formation distribution of a low-mass
central galaxy is highly correlated with its morphology. In
the high-mass regime, however, central galaxies begin to
quench following an "inside-out" pattern (Pan et al. 2015;
Belﬁore et al. 2017), thus massive galaxies with high−n do
not necessary to have high star formation concentrations.
A comparison between Figure 7 and Figure 4 suggests that
many massive SFGs are on the path transforming from the
cSF type into the cLIER type.
In the high-mass regime, we ﬁnd disk-dominated SFGs
with enhanced SFRs tend to have more centrally concen-


## Page 10


10
trated star formation distributions. This ﬁnding provides new
insights in understanding the structure formation of massive
galaxies. Using a sample of 394 MaNGA SFGs, Ellison et al.
(2018) investigated the stacked SFR radial proﬁles at differ-
ent ∆MS bins. They found that SFGs with ∆MS > 0.3 have
signiﬁcant SFR enhancement at their central regions (see
their ﬁgure 8), which is broadly consistent with the results
shown in our Figure 4. However, Ellison et al. (2018) neither
present the results for different mass bins, nor restrict to disk
galaxies, perhaps due to their small sample size. Ellison et al.
(2018) interpreted their results under a "compaction" sce-
nario, in which the central star formation enhancement is
triggered via mergers or disk instability induced gas inﬂow
(Dekel & Burkert 2014; Zolotov et al. 2015; Tacchella et al.
2016).
Wang et al. (2019) have interpreted the signiﬁcant
central star formation variation of massive SFGs using the
gas regulator model. They argued that this is because the
gas depletion time is short in the central region of massive
SFGs (see their ﬁgure 8).
If this was the case, the cen-
tral stellar mass buildup should be more efﬁcient in massive
SFGs. Regardless the detailed physics, Figure 8 and Fig-
ure 9 imply that compaction more frequently occurs in mas-
sive disk galaxies, eventually resulting a signiﬁcant central
mass buildup at log(M∗/M⊙) > 10.3 as suggested in Fig-
ure 6. This is also broadly consistent with the ﬁndings of
Pan et al. (2023).
Why there is no clear correlation between CHα and ∆MS
at low-mass? We speculate low-mass galaxies are too efﬁ-
cient to build up disk components due to their rich HI gas
reservoirs. With a high HI gas fraction, the disk build up
time scale should be short in the low-mass regime. In ob-
servations, a high HI gas fraction is coupled with a blue, ex-
tended disk component, supporting this picture (Wang et al.
2011; Chen et al. 2020; Pan et al. 2021). When a low-mass
galaxy moves to the upper envelope of the SFMS, the en-
hancement of SFR occurs across the entire galaxy, resulting
in no signiﬁcant changes in its SFR radial proﬁle (see ﬁgure
5 of Wang et al. 2019). Thus the enhancement of SFR leaves
little impact on the structure of the galaxy. In observations,
the majority of galaxies with log(M∗/M⊙) < 10.0 have Ser-
sic index n < 2.0 (see Figure 4), i.e., they are disk-dominated
systems, suggesting that disk growth overwhelmingly domi-
nates this mass regime. Following this picture, the more fre-
quent compaction events exhibited in the high-mass regime is
likely a natural consequence of inefﬁcient disk growth com-
pared to the low-mass regime, possibly due to the inefﬁcient
cool gas accretion. In the literature, the feedback from super-
massive blackholes (Croton et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2024) or
halo shock heating (Dekel & Birnboim 2006) are two most
mentioned mechanisms for halting cool gas accretion in mas-
sive galaxies. More future observation and simulation works
will shed light on this topic.
6. SUMMARY
In this work, we present a study on the Hα emission line
ﬂux concentration of ∼3000 low−z SFGs using the MaNGA
IFS data. The Hα ﬂux concentration indexCHα is determined
using the Hα ﬂux growth curve. We have found the follow-
ing.
1. CHα is strongly correlated with the luminosity weighted
stellar age gradient, supporting it as an useful indicator for
tracing galaxy assembly history. SFGs near the star forma-
tion main sequence ridge line have the lowest CHα, since they
are dominated by pure disk systems with n ∼1.
2. CHα is sensitive to environmental effects, in the sense
that low-mass satellite galaxies below the star formation
main sequence tend to have higher CHα. This is in line with
the "outside-in" star formation quenching mode exhibited in
the low-mass regime as reported in the literature.
3. Massive disk galaxies with enhanced star formation tend
to have higher CHα, while such a phenomenon is not seen
in the low-mass regime. We interpret this as evidence that
compaction events more frequently occur in the high-mass
regime, which eventually resulting in the buildup of bulges
in massive SFGs.
We conclude that CHα is a useful indicator for studying the
ongoing central mass buildup of SFGs. Our ﬁndings provide
new insights in understanding the structure formation picture
of galaxies in different mass regimes.

APPENDIX
In the main body of this work, we measure the concentration of Hα ﬂux based on the Hα growth curve out to 1.5 Re. In this
appendix we test whether our results are sensitive to the resolution effects. The angular FWHM of MaNGA sample is around
2.′′5. Normalized by Re, this corresponds to 0.2 −0.6 Re and 0.3 −0.9 Re for the Primary and Secondary sample, respectively
(Bundy et al. 2015). In this work, we focus on the correlations among CHα, M∗and n. Thus we investigate whether our measured
CHα show any additional dependence on resolution when M∗and n are ﬁxed.
We ﬁrst divide the cSF sample into two subsamples according to their morphologies, with n < 2.2 and n > 2.2. Figure 10
shows the CHα −M∗relations for these two subsamples. We use the FoV parameter from the Pipe3D catalog to characterize the
IFU resolution of a target galaxy. By deﬁnition, FoV is the IFU coverage normalized by Re. In the right column of Figure 10,
it can be seen that MaNGA galaxies have FoV larger than 1.5, i.e., the IFU coverage is out to at least 1.5 Re. Also, the FoV
distribution shows two clear peaks, corresponding to the Primary and Secondary sample, respectively.
Symbols of the CHα −M∗relation are color coded with FoV. By visually inspecting Figure 10 carefully, we ﬁnd that at ﬁxed
M∗, the distribution of CHα shows no clear dependence on FoV. This holds for galaxies with different Sersic index n. We thus
conclude that the measurement of CHα is not sensitive to the resolution effect for the MaNGA sample.
