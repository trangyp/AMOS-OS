ABSTRACT
We present the results of optical identifications for Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS) radio sources
employing the Ultraviolet Near Infrared Optical Northern Survey (UNIONS). A cross-match between UNIONS
and VLASS Epoch 2 catalogs yields 146, 212 radio galaxies down to r = 24.5 mag over a wide area of ∼
4, 200 deg2 in the northern hemisphere. We perform g−dropout selections and find ≳200 sources at z ∼4
optimistically. Of 63,019 sources with valid photometric redshifts, 8,692 are at zphoto ≥1 and 1,171 are at
zphoto ≥2. Based on spectral luminosities at 1.4 and 3 GHz using the valid UNIONS photo-z, we identify
∼49, 000 radio-loud AGNs (RLAGNs) with L1.4GHz > 1024 W Hz−1, and all radio galaxies at z ≥1 are RL.
Adopting radio loudness instead, 138,266 out of 146,212 UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies are RL. By virtue
of the wide area and medium depth provided by UNIONS, our catalog greatly increases the number counts
of RLAGNs at z > 1. We further cross-match the UNIONS-VLASS catalog with LOw-Frequency ARray
Two-metre Sky Survey (LoTSS) at 144 MHz and VLA Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty-cm (FIRST)
at 1.4 GHz, yielding 101,671 UNIONS-VLASS-LoTSS, 79,638 -FIRST, and 64,672 -LoTSS-FIRST sources,
respectively. This multifrequency radio dataset reveals sources of various spectral shapes, including the steep
spectrum of aged populations, the peaked spectrum of young populations, and the upturned spectrum that might
be associated with transient sources. The UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxy candidates will be covered by the
Euclid wide survey, bringing about legacy values to benefit multi-faceted studies related to radio galaxies and
beyond.

1. INTRODUCTION
Supermassive black holes (SMBHs) are believed to ubiq-
uitously reside in the center of most galaxies (Kormendy

& Ho 2013; McConnell & Ma 2013), and a tight correla-
tion between the mass of a SMBH and the properties of its
host galaxy has been observed since the 1990s (Magorrian
et al. 1998; Ferrarese & Merritt 2000; Gebhardt et al. 2000;
Tremaine et al. 2002).
The tight correlation implies that
SMBHs grow in tandem with the parameters of their host
galaxies, so-called “co-evolution”. How SMBHs co-evolve
with their host galaxies and how they impact their host galaxy
evolution are long-standing questions in modern astronomy.
One key phenomenon to shape such co-evolution is called
AGN feedback. Such a feedback can lead to the outflowing
and/or heating of the cold gas, suppressing the host galaxy
star formation and self-regulating the growth of the SMBH
(Fabian 2012; Alexander & Hickox 2012; Bischetti et al.
2022; Harrison & Ramos Almeida 2024; Talbot et al. 2024;
Huško et al. 2025), although a compression of cold gas that
enhances the star formation has also been observed (Shin
et al. 2019; Duncan et al. 2023). One mechanism of AGN
feedback is through the coupling between the host galaxy
ISM and the relativistic plasma, which is known as radio jets,
launched from the central SMBH. This radio/jet-mode feed-
back is considered the primary mode of AGN feedback in
radio-loud active galactic nuclei (RLAGNs).
RLAGNs are often selected via the observed radio loud-
ness Robs(= Fradio/Foptical > 10) defined as the ratio of radio-
to-optical flux densities (Kellermann et al. 1989; Balokovic
et al. 2012), or via the 1.4 GHz spectral luminosity L1.4GHz ≳
1024 W Hz−1; Peacock et al. 1986; Condon 1989; Tadhunter
2016; Padovani et al. 2017). In the local Universe, RLAGNs
are found to preferentially reside in early-type galaxies corre-
lated with high stellar masses, low star formation rates, and
radiatively inefficient AGNs (Best et al. 2005; Heckman &
Best 2014; Gürkan et al. 2015; Pierce et al. 2022; however,
see also Ramos Almeida et al. 2012; Pierce et al. 2023). At
higher redshifts (z > 1), there is an increasing number of
RLAGNs found to be radiatively efficient (Eddington ratio
λEdd ≳3×10−2; Merloni & Heinz 2008; Ichikawa et al. 2021)
and their host galaxies can be star-forming or even starburst
galaxies (Drouart et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2023; Zhong et al.
2024). These high-redshift radio galaxies (HzRGs) at z > 1
are found tightly correlated with galaxy mergers (Chiaberge
et al. 2015; Zhong et al. 2023; Heckman et al. 2024), sug-
gestive of merger-triggered star formation and launching of
radio jets. Therefore, RLAGNs across cosmic time, espe-
cially HzRGs, are valuable laboratories to study the interplay
between the host galaxy evolution, SMBH growth, and radio
jets, as well as the jet-accretion disc connections.
The rareness of RLAGNs (10 −20% among all AGN pop-
ulations; Kellermann et al. 1989; Urry & Padovani 1995;
Ivezi´c et al. 2002; Chiaberge & Marconi 2011) requires large
surveys to increase the sample size to statistically under-
stand RLAGNs, their host galaxies, and the connections in
between. Over the past decades, following the groundbreak-
ing Cambridge Catalogue of Radio Sources (e.g., Kellermann
et al. 1969), many radio sky surveys have been conducted
centered around ∼1 GHz, including the National Radio As-
tronomy Observatory (NRAO) Very Large Array (VLA) Sky
Survey (NVSS; 1.4 GHz; Condon et al. 1998) and VLA Faint
Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty-cm (FIRST; 1.4 GHz;
Becker et al. 1995), and Australian Square Kilometre Ar-
ray Pathfinder (ASKAP) Evolutionary Map of the Universe
(EMU; 944 MHz; Norris et al. 2011) and Rapid ASKAP
Continuum Survey (RACS; ∼900 −1600 MHz; McConnell
et al. 2020). The vast sky coverages (> 10, 000 deg2) of these
surveys offer great opportunities to build up an unbiased sam-
ple to elevate the statistical importance of studies related to
RLAGNs.
Wide-area optical surveys are necessary to identify the op-
tical counterparts of radio sources and determine their red-
shifts, either photometrically or spectroscopically. Using the
Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS; York et al. 2000), Ivezi´c
et al. (2002) and Helfand et al. (2015) identified the optical
counterparts for the FIRST radio core sources at 1.4 GHz,
adopting a positional cross-match between the two catalogs.
Due to the shallow optical sensitivity of SDSS r-band imag-
ing (r < 22.2 mag), the fraction of matches (fmatches) is only
∼30%, and a significant fraction of optically faint host galax-
ies may have been missed. Using the Subaru Hyper Suprime-
Cam (HSC) Subaru Strategic Program (SSP; Aihara et al.
2018), Yamashita et al. (2018) reaches fmatches = 50.6% op-
timistically for FIRST sources in the HSC field thanks to the
depth of HSC reaching a limiting magnitude of 25.9 in i-
band. Despite an improvement in associating radio sources
with faint objects, elevating the fairness of the radio galaxy
catalog, the survey area is 1, 400 deg2 such that the catalog
size is limited.
Other than the 1.4 GHz continuum, radio galaxy catalogs
have been constructed centering at 3 GHz and 144 MHz.
Based on the largest sample of uniformly selected and spec-
trally confirmed quasars provided by SDSS, Arnaudova et al.
(2024) identified 3,697 RL and 111,132 RQ sources in LOw-
Frequency ARray (LOFAR) observations at 144 MHz via the
radio loudness dichotomy. With 384 h observations of the
upgraded Karl G. Jansky VLA, the VLA-COSMOS 3 GHz
Large Project (Smolˇci´c et al. 2017) identifies 10,830 radio
sources in the COSMOS field. This project is equipped with
a sensitivity better than that of FIRST by almost two orders
of magnitude, greatly exploring the RQ populations. These
catalogs are advantageous explorations in the faint end of ra-
dio galaxies; however, they do not significantly expand the
number counts of RLAGNs.
To improve both the sample size of RLAGNs and the unbi-
asedness of host galaxy populations, we search for UNIONS
(Gwyn et al. 2025) optical counterparts for VLASS (Lacy

2. THE DATA
This exploration for radio galaxies is established on the
basis of angular separation-based cross-match between the
VLASS for radio sources at 3 GHz and UNIONS for opti-
cal counterparts. The cross-matched UNIONS-VLASS cata-
log is set as our reference and further cross-matched with the
FIRST 1.4 GHz and LoTSS 144 MHz radio source catalogs.
In Figure 1, we summarize the flowchart that illustrates the
criteria applied to each survey to filter out valid sources for
the cross-match and to exclude invalid sources in the resul-
tant cross-matches.
2.1. UNIONS
An elevation of the statistical importance of our radio
galaxies catalog requires a wide (> 1000 deg2) and deep
(r > 22 mag) optical survey. The Ultraviolet Near-Infrared
Optical Northern Survey (UNIONS; Gwyn et al. 2025), as
a science collaboration between wide imaging surveys that
cover the northern hemisphere, provides an excellent oppor-
tunity to abundantly discover the optical counterparts of ra-
dio sources. The UNIONS integrates multiband (five broad-
bands, u, g, r, i, z) observations from Canada-France-Hawaii
Telescope/Canada-France Imaging Survey (CFHT/CFIS, u +
r), Subaru/Waterloo-Hawaii-IfA G-band Survey (WHIGS,
g), The Panoramic Survey Telescope & Rapid Response Sys-
tem (Pan-STARRS, i), and Subaru/Wide Imaging with Sub-
aru HSC of the Euclid Sky (WISHES, z). The main UNIONS
footprint is located at a declination (DEC.) > 30◦and a galac-
tic latitude of |b| > 25◦, enclosing a total area of 4, 861 deg2.
Extended u−and z−(WISHES+) observations are being con-
ducted to cover the sky area at DEC. < 30◦, elevating the
sky coverage of UNIONS. Albeit UNIONS is motivated by
the Euclid wide survey (Euclid Collaboration et al. 2022),
efforts have been devoted to maintaining UNIONS as an in-
dependent survey with scientific values beyond what will be
brought about by the Euclid.
The r-band observation of CFHT/CFIS has an excel-
lent median seeing of 0.69′′ and reaches a surface bright-
ness limit of 28.4 mag/arcsec2 for extended sources. It is
set as the reference catalog by which the elliptical aper-
ture is defined for the forced photometry applied to all
five UNIONS bands.
Since the broadband observations
of UNIONS are conducted under multiple telescopes, the
Gaussian Aperture and Photometry (GAaP; Kuijken et al.
2015) is adopted to produce Gaussianized images for raw
imaging data collected from observations at different instru-
ments. The forced photometry with Gaussian-weighted, el-
liptical apertures can then be properly used to perform ho-
mogeneous multiband photometric measurements simultane-
ously. The depth of UNIONS is characterized by the 10σ
point source depth measured through a 2.0′′ aperture. The
current median depth of UNIONS is [u, g, r, i, zPS, zHSC] =
[23.7, 24.5, 24.2, 23.8, 23.3, 23.3], to be compared with the
original goal of [u, g, r, i, z] = [23.6, 24.5, 23.9, 23.6, 23.4]
(Gwyn et al. 2025).
In this work, we use the latest internal 5-band data release
UNIONS5000 (as of May 2025; UNIONS5000ugriz hereafter)
that includes ∼20, 600 tiles with a total processed survey
area of ∼4, 200 deg2, covering both north (NGC) and south
Galactic caps (SGC). UNIONS5000ugriz contains photomet-
ric measurements using forced photometry adopting an aper-
ture size of APER = 0.7′′ and APER = 1.0′′, and an addi-
tional one with the optimal aperture APERopt. In most cases,
the optimal one is equal to the smallest possible aperture of
APER = 0.7′′. In the case where the measurement adopting
APER = 0.7′′ fails, the optimal photometric measurement is
then that using APER = 1.0′′ (see also Kuijken et al. 2019).
We always use the optimal photometry for the data presented
in this work.

2.3. FIRST
FIRST (Becker et al. 1995) employs VLA to observe 8,444
deg2 square degrees of the NGC at 1.4 GHz and the survey
area is limited to a declination of < 65 degrees. In the SGC,
an area of 644 deg2 has been observed, whereas the survey
area does not overlap with the current internal data release
of UNIONS5000 (see Figure 5). In this work, we use the
FIRST final data release catalog (Helfand et al. 2015). In
B-configuration, the survey reaches a spatial resolution rep-
resented by a circular Gaussian with full width at half maxi-
mum (FWHM) 5.4′′ with a pixel size of 1.8′′ over the north-
ern sky. For bright, pointlike sources with measured extents
of ≤2.0′′, the statistical astrometry is 0.22′′. In a typical sit-
uation, the flux density detection limit is 1 mJy over the full
footprint.

2.4. LoTSS
To understand the evolution of radio galaxies, the spec-
tral shape plays a crucial role, which requires multifrequency
observations to construct the synchrotron spectral energy
distribution (SED). MHz-observations carried out by LOw-
Frequency ARray are ideal supplements to our GHz-VLASS
and FIRST data. The LoTSS (LOFAR Two-metre Sky Sur-
vey; Shimwell et al. 2017, 2022) employs the LOFAR to ob-
serve 27% of the northern sky at 120 −168 MHz, with a
central (reference) frequency of 144 MHz. The sky cover-
age has two separate regions that span 4,178 and 1,457, deg2
respectively. The full bandwidth Stokes I continuum imag-
ing of LoTSS yields images at a spatial resolution of 6.0′′,
centered at a frequency of 144 MHz. The median detection
sensitivity of the survey is 89 µJy beam−1 with a typical posi-
tional uncertainty smaller than 0.2′′. In this work, we use the
latest and widest coverage catalog, LoTSS DR2 (Shimwell
et al. 2022), which contains 4,396,228 radio sources derived
from integrated intensity maps in Stokes I polarization.
3. UNIONS-VLASS RADIO GALAXY CATALOG
In this section,
we describe how we construct the
UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxy catalog and how we supple-
ment this catalog with multifrequency radio surveys, includ-
ing FIRST and LoTSS. We also describe the key information
to be presented in our catalog, including redshifts, luminosi-
ties, spectral indices, and how a radio source is classified as
an extended source.
3.1. NN_dist Cut
Radio galaxies with low radio luminosities often have their
jets confined by the ISM and rapidly disrupted (Fanaroff &
Riley 1974; Turner & Shabala 2015). Resultantly, the posi-
tion of the peak surface brightness can be close to the host
galaxy. The nearest neighbor cross-match is thus a straight-
forward and efficient approach for identifying the optical
counterpart of a radio source (Williams et al. 2019).
On
the other hand, powerful radio jets can quickly overcome
the confinement imposed by the ISM of their host galaxies
without experiencing significant disruption (Mukherjee et al.
2016). Consequently, they often propagate far from their host
galaxies and terminate in strong shocks – referred to as ra-
dio hotspots – where the observed radio surface brightness
typically peaks (Turner & Shabala 2023). Identifying the op-
tical counterparts of such radio sources necessitates a larger
search radius, which, in turn, inevitably brings about more
contaminants (Banfield et al. 2015; Gordon et al. 2023).
To statistically minimize chance overlaps between opti-
cal sources and radio sources exhibiting large-scale, complex
structures, we first perform a cross-match between UNIONS
and VLASS2 catalogs adopting the criteria present in Gordon
et al. (2021) and choosing a search radius of 6′′. This cross-
match yields ∼550, 000 sources. The angular separation be-
tween a pair of UNIONS-VLASS sources versus NN_dist
of the corresponding VLASS source is shown in Figure 2. It
is clear that the number of sources that are cross-matched
shows a bimodal distribution along NN_dist.
Cross-
matched UNIONS-VLASS pairs with NN_dist ≤30′′ tend
to have large angular separations between their UNIONS and
VLASS centroids. We randomly select 36 VLASS sources
with NN_dist < 10′′ and show their QL images, overlaid
with VLASS and UNIONS source centroids, in Figure 18.
These radio sources often exhibit complex and extended mor-
phology, such as core-jet and double-jet structures. VLASS
could capture the centroid of the lobe or radio hotspot, nat-
urally leading to the large offsets between UNIONS and
VLASS. We, hence, choose a NN_dist cut of 30′′ to avoid
false positive associations between UNIONS-VLASS caused
by evolved radio structures that can extend well beyond the
host galaxy scale and overlap with unrelated galaxies.
It should be noted that this cut can remove a large frac-
tion of bright, evolved radio galaxies at low-z.
Quantita-
tively, for a given search radius, let M denote the number
of cross-matched sources with NN_dist ≤30′′ and N the
number with NN_dist > 30′′.
The fractional loss of the
completeness can then be expressed as floss = M/(N + M).
As the search radius decreases from 6′′ to 1.5′′, floss de-
creases from ∼25% – roughly the fraction of sources with
NN_dist ≤30′′ in the VLASS2 catalog – to approximately
12%, while the number drops from 64,275 to 19,539. There-
fore, in addition to NN_dist, the elimination of extended ra-
dio sources with complex structures is also sensitive to the
search radius resulting from the design of the cross-match
methodology described in the following sections.
Conse-
quently, we see that UNIONS-VLASS RGs are absent from
the bright end (F3GHz > 3000 mJy; Figure 9) and could be
biased toward compact, young radio AGN populations (Fig-
ures 15–16; see § 5.3 for details).

As a consequence, a sole reliance on USS could lead to a
bias in the statistical physical properties of the HzRGs since
the diverse nature could be over-represented by USS.
An alternative approach to select HzRGs, which can cir-

spectral index distributions, is the Lyman break technique
commonly adopted in the search for high-z galaxies. The ef-
fectiveness of this technique in discovering HzRGs has been
proven by Capetti et al. (2025), who identified 39 g−dropout
HzRGs expected to be at 3 < z < 4.5 over an area of
∼2, 000 deg2, and by Yamamoto et al. (2025), who con-
structed a photometric sample of 146 HzRGs at z ∼4 within
a total survey area of ∼560 deg2.
The Lyman break color selection technique selects sources
that exhibit a clear Lyman break and blue UV continuum in
their optical SEDs covered by broadband (ugriz) photometry.

5.3. Caveat to the Catalog
The effect of applying NN_dist > 30′′ to VLASS2 is
clearly reflected in the projected linear size, that is, the ma-
jority of our RGs are compact (ls ≤20 kpc), which means
our UNIONS-VLASS RGs have the tendency to be young
and evolving populations. Therefore, for HzRGs that are of-
ten selected by the USS feature in previous studies, there are
only a few of them showing USS at z > 2 amongst 15, 906
UNIONS-VLASS-FIRST sources with valid αFIRST
VLASS. In ad-
dition to the NN_dist cut, this global trend of being com-
pact for our RGs is more a result of the limited search ra-
dius of 1.5′′, as shown in Figure 2 and discussed in § 3.1.
To maintain the current cleanliness of the UNIONS-VLASS
catalog, if NN_dist ≤30′′ is not adopted, the inclusion of
bright and extended radio sources in the UNIONS-VLASS
leads to an increase of 20,000 in the sample size, which is
about 1/3 of the total number of sources with NN_dist ≤30′′
in VLASS2. We, thereby, remind the readers to be cautious
about the intrinsic and unresolved bias arising from the de-
sign of this work. Nonetheless, the vast size of this catalog
still ensures statistical studies of radio AGNs across various
evolution stages, offering valuable insights into the connec-
tions between radio AGNs and their host galaxies.
