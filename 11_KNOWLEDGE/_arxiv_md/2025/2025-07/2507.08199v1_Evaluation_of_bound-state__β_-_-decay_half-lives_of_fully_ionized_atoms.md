Evaluation of bound-state β−-decay half-lives of fully ionized atoms
Priyanka Choudhary1, ∗and Chong Qi1, †
1Department of Physics, KTH Royal Institute of Technology,
Roslagstullsbacken 21, SE-106 91 Stockholm, Sweden
(Dated: July 14, 2025)
Bound-state β−-decay is a rare radioactive process where the created electron is trapped in an
atomic orbital instead of being emitted. It can be observed in highly ionized atoms in particular
when normal beta decay is energetically forbidden, but bound-state decay is still possible. In this
work we present a systematic theoretical study on the bound-state β−-decay of fully ionized atoms
where key nuclear inputs include the nuclear matrix elements (expressed through ft values) and the
lepton phase-space volume function. We present a method to evaluate nuclear matrix elements for
fully forbidden β−transitions in neutral atoms, from the inverse electron capture process using the
Takahashi–Yokoi model and account for the impact of electron capture to different atomic orbitals on
the resulting half-lives. Decay rates for bound-state β−-decays of nuclei 163Dy, 193Ir, 194Au, 202Tl,
205Tl, 215At, 222Rn, 243Am, and 246Bk are calculated, where the normal beta decay is forbidden. In
addition, we compute the bound-state β−decay rates for nuclei 187Re, 227Ac, and 228Ra, observing
enhancements by factors of 102 to 104 relative to their neutral-atom counterparts. Our results show
that the half-lives of certain bare nuclei are significantly shorter than those of the corresponding
neutral atoms, identifying them as promising candidates for future experimental investigation. The
theoretically predicted half-lives of the bound-state β−decay could provide valuable inputs for
various astrophysical studies.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Nuclear β decay, governed by the weak nuclear
force, is one of the earliest known fundamental
processes of radioactive decay. This process is
crucial for understanding the stability of nuclear
matter [1], the nucleosynthesis of elements [2, 3],
as well as for understanding the weak interaction
and the nature of many fundamental particles in-
cluding the neutrinos [4]. β decay involves sev-
eral modes: beta minus (β−) decay, beta plus
(β+) decay, electron capture (EC), as well as the
rare neutrino capture.
The EC of the nucleus (of an electrically neu-
tral atom) undergo mostly by absorbing one of
its own inner atomic electrons (usually from the
K or L shell). A fully/completely ionized nu-
cleus (often called bare atom) can capture a free
electron from its surroundings. Such process can
be expected in high-temperature environments
like collapsing cores of supernovae, where atoms
can lose all their electrons through ionization
due to their intense thermal energy.
The β−
decay can be considered as the mirror process
of the EC. The difference, however, is that β−

decay mostly emits high-energy electrons into
the continuum, as the decay Qn value is often
much larger than the electron binding energy.
Bound-state β−-decay (βb-decay), first proposed
by Daudel et al. [5], is a process in which the
emitted electron occupies a vacant bound atomic
orbital instead of escaping into the continuum
[6]. While this channel can be negligible for most
neutral atoms due to the limited available phase
space in outer shells, it can become important
in highly ionized atoms where more electron or-
bitals are vacant. That could be the case again
in stellar plasma characterized by extreme tem-
peratures and densities, where atoms are often
partially or fully ionized. That high tempera-
ture environment can provide an ideal ground for
the βb-decay to dominate. In other words, the
βb-decay offers a unique probe of how electron
environments influence nuclear weak processes,
particularly in extreme astrophysical conditions
like supernovae or neutron star mergers.
Nuclear β-decay plays a pivotal role in the
rapid neutron-capture process (r-process) [7],
slow neutron-capture process (s-process) [8], and
the rapid proton-capture process (rp-process)
[9].
In particular, the s-process is responsible
for synthesizing approximately half of the ele-
ments heavier than iron and proceeds via a se-
quence of neutron captures and β−-decays, typi-

III.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
In the present study, we have investigated the
βb-decay rates of fully ionized atoms. The se-
lected candidate nuclei are listed in Table II,
along with their decay energies in both the neu-
tral and bare atoms. In these transitions, the
emitted electron can occupy the 1s1/2 and 2s1/2
orbital of the daughter atom. There are 11 tran-
sitions with negative Qn-values, indicating that
β−decay is energetically not allowed in neutral
atoms but becomes possible in bare atoms. As
shown in the table, the βb-decay transitions of
187Re (to the first excited state), 193Ir, 194Au,
202Tl, 205Tl, 215At, and 246Bk are only permit-
ted when the emitted electron is created in the
K-shell. In these cases, we extract the nuclear
matrix elements for the βb-decay using the ex-
perimental EC half-lives. Among them, EC half-
lives have been experimentally measured for 7
transitions, which are presented in Table III.
The first experimental observation of βb decay
was reported by Jung et al. in 1992 for fully ion-
ized 163Dy, using the experimental storage ring
(ESR) facility at GSI [16]. The measured half-
life was estimated to be 47(+5-4) days. This was
followed by measurements of the βb-decay for
187Re in 1996 [15] and 207Tl in 2005 [36]. More re-
cently, a new βb-decay experiment for bare 205Tl
was carried out and a half-life of 291(+33-27)
days was observed [11–14].
Following the Takahashi-Yokoi model, several
theoretical efforts have been made to calculate
the decay rates of the βb-decay for bare atoms.
Xiao et al. have explored the βb-decay half-lives
of experimentally measured highly ionized atoms
such as 163Dy, 187Re, and 205Tl [37], as well as
the stellar beta decay rate of 204Tl [38], employ-
ing the projected shell model [39–43]. Further-
more, allowed β−decay rates for bare atoms in
the mass range A = 59–81 have been computed
using the nuclear shell model, as reported in Ref.
[44].

SUMMARY
To summarize, we have done a systematic
study on the βb-decay of fully ionized atoms
where the nuclear matrix elements (the ft val-
ues) used to determine the βb-decay half-life are
taken from the comparative EC and β decay pro-
cesses as in the Takahashi-Yokoi model and the
impact of the capture to different electron or-
bitals are investigated thoroughly.
Firstly, we
have considered only βb-decay from bare atoms
for which normal β decay is forbidden and have
evaluated their half-lives based on the time-
mirrored EC process. The calculated half-lives
for fully ionized 163Dy and 205Tl atoms are pre-
dicted to be 121.70 and 412.98 days, respectively,
which are in good agreement with available ex-
perimental data. Further, we have done a survey
on some β−decay transitions which are possible
