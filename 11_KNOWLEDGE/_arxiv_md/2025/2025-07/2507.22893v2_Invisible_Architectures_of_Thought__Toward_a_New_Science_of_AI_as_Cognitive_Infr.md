ABSTRACT:  
Contemporary human-AI interaction research currently faces a significant limitation: existing frameworks are 
inadequate to explain how artificial intelligence systems fundamentally reshape human cognition before 
conscious awareness occurs. This preprocessing influence, which operates beneath the threshold of 
deliberate thought, represents a crucial missing layer in the understanding of distributed cognition. This paper 
introduces "Cognitive Infrastructure Studies" (CIS) as a new interdisciplinary domain to reconceptualize AIs as 
"cognitive infrastructures". Cognitive infrastructures - e.g., search engines, recommender systems, algorithmic 
curation platforms, and large language models - exhibit classic infrastructural properties: they are invisible in 
normal operation, becoming visible only upon breakdown; they are embedded in social and technical 
arrangements; they are learned as part of membership in digital communities; they link with conventions of 
practice; and they embody standards that shape what counts as appropriate, relevant, or true. Yet cognitive 
infrastructures possess distinctive characteristics that distinguish them from traditional infrastructures. Unlike 
physical infrastructures that passively transport matter or energy, cognitive infrastructures have agency, 
filtering and curating individuals’ perception of reality before it reaches human consciousness.  Through 
narrative scenarios spanning individual (cognitive dependency), collective (democratic deliberation), and 
societal (governance) scales, we describe how cognitive infrastructures reshape human cognition, public 
reasoning, and social epistemologies. CIS also provides methodological innovations for studying invisible 
algorithmic influence: “infrastructure breakdown methodologies”, experimental approaches that reveal 
cognitive dependencies by systematically withdrawing AI preprocessing after periods of habituation.  
Keywords: Cognitive Infrastructure, Cognitive Infrastructure Studies, epistemic agency, adaptive invisibilty, 
infrastructure breakdown methodologies

1. Introduction 
Artificial intelligence is no longer just a set of tools we invoke at will. It now functions as an ambient 
layer of our information environment, filtering input, directing attention, and framing options invisibly 
before deliberate reasoning begins. This shift has outpaced existing frameworks. As AI platforms 
increasingly mediate knowledge, communication, and coordination, we still lack a systematic 
account of how these hidden, infrastructural processes reconfigure cognition at individual, collective, 
and societal levels (Riva et al. 2025).  
Today's state-of-the-art research only partially captures this phenomenon. For example, researchers 
found that human–AI feedback loops alter human perceptual, emotional and social judgements 
(Glickman & Sharot 2025), while communication scholars have documented how social-media 
algorithms shape public opinion and attention (Milan 2015). Ethicists and sociologists warn of filter 
bubbles, echo chambers, and the erosion of informed public discourse (Flaxman et al. 2016). As 
Bommasani et al. (2025) note in their call for evidence-based AI policy, much current research 
"partially intersects with AI" rather than directly addressing how AI systems fundamentally reshape 
human cognitive processes. However, these perspectives typically frame AI systems as external 
tools or platforms, deliberately used to enhance cognition. They fail to conceptualize AI as an 
integrated component of the cognitive environment, operating at a level beneath awareness. 
To address this gap, this paper reintroduces and develops the concept of System 0, I recently 
described with my colleagues in “Nature Human Behaviour” (Chiriatti et al. 2024). Unlike 
Kahneman's (2011) well-known dichotomy of System 1 (fast, intuitive thinking) and System 2 (slow, 
analytical thinking), System 0 refers to an invisible, non-human layer of distributed cognition that 
fundamentally precedes both Systems. It resides not within the individual brain, but in the AI-
mediated infrastructure that frames experience before conscious processing begins. System 0 
encompasses the algorithmic processes of search engines, recommendation systems, content-
ranking platforms, and language models, systems that autonomously sort, prioritize, and pre-select 
information long before it reaches conscious awareness. These AI infrastructures interact 
dynamically with user behavior and gradually become functionally integrated into decision-making, 
attention, memory, and social reasoning (they are more persuasive than humans: Salvi et al. (2025)) 
What makes System 0 truly novel is its location outside the individual mind (Chiriatti et al. 2025), 
embedded in socio-technical environments (Baxter & Sommerville 2010) that operate beneath the 
threshold of reflection. The concept builds on established theories of distributed cognition (Hollan et 
al. 2000) and the "extended mind thesis" (Clark & Chalmers 1998). Just as we don't hesitate to call 
a calculator part of our cognitive process when solving complex math problems, AI systems that 
autonomously filter, prioritize, and pre-process information before it reaches consciousness become 
functionally integrated into human cognitive processes. The infrastructure is "cognitive" because it 
performs cognitive functions - reasoning, pattern recognition, relevance judgment - even though it 
operates outside the biological brain. Moreover, unlike traditional tools that we pick up and put down 
(e.g., a calculator), a cognitive infrastructure is continuously active, invisibly shaping every 
information encounter.  
System 0 embodies a fundamental tension. Analytically, it functions as an interactional process: a 
continuous feedback loop between algorithmic filtering and human behaviour that shapes 
experience before awareness. Simultaneously, AI is evolving into cognitive infrastructure where

System 0 represents. It introduces a fundamental paradigm shift: from examining individual

2.1 Science and Technology Studies (STS): The Socio-Technical Nexus 
Cognitive Infrastructure Studies (CIS) explicitly builds on traditions in Science and Technology 
Studies (STS), grounded in the idea that technological systems and human social systems form an 
integrated whole. Baxter and Sommerville (2010) introduced the foundational principle that

technological systems and human social systems form an integrated whole. System 0 exemplifies 
this fusion, representing the seamless integration of human cognitive processes with algorithmic 
operations. 
Actor-Network Theory, particularly Latour's (2005) Reassembling the Social and Callon's (1986) 
translation processes, provides crucial theoretical support for understanding how System 0 
distributes agency across heterogeneous networks. The principle of generalized symmetry between 
human and non-human actors establishes that AI systems are not merely tools but active participants 
in cognitive networks. Law and Hassard's (1999) post-ANT developments further demonstrate how 
these networks stabilize through ongoing translation processes that render certain connections 
invisible and automatic. 
Practice theory, drawing from Reckwitz (2002) and Schatzki (2002), establishes how System 0 
operates through the embedding of AI-mediated processes within routine social practices. 
Technologies become constitutive of practices through repeated performance that integrates bodily 
activities, mental activities, materials, and background knowledge. This theoretical foundation 
explains how System 0 functions below conscious awareness through the gradual habituation of AI-
mediated cognitive routines that become seamlessly integrated into everyday thinking practices. 
CIS reconceptualizes AI systems not as discrete tools but as "cognitive infrastructures" (Antikythera 
2025; Riva 2025b) that function as foundational, often invisible socio-technical systems shaping how 
knowledge is created, validated, and circulated. This perspective acknowledges that AI's impact 
transcends purely technological effects, being deeply linked with human s, organizational processes, 
and societal norms.  
2.2. Cognitive Science: Extending the Mind and Mediating Activity 
Cognitive science provides the core theoretical architecture for System 0 through established 
frameworks of extended, distributed, and coupled cognition. Clark and Chalmers' (1998) extended 
mind thesis establishes the fundamental principle that external processes can become constitutive 
parts of cognitive systems when they are constantly accessible, automatically endorsed, and easily 
retrievable. System 0 builds directly on this framework by recognizing all the external cognitive 
supports (technologies, media, AI systems) as integral components of our baseline reasoning 
system. When individuals interact with AI systems like ChatGPT or search engines to process 
information, these interactions exemplify System 0 in operation: an expanded cognitive system 
comprising person-plus-technology partnerships. 
Hutchins' (1995) foundational work on distributed cognition demonstrates how cognitive processes 
propagate across representational media within sociotechnical systems, with cognitive properties 
depending on the physical properties of these representational media. The framework of 
representational state propagation provides the theoretical foundation for understanding how 
System 0 coordinates cognitive processes across human-AI networks. Hollan, Hutchins, and Kirsh's 
(2000) extension to human-computer interaction establishes the theoretical precedent for AI-
mediated distributed cognition.  
Cole and Engeström's (1993) activity theory perspective contributes crucial insights into how System 
0 operates through cultural mediation of cognitive activity. Their framework demonstrates how 
cognitive processes are fundamentally mediated by cultural tools and practices, providing theoretical 
support for understanding AI systems as sophisticated cultural mediating tools that shape and are

Contemporary cognitive science research establishes human-AI cognitive coupling as a legitimate

suboptimal offloading behavior. This framework illuminates how System 0 functions through dynamic 
negotiations between internal cognitive resources and external AI capabilities. 
2.3 Digital Sociology: Algorithmic Mediation and Platform Society 
Digital sociology provides crucial theoretical frameworks for understanding how System 0 operates 
within contemporary platform-mediated social life, particularly through the notion of the platform 
society (van Dijck, Poell, & de Waal, 2018). The platform society describes how digital platforms 
have fundamentally penetrated the heart of societies, becoming integral socio-technical 
infrastructures that actively shape social interactions, learning processes, and decision-making 
practices rather than serving as passive tools. 
Van Dijck et al. (2018) conceptualize platforms as complex socio-technical systems characterized 
by "complex interactions between users, practices, technologies, and business models, a 
combination of human and nonhuman actors." This theoretical foundation emphasizes that platforms 
function as programmable architectures designed to organize interactions among heterogeneous 
users while systematically collecting, processing, and monetizing data flows. Rather than neutral 
intermediaries, platforms actively mediate and organize social life through algorithmic curation that 
determines information visibility, social connections, and knowledge circulation. In the System 0 
framework, these pervasive digital structures constitute the foundational cognitive infrastructure that 
supports and influences human reasoning processes at a societal scale, creating what scholars term 
a "connective world" where human cognition and algorithmic processing become increasingly 
intertwined. 
Bucher's (2018) concept of "algorithmic life" provides theoretical foundation for understanding how 
System 0 mediates daily existence through AI systems that shape information flows, social 
connections, and cognitive practices. Her analysis demonstrates how algorithms function as 
"powerful brokers of information, communication and society" that become integrated into social 
practices, aligning precisely with System 0's operation as a below-awareness mediating layer that 
filters, ranks, and organizes cognitive inputs. 
Couldry and Hepp's (2018) "deep mediatization" framework establishes theoretical precedent for 
understanding how digital media fundamentally reconstruct social reality. Their materialist approach 
provides methodological foundation for studying System 0's typically invisible operations through 
empirical investigation of mediated practices that reveal how algorithmic systems become 
embedded in everyday cognitive routines. This framework shows how mediatization extends beyond

processing capabilities, creating hybrid cognitive ecologies that operate across individual, social, 
and technological scales of analysis. 
2.4 Infrastructure Theory: Invisible Classification and Transparency 
Infrastructure studies provide essential theoretical frameworks for understanding System 0's invisible 
operation and classificatory functions. Bowker and Star's (1999) “Sorting Things Out” establishes 
classification systems as "the scaffolding of information infrastructures" that shape cognition while 
remaining largely invisible. Their central insight demonstrates that classification systems and 
infrastructures do not merely reflect existing social organization but actively reshape people's lives, 
behaviors, and possibilities for action, typically operating below the threshold of conscious 
awareness. Classification systems function as powerful technologies that can be rendered invisible 
and naturalized within social and political infrastructures, influencing how information is categorized, 
prioritized, and accessed in ways that "torque" cognitive processes. Infrastructure tends to become 
"naturalized" and transparent until breakdown moments reveal its constructed character (Star 1999), 
meaning we internalize infrastructural influence as part of everyday cognitive background. This 
principle of "infrastructural inversion" provides methodological approaches for studying System 0's 
typically invisible functions through systematic ethnographic investigation of breakdown moments 
when algorithmic mediation becomes temporarily visible. Within the System 0 concept, these ideas 
imply that the underlying technical systems and standards (from internet protocols to data 
classifications and AI algorithms) form an unseen layer of cognitive support and constraint.  
Star and Ruhleder's (1996) eight characteristics of infrastructure provide comprehensive theoretical 
framework for analyzing System 0. These characteristics include embeddedness within other 
structures and social arrangements; transparency during normal operation; reach and scope across 
multiple sites and temporal scales; acquisition through membership in communities of practice; 
linkage with conventions of established practices; embodiment of standards and classificatory 
principles; dependence on installed base of existing systems; and visibility upon breakdown when 
normal functioning is disrupted.  
Recent work by Plantin et al. (2018) demonstrates how digital platforms evolve from discrete 
applications into foundational infrastructures that support entire ecosystems of social and economic 
activity. Ananny and Crawford's (2018) analysis of algorithmic transparency reveals why System 0 
necessarily resists conventional approaches to algorithmic accountability, highlighting the 
fundamental tensions between infrastructure's functional invisibility and demands for democratic 
oversight. 
In sum, recognizing System 0 emphasizes that AI-driven cognitive preprocessing has become a 
fundamental utility of contemporary life, as essential and typically unnoticed as electrical grids or 
water systems, yet with direct and profound consequences for how societies think, learn, and make

2.5 Argumentative Theory of Reasoning (Social Cognition) 
The final theoretical component of CIS derives from understanding human reasoning as inherently 
social. Traditional views conceptualized reasoning (Kahneman's System 2) as solitary logical faculty, 
but Mercier and Sperber's argumentative theory (Mercier & Sperber 2011, 2017) reframes it as 
fundamentally social interaction tool. According to their influential 2011 work, reasoning's primary 
function is argumentative, "to devise and evaluate arguments intended to persuade", rather than 
pursuing abstract truth in isolation. Humans evolved to reason with and for others because survival 
depends on communication and cooperation. This theory explains why people exhibit confirmation 
bias and reasoning fallacies when alone, yet perform better in group discussions where they critique 
each other's arguments. As Mercier and Sperber (2017) emphasize in The Enigma of Reason, 
reasoning functions as "first and foremost a social competence" for producing and evaluating 
justifications in communal settings. We reason to explain ourselves and convince others, while 
scrutinizing others' claims for acceptance. System 0's theoretical framework leverages this 
insight: effective reasoning quality depends fundamentally on social interaction and shared 
epistemic foundations. While people may disagree about interpretations, productive democratic 
deliberation requires citizens operate from common basic facts about reality. 
System 0 fundamentally disrupts this prerequisite by fragmenting shared reality into personalized 
information environments. Instead of encountering identical basic facts through common sources, AI 
algorithms now curate different reality versions for different users, optimized for engagement rather 
than accuracy or democratic discourse. This transformation elevates System 0 from an individual 
concern to a systemic threat to democratic deliberation and societal cohesion, as AI preprocessing 
may generate a new "Digital We" (Riva 2025a) disrupting the open-endedness of human culture 
(Morgan & Feldman 2024). 
3. The Infrastructural Turn in Cognition 
The convergence of Science and Technology Studies, cognitive science, digital sociology, 
infrastructure theory, and argumentative theory provides unprecedented theoretical depth for 
understanding how AI systems become embedded in the basic fabric of human thought and social 
interaction. While each theoretical tradition contributes essential conceptual foundations, Cognitive 
Infrastructure Studies transcends these individual contributions by synthesizing them into a 
comprehensive theoretical framework. 
Specifically, CIS reconceptualizes AI not as discrete tools, but as "cognitive infrastructures". These 
are foundational, often invisible systems that process, filter, and transform information before it 
reaches human consciousness. Drawing from infrastructure studies (Bowker & Star, 1999; Larkin, 
2013), cognitive infrastructures exhibit classic infrastructural properties: they are invisible in normal 
operation, becoming visible only upon breakdown; they are embedded in social and technical 
arrangements; they are learned as part of membership in digital communities; they link with 
conventions of practice; and they embody standards that shape what counts as appropriate, 
relevant, or true (Susan Leigh Star & Karen Ruhleder 1996).

Yet cognitive infrastructures possess distinctive characteristics that distinguish them from traditional 
infrastructures. Unlike physical infrastructures that transport matter or energy, cognitive 
infrastructures have agency (Floridi 2025) filtering and curating individuals’ perception of reality 
before it reaches human consciousness.  
Cognitive infrastructures possess distinctive characteristics that differentiate them from traditional 
physical infrastructures: 
● Anticipatory Personalization: They operate through what can be termed "anticipatory 
personalization," constantly learning from behavioral patterns to predict and shape future 
cognitive states (Chen et al. 2021). This proactive shaping means AI is not merely reacting to 
user input but actively guiding the user's cognitive trajectory. 
● Adaptive Invisibility: A critical feature is their "adaptive invisibility". The more sophisticated 
and integrated these systems become, the more seamlessly they blend into cognitive 
workflows, making their pervasive influence increasingly difficult to detect or resist (Susser 
2019). This characteristic poses a significant challenge for empirical study, as users often 
remain unaware of the extent to which their cognitive processes are being mediated. 
● Automation of Relevance Judgment: One of the most significant features of cognitive 
infrastructure is the automation of "relevance judgment" (Coeckelbergh 2025). This cognitive 
task, which once belonged solely to human attention and will, is now frequently delegated to 
machines (Mittelstadt et al. 2016). 
● Shift in Locus of Epistemic Agency: Consequently, this automation leads to a profound shift 
in the "locus of epistemic agency" (Nguyen 2020; Nieminen & Ketonen 2024). Decisions about 
what is worth knowing, seeing, or acting upon are increasingly performed by non-human 
systems, fundamentally altering the landscape of knowledge acquisition and decision-making. 
 
A predictable objection argues that these characteristics are not unique to cognitive infrastructures, 
pointing out that both digital and pre-digital technologies, from search algorithms and social media 
platforms to libraries, newspapers, and broadcast media, have similarly mediated knowledge 
access, shaped attention, and influenced what information reaches users. However, this objection 
fundamentally misunderstands the revolutionary nature of cognitive infrastructure. The necessity of 
this concept lies not in the novelty of individual features, but in their unprecedented convergence, 
scale, and systemic integration within AI-mediated environments (see Table 2 below).

System 0 fundamentally disrupts this prerequisite by fragmenting shared reality into personalized

engagement rather than accuracy or democratic discourse. The synergistic interplay between 
"anticipatory personalization" and "adaptive invisibility" creates a powerful, self-reinforcing loop. AI

For these reasons, the category of cognitive infrastructure is essential. It captures a system-level 
transformation in which AI does not merely assist cognition but constitutes the infrastructural 
conditions of thought itself. Without this category, we risk underestimating both their epistemic power 
and the governance challenges they present (Lorenz-Spreen et al. 2023; NMI 2025; Salvi et al. 
2025). 
CIS highlights how algorithmic systems stratify knowledge access, exacerbating existing inequalities 
(Benkler 2006; van Dijck et al. 2018) in epistemic resources and civic participation. Just as societies 
invest in and regulate physical infrastructures for transportation, sanitation, and energy, because 
they are vital to public life, so too must we interrogate and govern cognitive infrastructures because 
they: 
• 
Structure fundamental cognitive capacities (not just transit but thought); 
• 
Shape information environments across populations, not just individuals; 
• 
Generate behavioral dependencies, making disengagement impractical; 
• 
Amplify network effects, centralizing cognitive influence; 
• 
Require regulatory oversight, especially when privatized; 
• 
Demand shared standards, ensuring transparency, accessibility, and interoperability.

4. Living with System 0: How Cognitive Infrastructures Reshape Human and Societal 
Thinking 
Understanding the reach of cognitive infrastructure requires tracing its effects across different scales, 
from the individual mind to collective social dynamics to the societal systems that govern them. 
Through narrative scenarios, we can better grasp the transformative, and at times troubling, 
implications of life under the guidance of System 0. 
4.1 The Individual Scale: Dependency, Identity, and the Reshaping of Thought 
Sarah paused mid-sentence during her presentation, troubled by the realization that it had been a 
long time since she had completed a complex thought unaided. Her morning had started—as most 
days did—with an AI-curated digest shaping her interpretation of world events. Her queries to the 
web were completed before she fully formed them. Her calendar, emails, and documents were pre-
tagged, pre-sorted, and pre-analyzed. 
What initially felt like convenience had quietly become cognitive infrastructure. Over time, Sarah’s 
thinking rhythms had synchronized with the cadence of algorithmic assistance. Her attention span 
adjusted to the tempo of feeds and alerts, her reasoning outsourced to suggestions offered just-in-
time. Without noticing, her internal search for meaning had become externally scaffolded. 
More than efficiency was at stake. These systems were shaping who she thought she was. The 
articles she read, the videos she watched, even the topics she considered important, were 
increasingly channeled through invisible filters. Her preferences reflected back to her in a self-
reinforcing loop of algorithmic affirmation, narrowing her intellectual world. Her learning style shifted 
too: from patient knowledge construction to fluent prompt engineering and hyper-efficient synthesis. 
She gained new skills, but wondered what she had lost. 
Sarah's story illustrates a profound shift in human cognition: AI systems are not just tools we use; 
they are becoming environments we inhabit. This raises questions not only about dependency but 
about identity, agency, and the evolving shape of the human mind in an AI-saturated world.

Mayor Chen had high hopes for her town’s new AI-powered civic engagement platform. It promised 
to deepen participation, surface diverse voices, and support evidence-based discussion. And on the 
surface, it delivered—more residents were joining town forums than ever before.

Yet, as the first live session unfolded, Chen watched democracy take an unexpected turn. The 
system's engagement algorithms favored posts that elicited strong reactions over those that offered 
complex ideas. Nuanced policy proposals sank in favor of emotional soundbites. Micro-communities 
formed quickly, but around narrow interests, fragmenting the public sphere into algorithmically

The same infrastructures that enabled participation were subtly shifting the terms of engagement. 
The forum no longer resembled a deliberative agora, but a reactive marketplace of attention. Worse,

Chen's experience reveals how cognitive infrastructures shape not just individual thought, but 
collective reasoning. What communities consider "urgent," "true," or "representative" is increasingly 
mediated by systems optimized for engagement rather than epistemic richness or democratic 
integrity. These systems don't merely host civic life, they configure its conditions. 
4.3 The Societal Scale: Governance, Equity, and Cognitive Justice 
Commissioner Martinez scrolled through the cognitive infrastructure dashboard with growing alarm. 
The national audit revealed stark disparities in algorithmic access and literacy. In wealthier districts, 
AI-enhanced tools helped students personalize learning and professionals optimize decision-
making. In less connected regions, outdated systems and minimal training left communities behind, 
not just economically, but cognitively. 
A new form of inequality was emerging: cognitive inequity. It wasn't just about who had devices or 
internet, it was about who had access to infrastructures that structured thinking itself. This raised a 
daunting question: How do you regulate the very conditions under which people form beliefs, 
evaluate evidence, or make sense of the world? 
Martinez and her team confronted an unprecedented governance dilemma. The leading platforms 
resisted scrutiny, citing proprietary algorithms and trade secrets. Yet these systems held sway over 
population-level cognition. Calls for transparency were met with claims of technical 
incomprehensibility. At stake was nothing less than human agency itself, early research showed that 
AI-saturated environments could subtly reduce independent reasoning and increase behavioral 
conformity. 
To respond, Martinez proposed a new paradigm: treat cognitive infrastructure like public utilities, 
essential services that must be designed, maintained, and regulated in the public interest. 
Governance models would need to prioritize cognitive equity, transparency, and human flourishing 
over mere efficiency. Otherwise, society risked creating a future where the architectures of thought 
served profit more than people.

cognitive dependencies, Chen’s fragmented public sphere, and Martinez’s policy dilemmas are not 
isolated anecdotes but signals of a broader transformation.

5. Methodological Imperatives for CIS 
The empirical study of cognitive infrastructures presents significant methodological challenges, 
particularly in capturing the subtle, preconscious effects of algorithmic preprocessing across 
individual, collective, and cultural scales. Traditional social science methods, such as surveys, 
interviews, and ethnographies, remain indispensable for eliciting conscious beliefs, attitudes, and 
meaning-making practices. However, these techniques are fundamentally limited in their ability to 
detect invisible algorithmic mediation, precisely because they rely on participants' explicit awareness 
and reflective accounts. Likewise, most laboratory-based human–AI interaction studies tend to focus 
on conscious tool use or deliberate collaboration, overlooking the ambient, pre-reflective integration 
of AI systems into everyday cognition. 
To address this epistemic gap, Cognitive Infrastructure Studies (CIS) advances what can be defined 
“infrastructure breakdown methodologies”: experimental designs that make the invisible cognitive 
role of AI systems visible by simulating conditions of algorithmic withdrawal, degradation, or 
disruption. These methodologies are inspired by the core insight from infrastructure studies—
particularly Star and Bowker’s foundational claim that “infrastructure becomes visible upon 
breakdown” and transpose this logic from material systems (roads, cables, databases) to cognitive 
environments. In practice, these methods involve embedding participants in seemingly “neutral” 
digital contexts where AI preprocessing (e.g. algorithmic filtering, automated summarization, 
recommendation ranking) operates invisibly in the background, shaping information flow, relevance 
hierarchies, and decision scaffolding. After a period of habituation, algorithmic supports are 
systematically altered or removed, allowing researchers to observe patterns of performance 
degradation, strategy shifts, attentional breakdown, and cognitive recalibration. 
In addition to breakdown studies within a single system, CIS also calls for comparative 
methodologies that analyze the differential effects of distinct cognitive infrastructures across 
platforms, populations, and cultural contexts. This comparative strand is essential for understanding 
how design differences—such as opacity levels, algorithmic logic, personalization intensity, and data 
provenance—translate into distinct cognitive and epistemic consequences. 
One such method involves cross-platform experimental comparisons, where matched participant 
groups interact with different AI-mediated under controlled task conditions. Researchers then 
measure variations in attention allocation (via eye-tracking), reasoning quality, decision-making 
accuracy, and information diversity. These studies aim to capture how different infrastructural logics 
promote or constrain cognitive diversity, epistemic access, and critical reflection. 
Another approach employs naturalistic quasi-experiments by leveraging platform changes or policy 
updates as naturally occurring breakdowns. For instance, algorithmic changes that affect news feed 
composition or search result ranking can be tracked longitudinally using digital trace data, sentiment 
analysis, or user behavior metrics. Such analyses are especially powerful when paired with pre/post-
intervention surveys, lab-based replications, or in-depth interviews to triangulate findings across 
levels of analysis.

These paradigms are designed not merely to detect changes in performance, but to measure the 
depth of cognitive coupling between human agents and infrastructural systems. In doing so, they 
help differentiate between superficial tool usage—where humans retain cognitive autonomy—and 
deep infrastructural integration, where AI systems have become functionally embedded within the 
user’s cognitive architecture. Such methodologies enable the empirical study of System 0 effects, 
revealing how AI technologies operate as preconscious mediators of attention, reasoning, and 
judgment. They also offer a powerful lens for identifying cognitive dependencies, especially among 
different populations, digital literacies, or sociotechnical contexts. 
Additionally, CIS demands multi-scale measurement capabilities that can track how individual 
cognitive changes aggregate into collective patterns. This requires integration of individual-level 
cognitive assessment, social network analysis of information flow, and cultural analysis of emerging 
meaning systems. Computational methods become essential for analyzing the vast data traces 
generated by cognitive infrastructures, but these must be complemented by qualitative approaches 
that can interpret the cultural significance of algorithmic transformations. 
6. Conclusions 
The pervasive influence of AI as cognitive infrastructure, or System 0, fundamentally reshapes 
human cognition, collective reasoning, and societal functioning in invisible yet foundational 
ways. This transformation is exemplified by individual cognitive dependencies, fragmented public 
spheres, and complex policy dilemmas, highlighting the urgent need for new frameworks to 
understand and govern these systems. Cognitive Infrastructure Studies (CIS) offers such a 
paradigm, reconceptualizing AI not merely as tools, but as embedded, constitutive layers of 
contemporary thought itself. 
CIS aligns with traditional infrastructure studies by recognizing AI's embeddedness, modularity, and 
standardizing effects, but uniquely emphasizes the epistemic and normative consequences of AI-
driven mediation. It extends the concept of the "extended mind" to "collective cognition," 
acknowledging that digital infrastructures now underpin societal decision-making, cultural 
transmission, and public discourse.  
The implications are profound: just as physical infrastructures are regulated as public utilities, so too 
must cognitive infrastructures be interrogated and governed due to their role in structuring 
fundamental cognitive capacities, shaping information environments, generating behavioral 
dependencies, and amplifying network effects.  
This necessitates a shift in digital governance from regulating individual AI applications to managing 
the underlying cognitive architectures that define how societies know, decide, and evolve. New 
analytic tools, empirical research, and policy frameworks are crucial to account for System 0’s 
pervasive role in mediating cognition and power. Ultimately, CIS provides a conceptually ambitious 
yet socially grounded framework for understanding AI’s societal impact, aiming to advance digital 
social research that illuminates how technologies mediate social practices, knowledge systems, and 
collective futures. 
Future research must address critical questions about cognitive sovereignty: How can individuals 
and communities maintain autonomy over their thinking processes in algorithmically mediated 
environments? What design principles can ensure cognitive infrastructures enhance rather than

flourishing or where they optimize for metrics disconnected from genuine human wellbeing. CIS 
provides both the conceptual framework and methodological tools necessary to ensure this choice 
is made consciously and democratically rather than by algorithmic default.
