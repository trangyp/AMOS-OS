Creating a cast of characters by attending to their relational dynamics is a critical aspect of most long-form
storywriting. However, our formative study (N=14) reveals that writers struggle to envision new characters
that could influence existing ones, balance similarities and differences among characters, and intricately flesh
out their relationships. Based on these observations, we designed Constella, an LLM-based multi-agent tool
that supports storywriters’ interconnected character creation process. Constella suggests related characters
(FRIENDS DISCOVERY feature), reveals the inner mindscapes of several characters simultaneously (JOURNALS
feature), and manifests relationships through inter-character responses (COMMENTS feature). Our 7–8
day deployment study with storywriters (N=11) shows that Constella enabled the creation of expansive
communities composed of related characters, facilitated the comparison of characters’ thoughts and emotions,
and deepened writers’ understanding of character relationships. We conclude by discussing how multi-agent
interactions can help distribute writers’ attention and effort across the character cast.

Introduction
"The single biggest mistake writers make when creating characters is that they think of
the hero and all other characters as separate individuals. [...] To create great characters,
think of all your characters as part of a web in which each helps define the others." — John
Truby, 2008 [143]
Characters are essential building blocks of fictional stories. While the heart of the story may rest
in one central character, its pulse comes to life through many. A well-constructed ensemble not
only propels the plot [95] but also creates emotional depth and thematic resonance by allowing
characters to challenge, support, and transform one another [34, 89, 95, 143]. Thus, crafting an
interwoven cast of characters is an important activity for all forms of storytelling—novels, plays,
comics, films, and TV shows—but is especially crucial for full-length stories. This is because full-
length works that are "the normal length, rather than being shorter than normal" [26], provide greater
scope for developing the lives of their characters [89]. While this process is expressed through

Acknowledging the importance of character creation, Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) re-
searchers have developed computational tools to aid creative writers. One line of work has im-
plemented chatbots embodying writer-defined personas, enabling users to iteratively shape and
interact with their characters through free-form conversation [113, 114, 125]. While these works
support some relational context (e.g., roleplaying), they primarily focus on single-character devel-
opment. In contrast, we aim to support character cast creation, and to do so, we see an opportunity
for leveraging Large Language Model-based Multi-Agents (LLM-MA).
LLM-MA are distinguished by their focus on a variety of agent profiles, interactions between
agents, and their collective decision-making processes [48]. Prior HCI research has leveraged LLM-
MA to provide users with opinions from diverse viewpoints, finding applications in domains that
require a multitude of expert suggestions [105], audiences [20, 104], or simulated users within social
computing systems [106, 107, 152]. Although these works are not directly related to the storytelling
context, they collectively underscore the potential of LLM-MA when used for interconnected
character creation. As noted by storywriting literature, "the most important step in creating your
hero, as well as all other characters, is to connect and compare each to the others" [143]. Thus, by
enabling the simultaneous exploration of contrasting character perspectives, LLM-MA can empower
writers to construct a complex, multilayered cast of characters.
To design an LLM-MA tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected character creation, we
first sought to understand the challenges they face during this process and ground our tool in
the strategies they find effective for overcoming these difficulties. To this end, we conducted a
formative study with 14 writers. We found that writers address key challenges through the following
strategies: 1) To conceptualize new characters capable of influencing existing ones, they brainstorm
a wide range of related characters. 2) To balance similarities and differences between characters,
they imagine each character’s inner world and how each would respond uniquely to the same
situation. 3) To flesh out character relationships, they often create moments of interaction regardless
of the main plot to gain insight into their dynamics.
With these observations, we derived three design goals for an LLM-MA tool to support inter-
connected character creation: 1) Suggest diverse characters related to existing ones, 2) support
the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities, and 3) manifest character rela-
tionships through exploratory interactions. To translate these goals into concrete system features,
we drew inspiration from interaction concepts found in social media (e.g., [90, 91, 116]), as they
provide effective means for designing social interactions among story characters that align with
each design goal. Specifically, these platforms enable users to connect with individuals beyond
their usual acquaintances through potential connection points, freely express differing or similar
thoughts on topics of mutual interest, and display or reinforce relationships by reacting to others’
content. We anticipated that drawing on these affordances to simulate character interactions would
intuitively facilitate our design goals by echoing everyday social behavior.
Thus, we propose Constella1, an LLM-MA tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected char-
acter creation through social media-inspired interactions (Figure 1). Constella enables writers to
iteratively build characters and their relationships via dedicated character profiles. Upon creating a
profile, the FRIENDS DISCOVERY feature helps writers uncover new characters linked to existing
ones by generating their mini profiles. Writers can also use the JOURNALS feature to contrast the
inner mindscapes of characters by generating their diary entries. Lastly, writers can elaborate on

Background and Related Work
This work aims to support storywriters’ interconnected character creation process through LLM-
MA. In this section, we first outline the theoretical foundations of interconnected character creation
in storywriting (§2.1). We then review prior AI tools designed to assist character creation (§2.2).
Finally, we position our work within the broader landscape of AI-assisted writing tools that move
beyond ready-made text generation to support creative processes (§2.3).
2.1
Interconnected Character Creation
Narratives consist of actions, happenings, characters, settings, and discourse [14]. Our research
focuses on the element of characters, which can be defined through archetypes2 [75, 89, 95, 124, 143],
backstories [11, 28, 75, 88, 89, 95, 127, 143], and various physical, psychological, and sociological
attributes [11, 14, 27, 33, 34, 37, 88, 127].
As full-length fictional stories typically feature an ensemble of characters, writing guides offer
suggestions for developing characters in relation to one another [32, 34, 89, 95, 143]. For instance,
McKee [88] uses the metaphor of a solar system, suggesting that writers imagine the main character
as a star and other characters as orbiting planets, each positioned based on their influence on the
protagonist and one another. He advises writers to begin by creating the central role, followed by
the incremental development of supporting roles. In contrast, Truby [143] stresses the importance
of developing characters simultaneously, encouraging writers to draw continuous comparisons
and connections among them. He argues that this process highlights each character’s individual-
ity, allowing even supporting characters to become more distinct and meaningful alongside the
protagonist.
While literature on storywriting underscores the value of creating characters in an interconnected
manner, it tends to focus on general principles rather than providing comprehensive explanations of
the real-world challenges writers face during interconnected character creation and the underlying
causes of these difficulties. Furthermore, the degree to which these theoretical approaches reflect
actual writing practices remains uncertain. As prior HCI research has highlighted the diversity
of writers’ practices and the variability of their needs [43, 47, 60], we anticipated discrepancies
between the formalized processes outlined in theory and the actual practices of writers in the wild.
Thus, to design a tool that effectively supports storywriters, we determined it was essential to
draw not only on theoretical insights but also to engage deeply with empirical evidence. Based
on this background, we grounded the development of our tool through in-depth interviews with
storywriters, using the previously cited writing guides as references when developing the interview
questions.
2.2
AI Support for Character Construction
In this section, we review LLM-based tools designed for storywriters, focusing on systems that
support the creation of new characters or facilitate character interactions through agents that
embody writer-defined personas.
Several works have utilized Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate new characters [16, 23,
56, 71, 94, 135, 137], often drawing from other elements of the story to align with the writers’ overall
vision and storytelling goals. For example, Dramatron [94] allows users to first define a logline3,
which is then used to generate not only the characters but also the title and plot. Patchview [23]

Another line of work has aimed to enable storywriter-character interactions through chatbots

CharacterMeet [113] extends this research by utilizing an LLM and implementing a conversation

created for children [21], implementations of agent-based character embodiments for storywriters
[45, 113, 125] have mostly concentrated on single-character development.
While we focus on providing support for fictional storywriters, prior research in the domain
of interactive storytelling has proposed tools [52, 83, 136, 146, 147] that utilize multiple character
embodiments via LLM-MA. As the emergent nature of LLM-MA poses challenges for writers to
steer the direction of interactive stories (e.g., games), these works focus on providing control
mechanisms that mediate between the author’s intent, the unpredictability of LLM-based agents,
and player-introduced actions. For example, Storyverse [147] translates high-level plot outlines into
characters’ action sequences during playtime, while the game player can also steer the unfolding
events, such as by killing a character.
Beyond interactive storytelling, several works [16, 56, 150] adapt LLM-MA for fictional storywrit-
ing, but their emphasis is on proposing automated story generation pipelines, thereby providing
limited support for writers’ iterative and incremental character construction processes [72]. For
instance, Yu et al.’s work [150] assumes that detailed character personas and the plot outline are
readily available and proceeds to generate inter-character simulations that produce the detailed text
for the story. Other systems [16, 56] limit the writer’s contribution to supplying a seed storyline;
from that point onward, agents automatically detail the plot and characters, multi-agent exchanges
generate the dialogue, and a final agent combines them into the complete narrative.
While these approaches demonstrate the potential of LLM-MA in storywriting, they primarily
utilize LLM-MA simulations as either real-time, emergent narratives or as components within
largely automated pipelines for complete story generation. In contrast, we focus on leveraging
LLM-MA to support fictional storywriters in constructing their character casts, specifically through
the design of scaffolding materials (e.g., journal entries and comments) rather than final story
text. This reflects our deliberate intent to uphold authorial agency by preserving the writers’ role
in the expressive act of the writing process. In line with this focus, we investigate how our tool
supports writers through a deployment study, which, to our knowledge, is the first research to
offer empirical insights into how a character-based LLM-MA tool integrates into the real-world
practices of fictional storywriters.
2.3
Assisting Writers Beyond Generating Ready-Made Text
Recent advancements in language models have spurred the development of various tools aimed
at assisting writers. One predominant trend in this space involves co-writing systems that offer
suggestions in the form of output-ready text, such as next sentence generation [10, 25, 77, 120, 128,
137, 149, 151], text variation [40, 118, 137, 151], and narrative generation [16, 52, 56, 83, 94, 101,
115, 136, 147, 150]. However, recent studies investigating writers’ perceptions of AI systems have
highlighted the importance of the "emotional values of writing" [7], values that may be diminished
when AI systems reduce opportunities for writers to "express their ideas on their own" [7].

In response, a growing body of research [4, 13, 30, 66, 132, 153, 155] has been actively proposing
alternative ways for generative models to support writers without direct text generation; that
is, text that can be immediately inserted into their final work without further modification. For
example, AI tools that provide experience advising [132], questions [4], or continuous summaries
to support process-oriented reflection [30] have been introduced. Collectively, these approaches
are promising ways to help writers retain a strong sense of control over their work.
We situate our tool in this body of work by proposing a Creativity Support Tool (CST) [22, 39, 129]
that offers open-ended resources for storywriters. Specifically, we provide resources in social media-
inspired formats such as mini profiles, diaries, and comments, which likely encourage writers to
iterate over, adapt, and interpret the generated content rather than directly copying and inserting
it as-is. This offers a way to support a form of agency that has been found to be vital in AI-assisted
storywriting yet relatively underexplored in prior HCI research: writing process control [12],
which is maintained when writers treat generated content as raw material to be further shaped
by themselves. In doing so, our approach makes room for the author’s expressive intent to flow
through the interpretation of underdetermined outcomes [69].
3
Formative Study
To inform the design of an LLM-MA tool that supports interconnected character creation, we
conducted semi-structured interviews with storywriters. We focused on identifying their approaches
to interconnected character creation, the challenges they face during this process, and the strategies
they use to overcome these difficulties. Insights from this study informed the three design goals for
our tool. The study process was approved by our university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB No.
KH2024-115).
3.1
Participants
We recruited writers with prior experience in creating full-length fictional stories. Considering
the domain of storytelling, we aimed to gather insights across diverse story types and genres.
Furthermore, we aimed to diversify participants based on their total duration of creative writing
experience to gain a comprehensive understanding from individuals relatively newer to storywriting
to those with greater familiarity. Lastly, we recruited participants with and without formal creative
writing education, as well as those with and without publication experience. This inclusive approach
was informed by Gero et al.’s work [43], which argues that formal education does not necessarily
equate to expertise and that relying on publication experience as a metric for expertise could
unnecessarily marginalize writers. Participants were recruited through online writer communities,
university communities, personal contacts, and social media posts uploaded by researchers and
their acquaintances. All participants were selected through purposeful sampling [102] to ensure
maximum variation across story types, genres, and total duration of creative writing experience.
In total, 14 participants (F=8, M=6) were recruited (Table 1). Story types ranged from films
(N=11), TV shows (N=10), novels (N=10), plays (N=3), comics (N=2), and animation (N=1). The
total number of writing genres they had experience with reached 32, including examples such as
comedy, romance, and historical fiction. The duration of their creative writing experience ranged
from 2 to 26 years, with an average of 11.93 years. All participants resided in South Korea.

3.2
Procedure
Fig. 2. Procedure of the formative study.
The formative study was divided into three phases: (1) Q&A of overall creative experience, (2)
memory-recall workshop, and (3) Q&A of selected creative experience (Figure 2). All three phases
were conducted sequentially within one day. Each participant completed the study either in person
(N=5) or remotely via Zoom (N=9), depending on participants’ preferences.
(1) Q&A of overall creative experience (40 minutes): After obtaining informed consent, we
introduced the goal of the study. To gain overarching insights into their interconnected character
creation process, we asked participants to answer the questions (Table 6 in Appendix A.1) based on
all their full-length stories. The questions inquired about their experiences in developing characters
with differing roles, comparing characters, and building character relationships.
(2) Memory-recall workshop (20 minutes): To help participants recall their work in prepa-
ration for the next phase, we offered worksheets that served as elicitation materials. Participants
selected one of their previously created full-length stories and completed three types of worksheets:
the Character List, Character Cards, and Character Relationship Map (Figure 3). For online par-
ticipants, the worksheets were shared via FigJam [38], while offline participants received printed
copies. Further details of the workshop materials are described in §3.3.
(3) Q&A of selected creative experience (30 minutes): In this phase, we aimed to gain a
deeper understanding of their interconnected character creation process through concrete, situated
examples. Thus, participants answered the same set of questions (Table 6 in Appendix A.1), but
now contextualized to the specific story they had selected in the second phase. All participants
received compensation of 30,000 KRW (approx. 21 USD) for their participation.
3.3
Materials for Memory-Recall Workshop
The worksheets for the memory-recall workshop were designed to help participants reflect on
the characters from one of their previously created full-length stories. The Character List helped
participants recall both main and supporting characters by listing their names. The Character
Cards prompted focused reflection on 3–4 main characters by writing their descriptions, an
approach inspired by character sheets used by storywriters [153]. The Character Relationship
Map guided participants in outlining relationships and interactions among the main characters,
inspired by character maps discussed in storywriting literature [95]. Acknowledging that these
materials may not align with their usual practices, participants were encouraged to recall their
work and creation process using alternative methods if preferred.

3.4
Analysis
We transcribed over 1,260 minutes of audio recordings from interviews with 14 participants. The
worksheets completed during the memory-recall workshop were used solely as reference materials
to clarify participants’ responses during the transcription process.
The interview data were qualitatively analyzed through three cycles of coding. In the first cycle,
the first author used structural coding [122] to categorize sections of the data in relation to our
study’s goal (approaches, challenges, strategies), resulting in our initial codes. Based on these initial
codes, the first author developed sub-codes through process coding [92] to identify patterns in
participants’ conceptual and observable actions. These sub-codes were then iteratively refined
through discussions among the researchers. Finally, the first author used pattern coding [122] to
cluster the sub-codes into three overarching categories that explain participants’ interconnected
character creation practices. This final analysis process was also reviewed by the other researchers.
The entire analysis process was conducted over two months.

3.5
Findings
We report participants’ approaches to interconnected character creation, the challenges they face
during this process, and the strategies they use to address these difficulties. These insights are
organized into three overarching categories that encapsulate their practices.
3.5.1
Branching Out Related Characters.
Approach. In line with writing theory on character cast construction [89], writers would often
start by creating their main characters, then branch out new characters in relation to these existing
characters (P1, P3, P4, P5, P6, P7, P8, P9, P10, P11). One specific method involved splitting the traits
of the main character and distributing them among other characters (P7, P9). P9 mentioned that
this method ensured that these characters would form a cohesive whole, as each character would
reflect different aspects of the main character’s personality. Another method was to think of new
characters that would complement existing characters or have opposing characteristics to create
conflict (P1, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10). One last notable method was to imagine specific situations where
the main characters would be placed and envision other characters that would naturally exist in
the same environment (P1, P7, P9).
Challenges. However, thinking of new characters, especially those who would influence the
protagonist in specific ways, was often challenging for some writers (P4, P7). The guidance offered
to the main character and the inner conflicts they experience depend on the supporting characters.
Therefore, participants placed great importance on finding characters that could appropriately
fulfill these roles.
"The character needs to act in a certain way, but who could be the essential character to
influence that character towards this direction? A friend, perhaps? What kind of personality
and values does this friend have, and how would they influence the protagonist? It’s not
just about what they say; it’s also about the kind of relationship they’ve had until now.
But who could that be?" (P4)
P7 also shared her experience, mentioning that the process of adjusting supporting characters
so they fit these roles seems "roughly correct at first, but roughly correct is not enough. One must
continuously refine these characters and repeat this process."
Strategies. In response to this challenge, P4 said he would brainstorm a wide range of characters
with a fellow writer, see how each character "feels," and swap them out until something clicks. P7
added that she would sometimes even change the settings of existing characters when things were
not working out.
In summary, writers often expand their character casts in relation to the main characters. However,
coming up with new characters that could appropriately influence these core characters was often
challenging. This process led to ideating a broad spectrum of potential characters and iteratively
refining them to create the perfect match. Thus, the tool should offer a wide selection of related
characters with key descriptions, allowing writers to quickly explore options and iteratively adapt
characters when needed.

start by thinking of ways his characters would contrast visually and progressively alter several
attributes of each character, such as their gender, when their identities overlapped. Second, this

character orchestration and the unity of opposites [34], which emphasize arranging contrasting yet
interdependent characters to sustain dramatic conflict.
Challenges. Despite these initial approaches, many writers found it challenging to create distinct
characters, leading to overlapping character identities (P1, P2, P5, P7, P8, P10, P11, P13). For
instance, P2 worried that her characters would reflect her own values and thoughts too much,
further explaining that individuals created from a single source would inevitably result in similar
personalities. In addition, writers explained that balancing character identities that would contrast
and align in different aspects was also challenging (P7, P8, P13).
"What feels more compelling to me is exploring the sparks that fly when two equally
powerful beings, each acting based on their beliefs or convictions, come into conflict. So,
it’s not just about aligning their social status but also about balancing the scale of their
beliefs and actions, which is what I find challenging." (P8)
Strategies. To create distinct character identities, writers aimed to capture essential traits that
could clearly define their characters through expert interviews, reading other stories, and observing
social media, among other sources. In addition, writers (P1, P2, P3, P5, P7, P8, P9, P10) expressed
the importance of becoming immersed in the character’s inner world and deeply empathizing with
them, such as by writing character backstories in the first person. To create characters that hold
both contrasting and shared identities, writers (P1, P2, P3, P4, P7, P8, P9) would place characters in
identical situations to further establish their personalities, a tactic also described as "character webs"
in writing theory [32]. Some writers used predefined questionnaires and answered these questions
for each character in the initial stage of characterization. Others would create such situations as
scenes within their stories to continuously refine character details.
In short, writers often compared character identities to create both distinguishable and similar
characters. Yet, balancing character similarities and differences remained a persistent challenge.
Strategies to tackle this issue included examining how several characters would respond to a
common situation, as well as deeply empathizing with and understanding characters’ inner worlds.
Therefore, the tool should also allow writers to explore how each character interprets identical
contexts in ways that reveal their internal thought processes and subjective perspectives.
3.5.3
Fleshing Out Relationships.
Approach. Consistent with prior work [153] that investigated writers’ use of tools for ideation,
we found that several writers created character relationship maps in the form of diagrams to
organize and explore new connections between characters (P1, P5, P9, P13, P14). Specifically, P5
would use these diagrams to get a broader view of character relationships and often eliminate
characters who remain isolated and unconnected to others. Similarly, P13 and P14 noted that these
diagrams help them discover new relationships between characters that they had not previously
considered.
Challenges. Despite these approaches, intricately fleshing out character relationships remained
a challenge for several writers (P5, P7, P9, P11, P13). For example, P9 explained:
"Characters A and B may be defined as close friends, but within that, there might be
nuances, like one of them not attending the other’s wedding. Even though they might all
be grouped as close friends from the outside, the deeper you delve, the more you uncover
different circumstances and histories that make the relationships more complex." (P9)

Strategies. To tackle these challenges, one major strategy used by writers (P9, P13, P14) was to
ideate a wide range of character interactions to deepen their understanding of these relationships,
even if they did not make it into the final narrative. Similar to the "free scene" [95] method in
which writers draft exploratory scenes that place characters in imagined situations to observe
how they behave, P13 referred to this technique as "hypothetical situations" and employed it to
determine whether the main characters would get along before drafting the main plot. In addition,
P9 mentioned that the emotions and interactions between characters would slowly accumulate
through these activities and naturally evolve into specific dialogue or episodes within the story.
To summarize, character relationship maps were utilized to identify and organize connections
between characters. However, writers struggled to thoroughly build upon these relationships.
Therefore, participants imagined how characters would interact with one another in various
situations, regardless of the story’s overall flow. This implies that the tool should also aim to reveal
character relationships through open-ended reactions towards each other.
3.6
Design Goals
Based on these findings, we identified three design goals (DG) to build a tool to support storywriters’
interconnected character creation.
DG 1. Suggest diverse characters related to existing ones. To support writers in branching
out related characters, the tool should focus on broadly recommending a wide range of
related characters with core traits, rather than extensive information about a single character
(§3.5.1). This goal focuses on the breadth of information.
DG 2. Support the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities. To assist
writers in balancing the similarities and differences between characters, the tool should
illustrate how each character interprets the same context in ways that reveal their inner
mindscapes (§3.5.2). This goal emphasizes the depth of information.
DG 3. Manifest character relationships through exploratory interactions. To help writers
flesh out character relationships, the tool should show open-ended reactions between char-
acters as testbeds for building and refining their dynamics (§3.5.3). This goal emphasizes
speculative information rather than narrative progression.
Through these three design goals, we aim to provide a plethora of resources that storywriters can
mold into concrete forms, use to understand their work deeply, or draw inspiration from while
leaving the generated results as-is.
4
Constella
Based on the design goals, we developed Constella, a creativity support tool designed to assist
storywriters’ interconnected character creation process through LLM-MA. Its core features include
FRIENDS DISCOVERY, JOURNALS, and COMMENTS. FRIENDS DISCOVERY generates three
characters in relation to a given character, aiding writers in expanding their ensemble with diverse
additions (DG 1). JOURNALS produces diary entries from multiple characters according to a shared
theme, revealing their internal emotions, thoughts, and behaviors (DG 2). COMMENTS enables
characters to respond to other characters’ journal entries, surfacing additional layers of character
relationships (DG 3). Here, we first discuss our source of inspiration when translating the design
goals into system features (§4.1). We then present the system’s components (§4.2), an example use
scenario (§4.3), and the implementation and prompt design (§4.4).

4.1
Metaphor and Interaction Concept
In this section, we explain the rationale for adopting social media as an overarching metaphor to
address our design goals. We also illustrate how specific social media interaction concepts inspired
the main features of our LLM-MA tool.
Metaphors have been utilized to provide conceptual models that influence the perception of and
interaction with AI systems [31, 76, 141]. Social media, in particular, contains intuitive interaction
concepts that facilitate social interaction. When used as a metaphor, it offers a familiar language
for modeling and communicating the affordances [97] of a system that naturally fosters networked
connections, polarized identity expression, and relational communication—all of which reflect our
design goals.
Specifically, social media offers unique features that allow people to easily connect with indi-
viduals beyond their usual acquaintances, creating an environment where everything becomes
a potential point of connection [103]. This characteristic aligns with DG 1. Drawing inspiration
from this, we designed FRIENDS DISCOVERY, a feature that allows writers to specify the types of
connections (e.g., Harry’s long-lost friend) they wish to seek for a given character. Subsequently,
the feature offers character candidates with relevant ties grounded in the defined connection point
and the seed character’s personal details.
Social media often reveals personal details about people’s lives, helping to build social bonds and
reinforce a sense of identity [82]. Furthermore, when anonymity is guaranteed, individuals are more
likely to engage in self-disclosure [112]. This often results in a shared environment where honest
and at times even polarizing [78] reactions to a single event are openly expressed and compared.
These traits match DG 2. Inspired by this, we designed JOURNALS, a feature that allows writers
to first define a theme (e.g., Ron has vanished) and then generate diary entries in which selected
characters articulate their inner thoughts on that shared topic.
Social media is frequently used to exhibit and strengthen relationships between users. For
instance, comment threads commonly serve as a space to express interpersonal affection, remark
on some mutual events, or proclaim relational status [5]. These characteristics map onto DG 3.
Based on this, we designed COMMENTS, a feature in which writers can select characters to freely
respond to each other’s journal entries, allowing their relationships to unfold through open-ended
interactions generated by this feature.
Lastly, across these features, outputs are presented in formats (e.g., diary entries) aligned with
their respective interaction concepts. By presenting text in forms distinct from conventional
narrative prose, we encourage writers to treat the generated content as raw materials to adapt
before incorporating them into the narrative, if incorporation happens at all. This approach aims
to render the integration of AI-generated content as a deliberate, transformative act and to ensure
that writing the final manuscript remains in the writer’s own hand, responding to prior calls to
carefully consider authorial agency in the design of AI-assisted writing tools [12].

micromanage previously generated content. Thus, we believed that a deliberate forgetting strategy

by eliminating a sense of finality.

5.2
Procedure
5.2.1
Tasks. Creating a full-length story is an extensive process that can take several months or
even years. Therefore, we split the process into a set of tasks focused enough to explore system
usage while aiming to align with participants’ usual writing practices.
(1) Characters. We asked participants to create at least five characters (profiles) within Constella.
Although the number of characters in fictional stories varies widely, Truby [143] proposed that
characters can be classified based on their roles: the hero, opponent, ally, fake-ally opponent, and
subplot character. While not all stories require characters for each of these five roles, some may
feature multiple characters fulfilling the same role. Thus, we did not prescribe specific character
roles but requested a minimum number of characters.
(2) Character Backstory. We asked participants to write a central character’s backstory. This is
a foundational activity according to storywriting literature (§2.1) and one that was also utilized by
our formative study participants (§3.5.2). Given that relationships often form the basis of backstories
[27], we asked participants to include how their central character influenced or was influenced by
other characters in their backstory. For this task, we asked writers to use FRIENDS DISCOVERY, as
this feature was designed to support writers in discovering related characters. We included this
task to ensure FRIENDS DISCOVERY could be utilized non-intrusively, eliminating the need for
participants to incorporate entirely new characters in their narratives, as they may have already
set clear goals for their character cast. In addition, we only asked participants to write a backstory
for one character, as this task was sufficient for trying FRIENDS DISCOVERY.
(3) Scenes. Participants were asked to write three types of scenes: Scene Type A, B, and C.
Scene Type A. A scene where several characters show contrasting or aligned perspectives. The
former can be interpreted as conflict and the latter as unity, both essential elements in stories
[34]. Therefore, we believed that this scene could naturally be integrated into their narrative. We
encouraged participants to use JOURNALS when writing this scene, as the feature is intended to
help writers delve into and compare their characters’ thoughts, making it relevant to this task.
Scene Type B. A scene that shows the relationship between two characters. As character rela-
tionships are also central components in stories, we expected these requirements to cause minimal
deviation from their narrative goals. We asked writers to use COMMENTS when writing this scene,
as this feature was designed to help writers elaborate on character relationships. We also note that
while participants could include any number of multiple characters per scene, we asked them to
focus on two-character relationships in Scene Type B, as COMMENTS mainly supports dyadic
interactions (§4.2.3).
Scene Type C. A scene that involved several characters. For this task, participants were informed
to use at least one of the system’s AI features. We provided minimal requirements for this scene to
explore additional system usage when writers were given more freedom to use Constella according
to their own needs.
(4) Story Outline. We asked participants to complete a story outline for a full-length story
featuring at least five characters (profiles). Before completion, we asked writers to use at least one
of Constella’s AI features. This task was included because this study aimed to explore system usage
within the context of writing a full-length story. The act of writing a story outline provided this

5.3.1
Quantitative Analysis. In this subsection, we describe our quantitative analysis of system
logs, supplemented by participants’ Notion diary entries and their final work. The term final work
refers to the backstory, scenes, and story outline that participants submitted at the end of the study.
To first gain a high-level understanding of which AI features participants used while working on
specific tasks, we combined system logs with participants’ Notion diaries. We used their Notion
diaries because they provided day-by-day records of which tasks participants were working on. This
resulted in Figure 14. We then collected only the system logs about the profiles for the characters
that appeared in their final work and the journal entries and comments that remained in participants’
Constella accounts for those characters, on the assumption that non-deletion in the system may
indicate potential relevance to their final work. We give an overview of these data in §6.1.1 and use
them as the basis for all subsequent analyses, referring to them as system data in this subsection.
Before probing deeper into the relationship between the system data and participants’ final
work, we analyzed text-level edits performed by writers within the system. This is because we
viewed editing as an indicator of appropriation, a step we expected would typically occur before
incorporating, if at all, the generated content into their final work. For each piece of content, we
computed three complementary metrics: 1) edit counts, 2) word retention rate, and 3) word insertion
rate. Edit counts reflect the volume of revision operations conducted by the user within the system.
Word retention rate is the proportion of word tokens in an item’s first saved version that were
preserved in the last version saved in Constella. Conversely, word insertion rate is the proportion
of word tokens in the item’s last version that were not present in the first saved version. In addition,
for profiles, we only counted edits to attribute and relationship attribute descriptions (see §4.2 for
their definitions) to exclude trivial organizational or labeling adjustments.
Next, we examined potential links between the system data and participants’ final work. Specifi-
cally, for character profiles, we first calculated the number of times each character was mentioned in

Qualitative Analysis. In this subsection, we present the qualitative analysis conducted on
participants’ interview data and diary entries, supplemented by their final work and system logs.
To prepare our data for analysis, we transcribed over 1,320 minutes of audio recordings from
interviews with 11 participants. The interview transcriptions and diary entries were then analyzed
through reflective thematic analysis [9].
Our analysis process was conducted through the following procedures. First, the first and second
authors repeatedly read through the entire dataset of the diary entries and interview transcriptions.
Then, these two authors developed initial codes by categorizing the data based on our tool’s features.

through discussions among all the authors.
As a result of this six-month process, we constructed themes related to use cases for intercon-

future usages, improvements, and limitations. We also note that we found no significant thematic
variation based on participants’ duration of creative writing experience or LLM experience and

Discussion
In this section, we first identify opportunities for AI interaction design in storywriting. To do so, we
begin by reflecting on our system’s design goals through revisiting patterns of user appropriation
(§7.1) and exploring how multi-agent interactions can be intentionally designed to encourage
balanced attention across the character cast (§7.2). We then present broader design implications for
AI-based writing support tools that value authorial agency by highlighting the value of deliberate
constraints and intermediary materials (§7.3). We also discuss the opportunities and challenges of
employing metaphors in AI-based CSTs (§7.4). Finally, we address the challenge of intent alignment,
advocating for AI assistance that reduces cognitive load without diminishing opportunities for user
reflection (§7.5).
7.1
Reflecting on the Design Goals
We reflect on our system design goals outlined in §3.6, assessing the extent to which they have
been achieved through our user study. We also use participants’ acts of appropriation [81] and
misuse [80] uncovered through the study as resources to identify design opportunities for future
AI-based storywriting support tools.
DG 1: Suggest diverse characters related to existing ones. FRIENDS DISCOVERY allows
writers to discover three new characters in relation to a given character by specifying their relation-
ship. In the study, all participants reported that our tool helped them discover various characters
aligned with their desired relational contexts (§6.2.1). As the tool offered multiple variations, we
also observed that several writers (N=4) engaged in a creative process of remixing suggested
characters by combining several of their attributes. This emergent use highlights a potential area
for improvement: generating new character variations based on remixed attributes. For instance, a
writer might select the element of a "magician" from one suggested character and the element of
"sleepwalking" from another. The system could then generate further character variations based on
their combinations while still aligning with the original relationship prompt. This approach could
potentially expand the diversity of characters writers can explore while preserving their relational
ties to existing ones.
DG 2: Support the comparison and investigation of in-depth character identities. JOUR-
NALS provide writers with characters’ introspective reactions toward a shared theme. All par-
ticipants in our study regarded the feature as a valuable aid for delving into their characters’
minds and personal viewpoints (§6.2.2). In addition, writers (N=8) mentioned that having multiple
characters reflect on the same theme helped them better understand and articulate contrasts and
similarities between characters. Lastly, we found that several writers (N=5) drew inspiration from

7.2
Balancing Attention through Multi-Agent Interactions
We introduced Constella, an LLM-based multi-agent tool that supports storywriters’ interconnected
character creation process. Our CSI score (§6.1.2), along with qualitative findings that illustrate the
diverse ways writers used the tool (§6.2), collectively suggest that Constella complemented a range
of approaches to character creation. Furthermore, one notable finding was that our tool not only
supported but also actively reshaped writers’ existing practices. In particular, Constella enabled
writers to broaden the scope of their attention to a greater extent across the character cast (§6.3.1).
This complements findings from prior chatbot-based tools for character creation [113, 125], by
showing how AI can support writers in noticing [144] and attending to other characters beyond
the protagonist. However, this does not imply that all characters receive equal attention; as our
quantitative analysis reveals, writers still invest more in their manually created characters with
more central roles (§6.1.1). Rather, we interpret this as a redistribution of attention, and we coin
this emergent phenomenon the balancing effect, which allows writers to engage with and further
develop characters that would otherwise be overlooked or sidelined. In this section, we examine
the underlying features that contributed to the balancing effect, as well as its implications as a
design affordance for future storywriting support tools.
Several features of Constella played a critical role in fostering the balancing effect. FRIENDS
DISCOVERY surfaced unexpected ties when generating new characters, prompting writers to
explore interwoven narratives they had not initially envisioned (§6.2.1). We believe this process
enabled several generated supporting characters to take on firm and meaningful roles within the
narrative, thereby underscoring their potential to shape story dynamics alongside central characters.
Through JOURNALS, writers received a chorus of perspectives from their characters, encouraging
them to shift focus more fluidly across the cast (§6.2.2). This process helped further deepen and
humanize these characters, ultimately reinforcing the notion that these characters, regardless of
role, have a story worth telling. We believe this underscores the ability of multi-agent tools to guide
writers towards the creation of narratives in which a broader range of characters can potentially
contribute more meaningfully to the evolving story.

7.3
AI Design for Authorial Agency
Our findings indicate that two design aspects of Constella played a critical role in helping writers
preserve a sense of agency during human-AI writing: constraining AI’s generative role and offering
intermediary forms of text.
Deliberate Constraints. To align with the idiosyncratic values of writers, existing discourses
have emphasized adaptability as a key principle for co-creative AI systems [7, 113, 114]. Yet, might
there also be value in applying deliberate constraints on AI within these tools? Our findings show
that these constraints, such as not generating the central character and providing only a minimal
set of attributes, can prevent writers from delegating creative decisions entirely to the AI system
(§6.3.2). These imposed boundaries also helped writers avoid over-reliance on AI and encouraged
more intentional and thoughtful decisions.
Echoing research advocating for meaningful limitations in technology [110, 111, 140], we believe
that carefully applied constraints can enable future writing support tools to create room for writers
to exercise their creative agency while still drawing on AI’s generative potential. For instance, this
could be done by constraining the scope of AI assistance to particular writing stages, as shown by
recent work [13] that aims to exclusively support prewriting activities. Another example could be
a tool that only generates the last sentence of a chapter, prompting writers to build the narrative
leading up to that conclusion. Such approaches may also help address what has been described
as "the dearth of the author" [69, 70], an issue wherein AI systems facilitate large volumes of text
with minimal authorial decision-making. Then, how to constrain AI capabilities to help writers
maintain authorial control while offering enough meaningful support will be a critical research
question to guide the design of future AI-based writing support tools.
Intermediary Materials. As mentioned in §6.4.4, several writers expected our tool to create
action sequences or dialogue in output-ready prose. However, writers also found that the format of

call for future research to further explore alternative forms of AI-based CSTs that do not merely

interpretive creative processes.
7.4
Adopting Metaphors for Designing Interactions in AI-based CSTs
Constella was designed with the social media metaphor. In this section, we critically reflect on
our own design process to examine the opportunities and challenges that emerged when using
metaphors to structure our tool’s interactions, and we broaden this reflection beyond Constella to
inform the design of other metaphor-driven AI-based CSTs.
As mentioned in §4.1, we adopted the social media metaphor to address our design goals, trans-
lating familiar social practices into an interface that intuitively supports character cast interaction.
During our design process, working with this metaphor helped us move beyond a common method
for interacting with LLMs, the chat-styled interface [21, 45, 113, 125], and instead imagine inter-
actions as acts of shaping a dynamic social space where characters connect, post, and respond to
one another. Recent work has similarly used metaphors to design novel interactions, including
Patchview’s generative dust-and-magnet metaphor for spatially organizing story elements [23]
and Toyteller’s toy-playing metaphor for manipulating symbolic characters to influence narrative
generation [24]. As such, we believe metaphors can enable more imaginative approaches when
designing AI systems for creative activities.
However, one concern persisted throughout the system design process: the potentially divergent
nature of social media and storywriting. For instance, because FRIENDS DISCOVERY resembles
‘friend suggestion’ features on social media, it could be interpreted as pulling characters from
existing stories rather than generating them. As this could raise concerns about plagiarism, we
addressed this potential confusion when introducing Constella to participants. Such tensions show
that the social media metaphor may carry over implicit assumptions that are at odds with writers’
values and expectations.

metaphors, as they can import unintended connotations from their original domains. For instance,

design of intelligent writing assistants [76]. However, anthropomorphic metaphors can inflate user

7.5
Aligning with Evolving Intent
We found that participants encountered a trade-off when detailing character profiles: they could
either invest more time to achieve generations that are better aligned with their envisioned char-
acters or exert less effort and receive more generic outputs (§6.4.3). This trade-off may disrupt
creative workflows, as writers are repeatedly forced to specify trivial details they already know
instead of immersing themselves in the act of storywriting.
Prior work [71] has also pointed out similar issues where AI systems fail to fully understand
users’ intent, as users often provide minimal prompts without fully expressing their goals or because
users themselves are uncertain about their creative goals during the early stages of creation. Our
tool addresses the latter challenge by helping users refine and reflect on their evolving intent by
manifesting their specified characters. However, our tool’s heavy reliance on manual inputs and
edits to character profiles appears to fall short in addressing the former challenge, mainly because
users sometimes found it a burden to actively type in and update character details.
One potential direction to support this process would be to treat AI-generated results as resources
for continuously refining character profiles. For example, when users make edits to the generated
outputs from JOURNALS and COMMENTS, the system could automatically reflect those changes in
the original character profiles. Additionally, a retrieval-augmented generation approach [79] could
help retain useful details for new outputs, reducing the need for users to specify some aspects in
their character profiles. These methods can also be applied to the writer’s story, as they may have
expressed these character details within their narrative, even if they were not explicitly defined in
the system.
While such AI-assisted mechanisms could reduce manual burden, they also raise important
questions about the impact of automation on user creativity. Manual tasks like updating character
personas, while seemingly tedious, may often spark reflection or new ideas [59]. Writers may not
be merely inputting data; they may also be reflecting on the character’s arc, emotional consistency,
and narrative role. If automation takes over that task, users may skip the opportunity to revisit
their ideas, even when they initially feel fully formed or obvious. Future research should therefore
explore how to best integrate these mechanisms in ways that preserve opportunities for writers to
reflect through their acts of creation.

Limitations and Future Work
Exploring Tool Usage During Entire Full-Length Story Creation. In our system evaluation
study, participants created characters, a backstory, scenes, and a story outline within the context of
developing a full-length story. However, these activities may not fully capture the tool’s potential
when used throughout the entire process of writing a complete story. This is because writing
an entire full-length story is a more prolonged and complex process, during which writers must
introduce additional characters and develop their relationships in greater detail. To assess the
tool’s long-term value, future work could build on the recommendations in §6.4.1 and conduct
longitudinal studies of entire full-length or even serialized story creation, where participants expect
the tool to provide even greater value.
Participant Cultural and Writing Backgrounds. While we made efforts to recruit writers
with diverse backgrounds, the participants in this study do not represent the full spectrum of
storywriters. From a cultural standpoint, all participants were based in South Korea, and this
shared cultural background may have influenced their perceptions of AI technologies and character
creation. In addition, while our recruitment aimed to include writers from a broad range of story
types and genres, writers ultimately chose specific ones during our evaluation study. Future work
could thus explore tool usage by focusing on writers from different cultural contexts and examining
how the tool supports a broader range of genres and story types. When doing so, incorporating
features suggested in §6.4.2 may further help accommodate their varied writing processes.
Prompt Engineering and Impersonation Quality. Constella currently uses prompt-based,
specifically nonparametric prompting [15], to guide each character’s behavior within a pre-trained
large language model. This technique is a common practice for roleplaying agents, offering a flexible
and lightweight method for assigning personas without requiring model retraining. However, vanilla
prompt engineering is prone to what has been termed the "overuse problem" [67], wherein the
model shows rigid adherence to a character profile and exploits persona attributes, including those
that deviate from the given topic. Future work may therefore explore integrating the PANDA
framework [67], which detects and mitigates persona overuse through fine-grained topic mapping
and context-aware scoring. Another direction to improve impersonation quality would be to
investigate fine-tuning strategies, as exemplified by CharacterGLM [156], which demonstrated
more contextually adaptive character behaviors compared to prompt-based approaches alone.
Nevertheless, even with improved prompting strategies and fine-tuning methods, it is important
to acknowledge that LLMs inherently carry biases rooted in their pre-training data [17, 68]. These
underlying tendencies may lead to stereotypical, homogenized, and culturally skewed outputs
[1, 2, 84, 93], potentially misaligning with the writer’s creative intent. Future efforts to mitigate
these issues will be crucial for enabling more inclusive, sensitive, and nuanced character portrayals
across a broader range of contexts.
