Abstract
In this paper, we tackle the dynamic mean-variance portfolio selection problem in a model-
free manner, based on (generative) diffusion models. We propose using data sampled from the
real model P (which is unknown) with limited size to train a generative model Q (from which
we can easily and adequately sample). With adaptive training and sampling methods that are
tailor-made for time-series data, we obtain quantification bounds between P and Q in terms
of the adapted Wasserstein metric AW2. Importantly, the proposed adapted sampling method
also facilitates conditional sampling. In the second part of this paper, we provide the stability
of the mean-variance portfolio optimization problems in AW2. Then, combined with the error
bounds and the stability result, we propose a policy gradient algorithm based on the generative
environment, in which our innovative adapted sampling method provides approximate scenario
generators. We illustrate the performance of our algorithm on both simulated and real data.
For real data, the algorithm based on the generative environment produces portfolios that
beat several important baselines, including the Markowitz portfolio, the equal weight (naive)
portfolio, and S&P 500.

Introduction
Portfolio selection, a crucial and fundamental task in financial engineering, has been a subject of
both theoretical and practical interest since the classical work Markowitz (1952). With the ability
to account for intertemporal hedging and rebalancing in a non-stationary market, formulating
portfolio selection as dynamic multi-period problems has become increasingly appealing; see Mossin
(1968), Merton (1971) for early works.
Researchers have explored this problem under various
specifications, such as continuous-time dynamic mean-variance problems, where return models are
prescribed by some stochastic differential equations Zhou and Li (2000), or discrete-time problems
with independent intertemporal returns Li and Ng (2000). However, due to incompleteness of the
information, estimation errors, or simply the infeasibility of fully modeling market uncertainty, we
lack access to the real model, as opposed to model-based approaches. Instead, we are restricted
to a limited set of financial data samples. Consequently, investigating portfolio selection problems
in a model-free manner has been a long-standing topic in financial engineering; see discussions of

To address this challenge, we propose to leverage score-based diffusion models. These models

Specifically, when the model s1:T ∼P is unknown, we propose a data-driven approach to train

We utilize the model Q as an oracle in the subsequent

learning (RL) agent using these simulated paths. Theoretically, we show that our method achieves
near-optimality in terms of Markowitz’s mean-variance criterion, thanks to two key contributions
of the present paper: (1) a quantification bound between P and Q, and (2) the model stability.
From an engineering perspective, the approximated model Q consists of a score network sθ, which
is the core of the score-matching technique used in generative models, and a Recurrent Neural
Network (RNN) encoder Rθ, which encodes the current state of the market. These two networks
are trained simultaneously using historical price data. The reinforcement learning agent is trained
on the generated data from Q, which are produced by evaluating sθ and Rθ in an adaptive manner.
Finally, the agent, fed with the encoded (and updated) market state, outputs portfolio selection
actions whose performance is evaluated through real-world experiments. For a summary of the
theoretical results, see Figure 1, and for the workflow when implementing the proposed solution for
model-free dynamic portfolio selection, refer to Figure 2.

the dynamic portfolio selection problem. First, because we now work under the surrogate model
Q, we must establish model stability: even if P and Q are close and we can solve the mean–variance
problem under Q, does the optimizer obtained from Q remain near-optimal for the original model
P? We answer this question in the affirmative:
Theorem (Theorem 3.8 and Corollary 3.9 in the main context). Denote by v(P) and v(Q) the
optimal values of the mean–variance problem under P and Q, respectively. Then, under appropriate
technical conditions, |v(P) −v(Q)| ≤CAW2(P, Q).
The final, yet still important and challenging, component of our framework is to efficiently
solve the mean–variance problem under Q. Although we can now sample from Q, the distribu-
tion is implicit, so numerical methods are required. Drawing inspiration from recent advances in
reinforcement learning (RL) within financial engineering (see Hambly et al. (2023) for a survey),
we adopt a deep RL approach and propose a policy-gradient algorithm (Algorithm 4). This is not
a straightforward application of classical policy-gradient methods because the problem possesses
4


## Page 5


two distinctive features. First, portfolio selection is constrained by data inadequacy and lacks an
episodic interaction structure: exploration cannot rely on repeatedly resetting the environment
unless one uses a large archive of historical data, which may fail to reflect current market condi-
tions. Instead, we generate fresh trajectories from Q using the most recent market information,
thereby promoting relevant exploration. Second, standard policy-gradient theory hinges on the
dynamic-programming principle (or the Bellman optimality equation), whereas the mean–variance
objective is known to violate dynamic programming Basak and Chabakauri (2010). To overcome
this obstacle, we employ a duality result from Labb´e and Heunis (2007), which reduces the primal
problem to a dynamic quadratic-hedging formulation. Consequently, our policy-gradient algorithm
effectively solves the dual problem, and the resultant portfolio policy is obtained by plugging the
optimal multiplier back into the duality relation. We further remark that this dual representation
is instrumental in the proof of model stability; see Section 3 for details.
To demonstrate the practicality of the proposed approach, we implement the complete workflow
shown in Figure 2. During the adaptive training and sampling of Q, the state space Rdt expands
sequentially for t = 1, 2, . . . , T, resulting in roughly dT 2 degrees of complexity. To curb this high di-
mensionality, we embed an RNN encoder that compresses x1:t into a feature space whose dimension
is independent of t; see Rasul et al. (2021), Yan et al. (2021). In practice, every conditional input
x1:t is replaced by its RNN encoding, and the encoder is trained jointly with the score network
sθ. Section 4.3 provides a comprehensive empirical evaluation in both synthetic and real-world
settings.
In the synthetic experiment, where the true sampler is available, we present training
logs showing that the policy gradient learns towards a model-based benchmark. In the real-world
study, our strategy produces portfolios that beat the S&P 500, an equal-weight portfolio, and
a historical-parameter Markowitz portfolio. We also investigate another diffusion-based strategy
that constructs a Markowitz portfolio using parameters estimated from data generated conditional
on the current market state. Although this diffusion-based Markowitz strategy is less stable than
the policy-gradient approach, it still surpasses the benchmarks and, for certain risk-aversion levels,
achieves the best overall performance. These findings highlight the effectiveness of the new training
and sampling procedure for time-series data.
To conclude, the contributions of this work are threefold:
1. We extend the score-based diffusion-model framework to time-series data. Using a novel adap-
tive training and sampling scheme, we establish theoretical error bounds under the adapted
Wasserstein metric. The framework also supports conditional sampling, which is crucial for
solving model-free portfolio-selection problems and constructing high-performance portfolio
strategies;
2. We prove a model-stability result in the adapted Wasserstein metric. Because we focus on
the mean–variance problem, the proof relies on a duality argument and a detailed analysis of
the associated quadratic-hedging problem;
3. We propose a policy-gradient algorithm that operates in a surrogate environment generated
by the diffusion model and exploits the dual formulation and dynamic programming. The
algorithm, together with a diffusion-based Markowitz strategy, outperforms several bench-
marks. Experimental results validate both the adaptive training-sampling procedure and the
effectiveness of conditional sampling.

Related works
The use of simulated price paths to inform investment and corporate-finance decisions pre-
dates modern portfolio theory. Hertz (1964) first popularized Monte Carlo simulation for capital-
5


## Page 6


budgeting analysis, and Boyle (1977) introduced path-wise Monte Carlo methods to option valua-
tion. During the 1970s–1990s, more sophisticated parametric dynamics, jumping diffusion Merton
(1976), stochastic volatility Heston (1993), and SV with jumps Bates (1996) - made large-scale
pathwise risk analysis computationally tractable.
Monte Carlo simulation entered mainstream risk management when the RiskMetrics Technical
Document popularized a scalable Value-at-Risk (VaR) framework J. P. Morgan (1996). Subsequent
regulatory amendments (Basel II, 2004 ff.) accelerated the adoption of simulation as a predominant
stress-testing device. Rockafellar and Uryasev (2000) then recast tail control as an optimisation
of Conditional VaR (CVaR), turning scenario-weighted losses into a direct portfolio-construction
objective.
Simulation also became a tool for taming estimation error. Michaud (1998)’s Resampled Effi-
cient Frontier averages optimal weights across thousands of bootstrap resamples, trading a slight
bias for a large variance reduction; the statistical foundations rest on Efron and Tibshirani (1994).
Variance reduction techniques, such as antithetic variates, control variates, stratified sampling, are
exhaustively cataloged by Glasserman (2004) and remain standard in parametric Monte Carlo.
A complementary line of work fits structured return models, simulates fresh paths, and optimises
portfolios on those synthetic scenarios.
For example, Paolella et al. (2021) feed non-Gaussian
orthogonal-GARCH simulations into a mean–CVaR programme with transaction-cost penalties;
Sahamkhadam et al. (2018) combine ARMA–GARCH marginals with EVT-copula tails to build
minimum-CVaR frontiers; Ang and Bekaert (2002) Monte-Carlo a hidden-Markov VAR to capture
regime shifts in international equity returns; Guastaroba et al. (2009) show multivariate-GARCH
scenarios dominate historical or bootstrap windows inside a mean–CVaR model; and Lim (2004)
derive closed-form mean–variance weights under stochastic-parameter diffusions, validating them
via large-scale wealth simulations.
Across parametric simulation, and resampling, classical research progressively broadens robust-
ness but still relies on explicit distributional or structural assumptions. Because these methods
remain only partially data-driven, they can falter when markets exhibit complex, non-stationary
joint tails or evolving dependence structures.
As asset universes became high-dimensional and tail dependencies more intricate, researchers
began exploring fully data-driven generators.
Early adversarial approaches such as TimeGAN
Yoon et al. (2019) and QuantGAN Wiese et al. (2020) demonstrated that generative adversarial
networks (GANs) can reproduce heavy tails, volatility clustering and cross-sectional dependence.
Specialised variants soon followed: CorrGAN targets realistic correlation matrices for risk-parity
and stress-testing tasks Marti (2020); Sig-Wasserstein GAN leverages the path-signature W1 metric
for conditional sampling that adapts to prevailing market states Liao et al. (2024); VolGAN special-
izes in generating implied volatility surfaces and is applied in constructing hedging portfolio Vuleti´c
and Cont (2024), Cont and Vuleti´c (2025); and Tail-GAN, trained with a VaR/ES-based objec-
tive that explicitly focuses learning on severe left-tail outcomes, enriches downside stress scenarios
beyond what standard GANs provide Cont et al. (2025).
These models supply high-resolution
scenario sets that far outstrip traditional resampling in capturing dependence structures, regime
shifts and tail behaviour, but they remain prone to training instability Mescheder et al. (2018) and
offer no tractable likelihoods for rigorous evaluation.
The state of the art has therefore shifted toward score-based diffusion models, which combine
stable optimisation with likelihoods that are at least numerically tractable and come with explicit
error bounds. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) by Ho et al. (2020), together with
the continuous-time score-SDE formulation of Song et al. (2021b), supply the theoretical foundation;
in practice, likelihoods are obtained via discretised importance sampling rather than closed form,
yet remain usable for model comparison.
Finite-sample convergence guarantees—expressed as
6


## Page 7


Wasserstein or KL error bounds under Lipschitz score error—have since been derived by Bortoli
(2022) and Gao et al. (2025), providing theoretical guarantees that are currently unavailable for
standard GAN objectives.
Building on these ideas, TimeGrad employs an autoregressive denoising architecture for mul-
tivariate prediction Rasul et al. (2021); ScoreGrad adapts continuous energy-based diffusion for
improved sampling efficiency Yan et al. (2021); and CSDI applies conditional score matching to
time-series imputation Tashiro et al. (2021). Subsequent advances such as TimeDiff Shen and Kwok
(2023) and TSDiff Kollovieh et al. (2023) show that diffusion generators can outperform transformer
baselines in forecasting, while FTS-Diffusion Huang et al. (2024a) focuses on high-fidelity uncondi-
tional synthesis and demonstrates downstream forecasting gains through data augmentation rather
than direct head-to-head benchmarks. Compared with GANs, diffusion models thus offer tractable
log-likelihoods, stronger convergence guarantees, and greater robustness, making them particularly
well suited to the dynamic mean–variance problems addressed in the present work.
Generative models are moving beyond passive scenario engines and into the decision loop
itself.
Auxiliary-classifier GANs that condition on market states can learn return distributions
whose samples feed directly into risk–return objectives, yielding portfolios that beat classical boot-
strap or historical-window benchmarks Kim and Lee (2023).
Diffusion-based methods advance
the idea further: denoising-diffusion probabilistic models have been trained to generate realistic
high-dimensional correlation matrices, enabling more faithful risk-parity and stress-testing exer-
cises than traditional parametrics Kubiak et al. (2024), and score-based (continuous-time) diffusion
model admits explicit statistical guarantee that addressing curse of dimensionality when there are
hidden low-dimensional structure Chen et al. (2025).
Beyond using AI to generate synthetic scenarios, researchers have also turned to reinforcement
learning (RL) for the allocation task itself. Modern RL systems learn portfolio policies end-to-
end, treating rebalancing as a sequential control problem with risk-adjusted rewards. Empirical
studies on cryptocurrencies and U.S. equities report multi-fold wealth growth and post-cost Sharpe
ratios exceeding two Jiang et al. (2017), Cong et al. (2021). A recent survey consolidates these
advances while highlighting ongoing challenges in sample efficiency, trading frictions, and risk-aware
exploration Hambly et al. (2023).
Existing work either builds a market simulator or learns an RL allocation rule, but not both
in one framework. We close this gap by first training a score-based diffusion model that produces
state-conditional market scenarios, and then training a policy-gradient portfolio strategy on those
simulated paths. A stability theorem ties the simulator’s adapted-Wasserstein error to the policy’s
mean-variance performance, so improving the diffusion model keeps the RL performance near-
optimal.

Organization of the paper
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present an adaptive training and
sampling framework of score-based diffusion models, and provide a AW2-quantification error bound
between the output distribution and data distribution. In Section 3, we turn to a specific mean-
variance portfolio selection problem, and establish the model stability in terms of the adapted
Wasserstein metric (i.e., the value function is Lipschitz in the adapted Wasserstein metric). Section
4 focuses on applications of the theoretical results and, in particular, develops a policy gradient
algorithm based on generative environment along with experimental results. 5 concludes the main
context. Appendices include technical discussions and proofs of the main results in Section 2.

4
Applications
In this section, we illustrate the application of diffusion-based adaptive sampling method proposed
in Section 2 to MV portfolio selection problems. Specifically, we draw sufficiently many samples
from the sampler, and use it as a proxy of the real data model. Results in Section 2 show that
these two distributions will be close in AW2, with sufficiently long diffusion time and sufficiently
small score matching error. Meanwhile, from Corollary 3.9 we know, value functions with these
two different models will also be close. It thus motivates us to use (deep) reinforcement learning
approach, with Q regarded as an approximated environment, which we call generative environment.
Because we can generate large amount of samples with similar statistic properties (P ≈Q) to the
limited amount of data, our approach potentially address the inadequacy and time-sensitivity of
financial data.

For reinforcement learning in financial applications, it is advisable to refrain from utilizing
long-term data, as certain global factors, such as macroeconomic conditions or policy changes,

implementation.

performance of the algorithm. With our approach, this issue is addressed by generating a sufficient
quantity of data from Q, which closely approximates the current real model, P. Additionally, we
can retrain Q with certain schedule to enable long-time implementation of our algorithm (see an
example of retraining trick in Subsection 4.3.2).

In the previous subsection, we showcase the performance of the reinforcement learning part of
