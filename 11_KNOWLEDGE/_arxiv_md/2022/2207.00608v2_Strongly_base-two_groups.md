Abstract. Let G be a ﬁnite group, let H be a core-free subgroup and let b(G, H) denote
the base size for the action of G on G/H. Let α(G) be the number of conjugacy classes of
core-free subgroups H of G with b(G, H) ⩾3. We say that G is a strongly base-two group
if α(G) ⩽1, which means that almost every faithful transitive permutation representation
of G has base size 2. In this paper we study the strongly base-two ﬁnite groups with trivial
Frattini subgroup.
1. Introduction
Let G ⩽Sym(Ω) be a transitive permutation group on a ﬁnite set Ωwith point stabilizer
H. A subset B of Ωis a base for G if the pointwise stabilizer of B in G is trivial. We write
b(G, H) for the base size of G, which is the minimal size of a base for G. Here H is a core-free
subgroup of G and b(G, H) is the smallest positive integer b with the property that

The base size of a ﬁnite permutation group is a classical invariant that has been studied
for many decades, ﬁnding a wide range of applications and connections to a diverse range of
problems in group theory and related areas (we refer the reader to the survey articles [3, 31]
and [10, Section 5] for more details). Determining the precise base size of a given group is
a diﬃcult problem and there has been an intense focus on establishing strong bounds on
the base sizes of primitive groups (here each point stabilizer is a maximal subgroup). In
this direction, there have been several major advances in recent years, partly motivated by
a circle of highly inﬂuential conjectures due to Babai, Cameron, Kantor and Pyber, all of
which have now been resolved.
There has also been substantial interest in understanding the base-two primitive groups,
which is an ambitious project initiated by Jan Saxl in the 1990s. Although a complete clas-
siﬁcation remains out of reach, there has been signiﬁcant progress towards this goal, which
is best described in terms of the Aschbacher-O’Nan-Scott division of the ﬁnite primitive
groups. For example, there are strong results of Fawcett [21, 22] on diagonal groups and
twisted wreath products, and work of several authors [23, 24, 29, 30] on aﬃne groups with
quasisimple point stabilizers. The study of base-two product-type groups was recently initi-
ated in [14] and there is an expanding literature for almost simple groups. For instance, the
base-two almost simple primitive groups with solvable point stabilizers are determined in [9]
and there is a complete classiﬁcation for the groups with socle an alternating or sporadic
simple group (see [11, 18, 27]). Some of these results have already found some interesting
applications. For example, see [12] for applications concerning strong 2-generation proper-
ties of almost simple groups and [15, 19] on the classiﬁcation of extremely primitive groups.

In this paper we introduce and study the following family of groups.
Deﬁnition. Let G be a ﬁnite group and let α(G) be the number of core-free subgroups H
of G (up to conjugacy in G) such that b(G, H) ⩾3 with respect to the natural action of G
on G/H. We call G a strongly base-two group if α(G) ⩽1.
For example, if G = M is the Monster sporadic simple group and H is a proper subgroup,
then [8, Theorem 3.1] states that b(G, H) ⩽3, with equality if and only if H = 2.B is the
centralizer of a 2A involution. It follows that α(G) = 1 and thus the Monster is a strongly
base-two group. It is therefore natural to ask if other simple (or almost simple) groups share
this property and we will answer this question in Theorem 2 below. More generally, our main
aim is to study the strongly base-two ﬁnite groups with trivial Frattini subgroup.
Deﬁnition. Let G be a ﬁnite group and let H be a subgroup. We say that H is large if
H ̸= G and |H|2 > |G|.
As a special case of Proposition 2.5 below, every ﬁnite nonabelian simple group T has
a large subgroup. As an immediate consequence, we deduce that α(T) ⩾1 and this partly
explains why we adopt the condition α(G) ⩽1 in the deﬁnition of a strongly base-two group,
rather than the more restrictive property α(G) = 0. This observation also leads us naturally
to the following deﬁnition, which we will need in order to state some of our main results.
Deﬁnition. A ﬁnite nonabelian simple group T is special if it has a unique Aut(T)-class of
large subgroups.

Theorem 2. Let G be a ﬁnite almost simple group with socle T. Then α(G) ⩾1, with

2. Almost simple groups
In this section we prove Theorems 1 and 2. We begin by recalling the following result,
which is [25, Theorem A].
Theorem 2.1. Let G be a ﬁnite group of composite order. Then G has a proper subgroup
H such that |H|2 ⩾|G|.
Remark 2.2. The proof of this result relies on the classiﬁcation of ﬁnite simple groups.
Indeed, by applying induction on |G|, it is straightforward to reduce the proof to nonabelian
simple groups, at which point one can inspect each family of simple groups in turn and
subsequently identify a maximal subgroup with the desired property. This extends earlier
work of Brauer and Fowler [6, Theorem 2D], who showed that every ﬁnite group G with even
composite order has a proper subgroup H with |H|3 ⩾|G| (if |G| is odd and composite, then
G is solvable by the Feit-Thompson theorem and it is easy to identify a proper subgroup H
with |H|2 ⩾|G|).
Remark 2.3. Observe that b(G, H) ⩾3 if H is a core-free subgroup of G and |H|2 ⩾|G|.
This is clear if |H|2 > |G|, so let us assume |H|2 = |G|. If b(G, H) = 2 then there exists g ∈G
such that the double coset HgH has size |H|2 = |G|, so G = HgH and thus G = HHg. Then
g = hk with h ∈H and k ∈Hg, so H = Hgk−1 = Hg and we have reached a contradiction.

To conclude, let us brieﬂy explain how one can use Theorem 8.2 and Proposition 8.5 to
analyze the groups with E(G) = Φ(G) = 1, c(G) = 2, L′ of odd order and α(G) = 1.
First observe that if H represents the unique conjugacy class of core-free subgroups of G
with b(G, H) ⩾3, then H must intersect F(G) nontrivially, but it cannot be contained in
F(G). Furthermore, there exists a normal subgroup J of L, as described in parts (iii) or (iv)
of Theorem 8.2 (see Remark 8.3). Then H = AJ, where A = Cp1 × · · · × Cpk is as described
in the proof of the theorem.
Now NL(A) = J (since ANL(A) is core-free and b(G, ANL(A)) ⩾3) and every subgroup
C of CF (G)(J) must be normal in G (otherwise H × C is core-free and b(G, H × C) ⩾3).
Moreover, L must act transitively on the set of subgroups B ⩽[J, F(G)] with the property
that B is normalized by J and the subgroups BJ and AJ have the same normal closure in
G. In particular, L/J acts simply transitively on the set of such subgroups. These conditions
essentially describe all the groups with α(G) = 1 in this setting, imposing various conditions
on the cyclic summands of [J, F(G)].
For example, if J = ⟨y⟩has the properties described in part (iii) of Theorem 8.2 and F(G)
has a subgroup W = (Cp)2 that is imprimitive as an L-module, then F(G) = W × CF (G)(y)
and every subgroup of CF (G)(y) is normal in G. Therefore, in case (iii) we may assume that
y acts as a scalar on each Vi and G has no imprimitive minimal normal subgroups. We leave
the task of describing the precise structure of the groups with α(G) = 1 to the interested
