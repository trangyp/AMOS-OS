Abstract: Non-linear dynamics of zonal flows is investigated in the context of
the gyrofluid modified Hasegawa-Wakatani model. Merging of zonal flows and the
chaotic developement of the initial zonal flow pattern is explored.
Conservation
equations for zonal flow momentum and energy with consistent finite Larmor radius
(FLR) effects are derived and used for a quantitative analysis of zonal flow mergers
in numerical simulations. The nonlinear local Reynolds stress transfer as opposed to
(hyper)viscous dissipation is found to be the main cause of merging. The applicability
of the concept of a ”phase transition” in the strict thermodynamical sense is discussed
in context of zonal flow transition hysteresis.

I.
INTRODUCTION
Understanding turbulence and instabilities in magnetized plasmas1,2 is foundational for
research on magnetically confined plasmas e.g. in fusion experiments. It remains an active
field of research even in highly simplified ”toy-models”. The delta-f, isothermal Hasegawa-
Wakatani3 model is the most basic system to study self-sustained, gradient-driven, quasi
two-dimensional4–7, resistive drift-wave turbulence8–11. It can be modified to contain the
interaction of turbulence and zonal flows12–21 and to include consistent flr effects within
the gyrofluid22–26 framework.
The gyrofluid modified Hasegawa-Wakatani (gmhw) model can be derived from higher
fidelity models. One option is by reducing 3D delta-f gyrofluid models27 using the parallel
Hasegawa-Wakatani closure. Another way to derive the gmhw model is to take the delta-f
limit on 2D full-f models28–30.
The transition of turbulence to zonal flows in the context of the modified Hasegawa-
Wakatani model14 has been investigated over two decades. Recently it was observed in Ref.
25 that zonal flows do not necessarily remain stable but undergo changes even in apparent
equilibrium states in long-time numerical simulations. These changes appear in the form of
the ”merging” of zonal flow streams. This is in line with the observations presented in Ref.
31 in the context of ITG drift-fluid simulations.
In Ref. 25 it was demonstrated that the resistive drift-wave turbulence to zonal flow
(forward and backward) transitions show aspects of a hysteresis loop. In Ref. 19 it was
suggested that this hysteresis is a feature of a phase transition19,32? .
In this article we further investigate the nonlinear dynamics of zonal flows in the context
of the gyrofluid modified Hasegawa-Waktani model during onset and merging of zonal flows.
The initial zonal flow patterns develop chaotically in the sense that they are sensitive to
(small) changes in the initial conditions. Nonlinear (”chaotic”) local momentum transfer
through the Reynolds stress appears to be a relevant ingredient for merging of zonal flows.
This nonlinear behavior is discussed in context of the question if turbulent zonal flow tran-
sitions in the Hasegawa-Wakatani system could be identified as a phase transition in the
strict thermodynamical sense.
To this end the hysteresis loop in the transition was reproduced in a numerical experiment.
Also it was extended to include finite Larmor radius (flr) effects. Non-linear effects in the

flr effects. In this section we also present the reproduction of the hysteresis loop mentioned
above and discuss the validity of the term phase transition from thermodynamics in this
context.

IV.
WHY DO ZONAL FLOWS MERGE?
In Ref. 25, it was shown that a significant change of zonal energy and transport can
happen suddenly even after the system has been seemingly stabilized in a nonlinear equi-
librium. This sudden rise of zonal energy coincides with a decrease of the radial zonal flow
8


## Page 9


modes. Two streams in one direction seem to ’merge’ and extinguish the stream in the other
direction, which has been between them before. A similar observation was reported in Ref.
31 for two-dimensional ITG turbulence.
The color bars in Figures 1 and 2 (top panel) both show the direction and amplitude of
zonal velocity profiles flows in y-direction ⟨vy(x, t)⟩y. Red streams go towards the positive
y−direction (upwards), blue streams go towards the negative y−direction (downwards).
Note that the positive upwards flow direction corresponds to the electron diamagnetic drift
direction, which is also the flow direction of the basic drift-wave mechanism6.
In Figure 1 three merger-events are presented in a simulation for τ = 0 and C = 1.7.
Three flows in positive y-direction (red) suddenly vanish or, in other words, the neighboring
flows in negative y-direction (blue) merge at these points.
We noted in many simulations that if a merger happens, then in virtually every instance
a (red) upward stream is vanquished by the merging of two (blue) downward streams. This
break of symmetry might be connected to an asymmetry in the zonal flow profiles themselves.
In Ref. 44 it was reported in the context of zonal flows in the Hasegawa-Wakatani system,
that upwards streams tend to be less sharp than downward streams.
This can also be
observed when estimating the curvatures of the zonal flow profile (blue, dashed line in the
bottom panel of Figure 2) by numerically calculating the second derivatives at the peaks
(upward flows) and the troughs (downward flows). The mean curvature for the troughs (≈
0.015) is almost twice as large than the mean curvature for the peaks (≈0.008). According
to equation (13) the nonlinear drive of zonal energy is proportional to the radial gradient of
the poloidal flow ∂x⟨vy⟩y. Therefore the total nonlinear zonal energy drive could be smaller
at the broader tops of the upwards flows compared to the sharper troughs of the downwards
flows.
This interrelation might be part of the explanation for the apparent asymmetry
in what flows are merging.
Another kind of symmetry break in zonal flows that could
influence this particular behavior by locking certain turbulent modes either in upwards or
in downwards flows was recently reported by Ref. 21.
A closer look at the ’merger’ happening at (x, t) ≈(104ρ0, 1500L⊥/c0) is presented in
Figures 2 and 3.
Merging events appear to happen due to the same reason, why zonal flows emerge in
the first place: momentum is locally transferred through nonlinear interactions given by
the radial gradient of the Reynolds stress and a smaller (but not negligible) hyperviscosity

We conclude this section with the proposition that, both the merging of zonal flows and
the generation of zonal flows from turbulence in the first place, are (mostly) nonlinear phe-
nomena with the same cause: the radial gradient of the Reynolds stress and the contribution
of hyperviscosity given in equations (9) and (10) respectively. In general we also observe
that mergers shift the radial spectrum towards lower mode-numbers (see also Figure 4 in
the subsection below).
Since both the emergence and the evolution of zonal flows appear to be nonlinear processes
one might ask the following two questions: 1) Which zonal flow patterns emerge in the first
place? 2) Is there an equilibrium state at all, or can mergers always happen in a driven
system like the Hasegawa-Wakatani model?

B.
Effects of resolution and domain-size on zonal flow patterns
A prior convergence study has been conducted in Ref. 46 with the result that a resolution
of nρ = 4 and a simulation domain of Lx = 128ρ0 are sufficient values for a parameter range
of 0.01 ≤C ≤1 and 0 ≤τ ≤1.
In that study, time-averages of values in nonlinear
equilibrium of single simulations were compared for different parameters. Also the Pad´e
approximations for the flr operators were used in the former study. It was noted in Ref. 46
that the results were partially inconclusive for high values of the parallel coupling parameter
C.
In this section, we reproduce the results of this study with an emphasis on zonal flow
patterns in order to verify, that the simulation parameters make sense for the experiments
presented in this publication. Also, we use the flr operators in their original forms as
presented in equations (4). Ensembles of simulations with small variations in the initial con-
ditions are used to produce distributions of the dominant radial wavenumbers qd
r as presented
in Figure 4. This takes into account the chaotic dependence of zonal flow patterns on small
(random) variations of the initial conditions. The mean values and standard deviations of
this distributions can then be compared for different sets of parameters.
In Figure 5 we present convergence of resolution and simulation domain using this method.
Figure 5a addresses the question whether the simulation domain is large enough to cover
the dominant radial modes. We observe that for low values of the parallel coupling C ≈0.25
together with the high value of τ = 2 convergence appears to be reached (within error-bars)
at Lx ≥100ρ0. For larger values C ≥0.7 in combination with smaller values of τ < 2 it
seems to be reached only at approximately Lx ≥100ρ0.
In general, we observed that structures tend to be larger for small values of C compared
to intermediate values1. Note that this trend does not continue into the highly adiabatic

V.
DOES AN EQUILIBRIUM STATE EXIST?
In this section we discuss the possibility of an actual equilibrium state for simulations,
where zonal flows are present.
We observed, that the merging of zonal flows tends to be a one-way process (towards
lower mode-numbers). On the contrary, it was observed for the ITG case31, that zonal flows
can also split up and thus in principle reverse the process of merging. In a parameter range
of 0.1 ≤C ≤2 and 0 ≤τ ≤2 we were unable to verify the existence of this reverse process
of splitting up of zonal flows.
Only in the highly adiabatic regime C ≫1 we observed fast radial propagation of zonal
flow momentum as an addition to the zonal flow dynamics described above. However, that
effect appeared to be less pronounced or even non-existent for higher resolutions (and lower
hyperviscosity parameters). It might thus be caused mainly by viscosity and therefore not
be that relevant for the simulation of a hot plasma. Also for a very high value of τ = 4
one simulation with a split up of zonal flows was discovered (data not shown here). An
investigation of those more extreme parameter ranges is computationally expensive and
postponed to future work.
If the merging of zonal flows is in fact a one-way process for this particular model and the
parameter range discussed here, the following question arises: Will the flows keep merging
over time and end up filling the whole simulation domain with one flow qd
r = 1? Or does
a stable stable zonal flow pattern exist, where no mergers will happen anymore once it has
been reached?

Theoretically the equations (9) and (10) imply the existence of equilibrium zonal flows
in the cold ion limit, when neglecting the hyperviscosity contribution.
Note that in Ref. 19 the viscosity contribution only acts on the non-zonal components of
the time-evolved quantities and therefore would not appear at all in the zonal flow momen-
tum (9) and energy (10) equations respectively. In that case only the nonlinear term with
the Reynolds stress appears as a contribution. Unfortunately we were not able to obtain
numerically stable simulations in our code framework using this specific non-zonal viscosity
term. Besides the trivial solution, any system of orthogonal periodic functions would effec-
tively be stable, while any flow that has a finite overlap of ˆvx and ˆvy will be unstable. As the
orthogonality relation must hold for the non-zonal velocity components, the corresponding
potential and density distribution is non-trivial and does not occur naturally. Orthogonal
configurations like ˆvx = cos x cos y, ˆvy = sin x sin y are technically stable but, similarly to the
co-advecting vortex problem, become unstable after some time due to numerical rounding
errors24. Thus in theory only trivial situations with ˆvx = 0 or ˆvy = 0 enable analytically
stable zonal flow configurations without hyperviscous contributions to the zonal velocity
components.
One way to ensure this stability of zonal flows would be the suppression of all medium-

make sense from a modeling perspective.
We conclude that once zonal flows play a role for quantities like the radial transport,
they should be preferably established through ensembles of simulations with varying initial
conditions and not through single simulations, since the initial zonal flow patterns are chosen
chaotically and the existence of a final equilibrium state could not be established analytically
or numerically with the parameters given in section II.

Both, the nonlinear dynamics within the hysteresis loop as well as the chaotic results
in sub-critical states can also be used as arguments, that the term phase transition19 from
thermodynamics might not be adequate in this context.
More fundamentally, the Hasegawa–Wakatani system does not seem to admit a free-
energy functional whose minima would define competing equilibrium states, as is required
for a genuine thermodynamic phase transition48. The Hasegawa-Wakatani system is non-
Hamiltonian in the sense, that it is both driven and dissipative. Therefore no free-energy
functional can be derived from the Hamiltonian from a variational principle. However with-
out a well-defined free-energy functional, there can be no equilibrium metastable states sep-
arated by an energetic barrier. Therefore, there seems to be no thermodynamic mechanism
capable of producing a true first-order transition.
In that strict sense the hysteresis seems to reflect the coexistence of more than one
dynamically sustained attractor in a non-equilibrium system, rather than the crossing of
free-energy minima.
VII.
CONCLUSION
We have shown that nonlinear terms in the gyrofluid Hasegawa-Wakatani model lead to
1) a chaotic development of initial zonal flow patterns and 2) chaotic dynamics of seemingly
stable zonal flow dominated systems through the merging of zonal flows. The merging of
zonal flows is explained with nonlinear local momentum transfer through Reynolds stress.
The sensitive dependence on initial conditions seems to suggest that time-averages of single
simulations might not be enough to predict e.g. transport coefficient in real live systems.
Rather the averages of ensembles of simulations with variations in the initial conditions
might be preferable.
Furthermore we investigated flr effects on zonal flows as well as their dependency on
the parallel coupling parameter C.
This was done by sampling ensembles of dominant
22


## Page 23


zonal flow mode numbers qd
r for a range of τ and C. Finite values of the ion to electron
temperature ratio τ significantly alter the dominant zonal flow modes with a simple model
qd
r(τ) ∝1/(1 + τ), whereas the connection to the parallel coupling parameter C is not as
conclusive.
Moreover we could reproduce previous results from Ref. 19 concerning the hysteresis in
the turbulence to zonal flow transition. This observation could be extended to finite values
of τ. Arguments against the appropriateness of calling this bifurcation a phase transition in
the strict thermodynamic sense were given.
The authors are well aware, that the 2D, isothermal, electrostatic, delta-f model presented
in this publication cannot be used to predict the behavior of magnetized plasma in fusion
experiments. zonal flows apparently do not play such a dominant role in 3D electromagnetic
full-f simulations of tokamak-edge plasmas27,49.
Nevertheless the chaotic developement of initial zonal flow patterns as well as a chaotic
dynamical behavior in seemingly saturated states might also be given in higher fidelity
models. However, the question of the relevance of the phenomena reported in this publication
has to be further investigated, using higher fidelity models (especially 3D).
