Abstract
Metacognition is a critical component of intel-
ligence, specifically regarding the awareness of
one’s own knowledge. While humans rely on
shared internal memory for both answering ques-
tions and reporting their knowledge state, this de-
pendency in LLMs remains underexplored. This
study proposes a framework to measure metacog-
nitive ability d′
type2 using a dual-prompt method,
followed by the introduction of Evolution Strat-
egy for Metacognitive Alignment (ESMA) to bind
a model’s internal knowledge to its explicit behav-
iors. ESMA demonstrates robust generalization
across diverse untrained settings, indicating a en-
hancement in the model’s ability to reference its
own knowledge. Furthermore, parameter analysis
attributes these improvements to a sparse set of
significant modifications.
1. Introduction
Human intelligence is characterized by a sophisticated ca-
pacity for metacognition to monitor and regulate one’s own
cognitive processes (Brown, 1987; Chi et al., 1989; Mosh-
man, 2004). Within the expansive domain of metacognition,
this study concentrates specifically on self-knowledge, often
defined as knowing what one knows.
Humans are typically capable of reporting their own knowl-
edge (Frith, 2012) because answers are formed by referenc-
ing semantic memory (Binder & Desai, 2011). Whether
providing a direct answer or assessing their awareness of
specific facts, individuals retrieve information from the same
internal source. Consequently, a strong association exists
between internal knowledge and explicit behavior across
various contexts.
While the metacognitive capabilities of LLMs have received
significant attention (Comanici et al., 2025; Yang et al.,

Second, Evolution Strategy for Metacognitive Alignment

across these untrained settings suggest that ESMA effec-

and explicit behavior.

performance effect. Instead, performance shifts are driven
by parameters undergoing significant modification. These
findings suggest that modifying every parameter is unneces-
sary, presenting new avenues for future optimization.

2. Related Work
Metacognition in LLMs
Research on LLM metacogni-
tion has largely addressed specific phenomena such as hallu-
cination (Ji et al., 2023), answer refusal (Zhang et al., 2024;
Wen et al., 2025), confidence calibration (Lin et al., 2022),
and self-evaluation (Madaan et al., 2023). However, these
studies primarily focus on enhancing utility by controlling
specific types of metacognitive errors in LLMs.
While some scholars report a lack of metacognition in LLMs
(Yin et al., 2023a; Kale & Nadadur, 2025), others suggest
models possess functional access to internal states (Comsa
& Shanahan, 2025). For instance, models have demon-
strated calibration in correctness assessment Kadavath et al.
(2022), the ability to restrict responses to provided informa-
tion Prato et al. (2024), awareness of learned characteristics
Betley et al. (2025), and superior behavioral prediction com-
pared to external observers Binder et al. (2025). Building
upon these findings, this study focuses on LLMs’ awareness
of their own knowledge and introduces a methodology to
strengthen the alignment between internal knowledge and
explicit behavior.
Evolution Strategies
Evolution Strategies (ES; Rechen-
berg, 1973; Schwefel, 1977) are gradient-free optimization
techniques that iteratively refine model parameters by evalu-
ating a population of perturbed candidates. Unlike backprop-
agation, which computes exact derivatives, ES requires only
forward passes. While traditionally limited by the high di-
mensionality of neural networks, recent advancements have
successfully extended this approach to the full-parameter
fine-tuning of LLMs (Qiu et al., 2025; Sarkar et al., 2025).
ES remains effective even for non-differentiable objectives
where gradient-based approaches typically fail. Since ES
generates individuals with updated weights prior to reward
calculation, it can model the entire behavioral patterns
across diverse situations as a single reward, rather than
focusing on isolated actions. Because the alignment be-
tween Direct and Meta Questions is such a holistic reward
function, ES was employed as the optimization method.
3. Measurement Framework
Measuring human metacognition is frequently operational-
ized via confidence-accuracy paradigms (Kunimoto et al.,
2001), where subjects solve a cognitive task and judge their
accuracy. Drawing on these psychological paradigms, this
work establishes a framework to measure d′
type2 in LLMs.
For details regarding the measurement of human metacogni-
tion and the proposed extension, refer to Appendix A.1.

Metric
d′
type2 is a standardized metric that quantifies
metacognitive ability. It measures a capacity to discriminate
between accurate and inaccurate judgments by calculating
the distance between the internal confidence distributions
for each decision type.
d′
type2 = Φ−1(Hit Rate) −Φ−1(False Alarm Rate), (1)
This formula quantifies metacognitive performance as the
statistical separation between mental states. A higher value
indicates that internal confidence reliably predicts accuracy.
In the framework of signal detection theory, d′ value is
typically interpreted as follows (Hautus et al., 2021):
• d′
type2 ≈0 (Chance-level): Inability to discriminate,
where the observer cannot distinguish between correct
and incorrect responses at all.
• d′
type2 ≈0.5 (Low Sensitivity): The lower bound of
typical experimental performance.
• d′
type2 ≈1.0 (Moderate Sensitivity): A standard level
of sensitivity often observed in discrimination tasks.
• d′
type2 ≥2.5 (Effective Ceiling): A level of perfor-
mance rarely encountered in standard tasks where dis-
crimination is virtually perfect.
While the d′
type2 metric is rigorous, it is helpful to also track
other metrics that are more intuitive:
• Raw Alignment: The averaged alignment between the
meta-response and the correctness of the direct out-
come. While this measure is intuitive, it’s suscepti-
ble to bias and statistical coincidences. For instance,
a model that indiscriminately answers “No” would
achieve a higher score in a difficult setup.
• Accuracy: The ratio of correct answers provided for
Direct Questions.
• Yes Ratio: The frequency with which the model pro-
vides a positive response to Meta Questions, indicating
a belief that it knows the answer.
• Yes Failure Ratio (YFR): The proportion of instances
where the model claims to know the answer but subse-
quently provides an incorrect response for the Direct
Question.
• No Failure Ratio (NFR): The proportion of instances
where the model claims it does not know the answer
but is actually able to provide the correct response.

nitive Alignment (ESMA). Please refer to Appendices A.2
and B.2 for further details on ES and comparison with SFT.

Reward Design
The reward function is designed along
two primary axes: the direct correctness of the model’s an-
swer and its metacognitive alignment. Optimizing solely
for direct correctness risks leaving the model metacogni-
tively blind, whereas optimizing only for meta-alignment
invites reward hacking. In the latter scenario, a model may
maximize rewards by intentionally providing incorrect an-
swers while consistently claiming ignorance. (Refer to Ap-
pendix B.1 for experimental validation).
To prevent such degenerate behaviors, a differential reward
system is applied based on the joint outcome of the direct
and meta question. Let C = 1 if the direct answer is correct
and C = 0 otherwise. Let A = 1 if the meta answer is
aligned with the actual knowledge state (e.g., saying “Yes”
when C = 1, or “No” when C = 0) and A = 0 otherwise.

Several experiments were conducted to assess the intrinsic
metacognitive levels of LLMs and evaluate the impact of
ESMA on their improvement. The study utilized open-
weights models including Qwen2.5, Llama3.2, and Gemma

Dataset
To isolate metacognition from confounding vari-
ables like linguistic fluency or complex reasoning, TriviaQA
(Joshi et al., 2017) was selected as the training source and
primary benchmark. TriviaQA is particularly suitable as it
comprises fact-based questions with short keyword answers
(e.g., “Which city does David Soul come from?”). By pri-
oritizing direct knowledge retrieval, the model’s ability to
recognize the presence or absence of specific information is
more effectively isolated.

5.2. Confidence-based Metacognition
To validate the model’s reliance on fundamental internal
knowledge sources, this analysis contrasts continuous con-
fidence scores with the discrete binary outputs (Yes/No)
utilized in prior settings. Examining whether metacognitive
alignment persists across these differing modalities deter-
mines if the model operates on robust internal represen-
tations rather than input-specific dependencies. For this
purpose, confidence D was calculated as the normalized
probability of a positive self-assessment, derived from the
logits of the “Yes” and “No” tokens.

Psychology research frequently utilizes Type 2 AUCROC
for continuous or granular metacognitive ratings. In align-
ment with this standard, the analysis applied a Receiver
Operating Characteristic (ROC) method. ROC curves il-
lustrate metacognitive ability by detailing the change in
the True Positive Rate (TPR) as the False Positive Rate
(FPR) increases. Consequently, the Area Under the Curve
(AUC) provides a threshold-invariant metric for evaluating
metacognitive sensitivity within continuous confidence data.
• AUC = 0.5: A random baseline where confidence
provides no diagnostic information about accuracy.
• 0.5 < AUC < 0.7: Low to marginal metacognitive
ability.
• AUC > 0.7: Moderate to high metacognitive ability,
where the model’s internal state reliably distinguishes
between known and unknown information.

metacognitive ability even without explicit tuning. This 3B

5.3. Visualization of Confidence Distribution
A density plot of the confidence scores provides a visual
understanding of the extent to which the previous confi-
dence separates the distributions of correct and incorrect
instances. High metacognitive ability is characterized by
a clear separation between these two distributions: correct
answers should cluster toward a confidence of 1.0, while in-
correct answers should cluster toward 0.0. Figure 3 reveals
a significant transformation in the ESMA models’ internal
confidence landscapes.
In the original models, the correct and incorrect density
distributions overlapped significantly. The smaller models
displayed the low metacognitive ability, with a centralized,
unimodal distribution where the model was unsure about its
own status. However, as model size increased to 7B, these
distributions naturally began to segregate, confirming that
scaling inherently improves baseline metacognitive ability.
ESMA shifted these distributions dramatically. Across all
scales, the correct (blue) and incorrect (orange) distributions
became significantly more separate and polarized. This
separation indicates that the model has evolved to represent
its answers in a way that allows it to distinguish the two
states better. Polarization (i.e. yes/no token probabilities
near 0 and 1) means that the model is now more confident
about this separation. In other words, ESMA effectively
pushes the model out of the overlapping region, forcing it
to decide whether it knows the information or not with high
confidence, thereby reducing the noise in its internal self-
monitoring signal. This analysis suggests that the improved
metacognitive abilities in Table 1 are based on a fundamental
shift towards metacognitive internal representations and
processes.

Experimental Design
The dual-question format is re-
placed with a single-prompt format by adding the instruction
“If you don’t know the answer, just return ‘I don’t know”’ to

In this IDK format, there are no separate Meta Questions.
Instead, the model’s output is considered as a merged re-

“No,” while any other response is categorized as a Meta
“Yes.” If indeed the ability is based on a genuine metacog-
nitive link rather than a simple pattern-matching response
to specific meta-questions, it should hold the ability even
when the model was questioned with this setup.

Three primary metrics were defined to assess
performance in this setting:

ES modifies all model parameters simultaneously at each
iteration. However, even when a specific modification im-
proves fitness, it does not necessarily follow that every in-
dividual parameter change contributes positively to the fit-

learned updates, this study examined whether the improve-

or by a subset of highly influential weight changes.
Experimental Procedure
The total parameter change,
∆W, is defined as the difference between the weights of
the final fine-tuned model, Wtuned, and the base model,
Wbase, as
∆W = Wtuned −Wbase.
(4)
To evaluate the impact of these changes, every individual
scalar value within ∆W was ranked by its magnitude (L1
norm). A patching ratio p ∈[0, 100]% was then defined,
representing the top p% of weight changes with the highest
absolute values. Finally, a partially patched model, Wp,
was constructed by adding only these selected updates to
the base model:
Wp = Wbase + Top(∆W, p),
(5)
where Top(∆W, p) is a sparse tensor containing only the
p% largest magnitude updates from ∆W and zeros else-
where. The parameter p was systematically increased from
0% (the base model) to 100% (the fully fine-tuned model),
and the resulting d′
type2 and raw alignment were measured.
Results
As illustrated in Figure 4, the experiment revealed
a highly non-linear relationship between the volume of
weight patching and the improvement in metacognitive per-
formance. The most striking observation was the efficiency
of the highest-magnitude updates. Applying only the top
10% of weight changes yielded a dramatic leap in perfor-
mance: the d′
type2 value rose sharply from 0.20 to 0.63, cap-
turing roughly 80% of the total improvement through the
entire training process. Similarly, raw alignment increased
from 53.30% to 63% within this initial 10% window.
Following this rapid ascent, the metrics showed diminishing
returns. Increasing the patching ratio from 10% to 50% led
to a more gradual improvement, with d′
type2 peaking at 0.76
and raw alignment stabilizing around 65%. Notably, the
remaining 50% of weight updates, consisting of the smallest
scalar changes, contributed almost no additional metacogni-
tive ability. The fact that the vast majority of improvement
was concentrated in the top 10% of updates indicates that
metacognitive alignment is driven by specific, high-impact
weight adjustments rather than a uniform shift across the
entire parameter space. Please refer to Appendix B.5 for
a more detailed analysis of the top weight change and the
results of bottom-up patching.
6. Discussion and Future Work
This study investigates whether LLMs possess introspection
regarding their own knowledge and whether this capability
can be enhanced. Functionally, we quantified metacognition
by measuring the discriminative power of the model’s meta
answers in predicting actual correctness using d′
type2.
The results suggest organized internal knowledge of LLMs
and their potential to conditionally reference this knowledge
in diverse contexts. Furthermore, the success of ESMA
demonstrates that this ability can be enhanced through train-
ing. Notably, the consistent results across unseen prompts
and different languages strongly support the premise that
this is not merely superficial data adaptation, but rather a
fundamental shift in how the model accesses knowledge.
Despite ESMA’s success, the absolute metacognition level
remains far below that of humans, especially given the very
low metacognitive demands of this QA task. Since metacog-
nition is a core requirement for human-level AI and a key
capability that enables agents to autonomously seek nec-
essary information, correct errors, and learn in a targeted
and precise manner, it is imperative to continue finding
innovative ways to enhance the metacognition of LLMs.
Methodologically, this work highlights the unique advan-
tages of evolution strategies for optimizing holistic behav-
ioral patterns across independent contexts where gradient-
based methods face limitations. This finding highlights
the potential of ES as a powerful framework for modeling
high-level cognitive functions that manifest only within the
collective behavioral repertoire in diverse environments.

A. Background
A.1. Measuring Human Metacognition
In psychology, the standard approach to measuring metacognitive ability involves assessing the correspondence between an
individual’s objective performance and their subjective awareness of that performance (Hart, 1965). In a standard setup,
participants are presented with a series of general knowledge questions and asked to provide an answer. For each question,
they also provide a metacognitive judgment, such as a feeling-of-knowing or a confidence rating, which reflects their internal
estimate of the likelihood that their answer is correct (Siedlecka et al., 2016; Kunimoto et al., 2001). By comparing these
subjective ratings against objective correctness, researchers can determine how accurately an individual monitors their own
knowledge state.
This measuring process is formally analyzed through the lens of Type 1 and Type 2 cognitive processes. Type 1 processing
refers to the primary cognitive action, such as answering a general knowledge question, while Type 2 processing involves the
secondary monitoring of that action, characterized by the subject’s ability to evaluate the correctness of their own responses.
Within the framework of Signal Detection Theory (SDT), Type 1 performance is quantified by d′ (d-prime), representing
the subject’s ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect information. To evaluate the second-order monitoring
process, researchers use Type 2 d′, which measures a subject’s ability to discriminate between their own correct and incorrect
responses based on their confidence levels (Maniscalco & Lau, 2012; Fleming & Lau, 2014).
To evaluate the second-order monitoring process, researchers use d′
type2, a standardized metric that quantifies metacognitive
ability. It measures a subject’s capacity to discriminate between accurate and inaccurate judgments by calculating the
distance between the internal confidence distributions for each decision type:
d′
type2 = Φ−1(Hit Rate) −Φ−1(False Alarm Rate),
(6)
where Φ−1 denotes the inverse cumulative normal distribution. Under this binary setup, hit rate is defined as the prob-
ability of the model responding “Yes” when its Type 1 answer was correct: P(Meta-Yes|Correct). Conversely, false
alarm rate represents the probability of the model responding “Yes” when its Type 1 answer was actually incorrect:
P(Meta-Yes|Incorrect).
Inspired by these human metacognition metrics, this study employed this framework to measure the metacognitive capacities
of LLMs. Specifically, the experimental design consists of a Direct Question and a Meta Question judgment. For Meta
Questions, prompting the model with “Do you know the answer to the following question?” utilizes its “Yes” or “No”
response as a form of self-report. This approach allows us to quantify the model’s subjective certainty in a manner analogous
to human metacognitive monitoring. This dual-prompting structure allows us to expand traditional behavioral metrics for
LLMs, enabling a direct comparison between an agent’s internal confidence in that knowledge.
A.2. Evolution Strategies
Evolution Strategies (ES; Rechenberg, 1973; Schwefel, 1977) form a class of black-box optimization techniques that
iteratively refine model parameters by evaluating a population of perturbed candidates, rather than relying on the calculus of
gradients. Unlike backpropagation, which computes exact derivatives through the chain rule, ES operates as a derivative-free
mechanism that requires only forward passes. While traditionally limited by the high dimensionality of neural networks,
recent advancements have successfully extended this approach to the full-parameter fine-tuning of multi-billion parameter
large language models (Qiu et al., 2025).
The primary obstacle in applying standard gradient-based learning to metacognition fine-tuning is the requirement to assess
the relationship between two distinct behavioral outputs: the direct response and the meta-evaluation. In standard deep
learning, training is performed using gradient descent on individual examples. Because a direct question and a meta-question
are processed as independent inputs, standard backpropagation cannot calculate a gradient across these separate contexts.
The model is updated based on the error of a single forward pass, making it impossible to optimize for joint coherence
directly.
ES shares similarities with reinforcement learning (RL) in that both methods aim to maximize rewards. However, RL is
inherently constrained to searching for improvements in the action space by modifying action probabilities. In contrast, ES
searches directly in the parameter space. This approach allows ES to modify the entire behavior of the model at once, making

it possible to optimize complex reward functions that integrate outcomes across different contexts where gradients cannot
be calculated. A distinct advantage of ES is the order of operations: variation occurs first, and application (reward-based
selection) follows. This property allows for the execution of multiple inferences across various scenarios to assign a joint
reward based on aggregated behavioral outcomes.
This distinction is analogous to the difference between biological conditioning and natural selection. While biological

low-intelligence agent execute the same immediate action of fleeing, the superior agent might encode the food’s location to
return later, whereas the inferior agent might simply escape and lose the resource. ES mimics this evolutionary advantage by
optimizing for the aggregate trajectory of a lifespan rather than isolated steps, effectively selecting for superior behavioral
patterns that maximize long-term survival and utility.

D. Experimental Details
D.1. Hyperparameters
The evolution training process is governed by several key hyperparameters that control the exploration and convergence of
the parameter space. Specifically, the mutation strength was set to σ = 10−3 and the learning rate to α = 5 × 10−4. The
optimization was conducted over T = 750 iterations, utilizing a population size of N = 32 individuals per generation. For
each fitness evaluation within an iteration, the model processed n = 256 data samples to ensure a stable estimation of the
joint reward. These values were selected to balance the trade-off between exploration stability and computational efficiency.
To evaluate the closed models, gpt-5.2-2025-12-11, claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929, and gemini-3-flash-preview models were
used. Eight NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 24GB GPUs were used for the experiments.
For supervised fine-tuning in Appendix B.2, the model was trained for approximately 30,000 steps with a batch size of 8, a
learning rate of 2 × 10−5, and a warmup ratio of 0.1, until the validation loss stopped decreasing. To teach Fictional QA, the
model was trained for 10 epochs with a batch size of 8, a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, and a warmup ratio of 0.1.
