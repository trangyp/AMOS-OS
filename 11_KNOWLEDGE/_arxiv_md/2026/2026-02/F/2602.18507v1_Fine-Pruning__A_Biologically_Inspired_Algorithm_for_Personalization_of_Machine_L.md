SUMMARY
8
Neural networks have long strived to emulate the learning capabilities of the human brain. While
9
deep neural networks (DNNs) draw inspiration from the brain in neuron design, their training
10
methods diverge from biological foundations. Backpropagation, the primary training method for
11
DNNs, requires substantial computational resources and fully labeled datasets, presenting ma-
12
jor bottlenecks in development and application. This work demonstrates that by returning to
13
biomimicry, specifically mimicking how the brain learns through pruning, we can solve various
14
classical machine learning problems while utilizing orders of magnitude fewer computational re-
15
sources and no labels. Our experiments successfully personalized multiple speech recognition
16
and image classification models, including ResNet50 on ImageNet, resulting in increased spar-
17
sity of approximately 70% while simultaneously improving model accuracy to around 90%, all
18
without the limitations of backpropagation. This biologically inspired approach offers a promis-
19
ing avenue for efficient, personalized machine learning models in resource-constrained environ-
20
ments.

Introduction
24
The pursuit of artificial intelligence (AI) that mimics human cognitive abilities has long been a
25
driving force in computer science. At the heart of this quest lies the development of artificial
26
neural networks, computational models inspired by the intricate web of neurons in the human
27
brain. These artificial neural networks, particularly in their deep and complex forms known as
28
Deep Neural Networks (DNNs)1,2, have revolutionized fields ranging from healthcare3 and fi-
29
nance4 to agriculture5 and speech recognition6. The impact of DNNs is perhaps most tangibly
30
felt in the realm of consumer technology, with applications like automatic speech recognition be-
31
coming ubiquitous since the debut of SIRI7 on smartphones in 20118,9. Despite their biological
32
inspiration, the learning mechanisms employed by DNNs have diverged significantly from their
33
neurological counterparts. The primary method for training these networks, known as back-
34
propagation10, relies heavily on large amounts of labeled data and substantial computational
35
resources11–13. This approach, while effective, presents significant challenges in real-world ap-
36
plications, particularly when deploying models on resource-constrained devices or in scenarios
37
where labeled data is scarce, which often requires greater domain knowledge to be used as part
38
of the training process to compensate14. Moreover, the generalized nature of these models often

41
Recent trends in machine learning have seen a renewed interest in biologically feasible learn-
42
ing algorithms16–18, seeking to bridge the gap between artificial and biological neural networks.
43
This resurgence is driven by the remarkable efficiency and adaptability of the human brain,
44
which continues to outperform artificial systems in many aspects of cognition. One particularly
45
promising avenue of research draws inspiration from the process of neural pruning observed
46
in the developing human brain. Synaptic pruning19, a fascinating aspect of brain development,
47
occurs during critical periods of neural development, particularly in early childhood and adoles-
48
cence. This process, counter-intuitively, involves the elimination of neural connections, with the
49
brain losing up to 50% - 70% of its synapses in some regions20,21. However, synaptic pruning is
50
not a random process of neural attrition, but rather a refined mechanism of neural optimization.
51
It preferentially eliminates weak or rarely used connections while preserving and strengthen-
52
ing frequently activated pathways22. This biological ’use it or lose it’ principle results in more
53
efficient neural circuits, improved signal-to-noise ratios, and enhanced cognitive abilities23. The
54
brain’s ability to sculpt its neural architecture in response to experience and specific environmen-
55
tal demands provides a powerful model for adaptive learning systems. During early stages of
56
development, the brain systematically eliminates unused or weak neural connections, a process
57
that paradoxically enhances cognitive function by reducing noise and focusing neural activity
58
on the most relevant pathways24. This biological phenomenon provides an intriguing approach
59
to addressing the challenges of model personalization and resource efficiency in deep neural
60
networks.
61
The impetus for searching for an alternative method for personalizing a DNN comes from
62
the observations that the running of conventional backpropagation requires orders of magnitude
63
more memory than even advanced Internet of Thing (IoT) devices have available. As an exam-
64
ple, an Apple iPhone has less than 2.5 billion of FLOPs25, whereas a model such as VGG-19
65
requires more than 20 billion FLOPs to train. Even modest architecture like ResNet-50 requires
66
around 4 GFLOPs, twice as many as are available26. Even if a user would accept the time re-
67
quired to train such models, the energy consumption would still be a major bottleneck for their
68
deployment on such devices. Looking again at power resources consumed by a typical appli-
69
cation27, training MobileNet, which was designed to be used on mobile devices, takes over 2x
70
the amount of power28. Looking at a the power consumption of a more conventional network,
71
such as DenseNet, it requires 10x as much power as a typical application, which is over 20%25
72
of the power available on this device. For a device like a smart phone, this can be an annoyance,
73
however this level of power consumption could cause catastrophic results in a device such as a
74
pacemaker. In such cases, an alternative is needed for increasing the accuracy without suffering
75
from these short comings.
76
Pruning is a tool for reducing the size of a model29,30. However, it has only used for this
77
purpose. Although it has been noted in previous works that pruning can result in some minimal
78
increase to accuracy31, the exact circumstances where pruning improves or reduces accuracy
79
has never been understood. Further, previous works have not elucidated how to maximize the
80
desired effect by changing the criteria used to determine which neurons in the network are
81
contributing to the correct predictions. Other methods for reducing the size of a model exist32–34,
82
however they do not add to the accuracy of a model, simply serve as a way to reduce their
83
memory usage.
84
In this work, we introduce Fine-Pruning, a method that leverages the principles of biologi-
85
cal neural pruning to personalize and optimize machine learning models. Inspired by synaptic
86
pruning in the brain, Fine-Pruning utilizes data-driven model compression to selectively remove
87
weights that contribute least to the model’s performance on a specific user’s data. This approach
88
not only reduces the computational footprint of the model but also enhances its accuracy on the

Pruning
119
Neural network pruning removes unnecessary nodes/weights to make the model run more effi-
120
ciently40. Our focus on pruning to achieve a more specialized model for the target data (as op-
121
posed to retraining that leverages backpropagation and labeled data), originates from research
122
observations in multi-task learning. A phenomenon that arises is that pruning will cause the
123
model to ’forget’ prior features from the source data. This is typically seen as a negative in
124
multi-task learning, however this can be leveraged in favor of personalization.
125

While we use pruning to increase the accuracy, the main purpose of pruning is to introduce
126
model sparsity, i.e., the percent of elements removed from the original model. There are a few
127
ways this is achieved: either removing blocks of nodes entirely (i.e., structured pruning41), or
128
zeroing out the element and allow for the low level architecture implementation optimize any op-
129
erations used (i.e., unstructured pruning42). This can be done by utilizing sparse matrix libraries
130
like BLAS43 on modern IoT and embedded devices. In this paper, we utilize structured pruning
131
based on the aggregated activations of a particular node.
132
Biological Inspiration.
133
Our work is inspired by biological synaptic pruning, a phase in the development of the nervous
134
system, is the process of synapse elimination that occurs between early childhood and puberty
135
in many mammals, including humans44. During pruning, both the axon (i.e., long projection
136
of a nerve cell - neuron) and dendrite (neuron’s extensions that propagate the electrochemical
137
stimulation received from other neurons to the cell body) decay and die off. The infant brain will
138
increase in size by a factor of up to 5 by adulthood, reaching a final size of approximately 86
139
billion neurons45. The total number of neurons, however, remains the same. After adolescence,
140
the volume of the synaptic connections decreases again due to synaptic pruning specifically for
141
each individual46. Although it is now known that neural synaptic pruning happens for a wide
142
variety of reasons47, the main mechanism in learning based pruning is activation, which is what
143
our work aims to parallel.

Results
145
The Fine-Pruning Algorithm
146
Fine-Pruning draws inspiration from the biological process of synaptic pruning observed in the
147
developing human brain. This process, which involves the elimination of unused or weak neural
148
connections, counterintuitively enhances cognitive function by reducing noise and focusing neu-
149
ral activity on the most relevant pathways. We apply this principle to artificial neural networks to
150
address the challenge of personalizing models for individual users or specific subsets of data,
151
particularly in resource-constrained environments such as mobile devices. Our method lever-
152
ages the observation that, just like in the brain, removing redundant or misused weights in an
153
over-parameterized model can increase its accuracy. By treating a general source model as an
154
over-parameterized version of the desired target model, we use pruning as a method to increase
155
accuracy for specific target datasets. This approach involves downloading a generalized model
156
onto various devices and using target data to prune the model, making it both smaller and more
157
accurate (Figure 1).
158
The core of our method is a data-driven absolute magnitude pruning algorithm. Unlike tra-
159
ditional pruning techniques that rely solely on weight magnitudes, our approach considers the
160
activation of each weight with respect to the target dataset. The algorithm evaluates the source
161
model on the target dataset, records activation maps for each layer, identifies weights that con-
162
tribute least to the model’s predictions on the target data, and prunes these weights to create a
163
sparse sub-model tailored to the target dataset. This process continues until a desired level of
164
sparsity is reached or accuracy begins to decline, mimicking the biological pruning of synapses
165
where the least used neural pathways are eliminated to enhance overall cognitive function. For
166
more information regarding our approach please refer to the Methods section.

Our experiments revealed several intriguing characteristics of Fine-Pruning, demonstrating its effec-
249
tiveness and efficiency across multiple dimensions. We conducted three additional experiments using
250

251

252

253

254
minimal improvement until reaching approximately 65% sparsity, after which it increased dramatically.
255
Peak performance was achieved at around 70% pruning. Beyond this point, accuracy declined sharply,
256
reaching the starting accuracy at around 86% sparsity. This finding suggests that the initial general model
257
was significantly over-parameterized for individual target datasets, despite its strong performance on the
258
broader source dataset. The sharp accuracy decline beyond 70% sparsity indicates a critical threshold
259
where essential features for the target task begin to be removed.
260
Next, we examined the data efficiency of Fine-Pruning compared to conventional retraining with back-
261
propagation (Figure 3 (b)). We varied the percentage of target data used for both Fine-Pruning and
262

263

264
the available target data. In contrast, conventional retraining required over 90% of the data to reach
265

266
Lastly, we explored how the similarity between target and source dataset distributions affects Fine-
267
Pruning’s performance (Figure 3 (c)). We varied the number of classes in the target dataset from 2 to 9
268

269

270

273

274

Discussion
288
Our study introduces Fine-Pruning as a different approach to model personalization, drawing inspiration
289
from the biological process of synaptic pruning. The results demonstrate that this method achieves signif-
290
icant improvements in model accuracy for individual users. In addition, it also addresses key challenges in
291
deploying machine learning models on resource-constrained devices. Here, we discuss the implications of
292
our findings, their relationship to existing work, and potential future directions. The effectiveness of Fine-
293
Pruning across diverse datasets and model architectures suggests a fundamental principle at work: the
294
removal of unnecessary connections can enhance model performance for specific tasks or user profiles.
295
This aligns with observations in neuroscience, where pruning of synaptic connections during development
296
is associated with cognitive refinement. Our finding that optimal performance is achieved at around 70%
297
sparsity is particularly intriguing, as it mirrors pruning rates observed in biological neural systems20,56,57.
298
This convergence between artificial and biological systems suggests that there may be universal princi-
299
ples governing efficient information processing in neural networks, whether biological or artificial. Further
300
analysis would be needed to find out the validity of this claim, but it would point towards a greater ability
301
to draw comparisons between live and artificial neural networks and may open new avenues for more
302
biomimicry.
303
The ability of Fine-Pruning to improve model accuracy without the need for labeled data or expensive
304
backpropagation represents a significant advancement in the field of personalized machine learning. Tra-
305
ditional methods often require substantial computational resources and large amounts of labeled data,
306
which are frequently unavailable in real-world scenarios, particularly on edge devices. By leveraging only
307
the forward pass information and working with unlabeled data, Fine-Pruning makes on-device person-
308
alization feasible for a wide range of applications, from speech recognition to image classification. Our
309
results also shed light on the nature of over-parameterization in neural networks used as source models.
310
The fact that we could achieve substantial sparsity while simultaneously improving accuracy suggests that
311
many trained models contain significant redundancy when applied to specific user data. This redundancy,
312
while potentially beneficial for generalization across a wide range of users, can be detrimental when tar-
313
geting individual use cases. Fine-Pruning effectively navigates this trade-off, retaining the benefits of a
314
well-trained general model while optimizing for individual performance. The superior data efficiency of
315
Fine-Pruning compared to conventional retraining methods is particularly noteworthy. In many real-world
316

applications, the amount of data available for personalization is limited. Our method’s ability to achieve
317
significant improvements with less data makes it more practical for deployment, and also, potentially,
318
more privacy-preserving, as it requires less user data to be collected or processed, and further it does
319
not require for the data or model to be transmitted to another device for retraining58. The relationship we
320
observed between feature sparsity in the target dataset and the effectiveness of Fine-Pruning provides
321
valuable insights for potential applications. The method appears to be especially powerful when person-
322
alizing models for users or tasks that utilize a subset of the full feature space of the original model. This
323
characteristic makes Fine-Pruning particularly well-suited for applications where users typically engage
324
with a limited portion of a model’s capabilities, such as personalized speech recognition or specialized
325
image classification tasks. From a computational perspective, the reduction in model size and complexity
326
achieved through Fine-Pruning has significant implications for deploying AI on edge devices. The de-
327
creased memory footprint and computational requirements make it feasible to run sophisticated models
328
on resource-constrained devices and contribute to energy efficiency, a critical factor in mobile and IoT
329
applications59. This aligns with the growing trend towards edge AI, where processing is done locally on
330
devices rather than in the cloud, offering benefits in terms of latency, privacy, and offline functionality.
331
Our comparative analysis against both traditional methods and state-of-the-art pruning techniques
332
further validates Fine-Pruning’s practical advantages. While newer pruning methods like DFPC and Dep-
333
Graph achieve higher sparsity rates ( 77%), they do so at the cost of accuracy, showing performance
334
degradation on target datasets. This trade-off between sparsity and accuracy represents a significant
335
challenge in existing pruning approaches. Fine-Pruning, in contrast, demonstrates that moderate sparsity
336
(65%) can be achieved while simultaneously improving accuracy by over 67% – substantially outperform-
337
ing both traditional methods like backpropagation and SVD, which show accuracy improvements around
338
55%. Perhaps more significantly, Fine-Pruning achieves this with markedly lower computational complex-
339
ity, as it doesn’t scale with the number of training examples. This combination of improved accuracy,
340
reasonable sparsity, and computational efficiency makes Fine-Pruning particularly well-suited for real-
341
world applications, especially in resource-constrained environments like mobile and IoT devices where
342
both model performance and computational efficiency are critical.
343
While our results are promising, they also open up new questions and avenues for future research.
344
One key area of investigation is the generalizability of Fine-Pruning across even more diverse types of
345
neural network architectures and tasks. While we’ve demonstrated its effectiveness in CNNs for speech
346
and image tasks, exploring its applicability to other architectures like transformers or recurrent neural
347
networks could further broaden its impact. Another important direction is related to the theoretical foun-
348
dations of why Fine-Pruning works so well. Understanding the mathematical principles underlying the
349
relationship between pruning, personalization, and accuracy could lead to even more effective algorithms
350
and potentially inform the design of new neural network architectures that are inherently more amenable
351
to personalization. The potential of Fine-Pruning in federated learning scenarios is also an exciting area
352
for future work. Given its ability to create personalized models without sharing raw data, Fine-Pruning
353
could be integrated into federated learning systems to create more efficient and privacy-preserving dis-
354
tributed learning frameworks. Lastly, the biological inspiration behind Fine-Pruning suggests that further
355
exploration of neuroscience-inspired algorithms could yield additional breakthroughs in AI. The success
356
of this approach invites us to consider what other principles from biological learning systems might be
357
advantageously applied to artificial neural networks.

In conclusion, Fine-Pruning represents a significant step forward in creating more efficient, accurate,

Limitation
366
Fine-pruning cannot train a model from nothing or randomized weight. While it is possible that this method
367
may be able to extract some accuracy from a model that has not been trained on any data whatsoever,
368
this falls outside the scope of this work. Fine-pruning is designed only to work on personalizing, or fine
369
tuning, a large source model to perform better on a target dataset. While this may seem like a strong
370
precondition, we see this in a multitude of real world applications, such as a general speech model that is
371
being personalized on an individual user’s device for their specific voice.
372
As can be seen in Figure 3 (c), as the number of classes grows the peak accuracy tends toward the
373
starting accuracy of the model. In other words, as the number of features in the target dataset approaches
374
the number of features in the source dataset, the benefits to accuracy of Fine-Pruning decrease. We are
375
not aware of any parallel to this phenomenon from our biological background. That being said, even if
376
the accuracy doesn’t improve, we still see the other benefits of conventional pruning. Those being the
377
reduction of memory usage and energy consumption. The exact point where the accuracy increase is
378
no longer seen as the target distribution’s features increases has not been observed to be a universal
379
constant across datasets, however from our experiments the amount seems to be far less than those
380
seen in a multitude of different real world domains, as shown in our results.
381
Further, as seen explained in our analysis of Figure 3 (a), after the optimal pruning rate is found, the
382
decrease to accuracy is sharp. This means that if you overshoot the optimal threshold you may find the
383
resulting accuracy is less optimal, or perhaps even below the starting accuracy. While our study, as well
384
as the biological inspiration, seems to suggest that a pruning rate from 60% - 70% works broadly for
385
different models and datasets, it may perhaps be safer to start with a lower amount and build up until
386
there is observed decrease to accuracy. Since the distribution is mono-modal, and the cost for pruning is
387
very small once the activation maps are tabulated, this should not be a costly procedure.

Methods
389
Fine-Pruning Algorithm
390
This work uses the following definitions:
391
• Source data is dataset for training that the model initially is learned over. These data points are,
392
for instance, speech samples from a large number of individuals, but not the individual (e.g., smart-
393
phone owner) that will ultimately use the model.
394
• Target data is what the model has to specialize to on each device. These data are later produced by
395
the end-users on each device (e.g., speech samples of a given phone owner) and are not labeled.
396
• Target accuracy refers to the model accuracy on the target data. This will differ from ’accuracy’,
397
which will be withheld for accuracy over the entire dataset (i.e. both source and target data).
398
Our study introduces Fine-Pruning, an alternative approach to personalizing trained neural network
399
models. This method draws inspiration from biological synaptic pruning, aiming to optimize model perfor-
400
mance for specific target datasets while reducing computational complexity.
401
The core of our approach is the Fine-Pruning algorithm, which leverages data-driven absolute magni-
402
tude pruning. Given a trained source model NSource and a target dataset T, we first evaluate NSource on
403
T. For each layer in NSource, we compute an activation map representing the contribution of each weight
404
to the model’s output for the target dataset. We then define a pruning threshold σ, which determines the
405
proportion of weights to be removed.
406

If the source model has the ability to learn a wide variety of diverse features, specifically enough to

413

414

416
In more computer science terms, our proposed Fine-pruning is similar in concept to the lottery ticket
417
hypothesis60. If we view the target network as the winning ticket within the source model, then this work’s
418

419
between this work and the lottery ticket hypothesis is that this work does not look to remain accurate on
420
the source data, but rather optimize and personalize for the target data.
421
More specifically, if we have a data domain of X, our objective is to search within an over-parameterized
422
source network NSource, trained on a source dataset S ⊂X, for a sub-network NTarget ⊂NSource, which is
423
tailored towards some target dataset T ⊂X. In order to find NTarget, we evaluate NSource[T] and consider
424
the activation map of each layer l of the network, Al.
425
For each Al, we select the indices i that contribute the least to the prediction of NSource over the target
426
dataset.
427
These indices i are just contributing noise to the prediction, hence removing the nodes associated
428
with i in NSource will reduce the noise with regards to T. This will increase the accuracy until the sparsity
429
gets to the point, where further pruning will remove the essential weights that are needed to predict T.
430

Datasets and Preprocessing
435
To evaluate the effectiveness of Fine-Pruning, we conducted experiments across multiple datasets in two
436
domains: speech recognition and image classification.
437
12


## Page 13


For speech recognition, we used the Free Spoken Digit Dataset51. The Free Spoken Digit Dataset is
438
larger, containing 500 speech samples per individual, totaling nearly 25 minutes of speech per person.
439
We preprocessed these datasets by converting audio samples into 2D spectrograms, enabling the use of
440
convolutional neural networks.
441
For image classification, we employed the CK+ dataset for facial emotion recognition and a subset
442
of ImageNet for general object classification. The CK+ dataset contains facial emotion images, from
443
which we selected individuals with more than 12 supported emotions, resulting in 14 individuals for our
444
experiments. For ImageNet, we created ”individuals” by selecting two random classes from the validation
445
set for each personalization experiment.
446
Model Architectures and Experimental Setup
447
We tested Fine-Pruning on several popular neural network architectures: VGG-7 for speech recognition
448
tasks, VGG-19 for the CK+ facial emotion recognition task, ResNet50 for ImageNet classification, and
449
MobileNetV2 to demonstrate Fine-Pruning’s effectiveness on mobile-optimized architectures. All models
450
were implemented using Keras with a TensorFlow backend, and trained weights were used as starting
451
points where applicable.
452
Our experimental procedure involved training or obtaining a trained model on the full source dataset,
453
selecting a subset of the data to serve as the target dataset (simulating an individual user’s data), applying
454
Fine-Pruning to the trained model using only the target dataset, and evaluating the pruned model’s per-
455
formance on a held-out portion of the target dataset. We used 5-fold cross-validation for all experiments
456
to ensure robust results.
457
Benchmarking and Evaluation
458
To benchmark Fine-Pruning’s performance, we compared it against two main alternatives: backpropaga-
459
tion based retraining and SVD-based model decomposition. For backpropagation based retraining, we
460
fine-tuned the trained models on the target datasets using standard backpropagation with the Adam op-
461
timizer. The SVD-based method followed the approach described in previous work on model complexity
462
reduction for personalization.
463
We evaluated performance using several metrics: accuracy (the primary metric for all classification
464
tasks), sparsity (the percentage of weights set to zero in the pruned model), data efficiency (the amount
465
of target data required to achieve a given level of accuracy improvement), and computational efficiency
466
(measured in terms of FLOPs required for both the pruning process and inference with the pruned model).
467

468
All experiments were conducted on a system with NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs, using Python 3.7, Tensor-
469

470

Broader Impact
477
The success of Fine-Pruning demonstrates how biomimicy can guide practical solutions in machine learn-
478
ing, extending beyond the basic neural inspiration of ANNs to incorporate established neurological pro-
479
cesses. While the primary contribution of this work is in model personalization, which has the capacity
480
13


## Page 14


to revolutionize how personalizing devices is done for mobile or IoT, our analysis suggests additional
481
potential applications that merit further investigation.
482
First, Fine-Pruning may contribute to privacy-preserving federated learning. In distributed scenarios
483
where a general model is deployed across multiple devices (e.g., smartphones), users typically must
484
share either their private data or model gradients to improve the general model’s accuracy. Since Fine-
485
Pruning creates personalized models that are strict subsets of the original model’s weights, it enables an
486
alternative update mechanism: devices can communicate their pruning masks rather than raw data or full
487
gradient information. This approach could reduce communication overhead while improving user privacy.
488
Second, the properties of Fine-Pruning suggest potential applications in user authentication through
489
model fingerprinting. The pruning patterns created for each user’s specific data distribution generate
490
distinctive masks that could serve as identifiers. Unlike hardware-based keys tied to specific devices,
491
pruning-based signatures would be reproducible across devices while remaining specific to each user’s
492
data characteristics. This mechanism warrants investigation for applications in distributed authentication
493
systems.
494
These potential applications, while requiring validation, indicate that Fine-Pruning impact may extend
495
beyond its core function of model personalization. More broadly, this work provides evidence that exam-
496
ining biological systems can yield practical solutions for contemporary challenges in machine learning,
497
particularly in areas such as model efficiency and adaptability.
498
The success of this algorithm serves as a reminder that there are many biological parallels that can
499
inspire solutions for practical, real-world problems in machine learning. Although ANNs are loosely based
500
off of real neurons in the brain, this work shows there are plenty of lessons to learned from real neurolog-
501
ical processes. We further propose that Fine-pruning may provide more benefits outside of fine-tuning or
502
pruning, which has the capacity to revolutionize how personalizing devices is done for mobile or IoT, that
503
could merit further investigation.
504
For example, end-user privacy and communication efficiency is desired in the use-case of distributing
505
a general model to different target devices (e.g., smartphones), where individual users have their own
506
private dataset (e.g., speech) as the new knowledge to improve the general model’s accuracy. However,
507
sending the entire target dataset is costly and could violate user privacy. There are methods to send only
508
the local gradients to the general model, but this could still be costly in practice. Since each remaining
509
target model is a sub-model of the general model (i.e., Fine-pruning does not create any new weights),
510
any retraining that occurs after the model is pruned can be utilized to enhance the accuracy of the general
511
model.
512
Trustworthy end-user fingerprinting is another possible benefit of Fine-pruning for model personaliza-
513
tion. With user-specific pruning masks, Fine-pruning essentially creates a unique, reproducible keys (user
514
fingerprints) that can be recreated on different devices. If the models of any user is sufficiently different
515
from all others, then the mask of the unpruned weights can be utilized like a fingerprint for the user. This
516
is potentially better than a hardware key, which is tied to a device, whereas this method generates the
517
same key given the same target data domain or same features that need to be extracted for each user.
518
These possible continuations of this work would have wide reaching implications in the space of com-
519
puter engineering, reaching further than just the field of biomimicy.

Conclusion
521
In this paper, we introduce Fine-Pruning, a biologically inspired algorithm that emulates the neural prun-
522
ing observed in biological systems, where connections are selectively pruned to enhance efficiency and
523
accuracy. Fine-Pruning utilizes a method inspired by absolute magnitude pruning, akin to how biologi-
524
cal neural networks adapt and specialize by fine-tuning based on observed stimuli, rather than extensive
525
retraining. This approach fine-tunes an already trained model directly on local end-devices without re-
526
quiring annotated data labels, thus mirroring the biological principle of adaptation without a known target
527
outcome. Unlike traditional methods that rely on computationally intensive backpropagation, Fine-Pruning
528
operates using only lightweight forward pass information, significantly reducing both computational and
529
memory demands during model fine-tuning and subsequent target inference. Demonstrating its effective-
530
14


## Page 15


ness, Fine-Pruning enhances the accuracy of a different deep learning model from 50% to nearly 90% on
531
local datasets, all while achieving over 70% model sparsity with minimal computational overhead and no
532
need for data labels. This highlights Fine-Pruning’s potential to advance personalized machine learning
533
by incorporating biologically inspired, resource-efficient algorithms.
