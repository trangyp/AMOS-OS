Abstract
A low out-degree orientation directs each edge of an undirected graph with the goal of
minimizing the maximum out-degree of a vertex. In the parallel batch-dynamic setting, one can
insert or delete batches of edges, and the goal is to process the entire batch in parallel with work
per edge similar to that of a single sequential update and with span (or depth) for the entire
batch that is polylogarithmic. In this paper we present faster parallel batch-dynamic algorithms
for maintaining a low out-degree orientation of an undirected graph. All results herein achieve
polylogarithmic span, with high probability (whp); the focus of this paper is on minimizing the
work, which varies across results.
Our first result is the first parallel batch-dynamic algorithm to maintain an asymptotically
optimal orientation with asymptotically optimal expected work bounds, in an amortized sense,
improving over the prior best work bounds of Liu et al. [SPAA ’22] by a logarithmic factor.
Our second result is a O(c log n) orientation algorithm with expected worst-case O(√log n)
work per edge update, where c is a known upper-bound on the arboricity of the graph. This
matches the best-known sequential worst-case O(c log n) orientation algorithm given by Berglin
and Brodal [Algorithmica ’18], albeit in expectation.
Our final result is a O(c + log n)-orientation algorithm with O(log2 n) expected worst-case
work per edge update. This algorithm significantly improves upon the recent result of Ghaffari
and Koo [SPAA ’25], which maintains a O(c)-orientation with O(log9 n) worst-case work per
edge whp.

Introduction
The low out-degree orientation problem is to assign a direction to each edge of an undirected graph
G = (V, E) while minimizing the maximum out-degree of the resulting directed graph G. This
problem is fundamentally linked to the notion of graph’s arboricity, a widely-used measure of
the sparsity of a graph. Formally, an orientation of an undirected graph is called a c-orientation
if every vertex has out-degree at most c. Dynamic low out-degree orientations, which maintain
an orientation as the graph is modified over a series of edge insertions and deletions were first
studied in the seminal work of Brodal and Fagerberg [13]. The motivation for their work was the
problem of constructing deterministic data structures for answering adjacency queries on sparse
graphs. In the subsequent years, dynamic orientation has emerged as a fundamental building block
for constructing dynamic algorithms for many other graph problems, including dynamic vertex
cover [43], coloring [30], independent sets [42], and matchings [7,8,29].
Brodal and Fagerberg’s algorithm [13] is extremely simple and intuitive, and can be shown
to maintain an O(c)-orientation with O(log n) amortized number of edge reorientations (flips) per
update, where c is the maximum arboricity over the sequence of updates.1
Intriguingly, their
algorithm is optimal in the sense that it is O(1)-competitive with the number of reorientations
made by any online or offline algorithm, regardless of its running time. In the subsequent years,
a rich body of work has significantly improved our understanding of low out-degree orientations
in the sequential setting, and on the tradeoff between maximum out-degree, update time, and
amortized vs. worst-case bounds [6, 15, 16, 29, 36].2 Some of these worst-case algorithms function
by finding a directed path from a high (or maximum) out-degree vertex to a low out-degree vertex,
and flipping all edges along this path: this has the net effect of decreasing the high vertex’s
out-degree by 1, increasing the low vertex’s out-degree by 1, and not changing the out-degree of
any of the intermediate vertices on the path. However, this approach can lead to high runtime
[15,23,35,44]. Currently, the best amortized bounds are still achieved by Brodal and Fagerberg’s
original algorithm, while for worst-case bounds several different algorithms offer a tradeoff between
the update time and the quality of the maintained orientation [6,15,29,36].
More recently, there has been progress in understanding how dynamic orientations can be
maintained in the parallel batch-dynamic setting, where the updates arrive as batches, and the goal
is to maintain a good quality orientation in low work (the total number of operations performed by
the algorithm) and span (the length of the longest chain of sequential dependencies). Typically, the
goal is to achieve work that is near the runtime for an equivalent sequence of sequential updates
and span that is polylogarithmic. A parallel batch-dynamic algorithm is considered work efficient
if it does only a constant factor more work per edge than the best sequential algorithm. We note
that the parallel batch-dynamic setting has received considerable interest in recent years, with
progress being made on batch-dynamic algorithms for a number of fundamental graph problems [1,
2,9,20,23,24,45–47]. Batching operations eliminates the serial bottleneck imposed by traditional
sequential dynamic algorithms, and thus allows applications to exploit more parallelism. Parallel
batch-dynamic algorithms have also emerged as a useful tool for constructing efficient (static)
parallel algorithms [3,22].

Technical Overview
In this paper, we revisit two of the fastest sequential algorithms, one classic and one recent, for
maintaining low out-degree orientations and study how to parallelize them [6, 13]. In the rest of
this section, we describe the challenges of parallelizing these algorithms and our solutions.
2.1
Amortized Low Out-Degree Orientation
The Brodal-Fagerberg Algorithm. In their seminal work, Brodal and Fagerberg [13] proposed
a simple dynamic algorithm for maintaining an O(c)-orientation with optimal bounds, where c is
an upper-bound on the maximum arboricity of the graph over the sequence of updates. Their
algorithm works as follows: For edge deletions, the algorithm simply deletes the edge. For edge
insertions, it orients the new edge arbitrarily out of one of its endpoints. If this causes the vertex’s
out-degree to exceed a cutoff threshold which is O(c), the algorithm flips all of its outgoing edges
and repeats this process for any other vertex, one by one, whose out-degree becomes excessive.
They analyze their algorithm by comparing the number of edge flips performed over a sequence
of updates to the number of edge flips performed by an optimal offline algorithm that sees the
entire sequence of operations in advance. Following their terminology, we call an edge good if it
agrees with the offline orientation and bad otherwise. They define a potential function equal to the
number of bad edges in the graph. Edge insertions and edge flips performed by the offline strategy
can increase the potential. However, if a vertex has many out-edges, most of them must be bad
(since there can be at most c good edges incident to that vertex). Thus, by flipping all of them,

Parallelizing the Brodal-Fagerberg Algorithm: Challenges and Solution. Unfortunately,
parallelizing this algorithm appears difficult due to the chains of sequential dependencies inherent
in its repeated-flipping approach. For instance, consider Figure 1. In each step, we flip one vertex
below 4c, but these flips cause another vertex’s out-degree to rise to 4c. Even if we flipped all
vertices with high out-degree in each step, operating on Figure 1 still has high span, because a
vertex does not become high out-degree until its neighbor flips.
Our solution is the following simple strategy: collect all of the outgoing edges of the vertices
that currently exceed the cutoff threshold (same as before), and run a parallel static orientation
algorithm on the local subgraph induced by these edges to orient them. This guarantees that the
out-degrees of these vertices are now bounded by the threshold of the static algorithm (i.e., static
threshold), whereas the rest of the vertices, in the worst case, have their out-degrees bounded by
the sum of the cutoff threshold and the static threshold of O(c).
Our parallel algorithm is arguably simpler than the original algorithm in the sense that it
requires a single round of static orientation on a local affected subgraph to fix the orientation. The
static orientation step effectively reduces the dependencies, but at the cost of a slightly worsened
bound (by constant factors) on the out-degree. At the same time, our approach of using static
orientation yields the same asymptotic potential reduction as Brodal and Fagerberg’s strategy. If
a high-degree vertex enters the static orientation, it will necessarily leave with far fewer out-edges
than it started with, meaning that many flips occurred off of this vertex—enough for a constant
amount of potential to be lost per flip.
Our amortized algorithm result is stated in Theorem 2.1 and proved in Section 4. We also show
that the algorithm is asymptotically optimal in terms of the number of flips performed, as stated
in Theorem 2.2, since it satisfies the same property as the sequential algorithm; i.e., it performs
asymptotically the same number of flips as any algorithm that maintains an O(c) orientation.

2.2
Orientation with Worst-Case Guarantees
The Berglin-Brodal Algorithm (k-Flips). Although the algorithm of Brodal-Fagerberg [13]
is optimal, it only provides amortized guarantees, and obtaining worst-case bounds is of both
strong theoretical and practical interest. In recent work, Berglin and Brodal [6] gave an extremely
simple algorithm for orientation with worst-case guarantees that we call the k-Flips Algorithm. The
algorithm has a single parameter, k. On each insert or delete operation, it repeats the following
k times: flip an edge incident on a (current) max out-degree vertex. Depending on the choice of
parameters, k will be between constant and O(log n).
To determine which edge is flipped, they store the out-edges in a queue, flipping edges from the
front of the queue, and adding them to the back. By using a queue, they avoid repeatedly flipping
the same edge back-and-forth between two max-degree vertices.
Brodal and Fagerberg’s approach [13] to analyzing their optimal amortized algorithm does not
apply to the k-Flips algorithm. In a single invocation of the k-Flips algorithm, the total number of
bad edges (potential) can increase. However, Brodal and Fagerberg’s analysis relies on the potential
decreasing greatly every invocation. Instead, Berglin and Brodal’s analysis [6] relies on a careful
accounting scheme.
First, potentials are introduced on edges so that the out-degree of a vertex is closely approx-
imated by its potential (the sum of its out-edges’ potential). These potentials are chosen so that
flipping an edge from a vertex with sufficiently high out-degree will lower the potential of the graph
overall. However, even as the overall potential of the graph decreases, it is still possible that the
potential (and hence out-degree) could accumulate on a single vertex over time. To bound this
accumulation, they develop an abstract counter game. In this counter game, played by a single
adversarial player, there is a set of counters with weights. On each turn, the player can move
weight from one counter to another, but with certain restrictions. For example, weights can only
be moved from counters with close to the maximum weight. They say that a counter game domi-
nates a potential state if each counter has weight greater than its corresponding vertex’s potential
value. Their analysis shows that this counter game is general enough to simulate the changes of
vertex potentials over time, but restricted enough to have bounded growth on the maximum weight
of any counter.

Why is Adapting the k-Flips Algorithm to the Batch Setting Hard? The sequential
worst-case algorithm immediately gives a parallel dynamic algorithm (parallelizing a single update),
because it only performs k = O(log n) flips, and thus runs in O(log n) span. However, in the parallel
batch-dynamic setting, applying the sequential algorithm on a batch of size b would yield Ω(b log n)
span. Therefore, we would like to find a way to perform many edge flips at once to lower the span
for batch updates. However, we run into four difficulties when flipping many edges simultaneously:
(1) We must decide which edges to flip. We cannot choose arbitrary edges to flip, because we need
a net reduction in potential.
(2) We must demonstrate that potentials do not accumulate significantly.
In Berglin-Brodal’s
counter game [6], a single move consists of shifting weight from a single counter that is close
to the maximum weight to other counters. In a batch update, we have many vertices change
and transfer potential. We must either demonstrate that there exists a sequence of moves of
their counter game that dominates a batch update, or find another method to bound potential
movements.
(3) Data structures that are trivial in the sequential setting, if naively adapted, become expensive
in the parallel batch-dynamic setting, contributing an additional O(log n) factor to the work.
For the potential argument, we need a container for the out edges with FIFO-like behavior.
Furthermore, we must maintain which vertices have high out-degree, so that we know from
which vertices to flip edges.
(4) In the k-flips algorithm, the choice of later edge flips strongly depends on the earlier flips, since
the maximum out-degree vertex changes with each flip. This dependence is important in the
analysis, as it implies that the maximum out-degree can worsen by at most 1 in a single call
of k-flips, which is critical for the counter game. However, because we must pick many edges
to flip at once, we seemingly lose this precision and the ability to bound the amount by which
the maximum out-degree can increase in a single time step.
We adapt the Berglin and Brodal k-flips algorithm [6] to the parallel batch-dynamic setting
while ensuring good worst-case bounds. We address these four issues as follows. Among them,
Issue 4—i.e., bounding the worsening of the maximum out-degree—is the most challenging.
Resolving Issue 1: Choosing Which Edges to Flip. An analog of flipping an edge from a
max-degree vertex is flipping a set of edges from high-degree vertices. We capture this notion using
a skyline.
Informally, we define a skyline to be a subset of edges of high out-degree vertices, where the
number of edges contributed by each vertex increases with its out-degree. A skyline is so named
because, examining the bar graph of vertex out-degrees and highlighting a portion of the bar equal
to the number of edges selected, the highlighted portion looks like the skyline of a city, as shown
in Figure 2. In Sections 5 and 11, we formally define skylines and show how to compute a skyline
with good work and span bounds.
We choose to flip skylines of edges and show that flipping an entire skyline of size K reduces
the potential by Kϵ, provided that the skyline is sufficiently high (Lemma 13.5). We also show that
statically orienting a skyline (which is flipping a subset of a skyline) does not harm the potential,
in Lemma 13.6.

Resolving Issue 4: Bounding the Maximum Out-Degree. Because k-flips always flips away
from a max-out degree vertex, their algorithm has a max out-degree increase of at most 1 per edge
update. The analysis of the k-flips algorithm relies on this fact. However, a batch insert, when
edges are arbitrarily oriented, can dramatically increase the out-degree of a vertex. For example,
inserting a star can worsen the out-degree of the “center” of the star by up to b. Furthermore,
flipping the skyline can cause certain vertices, such as hubs with many in-edges, to have their
out-degrees increase dramatically.
Because the batch counter game is the best tool we have to bound our out-degree, we are re-
stricted in our choice of algorithm. We design an algorithm to match the batch counter game, rather
than picking a seemingly efficient algorithm and then finding an analysis method that accompanies
it. We note that our algorithms are relatively simple, but proving the max out-degree bounds is
involved because we must connect the algorithm to the counter game. We give two solutions to
how we can limit the increase to the max out-degree. The first solution leads to a worse overall
out-degree bound but is work-efficient. The second leads to the same O(c+log n) orientation bound
that Berglin and Brodal [6] achieves, but costs an additional O(log n) factor in the work. With this
intuition, we can now discuss our algorithms.

3.1
Model and Primitives
Parallel Model. We use the work-span (or work-depth) model for binary fork-join parallelism with
atomics to analyze parallel algorithms [10,17,26]. The model assumes a set of threads that share
memory. A thread can fork two child threads that run in parallel. When all children complete,
the parent thread continues. The work W of an algorithm is the total number of instructions and
the span (depth) S is the length of the longest sequence of dependent instructions. Computations
can be executed using a randomized work-stealing scheduler in practice in W/P + O(S) time with
high probability on P processors [4,12,27]. The atomic operations Test and Set (TS) and Compare
and Swap (CAS) are permitted.
There are several variants of work-span models, including the CRCW PRAM [34]. Importantly,
however, most of these models are robust with respect with each other in terms of work.
In
particular, it is possible cross simulate the models while asymptotically preserving work, at least
assuming randomization [10, 26]. The span among models, however, can differ by a logarithmic
factor (e.g., simulating n-way forking of a PRAM with binary forking requires a logarithmic increase
in span). We therefore state precise asymptotic bounds for work, but focus just on polylogarithmic
span.
Parallel Batch-Dynamic Algorithms. Our algorithms operate in the parallel batch-dynamic
setting. In this setting, we have a sequence of batch updates B = {Bi} (edge insertions/deletions)
and a sequence of t + 1 undirected graphs G0, G1, . . . , Gt, where G0 is empty, and each Gi was
generated from Gi−1 with a batch edge update Bi = Ei−1△Ei. After each batch update on the
graph, we must update the data structure we are maintaining (i.e. the orientation) to the new
graph. We let bi = |Bi|. When clear, we refer to a particular batch update in the sequence as B,
and let b = |B|. Note that the batches can have different sizes. We assume that a batch update
will not insert edges already in the graph, nor delete edges not present, and will have no duplicate
edges. Furthermore, we assume that batch deletes give the pointer to the edge they are deleting.
Similar to design goals for efficient parallel algorithms, the gold standard for parallel batch-
dynamic algorithms is to design algorithms that are work-efficient (i.e., asymptotically perform
the same work as the best sequential dynamic algorithms that processes the updates in a batch
one-at-a-time) and have low (ideally poly-logarithmic) span in the worst case.
Our adaptive adversary is able to see the out-degree orientation we have chosen after each
time step, and may pick the next batch updates based on this knowledge. However, our adaptive
adversary may not see random bits that we have not yet requested.

4.1
Algorithm Description
Our approach adapts the classic sequential algorithm of Brodal and Fagerberg [13] to the parallel
batch-dynamic setting. For a batch of edge insertions, the algorithm first adds the new edges,
assigning them an arbitrary orientation. A “repair” step is then performed to correct the out-
degrees of vertices violating the degree bound. However, instead of iteratively flipping all outgoing
edges of violating vertices as in [13], we observe that performing a single static orientation on
the local subgraph induced by the violating vertices and all their out-edges is sufficient and yields
equivalent asymptotic bounds.
More specifically, we define two degree bounds: a cutoff threshold τ and a static threshold
τ ′. After inserting the batch of edges, the algorithm performs a parallel static τ′-orientation on
the subgraph induced by vertices with out-degree greater than τ and all their out-edges. This
guarantees that each of these violating vertices has its out-degree reduced to at most τ ′, while
ensuring the out-degree of any other vertex is bounded by τ + τ′. Batch deletions are handled
straightforwardly: edges in the batch are simply removed from the graph as this cannot increase
any out-degrees.
The full batch-dynamic update algorithms are detailed in Algorithm 1. Implementation-wise, in
addition to storing vertices in V , we maintain two bags, V low and V high, that partition the vertices
based on their out-degree: vertices with out-degree greater than τ are placed in V high, and those
with out-degree at most τ are placed in V low. Whenever a vertex’s out-degree changes, we update
its bag membership accordingly.

6.2.2
Effect of edge updates
Let B be the next batch of updates applied to G. The application of B (along with the offline
flips performed by the strategy κ) transforms the potential p into a new potential r. Crucially,
this update injects potential into the system; specifically, the total potential increases by at most
|B|ηϵ (Lemma 13.7). Consequently, the previous counter state u may no longer dominate r, nor
can we simply increase u to match r without violating the conservation rules of the counter game
(condition R2).
To restore the domination property while adhering to the counter game rules, our update
algorithms must transform the orientation from potential r to a final potential q such that we can
construct a new counter state w where:
1. w dominates q;
2. w is reachable from u via a legal T-move with cap H∗.
6.2.3
The (H, T)-Bounded Framework
The transition from r to q essentially involves releasing potential from the graph to offset the
injection caused by B. We formalize the requirements for this transition via the definition of an
(H, T)-bounded call (Definition 7.4). This definition encapsulates three critical properties required
to map the algorithmic steps to a valid counter game move:
1. Potential release: The algorithm must perform flips to ensure that the total potential
decreases by at least |B|ηϵ. This ensures that the global weight constraint of the counter
game (R2) is satisfied.

2. Limited drop: The algorithm must ensure that for any vertex x, the final potential q(x) does

3. Degree cap: The final maximum out-degree must be bounded by T +H. This maps directly

For an update procedure whose calls are (H, T)-bounded for H = H∗−β, we can show that

6.2.4

Our primary tool for releasing potential is the skyline, as defined in Section 5. By flipping a skyline
of sufficient height (Definition 5.4) and size x, we release at least xϵ potential (See Lemma 13.5).
Both of our algorithms proceed in two logical stages: a potential release stage, where skylines are
flipped to satisfy the potential release condition, and a degree reduction stage, where the graph is
modified to satisfy the degree cap H.
We present two algorithms that trade off work for the tightness of the parameter H, which
directly dictates the quality of the worst-case degree bound.
Algorithm 1: Two-Stage. As indicated by the name, this algorithm procedures in two stages
which naturally correspond to a potential release stage and degree reduction stage. In the first
stage, we identify and flip a skyline of size |B|η. This releases sufficient potential to offset the batch
update. However, flipping a skyline can drastically increase the maximum out-degree of the graph.

8
Conclusion
In this paper we have presented three different algorithms for parallel batch-dynamic low out-
degree orientation. Our first algorithm has optimal work bounds (at the cost of amortization and
randomness). We provide two worst-case solutions, which either give a slightly worse out-degree
bound or a slightly worse work bound. One interesting future work would be to explore low out-
degree orientations in cases with adaptive arboricity (where the bounds are with respect to the
current arboricity, not the overall maximum), as done in the sequential setting by Chekuri et
al. [15]. It would also be interesting to explore different points on the tradeoff curve, for example
if worst-case work-efficiency can be achieved for O(c) or O(c + log n) orientation for sparse graphs.
We note that the counter game does not take into account the density constraint given by the
arboricity of the graph. We believe it may be possible to improve the counter game by incorporating
this constraint.

the span down to O(log x), the creation of the binary tree is deferred until the end, where all binary

Delete. We keep a list of trees that we remove from the bag. Whenever we remove a single element
(a root), we store this as a tree with one vertex. Thus, after running delete, we have a list of length
O(log x) of removed elements. Some of the elements we wish to delete (and some elements we did
not want to delete) will have been removed from the bag, and some are still within the bag. We
want to place the elements we accidentally removed into the holes created by the deleted elements
still in the bag.
To do this, each deleted element marks that it is being deleted, and each removed element
marks itself as having been removed. Then, filter the deleted pointers list to only include those not
removed, and filter the removed elements list by those which should not have been deleted. Now
we can map the elements we want to return into pointers of deleted elements still in the bag.

Based on their game, we design a batch-counter game which more naturally applies to the parallel

Consider a function u that maps vertices (of which there are n) to positive, real counter weights.
The weights of these counters will eventually correspond to the potentials of vertices in the graph.
This game will consist of a single, adversarial player. On each turn, the player may adjust counter
weights, with some restrictions. We will show that in the game, the max counter weight is bounded.
In Section 7, we showed that the game is powerful enough to simulate the changes in potential in
our algorithm.

Appendix: Applications
14.1
Maximal Matching
Here we apply our batch-dynamic orientation algorithms to the problem of maintaining a maximal
matching. A maximal matching M of a graph G = (V, E) is a set of edges such that no two edges
in M share an endpoint, and every edge in E \ M shares an endpoint with some edge in M. The
connection between low out-degree orientations and dynamic maximal matching appears in many
papers in the low-outdegree orientation literature [35, 40]. The key idea is that a c-orientation
allows each unmatched vertex to efficiently propose matchings to a bounded number of neighbors,
enabling fast searches for “replacement” edges for the matching when a matched edge is deleted.
Amortized Batch-Dynamic Maximal Matching. Liu et al. [37] gave a parallel batch-dynamic
maximal matching algorithm based on their (4 + ϵ)c-orientation algorithm. Their approach main-
tains, for each vertex v, a hash table Iv of unmatched in-neighbors (vertices u with an edge oriented
from u →v where u is unmatched). On edge insertions, they run a parallel static maximal match-
ing [11, 21] on the subgraph induced by inserted edges between unmatched vertices.
On edge
deletions that remove matched edges, they first attempt to rematch the newly unmatched vertices
with their out-neighbors, and then use a doubling scheme to progressively query more in-neighbors
until all vertices are either matched or have no unmatched neighbors. The work analysis requires a
simple charging argument to amortize the cost of unsuccessfully checking an in-neighbor that gets
matched to another vertex in the same batch.
Our improved orientation algorithm (Algorithm 1) can be directly plugged into this framework.
The only change is replacing their (4 + ϵ)c-orientation with our 7c-orientation, which has better
work bounds. The matching algorithm itself and the analysis remains unchanged.
Theorem 14.1. Given a graph G with arboricity at most c, there exists a parallel batch-dynamic
algorithm that maintains a maximal matching M of G.
For a batch B of b edge updates, the
algorithm runs in amortized expected O(b(c + log n)) work and O(log2 n) span whp.
Proof. The proof follows the analysis of Liu et al. [37], substituting our orientation bounds. By
Theorem 2.1, our orientation algorithm performs amortized O(b log n) expected work per batch.
The matching maintenance costs O(bc) work per batch: processing out-neighbors costs O(c) per
newly unmatched vertex, and the cost of scanning in-neighbors amortizes to O(c) per vertex that
becomes matched (since each matched in-neighbor has out-degree O(c) and is queried at most
O(c) times before becoming matched). The static maximal matching subroutine runs in O(log2 n)
span whp [11, 21]. Summing the orientation and matching costs gives O(b(c + log n)) amortized
expected work.
The span is O(log2 n) whp, dominated by the static matching and orientation
subroutines.

a hash table), and checking all O(log n) colors in C(v) takes O(c log n) work and O(log n) span in
