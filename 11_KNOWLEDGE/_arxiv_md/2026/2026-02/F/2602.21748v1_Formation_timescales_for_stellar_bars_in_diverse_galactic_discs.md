ABSTRACT
We study the formation of stellar bars using 145 simulations of disc galaxies embedded in live and static dark matter haloes.
We use the exponential bar growth timescale, 𝜏bar, to quantify how disc structure and kinematics regulate the onset and rate of
secular bar formation. We extend previous work to thicker and more turbulent discs, motivated by those observed at high redshift
(𝑧> 1). By revisiting several commonly used disc stability criteria – the Efstathiou-Lake-Negroponte parameter (𝜖ELN), the
Ostriker-Peebles ratio (𝑡OP), and the disc stellar mass fraction within 2.2 disc scale radii ( 𝑓disc) – we find that 𝜏bar, when expressed
in terms of the disc’s orbital period, follows a tight power law with each criteria. In Milky Way-like discs embedded in live haloes,
bars form within a Hubble time if 𝑓disc ≥0.18, 𝑡OP ≥0.27, and 𝜖ELN ≤1.44. We show discs with higher velocity dispersion
experience delayed bar growth and introduce an empirical relation that correctly describes the bar formation timescales of all
our live halo models. Bars in static haloes grow at roughly half the rate of those in live haloes and require substantially greater
disc instability to do so.
Key words: instabilities – galaxies: bar – galaxies: kinematics and dynamics – galaxies: haloes
1 INTRODUCTION
Stellar bars are observed in roughly two-thirds of low redshift disc
galaxies (e.g. Eskridge et al. 2000; Sheth et al. 2008; Masters et al.
2011). Their prevalence has been attributed to the rapid onset of grav-
itational instabilities (Hohl 1971; Ostriker & Peebles 1973), which
readily develop in kinematically cool, stellar-dominated discs (e.g.,
Fujii et al. 2018; Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2023, hereafter F18 and
BH23, respectively). However, recent detections of barred galaxies
at 𝑧≳2 (e.g. Guo et al. 2023; Le Conte et al. 2024; Espejo Sal-
cedo et al. 2025) challenge this picture, and suggest that stellar bars
can also form in turbulent discs that are more common in the early
Universe (Förster Schreiber et al. 2009; Stott et al. 2016; Hamilton-
Campos et al. 2023; Birkin et al. 2024). How dynamically turbulent
conditions influence the onset and growth of stellar bars is not well
understood.
Early simulations established that isolated, self-gravitating discs
are prone to bar formation (e.g. Ostriker & Peebles 1973; Efstathiou
et al. 1982; Christodoulou et al. 1995), leading to the development of
simple analytic conditions linking galaxy properties to the onset of
bar instabilities. These criteria were developed for two-dimensional
discs embedded in rigid dark matter (DM) haloes, neglecting the
impacts of vertical disc structure and angular momentum exchange
between the disc and halo (e.g. Athanassoula 2003; Dubinski et al.
2009; Bournaud et al. 2011; Saha & Naab 2013; Collier et al. 2018;
Collier & Madigan 2021). Consequently, their applicability to real-
istic galaxies is limited (Athanassoula 2008; Yurin & Springel 2015;
Mayer & Wadsley 2004; Sellwood 2016; Izquierdo-Villalba et al.
2022; Romeo et al. 2023).
The growth rate of stellar bars can also be used to characterise

found that bars form more rapidly in galaxies with higher 𝑓disc, and
that bar growth within a Hubble time typically requires 𝑓disc ≳0.3.
BH23 extended this work and showed that the bar growth timescale
also depends on halo mass and disc gas fraction (see also Verwilghen
et al. 2024). However, neither study examined the role of other key
disc properties, such as disc thickness or stellar velocity dispersion,
on the onset and growth rate of bars.
The kinematic and structural properties of stellar discs play key
roles in bar formation (Athanassoula & Misiriotis 2002; Saha &
Naab 2013; Athanassoula et al. 2013; Long et al. 2014; Collier et al.
2018; Kataria & Das 2019; Ghosh et al. 2023). In particular, both
high in-plane radial velocity dispersions (Toomre 1964; Hohl 1971;
Kalnajs 1972; Toomre 1977; Sellwood & Carlberg 1984; Athanas-
soula 2003; Binney & Tremaine 2008), and high vertical velocity
dispersions in thick discs can delay or suppress bar growth (Klypin
et al. 2009; Aumer & Binney 2017; Ghosh et al. 2023). These trends
are found in both idealised simulations of isolated discs and cos-
mological galaxy formation models (e.g. López et al. 2024; Ansar
et al. 2025; Fragkoudi et al. 2025; Rosas-Guevara et al. 2025; Frosst
et al. 2025), and are consistent with observations showing that bars
are most common in massive, dynamically cold galaxies (e.g. Sheth
et al. 2012). However, these effects are often neglected in analytic
criteria for bar instabilities.

for warm discs formed from quasi-isothermal DFs (Vasiliev 2019).
2.2 The simulation code
We evolve our models with the GADGET-4 (Springel et al. 2021)
code. Gravitational accelerations between particles are computed us-
ing the Fast Multipole Method (FMM) with a third-order expansion
(𝑝= 3) as originally outlined in Greengard & Rokhlin (1987). We
use the default integration accuracy parameter 𝜁= 0.005 and find
that halving this value does not affect our results. Snapshots are out-
put in 50 Myr (≈0.66𝑡orb,MW) intervals for 10 Gyr (≈133𝑡orb,MW),
resulting in 200 snapshots per run.
To ensure reproducibility, we evolved ICs created using three dis-
tinct “seeds” by resampling the DFs of the fiducial live halo simula-
tions (see Table 1). The evolution of disc and bar properties between
different random initializations of the ICs were consistent with each
other, reassuring us of the robustness of the simulation results against
Poisson noise.

3.4 The effect of velocity dispersion on bar growth
Velocity dispersion is expected to play a critical role in regulating bar
formation. The radial velocity dispersion, imprinted in the Toomre
stability parameter, 𝑄, can reduce the responsiveness of the disc to
non-axisymmetric perturbations (e.g. Toomre 1964; Kalnajs 1972;
Athanassoula 2003; Binney & Tremaine 2008). Similarly, the verti-
cal velocity dispersion, which determines the disc scale height, 𝑧𝑑,
provides vertical support against self-gravity and drives stellar orbits
out of the disc plane (e.g. Klypin et al. 2009; Ghosh et al. 2023)
in a mechanism not captured by classical two-dimensional stability
criteria. In both cases, higher velocity dispersions are expected to
inhibit bar formation (e.g. Athanassoula 2003).
To quantify the effect of dispersion, we introduce five new suites of
isolated disc galaxy models that, when used in conjunction with the
fiducial models, systematically vary 𝑄min ∈{1.1, 1.5, 1.7, 2.0} and
ℎ𝑧∈{0.05, 0.1, 0.2}. Each new suite spans a range of disc-to-halo
mass ratios (𝑀d/𝑀h ∈{0.01, 0.03, 0.05}) and halo concentrations
(𝐶∈{5, 10, 20}), and all use live DM haloes. The time evolution of
the bar strengths of these new models are displayed in Fig. A1 and
Fig. A2, respectively, and their structural parameters and simulation
properties are available in Table 1. These models were generated
and run with the same techniques as the fiducial simulation suite
discussed in Sec. 3.2. Together, these simulations allow us to assess
how velocity dispersion influences the bar growth timescale, 𝜏bar,
across 95 distinct galaxy models.
Fig. 6 shows 𝜏bar/𝑡orb as a function of 𝑓disc, 𝑡OP, and 𝜖ELN, for

4 DISCUSSION
4.1 Caveats to our analysis
There are several caveats to this work that must be acknowledged. Our
simulations neglect star formation, energetic feedback, and galactic
components such as bulges, stellar haloes, DM substructure, and gas
discs. This deliberate simplicity allows us to better understand the
dynamical processes relevant for bar formation, but limits the realism
of our models. In particular, disc galaxies, especially those at higher
redshifts, are anticipated to contain substantial gas reservoirs, often
exceeding ≈50 per cent of the baryonic mass (e.g. Daddi et al. 2010;
Tacconi et al. 2010). Previous studies have shown that gas discs can
bothinhibit and accelerate barformationdependingonitsdistribution
and mass relative to the stellar disc. For example, Bournaud et al.
(2005) and Wozniak & Michel-Dansac (2009) found that high gas
fractions suppress bar growth, whereas BH23 and Verwilghen et al.
(2024) showed that moderate gas fractions (≈10–20 per cent of the
stellar disc mass) can halve the bar growth timescale relative to the
F18 relation (see also Berentzen et al. 1998; Athanassoula et al. 2013;
Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2024). Other works report either accelerated
(Robichaud et al. 2017) or negligible (Berentzen et al. 2007) effects.
This discrepancy may be related to the feedback prescriptions (or
lack thereof) of these simulations, which can induce turbulence into
the gas disc, accelerating the growth of bar-forming perturbations
(e.g. Robichaud et al. 2017; Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2024). Another
possible reason for the discrepancy between different conclusions is
that if working in dimensional units, systems with higher orbital times
(at 𝑅d) generally form their bars more slowly. Following the results of
this work it is imperative to suitably normalise bar formation times;
we recommend this is done by the measured orbital time 𝑡orb at the
disc scale radius 𝑅d.
Second, our live halos lack net rotation, restricting angular mo-
mentum transfer to a one-way flow from the disc to the halo. Previous
work indicates that this exchange significantly affects both the onset
and growth rate of stellar bars (e.g. Saha & Naab 2013; Kataria & Das
2018; Collier et al. 2019; Collier & Madigan 2021). Consequently,
discs embedded in rotating halos may develop bars differently than
those in non-rotating ones, even when other structural properties
remain the same.
Finally, our galaxies evolve in perfect isolation, while real galax-
ies are subject to a cosmological environment including repeated
mergers and tidal interactions throughout their history (e.g. Hammer
et al. 2009; Rodriguez-Gomez et al. 2015). Numerous studies have
demonstrated that such encounters can rapidly induce bar forma-
tion (e.g., Noguchi 1987; Berentzen et al. 2004; Lang et al. 2014;
Łokas et al. 2014; Łokas 2018), due to the strong tidal forces acting
on the disc (see also Mayer & Wadsley 2004; Romano-Díaz et al.
2008; Martinez-Valpuesta et al. 2017; Zana et al. 2018; Peschken
& Łokas 2019; Rosas-Guevara et al. 2024; Izquierdo-Villalba et al.
2022; Frosst et al. 2025). We exclude these effects here to maintain
controlled conditions and accurately quantify the intrinsic disc sta-
bility. In cosmological environments, where tidal perturbations and
interactions are common, the bar formation times are possibly shorter
and the parameter space for bar formation broader (as found in Frosst
et al. 2025).
4.2 Implications for high redshift bars
Disc galaxies dominate the low-redshift Universe (𝑧≲1; e.g. Con-
selice 2014), but are also observed in abundance at much earlier
cosmic times (e.g. Ferreira et al. 2023; Espejo Salcedo et al. 2025).
High redshift discs have been detected through ionised gas kinemat-
ics (e.g. Genzel et al. 2006), Hubble Space Telescope (HST) obser-
vations (e.g. Johnson et al. 2017), and increasingly with the James
Webb Space Telescope (JWST; Guo et al. 2025), in some cases out to
𝑧≳6 (e.g. Kartaltepe et al. 2023). Although many of these systems
show ordered rotation, they are nevertheless typically thicker, more
turbulent, and more frequently disturbed than their low-𝑧counter-
parts (Förster Schreiber et al. 2009; Stott et al. 2016; Birkin et al.
2024). Remarkably, however, a non-negligible fraction of these high
redshift discs host stellar bars (Guo et al. 2025; Le Conte et al. 2024;
Espejo Salcedo et al. 2025), with such detections now extending to
𝑧≈3 −4 (e.g. Huang et al. 2023; Costantin et al. 2023; Smail et al.
2023; Amvrosiadis et al. 2025).
Our results help explain the common occurrence of bars in high-
redshift observations, which may seem surprising in light of the
high turbulence (velocity dispersion) seen in many of these systems.
Our simulations show that even discs with relatively high radial
dispersions (𝑄min = 2.0) can still form bars within a few Gyr provided
they are sufficiently disc-dominated. With the growing observational
evidence for many massive galaxies at higher redshifts being centrally
baryon-dominated (e.g. Wuyts et al. 2016; Genzel et al. 2020; Price
et al. 2021; Danhaive et al. 2025), bars could be expected to be
commonplace. Adding to this, external tidal perturbations caused
by close encounters and mergers are more common at high redshift,
which may further trigger bars in discs that would otherwise be stable
against bar formation (e.g. Noguchi 1987; Romano-Díaz et al. 2008;
Lang et al. 2014; Zheng & Shen 2025; Frosst et al. 2025).
Cosmological simulations provide a pathway to study bar growth
at high redshifts (e.g. Rosas-Guevara et al. 2022; Łokas 2025), though
their output cadence often lacks the time resolution needed to pre-
cisely measure 𝜏bar. Additionally, constant accretion and frequent
interactions in these environments mean that bars may not follow a
simple exponential growth phase (Frosst et al. 2025). Despite these
complexities, determining bar formation times in cosmological discs
would enable a direct comparison to our idealised models, clarifying
how 𝜏bar scales with 𝑓disc in realistic environments and identifying
how disc kinematics might suppress bar formation.
5 SUMMARY
In this work, we examined the secular evolution of stellar bars us-
ing 145 self-consistent scale-free models of isolated galactic discs
embedded in live and rigid dark matter haloes. These models were
evolved using highly resolved dissipation free 𝑁-body simulations,
following the methods of Frosst et al. (2024), which offer converged
bar properties with negligible numerical heating. We measured the
bar growth as a function of time and quantified how variations in disc
structure and kinematics affect bar formation. Our goal was to extend
the analyses of F18 and BH23 to also include thicker, more turbulent
discs, more representative of those observed at higher redshift. Our
main conclusions are as follows:
(i) All bars forming in our simulations grow exponentially un-
til they experience buckling. Bar formation times are therefore best
characterised by the exponential time-scale 𝜏bar. Unlike other stan-
dard metrics, such as the time where 𝐴2 surpasses 0.2, the exponential

estimator that accounts for stellar velocity dispersions, this work ex-

representative of structurally diverse galaxies across cosmic times.
While our analysis has been limited to idealised, isolated systems,
it underscores the importance of considering both vertical and ra-
dial disc structure when characterising the assembly phase of bar
formation. Future work should test these findings in full-physics
simulations, including the effects of gas discs, central bulge compo-
nents, and realistic star formation and feedback models. Furthermore,
our results should be investigated in cosmological simulations where
environmental effects and the broader assembly history of galaxies
have been shown to shape the pathways to bar formation. In this
way, the results presented here serve as a step toward a more general
predictive theory of bar growth that can be connected directly with
observations across cosmic time.
