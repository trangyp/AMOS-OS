Abstract
In the Vertex Multicut problem the input consists of a graph G, integer k, and a set
T = {(s1, t1), . . . , (sp, tp)} of pairs of vertices of G. The task is to find a set X of at most k
vertices such that, for every (si, ti) ∈T, there is no path from si to ti in G −X. Marx and
Razgon [STOC 2011 and SICOMP 2014] and Bousquet, Daligault, and Thomass´e [STOC 2011
and SICOMP 2018] independently and simultaneously gave the first algorithms for Vertex
Multicut with running time f(k)nO(1). The running time of their algorithms is 2O(k3)nO(1)
and 2O(kO(1))nO(1), respectively. As part of their result, Marx and Razgon introduce the shadow
removal technique, which was subsequently applied in algorithms for several parameterized cut
and separation problems. The shadow removal step is the only step of the algorithm of Marx and
Razgon which requires 2O(k3)nO(1) time. Chitnis et al. [TALG 2015] gave an improved version
of the shadow removal step, which, among other results, led to a kO(k2)nO(1) time algorithm for
Vertex Multicut.
We give a faster algorithm for the Vertex Multicut problem with running time kO(k)nO(1).
Our main technical contribution is a refined shadow removal step for vertex separation problems
that only introduces an overhead of kO(k) log n time. The new shadow removal step implies a
kO(k2)nO(1) time algorithm for Directed Subset Feedback Vertex Set and a kO(k)nO(1)
time algorithm for Directed Multiway Cut, improving over the previously best known al-
gorithms of Chitnis et al. [TALG 2015].

Introduction
The minimum s–t cut problem is a fundamental problem in graph theory and combinatorial op-
timization. This classical polynomial-time solvable problem forms the cornerstone of numerous
separation and partitioning problems in graphs. A natural generalization arises when one aims to
separate not just a single pair of terminals, but several terminals or terminal pairs simultaneously.
If the goal is to separate all vertices in a given vertex set T of size p from each other by deleting
at most k edges or vertices, the problem is known as the Multiway Cut problem (also called the
Multi-terminal Cut problem). If, instead, the input specifies a collection T of p pairs of vertices
in G, called terminal pairs, that must each be disconnected, the problem becomes the Multicut
problem. Clearly, Multiway Cut is a special case of Multicut, where every pair of terminals
defines a terminal pair. Depending on whether the graph is directed or undirected and whether
we delete edges or vertices, we obtain four variants: Edge Multicut, Vertex Multicut, Di-
rected Edge Multicut, and Directed Vertex Multicut. All of these variants have been
extensively studied in both approximation and parameterized algorithm frameworks.
In this work we focus on parameterized algorithms for the Vertex Multicut problem in
undirected graphs parameterized by the solution size k.
Vertex Multicut (VMC)
Input:
A graph G = (V, E), a set T of vertex pairs and an integer k.
Parameter:
k.
Question:
Determine whether there is a vertex subset X ⊆V with |X| ≤k such
that ∀(s, t) ∈T, s, t are in different components in G −X.
Our main result is an algorithm for Vertex Multicut with running time O∗(kO(k))1.
Theorem 1 (Main). There is an algorithm that solves Vertex Multicut in time kO(k)nO(1).
Theorem 1 improves over a O∗(2O(kO(1))) time algorithm due to Bousquet, Daligault, and
Thomass´e [BDT18], a O∗(2O(k3)) time algorithm of Marx and Razgon [MR14], and over the current
fastest O∗(kO(k2)) time algorithm of Chitnis et al. [CCHM15].
Background and Complexity. There is a simple polynomial time reduction from Edge Multi-
cut to Vertex Multicut that exactly preserves solution size k, thus our result implies the same
bound for Edge Multicut. Vertex Multicut in turn reduces to Directed Vertex Mul-
ticut by replacing undirected edges by edges in both directions. Directed Vertex Multicut
reduces to Directed Edge Multicut by the well-known “vertex-splitting” trick that replaces
every vertex with two copies, one for the in-neighbors and one for the out-neighbors, and adds an
edge from the in-vertex to the out-vertex. Finally Directed Edge Multicut reduces back to
Directed Vertex Multicut by a directed version of the reduction from Edge Multicut to
Vertex Multicut. Thus the pecking order of problems (from easiest to hardest) is
Edge Multicut ≤Vertex Multicut ≤Directed Edge/Vertex Multicut

as there are three or more terminal pairs [DJP+94]. From the perspective of approximation algo-
rithms, both Edge and Vertex Multicut in undirected graphs admit an O(log p)-approximation
via the Multicommodity Max-Flow approach [GVY96]. On the other hand, under the Unique

Parameterized Algorithms for Cut and Separation Problems. In the framework of param-
eterized algorithms [CFK+15, DF+13], the Multicut and Multiway Cut problems have been
considered with respect to the parameters cut-size k and the number of terminal pairs p. A system-
atic study was initiated by Marx [Mar06], who gave an algorithm for Vertex Multiway Cut with
running time O∗(2O(k3)) and for Vertex Multicut with running time O∗(2O(k3+kp)). The ques-
tion whether Vertex Multicut and Edge Multicut are fixed parameter tractable (FPT) with
respect to parameter k, that is whether there exists an algorithm with running time O∗(f(k)) for
some function f, was a well known open problem re-stated several times [Mar06,CLL09,GHK+08,
BFH+10].
Marx [Mar06] introduced the notion of important separators (see Section 2.2), which have be-
come a standard tool in parameterized algorithms [CFK+15]. Marx [Mar06] proved that there
are at most 4O(k2) important s-t separators of size at most k.
This bound was subsequently
improved to 4k by Chen et al. [CLL09], who used the improved bound to get a O∗(4k) time al-
gorithm for Vertex Multiway Cut. Important separators were a key tool for the first fixed
parameter tractable algorithms for Directed Feedback Vertex Set [CLL+08] and Almost
2-SAT [RO09], each of which resolved prominent open problems in parameterized complexity. The
current fastest parameterized algorithms for Vertex Multiway Cut [CPPW13] and Edge Mul-
tiway Cut [Xia10, CCF14] run in time O∗(2k) and O∗(1.84k), respectively, and use related, but
different, techniques.
The question of whether there exists a fixed parameter tractable algorithm for Vertex Multi-
cut was resolved simultaneously and independently by Marx and Razgon [MR14] and by Bousquet,
Daligault, and Thomass´e [BDT18], who gave algorithms for Vertex Multicut with running time
O∗(2O(k3)) and O∗(2O(kO(1))) respectively.
Marx and Razgon [MR14] introduced the powerful technique of shadow removal. Loosely speak-
ing shadow removal works whenever input is a graph G, a set W of terminals, and potentially some
additional information, and the solution we seek is a vertex set X. The shadow of X is the set R of
all vertices in G −X that can not reach W. Shadow removal works for all problems where adding
vertices to a solution results in a solution, and furthermore for every solution X and every vertex
v ∈X, if v is in the shadow of X −{v} then X −{v} is also a solution (in fact it works in a slightly
more general setting, see Section 4). A shadowless solution is a solution X whose shadow is empty.
The shadow removal technique of Marx and Razgon [MR14] allows one to assume, at the cost of a
O∗(2O(k3)) factor in the running time, that the solution is shadowless. This often greatly simplifies
the search for a solution.
Shadow removal has found multiple applications in parameterized algorithms, including al-
gorithms for Directed Multiway Cut [CHM13], Directed Vertex Multicut parameter-
ized by k + p in directed acyclic graphs [KPPW15], Directed Subset Feedback Vertex
Set [CCHM15], certain graph clustering problems [LM13] and a parameterized approximation
algorithm for Directed Odd Cycle Transversal [LRSZ20].
The overhead of O∗(2O(k3)) for the shadow removal step was improved to O∗(2O(k2)) by Chitnis
et al [CCHM15], who note that this improvement also directly translates into a faster parameter-
ized algorithm for Directed Multiway Cut. The improved shadow removal step of Chitnis et

Related Work.
We briefly mention a few additional results regarding parameterized algorithms
and approximation algorithms for variants of the Multicut and Multiway Cut problems. In
undirected graphs, Edge Multiway Cut admits a 1.3438-approximation algorithm [KKS+04],
while Vertex Multiway Cut allows a 2-approximation [GVY04]. In directed graphs, the edge
and vertex versions coincide, and a 2-approximation is also known [NZ01].
Unlike the undirected case, Directed Multicut is known not to admit an FPT algorithm
parameterized by solution size k unless FPT = W[1] [MR14]. Therefore, most work focuses on the
setting where the number of terminal pairs p is fixed. When p ≤3, the problem is FPT [CHM13,
HJL+23], although only a randomized FPT algorithm is known for the case p = 3. For p = 4 the
problem becomes W[1]-hard [PW18].
Variants of Multicut have also been studied on restricted graph classes. For example, Edge
Multicut on trees is NP-hard [GVY97], but admits an FPT algorithm [GN05] and a polynomial
kernel [BDTY09] when parameterized by the cut size k. When parameterized by the treewidth tw,
both Edge Multicut and Vertex Multicut become FPT if the treewidth remains bounded
after connecting each terminal pair [GL07, PRW10] by an auxiliary edge.
For bounded-degree
graphs with bounded treewidth, Edge Multicut admits a PTAS [CFR03].
Prior to designing their FPT algorithm for Vertex Multicut [MR14], motivated by a lack of
progress on achieving an FPT algorithm for this problem, Marx and Razgon [MR09] gave a factor
2 approximation algorithm for Vertex Multicut running in time O∗(2O(k log k)). Much more
recently, motivated by a lack of progress for achieving a faster FPT algorithm for this problem,
Lokshtanov et al. [LMR+21] give a 2-approximation algorithm for Vertex Multicut running in
time O∗(2O(k)).
Recently, the weighted versions of Vertex Multicut and Multiway Cut are proved FPT
by Kim et al. [KMP+24], using flow augmentation [KKPW25].
1.1
Proof Outline
We give a brief outline of the proof of Theorem 1. We start with a summary of the algorithm of
Marx and Razgon [MR14]. This algorithm proceeds in four steps. The first step is an application
of the iterative compression technique, and allows us, at the cost of a O∗(kO(k)) overhead in the
running time, to solve a Vertex Multicut Compression instance instead. Here, in addition
to the instance (G, T, k) we are given a vertex set W of size at most k + 1, such that W is a
solution to (G, T, k), and we are looking for a solution X to (G, T, k) which is disjoint from W, and
simultaneously is a multiway cut for W. In particular no connected component of G −X should
contain more than one vertex of W.
The second step of the algorithm is shadow removal. Recall that the shadow of a solution X
is the set of vertices of G that can’t reach W in G −X. A solution X of a Vertex Multicut
Compression instance is shadowless if its shadow is empty.
Marx and Razgon design an algorithm that given an instance (G, T, W, k) of Vertex Multicut
Compression and outputs 2O(k3) instances so that (G, T, W, k) is a yes-instance if and only if one
of the output instances has a shadowless solution of size at most k.
This is the step that was improved by Chitnis et al. [CCHM15], they are able to produce only
2O(k2) instances while maintaining the same property.
The third step is to reduce Vertex Multicut Compression to it self but with bipedal in-
stances. Here an instance (G, T, W, k) is bipedal if every connected component C of G−W satisfies
|NG(C)| ≤2. The algorithm of Marx and Razgon takes as input an instance (G, T, k) of Vertex
Multicut Compression and produces at most 2O(k3) (in fact at most kO(k2), as discussed above)
bipedal instances such that (G, T, W, k) admits a shadowless solution of size at most k if and only

Framework of Our Algorithm.
Our algorithm for Vertex Multicut has the same framework
as the algorithm of Marx and Razgon, but implements the two bottleneck stages (namely shadow
removal and reduction to the bipedal case) in completely new ways.
The first step of iterative compression is essentially the same. At the cost of a O∗(kO(k)) overhead
in the running time the problem reduces to solving an Vertex Multicut Compression. Here,
in addition to the instance (G, T, k) we are given a vertex set W of size at most k, such that W is
a solution to (G, T, k), and we are looking for an vertex set solution X ⊆V (G) to (G, T, k) such
that, additionally, X is a multiway cut for W.
The second step of our algorithm is an improved shadow removal step that takes an instance
(G, T, W, k) of Vertex Multicut Compression and outputs kO(k) instances so that (G, T, W, k)
is a yes-instance if and only if one of the output instances has a shadowless solution of size at most
k 3.
We completely re-design the reduction to bipedal instances, and obtain an algorithm with
following specifications. Given an instance (G, T, W, k) of Vertex Multicut Compression, we
produce kO(k) bipedal instances, such that (G, T, W, k) is a yes-instance with a shadowless solution
if and only if one of the output instances is. Thus, at the cost of a factor of kO(k) in the running
time we may assume that the input instance is bipedal.
In the fourth and final step we just invoke the O∗(2O(k)) time algorithm of Marx and Razgon
for finding shadowless solutions for bipedal instances of Vertex Multicut Compression.
We now sketch how we implement the two steps where our algorithm crucially differs from the
one of Marx and Razgon, namely the shadow removal step and the reduction to bipedal instances.
Out of the two, the shadow removal step is the one that appears to have the widest applicability,
so we present it first.
Improved Shadow Removal.
We consider the following general setting, which encompasses
Vertex Multicut Compression. Suppose that we are working with a graph G, which has a
special set W of vertices. Every subset X ⊆V (G) −W is either a solution or not. This can for
example be formalized as a function Φ that takes as input a pair (G, W) and outputs a subset of
2V (G)−W , where X ∈Φ(G, W) means that X is a solution. The shadow of a vertex set X is the set
of vertices that can’t reach W in G −X.
We will concern ourselves only with problems (i.e. functions Φ) that satisfy the following two
axioms: (i) if X is a solution and Y ⊆V (G) −W, then X ∪Y is a solution. (ii) If X ∪{v} is
a solution and v is in the shadow of X, then X is a solution. It is not too difficult to verify that
Vertex Multicut Compression satisfies the two axioms above, indeed (i) trivially holds. For
(ii) suppose that X ∪{v} is a solution and v is in the shadow of X, and let P be a path from si, ti
for (si, ti) ∈T or between two distinct vertices of W. Then X ∪{v} intersects V (P), since X ∪{v}
is a solution. If X intersects V (P) we are done, so suppose v ∈V (P). Since W is a solution, W
intersects P. But then P connects v to W, and thus, since v is in the shadow of X, V (P) intersects
X as well. So X is a solution. Hence Vertex Multicut Compression satisfies (ii).

The main step in the shadow removal algorithm (ours, of Marx and Razgon [MR14], and of
Chitnis et al. [CCHM15]) is a randomized algorithm, which we call shadow covering, with the
following property. Given (G, W, k) the procedure outputs a vertex set Z. If there exists a solution
X′ of size at most k then there exists a solution X of size at most k, such that on every call of
the procedure on (G, W, k), with probability at least p (the success probability) the output set Z is
disjoint from X and Z contains the shadow of X.
Given a shadow covering procedure with success probability p one can get a randomized shadow
removal algorithm that produces O(1/p) instances and succeeds with probability 1−c for any c > 0.
All we need is a graph operation that allows us to remove vertices from the graph that are not
allowed to be selected into the solution (since we safely can declare the vertices in Z as such
unselectable vertices).
Such an operation for Vertex Multicut Compression was given by
Marx and Razgon. Essentially, all we need to do is to remove vertices z in Z from the graph one
by one, when z is deleted its neighborhood is turned into a clique. Some care must be taken to
also preserve the terminal pairs in T whose endpoints are deleted. Furthermore, all known shadow
removal procedures (including ours) can be de-randomized in a standard way using splitters [NSS95]
at a negligible cost to the running time and the number of output instances. Hence in this overview
we will just focus on giving a shadow covering procedure.
Marx and Razgon [MR14] give a shadow covering procedure with success probability 2−O(k3),
Chitnis et al. [CCHM15] design one with success probability 2−O(k2), while we now give one with
success probability k−O(k).
Improved Shadow Covering.
Just as the previous shadow covering procedures, ours relies on
important separators. We give a very brief introduction to important separators tailored to our
particular setting. For actual definitions, see Section 2. We consider the setting where we are given
an instance (G, W, k) of a problem which satisfies the axioms (i) and (ii) of shadow removal.
Let v be a vertex of G. A set X disjoint from v and W is a v −W separator if the component
of G−X containing v does not contain any vertex of W. A v −W separator X is a minimal v −W
separator if no proper subset of X is also a v −W separator. A minimal v −W separator X is an
important v −W separator if there is no v −W separator X′ distinct from X such that |X′| ≤|X|
and the (vertex set of the) component C′ of G −X′ containing v contains the component C of
G −X containing v. The important thing to know about important separators is that, for every
G, W, v, k there are at most 4k important v−W separators of size at most k in G [CLL09,CFK+15].
The two axioms (i) and (ii) imply that, if there exists a solution X′ to (G, W) of size at most k
then there exists a solution X of size at most k such that (a) for every vertex v in the shadow of X,
the neighborhood of the component C of G −X that contains v is an important v −W separator,
(b) for every v in X there is a path from v to W disjoint from X′ −{v}. The existence of X given
X′ is a step which is done in every previous paper that does shadow removal, so we do not repeat
it here (it is the now standard “pushing” argument for important separators). We give a shadow
covering procedure that outputs a vertex set Z, such that for every solution X that satisfies (a)
and (b), Z is disjoint from X and covers the shadow of X with probability at least k−2k/e.
The procedure relies on the following interesting property of important separators. For every
(G, W, v, k) there exists a vertex set Q of size at most k, such that for every important v −W
separator S of size at most k, Q has non-empty intersection with S. To the best of our knowledge,
this lemma was first proved by Korhonen and Lokshtanov [KL23]. We need a slight strengthening
of this lemma, namely that for any vertex set X that does not separate v from W there exists a
vertex set Q of size at most k, disjoint from X, such that for every important v −W separator
S of size at most k, Q has non-empty intersection with S. The proof of this strengthened version

Reduction to Bipedal Instances.
We now describe our reduction from Vertex Multicut
Compression to its bipedal instances.
The reduction relies on the LP relaxation of Vertex
Multiway Cut. For a Vertex Multiway Cut instance (G, W, k), we can build a linear program
where the variable set is {dv : v ∈V (G) \ W} and the constraints are P
v∈V (P)\W dv ≥1 for every
path P connecting two different vertices in W. Vertex Multiway Cut is equivalent to the integer
version of this LP. In the relaxation version, we allow variables to take any non-negative real value.
Let’s call this LP LPV MC for now. It is known that LPV MC is half-integral [GVY04, Gui08], i.e.
there is always an optimal solution in which every variable takes value 0, 1 or 1/2. This property
is used to develop an algorithm for Vertex Multiway Cut running in time O∗(2k) [CPPW13].
The optimal value of relaxation version serves as a lower bound for the integer version. Recall
that given an instance I = (G, T, W, k) of Vertex Multicut Compression, any solution of the
instance is a multiway cut for W. Thus, if k < OPT(LPV MC) we can output no for the instance.
Starting from this observation, our first branching rule is to try to pick a vertex v ∈V (G)\W such
that if we force dv = 0, OPT(LPV MC) increases by at least 1/2. Such a vertex is called a non-zero
vertex.
If there is a non-zero vertex v, then we can branch into two cases: the optimal solution for
I contains v or not. If it contains v, we branch into the instance (G −v, T −v, W −v, k −1),
where T −v means deleting every pair containing v from T; if it does not contain v, we branch
into the instance where v is forced not to be selected (can be encoded using graph torso). In the
first branch, k decreases by 1 and in the second branch, OPT(LPV MC) increases by 1/2. Thus,
2k −OPT(LPV MC) always decreases by at least 1/2. Indeed, we use 2k −OPT(LPV MC) as our
measure.
In the case where there is no non-zero vertex, the above branching rule does not apply. Here we
introduce the notion of isolating min separators. Note that this notion and the related branching
ideas are also used in previous works [Xia10].
An isolating w-separator is a vertex set N(C) for a vertex set C that contains w and C ∪N(C)

Basics
Graphs considered in this paper are undirected by default, without self-loops and parallel edges.
A graph is denoted by G = (V, E), where V = V (G) is its set of vertices and E = E(G) is its
multiset of edges. We use n to denote the number of vertices in G when G is clear in the context.
An edge between u and v is written as an unordered pair “(u, v)” and is also considered as a set of
two vertices, so u ∈(u, v) denotes u is an endpoint of edge (u, v). Let S ⊆V (G) be a vertex set.
We denote by G −S the resulting graph after removing all vertices in S and all edges incident to
vertices in S. For a set of vertex pairs T, we denote by T−S the subset of T obtained by removing
all vertex pairs containing vertices in S. Similarly, for an edge set X ⊆E(G) we denote by G −X
the resulting graph after removing all edges in X.

E}. The closed neighborhood is then defined as NG[S] = NG(S) ∪S. We denote the set of edges
going out of S as ∂G(S) = {(u, v) | u ∈S, v /∈S, (u, v) ∈E}. If the set S is a singleton, i.e.
S = {v}, we may simplify the notation to NG(v), NG[v], and ∂G(v). We often drop the subscript

In this paper, we use [l, r] to denote the set of integers {l, l+1, l+2, ..., r−1, r}, and let [r] = [1, r].

2.2
Separators
Let G be an undirected graph and S, T ⊆V (G) be disjoint vertex sets. An S −T separator is
vertex set X ⊆V (G) \ (S ∪T) such that there are no paths from S to T in graph G −X.
Let X be any minimal S −T separator. Let C be the set of all vertices which are connected to
S in G −X. Since N(C) are not connected to S in G −X, we have N(C) ⊆X. By minimality,
we have X = N(C). Thus, we also use N(C) to denote a minimal separator. When this notation
is used, C implicitly refers to the set of vertices connected to S in G −X.
Let X = N(C) be a minimum S −T separator, which is thus also a minimal one. We say X is
farthest with respect to S if for all C′ ⊃C, it holds that |N(C)| < |N(C′)|. The following lemma
states the uniqueness of the farthest minimum separator, which can be found in Chapter 8 in the
textbook of parameterized algorithms [CFK+15].
Lemma 1. If there exists an S −T separator, then there exists a farthest S −T minimum separator
which is unique and can be found in nO(1) time.
Based on this lemma, a theory about “important separators” is established [CFK+15].
Definition 1 (Important Separator). Let G = (V, E) be a graph. Fix source S ⊆V and sink
T ⊆V where S ∩T = ∅. A minimal S −T separator N(C) is an important separator if, for any
minimal S −T separator N(C′) s.t. C′ ⊃C, it holds that |N(C′)| > |N(C)|.
It’s easy to see the farthest minimum separator is an important separator. Moreover, we have
the following lemma:
Lemma 2. Let N(C∗) be the farthest minimum S −T separator. Then for any S −T important
separator N(C), it holds that C∗⊆C.
Important separators remain important, even if we expand the source set.
Lemma 3. Let N(C) be an important S −T separator. Then for any S′ such that S ⊆S′ ⊆C,
N(C) is also an important S′ −T separator.
Proof. Observe that N(C) is a minimal S′ −T separator, because any S′ −T separator is also an

2.3
Multicut and Multiway Cut
The concepts of vertex multicut and multiway cut are central in this paper. These are defined as
follows.
Definition 2 (Vertex Multicut). Given a graph G = (V, E) and a set of vertex pairs T ⊆V × V .
A set X ⊆V of vertices is a vertex multicut of (G, T) if every pair (s, t) ∈T are not connected in
G −X.
Based on the above definition, the goal of Vertex Multicutcan also be stated as finding a
vertex multicut of size at most k for (G, T) from the input.
Definition 3 (Vertex Multiway Cut). Given a graph G = (V, E) and a set of vertices W ⊆V . A
set X ⊆V \ W of vertices disjoint from W is a vertex multiway cut of (G, W) if every component
of G −X contains at most one vertex in W.
Throughout this paper, we do not distinguish the terms “separator” and “cut”. We are always
considering vertex separators (cuts).

Shadow Removal
Given two vertex subsets Y and W, the shadow of Y with respect to W is defined as vertices
not reachable from W after removing Y from the graph.
In many connectivity related prob-
lems [CCHM15,CHM13,MR14], it has been shown that finding a shadowless solution, i.e., a solu-
tion that results in an empty shadow, is often much easier. The shadow removal technique aims to
eliminate the shadow of the original solution with respect to some W. In this section, we explore
how to improve the running time of the general shadow removal technique, and we then show how
to apply shadow removal on Vertex Multicut Compression to prove Theorem 3.
4.1
Shadow Removal
Definition 5 (Shadow). Let G = (V, E) be a graph. Let W ⊆V and Y ⊆V be vertex sets such
that W ∩Y = ∅. The shadow of Y with respect to W is defined as the set of vertices that are not
connected to W in the graph G −Y , that is,
RG,W (Y ) = {v ∈V \ W | v and W are not connected in G −Y }.
When the graph G is clear from the context, we simply write RW (Y ).
The Shadow removal technique is applicable for the “closest” solutions. This means the solution
Y is pushed as close as possible to W. The concept of “closest” is formalized as k-shadow-removable
in Definition 6.
Definition 6 (k-shadow-removable Set). Let G = (V, E) be a graph. Let W ⊆V and Y ⊆V be
two disjoint vertex sets. We say that Y is k-shadow-removable with respect to W if
• |Y | ≤k;
• for each vertex v ∈RW (Y ), there is an important {v} −W separator N(C) of size at most k
such that N(C) ⊆Y ;
• for each vertex v ∈Y , any important {v} −W separator N(C) of size at most k is not
completely contained in Y , i.e. |N(C) \ Y | ≥1.
Generally speaking, the shadow-removal technique generates a family Z of vertex sets indepen-
dently of the unknown solution Y , with the guarantee that there exists at least one set Z ∈Z
that covers the shadow of Y while remaining disjoint from Y . For each Z ∈Z, we construct a
new graph in which the vertices in Z are not present, while the connectivity among the remaining
vertices is preserved. Since there always exists some Z ∈Z that covers the shadow of the solution,
the problem reduces to finding a shadowless solution in the resulting graphs. In the vertex-based
setting, the family Z can be handled analogously by applying the so-called torso operation.

first prove the randomized version in Theorem 6, and the derandomized version in Theorem 7 is
obtained by standard tools.

Conclusion and Discussion
In this work, we presented a kO(k)nO(1)-time algorithm for the Vertex Multicut problem, im-
proving upon the previous best kO(k2)nO(1)-time result by Chitnis et al. [CCHM15]. Our main
technical contribution is a refined shadow removal procedure. Together with a deep analysis of the
reduction to bipedal instances, this yields a faster algorithm while preserving the overall framework
of Marx and Razgon [MR14]. Our refined shadow removal procedure also implies a kO(k2)nO(1) time
35


## Page 36


algorithm for Directed Subset Feedback Vertex Set and a kO(k)nO(1) time algorithm for
Directed Multiway Cut, improving over the previously best known algorithms of Chitnis et al.
[CCHM15]. We believe that the refined shadow removal technique and other techniques developed
in this paper can be applied to other parameterized cut and separation problems, particularly those
that can be expressed in terms of bipedal compression or shadowless solutions.
An interesting direction for future work is to explore the possibility of obtaining single-exponential
parameterized algorithms for Edge Multicut, that is, algorithms running in time 2O(k)nO(1). An-
other problem is to improve over the bound in the shadow removal theorem, or to prove a lower
bound.
