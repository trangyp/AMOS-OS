Abstract
The application of generative artificial intelligence in Creativity
Support Tools (CSTs) presents the challenge of interfacing two
black boxes: the user’s mind and the machine engine. According to
Artificial Cognition, this challenge involves theories, methods, and
constructs developed to study human creativity. Consistently, the
paper investigated the relationship between semantic networks or-
ganisation and idea originality in Large Language Models. Data was
collected by administering a set of standardised tests to ChatGPT-4o
and 81 psychology students, divided into higher and lower creative
individuals. The expected relationship was confirmed in the compar-
ison between ChatGPT-4o and higher creative humans. However,
despite having a more rigid network, ChatGPT-4o emerged as more
original than lower creative humans. We attributed this difference
to human motivational processes and model hyperparameters, ad-
vancing a research agenda for the study of artificial creativity. In
conclusion, we illustrate the potential of this construct for designing
and evaluating CSTs.

Introduction
Creativity is a complex aspect of human cognition with wide-
ranging implications for individual, societal, and technological
progress [4, 39]. The standard psychological definition describes cre-
ativity as a multidimensional construct [107] based on the ability to
produce original and effective products [99]. Despite the extensive
application of this definition in many disciplines, Human-Computer
Interaction (HCI) research has long lacked conceptual clarity in
defining what creativity means [52]. Furthermore, the application of
generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) to creative tasks has added
further terminological complexity. As a result, the word creativity is
now used indifferently to denote human and machine performance,
as well as their interaction [24, 56, 90, 97]. However, psychology

The question of whether GenAI can be creative has stimulated a
stream of research comparing the performance of machines and hu-
mans using psychological tests [58]. The results are contradictory,
with some studies showing better machine performance [45, 64, 68],
equal performance [65], or better human performance [11, 108].
Despite their differences, this research shares an implicit product-
centred definition of creativity [99]. GenAI is considered creative
because it generates outcomes that would be defined creative if pro-
duced by humans [97, 98]. Inevitably, this definition is bound to have
a limited impact on the design and development of rapidly evolving
obscure models and algorithms. The product-centred definition ac-
knowledges the occurrence of creative outputs but cannot explain
how they were generated. The question is no longer whether GenAI
can be creative or which model generates more creative outputs
[97]. The pertinent question is: what are the mechanisms that could
lead to artificial creativity? Only this answer will provide designers
of Creativity Support Tools, CSTs [52, 103, 104], the control they
need to infrastructure effective and original interactions, which by
definition are creative solutions to complex interfaces.
The distinction between human and artificial creativity [97, 98]
is relevant to the design of CSTs [52, 103, 104]. The ultimate chal-
lenge is how to interface two different types of black-boxes: the
human mind and nondeterministic systems. In this fussy context,
the outcome of the interaction is difficult to predict [114], and
therefore potentially creative. Both human and artificial agents pro-
vide unique requirements for creAItive collaboration [10], cyber-
creativity [36, 38], or entangled authorship [20, 21]. Such complex-
ity requires psychological knowledge [52, 53] because: “Compared
to nearly seven decades of pathbreaking contributions from psycho-
logical creativity research, it is evident that HCI-oriented creativity
research does not have an equally strong research tradition” [52,
p.3]. To overcome this gap, the current paper investigates artificial
creativity by applying theories and methods of creative cognition
[17]. Accordingly, creativity is the process that emerges from the
combination of basic neurocognitive mechanisms (memory, atten-
tion, and control) in divergent and convergent thinking. The paper
focuses on the association between semantic memory network
(the representation of the organisation of the conceptual units
in memory) and idea originality in a divergent task. Psychology
has demonstrated that more flexible networks are associated with
higher originality [70, 71]. Therefore, the specific research question
addressed in this paper is:
RQ What is the relationship between semantic networks and
idea originality in ChatGPT-4o?
To answer it, we conducted an experiment comparing human
and artificial originality. The machine data was collected by using
the chat interface of GPT-4o, which reproduced a complete black
box example, as no information on model hyperparameters was
available. The human sample included 81 psychology students di-
vided into higher and lower creative individuals. Results confirmed
the expected association between semantic networks and original-
ity only with respect to higher creative individuals. Consistently,
ChatGPT-4o was less original than higher creative humans, who
also displayed a more flexible semantic memory network. However,
the expected association did not manifest in the comparison with
lower creative individual. Despite having a more rigid network,
ChatGPT-4o emerged as more original than them.
The contribution of this paper to the CHI community is empiri-
cal, operational, and methodological. To the best of our knowledge,
the paper provides the first empirical evidence of an association
between semantic networks (an indication of the creative process)
and idea originality (an indication of the creative product). The
relationship mirrored human creativity only with regard to the
comparison with higher creative individuals. This unexpected out-
come inspired new fascinating research directions for the study of
artificial creativity in HCI. In short, the paper has the following
organisation. Section 2 presents the related work about creativity in
HCI and psychology, with the aim to seed a ground for our research
agenda. Section 3 presents the method of the study advancing best
psychological practices. Section 4 reports the results and Section
5 discusses their relevance and limitations as directions for future
work. Section 6 advances the definition of artificial creativity as a
relevant construct for the design of CSTs, discusses contributions,
and provides two operational insights (OPI) and three methodolog-
ical recommendations (MER) for a systematic research agenda on
artificial creativity. Finally, Section 7 concludes the manuscript with
a call for further research on artificial creativity.
2
Related Work
According to Rhodes [96], the study of creativity can be differenti-
ated in four areas: person (personality traits, abilities, attitudes, and
self-concepts), product (originality and effectiveness), process (cogni-
tive and neurophysiological mechanisms), and press (environmental
context). Following this categorisation, we identify the specific re-
mit of HCI in the press and review research on the application of
GenAI in artistic practices as a paramount example [26, 28, 57].
2.1
HCI and Creativity
The interest in CSTs dates back in time [103, 104], and extends
across a wide range of practices [27, 52, 53, 95] recently challenged
by the widespread adoption of AI [24, 29, 34, 82]. On the one hand,
GenAI provides immediate feedback and hints, thus offering users
the possibility to explore new ideas [34, 93]. On the other hand,
designers must be aware that these systems may distract the cre-
ative process [109]. Negative outcomes on the long run include
over-reliance and homogenisation [7, 10, 24, 50, 75]. In artistic do-
mains, HCI researchers and designers should take into account that
GenAI may eventually lead to a reduction of variability and diver-
sity in divergent thinking [7, 50, 75], and induce a form of Creative
Mortification [15] where people perceive their own work as less
valuable than those generated by AI. Additional concerns relate
to ownership, plagiarism, and originality [30, 91, 114]. Unresolved
constraints have emerged in the context of creative writing due to

2.2
Creativity as a Cognitive Process
To move beyond product-oriented research [99], we follow the
definition of creativity as the dynamic interplay between inconclu-
siveness (when originality or effectiveness has not yet been reached)
and achievement (the fulfilment of both originality and effective-
ness). In other words, creativity requires “potential” originality and
effectiveness [35]. The term potential allows the shift from a fo-
cus on the product to a focus on the process. It acknowledges the
existence of countless instances in which the value of a product
was not initially understood but was rediscovered and recognised
later, as well as cases in which the outcome itself is not primarily
important [36].
As illustrated in Figure 2, the creative process emerges from basic
neurocognitive abilities: memory, attention, and cognitive control
[17]. They converge into two generative modalities: convergent
and divergent thinking [63]. Convergent thinking considers all
available information to assemble it in the most compact form [54]
and possibly arrive at a single result through integration [78]. In
contrast, divergent thinking is an exploratory cognitive mode that
aims to generate multiple solutions or ideas for a problem or task.
The current paper investigates the relationship between semantic
memory network as a basic mechanism of the creative process and
idea originality as an indicator of divergent creative outcomes.
2.3
Divergent Creativity
Previous works have demonstrated how the divergent creative po-
tential of LLMs is improving over time [113]. Furthermore, research
suggests that LLMs reflect typical human patterns in idea genera-
tion, such as the serial order effect, whereby later responses tend
to be more novel than earlier ones [13, 58, 108]. Other authors
have suggested that it is possible to enhance LLM creativity by
incrementally prompting the model more forcefully to generate
original responses across interactions [59]. Most of these studies
investigated artificial creativity using experimental divergent tests.
As a reliable indicator of real world achievements [69, 100], di-
vergent thinking can be assessed through a set of tasks, including
verbal and visual stimuli [TTCT; 111], ambiguous figures [FIQ; 61]

this methodology, we posit that HCH will outperform ChatGPT-4o

2.4
Semantic Memory
Memory plays a crucial role in human creativity [16, 17]. However,
the study of the memory-creativity link is complex as there are
different types of memory (working, semantic, episodic, short/long-
term) and different modalities of creative thinking (e.g., divergent
and convergent). According to the MemiC framework, the role of
memory in the creative ideation stages extends from the search for
memory and the construction of candidate ideas to the evaluation
of novelty and effectiveness [16]. In particular, the role of semantic
memory in creative thinking has recently been explored. Recent
research has highlighted how semantic memory structures and
the ability to access these structures are fundamentally related to
creative performance across the lifespan [25, 40–42].
The structure of semantic memory can be represented as a net-
work and analysed based on mathematical graph theory [105]. The
network consists of nodes (the basic unit of the system) and edges
(the semantic associations between two concepts). Data to construct
these networks is generally collected by verbal production tasks
[70, 77]. They require participants to generate as many words as
possible belonging to a given semantic category or related to a
target word, within a fixed amount of time. In the resulting net-
work, each node represents a category exemplar (e.g., tiger), and
the edges represent associations between two exemplars. In other
words, these associations constitute the tendency to generate an
exemplar b (lion) when a (tiger) is produced.
A large body of psychological research has shown how the se-
mantic network of higher creative individuals differs from that of
lower creative individuals [18, 66, 70, 71, 86]. As summarised in
Table 2, the former is characterised by (a) lower distance to traverse
from one node to another, ASPL; (b) higher connectivity between
conceptual nodes, CC; and (c) fewer sub-communities within the
network, Q [70, 71]. Moreover, higher creative individuals appear
to have a more resilient and flexible network, which is more resis-
tant to targeted “attacks” than lower creative individuals, R [72].

Despite semantic network flexibility being a reliable indicator of
human creative performance, it remains unclear how such struc-
tures are represented in LLMs [46, 74]. Moreover, much uncertainty
exists about the association between network organisation and idea
originality in artificial creativity. To the best of our knowledge, only
one published study has investigated the difference in semantic net-
work structures between humans and different LLMs [115]. Human
data was obtained from a random selection of 30 participants in the
animal category of the semantic fluency database reported in [116].
LLM data was extracted by instructing the LLMs to play specific
roles related to 30 common occupations (e.g., pretend that you are
a chef) before performing the task. This procedure was intended
to enrich the diversity of the machine responses [101]. The results
showed that the semantic network of LLMs has higher ASPL, lower
CC, and higher Q compared to humans [115]. Despite creativity
not being a variable of the study, the authors concluded that “LLMs
have poorer creativity and flexibility compared to humans” [115,
p. 6].
The current study was designed to explicitly study the associ-
ation between semantic networks and originality in artificial vs.
human creativity. Following Artificial Cognition [110] and expand-
ing the study of Wang et al. [115], we expected GPT to have sig-
nificantly different semantic networks than HCH and LCH, given
the expected direction of idea originality stated in H1.1. We tested
this hypothesis with structural and percolation analysis using the
measures described in Table 2. Consistently, we hypothesise that
GPT would display a more flexible network compared to LCH and
the reverse compared to HCH (H1.2).
3
Method
The study was designed to test the hypothesis that the semantic
network flexibility of an LLM provides an indication of its idea
originality (H1). Instantiating the principle of Artificial Cognition
[110] with research on human creativity [17, 115], we refined it
into two sub-hypotheses, both of which were expected to be true.
• H1.1 — Idea Originality: HCH > GPT > LCH
• H1.2 — Network Flexibility: HCH > GPT > LCH
3.1
Participants and Design
Human responses were obtained from the dataset collected by
Campidelli et al. [25]. The sample analysed in the present study
included 81 psychology students (mean age = 20.3 years, SD = 1.71,
men = 12, women = 69). Machine data was obtained on the 6th and
7th of March 2025 by using ChatGPT-4o chat interface1 with the
“Plus” subscription package [113]. This interface neither displayed
nor allowed for the modification of the values of the hyperparame-
ters. Based on the mean originality scores of each human partici-
pant, the human sample was median split into (a) LCH and (b) HCH.
Accordingly, the experiment compared the networks and creative
performances of three groups: GPT, LCH, and HCH.
3.2
Procedure
The experiment with human participants took place in a room free
of acoustic and visual distractors and was conducted by an expert
researcher. After signing informed consent, they sat in front of
an empty desk and were asked to complete two different verbal
production tasks and the AUT. The target words were printed
on a sheet of paper, which remained on the desk for the entire
duration of the tasks. The responses were recorded with an audio
recorder and manually transcribed by one of the experimenters. The
procedure was repeated by creating a new separate chat for each of
the 81 ChatGPT-4o participants (Appendix A) [113]. All human and
machine responses were merged, anonymised, sorted in alphabetic
order within each object, and finally scored for originality by two
expert coders, blind to the experimental goals and the origin of the
data, as recommended by Silvia et al. [106]. The interrater reliability
was high, ICC = .93.
3.3
Experimental Tasks
Appendix A presents the instructions given to humans and prompts
received by ChatGPT-4o across three tasks. The Verbal Fluency
Test and the Free Association Task were used to construct semantic
networks. Finally, the AUT was used to estimate originality. The
Verbal Fluency Test required participants to produce as many words
as possible belonging to the “animals” and “fruit and vegetables”
categories within one minute for each target [60]. The Free Associ-
ation Task required participants to verbally produce as many words

3.4
Semantic Networks
The network estimation followed the procedure and pipelines pro-
vided by Christensen et al. [31]. The entire verbal production data-
base was translated from Italian to English and pre-processed with
the R packages SemNetDictionaries and SemNetCleaner. The cleaned
data was entered into a binary response matrix with participants
in rows and words in columns. Each cell was filled with 1 if the
participant had produced the word and 0 if they had not. The net-
works were estimated using the SemNet package and applying the
correlation-based method [31]. The cosine similarity function was
employed to estimate the edges between nodes and compute the
association profiles of the responses. To control for confounding
factors (e.g., different numbers of nodes or edges among groups),
the matrices only included responses that were provided by at
least two participants in each group [see, for example, 22, 42]. Fur-
thermore, in line with the literature on network construction [31–
33, 42, 77, 80, 115], the Triangulated Maximally Filtered Graph
(TMFG) was adopted to minimise noise and spurious association
by creating different networks with the same number of nodes and
edges [31, 32, 80]. This method aims to maximise the strength of
node associations while maintaining the extant network planar
to be depicted graphically [32]. TMFG represents the only consol-
idate alternative to the Planar Maximally Filtered Graph, PMFG
[73, 80, 112]. Generally, the two methods align with one another
[31], but TMFG is more efficient than PMFG [80]. The resulting
networks of GPT, LCH, and HCH were compared using structural
and percolation analyses.

Extending Artificial Cognition [110] with research on creative cog-

analysed constructs (Table 6).

ChatGPT-4o was less original than higher creative humans but
more original than lower creative humans (Table 6). The effect
emerged consistently, considering both the total production and
the originality peaks. Differently, the results of the structural and
percolation analyses only partially confirmed H1.2 related to net-
work flexibility. Consistent with previous studies [70, 71], higher
creative humans were characterised by a more organised, flexible,

To summarise, ChatGPT-4o semantic network was characterised

This configuration indicates higher internal rigidity and less cohe-

different sub-communities [70, 115]. It suggests an increased diffi-
culty in “navigating” the semantic structures to reach remote con-
cepts and to create original associations between them [14, 16]. This
conclusion was confirmed by the percolation analysis. It showed
that the semantic network of ChatGPT-4o broke apart faster as its
intensity thresholds increased.
5.1.2
Association between Idea Originality and Network Flexibility.
Our main hypothesis (H1) stated that the semantic network flex-
ibility provides an indication of idea originality in LLMs. Results
revealed that higher creative humans consistently outperformed
ChatGPT-4o in terms of originality. This result supports psycholog-
ical literature suggesting that highly creative individuals, who are
characterised by a less spread out, less segregated, more flexible
and robust semantic memory network, exceed AI in creative tasks
[46, 74]. However, despite a more rigid and segregated network,
ChatGPT-4o consistently exceeded lower creative humans in idea
originality (Table 6).
These results seem to suggest independence between semantic
network structures and creative performance in machines. Yet,
they may be better understood in light of the Entropy Modulation
Theory of Creative Exploration [67]. The authors of this theory
suggest that even when the semantic memory structure of groups of
individuals is equivalent, higher creative ones navigate the network
faster by increasing activation variance or lowering production
threshold. While in humans this capacity reflects executive control,
in ChatGPT-4o it may be mimicked by different hyperparameters
of the model, such as temperature, top_p, max tokens, frequency
and presence penalty.
Temperature, for example, defines the level of randomness of
text generation, with lower values producing more deterministic
responses, and higher values increasing unpredictability [47, 88].
However, preliminary results suggest a weak effect of tempera-
ture on creative outputs, despite it being described as the creativity
parameter in the literature [88]. The effect of the other hyperparam-
eters remains largely unexplored and much uncertainty still exists
[47]. In this study, we tested ChatGPT-4o using its chat interface.
Accordingly, although its network was more rigid, ChatGPT-4o
may have emerged as more original than lower creative humans
because this model configuration allowed the generation of original
alternative uses without sacrificing coherence with respect to the
AUT prompt.
Another explanation for these inconsistencies could be related
to the impact of affective and motivational processes on human
creative performance [2, 5, 6, 37, 49, 74, 79]. While we might as-
sume that ChatGPT-4o consistently performed the task at the best
of its incredibly fast computational capabilities [74], lower creative
humans might have experienced a higher cognitive effort. Further-
more, they were likely more affected by scarce motivation to do
the task, low self-efficacy, external pressure of being evaluated,
time constraints, or cognitive fatigue than higher creative humans.
Consequently, even with a less flexible network, ChatGPT-4o per-
formed the test consistently at its best capacity and generated more
original responses than participants with lower creative abilities.
Finally, these inconsistent results could be due to the impact of the
ChatGPT-4o speed in processing large data structures [74]. Even
if its network was not flexible, its rapidity in navigating complex
information structures might have led to the retrieval of remote as-
sociations at a pace that compensated for its structural limitations.
5.2
Limitations and Future Directions
This paper provides a first step in linking semantic networks and
artificial creativity. The novelty of the approach encompasses a
number of limitations, which limit the generalisability of our results.
These pertain to the data sample, the study procedure, and the
complexity of the creativity construct.
5.2.1
Data Sample. A main limitation concerns the type of human
sample and the reliance on only ChatGPT-4o. The human partici-
pants were psychology students, who may have been more intrin-
sically motivated to perform the tasks because of their academic
background as compared to other individuals. Since motivation is a
key dimension of the creative process [5, 6, 37, 49, 74, 79], it is pos-
sible that different participants would have led to different results.
Furthermore, we only tested ChatGPT-4o using its chat interface
[113]. This choice tested an extreme black-box that improved eco-
logical validity as it represents the typical interface employed by
most users. However, the chat interface does not allow to know and
modify the values of the model hyperparameters. Future research
is needed to compare different models while manipulating hyper-
parameters, in order to investigate their effects on the correlation
pattern between semantic network flexibility and originality.
5.2.2
Study Procedure. Following a strict application of Artifi-
cial Cognition [110] and consistent with [45, 61, 64, 65, 113], we
prompted ChatGPT-4o with the same instructions given to human
participants. Yet, research suggests that prompting plays a fun-
damental role in the potential creativity of GenAI [84]. There is
evidence that LLMs can be incrementally enforced to generate orig-
inal responses across interactions [59] and creative outcomes are
more likely to occur during roleplay [62, 101]. Furthermore, both
humans and ChatGPT-4o were given the same time constraints (Ta-
ble 7), but we controlled it only for the human sample. Although it

has been suggested that ChatGPT may reflect the serial order effect
typical of human creativity [13, 58, 108], no rigorous investigation
has yet examined how time limitation affects both machine and
human performance. Altogether, these considerations highlight the
urgency of research on how prompting affects the production of
artificial creativity.
5.2.3
Construct Complexity. Following previous research, we in-
vestigated divergent thinking through the AUT focusing on orig-
inality [10, 11, 65, 74, 108]. AUT is “regularly used in work on AI
creativity” [10, p. 2] (Table 1). Nevertheless, divergent thinking rep-
resents only one aspect of the multi-componential construct that
is creativity [35, 61], and originality is only one requirement of a
creative idea [97]. Due to its popularity in research, AUT responses
may have been included in the training databases of the machine
[58, 65]. However, as Haase & Hanel [65] noted, this possibility
does not imply that these words would appear in the generated
output. Future research should extend to more ecological methods
to assess creativity, including real world activities, and non-verbal,
visual, or figurative performance. Additional creativity measures
(i.e., flexibility, elaboration) should also be considered to compre-
hensively investigate both human and artificial creativity. Finally,
future studies should clarify the the directionality of the association
between network flexibility and creativity. Altogether, these limita-
tions provide directions for future research on artificial creativity.
6
Artificial Creativity
Given all the limitations acknowledged in the previous section,
we now reflect on the relevance of artificial creativity [97, 98] for
CSTs design and evaluation [52, 103]. Following [97, 98], artificial
creativity refers to the potential of a machine to provide output that
if generated by humans would be defined as creative. Building on
this definition, we provide two operational insights (OPI) and three
methodological recommendations (MER) for a systematic research
agenda on artificial creativity in HCI.
OPI1 Artificial Creativity is Average Performance: The mean origi-
nality scores of ChatGPT-4o on the AUT reflected the aver-
age human performance [47], but cut out lower and higher
creative individuals. Therefore, the machine outcome was
good enough, but it could not achieve excellence. This re-
sult supports and explains previous qualitative research
showing how at first encounter artists tended to perceive
artificial creativity as inadequate, constrained, or even con-
straining [28, 44, 48, 57, 62]. In particular, they were con-
cerned about a risk of homogenisation which threatened
their professional identity and personal style. It was feared
that GenAI could make creativity less authentic [8] and
elaborated [10], fostering convergence towards similar av-
erage results [7, 24, 50, 75]. On the other hand, this average
definition suggests that GenAI could be beneficial, specifi-
cally for less creative individuals, whose output becomes
more novel, enjoyable, and useful when supported by AI
[50]. Therefore, it is of primary importance to consider the
user experience and skills when introducing users to arti-
ficial creativity. Regardless of their background, all users
require new interfaces to bridge human and artificial cre-
ativity. Therefore, designers of human-AI systems should
consider the average nature of artificial creativity when
designing interfaces connecting human and artificial cre-
ativity to ensure that the former benefits from rather than
being hindered by the interaction [38, 75].
OPI2 Artificial Creativity is a Process, not only a Product: Artifi-
cial creativity expands and connects HCI and psychological
research by advancing a dynamic definition of artificial
creativity [35]. Not only does this definition address the
question of whether AI can be creative, but it also attempts
to understand the processes embedded in AI mechanisms.
The current paper provides preliminary evidence of a par-
tial association between idea originality (as an indication of
the product) and semantic networks (as an indication of the
process) in ChatGPT-4o. The results confirmed the expecta-
tion derived by human cognition [70, 71] of a relationship
between originality and network flexibility only with re-
spect to the comparison with higher creative individuals.
Therefore, the potentiality of generating original ideas may
not be strictly associated with the semantic structure, but
certain hyperparameters may partly compensate for the
rigidity of the network and enable the production of original
responses. This papers offers HCI researchers a structured
methodology for systematically addressing artificial cre-
ativity. Future research should also focus on supporting
different stages of the creative process [27, 36, 98], and how
their use affects users’ performance in subsequent tasks
with and without them [10, 75].
MER1 Human Creativity Provides Methodological Scaffolding: Re-
search on Artificial Cognition draws parallels between the
AI black-box and the human mind — the original black box
[110]. In sharp contrast with the influential Media Equa-
tion Theory [94], artificial creativity does not assume a
similarity between machine and human behaviour. On the
contrary, this paper provides evidence of differences in idea
originality and its association with semantic memory struc-
ture between human and artificial creativity. The evidence
enriches related work describing higher level psychological
differences in drivers, experiences and norms [1, 97, 98].
The framework of creative cognition [17] provides concepts,
methods, and theories to approach the study of artificial cre-
ativity. From a design perspective, it addresses the interface
at a micro-level, directly connecting machine hyperparam-
eters and human executive functions. In this paper we have
analysed semantic memory, future research should address
attention and control (Figure 2), as relevant dimensions of
both human and artificial creativity.
MER2 Collecting Reliable Human Baselines: The human sample
used as a baseline is of critical importance for the study of
artificial creativity. From a cognitive perspective, indeed,
creativity emerges as the expression of personal abilities,
which vary across individuals and along the lifespan [97].
Furthermore, the natural black box is affected by hidden cog-
nitive functions, motivations, and experiences, all of which
can affect creative performance. Consequently, an average
representation of creativity only applies to the machine,
but it fails to explain the richness of the human counterpart
(Figures 1 and 3). It makes limited sense to talk about the

average creativity of humankind. Consequently, it needs to
be better characterised taking into account possibilities and
limitations. The median split applied in this study offers a
simple way to obtain a finer-grained operationalisation of
creativity for future research. Other individual and contex-
tual factors also need careful consideration. They relate to
participants’ recruitment and experimental design.
MER3 Artificial Creativity Provides Metaphors for Design: Over
20 years ago, Shneiderman [103] suggested that “Graphics
composition tools show the enormous power of software
to enable more people to be more creative ” [103, p. 119].
Consistently, the metaphor of semantic network opens up
stimulating design trajectories for HCI research. We can
imagine new CSTs allowing different types of users (artists
and researchers) to visualise and manipulate the semantic
networks of the model. By modifying these networks as
creative interfaces, artists may explore new semantic spaces
which may lead to seed incidents at the core of their mas-
terpieces [48]. There is evidence indeed that experimental
artists engage in experiments with extensive data curation
[26], subverting conceptual spaces, forcing the machine to
fail and hallucinate [20, 21], or introducing deliberate fric-
tions in tangible interactions [43]. Experimental artists may
value the tool as a medium for transformational practices
[20, 21, 26]. Scientists, on the other hand, may exploit the
interactive visualisation of semantic networks to support a
systematic program of research on artificial creativity. They
could manipulate prompts, hyperparameters, and tasks to
understand their effect on the system output. This research
requires different methods and sensibilities when testing
humans or machines. Current research has paid extensive
attention to the prompt provided to the machine [59, 84].
This paper instead highlighted methodological considera-
tions for user research.
7
Conclusion
To conclude, we go back to the question posed in the introduc-
tion about the the mechanisms that could lead to artificial creativity.
Extending the framework of Artificial Cognition [110] to account
for creativity [17], this paper provides initial evidence of the role
of semantic networks on idea originality in LLMs using the the
Alternate Uses Task. We demonstrated that this role differed ac-
cording to the human sample used for the comparison. Higher
creative individuals were more original than ChatGPT-4o, which,
despite its rigidity, emerged as more original than lower creative
individuals. Taken together, the evidence extends previous studies
comparing human and artificial creativity by providing operational
and methodological advances. Finally, we provide methodological
guidance for a more rigorous investigation of artificial creativity in
the field of HCI.
