This paper elaborates on the implications of the relationship between the Second and Third Laws
and provides a comprehensive formal and historical justification for the logical redundancy of the
Nernst heat theorem. By revisiting the Nernst-Einstein debate, the underlying hypotheses that lead
to the traditional view of the Third Law as an independent postulate are examined. It is argued that
the historical rejection of Nernst’s proof—motivated by Einstein’s insistence on the practical non-
performability of cycles at absolute zero—overlooks the fact that a universal Second Law already
precludes such cycles, rendering an independent Third Law an unnecessary complexity. Ultimately,
the Nernst theorem is shown to be an essential consistency regulator rather than an independent
physical discovery.

the idea that a Carnot engine must be able to operate
at T = 0, if only to measure T = 0.[42] This debate
concerns the very limits of thermodynamic reasoning; for
a comprehensive review of the historical stakes, see Kox
[23].
The purpose of this work is to clarify the underlying
hypotheses that render the Second Law an “incomplete”
principle—one that ostensibly requires an independent
Third Law to provide an evolution criterion across the
full temperature domain—and to identify the alternative
hypotheses that allow the Second Law to remain self-
sufficient. The following analysis considers only thermo-
dynamic arguments, excluding any microscopic descrip-
tion in relation to the limit T = 0. By focusing on the
formal architecture of the theory, we show that the Third
Law is a requirement for logical consistency.

the perspectives of physics and epistemology, linking the

3.
WHY IS A THIRD LAW OF
THERMODYNAMICS NOT NEEDED?
Landsberg [7] contends that the crux of the contro-
versy lies in the status of boundary points (equilibrium
states at T = 0) relative to an open set of points (regular
equilibrium states at non-zero temperature). He notes:
“In mathematics the status of such points is often the
subject of a separate assumption. Nernst assumed that,
in the absence of information to the contrary, the points
could be regarded as belonging to the set. Einstein sug-
gested that they should not belong to the set. From the
axiomatic point of view, however, both views represent
an additional postulate.”
While this interpretation holds in certain abstract
mathematical contexts, the physical challenge is to vali-
date the consequences of the framework originating from
the Second Law and determine if these consequences
align with empirical evidence—particularly in the limit
as T →0. This section demonstrates the various ways in
which the Second Law implicitly predicts the Nernst heat
theorem, and the various ways in which the framework
originated by Einstein’s rebuttal fails to address founda-
tional issues.

3.1.
The continuity of Planck’s statement of the
Second Law at the boundary
A consistent physical theory requires that the conse-
quences of the Second Law do not merely apply to the
interior of the state space, but remain continuous and
well-behaved as the boundary of the state space is ap-
proached. This section is devoted to show that this idea
derives Nernst’s theorem from Planck’s statement

Carnot [46] that motive power is derived from the trans-

exchanges: (i) with a boiler, which is cooled (mandated
by energy conservation), and (ii) with a cooler, which
must be heated (mandated by Planck’s statement). This
constitutes the fundamental Carnot engine.

3.2.
The ontological consistency of the Second Law
Notwithstanding the derivation in Section 3.1, this sec-
tion shows an alternative line of reasoning that directly
links Planck’s statement to the Nernst heat theorem.
In the classical rebuttal of Nernst’s proof, much is
made of the “point of logic” connecting Nernst’s con-
tradiction to the hypothetical experiments that would be
feasible if the behavior in Figure 1B were possible.[39, 40]
However, these remarks may ultimately be irrelevant to
the core theoretical discussion.
Planck’s statement and the principle of energy conser-
vation constitute the axioms of a framework that yields
both the concept and metric of temperature (5), and
the concept and metric of entropy (6), see section 3.1.
This framework is built upon the postulate that Planck’s
statement formalizes a criterion of evolution for discrim-
inating whether a process is possible. This criterion is
typically expressed via the law of entropy increase and is
universally accepted as robust at non-zero temperatures.
At T = 0, Einstein’s rebuttal effectively maintains that
this criterion is no longer operative; he suggests that
Planck’s statement is not the arbiter for the ABCDA
cycle, but rather that practical, extrinsic limitations ren-
der the process impossible.
While the Third Law, as
an independent postulate, restores this criterion at the
boundary, one must ask: is this the most parsimonious
way to describe the laws of nature?
It is crucial to note that Planck’s statement did not in-
troduce a previously unknown property—it was already
well understood that heat cannot be fully converted into
work, just as it was known that heat does not flow spon-
taneously from cold to hot. Instead, Planck [5] elevated
a known physical observation to a first principle from
which all subsequent consequences are derived. As long
as these consequences do not contradict empirical evi-
dence, the statement prevails.
A commitment to Planck’s statement as a universal
criterion for discriminating whether a process is pos-
sible yields the Nernst theorem—for instance, through
Nernst’s original proof by contradiction—which aligns
perfectly with experimental evidence.

3.3.
Ideal adiabatic processes
Carath´eodory [6] reformulated thermodynamics by in-
troducing the concepts of adiabatic connectivity and ac-
cessibility, relating the former to the First Law and the
latter to the Second Law. He defined a simple system
and noted: “Quasi-static, adiabatic changes of state of
a simple system are ‘reversible’.” Regarding non-simple
systems, Carath´eodory added: “Our theory cannot be
readily applied to such systems, which is likely also the
case for classical thermodynamics.” Indeed, this limita-
tion is inherent to the classical framework, as reversible
Carnot cycles necessitate two adiabatic processes that
must themselves be reversible.
Temperature is conceptually alien to the reversibility
of adiabatic state changes. To perform such a process, an
experimentalist requires only a perfectly adiabatic enclo-
sure, a controlled mechanical variable (such as pressure
p), and a record of the system’s response via the con-
jugate mechanical parameter (such as volume V ). Re-
versibility is demonstrated when the conjugate parameter
is restored to its initial value after the controlled variable
is slowly varied and subsequently returned to its starting
point. Because the system is isolated, no thermometer
is required. Consequently, the characterization of quasi-
static adiabatic processes as reversible is, prima facie, a
valid assumption across the entire temperature domain.
Figure 1B describes a non-simple substance in this
context. If Einstein’s viewpoint is adopted, the process
B →C →E is quasi-static and adiabatic, yet inher-
ently non-reversible: at point E, if x is quasi-statically
and adiabatically restored to x1, the original equilibrium
state B would not be recovered.
Accepting this view requires the introduction of a re-
strictive auxiliary assumption: that a quasi-static, adia-
batic process is reversible only if T > 0. This is theo-
retically problematic because adiabatic reversibility can
be defined prior to the concept of temperature—that is,
before thermal contact between two systems is even es-
tablished.
Under Einstein’s logic, one must introduce
this restriction only to later formalize a third, indepen-
dent law whose sole purpose is to extend the assumption
of reversibility down to T = 0.
Instead, following the logic of Carath´eodory [6], we
may conclude that simple systems must, by definition,
obey the Nernst theorem.
Therefore, the persistent
observation that finite-density, homogeneous substances
abide by the theorem serves as empirical evidence that
they can be treated as simple systems down to T = 0.
3.4.
Ideal isotherms at T = 0
Much of the early 20th-century discourse regarding the
boundary of accessible states centered on the charac-
terization of an ideal (reversible) isothermal process at
T = 0. This discussion was largely dominated by the
paradoxes inherent in the behavior depicted in Figure 1B.
Nernst maintained that if, ex hypothesi, T = 0 is reached
via a process occurring in “finite dimensions” (i.e., non-
asymptotically), such as path BC in Figure 1B, then the
“compression” CD must be possible; at this limit, in-
ternal energy would coincide with free energy, the ther-
modynamic potential driving an ideal isotherm.
Con-
versely, Einstein and Epstein argued that any isotherm
at absolute zero must effectively be adiabatic, and that
a compression would inevitably displace the system from
T = 0, ideally returning it to state B. Interestingly, this
latter view relies heavily on empirical knowledge derived
from substances that already satisfy the Nernst theorem,
rather than those behaving like Figure 1B, which is em-
pirically unknown.
In a sense, both perspectives are simultaneously valid
and flawed.
The crux of the issue is that Figure 1B
presents a bifurcation at point C: the experiment “adi-
abatic compression from x2 →x1” offers two distinct
paths: CD (zero heat because T = 0, as preferred by
Nernst) and BC (zero heat because dS = 0, as preferred
by Epstein). Such a substance literally violates the no-
tion of “simple” system, see Section 3.3.
Beyond these historical “mind games,” ideal isotherms
are formally required to establish the metrics of both
temperature (5) and entropy (6). They are, therefore,
essential to the definition of the T = 0 axis itself. In this
context, the limit of the right-hand side of Equation (6)

Temperature is an intuitive property, the characteri-
zation of which laid the foundations of thermodynam-
ics.
While many classical texts introduce temperature
and thermal equilibrium as primitive concepts justified
by empiricism [4, 5, 7, 39, 49], temperature actually orig-
inates from the order relation established by the Sec-
ond Law.[50] Equation (5) characterizes the isomorphism
mapping physical bodies onto the set of real numbers
through reversible Carnot engines.† The ordering intro-
duced by Planck’s statement refers to the fact that the
two bodies in a heat engine cannot exchange roles with-
out violating the principle, much like the elements in a
less than mathematical relation.
A quantitative metric of temperature then requires a
universal zero. Historically, T = 0 was extrapolated from
gas properties. In the Nernst-Einstein debate, it was of-
ten associated with p = 0. However, within the formal-
ism of the Second Law, T = 0 is uniquely defined by a
reversible Carnot engine where Qc = 0.[42] This assign-
ment is universal and leads directly to the Nernst heat
theorem as demonstrated in (10). In short, if the T in
Nernst’s theorem is understood strictly as Carnot’s tem-
perature, then the theorem is proven by definition.
This assignment involves a limit process that is nec-
essarily consistent with Planck’s statement. Figure 1A
illustrates a generalized Carnot cycle abcda consisting of
two reversible isotherms (ab and cd) and two processes (bc
and da) differing only by a shift in entropy. Because the
heat exchanges in bc and da balance, the cycle remains
binary. In ab, the experimentalist determines a finite ∆S
taken from the boiler. In cd, the full domain of the me-
chanical parameter is utilized to reject that same ∆S at
the cooler’s temperature, measured by the ratio Qc/∆S.
As ∆S →0, the engine degenerates into a round trip with
unit efficiency but no net heat or work, preserving the
integrity of Planck’s statement.[51] In contrast, apply-
ing this to Figure 1B reveals the fundamental problem:
at T = 0, the cycle either negates Planck’s statement
or becomes ill-defined, leaving absolute zero without a
meaningful thermodynamic characterization.

temperature, yet the statement is valid irrespective of
temperature, as wisely noted by Planck [5, Chapter II,

to address the limit T = 0, the interpretation of Nernst’s
empirical observations follows this logic: they provide the
purest evidence that the Second Law holds at any tem-
perature, including the boundary T = 0. It can hardly
be otherwise if one acknowledges that temperature is not
an independent background variable, but a consequence
of the statement, and the nature of irreversibility.
In his autobiographical notes, Einstein [52] famously
remarked that “a theory is the more impressive the
greater the simplicity of its premises, the more differ-
ent kinds of things it relates, and the more extended its
area of applicability.” It is an irony that Einstein wrote
these words as a specific tribute to the universal content
of thermodynamics.
Yet, in his historical rejection of
Nernst’s proof, he advocated for a more restricted, less
universal Second Law—one that required a “patch” in
the form of a Third Law to maintain consistency at the
boundary. By maintaining the universality of Planck’s
statement irrespective of temperature, we do not merely
add a Third Law; we fulfill Einstein’s own desire for a
theory of universal applicability. This extension reduces
the complexity of the premises by removing the need for a
separate, independent postulate to govern the boundary.
The logical interdependence of these results is summa-
rized in Figure 2, which maps the axiomatic descent from
Planck’s statement to the boundary T →0, illustrating
also how the vanishing of the specific heats arises from a
pure thermodynamic logic.[34]
The history of science often conflates the order of dis-
covery with the order of logical necessity. One could eas-
ily imagine a counter-factual history in which a theorist,
working strictly from the axiomatic frameworks of clas-
sical thermodynamics, predicted the vanishing of specific
heats and the convergence of entropy as a requirement
for the consistency of the Second Law. In such a sce-
nario, Nernst’s empirical observations would have been
hailed as the brilliant experimental confirmation of an
existing theoretical prediction, rather than the discovery
of an independent law that only introduces an unneces-
sary complexity.
In conclusion, the Third Law is not a separate pillar of
thermodynamics, but the necessary condition that pre-
serves the integrity of the Second Law at its limit. Ac-
knowledging this redundancy does not diminish Nernst’s
heat theorem; rather, it elevates the Second Law to the
truly supreme position [11] that Einstein himself ad-
mired.
