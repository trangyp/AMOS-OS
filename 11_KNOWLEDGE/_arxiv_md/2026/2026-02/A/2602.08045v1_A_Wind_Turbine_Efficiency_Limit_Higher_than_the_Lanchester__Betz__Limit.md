A Wind Turbine Efficiency Limit Higher than the 
Lanchester (Betz) Limit 
Thad S. Morton 
 
It is shown that the upper limit on the fraction of power that can be extracted from an 
airstream approaching a wind turbine is about 78% rather than the historical value of 
59%. This higher limit is based on the assumption that the wind turbine cannot 
accelerate the flow at its exit plane above the freestream velocity. The derivation of the 
historical limit of 59% must either violate the angular momentum equation or the 
continuity equation. 
 
1. Background 
 
The efficiency of a wind turbine system normally includes the aerodynamic efficiency of 
the blades, the efficiency of the gearbox, if any, and that of the generator. The aerodynamic 
efficiency referred to in this study is the fraction of power reaching the rotating shaft divided 
by the total rate at which energy approaches the turbine in the form of wind. This is 
sometimes called the power coefficient. 
 
After Hütter (1977) suggested that the well-known wind turbine aerodynamic efficiency 
limit of 59% was not a definite upper limit, and Inglis (1979) pointed out that at least one 
wind turbine may have achieved an efficiency higher than this limit, Greet (1980) observed 
problems in the force balance implied by the historical derivation and stated, consequently, 
that no such limit could be found in a one-dimensional analysis. Rauh and Seelert (1984) 
confirmed that no theoretically grounded optimum for the aerodynamic efficiency of 
windmills existed. 
 
As van Kuik (2007) pointed out, the limit was first derived by Lanchester (1915) and 
then by Joukowsky (1920) and Betz (1920, 1928), so it is called the Lanchester-Betz-
Joukowsky limit (see also Bergey 1979). However, the work of Betz was the most available to 
readers, so the name “Betz limit” was used for some years. For further discussion on this 
topic and some inefficiencies of wind turbines, see Georgiou and Theodoropoulos (2011). To 
the author’s knowledge, no study to date has incorporated an angular momentum analysis to 
propose an alternate limit.

5. Conclusion 
 
It is remarkable that all three men—Lanchester, Betz, and Joukowsky—arrived at the 
same erroneous limit of 59% while working independently. The error stems from assuming 
(11), which imposes a strict limitation to a one-dimensional analysis of the wind turbine and 
effectively relies on an impossible extinction of the energy associated with swirl (a higher 
dimensional phenomenon), which violates either conservation of axial mass flow or the 
balance of torque and angular momentum. This inconsistency arose in the Lanchester-Betz-
Joukowsky limit of 59% because it excluded an analysis of angular momentum. The purpose 
of this study was not to re-examine the limits of a one-dimensional analysis but to estimate a 
realistic theoretical maximum for the power coefficient of a wind turbine while attempting to 
hold any added complexity to a minimum. This requires a two-dimensional analysis and an 
assumption that the wind turbine cannot accelerate the flow at its exit plane above the 
freestream velocity. This procedure yields an upper efficiency limit of about 78%.
