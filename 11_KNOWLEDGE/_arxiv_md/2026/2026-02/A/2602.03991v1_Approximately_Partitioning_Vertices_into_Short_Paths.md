Introduction
Throughout this paper, we assume that k ≥1 is a fixed positive integer and the input G = (V, E) is always
a simple undirected graph where V and E are the set of vertices and edges of G, respectively. Given a
path (respectively, cycle) P in G, the order ℓof P is the number of vertices in P and we call P an ℓ-
path (respectively, ℓ-cycle). Similarly, an ℓ−-path (respectively, ℓ+-path) is a path having order at most
(respectively, at least) ℓ. A k−-path partition, denoted by k-pp for short, of G is a set of vertex-disjoint k−-
paths in G such that every vertex in G appears in one of the paths in the set. The k−-path partition problem,
denoted by kPP, seeks to find a k-pp of G whose size is minimized. kPP has various real-life applications
in vehicle routing, network monitoring and others [24, 23, 3, 18]. We study kPP from the perspective of
approximation algorithms. An algorithm is ρ-approximation for a minimization (respectively, maximization)
problem Π if it runs in a polynomial time in the input size and outputs a feasible solution that is within
(respectively, at least) a guaranteed factor ρ of the optimal solution. Therefore, ρ ≥1 if Π is a minimization
problem and otherwise, ρ ≤1.
We note that 1PP is trivial. Moreover, 2PP is equivalent to finding a maximum matching of G and hence
can be solved in O(
p
|V ||E|) time [20]. Unfortunately kPP becomes NP-hard [8] when k ≥3. On the other
hand, kPP can be solved in polynomial time on some special graphs such as trees [24], cographs [22] and
bipartite permutation graphs [23].

Basic Definitions
We fix a simple undirected graph G = (V, E) and a positive integer k for discussion. Let |V | = n and
|E| = m. The order of G is the number of vertices in G, i.e., n. A graph G′ = (V ′, E′) is a subgraph of G
if V ′ ⊆V and E′ ⊆E. Furthermore, if V ′ = V and E′ ⊆E, then G′ is a spanning subgraph of G. For
simplicity, we also use V (G′) and E(G′) to denote the set of vertices and edges in G′, respectively.
The degree of a vertex in G is the number of edges incident to it in G. A star is a connected subgraph
of G such that exactly one vertex, called the center, is of degree at least 2, and the other vertices, called
the satellites, are of degree exactly 1. A cycle in G is a connected subgraph such that each vertex has
degree exactly 2 and a k-cycle is a cycle with order exactly k.
A 3-cycle is also called a triangle.
A
k-path P of G is a subgraph of G such that the vertices can be ordered as a sequence v1, . . . , vk and
E(P) = {{vi, vi+1} : i = 1, . . . , k −1}. Similarly, a k−-path is a path with order at most k.
A path-cycle cover of G is a spanning subgraph such that each connected component is a path or cycle. If
a path-cycle cover has no triangles, then it is called a triangle-free path-cycle cover. A maximum triangle-free
path-cycle cover is one with the maximum number of edges. In [14], the author presented an algorithm that
computes a maximum triangle-free path-cycle cover in O(n3m2) time.
Given the positive integer k, a k−-path partition of G, denoted by k-pp for short, is a spanning subgraph
of G in which each connected component is a k−-path. Hereafter, by a path in a k-pp, we always mean a
connected component in the k-pp. We next formally define kPP.

Problem 1 (kPP) Given G, the problem aims to find a minimum k-pp of G, where “minimum” means that
the number of paths is minimized.
Given a k-pp P of G, let |P| denote the number of paths in P and we always have
|V | = |P| + |E(P)|.
(1)
Therefore, to some extent, minimizing the number of paths is equivalent to maximizing the number of edges
in the k-pp. This induces the following problem, denoted by kPPE for simplicity, where “E” indicates that
the objective is to cover as many edges as possible.
Problem 2 (kPPE) Given G, the problem aims to find a k-pp of G such that the total number of edges in
the k-pp is maximized.
Lemma 1 An α-approximation algorithm for kPPE is a ((1 −α)k + α)-approximation algorithm for kPP.
Proof.
Suppose that we have an α-approximation algorithm A for kPPE and let P be the k-pp produced
by A. Let Q be a minimum kPP for G and thus |Q| ≥|V |
k . Since Q is a feasible solution of kPPE and A is
α-approximation, we have |E(P)| ≥α × |E(Q)|. It follows from Eq.(1) that
|P| = |V | −|E(P)| ≤|V | −α|E(Q)| = α|Q| + (1 −α)|V | ≤(α + (1 −α)k)|Q|,
which indicates that A is indeed a ((1 −α)k + α)-approximation algorithm for kPP. The proof is completed.

3.1
Connecting short cycles in F to the outside
By the discussion in the last paragraph, we have to present a process to deal with short cycles in F. In
fact, to connect as many short cycles as possible, our idea is to compute another path-cycle cover W in an
auxiliary graph G′ so that more edges can be involved in the final solution. The following definition is for
constructing the graph G′.
Definition 1 The auxiliary graph G′ is a spanning subgraph of G where its edge set E(G′) consists of all
edges {u, v} ∈E(G) such that u and v are in different connected components of F and at least one of them
is in a short cycle.
A short cycle C in F is saturated by an edge e ∈E(G′) if e is incident to a vertex in C. Similarly, a short
cycle C is saturated by a subset E′ ⊆E(G′), if C is saturated by at least one edge in E′.
Definition 2 Let η = 1{k≥11} where 1 is the indicator function. Given a subset E′ ⊆E(G′), for i ∈{4, 5},
let Fi be the set of i-cycles in F saturated by E′. The weight of E′ is
|F4| + η × |F5|.
(2)
In other words, when k ∈{9, 10}, the weight is the number of 4-cycles saturated by E′ and when k ≥11, the

Lemma 4 A maximum-weight path-cycle cover W in G′ can be computed in O(mn log n) time.
Proof.
We first introduce the maximum-weight [f, g]-factor problem defined on an edge-weighted graph
G1 where f, g are two functions mapping each vertex v ∈V (G1) to non-negative integers f(v), g(v) with
f(v) ≤g(v). An [f, g]-factor of G1 is a spanning graph of G1 such that the degree of each vertex v is in
the interval [f(v), g(v)]. The weight of an [f, g]-factor of G1 is the total weight of edges in the factor. A
maximum-weight [f, g]-factor of G1 can be computed in O(m1n1 log n1) time [7], where m1 = |E(G1)| and
n1 = |V (G1)|.

be the short cycles in F. From the graph G′ in Definition 1, we construct an edge-weighted graph G1 =
