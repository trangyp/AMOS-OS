Abstract 
One major challenge for engineering students learning advanced topics—especially through 
question-and-answer interactions with LLMs or VLMs—is that fields such as robotics are 
fundamentally conceptual rather than memorization-based. Mastery of this subject requires spatial 
reasoning and mathematical skills that LLMs or VLMs alone cannot fully support. To address 
these limitations, this study proposes an intelligent multi-agent framework built on LLMs and 
VLMs and specifically tailored to robotics. The goal is to integrate the strengths of LLMs and 
VLMs with computational tools to automatically analyze and solve problems related to robotic 
manipulators. Our developed framework accepts both textual and visual inputs and can 
automatically perform forward and inverse kinematics, compute velocities and accelerations of 
key points, generate 3D simulations of the robot, and ultimately execute motion control within the 
simulated environment, all according to the user’s query. To evaluate the framework, three 
benchmark tests were designed, each consisting of ten questions. In the first benchmark test, the 
framework was evaluated while connected to GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3.2, and Claude-Sonnet-4.5, 
as well as their corresponding raw models. The objective was to extract the forward kinematics of 
robots directly from textual descriptions. The results showed that the framework integrated with 
GPT-4o achieved the highest accuracy, reaching 0.97 in computing the final solution, whereas the 
raw model alone attained an accuracy of only 0.30 for the same task. Similarly, for the other two

accuracy.  The second benchmark test was identical to the first, except that the input was provided

results showed that the framework achieved an accuracy of 0.93 in obtaining the final answer,

1 INTRODUCTION 
In recent years, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has experienced widespread expansion across various 
fields, leading to a fundamental transformation in diverse application domains. With advances in 
Transformer architectures [1], Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged, enhancing human–
machine interaction and opening new research avenues for autonomous systems capable of human-
like reasoning. For example, Yiwei Wang et al. proposed an LLM-based autonomous agent for a 
robotic system, enabling effective human–robot collaboration and allowing the robot to understand 
natural language instructions and execute complex industrial tasks autonomously [2]. In another 
study, Shuguang Liu et al. [3] investigated the problem of motion planning for industrial mobile 
robots in factory environments with the assistance of LLMs. Furthermore, at a lower level of 
robotic operation, LLMs have also been utilized in the adaptive control of robotic manipulators 
[4]. Moreover, LLMs are not limited to industrial robotics applications. In this regard, Merino-
Fidalgo et al. employed an LLM as the decision-making core of the social robot Temi, enabling it 
to carry out tasks such as verbal interaction, reminders, and autonomous navigation within a 
domestic environment [5]. Overall, the emergence of LLMs has enabled the automation of

numerous tasks that previously required substantial human involvement. Furthermore, as 
mentioned, LLMs possess strong natural language reasoning capabilities. Consequently, one of 
their most common applications is quick and effortless access to information through question 
answering. For instance, individuals can pose their inquiries to these models and, in many cases, 
receive highly appropriate and reliable responses. Therefore, the capacity for serious utilization of 
these models in the field of education has expanded significantly in recent years. For example, 
Latif et al. introduced a bot called PhysicsAssistant which, by leveraging GPT-3.5-turbo, plays the 
role of an intelligent educational assistant in a physics laboratory setting, assisting students with 
laboratory activities to facilitate the learning process [6]. Furthermore, OpenAI has recently added 
a new feature called Study Mode to ChatGPT. This feature is designed to support the learning 
process of students. In this mode, instead of directly providing the correct answer, the language 
model first assesses the user's level of understanding and knowledge by posing relevant questions. 
Subsequently, much like a tutor, it guides the user step-by-step, encouraging active participation, 
critical thinking, and engagement with the problem, which ultimately leads to deeper user learning 
[7]. In addition to these studies, Bo Ni et al. introduced a platform supported by multi-agents based 
on language models that has the capability to automatically solve elasticity problems using the 
Finite Element Method (FEM) [8]. This type of utilization of language models for solving 
scientific problems requires the precise design of interactions and the purposeful guidance of the 
model towards a solution. To achieve such goals, Prompt Engineering and intelligent agents based 
on language models are employed. Prompt Engineering is the process of designing precise and 
effective instructions, known as prompts, with the goal of directing the language models towards 
producing desired outputs. An agent is also a language model combined with auxiliary tools to 
extend its capabilities. For instance, since language models are trained solely on massive volumes 
of text, they perform simple operations like addition and subtraction based only on the textual 
patterns they hold in memory, lacking genuine mathematical understanding. However, by 
converting the language model into an intelligent agent, it can be equipped with tools such as a 
calculator. In this scenario, when a mathematical question is asked, instead of attempting to answer 
based solely on pre-learned data (and guessing the next token), it sends the problem parameters to 
the calculator function, receives the precise answer, and then presents it to the user. Thus,

1.1 Problem Statement and the Goals of this Research 
These advancements motivated us to build a framework that can serve as an Educational Assistant 
for the Robotics course, so that students worldwide will be able to interact with it easily and inquire 
about their robotics problems (with a focus on robotic arms). Such a framework can even be 
utilized as a Teacher Assistant in schools and universities and interact with the user just like a 
language model. The significance of building this system lies in providing accessible education 
for everyone globally. Therefore, the objective of this research is to develop a highly modern 
educational assistance framework in the domain of robotic arms, based on intelligent agents driven 
by LLMs. In this study, the designed framework called “RoboSolver”, by utilizing the Natural 
Language Processing (NLP), decision-making, thinking, and advanced reasoning capabilities of 
LLMs (while being equipped with its own smart, specialized tools), helps the system to interpret 
high-level (and even colloquial) user instructions regarding manipulators and generate appropriate 
responses in real-time. Therefore, the proposed framework, by leveraging its comprehensive suite 
of dedicated robotics tools and the advanced capabilities of LLMs, is capable of intelligently 
solving an acceptable range of problems posed in classic robotics courses. These integrated tools 
encompass the most useful concepts in Robotics courses: 
• 
Parameter Extraction: Tools for derive necessary parameters for robot modeling from 
descriptive text or even images of the robotic arm 
• 
Kinematic Analysis: Tools for solving forward kinematics analytically and numerically, as 
well as inverse kinematics numerically 
• 
Motion Analysis: Tools for computing the Jacobian matrix, and the velocity and 
acceleration of key points on the robot 
• 
Simulation: Tools for 3D robot simulation within environments such as Swift or Matplotlib 
•    Control: Tools for velocity control of the joints or the end-effector, as well as trajectory 
control for guiding the robot from a specified point toward a desired target

2 RELATED WORKS 
2.1  Introduction 
In the book “Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach”, an agent is defined as anything that can 
perceive its environment through sensors and act upon that environment through effectors [9]. 
Furthermore, Wikipedia states that in the context of Artificial Intelligence, an intelligent agent is 
an entity that independently perceives its surrounding environment, takes appropriate actions to 
achieve its goals, and has the ability to improve its performance through machine learning or 
knowledge acquisition [10]. The key concept is that agents receive feedbacks from the 
environments. The environment with which an agent can interact may be physical (such as the 
surroundings of a robot or an autonomous vehicle), digital (like interacting with operating systems 
and software, such as Siri), or even human (such as the interaction of chatbots with human users). 
Among the various types of environments with which agents can interact, the human environment 
holds a special place; chatbots are among the most prominent examples of this type of interaction. 
The first known chatbot, ELIZA, was developed in 1966 by Joseph Weizenbaum at the MIT 
Artificial Intelligence Laboratory  [11]. This system operated based on a set of simple, predefined 
rules. Its architecture relied on rule-based patterns (such as if-then logic) and was only capable of 
responding within highly specific and constrained domains. These types of chatbots required 
manual design and programming and were ineffective in handling complex or out-of-scope 
scenarios. Subsequent technological advancements—particularly in the field of recurrent neural 
networks—led to a shift away from rule-based frameworks toward data-driven learning 
approaches. This transition enabled the development of systems capable of automatically learning 
patterns from large-scale textual datasets, allowing them to perform effectively across a broader 
range of domains without direct human intervention. For instance, in 2016, Jiwei Li and colleagues 
developed an experimental conversational model based on LSTM networks [12]. The model was 
trained on 23 million short Twitter conversations, enabling it to learn common patterns in everyday 
dialogues and generate contextually relevant responses. However, due to the nature of the training 
data, the model became biased toward the brief style of Twitter conversations, often producing 
short responses during interactions. On the other hand, incorporating longer conversations into the


## Page 7


7 
training process to mitigate this bias was not feasible, as LSTM networks—despite significant 
advancements—still faced inherent limitations in handling long-term dependencies. 
Finally, with the advent of the Transformer architecture, new language models entered the arena 
that were capable of effectively retaining long-term information. This provided a smoother, more 
accurate, and more natural experience in user interaction. On the other hand, since these language 
models can communicate with their environment (which, in this case, is human), perceive it, and 
perform actions based on it—such as generating responses—they can be considered a type of agent 
based on the classic definition provided. However, the convention in the literature is that a 
language model is only recognized as a true intelligent agent when it leverages tools for active 
interaction with the environment [13] (such as web search, file reading, or code execution). 
Furthermore, receiving a "role-based prompt," which specifies the agent's role, objective, and 
scope of action, is essential for correctly directing the agent's decision-making and action. 
Given the nascent nature of this research domain, a review of the background can provide a better 
understanding of the available opportunities. Subsequently, the literature related to agents and 
research conducted in the field of education will be examined. This literature review will analyze 
and evaluate the strengths, weaknesses, and existing research gaps in previous studies to clearly 
delineate the position and necessity of the current research. The research conducted in this study 
can be categorized into two main axes. The first section examines studies that address the 
fundamental question of whether LLMs can play an effective role in education, particularly in 
Mechanical Engineering education, which necessitates a deep understanding of technical and 
analytical concepts. The second section reviews studies related to the application of agents and 
language models in solving engineering problems and education within this domain. Finally, 
relying on a comprehensive analysis of these two research categories, the objective, position, and 
trajectory of the present study will be determined. 
2.2  Background 
In recent years, with the advances made in the field of artificial intelligence and the emergence of 
fundamental changes in various aspects of life, education has also begun to embrace more modern 
approaches. Moreover, it is anticipated that over the next decade, educational methods will


## Page 8


8 
undergo profound transformations, moving toward greater interactivity, the delivery of more 
effective visual content, and faster, easier, and more widespread accessibility. The realization of 
this vision will largely be enabled through the use of artificial intelligence, particularly advanced 
language models. Initially, the primary objective of this proposed research was the design and 
development of an educational assistant capable of solving problems related to robotic 
manipulators in a precise and generalizable manner. To this end, various studies and evaluations 
were conducted to identify an appropriate pathway toward achieving this goal. Ultimately, the 
results of these investigations indicated that one of the most suitable approaches for addressing 
robotic problems is the simultaneous use of language models and vision–language models 
(VLMs). This combination can, on the one hand, provide the ability to understand natural language 
and, on the other hand, offer high flexibility in solving various problems through the interpretation 
of visual inputs. However, prior to designing an educational assistant based on language models 
in the field of robotic arm, the most critical question is whether language models are capable of 
understanding a mechanical problem. As we know, in mechanical engineering education, the mere 
ability to comprehend textual information is not sufficient (however, it should be considered that 
these models are continuously progressing in different aspects such as education!). Thus, although 
language models demonstrate strong capabilities in understanding natural language, the 
requirements of specialized educational domains are far more complex and extensive than 
understanding simple text [14]. In other words, because mechanics involves foundational, spatial, 
and abstract concepts of its own, understanding it requires, in addition to comprehension skills, 
other abilities such as spatial visualization, mathematical computation, and reasoning [15]. In this 
regard, Bingling and colleagues conducted a study evaluating the inferential capabilities of 
ChatGPT-4 in the field of mechanical engineering. The results showed that the model may generate 
incorrect answers when solving certain mechanical problems, such as deriving equations, and by 
insisting on the correctness of these answers, it can potentially cause confusion for students, 
particularly beginners. These findings indicate that despite the remarkable textual processing 
capabilities of ChatGPT-4, its use in specialized engineering problems requires caution [14]. 
Therefore, measures must be taken to reduce such errors. For example, in operations involving 
symbolic equations, instead of relying on the inherent capabilities of a language model, one can 
use a library such as SymPy as a dedicated tool—a powerful Python library specifically designed


## Page 9


9 
for symbolic computation. The rationale behind this choice is that language models are 
fundamentally trained on next-token prediction within a textual sequence and, by design, lack the 
intrinsic computational structure required for precise and fully symbolic mathematical problem 
solving. In contrast, libraries such as SymPy, by employing rigorous symbolic and algebraic 
methods, are capable of performing complex operations such as simplification, differentiation, and 
equation solving with complete accuracy. In a more in-depth and analytical study, Tian and 
colleagues systematically evaluated the capabilities of ChatGPT 3 and 4 in solving mechanical 
engineering problems. To this end, a comprehensive test consisting of 126 questions—designed 
by a group of experts in the field—was employed to assess the models’ level of proficiency. In a 
thorough evaluation, the performance of these two models in solving mechanical engineering 
problems was compared with that of domain experts, including faculty members from mechanical 
engineering departments. The results of this study indicated that the aforementioned models 
outperformed university professors in many specialized subfields [16]. It is worth noting, however, 
that the test in question was administered in a closed-book format—conditions that naturally favor 
large language models. These models, by leveraging their extensive pretrained knowledge, possess 
the ability to accurately retrieve memorized information such as definitions, laws, and even less 
common formulas. In contrast, a professor—despite having the ability to understand and solve 
problems—may not recall certain formulas or memorized details due to the passage of time. 
Therefore, in situations where access to external resources is restricted, the superior accuracy of 
language models compared to humans who rely on memory is natural and expected. Moreover, 
the results indicated that ChatGPT models, when prompted to first provide their step-by-step 
reasoning and logical explanation (chain of thought [17]) and then present the final answer based 
on that reasoning, demonstrated a higher level of accuracy and logical reasoning in the subjects of 
dynamics and statics. In addition, when the prompt for ChatGPT-4 explicitly specified the domain 
in which the model was expected to answer questions, higher accuracy was achieved in the 
evaluations. This is because the model’s distribution shifts toward the appropriate region of the 
problem space, enabling it to respond with greater expertise in the specified domain. Overall, the 
findings of this study—which indicate that ChatGPT-4 demonstrates higher accuracy in answering 
questions compared to university faculty—highlight the remarkable potential of this model to 
serve as an intelligent, precise, and reliable assistant in the field of engineering. Continuing the


## Page 10


10 
examination of language models’ capabilities in solving mechanical engineering problems, the 
MechAgents framework offers an innovative example of leveraging multi-agent language-model 
collaboration for the automated solution of mechanical problems, particularly in the domain of 
elasticity [8]. In this framework, a group of language models functions as agents with specialized 
roles—such as planner, coder, executor, and critic—working interactively to write and execute 
finite element method (FEM) code, analyze the resulting outputs, and correct any errors that arise. 
This architecture, implemented using GPT-4 and the AutoGen platform, has been able to correctly 
solve a wide range of problems in solid mechanics without human intervention, demonstrating that 
task decomposition, reciprocal criticism, and dynamic interaction among agents lead to increased 
accuracy and coherence. One of the key features of this architecture is the role of the critic agent 
in the process of validating the responses. This agent evaluates the consistency of numerical or 
graphical outputs from simulations with physical principles, and even when the code runs without 
error, it can detect hidden anomalies (such as inconsistencies in the stress pattern) and provide 
appropriate feedback to other agents for correction. This level of self-monitoring goes beyond the 
capabilities of individual agents and is one of the major advantages of a multi-agent structure. 
Nevertheless, this framework is not without limitations. First, although the AutoGen platform 
supports the use of external functions (such as invoking specialized tools), this capability was not 
utilized in the study, and the approach relied solely on code generation and execution by the 
language model itself—an aspect that may become inefficient when dealing with more complex 
problems. Second, in the architecture of the AutoGen platform, all agents read each other’s 
messages. While this leads to comprehensive oversight of the system’s performance by all agents, 
it may be suboptimal in larger tasks or at broader scales in terms of token consumption. In another 
study conducted by Zahedifar et. al., an LLM-Agents-Controller was developed that is capable of 
solving classical control problems [18]. By employing multiple specialized agents, they 
intelligently distributed the tasks to enable their framework to operate effectively. For example, in 
this system, the user’s query is first received by the Supervisor Agent, which determines—based 
on the type of question—which auxiliary agent should be invoked. If external data are required, 
the Retriever Agent extracts the relevant information, the Researcher Agent searches the internet 
for the necessary content, and the Reasoner Agent performs logical reasoning using methods such 
as chain-of-thought or tree-of-thought [19]. If no external assistance is needed, the Supervisor

Agent delegates the question to the Planner Agent, which determines the sequence of tools that 
should be executed. The Controller Agent then generates the response using the designated tools, 
and in the event of an error, the Debugger Agent resolves the issue. Subsequently, the generated 
answer is evaluated by the Critic Agent, and any required improvements are applied. Finally, the 
Communicator Agent delivers the response to the user in an appropriate format and style. The 
implementation of this framework represents an important step toward realizing the vision of fully 
automated educational architectures based on intelligent agents—systems capable of solving 
mechanical engineering problems comprehensively, from understanding the problem to delivering 
the final solution. However, one criticism of this architecture lies in the large number of agents 
involved in the problem-solving process, which increases system complexity, prolongs processing 
time, and leads to high token consumption. Therefore, by redesigning and reorganizing the agent 
structure—such as removing nonessential components or merging overlapping agents—a more 
optimized framework can be achieved; one that, while maintaining reliable performance, is also 
more efficient in terms of resource utilization. These efforts are being pursued while the automatic 
solution of dynamic and physical problems using language models is still in its early stages. Given 
the emerging nature of LLM agents and the inherent complexity of automating the solution of 
robotic and dynamic problems, the existing work in this domain remains scarce and limited. For 
example, Du et al., in an innovative study, addressed the problem of discovering governing 
equations of physical phenomena directly from experimental data. In their approach, instead of 
relying on predefined physical laws or classical mathematical analysis, LLMs are employed to 
automatically propose equations that can describe the system’s dynamics based on a set of 
numerical observations. This method replaces the traditional and time-consuming process of 
deriving equations with the inferential capabilities of language models, opening new horizons for 
data-driven analysis of physical phenomena [20]. In another study conducted by Gerstmayr and 
colleagues, the Exudyn Python library and large language models were employed for dynamic 
modeling. In this approach, the user first provides a textual prompt describing the dynamic 
problem, thereby supplying the necessary information to the language model. The model then uses 
the Exudyn library to generate Python code and implement the system’s dynamic model [21]. 
Despite the novelty of this method, several limitations exist. First, preparing a precise and 
comprehensive prompt to fully describe the system can be a challenging and time-consuming task

12 
for the user. Second, their approach lacks the ability to directly interpret problems from images. 
Third, the study does not introduce any tool or function designed to optimize the interaction

As shown, numerous studies in recent years have focused on the development of intelligent agents, 
particularly within the field of education. However, to the best of our knowledge, no framework 
based on LLM agents has yet been presented that is specifically capable of solving problems 
related to robotic arms and acting as an intelligent educational assistant for learners. Therefore, 
inspired by previous studies, we decided to design the RoboSolver framework, which is based on 
LLM agents and is capable of solving problems in the domain of manipulators. This framework 
will be capable of receiving textual and visual inputs (in contrast to the work of Gerstmayr et al., 
which relied solely on textual input) and using them for robot modeling. Once the robot model is 
obtained, it will also be able to perform analyses such as forward and inverse kinematics, 
calculation of key-point velocities and accelerations, as well as robot simulation and control. 
 
3 METHODOLOGY 
As discussed in the previous section, the remarkable advances in LLMs have significantly 
transformed research approaches in the development of autonomous educational agents. These 
agents can now, much like the conductor of an orchestra, coordinate and manage all stages of the 
problem-solving process in order to address the user’s needs accurately and efficiently. In this 
regard, an appropriate foundation was established for designing a framework capable of serving 
as an intelligent educational assistant in the field of robotics. The proposed framework in this study 
will employ a multi-agent architecture, similar to the work of Zahedifar et al. [18], but with a key 
distinction: to optimize resource usage, the number of agents will be reduced. Moreover, unlike 
the MechAgents study [8], which utilizes the AutoGen platform, this framework will be 
implemented using LangChain, since in this platform only the necessary agents are activated at 
the appropriate time, eliminating the need for all agents to read one another’s messages. 
Additionally, in contrast to the approach presented by Gerstmayr et al. [21], the agents in our


## Page 13


13 
framework will not be required to generate programming code from scratch. Instead, a set of 
predefined tools (developed by us) is made available to the agents, and each agent is responsible 
only for selecting the appropriate tool and determining the correct input needed to obtain the final 
answer. This approach not only substantially reduces the likelihood of errors in code generation 
and execution, but also allows the agents to focus on logical reasoning and goal-oriented decision-
making rather than becoming entangled in technical implementation details—ultimately leading 
to a significant improvement in the model’s accuracy. Consequently, the proposed framework can 
deliver faster, more optimized, and highly reliable performance compared to existing approaches. 
On the other hand, to successfully handle a complex request, an agent must be able to decompose 
it into a set of smaller sub-tasks and then select and execute the appropriate tool for each step. The 
ReAct architecture was one of the first approaches to implement this process effectively and in a 
generalizable manner [22], thereby paving the way for subsequent research in this direction. In this 
method, the language model plays a central role in both the reasoning and action components. For 
each step of the problem, the model first analyzes the situation through linguistic reasoning and 
then selects and executes an appropriate action based on that reasoning. This process continues as 
an iterative loop until the agent reaches the user’s final objective. One of the key features of this 
architecture is that if the model makes a mistake at any step or arrives at an incorrect result, it can 
detect the error by analyzing previous outcomes in the next iteration and subsequently adjust its 
decision-making path. For this reason, the agents in the proposed framework make decisions based 
on the ReAct architecture. The overall structure of our proposed framework is illustrated in the 
Figure 2. In the following sections, we introduce the components of this framework and examine 
its architecture in detail.

3.1  Framework Architecture 
Supervisor Agent  
In this framework, the supervisor agent functions as the central brain of the system and also serves 
as the primary interface between the user and the other components of the system. Its secondary 
role is to establish and maintain an effective and dynamic linguistic interaction with the user so 
that it can accurately understand the user’s request and ensure coherent communication between 
the human and the system. Once the user’s request is clearly identified through this linguistic 
interaction, the agent’s main task begins: it must select and activate the most appropriate agent or 
pipeline to address the user’s request. Decision-making at this stage is based on the nature of the 
query, the type of input, and the objective of the problem, ensuring that the processing path is 
correctly determined and the user’s needs are met accurately and efficiently. The supervisor agent 
has four decision-making routes at its disposal:  
1- Researcher Agent 
2- Retriever Agent 
3- Extractor Pipeline 
4- Planner Pipeline. 
 
Researcher Agent 
Whenever responding to the user’s request requires searching reliable online sources, the 
supervisor agent invokes the researcher agent to perform this process using its dedicated tools. 
After gathering the necessary information and generating a response, the researcher agent returns 
the results to the supervisor agent, which then re-evaluates the situation—taking into account the 
newly obtained external information—to determine the most appropriate route for continuing the 
processing.


## Page 16


16 
Retriever Agent 
When the user provides a file (such as a PDF) and submits a request related to its content, the 
supervisor agent activates the retriever agent. This agent first extracts the relevant information 
from the provided source using its designated tools, and then the language model within the agent 
generates an accurate and context-appropriate response based on the retrieved data. Finally, the 
response is returned to the supervisor agent, which re-evaluates the situation—taking into account 
the newly obtained retrieved information—to decide on the next appropriate processing route. 
 
Extractor Pipeline 
This pipeline is activated by the supervisor agent when the user submits an image of a robotic arm 
or a textual description of its structure. Depending on the type of input, the pipeline uses a VLM 
for images and an LLM for textual descriptions, along with a set of predefined specialized prompts, 
to extract the parameters required for robot modeling. The traditional and widely used approach 
for modeling serial robots has been the Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) method. However, in 2007, 
Peter Corke introduced a new method called the Elementary Transform Sequence (ETS), which is 
simpler and more intuitive than the conventional approach [23]. Based on the qualitative 
evaluations conducted in this study, it was observed that even VLMs exhibit better compatibility 
with the ETS method and demonstrate superior performance in extracting its parameters from 
images of the robot compared to the Denavit–Hartenberg method. Thus, the prompts are designed 
so that the LLM or VLM is able to extract the ETS parameters from texts or images. Since 
possessing these parameters enables the complete modeling of robotic arms, this stage plays a 
pivotal role in the subsequent process of addressing the user's request, and any error introduced 
here can directly affect the accuracy of the final outcome. In the ETS method, the kinematic chain 
of the robot is modeled as a sequence of elementary transformations consisting of basic rotations 
and translations along or about the coordinate axes. The following section examines how this 
approach is employed in the kinematic modeling of a robotic arms.

Planner Pipeline 
Based on the data received from the supervisor agent or the extractor pipeline, this pipeline 
determines which of the available tools in the Robosolver agent—consisting of both analytical and 
numerical components—are required to address the user’s problem. It then optimally determines 
the order in which these tools should be executed. Subsequently, the output of the planner pipeline 
is delivered to the Robosolver agent as a decision-making guideline. This allows the Robosolver 
agent—without needing to perform any tool-selection analysis—to focus solely on determining 
the appropriate inputs and executing the required tools efficiently, ultimately resulting in more 
effective and accurate performance. An important point in this section is that only the tools selected 
by the planner agent are provided to the Robosolver agent. This ensures that the LLM within the 
Robosolver agent receives a more concise input, as descriptions of tools that are not intended to 
be used are omitted. Consequently, the number of input tokens is reduced, leading to lower API 
costs for the LLM. Moreover, with a smaller and more focused input, the model can concentrate 
more effectively on the problem at hand, ultimately improving its accuracy. 
RoboSolver Agent 
In this stage, the Robosolver agent, guided by the planner pipeline and based on the user’s query, 
determines the input for the selected tool and invokes it. After observing the tool’s output, the 
agent leverages its language-based reasoning capability to analyze the result and, accordingly, 
identify the appropriate input for the next tool and execute it. This process continues as an iterative 
cycle of reasoning and action until the Robosolver agent determines that the final answer has been 
successfully obtained. 
In our platform, a total of 16 tools have been designed for this agent, categorized into two groups: 
analytical and numerical. The analytical tools were developed from scratch using the SymPy 
library, whereas the numerical tools were built upon the classes and functions provided by the 
Robotics Toolbox for Python library. As mentioned earlier, once the ETS parameters are available, 
the robot can be modeled with ease. Therefore, after these parameters are obtained from the 
extractor pipeline and passed to the Robosolver agent, the agent can readily utilize the available 
tools to perform the following operations:

Inspector Agent 
The final response generated by the Robosolver agent is passed to the inspector agent, whose role 
is to verify whether the user’s request has been correctly fulfilled. In other words, if any errors 
occur during tool execution or if inconsistencies arise between the user’s intended goal and the 
output produced by the Robosolver agent, the inspector agent provides corrective feedback to the 
Robosolver, prompting the problem-solving process to be repeated with the necessary 
improvements. Once the final response is validated in terms of accuracy and consistency, the 
inspector agent forwards the result to the supervisor agent, which then presents it to the user in an 
organized manner. Finally, the response—along with the user’s original query—is stored in the 
internal memory of the supervisor agent. This memory helps the agent in future interactions, 
enabling it to correctly recognize any implicit or explicit references the user makes to previous 
questions or answers, and to effectively incorporate that information when interpreting the user’s 
new request.

than those of the other frameworks built on DeepSeek-V3.2 and Claude-Sonnet-4.5. Notably, 
however, the framework employing DeepSeek-V3.2 achieved a score of 93%, while incurring 
approximately 87% lower cost compared to the GPT-4o-based framework. This suggests that 
despite slightly lower accuracy, DeepSeek-V3.2 may offer a highly cost-effective solution for 
commercial applications. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the response time of the 
framework using DeepSeek-V3.2 was nearly twice that of the GPT-4o-based framework, which 
could impact user experience in certain real-time applications. Another noteworthy observation is 
that the raw version of DeepSeek-V3.2 achieved an 𝓜𝑪 score of 80%—only 13% lower than its 
tool-enhanced counterpart. Qualitative analysis shows that this model, even without tool support, 
was able to carry out complex matrix multiplications correctly by leveraging step-by-step 
reasoning. However, its response time was nearly twice that of the framework version using 
DeepSeek-V3.2, since in the absence of computational tools, the model had to perform matrix 
operations purely through linguistic inference. On the other hand, the framework based on Claude-
Sonnet-4.5 exhibited lower accuracy despite having a cost nearly 20 times higher than that of the 
DeepSeek-V3.2-based framework. One of the primary reasons for this accuracy drop was that, in 
many cases where the framework failed to generate a final answer, the issue stemmed from the 
Supervisor Agent not passing the required input to other agents or pipelines. This lack of 
information exchange between agents often led to incomplete task execution and, ultimately, 
failure to arrive at a final answer. Given its lower cost and higher accuracy, the framework that 
uses DeepSeek-V3.2 is undoubtedly more favorable than the one based on Claude-Sonnet-4.5—
especially considering that it is approximately 5 seconds slower in response time, a difference that 
is not significant enough to outweigh its overall advantage. Overall, the frameworks consistently 
outperformed the raw versions of the same LLM in terms of accuracy, demonstrating the 
effectiveness of the Robosolver framework in solving forward kinematics for robots described 
through textual input.

6 CONCLUSION 
In this work, we introduced RoboSolver, an intelligent multi-agent framework that integrates 
LLMs, VLMs, and computational tools to support learning and problem-solving in robotics. 
Through three benchmark tests, the framework consistently outperformed the corresponding raw 
models across the defined accuracy metric. In forward-kinematics problems based on textual input 
data, the GPT-4o–based framework achieved an 𝓜𝑪 of 0.97, representing a 67% improvement 
over the raw model. In comparison, the DeepSeek-V3.2–based variant attained a competitive 𝓜𝑪 
of 0.93, delivering acceptable performance while incurring substantially lower operational costs 
than the GPT-4o–based framework. For visually defined problems, the combination of GPT-4o 
with Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview yielded an 𝓜𝑪 of 0.93, representing approximately a 20% gain over 
the raw VLM. In Benchmark 3, which included Jacobian computation, inverse kinematics, velocity 
and acceleration analysis, simulation, and robot control, the framework again achieved an 𝓜𝑪 of 
0.97, demonstrating robust performance across a wide range of robotic problems. The results 
indicate that equipping LLMs with dedicated computational tools and designing structured 
prompts that transform them into role-specialized agents significantly enhances their problem-
solving capabilities. This integration enables accurate mathematical computation, structured 
reasoning, and reliable task execution beyond the limitations of standalone models. Moreover, 
within the multi-agent architecture, tasks are distributed among different agents in such a way that 
each agent remains focused on its specialized responsibility. This structured division of labor 
enhances accuracy, reduces error propagation, and improves coherence in the problem-solving 
process, ultimately leading to superior overall performance in complex robotic tasks. From an 
educational perspective, the framework can serve as an intelligent assistant that provides step-by-
step solutions and visual simulations, thereby improving the quality of instruction and supporting 
deeper conceptual understanding. Finally, the RoboSolver framework can increase students’ 
motivation while reducing their dependence on continuous human supervision by offering an 
interactive, automated problem-solving environment.
