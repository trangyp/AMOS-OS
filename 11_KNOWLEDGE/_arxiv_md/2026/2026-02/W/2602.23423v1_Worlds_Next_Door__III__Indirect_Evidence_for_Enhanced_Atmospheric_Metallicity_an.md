ABSTRACT
We present the most sensitive direct imaging search for the nearest (d = 3.2 pc) Jupiter-analog
exoplanet, ϵ Eri b, with JWST/NIRCam coronagraphy between 4–5 µm (F444W). We achieve a 5σ
contrast sensitivity ≈3.0×10−7 (∆≈16.3 mag) in the F444W filter at the expected planet separation
of ≈1′′. This is the deepest 4–5 µm contrast performance achieved for any JWST/NIRCam observation
to date at these separations (and > 10× better than ground-based limits). Yet, the planet remains
elusive to imaging. We update the star’s age to 1.1±0.1 Gyr, older than previous age estimates, using
the latest gyrochronology relations. This significantly impacts ϵ Eri b’s inferred effective temperature
(Teff), which is now expected to lie between 150–200 K based on evolutionary models for a 1 MJup
planet. Using cloud-free Sonora Flame Skimmer models and custom PICASO patchy cloud models in
the above Teff range, we find that the F444W non-detection of ϵ Eri b can be explained by a metal-
enriched atmosphere and/or an atmosphere containing water ice clouds. Both possibilities suggest that
ϵ Eri b’s atmosphere is strikingly similar to that of Jupiter in our Solar System. Alternatively, if we do
not enforce the dynamical mass (0.98 ± 0.09 MJup), a solar metallicity, cloud-free, ≲0.81 MJup planet
would be consistent with the NIRCam upper limit based on the Sonora Flame Skimmer evolutionary
models. Finally, we place limits on the size of a potential ring system using the NIRCam/F210M data
and discuss the opportunity to directly image ϵ Eri b with additional JWST observations, the Roman
Coronagraph Instrument, the ExtraSolar Coronagraph on the Lazuli Observatory, and EELT/METIS.

1. INTRODUCTION
ϵ Eridani (ϵ Eri) is a nearby (3.2 pc), adolescent (1.1±
0.1 Gyr; this work, §2), debris disk- and giant planet-
hosting 0.8 M⊙K2V star (R. O. Gray et al. 2006; G.
Gonzalez et al. 2010).
It is reminiscent of a younger
version of the Solar System and, as the ninth closest star
system to the Sun, serves as a benchmark for detailed
characterization studies.

JWST/NIRCam coronagraph to date. For this program,
the observations were specifically designed to avoid the
bright lobes of the NIRCam PSF at the predicted planet
position and achieve maximum contrast performance.

2. STELLAR ROTATION AND
GYROCHRONOLOGICAL AGE
Table 1 lists observational data and updated stellar
parameters for ϵ Eri from literature and in some in-
stances recalculated. We re-evaluate the age of ϵ Eri con-
sidering its rotation in light of updated rotation/activity
data for members of age-dated star clusters.
The observation that low-mass stars in clusters of var-
ious ages show systematic signs of spinning down as they
age has led to development of stellar rotation as an age
indicator — “gyrochronology” (S. A. Barnes 2007). For
K-type dwarf stars like ϵ Eri, it is one of the better cal-
ibrated and understood age indicators (E. E. Mamajek
& L. A. Hillenbrand 2008; D. R. Soderblom 2010; L. G.
Bouma et al. 2023a). The bounty of rotation periods
measured for low-mass members of age-dated star clus-
ters, mainly from the Kepler and TESS missions, has
led to refined calibrations of the empirical trends in ro-
tation period as a function of age and color or effective
temperature. L. G. Bouma et al. (2023a) provide an up-
dated calibration of gyrochronology using rotation data
for 10 age-dated star clusters which they validate over
the effective temperature range 3800 K < Teff < 6200 K
and ages of 0.08–4.0 Gyr. These generously bound the
previous Teff and age estimates for ϵ Eri.
The rotation period of ϵ Eri has been measured by
numerous studies in recent decades. Here we combine
several modern measurements to estimate a median ro-
tation period and uncertainty in order to calcuate a
gyrochronological age for ϵ Eri.
Differential rotation
has been observed for ϵ Eri, with seasonal estimates
ranging from ∼11 days to ∼12 days (R. A. Donahue
et al. 1996).
We combine multiple independent esti-
mates from different methods and observatories, includ-
ing a value using time-series photometry data from a
single sector of TESS data (T. Fetherolf et al. 2023,
Prot = 11.039 ± 2.233 d) and 2 periods from MOST (B.
Croll et al. 2006, Prot = 11.35 d, 11.55 d), and periods

1996, Prot(min) = 11.04 d, Prot(max) = 12.18 d) and a
season of TIGRE data (A. Hempelmann et al. 2016,
Prot =11.76 ± 0.08 d). The average for these 14 seasons
of rotation periods for ϵ Eri is Prot = 11.60 d.

3. OBSERVATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION
3.1. Executed Sequences
We obtained F210M (1.992–2.201 µm) and F444W
(3.881–4.982 µm) JWST/NIRCam imaging of ϵ Eri on
UT 2025 August 29 as part of Cycle 4 program DD 9431
(PI: J. Llop-Sayson, Co-PI(s): A. Sanghi, W. Thomp-
son). The sequence consists of a two-roll angle observa-
tion centered on ϵ Eri followed by imaging of reference
star δ Eridani (δ Eri) with the 9-POINT-CIRCLE dither
pattern to improve sampling of the PSF (see e.g., R.
Soummer et al. 2014; S. Hinkley et al. 2023). This en-
ables the use of both angular differential imaging (ADI:
M. C. Liu 2004; C. Marois et al. 2006) and reference star
differential imaging (RDI: D. Lafreni`ere et al. 2009) to
search for the giant planet ϵ Eri b.
The full set of observations were carried out be-
tween UT 2025 August 29 16:33:54 and 22:49:24 in a
non-interruptible sequence for a total of ≈6.25 hours
(including observatory overheads) simultaneously with
the F210M/F444W filter combination and using the
MASK335R coronagraph. Random centering errors in

position (Figure 2). To avoid this issue and achieve the
best contrast performance possible, the ϵ Eri observa-
tions in this work were conducted at telescope V3 po-

3.3. Contrast Calibration
We estimate the contrast sensitivity of our observa-
tions using injection-recovery tests, implemented with
functionality in the spaceKLIP package, following the
methods outlined in A. Sanghi et al. (2025). PSF in-
jections are carried out over the following separation (ρ)
and position angle (θ, East of North) grid: ρ ∈[0.′′8, 1.′′3]
in steps of 0.′′1 and θ ∈[0◦, 330◦] in steps of 30◦, exclud-
ing position angles overlapping with the expected planet

to a false positive rate of 2.9×10−7 (but a higher value).

overall low chance of high SNR false probability events.

beyond the scope of this work. Given this caveat, we
proceed with interpreting the NIRCam 5σ upper limit
in the context of the planet’s properties in the following
sections. We do note that the well-constrained predic-
tion for the planet’s position based on the latest orbit
solution (W. Thompson et al. 2025) helps reduce the im-
pact of the unknown false positive rate on the calculated
flux upper limit.
4. EVOLUTIONARY AND ATMOSPHERIC MODEL
ANALYSIS
The estimated dynamical mass for ϵ Eri b from joint
modeling of radial velocity and absolute astrometry data
is 0.98 ± 0.09 MJup (W. Thompson et al. 2025). Com-
bining this precise mass measurement with an age of
1.1 ± 0.1 Gyr, we first estimate the range of possible
fundamental parameters for the planet using different
evolutionary models. Next, given ϵ Eri b’s fundamen-
tal parameters, the deep NIRCam upper limit on the
planet’s 4–5 µm flux enables us to constrain possible at-
mosphere scenarios for the planet. We investigate the
consistency of both cloud-free and cloudy (H2O) atmo-
spheres with the observations using the latest generation
of low-temperature self-consistent grid models. Finally,
we investigate the possibility of a lower planet mass,
compared to the dynamical mass, as an alternate ex-
planation for the NIRCam non-detection using flux pre-
dictions from the Sonora Flame Skimmer evolutionary
models.

4.2. Cloud-free Model Grid
The Sonora Flame Skimmer models (Mang et al., in
preparation) extend the cloud-free Sonora Elf Owl grid
(S. Mukherjee et al. 2024; N. F. Wogan et al. 2025)
to colder effective temperatures, lower surface gravi-
ties, and a broader range of metallicities. These mod-
els include both equilibrium and disequilibrium chem-
istry and incorporate rainout chemistry for H2O, CH4,
and NH3—even in cloud-free atmospheres—similar to
the treatment in Sonora Bobcat. They also address the
underestimation of CO2 found in the Sonora Elf Owl
models (S. Mukherjee et al. 2024), which has since been
revised in N. F. Wogan et al. (2025). We use models
spanning the Teff range and bracketing the g expected
for ϵ Eri b from the evolutionary model analysis. The
full set of parameter values are presented in Table 3.

atmospheric model using the transmission curve from

Another relevant cloud parameter is the sedimentation
efficiency, fsed, which sets the vertical extent of the wa-
ter cloud. Generally, the optical thickness and vertical
extent of the cloud decreases and particle size increases
with increasing fsed. For our grid, we use h = 0.25 and
fsed = 8 for all models. For the considered planet tem-
peratures (150–200 K), we encountered challenges with
model convergence for smaller h (greater cloud coverage
fraction) and smaller fsed (more optically thick) values.
Previous work has shown that H2O clouds can become
extremely optically thick and introduce strong radiative
feedback that complicates convergence in self-consistent
atmospheric models (C. V. Morley et al. 2014b). This
motivated the use of a lower cloud fraction in the patchy
cloud prescriptions to stabilize solutions. The full set of
parameters explored in this mini-grid of cloudy models
are summarized in Table 3.
Following the modeling procedure described in §4.2,
we evaluated the consistency of the various patchy wa-
ter cloud models with the NIRCam non-detection. The
results are presented in Figure 9. The inclusion of wa-
ter clouds suppresses the F444W flux in all models. For
a subset, where the cloud-free counterparts were previ-
ously inconsistent (§4.2), the water clouds bring the flux
below the NIRCam upper limit (example in left panel
of Figure 9). However, the suppression is not sufficient
to make the 200 K, < +0.5 dex metallicity models con-
sistent with the upper limit.
For both Teff = 175 K
and Teff = 200 K, the cloudy models still favor a metal-
enriched atmosphere for ϵ Eri b.
While the patchy cloud prescription represents a con-
servative scenario for cloud-induced flux suppression in
the F444W bandpass, it provides a more realistic sce-
nario for cold giant planets, as clouds are not expected to
homogeneously blanket their atmospheres, analogous to
the spatially heterogeneous band structures observed on
Jupiter (B. E. Carlson et al. 1992; M. Roos-Serote et al.
2000; J. Arregi et al. 2006). Fully cloudy models would
result in even greater flux suppression and would there-
fore remain consistent with the NIRCam upper limit.
In addition to H2O, NH3 can also condense to form
clouds in the upper atmosphere of these cold Jupiter
analogs (e.g., A. Burrows et al. 1997; D. Sudarsky et al.
2000, 2003).
These clouds are expected to condense
at low pressures, initially remaining optically thin and
becoming thicker as atmospheric temperatures decrease
(C. V. Morley et al. 2014b). Although NH3 clouds form
well above the expected water cloud decks, H2O clouds
can strongly influence the atmospheric thermal struc-
ture (temperature profile of the upper atmosphere) and,
in turn, affect whether NH3 clouds are expected to form.
NH3 clouds could also condense onto H2O ice particles,
producing mixed cloud compositions (R. Hu 2019; T.
Guillot et al. 2020). In this section, we presented pre-
liminary results on the possible presence of water clouds
in ϵ Eri b’s atmosphere. Given the interplay of physical
processes described above, modeling NH3 clouds would
be more relevant and informative once a direct detection
of the planet is obtained.
4.4. Lower Planet Mass as an Alternate Explanation
for ϵ Eri b’s Non-detection
The goal of this section is to investigate what mass
the NIRCam upper limit implies for ϵ Eri b if it’s at-
mosphere is neither metal-enriched nor cloudy. Thus,
for the following analysis, we use the solar metallicity,

5. CONSTRAINTS ON A POTENTIAL
PLANETARY RING SYSTEM
Ring systems are a characteristic property of the four
giant planets in our Solar System.
For mature exo-
planets, rings can influence planetary bulk properties,
atmospheric composition, and thermal structure (e.g.,
J. O’Donoghue et al. 2016; J. H. Waite et al. 2018).
They may additionally hold clues to a giant planet’s dy-
namical evolutionary history (e.g., R. M. Canup 2010;
A. J. Hesselbrock & D. A. Minton 2019). Recently, R.
Bowens-Rubin et al. (2025a) suggested the possibility of
detecting large exoring systems in near-infrared (2 µm)
reflected-light imaging with the NIRCam coronagraph.
While ϵ Eri b was not detected in our F210M images,

6. FUTURE OPPORTUNITIES FOR DIRECT
DETECTION
The quest to image our closest Jupiter-analog, ϵ Eri b,
is far from complete. With improved understanding of
current instrument performance and systematics, the
development of novel data processing techniques, and
the upcoming first light of next-generation ground- and
space-based facilities, numerous avenues are opening up
to push current sensitivity limits and detect ϵ Eri b.
6.1. Additional JWST Observations
Can JWST still image ϵ Eri b? To address this ques-
tion, we estimate the planet-to-star contrast ratio and
ϵ Eri b’s apparent magnitude in the NIRCam and MIRI

Among the NIRCam filters, F480M is the most

6.2. Roman Coronagraph Instrument
The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, planned
to launch in late 2026, will be the first space-based ob-
servatory to demonstrate high-contrast imaging with ac-
tive wavefront control using its Coronagraph Instrument
(N. J. Kasdin et al. 2020; I. Poberezhskiy et al. 2021; B.
Mennesson et al. 2022). Current best estimates of in-
strument performance on orbit achieve slightly better
than 10−8 contrast (E. Cady et al. 2025; H. Zhou et al.
2025). This unlocks the exciting capability to directly
image mature gas giants in reflected light for the first
time. Here, we evaluate the opportunity to directly im-
age ϵ Eri b with the Roman Coronagraph, in light of
the JWST/NIRCam non-detection. We note that the
following analysis is also broadly applicable to potential
observations with the ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC)
on the Lazuli Space Observatory (A. Roy et al. 2026),
a recently announced addition to the Eric and Wendy
Schmidt Observatory System.

7. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we presented the most sensitive search
for the nearest Jupiter-analog exoplanet, ϵ Eri b, at 4–
5 µm (F444W) using the JWST/NIRCam coronagraph.
The planet continues to evade direct imaging detection,
but, in the process, reveals more about itself. Our main
conclusions are summarized below:
1. The observations achieved a 5σ contrast sensitiv-
ity ≈3.0 × 10−7 (∆= 16.3 mag) at the expected
planet separation of ≈1′′. This is the deepest con-
trast achieved with the NIRCam coronagraph at
these separations to date.
2. We combined multiple independent estimates in
literature to calculate a rotation period of 11.60 ±
0.16 d for ϵ Eri. Using the gyrochronology age esti-
mation code gyro-interp from L. G. Bouma et al.
(2023a), we updated ϵ Eri’s age to 1.1 ± 0.1 Gyr.
This is significantly older than previous estimates
between 400–800 Myr and impacts the expected
temperature, and thus, flux, of the planet.
3. For a dynamical mass ≈1 MJup and the new gyro-
age estimate, we found ϵ Eri b’s Teff ∈[150, 200] K
and R ∈[0.9, 1.1] RJup using the latest genera-
tion of evolutionary models. Evolutionary model-
predicted F444W fluxes (≈17.3 mag, assuming
solar metallicity, solar C/O ratio, chemical equi-
librium) for a 1 MJup planet are inconsistent with
the NIRCam upper limit (≈17.95 mag) indicating
that there is significant suppression of the 4–5 µm
flux.
4. A consistency analysis with cloud-free Sonora
Flame Skimmer models and custom PICASO
patchy water cloud models (bracketing the above
fundamental parameters) found that the flux sup-
pression can be explained by enhanced atmo-
spheric metallicity and/or the presence of water ice
clouds. This indicates ϵ Eri b’s atmosphere shares
similarities with Jupiter’s atmosphere.
Consid-
ering all models consistent with the upper limit,
the F444W flux is expected to be between 18.0–
21.4 mag, with a mean value of 19.1 mag.
5. An alternate explanation is that the planet is
< 1 MJup. Using the Sonora Flame Skimmer so-
lar metallicity evolutionary models, we found that
ϵ Eri b would have to be ≲0.81 MJup to explain
the NIRCam non-detection. This limit is 2σ away
from the latest dynamical mass (0.98±0.09 MJup)
but consistent with other estimates in literature.
6. The NIRCam/F210M observations achieved a 5σ
contrast sensitivity ≈1.3 × 10−7 at ϵ Eri b’s sep-
aration.
Assuming an optically thick ring with
negligible 2 µm thermal emission and Saturn-like
ring albedo (Ag = 0.36), the F210M non-detection
ruled out ring systems with visible reflecting areas
≳30× the area of Saturn’s rings. We also found
that achieving a contrast limit of 10−8–10−7 in
the shorter wavelength NIRCam filters (F070W,
F090W, F115W) could enable the detection of ex-

area of Saturn’s ring.
7. There is an opportunity at the beginning of 2027,
but before the beginning of 2028, to image ϵ Eri b
with the Roman Coronagraph near its most op-
timal phase angle (α = 50◦), requiring a Band 1
contrast between 10−9–10−8 for detection. This
range encompasses both clear and cloudy atmo-
sphere scenarios and the various possible planet
radii. Follow-up imaging in Band 4 after an initial
detection could help constrain the planet’s cloudi-
ness. We also discussed additional opportunities
with JWST and EELT/METIS.
This JWST/NIRCam search represents yet another
step down the contrast ladder and towards the direct
detection and characterization of mature gas giants. As
a younger analog to Jupiter, ϵ Eri b remains one of the
most exciting targets for detailed atmospheric charac-
terization, investigating giant planet formation, study-
ing planet-disk interactions, and conducting compara-
tive planetology studies to place the Solar System in
the broader exoplanetary context.

APPENDIX
A. EFFECT OF SYSTEMATIC UNCERTAINTIES ON AGE AND EFFECTIVE TEMPERATURE
In §2, we obtained an age estimate of 1.1 ± 0.1 Gyr from stellar gyrochronology. Ages < 800 Myr are ruled out at
> 3σ for this revised age. However, there are two sources of uncertainty that may reduce the precision of the above
age estimate. First, the analysis used a rotation period with a 1.4% uncertainty and a stellar effective temperature
with a 0.6% uncertainty. Potential systematic errors in the above input parameters to gyro-interp can modify the
age posterior. For example, if we assume a 5% uncertainty on the rotation period (due to biases induced by variation
in spot latitudes) and 2% uncertainty on the stellar effective temperature (comparable to the systematic noise floor
estimated by J. Tayar et al. 2022), the estimated age is 1.10+0.14
−0.17 Gyr. This brings a younger age (700–800 Myr)
within the 3σ range. Additionally, in Figure 1, we observe a few outlier cases in Praesepe, stars with slower rotation
periods than ϵ Eri. This raises the question whether ϵ Eri could similarly be a younger but slowly rotating outlier.
The current model for intrinsic dispersion in the rotation period-effective temperature relations does not include the
Praesepe outliers (L. G. Bouma et al. 2023a). However, the astrophysical reason for the existence of these outliers is
not known and it is not possible to confidently ascertain whether ϵ Eri belongs to this category of objects. As such,
based on the above, if we assume a younger age for ϵ Eri (e.g., 700–800 Myr), the inferred planet temperature using
evolutionary models lies between 160–225 K, which significantly overlaps with the 150–200 K planet temperatures
already considered in our analysis. For a planet temperature of 225 K, consistency with the non-detection requires
an atmospheric metallicity = +1.0 dex in the cloud-free scenario. The optical thickness of water clouds decreases for
increasing temperature. Thus, the inclusion of water clouds would not change the requirement of enhanced metallicity
(already the case at 200 K, see Figure 9). Overall, potential systematic uncertainties in the age estimate do not affect
the conclusions of this work.
