Abstract
Recently, a new paradigm was introduced in automata theory. The main idea is to classify regular
languages according to their propensity to be sorted, establishing a deep connection between automata
theory and data compression [J. ACM 2023]. This parameterization leads to two hierarchies of regular
languages: a deterministic hierarchy and a non-deterministic hierarchy. While the deterministic
hierarchy is well understood, the non-deterministic hierarchy appears much more complex. This
is true even for the richest and most studied level of the hierarchies, corresponding to the class of
Wheeler languages.
In this paper, we study Wheeler language through the lens of bisimulations. We first show that
the standard notion of bisimulation is not appropriate. Then, we introduce Wheeler bisimulations,
that is, bisimulations that respect the convex structure of the considered Wheeler automata.
Although there are some differences between the properties of bisimulations and the properties of
Wheeler bisimulations, we show that Wheeler bisimulations induce a unique minimal Wheeler NFA
(analogously to standard bisimulations). In particular, in the deterministic case, we retrieve the
minimum Wheeler deterministic automaton of a given language. We also show that the minimal
Wheeler NFA induced by Wheeler bisimulations can be built in linear time. This is in contrast with
standard bisimulations, for which the corresponding minimal NFA can be built in O(m log n) time
(where m is the number of edges and n is the number of states) by adapting Paige-Tarjan’s partition
refinement algorithm.

Introduction
A basic yet fundamental idea in the design of algorithms is sorting: by imposing an order on
our data, we can typically solve queries more efficiently. Recently, this paradigm was extended
to automata and regular languages [23]. Recall that the co-lexicographic order on strings is
obtained from the standard dictionary order by comparing two given strings starting from
the end (and not the beginning). The idea is to (partially) sort the states of an automaton
in a way that is consistent with the strings reaching each state. Consider Figure 1. The set
of all strings that we can read from the initial state s to u is Iu = {ab, aab, aaab, aaaab, . . . }
and the set of all strings that we can read from s to v is Iv = {cb, cab, caab, caaab, . . . }.
Then, every string in Iu is co-lexicographically smaller than every string in Iv, so we can say
that the state u is smaller than the state v. However, we cannot unambiguously compare
states u′ and v′, because b, bc ∈Iu′, c ∈Iv′, b is co-lexicographically smaller than c, but bc is
co-lexicographically larger than c.
To every automaton, we can associate an integer parameter 1 ≤p ≤n (called the width
of the automaton), where n is the number of states. Intuitively, the smaller p, the more the
partial order that we have described is close to being a total order. In particular, we have a
total order if and only if p = 1. The parameter p establishes unexpected connections between
automata theory, algorithms and data compression. Consider a non-deterministic automaton
A with n states, e edges and width p on an alphabet of size σ.
The DFA obtained from A through the powerset construction (the classical algorithm for
converting a non deterministic automaton into a deterministic automaton that recognizes
the same language, see [46]) has at most 2p(n−p+1)−1 states [25]. In the worst case, we
have p = n, and we retrieve the well-known upper bound 2n−1 (for incomplete automata),
which is tight [41]. This result implies, for example, that the PSPACE-complete problem
of deciding whether two non-deterministic automata recognize the same language [49] is
fixed-parameter tractable with respect to p.
The automaton A can be stored using only e(log σ + 2 log p) + O(e) bits. Notice that at
the very least we need e log σ bits to store the edge labels (we need log σ bits per edge),
so intuitively p captures the topological complexity of A.
Given a string of length m, in O(mp2 log log(pσ)) time we can decide whether the string
is accepted by A (by only using the compressed representation described in the previous
point). For general automata, the complexity is Ω(me1−ϵ), where ϵ > 0 is an arbitrarily
small constant (under the Orthogonal Vector hypothesis) [30]. This means that for small
values of p we can do better.

Intuitively, these widths capture the propensity of a regular language (not a specific automaton
recognizing the language!) to be sorted. In this way, we obtain two parameterizations of
the class of all regular languages, the deterministic hierarchy and the non-deterministic
hierarchy. The deterministic hierarchy is well understood: every regular language has a
canonical automaton, the Hasse automaton [23]. However, the non-deterministic hierarchy
appears much more complex [19, 10].
To better understand the role of nondeterminism when sorting automata, let us focus
on the first level of the two hierarchies. Intuitively, this corresponds to the case p = 1,
so we only consider automata that can be totally ordered (for example, the automaton in
Figure 1 cannot be totally ordered because we cannot compare u′ and v′). These automata
are called Wheeler automata, and the languages recognized by these automata are called
Wheeler languages [31, 4]. Wheeler automata have close connections with applications in
bioinformatics (in particular, with de Bruijn graphs [13]), and Wheeler languages have
been extensively studied. Indeed, the class of Wheeler languages is one of the most elegant
subclasses of regular languages:
If a regular language is recognized by some Wheeler non-deterministic automaton, it is
also recognized by some Wheeler deterministic automaton [5].
Every Wheeler language admits a unique minimal Wheeler deterministic automaton
(which, in general, is larger than the standard minimal automaton), and a Wheeler
deterministic automaton can be minizimed efficiently [3].
Wheeler language admits an algebraic characterization based on the Myhill-Nerode theorem
[42, 43].
It is possible to efficiently detect if a regular language is Wheeler by looking at the cycle
structure of its (standard) minimal automaton [9].
Among the research directions that have been considered, we mention studying string-labeled
Wheeler NFAs [20], the state complexity of Wheeler languages [27], their relationship with
locally testable languages [8], and their applications in data compression [17, 24, 6]. We refer
the reader to [22] for a recent survey on Wheeler automata and Wheeler languages.
1.1
Our Contribution
In this paper, we study the relationship between Wheeler languages and nondeterminism
through the lens of bisimulations. The idea of bisimulation was first introduced by Milner
and Park in the early 1980s to model processes that cannot be distinguished by an external
agent [40, 45]. The intuition is that, in the deterministic setting, the Myhill-Nerode theorem
provides a smooth connection between automata and equivalence relations, which leads to a
unique minimal deterministic automaton for every regular language, and any deterministic
automata can be minimized efficiently through Hopcroft’s algorithm ([33], see also [52]). In
the non-deterministic setting, a similar algebraic characterization is missing, the uniqueness
of a minimal automaton is lost, and the minimization problem becomes PSPACE-complete
[49]. Bisimulations make it possible to retrieve a cleaner framework by adding additional
constraints: two bisimilar automata recognize the same language, but two automata that
recognize the same language need not be bisimilar. The idea of bisimilar systems goes beyond
automata theory and has been adapted to multiple formalisms for modeling processes, such
as Petri nets [53, 12] and Kripke structures [28]. This explains why bisimulation is a pervasive
notion that has influenced numerous areas, including modal checking, concurrency theory,
logic, programming languages and category theory. We refer the reader to standard books
on the topic [7, 48, 36, 2].

We start our investigation by observing that, in general, Wheeler languages are not
recognized by a unique minimal Wheeler NFA (Example 5), and the standard notion of
bisimulation is not appropriate (Example 8). Then, we introduce Wheeler bisimulations,
that is, bisimulations that respect the convex structure of the considered Wheeler automata.
Although there are some differences between the properties of bisimulations and the properties
of Wheeler bisimulations, we show that Wheeler bisimulations induce a unique minimal
Wheeler NFA (analogously to standard bisimulations), see Section 4.3. In particular, in the
deterministic case, we retrieve the minimum Wheeler deterministic automaton of a given
language. We also show that the minimal Wheeler NFA induced by Wheeler bisimulations
can be built in linear time, see Section 4.4. This is in contrast with standard bisimulations,
for which the corresponding minimal NFA can be built in O(m log n) time (where m is
the number of edges and n is the number of states) by adapting Paige-Tarjan’s partition
refinement algorithm. In addition, we present several related results, developing a rich theory
for Wheeler bisimulations.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss notation and preliminary
results. In Section 3, we introduce Wheeler automata. In Section 4, we describe the main
results and the key ideas of the paper, which are presented in detail in the subsequent sections.
In Section 9, we explore potential directions for future work.

2.2
Convexity
Our definition of Wheeler bisimulations will rely on convexity. Let Q be a set, and let ≤be
a total order on Q. Let C ⊆Q. We say that C is ≤-convex if for every u, v, w ∈Q such that
u ≤v ≤w and u, w ∈C we have v ∈C. In particular, ∅and Q are ≤-convex, and {u} is
≤-convex for every u ∈Q.
In general, if C1 and C2 are ≤-convex, then C1 ∩C2 need not be convex. For example,
if Q = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, ≤is the total order on Q such that 1 < 2 < 3 < 4 < 5, C1 = {1, 2}
and C2 = {4, 5}, then C1 and C2 are ≤-convex, but C1 ∪C2 is not ≤-convex. However, if
C1 ∩C2 ̸= ∅, then C1 ∪C2 must be convex, as shown in the following lemma.
▶Lemma 1. Let Q be a set, and let ≤be a total order on Q. Let C1 and C2 be ≤-convex
sets. If C1 ∩C2 ̸= ∅, then C1 ∪C2 is ≤-convex.
Lastly, we extend the notion of convexity to partitions and equivalence relations. Let
Q be a set, and let ≤be a total order. A partition P of Q is ≤-convex if every U ∈P is
≤-convex. An equivalence relation ≡on Q is ≤-convex if the partition P≡of Q is ≤-convex
(equivalently, if [u]≡is convex for every u ∈Q).
2.3
Automata
Let Σ be a fixed finite alphabet. We denote by Σ∗the set of all finite strings over Σ, where
ϵ ∈Σ∗is the empty string. As customary in the literature on Wheeler languages (e.g.,
[4, 23, 17]), we fix a total order ⪯on Σ. In our examples, we always assume that Σ is the
English alphabet and ⪯is the usual total order on Σ such that a ≺b, b ≺c, c ≺d, and so on.
Moreover, we extend ⪯to Σ∗co-lexicographically, that is, for every α ∈Σ∗we have α ≺β
if and only if the reserve string αR is smaller than the reverse string βR in the standard
dictionary order (e.g., dba ≺ca). Given a language L ⊆Σ∗, we denote by Pref(L) the set of
all prefixes of some element of L, that is, Pref(L) = {α ∈Σ∗| (∃α′ ∈Σ∗)(αα′ ∈L)}.
A non-deterministic finite automaton (NFA) is a 4-ple A = (Q, E, s, F), where Q is the
finite set of states, E ⊆Q × Q × Σ is the set of edges, s ∈Q is the initial state and F ⊆Q
is the set of final states. We denote by L(A) the language recognized by A, that is, the
set of all α ∈Σ such that α can be read by following some walk from s to a final state.
Throughout the paper, for every u ∈Q, we assume that u is reachable in A (that is, there
is a walk from the initial state s to u) and u is co-reachable in A (that is, either u ∈F, or
there is a walk from u to a final state). These are standard assumptions in automata theory
because, if L(A) ̸= ∅and a state u is not reachable or is not co-reachable, then it can be
removed without changing L(A). By convention, the empty language is recognized by the
empty automaton (the empty automaton will not be further considered in the paper).

4.2

Standard bisimulations induce a unique minimal NFA thanks to two key properties: (i) the
union of two bisimulations is always a bisimulation, and (ii) for every NFA A, there exists a

Wheeler autobisimulations slightly differently. The key idea is to add one more requirement,
namely, reflexivity. The intuition is that, in this way, we can use Lemma 1 to infer that the
union of Wheeler autobisimulations must be a Wheeler autobisimulation.

4.4
Algorithms
Given a NFA A = (Q, E, s, F), with |Q| = n and |E| = m, the minimal NFA A′ such that
there exists a bisimulation from A to A′ (unique up to isomorphism) can be computed in
O(m log n) time. In the literature (see, e.g., [7]), it has been generally assumed that an
algorithm with this running time can be obtained by a simple modification of (the elegant)
Paige-Tarjan’s partition refinement algorithm [44]. However, achieving O(m log n) time
requires much care with the implementation details, and this gap in the literature was filled
by Valmari only less than 20 years ago (see [51] for the algorithm and a historical account).
The bound O(m log n) is achieved under the (standard) assumption that the edge labels can
be sorted in linear time (see [51, Section 4.3]).
In our setting, we can do better: under the same assumption, we can achieve linear time
(while in the more general comparison model there is an Ω(m log m) lower bound, see Section
8.1). This is not entirely surprising because it is already known that a Wheeler DFA can be
minimized in linear time [3]. However, in the non-deterministic setting, the algorithm and the
correctness proof become much more involved. At the same time, our algorithm only requires
a queue to achieve linear time, while the algorithm in [3] relies on auxiliary data structures.
Intuitively, the effort required to obtain our algorithm from the less general algorithm in [3] is
comparable to the (significant) effort required to obtain Paige-Tarjan’s partition refinement
algorithm [44] from (the less general) Hopcroft’s algorithm for minimizing a DFA [33].
Here is the main result.
▶Theorem 15. Let (A, ≤) be a Wheeler NFA, with A = (Q, E, s, F). We can build the
Wheeler NFA (A∗, ≤∗) of Lemma 12 in O(|E|) time.
As a corollary, we can also check in linear time whether two Wheeler NFAs are in the
same ∼C-equivalence class.

Lower Bounds
Let (A, ≤) be a Wheeler NFA, with A = (Q, E, s, F). Theorem 15 states that we can build
the quotient Wheeler NFA in linear time, under the assumption that the edge labels can
be sorted in linear time. It is natural to wonder if it is possible to achieve linear time
even when the edge labels cannot be sorted in linear time. In principle, this does not seem
unreasonable: we know the Wheeler order ≤, and for every u, v ∈Q, if u < v, then by Axiom
2 all characters labeling some edge entering u must be smaller than all characters labeling
some edge entering v. In other words, the Wheeler order ≤inherently contains information
on the mutual order of the edge labels. However, from the Wheeler order we cannot retrieve
any information on the mutual order of the characters labeling some edge entering the same
state, and we will see that this prevents us from achieving linear time.
More concretely, we consider the comparison model, in which the only query allowed on
the elements of the alphabet Σ is the following: given a1, a2 ∈Σ, decide whether a1 ⪯a2.
The model assumes that each such query can be solved in O(1) time. It is well known
that the complexity of sorting n elements in the comparison model is Ω(n log n) (see, e.g.,
[35, 18]).
We now show that, in the comparison model, building the quotient NFA requires
Ω(|E| log |E|) time. To this end, let us recall the element distinctness problem. Given
a string T = b1b2 . . . bn of length n, the goal is to decide whether there exist 1 ≤i ≤j ≤n
such that bi = bj. In the comparison model, the complexity of the element distinctness
problem is also Ω(n log n) (see, e.g., [11, 21]).

Conclusions and Future Work
In this paper, we have only initiated the study of Wheeler bisimulations: the ubiquitous role
of bisimulations calls for a systematic investigation. On the one hand, Wheeler bisimulations
are of great potential in modal checking and concurrency theory, because they provide an
intriguing trade-off between generality and efficiency: while Wheeler bisimulations can only
describe systems that satisfy some convexity property, the natural algorithmic problems
in this class (such as checking whether there exists a Wheeler bisimulation between two
automata) can be solved more efficiently than in the general case. On the other hand,
Wheeler bisimulations give a better understanding of the non-deterministic hierarchy of
regular languages described in the introduction.
Definition 6 refers to the most popular notion of bisimulation, often called strong bisimu-
lation. However, many variants appear in the literature. The properties listed in Definition 6
require a bisimulation to be propagated when following edges in a forward fashion, but we
may require the same compatibility to hold when edges are followed in a backward fashion
(or both in a forward fashion and a backward fashion). In addition, several notions of weak
bisimulation have been proposed (such as stutter bisimulation and normed bisimulation),
which typically still induce a unique minimal NFA. Usually, every (strong) bisimulation is a
weak bisimulation, so weak bisimulations yield smaller minimal NFAs. For details, we refer
the reader to [15, 7]. Moreover, as we have seen in the introduction, bisimulations appear in
different domains, so there has been much effort to provide a unified view of bisimulation
[47], also via algebraic and co-algebraic approaches [39, 1]. This means that our theory of
Wheeler bisimulations can be extended in two directions: (i) by modifying the definition

The definitions of bisimulation and Wheeler bisimulation are symmetrical: each property

Definition 9 (because Property 2 and Property 3 are symmetrical).
One important point that needs further clarification is the relationship between Wheeler
bisimulations and logic. It is well known that the largest bisimulation on an NFA can be
characterized through several logics, notably CTL and CTL* ([16], see also [7]). It is not
obvious to extend these results to Wheeler automata because, given a Wheeler NFA (A, ≤),
there is not a simple relationship between the largest bisimulation ≡A on A (which is always
an equivalence relationship) and the largest Wheeler bisimulation ≡A,≤defined in Theorem

Wheeler languages admit a unique minimal deterministic automaton but, in the higher
levels of the deterministic hierarchy of regular languages described in the introduction, the
uniqueness is lost [23]. The uniqueness is also lost if we consider deterministic generalized
automata, in which edges can be labeled with strings (and not only characters) [32]. Wheeler
bisimulations might be the first step required to introduce an elegant and meaningful notion
of uniqueness in these more general settings.
In general, the relationship between Wheeler languages and logic is not well understood.
We have seen in the introduction that Wheeler languages enjoy the typical properties of
the class of regular languages: determinism and non-determinism have the same expressive
power, there exists a unique minimal Wheeler DFA, and Wheeler languages admit a Myhill-
Nerode theorem. Nonetheless, no logical characterization of Wheeler languages is known,
while regular languages can be characterized via the monadic second-order logic [50]. A
characterization of Wheeler languages in terms of syntactic monoids is also missing.
Consider the class of all regular languages. Bisimulations lead to the most interesting
notion of uniqueness (up to isomorphism) for NFAs, but other formalisms achieve the same

for residual automata. Indeed, residual automata are closely related to the Myhill-Nerode
