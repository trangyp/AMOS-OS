ABSTRACT
We used MIST isochrone fitting and a dedicated grid of stellar evolution models computed with MESA to constrain the ages of the
components of the γ Persei binary system. While individual stars can be matched to the models at specific metallicities, no joint
isochrone solution reproduces both the observed masses and evolutionary states.
The stellar evolutionary tracks calculated by MESA reveal a clear evolutionary mismatch. The primary component of the system is in
a post–main-sequence phase consistent with the red giant branch or red clump. In contrast, the lighter secondary component lies near
the turn-off point of the main-sequence or is in the early phase of the subgiant branch.
This discrepancy can be overcome by assuming that the γ Persei system was born as a triple and the primary component is a rejuve-
nated star formed through a merger of a close-by pair of main-sequence stars. We show that the merger must have occurred no later
than a few hundred Myr after system formation, and the progenitor masses of the merging stars are restricted by a combination of
stars that fall within a narrow band in the (M1,a, M1,b) plane, corresponding to M1,a ≃0.9–2.1 M⊙and M1,b ≃2.3–2.5 M⊙.

1. Introduction
γ Persei is a known, long-periodic spectroscopic binary system,
with an orbital cycle of ∼14.6 years. Its binary nature was
first mentioned by Maury & Pickering (1897); Campbell (1908,
1909), and the orbital period was first determined by McLaugh-
lin (1948) from radial velocity measurements. Although the sys-
tem is bright enough to be visible to the naked eye, the first total
eclipse was observed only in 1990 by Griffin et al. (1994).
The system has since been the focus of diverse research
using several observational methods, which include both vi-
sual (Hartkopf et al. 2001) and spectroscopic observations
(McWilliam 1990; Pourbaix 1999, 2000; Pourbaix et al. 2004;
Piccotti et al. 2020; Diamant et al. 2023), revealing a slight
metallicity deficit, compared to the Sun ([Fe/H] ≃−0.19 −
(−0.20) in the literature). Moreover, Pourbaix et al. (2004) clas-
sified both components as luminosity class III giants, based on
detailed spectroscopic data. Orbital elements of the system were
calculated by Pourbaix (1999), confirming that the system has
a highly eccentric orbit (e = 0.785). Based on its parallax
(14.1252”), the distance of γ Persei is measured to be ∼70.8
pc (Gaia Collaboration 2020).
The system is a member of the ζ Aurigae class (Ake & Grif-
fin 2015). This class consists of double-lined binaries with a cool
G–K giant primary and a hotter A-type secondary. In the case
of γ Persei, the primary component is a luminous G-type giant
with a Johnson V-band magnitude of 2.91 (Ginestet & Carquillat
2002), dominating the system’s brightness. The secondary star
is significantly fainter, with an estimated V magnitude of 4.54.

The system’s total brightness is V = 2.93 and B = 3.63 mag,
with eclipse depths of 0.28 mag in V and 0.54 mag in B (Griffin
et al. 1994). These values yield to the individual color-indices
of (B −V) = +0.96 mag for the primary and +0.11 mag for
the companion component, consistent with a G-type and A-type
classification, respectively.
Following the first documented 1990 primary eclipse, the
next event in 2019 was reported by Diamant et al. (2023), en-
abling a precise refinement of the orbital period. The previous
eclipse in 2005 occurred close to the solar conjunction, thus
impossible to observe from Earth. It was later recovered and
published in a recent work of Ádám & Molnár (2025), based
on archival data from the Solar Mass Ejection Imager (SMEI),
thereby filling the observational gap between the two known
events and further strengthening the orbital period determina-
tion.
The 2019 eclipse was observed using DSLR CMOS pho-
tometry, which was calibrated to the 1990 Johnson B-band light
curve using spectrophotometric reference data (Diamant et al.
2023). This approach allowed the DSLR measurements to be
transformed onto a standard photometric scale, enabling direct
comparison with the earlier eclipse and precise timing analy-
sis. The calibration was sufficient to refine the orbital period to
5329.0±0.05 days, in agreement with the previous spectroscopic
data. Additional partial eclipse phases were sampled by Telesco-
pio Internacional de Guanajuato Robótico-Espectroscópico (TI-
GRE) spectra and Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS)
photometry, the latter resolving the first contact phase and total-
ity with high cadence, ending right around fourth contact. From
the eclipse geometry and projected orbital velocity (∼40 km/s),

loss, dynamical capture, and episodic mass transfer during the

evolved jointly without significant disturbance, consistent with
the present-day configuration of γ Persei. In the following, we

potential co-evolutionary configurations, focusing on metallicity

2. Methods
2.1. MIST-Based Isochrone Model Setup
To validate co-evolution of the binary system of the γ Persei,
the isochrone package of the MESA Isochrones & Stellar Tracks
(MIST) database (Dotter 2016; Choi et al. 2016) was utilized.
The package offers a comprehensive grid for stellar evolution-
ary modelling, providing isochrones and synthetic photometry
across a broad range of physical parameters. The full metallicity
([Fe/H]) range supported by MIST spans from −4.0 to +0.5 dex.
In our analysis, we varied metallicity with [Fe/H] values from
−0.2 and −0.19 down to −2.0 dex to probe lower-metallicity
regimes as well.
Synthetic magnitudes were extracted for the Gaia photo-
metric system, with extinction (Av) fixed at 0.0 for all mod-
els, although the database covers a range of Av between 0 and
6. Isochrones for both rotational configurations available in the
grid were investigated: v/v(crit) = 0.0, representing non-rotating
stars, and v/v(crit) = 0.4, corresponding to moderately rotating
stars. Here, v(crit) denotes the critical rotation velocity at which
centrifugal acceleration at the stellar equator balances gravita-
tional acceleration. Exceeding this threshold, the star would be
disrupted by centrifugal forces.
For the zeroth-order constraint, we adopted the standard
MIST age grid, which consists of 107 discrete age points uni-
formly spaced in log(Age [yr]) from 5.0 to 10.3 dex in 0.05 dex
increments. Upon identifying the subset of isochrones that pro-
vided satisfactory fits to both stellar components, we refined the
age resolution locally by resampling the grid in steps of 0.01 dex,
allowing for more precise age determination for the system.
2.2. MESA Stellar Evolution Tracks
In addition to the parameter space offered by the MIST grid
(e.g., rotation and metallicity), we performed a set of stellar
evolution calculations using the stable version of 23.05.1 of the
open-source ’Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics’
(MESA) software package (Paxton et al. 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018,
2019; Jermyn et al. 2023). All evolutionary tracks are available
under the Zenodo doi: 10.5281/zenodo.17229941.
2.2.1. Metallicity, Heavy Element Abundance and Stellar
Mass Grid
For each component of the binary system, we constructed a
mass grid centered at 3.5 ± 0.3 M⊙and 2.4 ± 0.2 M⊙, respec-
tively, with a resolution of 0.1 M⊙(Griffin et al. 1994; Diamant
et al. 2023; Ádám & Molnár 2025, Ádám et al., in prep.). This
results in 7 and 5 mass points per star. The primary compo-
nent was evolved up to the Terminal Age Core Helium Burning
(TACHeB, stop_at_phase_TACHeB = .true.) phase, while
the secondary was evolved to the tip of the Red Giant Branch
(RGB, stop_at_phase_He_Burn = .true.). We adopted a
solar composition for both stars and varied the metallicity sepa-
rately for each star: [Fe/H] = −0.19 for the primary and [Fe/H] =
−0.20 for the secondary, each with a local grid of ±0.1 dex in 0.1
dex steps, yielding 3 metallicity values per component (Rosas-
Portilla et al. 2024; Ádám & Molnár 2025). These values corre-
spond to Z ≃0.0089 and Z ≃0.0084, respectively, assuming the
relation of

2.2.5. Spatial and Temporal Resolution
The spatial and temporal resolution of the models can be
adjusted by modifying MESA’s default refinement parameters,
namely mesh_delta_coeff and time_delta_coeff (see,
e.g., Li & Joyce 2025). These coefficients scale the internal
adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) scheme used by MESA. In the
present work, we retained the default resolution settings, with
mesh_delta_coeff = 1.0 and time_delta_coeff = 1.0.
For improved numerical stability and energy conservation,
the stellar energy equation was solved using the dedt for-
mulation (energy_eqn_option = ’dedt’). In addition, the
make_gradr_sticky_in_solver_iters option was activated
to enhance convergence at the interfaces between convective and
radiative regions (see, Paxton et al. 2018, 2019).
2.3. Matching Ages for Different Evolutionary Tracks
In order to identify which combinations of model configurations
yield a consistent age for both stellar components, we used a
dedicated Python (Van Rossum & Drake 2009) script (based on
pandas, numpy, and matplotlib; McKinney 2010; van der Walt
et al. 2011; Hunter 2007), to compare evolutionary tracks and
extract age-matching solutions.
The analysis proceeded through the following steps:
– First, it identifies where each stellar component intersects the
calculated evolutionary track beyond the pre-main-squence
phase within the observational uncertainties listed in Table 1.
– In cases where no track point falls explicitly within the ob-
servational error box, but the track segment crosses it, the
script estimates the intersection age via linear interpolation
between the segment endpoints. This allows us to identify
age-matching solutions even when no direct data point lies
within the uncertainty region.
– Finally, the script compares the resulting age distributions
for both stars and identifies model configuration pairs that
yield the smallest age difference. These are considered the
best candidates for a physically consistent binary solution.
As an additional validation step, we applied a threshold of
0.1 dex in log10(age) to identify model configuration pairs with
sufficiently consistent ages. This can be calculated as follows:

3. Results
In this section, we present our results on matching the ages of
the two components of the γ Persei binary system.
3.1. MIST Isochrone Fitting
As a first approach, we examined the stellar parameters of the
two stars in the system using MIST isochrones. The results of
this analysis is listed in Table 4.
While the secondary component of the system can be con-
sistently matched to MIST isochrones across the full range of
metallicities explored, the primary component cannot be fitted
in all cases. In particular, for metallicities of [Fe/H] = −0.1,
−2.5, and −3.0, the primary component of the γ Persei system
cannot be fitted on the given isochrones from the MIST database.
Furthermore, a joint fit for both components is only achiev-
able for metallicities [Fe/H] = −1.0 and [Fe/H] = −1.5. In the
case of [Fe/H] = −1.5, both stars can be placed on a single
isochrone with ages in the range 728 −871 Myr. In this case,
the inferred masses are 1.96–1.84 M⊙for γ Persei 1 and 1.93–
1.80 M⊙for γ Persei 2. Similarly, for [Fe/H] = −1.0, a com-
mon isochrone solution is found at ages 708 −794 Myr, yielding
masses of 2.17–2.08 M⊙for the primary and 1.98–1.89 M⊙for
the secondary.
However, in both cases the inferred stellar masses and metal-
licities differ substantially from the values reported in the lit-
erature (see Table 1). Consequently, although formal isochrone
solutions exist for certain metallicities, they are astrophysically
implausible.

3.3. Evolutionary State of the Two Components
The locations of the two components of the γ Persei system were
examined in detail using the MESA evolutionary tracks described
in Section 2.2. Representative evolutionary tracks are shown in
Figures 1 and 2, with the observationally determined positions
of the two stars overplotted. Appendices A and B contain the
full set of evolutionary tracks for the whole explored parameter-
space (see Figures A.1 – A.3 and B.1 – B.3).
For the primary, we consistently find that its position in the
Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (HRD) is near or beyond the red
giant branch. Depending on the adopted model parameters, the
star is placed onto the HRD either: (i) near the luminosity dip
at the end of the subgiant branch, (ii) in the red clump phase,
corresponding to stable core helium-burning, or (iii) on the red
giant branch or in the early phase of the asymptotic giant branch
(see Figure 1).
Their position on the HRD shows only a mild dependence
on the adopted input parameters (see Figures . In general, lower
metallicity or larger overshoot values tend to shift the primary
component toward the red clump, while higher metallicity or
smaller overshoot favour a location near the subgiant-branch dip.
However, across all combinations of αMLT, [Fe/H], and over-
shoot, the primary remains in a post–main-sequence phase (see
Figures A.1–A.3).
Considering the secondary component, the dependence on
the input parameters is similarly modest. However, we found
that only models within the 2.2–2.4 M⊙range align with the po-
sition of the star (see, e.g., Figure B.1). Higher-mass tracks (e.g.
2.6 M⊙) do not fit the measurements of the star for any combi-
nation of αMLT, [Fe/H], or overshoot (see Figures B.1–B.3). This
yields a more precise mass determination for this component of
the system, effectively tightening the observational limits for the
given set of model assumptions.

MESA evolutionary tracks indicate that the position of this com-
ponent is either close to the turn-off point of the main-sequence
or on the very early phase of the subgiant branch (see Figure 2).
The combination of these evolutionary states implies a sub-
stantial discrepancy between the ages inferred for the two com-
ponents under the assumption of single-star evolution. While
the secondary component requires an age sufficient to reach he-
lium ignition, the primary component remains near the main-
sequence turn-off or early subgiant phase. This evolutionary mis-
match persists across the explored range of stellar parameters.

5. Conclusions
In this work, we investigated whether the present-day configura-
tion of the γ Persei system can be explained under the assump-
tion of coeval formation and standard single-star evolution, using
the observational constraints reported in the literature (e.g., Grif-
fin et al. 1994; Pourbaix et al. 2004; Diamant et al. 2023; Ádám
& Molnár 2025; Rosas-Portilla et al. 2024) and in the compan-
ion paper presenting the asteroseismic analysis of the primary
(Ádám et al., in prep.).
Isochrone fitting based on the MIST database demonstrates
that, although formal coeval solutions exist for certain metallic-
ities, these solutions require stellar masses and chemical com-
positions that are inconsistent with the directly measured sys-
tem parameters. In particular, joint isochrone solutions system-

atically favor significantly lower masses and more metal-poor

therefore conclude that MIST-based coeval solutions are astro-

To further investigate the coevality of the system, we con-
structed a grid of stellar evolution models using MESA. Across
all the applied model parameters, we consistently found an evo-
lutionary mismatch between the two components. The primary
component is found to be in a post-main-sequence evolutionary
phase, i.e., on the red giant branch, or most likely, in the red
clump. In contrast, the secondary component lies close to the
turn-off point of the main-sequence or at the very beginning of
the subgiant branch.
To overcome this discrepancy, we conclude that the primary
component of the system is a rejuvenated (former blue strag-
gler) object formed through a merger of two main-squence stars
(e.g., Glebbeek & Pols 2008; Schneider et al. 2016; Schneider
2025). In this scenario, the present-day, the secondary compo-
nent reflects the true age of the system, while the merger product
appears significantly younger due to its placement on the HRD
and the replenishment of hydrogen in its core.
This suggests that the γ Persei system formed as a triple-star
system, where an inner, closer binary merged during the main-
sequence phase, and the outer component evolved mainly undis-
turbed. Using the evolutionary state of the non-merged star as
an age anchor, we calculated the true age of the system to be
∼750–900 Myr. The merger is constrained to have occurred
∼500–775 Myr after system formation, and the total mass of
the progenitor binary is limited to 3.4–4.2 M⊙.
Furthermore, we conclude that the progenitor masses do not
span independent intervals for M1,a and M1,b, but must instead
lie within the narrow diagonal band shown in Fig. 5. Within this
band, assuming that M1,b ≥M1,a, the primary component is con-
strained by the turn-off mass as an upper limit and MTO/2 as
a lower limit, M1,b falls within a mass range of M1,b ≃1.7–
2.5 M⊙). To the contrary, the lower mass companion lies within
the range M1,a ≃0.9–2.1 M⊙, depending on the total mass of the
system.
