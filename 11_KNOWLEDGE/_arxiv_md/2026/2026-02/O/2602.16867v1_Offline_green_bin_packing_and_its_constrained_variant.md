Abstract
In this paper, we study the green bin packing (GBP) problem where β ≥0 and G ∈[0, 1]
are two given values as part of the input. The energy consumed by a bin is max{0, β(x −G)}
where x is the total size of the items packed into the bin. The GBP aims to pack all items into
a set of unit-capacity bins so that the number of bins used plus the total energy consumption is
minimized. When β = 0 or G = 1, GBP is reduced to the classic bin packing (BP) problem. In
the constrained green bin packing (CGBP) problem, the objective is to minimize the number of
bins used to pack all items while the total energy consumption does not exceed a given upper
bound U. We present an APTAS and a 3
2-approximation algorithm for both GBP and CGBP,
where the ratio 3
2 matches the lower bound of BP.
Keywords: Green bin packing; constrained green bin packing; approximation scheme; offline

Introduction
In the classic bin packing [6] (BP) problem, we are given a set of items where the size of item i is
denoted by si ∈(0, 1] and an infinity number of unit-capacity bins. The item i can be placed into a
bin only when the remaining capacity of the bin is at least si. The objective of BP is to minimize
the number of bins used to pack all items. Recently, the authors [2] proposed a new variant of
BP, called green bin packing (GBP), in which each used bin incurs a certain amount of energy
consumption. In detail, suppose G ∈[0, 1] and the capacity within [0, G] is defined as the green
space, which does not consume any energy. However, using each unit of the capacity outside the
green space will incur energy consumption β ≥0. Therefore, if the total size of the items packed
into a bin is x, then the energy consumed by the bin is fβ,G(x) = max{0, β(x −G)} as shown in
Definition 1. The GBP aims to pack all items into bins so that the number of opened bins plus the
total energy consumption is minimized. One clearly sees that GBP is reduced to BP when β = 0
or G = 1 and thus, GBP must be NP-hard. As mentioned in [2], GBP is motivated by practical
scenarios in cloud computing. One such scenario is greening geographical load balancing [12, 11], in
which each data center has a limited capacity (i.e., the quantity G in GBP) of carbon-free energy
but additional energy is required when the demand exceeds the capacity.
If all sizes as well as G and β are given, then both BP and GBP are offline. By contrast, in
the online setting, items arrive one by one and each item should be packed into an existing or
newly opened bin upon its arrival before the next item comes. In [2], the authors mainly studied
the online GBP from the perspective of online algorithms. Next, we introduce the corresponding
results for online BP and GBP even though this paper focuses on the offline setting.
1.1
Online BP and GBP
Given an online minimization problem, we let OPT(I) denote the objective value of an optimal
offline solution for an instance I. Similarly, we let A(I) denote the objective value of the solution
produced by a specific online algorithm A for I. If for every instance I, we have A(I) ≤α·OPT(I),
then we say A is α-competitive, where the value α is also called the absolute competitive ratio. On
the other hand, if there exists another value γ ≥0, independent of the instance I, such that
A(I) ≤α · OPT(I) + γ, then we say A is asymptotic α-competitive and α (respectively, γ) is the
asymptotic competitive ratio (respectively, additive term) for A. In other words, A is asymptotic
α-competitive if A(I) ≤α · OPT(I) when OPT(I) tends to infinity.
On one hand, the authors in [1] showed that no algorithm for online BP can achieve an absolute
competitive ratio smaller than 248
161 ≈1.54037. On the other hand, online BP has been extensively
studied in the literature and many well-known algorithms, for example, Next Fit, First Fit and
Harmonic, are presented for it. Next Fit [7] places each item into the most recently opened bin if
possible; otherwise, it creates a new bin for the item. The absolute competitive ratio of Next Fit
is 2 and the ratio is tight. If each item is packed into the earliest opened bin in which it fits, then
Next Fit reduces to First Fit [7], another fundamental algorithm for BP. In [14], the author showed
that First Fit is asymptotic 1.7-competitive with an additive term 3. The ratio 1.7 is asymptotic
tight since there exists an instance I such that OPT(I) = 10 and the items in I can be packed
into 17 bins by First Fit [14]. The additive term 3 experienced several improvements. Johnson
et al. improved the additive term from 3 to 2 [8], which was further improved to 0.9 in [5] and
to 0.7 in [15]. Finally, D´osa and Sgall [4] proved that for every instance I, First Fit uses at most
⌊1.7·OPT(I)⌋bins to pack all items in I, which settled the additive term in the competitive analysis
of First Fit. Given a parameter H, the Harmonic algorithm first partitions the interval (0, 1] into

1.2
Offline GBP and its constrained variant
Given an offline minimization problem, we say that an offline algorithm A is α-approximation if for
every instance I, we have A(I) ≤α·OPT(I) where A(I) and OPT(I) denote the objective values of
the solution produced by A and an optimal solution, respectively. The value α is referred to as the
absolute approximation ratio. Similarly to online algorithms, if A(I) ≤α·OPT(I)+γ, then we say
A is asymptotic α-approximation, where α is called the asymptotic approximation ratio and γ ≥0
is the additive term. An asymptotic polynomial time approximation scheme (APTAS) is a family
of offline approximation algorithms such that given a fixed 0 < ϵ ≤1, it contains an approximation
algorithm A that runs in polynomial time in the input size and A(I) ≤(1+ϵ)OPT(I)+γ for some
γ ≥0. If the additive term γ is 0 in the definition of APTAS, then APTAS becomes a polynomial
time approximation scheme (PTAS).
For offline BP, no polynomial-time algorithm can have an absolute approximation ratio smaller
than 3
2 unless NP=P [6]. Simchi-Levi [13] presented the famous First Fit Decreasing (FFD) algo-
rithm that first sorts the items in the non-increasing order of their sizes and then applies the First
Fit algorithm to pack the items. Luckily, FFD is a 3
2-approximation algorithm, matching the lower
bound of offline BP. The authors in [3] developed an APTAS for offline BP.

Our contributions
In this paper, given an optimal packing σ∗for GBP or CGBP, we present an algorithm that
produces a set of polynomially many bin packings in polynomial time such that there exists a bin
packing σ, in which the number of bins used is at most 1 + ϵ times that of σ∗plus 1 and the total
energy consumption does not exceed that of σ∗. For GBP, the algorithm returns a bin packing
from all bin packings produced with the minimum objective value, while for CGBP, the algorithm
returns a bin packing that minimizes the number of bins used subject to the energy constraint.
Recall the properties of σ and clearly, our algorithm is an APTAS for both GBP and CGBP. Recall
again that β, G and U are all part of the input and therefore, our APTAS for GBP extends the
one in [2]. Using the APTAS for GBP or CGBP, we develop the first 3
2-approximation algorithm,
which matches the lower bound for the classic BP problem.

Preliminaries
In the green bin packing (GBP for short) problem, each bin has a capacity of 1 and is associated
with two parameters G ∈[0, 1] and β ≥0. There are n items I with sizes s1, . . . , sn, which should
be packed into a set of bins. Without loss of generality, we can assume 1 ≥s1 ≥. . . ≥sn > 0.
It is worth mentioning that both β and G are assumed to be fixed constants in [2]; in contrast,
our formulation treats both of them as part of the input. One clearly sees that our formulation
generalizes the one in [2]. The capacity within [0, G] is referred to as the green space and each unit
of capacity beyond the green space incurs an energy consumption of β. In other words, if the load,
i.e., the total size of the items packed in the bin, is x, then the energy consumed by the bin is
fβ,G(x) = max{0, β(x −G)} as defined in Definition 1.
Definition 1 The energy consumption function fβ,G : [0, 1] →R≥0 maps the load of a bin to a
non-negative real number, which can be expressed as fβ,G(x) = max{0, β(x −G)}. Clearly, given
β ≥0 and G ∈[0, 1], the function fβ,G(x) is non-decreasing in x.
The GBP aims to minimize the number of bins opened plus the total energy consumption.
Problem 1 (Green Bin Packing, GBP for short) Given a set of items I = {1, . . . , n} and two
parameters β ≥0, G ∈[0, 1], each item i has a size of si ∈(0, 1] and the items should be packed
into a set of unit capacity bins with the objective of minimizing the number of opened bins plus the
total energy consumption (See fβ,G in Definition 1).
The constrained green bin packing (CGBP for short) problem is a variant of GBP, whose ob-
jective is to minimize the number of opened bins such that the total energy consumption does not
exceed a given upper bound U ≥0. One clearly sees that if β = 0 or G = 1, then fβ,G(x) = 0 for
any x ∈[0, 1], which indicates that the energy consumption is always 0. So, both GBP and CGBP
are reduced to the classic bin packing problem.
Recall that fβ,G(x) is non-decreasing in x and therefore, packing each item in a single bin yields
a bin packing with the least energy consumption Pn
i=1 fβ,G(si). It follows that CGBP admits a
feasible solution if and only if U ≥Pn
i=1 fβ,G(si).

is still interesting to prove whether or not the lower bound is tight for some other values of β and
G. Moreover, it would also be interesting to extend our model to non-uniform bin capacities where
the function fβ,G in Definition 1 may be defined in terms of the portion of occupied capacity in a
