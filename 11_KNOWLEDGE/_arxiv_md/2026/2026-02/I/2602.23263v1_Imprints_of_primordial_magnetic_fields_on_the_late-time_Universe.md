ABSTRACT
Context. Primordial magnetic fields (PMFs) generated in the early Universe may leave observable imprints in the present-day large-
scale structure. However, it remains unclear on which spatial scales primordial signatures can survive the nonlinear processes accom-
panying structure formation.
Aims. The aim of this study is to investigate the evolution of PMFs during gravitational collapse and to determine the spatial scales
on which primordial signatures can persist.
Methods. We perform a suite of high-resolution direct numerical simulations of self-gravitating, magnetized halos. By varying the
viscosity, we probe different Reynolds-number regimes and follow the coupled evolution of gravitational collapse and magnetohydro-
dynamic turbulence.
Results. At sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, turbulence generated during collapse triggers the onset of a small-scale dynamo,
which amplifies magnetic energy below the Jeans scale and modifies the magnetic energy spectrum significantly. Whether dynamo
amplification dominates the magnetic field evolution is determined by the competition between the dynamo growth time and the
free-fall time.
Conclusions. Our results highlight the importance of resolving the Jeans scale and the associated turbulent inertial range in cosmolog-
ical MHD simulations to accurately capture the interplay between gravitational compression and dynamo amplification and to assess
which structures retain memory of primordial fields.

1. Introduction
Answers to some of the most fundamental questions of mod-
ern physics may be found through observing relics of the hot,
dense plasma that permeated the early Universe. One such relic
that could have persisted to the present day is the primordial
magnetic field (PMF) (Durrer & Neronov 2013; Subramanian
2016). By placing observational constraints on PMFs, it may be
possible to narrow the landscape of viable models for cosmic
inflation (Turner & Widrow 1988; Ratra 1992), baryogenesis
(Cornwall 1997; Grasso & Rubinstein 2001; Kamada & Long
2016; Fujita & Kamada 2016), and cosmological phase transi-
tions (Vachaspati 1991; Baym et al. 1996; Tevzadze et al. 2012).
To connect observational signatures of PMFs to early-
Universe physics, it is essential to understand how they evolve.
Their pre-recombination evolution is studied in the framework
of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) (Brandenburg et al. 1996),
or, in epochs before the Universe cooled below approximately 10
MeV, of chiral MHD (Fröhlich & Pedrini 2000; Boyarsky et al.
2015;
Brandenburg et al.
2017;
Rogachevskii et al.
2017;
Schober et al. 2022; Kamada et al. 2023; Schober et al. 2024).
In the absence of continued energy injection, PMFs decay while
simultaneously driving turbulence via the Lorentz force. This
process is well understood in the case of decaying helical MHD

turbulence, where magnetic helicity is conserved in ideal MHD
and leads to an inverse cascade – a transfer of magnetic energy
from smaller to larger spatial scales1. It is usually assumed
that turbulent decay ceases at recombination when the plasma
becomes neutral and the magnetic field becomes passively
stretched by the expanding Universe, effectively freezing into
the Hubble flow. Although Banerjee & Jedamzik (2004) have
also shown that depending on the post-recombination strength
of the magnetic field, turbulence can still be sourced across
the recombination or in the Dark Ages epoch e.g., due to the
free-streaming of photons and hydrogen atoms, respectively.
Searches for PMFs focus on the most underdense regions
of the Universe – cosmic voids – where a PMF may have
been preserved throughout cosmic evolution, because these
environments are unlikely to sustain the turbulence needed
to efficiently amplify magnetic fields via dynamo instabilities
(Brandenburg & Subramanian 2005). Traditional methods for
detecting cosmic magnetism – such as the Faraday rotation tech-
nique (Kronberg et al. 1977) – lack the sensitivity required to
probe magnetic fields in cosmic voids, providing only upper lim-

The small-scale dynamo amplifies magnetic fields through
stretching, twisting, and folding of magnetic field lines, resulting
in exponential amplification of magnetic energy. This mecha-

demonstrates that the dynamo growth rate increases with the

idealized turbulence setups, increasing numerical resolution
has enabled simulations to reach higher Reynolds numbers,
thereby allowing small-scale dynamo action to develop in more
realistic environments. Consequently, the small-scale dynamo
has been studied in simulations of isolated gravitational col-

2. The model
Prior to the onset of nonlinear gravitational collapse, baryonic
matter resides in mildly overdense regions that grow via grav-
itational instability within the cosmic web. As dark matter ha-
los assemble, baryons fall into the associated potential wells and
are compressed, eventually decoupling from the Hubble expan-
sion. In many situations of interest – such as the formation of
primordial star-forming minihalos or dense gas clouds within
young galaxies – the central gas can be approximated as nearly
isothermal over a substantial density range due to efficient ra-
diative cooling (Bromm 2013). In this regime, the collapse of a
self-gravitating, pressure-confined sphere provides a useful ide-
alized model of the nonlinear phase of structure formation. A
supercritical Lane–Emden density profile captures the transition
from quasi-hydrostatic equilibrium to runaway gravitational col-
lapse (Shu 1977), while allowing controlled exploration of the
turbulence generated during infall. Although real cosmological
collapse proceeds within dark matter halos and along filamen-
tary accretion flows, this setup isolates the essential physics of
compressive amplification, turbulent energy injection, and mag-
netic cascade processes that govern the evolution of PMFs on
sub-halo scales.

2.2.2. Two regimes of magnetic evolution
Depending on the relative ordering of tSSD and tff, two different
regimes emerge:
If tSSD ≪tff, the small-scale dynamo operates efficiently, am-
plifying magnetic energy on the viscous scale well before the gas
completes its collapse. In this regime, the growth of the magnetic
spectrum is driven primarily by turbulence.
If tSSD ≫tff, the dynamo action is too slow to compete
with the gravitational contraction. The magnetic field evolves
passively, scaling with the gas density, and the spectrum largely
mirrors the density distribution.
2.2.3. Small-scale dynamo characteristics
The small-scale dynamo is a mechanism that converts turbu-
lent kinetic energy into magnetic energy. It therefore depends
intimately on the statistics of the velocity field. Traditionally,
Kolmogorov-type turbulence has been studied, where the ki-
netic energy spectrum scales as EK ∝k−5/3 in the inertial range.
This type of turbulence is ideal because the dynamo seems to
occur most naturally if the field lines are stretched, twisted,
and folded by turbulent eddies (Kazantsev 1968). However, the
models can be extended to compressive turbulence, the limiting
case of which would be Burgers turbulence, where EK ∝k−2
(Schober et al. 2012b; Martins Afonso et al. 2019). For the on-
set of small-scale dynamo instability, the magnetic Reynolds
number Rm = σvrJ/(2πη), where η is the magnetic resistivity,
must exceed a critical value Rmc. For Kolmogorov turbulence
Rmc ≈100, while for Burgers turbulence Rmc is much higher
(Haugen et al. 2004).

2.3. Numerical simulations
We use the Pencil Code (Pencil Code Collaboration et al. 2021)
to simulate the evolution of an initial magnetic field with a given
magnetic energy spectrum during the collapse of an overdense
region in space. Specifically, we solve Eqns. (1)-(3) and (4) on
a three-dimensional grid. The numerical domain has a size of
L3 and periodic boundary conditions. The runs presented in the
main part of the paper have resolutions of 11523 and 23043; the
dependence on numerical resolution is discussed in Appendix A.
The length scales and wavenumbers reported in the DNS
analysis are normalized in terms of the initial Jeans radius rJ,0
and the initial Jeans wavenumber kJ,0 = 2π/rJ,0, respectively.
Time is normalized in terms of the initial free-fall time tff,0 and
velocities in terms of the sound speed cs. Finally, the magnetic
field is reported in terms of the Alfvèn velocity, which is again
normalized by the sound speed cs.

3. Results from DNS
3.1. Reference run
In this section, we take Run H2b as our reference case. This
particular run is selected because it achieves a sufficiently high
Re to potentially support small-scale dynamo action. At the same
time, the inertial range remains resolved throughout the majority
of the simulation. As with all runs, Run H2b crashes once the
infall velocity becomes too large, i.e. when it becomes larger
than the speed of sound and shocks develop. Therefore, we can
only study the initial phase of the collapse with these DNS.
3.1.1. Collapse analysis
Figure 1 presents slices of the simulation box from Run H2b
at various times, progressing from the initial conditions on the
left to a snapshot near the final simulation time on the right.
Initially, the system is configured as an isothermal supercriti-
cal Lane-Emden density profile, as described in Sec. 2.3.1. By
t = 0.86 tff,0, where tff,0 denotes the initial free-fall time of the
system, the density distribution is significantly influenced by the
turbulent velocity field initially imposed. At later times, how-
ever, gravitational collapse dominates, driving a rapid and con-
tinued increase in central density.
The evolution of the rms of the total velocity field is pre-
sented in the second row of Fig. 1. The velocity field is initially
set up with random fluctuations, but no artificial forcing is ap-
plied in the Navier-Stokes equation, as described in Sec. 2.3. In
the first half of the simulation, these random fluctuations are the
dominant component of Urms. At t ≳1.7 tff,0, the infall veloc-
ity dominates, as can be clearly seen in the slices. Note that the
distance from the center, where Urms reaches its spatial maxi-
mum coincides with the radius where the spatial gradient of ρ is
highest (see upper panel of Fig. 1), as expected from Eq. (3).
In the bottom row of Fig. 1, the evolution of the rms of the
magnetic field is shown. The initial fluctuations in Run H2b are
located mostly on the largest scale of the box. Later, Brms is af-
fected by both, the initial velocity fluctuations and the gravita-
tional collapse, as the magnetic field is frozen in the gas. The
magnetic field evolution will be discussed in more detail later.

3.2. Transition to dynamo regime
We turn to the question of how magnetic field amplification in
a self-gravitating collapsing cloud is influenced by the level of
turbulence in the system. For this purpose, we conduct a param-
eter study by varying the viscosity ν (and likewise the magnetic
resistivity η) in our simulations.
In Fig. 7, we compare simulations with different ν and hence,
different Reynolds numbers Re. The top panel of Fig. 7 shows
the time evolution of Re for different runs. The highest Re ≈
4000 is reached in Run H2d, although the viscous and resis-
tive scales, kν = kη, are not resolved in this case. Our reference
run, Run H2b, has the highest Re ≈400, while kη remains re-
solved until the simulation is over. Run H1a starts with the lowest
Re ≈10, well below the critical value of Re ≈100 required for
small-scale dynamo action (Haugen et al. 2004). Generally, we
find that Re decreases over time in all runs throughout the simu-
lated period. This occurs despite initializing the simulations with
velocity fluctuations that are later sustained by turbulence driven
during gravitational collapse, keeping Urms approximately con-
stant. However, the turbulent driving scale, set by the evolving
Jeans scale, decreases over time, leading to the observed de-
crease in Re.

netic energy integral scale, kM. As expected for a small-scale dy-
namo, kM increases faster with decreasing ν, i.e., with increas-

4.2. Comparison with other studies
We now compare our direct numerical simulations of magnetic-
field evolution during gravitational collapse with two comple-
mentary approaches: (i) the analytical forward-cascade model
proposed by Abramson et al. (2025), and (ii) cosmological sim-
ulations of large-scale structure formation with different initial
magnetic energy spectra, EM, from Mtchedlidze et al. (2022). In
all three cases, EM grows at large k (and in some cases simul-
taneously at low k as well). To enable a quantitative compar-
ison of the spectral evolution, we compute the magnetic-field
correlation length in each model and show its time evolution in
Fig. 9. We caution the reader, however, that one-to-one compar-
ison of our results with these studies is not possible since e.g.,
Abramson et al. 2025 uses an idealized model of collapse, while
Mtchedlidze et al. 2022 has large number of halos, although un-
resolved, as already mentioned earlier, and their statistics are
dominated by the dynamics of magnetic fields on larger scales
(filaments and cosmic voids).
The analytical forward-cascade model by Abramson et al.
(2025)) assumes a constant collapse rate, i.e. no evolving den-
sity, and therefore applies only over a limited time interval. Al-
though Abramson et al. (2025) show that the spectral peak shifts
toward higher k, this shift is accompanied by a simultaneous de-
crease of EM at low k, such that the resulting value of kM changes
little, see Fig. 9. Overall, the model is in rough agreement with
our Run H1a, in which no turbulence develops.

4.3. Limitations and scope
The simulations presented in this work are designed as con-
trolled direct numerical experiments to isolate the physical
mechanisms that govern the spectral evolution of PMFs dur-
ing gravitational collapse. As such, several limitations should be
kept in mind when interpreting the results.
First, our DNS follow the collapse only until the onset of
strongly supersonic infall, at which point unresolved shocks de-
velop and the simulations terminate. Consequently, we focus on
the pre-shock phase of collapse and primarily capture the onset
of small-scale dynamo amplification rather than its fully devel-
oped nonlinear evolution or saturation. While the dynamo signa-
tures identified here (growth of Brms/⟨ρ⟩2/3, work balance, and
scale-dependent growth rates) provide robust evidence for dy-
namo activity at sufficiently high Reynolds numbers, longer inte-
grations would be required to quantify the subsequent nonlinear
transfer of magnetic energy back toward larger scales.
Second, our model employs an idealized collapse config-
uration (a supercritical Lane–Emden density profile in a peri-
odic domain) and assumes an approximately isothermal equation
of state. This setup isolates the interplay between gravitational
compression, turbulence driven on the Jeans scale, and magnetic
spectral transfer, but it does not capture additional physical in-
gredients present in realistic cosmological environments, such
as continuous accretion, filamentary inflows, dark-matter poten-
tials, cosmological expansion, or radiative feedback. We there-
fore interpret our results primarily in terms of the underlying
dimensionless control parameters (notably the Reynolds num-
ber and the ratio tSSD/tff), which should remain relevant across a
broad class of collapsing structures.
Finally, our simulations operate in the regime Pm ≈1, with
explicit viscosity and resistivity chosen such that ν = η. This
choice is motivated by numerical constraints and allows a sys-
tematic exploration of the Reynolds number, but it does not
probe the extreme magnetic Prandtl numbers expected in many
astrophysical plasmas. Nevertheless, the qualitative distinction
between forward-cascade-dominated evolution at low Re and
dynamo-dominated evolution at high Re is expected to be ro-
bust.
5. Conclusion and outlook
In this paper we presented a series of high-resolution direct nu-
merical simulations of magnetized halos collapsing under self-
gravity and analyzed the evolution of the magnetic energy spec-
trum. Our main findings can be summarized as follows:
1. Gravitational collapse drives a forward cascade of magnetic
energy when the initial peak of the magnetic energy spec-
trum, kpeak,0, lies at wavenumbers smaller than the initial
Jeans wavenumber (i.e., when magnetic energy is concen-
trated on scales larger than those of the density field), kJ,0
(the opposite ordering leads to inverse transfer and decay
of the magnetic field). In the default configuration consid-
ered in this work, kpeak,0 < kJ,0, and for runs in which turbu-
lence does not fully develop due to large dissipation param-
eters, the magnetic spectrum evolves predominantly through
compression and forward spectral transfer. In this regime,
our results are in approximate agreement with the analyti-
cal forward-cascade model of Abramson et al. (2025) when
considering a simple toy collapse rate and with the cosmo-
logical simulations of Mtchedlidze et al. (2022), which con-
sider PMFs with different coherence scales.
2. In addition to the forward cascade, a small-scale dynamo
emerges at sufficiently high Reynolds numbers. Our study
shows that whether dynamo amplification becomes dynami-
cally important is determined by the competition between the
dynamo growth timescale tSSD (the inverse growth rate of the
small-scale dynamo) and the free-fall time tff. If tSSD ≪tff,
the dynamo efficiently amplifies magnetic energy on the vis-
cous scale during collapse. If tSSD ≫tff, dynamo action is
too slow to compete with gravitational contraction and mag-
netic amplification is dominated by compression and forward
spectral transfer.
3. Dynamo action modifies – and can erase – primordial spec-
tral features on small length scales. In particular, the small-
scale dynamo operates predominantly below the Jeans scale,

where turbulence is efficiently generated during gravitational
collapse. Therefore, only magnetic structures on sufficiently
large scales can retain memory of PMFs, while smaller scales
are dynamically regenerated during structure formation.
Our results suggest that cosmological MHD simulations,
which do not resolve the Jeans scale – and therefore do not cap-
ture the turbulent inertial range associated with gravitational col-
lapse – are likely to underestimate magnetic-field amplification
due to the absence of the small-scale dynamo on small scales.
In such simulations, the evolution of PMFs may appear domi-
nated by compression and large-scale advection, while the rapid
generation of magnetic energy on sub-Jeans scales remains un-
resolved. However, the gravitationally driven forward cascade
of magnetic energy toward smaller scales is still captured, even
if the small-scale dynamo itself is not fully resolved. Conse-
quently, large-scale spectral shifts may be reproduced, whereas
the additional dynamo-driven amplification and restructuring of
the magnetic spectrum occurs on scales up to the Jeans scale.
More broadly, our findings have important implications for
interpreting future measurements of magnetic fields in the large-
scale structure. In collapsing environments, turbulent dynamo
action can quickly reshape the magnetic energy spectrum and
erase primordial spectral features on small scales. Accurately
connecting observed magnetic statistics to primordial initial con-
ditions, therefore, requires simulations that resolve the turbulent
cascade and the relevant dissipation scales, enabling a solid treat-
ment of the competition between gravitationally driven forward
transfer and dynamo amplification.
The simulations presented here represent a first step toward
disentangling the coupled effects of gravitational collapse and
turbulent amplification on the evolution of PMFs in the late Uni-
verse. Follow-up studies—such as idealized galaxy simulations
embedded in a cosmological framework—are needed to quan-
tify more precisely the scales on which dynamo amplification
dominates magnetic-field growth. In addition, a broader explo-
ration of initial magnetic-field configurations (e.g., varying the
coherence scale relative to the collapsing region, extending stud-
ies such as Federrath et al. 2011; Seta & Federrath 2020) will be
essential to assess how the initial conditions influence the onset,
efficiency, and characteristic scales of dynamo action.

Appendix A: Dependence on resolution
Gravitational collapse in the simulations presented in this work is caused by self-gravity of an overdense region in the center of the
numerical domain. Collapse due to self-gravity is driven by the gradient in the gravitational potential, which, in turn, is related to
the gradient in density. In our simulations, the initial density profile is a solution of the Lane-Emden equation. The resulting initial
condition is a density profile with ρc at the center and a smooth decrease with radius. This density gradient becomes highest at
approximately one-half of the radius.
Higher resolution allows for a more accurate representation of the density gradient, particularly at half the box radius, leading to
a more rapid increase in radial velocity. Consequently, higher-resolution simulations exhibit an earlier onset of supersonic shocks,
ultimately causing the collapse to proceed more quickly and leading to an earlier disruption of the system.
In Fig. A.1 we show different quantities related to the gravitational collapse as a function of resolution and for different times.
In particular both the spatial maximum of the velocity dispersion σmax and the spatial maximum of the total velocity Umax increase
strongly with increasing resolution. This leads to a faster development of shocks in high-resolution runs compared to runs with low
resolution.

Appendix C: Run with initial small-scale magnetic fluctuations
In this appendix, we present Run H2b’, in which the initial magnetic energy spectrum is peaked at k > kJ,0. A further distinctive
feature of this run is that the initial magnetic energy is approximately 1/20 of the initial kinetic energy, which is significantly higher

a small-scale dynamo can develop more efficiently when strong magnetic fluctuations are already present at large wavenumbers.
Figure C.1 shows the analysis of run H2b’. As anticipated, we do not observe a forward cascade. Instead, the magnetic energy
EM decays rapidly at high k, and the peak of the spectrum kpeak shifts to smaller wavenumbers. This resembles an inverse cascade. At
the final time of Run H2b’, the peak of EM coincides with kJ. Interestingly, this decay of magnetic energy on the initial peak of EM is
similar to the trend seen in the cosmological simulations of Mtchedlidze et al. (2022) (see their Figure 6 for helical and non-helical
cases; however, as structures start to form, the power spectrum shows forward cascade, while insignificant growth on larger scales
remains), even though there kJ,0 ≫kpeak,0 (and magnetic field growth in their case is also dominated by the perturbations in the total
density field), while in our DNS kJ,0 < kpeak,0. While in Run H2b’ there is some buildup of magnetic energy on scales k < kpeak, the
overall Brms decreases throughout the entire simulation, as can be seen on the right upper panel of Fig. C.1.

This may indicate the onset of a small-scale dynamo. However, we cannot confirm this interpretation, as there is no corresponding

Appendix D: Signatures of the dynamo in the evolution of work terms
In this appendix, we analyze the potential dynamo activity by comparing the different work components in the flow, following the
analysis presented in Brandenburg & Ntormousi (2022).
In the left panel of Fig. D.1, we compare the time evolution of the work done by the Lorentz force WL = ⟨U · (J × B)⟩, the
work done by the pressure force Wp = −⟨U · ∇p⟩= ⟨p∇· U⟩, and the work done by the gravity term WJ = −⟨ρU · ∇Φ⟩. All of these
work terms are negative, and the absolute values of WJ and Wp are greater than that of WL. However, WL is slightly larger than the
Joule dissipation term QM = ⟨µ0ηJ2⟩, as can be seen in the middle panel of Fig. D.1. Viscous dissipation, QK = ⟨2ρνS2⟩, is initially
approximately two orders of magnitudes greater than QM, and after ≈1 tff,0 the difference reduces to one order of magnitude.
The right panel of Fig. D.1 shows the time evolution of (−WL −QM)/QM. A value of (−WL −QM)/QM > 0, which we find here
throughout the simulation, indicates that more magnetic energy is produced than dissipated, thus pointing to dynamo activity.

Appendix E: Correlation between magnetic field and velocity field
The small-scale dynamo amplifies the magnetic field through stretching, twisting, folding, and merging of field lines by turbulent
eddies. It should be most efficient in turbulence that is dominated by vorticity, and the magnetic field grows fastest in regions where
vorticity is significant. If the dynamo is present, we therefore expect a correlation between Brms and ωrms = (∇× U)rms.
We plot the correlation between Brms and ωrms at different times for Run H2b in Fig. E.1 in orange color. As this simulation
crashes early, a correlation between Brms and ωrms never develops.
As mentioned in Appendix A, higher resolution leads to earlier crashes, as the collapse proceeds faster. Run H2b has a resolution
of 23043, and we compare the correlations in this run with a run that has the same parameters and initial conditions, but a resolution
of 1443. The result for the 1443 run is presented in Fig. E.1 in blue color. The latter simulation runs longer, and we see that a
correlation between Brms and ωrms has developed at t = 4.28 tff,0, see the upper right panel.
How much the buildup a correlation between Brms and ωrms is a sign of a small-scale dynamo should be tested in future idealized
simulations.
