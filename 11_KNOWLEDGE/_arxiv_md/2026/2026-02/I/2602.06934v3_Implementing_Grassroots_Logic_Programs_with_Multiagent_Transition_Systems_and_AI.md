Abstract
Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP) is a multiagent, concurrent, logic programming language designed
for the implementation of smartphone-based, serverless, grassroots platforms. Here, we start from
GLP and maGLP—concurrent and multiagent abstract nondeterministic operational semantics for
GLP, respectively—and from them derive dGLP and madGLP—implementation-ready deterministic
operational semantics for both—and prove them correct with respect to their abstract counterparts.
dGLP was used by AI (Claude) as a formal specification from which it developed a workstation-based
implementation of GLP in Dart; madGLP is being used by AI as a formal specification from which
it develops a smartphone-based multiagent implementation of GLP in Dart. The key insight is
that maGLP shared variable pairs spanning agents can be implemented as local variable pairs
connected by global links, with correctness following from disjoint substitution commutativity (from
GLP’s single-occurrence invariant) and persistence. We prove that both madGLP and maGLP are
grassroots.

Introduction
Grassroots platforms. A digital platform is grassroots [43, 48] if it can have multiple
instances that can (i) operate independently of each other and of any global resource other
than the network, and (ii) coalesce into ever larger instances, possibly resulting in a single
global instance. Grassroots platforms that have been specified include the grassroots social
graph [48], grassroots social networks [44], grassroots cryptocurrencies and bonds [45, 23, 47],
and grassroots federation [51].
GLP. Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP) [46] is a multiagent, concurrent, logic programming
language, designed for the implementation of smartphone-based, serverless, grassroots plat-
forms. GLP extends LP by (i) adding a paired reader X? to every “ordinary” logic variable
X, now called a writer; (ii) restricting variables in goals and clauses to have at most a single
occurrence (SO); and (iii) requiring that a variable occurs in a clause iff its paired variable
also occurs in it (single-reader single-writer, SRSW). The result eschews unification in favour
of simple term matching, is linear-logic-like [14], and is futures/promises-like [13]: each
assignment X := T is produced at most once via the sole occurrence of a writer (promise)
X, and consumed at most once via the sole occurrence of its paired reader (future) X?; the
term T may contain further readers and/or writers, enabling the concise expression of rich
multidirectional communication modalities.
Being relational and nondeterministic (and thus not functional), GLP—like its predecessor
concurrent logic languages [41]—can express nondeterministic asynchronous concurrent
processes such as the fair merging of streams. The SRSW restriction is what makes GLP
suitable for grassroots multiagent distributed execution: each variable assignment has exactly

GLP by example. Consider the quintessential concurrent logic program for fairly merging
two streams, written in GLP:
merge([X|Xs], Ys, [X?|Zs?]) :- merge(Ys?, Xs?, Zs).
merge(Xs, [Y|Ys], [Y?|Zs?]) :- merge(Xs?, Ys?, Zs).
merge(Xs, [], Xs?).
merge([], Ys, Ys?).
and the goal merge([1,2,3|In1?],[a,b|In2?],Out). Both the goal and each clause satisfy
SO, and each clause satisfies SRSW. In the goal, Out is a writer: the output stream that
merge will produce. The readers In1? and In2? are open tails of the input streams: other
processes may extend these streams by assigning their paired writers In1 and In2.
The first clause matches a list cell on the first input stream: the head element’s writer
X in the clause head is assigned, and its reader X? is placed in the output stream. The
recursive call merge(Ys?, Xs?, Zs) swaps the two input streams, ensuring fairness when
both streams are available. If neither input stream has a list cell available (both start with a
reader), the goal suspends until one of them is assigned. Different scheduling of assignments
to In1 and In2 may result in different interleaving orders in the output stream—this is
and-nondeterminism, a hallmark of concurrent logic programming.
What this paper contributes. An earlier paper [46] defined GLP and maGLP—concurrent
and multiagent abstract nondeterministic operational semantics for GLP, respectively—
via transition systems and multiagent transition systems [42]; another [49] defined and
implemented a type and module system for GLP. Here, we derive from GLP and maGLP
implementation-ready deterministic operational semantics for single-agent and multiagent
GLP, referred to as dGLP and madGLP, respectively. We prove each of them correct with
respect to their abstract counterparts. dGLP was used by AI (Claude) as a formal specification,
from which it developed a workstation-based implementation of GLP in Dart [15]; madGLP
is being used by AI as a formal specification from which it develops a smartphone-based
multiagent implementation of GLP in Dart.
Technical contributions include:
1. maGLP shared variable pairs spanning agents are implemented in madGLP as local
variable pairs connected by global links and global_send goals, with all communication
reduced to message passing. Correctness is proved via outcome equivalence: GLP’s SO
invariant yields disjoint substitution commutativity, and persistence ensures that enabled
transitions remain enabled throughout the decomposition into unary transactions.
2. We prove that both madGLP and maGLP are grassroots, using the new grassroots
definition of [50] based on interleavings of correct runs of disjoint agent groups.
AI development methodology. The AI-based development process employs three layers of
abstraction: (i) the mathematical specification (this paper); (ii) an English+code fragments
informal specification derived by AI from the math; and (iii) the Dart code derived by AI
from the informal specification. While the authority flows math→spec→Dart, the three layers
were harmonized via back-and-forth communication, with programming in Dart revealing
gaps and inconsistencies in the informal specification, which in turn revealed gaps and
inconsistencies in the math. The latter resulted in several major redesigns of madGLP, the
outcome of which is presented here.

Transition Systems and Implementations
This section presents transition systems and implementations among them, providing the
mathematical foundation for proving that one transition system correctly implements another.
2.1
Transition Systems
▶Definition 2.1 (Transition System). A transition system is a tuple TS = (C, c0, T) where
C is an arbitrary set of configurations, c0 ∈C is a designated initial configuration, and
T ⊆C × C is a transition relation, with transitions written c →c′ ∈T.
A transition c →c′ ∈T is enabled from configuration c. A configuration c is terminal
if no transitions are enabled from c. A computation is a (finite or infinite) sequence of
configurations where for each two consecutive configurations (c, c′) in the sequence, c →c′ ∈T.
We write c
∗−→c′ to denote the existence of a computation (empty if c = c′) from c to c′.
A run is a computation starting from c0, which is complete if it is infinite or ends in a
terminal configuration. The outcome of a complete run is determined by a domain-specific
function from complete runs to an outcome space.
Liveness. The framework we follow [42] has an elaborate notion of liveness. As GLP is a
simple special case, we do not recall it here, but assume the notion of fair runs abstractly
and instantiate it concretely when defining GLP (Section 3).

GLP and Its Implementation
3.1
GLP
This section presents Grassroots Logic Programs (GLP), a concurrent logic programming
language in which logic variables are partitioned into paired readers and writers.
GLP Syntax.
▶Definition 3.1 (GLP Variables). Let V denote the set of writers (identifiers beginning with
uppercase). Define V? = {X? | X ∈V}, called readers. The set of all GLP variables is
ˆV = V ∪V?. A writer X and its reader X? form a variable pair.
▶Definition 3.2 (GLP Terms and Goals). A term is a variable in ˆV, a constant (numbers,
strings, or the empty list []), or a compound term f(T1, . . . , Tn) with functor f and subterms
Ti. Let T denote the set of all terms. We use standard list notation: [X|Xs] for a list cell,
[X1,...,Xn] for finite lists. A term is ground if it contains no variables.
A unit goal is a compound term. A goal is a multiset of unit goals; the empty goal is
written true. A clause A :- B has head A (a unit goal) and body B (a goal); a unit clause
has empty body.
▶Definition 3.3 (Single-Occurrence (SO) Invariant). A term, goal, or clause satisfies the
single-occurrence (SO) invariant if every variable occurs in it at most once.
▶Definition 3.4 (GLP Program). A clause C satisfies the single-reader/single-writer
(SRSW) restriction if it satisfies SO and a variable occurs in C iff its paired variable also
occurs in C. A GLP program is a finite set of clauses satisfying SRSW; clauses for the
same predicate form a procedure. Let ˆG(P) denote the set of goals constructible from ˆV and
the predicate symbols and functors of a GLP program P.
The SO and SRSW restrictions ensure that GLP eschews unification in favour of simple
term matching.
The result is linear-logic-like [14] and futures/promises-like [13]: each
assignment X := T is produced at most once via the sole occurrence of a writer (promise)
X, and consumed at most once via the sole occurrence of its paired reader (future) X?.
GLP Operational Semantics.
▶Definition 3.5 (Writers Substitution, Assignment, Readers Substitution and Counterpart).
A GLP writer assignment is a term of the form X := T, X ∈V, T /∈V, satisfying SO.
Similarly, a GLP reader assignment is a term of the form X? := T, X? ∈V?, T /∈V,
satisfying SO. A writers (readers) substitution σ is the substitution implied by a set
of writer (reader) assignments that jointly satisfy SO. Given a writers assignment X := T,
its readers counterpart is X? := T, and given a writers substitution σ, its readers
counterpart σ? is the readers substitution defined by X?σ? = Xσ.
▶Definition 3.6 (GLP Renaming, Renaming Apart). A GLP renaming is an injective
substitution ρ : ˆV →ˆV such that for each X ∈V: Xρ ∈V and X?ρ = (Xρ)?. Two GLP
terms have a variable in common if for some writer X ∈V, either X or X? occurs in
both. A GLP renaming σ renames T ′ apart from T if T ′σ and T have no variable in
common.
▶Definition 3.7 (Writer MGU). Given two GLP unit goals A and H, a writer mgu is
a writers substitution σ such that Aσ = Hσ and σ is most general (under the standard
subsumption ordering) among such substitutions. A writers substitution applied to a term
assigns only writers; readers are left unchanged.

▶Definition 3.11 (GLP Transition System). Given a GLP program P, an asynchronous
resolvent over P is a pair (G, σ) where G ∈ˆG(P) and σ is a readers substitution.
A transition system GLP(P) = (C, c0, T ) is a GLP transition system over P and

2. c0 = (G0, ∅)

The GLP Communicate rule realizes the use of reader/writer pairs for asynchronous
communication: it communicates an assignment from its writer to its paired reader.
GLP has two forms of nondeterminism: the choice of A ∈G (and-nondeterminism), and
the choice of which Communicate to apply.

We now discuss GLP persistence and define fair runs, needed to prove a GLP implement-
ation to be live.
Persistence and fairness require identifying “the same transition” across configurations as
goals are instantiated by reader substitutions. Following [48], we define goal identity and
transition equivalence.
▶Definition 3.14 (Goal Identity). Each unit goal in a GLP computation carries a unique
identifier assigned at spawn time: goals in the initial goal G0 receive distinct initial identifiers,
and goals spawned by a Reduce transition receive fresh identifiers. Communicate transitions
preserve identifiers: the goal containing X? retains its identity after instantiation.
▶Definition 3.15 (Transition Equivalence). Given a set of transitions T, a transition
equivalence [50] is an equivalence relation ∼on T. We write [t] for the equivalence class of
t under ∼. An equivalence class [t] is enabled in configuration c if some t′ ∈[t] is enabled
in c.
For GLP, t1 ∼t2 if:
both are Reduce transitions that reduce the same goal (by identity) with the same clause,
or
both are Communicate transitions that apply the counterpart of the same writer assignment
to the same goal (by identity).
Two equivalent Reduce transitions may operate on different instantiations of the same
goal—with different reader substitutions applied—but produce the same writer mgu (since
the writer mgu assigns only writers and is unaffected by reader substitutions).
▶Definition 3.16 (Persistent Transition System). A transition system with transition equi-
valence ∼is persistent if: whenever a transition t is enabled in configuration c, and
c = c0 →c1 →· · · →ck = c′ is a computation in which no t′ ∼t is taken, then some t′′ ∼t
is enabled in c′.
▶Lemma 3.17 (GLP Persistence). GLP is persistent.

3.2
Deterministic Implementation of GLP
Motivation. dGLP serves as the formal specification for AI to implement single-agent GLP
on workstations. The discipline is: from the mathematical definition (this section), AI derives
an English+code specification, and from this specification derives Dart code.
While the GLP transition system (Definition 3.11) is nondeterministic in goal selection
and abstracts away suspension and failure, dGLP provides:
1. Deterministic FIFO scheduling of active goals
2. Explicit tracking of suspended goals with their suspension sets
3. Explicit tracking of failed goals
4. Automatic reactivation of suspended goals when blocking readers are instantiated
The key simplification compared to multiagent GLP is that all variables are local, so reader
assignments can be applied immediately rather than through asynchronous communication.
▶Definition 3.21 (Reduction Suspension and Failure). Given unit goal A and clause C, with
H :- B being the result of the GLP renaming of C apart from A:
The reduction succeeds if A and H have a writer mgu σ (Definition 3.10)
The reduction suspends with suspension set W if no writer mgu exists, but there
exists a set W ⊆V? of readers occurring in A such that for some ground substitution θ
on W, the terms Aθ and H have a writer mgu. W is the minimal such set.
The reduction fails if it neither succeeds nor suspends
▶Example 3.22 (Suspension and Failure). Consider the goal merge(Xs?, Ys?, Zs) where

exist. Hence the reduction suspends with suspension set W = {Xs?}. The goal is suspended

▶Lemma 3.29 (σ is Live). The implementation mapping σ is live.
Proof. Since dGLP is deterministic (Remark 3.26), every complete dGLP run is trivially
fair—there is at most one enabled transition at any configuration, and it is taken. By
Lemma 3.17, GLP is persistent.
Reduce: Since dGLP’s FIFO scheduling eventually processes every goal that enters the
queue, for every GLP Reduce equivalence class [t] that becomes enabled, eventually some
equivalent reduction is taken in σ(r′).
Communicate: In dGLP, reader substitutions ˆσ? are applied immediately during Reduce
(Remark 3.27). Each such application corresponds to the GLP Communicate equivalence
classes that would apply those reader assignments. Hence every enabled GLP Communicate
equivalence class is taken when the corresponding dGLP Reduce fires.
Therefore σ maps fair dGLP runs to fair GLP runs.

▶Lemma 3.30 (Disjoint Substitution Commutativity). Let ˆσ1 and ˆσ2 be writers substitutions
produced by two GLP Reduce transitions on distinct goals A1 and A2 in the same run. Then
ˆσ1 and ˆσ2 assign disjoint sets of variables, and ˆσ1 ◦ˆσ2 = ˆσ2 ◦ˆσ1.
Proof. By the SO invariant, each writer occurs at most once in the goal multiset G. Since
A1 ̸= A2 are distinct goals, they have disjoint writers. Each Reduce assigns only writers from
its reduced goal (by Definition 3.7). Therefore ˆσ1 and ˆσ2 have disjoint domains. Substitutions
with disjoint domains commute.

5.1
Multiagent GLP
This section extends GLP to multiple agents, defining multiagent GLP (maGLP) as a
transactions-based multiagent transition system using the framework developed in Section 4.
From GLP to Multiagent GLP. In extending GLP to multiple agents, each agent
maintains its own asynchronous resolvent as its local state. The key insight is that GLP’s
variable pairs provide natural binary communication channels: when agent p assigns a writer
X for which the paired reader X? is held by agent q, the assignment X := T must be
communicated to q.
A key difference between single-agent GLP and multiagent GLP is in the initial state.
In a multiagent transition system all agents must have the same initial local state s0
(Definition 4.3). This precludes setting up an initial configuration in which agents share logic
variables, as this would imply different initial states for different agents.
We resolve this in two steps. First, we employ only anonymous logic variables “_” in
the initial local states of agents: Anonymous variables are, on the one hand, syntactically
identical, hence allow all initial states to be syntactically identical, and on the other hand
represent unique variables, hence semantically all initial goals have unique, local, non-shared
variables. The initial state of all agents is the atomic goal agent(ch(_?,_),ch(_?,_)), with
the first channel serving communication with the user and the second with the network.

▶Remark 6.19 (Outgoing Messages via global_send). The Reduce transaction does not directly
generate outgoing messages. Instead, when a writer X is assigned, X? becomes known,
which may trigger a global_send goal watching X?. That goal’s reduction (via the ’_send’

▶Remark 6.20 (Automatic Forwarding). The Receive transaction simply assigns the local
writer and removes the entry. If the assigned writer’s reader (X?) is being watched by a
global_send goal (because it was also exported), that goal will fire on a subsequent Reduce,
automatically forwarding the value. No special forwarding logic is needed in Receive.
▶Remark 6.21 (Transaction Degrees). All madGLP transactions are unary: Reduce operates
on a single agent’s resolvent, Send removes a message from one agent’s outbox, and Receive
processes an incoming message at one agent. Cold-calls, which in maGLP require binary
transactions, are implemented in madGLP using the index-0 serializer mechanism, which
decomposes into unary Send and Receive transactions.
▶Remark 6.22 (Determinism). madGLP is deterministic at the agent level: given a local
state, exactly one Reduce transition is enabled (FIFO goal selection, first-clause matching).
However, madGLP is nondeterministic at the system level due to communication asynchrony:
the order in which messages arrive at an agent depends on network timing, and different
arrival orders may lead to different computations.

AI Development Methodology
A key goal of this paper is to offer a precise interface between the human designer and the AI
programmer: formal operational semantics serve as a specification language, constraining the
space of legal implementations. The emerging discipline [12, 35, 6] we advocate and employ
is for the human designer and AI to jointly develop and agree upon (i) formal semantics
(this paper); (ii) an informal (English+code) specification (derived by AI from the math);
and only then let AI attempt to write (iii) Dart code that complies with these formal and
informal specifications.
The AI used throughout is Claude (Anthropic). While the authority flows math→spec→Dart,
the three layers were harmonized via extensive back-and-forth communication: programming
in Dart revealed gaps and inconsistencies in the informal specification, which in turn revealed
gaps and inconsistencies in the math. The remarks in this paper were generated by AI as
part of the specification-derivation process, to verify and document its own understanding of
the mathematical definitions, and document these understandings for subsequent AI sessions
(which do not retain context from prior sessions). The current madGLP English+code
specification is available as supplementary material.
We now describe concrete examples of revisions to the mathematical definitions (this
paper) driven by problems discovered during spec derivation and Dart implementation.
Eliminating variable migration. The initial madGLP design used routing tables to track
variable locations across agents. When variables migrated between agents—for example,
when an agent exported a term containing variables to another agent—the routing tables
required complex updates to ensure messages could still reach their targets. During Dart
implementation, edge cases emerged where routing table updates were missed or applied in
the wrong order; these were invisible at the mathematical specification level. The current
design eliminates variable migration entirely: globally-shared variable pairs are realized by
two local pairs connected by a global link (Definition 6.26), with all variables remaining in
their originating agent’s resolvent.
Unifying cold-calls with regular communication.
Cold-calls—messages between
agents with no prior shared variables—were initially a separate mechanism with dedicated
transaction types. During implementation, this created redundant code paths that performed
essentially the same operations as regular communication.
The current design realizes
cold-calls uniformly via the reserved index-0 serializer (Definition 6.7), reusing the same
globalization, localization, and message-passing machinery. The only special case is that the
serializer entry at index 0 is updated rather than removed upon receipt, allowing multiple
senders to append to an agent’s network input stream.
Correcting the Localize definition. The Localize operation (Definition 6.13) was initially
defined with the wrong half of the variable pair stored in global writers table entries. When
agent q receives a writer global name _w(p, i), it creates a fresh local pair (Yq, Yq?) and must
store the writer Yq in the entry—so that when the assignment message arrives, Yq can be

A protocol is a family of multiagent transition systems, one for each finite set of agents
P ⊂Π, sharing an underlying set of local states with a designated initial state.
▶Definition 8.1 (Local-States Function). A local-states function S : 2Π →2S maps every
finite set of agents P ⊂Π to a set of local states S(P) ⊂S that includes s0 and satisfies
P ⊂P ′ =⇒S(P) ⊂S(P ′).

Given a local-states function S, we use C(P) := S(P)P for configurations over P and
c0(P) := {s0}P for the initial configuration.
▶Definition 8.2 (Protocol). A protocol F over a local-states function S is a family of
multiagent transition systems with exactly one transition system F(P) = (C(P), c0(P), T(P))
for every finite P ⊂Π.
A correct run of a transition system is a complete fair run.
Informally, in a grassroots protocol two disjoint groups of agents can each operate
independently—their interleaved correct runs are correct runs of the combined system—yet
the combined system offers genuinely new behaviours that neither group could produce on
its own. To define this formally, we first define the interleaving of runs of two disjoint groups.
▶Definition 8.3 (Interleaving). Let P, P ′ ⊂Π be disjoint nonempty sets of agents, r =
c0, c1, . . . a run of F(P), and r′ = d0, d1, . . . a run of F(P ′). An interleaving of r and r′
is a sequence e0, e1, . . . of configurations in C(P ∪P ′) for which there exist non-decreasing
sequences of indices (ik)k≥0 and (jk)k≥0 with i0 = j0 = 0 such that for every k ≥0:
1. (ek)p = (cik)p for every p ∈P,
2. (ek)q = (djk)q for every q ∈P ′,
3. if ek+1 exists, then exactly one of: (a) ik+1 = ik + 1 and jk+1 = jk (a P-step), or (b)
ik+1 = ik and jk+1 = jk + 1 (a P ′-step).
Note that an interleaving is well-defined: since P ⊂P ∪P ′, the local-states function
ensures S(P) ⊂S(P ∪P ′), and similarly for P ′, so each ek is a valid configuration in C(P ∪P ′).
Also, e0 = c0(P ∪P ′), since (e0)p = (c0)p = s0 for all p ∈P and (e0)q = (d0)q = s0 for all
q ∈P ′.
▶Definition 8.4 (Oblivious, Interactive, Grassroots). A protocol F is:
1. oblivious if for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂Π, every interleaving of a correct run
of F(P) and a correct run of F(P ′) is a correct run of F(P ∪P ′).
2. interactive if for every disjoint nonempty P, P ′ ⊂Π, there exists a correct run ˆr of
F(P ∪P ′) such that for every correct run r of F(P), every correct run r′ of F(P ′), and
every interleaving e of r and r′, ˆr ̸= e.
3. grassroots if it is oblivious and interactive.
Being oblivious means that two disjoint groups of agents, each running the protocol
independently and correctly, do not interfere with each other: any interleaving of their
independent correct runs is a correct run of the combined system. Being interactive means
that two disjoint groups, when brought together, can do something genuinely new: there
exists a correct run of the combined system that could not arise from the two groups operating
independently. In an interleaving, each step changes the local states of agents in only one
group; therefore, any transaction whose active participants span both groups yields a step
that cannot occur in any interleaving.

Related Work
Concurrent Constraint Programming. GLP’s monotonic semantics builds directly on
the concurrent constraint programming (CCP) paradigm established by Saraswat, Rinard,
and Panangaden [39]. Their foundational insight—that concurrent agents communicate
through a shared constraint store that grows monotonically—provides the semantic template
for GLP’s single-assignment variables. In CCP, computation proceeds by adding constraints;
constraints are never retracted.
GLP inherits this monotonicity: variable assignments
accumulate but are never undone, and agent states grow through message reception. The
lattice-based constraint stores with monotonic information accumulation in CCP are the
direct theoretical ancestor to GLP’s monotonic multiagent framework.
GLP belongs to the family of concurrent logic programming languages that emerged in the
1980s: Concurrent Prolog [40], GHC [54], and PARLOG [8]. These languages interpret goals
as concurrent processes communicating through shared logical variables, using committed-
choice execution with guarded clauses. A comprehensive survey [41] documents this family
and its design space, detailing operational semantics of guarded Horn clause languages,
single-assignment variables, and writer/reader communication mechanisms that GLP refines
and extends.
A key evolution was flattening: restricting guards to primitive tests only. Flat Concurrent
Prolog [33] and Flat GHC demonstrated that flat guards suffice for practical programming
while dramatically simplifying semantics and implementation. GLP can be understood as
Flat Concurrent Prolog with the Single-Reader Single-Writer (SRSW) constraint added. This
resolves the semantic difficulties of read-only unification identified by Levi and Palamidessi [22],
ensuring that term matching suffices without full unification.
Modes and Linearity. Mode systems for concurrent logic programming assign polarity to
variable occurrences. Ueda’s moded Flat GHC [55, 57] is most directly relevant: his mode
system assigns polarity to every variable occurrence (positive for input/read, negative for
output/write), with the well-modedness property guaranteeing each variable is written exactly
once. This establishes the “programming as wiring” paradigm where programs describe
dataflow networks. Ueda’s subsequent linearity analysis [56] identifies variables read exactly
once, enabling compile-time garbage collection for single-reader structures.
GLP enforces both single-reader and single-writer universally as a syntactic restriction,
whereas Ueda’s system guarantees single-writer with single-reader as an optional refinement.
This stronger restriction enables GLP’s simpler distributed execution model.
The SRSW discipline connects to linear logic’s [14, 58] resource sensitivity: each variable
assignment is produced at most once and consumed at most once. Hodas and Miller’s Lolli [18]
presents logic programming in a fragment of intuitionistic linear logic where resources must
be used exactly once unless marked with the exponential modality—conceptually related to
GLP’s variable restrictions.
Session Types and Linear Logic. GLP’s writer/reader pairs have deep connections to
session types via the Curry-Howard correspondence for concurrency. Caires and Pfenning [7]
established this correspondence, interpreting linear logic propositions as session types where
channels are used exactly once—directly paralleling GLP’s single-use semantics. Their
treatment of dual session endpoints mirrors GLP’s writer/reader pair structure.
Wadler’s influential work [59, 60] extended this to classical linear logic, where the duality
A⊥precisely reflects writer/reader duality in GLP channels. The multiplicative connectives
⊗(output then parallel) and
&
(input then parallel) model communication patterns relevant
to GLP’s restrictions.

Honda, Yoshida, and Carbone’s multiparty asynchronous session types [19] introduce
global types and projection mechanisms for coordinating multiple agents—relevant to GLP’s
multiagent setting where agents communicate through shared variables.
A companion
paper [49] develops a full type system for GLP based on these connections.
The session types literature focuses on type safety: ensuring that communication follows
a prescribed protocol. This paper addresses a different question: proving that a distributed
implementation (madGLP) correctly implements an abstract specification (maGLP), using
simulation relations rather than type-theoretic methods.
Futures and Promises. A GLP reader/writer pair is operationally a future or promise [4,
13]: the writer creates a promise for a value, the reader awaits fulfillment.
Flanagan
and Felleisen [11] provide increasingly intensional operational semantics for futures, with
their third semantics explicitly modeling placeholder objects and touch operations—the
synchronization between value producers and consumers directly analogous to GLP’s single-
assignment variables that block readers until writers provide values.
Most relevant is Pruiksma and Pfenning’s work on linear futures [37], developing con-
current languages based on adjoint logic that encode write-once shared-memory semantics
analogous to single-assignment variables. They establish connections between futures and
session types through Curry-Howard correspondence. Unlike futures in mainstream languages,
GLP futures can carry logical terms containing further reader/writer pairs, enabling recursive
communication structures—a capability shared with channel-passing calculi such as the
π-calculus [34]. None of these works address distributed implementation of futures across
independent agents; that is the specific contribution of madGLP’s global links.
Transition Systems and Refinement. The correctness framework in this paper employs
simulation relations between transition systems, following the I/O automata methodology
of Lynch and Tuttle [28, 29]. Their forward and backward simulation techniques [30, 31]
provide the template for proving that implementations correctly refine specifications. Abadi
and Lamport’s theory of refinement mappings [1] establishes that such mappings can always
be found (under suitable conditions) by adding history and prophecy variables—a result
awarded the LICS Test of Time Award for its foundational importance.
Our approach differs in two respects. First, we use outcome equivalence rather than
trace refinement: dGLP and madGLP may take transitions in different orders than GLP
and maGLP, but we prove they produce the same outcomes, using disjoint substitution
commutativity (from the SO invariant). Second, our proofs use forward simulation directly
without requiring history or prophecy variables—the SO invariant and persistence provide
sufficient structure.
Sangiorgi’s work on bisimulation proof methods [38] develops “up-to” techniques that
reduce the size of relations needed to establish bisimilarity. We use simulation (trace inclusion)
rather than bisimulation (trace equivalence), because GLP’s committed-choice clause selection
means that dGLP and GLP are not bisimilar—they may make different choices when multiple
clauses apply in different orders—but they produce the same outcomes.
Atomic Transactions. Our key technical contribution—that binary atomic transactions
can be correctly implemented by sequences of unary transactions—builds on the theory of
atomic transactions in distributed systems. Lynch et al.’s foundational treatment [26, 27]
establishes the formal framework for transaction serializability and correctness. Bernstein,
Hadzilacos, and Goodman [5] provide comprehensive coverage of concurrency control.
Herlihy and Wing’s linearizability [17] defines the gold standard for concurrent object
correctness: operations appear to take effect atomically at some point between invocation
and response. Our transaction decomposition achieves a related but distinct goal: showing

Grassroots and Monotonic Distributed Systems. The grassroots property—that any
subset of agents forms a functioning subsystem without requiring permission or coordination—
was introduced for distributed platforms [43] using multiagent transition systems with
monotonicity as a key property. The definition used here follows [50], formulated in terms of
interleavings of correct runs of disjoint agent groups. Applications include grassroots social
networks [44], cryptocurrencies [45, 23], and federated governance [16, 51].
Conflict-free Replicated Data Types (CRDTs) [52] achieve eventual consistency without
coordination by requiring that concurrent updates commute. The mathematical structure is
similar: CRDTs use join-semilattices where states merge monotonically, just as GLP agents’
states grow monotonically through message reception. Kleppmann’s work on Byzantine-
tolerant CRDTs [21] extends this to adversarial settings. The Blocklace [2] demonstrates how
independent subsystems can operate and later merge state—precisely the compositionality
that grassroots systems require.
Related Language Implementations. Several concurrent languages share design goals
with GLP. Erlang [3] provides lightweight processes with message passing; Dialyzer [24] adds
success typing. Go [36] offers goroutines and channels. Rust [32] enforces ownership for
memory safety. Session-typed languages like Links [10, 25] and SILL [53] integrate linear
types with communication. Actor languages like Pony [9] use capability systems for data-race
freedom. The ABS language [20] combines active objects with cooperative scheduling and
futures.
GLP differs from all of these in its logic programming foundation: programs are declarative
specifications that also serve as executable distributed code, and the SRSW discipline emerges
from logical structure rather than a separate type system. More specifically, none of the
above provide formal proofs that their distributed implementations correctly implement their
abstract semantics—the specific contribution of this paper.
AI-Assisted Formal Methods. The use of AI to derive implementations from formal
specifications is an emerging discipline.
Fowler et al. [12] propose specification-driven
development where AI generates code from formal specs. Mundler et al. [35] use types to
guide AI code generation. Blinn et al. [6] explore typed interfaces between human designers
and AI programmers. Our approach shares the premise that formal specifications constrain
AI-generated code, but differs in employing three layers of abstraction (math→informal
spec→Dart) with bidirectional feedback, and in the scale of the formal development: the
mathematical specifications in this paper underwent several major revisions driven by
difficulties discovered during AI implementation, as described in Section 7.

with respect to their abstract counterparts. The key insight is that disjoint substitution
commutativity (from the SO invariant) and Persistence (Lemma 3.17) enable correctness
proofs that carry over directly from the single-agent to the multiagent case. The madGLP
design—local variable pairs connected by global links—provides a clean separation between
