Abstract
Abstract. We develop a statistical framework for wealth allocation in which agents hold discrete units of wealth
and macrostates are defined by how wealth is distributed across agents. The structure of the economic state
space is characterized through a value–convertibility function Vi(w), which captures how effectively additional
wealth can be transformed into productive or meaningful value. The derivative of this function determines the
effective number of internally distinct configurations available to an agent at a given wealth level. In a closed
setting with fixed total wealth and a fixed number of agents, we show that equilibrium wealth distributions follow
directly from unbiased counting of admissible configurations and may display a condensation phenomenon, where
a finite fraction of total wealth accumulates onto a single agent once the remaining agents can no longer absorb
additional wealth. We then extend the framework to open systems in which both total wealth and the number of
agents may vary. By embedding the system within a larger closed environment and analyzing a finite subsystem,
we show that exponential weighting in wealth and agent number emerges naturally from counting arguments
alone, without invoking explicit optimization or entropy maximization principles. This extension leads to a
richer interpretation of wealth concentration: accumulation is no longer driven solely by excess wealth, but
by a balance between wealth growth and the system’s capacity to accommodate new agents. Condensation
arises when this capacity is limited, forcing surplus wealth to concentrate onto a few agents. The framework
thus provides a minimal and structurally grounded description of wealth concentration in both closed and open
economic settings.
Keywords: entropy geometry; condensation phenomena; microcanonical ensemble; wealth allocation; econo-
physics

Introduction
Understanding how wealth distributes across agents re-
mains a central problem in complex systems and so-
cioeconomic theory. A wide range of approaches have
been developed to study wealth distributions, includ-
ing stochastic growth models, agent-based exchange
processes, and network-mediated interactions, many of
which reproduce empirical features such as heavy-tailed
distributions and scaling behavior [1–4].
These ap-
proaches typically specify explicit mechanisms—rules of
exchange, saving, growth, or institutional constraints—
and then analyze the resulting steady states or long-
time behavior. A complementary question is whether
some large-scale features of wealth allocation can
emerge even before specifying any particular mecha-
nism, purely from the str ucture of the admissible eco-
nomic configuration space.
At an abstract level, the problem can be viewed as
distributing an extensive quantity among distinguish-
able agents, where each agent is associated with an
internal multiplicity that depends on its local wealth.
Closely related concentration phenomena are known
in the econophysics literature, including ‘wealth con-
densation” in Pareto macroeconomies and exchange-
driven models [3, 5], as well as condensation transitions
in broader classes of occupancy and mass-transport
models [6–9].
In most such settings, the functional
weights controlling concentration are imposed through
exchange rates, growth rules, or model-specific steady-
state measures.
The present work instead derives
equilibrium-like allocations and concentration thresh-
olds from static configuration counting, with the struc-
tural input encoded in a value–wealth mapping.
In this work, we introduce a framework in which
wealth allocation is governed by unbiased counting of
admissible configurations in a coarse-grained value–
wealth space.
The central object of the theory is a
value–wealth convertibility function, which maps the
wealth held by an agent to an effective scale associated
with its internal productive differentiation. This func-
tion is treated as a primitive structural input, encod-
ing how additional wealth expands (or fails to expand)
the economically meaningful distinctions accessible at a
given wealth level. Importantly, value is not identified
with utility, payoff, or measured output; rather, it rep-
resents the resolution of productive configurations that
are not tracked explicitly in the coarse-grained descrip-
tion.
Wealth is modeled as a non-negative extensive quan-
tity distributed among distinguishable agents, with no
upper bound on individual holdings. Within a given

While the initial for-

wealth and a fixed number of agents, many economic
environments are effectively open: wealth may be cre-

tion. We show that an extension allowing both total
wealth and the number of agents to vary can be con-
structed without altering the underlying logic of state
counting, by viewing the observed economy as a finite
subsystem embedded in a larger closed environment.
In this setting, effective weighting in both subsystem
wealth and subsystem size emerges directly from com-

phenomenologically.
Extremization of the resulting entropy yields a sta-
tionary condition that equalizes a structural marginal
across agents, expressible in terms of the local shape
of the value–wealth convertibility function. Depending
on these structural properties, entropy maximization
admits two qualitatively distinct outcomes: a stable in-
terior allocation, or a regime in which no stable interior
optimum exists and the most probable allocations lie at
extreme, highly concentrated boundaries of the feasible
set.
Even when a stable interior allocation exists, the reg-

concentration arising from the absence of a stable inte-

Overall, the framework isolates how the structural
form of value–wealth convertibility shapes macroscopic

It provides a unified minimal de-

stability, and capacity-driven concentration, all derived
from configuration counting without assuming explicit
behavioral rules or dynamical growth models.

6.3
Structural origin of non-equilibrium

Instability-driven condensation is a purely structural
phenomenon arising from the geometry of the value–
wealth mapping.
When marginal value convertibility
fails to diminish sufficiently with wealth, entropy is en-
tropically biased toward configurations in which wealth
accumulates without bound on a subset of agents.
Importantly, this mechanism does not rely on prefer-
ential attachment, strategic behavior, growth dynamics,
or feedback processes. Wealth concentration emerges
because the configuration space itself lacks an entropy-
maximizing interior point compatible with the global
constraint.
6.4
Contrast with equilibrium conden-
sation
The distinction between the two condensation mecha-
nisms may be summarized as follows. In the equilibrium
regime, entropy possesses a stable interior maximum,
but the regular sector has a finite capacity to absorb
wealth; condensation occurs only when the conserved
wealth exceeds this capacity. The intensive parameter
λ remains well-defined and is pinned at its critical value.
By contrast, in the instability-driven regime, no sta-
ble interior extremum exists for any value of λ. Con-
densation is unavoidable regardless of the total wealth
and reflects a fundamental incompatibility between the
value–wealth geometry and entropy maximization.
This distinction highlights that wealth concentra-
tion can arise either as a capacity-limited equilibrium
phenomenon or as a genuine non-equilibrium outcome
driven by entropy instability [7, 8].

7.5
Interpretation
Allowing both wealth and the number of agents to vary
leads to a richer interpretation of wealth concentration.
Accumulation may occur not only because wealth ex-
ceeds the absorptive capacity of existing agents, but
also because the creation of new agents is constrained.
When demographic accommodation is limited, excess
wealth is forced to concentrate, even in the absence of
instability in the underlying entropy structure. This ex-
tension shows that the condensation mechanisms iden-
tified in the closed system persist in open environ-
ments and acquire an additional structural dimension.
Wealth concentration reflects a balance between wealth
growth and the system’s capacity to distribute that
wealth across agents, rather than the presence of ex-
plicit growth dynamics or strategic advantages.

Limitations and scope
The framework developed in this work is intention-
ally structural and minimalist, and several limitations
should be kept in mind when interpreting the results.
First, the theory is static in nature. It characterizes the
most probable wealth configurations compatible with
global constraints, but does not describe the tempo-
ral pathways by which an economy evolves toward such
configurations.
As a result, questions of convergence
time, transient behavior, and path dependence lie out-
side the present scope. Second, agents are treated as
passive carriers of wealth, distinguished only by their
value–wealth convertibility functions. Strategic behav-
ior, learning, negotiation, institutional design, and ex-
plicit exchange mechanisms are not modeled.
While
this abstraction allows structural effects to be isolated
cleanly, it also means that the theory does not cap-
ture feedback between agent behavior and the underly-
ing configuration space. Third, the value–wealth con-
vertibility function is taken as an exogenous structural
input.
In real economic systems, convertibility may
itself evolve with wealth, time, or institutional con-
text. Endogenizing this function would introduce ad-
ditional feedback mechanisms that are not addressed
here.
Fourth, the notion of wealth employed in the
model is deliberately coarse-grained. Wealth quanta are
treated as homogeneous and perfectly transferable, and
distinctions between different asset classes, liquidity
constraints, or non-monetary resources are neglected.
These simplifications are necessary for a tractable state-
counting approach but limit direct empirical interpre-
tation.
Finally, while the extension to open systems
allows both wealth and the number of agents to vary,
the environment itself is treated implicitly through a
reservoir construction.
External shocks, policy inter-
ventions, and historical contingencies are therefore not
modeled explicitly, but only through their effect on
aggregate constraints.
Despite these limitations, the
framework serves a complementary purpose to dynami-
cal and agent-based approaches. By isolating the role of
configuration-space structure and absorptive capacity,
it identifies conditions under which wealth concentra-
tion becomes unavoidable, independent of behavioral
detail. In this sense, the theory should be viewed not
as a predictive model of economic dynamics, but as a
structural baseline against which more detailed mecha-
nisms may be compared.
9
Conclusion and Outlook
In this work we have developed a structural framework
for wealth allocation based on unbiased counting of ad-
missible configurations in a value–wealth space. Wealth
is treated as an extensive quantity distributed across
agents, while productive structure enters through an
agent-dependent value–wealth convertibility function.
Entropy is defined directly from microstate counting
and extremized under minimal global constraints, yield-
ing a stationary condition governed by the curvature of
the convertibility function.
A central outcome of the framework is the identifi-
cation of two distinct routes to wealth concentration.
In the equilibrium regime, entropy admits a stable in-
terior extremum, but the regular sector has a finite ca-
pacity to absorb wealth.
When total wealth exceeds
this capacity, excess wealth must accumulate onto a
subset of agents, giving rise to a capacity-driven con-
densation phenomenon. In contrast, when the entropy
extremum is unstable or absent, wealth concentration
arises through boundary-dominated configurations, re-
flecting a structural incompatibility between the value–
wealth geometry and entropy maximization.
We have further shown that these mechanisms ex-
tend naturally to open economic environments in which
both total wealth and the number of agents may vary.
By embedding the system within a larger closed en-
vironment and analyzing a finite subsystem, effective

weighting in both wealth and agent number emerges
directly from state counting, without invoking explicit
optimization or dynamical assumptions.
In this gen-
eralized setting, wealth concentration reflects not only
excess wealth but also limitations on the system’s abil-
ity to accommodate new agents. When demographic
accommodation is constrained, surplus wealth is forced
to concentrate even in the absence of instability in the
underlying entropy structure.
The framework therefore provides a unified struc-
tural interpretation of wealth concentration across both
closed and open settings. Accumulation appears as a
consequence of finite absorptive capacity in the space
of admissible configurations, rather than as a result of
agent preferences, strategic behavior, or growth dynam-
ics. By distinguishing equilibrium saturation from gen-
uine instability-driven concentration, the theory clar-
ifies multiple pathways through which inequality can
emerge.
The present approach is deliberately foundational. It
does not aim to model temporal evolution, institutional
detail, or policy intervention, but rather to identify min-
imal structural conditions under which wealth concen-
tration becomes unavoidable. Future work may extend
the framework to explicitly time-dependent settings, in-
corporate stochastic wealth injection and dissipation, or
explore how heterogeneity in value–wealth convertibil-
ity influences collective outcomes. More broadly, the
results suggest that configuration-space methods offer
a useful lens for understanding inequality as an emer-
gent property of economic structure.
