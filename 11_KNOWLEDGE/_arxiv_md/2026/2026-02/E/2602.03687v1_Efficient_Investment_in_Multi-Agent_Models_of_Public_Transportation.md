EFFICIENT INVESTMENT IN MULTI-AGENT MODELS OF PUBLIC
TRANSPORTATION
MARTIN BULLINGER, EDITH ELKIND, AND KASSIAN KÖCK
Abstract. We study two stylized, multi-agent models aimed at investing a limited, indivisible resource
in public transportation. In the first model, we face the decision of which potential stops to open along
a (e.g., bus) path, given agents’ travel demands. While it is known that utilitarian optimal solutions can
be identified in polynomial time, we find that computing approximately optimal solutions with respect to
egalitarian welfare is NP-complete. This is surprising as we operate on the simple topology of a line graph.
In the second model, agents navigate a more complex network modeled by a weighted graph where edge
weights represent distances. We face the decision of improving travel time along a fixed number of edges.
We provide a polynomial-time algorithm that combines Dijkstra’s algorithm with a dynamical program
to find the optimal decision for one or two agents. By contrast, if the number of agents is variable, we
find NP-completeness and inapproximability results for utilitarian and egalitarian welfare. Moreover, we
demonstrate implications of our results for a related model of railway network design.
Keywords: Public transport, Egalitarian welfare, Utilitarian welfare, Dijkstra’s algorithm
1. Introduction
Urban transport is not merely a means of moving people from one place to another; rather, it plays a crucial
role in enabling the efficient functioning of, and access to, wealth-generating activities, thereby contributing
to overall societal welfare (Banister, 2006). As cities continue to grow and urban areas become increasingly
denser, it has often been said that transport planning is at a crisis point and that it underestimates key
challenges faced by urban planners (Banister, 2008). In particular, the problems of congestion, environmental
impact, and unequal access to services have spurred a significant body of research focused on optimizing
transit systems (Nnene et al., 2023).
Early studies in urban planning and public policy have demonstrated that a focus on minimizing travel
time or reducing operational costs often overlooks important aspects of service quality, such as the eq-
uitable distribution of transit resources among diverse population groups (Litman, 2017). This evolving
understanding underscores the necessity for transit planning frameworks that balance efficiency with social
equity, ensuring that improvements in operational performance do not come at the expense of marginalized
communities (Sánchez et al., 2003).
This issue could be phrased in terms of utilitarian vs. egalitarian social welfare. Indeed, utilitarian welfare
aims to maximize overall societal benefit by focusing on aggregate well-being.
In the context of public
transportation, this approach would prioritize efficiency and productivity by reducing total travel times,
lowering operational costs, and increasing service frequency to optimize mobility for the greatest number of
users (Vickrey, 1969). However, while utilitarian principles can lead to highly efficient systems, they may
also inadvertently reinforce inequalities, as they tend to favor high-demand areas and neglect regions with
lower population densities or reduced economic activity (Amaral et al., 2009). On the other hand, egalitarian
welfare prioritizes fairness and the equitable distribution of resources, ensuring that no individual or group is
disproportionately disadvantaged (Rawls, 1971). In transportation planning, this means designing systems
that provide access not only for those in high-density, economically vibrant areas but also for individuals
in lower-income or remote communities (Behbahani et al., 2019). Equity-based transit planning ensures
an inclusive urban environment where mobility is a right accessible to all, rather than a privilege enjoyed
primarily by those in well-served regions (Behbahani et al., 2019).

We study both notions of welfare in two stylized, multi-agent models. The first one models the setting
where a bus traverses its route and a central authority has to decide where to open bus stops.
Agents
specify between which locations they would like to travel. This question was recently conceptualized by
Bullinger et al. (2025) who aimed at a utilitarian-efficient and proportionally fair solution. In particular,
they provide a dynamic program allowing to maximize utilitarian welfare in polynomial time. However,
they also show that utilitarian efficiency and a weak notion of proportional fairness are incompatible. An
alternative solution to combine efficiency and fairness is to optimize egalitarian welfare. Clearly, minimizing
such a quantitative measure on a finite solution space guarantees the existence of solutions. However, we
show that maximizing egalitarian welfare is NP-complete. This is somewhat surprising as we operate on
the simple topology of a line graph. Even more, we show that approximating egalitarian welfare by a factor
of less than 2 is intractable.
Our second model concerns the scenario of traveling within a transportation network as modeled by a
weighted graph, where edge weights correspond to travel times. A global decision maker has to decide on
investments in a limited number of edges to reduce the travel time to a fraction of α. We first study the
question of finding the optimal investments to reduce the travel time for a single agent. For instance, this
can be applied to identify the optimal investments to connect major transportation hubs. We develop a
dynamic programming variant of Dijkstra’s algorithm where we iteratively identify optimal travel distances
to a given destination with a fixed budget. This yields a polynomial-time algorithm. We demonstrate the
power of this algorithm by applying it to solve the (utilitarian and egalitarian) optimization problem when
simultaneously traversing routes for two agents in polynomial time. However, if the number of agents is not
fixed, the task becomes much harder. A first indication for this is that two natural greedy algorithms do not
yield a better worst-case approximation guarantee than the trivial solution of not investing at all. Formally,
we prove several computational hardness results: First, for any α ∈[0, 1), both maximizing utilitarian or
egalitarian welfare is NP-hard. Second, if α = 0, i.e., if an investment into an edge reduces the travel
time to 0, then achieving any finite approximation guarantee of utilitarian or egalitarian welfare cannot be
computed in polynomial time unless P= NP. Third, if α ∈(0, 1), then approximating egalitarian welfare
by a factor of more than α+1
2α cannot be done in polynomial time unless P= NP. Note that this expression
is upper bounded by 2 and converges to 1 when α converges to 1. Hence, the decision maker in our second
model faces strong computational challenges.
2. Related Work
A prominent framework for the practical and theoretical study of public transport are multi-objective
vehicle routing problems (VRPs) (Jozefowiez et al., 2008). For example, Jozefowiez et al. (2009) introduce
a bi-objective VRP framework that aims to minimize both the total route length and the imbalance across
routes, defined as the difference between the longest and shortest route lengths.
In addition to VRPs, substantial research has focused on optimizing existing transportation schedules
to balance operator and passenger costs.
For instance, Yu et al. (2011) propose a mathematical model
to determine optimal bus headways, factoring in constraints such as passenger demand, vehicle capacity,
and operational resources, to minimize waiting, boarding, and operating costs. Building upon this, Huang
et al. (2020) develop a real-time dynamic route optimization framework for customized bus services. Their
approach priorities both customer satisfaction and operator profitability by allowing flexible route adjust-
ments to accommodate real-time requests. Yan et al. (2012), on the other hand, target schedule stability by
introducing optimal slack times into bus schedules to minimize deviations.
While these scheduling innovations address general transit challenges, a more specialized application can
be found in the school bus routing problem. It focuses on transporting students from scattered pick-up points
to a common school location. Konstantinos and Dimitra (2023) employ Dijkstra’s algorithm to optimize route
starting points, reducing total student travel time, walking distances, and bus fleet size. However, in contrast
to our setting, their algorithm does not have to adhere to dependencies of the edge weights on the undertaken
investments. Similarly, Arias-Rojas et al. (2012) apply an ant colony optimization metaheuristic to achieve
efficient routing under complex constraints.
Moreover, applying our variant of Dijkstra’s algorithm for
navigating two agents has a similar flavor as the disjoint-paths problem, which is known to be fixed-parameter
tractable with respect to the number of terminal pairs (Robertson and Seymour, 1995).
Recent studies pivot towards stylistic models for designing transportation networks based on specific
demand profiles.
Most related to our first setting, Bullinger et al. (2025) explore the placement of bus

Upgrading a limited number of edges in a network has also been considered for related network optimiza-

flow when upgrading edges subject to a budget constraint. In principle, edges can have several upgrade levels
at different costs. They provide an integer program formulation of their model, prove general NP-hardness,
and then introduce and analyze two heuristics to solve the problem. While our models share the investment
of a limited resource, our approach differs fundamentally. Modeling travel as a network flow leads to a global
optimization of the network, which does not incorporate relationships between the specific start and end of
of a trip. Finally, Landete et al. (2023) study budgeted edge upgrading for the graphical traveling salesman
problem. Similar to Duque et al. (2013), they allow for multiple upgrade levels with different costs and
benefits.

As a final preliminary remark, we note that evaluating an agent’s travel cost for a given solution can be
done in polynomial time for both models. In the PTP, an agent can simply compare the direct walking cost
(i.e., the Euclidean distance between their two terminals) to the cost of using the nearest stops on either
side of their endpoints. In the NTP, computing costs can be done with shortest-path algorithms on the graph
where the discounted edge weights are set to an α fraction of the original weight. One can then apply
Dijkstra’s algorithm per agent or use the Floyd–Warshall algorithm to obtain all-pairs distances, leading to
a worst case running time of O(|E| + |V | log |V |) per agent or O(|V |3) overall, respectively (Cormen et al.,
2009). Using a solution as a certificate, this implies membership in NP for both our problems.
Observation 3. PTP⋆and NTP⋆are contained in NP.
4. Path Transit Problem
In this section, we analyze the complexity and approximability of the path transit problem. Recall that
a smaller discount factor α (for instance, α = 0) makes bus travel considerably more attractive relative to
walking. In such cases, agents may even walk “backwards” from one terminal to reach an established stop.
Conversely, when α is close to 1, the advantage of taking the bus is small, and agents will typically only
board at the first stop and exit at the last stop within their journey interval.
As demonstrated in the introductory example, the bus-stop placement that minimizes the egalitarian cost
can differ from the one that minimizes the utilitarian cost. We will now see that these objectives additionally
exhibit contrasting computational behavior. Bullinger et al. (2025) present an algorithm for finding a optimal
utilitarian solution in time O(nm + m3). Their approach hinges on the fact that, if we have already decided
on stops up to a location h, then the marginal reduction in total travel cost obtained by adding a stop
at position k > h depends only on h and k, and can be computed efficiently with a dynamic program. By
contrast, optimizing the egalitarian objective is significantly harder. We establish the following intractability
result. We defer the proof to Section B.1.
Theorem 4. PTP(EG) is NP-complete, even when α = 0.
A natural follow-up question concerns the robustness of this result, i.e., whether efficient approximation
algorithms exist. Observe that, for any placement of bus stops, each agent’s travel cost cannot drop below
α times their pure walking distance. Consequently, the optimal utilitarian and egalitarian cost are at least
α · c(UT)(∅) and α · c(EG)(∅), respectively. It follows immediately that the trivial solution of purchasing no
stops (or any up to β stops) yields an α−1-approximation (for positive α) under either measure.

5. Network Transit Problem
In this section, we present our results for the network transit problem. We begin by considering two natural
greedy algorithms and show that they do not provide any approximation guarantee. Towards algorithmic
feasibility, we then introduce a modified version of Dijkstra’s algorithm that yields optimal solutions to NTP⋆
for a single agent. This approach generalizes to scenarios involving two agents by multiple executions of the
algorithm. For k ∈N, we define k-NTP⋆as the subproblem where the number of agents in the underlying NTP
is exactly k. We will conclude the section with NP-hardness and inapproximability results for a variable
number of agents.

by means of an illustration, and defer a formal proof to the appendix.
For a fixed α, we create a family of instances parameterized by a budget β. An illustration is given in
Figure 4. There are β + 1 agents and for i ∈[β]0, the ith agent wants to travel between si and ti. There
are essentially two routes they can take: First, they can travel on a direct route along one heavier edge and
β edges of weight 1. Second, they can travel to a hub q and then along a different path, which we call the
motorway, to another hub q′, from where they reach their final destination.
As taking the motorway involves traveling to and from the hubs, removing a single edge of the motorway
does not improve any agents cost. Hence, G↑will only add edges on direct routes. For both cost functions,
these will be the β heaviest edges, one for each except the 0th agent.
Similarly, G↓will first delete edges not on shortest paths and then the lighter remaining edges. This leads
to the same solution. However, a better solution is achieved, when investing in β edges on the motorway
because all agents benefit from this investment. In the limit, when β tends to infinity, this decreases the cost
to a fraction of α. Hence, none of the greedy algorithm achieves an approximation guarantee of better than
α−1.

We define a routing pair as a pair (v, b) of a vertex v ∈V and a budget indicator b ∈[β]0. Algorithm 1
operates similarly to Dijkstra’s algorithm, but instead of visiting vertices, it processes routing pairs. The
idea is to compute, for each routing pair (v, b) the length of a shortest path from s to v when discounting
at most b edges. These distances are captured by variables D[v, b] that are iteratively determined by the
algorithm. We initialize D[v, b] = ∞for each routing pair (v, b) with the exception of the starting pair (s, 0),
for which we set D[s, 0] = 0.
In each iteration, we select the routing pair (v, b) with the smallest value D[v, b] among unselected pairs
and refer it as the pivot pair. The minimality ensures that it would not be selected in further updates. We
use the pivot pair to update distances to “adjacent” routing pairs. There are three types of updates:
• Non-reducing update: simulates a path where the final edge cost is not reduced.
• Reducing update: simulates a path to a neighbor where the final edge cost is reduced.
• Budget-increasing update: simulates a path that is using less than the maximum budget.
The first two of these updates consider updates to neighbors through paths where the last edge is from
the vertex of the pivot pair, and the third type of update is due to using an identified shortest path even for
a higher budget. We could, in principle terminate the algorithm once we have processed (t, β). However, in
our presentation, we explore all possible pairs, which additionally ensures that we find paths from s to all
other vertices in the graph, under any possible budget up to β.

5.4. Variable Number of Agents
Prior work by He et al. (2024) (see a detailed comparison in Appendix C) proved that both egalitarian and
utilitarian cost minimization in their railway-network model are NP-complete via a reduction from 3-SAT.
The same reduction applies to NTP for fixed α = 0. We now extend the hardness result for every fixed α
with 0 ≤α < 1 via a more straightforward reduction from SetCover. This yields the following theorem.
Theorem 11. NTP⋆is NP-complete for any α ∈[0, 1[.
Proof. Memebership in NP is straightforward because the cost and feasibility of a given candidate solution
to NTP can be verified in polynomial time, see ?? 3.
For NP-hardness, we utilize the NP-complete problem SetCover (Garey and Johnson, 1979), which is
formally defined as follows: an instance ⟨U, S, ρ⟩is given by a collection S of subsets of a finite ground set
U and a positive integer ρ. It is a Yes-instance if there exists S′ ⊆S with |S′| ≤ρ that covers U.
Now, consider an instance I = ⟨U, S, ρ⟩of SetCover and a discount factor α ∈[0, 1[. Construct an
instance H′ = ⟨G, A, α, |U| + ρ⟩of NTP with underlying graph G = (V, E, w) as follows: The vertices are
V = VU ∪VS ∪{t}, VU = {xu : u ∈U} are called item vertices, VS = {yS : S ∈S} subset vertices, and we have
a single target vertex t. The edges E = EI ∪ET consist of item edges EI = {(xu, yS): S ∈S, u ∈S} and
target edges ET = {(yS, t): S ∈S}. All edges have the same weight, i.e., we set w(e) = 1 for all e ∈E. The
agent set is given by A = {{(xu, t): u ∈U}}. An illustration of H′ is provided in Figure 7. Let κ(EG) = 2α
and κ(UT) = 2α|U|. Our reduced instance is the instance I′ = ⟨H′, κ⋆⟩of NTP⋆.
Informally, this construction creates the bipartite incidence graph of items and subsets. Each item u ∈U
is represented by an item vertex xu and each subset S ∈S by a subset vertex yS. We connect xu to yS
whenever u ∈S, and each yS links to the shared target t. Agent (xu, t) reaches t by a two-step path from
xu to yS and then to t, where S must contain u. The budget β is set to |U| + ρ, which allows to reduce the
cost for a connection between each item vertex to some set vertex, and an additional ρ edges from the sets
to the target vertex. Intuitively, to reach our cost target, these connections have to correspond to a cover of
U of size at most ρ.
We proceed with a formal proof of the correctness of our reduction. We claim that I is a Yes-instance of
SetCover if and only if ⟨H′, κ⋆⟩is a Yes-instance of NTP⋆.
Since the minimal path cost per agent is 2α, we have, for every solution F ⊂E c(EG)(F) = 2α if and only
if c(UT)(F) = 2α |U|. Hence, the result for NTP(UT) follows immediately from the one for NTP(EG).

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we have investigated two stylized models related to public transportation in which a central
authority faces the decision of investing a limited resource into transportation infrastructure. In the first
model, the decision is to select the stops of a bus line. These are conceptualized by the vertices of a line
graph. In the second model, the decision maker can invest in bus routes to improve the travel time along
them. This is captured by selecting edges in an undirected graph.
Our results are summarized in Table 2. For the path transit problem, we have shown that while maximizing
utilitarian welfare can be performed in polynomial time (Bullinger et al., 2025), maximizing egalitarian
welfare leads to computational hardness and inapproximability. In the network transit problem, we found
polynomial-time algorithms for one or two agents, while we encountered significant hardness results for a
variable number of agents. Our results provide theoretical evidence that aiming at an equitable design of
public transport can face severe computational challenges.
Improving public transport will be an essential cornerstone in transforming the modern society, facing
unprecedented challenges such as climate change. We believe that studying simple models serves as an impor-
tant theoretical foundation for a more complex discourse. There are multiple research directions to build upon
our work. First, there is a gap between our inapproximability results and the trivial α−1-approximation. It
would be interesting to see if one can achieve a better approximation guarantee in polynomial time. Another
immediate direction would be to extend our polynomial-time algorithms for the network transit problem for
one and two agents to an arbitrary fixed number of agents. For our negative results, it would be intriguing
to investigate the possibility of circumventing them in restricted domains for networks that are reasonable
in road networks, e.g., planar graphs. Finally, it would be interesting to enhance the simple models of public
transport by incorporating further features, such as directed travel or online arriving agents.
