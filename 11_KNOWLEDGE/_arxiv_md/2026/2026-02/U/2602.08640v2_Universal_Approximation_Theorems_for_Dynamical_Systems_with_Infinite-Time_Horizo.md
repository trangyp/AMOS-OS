Abstract
Universal approximation theorems establish the expressive capacity of neu-
ral network architectures. For dynamical systems, existing results are limited
to ﬁnite time horizons or systems with a globally stable equilibrium, leaving
multistability and limit cycles unaddressed. We prove that Neural ODEs achieve
ε-δ closeness—trajectories within error ε except for initial conditions of mea-
sure < δ—over the inﬁnite time horizon [0, ∞) for three target classes: (1)
Morse-Smale systems (a structurally stable class) with hyperbolic ﬁxed points,
(2) Morse-Smale systems with hyperbolic limit cycles via exact period match-
ing, and (3) systems with normally hyperbolic continuous attractors via dis-
cretization. We further establish a temporal generalization bound: ε-δ close-
ness implies Lp error ≤εp +δ ·Dp for all t ≥0, bridging topological guarantees
to training metrics. These results provide the ﬁrst universal approximation
framework for multistable inﬁnite-horizon dynamics.

Introduction
Universal approximation results provide rigorous guarantees about the expressive
capacity of neural networks. For dynamical systems, Recurrent Neural Networks
(RNNs) are widely cited as universal approximators47, justifying their deployment in
models of neural computation38,166. However, a fundamental gap exists between
this theoretical promise and biological reality. Existing guarantees are strictly lim-
ited to ﬁnite time horizons or systems with a globally stable equilibrium (the fading
memory property). This restriction explicitly excludes multistability—the coexis-
tence of multiple attractors—which is the dynamical basis of essential cognitive
functions. Decision-making relies on selecting among distinct basins of attraction;
working memory requires self-sustaining persistent activity; and neural oscillations
(limit cycles) drive rhythmic motor control16,85,138,161. Consequently, current theo-
ries fail to address the very dynamical regimes required for computation. Extending
guarantees to inﬁnite time is crucial for temporal generalization: ensuring models
coherently replicate dynamics over indeﬁnite durations rather than ﬁnite windows.
Three fundamental failure modes prevent naive extension of ﬁnite-time results:

• B-type error (Basin mismatch): Small approximation errors near separatrices
push trajectories into incorrect basins of attraction.
• P-type error (Phase drift): For limit cycles, minute period mismatches cause un-
bounded phase divergence as t →∞.
• D-type error (Discretization): Continuous attractors are not structurally stable;
generic perturbations destroy their continua of marginally stable ﬁxed points.
These topological obstructions require specialized treatment beyond standard Grönwall-
based error analysis, which yields exponentially growing bounds unusable for inﬁ-
nite time.
This work establishes the ﬁrst universal approximation results for multistable
dynamical systems over inﬁnite time horizons. Our approach exploits structural
stability theory: Morse-Smale systems—a signiﬁcant subset of all structurally stable
systems—are robust to small C1 perturbations, enabling inﬁnite-time bounds. For
limit cycles, we additionally require exact period matching via a localized correction
procedure.
Our analysis adopts a learning-theoretic framework: we deﬁne target classes
F of dynamical systems to be approximated, a hypothesis class ˆF of Neural ODEs
(Deﬁnition 1), and an approximation criterion (ε-δ closeness, Deﬁnition 9). Uni-
versal approximation means: for any f ∈F and any ε, δ > 0, there exists ˆf ∈ˆF
achieving ε-δ closeness over inﬁnite time.

Main Results (Informal)
We establish universal approximation for three target classes with increasing gen-
erality. Throughout, ε-δ closeness means: the volume of initial conditions with
trajectory error exceeding ε is less than δ (Deﬁnition 9).
Theorem FP (Fixed Points, Informal; see Theorem 3). For any Morse-
Smale system with hyperbolic ﬁxed points and any ε, δ > 0, there exists a
ﬁnite-size Neural ODE that is ε-δ close to the target for all t ∈[0, ∞).
Theorem LC (Limit Cycles, Informal; see Theorem 4). For Morse-Smale
systems with hyperbolic limit cycles, Neural ODEs achieve ε-δ closeness via
exact period matching through localized vector ﬁeld scaling.
Theorem CA (Continuous Attractors, Informal; see Theorem 5). For sys-
tems with normally hyperbolic continuous attractors (line attractors, ring
attractors, isochronous cylinders), Neural ODEs achieve ε-δ closeness via
tiling—approximation by a dense grid of discrete attractors with spacing
< ε.
Finally, we establish a temporal generalization bound to bridge these topologi-
cal guarantees to practical training metrics:
Theorem TG (Temporal Generalization, Informal; see Theorem 6). If ˆφ is
ε-δ close to φ, then the time-averaged Lp error satisﬁes Ep,∞≤εp + δ · Dp,
where D is the domain diameter. This bridges topological guarantees to
practical training metrics like Mean Squared Error (MSE).

1.3
Related Work
The theoretical capability of neural networks to model dynamical systems is well-
established, but existing results largely bifurcate into two regimes: ﬁnite-time ap-
proximation for general systems, and inﬁnite-time approximation for restricted classes
of stable systems.
Universal approximation for RNNs on ﬁnite time horizons was established by Fu-
nahashi and Nakamura 47 and Li 102, however, these guarantees rely on Grönwall
inequalities where error bounds grow exponentially with time, rendering them
vacuous for long-term behavior153. This lineage was expanded by Kimura and
Nakano 89 and proven for various architectures by Chow and Li 25, extending to
time-variant systems with ﬁxed initial states105. This limitation is universal across
architectures: it applies to discrete-time recurrence1,84, time-variant systems105,
and Neural ODEs18. While Neural ODEs are universal approximators for home-
omorphisms177 and ﬂow maps103, these results only approximate the diﬀeomor-
phism ϕt for a ﬁxed t, providing no guarantee of topological equivalence or attrac-
tor preservation as t →∞.
For inﬁnite time, existing results require the fading memory property10, which im-
plies convergence to a unique equilibrium and excludes multistability. Echo State
Networks80 and reservoir computing55 achieve inﬁnite-time universality only for
fading memory systems. The fading memory property ensures that the inﬂuence

Alternatively, operator-theoretic approaches attempt to globalize dynamics by
lifting the state to an inﬁnite-dimensional space where evolution becomes linear.
Koopman operator theory93,119 lifts dynamics to a space of observables, while Car-

ments15,44. While ﬁnite-dimensional truncations like Extended Dynamic Mode De-
composition (EDMD)170 can capture global features, they theoretically require inﬁ-
nite width to eliminate closure error and generally lack rigorous uniform trajectory
guarantees12.
Our work extends these results to inﬁnite time for structurally stable systems.
For a comprehensive review of the literature on universal approximation for dynam-
ical systems, including ﬁnite-time results, fading memory systems, and reservoir
computing, see Appendix F.
Remark (Direct vs. embedding approximation). Finite-dimensional RNNs face a struc-
tural limitation: they cannot express an arbitrary number of attractors without in-
creasing dimension47,76,77,172. Consequently, RNN universality requires embedding
the target into a higher-dimensional latent space. In contrast, Neural ODEs enable
direct approximation: the learned vector ﬁeld operates in the same state space as
the target, preserving the original coordinates and attractor geometry. This distinc-
tion is crucial for mechanistic interpretability of learned dynamics.

Theorem 1 (Palis-Smale128). Every Morse-Smale system is structurally stable: small C1
perturbations yield topologically equivalent ﬂows.
Remark (Morse-Smale systems). Morse-Smale systems are “generic well-behaved”
dynamics: all trajectories converge to ﬁnitely many stable ﬁxed points or limit cy-
cles, with no degenerate connections (homoclinic tangencies, heteroclinic cycles)
that would cause bifurcations. Condition (1) rules out chaotic attractors; condi-
tion (2) ensures structural stability. Morse-Smale systems are not the only struc-
turally stable class—Axiom A systems148 with strange attractors are also struc-
turally stable—but our trajectory-tracking analysis does not extend to chaotic dy-
namics.
Theorem 2 (Stable Manifold Theorem23). Let x∗be a hyperbolic ﬁxed point of a C1
vector ﬁeld f with k stable eigenvalues (negative real part) and n−k unstable eigenval-
ues. Then:
1. The stable manifold W s(x∗) = {x : φ(t, x) →x∗as t →+∞} is a C1 embedded
submanifold of dimension k.
2. The unstable manifold W u(x∗) is a C1 embedded submanifold of dimension n−k.
3. Trajectories in W s(x∗) converge to x∗exponentially.
Remark. For saddle points (0 < k < n), the stable manifold has dimension < n,
hence Lebesgue measure zero. Since basin boundaries in Morse-Smale systems
are ﬁnite unions of stable manifolds of saddles, they have measure zero—a key fact
for controlling B-type error.
Deﬁnition 6 (Normally hyperbolic invariant manifold). A compact invariant mani-
fold M is normally hyperbolic if the linearized ﬂow contracts/expands transverse
to M at exponential rates dominating any expansion/contraction tangent to M42.
Deﬁnition 7 (Strictly inward-pointing). Let X ⊂Rn have smooth boundary ∂X with
outward unit normal ν(x). A vector ﬁeld f is strictly inward-pointing at ∂X if there
exists γ > 0 such that f(x) · ν(x) < −γ for all x ∈∂X.
Remark. The strictly inward-pointing condition ensures that any C1-close approx-
imation ˆf with ∥f −ˆf∥C0 < γ automatically satisﬁes ˆf(x) · ν(x) < 0 on ∂X, so
trajectories of ˆf remain in X (Lemma 13). This can always be achieved by choosing
X appropriately (e.g., a sublevel set of a Lyapunov-like function).

Deﬁnition 9 (ε-δ closeness). Flows φ and ˆφ are ε-δ close if ∥φ −ˆφ∥ε < δ.
Remark. The ε-δ condition decouples:
• Precision (ε): trajectory accuracy for the majority of initial conditions.
• Reliability (δ): fraction of initial conditions with B-type error (basin mismatch).
Near separatrices, perfect trajectory tracking is impossible; the δ term captures this
unavoidable failure region.
This (ε, δ)-closeness is more than a practical heuristic; it induces a rigorous
topology on the space of dynamical systems. Speciﬁcally, it corresponds to the
topology of convergence in measure with respect to the trajectory supremum norm,
which is metrizable via the Ky Fan metric. We provide the topological proofs and
formal basis properties in Appendix B.

Deﬁnition 12 (Isochronous manifold). A normally hyperbolic invariant manifold M
foliated by periodic orbits {γθ}θ∈Θ is isochronous if all orbits have the same period:
T(γθ) = T for all θ ∈Θ.
Deﬁnition 13 (Target class FCA). FCA ⊂X1(X) is the set of vector ﬁelds whose
ω-limit sets are: (i) hyperbolic ﬁxed points, (ii) hyperbolic periodic orbits, (iii) nor-
mally hyperbolic manifolds of ﬁxed points, or (iv) isochronous normally hyperbolic
manifolds of limit cycles (Deﬁnition 12).
Remark (Isochrony requirement). The isochrony condition in (iv) is necessary to pre-
vent P-type error from compounding D-type error. When tiling an oscillatory man-
ifold with discrete limit cycles, each tile inherits the local period. If the original
manifold has a frequency gradient (periods vary along the attractor), neighboring
tiles will have mismatched periods, causing unbounded phase drift between them
as t →∞. Isochrony ensures all tiles can be tuned to the same period via the
correction in Theorem 4. Example: consider a cylinder attractor S1 × [0, 1] where
each circle at height z is a periodic orbit with period T(z). If T(z) = T for all z,
the cylinder is isochronous. If T(z) varies with z (a frequency gradient), it is not
isochronous, and our tiling strategy fails.

Discussion
Summary.
We established the ﬁrst universal approximation theorems for multi-
stable dynamical systems over inﬁnite time horizons. Our results apply to any
Neural ODE architecture with the universal approximation property and provide
explicit ε-δ guarantees linking vector ﬁeld approximation to trajectory bounds. For
systems with multiple stable ﬁxed points (FFP), the approximation error vanishes
asymptotically; for systems with limit cycles (FLC), error remains bounded and is
captured by our metric. Finally, for continuous attractors (FCA), we show that ar-
bitrary precision is achievable via dense tiling of discrete ﬁxed points, overcoming
their inherent structural instability . Furthermore, we established a rigorous link
between our topological ε-δ condition and the squared error loss used in training.
For ﬁnite-time approximation, almost any architecture suﬃces, including lin-
ear state-space models. However, their temporal generalization behavior can vary
wildly, rendering ﬁnite-time guarantees inadequate for accurate, reliable long-time
modeling of a system94,155. If we require the causal relations in a mechanism13,14,87
to be captured by a vector ﬁeld, then inﬁnite-time horizon results provide for mech-
anistic interpretability guarantees at the state space level. This distinguishes NODEs
from ﬁnite-dimensional RNNs59,126, which require embedding-type approximations
that transform the state space and introduce extra state variables164; NODEs in-
stead enable direct approximation of the vector ﬁeld in the original coordinates.
This also makes XFADS33 more expressive compared to LFADS129,156, as it allows
for the direct topological reconstruction of the multistable attractor landscape.
Beyond existence proofs, our framework provides a practical taxonomy for an-
alyzing model failure in computational neuroscience. By classifying errors into
basin-type, period-type, and trajectory-type, researchers can diagnose speciﬁcally
why a model fails to to a true biological implementation. For example, a Basin-
type error corresponds directly to a failure in reliable switching or memory storage,
whereas Period-type errors reﬂect the fragility of oscillatory binding. Furthermore,
this framework introduces a novel metric for the complexity of learning a compu-
tation, quantiﬁed by geometric properties such as the total length of separatrices
required to deﬁne decision boundaries. This enables a more comprehensive and
uniﬁed approach to comparing models of neural computation, moving beyond sim-
ple goodness-of-ﬁt metrics to geometric evaluations of dynamic ﬁdelity.

Limitations.
Learnability gap. Our theorems guarantee existence of approxima-
tions, not their learnability. The loss landscape for inﬁnite-horizon systems is prone
to exploding gradients139, and practical training requires specialized algorithms
such as multiple shooting, teacher forcing68 or homotopy-based training91, along
with strong inductive biases.
Fragility of period matching. Exact isochrony is not structurally stable: in the
space of vector ﬁelds, those with a prescribed period T form a measure-zero set.
Perturbation to network weights generically change the period, reintroducing phase
drift. Thus, while Neural ODEs are universal approximators for limit cycles, they are
not robust implementations for tasks requiring inﬁnite-time phase locking. To mit-
igate this, we propose that it is more eﬃcient to explicitly parametrize the period
of limit cycles. This explicitly separates the approximation of attractor geometry
from the ﬂow speed along it142. Structurally unstable and chaotic systems. Our uni-
form convergence metric is ill-suited for chaotic systems due to sensitive depen-
dence on initial conditions69. We term this failure mode C-type error: even with
accurate local vector ﬁeld approximation, trajectories diverge exponentially. Vali-
dating chaotic models therefore requires shifting from trajectory synchronization
to invariant set reconstruction, such as matching Lyapunov spectra63, minimizing
attractor Hausdorﬀdistance68 or shadowing of the attractor70. Similarly, quasiperi-
odic ﬂows on tori are susceptible to frequency locking, where generic perturbations
collapse dense trajectories into stable periodic orbits131. Center manifolds and ho-
moclinic cycles present even stricter challenges: their dynamics are often deter-
mined by higher-order terms or precise intersection of stable/unstable manifolds.
While robust heteroclinic cycles exist in high-dimensional systems with symme-
try58, generic cycles and non-hyperbolic ﬁxed points are destroyed by inﬁnitesimal
approximation errors, converting them into limit cycles or chaotic tangles. Conse-
quently, inﬁnite-time guarantees for these classes likely require imposing speciﬁc
architectural constraints (e.g., symmetry or energy conservation) rather than rely-
ing on general-purpose approximation.
Non-autonomous systems. Extending inﬁnite-time guarantees to input-driven
dynamics remains open, requiring the analysis of pullback attractors rather than
static invariant sets90. Speciﬁcally, any trajectory can be approximately realized by
a continuous-time recurrent network on a ﬁnite interval and on an inﬁnite interval
for a periodic input124 Recent developments have further established ﬁnite-time
horizon approximation for input-driven systems using NODEs91,103,175 and neural
ﬂows6.
Stochastic dynamics. Real-world systems are invariably subject to noise. Recent
work has begun to establish universal approximation properties for stochastic reser-
voirs and ﬁlters8,39,51. Key developments include approximation guarantees for the
Wasserstein distance between path measures2,22 and ﬂow matching techniques
for generative modeling109,121.

Extended Literature Review
This appendix provides a comprehensive review of universal approximation results
for dynamical systems, establishing the context and novelty of our contributions.
F.1
Approximation on Finite Time Intervals
Universal approximation theorems for dynamical systems primarily address two
regimes: ﬁnite-time simulation via high-dimensional embedding and direct trajec-
tory approximation in the native state space.
Foundational results.
Seminal works by Li 102, Funahashi and Nakamura 47 and
Doya 34 established that RNNs approximate general dynamical systems by embed-
ding dynamics into high-dimensional hidden states. This lineage was extended to
the approximation of continuous functionals and nonlinear operators by Chen and
Chen 20 and Chen and Chen 21, broadening the scope of universality to map input
signals to outputs. This lineage of results extends back to homogeneous state-
aﬃne systems, which were shown to possess similar universality properties in the
discrete-time setting for compact time intervals43,150,151. Crucially, these guaran-
tees are strictly limited to bounded time intervals, leaving long-term asymptotic be-
havior unconstrained.
Grönwall-based bounds.
Most ﬁnite-time results rely on Grönwall’s inequality to
control trajectory diﬀerences, incurring exponential degradation of accuracy over
time25,47,105,152. For time horizon T, if ∥f −ˆf∥C0 < η, then ∥φ(t) −ˆφ(t)∥≤η(eLt −
1)/L, which grows unboundedly as t →∞.

Discrete-time sequences.
RNNs act as universal approximators for various tem-
poral mappings, though often constrained to ﬁnite-time horizons. They can uni-
formly approximate the dynamics of continuous ﬁnite-memory systems on com-
pact domains60 and state-space trajectories over closed discrete-time intervals84,132.
More recent work has explicitly established the ﬁnite-time universality of discrete-
time RNNs1 and linear recurrences with nonlinear projections127, alongside their
ability to map continuous past-dependent sequence-to-sequence functions149. Fur-
thermore, CNNs have emerged as eﬀective alternatives for modeling sequences
with ﬁnite memory3,83.
Continuous-time trajectories.
The universal approximation properties of static
neural networks46 initially established them as powerful tools for modeling non-
linear systems125,168, often employing multilayer perceptrons or radial basis func-
tions17,19,24,48,158. Expanding this to dynamic contexts, the capabilities of continuous-
time recurrent networks for autonomous systems were demonstrated by Funa-
hashi and Nakamura 47 and extended by Kimura and Nakano 89 and Chow and
Li 25. For non-autonomous systems, any trajectory can be approximately realized
on ﬁnite intervals124, a property also held by Neural ODEs for input-driven sys-
tems103,175. Furthermore, even networks with randomly initialized, ﬁxed weights
can achieve universal approximation of continuous functions and dynamical sys-
tems over compact intervals by learning only the bias parameters169.
For non-autonomous systems, where the dynamics explicitly depend on exter-
nal inputs, it has been shown that the states of the output units of a continuous-
time RNN, can approximate the solution of ODEs on compact time intervals48 and
linear, continuous and regular functionals107. A subset of an RNN’s units can ap-
proximate any smooth (C1) dynamical system (input-aﬃne) with arbitrary precision
for ﬁnite time86. Neural oscillators have the universal approximation property on
compact time intervals (approximation of causal and continuous operators)74,100.
Any ﬁnite trajectory of an n-dimensional continuous dynamical system can be ap-
proximated by the internal state of the hidden units and n output units of an Liquid
time-constant (LTC) network65. Finally, Maass et al. 111 extended these results to
biological feedback models, proving that neural networks with dynamic synapses
are universal approximators for dynamical systems, particularly in closed-loop feed-
back conﬁgurations.
Flow approximation.
Flow-based approximations for ﬁnite time have been estab-
lished for architectures including Recurrent High-Order Neural Networks96. Any
continuous dynamical system can be approximately realized on ﬁnite intervals by
an RNN25, extended to time-variant systems with ﬁxed initial states105. More re-
cently, Tabuada and Gharesifard 157 established that Neural ODEs are universal ap-
proximators for monotone analytic functions homotopic to the identity, proving
that the ﬂow of a single-layer system with time-varying parameters can approxi-
mate such mappings over compact sets. Despite these advances in representing
complex mappings through ﬂows, these results remain conﬁned to ﬁnite-time hori-
zons and do not address the long-term topological stability of multistable systems.
F.2
Fading Memory Systems
For inﬁnite time horizons, existing results predominantly require the fading mem-
ory property (FMP).
Deﬁnition 28 (Fading Memory Property10). An operator H mapping input histories

to outputs possesses the FMP if it is continuous with respect to a weighted norm
that discounts the past: there exists a weighting function w(z) →0 as z →∞such
that closeness in the weighted past implies closeness in output.
Topological constraint.
The FMP imposes a severe restriction: systems with fad-
ing memory “forget” initial conditions, implying global asymptotic stability (monos-
tability). This inherently excludes multistable systems—those with multiple ﬁxed points
or limit cycles where long-term behavior depends on initial conditions. A simple
bistable system has no fading memory: the ﬁnal state depends entirely on the basin
of attraction.
Reservoir computing.
Echo State Networks (ESNs) were shown to be universal
for fading memory systems by Jaeger 80, formalized by Grigoryeva and Ortega 55,56.
Liquid State Machines achieve universal computation for ﬁlters with FMP110. Vari-
ous reservoir architectures approximate FMP systems: linear systems with polyno-
mial readouts10, state-aﬃne systems53, and Simple Cycle Reservoirs101.
Other stability conditions.
Results by Li et al. 106,108 show linear RNNs approx-
imate regular linear functionals (which have fading memory). State Space Mod-
els are universal for exponentially decaying memory167. Recently, Yasumoto and
Tanaka 171 established “uniform strong universality” for RNN reservoirs, showing
that a ﬁxed, parallelized reservoir can approximate a speciﬁc class of dynamical
systems solely by tuning linear readouts. However, these results generally rely on
the target class admitting a uniform error bound independent of the speciﬁc tar-
get, a property often linked to fading memory constraints. Inﬁnite-time results
by Hanson and Raginsky 61 depend on uniform asymptotic incremental stability,
precluding multistability. Similarly, Bishop 7 establish universal approximation for
RNNs in a stochastic setting, but the result relies on the target system being expo-
nentially p-contractive. This requirement ensures that the inﬂuence of initial condi-
tions vanishes over time, eﬀectively restricting the scope to systems with a single
global stochastic attractor. More recently, Bishop et al. 9 extended this framework
to Bayesian ﬁlters, proving that multilayer RNNs can uniformly approximate the op-
timal ﬁlter. However, this result similarly assumes the ﬁlter is exponentially stable
(contractive), a condition that ensures the ﬁlter forgets its initial distribution. While
these results provide powerful guarantees for ﬁltering and tracking, their reliance
on contraction prevents them from capturing the history-dependent bifurcation
and multistable phenomena essential for many cognitive tasks.
FMP trivializes approximation.
Every fading-memory system144 can be uniformly
approximated by linear dynamics117. Typically, the FMP is used to reduce the ap-
proximation problem to one over a ﬁnite, bounded index set, and then appeal to
the density of fully connected neural network to obtain approximation52. This con-
straint is so restrictive that Boyd and Chua 10 proved FMP is the necessary and
suﬃcient condition for a system to be uniformly approximated by a ﬁnite Volterra
series45,165. The FMP implies “asymptotic independence” of the state from initial
conditions26,114,120, making multistability impossible. Our work addresses this fun-
damental gap.

F.3
Approximation of Diﬀeomorphisms and Flows
Flow maps.
Neural ODEs and invertible neural networks are universal approxima-
tors for diﬀeomorphisms73,79,82,98,116,159,160. For dynamical systems, this guarantees
only ﬁnite-time approximation: the ﬂow φt is a diﬀeomorphism for each ﬁxed t, but
approximating φT does not control behavior for t > T.
Topological conjugacy.
ESNs can be trained to have topologically conjugate dy-
namics to structurally stable systems62. For NODEs, this follows immediately from
structural stability (Theorem 1): C1-close vector ﬁelds yield topologically equiva-
lent ﬂows. Our contribution is making this observation precise with quantitative
ε-δ bounds. Finally, we would like to mention the possibility to construct dynamics
with Cohen-Grossberg networks for arbitrary sets of ﬁxed point and limit cycles
through embedding of dynamics28. Finally, models can be constructed to realize
attractors30, though general inﬁnite-horizon guarantees for these results are lack-
ing.
F.4
Why Multistability Requires New Theory
Fundamental obstructions.
Multistable systems exhibit two failure modes absent
in FMP systems:
1. B-type error: Near separatrices, arbitrarily small perturbations cause trajecto-
ries to converge to diﬀerent attractors (Figure 2).
2. P-type error: For limit cycles, any period mismatch causes unbounded phase
drift as t →∞.

Structural stability as the key.
Morse-Smale systems are the natural target class:
they are structurally stable (small perturbations preserve qualitative dynamics) yet
allow multistability. This paper provides the ﬁrst universal approximation results
exploiting this property.
Beyond Morse-Smale.
Chaotic attractors (C-type error) require fundamentally
diﬀerent metrics, for example, invariant measures or attractor reconstruction32,63,
and remain outside our framework.
F.5
Computational Universality
For completeness, we note that RNNs are Turing universal88,122,123,135,145,146. This
computational universality mirrors that of smooth ODEs11, Hopﬁeld nets147, and
ﬁnite automata simulations78,95,97,154. However, computational universality is or-
thogonal to approximation: it concerns symbolic computation, not trajectory track-
ing. Our results address the latter.
