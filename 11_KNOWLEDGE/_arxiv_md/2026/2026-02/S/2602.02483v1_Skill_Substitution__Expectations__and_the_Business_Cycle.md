Skill Substitution, Expectations, and the Business Cycle∗
Andreas Leibing†

Abstract
This paper studies how labor market conditions around high school graduation
affect postsecondary skill investments. Using administrative data on more than
six million German graduates from 1995—2018, and exploiting deviations from
secular state-specific trends, I document procyclical college enrollment. Cyclical
increases in unemployment reduce enrollment at traditional universities and shift
graduates toward vocational colleges and apprenticeships. These effects translate
into educational attainment. Using large-scale survey data, I identify changes in
expected returns to different degrees as the main mechanism. During recessions,
graduates expect lower returns to an academic degree, while expected returns to a
vocational degree are stable.
Keywords: college enrollment, expectations, business cycles, apprenticeships

I.
INTRODUCTION
Skills are a key driver of economic growth (e.g., Hanushek and Woessmann, 2008),
and the type of skills acquired is crucial for individual earnings trajectories. While
general skills offer higher long-term returns and greater adaptability, specific skills pro-
vide immediate labor market advantages but may become obsolete more quickly (e.g.,
Hanushek et al., 2017, Deming and Noray, 2020). Economic shocks can affect human
capital investments by shifting expected returns and the value of outside options (e.g.,
Becker, 1962). But how do they affect the choice between general and specific skills?
While countercyclical college-going is a stylized fact in economics, less is known about
how economic conditions shape the skill content of educational choices – particularly
the tradeoff between general and specific skills.1
Germany’s education system provides an ideal case to study how individuals sub-
stitute between general and specific skill investments. Traditional universities empha-
size general skills, while other colleges, such as universities of applied sciences, blend
general and vocational training. Apprenticeships, the typical outside option, focus on
specific skills. Unlike the U.S., Germany’s centrally funded higher education system
and regulated labor market resemble those of many OECD countries. Both aspects
may affect not only the cyclicality of outside options but also how economic shocks
influence the expected returns to different skill investments.2
This article exploits deviations from secular state-specific trends in the unemploy-
ment rate around high school graduation to study how economic conditions affect
postsecondary skill investments. Using administrative microdata on German higher
education, I construct a panel at the commuting zone level on the share of more than
six million high school graduates who enroll in and graduate from different institu-
tions between 1995 and 2018. To distinguish between expected returns and outside
options as mechanisms, I supplement my analysis with administrative data on the ap-
prenticeship system, large-scale survey data on the expected returns to college, and a
decomposition of business-cycle fluctuations into their national and local components.
I find that economic shocks reduce overall college enrollment, particularly at tradi-
tional universities. A 1 percentage point (pp) deviation of the state unemployment rate
(UR) from its long-term quadratic trend in the senior high school year reduces first-
time college enrollment by 2.10 pp. Enrollment effects are concentrated at traditional
universities and result in a long-term reduction in educational attainment at these in-
stitutions (-1.04 pp). In contrast, attainment at more vocationally oriented colleges

This paper makes three main contributions. First, it contrasts the conventional
wisdom of countercyclical college-going in predominantly Anglo-Saxon contexts (see
Graves and Kuehn, 2022, for a survey). I find that college enrollment is procyclical.
Focusing on the German context further allows me to contribute to the literature by
examining different enrollment options that entail substantially different skill content.
While in the U.S., especially community colleges serve as “safe port in a storm” (Betts
and McFarland, 1995), my findings suggest that rather than enrolling in college, more
graduates choose apprenticeships as a highly regulated outside option that focuses
on specific skills. Higher state-level unemployment rates affect traditional universities
that emphasize general skills. Enrollment at universities of applied sciences, which are
a hybrid, serves as a middle ground and is less affected.4
Second, this paper contributes to a better understanding of how economic condi-
tions shape human capital investment by distinguishing between national shocks and
shocks at the (sub-) state level. Traditionally, the literature on graduating in a recession
has focused on more macroeconomic shocks to study different outcomes.5 Recently, the
literature focuses more on local labor markets (Clark, 2011, Sievertsen, 2016, Deneault,
2025, e.g.,), or sector and occupation-specific shocks, (Charles et al., 2018, Han and
Winters, 2020, Acton, 2021, Weinstein, 2022, Bütikofer et al., 2025). I synthesize the two
strands of the literature by showing that local unemployment shocks affect outside op-
tions, while macro shocks affect both outside options and expectations. Conditioning
on year fixed effects may change signs by closing the expectations channel.

Lastly, this paper contributes to the literature on belief formation and its interplay
with the business cycle (e.g., Beaudry and Portier, 2006, Blanchard et al., 2013, Enke
and Zimmermann, 2019, Nowzohour and Stracca, 2020). Based on large-scale survey
data on the expected value of different degrees, I show that aggregate UR increases are
substantially more salient than local conditions and reduce the expected value of an
academic degree. At the same time, the expected value of a vocational degree is stable
over the business cycle. For low-achieving male graduates without college-educated
parents, recessions even increase the expected value of a vocational degree. This belief
is at odds with empirical evidence showing that college graduates, especially those in
majors with more general skill sets, workers with more general skills, and graduates
from selective institutions are less vulnerable to recessions (see Hoynes et al., 2012, Al-
tonji et al., 2016, Eggenberger et al., 2022, Weinstein, 2025, respectively). The literature
on expectations in education has not yet explicitly studied the influence of the business
cycle (see Giustinelli, 2023, for an overview).

II.
INSTITUTIONAL BACKGROUND
I focus on high school graduates who obtained the highest leaving qualification (allge-
meine Hochschulreife, aka Abitur). Typically, this takes 12–13 years of schooling. These
graduates represent the largest subgroup, with about 75 percent of all high school grad-
uates (including, e.g., Fachgebundene Hochschulreife, aka Fachabitur) and 40 percent of
the population aged 18–19. Graduates with Abitur have the full choice set: They can
enroll at any institution, in any degree, and, sometimes, depending on their GPA, in any
major. In the main analysis, I abstract from the specific institutional type and major and
distinguish between three options that vary in their underlying skill content:
1. University: Traditional universities in Germany are publicly financed, offer a
wide range of academic programs, and are known for providing a comprehen-
sive and theoretical education. University degrees are typically full-time and em-
phasize the acquisition of general skills. Internships are allowed but usually not
mandatory. There is, hence, greater emphasis on general skills.
2. Other colleges: All other colleges, such as universities of applied sciences (Fach-
hochschule), typically offer a more practice-oriented education. They focus on en-

gineering, business, design, media, and technology programs. Their curriculum
often includes internships. They can be privately financed and typically offer
bachelor’s degrees through dual study programs, in which students spend much
of their studies in paid on-the-job training. Depending on the degree type chosen
(dual or full-time), the focus on specific skills is stronger.
3. Apprenticeship: High school graduates who enter the labor market typically
start an apprenticeship at a firm. Apprenticeships combine on-the-job training
at the firm with theoretical education at a vocational school (Berufsschule). These
vocational training programs cover various occupations, e.g., traditional crafts,
technical fields, and healthcare. By design, apprenticeships are highly firm- and
occupation-specific, as is their skill content.
Higher education has low direct costs, as there are no general tuition fees in Germany.6
A means-tested federal aid program (BAFöG) supports around 25 percent of full-time
students. However, only 28 percent of formally eligible students (i.e., those under 30)
receive any BAFöG, either because they failed the means test, or because they did not
apply (Fidan and Manger, 2022). Similarly, only five percent of students take on a pri-
vate loan. Of the average monthly income of students under 21, 66 percent is parental
allowances, 13 percent is own earnings, twelve percent stems from federal aid BAFöG,
and nine percent is from other sources (Middendorff et al., 2017). I discuss credit con-
straints as a mechanism in Section VII.B.
The largest reform in the European Higher Education sector was arguably the Bologna
Process, which began in 1999 (Crosier and Parveva, 2013). In Germany, the main
change, alongside the introduction of the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation
System (ECTS), was the transition from a single-tier to a two-tier degree system. Adop-
tion accelerated in the mid-2000s: by 2008, bachelor’s programs had become the dom-
inant entry route, with the sharpest compositional shift around 2005 (see Figure A.1).
Relative to the Diplom and Magister degrees, the median time-to-degree for the first cy-
cle was reduced from 6 to 4 years (Bietenbeck et al., 2023), while apprenticeships typi-
cally take around 2.5 years. A further institutional change was the disappearance of the
“Diplom (FH)” suffix for universities of applied sciences with the BA/MA harmoniza-
tion, which reduced visible credential segmentation between institution types and fa-
cilitated “stacking” across pathways (e.g., combining apprenticeship and BA) within a
similar overall time window. These shifts plausibly altered substitution patterns across
educational options and the horizon over which students map current macroeconomic
conditions to expected returns. Consistent with this, the mid-2000s mark a structural
change in both levels and slopes of the enrollment series (see Section IV.B). I therefore

IV.
DATA AND IDENTIFICATION
IV.A
Main Data Sources
The Studierendenstatistik (student register), provided by the Federal Statistical Office,
covers the universe of students enrolled in higher education in Germany (RDC, 2019b).
Each observation represents one individual enrollment spell and contains information
on the institution, degree, field of study, final grade, entry qualification, gender, age,
county, and year of high school graduation. Due to strict data protection, it does not
contain individual identifiers. County-level data on the number of high school grad-
uates, population, and economic indicators stems from the Regionaldatenbank; county-
level unemployment rates from the Federal Employment Agency (BA). I link student
register data on the number of first-year students who graduated from high school in
a given county and year to the county-level data described above.
To account for the relevance of local labor markets (e.g., Amior and Manning, 2018,
Hershbein and Stuart, 2024) and the high share of commuting across county borders
in Germany (Krebs and Pflüger, 2023), I aggregate all counties to the so-called Rau-
mordnungsregionen level. These regions are groups of counties that reflect a larger com-
muting zone (CZ). Throughout the analysis, I use the current 96 CZ delineations from
the Federal Office for Building and Regional Planning (BBR). My final sample spans
the years 1995 to 2018 and includes 6,296,662 high school graduates.9
As main outcome variables, I use the share of high school graduates with Abitur,
from commuting zone r and graduation cohort t, enrolling in different colleges c within
one year after high school graduation.10 I focus on individuals i in the student register
(Enrolled) with Abitur and use the number of high school graduates from the CZ with
Abitur as the denominator. Until 2011, male high school graduates had to fulfill one
year of civil service. Hence, I consider enrollment up to one year after graduation.

A first omitted variable suspect is technology shocks. For example, high-skill aug-
menting technology shocks may simultaneously increase the expected return to gen-
eral skill investments and affect unemployment rates. Yet, larger technology shocks
often take time to be adopted and cause reallocations in the labor market (see, e.g.,
Comin and Hobijn, 2010). In turn, adoption speed varies geographically and depends
on economic conditions (Hershbein and Kahn, 2018). As a result, a larger share of
technology-induced changes in unemployment may be captured by state-specific trends.
Most importantly, however, the effects of technology shocks on the labor market are
typically ambiguous ex-ante.12 Hence, it is unlikely that technology shocks immedi-
ately affect average college-going.13 Overall, while I cannot rule out that single tech-
nology shocks may affect enrollment choices in specific periods, a systematic bias due
to unobserved technology shocks is unlikely.
A second main suspect are firm’s expectations. If firms were to lower their economic
expectations, e.g., due to political uncertainty, then they may hire fewer workers and
apprentices (Bloom, 2009, Mühlemann et al., 2020), and the unemployment rate would
increase. As a result, high school graduates with Abitur may fail to find an apprentice-
ship and enroll at college instead. If this were the case, my finding of procyclical col-
lege enrollment would be attenuated and may represent a lower bound. Importantly,
however, such expectation-driven changes in firms’ hiring and training behavior are
arguably part of the fluctuations in labor demand that my analysis intends to capture.

ii. Capacity constraints. Another alternative mechanism for procyclical enrollment
is supply-side adjustment, for example, a reduction in the maximum number of stu-
dents a university would admit to a given program. For the U.S. (Deming and Walters,
2017) show that a 10 percent increase in spending increases enrollment by 3 percent.
In Germany, traditional universities are financed through state budgets and multi-year
framework agreements that use upper-bound projections of student numbers (Eury-
dice, 2025). Around 90 percent of university funding comes from public sources, with
roughly three-quarters provided by the states and the remainder by the federal govern-
ment through joint programs such as the Higher Education Pact (Hochschulpakt) and
its successor, the Contract for the Future of Higher Education and Teaching (Zukun-
ftsvertrag Studium und Lehre stärken) (GWK, 2022, HRK, 2025). It is explicitly aimed at
maintaining need-based study capacity, and provides dynamically increasing federal
co-financing, largely independent of short-run state-level unemployment shocks.
Consistent with this institutional background, there is only a small and statistically
insignificant correlation between the state UR and the log total annual spending across
college types in the current year (see Table A.11) However, since the expenditure data
do not separate teaching from research, the estimates in Table A.11 only have limited
information value for any cyclical adjustment on the teaching margin. Overall, this sug-
gests that federal funding arrangements largely stabilize aggregate capacity, and that
supply-side restrictions play a secondary role at best. This is consistent with Marcus
and Zambre (2019), who find no effects of large double cohort shocks (see Section IV.C)
on the number of study programs using any GPA-based admission cutoffs, as a proxy
for capacity constraints.
VIII.
DISCUSSION
What explains the opposing enrollment patterns between Germany and the U.S.? And
how representative is the German case? First and foremost, Germany’s apprentice-
ship system poses a fundamentally different outside option for high school graduates.
25


## Page 27


While apprenticeships are certainly not acyclical per se, they are relatively less cycli-
cal than direct labor market entry in the U.S. (see,e.g., Wolter and Ryan, 2011, for an
overview). Moreover, wages in an increasing number of apprenticeships are bound
to collective agreements, and marginal high school graduates who choose an appren-
ticeship in a recession would often compete with school graduates from lower tracks
for remaining positions. Lastly, apprenticeship training in regions with high unem-
ployment is subsidized by the federal and state governments (see, e.g., OECD, 2025,
for an overview). As a result, Germany may be comparable to other countries with an
established dual education system, such as Switzerland, Austria, and Denmark.22
Figure A.8 shows that Germany may also be representative of several countries
with centrally funded higher education and less cyclical labor markets. One exam-
ple along these two dimensions is Spain. For Spain, the time series presented in Albert
(2000) shows a procyclical pattern for university enrollment. Her regression analysis,
however, relies on within-year variation in unemployment across regions and yields
a positive effect of unemployment on enrollment. My decomposition helps reconcile
this tension: macroeconomic unemployment fluctuations are the main driver of belief
changes in my data, whereas more local deviations primarily shift outside options and
need not generate the same cyclicality as aggregate shocks.23
Another difference between Germany and the U.S. is that enrollment effects may oc-
cur at a different margin. In the U.S., effects are concentrated at two-year and weaker
at four-year colleges (see e.g., Charles et al., 2018). Individuals who would have oth-
erwise started or continued employment now enroll for more short-term degrees or
retrain to wait out the recession. However, the German higher education system does
not offer comparable associate degrees. Instead, more vocational institutions offer for-
mally equivalent bachelor’s degrees. Thus, college enrollment in Germany is generally
a longer-term commitment, and retraining at college is less common. The de facto
study duration of a bachelor’s degree in Germany is about four years, but it used to be
about six years before the stepwise Bologna reform in the early 2000s (Bietenbeck et
al., 2023). However, retraining alone is unlikely to explain the different results. Like
this study, work from the U.S. mostly focuses on first-time enrollment at college-going
age (e.g., Lovenheim, 2011, Charles et al., 2018) and papers that consider retraining
generally have similar conclusions (e.g., Betts and McFarland, 1995).

variation conditional on CZ and cohort fixed effects.

IX.
CONCLUSION
This paper examines how labor market conditions at high school graduation affect
postsecondary skill investments, distinguishing between general academic education
and more specific vocational pathways. Using administrative microdata on the uni-
verse of students in German higher education, as well as administrative data on the
apprenticeship market, I find that higher unemployment at graduation can cause stu-
dents to shift toward vocationally oriented colleges and apprenticeships.
I embed my analysis in a simple Roy model where students weigh general and spe-
cific skill investments based on their expected returns. My empirical analysis shows
that high school graduates respond mainly to macroeconomic, rather than local la-
bor market conditions, consistent with the role of salient macroeconomic conditions
in shaping expectations. Survey data on subjective expectations shows that for the
average student, downturns reduce the expected value of an academic degree, while
low-performing male students even expect greater returns to a vocational degree in a
recession. I find that capacity and credit constraints only play a negligible role.
My findings contrast with conventional wisdom on countercyclical college-going
and demonstrate that downturns can cause a shift to investments in more specific skill
investments. The results highlight that students’ adjustment to business cycle condi-
tions depends critically on the available outside options. In systems with established
vocational pathways, recessions can shift students toward investments in specific skills,
while overall college enrollment declines. Countries that wish to adopt elements of the
German apprenticeship system should be aware of how these changes affect the re-
sponsiveness of human capital investments to economic shocks. Ultimately, my find-
ings highlight the need to clearly distinguish between the effects of macroeconomic
and local conditions on human capital investment.
