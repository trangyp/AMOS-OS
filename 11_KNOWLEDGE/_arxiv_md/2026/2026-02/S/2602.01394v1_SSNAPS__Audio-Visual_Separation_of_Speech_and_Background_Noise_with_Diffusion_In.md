Abstract
This paper addresses the challenge of audio-
visual single-microphone speech separation and
enhancement in the presence of real-world en-
vironmental noise. Our approach is based on
generative inverse sampling, where we model
clean speech and ambient noise with dedicated
diffusion priors and jointly leverage them to re-
cover all underlying sources. To achieve this, we
reformulate a recent inverse sampler to match
our setting. We evaluate on mixtures of 1, 2,
and 3 speakers with noise and show that, despite
being entirely unsupervised, our method consis-
tently outperforms leading supervised baselines
in word error rate (WER) across all conditions.
We further extend our framework to handle off-
screen speaker separation. Moreover, the high
fidelity of the separated noise component makes
it suitable for downstream acoustic scene detec-
tion.

1. Introduction
A central problem in speech processing is extracting clean
speech from recordings made in noisy conditions. Over the
past decade, this field has shifted towards neural network-
based approaches. Most of these methods tackle noise sup-
pression in a supervised manner: a neural network is trained
to reconstruct clean speech from its noisy counterpart. Such
supervised approaches can achieve outstanding performance
on the specific tasks and conditions for which they were
trained (Subakan et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Jung et al.,
2024; Richter et al., 2023; Lemercier et al., 2023; Gao &
Grauman, 2021). However, one of their key limitations is a
lack of flexibility. When the acoustic environment changes,
supervised models typically need to be retrained for the new
conditions to prevent a drop in performance.

In contrast, unsupervised speech processing follows a dif-
ferent strategy. Specifically, unsupervised generative frame-
works train a prior speech model that captures the data
distribution of clean speech signals. This prior model then
exploits its knowledge of clean speech to infer the desired
speech signal from a degraded observation. The key advan-
tage of this approach is that the speech model is not tied
to the observation model. Unsupervised speech estimation
is thus motivated by this flexibility. As it does not require
training a dedicated model for each specific type of degra-
dation, it can operate with arbitrary speech or noise models
and can handle any number of speakers. This versatility
allows a range of speech processing tasks to be addressed
within a single, unified paradigm.
The main interest of this paper is unsupervised speech sep-
aration in the presence of background noise. In particular,
we focus on algorithms that use diffusion models (Ho et al.,
2020; Song et al., 2021; Karras et al., 2022) as data priors.
More specifically, our goal is to deploy the inverse prob-
lem framework, in which an estimate of the clean data is
obtained by sampling from the posterior distribution condi-
tioned on the observation. While this technique has been suc-
cessfully applied to various speech processing tasks, such as
(Moliner et al., 2024b; 2023; 2024a; Xu et al., 2025), the lit-
erature on diffusion-based speech separation in the presence
of background noise is scarce (Nortier et al., 2024; Ayilo
et al., 2025; Yemini et al., 2025; Ayilo et al., 2026). This
is particularly surprising, given the abundance of diffusion
inverse samplers, e.g. (Chung et al., 2023; Song et al., 2023;
Zhang et al., 2025; Cardoso et al., 2024; Dou & Song, 2024;
Kawar et al., 2022; 2021).
In this work, we present SSNAPS, standing for Separation
of Speech and Noise with Annealed Posterior Sampling. We
adopt the approach presented in (Yemini et al., 2025) and use
two diffusion priors in conjunction: a clean speech prior and
an environmental noise prior. The two data priors are jointly
utilised to discern speech from noise. The former is further
harnessed to separate competing speech sources. We primar-
ily focus on the audio-visual setting, i.e., we assume that
for each speech-and-noise mixture, lip-region videos for all
speakers are available. Unlike (Yemini et al., 2025), which
used the diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) (Chung et al.,

multiple statistically independent signals from two distinct

speakers. While in (Yemini et al., 2025) evaluations were
conducted only on two-speaker mixtures with noise, we
evaluate on noisy mixtures of 1, 2, and 3 speakers. Our
experimental study shows that SSNAPS achieves the best
WER scores, even compared to top-tier supervised counter-
parts. Thirdly, we propose an extension of SSNAPS that
allows it to perform off-screen speech separation. To the
best of our knowledge, we are the first to propose an inverse
problem technique for this scenario. Lastly, we demonstrate
that the high-fidelity separation of the ambient noise further

4. Method
The goal of our proposed method SSNAPS is to recover
concatenated speech signal c, and the ambient noise n by
sampling from the posterior p(c, n | y). To this end, in
this section, we reformulate DAPS to address our scenario,
enabling it to estimate multiple statistically independent
signals from two distinct distributions: clean speech and
background noise. For the sake of formulation simplicity,
we assume that all speakers have visual cues in the form of
lip videos matching their speech recordings. Later on, we
present an extension to handle unseen speakers.

5. SSNAPS for Off-Screen Speech Separation
The video for each speaker guides the inverse sampling
process to match the speaker’s lip movements. It helps dis-
cern, e.g., between speakers with similar pitch, and between
speech and noise with overlapping spectral profile. How-
ever, in many real-world cases, not all speech recordings
have corresponding lip videos.
In the absence of visual guidance, without special care, sep-
aration techniques may be prone to signal leakage between
estimated sources, also known as crosstalk. This is mani-
fested as one source being audible in the recovered signal of
another source. The primary cause of this crosstalk is typi-
cally the difficulty of the separation model in distinguishing
between acoustically similar sources, for example, in sep-
arating same-sex speech. Although the off-screen speech
separation scenario is highly realistic, this field remains
underexplored. While a few supervised paradigms have
been proposed (Pan et al., 2024; Yoshinaga et al., 2023), to
the best of our knowledge, we are the first to address this
challenge in the unsupervised regime.
Here we assume that among K speakers, only K −1 have
visual cues. The K-th speaker is arbitrarily defined as the
one lacking visual guidance. We further assume that K > 1,
i.e., our focus is on joint speech separation and enhancement.
Since SSNAPS is an unsupervised technique, only the case
of a single video-less speaker is considered. Otherwise,
major methodological changes will likely be required due
to ambiguity in source attribution, which is left for future
work.

6. Experimental Results
In this section, we present the experimental study used to
evaluate the proposed method. We describe the datasets used
for evaluations and key implementation details. SSNAPS is
compared to leading baselines on speech separation, speech
enhancement, and off-screen speech separation. Finally,
we verify the suitability of the recovered noise signal for
acoustic scene detection.
6.1. Setup
Data. The clean speech dataset used for training and evalu-
ation was VoxCeleb2 (Chung et al., 2018). The English-only
version (Bowen et al., 2022) of the audio-visual dataset was
used to align with the dataset on which the visual encoder
was trained. This amounted to roughly 628,000 videos,
equivalent to 1,759 hours. 2,000 videos were reserved for
testing. The audio and video have sampling rates of 16 KHz
and 25 Hz, respectively. For SSNAPS, lip-region videos are
extracted from full-face videos by following the prescription
in (Haliassos et al., 2024).
For ambient noise recordings, two datasets are used. The
first dataset is DNS (Dubey et al., 2022), which is used
for both training and testing. It is a subset of AudioSet
(Gemmeke et al., 2017) with additional recordings from
Freesound1. Overall, it consists of about 65,000 clips from
150 noise classes, totaling 181 hours of recordings. We
use the train/validation/test splits defined by (Yemini et al.,
2025), resulting in 64,000 noise signals for training, 100 for
validation, and 900 for evaluation.
The second noise dataset was DCASE 2020 Challenge (Heit-
tola et al., 2020), which was used only for evaluation. It
contains data recorded in ten European cities and nine de-
vices. There are ten noise classes, and all recordings have a
fixed length of 10 s, sampled at 44.1 KHz, which we down-
sampled to 16 KHz. Only the test split was used, totaling
19 hours.
Networks architecture.
The speech diffusion denoiser
Fθ was trained on 4s-long clean speech signals from the
English-only version of VoxCeleb2. The background noise
diffusion denoiser Gϕ was also trained on 4s signals, but
of noise recordings drawn from DNS. They are both based
on the lightweight U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) archi-

tecture NCSN++M proposed in (Lemercier et al., 2023).
The NCSN++M backbones of Fθ and Gϕ are wrapped with
STFT and inverse STFT, similarly to (Moliner et al., 2024b).
All STFT operations use a Hann window of 510 samples
and a hop length of 160. This results in 256 frequency bins.
For Fθ, the NCSN++M backbone is augmented with visual
features integrated via FiLM layers (Perez et al., 2018), and
was trained with classifier-free guidance. The lip video
frames are encoded using the BRAVEn model (Haliassos
et al., 2024) variant finetuned on visual speech recognition.
Each lip video frame is encoded to a feature vector in R1024.
Altogether, the number of trainable parameters of Fθ and
Gϕ is 129.5M and 39.7M trainable parameters, respectively.
Baselines.
SSNAPS is primarily compared against two
top-tier audio-visual supervised baselines, which we trained
from scratch. The first competing supervised method is
FlowAVSE (Jung et al., 2024), an audio-visual speaker ex-
traction method. Its architecture is based on cascaded dis-
criminative and generative models. The generative model is
based on flow matching, which aims to improve the signal
quality of the extracted speech at the output of the discrim-
inative network. FlowAVSE has 60.2M parameters. The
second supervised baseline is RAVSS (Pan et al., 2024),
a recently proposed powerful audio-visual discriminative
technique based on the transformer architecture (Vaswani
et al., 2017) with 24.3M trainable parameters. Its design
allows it to separate a variable number of speakers using a
single model and to address scenarios with missing visual
streams. Both baselines outperform several previously pro-
posed discriminative and generative audio-visual paradigms.
Since RAVSS did not originally consider noisy mixtures,
we treat the noise as an additional source lacking visual
features.
We train FlowAVSE and RAVSS on speech-noise com-
binations from VoxCeleb2+DNS, where the signal-to-
interference ratio (SIR) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) used
to create the training mixtures matched the evaluation proto-
col described in the sequel. Importantly, during the training
stage of RAVSS, visual streams are randomly discarded to
make the model robust to missing visual features. To demon-
strate the performance improvement provided by SSNAPS
relative to unsupervised techniques, we also compare it with
DAVSS-NM (Yemini et al., 2025) for two-speaker speech
separation. It also utilises speech and environmental noise
priors and estimates the individual sources using DPS as the
inverse sampler.
Evaluation protocol. All methods were evaluated on mix-
tures of 1, 2, and 3 speech signals and a single background
noise recording. All mixtures were 4 seconds long, result-
ing in d = 64, 000. Each scenario comprised 500 mixtures.

6.4. Acoustic Scene Detection
Beyond extracting the individual speech sources, SSNAPS
also provides an estimate of the ambient noise signal. To
assess its suitability for acoustic scene detection, we deploy
a classifier (Hu et al., 2020) trained for the DCASE 2020
Challenge (Heittola et al., 2020), addressing the task of
assigning 10s-long noise recordings to one of ten classes.
Each noise class corresponds to a distinct environment. (Hu
et al., 2020) proposed to group the ten noise classes into
three hyperclasses. It then trains two classifiers, one for
the original classes and one for the hyperclasses. Finally,
the classification results are fused and translated to a final
assignment of the noise recording to one of the original ten
classes.
We evaluate the noise classification results for mixtures of
a single speaker and noise at different SNR levels. As (Hu
et al., 2020) assumes pure environmental noise signals, it
is expected that overlapping speech will degrade the per-
formance of the classifier. Consequently, unlike speech en-
hancement, where higher SNR leads to better performance,
in acoustic scene detection in the presence of speech, lower
SNR is preferred.
The classification results are presented in Table 4 for 100
mixtures. The classification accuracy of the 10-class classi-
fier, the 3-class classifier, and their fusion is computed for
the raw mixture and the noise signal recovered by SSNAPS.
It is evident that, for both classifiers and their fusion across
all SNR levels, the noise signal estimated by SSNAPS im-
proves accuracy.
It should be noted that (Hu et al., 2020) trained the classifiers
on 10s-long noise recordings sampled at 44.1KHz. However,
we use only 4s-long mixtures, sampled at 16KHz. There-
fore, for computing the results in Table 4, only DCASE
noise signals that are accurately classified after truncation,
downsampling to 16KHz, and upsampling to 44.1KHz are
considered.
7. Conclusion
We presented a novel unsupervised generative audio-visual
method for speech separation and enhancement, which esti-
mates several speech signals and a background noise signal
from their mixture. Our method, SSNAPS, utilises both
clean speech and noise priors represented as diffusion mod-
els. It leverages the inverse problems framework to estimate
the constituents of the mixture by sampling from the pos-
terior. As SSNAPS is an unsupervised technique, it can be
applied to various speech processing problems with minimal
changes.
