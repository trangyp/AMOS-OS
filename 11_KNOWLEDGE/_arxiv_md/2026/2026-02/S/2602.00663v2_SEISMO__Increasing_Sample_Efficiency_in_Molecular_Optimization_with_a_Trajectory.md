Abstract
Optimizing the structure of molecules to achieve
desired properties is a central bottleneck across
the chemical sciences, particularly in the pharma-
ceutical industry where it underlies the discovery
of new drugs. Since molecular property evalu-
ation often relies on costly and rate-limited ora-
cles, such as experimental assays, molecular op-
timization must be highly sample-efficient. To
address this, we introduce SEISMO, an LLM
agent that performs strictly online, inference-time
molecular optimization, updating after every ora-
cle call without the need for population-based or
batched learning. SEISMO conditions each pro-
posal on the full optimization trajectory, combin-
ing natural-language task descriptions with scalar
scores and, when available, structured explana-
tory feedback. Across the Practical Molecular
Optimization benchmark of 23 tasks, SEISMO
achieves a 2–3 times higher area under the opti-
misation curve than prior methods, often reaching
near-maximal task scores within 50 oracle calls.
Our additional medicinal-chemistry tasks show
that providing explanatory feedback further im-
proves efficiency, demonstrating that leveraging
domain knowledge and structured information is
key to sample-efficient molecular optimization.

1. Introduction
Generating molecules with desired properties is a central
goal in chemistry. A prominent example is the pharma-
ceutical industry, which requires the discovery of novel
molecules in order to bring new drugs to the market. Such
molecules must satisfy multiple objectives, including po-
tency, selectivity, and a range of pharmacokinetic and
physicochemical properties (Lipinski et al., 2012), which
can conflict and therefore require explicit trade-offs. At
the same time, the searchable chemical space is enormous,
often estimated at around 1060 plausible small drug-like
molecules (Bohacek et al., 1996).
Because this chemical space is far too large to be explored
exhaustively, practical molecular design is instead carried
out as an iterative optimization process in which candidate
molecules are proposed, evaluated, and refined over suc-
cessive steps (Ghiandoni et al., 2024). For the evaluations,
experimental assays provide the most reliable assessment of
molecular properties, but their high cost and limited through-
put make them impractical for use at every iteration. As
a result, molecular optimization pipelines rely on compu-
tational methods that approximate experimental outcomes
(Ghiandoni et al., 2024). In practice, these methods span
a spectrum of cost and fidelity: lower-cost approximations
often provide weaker correlations with experimental mea-
surements, while more accurate approaches, such as physics-
based simulations and protein–ligand co-folding methods,
are computationally expensive and rate-limited (Meli et al.,
2022; Passaro et al., 2025; Abramson et al., 2024). Reduc-
ing the number of required evaluations therefore has direct
practical value: sample-efficient optimization methods en-
able the use of higher-quality evaluations, which in turn
leads to molecules that are more likely to perform well in
practice.
To systematically assess sample efficiency in molecular op-
timization, the Practical Molecular Optimization (PMO)
benchmark was introduced, consisting of a diverse set of
molecular design tasks (Gao et al., 2022). Results reported
for PMO show that current reinforcement learning and ge-
netic algorithm methods can achieve strong final perfor-
mance, but they typically still require thousands of evalua-

We hypothesize that sample efficiency in molecular opti-
mization can be improved by more effectively leveraging
available chemical and biological prior knowledge, rather
than framing optimization as a purely black-box search
driven only by scalar objective values. Motivated by this,
we introduce a goal-directed, highly efficient large language
model (LLM) agent that we call SEISMO (Sample-Efficient
Inference-Stage Molecular-Optimization agent). SEISMO
uses the LLM itself as the optimizer and performs strictly on-
line, inference-time molecular optimization by conditioning
each proposal on the full optimization trajectory, including
prior knowledge and oracle feedback.
2. Related work
Molecular optimisation in drug discovery is commonly
framed as black-box search over chemical space, with ap-
proaches ranging from objective-guided generative models
to population-based and surrogate-assisted search. REIN-
VENT exemplifies the first class, optimising a molecule
generator via policy-gradient reinforcement learning using
batch-level scalar rewards (Olivecrona et al., 2017). Graph-
GA is a standard baseline for the second class, evolving
molecules through graph-based crossover and mutation op-
erators (Jensen, 2019). Gaussian process Bayesian optimi-
sation (GP BO) is another widely used approach, fitting a
surrogate over molecular fingerprints and selecting candi-
dates via acquisition functions (Tripp et al., 2021).
Follow-up work has explored improving the sample ef-
ficiency of REINVENT-style methods, with Augmented
Memory (Guo & Schwaller, 2024) and Beam Enumeration
(Guo & Schwaller, 2023) reusing and filtering previously
evaluated molecules through experience replay, SMILES
augmentation, or substructure-based conditioning. Despite
these improvements, optimisation proceeds in fixed batches,
imposing a minimum number of oracle calls between policy
updates.
Recent methods incorporate LLMs into population-based
molecular search. MolLEO extends Graph-GA by using an
LLM to perform crossover and mutation operations based
on parent molecules (Wang et al., 2024). Unlike MolLEO,
LICO uses an evolutionary algorithm to generate candidate
molecules via standard crossover and mutation, and employs
an LLM as a surrogate model to predict a mean and standard
deviation for these candidates, selecting evaluations using
an acquisition function (Nguyen & Grover, 2024).
In contrast to prior molecular optimisation methods,
SEISMO treats optimisation itself as an inference-time,
sequential decision process executed entirely within the
LLM’s context, where each oracle call immediately up-
dates the state on which the next proposal is conditioned.
Rather than learning a policy, surrogate, or population dy-
namics, SEISMO relies on trajectory-level reasoning over
past molecules, scores, and feedback, making the optimisa-
tion history the central learning signal.
LLM-based agents in chemistry have recently emerged
to support scientific workflows. ChemAgent (Tang et al.,
2025) and CACTUS (McNaughton et al., 2024) target free-
form chemistry question answering by augmenting LLM
reasoning with external tools such as literature search and
standard cheminformatics libraries, while DrugAgent (Inoue
et al., 2025) focuses on drug–target interaction prediction.
Other systems extend this agentic framing to broader end-
to-end assistance in medicinal chemistry. ChemCrow (Bran
et al., 2024) and Frogent (Pan et al., 2025) integrate tool
use for information retrieval, synthesis planning, and work-
flow execution, with ChemCrow additionally enabling au-
tonomous laboratory execution. MADD (Solovev et al.,
2025) similarly emphasizes early-stage hit identification
by coupling LLM agents to specialised drug-design tools.
Across these approaches, the LLM primarily acts as a con-
troller that orchestrates external tools, while optimisation
itself is delegated to learned models, heuristics, or down-
stream pipelines.

which, to our knowledge, have not been used as control
signals in molecular optimisation.

4. Approach
A natural way to incorporate the aforementioned priors and
side-information is through LLMs. As LLMs are trained on
large, heterogeneous text corpora that include substantial
scientific and technical content, they can serve as a practical
proxy for the kind of accumulated domain knowledge de-
scribed above. While such knowledge is not guaranteed to
be perfectly accurate or complete, it provides an inductive
bias that can guide the search toward plausible, high-scoring
regions of chemical space.
Crucially, LLMs operate on natural language, which makes
it straightforward to provide rich, structured feedback be-
yond a scalar objective. In particular, we can supply the
agent with the task description, the sub-score decomposition

5. Experiments
We implement SEISMO as a stateful LLM workflow that
realizes the trajectory-conditioned, inference-time optimiza-
tion procedure described above.
Figure 1 provides an
overview of the workflow. The concrete workflow graph
structure, validation logic, prompting, and implementation
are described in detail in Appendix A. An ablation study of
the LLM backbone is provided in Appendix B. An example
output trajectory is shown in Appendix E.4.
Overall, we perform three experiments. First, we evaluate
SEISMO on the established PMO benchmark. Then we
perform an experiment to more directly mirror common
medicinal-chemistry optimization loops (drug-discovery
workflow proxies). Unlike PMO, these tasks use oracles
under our control, allowing us to not only assess SEISMO’s
general drug-discovery performance, but to investigate the
influence of additional explanatory feedback beyond the
scalar score on performance metrics. Lastly, we qualita-
tively evaluate SEISMO in co-folding-based optimization
regimes where simple surrogate oracles are inadequate. To
this end, we use a more expressive but computationally ex-
pensive protein–ligand structure prediction oracle based on
the Boltz-2 model (Passaro et al., 2025) to evaluate SEISMO
on a target with no known binders and limited task-specific
prior knowledge. The following paragraphs describe each
experiment and their results in more detail.
5.1. PMO benchmark
Setup: We evaluate SEISMO on the PMO benchmark of
23 molecule optimization tasks (Gao et al., 2022). For a
fair comparison with prior work, we use the original PMO
oracles and provide the agent only with the scalar oracle
score at each iteration, without additional explanation. We
compare SEISMO to established molecular optimization
baselines, including REINVENT, Graph-GA, and Gaussian-
process Bayesian optimization (Olivecrona et al., 2017;
Jensen, 2019; Tripp et al., 2021). Then, to provide a broader
comparison against more recent approaches, we compare
against the results reported by Nguyen and Grover (Nguyen
& Grover, 2024), which include a wide range of optimiza-
tion models such as MolLeo and LICO.
We consider two SEISMO variants: In the task described
variant, the optimization objective is stated in the initial
prompt using descriptions (cf. Appendix E.2) comparable
to those used in other work (Wang et al., 2024). In the no
description variant, the initial prompt omits any descrip-
tion of the objective and the agent treats the oracle as fully
black-box. For each task, we run both variants for three
independent repetitions with a budget of 50 oracle calls.
Following PMO, we evaluate sample efficiency using the
area under the optimization curve (AUC). The optimization
curve tracks the best oracle score achieved up to a given
oracle call and is defined as
st = max
i≤t F(mi),
where F(mt) ∈[0, 1] is the oracle score of the molecule
evaluated at oracle call t. AUC is defined as the area under
st, normalized by the oracle budget, such that AUC val-
ues lie in [0, 1]. Higher AUC values correspond to faster
discovery of high-scoring molecules.
We report top-1 AUC over 50 oracle calls and over 10,000
oracle calls. We also report the best score achieved after 50
oracle calls, defined as s50 = maxi≤50 F(mi). For compar-
ison to recent work (Nguyen & Grover, 2024), we addition-
ally report top-10 AUC over 1,000 oracle calls, where the
optimization curve tracks the average score of the ten best
molecules found so far at each oracle call.
For metrics evaluated beyond 50 oracle calls, SEISMO is
only allowed to propose molecules during the first 50 calls.
For t > 50 we hold st fixed at its value after 50 calls, while
other methods continue optimizing over the full budget. This
is due to LLM API cost given the number of tasks, variants,
and repetitions. Implementation details are in Appendix A.
When reporting aggregate results across the 23 PMO tasks,
we follow PMO and sum per-task metrics across tasks. Since
all reported metrics are bounded in [0, 1], the maximum
achievable aggregate score is bound by 23.

In the no description mode, the initial prompt only specifies
that the model should optimize a score. In all other modes,
the task is stated explicitly (cf. Figure 1).
We evaluate on two tasks reflecting common drug discovery
settings: (i) hit identification, which involves discovering ac-
tive molecules for a target protein, and (ii) lead optimization,
which focuses on finding close analogs under additional con-
straints (More details in Appendix A).
For the hit identification proxy, we use the predicted in-
hibitory concentration (IC50) against the SARS-CoV-2 main
protease (Mpro) as the main objective, where lower values
indicate stronger inhibition. The final oracle (IC50–QED–
Novel) also includes novelty and drug-likeness constraints
to restrict the search to chemically plausible and marketable
molecules.
We evaluate sample efficiency using the optimization curve
defined as the best feasible prediction so far over oracle calls,
where feasibility is defined by satisfying the imposed drug-
likeness and novelty constraints. Specifically, the curve
records the lowest predicted inhibitory concentration among
feasible molecules at each oracle call, and we quantify per-
formance by the area under this constraint-filtered curve
(AUC) over 50 oracle calls. Because this is a minimization
task with unbounded scores, the resulting AUC is not nor-
malized, with lower values indicating more sample-efficient
optimization.
For the lead-optimization proxy, we study a similarity-
constrained optimization task in which the agent seeks
close analogs of a reference molecule with improved drug-
likeness (Sim-QED). The scalar objective combines similar-
ity to the reference and drug-likeness into a single score. We
evaluate this task in two regimes: (i) a praxis-motivated case
study using Quercetin as the reference molecule, which has
known medicinal relevance but poor drug-like properties
(Gugler et al., 1975), and (ii) a randomized setting with 20
diverse reference molecules sampled to test robustness on
targets with low a priori familiarity to the LLM.
We report top-1 AUC over 50 iterations for the scalar objec-
tive combining similarity and drug-likeness, averaged across
target molecules. For the Quercetin case study, we report
a goal-based metric: the number of oracle calls required to
first reach a predefined target score corresponding to a close
analog with improved drug-likeness (cf. Appendix A).
Depending on the mode, the oracle additionally returns
an explanation of the score. Explanations for QED and
Similarity provide property-level and substructure-level de-
compositions, respectively. For predicted IC50 values, we
use the feature attribution method SHAP. See Appendix E.3
for example explanations. In the partial explanation mode,
only the drug-likeness QED score is being explained. We
run SEISMO for 50 iterations in all four workflow modes
for each task. All results are reported with 95% confidence
intervals computed via bootstrap resampling.

5.3. Co-folding-based oracle
Setup: This oracle uses a so-called co-folding model to pro-
duce an evaluation function. A co-folding model (Abramson
et al., 2024; Passaro et al., 2025; Corley et al., 2025) can
take protein sequences, small compounds as SMILES and
other biologically relevant molecules as inputs and produce
a 3D representation (also called a structure) of the complex
made up of the molecules. A special case in the co-folding
space is the Boltz-2 model (Passaro et al., 2025), which
built a so-called binding affinity prediction head on top of
the structure prediction, which estimates how strong a small
molecule will bind to a given protein. We employ the output
of the affinity head as our score to guide the LLM to generate
molecules keeping the protein sequence fixed. We randomly
select a protein sequence from the MGnify metagenomics
database (Richardson et al., 2023) that lies in a low-density
region of the ESMAtlas embedding space (Lin et al., 2023),
while still having a high-confidence predicted 3D structure.
The chosen protein is MGYP001550541752. These restric-
tions were chosen to challenge our agent: We wanted a
protein with no known binders to reduce the chance of mem-
orization by the LLM while at the same time still having a
confident 3D structure for reliable Boltz-2 performance.
Similar to the previous experiments, the co-folding-based
oracle was evaluated with three feedback conditions:
No sequence: we provided the Boltz-2 binding affinity
probability together with a task description, but without the
protein sequence of the target.
No explanation: as before, but the LLM has access to the
protein sequence.
Full explanation: in addition to the previous information,
the oracle provides the three-letter-codes of all residues that
are closer than 5 ˚A to the molecule together with Boltz-2’s
confidence scores (cf. Appendix C and E.3).
In Appendix C, we evaluate SEISMO on a well-studied
(SARS-CoV-2 Mpro) and a challenging target (TRIB2 pseu-
dokinase), with abundant and limited known reference
binders, respectively. In addition, we report ablations of
the co-folding-based oracle.
Results: All three oracle feedback variants using the Boltz-2
oracle highlight that the LLM can use the binding affinity
probability as a signal to optimize molecules. At the same
time, the mean score never passes 0.75, which indicates
that the agent is unable to identify a strong binder. Nev-
ertheless, the results show that the agent can optimize for
novel binders using expensive, but expressive oracles. As
depicted in Figure 2(c), the added explanation (blue curve)
leads to faster optimization compared to no explanation and
no protein sequence. Interestingly, the optimization curves
for the latter two cases are nearly indistinguishable, sug-
gesting that for this protein, the protein sequence alone is
not enough to help the agent improve the molecules more
sample-efficiently.
6. Discussion
This work introduced SEISMO, a goal-directed LLM agent
that performs inference-time optimisation by leveraging the
chemical and biological priors encoded in large language
models to navigate chemical space. Unlike population-
based or batched acquisition methods, SEISMO operates
in a strictly online loop, where every oracle call is immedi-
ately incorporated into the next proposal. This removes the
sample-efficiency limits imposed by methods that must first
evaluate an entire batch or population before any learning
can occur. On the PMO benchmark, this design yields large
gains in sample efficiency. SEISMO reaches near-optimal
scores within 50 oracle calls and achieves 2–3× higher AUC
than established baselines, even when those baselines are
allowed to continue optimising for thousands of additional
evaluations.
A central finding is that SEISMO’s sample efficiency de-
pends on the type and amount of information provided to
the agent. SEISMO integrates three types of information:
natural-language objective descriptions, oracle scores, and,
when available, explanations of those scores. Our ablations
isolate the contribution of each component. When SEISMO
receives only a scalar score without a task description, per-
formance collapses across PMO and all case studies. Pro-
viding a task description leads to a dramatic improvement,
demonstrating that the agent actively exploits prior chemical
and biological knowledge encoded in the LLM to interpret
the objective and propose meaningful starting molecules and
molecular changes. However when using our co-folding or-

method performance. One reason might be that the LLM
does not know and cannot guess the correct binding site for
this unfamiliar protein. Hence, limiting the use of sequence
information alone. This theory is further strengthened by
the observation that providing close residues as feedback
improves performance. Consistent with this, adding expla-
nations for why a molecule received a given score improves
sample efficiency across all investigated tasks. This result
has a broader implication: post-hoc explainability methods
can be used not only for human-centred model interpreta-
tion, but as a machine-understood control signal that directly
guides optimisation. In SEISMO, these explanations turn
opaque oracle evaluations into actionable feedback about
structure–property relationships, enabling more efficient and
targeted search.
A few other recent methods incorporate LLMs into molecu-
lar optimisation. They are using the LLM as a component
inside an evolutionary algorithm though to propose muta-
tion or crossover candidates (Wang et al., 2024; Nguyen
& Grover, 2024). In these approaches, the LLM does not
maintain an explicit record of the optimisation trajectory.
Our results show that this is an inefficient way to use LLMs.
In such schemes, the LLM is blind to the optimisation tra-
jectory and therefore cannot reason about cause and effect
across iterations. SEISMO instead uses the LLM as the
optimiser itself: the model conditions on the task specifi-
cation and the full history of oracle feedback to make each
successive proposal, enabling genuinely sequential decision-
making. This allows SEISMO to reach strong solutions
within minimal oracle calls.
Due to the fact, that LLM inference is computationally
expensive, SEISMO is not optimized for settings with very
low-cost oracles where millions of evaluations are feasible.
Instead, it excels in regimes where each oracle call is costly,
so that reducing the number of oracle calls dominates the
LLM compute.
A second implication of conditioning on the full optimi-
sation trajectory is that the effective optimisation horizon
is bounded by the LLM’s context window. For all tasks
considered in this work, this never became a limiting factor,
though it may matter in very long-horizon settings. History
summarisation could mitigate this limitation, but we do not
explore it here.
We want to emphasize that practically relevant drug-
discovery problems are not tabula rasa optimisation tasks.
They are strongly structured by prior physical and chem-
ical knowledge. Small-molecule binding is governed by
hydrogen bonding, electrostatics, hydrophobicity, desolva-
tion, and conformational strain, which sharply constrain
which molecular modifications can plausibly improve affin-
ity. These principles induce strong inductive biases over
chemical space, ruling out large classes of unproductive
or physically implausible changes. Decades of medicinal-
chemistry research have further established generalizable
relationships between molecular structure and properties
such as potency, selectivity, solubility, permeability, and
metabolic stability.
An optimiser that ignores this ac-
cumulated knowledge must rediscover these regularities
from scratch, which is fundamentally sample-inefficient.
SEISMO explicitly leverages this prior knowledge through
the pretrained LLM, allowing it to start the search in chemi-
cally meaningful regions of the space and to propose edits
that are aligned with known structure–property trends.
Nevertheless, relying on prior knowledge raises the question
of how SEISMO behaves when such knowledge is limited
or unavailable. We therefore evaluated the method on three
settings designed to reduce the usefulness of memorized
target-specific information: similarity and drug-likeness
optimisation to 20 random molecules, SARS-CoV-2 Mpro
inhibitor discovery under a novelty constraint, and optimisa-
tion for binding to a novel protein with no known ligands.
Across all three cases, SEISMO still exhibits strong opti-
misation behaviour. It identifies highly similar molecules
with substantially improved drug-likeness, proposes novel
SARS-CoV-2 Mpro inhibitors with high predicted IC50 val-
ues, and substantially increases the binding probabilities
for a protein with no known binders. This indicates that
SEISMO primarily exploits general chemical and biophys-
ical priors rather than relying on memorized examples of
specific targets.
SEISMO requires no training, no fine-tuning, and no hyper-
parameter tuning by the user. It combines pretrained domain
knowledge from the LLM with information extracted from
oracle calls via post-hoc explainability, yielding an optimiser
that is easily usable in new tasks. Because SEISMO directly
builds on general-purpose LLM capabilities, and those ca-
pabilities continue to improve, the method is expected to
scale naturally with model progress. As LLMs become
better at chemical reasoning, causal inference, and long-
horizon planning, the same optimisation framework should
become correspondingly more powerful without changing
the underlying algorithm.
7. Conclusion
We introduced SEISMO, a goal-directed LLM agent that
performs strictly online, inference-time molecular optimiza-
tion by leveraging chemical and biological priors encoded in
large language models. Across the PMO benchmark and ad-
ditional medicinal-chemistry tasks, SEISMO achieves sub-
stantially higher sample efficiency than established optimiza-
tion methods, often reaching near-optimal solutions within
a few dozen oracle calls. Our ablations show that providing
richer task-relevant information, via objective descriptions

results position information-aware, trajectory-conditioned
LLM agents as a practical framework for molecular opti-
mization in regimes where oracle evaluations are expensive.
Impact Statement
This work introduces an LLM-based agent for molecular
optimization that achieves substantially higher sample effi-
ciency than existing approaches. In many realistic molecular
design settings, each evaluation corresponds to an expensive
or rate-limited computation or experiment, making the num-
ber of oracle calls the central bottleneck. By converging to
high-quality solutions within a small number of evaluations,
the proposed method directly addresses this bottleneck. As a
result, the approach has strong relevance for early-stage drug
discovery and other molecular design workflows, where
careful use of resources is essential.
The proposed approach is designed as a decision-support
tool rather than an autonomous discovery system and relies
on externally defined evaluation oracles. Any real-world
application would therefore remain subject to domain ex-
pertise, experimental validation, and regulatory oversight.
While the method could in principle be applied to optimiz-
ing molecules with undesirable or harmful properties, this
risk is not unique to the approach and is shared by existing
molecular optimization techniques. In practice, such risks
are mitigated through controlled access to evaluation oracles
and established institutional safeguards. We do not identify
ethical concerns beyond those already well established in
machine learning–based molecular design.

A. Implementation Details
Agent Architecture.
We implement our molecule op-
timization agent as a cyclic workflow using LangGraph
(LangChain AI, 2025).
Our workflow consists of five
nodes: generation, parsing, validation, prediction, and fi-
nalisation. The conversation history begins with a system
prompt providing general instructions (cf. Appendix E.1)
followed by a task-specific first message describing the
optimization objective (cf. Appendix E.2). At each itera-
tion, the generation node invokes an LLM with this full
history and instructs it to output structured JSON contain-
ing a SMILES string (Weininger, 1988) and rationale. The
parsing node extracts the JSON, and the validation node
uses RDKit (RDKit, 2025) to verify SMILES validity. In-
valid outputs trigger a re-generation with corrective feed-
back; we count these as iterations to avoid infinite loops.
Valid molecules proceed to the prediction node, where a
pluggable oracle computes a score and optionally an ex-
planation. We then construct feedback including the score,
iteration count, and any explanation-derived insights, which
is appended to the conversation history for the next gen-
eration step. The loop terminates upon reaching a target
score or an iteration limit. The finalisation node then gen-
erates a natural language summary of the optimization tra-
jectory grounded in the structured per-step records (gener-
ated SMILES and rationales) and oracle scores. The sum-
mary highlights which structural modifications improved or
degraded performance, aimed to help medicinal chemists
interpret the agent’s reasoning and extract actionable in-
sights. Unless stated otherwise, the generation and finalisa-
tion nodes use claude-opus-4-5-20251101-v1:0
from Anthropic. We selected this LLM based on an ablation
study (cf. Appendix B).
Oracle Instantiation and Benchmarking Protocol.
The
prediction node is implemented as a modular oracle inter-
face. When switching between tasks, we only replace the
oracle callable and the initial task message used to initial-
ize the conversation history, while keeping the rest of the
workflow unchanged. For head-to-head comparison with
established molecule-optimization methods, we evaluate
on the Practical Molecular Optimization (PMO) bench-
mark, which consists of 23 different molecule optimisa-
tion tasks and was designed to evaluate sample efficiency
in molecular optimisation (Gao et al., 2022). The tasks
span a diverse set of medicinal-chemistry objectives, in-
cluding drug-likeness, bioactivity against targets, similarity-
and rediscovery-based goals, isomer and substructure con-
straints, and multi-property optimization, and require op-
timizing molecular structures through oracle calls. To run
our agent on PMO tasks, we call the benchmark oracles
through the TDC package, following the same oracle access
pattern used in the original PMO setup (Huang et al., 2021).
Importantly, to avoid altering the benchmark oracles, the
oracle feedback provided to the agent is limited to the scalar
score returned by the oracle without any further explanation
of the score.
Within this constraint, we test two variants that differ in
whether the LLM is told what the oracle measures in the
description. In the task described variant, the initial task
message explicitly states the objective (e.g., “maximize the
geometric mean of ...”), while the per-iteration feedback
still contains only the scalar score. Our descriptions of
the tasks are comparable to the descriptions used in other
work (Wang et al., 2024; Nguyen & Grover, 2024) and
are specified in Appendix E.2 and our GitHub. In the no
description variant, the initial message omits any description
of the objective, so the agent must treat the oracle as fully
black-box and rely purely on observed (molecule, score)
pairs. For each of the 23 PMO tasks, we run both variants
for 3 independent repetitions. Due to the API costs for LLM
inference, we cap each run at 50 LLM calls.
We follow PMO’s recommended sample-efficiency evalua-
tion via the area under the optimization curve (AUC) (Gao
et al., 2022). The optimization curve is defined as the top-n
score achieved so far versus the number of oracle calls; we
focus on n = 1 (top-1) because we are interested in finding
a high-scoring molecule rather than optimizing finding a
batch of molecules in our setting (cf. problem formulation).
We report top-1 AUC over the first 50 oracle calls, matching
our agent’s optimization budget, and top-1 AUC over 10,000
oracle calls for consistency with PMO reporting (Gao et al.,
2022). Our agent is only allowed to propose molecules for
the first 50 oracle calls; for calls 51–10,000 we keep its
best-so-far score fixed (no further optimization). In contrast,
PMO baselines are evaluated over the full 10,000 oracle
calls using their published trajectories. We also report the
best score achieved after 50 oracle calls. We report 95% CIs
that we computed via bootstrap resampling of the different
repetitions across the 23 tasks.
To provide a broader comparison that also includes more
recent models, we additionally evaluate top-10 AUC over
1,000 oracle calls and compare against the values reported
by Nguyen et al., who provide results for a broad range
of optimization models (Nguyen & Grover, 2024). For
our agent, we stop optimization after 50 oracle calls and
keep the top-10 score fixed for the remaining 950 iterations,
whereas all other methods from Nguyen et al. were allowed
to optimize for the full 1,000 oracle calls.
For baseline comparisons within the PMO benchmark, we
use the released per-iteration trajectories (including the pro-
posed SMILES and corresponding oracle scores) and re-
compute our metrics (top-1 AUC at 50 and 10,000 calls, and
best-at-50) from those raw traces to ensure identical metric
definitions. We include the top three methods reported by

Extended Tasks with Interpretable Oracle Feedback.
Beyond PMO, we evaluate the agent on two tasks that more
directly mirror common medicinal-chemistry iteration loops:
improving a predicted activity signal under a drug-likeness
constraint, and improving drug-likeness while preserving
similarity to a reference molecule. Since for these tasks
we implement the oracles ourselves, we can also control
the amount of explanation returned to the agent and ablate
different feedback settings.
Hit-identification proxy: SARS-CoV-2 Mpro inhibition with a
QED and novelty constraint. The objective is to minimise a
predicted IC50 (the half-maximal inhibitory concentration)
while satisfying QED ≥0.6 (Quantitative Estimate of Drug-
likeness). The oracle returns (i) a scalar optimisation score
used by the agent and (ii) a structured breakdown containing
the predicted IC50 and QED. Predicted IC50 is produced by
an XGBoost regressor (Chen, 2016) trained on a dataset of
measured IC50 values against SARS-CoV-2 main protease
(training details are provided at the end of this part of the
Appendix). Molecules are featurised using MACCS keys
(Durant et al., 2002). QED is computed with RDKit (RDKit,
2025) and is a heuristic measure derived from the empirical
distribution of molecular properties among approved oral
drugs (Bickerton et al., 2012). This constraint additionally
helps to keep the search in regions where the XGBoost
predictor is more reliable. We additionally constrain on
novelty, which we define as absence from PubChem (Kim
et al., 2025); molecules that are present in PubChem are
treated as non-novel and are considered invalid proposals.
In the task description we refer only to a generic protease
target (rather than explicitly naming SARS-CoV-2 Mpro),
since explicitly naming the target triggered LLM refusal
behaviour due to its safety filters.
Lead-optimisation proxy: Similarity to a reference molecule
with improved QED. Given a reference molecule x⋆, the
goal is to maximise structural similarity to x⋆while increas-
ing QED. Similarity is computed as Tanimoto similarity
between MACCS-key fingerprints of x⋆and the proposed
molecule (Durant et al., 2002); QED is computed with
RDKit (RDKit, 2025). The oracle returns both sub-scores
(sim, QED) and a scalar objective defined as their average
s = 1
2(sim + QED), which is the quantity optimized by
the agent. Consequently, s ≥0.8 corresponds to an average
similarity and QED of at least 0.8.
We study two scenarios for this task. (i) A praxis-motivated
case study with x⋆= Quercetin, which has been discussed
as a potential therapeutic but is known for poor solubil-
ity/bioavailability and rapid metabolism. The aim is to find
close analogs with improved drug-like characteristics. (ii) A
setting intended to evaluate the performance on molecules
with low a priori familiarity to the LLM: we sample 20 tar-
get molecules uniformly at random from PubChem under
constraints designed to ensure diversity and a non-trivial
QED-improvement problem. Concretely, each sampled tar-
get has molecular weight < 500, QED ≤0.5, and maxi-
mum pairwise Tanimoto similarity ≤0.2 with all previously
selected targets (cf. Figure A1).
Oracle explanations. For the XGBoost IC50 model, expla-
nations are generated with SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017)
over MACCS keys, yielding per-feature attributions indi-
cating which substructures most increased or decreased the
predicted IC50. We present the 10 substructures with high-
est attribution (driving lower IC50) and the 10 with lowest
attribution (driving higher IC50), each annotated with its
presence in the current molecule and SMARTS pattern to
guide structural modifications. For QED, we provide a
property-level decomposition (e.g., molecular weight, logP,
HBD/HBA, PSA, rotatable bonds) and indicate which prop-
erties most reduced QED for the current proposal and what
ranges would be more favourable. For similarity, we explain
which MACCS keys differ between x⋆and the proposal
(keys present in exactly one of the two), and map these keys
to their corresponding SMARTS patterns to make the struc-
tural mismatch interpretable. See Appendix E.3 for example
explanations.
Evaluation protocol and metrics. For the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro
task and the Quercetin lead-optimisation task, we run 3 in-
dependent repetitions for each of the four information con-
ditions, for up to 50 iterations. For the 20 random PubChem
targets, we run one 50-iteration optimisation per target and
information condition, and report results averaged over the
20 targets.
For the SARS-CoV-2 Mpro task, we evaluate sample effi-
ciency using the optimisation curve of the best (lowest)
predicted IC50 among molecules that satisfy the constraints
(QED ≥0.6 and novel) versus oracle calls. If no molecule
that satisfies the constraints has been found yet, we set the
curve value to 1 mM to allow AUC computation. We report
the AUC over 50 oracle calls under this constraint-filtered
curve. For the 20 random-target lead-optimisation setting,
we report top-1 AUC over 50 iterations for the scalar ob-
jective s = 1
2(sim + QED), averaged across targets. Since
we include Quercetin as a concrete example, we evaluate it
with a more directly interpretable, goal-based metric: the
number of oracle calls required to first reach s ≥0.8 (i.e.,
average similarity and QED of at least 0.8), corresponding

XGBoost training.
The XGBoost regression model was
implemented using the xgboost python package (ver-
sion 3.1.2) and trained to predict pIC50 values for SARS-
CoV-2 Mpro inhibition using the ASAP Discovery antivi-
ral potency dataset (asap-discovery/antiviral-potency-2025-
unblinded) accessed via the Polaris platform (Wognum
et al., 2024). Molecular structures were encoded as 167-bit
MACCS fingerprints using RDKit. The XGBRegressor was
configured with 2,000 estimators, a learning rate of 0.03,
subsample ratio of 0.8, L1 regularization (α = 0.1), L2 regu-
larization (λ = 2), histogram-based tree construction with
loss-guided growth policy, and a maximum of 128 leaves
per tree. Hyperparameter optimization was performed via
random search over 40 trials, exploring combinations of
maximum tree depth (3, 4, 5, 6), minimum child weight (1,
3, 5, 7), gamma (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0), and column subsampling
ratio (0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0). The model with the lowest root
mean squared error (RMSE) on the held-out test set was
selected as the final model.
Co-folding-based oracle.
The co-folding-based oracle
has access to the target protein sequence and receives a
molecule proposal from the LLM in the form of a SMILES
string (Weininger, 1988). These two inputs are passed to
the Boltz-2 co-folding model. We invoke Boltz-2 with its
default parameters, except that we store the predicted struc-
ture in the PDB format and do not pre-compute multiple
sequence alignments (MSAs), but let Boltz-2 handle that via
their default MSA-server. We run the model on an NVIDIA
A100 GPU and give it a time limit of five minutes. If the
time out is reached or the model process reports a non-zero
exit code the oracle returns NaN as its score. On model
success, the oracle extracts the binding affinity probability
score (affinity probability binary), which is a
sigmoid probability representing Boltz-2’s belief that the
compound binds to the protein. In addition, Boltz-2 pro-
vides a binding affinity value (affinity pred value),
which aims to predict the affinity via the IC50 value of the
system. We decided to use the probability as the oracle’s
score, as the IC50 score is not normalized and preliminary
experiments showed that the agent had difficulty optimizing
it. If the oracle failed to parse the affinity scores, we again
reported a NaN to the agent.
As an explanation, the oracle reported Boltz-2’s non-zero
confidence scores and the protein’s residues that were close
to the ligand. In particular, we reported Boltz-2’s aggre-
gated confidence score (confidence score), the (inter-
face and ligand) predicted template modeling (TM) score
([ligand |i]ptm), the (interface) predicted local dis-
tance difference test score (complex [i]plddt), and the
(interface) predicted distance error (complex [i]pde).
Close residues were defined as all residues which have at
least one heavy atom (any non-hydrogen atom) that is no
more than 5 ˚A away from a compound heavy atom. We
used the biotite (Kunzmann et al., 2023) and scikit-learn
(Pedregosa et al., 2011) libraries to compute these distances.
We reported the three letter code and index of all residues
in the protein sequence satisfying our closeness definition
to the LLM. If any of the above computations failed, we
reported an empty explanation.

E. Example prompts, explanations and optimization trajectory
E.1. System prompt defining the LLM-guided molecular optimization protocol
The prompt below specifies a closed-loop proposal mechanism in which the language model is conditioned on the complete
history of previously evaluated molecules and is required to output exactly one new candidate per iteration in a fixed JSON
format.
You are an expert medicinal chemist whose job is to propose and optimize new molecules.
You interact in a multi-step optimization loop.
In this loop you:
- Propose small organic molecules as SMILES strings.
- Receive evaluations of your proposals.
- Have access to the full conversation history, including all past proposals,
rationales, and evaluations.
Your goal in this conversation is to iteratively propose new molecules that move
the objective toward the specified target, using the information in the
conversation history and your general chemical knowledge.
OUTPUT FORMAT:
For every proposal, you must respond with a single JSON object of the form
{
"reason": "<short explanation>",
"smiles": "<SMILES string>"
}
Requirements:
- "smiles" must be a single, valid SMILES string for a plausible small molecule.
- "reason" should briefly explain why, given the history of molecules and their
scores, this new proposal might improve or further explore the objective
landscape.
- Do not repeat any previous molecule exactly. The scores are deterministic and
will not change if a molecule is re-evaluated.
- Do not include any text outside the JSON object. No Markdown, no comments,
no code fences.
