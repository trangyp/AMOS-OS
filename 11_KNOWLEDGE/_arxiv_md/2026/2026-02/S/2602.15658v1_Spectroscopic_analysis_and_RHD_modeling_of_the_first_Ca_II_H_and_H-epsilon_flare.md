Abstract We analyze decay phase observations of the GOES class C6.7 flare
SOL2022-08-19T20:31 by the Visible Spectropolarimeter (ViSP) on the National
Science Foundation’s Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope (DKIST). The data
include the first flare-time DKIST observations of the chromospheric Ca II H
396.8 nm and Hϵ 397.0 nm spectral lines. These diagnostics have rarely been
studied together during the modern era of high-resolution solar flare obser-
vations, and never at the spectral and spatial resolution of the DKIST. We
directly compare DKIST spectra to state-of-the-art RADYN+RH simulations,
including one heated by a nonthermal electron beam and one by in-situ thermal
conduction. While certain salient properties of the spectra such as the width of
Hϵ are reproduced, the models severely underestimate the width of Ca II H in
the red wing and fail to reproduce the exact relative intensity of Ca II H to Hϵ.
The models exhibit a range of condensation electron densities spanning over an
order of magnitude. Unlike the modeled lower-order Balmer-series lines, we find
that the width of Hϵ is not solely related to the condensation properties; the
widths and intensities are also sensitive to the deeper flare layers. We outline
possible avenues towards improvement of flare models, such as a comprehensive

1. Introduction
Spatial and spectral evolution of the photosphere, chromosphere, and low corona
during solar and stellar flares continues to evade comprehensive and self-consistent
modeling (e.g. Fletcher et al. 2011; Kerr 2022, 2023; Kowalski 2024). According
to the CSHKP flare model (Carmichael 1963; Sturrock 1966; Hirayama 1974;
Kopp & Pneuman 1976), flares result from the conversion of magnetic free energy
into other forms of energy due to magnetic reconnection. The interaction between
magnetic fields from different topological domains accelerates high-energy parti-
cles from the coronal reconnection site along magnetic loops and into the dense
chromosphere (e.g. Shibata & Magara 2011; Kontar et al. 2017; Kowalski 2024).
In the collisional thick-target model (CTTM, Brown 1971; Aschwanden 2002;
Holman et al. 2011; Kowalski 2024), these precipitating particles release most
of their energy upon impact in the chromosphere. This energy release occurs at
frequencies across the electromagnetic spectrum; for example, in the ultraviolet
and extreme ultraviolet (UV/EUV) 304 ˚A and 1600 ˚A emission that we observe
as bright flare “ribbons” (see e.g. review by Kazachenko et al. 2022).
Past studies have analyzed spectroscopic observations of chromospheric dy-
namics to deduce the properties of the flaring solar atmosphere. Graham &
Cauzzi (2015) studied the evolution of emission lines indicating the motion
of coronal and chromospheric layers during an X-class flare captured by the
Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph (IRIS, De Pontieu et al. 2014), finding
coronal upflows (blueshifts) of up to 300 km s−1 and chromospheric downflows
(redshifts) of 40 km s−1 in flare-time spectral lines from the observed ribbons.
For the same flare, Graham et al. (2020) applied the interpretation by Kowalski
et al. (2017a) to show that the Fe I, Fe II, Mg II, C I, and Si II emission lines
can be decomposed into separate Gaussian components revealing the downward
motion (“condensation”) of the upper-chromosphere with velocities of up to
50 km s−1 and prominent emission from deeper stationary layers excited by
high-energy electrons. Xu et al. (2023) analyzed IRIS observations of an X-
class flare with multi-Gaussian fitting of chromospheric and transition region
lines and found redshifted components with very large velocities, up to 160
km s−1. Many additional studies report redshifted, blueshifted, and otherwise
asymmetric emission line profiles during flares (e.g. Li et al. 2015; Tian et al.
2015; Tian & Chen 2018; Libbrecht et al. 2019; French et al. 2025).
To reproduce the emission line profiles observed in solar flares and under-
stand the underlying physics, many studies (e.g. Kuridze et al. 2015; Graham
et al. 2020; Kerr et al. 2024b) compare observations to simulated flare spectra
produced using the 1D radiative-hydrodynamic (RHD) code RADYN (Carlsson
& Stein 1992, 1995, 1997; Abbett & Hawley 1999; Carlsson & Stein 2002; Allred
et al. 2015) and the 1D statistical equilibrium code RH (Uitenbroek 2001).

These models characterize the atmospheric response to a sudden influx of energy
from an electron beam injected along a single coronal loop. With the resulting
model spectra, it is possible to constrain atmospheric flare properties such as
temperature, electron density, bulk velocity, and microturbulence (e.g. Rutten
2003; Carlsson et al. 2016; Kowalski 2024).
Even when employing updated prescriptions for pressure broadening from
ions and electrons (the quadratic Stark, or Stark-Lo Surdo, effect1), there remain
discrepancies between the simulated and observed broadening of chromospheric
lines. Zhu et al. (2019) find that when employing an updated prescription for
pressure broadening in RH, an additional multiplicative factor of 30 on the Stark-
Lo Surdo width in the simulation source code is needed in order to accurately
reproduce the Lorentzian wings of the Mg II lines (see also Kerr 2022, 2023).
Additionally, certain peculiarities of solar flare spectra call into question the
source of heating during a flare. Besides the particle beams discussed above, both
thermal conduction from the corona (e.g. Rust & Somov 1984; Polito et al. 2018;
Ashfield & Longcope 2021; L¨orinˇc´ık et al. 2025) and heating via Alfv´en waves
(e.g. Fletcher & Hudson 2008; Kerr et al. 2016; Reep & Russell 2016) produce
distinctly different simulated flare spectra. When, and in what proportions, these
mechanisms are relevant in a realistic flare scenario is not a new question (e.g.
Neidig 1989) but remains a major object of research.
Features observed more often during stellar flares (due in part to the typically
higher energies associated with these flares compared to the solar case, and
differences in the observed spectral bands) have also raised questions that the
finer spatial details in solar data may help to answer (for a review of this topic,
see Kowalski 2024). Spectra of red dwarf flares show that the hydrogen Balmer
lines peak much earlier and become much broader than Ca II lines (e.g. Hawley
& Pettersen 1991; Kowalski et al. 2013), despite the similar chromospheric for-
mation heights of these lines. One example is discussed in Kowalski et al. (2024)
using data from a large flare on the dM4.5e star YZ CMi (Vida et al. 2019)
wherein the Ca II H 396.8 nm line is much narrower than Hϵ 397.0 nm (see also
Paulson et al. 2006). Some solar spectra indicate that similar dynamics occur on
the Sun when comparing the hydrogen Paschen series to Ca II IR triplet lines
(Neidig & Wiborg 1984). In the context of recent work (e.g. Namekata et al.
2020; Notsu et al. 2025), flare-time line broadening in solar flares can be used to
contextualize similar spectral properties during stellar flares, which are spatially
unresolved.
One possible explanation for the difference in line width between Ca II H
and Hϵ is that separate heating mechanisms in distinct regions of a flare kernel -
namely, a bright impulsive “core” and a diffuse gradual “halo” (Neidig et al. 1993;
Kawate et al. 2016; Namekata et al. 2022a) - may be responsible for emission
in different lines. The Balmer series emission lines, which are highly sensitive
to electric pressure broadening from the linear Stark-Lo Surdo effect (distinct

Although flares are prevalent on both our Sun and other stars, only for the
Sun do we have access to direct, high-spatial-resolution observations. With these,
we can capture the evolution of flare spectral lines while also tracking the spatial
evolution of the plasma in the photosphere, chromosphere, and corona. Unfor-
tunately, solar data have often lacked the necessary spectral coverage to provide
an unambiguous explanation for differences in the width of the broad hydrogen
Balmer series and narrow Ca II lines. While there have been solar observations
of, for example, the Ca II H and Hϵ lines in the past (e.g. ˇSvestka 1976), these
lines have not been observed at high spectral resolution in the modern era of
high-cadence, high-resolution observations.
Several instruments on the newly commissioned Daniel K. Inouye Solar Tele-
scope (DKIST, Rimmele et al. 2020) are now able to provide spectroscopic
observations of flaring regions in multiple spectral windows. In particular, the
Visible Imaging Spectropolarimeter (ViSP, de Wijn et al. 2022) is poised to
provide novel insight on the physical mechanisms producing chromospheric flare
emission lines by producing spatially and spectrally resolved data. For a more
detailed discussion of questions regarding line broadening that DKIST data may
help to answer, see Kowalski et al. (2022).
In this work, we present DKIST observations of a GOES X-ray class C6.7
flare at 20:42 UT on 2022 August 19, including the Ca II H 396.8 nm and Hϵ
397.0 nm line. While observing conditions were not optimal, these are the first
flare observations of these lines with DKIST, representing a unique opportunity
to assess the diagnostic power of these lines in combination with modern flare
simulations. We perform an initial comparison of DKIST spectra in this spectral
range to simulated spectra from the RADYN and RH codes.
In Section 2 we describe flare observations collected with DKIST. In Section 3
we detail the evolution of Ca II H and Hϵ during the flare and describe differences
in Ca II H emission line profiles across the ribbon. In Section 4 we compare data-
inspired RHD models of these lines to the observed emission lines. In Section 5
we discuss our findings and identify potential paths towards improved modeling
of these chromospheric lines. We summarize our work in Section 6.

2.1. Instrument configuration
ViSP spectral coverage included (i) the Fe I 630.2 nm line on Arm 1, with a
spectral range of ∼1 nm and (ii) the Ca II H 396.85 nm and Hϵ 397.01 nm
lines together on Arm 2, with a spectral range of ∼0.76 nm. Although we are
interested only in the intensity profiles of the lines observed, the option for
collecting only Stokes I observations was unavailable during DKIST Observing
Cycle 1. Therefore, the instrument was observing in polarimetric mode at a frame
rate of 33 Hz with 10 modulation states and 2 modulation cycles. The slit width
was 0.1071”, and ViSP performed a small four-step raster scan with a step size
of 1.99”. The slit lengths of Arm 1 (Fe I 630.2 nm) and Arm 2 (Ca II H 396.8 nm)
are 75”.8 with spatial sampling 0”.0298 pix−1 and 62”.3 with spatial sampling
0”.0245 pix−1 respectively. The time between steps was 1.8 s for a scan time of
∼7.2 s, though instrument overhead lengthened the interval between successive
map repeats (the map cadence) to 26 s. The scan was repeated ten times. After
wavelength calibration (Appendix A), we find the spectral sampling of the Fe I
and Ca II H arms to be 12.7 m˚A and 7.7 m˚A respectively.
We determine the achieved spatial resolution of the data along the slit by
examining the spatial frequency at which there is no significant power from solar
structures; higher frequencies are dominated by instrumental or white noise. We
find that this occurs for spatial scales smaller than ∼1′′ ≈720 km. This was due
to poor seeing conditions (the median Fried parameter was r0 = 3.7 ± 0.9 cm
during the flare observing period).

3. Spectroscopic Analysis of ViSP Data
3.1. Summary of observed Ca II H and Hϵ emission lines
As described in Section 2.1, the ViSP was observing in polarimetric mode. In this
work we investigate only the evolution of Stokes I profiles. Figure 5 shows the
emission line profiles of Ca II H and Hϵ at the flare ribbon center for the entirety
of DKIST observations from 20:42:07 UT to 20:46:33 UT. Both Ca II H and Hϵ
were in emission at the start of observations, but while Ca II H was in emission
throughout the observations, Hϵ returns to the non-flare profile at 20:44:43 UT
and is not appreciably in emission thereafter. At the beginning of the observing
period, Ca II H is single-peaked with peak maximum intensity slightly in the
blue and a broader red than blue wing at the brightest ribbon location. Ca II H
becomes less intense throughout the observations, but the decrease in intensity
is not constant, likely due to variations in observing conditions. At the location
of maximum intensity in the ribbon, Ca II H develops a double-peaked profile
with a blue peak maximum at 20:45 UT and remains double-peaked thereafter.
While in emission, Hϵ is symmetric and single-peaked. By the end of the ViSP
observations at 20:46 UT, Hϵ has effectively returned to pre-flare levels. However,
emission in Ca II H was still observed by ViSP at 20:55 UT (not shown), though
this dataset lacks context images from VBI-red.

4. Modeling
The lines of the Ca II ion are often studied in the context of solar flare physics
and are known to exhibit optically-thick properties similar to Mg II in the quiet

4.1. Beam-heated simulations
4.1.1. Estimating beam parameters with the F-CHROMA grid
The observed hard X-ray spectrum of a flare is normally used to deduce the
spectral properties of the electron beam (namely, the flux F, low-energy cut-
off Ec, and spectral index δ) that heats the flare atmosphere in a RADYN
simulation. Unfortunately, the STIX instrument (Krucker et al. 2020b) did not
collect observations of the flare ribbons because Solar Orbiter was located almost
completely on the opposite side of the Sun2. Fermi/GBM spectra also can not in
this case be used to constrain beam properties. This is because there is spurious
background emission, not related to the flare, at energies above 25 keV. This
feature is present in all sunward-facing detectors and obscures the flare-only
HXR spectrum. Since no single detector is free from this effect, analysis of GBM
spectra to diagnose beam properties is unreliable.
Therefore, we interrogate the F-CHROMA grid (Carlsson et al. 2023) of
electron-beam-heated RADYN models to trial a range of values for δ, Ec, and the

The F-CHROMA grid of models is widely used to easily compare flare obser-

in atomic levels and in the initial atmosphere. Carlsson et al. (2023) describes
the lines included in F-CHROMA, including those of a six-level hydrogen atom,
the Ca II ion, and the He I and He II ions. The grid varies the total energy
(Etot = 3 × 1010, 1 × 1011, 3 × 1011, 1 × 1012 erg cm−2), low-energy cutoff

starting atmosphere, see Carlsson et al. (2023). In all F-CHROMA models, we
are limited to a half-loop length of 10 Mm. From the AIA imagery (Figure 2(c)),
the activated flare loops at the time of the DKIST observations may be larger
(up to ∼20 Mm). We discuss this in Section 5.
The beam energy flux is injected in a triangular temporal pattern, with a
linear 10 s increase to maximum flux at t = 10 s and a linear 10 s decrease to
zero beam flux at t = 20 s followed by a 30 s relaxation period. We note that
although we often reference the electron beam flux (similarly to e.g. Kowalski
2024, though we refer specifically to the peak beam flux Fmax), we will also
refer to the total beam energy Etot, which is varied in the F-CHROMA grid. For
the triangular injection of energy in each F-CHROMA model (though not the
custom models described below with constant energy injection), the relationship
between these two is Etot = Fmax × 10 s. The relaxation phase is included in
the F-CHROMA models to facilitate investigation of the persistent emission in
chromospheric spectral lines after beam injection has ceased, a possible scenario
for our flare-decay-phase DKIST observations.

after the flare impulsive phase. As a test, we also compare the DKIST data

4.3. The RADYN+RH bridge
Notably, the RADYN models are produced using a 6-level hydrogen atom, which
is insufficient for synthesis of the Hϵ nj = 7 to ni = 2 transition. Incorporation of
a 7-level hydrogen atom into RADYN is not preferred due to RADYN’s handling
of overlapping transitions such as Ca II H and Hϵ. Therefore, we feed snapshots
of the RADYN atmosphere details, non-equilibrium electron densities, and hy-
drogen population densities from F-CHROMA to the statistical equilibrium 1D
RH code, which can include a 20-level hydrogen atom and handles overlapping
transitions self-consistently (Uitenbroek 2001, 2002). Following the findings of
e.g. Zhu et al. (2019),Yadav et al. (2021), Sainz Dalda & De Pontieu (2023),
and Kerr et al. (2024b), for some models we adjust the ad hoc microturbulence
parameter vturb in the input RH atmosphere file to 7 km s−1 from the default
2 km s−1. We comment on this choice of vturb further in Section 5. Hereafter we
refer to the resulting “bridge” simulation as RADYN+RH.
The hydrogen broadening prescription calculated using the modifications of
Tremblay & Bergeron (2009) and occupational probability formalism of Hummer
& Mihalas (1988) (TB09+HM88) was incorporated into the RH code by Kowal-
ski et al. (2017b). RH includes partial frequency redistribution (PRD) effects.
Although the RH code is in statistical equilibrium (SE), this assumption does
not significantly affect the line profile of higher-order hydrogen line transitions
when non-equilibrium (NEQ) electron densities from RADYN snapshots are used
as input to RH. A direct comparison of RADYN and RH calculations of Hϵ is
not possible. However, Figure 9 of Kowalski et al. (2022) shows a comparison
of the RADYN and RH calculations of Hγ, which suggests that the statistical
equilibrium assumption is likely sufficient for higher-order Balmer series lines.
In Appendix C we demonstrate that the statistical equilibrium assumption also
does not substantially affect the line width of Ca II H during the simulation
relaxation phase.

5. Discussion
5.1. Interpretation of line asymmetry measurements
At the ribbon center, the DKIST spectra show asymmetric, single-peaked Ca II H
emission line profiles with a wider red than blue wing (Figure 6). For opti-
cally thin chromospheric lines like Si IV, asymmetries are often explained using
a chromospheric evaporation/condensation model, where red- and blue-shifted
lines or line components represent down- and up-flowing chromospheric material
respectively (e.g. Ashfield et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023). This method has also been
applied to optically thick lines such as the Mg II triplet when these lines exhibit
clear Gaussian components (e.g. Graham & Cauzzi 2015; Graham et al. 2020).
We characterize the line profile shape of Ca II H using a similar method in
Section 3.2 (Figure 6(c4-c5)) and find that the single-peaked Ca II H line is
well-represented by a double-Gaussian model with a blue-shifted component at
∼−16 to −19 km s−1 and a red-shifted component at ∼6 to 11 km s−1. The
velocity shift of these components varies across the flare ribbon, with a slightly
larger redshift at the ribbon trailing edge during the flare gradual phase (see the
red profile in Figure 6(c1)), possibly indicative of coronal rain (e.g. Lacatus et al.
2017; Reep et al. 2020), though we do not test this possibility here. We find that
at the ribbon center, the blue component of Ca II H slightly increases in velocity
and the red component decreases in velocity over the duration of observations.
We caution that evolution of the components of the line profile should not
necessarily be interpreted as evidence of mass motion. First, the data are limited
to seven scans covering only ∼2.5 minutes of the gradually-evolving decay phase
(Figure 1) and are subject to variable seeing. Also, the physical interpretation of
a double-Gaussian model is less certain for observations of optically thick lines
like Ca II H without spectrally distinct Gaussian components. Therefore, despite
the accuracy of the simple double-Gaussian model in decomposing the observed
emission line, in this paper we hesitate to claim an evaporation-condensation
model, indicated by double-Gaussian fitting, as an explanation for the line profile
width.
5.2. Remaining model-data discrepancies in the Ca II H emission line
The width of Ca II H in the red wing is significantly underestimated by the RHD
models investigated here (Figure 8(a)). Zhu et al. (2019) found a qualitatively
similar (though symmetric) discrepancy in their analysis of the Mg II h, k, and
subordinate lines. They found that a multiplicative factor of 30 on the quadratic
Stark-Lo Surdo line width was required to resolve this difference, suggesting that
the RADYN and RH codes neglect some relevant Lorentzian effect on the line
width.
As also demonstrated here, non-Lorentzian sources of broadening are also
important when accurately resolving the line width. For example, the ad hoc
microturbulence parameter has a Gaussian effect on the line width. Kerr et al.
(2024a) found that microturbulent velocities of 7.5 −10 km s−1 are appropriate
in a flare atmosphere. Zhu et al. (2019) investigated the effect of increasing
SOLA: DKIST_paper.tex; 18 February 2026; 1:54; p. 21


## Page 22


microturbulence manually at different heights (up to 50 km s−1 at lower heights)
and found that large values of microturbulence produced an unrealistically en-
hanced line core in Mg II lines and did not resolve the discrepancy between
simulations and observations in the far wings of the line. Modeled profiles with
a uniform microturbulence of 5 km s−1 in their work seem to better reproduce
the Mg II line core, although they do not discuss this extensively. Using the
spectral inversion code STiC (de la Cruz Rodr´ıguez et al. 2019) to quantify
microturbulence among other atmospheric parameters, Sainz Dalda & De Pon-
tieu (2023) found an atmospheric microturbulence of ξ = 5 −15 km s−1. Yadav
et al. (2021) inverted ground-based Ca II 8542 ˚A and Ca II K flare observations,
finding values of microturbulence ranging from a few km s−1 to 5 −10 km s−1
in the upper chromosphere. Our choice of vturb = 7 km s−1 generally explains
the discrepancy in predicted blue wing width for Ca II H, but fails to resolve
the red wing discrepancy. This remaining issue may be explained by spatial
resolution effects, by constraints placed on Lorentzian effects such as in Zhu
et al. (2019), or by some other physical effect not included in the model. We
note also that given the clear spectral variability across the flare ribbon in data
with suboptimal observing, it is likely that resolution effects may also contribute
to the unexplained elevated Ca II H red wing.
Finally, we note that the relative intensity of Ca II H to Hϵ is underestimated
by the electron beam-heated model EB1, and overestimated by the conduction-
driven model TC1 (Figure 8(a)). This could be due to uncertainty in the beam
parameters or input conductive flux. Alternatively, the discrepancy in relative
line intensity may suggest that a combination of both heating mechanisms is
more appropriate for comparison to decay-phase flare spectra. A more compre-
hensive modeling investigation considering these points is necessary to address
this issue, but outside the scope of this work. Such a study may include a com-
parison of DKIST flare data to RADYN simulations heated by other mechanisms
(e.g. proton beams, Alfv´enic waves) and constraints placed by direct observations
of the HXR spectra, which are unreliable for this flare.
5.3. Chromospheric ne and the formation height of Hϵ
The presence of Hϵ in the same spectral window as Ca II H is a novel feature in
modern high-resolution solar flare spectra, although the lines were observed often
during flares in the mid-to-late-20th century (e.g. ˇSvestka 1976). The proximity
of Ca II H to Hϵ presents the opportunity to produce a self-consistent RHD
simulation that simultaneously captures the dynamics of Ca II H and Hϵ lines,
subject to distinct constraints in atmospheric and beam parameters. In high-
energy flares observed on both the Sun and other stars, Hϵ is often very broad,
with a blue wing blending heavily with the entire Ca II H line. The relatively
narrow decay-phase Hϵ line profile in the flare reported here is therefore notable,
as is the consistency of the models employed in reproducing the narrow Hϵ line
width (Figure 8(b)). We determine that the condensations in the late relaxation
phase contributing to broadened emission in Hϵ have electron densities in agree-
ment with previous work (e.g. Neidig & Wiborg 1984; Johns-Krull et al. 1997,
and many others). These densities are a factor of ∼10 lower than the electron
SOLA: DKIST_paper.tex; 18 February 2026; 1:54; p. 22


## Page 23


densities that lead to broadening in the low-nj Balmer lines in Kowalski et al.
(2022), though the predictions in that work are intended for impulsive phase
kernels, when larger beam flux densities are inferred.
Since the reproduced line widths in these models are all fairly consistent de-
spite a wide range of condensation densities, factors other than the condensation
density seem to be relevant to the broadening of Hϵ. For example, as shown in
Figure 8(b), model EB4 at t = 21 s (one second after the cessation of beam
energy injection) overestimates the width of Hϵ compared to the other models
shown despite a ∼30% lower condensation electron density at t = 21 s compared
to t = 43 s. At t = 21 s in model EB4, there is a significant contribution to the
line width (particularly in the wings) from a layer lower in the chromosphere
with ne ≈9 × 1012 cm−3. Further, model EB1 at 500 s and EB2 at 43 s produce
Hϵ lines with approximately the same width despite very different condensation
electron densities of ne ≈9 × 1012 cm−3 and ne ≈2 × 1012 cm−3 respectively -
the latter is more than a factor of 10 lower than the ne in model EB4 at 21 s.
Model TC1 at t = 90 s has a condensation electron density ne = 8 × 1013 cm−3,
much higher than the other models, but produces a Hϵ line width very similar
to that of model EB1.
We therefore suggest that in addition to condensation electron density, con-
tributions from lower layers and the height of the condensation may also play a
significant role in the formation of Hϵ. Predictions of Balmer series line broad-
ening from Kowalski et al. (2022) indicate that, while low-nj Balmer lines form
mostly in chromospheric condensations with ne >∼1014 cm−3 during a flare owing
to their large optical depths, the less-optically-thick high-nj lines (specifically,
nj = 12−16) may form largely in lower, stationary layers with lower ne and small
curve-of-growth broadening enhancements. Although it has a lower nj than the
lines investigated in that work, Hϵ (nj = 7) may also form partially in lower
atmospheric layers. Further investigation into the factors involved in producing
an Hϵ line during the relaxation phase of a flare simulation is required in order
to explain why condensation electron density alone does not seem to predict the
width of Hϵ.
5.4. Additional avenues for further investigation
Future work that may resolve the discrepancy between model and data includes
investigation of a larger grid of RHD models. As discussed at length in e.g.
Section 3 of Kerr (2023), Alfv´enic waves (e.g. Kerr et al. 2016; Reep & Russell
2016), conductive heat fluxes (e.g. Polito et al. 2018; Allred et al. 2022), and
even non-thermal electron beams (e.g. Kowalski et al. 2017a) or proton beams
(e.g. Proch´azka et al. 2018; Sadykov et al. 2023; Kerr et al. 2023, 2026) all
produce simulated spectral lines consistent with certain aspects of observed
flare spectra. A combination of these heating effects could be responsible for
the remaining observed discrepancies in synthesizing Ca II H, particularly in
the gradual decay phase. The presence of Hϵ, which broadens due to different
mechanisms than Ca II H, in this spectral window may help provide simultaneous
additional constraints when testing the effects of these heating sources.
Additionally, the F-CHROMA models in particular are limited in their as-
sumptions regarding the starting atmospheric structure. For example, although
SOLA: DKIST_paper.tex; 18 February 2026; 1:54; p. 23


## Page 24


the 10 Mm loop length used in the F-CHROMA grid (and 11 Mm loop length
in models EB1 and TC1) appears close to the loop length estimated from AIA
images, this parameter should be varied in future work to explore the effects on
the beam heating and line evolution and potentially resolve remaining model-
data discrepancies. Thermal conduction may be more important in longer loops
due to the increased beam-stopping power of the coronal densities (e.g. Polito
et al. 2018; Kerr 2022).
In the future we will also perform a similar comparison of RHD simulations
to impulsive phase flare observations to further address remaining questions in
chromospheric line broadening observed in both solar and stellar flare studies
(e.g. Kowalski et al. 2022; Notsu et al. 2024, 2025, among many others). Model
Ca II H and Hϵ lines during or immediately following the phase of beam energy
injection are much more intense than observed in the decay phase here, and are
typically double-peaked or redshifted, much different from the persistent single-
peaked, slightly asymmetric observed line (see Figure 8(a) for an example of the
double-peaked profile). Impulsive phase observations would clearly determine
whether the significant asymmetries predicted by the model during the energy
injection phase are accurate. Additionally, the availability of co-temporal hard
X-ray observations from SolO/STIX, Fermi/GBM, or the Hard X-ray Imager on
the Advanced Space-based Solar Observatory are crucial to accurate assignment
of the injected electron beam parameters.
We note that for optically thick lines, the pre-flare atmosphere has significant
bearing on the flare-time spectral behavior. Here we have avoided the influence of
the pre-flare atmosphere on flare spectra by subtracting the non-flare spectrum
from both observations and models. While this successfully isolates the flare
emission in the two lines studied, there are caveats associated with this approach.
For example, the elevated Fe I emission in the red wing of Hϵ (e.g. Figure 6(b1))
is due to this subtraction. Ideally, RADYN models should be run using a pre-
flare atmosphere specifically generated for the flare studied, but the generation
of a new starting atmosphere is nontrivial and outside the scope of this work.
We have yet to investigate the possibility of spatial differences in the formation
of these lines relative to the flare ribbon - for example, the core-halo scenarios
discussed in Section 1. Additionally, the present work mainly focuses on only
one scan of the ViSP. This is primarily because of the relatively low ViSP map
cadence during these observations and also because spectra evolve less rapidly
in the decay phase than in the impulsive phase. Nevertheless, a comprehensive
model should capture the time evolution of spectra during all flare phases. In a
future study, we will (i) investigate comprehensive impulsive phase and gradual
decay phase observations of Ca II H and Hϵ from DKIST to explicate precise
differences in line evolution of an elementary flare kernel in both time and space;5
(ii) collect observations of other Balmer series lines such as Hα, Hβ, or Hγ
simultaneously with Ca II H/Hϵ as suggested by Kowalski et al. (2022), and (iii)
perform a similar comparison to RHD simulations, but with a larger parameter

6. Conclusions
In this study, we present the first flare-time observations of Ca II H and Hϵ
emission line spectra with the DKIST/ViSP instrument, during the decay phase
of a GOES X-ray class C6.7 flare at 20:42 UT on 2022 August 19. We analyze
the evolution of the unique spectral window that includes both lines. With the
advent of DKIST observations, these lines will likely be studied frequently in the
context of flare physics in the coming years.
i) We describe the evolution of the Ca II H and Hϵ emission lines during ViSP ob-
servations. We characterize the asymmetry and width of the observed Ca II H
line. At the flare ribbon center, Ca II H is single-peaked with a broad red wing,
while Hϵ is less intense and more symmetric.
ii) We demonstrate that a 1D RADYN+RH bridge simulation can produce emis-
sion lines with key characteristics of the observed spectra in the late relaxation
phase (t = 500s), such as the width of Hϵ and a generally single-peaked Ca
II H. In the absence of reliable hard X-ray spectra for this flare, we infer
reasonable beam parameters by comparing spectra from the F-CHROMA
database of RADYN models to the ViSP spectra. The model (EB1) shows a
slight central reversal, as do many of the off-kernel-center Ca II H spectral
profiles in Figures 5 and 6. The conduction-driven model TC1 displays similar
features, although Ca II H is much more intense in that model than in EB1.
In model TC1, Ca II H does not exhibit a central reversal.
iii) All RHD models studied underestimate the width in the red wing of Ca
II H significantly, suggesting that improvements to the models and better
observing conditions are required to accurately model this line. As addressed
by e.g. Zhu et al. (2019), this is a persistent problem in modeling of non-
hydrogenic chromospheric spectral lines in flare codes. Adjustment of the
microturbulence parameter vturb accounts for most of the difference in the
blue wing of Ca II H.
iv) The line width of Hϵ is slightly underestimated by the models in the late
relaxation phase, and it is overestimated in the early relaxation phase. The
simulations produce a wide range of condensation electron densities from
ne ≈2 × 1012 cm−3 to 8 × 1013 cm−3 despite relatively similar Hϵ widths
across models. Other atmospheric characteristics such as the depth of the
chromospheric condensation and contributions to line formation from lower
SOLA: DKIST_paper.tex; 18 February 2026; 1:54; p. 25


## Page 26


layers seem to have a strong effect on Hϵ line width. The flare-time formation
of Hϵ may differ from that of lower-nj lines like Hα. Although it was assumed
that Hϵ would more directly probe the condensation electron density, the nar-
row profiles observed here (compared to e.g. the broad Hϵ reported by Neidig
& Wiborg (1984) and ˇSvestka (1976)) suggest otherwise. Peak condensation
electron density is not a direct predictor of Hϵ line width. Further observations
of Hϵ and also lower-order Balmer series lines are required to investigate this
possibility.
The prospect of new observations with DKIST and other instruments, along-
side updated forward modeling using improved constraints and state-of-the-art
spectral inversion codes, represents a massive opportunity and body of future
work for the flare community. The employment of these techniques in studies of
flare data has led to significant advances in our understanding of flare physics.
Our study provides one example of the new science to come and the remaining
questions we have left to answer as the community begins to process data from
DKIST with new and improved analytical tools. Investigation of impulsive phase
flare observations in comparison to RHD simulations during the energy injection
phase, coupled with the investigation of heating sources other than an electron
beam, is left to a future study and may help to resolve the remaining differences
between models and observations.

for each time step, and the process must be repeated for each image frame or
ViSP scan.
