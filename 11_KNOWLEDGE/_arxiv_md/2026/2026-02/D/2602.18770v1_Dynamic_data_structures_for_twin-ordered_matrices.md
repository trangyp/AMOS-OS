Introduction
The notion of twin-width, introduced by Bonnet, Kim, Thomassé, and Watrig-
ant [9], has rapidly emerged as a unifying structural parameter for graphs (both
sparse and dense), matrices, and permutations. The basic idea behind twin-
width, expressed through the underlying decomposition notion called a contrac-
tion sequence, is to “fold” the given structure (be it a graph, a matrix, or a per-
mutation) by iteratively merging elements into larger and larger parts. At each
point, we require that every part has a homogeneous relation to all the other
parts, except for a bounded number of exceptions. The best possible bound on
the number of exceptions is then precisely the twin-width. This concept has
a surprisingly wide range of applicability: for example, H-minor-free graphs (for
a fixed H), graphs of bounded cliquewidth, and pattern-free permutations all
have bounded twin-width [9].
Despite being a relatively new concept, the fundamental character of twin-
width has already been well-understood. Twin-width uncovers deep links be-
tween structural graph theory and algorithmic finite model theory, see e.g.
[1,2,6,9,10,13,14,15,18]. In this direction, probably the most important result is
the theorem of Bonnet et al. [9] stating that the model-checking problem for
first-order logic is fixed-parameter tractable on graph classes of bounded twin-
width, provided a suitable contraction sequence is given as input. Combinatorial
aspects of twin-width were explored in [3,4,6,7,11,16], among many other works.
Several further algorithmic applications can be found in [5,8,17].
In this work, we study binary matrices of bounded twin-width – let us now
define the notion precisely. We consider a binary matrix M, i.e., one with entries
in {0, 1}. A contraction sequence for M is a sequence of pairs
(R0, C0), (R1, C1), . . . , (Rp, Cp),
where for every i = 0, 1, . . . , p we have Ri and Ci are partitions of rows and
columns of M into row blocks and column blocks respectively. Moreover,
– in (R0, C0) each row and each column belongs to a separate block;
– in (Rp, Cp) all rows belong to a single block and all columns belong to a
single block;
– (Ri, Ci) is obtained from (Ri−1, Ci−1) by either merging two consecutive row
blocks into a single row block or merging two consecutive column blocks into
a single column block.
For an example of a contraction sequence, see Figure 1. For a row block R ∈R
and a column block C ∈C, the zone M[R, C] is the submatrix consisting of all
the cells at the intersection of the rows of R and the columns of C. We say that
a zone is non-constant if it contains both an entry 0 and an entry 1. The width of
the contraction sequence is the least d such that at every point in the sequence,
in every row block and in every column block there are at most d non-constant
zones. We say that M is d-twin-ordered if it admits a contraction sequence of
width at most d, and M has twin-width at most d if one can permute rows and

The connection between graphs and matrices of bounded twin-width lies at
the heart of the theory of twin-width and was observed already in the original
work of Bonnet et al. [9]. Later, Bonnet, Giocanti, Ossona de Mendez, Simon,
Thomassé, and Toruńczyk studied the twin-width of matrices from the combina-
torial and the model-theoretic viewpoints [6], whereas Bonnet, Giocanti, Ossona
de Mendez, and Thomassé investigated algorithmic applications of twin-width
for problems such as matrix multiplication [5]. The matrix viewpoint was also
pivotal in the work on χ-boundedness of graphs of bounded twin-width [11,16].
The starting point of our work is the result of Pilipczuk, Sokołowski, and
Zych-Pawlewicz [17], who approached the setting of matrices of bounded twin-
width from the point of view of data structures, or compact representations.
Precisely, they designed a data structure that stores a d-twin-ordered binary
n × n matrix M using Od(n)4 bits (which is asymptotically optimal) while sup-
porting single-entry queries in time Od(log log n). Their data structure, however,
is fundamentally static: once it is initialized on a matrix M, it cannot be easily

modified upon updates to M. It is natural to ask whether a similarly compact
and efficient data structure can be also proposed in the dynamic setting, where
M can be updated subject to a guarantee that it always stays d-twin-ordered.
Our results. In this work, we address the above question and present a dynamic
data structure for d-twin-ordered binary matrices that supports both queries and
updates efficiently, while guaranteeing near-linear space complexity. Formally, we
show the following theorem.
Theorem 1. Let d ∈N and M ∈{0, 1}n×n be a binary matrix that is updated
over time subject to a guarantee that M is d-twin-ordered at every moment.
Then, there exists a dynamic data structure for maintaining M that uses Od(n)
memory and supports the following operations:
– Init(n, K): initialize the data structure with a list K of disjoint rectangles
representing all ones in M in Od((n + |K|) log log n) time.
– QueryBit(i, j): return the entry M[i, j] in O(log log n) expected worst case
time.
– Update(i, j): flip the entry M[i, j] in O(log log n) expected worst case time.
Throughout the paper we work in the standard word RAM model with words
of length O(log n), hence space complexity Od(n) means storing the matrix using
Od(n log n) bits. In the static setting, Pilipczuk et al. [17] managed to obtain
a representation that uses only Od(n) bits, hence our result trades a slight in-
crease in the space complexity for making the data structure dynamic. Note
that only the space complexity of the data structure and its initialization time
depend on the parameter d, whereas the bounds on the time complexity of all
the other operations do not involve d. Following [17] we allow our data structure
to be initialized on a compact description of the matrix through a list of disjoint
rectangles comprising all the entries 1 (which we formally define in Section 2
and call slabs); so in particular, one may efficiently initialize a dense matrix. It
is known (see [17, Lemma 10] for details) that such a description with Od(n)
rectangles can be easily extracted from a contraction sequence of width d.

All logarithms in this paper are base 2. We assume the standard RAM model
of computation. For a, b ∈N with a ⩽b, we define the segment [a, b] := {a, a +

Dictionaries. In this work we make extensive use of van Emde Boas dictionaries,
called also van Emde Boas trees [19]. The van Emde Boas tree maintains a set
of keys S, which is a subset of the domain {1, . . . , U} for U ∈N, by supporting
the following operations:
– Insert(x): insert key x to S;
– Delete(x): remove key x from S;
– Lookup(x): verify whether x belongs to the maintained set of keys S;
– Successor(x): find the smallest key in S greater than x;
– Predecessor(x): find the largest key in S smaller than x.
All operations work in worst case time O(log log U) and the required space is
O(U) [19].
Recall that a segment [a, b], for a, b ∈N, a
⩽b, is defined as the set of
consecutive natural numbers between its coordinates a and b: [a, b] = {a, a +
1, . . . , b−1, b}. Two segments [a, b] and [a′, b′] are called adjacent if either b+1 =
a′ or a−1 = b′. In the next lemma we describe a modification of van Emde Boas
trees, which gives us the data structure to maintain a set of disjoint and non-
adjacent segments with coordinates in {1, . . . , n}, which we shall call an adhesive
segment set.
Lemma 3. Let n ∈N. There is an adhesive segment set data structure S that
maintains a dynamic set S of pairwise disjoint and pairwise non-adjacent seg-
ments whose coordinates belong to {1, . . . , n}. The data structure supports the
following methods:
– Containing([a, b]): Returns the segment of S that contains [a, b], if existent.
– Adjacent([a, b]): Returns the (at most two) segments in S adjacent to [a, b].
– Disjoint([a, b]): Returns whether [a, b] is disjoint from all the segments in
S.
– Merge([a, b]): If [a, b] is not disjoint from all the segments in S, the method
does nothing, and otherwise it performs the following modification. If [a, b]
is not adjacent to any segment in S, the method adds [a, b] to S. If [a, b] is
adjacent to one segment s ∈S, the method replaces s with s′ := s ∪[a, b]. If
[a, b] is adjacent to two segments s1, s2 ∈S, the method replaces s1 and s2
with s′ := [a, b] ∪s1 ∪s2.
– Split([a, b]): If [a, b] is contained in some segment [a′, b′] ∈S, the method
replaces the segment [a′, b′] in S with segments [a′, a −1] and [b + 1, b′]. If
any of these segments turns out to be empty, it is not added to S. If there is
no segment [a′, b′] ∈S containing [a, b], the method does nothing.
The data structure occupies O(n) memory and all operations run in O(log log n)
worst case time. The data structure can be initialized on an empty S in time
O(n).
Proof. We use the van Emde Boas dictionary D with universe [1, n]. We store
in D the coordinates of the segments of S. Additionally, for each coordinate we
store an extra bit determining if it is the first or the second coordinate. These

To implement Containing([a, b]) we issue a′ := Predecessor(a + 1) and
b′ := Successor(b−1) to D. We return [a′, b′], provided a′ is the first coordinate
of a segment, b′ is the second coordinate of a segment, and b′ = Successor(a′);
otherwise there is no segment in S containing [a, b]. Queries Adjacent([a, b]) and
Disjoint([a, b]) are implemented analogously.
To implement Merge([a, b]), we first check whether [a, b] is disjoint from all the
segments in S by invoking Disjoint([a, b]). If that is not the case, we do nothing.
Otherwise, we issue a′ := Predecessor(a) and b′ := Successor(b) queries to D.
Since [a, b] is disjoint from segments in S, a′ is the second and b′ is the first
coordinate of a segment in S. If a′ + 1 = a, we issue a′′ := Predecessor(a′)
query to D to find the first coordinate of the segment [a′′, a′] ∈S. Then, we
delete a′ from D and insert b, indicating that this is the second coordinate of
a segment. Then we proceed with b′ in an analogous fashion.
To implement Split([a, b]), we issue [a′, b′] = Containing([a, b]) to find the
interval [a′, b′] ∈S containing [a, b]. If there is no such interval, there is nothing
to do. Otherwise, we insert to D key a −1 indicating that it is the second
coordinate, and we insert key b + 1 indicating that it is the first coordinate.

Canonical slab decomposition
From Lemma 2 we know that for any d-twin-ordered n × n matrix there always
exists a slab decomposition into Od(n) slabs, which is a crucial property of our
dynamic data structure. However, we need to provide an efficient algorithm to
compute such decomposition. In this Section, we describe the canonical slab
decomposition, which is a slab decomposition with additional structural proper-
ties. This allows us to use the adhesive segment set data structure, described in
Lemma 3, to compute a desired decomposition in Theorem 3.
The slab decomposition we are going to compute is, in some sense, canonical.
To describe it, we need a few definitions. Recall that M is a binary n×n matrix.
For i ∈[1, n], an i-strip is a slab of the form (a, b, i, i) for some 1 ⩽a ⩽b ⩽n
such that neither (a −1, b, i, i) nor (a, b + 1, i, i) is a slab. In other words, an
i-strip is a slab of width 1 contained in the i-th column such that it is not
possible to extend this slab upwards or downwards, due to either finding an
entry 0 there or the border of the matrix M. By stripsi we denote the set of all
i-strips; and strips := S

if (0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1) are the consecutive entries of the ith column of a matrix,

Lemma 4. Let R be the canonical slab decomposition of a binary n × n matrix

the constant dependent on d defined in Lemma 1.
Proof. Let (a, b, c, d) be a canonical slab in M with 1 < a ⩽b < n and 1 < c ⩽
d < n. Our goal is to show that (a, b, c, d) must intersect a corner in M. This
will prove the claim, as there can be at most 4n canonical slabs satisfying a = 1
or b = n or c = 1 or d = n, whereas the number of corners in M is at most
fd(n + 2) by Lemma 1 and each corner in M can intersect at most 4 slabs of R.
Let Z = M[[a, b], [d + 1, d + 1]]. As (a, b, c, d) is a canonical slab, we have
(a, b, d + 1, d + 1) /∈stripsd+1; that is, Z is not a (d + 1)-strip. If Z contains
both an entry 0 and an entry 1, then the zone M[[a, b], [d, d + 1]] must contain
a corner that intersects (a, b, c, d). If all the entries of Z are 1 (i.e., Z is a slab),
then by (a, b, d, d) ∈stripsd and (a, b, d + 1, d + 1) /∈stripsd+1 we have that
one of the zones M[[a−1, a], [d, d+1]] or M[[b, b+1], [d, d+1]] must be a corner
that intersects (a, b, c, d). Finally, if all the entries of Z are 0, then both M[[a −
1, a], [d, d+1]] and M[[b, b+1], [d, d+1]] are corners that intersect (a, b, c, d).

Theorem 3. There is an algorithm Decompose(n, K), that given an n×n binary
matrix M provided by specifying its slab decomposition K, computes the canonical

that is, [a, b] ∈stripsi if and only if (a, b, i, i) is an i-strip.

Our first goal is to compute, for each i ∈[1, n], the sets Ai = stripsi \
stripsi−1 and Bi := stripsi \ stripsi+1, where strips0 = stripsn+1 = ∅
by convention. Note that by the definition each canonical slab must start with
some strip belonging to Ai and end with a strip belonging to Bj for some i ⩽j,

i ∈[1, n] from the given slab decomposition, and later we explain how to obtain
the canonical slab decomposition from them.
Let us start with computing for each i ∈[1, n] the sets of opening and closing
segments of K in column i.

Ci := {[a, b] | (a, b, c, i) ∈K for some c ⩽i}.

We describe now how by a sweep on the set of strips from left to right we
can compute sets Bi for i ∈[1, n]. We illustrate the procedure in Figure 4.
We initialize an adhesive segment set data structure S with the universe [1, n].
Next, we iterate through all of the column indices i ∈[1, n] maintaining the
following invariant: after the i-th iteration, the set of segments stored in S is
equal to stripsi+1. We initialize the iteration by invoking Merge(s) for each
s ∈O1 (which always adds the segment to the structure, as they are pairwise
disjoint, and possibly merges it with adjacent segments); so after this operation,
S stores exactly strips1 and therefore the invariant is satisfied for i = 0. In the
i-th iteration, we need to apply updates to S so that the stored set of segments
is modified from stripsi to stripsi+1, and at the same time compute the set
Bi = stripsi\stripsi+1. We start with constructing a list L of all the segments
of stripsi that may be possibly affected by the update. These are:
– all the intervals s ∈stripsi containing some s′ ∈Ci, and
– all the intervals s ∈stripsi adjacent to some s′ ∈Oi+1.
The list L can be constructed in time O((|Ci| + |Oi+1|) log log n) by invoking in
our data structure S procedure Containing(s′) for each s′ ∈Ci and Adjacent(s′)
for each s′ ∈Oi+1, and putting all the resulting segments in L. Note that we
may filter out any duplicates in L using an additional static boolean table with
entries [1, n] where we mark lower endpoints of segments already added to L;
and the next attempt of insertion of an already inserted segment is ignored. By
construction, we have |L| ⩽|Ci| + 2|Oi+1|. Having constructed L, we perform
the following update from stripsi to stripsi+1. First, we invoke Split(s) for
each s ∈Ci, deleting it from the structure. Next, we invoke Merge(s) for each
s ∈Oi+1, adding it to the structure.
Finally, we compute Bi = stripsi\stripsi+1 by iterating over all s ∈L and
verifying, using the method Containing(s), whether s still belongs to stripsi+1.
The set Bi comprises of all the segments s ∈L that fail this test.

An amortized data structure
In this section we present a weaker variant of the data structure promised in
Theorem 1, where we allow amortized running time of the updates. We will
de-amortize this data structure using standard methods in Section 5.
Theorem 4. There is a data structure A that maintains a d-twin-ordered n×n
binary matrix M by supporting the following operations:
1. Init(n, K): Initialize the data structure with a slab decomposition K of M
in O((n + |K|) log log n) expected time.
2. QueryBit(i, j): Return the entry M[i, j] in O(log log n) expected worst case
time.
3. Update(i, j): Flip the entry M[i, j] in O(log log n) expected amortized time.
The data structure A uses Od(n) space.
Proof. The data structure A internally maintains:
1. a static 2D orthogonal point location data structure provided by Theorem 2,
referred to as P, storing at most 4fd(n + 2) + 4n ∈Od(n) slabs with all
coordinates in the range {1, . . . , n}; and
2. a dictionary (implemented as a hash table) Q where the keys are from uni-
verse {1, . . . , n} × [1, n], the values are boolean, and the number of elements
that will be added is bounded by 8fd(n+2) ∈Od(n). We assume a standard
implementation of a hash table with space linear in the number of added
elements and constant expected query time.
The intuition behind maintaining P and Q is the following: P stores a slab
decomposition of M computed at some point in the past. When the next updates
arrive, instead of immediately recomputing the slab decomposition, we store the

updates in Q as long as the size of Q is bounded by 8fd(n + 2). Only when the
size of Q reaches this value, we recompute the up to date slab decomposition
of M and store it in P. This allows us to amortize for the recomputation cost.
Thus, we maintain the following invariant after each update: If (i, j) ∈Q, then
M[i, j] = Q[i, j]. Otherwise M[i, j] = 1 if and only if (i, j) belongs to one of the
slabs stored in P.
Initialization: The method Init(n, K) applies the following steps. First, it ini-
tializes an empty dictionary Q. Then, it applies the algorithm Decompose(n, K) of
Theorem 3 to obtain the canonical slab decomposition R of M. This runs in time
O(n + |K| log log n), and by Lemma 4 we have |R| ⩽4fd(n + 2) + 4n ∈Od(n).
We conclude by initializing P with R in Od(n log log n) time. Thus, the total
initialization time is O((n + |K|) log log n).
Query: The method QueryBit(i, j) looks up (i, j) in Q and upon a successful
search returns the value of (i, j). This takes O(1) worst case expected time. If
the search fails, a point location query (i, j) is issued to the point location data
structure P. This takes O(log log n) worst case time. If the point location query
is successful, we return 1, otherwise we return 0.
Update: The method Update(i, j) looks up (i, j) in Q. If it finds it, then it negates
its value. This takes constant time in expectation. Otherwise it issues a point
location query (i, j) to P (O(log log n) worst case time) and adds (i, j) to Q with
the negated result of the query (constant time in expectation). Finally, if the
size of Q reaches the bound of 8fd(n + 2), the method recomputes P and sets Q
to be empty for accommodating the next 8fd(n + 2) updates. We now describe
the process of recomputing P (see Figure 6 for an illustration).
In essence, recomputing P boils down to finding a slab decomposition K that
describes the matrix M after all updates stored in Q have been introduced. Once
we have such a decomposition K at hand, we use the method Decompose(n, K)
to compute the canonical decomposition R in time O(n+|K| log log n) by Theo-
rem 3. As |R| ⩽4fd(n + 2) + 4n ∈Od(n) by Lemma 4, we may then re-initialize
P with R in time Od(n log log n), by Theorem 2. So let us proceed to describing
how to find the decomposition K of small enough size, given the list of slabs
P from P (that can be computed upon the last recomputation of P and stored
since then until now) and the list of updates Q from Q, that can be extracted
from Q in time proportional to the size of Q, bounded by 8fd(n + 2).
The updates in Q can be partitioned into 0-updates, i.e., the entries (i, j) in
Q whose value is 0, and 1-updates, i.e., the entries (i, j) in Q whose value is 1. We
first sort Q lexicographically in O(|Q|) time. Using P, we assign the 0-updates to
slabs of P that contain them. As a result, each slab P ∈P is assigned a list LP of
0-updates from Q, each of which is contained in P, sorted lexicographically. Next,
for each slab P ∈P, we iterate through LP successively splitting P into smaller
slabs. We will process LP in batches, each batch corresponding to updates of LP
with equal first coordinate. Thus, let Li be the sorted list of second coordinates

2)) log log n) time, and P can be re-initialized in O((fd(n + 2)) log log n) time
as well. Amortized over 8fd(n + 2) updates, this gives an amortized expected
time of O(log log n) per update operation.

De-amortization
In this section we show how to de-amortize the data structure of Theorem 4.
Precisely, we prove the following statement, which immediately implies Theo-
rem 1.
Theorem 5. There is a data structure W, which maintains a d-twin-ordered
n × n binary matrix M by supporting the following operations:
1. Init(n, K): Initialize the data structure with a slab decomposition K of M
in O((n + |K|) log log n) time.
2. QueryBit(i, j): Return the entry M[i, j] in O(log log n) expected worst case
time.
3. Update(i, j): Flip the entry M[i, j] in O(log log n) expected worst case time.

13
The data structure W uses Od(n) space.
Proof. We apply a standard de-amortization technique to the data structure A
of Theorem 4. For the simplicity of exposition, we firstly present a slightly flawed
simpler version of the argument, and then we explain what was the issue and
how to fix it.
Let L = 8fd(n + 2) be the limit on the size of Q from Theorem 4 and let us
partition all updates into epochs of L updates. Let us denote the ith epoch as
Ei, with the first epoch being E0. For each valid i ⩾1, let us define the data
structure Ai that will exist throughout epochs E2i−2, E2i−1, E2i, E2i+1 and the
data structure A0 that will exist throughout epochs E0 and E1 (in particular,
there will be only two different data structures existing at any point of time).
The data structure A0 will be initialized with the Init(n, K) call and responsible
for answering queries of epochs E0 and E1. The data structure Ai for i ⩾1 will
be initialized throughout epochs E2i−2 and E2i−1 and responsible for answer-
ing queries during epochs E2i and E2i+1. At the start of epoch E2i−2, we run
Init(n, K), where K is the slab decomposition obtained from the current state of
Ai−1, similarly as in the case of recomputation of the data structure in the proof
of Theorem 4. We have that |K| = O(L), so that call takes O(L log log n) total
time. Hence, we can distribute this work over L updates of epoch E2i−2 adding
O(log log n) worst case time overhead to each update. At the end of E2i−2 the
Init(n, K) call will be finished, but Ai will consequently be L updates behind
the current state, as its state reflects M before E2i−2. During the epoch E2i−1
the data structure Ai catches up by processing two updates when Ai−1 processes
one. That multiplies the work on each update only by an absolute constant. At
the end of E2i−1, Ai caught up to the current state and is ready to take over
the role of being the data structure that actually answers incoming queries.
The above description was slightly flawed, as the Init(n, K) call initializing
Ai needs to know K. However, it takes O(|K|) = O(L) time to create Ai and
it requires uninterrupted access to the internal data of Ai−1. However Ai−1
needs to be answering new queries, from the epoch E2i−2, so it cannot grant
uninterrupted access to its internal P and Q for the sake of creating K. The trick
to mitigate this issue is to create two copies of each data structure — Ai,0 and
Ai,1. Up to the moment of starting E2i epoch, they are initialized in the same
way as Ai was in the previous description. However, at this moment Ai,0 takes
the responsibility of answering incoming updates (and consequently, requires
changes to its internal data), while Ai,1 does not process any updates and serves
the role of the snapshot of Ai after its full initialization, lending itself to Ai+1
that required uninterrupted access to Ai for its initialization.
