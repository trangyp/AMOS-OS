Abstract. When students are unsure of the correct answer to a multiple-
choice question (MCQ), guessing is common practice. The availability
heuristic, proposed by A. Tversky and D. Kahneman in 1973, suggests
that the ease with which relevant instances come to mind, typically oper-
ationalised by the mere frequency of exposure, can offer a mental short-
cut for problems in which the test-taker does not know the exact answer.
Is simply choosing the option that comes most readily to mind a good
strategy for answering MCQs? We propose a computational method of
assessing the cognitive availability of MCQ options operationalised by
concepts’ prevalence in large corpora. The key finding, across three large
question sets, is that correct answers, independently of the question stem,
are significantly more available than incorrect options. Specifically, using
Wikipedia as the retrieval corpus, we find that always selecting the most
available option leads to scores 13.5% to 32.9% above the random-guess
baseline. We also find that LLM-generated MCQ options show similar
patterns of availability compared to expert-created options, despite the
LLMs’ frequentist nature and their training on large collections of tex-
tual data. Our findings suggest that availability should be considered in
current and future computationally modelling student behaviour.
Keywords: Availability Heuristic · Multiple-Choice Question Evalua-
tion · Information Retrieval

Introduction
During test-taking, examinees often use “test-wiseness strategies” of varying com-
plexity to achieve a higher score [7]. For example, in the context of Multiple-
Choice Questions (MCQs), examinees might quickly eliminate absurd options,
or use an “umbrella term” strategy, by which they choose the option that encom-
passes all known correct options [6,11]. In this work, we evaluate the “availability”
heuristic as a test-wiseness strategy.
The availability heuristic, introduced by A. Tversky and D. Kahneman is a
strategy by which the likelihood of an event is estimated based on the ease of
cognitive retrieval of relevant memories or associations [12]. In the MCQ context,

We further make a distinction between in- and out-of-context availability:
While the availability of “Paris” is high out-of-context, the availability of “An-
tananarivo” increases when conditioned on the question “Which of the follow-
ing is an African capital?”. In other words, we can consider the availability of
MCQ options with or without the question stem. In this work, we propose an
availability quantification methodology based on the prevalence of the content
of each option in large text corpora 1. With this approach, in this work, we
evaluate whether correct MCQ answers show higher availability compared to
incorrect options (also known as distractors), therefore offering a useful test-
wiseness strategy to test-takers. We are particularly interested in out-of-context
availability, as it presents a stronger case for the availability heuristic as a use-
ful test-wiseness strategy (see Section 3.2 for details). Henceforth, we refer to
out-of-context availability simply as availability.
In recent times, various techniques leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs)
have been proposed for the generation of MCQ distractors [2]. This is seemingly
a low-hanging fruit, as these tools are widely accessible and the well-defined na-
ture of the task lowers the barrier of entry for educators. At the same time,

assessing the pedagogical quality of distractors is also an open field of research,

of crowdsourced distractors as a middle ground between the efficiency of LLMs
and the domain knowledge of human experts.
Concretely, our work is structured around three Research Questions:
RQ1 Do correct answers of MQs exhibit higher availability compared to dis-
tractors?
RQ2 Are high availability distractors more commonly selected by students com-
pared to low availability distractors?
RQ3 Do machine-, crowd-, and expert-generated distractors exhibit differences
in availability?
More broadly, given that most student simulation nowadays are based on
models trained on large corpora, with this research we assess how the availability
heuristic, as proxied by frequency, influences answer selection to MCQs.

Methodology
To tackle the outlined research questions, it is necessary to have an appropriate
method of determining the availability of the MCQ options. To this end, we use
a multi-step retrieval-based approach, described in Section 2.1. Furthermore, the
human-curated question sets and the altered versions containing LLM-generated
distractors are detailed in Sections 2.2 and 2.3, respectively.
2.1
Determining Option Availability
In the context of this research, we use the prevalence of a concept (i.e., an MCQ
option) in large corpora as a signal of availability: We assume that frequently
occurring concepts in the corpus correspond to higher cognitive availability for
a test-taker. To operationalise this intuition, for a given MCQ, we retrieve a
pre-determined number of relevant text passages from a large corpus and record
how they are semantically distributed across the options of the MCQ. As a hypo-
thetical example, for the question “What is the capital of France?” with options
“Paris”, “Tallinn” and “Antananarivo”, we determine each option’s availability by
its prevalence in a large corpus containing general knowledge.

Knowledge Corpora As sources for the passage retrieval, we use the En-
glish Wikipedia [14] and BEIR, a large information retrieval dataset [10]. Using
Wikipedia is a natural choice, as it contains large amounts of highly curated fac-
tual information. On the other hand, the passages in BEIR cover a much broader
scope (e.g., news articles, forum threads & financial investment articles), albeit
with less detail. We consider this difference relevant to our study: If large differ-
ences in the availability of MCQ options are observed using Wikipedia but not
BEIR, it suggests that only individuals with expert domain knowledge might be
able to leverage the availability heuristic. Wikipedia and BEIR are of similar
size, containing 41.5 and 48 million passages respectively.
Retrieving Passages In order to retrieve passages relevant to a given MCQ,
we leverage semantic similarity between the MCQ options and the passages in
the corpus. For this, relying on textual embeddings instead of n-gram frequency
is preferable, as the former better capture conceptual nuance. Specifically, we use
Cohere’s “Embed v3” [4] model to compute textual embeddings for each passage
in the corpus and the combined options of each MCQ (e.g., “Paris Tallinn An-
tananarivo”). We only use the combined options as the retrieval query, excluding
the question stem, as to measure out-of-context, instead of in-context availabil-
ity. This choice also reduces the bias towards the correct answer: building on the
earlier example, the presence of “France” in the query will lead to the retrieval
of more passages related to Paris, thus obstructing its genuine availability. It
is worth noting that the presence of the co-options during retrieval still offer
some context, albeit less than the presence of the question stem. We explore the
effect of the retrieval query in more detail in Section 3.2. Lastly, we also explore
the effect of the number of passages retrieved, by retrieving 20 or 60 passages
for each MCQ. We do this to study the over-representation of certain domains
in corpora (e.g., general geography content is significantly more prevalent than
specialised immunopharmacology).
Assigning Passages Once the passages relevant to the MCQ options are re-
trieved, each is assigned to one of the options based on semantic similarity. To
achieve this, we compute the textual embedding of the passage and each option.
The passage is then assigned to the option with the highest cosine similarity,
leading to a distribution of passages across the options (Figure 1).
2.2
Distractors Generation Method
We further aim to evaluate whether human- and LLM-generated options show
similar patterns in terms of availability. To this end, we employ three LLMs from
the Qwen 3 family [15], specifically the instruction-tuned/chat variants with 8,
30, and 80 billion parameters. Using three LLMs of the same family but differing
in size allows us to also explore whether any observed option availability effects
are mitigated or enhanced by using more powerful LLMs. We chose to focus on

the Qwen 3 family as its models are capable, offer the aforementioned size scaling,
and are more lightweight compared to proprietary closed-source alternatives.
To generate alternative distractors for a given MCQ, a simple generation
prompt is used that includes the question stem, the correct answer, and the
expected number of distractors (Figure 2). A default generation temperature of
τ = 0.7 is used, as suggested in the model documentation [15], to ensure some
generation stochasticity. In approximately 0.5% of cases, the wrong number of
distractors was generated, the correct answer was presented as a distractor, or
generated distractors were repeated. When this occurred, generation was re-
peated until the conditions were satisfied. While more curated prompts can lead
to pedagogically better distractors [2], we rely on a simpler instruction as this
more accurately reflects an intuitive and natural interaction with the model.

2.3
Manually Created Question Sets
We conduct our main experiments using two exam question sets covering the
topics of Biopsychology and Immunopharmacology. These datasets satisfy three
critical requirements for this research. First, these question sets target factual
knowledge, where any availability differences between options are most likely to
prevail. Secondly, they include student selection rates per option, allowing us
to study whether, for example, high availability distractors are more commonly
selected by students compared to lower availability distractors (RQ2). Lastly,
the two sets are private, ensuring that the LLMs used to generate alternative
distractors (as described in Section 2.2) have not encountered the human-created
distractors during their training.
Both datasets originate from undergraduate courses taught at our institution.
The Biopsychology data includes 396 three-option and 380 four-option MCQs
from the Psychology programme, covering the textbook “Biological Psychology”
[5], with selection rates reflecting an average of 300 responses. In comparison, the

3.1
LLM-Generated Distractors
To evaluate whether LLM-generated distractors exhibit differences in availabil-
ity, we employ the methodology described in Section 2.2 to create three sets
of alternative distractors for each item. The LLMs Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-30B, and
Qwen3-80B are used, representing LLMs of generally varying capabilities. Since
student selection rates are not available for the LLM-generated options, we com-
pare the availability of the correct answer against the average availability of the
distractors for each item. For this analysis, we present results based on Wikipedia
as the retrieval corpus, retrieving 20 passages per item. To this end, a Bayesian
Dirichlet-Multinomial model (uniform prior, α = 1) was used to compare the
availability of the correct answer against the average availability of the distrac-
tors. We report the probability of direction pd, representing the certainty that
the correct answer is more available than the distractors, along with the mean
difference and 95% High Density Intervals (Figure 4).
Regarding RQ3, we observe from this analysis that human and LLM-generated
distractors exhibit a similar availability pattern: the correct answer is consis-
tently more available than the distractors across all experiments. Additionally,
we find that this behaviour holds for LLMs of different capabilities, suggesting
that increased general capabilities do not lead to the generation of distractors
with more similar availability to the correct answer. These findings extend to
SciQ, the non-expert crowdsourced dataset, indicating that, in terms of avail-
ability, domain experts, crowd-workers and LLMs generate similar distractors.

In- and Out-of-Context Availability As described in Section 1, we draw a
distinction between in- and out-of-context availability, both of which are mea-
sured by concept prevalence in large corpora. Briefly, in the MCQ setting, in-
context availability asks: Given this question, which option comes most readily
to mind? Meanwhile, out-of-context availability asks: Regardless of the question
stem, which of these options is inherently more prevalent? Using our methodol-
ogy, we can capture in- and out-of-context availability by modifying the passage
retrieval query, to either include or exclude the question stem. As expected, in-
context availability favours the correct answer even more clearly. This pattern is
confirmed by the results in Table 3, where in-context availability using Wikipedia
as the retrieval corpus is consistently higher than out-of-context availability. Us-
ing BEIR this pattern is not observed, reinforcing the suggestion that, despite
its large size, BEIR does not contain relevant passages to the questions. The
implications of this result are further discussed in Section 4.

3.3
Availability as a Guessing Strategy
We further evaluate whether the disparity in availability between correct answers
and distractors can be leveraged to achieve high scores by calculating the accu-
racy achieved when always selecting the most available option. Table 4 presents
the accuracy achieved on each question set using in- and out-of-context avail-
ability measured by retrieving 20 passages from the Wikipedia corpus for each
MCQ. In other words, these results indicate the proportion of MCQs for which
the correct answer has higher availability than the distractors.
Consistent with the findings in Section 3.2, in-context availability yields sig-
nificantly higher performance. This result is expected, as incorporating the ques-
tion into the retrieval query naturally prioritizes passages relevant to the correct
answer. Interestingly, out-of-context availability also performs substantially bet-
ter than chance (25% for 4-option and 33% for 3-option questions). Specifically,
on human-curated datasets, we observe performance gains over the baseline rang-
ing from 13.5% to 32.9%. Lastly, in line with the results in Section 3.1, we do not
see any large differences between human- and LLM-generated sets of distractors.

Discussion
In this work we explored a computational method of quantifying the availability
heuristic in the MCQ setting by measuring the prevalence of concepts in large
text corpora. We found that the availability of the correct answer is significantly
higher than that of the distractors, even when not accounting for the question
(RQ1). We furthermore find that availability is, by itself, not sufficient to explain
why some distractors are more often selected than others (RQ2). Lastly, we find
that these conclusions do not only hold for expert-created distractors, but also
extend to non-expert crowdsourced and LLM-generated distractors (RQ3).
A consistent finding across all experiments is the discrepancy in availabil-
ity depending on the retrieval corpus used. While availability differences are
pronounced when the Wikipedia corpus is used, they are negligible using BEIR.
Given Wikipedia’s highly specialised, factual nature compared to BEIR’s broader
but shallower scope, this suggests an intriguing distinction: Wikipedia allows us
to approximate the availability heuristic of an expert (who possesses prior do-
main knowledge), whereas BEIR mirrors a novice’s perspective. In the broader
context, this distinction highlights the care that needs to be given in selecting
appropriate datasets when using computational approaches for education.
Shifting the focus to MCQ evaluation, we propose that our corpus-based
methodology for quantifying the availability heuristic can assess how competitive

this does not ensure the distractor set is effective, as other aspects contribute to

tationally measuring option availability holds promise for better characterizing
MCQ option quality.
More broadly, our findings suggest that the availability heuristic, as proxied
by frequency, plays a strong role in answer selection to MCQs. As most compu-
tational approaches to student simulation nowadays are based on models trained
on large corpora, this affects the faithfulness of such simulations and should be
accounted for, including when developing teaching and testing materials. In this
research, we proxied the availability heuristic computationally. A complementary
approach could measure this availability heuristic directly through behavioural
experiments in human participants.
5
Limitations
Because the experiments focused on factual knowledge, the datasets likely high-
light differences in the availability of specific MCQ options, specifically because
these options often represent concepts. We expect that these findings would be
more subtle in reasoning-heavy domains like mathematics. However, arguably,
MCQs are inherently not the best way to assess reasoning skills.
Moreover, in Section 3.1, we evaluated the difficulty of the question sets
using three LLMs of varying capabilities, drawing a parallel between student and
LLM-perceived difficulty. This comparison is supported by previous literature,
which identifies a consistent correlation between student and LLM performance
on MCQs [9,16]. However, Zotos et al. also note that LLMs and students are
influenced by different factors (e.g., LLMs are significantly more sensitive to
MCQ phrasing than students). Finally, while this parallel exists, difficulty is
only one aspect of MCQ quality. Therefore, we do not claim that distractors
(whether human- or machine-generated) are of higher quality simply because
they are easy or difficult for students.
Finally, our methodology used a simple instruction prompt to generate dis-
tractors using LLMs. This contrasts with extensive prior work regarding strate-
gies for creating pedagogically sound distractors [2]. Nevertheless, given the time
constraints faced by educators, we argue that a simple prompt is more represen-
tative of real-world usage than sophisticated distractor-generation pipelines.
