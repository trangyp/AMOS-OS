with n vertices.

Introduction
Testing monotonicity on structured domains is a central problem in property testing and a standard
example in the sublinear-time model; see, e.g., [17, 21, 8, 23, 29].
It was first studied on the
Boolean hypercube and product domains and has since been extended to richer product structures
and general posets/graph families [1, 7, 11, 16, 19, 22, 25, 28]. One way to model posets is via
reachability in directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). In this work we study the massively parameterized
model [29, 21], where the entire DAG is given explicitly.
1.1
Problem and background
Let G = (V, E) be a DAG with n = |V | vertices. A function f : V →R is monotone if f(u) ≤f(v)
whenever (u, v) ∈E. Given full access to G, a proximity parameter ε > 0, and oracle access to
f, the goal is to distinguish monotone functions from those that are ε-far from monotonicity with
constant success probability. We do not charge for preprocessing or computations on G; only queries
to f are counted. We consider both non-adaptive and adaptive testers, with one-sided or two-sided
error. A tester is non-adaptive if all queries are fixed in advance (adaptive otherwise), and one-sided
if it always accepts monotone functions (two-sided otherwise). Much of the monotonicity-testing
literature on both upper and lower bounds focuses on product domains such as the hypercube and
hypergrid, where in some settings tight bounds are known [5, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16].
For general posets, the classical non-adaptive one-sided tester of Fischer et al. [19] achieves

1.3
Technical overview
1.3.1
Non-adaptive two-sided lower bound
Lower bounds: from induced matchings to positive matchings.
The classical lower-bound
approach for monotonicity testing on general posets, pioneered by Fischer et al. [18], is built around
Ruzsa–Szemer´edi (RS) graphs: one packs many large induced matchings and uses them to generate
hard YES/NO instances.
The relevance of matchings is that violated pairs in a matching are
vertex-disjoint, so repairing all of them requires changing at least one endpoint per pair, which
directly yields a distance-from-monotonicity lower bound. For query lower bounds, matchings also
localize evidence: a tester typically needs to query both endpoints of one matched pair to observe
informative violations. Inducedness then plays a dual role there. It is what makes the violation
pattern “clean” for Boolean-valued functions (extra comparabilities among matching endpoints
would immediately create additional violations), and at the same time it becomes the bottleneck:
extremal graph theory severely limits how many linear-size induced matchings can coexist in a
single graph, which is why RS-based arguments stall far below the ˜Θ(√n) regime.
Our first step is to identify the right analogue of induced matchings for real-valued functions.
On bipartite DAGs (all edges oriented left-to-right), the question “can a matching be realized as
exactly the set of violated edges?” has a precise answer: this holds if and only if the matching is
positive (Lemma 3.1). Informally, “positive” means there is a vertex weight assignment w such that
w(ℓ) + w(r) > 0 exactly on matching edges and w(ℓ) + w(r) ≤0 on all other edges. Equivalently,
positivity excludes alternating cycles/closed walks on the matched vertices (Theorem 2.3). This
motivates the notion of positive-matching RS (PMRS) families, obtained by relaxing RS from
induced matchings to positive matchings (Definition 3.3). This relaxation is specific to the real-
valued setting and bypasses the inducedness barrier in the Fischer et al. argument.
From PMRS to a near-tight non-adaptive lower bound.
Once a PMRS decomposition is
available, the lower bound becomes essentially information-theoretic. Given s edge-disjoint pos-
itive matchings M1, . . . , Ms (each of linear size), we hide a random index i ∈[s] and construct
two distributions over functions. The YES distribution is monotone, built from a base monotone
function with a constant margin on non-matching edges (so small random noise does not create
new violations), while the NO distribution is ε-far because it violates a constant fraction of edges in
the hidden matching Mi (Section 3.3). Crucially, the two distributions have identical single-vertex
marginals; they differ only in a local correlation between the two endpoints of a matching edge.
Therefore, a non-adaptive algorithm can obtain distinguishing information only if it queries both
endpoints of some edge in Mi. Since the matchings are edge-disjoint, a q-query set can fully cover
at most O(q2) edges, and the probability of hitting the hidden matching index is at most O(q2/s),
yielding the Ω(√s) lower bound (Theorem 3.8). A useful technical fact (as with induced matchings)
is that positivity is closed under taking submatchings (Lemma 3.2), which lets us refine each large
matching into many smaller positive matchings and obtain the 1/√ε dependence (Lemma 3.10
and corollary 3.11).

challenge is to build explicit graphs with many large edge-disjoint positive matchings.

1.3.2
Adaptive one-sided lower bound
Violations on a hidden matching and the witness principle.
Moving from non-adaptive to
adaptive testers, and from two-sided to one-sided error, requires a different lower-bound argument.
In the bipartite-DAG setting (all edges oriented left-to-right), one-sidedness comes with a very
strong “witness principle” (Lemma 4.1): if the tester has not queried a violated edge, then the
partial transcript can always be extended to a globally monotone function. In other words, a one-
sided tester is not “estimating distance” indirectly; it is literally searching for a witnessed violation.
The challenge is that adaptivity seems to help precisely with such search problems: after observing
a few values, the tester can steer future queries toward edges that are more likely to be violated.
Section 4 turns this search problem into an indexing problem. As in the PMRS-based non-
adaptive lower bound, we start from a base graph containing many edge-disjoint positive matchings
M1, . . . , Ms, and we hide a random index I ∈[s].
The goal is to generate a distribution over
functions such that (i) with high probability the sampled function is ε-far from monotone because
it violates almost all edges of the hidden matching MI, (ii) edges outside MI are almost never
violated (so the tester cannot reject without learning something about I), and (iii) even an adaptive
algorithm learns only limited information about I per query. The last two requirements are exactly
where naive constructions that simply prescribe a violating matching break down: once we condition
on an adaptive prefix, the remaining unqueried variables are no longer independent, and a tester
might try to create or expose violations on non-matching edges by strategically revealing endpoints.
Our lower-bound argument uses a truncated strongly log-concave prior, namely a Gibbs distri-
bution {µi}i∈[s] over real-valued labelings f supported on a box. The Hamiltonian has three roles.

Spurious violations under adaptivity.
Even if a fresh non-matching edge is very unlikely to be
violated unconditionally, an adaptive tester might try to bias it by first querying one endpoint and
then deciding whether to query the other. The analysis therefore must be prefix-conditioned: after
any transcript prefix, when the tester “closes” a new edge, the conditional probability of witnessing
a violation on an edge outside MI must still be tiny. This is handled by a careful one-dimensional
reduction on the difference coordinate d = f(ℓ) −f(r) for an edge e = (ℓ, r): after conditioning on
everything except the two endpoints, the induced density of d is log-concave with explicit strong
curvature coming from the quadratic term. The hinge term sets a target point (+γ/2 for matching
edges and −γ/2 for non-matching edges), and strong convexity translates “the derivative at the
target has the right sign” into an exponentially small tail probability of landing on the wrong side.
The subtlety is the external drift contributed by all other incident constraints: under adaptive
conditioning, this drift is random and depends on the revealed neighborhood. Section 4 controls this
drift at the two critical points d = ±γ/2 with a high-probability argument: for C4-free base graphs,
the relevant neighborhood variables become independent after conditioning on the endpoints, and
strong log-concavity gives subgaussian tails for the drift; see Lemma 4.6. This is paired with a
separate bound on the conditional mean of the drift; in the final instantiation on our explicit dense
base graph (Section 4.7), this is the main analytic difficulty.
There is a second obstacle specific to the one-sided setting: the box truncation introduces
boundary effects, and an adaptive tester might try to push queried values near ±B, where the
feasible interval of d shrinks and the clean tail bound could fail. The fix is a deterministic inward
bias (Lemmas 4.3 and 4.4): when β > λ, the hinge potentials create a deterministic force that pushes
single-site conditionals away from the dangerous boundary strips. This yields a bound uniform
over adaptive strategies on the probability that any query ever lands near ±B (Lemma 4.12), and
hence bounds the probability that an adaptive strategy can exploit truncation to manufacture
spurious witnesses. Putting these together yields a theorem controlling spurious queried violations
(Lemma 4.11): with high probability, any q-query adaptive strategy witnesses no violated edge
outside the hidden matching unless it hits an exponentially unlikely boundary event.
Information leakage from adaptive queries.
Even if spurious violations are suppressed, an
adaptive tester could hope to learn the hidden index I from the queried values, and then target
an edge of MI. Section 4 controls this via a mutual-information upper bound I(I; T), where T
is the full adaptive transcript. The main analytic ingredient is a “Green-function comparison”
inequality for uniformly convex Gibbs measures (Lemma 4.15): for any prefix conditioning τ, the
KL divergence between the one-site conditional distributions under µi(· | τ) and µj(· | τ) is bounded
by a weighted sum of local discrepancies, with weights given by entries of (αI +2λLτ)−1, where Lτ
is the Laplacian on the still-unqueried vertices after conditioning on the prefix τ. This comparison
needs no global weak-dependence or uniqueness assumption: it uses only the strong convexity
provided by the quadratic term and survives arbitrary adaptive conditioning. A simple row-sum
bound on this Green function then gives a per-query leakage bound κQ, leading to the budgeted
bound I(I; T) ≤qκQ uniformly over all adaptive strategies with q ≤Q (Lemma 4.17).

1.3.3
Upper bounds
Bottleneck routing from a violation matching.
Our algorithmic upper bounds use the fol-
lowing combinatorial fact: distance to monotonicity is witnessed by a large matching of violated
comparable pairs in the transitive closure. Concretely, if f is ε-far, then the violation graph (on
comparable pairs) contains a matching M of size |M| ≥εn (via Konig’s theorem), so there are
many disjoint source–sink violations (u ⇝v, f(u) > f(v)). The difficulty is that these violations
typically live in the transitive closure, denoted by TC(G), while the input provides only the tran-
sitive reduction (Hasse diagram), whose edge set can be much smaller and may contain very few
direct violations. The key observation is that if direct violating edges are scarce, then they act as
bottlenecks through which many matched violations are routed, forcing a large supply of violated
pairs that can be detected by simple sampling.

violating edge in the transitive reduction. Fix, for each (u, v) ∈M, an arbitrary directed path Puv
in the transitive reduction from u to v. Since f(u) > f(v), along that path there must be a first
edge (x, y) where the inequality flips, i.e., a violating edge of the transitive reduction. Assign (u, v)

Related work
Monotonicity testing.
Monotonicity testing on the Boolean hypercube was initiated by Goldre-
ich et al. [22]. For product domains, subsequent work gave improved testers, near-optimal bounds
for hypercubes and hypergrids, and lower bounds over product domains and hypergrids [11, 12,
16, 5, 10, 13, 14, 15]; connections to isoperimetric inequalities for the Boolean cube were developed
in [26]. For general posets, testing was initiated by Fischer et al. [19], and a complementary line
used transitive-closure spanners (TC-spanners) and reachability sparsification to design testers on
structured graph families [7, 28]. Beyond standard testing, distance estimation and tolerant testing
were studied in [2, 27], while local reconstruction of monotone functions and its limitations appear
in [6, 31]. More recently, for real-valued functions on product domains, monotonicity has been
connected to isoperimetric inequalities for ordered ranges [9, 12]. In contrast, for explicit-DAG
monotonicity testing with real-valued ranges, prior lower bounds in both the non-adaptive and
adaptive models remained far from the √n scale.
Graph-theoretic background.
Graphs whose edges can be partitioned into induced matchings
(Ruzsa–Szemer´edi graphs) have been extensively studied in extremal graph theory since [30]. In the
linear regime r = cn, the number of induced matchings t is tightly constrained in several ranges of
c: Fox, Huang, and Sudakov show that t = O(1) for c > 1/4, that t = Θ(log n) for c = 1/4, and that
t = O(n/ log n) for every fixed 1/5 + ε ≤c < 1/4 [20]. On the other hand, for every fixed c < 1/4,
Fischer et al. construct graphs with induced matchings of size cn and with t = nΩ(1/ log log n) such
matchings, which underlie their Boolean monotonicity-testing lower bound on general posets [19].
If one allows slightly sublinear matchings instead, Alon, Moitra, and Sudakov construct nearly
complete graphs whose edges decompose into induced matchings of size n1−o(1), with total edge

Decompositions into positive matchings have also been studied under the name of positive
matching decompositions [18]. In that line of work, the natural question is whether the edge set
of a graph can be partitioned into positive matchings, and if so, into how many parts. Concretely,
positive matchings are characterized by the absence of M-alternating closed walks (where M denotes
the matching under consideration) on the M-saturated vertices [18]; in bipartite graphs this is
equivalent to being alternating-cycle-free (uniquely restricted matching) [24]. For our purposes,
PMRS is the real-valued analogue of RS packings on bipartite graphs, and positivity is exactly the
condition that allows a matching to appear as the set of violated edges.

known as uniquely restricted.

2.4
Testers and query complexity
We consider randomized algorithms with oracle access to f : V →R. A tester queries f at selected
vertices and, based on the query answers (and its internal randomness), outputs accept or reject. We
require completeness and soundness: for every monotone f, the tester accepts with probability at
least 2/3 (and with probability 1 for one-sided testers), and for every f that is ε-far from monotone,
it rejects with probability at least 2/3. A tester is non-adaptive if its query set depends only on its
internal randomness, and one-sided if it always accepts every monotone f. We work in the standard
massively parameterized model: the input DAG G is given explicitly and only oracle queries to f
are counted. Hence any computation on G (including computing TR(G), TC(G), ℓ, reachability
tests, and sampling uniformly from E or E(TC(G))) is free.
