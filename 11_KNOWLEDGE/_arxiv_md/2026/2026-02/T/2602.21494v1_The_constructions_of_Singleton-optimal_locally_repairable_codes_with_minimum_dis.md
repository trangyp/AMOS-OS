YANZHEN XIONGa AND JIANBING LUa,∗
Abstract. In this paper, we present new constructions of q-ary Singleton-optimal
locally repairable codes (LRCs) with minimum distance d = 6 and locality r = 3,
based on combinatorial structures from finite geometry.
By exploiting the well-
known correspondence between a complete set of mutually orthogonal Latin squares
(MOLS) of order q and the affine plane AG(2, q), We systematically construct fami-
lies of disjoint 4-arcs in the projective plane PG(2, q), such that the union of any two
distinct 4-arcs forms an 8-arc. These 4-arcs form what we call 4-local arcs, and their
existence is equivalent to that of the desired codes. For any prime power q ≥7, our
construction yields codes of length n = 2q, 2q −2, or 2q −6 depending on whether
q is even, q ≡3 (mod 4), or q ≡1 (mod 4), respectively.
Keywords: Locally repairable code; Singleton-type bound; Affine plane; Projective
plane; Arc; Latin square

1. Introduction
Let Fq be the finite field with q elements, where q is a prime power. An [n, k]q linear
code C is a k-dimensional subspace of Fn
q . The parameter n is called the length of
C. The vectors in C are called codewords. Let c ∈C be a codeword. The support of
c = (c1, c2, . . . , cn) is defined as supp(c) = {i ∈[n] : ci ̸= 0}, where [n] denotes the set
{1, 2, . . . , n}. The weight of c is the cardinality of its support, i.e., wt(c) = # supp(c).
An [n, k, d]q linear code C is an [n, k]q linear code with minimum distance d, defined
as d = min0̸=c∈C wt(c).
Usually, if the context is clear, the subscript q is omitted
by convention in the sequel. The i-th symbol of C is said to have locality r if there
exists a subset Ri ⊂[n] (called a repair group for the symbol) containing i, with size
|Ri| ≤r+1, such that ci can be expressed as a linear combination of the other symbols
{cj}j∈Ri\{i}. A linear code C is called a locally repairable code (LRC) with locality r
if each of its symbols has locality r. This concept was first introduced by Gopalan et
al. [10]. We denote such a code as an (n, k, d; r)-LRC. An LRC is said to have disjoint
local repair groups if the set of coordinates [n] is exactly the disjoint union of some
repair groups, i.e., [n] = S
i Ri, and |Ri| = r + 1.

An (n, k, d; r)-LRC achieving equality in (1.1) is called Singleton-optimal. There has
been extensive work on constructing Singleton-optimal LRCs.
In 2014, Tamo and
Barg [19] first gave a construction with length n up to the alphabet size q. This was
later improved to q + 1 by Chen et al. using cyclic codes [4] or constacyclic codes
[2]. In [14], Jin et al. also achieved length q + 1 via the automorphism groups of
rational function fields. For certain localities r ∈{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 23} with the additional
condition (r+1) | d, constructions based on elliptic curves achieve lengths up to q+2√q
[16]. For minimum distance d = 3 or 4, cyclic Singleton-optimal LRCs with unbounded
length were proposed by Luo et al. [17]. In [13], Jin gave an explicit construction of
q-ary Singleton-optimal LRCs of length Ωr(q2) for d = 5, 6 and r ≥4. Xing and Yuan
[22] later generalized Jin’s results to all d ≥7 using hypergraph theory.
Certain Singleton-optimal LRCs can be characterized by specific geometric objects
in projective plane PG(2, q), (see, e.g., [3, 8, 9, 20]). An n-arc in PG(2, q) is a set of
n points such that every line intersects the arc in at most 2 points. It is known that
n ≤q + 2, with equality holding if and only if q is even. In [9], the authors provided
sufficient and necessary conditions for the existence of Singleton-optimal (n, k, d; r)-
LRCs with d = 6, r = 3, and disjoint repair groups.
Lemma 1.1. [9, Theorem 6] Suppose 4 | n. Then, there exists a q-ary Singleton-
optimal LRC of length n, minimum distance d = 6 and locality r = 3 with disjoint
repair groups if and only if there exist n/4 sets S1, S2, . . . , Sn/4, each of which consists
of 4 points in PG(2, q), such that for any 1 ≤i ̸= j ≤n/4,
(i) Si ∩Sj = ∅;
(ii) no 3 points in Si ∪Sj lie on a common line.
By Theorem 1.1, the existence of such a code is equivalent to the existence of a
4-local arc in PG(2, q); that is, a collection of n/4 disjoint 4-arcs Si such that the
union of any two distinct 4-arcs forms an 8-arc.
A Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix filled with n distinct symbols, each
occurring exactly once in each row and once in each column [5, 7]. Latin squares
originate from Leonhard Euler’s “Thirty-Six Officers Problem”, which is equivalent to
the search for a pair of orthogonal Latin squares of order 6. A set of Latin squares
is called a set of mutually orthogonal Latin squares (MOLS) if any two squares in
the set are orthogonal; that is, when superimposed, every ordered pair of symbols
appears exactly once. This concept is fundamental to many applications in design
theory [1]. The connection between Latin squares and coding theory is fruitful [6].
In particular, they serve as powerful tools for constructing low-density parity-check
(LDPC) codes [15, 21, 24], LRCs [23], error-distributing codes [11], and developing
efficient secret-sharing schemes [6].
In this paper, we leverage this correspondence to provide new explicit constructions
of Singleton-optimal (n, k, d = 6, r = 3)-LRCs. Our approach starts from a complete
set of MOLS of order q, from which we obtain the associated affine plane AG(2, q). We
then systematically produce families of disjoint 4-arcs in the projective plane PG(2, q).
The key combinatorial objects in our method are transversals of specially structured
Latin squares—closely related to the addition table of Fq—that directly provide point
configurations in AG(2, q) satisfying the required ”no-three-collinear” condition both
inside each 4-arc and between different 4-arcs. These collections of 4-arcs precisely
form 4-local arcs (Definition 2.2) whose existence, by Lemma 1.1, is equivalent to
that of our target codes. This approach offers a unified combinatorial framework for

2.2. Affine planes and projective planes. Now, we recall the fundamental defi-
nitions and constructions of the affine and projective planes over a finite field. An
affine plane is an incidence structure of points and lines satisfying the following three
axioms:
(1) For any two distinct points, there is exactly one line through both;
(2) Given any line ℓand any point P not on ℓ, there is exactly one line through P
that does not meet ℓ;
(3) There exist four points such that no three are collinear.
One constructs a classical affine plane AG(2, q) by taking ordered pairs (x, y) ∈Fq as
points; and subsets of the form y = mx + b or x = a (for fixed a, m, b ∈Fq) as lines;
that is, they are point sets of the form
{(x, mx + b) : x ∈Fq} for m, b ∈Fq;
{(a, y) : y ∈Fq} for a ∈Fq.
The incidence is the natural one: a point is on a given line if and only if it satisfies
the required linear equation. Two lines ℓ1 and ℓ2 in AG(2, q) are said to be parallel
if ℓ1 = ℓ2 or they have no point in common. We can check that Parallelism is an
equivalence relation on the lines of AG(2, q). For each m ∈Fq ∪{∞}, lines of slope
m form a parallel class of lines (Lines of slope ∞are simply vertical lines of the form
x = constant). The classical projective plane PG(2, q) can be obtained from AG(2, q)
by the following standard extension:
(1) Embed the affine points into the projective plane via the mapping (x, y) 7→
⟨(1, x, y)⟩;
(2) For each parallel class of lines with slope m ∈Fq, add a new point at infinity,
denoted by ⟨(0, 1, m)⟩. For the vertical lines (m = ∞), add the point at infinity
⟨(0, 0, 1)⟩.
(3) Add a new line, called the line at infinity, consisting of all the points at infinity
added in step (2).

2.3. Latin square and MOLS. Let S be a set of n distinct symbols. A Latin square
of order n, denoted by L is an n × n matrix with entries from S such that for any
distinct positions (i, j) and (i′, j′), it holds L(i, j) ̸= L(i′, j′) if either i = i′ or j = j′.

A partial transversal of L is a set Γ of ordered pairs such that for any two distinct
(i, j) and (i′, j′) in Γ, it holds that i ̸= i′, j ̸= j′ and L(i, j) ̸= L(i′, j′). The number of
pairs in Γ is called its length. Clearly, the length of a partial transversal cannot exceed
n. A partial transversal of length n is called a transversal.
Let L and L′ be two Latin squares of order n with entries from S. We say that L
and L′ are orthogonal if for every ordered pair (s, s′) ∈S × S, there exists a unique
position (i, j) such that (L(i, j), L′(i, j)) = (s, s′). If they are orthogonal, we say that
L is an orthogonal mate of L′ or vice versa. A set of Latin squares of order n is called
a set of mutually orthogonal Latin squares (MOLS) if every two distinct Latin squares
in the set are orthogonal. When n is a prime power, a set of MOLS is said to be
complete if it consists of exactly n −1 Latin squares. For basic properties and further
background on Latin squares and MOLS, we refer to [7].

The constructions presented in Theorems 4.2 and 4.5 achieve lengths that grow linearly
in q, which is considerably below this asymptotic bound. Moreover, when q = 9, our
method yields n = 12, whereas the upper bound evaluates to 16 (see [9, Example 1]).
This substantial gap suggests that either the known upper bound could be improved,
or new constructions approaching the bound may exist. Investigating this discrepancy
is therefore an interesting direction for future work.
A natural combinatorial extension of our work is to replace the 4-arc blocks by k-arc
blocks for a fixed integer k ≥4, while requiring that the union of any two distinct
blocks forms an 2k-arc. We call such a configuration a k-local arc in PG(2, q).
Several purely geometric and combinatorial questions arise immediately:
• Existence and construction: For which pairs (q, k) do k-local arcs exist?
Can one give explicit constructions?
• Maximal size: What is the maximum possible number of blocks (or equiva-
lently, the maximum total number of points) in a k-local arc? How does this
maximum grow with q and k?
• Potential coding-theoretic implications: The geometric study of k-local
arcs could inspire the construction of new linear codes. Exploring this connec-
tion remains an open direction at the intersection of finite geometry and coding
theory.
Studying k-local arcs therefore offers a natural next step in understanding the combi-
natorial landscape of arc-type configurations in projective planes, independent of their
potential coding-theoretic applications.
Finally, LRCs with different parameters d and r correspond to various geometric
objects. For example, [20, Theorem 10] shows that the existence of a q-ary Singleton-
optimal (n = ℓ(δ + 1), k, d = 2δ + 2, r = 2, δ)-LRC with disjoint local repair groups
is equivalent to the existence of a set of points in PG(2, q) with certain properties
on secant and tangent lines. Exploring the connections between LRCs with various
parameters and finite geometric objects is also worth further attention.
