Abstract
Kirkpatrick et al. [ALT 2019] and Fallat et al. [JMLR 2023] introduced non-clashing teaching and proved that it is
the most efficient batch machine teaching model satisfying the collusion-avoidance benchmark established in the sem-
inal work of Goldman and Mathias [COLT 1993]. Recently, (positive) non-clashing teaching was thoroughly studied
for balls in graphs, yielding numerous algorithmic and combinatorial results. In particular, Chalopin et al. [COLT
2024] and Ganian et al. [ICLR 2025] gave an almost complete picture of the complexity landscape of the positive
variant, showing that it is tractable only for restricted graph classes due to the non-trivial nature of the problem and
concept class.
In this work, we consider (positive) non-clashing teaching for closed neighborhoods in graphs. This concept
class is not only extensively studied in various related contexts, but it also exhibits broad generality, as any finite
binary concept class can be equivalently represented by a set of closed neighborhoods in a graph. In comparison to
the works on balls in graphs, we provide improved algorithmic results, notably including FPT algorithms for more
general classes of parameters, and we complement these results by deriving stronger lower bounds. Lastly, we obtain
combinatorial upper bounds for wider classes of graphs.

Introduction
In classic machine learning models such as PAC-learning, a learner must reconstruct a concept C from a concept
class C using a random sample of examples. In contrast, in machine teaching, the examples are selected from the
domain by a teacher whose goal is to aid the learner. Specifically, the teacher wants to minimize the number of
labeled examples shown to the learner such that the learner can uniquely reconstruct the concept. Machine teach-
ing is a core area in learning theory, with several models proposed in the literature [SM91, GK95, GM96, Bal08,
ZLHZ11, GRSZ16, GRSZ17, MCV+19]. Moreover, it has many applications in machine learning, such as sample
compression [KW07, DFSZ14, MSWY15, CCMW22], inverse reinforcement learning [HLM+16, BN19], training
data security [MZ15, ZZW18], and human-robot interaction [TC09, ACYT12].
In this work, we focus on the non-clashing teaching model [KSZ19, FKS+23], a batch teaching framework where
the teacher presents a set of examples to the learner simultaneously. This model has gained significant attention in
recent years, since it was shown [KSZ19, FKS+23] to require the fewest examples among batch teaching models that
adhere to the Goldman-Mathias collusion-avoidance criterion [GM96], a fundamental principle that prevents teacher-
learner collusion (e.g., through encoding).
In non-clashing teaching, given a finite binary concept class C, a teacher maps each concept C ∈C to a labeled set
of examples T(C) that is consistent with C and called the teaching set of C. The teaching map T must satisfy the non-
clashing condition: for each pair of distinct concepts C, C′ ∈C, at least one of the examples in T(C)∪T(C′) must be

consistent with exactly one of the two concepts C and C′. If T satisfies this condition and maxC∈C |T(C)| = k, then
T is a non-clashing teaching map (NCTM) of size k for C. Notably, the teacher needs to present at most k examples
(specifically, T(C)) for the learner to uniquely reconstruct any concept C ∈C. The non-clashing teaching dimension
of C, denoted by NCTD(C), is the smallest size of an NCTM for C.
Machine teaching is often studied in the setting where the teacher is restricted to presenting only positively-labeled
examples. Research in this setting dates back to 1980 [Ang80b, Ang80a] and has applications in, e.g., recommendation
systems [SPK00], grammatical inference [SO94, Den01], and bioinformatics [WDMH06, YJSS08]. Notably, when
T(C) may only consist of positively-labeled examples for all C ∈C, the corresponding positive non-clashing teaching
dimension of C, denoted by NCTD+(C), has been extensively studied [KSZ19, FKS+23, CCMR24, GKMR25].
The initial works [KSZ19, FKS+23] on non-clashing teaching studied the relationship between the NCTD and the
VC-dimension (VCD), due to its value in learning theory. A key open question of theirs is whether there is a concept
class C such that NCTD(C) > VCD(C). It was also shown that deciding whether NCTD+(C) ≤1 (NCTD(C) ≤1,
resp.) is NP-complete when C is a set of open neighborhoods in a graph G [KSZ19]. Although unstated, by those
results, unless the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH)1 fails, there is no algorithm running in 2o(f(k)·√
|V (G)|) time
for either problem for any computable function f of the solution size k.2
Any finite binary concept class can be equivalently represented by a set of balls in a graph G (see Fig. 1), where the
ball of radius r ∈N centered at the vertex v ∈V (G) is the set Br(v) := {u | dist(u, v) ≤r, u ∈V (G)} [CCM+23].
This was one of two main motivations for the recent works [CCMR24, GKMR25] on non-clashing teaching for balls
in graphs. The other was that this is a natural and fundamental concept class that has been investigated for related
topics like sample compression [CCM+23], and its VC-dimension is well-studied [CEV07, BT15, DHV20].
Given a graph G, a set of balls B in G, and k ∈N, NON-CLASH asks whether NCTD+(B) ≤k. If B contains
every ball in G, then this problem is known as STRICT NON-CLASH.3 When G is restricted to the class of split or
(co-)bipartite graphs, STRICT NON-CLASH remains NP-hard [CCMR24], even if k = 2 for the former [GKMR25].
STRICT NON-CLASH can be solved in 22O(vc(G)) · |V (G)|O(1) time, where vc(G) is the vertex cover number of
G, and this is tight under the ETH [CCMR24]. This FPT4 algorithm was improved upon by considering a more
general parameter and problem, as it was shown that NON-CLASH is FPT parameterized by the vertex integrity of
G [GKMR25]. As NON-CLASH is W[1]-hard5 parameterized by the feedback vertex number, pathwidth, and solution
size k combined [GKMR25], these results give a nearly complete picture of its complexity landscape. There is a
2O(|V (G)|·d·k·log |V (G)|)-time algorithm for NON-CLASH, and unless the ETH fails, there is no 2o(|V (G)|·d·k)-time
algorithm, even for STRICT NON-CLASH, where d is the diameter of G [GKMR25]. Finally, (near-)optimal NCTMs
are known for balls if G is a tree, cycle, cactus or interval graph [CCMR24].

We explore non-clashing teaching for balls in graphs from a combinatorial and computational perspective, provid-
ing a comprehensive understanding of the problem. As discussed above, the problem is inherently algorithmically

rameters, we study both variants of non-clashing teaching for closed neighborhoods (balls of radius 1) in graphs:

the treedepth of G (Theorem 5) and N-NCTD is FPT parameterized by the vertex cover number of G (Theorem 7).
These are two well-established structural graph parameters that have been considered in, e.g., Bayesian network learn-
ing [KP15, HK23] and other areas of AI [GPSS20, EGHK23]. Notably, we again achieve positive results that were
not obtained in the setting of balls in graphs as treedepth is a more general parameter than vertex integrity,6 and no
FPT algorithm parameterized by a structural graph parameter was known for any concept class when the teacher may
use negatively-labeled examples.
Finally, in Section 4, we obtain combinatorial bounds for larger graph classes than those in [CCMR24]. Exploiting
forbidden minors, we prove that, if B is a set of closed neighborhoods in a planar graph G, then NCTD+(B) ≤7
(Theorem 12) and NCTD(B) ≤5 (Theorem 13). Employing geometric arguments, we prove that, if B is a set of
closed neighborhoods in a unit square graph G, then NCTD+(B) ≤4 (Theorem 14). See Table 1 for a summary of
our results and the literature.

Improved Algorithmic Upper and Lower Bounds
In this section, we first prove an algorithmic lower bound for N-NCTD that is stronger than the previous one from the
literature, and then tight algorithmic bounds for N-NCTD+. We begin by introducing problem-specific terminology.
Let G be a graph, B a set of closed neighborhoods in G, and T a teaching map for B. If, for all B ∈B, T(B) ⊆B,
then T is a positive teaching map for B. In either case, T is non-clashing for B if, for all distinct N[u], N[v] ∈B,
there exists w ∈T(N[u]) ∪T(N[v]) such that w ∈N[u] ∪N[v] and w /∈N[u] ∩N[v]; we say that w distinguishes
N[u] and N[v]. For all n ∈Z+, we set [n] := {1, . . . , n}.
From the exact exponential algorithm for NON-CLASH in [GKMR25], it can be observed that N-NCTD can be
solved in 2O(k·|V (G)|·log |V (G)|) time. In the next theorem, we provide an improved lower bound, via a reduction from
3-SAT, that almost matches this upper bound. However, we first need the following lemma, which will be useful for
the proof of that theorem. Note that, given a graph G, two distinct vertices u, v ∈V (G) are false twins if and only if
N(u) = N(v).

T of size 1 for B, there exists i ∈[4] such that T(N[ui]) = {ui}.
Proof. If no NCTM of size 1 for B exists, then the statement trivially holds. So, let T be an NCTM of size 1 for B.
When only considering N[u1], . . . , N[u4] from B, for any i ∈[4] and v ∈T(N[ui]),
• if v = ui, then v only distinguishes N[ui] and N[uj] for all j ∈[4] \ {i};
• if v = uj for some j ∈[4] \ {i}, then v only distinguishes N[ui] and N[uj];
• if v /∈{u1, . . . , u4}, then v does not distinguish N[ui] and N[uj] for any j ∈[4] \ {i}.
Toward a contradiction, suppose that T(N[ui]) ̸= {ui} for all i ∈[4]. By the above analysis, for all i ∈[4], the sole
vertex in T(N[ui]) distinguishes N[ui] and at most one other closed neighborhood in {N[u1], . . . , N[u4]} \ {N[ui]}.
As there are 4(3)/2 = 6 such pairs that need to be distinguished, T does not satisfy the non-clashing condition for
some pair in {N[u1], . . . , N[u4]}, a contradiction. Thus, there exists i ∈[4] such that T(N[ui]) = {ui}.

Thus, w.l.o.g., for all i ∈[q −1], let ui ∈T(N[ui]). By the above analysis, the teaching sets of N[uq], . . . , N[uℓ]
combined distinguish at most (ℓ−q + 1)k pairs in {N[uq], . . . , N[uℓ]}. Note that (ℓ−q + 1)(ℓ−q)/2 such pairs
must be distinguished and
(ℓ−q + 1)(ℓ−q)/2 > (ℓ−q + 1)k ⇐⇒(ℓ−q)/2 > k ⇐⇒ℓ> q + 2k.
Thus, since ℓ> q + 2k, T does not distinguish a pair in {N[uq], . . . , N[uℓ]}, a contradiction.
Lemma 10. Reduction rule 2 is safe for N-NCTD.
Proof. Let Q ⊆I be a set of q + 2k + 1 pairwise false twins, and let T be an NCTM of size at most k for B. By
Lemma 9, there exist u, v ∈Q such that u ∈T(N[u]), v ∈T(N[v]), T(N[u]) ∩X = T(N[v]) ∩X, T(N[u]) ∩(Q \
{u}) = ∅if and only if T(N[v]) ∩(Q \ {v}) = ∅, and T(N[u]) ∩S = ∅if and only if T(N[v]) ∩S = ∅for any
equivalence class S ̸= Q of I with respect to X. Select any vertex w ∈V (G) \ {v} such that v ∈T(N[w]). We show
that removing v from T(N[w]) and adding z ∈Q to T(N[w]) maintains that T is an NCTM of size at most k for B
in G. Specifically, if u /∈T(N[w]), then z = u. Otherwise, z is any vertex in Q \ {u, v} such that z /∈T(N[w]) (if
Q \ {v} ⊆T(N[w]), then no vertex is added to T(N[w])).
Let T ′ be the teaching map for B obtained from T by applying the above procedure for all w ∈V (G) \ {v} such
that v ∈T(N[w]). Note that T ′ has size at most k. We show that T ′ is an NCTM for B. Toward a contradiction,
suppose there exist x, y ∈V (G) such that T ′ is not non-clashing for N[x] and N[y]. Due to how T ′ was obtained,
and as T is an NCTM for B while T ′ is not, then v is the only vertex in T(N[x]) ∪T(N[y]) distinguishing N[x] and
N[y]. So, u ∈T ′(N[x]) ∪T ′(N[y]) and, w.l.o.g., v ∈N[x] and v /∈N[y]. We do a case analysis (see Fig. 7).
Case 1: x = v. Here, v ∈T ′(N[x]) distinguishes N[x] and N[y], a contradiction.
Case 2: x ∈N(v) and y ̸= u. As v ∈N[x], v /∈N[y], u and v are false twins, and y ̸= u, then u ∈N[x] and
u /∈N[y]. So, u ∈T ′(N[x]) ∪T ′(N[y]) distinguishes N[x] and N[y], a contradiction.
Case 3: x ∈N(v) and y = u. As in Case 2, it must be that u ∈N[x]. Since v ∈N[x], there exists r ∈
(T(N[x]) ∪T(N[v])) \ {v} that distinguishes N[x] and N[v]. We now study subcases.
Case 3.1: r ∈T(N[v]) ∩X. Here, r ∈T ′(N[u]) ∩X distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a contradiction.
Case 3.2: r ∈T(N[v]) ∩S for an equivalence class S of I with respect to X. If r = u, then S = Q and there
exists z ∈(T ′(N[u]) ∩Q) \ {u, v} that distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a contradiction. Otherwise, there is r′ ∈
(T ′(N[u]) ∩S) \ {u, v} that distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a contradiction.
Case 3.3: r ∈T(N[x]). In this case, r ∈T ′(N[x]). If r ̸= u, then r ∈T ′(N[x]) distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a
contradiction. Otherwise, r = u. Since we have dealt with all the other cases, we can further assume that r = u is the
only vertex in (T(N[x]) ∪T(N[v])) \ {v} that distinguishes N[x] and N[v]. Due to this property of r and the fact
that T satisfies the non-clashing condition for N[x] and N[u], either v ∈T(N[x]) or some vertex r′ ∈T(N[u]) \ {u}
distinguishes N[x] and N[u]. In the former case, as u, v ∈T(N[x]), there exists z ∈(T ′(N[x]) ∩Q) \ {u, v} that
distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a contradiction. In the latter case, if r′ ̸= v, then r′ is also in T ′(N[u]), and so, it
distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a contradiction. Otherwise, r′ = v, and hence, there exists z ∈(T ′(N[x]) ∩Q) \ {u, v}
that distinguishes N[x] and N[u], a contradiction.
That completes the case analysis, and thus, T ′ is an NCTM for B. Let G′ := G \ {v} and let B′ equal B restricted
to G′. Since the only teaching set of T ′ containing v is T(N[v]), then T ′ restricted to B′ is an NCTM of size at most
k for B′ in G′. This completes the first direction of the proof.

Combinatorial Upper Bounds for Graph Classes
We finish by providing upper bounds on the (positive) non-clashing teaching dimension for closed neighborhoods B of
a graph G when G is restricted to planar or unit square graphs. Note that upper bounds for any B hold for any B′ ⊆B,
and VCD(B) ≤4 in both cases [DKP24].
Theorem 12. For any planar graph G, if B = {N[v] | v ∈V (G)}, then NCTD+(B) ≤7.
Proof. We construct a positive NCTM T of size at most 7 for B. For all v ∈V (G), if d(v) ≤6, then set T(N[v]) :=
N[v]. Otherwise, for each v ∈V (G) with d(v) ≥7, place any 3 neighbors of v in T(N[v]). Consider any one such
vertex v and let v1, v2, v3 be its 3 neighbors in T(N[v]). Since G is planar, there is at most one other vertex u ∈V (G)
such that v1, v2, v3 ∈N(u), as otherwise there would be a K3,3. Hence, T satisfies the non-clashing condition for
N[v] and N[x] for all x ∈V (G) \ {v, u, v1, v2, v3} if u exists, and for all x ∈V (G) \ {v, v1, v2, v3} otherwise.
As long as there exists a pair N[v], N[y] ∈B such that d(v) ≥7, N[v] ̸= N[y], and T does not currently satisfy
the non-clashing condition for the pair, then there exists w ∈(N[v] \ N[y]) ∪(N[y] \ N[v]) that will be added to
T(N[v]) or T(N[y]) to distinguish the pair. Specifically, if w ∈N[v] \ N[y], then add w to T(N[v]), and otherwise,
add w ∈N[y] \ N[v] to T(N[y]). In either case, T then satisfies the non-clashing condition for N[v] and N[y]. Once
this process is completed, T is a positive NCTM for B as it is non-clashing for N[v] and N[y] for any vertex v with
d(v) ≥7, but also for the closed neighborhoods of any two vertices of degree at most 6 since their teaching sets
coincide with their closed neighborhoods in this case. For each v ∈V (G) with d(v) ≥7, as there were at most 4
possibilities for y (the vertices corresponding to u, v1 , v2, and v3 above), then at most 4 vertices were added to any
teaching set during this process. Thus, T has size at most 7.

Conclusion
We studied non-clashing teaching for closed neighborhoods in graphs, notably providing highly non-trivial FPT al-
gorithms and strong algorithmic lower bounds. Several open directions remain for future work, such as exploring
the complexity with respect to treewidth or planarity for N-NCTD+ and N-NCTD, as well as vertex integrity and
treedepth for N-NCTD. However, like NON-CLASH [GKMR25], we expect that they are W[1]-hard parameterized
by the treewidth. It is also important to determine whether Theorem 13 is tight, since the balls of a planar graph has
been mentioned [CCMR24] as a potential concept class C negatively answering the question of [KSZ19, FKS+23],
i.e., such that NCTD(C) > VCD(C).
