Joel Rybicki · joel.rybicki@hu-berlin.de · Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin

Abstract. We investigate space-time trade-offs for population protocols in sparse
interaction graphs. In complete interaction graphs, optimal space-time trade-offs are
known for the leader election and exact majority problems. However, it has remained
open if other graph families exhibit similar space-time complexity trade-offs, as existing
lower bound techniques do not extend beyond highly dense graphs.
In this work, we show that – unlike in complete graphs – population protocols on
bounded-degree trees do not exhibit significant asymptotic space-time trade-offs for
leader election and exact majority. For these problems, we give constant-space protocols
that have near-optimal worst-case expected stabilisation time. These new protocols
achieve a linear speed-up compared to the state-of-the-art.
Our results are based on two novel protocols, which we believe are of independent
interest. First, we give a new fast self-stabilising 2-hop colouring protocol for general
interaction graphs, whose stabilisation time we bound using a stochastic drift argument.
Second, we give a self-stabilising tree orientation algorithm that builds a rooted tree in
optimal time on any tree. As a consequence, we can use simple constant-state protocols
designed for directed trees to solve leader election and exact majority fast. For example,
we show that “directed” annihilation dynamics solve exact majority in O(n2 log n) steps
on directed trees.

Introduction
Population protocols are a model of asynchronous, distributed computation in systems with anony-
mous, identical and computationally-restricted agents [5, 7]. In this model, the agents reside on the
nodes of a connected graph G = (V, E) with n nodes. The computation is controlled by a scheduler
that, in each time step, selects an edge {u, v} ∈E uniformly at random; the two nodes u and v then
interact by reading each other’s current states and update their local state.
The time complexity is the expected number of global interactions required to reach a configuration
with correct and stable outputs that cannot change no matter which interactions occur in the future.
The space complexity of a protocol is measured by the number of distinct states an agent can take.
Space-time complexity thresholds on cliques.
The past decade has seen a focused research
program investigating space-time complexity trade-offs for population protocols on complete graphs [1,
2, 13, 14, 19, 20, 30]. This research has identified problems that exhibit asymptotic complexity
thresholds: protocols with space complexity below the threshold are a factor ˜Θ(n) slower than
time-optimal protocols, and conversely, optimal stabilisation time can be achieved by protocols
that use number of states above this threshold. There are two prototypical problems with such
complexity thresholds:
– For leader election, the threshold is at Θ(log log n) states: the problem can be solved in optimal
Θ(n log n) time with Θ(log log n) states [13, 29]. Any o(log log n)-state protocol requires ˜Ω(n2)
time [1] and a simple constant-state protocol solves the problem in Θ(n2) time [19].
– For exact majority, the threshold is widely conjectured to be at Θ(log n) states. Alistarh et
al. [2] showed that time-optimal protocols satisfying certain technical “output-dominance” and
“monotonicity” properties – which are satisfied by all known clique protocols – need Ω(log n)
states [2]. This is matched by the time-optimal protocol of Doty et al. [20] that uses Θ(log n)
states and Θ(n log n) time. A constant-state protocol solves the problem in Θ(n2) time [21, 26].
Graphical population protocols.
Recent work has started to extend the study of space-time
complexity trade-offs from cliques to general interaction graphs [3, 4, 28, 33, 34]. On general
graphs, time and space complexity typically depend on the expansion properties of the interaction
graph. So far, all state-of-the-art upper bounds for general graphs parallel those in the clique.
Leader election [4, 34] and exact majority [28] protocols that use super-constant number (typically
polylogarithmic in n) of states are a factor ˜Θ(n) faster than known constant-state protocols.
However, it is not known whether super-constant number of states is necessary to attain such
near-linear speed-up in sparse and/or high-diameter graphs. The main obstacle is that the surgery
technique, which is a key ingredient for proving space-time trade-offs in complete graphs [1, 2, 19],
does not easily generalise to other graph families – with the exception of dense random graphs [4].
Indeed, Alistarh et al. [4] pointed out that in star graphs a leader can be elected in constant
time and space, because the underlying graph helps break symmetry fast. This simple example rules
out that space-time trade-offs must hold in all graphs. Nevertheless, they showed that the surgery
technique sometimes remains applicable outside of cliques by proving that constant-state leader
election requires ˜Θ(n2) expected steps in dense random graphs (with constant expected diameter).
1.1
Our focus: space-time trade-offs in sparse graphs
In this work, we move away from highly dense graphs to study the other extreme of highly sparse
interaction graphs, namely trees. For bounded-degree trees, the state-of-the-art exhibits a ˜Ω(n) factor

difference between the state-of-the-art stabilisation times of constant-state and super-constant-state
protocols for both leader election [4, 9] and exact majority [21, 28].
For example, on paths, the known constant-state protocols for leader election and exact majority

So far, it is not clear if we are simply missing the right lower bound techniques for proving
space-time trade-offs in sparse graphs or if such graphs exhibit fundamentally different behaviour.
In the light of the above, we investigate the following open question raised in prior work [4, 28]:
Do protocols in sparse interaction graphs exhibit significant space-time trade-offs?
1.2
Population protocols on graphs
Let G = (V, E) be a connected, undirected graph with n nodes and m edges. The distance between
two nodes u and v is dist(u, v). We write D for the diameter and ∆for the maximum degree of G.
Population protocols.
Let G be a class of graphs. Formally, a probabilistic population protocol
on G consists of a finite set Λ of states, a state transition function Ξ: Λ × [0, 1] × Λ × [0, 1] →Λ × Λ,

the set Λ can depend on the graph class, but not on the specific input graph G. For example, on
bounded-degree graphs, the protocol can depend on the bound on the maximum degree ∆.

is a map x: V →Λ. An execution of the protocol under the schedule σ is a sequence (xt)t≥0 of

1.3
Our contributions
We give near-time optimal constant-state protocols for exact majority and leader election in bounded-
degree trees. This gives the first example of a non-trivial class of graphs, where (non-self-stabilising)
population protocols do not exhibit significant, asymptotic space-time trade-offs in terms of n. On
the way to obtain these results, we give new protocols we believe are of independent interest:
(1) A fast self-stabilising 2-hop colouring protocol for general graphs.
(2) A time-optimal, self-stabilising protocol that orients 2-hop coloured trees.
These two technical ingredients allows us to orient an undirected tree fast. We then show that
oriented trees admit fast, constant-state protocols for leader election and exact majority.
We note that while we mostly focus on bounded-degree trees, i.e., the case ∆∈O(1), the exact
majority and leader election protocols work in fact for any undirected tree – however, the space
complexity is of order 2poly(∆), and similarly, the time complexities hide an additional ∆-factor.
1.3.1
Ingredient 1: Fast self-stabilising 2-hop colouring
Our first ingredient is a new protocol for self-stabilising 2-hop colouring in general graphs with
maximum degree ∆. A 2-hop colouring of a graph G = (V, E) is a function f : V →{1, . . . , χ},
where f(u) ̸= f(v) for all u, v ∈V such that u and v share a neighbour. That is, for any node
v ∈V , the neighbours of node v must have distinct colours. A 2-hop colouring allows the nodes to
distinguish their neighbours, and in a sense, it gives a “port-numbering” in the population protocol
model; see Figure 1b. Note that in a 2-hop colouring a node may have the same colour as (at most)
one of its neighbours.
Theorem 1. There is a self-stabilising 2O(∆2)-state protocol that stabilises on any graph to a 2-hop
colouring with O(∆2) colours in O(∆m log n) time steps in expectation and with high probability.
Our protocol is inspired by the 2-hop colouring algorithm of Angluin, Aspnes, Fischer and
Jiang [6]. They showed that their protocol always stabilises, but they did not analyse its time
complexity under the stochastic scheduler.

In Section 2, we design a new protocol that randomly samples colors when it detects a conflict.
Similarly to all existing population protocols for 2-hop colouring [6, 24, 31], ours also uses “stamps”
to detect if the condition for the 2-hop colouring is violated. However, our protocol comes with some
additional tweaks – namely, a simple stamp clearing mechanism – to speed-up its stabilisation.
For the time complexity analysis, we use a “block-wise” stochastic drift argument over sufficiently
long time intervals. This gives us enough control on the drift and allows us to obtain a fairly good
bound on the stabilisation time. Our protocol is time-optimal up to the ∆-factor: there is no protocol
that stabilises in o(m log n) expected steps, as we show in Section 2.4. Here, the main challenge to
carry out the drift analysis is to keep track of different types of conflicts so that they can be used to
bound the drift; naïve analysis easily leads to unnecessary polynomial factors in ∆. In order to run
this protocol, we assume nodes have access to O(log ∆) random bits per interaction to sample a new
colour from a palette of size O(∆2).
1.3.2
Ingredient 2: Time-optimal self-stabilising tree orientation
We give a self-stabilising protocol that orients any 2-hop coloured diameter-D tree in optimal
Θ(max{D, log n} · n) time with high probability. An orientation of a tree assigns a direction for
each edge such that all edges point towards a single root node v ∈V .
In our protocol, each node maintains pointers to its parent and children using the 2-hop colouring.
Prior to stabilisation, neighbouring nodes may have inconsistent state variables, and in such cases,
the orientation of the corresponding edge is ill-defined. These inconsistencies are resolved upon each
interaction by updating the pointers to correctly orient the edge. To do this, it is important to use
a suitable local rule ensuring that “badly” oriented edges are rapidly pushed out of the tree. This
mechanism is formalised in Section 3.

2-hop coloured with at most χ colours in O(n · max{D, log n}) steps in expectation and with high

an orientation algorithm, where the root knows it is the root would solve self-stabilising leader
election on trees. We show in Section 6.2 that our orientation protocol is essentially time-optimal by
extending the lower bound technique of Alistarh et al. [4] to trees.
Theorem 4. There is no tree orientation protocol that stabilises in o(Dn) expected steps on trees.

Application 1: Optimal leader election for bounded-degree trees
The tree orientation protocol allows us to easily construct a fast non-self-stabilising stable leader
election protocol. In the stable leader election problem, as input we get a nonempty subset of

Corollary 7. On bounded-degree trees, there is a constant-state protocol that solves leader election

1.3.4
Application 2: Fast space-efficient exact majority for trees
Finally, we consider the exact majority task on oriented trees. In this task, each node v receives an
input bit f(v) ∈{0, 1}, and the task is to reach consensus on the bit that was given as input to the
majority of the nodes. In case of ties, consensus on an arbitrary bit should be reached. On paths,
the existing protocols for exact majority use ˜Ω(n3) time [21, 28].
We give a new protocol that stabilises in only O(n2 log n) time on any tree, and on bounded-degree
trees, uses only constant space. Specifically, we consider a “directed” variant of the two-species
annihilation dynamics recently studied by Rybicki et al. [28]. Each node starts with a token A or B,
depending on its initial input bit. When an A-token and a B-token meet, they both get removed,
leaving behind two “empty” tokens of type C. The tokens of type A and B are always pushed towards
the root (so that the “empty” tokens move away from the root). Figure 1e illustrates an example
configuration of this process.
We show that the directed annihilation dynamics stabilise in O(n2 log n) steps into a configuration
where only the majority input token remains (or in the case of a tie, only C-tokens are left). In case
of a majority, the root will hold one of the remaining input tokens. The output is then propagated
from the root to all nodes by having each node with a C-token copy the output of its parent. This is
explained in more detail in Section 5.
Theorem 8. There is a self-stabilising exact majority protocol for trees that uses 2O(∆4) states and
stabilises in O(n2 log n) steps in expectation and with high probability.
For paths, this is optimal up to the log n factor due to the recent Ω(Dm) time lower bound on
general graphs [28]. While our protocol is simple, its analysis requires care. A naïve coupon collector
analysis yields a bound of O(n3 log n) steps. However, to get to the worst-case optimal Θ(n2 log n)
steps, a more careful analysis is needed to take into account “congestion” that may happen with the
process: tokens of the same type block each other from propagating towards the root.
Our technique for analysing the directed annihilation dynamics on trees is inspired by the analysis
of the fun-sort algorithm of Biedl et al. [16]. The fun-sort algorithm corresponds to a simpler process
with only two token types on a path, where the tokens do not annihilate each other, but rather
traverse in the opposite directions on a path.

1.3.5
Further example applications
The self-stabilising tree orientation algorithm can be used to solve also other problems, where
information needs to be aggregated to the root and/or information needs to be spread from the root.
Here we sketch two simple example applications. The time-optimality of both protocols follow from
standard arguments and a straightforward application of our lower bound lemma in Section 6.1.
Time-optimal self-stabilising 2-colouring of bounded-degree trees.
Given the tree orienta-
tion protocol, it is easy to design a self-stabilising 2-colouring algorithm for bounded-degree trees
that is asymptotically time optimal: each node stores an output bit and whenever they interact with
their parent, their copy and flip the output bit of their parent. The time complexity follows from
the combination of Corollary 3 and the time for the parent to broadcast its bit to all other nodes;
the latter is known to take Θ(Dn) steps in expectation and with high probability [4].
Exact population size counting in trees.
Counting the population size is a fundamental
problem in population protocols [8, 10, 12, 22]. In trees, we can now count the exact size of the
tree easily as follows: each node starts with a token “1”. The tokens are pushed towards the root.
When a token with value x and y meet, the parent’s token is replaced with the value x + y and the
child retains a token with value 0. In parallel, a broadcasting process is run, where the maximum
observed token value is broadcast by all the nodes. It is easy to check that even while the tree
orientation algorithm is stabilising, the sum of all token values remains n. After the tree has been
oriented, the counting protocol stabilises in Θ(max{D, log n} · n) steps in expectation and with high
probability [4]. The protocol uses O(n) additional states on top of the tree orientation algorithm.

Related work
Table 1 summarises our results and existing prior work. We note that for leader election and exact
majority, the prior protocols work in general connected graphs rather than just trees; the table
reports the resulting bounds for trees. Below we compare our results to prior work in more detail.
Self-stabilising 2-hop colouring.
Angluin et al. [6] were the first to describe population protocols
for 2-hop colouring. Our algorithm is inspired by their approach, where each agent memorises a
one-bit stamp for each of the Θ(∆2) colours; this results in 2O(∆2) space complexity. However, they
do not provide time complexity bounds under the stochastic scheduler. Indeed, establishing such
bounds seems like a challenging task, and we suspect that their protocol is significantly slower than
ours in the absence of any “stamp clearing” mechanism.
In constrast, the randomised protocol by Sudo et al. [31] uses only a single stamp per node.
However, it relies on a large colour palette with a dependency on N, where N is a known upper
bound on n, leading to a Θ(N4) state complexity. They show that their protocol stabilises in
O(mn2 log n) time. Recently, Kanaya et al. [24] obtained a better time complexity of O(mn) steps
by using even more states: their randomized algorithm employs a colour palette of size Θ(N3∆2)
and requires memorising ∆colours during the execution, resulting in NO(∆) states. Their slower,
but deterministic, algorithm requires 2O(N) states and is not mentioned in Table 1. Compared to
these results, when ∆is small, our new protocol achieves significantly better state complexity and
stabilisation time with a smaller colour palette of size O(∆2).

stabilisation time of the 2-hop colouring protocol. We note that Bertschinger et al. [15] also used

Tree orientation.
We are not aware that tree orientation problem has been previously studied in
the context of population protocols. However, we note that Angluin et al. [6] gave a self-stabilising
protocol that finds a spanning tree in 2-hop coloured regular graphs of diameter D using poly(D)
states. Thus, our protocols for directed trees could be also applied in more general graphs by running
them in the resulting spanning tree, with the additional poly(D) state complexity overhead.
Leader election on graphs.
Chen and Chen [17] gave a constant-space self-stabilising leader
election protocol for cycles with an exponential stabilisation time. Later, they gave a poly(∆)-state
protocol for general ∆-regular graphs [18]. Yokota et al. [33] gave a time-optimal algorithm for
self-stabilising leader election on cycles that stabilises in Θ(n2) steps and uses O(n) states. Recently,
Yokota et al. [34] gave a near-time-optimal algorithm that stabilises in O(n2 log n) steps and uses
only polylog n states.
Unlike for regular graphs, Angluin et al. [6] showed that self-stabilising leader election cannot be
solved in non-simple graph classes. A class of graphs G is non-simple if it contains a graph with two
disjoint subgraphs that are also contained in G. For example, trees are non-simple. Our protocol for

trees is “almost” self-stabilising in the sense that as long as the initial configuration contains at least
one leader candidate, then a leader is elected (but otherwise the initial states may be arbitrary).
Beauquier, Blanchard and Burman [9] gave a (non-self-stabilising) constant-state leader election
protocol that stabilises in any connected graphs. Using hitting time arguments, this protocol can be
shown to stabilise in O(n4 log n) time on any graph and in O(n3 log n) time on regular graphs [4, 32].
For trees, the protocol stabilises in O(Dn2 log n) time, as the hitting time of the population random
walk in a diameter-D tree is O(Dn2). The (non-self-stabilising) leader election protocol of Alistarh
et al. [4] uses O(log2 n) states in stabilises in O((D + log n)m log n) steps on any graph, and on
graphs with edge expansion β > 0, the protocol stabilises in O(m/β log2 n) time. For trees, their
protocol achieves O((D + log n)n log n) stabilisation time. In comparison, our protocol is strictly
better in terms of space and time complexity when ∆is small, and stricly worse when ∆is large,
e.g., ∆≫√log log n.
Exact majority on graphs.
There is a simple 4-state protocol that solves exact majority on
any connected graph [11, 21, 26]. Mertzios et al. [26] showed that there is no always-correct 3-state
protocol. Draief and Vojnović [21] studied the stabilisation time of the 4-state protocol in terms
of the spectral properties of the interaction graph, and recently, Rybicki et al. [28] extended their
analysis to give a bound of O((τ/γ) · log n) expected steps, where τ is the relaxation time of the
population random walk and 1/n < γ < 1 is the normalised bias of the two input values.
In trees, the relaxation time τ of the population random walk can range from O(n) (e.g., stars) to
Θ(n3) (e.g., paths). However, plugging in the worst-case values for τ and γ, leads to too pessimistic
bounds. Again, with simple hitting time arguments, such as those used by Sudo et al. [32] and
Alistarh et al. [4], one can show that the 4-state protocol stabilises in O(Dn2 log n) steps in any
diameter-D tree.
Rybicki et al. [28] also gave an O(log n · (log(∆/δ) + log(τ/n))-state protocol that stabilises in
O((τ∆/δ) log2 n) steps, where δ is the minimum and ∆is the maximum degree of the input graph.
The protocol performs well on almost-regular graphs with good expansion. However, in worst-case
graphs, such as lollipop graphs, relaxation time τ can be Ω(n4), making the protocol slow. Moreover,
the clocking mechanism performs poorly in graphs where ∆/δ is large. On trees of diameter D,
one can use the bound τ ∈O(Dn2) to get a running time bound of O(∆Dn2 log2 n) = O(n4 log2 n).
However, we suspect that this bound could be improved by a Θ(n)-factor with a more careful analysis
of the underlying cancellation-doubling dynamics on undirected trees.
In contrast, our new majority protocol trees stabilises in O(n2 log n) steps on any tree. In

colouring algorithm of Angluin et al. [6] that uses stamping to detect conflicts. However, to ensure
fast stabilisation under the stochastic scheduler, our algorithm is slightly different in construction and
assumes that nodes can sample O(log ∆) i.i.d. random bits per interaction. We prove the following:

Conflict edges and recolouring steps.
For the analysis, we need a few additional definitions.
We write N(v) for the neighbourhood of v. A path (u, v, w) of length 2 is a c-conflict path at time
t if colourt(u) = colourt(w) = c. We say that an edge e = {u, v} has a colour conflict at time t
if there exists w ∈N(v) \ {u} such that colourt(w) = colourt(u). Colour conflicts correspond to
edges where the condition for having a 2-hop colouring is not satisfied (i.e., one node has at least
two neighbors with the same colour). In particular, an edge with a colour conflict lies on at least
one conflict path; see Figure 2 for an illustration.
If the edge et sampled at time t has a stamp conflict, then the 2-hop colouring algorithm triggers
a recolouring, i.e., it enters the ‘if’ statement and changes the colour of both activated nodes. We
call such steps recolouring steps and write Rt to denote the event: “Time step t is a recolouring step”.
We say that e is a conflict edge at time t if it has a colour conflict or a stamp conflict at time t.
Note that a stamp conflict does not imply a colour conflict, and vice versa. A recolouring step can
only happen when an edge with a stamp conflict is sampled. Note also that after a recolouring step,
all the stamps of u and v are cleared to the value ⊥. However, after a recolouring step, we may still
have that the colours of u and v remain the same.
We will analyse the stabilisation time of the protocol using stochastic drift analysis on the
expected number of conflict edges. First, we analyse the effect of a single interaction step, and then
we consider how the number of conflict edges decreases in a sequence of m interaction steps.

The exact majority algorithm
We set out to prove Theorem 22. We now assume that G be an oriented tree with all edges pointing

The protocol will be self-stabilising in the following sense: no matter what initial configuration
the adversary chooses, the protocol will reach consensus. The consensus value will be A if token0(·)
has a strict majority of A at time 0, and B if there is a strict majority of B. Otherwise, the consensus
value can be arbitrary.

The majority algorithm for directed trees.
The protocol is as follows. When an edge oriented
from u to v is sampled, the nodes u and v perform the following in order:
(1) If {token(u), token(v)} = {A, B}, then both set their tokens to C.
(2) If token(u) ∈{A, B} and token(v) = C, then u and v swap tokens.
(3) If token(v) ̸= C, then v sets output(v) ←token(v).
(4) Finally, node u sets output(u) ←output(v).
The first two rules implement “directed” version of the annihilation dynamics recently studied by
Rybicki et al. [28]. Rule (1) dictates that A and B annihilate each other when meeting and Rule (2)
ensures that C-tokens are pushed towards the leaves and other tokens are pushed towards the root.
We can show that the directed version performs much faster than the undirected version on trees.
The last two rules only handle output propagation: once the protocol stabilises, the root will
hold either an A- or a B-token corresponding to the majority input. Any other node will either hold
a token of the same type as the root or a C-token. The rules (3)–(4) then propagate the token value
held by the root towards all the children.

Conclusions
In this paper, we described and analysed simple population protocols that can be combined to
solve several fundamental problems on trees: tree orientation, leader election, and exact majority.
These protocols are specifically designed for bounded-degree trees, on which they achieve optimal
stabilisation time while requiring only a constant number of states. However, they still work on any
tree, but the space and time complexity then grow with the maximum degree ∆of the tree.
As a key building block, we introduced a new population protocol for 2-hop coloring on arbitrary
graphs. We showed that on bounded-degree graphs the protocol outperforms the state-of-the-
art [24, 31], both in terms of time and space complexity. Indeed, on bounded-degree graphs, the
colouring protocol is asymptotically space and time optimal.
All of our algorithms are self-stabilising, with the exception of the leader election protocol, as
self-stabilising leader election is known to be impossible on trees. Moreover, our protocols require
knowledge only of ∆; in particular, they are uniform on bounded-degree graphs and trees.
A major consequence of our results is that there is no space-time trade-off for leader election in
the class of bounded-degree trees [6]. This distinguishes bounded-degree trees from complete graphs,
in which both leader election and exact majority exhibit space-time trade-offs in terms of the size n
of the population [1, 2, 13, 14, 19, 20, 30]. Moreover, our work shows that on paths, constant-state
exact majority protocols can achieve near-optimal time complexity of O(n2 log n) steps.
Open problems.
We conclude by raising a few interesting open problems for future work:
(1) Are there constant-state protocols that are time-optimal in all trees?
(2) Are there time-optimal tree orientation protocols that use fewer states for large ∆?
(3) Is there a constant-state protocol for exact majority that runs in O(Dn log n) time in trees?
