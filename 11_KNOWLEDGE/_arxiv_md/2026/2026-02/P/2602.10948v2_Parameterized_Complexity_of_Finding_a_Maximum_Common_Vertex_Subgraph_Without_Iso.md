Abstract
In this paper, we study the Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph problem: Given two input
graphs G1, G2 and a non-negative integer h, is there a common subgraph H on at least h vertices
such that there is no isolated vertex in H. In other words, each connected component of H has
at least 2 vertices. This problem naturally arises in graph theory along with other variants of
the well-studied Maximum Common Subgraph problem [28, 29, 32] and also has applications in
computational social choice [22, 23, 24]. We show that this problem is NP-hard and provide an FPT
algorithm when parameterized by h. Next, we conduct a study of the problem on common structural
parameters like vertex cover number, maximum degree, treedepth, pathwidth, and treewidth of one
or both input graphs. We derive a complete dichotomy of parameterized results for our problem
with respect to individual parameterizations as well as combinations of parameterizations from the
above structural parameters. This provides us with deep insight into the complexity theoretic and
parameterized landscape of this problem.

Introduction
The problem of finding a maximum common subgraph of two given graphs is well-studied in
algorithmic and structural graph theory. The most prominent variants include (i) Maximum
Common Induced Subgraph, asking to maximize the number of vertices of a common
induced subgraph, (ii) Maximum Common Edge Subgraph, asking to maximize the
number of edges of a common subgraph, and (iii) Maximum Common Induced Connected
Subgraph, asking to maximize the number of vertices in a common connected induced
subgraph. A similar natural problem is to ask for a common subgraph with the maximum
number of vertices for two input graphs G1 and G2. However, this problem admits a trivial
solution: a graph consisting of min(|V (G1)|, |V (G2)|)-many isolated vertices. Evidently, such
a trivial graph maximizes the number of vertices among all common subgraphs.
In this paper, we consider the next natural variant, the problem of Maximum Common
Vertex Subgraph, which asks to maximize the number of vertices in a common subgraph
of two input graphs, but where the common subgraph has no isolated vertices. Interestingly,
forbidding isolated vertices makes this problem significantly non-trivial, and in this paper we
show that Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph is NP-hard and boasts a rich landscape
in parameterized complexity. We study the parameterized complexity of Maximum Common
Vertex Subgraph under various choices of parameters: the size of the maximum common
subgraph, maximum degree, treewidth, pathwidth, treedepth, and vertex cover number.
Apart from being an interesting and natural problem in graph theory in its own right,
Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph finds its application in graphical variants of the
house allocation problem in computational social choice. We state the following motivation.
Let A be a set of agents and H be a set of houses. Let GA be a graph with A as the
vertex set, where edges depict some social relationship among the agents (e.g. friendship,
acquaintances, collaborators, etc). Similarly, let GH be a graph with H as the vertex set,
where edges depict some notion of ‘nearness’ (e.g. geographic, transit-accessible, etc). Each
agent a ∈A has to be allocated a unique house ϕ(a) ∈H. An agent a ∈A is happy if at least
one of the neighbours of a (in GA) is allocated to a neighbouring house (in GH) of ϕ(a). The
problem of Maximum Neighbour-Happy House Allocation asks to find an allocation
which maximizes the number of happy agents. Although being a natural extension to other
well-studied variants of graphical house allocation [22, 23, 24], the problem is identical to
the Maximum Common Vertex Subgraph problem on the graphs GA and GH.

Related Work
The Subgraph Isomorphism problem asks whether there exists an injective mapping
ϕ : V (H) →V (G) from a pattern graph H to a host graph G such that {u, v} ∈E(H)
implies {ϕ(u), ϕ(v)} ∈E(G). This problem is NP-hard [17]. In fact a trivial reduction from
Maximum Clique shows that the problem is W[1]-hard when the size of the pattern graph
is the parameter. Kann [28] observes that the problems of Maximum Common Induced
Subgraph, Maximum Common Edge Subgraph and Maximum Common Induced
Connected Subgraph generalize the Subgraph Isomorphism problem, and hence are
NP-hard. This work further studies the approximability of these problem. Maximum Com-
mon Induced Subgraph is shown to be at least as hard to approximate as Maximum
Clique. The Maximum Common Induced Connected Subgraph is NPO PB-complete.
Kann also related node- and edge-based formulations via L-reductions, establishing founda-
tional approximation and hardness results. Khanna et al. [29] studied the Largest Common
Subtree problem, which asks for the largest tree that appears as a common subgraph in
a collection of input trees. They showed that for bounded-degree trees, an approximation

remains NP-hard even for biconnected series–parallel graphs (partial 2-trees) where all but
one vertex have degree at most three. Hoffmann et al. [21] introduced k-less subgraph
isomorphism, where up to k vertices of the pattern graph may be deleted before embedding.
They developed weakened filtering invariants generalizing degree- and path-based constraints,
implemented in a constraint-programming style algorithm.
As we showed, our problem can be equivalently thought of as finding the largest common
star forest. Star-packing in graphs is very well-studied. Prieto and Sloper [34] studied
packing k vertex-disjoint copies of a graph H into G, focusing on the case where H is a
star, K1,s. For the special case of s = 2, i.e., H = K1,2, they provided a linear kernel and
an algorithm running in time O(25.301k · k2.5 + n3), improving upon the quadratic kernel
for the general packing of k copies of H = K1,s. Xi and Lin [37] investigated maximum P3
packing problem on claw-free cubic planar graphs, showing it to be NP-hard even under this
structural restriction. In their follow up work [38], they prove NP-hardness for K1,3-packing
on claw-free cubic graphs. They designed a linear-time approximation algorithm covering at
least (3n −8)/4 vertices, leveraging structural properties and local augmentation arguments.
Huang et al. [25] studied k+-star packing (i.e., packing of stars with at least k leaves) and
improved approximation guarantees, achieving an approximation ratio of 9/5 for k = 2 and
(k + 2)/2 for general k ⩾2 using a local search framework.

Our Contributions
We show that MCSFG is an NP-hard problem. In fact, we show that MCSFG is NP-hard
even when the input graphs are planar with maximum degree at most 3 (Theorem 19). In the
rest of the paper we provide the parameterized complexity landscape of MCSFG. We start
by studying the problem parameterized by the size of the common star forest. Theorem 21
shows that MCSFG admits an FPT algorithm in that case.
This is in stark contrast
with the problems Maximum Common Induced Subgraph, Maximum Common Edge
Subgraph and Maximum Common Induced Connected Subgraph being W[1]-hard as
they generalize Maximum Clique.
Next, we study MCSFG parameterized by various structural parameters of the input
graphs. In particular we consider the parameters of vertex cover number, treedepth, pathwidth
and treewidth. Such structural parameters are frequently bounded in graphs that appear
in real applications, and are exploited to push beyond the limits of otherwise intractable
problems [10]. For a graph G, it is well known [18] that tw(G) ⩽pw(G) ⩽td(G) −1 ⩽τ(G)
where G has treewidth tw(G), pathwidth pw(G), treedepth td(G) and vertex cover number
τ(G). Alongside these parameters, we also consider the maximum degree ∆(G) of the graph,
and explore all possible combinations of the parameter ∆(G) with the structural parameters
tw(G), pw(G), td(G), and τ(G).
First, when the parameter is the vertex cover number of one of the graphs, then MCSFG
is W[2]-hard (Theorem 23). In contrast, we show in Theorem 25 that MCSFG admits an
FPT algorithm when parameterized by the maximum vertex cover number of both input
graphs.
This motivates us to study the parameterized complexity of MCSFG with respect to
other structural parameters of both input graphs. We use a result due to Bodlaender et
al. [7] to show that MCSFG is para-NP-hard when parameterized by the treedepth of both
input graphs (Theorem 27). This immediately implies para-NP-hardness when parameterized
by pathwidth or treewidth of both input graphs.
The para-NP-hardness results of MCSFG when maximum degree of both graphs is the
parameter, and when structural parameters of both graphs is the parameter motivates us to
study the parameterized complexity when we look into a combination of these parameters.
Indeed, we show that MCSFG admits an FPT algorithm when parameterized by the combined
parameter of treedepth and maximum degree of both input graphs (Corollary 29). We show
this by first providing an FPT algorithm solving MCSFG parameterized by the maximum
size of the connected components across both graphs (Theorem 28).
Next, we consider parameterization by pathwidth and maximum degree of both input
graphs and show that MCSFG is W[1]-hard parameterized by the maximum degree of both
input graphs on forests of pathwidth at most 4 (Theorem 30). Again, this result implies
W[1]-hardness of MCSFG when parameterized by the maximum degree of both graphs, when
treewidth of both graphs is 1. Therefore, tractable algorithms with respect to pathwidth or
treewidth as parameters are only expected to exist when the maximum degree of at least one
of the graphs is constant. In Theorem 40, we provide an FPT algorithm to solve MCSFG
when parameterized by the maximum treewidth of both input graphs and the maximum
degree of one of the graphs is constant. As a consequence, the result also holds for the
parameter of pathwidth of both input graphs given that one of the graphs has constant
maximum degree. These structural results and their implications for MCSFG are listed in

Parameterized Complexity and Structural Parameters
In decision problems where the input has size n and is associated with a parameter k, the
objective in parameterized complexity is to design algorithms with running time f(k) · nO(1),
where f is a computable function that depends only on k [11]. Problems that admit such
algorithms are said to be fixed-parameter tractable (FPT). An algorithm with running time
f(k) · nO(1) is called an FPT algorithm, and the corresponding running time is referred to as
an FPT running time [10, 15].

2.3
Approximation Algorithms
An algorithm A for a problem P is said to be efficient if it solves all instances of the problem
P optimally in polynomial time. However, unless P = NP, we cannot hope to have efficient
algorithms for the class of problems belonging to NP-hard. A natural way out is to choose
any two from the three requirements. In the realm of approximation algorithms, we relax
the requirement of optimality [36].
▶Definition 11. An α-approximation algorithm for an optimization problem is a polynomial
time algorithm that for all instances of the problem produces a solution whose value is within
a factor of α of the value of an optimal solution.
We call α as the approximation ratio. Typically, for minimization problems, α > 1 and
for maximization problem, α < 1 [36].
▶Definition 12 (PTAS). A polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS) is a family

Theorem 30 also proves the W[1]-hardness of MCSFG parameterized by the maximum

Our approach is based on the observation that any common subgraph without isolated
vertices can be decomposed into a star forest, where each star has a centre vertex and
possibly several leaves. By first designing a dynamic programming algorithm on nice tree
decomposition for finding a maximum-cardinality star forest in a single graph in Lemma 38,
we extend it in Theorem 40 to compute a maximum common subgraph of two graphs. At a
high level, each DP-table entry at a bag encodes a partial star forest formed in the subgraph
induced by the vertices processed in the corresponding subtree of the decomposition. For
each vertex in the bag, we record its role: unused, a leaf (together with its centre), or a
centre (with its current number of attached leaves), thereby describing how it participates in
the partial solution. In addition, we maintain a star-count vector that stores how many stars
of each size have already been fully formed inside the subtree.
