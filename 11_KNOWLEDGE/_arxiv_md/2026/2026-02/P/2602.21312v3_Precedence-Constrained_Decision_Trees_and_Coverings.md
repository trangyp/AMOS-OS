Abstract
This work considers a number of optimization problems and reductive relations between them. The
two main problems we are interested in are the Optimal Decision Tree and Set Cover. We study these two
fundamental tasks under precedence constraints, that is, if a test (or set) X is a predecessor of Y , then
in any feasible decision tree X needs to be an ancestor of Y (or respectively, if Y is added to set cover,
then so must be X). For the Optimal Decision Tree we consider two optimization criteria: worst case
identification time (height of the tree) or the average identification time. Similarly, for the Set Cover we
study two cost measures: the size of the cover or the average cover time.
Our approach is to develop a number of algorithmic reductions, where an approximation algorithm
for one problem provides an approximation for another via a black-box usage of a procedure for the
former. En route we introduce other optimization problems either to complete the ‘reduction landscape’
or because they hold the essence of combinatorial structure of our problems. The latter is brought by
a problem of finding a maximum density precedence closed subfamily, where the density is defined
as the ratio of the number of items the family covers to its size. By doing so we provide O∗(√m)-
approximation algorithms for all of the aforementioned problems. The picture is complemented by a
number of hardness reductions that provide o(m1/12−ϵ)-inapproximability results for the decision tree
and covering problems. Besides giving a complete set of results for general precedence constraints, we
also provide polylogarithmic approximation guarantees for two most typically studied and applicable
precedence types, outforests and inforests. By providing corresponding hardness results, we show these
results to be tight.
Keywords: Optimal decision trees, Set Cover, Precedence Constraints, Approximation Algorithms

Introduction
Consider a set H of n hypotheses, a set T of m tests and an unknown target hypothesis h∗∈H that
needs to be discovered through testing. Each test t ∈T is a partition of H, that is, t consists of subsets of
H such that x ∩y = ∅for any x, y ∈t and S
x∈t x = H. As a result of executing a test t ∈T , questioner
receives a reply that reveals H ∈t such that h∗∈H. Upon receiving this reply, the questioner selects
another test t′ ∈T to perform next. Without formally stating an optimization criterion we refer to the above
as the Optimal Decision Tree (DT) problem. (For formal statements of our problems see Section 2).
In this work we generalize this process by considering precedence relation between tests given as an
arbitrary partial order (T , ⪯). A test t can be performed only if all its predecessors have been performed
previously (to which we refer as precedence-closed solution). On the applicability side, the problem in-
cludes numerous use cases, some of them in the area of biology Ben-Or and Hassidim [2008]. For example
hypotheses may represent possible conditions or diseases and tests may represent medical procedures. In
such case it may happen that some tests are less invasive or expensive than others, and should be performed
firstly. Additionally, it can also happen that a given test is a prerequisite for another test, e.g., a blood test
may be required before performing a biopsy. At last it might also be the case that a large test consists of
multiple stages, each with its own outcomes, and the later stages can be performed only after the earlier ones.

The aforementioned precedence constraints make the decision tree problem significantly more challeng-
ing. In a classical setup (without such constraints) the usual way of proceeding is to select at each step a test
that maximizes some measure of information gain (e.g., reduction in entropy or number of hypotheses pairs).
However, when precedence constraints are present, it may happen that the most informative test cannot be
chosen at the current step, as some of its predecessors have not been performed yet. To address this issue we
use a series of ‘reductions’ between other optimization problems i. e., using a particular algorithm for one
problem as a subroutine for the other. Particularly, we study set covering with precedence: We are given a

Related Work
Optimal Decision Tree
Optimal decision tree, also referred to as binary search trees Bose et al. [2020],
Demaine et al. [2007], is an extensively studied problem in computer science, starting with Garey and Gra-
ham [1974] in the 1970s. Since then it has gathered a lot of attention due to its numerous applications
including medical diagnosis, troubleshooting, active learning, and information retrieval. Usually two opti-
mization criteria are considered: the worst-case and the average-case cost. Both versions of the problem
are NP-Hard and cannot be approximated within an o(log n) factor Hyafil and Rivest [1976], Laber and
Nogueira [2004], Chakaravarthy et al. [2009], Cicalese et al. [2014b]. Moreover, this bound is tight and
several O(log n)-approximation algorithms are known for the average-case both with non-uniform proba-
bilities and test costs Kosaraju et al. [1999], Dasgupta [2004], Chakaravarthy et al. [2009], Guillory and
Bilmes [2009], Adler and Heeringa [2011], Chakaravarthy et al. [2011], Gupta et al. [2017], Cicalese et al.
[2014b], Navidi et al. [2020], Esfandiari et al. [2021], Cui and Nagarajan [2023] as well as the worst case
Arkin et al. [1998], Hanneke [2006], Cicalese et al. [2014b]. Few special cases are known to admit o(log n)-
approximation. For the average case, this includes an O(log n/ log log n)-approximation when tests have a
constant number of possible outcomes and all probabilities and costs are uniform Li et al. [2020]. Moreover
achieving any approximation factor above 9 is not NP-Hard assuming ETH Li et al. [2020]. For the worst
case, when the underlying search space is a partially ordered set with one maximum element and costs are
uniform an O(log n/ log log n)-approximation is known Dereniowski [2008].
Searching in Trees
The special case when the instance represents binary searching in a tree has also been
extensively studied. For uniform costs and the worst-case criterion, a linear time algorithms are known for

problem is known to be NP-Hard Jacobs et al. [2010] and a greedy strategy achieves 3/2-approximation
Jacobs et al. [2010], Cicalese et al. [2014a], Høgemo [2024]. For non-uniform costs, the vertex query model
generalizes edge query model which is known to be NP-Hard Dereniowski [2006], Cicalese et al. [2012,

Preliminaries and problem definitions
Partial orders and precedence constraints. Throughout this paper, we work with POSETs (X, ⪯), where
⪯is a reflexive, transitive, and antisymmetric binary relation. For elements x, y ∈X, we say that y is a
predecessor of x if y ⪯x, and that x is a successor of y. A subset S ⊆X is precedence-closed if for all
x ∈S and all y ∈X such that y ⪯x, it holds that y ∈S. A sequence (x1, x2, . . . , xk) of elements from
X is precedence-closed if for any xi and xj such that xi ⪯xj, we have i < j. We consider several special
types of partial orders: An inforest is a partial order in which every element has at most one successor. An
outforest is a partial order in which every element has at most one predecessor.
Decision trees. In decision tree problems, the goal is to design a strategy S understood as an adaptive
algorithm, that based on the previous replies, gives questioner the next test to be performed. This strategy
is typically represented as a decision tree, a rooted tree D in which internal nodes represent tests to be
performed, edges represent replies and each leaf represents a hypothesis. Formally, a decision tree is defined
recursively as follows. If r = {H1, . . . , Hl} ∈T is the first test performed by S, then r is the root of D.
For each reply Hi, i ∈{1, . . . , l}, take inductively defined decision tree Di derived from the tests done by
S when the reply to r is Hi. Then, D is obtained by making the roots of D1, . . . , Dl the children of r, and
the edge connecting r to the root of Di is labeled with the reply Hi. To complete the inductive definition, if
S is a trivial strategy that outputs the target, then the corresponding tree D is a leaf labeled with the target.
It should be remarked that it is possible for a test to appear multiple times in the decision tree. However,
with a slight abuse of notation we use tests as nodes of D whenever there is no ambiguity. When the tests
are subject to a precedence relation (T , ⪯), a decision tree D is precedence-closed if for every node v in D,
the set of tests on the path from the root to v forms a precedence-closed sequence so that a test t is executed
only if all its predecessors in (T , ⪯) have already been performed
Cost measures for decision trees. Let tests(D, v) denote the sequence of tests on the path from the root
to v in D including v. The cost of identifying a hypothesis h using a decision tree D is COST(D, h) =
|tests(D, v)|, where v is the leaf of D that represents identifying h as the target. We consider two cost
measures for decision trees: the worst-case cost COSTW (D) = maxh∈H COST(D, h) and the average-case
cost COSTA(D) = P
h∈H ·COST(D, h).

provide algorithms in Section 3. Throughout the paper we use I to denote instances of particular problems,
and OPT(I) denotes then the cost of an optimal solution on instance I.
Problem 1 (Precedence Constrained Worst Case Decision Tree (PCWCDT)). Given a set of hypotheses
H, a set of tests T and a precedence relation (T , ⪯) on tests, find a precedence-closed decision tree D that
minimizes the worst-case cost COSTW (D) = OPT(I).
Problem 2 (Precedence Constrained Average Case Decision Tree (PCACDT)). Given a set of hypotheses
H, a set of tests T and a precedence relation (T , ⪯) on tests, find a precedence-closed decision tree D that
minimizes the average-case cost COSTA(D) = OPT(I).
Additional notation for decision trees. Suppose that a decision tree strategy D performed some tests
T ⊆T . The set HT (D) ⊆H consists of all hypotheses h such that for each test t ∈T the reply is a set
Ut ∈t such that h ∈Ut. Note that HT (D) does not depend on the order of performing these tests. Likewise,
if v is any node of a decision tree D then Hv(D) = HT (D), where T = tests(D, v). We usually write HT
when D is clear from the context. For any node v of D, Dv is the subtree of D that consists of v and all its
descendants.
