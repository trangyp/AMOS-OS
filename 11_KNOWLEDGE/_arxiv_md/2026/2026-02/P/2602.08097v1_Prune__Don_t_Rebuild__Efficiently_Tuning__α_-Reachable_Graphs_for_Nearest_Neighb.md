Abstract
Vector similarity search is an essential primitive in modern AI and ML applications. Most vec-
tor databases adopt graph-based approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search algorithms, such as
DiskANN [11], which have demonstrated state-of-the-art empirical performance. DiskANN’s graph
construction is governed by a reachability parameter α, which gives a trade-off between construction time,
query time, and accuracy. However, adaptively tuning this trade-off typically requires rebuilding the index
for different α values, which is prohibitive at scale. In this work, we propose RP-TUNING, an efficient
post-hoc routine, based on DiskANN’s pruning step, to adjust the α parameter without reconstructing
the full index. Within the α-reachability framework of prior theoretical works [9, 8], we prove that
pruning an initially α-reachable graph with RP-TUNING preserves worst-case reachability guarantees in
general metrics and improved guarantees in Euclidean metrics. Empirically, we show that RP-TUNING
accelerates DiskANN tuning on four public datasets by up to 43× with negligible overhead.

Introduction
Vector similarity search is an essential primitive in modern AI and ML applications, such as retrieval
augmented generation [6], linking and matching for knowledge graph construction [18], and curating datasets
for model training and fine-tuning. Most vector database implementations adopt graph-based approximate-
nearest-neighbor (ANN) search algorithms. At scale, DiskANN [11] is widely used, e.g., in Milvus [17]
and Microsoft’s CosmosDB [16], both because of its state-of-the-art empirical performance and its ability to
scale the index structure beyond RAM (in contrast to more widely known algorithms like HNSW [13]). A
challenge with indices like DiskANN is that the tradeoffs between accuracy, latency, and space are not well
understood — requiring users to empirically evaluate the indices under different hyperparameter settings,
until the right tradeoffs are found.
To address the challenge, we propose RP-TUNING, an efficient post-hoc pruning algorithm derived from
the ROBUSTPRUNE subroutine used in the DiskANN index construction. RP-TUNING enables dynamic
adjustment of DiskANN’s reachability parameter, giving a tunable trade-off between accuracy, latency, and
index size. By starting from a high-quality base graph, it avoids the cost of repeated index reconstruction.
Leveraging recent theoretical frameworks for α-reachable graphs [9, 8], we provide a rigorous analysis
of RP-TUNING. Namely, we prove that a graph pruned via RP-TUNING preserves specific worst-case
reachability guarantees in general metrics while achieving superior worst-case bounds in Euclidean metrics.
Our empirical evaluation across four public datasets demonstrates that RP-TUNING accelerates the
DiskANN tuning process by up to 43×. Moreover, indices tuned with RP-TUNING exhibit performance
gains over those rebuilt with the same reachability parameters. Similarly to model distillation, our techniques
allow for the “distillation” of an index into different configurations, suitable for different hardware capabilities,
with predictable accuracy.

Preliminaries
We consider a dataset P in a metric space (X, D) where X is the universe of points and D is the distance
function. We use λ and ∆to denote the doubling dimension and the aspect ratio of P. A graph-based ANN
index for P is given by a directed graph G = (P, E); we use (a, b) to denote the one-way edge from a to b.
Note that we will abuse P to refer to the dataset and the vertex set of the graph, since in this paper, points in
the dataset have a one-to-one correspondence to vertices in the graph. We use Nout(a), Nin(a) to denote the
set of out-neighbors and in-neighbors, respectively, of point a ∈P when the graph in the context is clear.
We give a brief review of the DiskANN algorithm and its theoretical guarantees below. We refer readers
to [11, 9, 8] for full details.
2.1
DiskANN Algorithm Review
There are multiple variants of DiskANN [7, 10, 2]. We review two variants that are relevant to this paper: the
original Vamana heuristics proposed in [11] and the “slow preprocessing” variant formalized in [9].
The search algorithm is the same for both variants (see Algorithm 2). Given the index graph, the search is
initialized by seeding a candidate set L with a designated start vertex s (typically the medoid) and maintaining
an empty set of visited vertices V. In each iteration, the algorithm explores the graph by selecting the point
p∗from the unexplored candidates in L that is most proximal to the query q. By immediately adding the
out-neighbors Nout(p∗) to the candidate set L and marking p∗as visited, the algorithm effectively “walks”
through the graph’s edges toward the query. To ensure the search remains computationally tractable, the
candidate set L is restricted to a maximum size L; if the addition of neighbors causes the set to exceed this
bound, L is pruned to retain only the L points closest to q. This iterative process of greedy selection and
neighborhood expansion continues until no unvisited points remain in the search beam, at which point the k
best-found candidates are returned.
The index construction of both variants relies on the subroutine ROBUSTPRUNE (see Algorithm 1).
ROBUSTPRUNE prunes out-neighbor candidates for the input vertex p and utilizes a parameter α ≥1 to
favor “diverse” neighbors that provide long-range shortcuts across the dataset. The algorithm proceeds by
iteratively selecting the point p∗in the candidate set V that is closest to the target point p. Once a candidate is
added to Nout(p), a pruning condition is applied to all remaining candidates: any point p′ that is closer to the
newly added out-neighbor p∗than it is to the target point p by a factor of α (i.e., D(p∗, p′) < D(p, p′)/α) is
removed from the candidate set V. The intuition is that the edge (p, p′) is potentially not important if moving
from p to p* decreases the distance to p’ by a factor of α.
The “slow preprocessing” construction executes ROBUSTPRUNE(p, P \ {p}, α, n −1) for every vertex
p in the graph. Notice the candidate set V = P \ {p}, and the degree limit R = n −1, which results in an
O(n3) time complexity that is impractical at scale. However, it provides a rigorous structural foundation,
α-reachability, for analyzing the algorithm’s worst-case performance on construction time, query time, and
approximation factor.
Definition 2.1 (α-reachable and α-reachability). Let α > 1. We say, in a graph G = (P, E), q is α-
reachable from another vertex p if either (p, q) ∈E, or there exists p′ s.t. (p, p′) ∈E and D(p′, q) · α ≤
D(p, q). We say a graph G is α-reachable if any vertex in P is α-reachable from any other vertex in P.
Moreover, we define the reachability of a graph G as the maximum number α∗such that G is α∗-reachable.
Then, the following result on the “slow preprocessing” construction can be proved. Recall λ and ∆
denote the dataset’s doubling dimension and the aspect ratio.
Lemma 2.2 (Lemma 3.2 and 3.3 in [9]). Applying ROBUSTPRUNE(p, P \ {p}, α, ∞) for every p ∈P yields
an α-reachable graph with maximum degree O((4α)λ log ∆).

RP-TUNING and Worst-Case Guarantees
In this section, we propose RP-TUNING, a simple yet disciplined pruning algorithm based on ROBUSTPRUNE,
which efficiently prunes an α-reachable graph G into a sparser graph with a theoretical guarantee of worst-case
performance trade-off.
RP-TUNING
RP-TUNING takes an initially α1-reachable graph G = (P, E), and a new reachability target
α2 < α1 as inputs. It simply runs ROBUSTPRUNE(p, Nout(p), α2, ∞) for every p ∈P and outputs the
modified graph G′ = (P, E′).
We study the worst-case reachability of the output G′ of RP-TUNING(G, α2) where G is an α1-reachable
graph. It turns out that the worst-case reachability of G′ is a function of α1, α2 and depends on the metric
space and whether the pruning subroutine ROBUSTPRUNE sorts the out-neighbors. To distinguish the
unsorted version from ROBUSTPRUNE, we use UNSORTEDROBUSTPRUNE to denote the procedure that is
the same as ROBUSTPRUNE but only replaces the step “p∗←arg minp′∈V D(p, p′)” in Algorithm 1 with
“p∗being an arbitrary out-neighbor of p”. Our results on the reachability of G′ in different settings are

Readers may find an analogy in our results to [8], where Euclidean metrics and neighbor sorting guarantee
a better worst-case reachability. In our problem, neighbor sorting alone already admits a better reachability,
and it is further improved in Euclidean metrics. However, Euclidean metrics alone do not admit better

The reason why the reachability of the pruned graph G′ is crucial is as follows. First, the following
lemma in [9] can help us to bound the maximum degree of the pruned graph G′.
Lemma 3.3 (Lemma 3.3 in [9]). For any α > 1, graph G = (P, E), and p ∈P, applying ROBUSTPRUNE(p, Nout(p), α, ∞)
to every p ∈P guarantees that the maximum degree of the modified graph is O((4α)λ log ∆).
Using the above lemma, we know that the size of G′ is n · O((4α2)λ log ∆). The query time of G′ now
only depends on the number of steps during search, and the approximation of G′ can be quantified by its
reachability via Theorem 2.3, where the larger the reachability is, the smaller both the number of steps and
approximation are. Therefore, the rest of analysis on query time and approximation is reduced to exploring
the reachability of G′. We want it to be as large (or as close to α2) as possible.

Experimental Evaluation
We have shown theoretical guarantees of the α-reachable graphs pruned by RP-TUNING. In this section, we
test RP-TUNING on DiskANN indices to evaluate its real-world performance. We will use 100-recall@100,
a widely adopted metric in practice, to measure query accuracy. 100-Recall@100 is the proportion of the true
top 100 nearest neighbors contained in the top 100 results returned by the algorithm.
4.1
Setup
Hardware. We conduct all experiments on a cloud instance equipped with an AMD EPYC 9B14 CPU (15
cores, 30 threads) and 256GB RAM, running Debian GNU/Linux 12.
Datasets. We evaluate our methods on four benchmark datasets obtained from the BigANN benchmark
repository [14]. SIFT-1M and GIST-1M [12] are classical computer vision datasets; the former consists of 1
million 128-dimensional SIFT feature vectors with 10,000 query vectors, while the latter contains 1 million
960-dimensional GIST descriptors capturing global image structure, with 1,000 query vectors. Deep-1M [3]

All datasets use Euclidean distance as the similarity metric.

We implemented the original DiskANN (Vamana)1 in [11] in Python.

Related Work
Approximate nearest-neighbor index structures are core to every vector database system, and their importance
has motivated recent NeurIPS challenges [14, 15]. Early real-world systems leveraged the FAISS library [5],
often using its implementation of the Hierarchical Navigable Small Worlds [13] (HNSW) algorithm. However,
few theoretical properties are known about HNSW, and it targets scenarios where the entire index fits into
RAM. Other techniques more appropriate to disk-based storage, such as ANNOY [1] (based on random
hyperplane splits into memory-mapped files), and inverted-list-based methods, such as SPANN [4], are not
well understood theoretically. DiskANN [7, 11, 2] remains a commonly used, state-of-the-art method for
disk-based indexing. Its graph-based approach has been studied theoretically [9, 8]. Our work builds upon
the formal understanding of DiskANN, but focuses on leveraging the theoretical insights to obtain practical
index tuning.
