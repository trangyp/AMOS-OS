Abstract
A growing body of work attempts to evaluate the theory of mind (ToM)
abilities of humans and large language models (LLMs) using static, non-
interactive question-and-answer benchmarks. However, theoretical work
in the field suggests that first-personal interaction is a crucial part of ToM
and that such predictive, spectatorial tasks may fail to evaluate it. We
address this gap with a novel ToM task that requires an agent to persuade
a target to choose one of three policy proposals by strategically reveal-
ing information. Success depends on a persuader’s sensitivity to a given
target’s knowledge states (what the target knows about the policies) and
motivational states (how much the target values different outcomes). We
varied whether these states were REVEALED to persuaders or HIDDEN, in
which case persuaders had to inquire about or infer them. In Experiment 1,
participants persuaded a bot programmed to make only rational inferences.
LLMs excelled in the REVEALED condition but performed below chance in
the HIDDEN condition, suggesting difficulty with the multi-step planning
required to elicit and use mental state information. Humans performed
moderately well in both conditions, indicating an ability to engage such
planning. In Experiment 2, where a human target role-played the bot, and
in Experiment 3, where we measured whether human targets’ real beliefs
changed, LLMs outperformed human persuaders across all conditions.
These results suggest that effective persuasion can occur without explicit
ToM reasoning (e.g., through rhetorical strategies) and that LLMs excel at
this form of persuasion. Overall, our results caution against attributing
human-like ToM to LLMs while highlighting LLMs’ potential to influence
people’s beliefs and behavior.
Keywords: theory of mind; planning; large language models; persuasion;
causal reasoning; mentalizing; social interaction

Introduction
Planning with Theory of Mind
Theory of Mind (ToM) describes the ability of an agent to understand behaviors of other
agents in terms of underlying mental states. This ability plays a crucial role in human social
interaction. Impressed with LLM performance on various benchmark tests for ToM, some
researchers suggest that LLMs may exhibit human-like reasoning about mental states. This
suggestion raises two questions. First, what constitutes human-like ToM? And second, how
do we best investigate this capacity, in humans and LLMs alike?
ToM is often studied via behavioral prediction measures (Wimmer and Perner, 1983; Baron-
Cohen et al., 1985; Wellman and Liu, 2004; Peterson et al., 2012). Classic ToM tasks have
participants predict the outcome of a story given some initial information about a character’s
mental states. In the false belief task, a character, Sally, places her marble in a basket. Where
will Sally look for the marble if she put it in the basket but did not see it get moved to the
box (Wimmer and Perner, 1983; Baron-Cohen et al., 1985)? Success in this task only requires
predicting Sally’s behavior, not causally understanding how her beliefs generate her behavior;
a participant may succeed using only a heuristic such as ‘people look for things where they
last saw them’ without attributing any beliefs (Ho et al., 2022; Tamir and Thornton, 2018).
In addition, these tasks are spectatorial: participants predict the outcome of situations that
they are not themselves part of. This is the case for a range of common ToM tasks (Wimmer
and Perner, 1983; Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Happ´e, 1994; Wellman and Liu, 2004; Peterson
et al., 2012).
Human ToM is not exhaustively described by predictive and spectatorial functions. Classic
accounts, such as the theory-theory, emphasize that ToM also supports explanation and
understanding of behaviors through a causal model of the relationship between actions
and mental states (Gopnik and Wellman, 1992; Gopnik and Meltzoff, 1997; Gopnik and
Wellman, 2012). Moreover, many researchers highlight that the most natural context for
ToM is in active interaction with other agents (Hutto, 2012; Schilbach et al., 2013). More
recent work emphasizes planning and intervention as key elements of human ToM (Ho
et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024). A causal understanding of the mind allows us to plan and
carry out interventions on other people’s mental states, e.g. as in persuasion. After Ho et al.
(2022), we call this capacity Planning Theory of Mind (PToM).
PToM captures both a dimension of causal understanding and of social interaction. To carry
out an effective intervention, one must be engaged in an interaction with an interlocutor one
can intervene on, and one must have a causal understanding of how the intervened-upon
mental states can bring about a desired behavior (Ho et al., 2022).
Causal and interactive ToM measures
In response to limitations of classic spectatorial and predictive ToM tasks, researchers in
developmental and comparative psychology have developed several interactive adaptations
of classic tasks. Call and Tomasello (1999) developed a false belief task where a child
must not only rely on a character for cues about the location of a hidden reward, but
must also recognize when the character’s cues are unreliable: when the character could
not know the reward’s true location. In the active helping paradigm, the child must help
an experimenter access the toy by e.g., unlocking one of two boxes that the character is
struggling to open (Buttelmann et al., 2014). Similar interactive designs exist in comparative
psychology: the active helping paradigm has been adapted for Great apes (Buttelmann et al.,
2017), and competitive games assess understanding of false beliefs in both chimpanzees and
children (Kaminski et al., 2008). Still, while interactive, these tasks do not target a causal
understanding of mental states.
Other paradigms integrate robust measures of causal understanding, but lack an interactive
component. The strange stories task measures childrens’ causal understanding of mental
states (Happ´e, 1994). Participants must explain why characters behaved as they did in
complicated social scenarios (e.g., from bluffs to sarcasm). To succeed, participants must

cite mental states as reasons or causes for behaviors. Similarly, the short stories task asks
adult participants to explain nuanced character behaviors in a story in terms of mental states
(Dodell-Feder et al., 2013). These tasks highlight the explanatory role of ToM and, in turn,
the causal understanding it involves. Nevertheless, they involve spectatorial contexts—not
interaction.
Persuasion paradigms in developmental psychology most clearly (1) measure a causal
understanding of mind in (2) an interactive context. Good persuasion skills plausibly require
a causal model of the target, which the persuader can use to ask questions such as “if
I say X, what impact will that have on my target?” For example, if you are trying to
persuade someone to adopt a vegan diet, it might be valuable to know whether they
value animal welfare, are concerned about the environment, or dedicate lots of time to
cooking. A persuader could inquire about or infer these values and adapt their persuasion
strategy accordingly. In such persuasion tasks with children, researchers, e.g., present a
child participant with a puppet that refuses to pet a kitten and ask the child to persuade the
puppet to pet the kitten (Bartsch and London, 2000; Bartsch et al., 2010; 2011; Peterson et al.,
2018). The puppet might say that they think the kitten will scratch them, but the participant
knows that the kitten is really both clean and friendly. The participant must then choose
what information to reveal to the puppet to effectively persuade it. Hence participants
must tailor their interventions to the preferences of the target to succeed. This intervention
provides evidence of an underlying causal model because the desired action (petting the
kitten) can be generated by intervening on the beliefs or desires that jointly cause behaviors.
A clear limitation of these persuasion designs is that they are circumscribed, often relying on
forced choices between few options; given this, they are too simple to illustrate meaningful
differences in adult populations.
LLM performance on ToM tasks
A growing body of work has evaluated Large Language Models (LLMs) on ToM tasks, in-
vestigating the extent to which LLMs’ human-like behavior extends to classic psychological
tasks (Binz and Schulz, 2023; Binz et al., 2025). Some studies have focused on measuring the
capabilities of LLMs, treating ToM tasks as benchmarks for social reasoning (Sap et al., 2022;
Hu et al., 2025; Kosinski, 2024). Other studies have used LLMs as explicit computational
operationalizations of theories of human cognition and tested whether they can explain
human behavior (Trott et al., 2023; Binz et al., 2025).
While the literature on animal, child, and adult-human ToM has taken steps to address the
limitations of predictive and spectatorial tasks, research on LLMs has tended to focus on
only predictive measures. Early work established that LLMs can produce correct answers in
text-based versions of the false belief task (Trott et al., 2023; Kosinski, 2024). However, later
work suggested that LLMs showed inconsistent performance on slightly modified versions
of these stimuli, suggesting that their responses were not based on robust causal models
(Ullman, 2023; Shapira et al., 2024). Many papers have now assessed LLMs on batteries of
ToM tasks reframed as “benchmarks”, including: tracking the beliefs of multiple agents
(Kim et al., 2023), tracking beliefs recursively (Jones et al., 2024; Street et al., 2024; Xu et al.,
2024), detecting social norm violations (Strachan et al., 2024), and interpreting non-literal
language (Jones et al., 2023). However, these tasks are largely predictive and spectatorial.
Some studies have used tasks that provide richer evidence for a causal model. Gandhi
et al. (2023) tested whether LLMs can infer beliefs “backward” from actions, rather than
“forward” from world-states to actions or beliefs. Yu et al. (2025) and Chan et al. (2024), used
pre-existing dialogues to test whether LLMs can predict how participants will behave and
respond in real persuasion and negotiation contexts. Jones et al. (2023) evaluated LLMs on
the short story and strange stories tasks. While these tasks make steps toward requiring
inferences about the mental causes of behavior, they are still fundamentally spectatorial
tasks focused on passively inferring the beliefs of other agents. To our knowledge, no
research has tested LLMs’ PToM ability by having them dynamically interact with a human
or artificial partner.

Persuasion measures in LLMs
One of the most promising directions for measuring the causal and interactive PToM capacity
could be measuring persuasion ability. An emerging subfield tries to measure the extent
to which conversations with LLMs can change people’s beliefs (Rogiers et al., 2024; Jones
and Bergen, 2024; Bozdag et al., 2025). Although these tasks were not motivated as ToM
measures, but rather by the intrinsic benefits and risks of measuring the persuasion abilities
of LLMs (Jones and Bergen, 2024; El-Sayed et al., 2024), they nevertheless provide important
evidence of the abilities of models to influence people’s mental states.
These studies typically measure participants’ beliefs before and after interacting with an
LLM (Rogiers et al., 2024). Research suggests LLMs can alter people’s beliefs and behavior
toward policy issues (Salvi et al., 2024; Hackenburg et al., 2025; Potter et al., 2024), factual
questions (Schoenegger et al., 2025), conspiracy theories (Costello et al., 2024), consumer
choices (Werner et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024), charitable donations (Phuong et al., 2024),
and business decisions (Benton et al., 2024). Some studies have also found that LLMs are
more effective at persuading human participants than are other humans (Salvi et al., 2024;
Schoenegger et al., 2025).
However, while these results are impressive, they do not provide good estimates of ToM
abilities in general, nor PToM abilities in particular. Open-ended persuasion tasks allow
multiple paths to success that do not necessarily require sensitivity to interlocutors’ mental
states. LLMs may succeed through memorized persuasive arguments, classical rhetorical
techniques, or simply by providing a target with novel facts or statistics. Without experimen-
tal control over what information the persuader has access to—and thus what mental states
they could be modeling—existing tasks cannot distinguish between persuasion through
strategic mental state modeling versus persuasion through memorized rhetoric and generic
strategies.
The Present Studies
We address the gaps in these areas by extending the MindGames advanced PToM task
into a flexible task framework (Authors, 2025). Our task involves persuaders interact-
ing with another agent (the “target”) through an online conversation game. Persuaders
must convince targets to choose the persuader’s preferred policy proposal—out of three
proposals—by strategically revealing information about the proposals. Each proposal affects
three attributes differently. For example, in a scenario about AI policy, each proposal might
increase or decrease one or all of safety and control of LLMs, development speed, and public
trust in LLMs. Importantly, persuaders and targets have different value functions over the
attributes—what the persuader favors may be neutral or unfavorable to the target.
In our task, the persuader always has complete information about all proposals, while the
target initially has partial information. This information asymmetry means persuaders can
make their preferred proposal attractive to the target by selectively disclosing favorable
information and, at the same time, they can retain other information that would make other
proposals more attractive. Success in the task depends on understanding both what the
target knows (informational states) and what they value (motivational states) and using this
understanding to plan interventions (in the form of disclosures).
We manipulate PToM demands through two conditions. In REVEALED, persuaders can see
the target’s value function and existing knowledge of the proposals. In HIDDEN, persuaders
must infer these mental states through dialogue. While persuaders still must interact with
the target in REVEALED, HIDDEN requires more complex counterfactual planning (reflecting
PToM (Ho et al., 2022)). In HIDDEN, persuaders must first inquire about the target’s mental
states, then simulate how specific disclosures would update those states, before intervening
to produce the desired behavior change.
We use this framework to address a tradeoff between internal and external validity that has
plagued existing work on ToM. On one hand, tightly controlled studies, like those based on
the false belief task, allow clear inferences about specific cognitive capacities, but may poorly
reflect real-world use of such capacities. Conversely, externally valid tasks like open-ended

We address this tradeoff by conducting three experiments that progressively sacrifice internal
for external validity. Experiment 1 maximizes internal validity by using a rational bot
as the target that responds only to appeals to its mental states, ensuring success depends
on PToM (§2). Experiment 2 introduces human targets role-playing assigned values,
introducing the potential for rhetorical and other strategies (§3). Finally, Experiment 3
allows human targets to act on their own values, in order to approximate real persuasion
where targets’ preferences are mutable (§4). Through these experiments we test both whether
LLMs possess PToM abilities and whether such abilities matter for naturalistic persuasion.
Our contributions are as follows:
1. We introduce a family of experimental designs that trade off internal for external
validity to systematically explore the relationship between PToM and persuasion.
2. We conduct a comparison of adult human and LLM abilities across experimental
conditions requiring different levels of mental state reasoning, addressing limita-
tions of prior evaluations of LLMs on ToM tasks.
3. We demonstrate that while LLMs lack human-like PToM abilities on controlled
tasks (Experiment 1, HIDDEN; Fig. 3), they can nevertheless be highly effective
persuaders of human targets (Experiments 2 and 3; Figs. 4 and 6), suggesting that
PToM and persuasion success are dissociable.
4. We provide evidence that persuader performance is driven partly by properties of
human targets, such as cooperativeness and susceptibility to rhetoric, which creates
opportunities for persuasion success without PToM (§5.3).

Experiment 1: Rational Bot Persuasion
In Experiment 1, we sought to maximize internal validity over the target’s mental states,
robustly measuring PToM. To do so, the design screened off non-PToM persuasion strategies.
First, by randomly varying the target’s informational and motivational state, we ensured
a dynamic target that was unlikely to be persuaded by one general persuasion strategy.
Second, we designed the target of persuasion to be impervious to persuasion strategies
that do not rely on planned interventions on mental states. We accomplished this by
implementing the target as a “naively rational“ automated bot. This target was assigned
a value function that specified how it valued different policies and was designed to only
change its preferred proposal if its value function was better satisfied by the information
revealed by the persuader. This meant that the target was immune to any appeal that was
not an intervention on its informational states. These two constraints ensure that success
in this experiment depends on the persuader’s ability to gather information about mental
states through interaction with the target, and then to use that information in interventions.
The REVEALED and HIDDEN conditions allow us to contrast different PToM demands. By
design, a persuader can be successful in the REVEALED condition with a single message
explaining that the target’s preferred proposal has drawbacks that do not align with the
target’s value function, and that the persuader’s preferred proposal has advantages that
align with the target’s value function.
Although a basic level of planned intervention is needed to do well in the REVEALED
condition, the HIDDEN condition is far more demanding. Because the persuader does not
have full information about the target’s value function, they must seek out this information
and monitor when they have enough to intervene. This means that they must evaluate
whether their causal model of the interlocutor’s informational and motivational states is
sufficiently detailed for successful intervention.
An agent that performs well on the HIDDEN condition shows clear evidence of PToM. An
agent that only performs well on the REVEALED condition displays only a limited capacity
for PToM. For this reason, we treat above-chance performance on the HIDDEN condition as
a signature of PToM.

2.1.1
Materials
We presented persuaders with five different scenarios each of which had a different payoff
matrix (see Tab. 1). A scenario involved a theme (such as AI policy) and three different
proposals. The persuader must persuade the target to choose their preferred proposal. Each
proposal had different effects on three different attributes unique to each scenario (such as
safety and control of LLMs, development speed, and public trust in LLMs). Persuaders and
targets valued attributes differently (e.g., preference for high development speed, neutrality
toward safety, preference for low public trust in LLMs) given different value functions.
The persuader and the target communicated via free-response messages. Fig. 1 depicts an
example of the MindGames interface with a scenario and a payoff matrix (a game).

2.1.2
Procedure
In Experiment 1, persuaders had to persuade a rational bot. The rational bot updates
its preferences based on its assigned value function and available information, answers
questions truthfully when asked, but never volunteers information. We used gpt-4o to
classify whether any incoming persuader message: 1) discloses information about the
proposals or 2) inquires about the target’s (a) informational or (b) motivational states. We
then generated a canned response from the rational bot to: 1) acknowledge information
revealed, 2) reveal states appealed to, or 3) respond generically (neither 1 nor 2). We call
this target rational because it always chooses the proposal that is maximally in line with its
preferences and current information, and naive because it freely discloses its preferences
and information and takes all disclosures of information at face value.
Disclosures and Appeals.
A persuader had to strategically disclose information to per-
suade the rational bot. If the persuader discloses no information, the rational bot will
never switch from a proposal the persuader disprefers. If the persuader discloses all of the
information, the bot will choose a different proposal that the persuader also disprefers.
Persuasion in HIDDEN vs. REVEALED.
In HIDDEN, the persuader does not know the
target’s value function nor the information they have. In REVEALED, we reveal the target’s
mental states to the persuader.
Hence, to know which pieces of information to disclose in HIDDEN, a persuader must

2.4
Discussion
Different ToM styles
Experiment 1 shows that humans significantly outperform o3 in the
PToM-intensive HIDDEN condition of MindGames. This supports the claim that earlier LLM
successes on predictive ToM tasks, such as the false belief tasks, are not evidence of human-
10


## Page 11


like ToM. Human-like ToM characteristically bears the marks of both a causal understanding of
mental states and interaction as its primary context. This may reflect fundamental differences
in ToM styles between LLMs and humans; the ToM capacities of LLMs may merely involve
a statistical model of mental states. This model relies on associations between different
observable states, as opposed to causally-efficacious underlying mental states that generate
behaviors. Whereas an LLM may possess an association-based understanding of mental
states that can support predictions in many common contexts, they do not then possess a
causal understanding of mental states as underlying causes of behaviors, as is characteristic
of PToM. This contrasts clearly with human ToM, which is characterized by both associative
and causal models of mental states.
The data on appeals and disclosures supports the interpretation that humans and LLMs
deploy different ToM styles. Humans are selectively information-seeking when faced with
an opaque interlocutor in HIDDEN and seemingly seek information to support surgical
interventions. This suggests that our human participants actively model the target’s infor-
mational and motivational states by seeking out information where this model is incomplete
and use this information to drive surgical interventions. In contrast, o3’s performance is
characterized by more disclosures on the basis of less information. Moreover, its behavior
does not change as markedly between the conditions as the behavior of human participants.
In short, o3 is not particularly sensitive to what it knows about the target. This pattern is
what we would expect if o3 was not relying on a model of the target’s mental states to guide
its disclosures and appeals.
Performance differences between conditions offer a final piece of support for the different
ToM styles interpretation. In earlier work, we found that humans perform equally well
on the HIDDEN condition and the REVEALED condition (Authors, 2025). In contrast, the
difference between these conditions is very large for o3, with markedly better performance
in REVEALED. Success in REVEALED can be association-based: the persuader need not
monitor whether it has enough information about its interlocutor’s mental states to carry
out a successful intervention. Because all information about the target’s mental states is
immediately available in REVEALED, an agent can perform well if they look for matches
between the target’s preferences and the information available to the persuader, using the
heuristic that targets will endorse proposals that best satisfy their preferences. o3 performs
poorly in HIDDEN, where the demand for causal modeling is high, but without the demand
for a causal model of mental states in REVEALED, o3 improves. This is what we would
expect if o3 relies on an associational ToM, as opposed to the causal PToM.
Planning interventions for persuasion
We proposed that persuasion is a natural context
to use PToM. A subset of successful persuasion attempts (for humans in HIDDEN) seem
to rely on causal models of the target’s mental states. Experiment 1 shows that PToM is
relevant to persuasion, suggesting a relationship between them.
However, Experiment 1 is limited by its low external validity. MindGames is a significantly
more open-ended and more complex persuasion task than previous ToM tasks (Bartsch and
London, 2000; Bartsch et al., 2010; 2011; Peterson et al., 2018). Nonetheless, the rational bot
is a highly circumscribed interaction partner; it is only sensitive to exactly those persuasion
attempts that draw on PToM. This is by design. To provide the clearest measure of PToM
in our samples, Experiment 1 aims for a high degree of internal validity. We can achieve
higher external validity by using a less strict target—one susceptible to a wider range of
persuasion. Consequently, Experiment 1 points to a substantive difference in ToM capacities
between humans and LLMs, but fails to establish a general relationship between persuasion
and PToM.
3
Experiment 2: Role-Play Persuasion
In Experiment 1, we could entirely determine the information that the rational bot had
access to, the circumstances under which it would reveal information, and the inferences
that it would make from the persuader’s behavior. While this ensured that persuaders must
11


## Page 12


strategically inquire about and use mental state information to guide interventions, it differs
from persuading humans in many ways.
Human targets might be susceptible to rhetorical strategies (such as appeals to emotion
or authority) that do not depend on accurate mental state modeling (Cialdini et al., 2009).
They might provide more cues to their mental states—both explicitly and implicitly—than
the rational bot, making their beliefs and desires easier to infer without direct questioning.
Finally, humans might make decisions that deviate from strict utility maximization, either
through reasoning errors or by weighing considerations beyond the stated attributes.
These differences matter for interpreting our Experiment 1 results. Suppose we observe the
same pattern of results with humans role-playing a rational target. This would suggest that
LLMs’ lack of PToM means they are unlikely to persuade effectively in the real world when
facing the kind of information asymmetry that our task exhibits. Alternatively, suppose
LLMs persuade human targets in HIDDEN more effectively than they were able to persuade
the rational bot. This would suggest that LLMs are capable of persuading people by means
other than PToM. Moreover, it would suggest LLM success in this task is based on quite
different mechanisms to the ones that we theorize successful human persuaders use.
Experiment 2 sacrifices some internal for external validity, by replacing the rational bot with
human participants. However, as with the rational bot in Experiment 1, target participants
were given a pre-specified value function over the attributes rather than using their own
values. They were also financially incentivized to select the optimal choice with respect to
these values. We called this ‘role-play’ persuasion because the human targets were playing
the role of someone else who had the values assigned to them.
If human targets behave as rational agents who simply communicate more naturally than
our automated bot, we should observe similar patterns to Experiment 1: human persuaders
should maintain their advantage over LLMs in the HIDDEN condition, and LLM persuaders
ought to perform better in the REVEALED versus the HIDDEN condition. However, if human
targets are persuaded through rhetorical means that bypass mental state modeling, or if
they cooperatively disclose information without prompting, we may see LLMs narrow or
eliminate these performance gaps.

3.4
Discussion
In Experiment 1, we found that humans and LLMs display very different styles of ToM and
that this led to divergent levels of persuasive success in a tightly controlled task. Experiment
2 asked whether these conclusions would generalize to a more realistic setting where a
human plays the role of the persuasion target.
Do humans continue to outperform LLMs in the HIDDEN condition?
In contrast to
Experiment 1, o3’s persuasive success was better than would be expected by chance (H1),
no worse than its performance in the REVEALED condition (H2), and no worse than human
performance in the same condition (H3). Collectively, these results suggest that the LLM is
able to persuade human targets in ways that it was unable to persuade the rational bot. If o3
15


## Page 16


lacks the PToM ability to persuade the rational bot, but can nevertheless persuade human
targets, this implies that interacting with humans bypasses some of the need for PToM.
Why did o3’s persuasion success increase?
There are several differences between Experi-
ments 1 and 2 which could have led to o3’s increased persuasive success.
First, human targets may simply be more susceptible to persuasion than the bot. The success
of human persuaders was also higher in Experiment 2 than in Experiment 1, suggesting
it may simply have been easier to persuade people. More specifically, where two options
had apparently equal utility (given the information available to the target), the rational bot
would stubbornly maintain its preference for whichever option it had favored first, a policy
which would always disadvantage the persuader (see Fig. 10). By contrast, human targets
might have been sensitive to the motivations of the persuader, opting for the persuader’s
desired choice where this appeared to be costless.
In theory, humans may have been easier to persuade because they made irrational decisions
given the information available to them. However, our results suggest that humans virtually
never made decisions that had worse utility than the decision that the naively rational target
would have made (see Figure 5). Thus, it appears that o3 was not performing better in E2
due to human error.
On the contrary, in many cases the human target made a choice that had a higher utility than
the one the rational bot would have chosen. Payoff matrices were sometimes set up such
that there was an option that benefited both the persuader and the target more than the
target’s initial choice. This nonzero-sum nature of the payoffs explains why games with
human targets led to both higher Persuasion Success and higher target utility compared to
the rational bot.
Third, humans were more cooperative communicators. The rational bot never revealed
information unless explicitly asked. Human targets, by contrast, might spontaneously
volunteer information about their value function (e.g. “Which option should I pick to
maximize environmental benefits?”) or informational state (e.g. “What impact will plan
A have on tech progress?“). This cooperativity might decrease the need for appeals about
mental states in HIDDEN, masking the PToM limitations of the LLM. Indeed, while the
number of target disclosures remained constant across experiments, the number of appeals
went down (see Fig. 8), suggesting that persuaders were acquiring information without
having to ask for it. Strikingly, o3 made almost no appeals in HIDDEN in Experiment 2, but
was nevertheless able to successfully persuade over 50% of the time.
Collectively, the results suggest that humans and o3 continue to show very distinct styles
of ToM in Experiment 2. However, in contrast to Experiment 1, this difference did not
lead to a large divergence in Persuasion Success. Nevertheless, the role-play persuasion
we investigated in Experiment 2 is still importantly different from real-world persuasion.
Participants were assigned values to defend, and may have behaved differently because
they were not reasoning about their real beliefs. We address this issue in Experiment 3 by
having persuaders attempt to change participants’ real preferences over the policies.

4.3
Discussion
Experiment 3 investigated whether the patterns observed in the more controlled settings
of Experiments 1 and 2 generalized to real persuasion. We sought to understand how
persuaders’ strategies and performance changed when human targets could act on their
own mutable preferences rather than assigned value functions.
Overall, Persuasion Success peaked in this experiment. Both o3 (68.4%) and human per-
suaders (43.8%) achieved higher Persuasion Success than in Experiment 2 (57.7% and 35.2%,
respectively) and substantially higher than in Experiment 1.
The open-ended environment of Experiment 3 proved particularly advantageous for o3.
The model outperformed human persuaders in both HIDDEN and REVEALED (though
only significantly in the latter). This continues the trend from Experiment 2 and contrasts
Experiment 1, where o3 failed to perform above chance in HIDDEN.
Our exploratory analysis highlights a change in strategy for both human persuaders and
o3. Both human persuaders and o3 asked minimal diagnostic questions in the HIDDEN
condition, appealing to the target’s mental states only 1.3 times and 0.6 times per game,
respectively. This is a notable departure from Experiment 1, where humans made 6.1 appeals
and the model made 4.7 appeals per game. In Experiment 3, when constraints on the target’s
actions had significantly eased, both humans and o3 relied minimally on explicit inquiries
about mental states.
Instead, o3 succeeded in Experiment 3 when flooding conversations with information
disclosures. o3 made substantially more disclosures per game than humans in the HID-
DEN condition across all experiments. One possible explanation is that the model may
have learned a heuristic association between verbosity (or high information disclosure)
and reward based on its training data, without a causal understanding of why acquiring
information about the target’s mental states is helpful. However, we note that possible infor-
mation disclosure in this task is constrained relative to natural dialogue. Another possible
explanation is that the model may retrieve familiar arguments statistically associated with
the discussion topic (e.g., recycling arguments for action on AI policy), rather than tailoring
its points to the target’s belief or motivational states.
The efficacy of this high-information-disclosure strategy depended on the target. In Experi-
ment 1, the strategy failed because the rational bot required specific information and could
penalize irrelevant disclosures. In Experiment 2, the strategy achieved moderate success; the
human targets were likely more tolerant of verbosity than the rational bot, yet the assigned
value functions still limited the effectiveness of a high volume information strategy. In
Experiment 3, however, human targets with their own values were tolerant of—and perhaps
even persuaded by—the volume of information, even if it wasn’t specifically tailored to
information they lacked about the proposals.
Finally, the gap between Persuasion Success and Rational Bot Success was at its widest in this
experiment. Both o3 and human persuaders succeeded far more often than they would have
against the rational bot given the same information and initial value functions. (We inferred
the target’s value function in Experiment 3, which may not accurately reflect the target’s real
values). This discrepancy suggests that success in Experiment 3 was driven by more than
just target error, which was possible in Experiment 2, or the specific information disclosures

5
General Discussion
Our results suggest a dissociation between planning Theory of Mind (PToM) and persua-
sion capabilities, cautioning against premature attributions of human-like ToM to LLMs
while nonetheless highlighting LLMs’ potential to influence people’s beliefs and behavior.
o3 failed at causal mental state reasoning in controlled conditions yet remained highly
effective at persuading human targets.
The MindGames framework made this dissociation visible through three tasks that system-
atically trade off internal validity against external validity, with two conditions. Human
participants outperformed o3 in the tightly controlled HIDDEN condition of Experiment
1, yet o3 matched or exceeded human performance in the more externally valid settings
of Experiments 2 and 3. Importantly, o3 achieved this performance while making fewer
appeals to targets’ mental states than in Experiment 1. The dissociation between PToM
and persuasion capabilities has implications for both ToM research and research on the
persuasive capabilities of LLMs, which we explore below.
5.1
Humans are capable of PToM and persuasion
MindGames provides a first-personal, interactive measure of PToM that requires persuaders
to engage directly with a target to change their beliefs and behavior. In doing this, we
provide evidence of an advanced capacity for PToM in humans. We build on existing
persuasion paradigms used with children (§1), to create a rich, dynamic and interactive
task fit for use with even mature mindreaders. Success in Experiment 1 HIDDEN requires
counterfactual reasoning: persuaders must inquire about a target’s beliefs and values,
then simulate how a specific disclosure might update that target’s mental states, before
intervening to generate the desired behavior change.

HIDDEN; in Experiment 2, humans made roughly similar numbers of appeals and disclo-

that in the latter two settings, persuaders were not deploying PToM in the same way that
Experiment 1 required them to. In parallel, Rational Bot Success for human persuaders in
Experiment 2 was near zero, while Persuasion Success was substantially higher, and this
gap grew further in Experiment 3. These patterns across experiments suggest humans drew
on PToM when the task demanded it but shifted to other strategies when interacting with
more cooperative human targets—consistent with the theory that explicit ToM is cognitively
costly and deployed selectively (Apperly and Butterfill, 2009).
5.2
LLMs lack PToM but are still effective persuaders
For o3, we observed a clear dissociation between performance by condition in Experiment 1.
In REVEALED, o3 substantially outperformed human participants; in HIDDEN, o3 performed
below the chance baseline. These results extend those for earlier models (Authors, 2025),
suggesting that the dissociation in performance persists in newer reasoning-focused models.
We characterize the different strategies of o3 and humans in Experiment 1 HIDDEN as reflect-
ing distinct “styles” of ToM. Humans appear to build a causal model of the target’s mental
states, while o3 relies on associative processes—pattern matching between observable game
states and persuasive strategies—without representing the target’s mental states in a causal
model. This associative approach succeeded when information about the target’s mental
states was provided to the persuader (Experiment 1 REVEALED), or when familiar persua-
sive strategies happened to work (e.g., Experiment 2 and Experiment 3), but failed when
success required building a model of the target’s mental states through dialogue (Experiment
1 HIDDEN). Notably, o3’s improvement in Experiments 2 and 3 occurred despite using the
same low appeal, high disclosure strategy that failed in Experiment 1. The LLM succeeded
at persuading humans without relying on PToM.
5.3
Human targets allow multiple paths to Persuasion Success
Both o3 and human persuaders achieved significantly higher Persuasion Success in Experi-
ments 2 and 3 than in Experiment 1 (in the HIDDEN condition). Several factors suggest why
persuasion became “easier” as the external validity of the experiments increased.
First, the rational bot and human targets differ by design. The rational bot provides informa-
tion only when directly asked, updates preferences based only on disclosed information, and
cannot be moved from its value function. Human targets, on the other hand, can volunteer
information freely, update their preferences based on context they deem pertinent, and
update their value function (inadvertently in Experiment 2 or deliberately in Experiment 3).
Second, human targets were more cooperative than the rational bot. Appeals declined
significantly in Experiments 2 and 3 relative to Experiment 1 across both persuader types and
conditions. Yet target disclosures remained relatively constant (indeed, they progressively
increased for o3 in HIDDEN: Fig. 8). Human targets appeared to volunteer information to
persuaders, which reduced the need for persuaders to make explicit appeals.
Human conversation is structured by cooperative norms that our rational bot explicitly
violates: where Grice’s (1975) cooperative principle predicts that speakers will volunteer
relevant information and signal their communicative intentions, our bot responds only
when directly queried and never attempts to facilitate mutual understanding (cf. Clark
and Brennan, 1991). In short, human communicators cooperatively scaffold successful
communication.
The presence of this communicative scaffold in Experiment 2, may explain why LLMs per-
form much better here than in Experiment 1. LLMs, trained overwhelmingly on cooperative
human discourse, may be well-calibrated for exploiting this scaffold, but poorly equipped
for the genuinely adversarial epistemic environment that the bot created. A general conse-
quence is that, if LLMs can persuade effectively through rhetoric and pragmatic exploitation
alone, then high performance on existing persuasion benchmarks may tell us little about
ToM—and much about the vulnerability of human interlocutors.

However, human cooperativeness alone cannot explain the relative ease of Experiments 2
and 3 compared to Experiment 1 (HIDDEN). If performance differences in these experiments
were driven solely by human targets volunteering more information, then this additional
information should have made it easier for persuaders to identify strategically optimal
disclosures, and those disclosures would also have convinced the rational bot. Yet Rational
Bot Success was substantially lower than Persuasion Success for both persuader types
in Experiments 2 and 3. Whether persuaders failed to translate volunteered information
into strategically optimal disclosures or never attempted to, cooperativeness alone cannot
account for their success. Persuaders must have also succeeded through means by which
the rational bot was immune. For example, in the transcripts in Fig. 14, o3 appeared to try
to reframe the target’s values rather than updating their information by arguing that “para-
doxically, tight safety controls often hide problems.” More broadly, persuaders in Experiments 2
and 3 may have succeeded not only by disclosing information (as in Experiment 1) but also
by exploiting reasoning errors or changing what targets valued (motivational states).
5.4
Implications for real-world persuasion
Our results suggest that even though LLMs can be effective persuaders, this is not evi-
dence that LLMs possess advanced ToM capacities. More generally, real-world persuasion
may not call for much PToM at all. Persuaders may rely on a suite of alternative strategies.
Humans and language models alike may exploit observed associations between certain
persuasion strategies (such as verbosity) and desired outcomes, or may heuristically rely on
rehearsed general-purpose arguments rather than interventions tailored to their interlocu-
tor’s specific beliefs and desires. A persuader who relies on association-based heuristics
may persuade very well using only associative ToM—creating associations between behav-
ioral events—and not a causal one, that allows them to model the internal causes of such
behavioral events.
The split between tailored and heuristic persuasion strategies mirrors dual process theories
of persuasion, such as the Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986). While
we have focused on the cognitive capacities of the persuader, the Elaboration Likelihood
Model is a cognitive model of the target’s information processing during a persuasion
attempt. Targets can be persuaded via two routes: the central route and the peripheral route.
If the persuasion attempt, in the form of e.g., an utterance, travels through the central
route and the target is motivated and capable of evaluating the content of the utterance
thoroughly, persuasion will likely result from a reflective process on the part of the target. If
the persuasion attempt travels through the peripheral route, it is not examined as thoroughly,
and successful persuasion will result from the target’s identification of positive or negative
cues in the utterance. In short, targets evaluate peripheral route messages heuristically. The
peripheral route is the default for humans when processing incoming messages, because
it is less effortful (Petty et al., 1981). For this reason, it is unsurprising that our human
participants likewise largely rely on heuristic persuasion strategies.
Our findings suggest that humans may be more effective persuaders than LLMs when
targets process incoming messages through the central route, as humans can deploy PToM
to design arguments that specifically appeal to a motivated and thorough target. Although
tailored arguments are not strictly necessary for central route persuasion (e.g., the rehearsed
argument could still be compelling), it stands to reason that tailored arguments should
have greater success with reflective targets. Future work should compare persuasion in
LLMs and humans, manipulating whether the target processes incoming messages through
the central or peripheral route. Our findings suggest that humans should significantly
outperform LLMs in persuading more reflective targets.
5.5
What is Theory of Mind, really?
In a seminal paper, Gopnik and Wellman (1992) argued that our everyday understanding of
the mind is structured like a scientific theory. Scientific theories posit unobservable entities
(gravity, viruses) to explain observable phenomena (the movement of bodies, the spread of
diseases). Gopnik and Wellman contend that to explain the phenomenon of behavior, our
23


## Page 24


ToM relies on unobservable mental states as its explanatory posits. These unseen mental
states explain observable behaviors because they cause these behaviors. Consequently, a
causal understanding of mental states props up human social interaction, rendering us
understandable to each other.
Gopnik and Wellman’s theory-theory has been very influential on the ToM literature. How-
ever, our findings suggest that in some social interaction contexts (i.e., persuasion), a
causal understanding of mental states is less and less important the more we approach
naturalistic contexts. This means that the causal understanding of mental states that we
have referred to as PToM is at once characteristic of human ToM and also rarely relied on by
humans.
The claim that sophisticated ToM capacities, such as PToM, are rare in social interaction has
been developed in the dual process theory of ToM. This theory distinguishes implicit and
explicit levels of ToM, each with its own associated system (Apperly and Butterfill, 2009;
Butterfill and Apperly, 2013). A fast, automatic system maintains implicit forms of ToM
and a slow, effortful system maintains explicit forms of ToM. According to the dual process
theory, explicit ToM is rarer than implicit ToM precisely because it is effortful.
PToM is likely also highly effortful, compared to associative ToM alternatives, suggesting
that the explicit/implicit distinction aligns with our distinction between associative and
causal ToM styles. The cognitive demands of explicit ToM also means that it is only
deployed in service of the agent’s goals (Apperly, 2012; Westra, 2017). Our design enforces
goal-dependent processing by rewarding successful persuaders. It is surprising, then,
that participants in Experiments 2 and 3 relied very little on PToM. This suggests either
that PToM is prohibitively expensive (deployed only when no alternative exists, as in
Experiment 1), or that persuasion simply calls for other strategies entirely. To examine the
relative contributions of the factors of costs and relevance, future work should investigate
the cognitive costs of PToM and its deployment across other contexts that may call for causal
models of mental states.
More fundamentally, why should we care about PToM if it is rarely deployed to alter people’s
opinions in naturalistic interaction?
The importance of PToM may not primarily lie in its ability to explain the behavior of par-
ticipants in natural interaction, but rather in its ability to map the space of socio-cognitive
representations. Highly controlled designs are commonplace in psychology. Paradigms
such as the Stroop task (Stroop, 1935), garden-path sentences (Bever, 1970), and verbal
interference (Baddeley and Hitch, 1974) all involve creating artificially challenging circum-
stances precisely to uncover cognitive mechanisms. The scenarios presented in classical
false belief tasks are themselves highly artificial, as we have argued (§1). The importance
of the false belief task does not lie in its ability to adequately model the child’s everyday
cognitive challenges, but to reveal whether children understand the perspectival nature of
thought, which is a crucial building block of a mature understanding of the mind. Similarly,
although our identification of PToM in Experiment 1 may have only limited applicability in
explaining natural persuasion behaviors, it provides evidence that humans have a causal
understanding of mental states that they can use to plan and act out interventions on their
environments.
PToM is important for a variety of reasons. For example, it likely contributes to our explicit
understanding of behaviors (Cavallini et al., 2013). It may also enrich children’s developing
ToM, because it supports interventions to test specific hypotheses about the mental causes
of behavior. (Gopnik and Schulz, 2004; Gopnik, 2012; Gopnik and Wellman, 2012).
Since PToM is important at the levels of development and cognitive mechanisms, it raises
deeper questions about the relationship between ToM and persuasion. Even if adult humans
mostly get by on associative reasoning in persuasion, the capacity to identify and learn
the relevant associations may depend on an earlier ability to bring about evidence of such
associations through interventions. For this reason, participants who perform strongly
in measures of PToM (Experiment 1) may show surprising transfer effects to measures of
naturalistic persuasion (Experiments 2 and 3). Future work could investigate within-subject
correlations of performance in Experiment 1 and Experiment 3 to test this hypothesis.

The upshot of the above discussion is that real ToM is neither effortful deliberation nor
frugal heuristics but the broader capacity to deploy both of these capacities flexibly and
effectively. An understanding of human social cognition, requires that we can both identify
representational mechanisms in controlled conditions and evaluate how they are and are not
deployed in externally valid environments. Our work explicitly engages in this explanatory
project.
6
Conclusion
We used a persuasion-based measure to compare the capacity for Planning Theory of Mind
(PToM; §1) in adult humans and LLMs. We adapted this task for three experiments that
systematically traded off internal for external validity. Humans significantly outperformed
LLMs in Experiment 1 HIDDEN (Fig. 3), where success required PToM, while LLMs outper-
formed humans on Experiments 2 (Fig. 4) and 3 (Fig. 6), which gradually relaxed PToM
demands. Our findings suggest that humans and LLMs may fundamentally diverge in their
ToM styles: humans can represent mental states as causes of behaviors whereas LLMs rely
only on associations between behaviors. Consequently, we recommend a cautious attitude
toward exaggerated claims about human-like ToM in LLMs. Nonetheless, LLMs can still be
impressively persuasive.
Our findings raise further questions about the relationship between persuasion and PToM,
as well as foundational questions about the nature of ToM itself. Restricted persuasion
contexts (as in Experiment 1) are a powerful tool for revealing PToM capacities, and they
may even be especially useful for persuading more reflective targets—a target that can only
be evaluated through the central route (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986). We propose that the broad
capacity for ToM may be flexible: fast, efficient, and based largely on association when the
contextual scaffold is present, and slow, costly, and based on causal understanding when the
context is challenging. This paints a multifaceted picture of ToM that aligns with theories
that divide the capacity into the reflective and the heuristic, the explicit and the implicit.

would initially choose one proposal, p; given all the information an optimal target would

A.2
Selecting a Game
We avoid giving a participant a payoff matrix they have already seen. The scenario is chosen
first, then a payoff matrix is selected.
In the role-play persuasion experiment, we choose any unseen payoff matrix of the required
type.
In the real persuasion experiment, we infer the target’s value function from their initial survey
answers (binned to -1/0/+1) and select an unseen payoff matrix whose target coefficients
match that value function (using any 3-attribute subset when the scenario has more than
three). If no match is found, we fall back to the first available matrix. This can occur, e.g.,
when a participant answers “neutral” to more than two attributes.
