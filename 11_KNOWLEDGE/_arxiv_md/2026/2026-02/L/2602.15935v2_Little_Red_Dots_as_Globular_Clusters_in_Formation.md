ABSTRACT
Little Red Dots (LRDs), among the most enigmatic high-redshift discoveries by JWST, are commonly
believed to be powered by accreting supermassive black holes. Here, we explore the possibility that
these sources are globular clusters in formation, with rest-frame UV arising from a very young stellar
population and rest-frame optical from a short-lived supermassive (> 104 M⊙) star. The spectral
profiles of LRDs are broadly consistent with this scenario, though the observed temperatures and
bolometric luminosities favor emission reprocessed by optically thick, continuum-driven winds not
fully captured by current models. The LRD z ∼5−7 UV luminosity function naturally evolves, under
standard evolutionary and mass-loss prescriptions, into a present-day mass function with a turnover at
log10(M∗/M⊙) = 5.3 and an exponential cutoff at high masses, consistent with local globular-cluster
populations. We estimate the total present-day number density of LRDs formed across all redshifts
to be ≈0.3 Mpc−3, similar to local globular clusters.
The observed LRD redshift range matches
the age distribution of metal-poor globular clusters, without current LRD counterparts to the metal-
rich population. If LRDs are globular clusters in formation, we predict chemical abundance patterns
characteristic of multiple stellar populations, including enhanced He and N, and potential Na-O and
Al-Mg anti-correlations. These results offer a local perspective to explore this surprisingly abundant
population of distant sources, and a potential new window into extreme stellar astrophysics in the
early Universe.

JWST at high redshift. The discovery of a cosmically
abundant population of compact red sources at z > 3,
largely missed by previous telescopes, quickly captivated
the community (I. Labb´e et al. 2023). The LRD spec-
tral shape became their hallmark:

The discovery of broad (≳1000 km s−1) Hα emis-

(Y. Harikane et al. 2023; D. D. Kocevski et al. 2023; J.

J. E. Greene et al. 2024). If LRDs are powered by ac-
creting black holes, they are remarkably distinct from
other known black hole populations. LRDs rarely have
detected X-rays (T. T. Ananna et al. 2024; I. Juodˇzbalis
et al. 2024), many exhibit Balmer absorption even in
Hα (J. Matthee et al. 2024), lack the rest-frame mid-IR
colors typical of a dusty torus (P. G. P´erez-Gonz´alez
et al. 2024), occupy lower mass halos than standard
quasars (E. Pizzati et al. 2025), and have inferred black-
hole-to-stellar-mass ratios approaching unity (L. J. Fur-
tak et al. 2024; R. Maiolino et al. 2024).
Individu-
ally, these properties can each be explained as black
holes forming before the bulk of stars by accreting at
super-Eddington rates (E. Lambrides et al. 2024) with
their accretion disks enshrouded by dense neutral gas
cocoons (K. Inayoshi & R. Maiolino 2025; R. P. Naidu
et al. 2025). Since accreting black holes are expected
to evolve strongly with redshift, the extreme accretion
physics powering the LRD population was viewed as a
natural extension of early black hole formation.
The discovery of ‘‘Black Hole Stars” (R. P. Naidu et al.
2025; A. de Graaff et al. 2025a), which resemble LRDs
without significant UV continua, further highlights their
unusual properties. These objects suggest the character-
istic LRD V-shape can be spectroscopically decomposed
into two distinct components: one in the rest-frame UV
and one in the rest-frame optical (G. Barro et al. 2026;
W. Q. Sun et al. 2026).
The rest-frame UV is well-
described by a young stellar population, while the rest-
frame optical is well-fit by a modified blackbody with
temperatures 2000-7000 K and luminosities 109−11 L⊙
(A. de Graaff et al. 2025b). The spectral inflection oc-
curs due to the relative strength of the UV and optical
components, shifting as red as ∼6000 ˚A (A. de Graaff
et al. 2025b).
Standard sub- or super-Eddington accretion flow mod-
els produce multi-temperature spectra with significant
EUV/X-ray emission (N. I. Shakura & R. A. Sunyaev
1973; J. E. Pringle 1981; M. A. Abramowicz et al. 1988;
A. Sadowski et al. 2011; E. Lambrides et al. 2024). If
accretion powers LRDs, this intrinsic high-energy radia-
tion must be reprocessed by an optically thick medium.
The emergent spectrum must be converted into a single-
temperature cool modified blackbody while suppressing
strong multi-wavelength radiative signatures of cooling
and heating: X-ray emission, radio free-free emission,
prominent metal cooling lines, and dust. The observed
Planck spectrum further implies that the broad emis-
sion lines cannot originate deep within fully thermal-
ized layers; instead they must arise in gas exterior to
the photosphere, possibly in a stratified atmosphere. At
these cool temperatures the photosphere is largely neu-
tral, implying that Balmer emission is likely collisionally
excited in the atmosphere (A. Torralba et al. 2025). The
observed single-temperature blackbodies place stringent
radiative-transfer and energetic constraints of LRDs.
Here, we explore an alternative scenario in which
LRDs are globular clusters in formation (e.g., M. Gieles
et al. 2018). In this scenario, the LRD V-shape arises
from an ultra-young stellar population producing the
rest-frame UV continuum, while a supermassive star
(SMS) produces the rest-frame optical.
Young stellar
clusters are extremely dense (A. Adamo et al. 2024; A.
Claeyssens et al. 2023, 2026) with high stellar multiplic-
ity that suffer frequent dynamical interactions (S. S. R.
Offner et al. 2023) that can drive runaway stellar merg-
ers to form SMSs, even in relatively metal-rich envi-
ronments (M. Gieles et al. 2018; M. C. Vergara et al.
2025).
Thus, SMS formation may naturally occur in
dense stellar environments to resolve both the globular
cluster mass-budget problem (A. Renzini et al. 2015)
and the origin of multiple populations (E. Carretta et al.
2010; G. Piotto et al. 2015; R. G. Gratton et al. 2010; N.
Bastian & C. Lardo 2018; A. P. Milone & A. F. Marino
2022).
SMSs remain largely theoretical (A. Kuruvanthodi
et al. 2023), but they may reproduce many LRD fea-
tures (F. Martins et al. 2020; L. J. Furtak et al. 2023;
D. Nandal & A. Loeb 2025). They are theorized to have
masses of 104−6 M⊙with luminosities near 107−11 L⊙
(M. Gieles et al. 2018; F. Martins et al. 2020; D. Nan-
dal & A. Loeb 2025). Their extreme luminosities drive
dense winds that can mimic the broad Hα observed in
LRDs (F. Martins et al. 2020) through continuum opac-
ities that include electron scattering and H−(C. Dotan
& N. J. Shaviv 2012; P. A. Denissenkov & F. D. A.
Hartwick 2014; J. S. Vink 2018). SMSs are expected to
be large (1, 000 au) leading to relatively cool temper-
atures, Teff ∼3000 −10, 000 K (L. Haemmerl´e et al.
2018, 2019; F. Martins et al. 2020; M. Bernini-Peron
et al. 2026).
The immense gravitational potentials of
SMSs require a continuous accretion of fresh hydrogen
to stave off collapse to a black hole (M. C. Begelman
2010; T. E. Woods et al. 2017), and SMSs likely only
exist for order of magnitude ∼1 Myr (S. F. Portegies
Zwart & S. L. W. McMillan 2002; M. Gieles et al. 2018).

SMSs are predicted to have a ‘‘conveyor-belt”-like fully-

explain the unique odd-element enhancements observed
in local globular clusters including Na-O and Al-Mg
anti-correlations, as well as He and N enhancements (E.
Carretta et al. 2010; G. Piotto et al. 2015; R. G. Grat-

Here, we critically examine this hypothesis by envi-
sioning that LRDs are a short-lived phase of globular
cluster formation during which both an SMS and a stel-
lar cluster coexist. We first demonstrate that the ob-
served V-shapes profiles are plausible with a globular
cluster hosting an active SMS (section 2) and explore the
limitations of current SMS models lacking sufficiently
optically thick winds (subsection 2.1). We then use the
observed LRD UV luminosity function to estimate the
z ∼7 LRD mass function and passively evolve it to z = 0
(section 3). We compare the shape (subsection 3.1) and
number counts (subsection 3.2) of this mass function to
local globular clusters (section 4). Finally, in section 5
we present three testable predictions for the hypothesis
that LRDs are globular clusters in formation.

2.1. Potential Supermassive Star Properties
The globular cluster in formation model in Figure 1
does not perfectly reproduce the observed LRDs, par-
ticularly near 3500 ˚A where the spectrum transitions
from cluster to SMS dominated.
These discrepancies
may indicate (1) the SMS model is too hot, or (2) the
SMS winds are insufficiently dense. Cooler blackbod-
ies peaking near 5, 000 ˚A may match the data better.
However, CMFGEN (D. J. Hillier & D. L. Miller 1998)
does not include molecular opacities (e.g., H−), and no
SMS atmosphere model cooler than 7, 000 K currently
exists. An optically thick SMS wind would provide its
own opacity that may explain the cooler observed LRD
temperatures.
Thus, the observed discrepancies may
constrain the stellar astrophysics of SMSs rather than
rule out the proposed scenario.

3. LRD NUMBER COUNTS
Having shown that the V-shaped spectral morphology
of LRDs is plausibly reproduced by a globular cluster
in formation with an active SMS, we now turn to the
observed LRD luminosity function and number counts.
In the next two sub-sections, we first use the z ∼5 −
7 UV luminosity function to predict the shape of the
z ∼0 mass function, adopting the deliberately simple
assumption of a constant cluster mass-loss rate from z ∼
7 to z ∼0 (subsection 3.1). We then estimate the total
number of LRDs formed over the entire observed redshift
range (subsection 3.2).
3.1. The present-day shape of the LRD mass function
The LRD UV luminosity function (UVLF) is con-
structed using photometric samples selected for com-
pactness and V-shaped spectral profiles (D. D. Kocevski
et al. 2023; V. Kokorev et al. 2024). A Schechter func-
tion (P. Schechter 1976) matches the LRD UVLF, with
minimal redshift evolution between z ∼5 and z ∼7 (V.
Kokorev et al. 2024). We adopt the LRD UVLF from V.
Kokorev et al. (2024) with a power-law slope α = −1.5±
0.4, a characteristic MUV of M ∗= −20.7±0.7 mag, and
a normalization of ϕ∗= (5 ± 2) × 10−6 Mpc−3.
Consistent with the interpretation of LRDs as glob-
ular clusters (see subsection 5.1), many LRDs are as-
sociated with extended companion galaxies (P. Rinaldi
et al. 2025; Y. Zhang et al. 2025). We conservatively
assume 20% of the total UV luminosity arises from the
compact object, and that the SMS contributes 20% of
this compact emission (W. Q. Sun et al. 2026). Thus,
the cluster light is ∼16% of the total UV luminosity.

LRDs are globular clusters in formation. We adopt an
age of ∼1 Myr, motivated by the short lifetimes of
SMSs (M. Gieles et al. 2018). Forming an SMS requires

Once young clusters form, how do they evolve? After
the LRD phase, we assume the SMS exhausts its core
hydrogen and collapses to a black hole (M. C. Begelman
2010; T. E. Woods et al. 2017). The cluster then ceases
being identified as an LRD because the SMS is no longer
producing rest-frame optical continuum. The remaining
massive stars continue to produce blue continua until
they explode as supernovae after 3-10 Myr. This stellar
feedback clears the remaining dust and gas from the
cluster to shut off star formation (J. A. Turner et al.
2022). The cluster then passively evolves until z ∼0.
Over the ensuing ∼13 Gyr, the quenched cluster loses
M∗through stellar evolution and dynamical interactions
such as tidal stripping and evaporation (J. P. Ostriker
et al. 1972; L. Spitzer 1987; S. M. Fall & Q. Zhang 2001;
H. J. G. L. M. Lamers et al. 2010). We adopt the sim-
plest approach of a total constant mass-loss of 105 M⊙
(≈10 M⊙Myr−1), consistent with recent analyses of
Milky Way stellar streams and N-body simulations (M.
Gieles & O. Y. Gnedin 2023; Y. Chen et al. 2025a,b).
We tested more complex mass-loss prescriptions, such as
a M∗-dependent mass-loss (with γ = 2/3; H. J. G. L. M.
Lamers et al. 2010), and find a largely unchanged shape
to the final mass function.
The z ∼7 LRD mass function evolved to z ∼0 with
constant mass-loss is shown as the blue curve in Fig-
ure 3. The exponential cut-off for > 106 M⊙remains at
z ∼0 because the mass-loss does not disrupt the most
massive clusters, whereas the lowest mass clusters are
dissipated. The surviving number of clusters depends
on the initial low-mass power-law slope because it sets
the abundance of clusters initially near the destruction
mass. Consequently, the steep initial mass function de-
velops a turnover near log(M∗/M⊙) ∼5.3.
For comparison, Figure 3 also shows the z ∼7 galaxy
stellar mass function (J. R. Weaver et al. 2023; R.
Navarro-Carrera et al. 2024).
At the M∗relevant for
globular cluster formation, the extrapolated galaxy stel-
lar mass function is a single power law.
Without an
exponential cutoff at >106 M⊙, it does not naturally
evolve into the same shape as the LRD scenario.

5. PREDICTIONS IF LITTLE RED DOTS ARE
GLOBULAR CLUSTERS IN FORMATION
The previous three sections have shown it is plausible
that LRDs are globular clusters in formation. Here, we
conclude with testable predictions of this hypothesis us-
ing known globular cluster properties. These concrete
predictions provide an opportunity to stress-test the hy-
pothesis to hopefully gain a better understanding of the
true nature of LRDs.
5.1. LRDs may represent metal-poor globular clusters
Globular clusters are generally quite old, with obser-
vations and models indicating a mean formation epoch
at z ≳2 and formation rates peaking near z ∼4 −6
(J. G. Cohen et al. 1998; J. M. D. Kruijssen 2015; D. A.
Forbes & T. Bridges 2010; N. Bastian & C. Lardo 2018;
K. El-Badry et al. 2019; J. M. D. Kruijssen et al. 2019;
J. M. Ying et al. 2025; F. Aguado-Agelet et al. 2025;
L. M. Valenzuela et al. 2025). If LRDs trace globular
cluster formation, they should form primarily at these
epochs.
This formation timescale roughly matches the ob-
served LRD redshift range of z ∼4 −8 (D. D. Ko-
cevski et al. 2023; V. Kokorev et al. 2024; Y. Ma et al.
2025). In Figure 4, the LRD peak (D. D. Kocevski et al.
2023) aligns with the old, metal-poor Milky Way glob-
ular cluster population (dark blue; D. A. Forbes & T.
Bridges 2010). In contrast, the metal-rich globular clus-
ters (light-blue dashed line) lack a corresponding LRD
population at z ∼1 −2. Metal-rich globular clusters

formed at lower redshift tend to show lower stellar sur-

trend likely reflects not only metallicity, but also ISM
pressures and environmental conditions which shape
cluster collapse and structure (J. M. D. Kruijssen 2015).

runaway stellar collisions (M. Gieles et al. 2018), then

be less likely to reach sufficient densities to form SMSs.
In this scenario, low-redshift globular clusters would
not produce SMSs and would lack the characteristic V-
shaped spectral profile required for LRD selection.
The redshift distribution provides an empirical test
for the LRD-globular clusters hypothesis. The observed
LRD peak aligns with metal-poor globular clusters,
while no LRD population comparable to the metal-rich
systems are seen at lower redshift. This absence may re-
flect either an observational bias against detecting them
in dense, metal-rich environments or a metallicity ceiling
for SMS formation.
5.2. Some LRDs should have high He abundances and
anti-correlations between Na-O, Al-Mg, and N-C
Globular clusters have distinct, and oftentimes multi-
ple, abundance patterns due to neutron capture (R. G.
Gratton et al. 2012). Many show helium-enhanced pop-
ulations (R. G. Gratton et al. 2010; A. K. Dupree &
E. H. Avrett 2013), along with anti-correlations between
odd and even atomic number elements, including: Na-
enhanced and O-poor, Al-enhanced and Mg-poor, and
N-enhanced and C-poor populations (N. Bastian & C.
Lardo 2018; A. P. Milone & A. F. Marino 2022). Al-
though not universal, the trends are most prominent
in the most massive (E. Carretta et al. 2010) and oldest
(N. Bastian & C. Lardo 2018) globular clusters. If LRDs
represent globular clusters in formation, similar chemi-
cal signatures may be present in a subset of LRDs.
These abundance patterns are challenging to diag-
nose in LRDs due to limited spectral resolution and the
lack of sufficient SMS models. He I emission lines are
among the strongest lines detected in nearly all LRDs
(J. Matthee et al. 2024; I. Labbe et al. 2024; V. Koko-
rev et al. 2025; A. Torralba et al. 2025; W. Q. Sun
et al. 2026), with strengths comparable to hydrogen
lines.
While a detailed He/H abundance has not yet
been reported, SMSs nucleosynthesis models predict en-
hanced helium abundance.
The rest-frame optical contains strong neutral sodium
transitions (Na D 5890, 5896 ˚A˚A), while the rest-UV
has resonant aluminum lines (Al II 1670 ˚A and Al III
1855, 1863 ˚A˚A). However, few LRD spectra have suffi-
cient resolution to isolate these lines (F. D’Eugenio et al.
2025). A2744-45924, shown in Figure 1 (I. Labbe et al.
2024), has a tentative Al III 1860 ˚A detection and rel-
atively weak Mg II 2800 ˚A emission blended with Fe II
lines. The Al, Mg, and Fe lines could provide a direct
comparison to local globular clusters, with [Al/Mg]> 0
supporting the globular cluster in formation picture (E.
Carretta et al. 2010; N. Bastian & C. Lardo 2018; A. P.
Milone & A. F. Marino 2022).
Carbon emission is reported in several LRDs (H. B.
Akins et al. 2025b; M. Tang et al. 2025; H. B. Akins et al.
2026), whereas nitrogen is less common because broad
Hα contaminates the [N II] 6549, 6584˚A˚A lines. Alter-
natively, the UV high-ionization lines may directly probe
the nitrogen abundance (e.g., I. Labbe et al. 2024). A re-
cent systematic search in the Dawn JWST Archive iden-
tified high-ionization nitrogen lines in six LRDs (I. Morel
et al. 2025). These objects lack UV carbon lines, and
have weak rest-optical oxygen lines, suggesting a tenta-
tive N-C and N-O anti-correlation. However, the current
evidence for distinct abundances patterns in LRDs re-
mains weak and relies on sparse samples. A systematic
study of the relative abundances is required to robustly
test the globular cluster hypothesis.
5.3. Should metal-poor globular clusters host
intermediate mass black holes?
Globular clusters have long been modeled with nu-
merical simulations and evolutionary models to match
their surface brightness and velocity dispersion profiles.
A central SMS remnant, most plausibly a black hole,
would increase the central velocity dispersion and de-
lay core collapse. H. Baumgardt (2017) modeled local
globular clusters including central black holes and de-
rived upper-limits of log(MBH/M⊙) ≤4 for most lo-
cal globular clusters. Observationally, whether globular
clusters host black holes is actively debated (M. Mezcua
2017; J. E. Greene et al. 2020). ω Centauri is the most
likely candidate (E. Noyola et al. 2008; M. H¨aberle et al.
2024), while most others remain undetected. Local glob-
ular clusters put stringent mass constraints on SMS rem-
nants.
What black hole mass is expected from an SMS? If
LRDs radiate near Ledd, the inferred SMS masses could
reach log(M∗/M⊙) ≈6.5, potentially exceeding the dy-
namical upper-limits in local globular clusters.
How-
ever, SMS winds may remove ≳90% of the SMS mass
prior to collapse (C. Dotan & N. J. Shaviv 2012; P. A.
Denissenkov & F. D. A. Hartwick 2014), substantially
reducing the resulting black hole mass. Such mass loss
also provides a natural resolution to the multiple abun-
dances populations (M. Gieles et al. 2018). Improved
SMS atmosphere and wind models are needed to de-
termine whether optically thick winds yield remnant
masses consistent with those inferred from local glob-
ular clusters.
6. CONCLUSION
We have tested the hypothesis of Little Red Dots as
globular clusters in formation with an active supermas-
sive star. This scenario is motivated by the legacy of


## Page 9


9
local globular cluster observations and provides a physi-
cally coherent framework for interpreting LRD observa-
tions. While the scenario remains plausible, no definitive
conclusion can be currently made and we outline several
observations to stress-test this hypothesis. If confirmed,
Little Red Dots offer a direct view of globular cluster
formation and open a new window onto extreme stel-
lar astrophysics characterized by intense radiation fields.
Their high luminosities suggest that similar systems may
be observable at even earlier epochs, potentially charac-
terizing the first generations of stars.
