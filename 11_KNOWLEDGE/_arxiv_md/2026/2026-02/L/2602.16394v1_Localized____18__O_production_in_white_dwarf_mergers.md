Abstract
The merger of a He white dwarf (WD) and a CO WD is the favored formation channel for R Coronae
Borealis (RCB) stars. These stars exhibit 16O/18O ratios that are orders of magnitude lower than
the solar value. However, it is not fully understood whether such low 16O/18O ratios can be achieved
in WD merger remnants for the predicted lifetime of RCB stars of around 104 years. In this work,
we perform detailed nucleosynthesis calculations of a 3D magnetohydrodynamical simulation of a
merger of a 0.3 M⊙He WD and a 0.6 M⊙CO WD for 4000 s at which point a steady state in
temperature and density is reached. From this point, we follow several radial zones to study the
long-term production of 18O and its variability throughout the burning region.
We find that the asymmetric merger process leaves an imprint on the distribution of the abundances
at the end of our hydrodynamic simulation. During the long-term evolution up to 100 years, we
observe 16O/18O ratios of order of unity, although the timescale on which 18O is destroyed again
is highly location dependent. Importantly, our calculations suggest that in the outer layers of the
burning shell, the dominant production channel is 14C(α, γ)18O instead of the commonly considered
14N(α, γ)18F(β+)18O reaction, whereby the former can be sustained for longer periods of time.
Furthermore, these outer regions do not reach the conditions necessary for fast α-captures in 18O to
22Ne, thus being favorable to maintaining a low 16O/18O ratio.

1 Introduction
R Coronae Borealis (RCB) stars (Webbink 1984;
Clayton 1996) are commonly associated with the
product of a merger of a He white dwarf (WD)
with a CO WD as the favored formation chan-
nel (Iben and Tutukov 1984; Clayton et al. 2007;
Clayton 2012). Although their precise origin is
still disputed — for example, a He shell flash in
a post-asymptotic giant branch star has also been
suggested — recent modeling efforts (e.g., Staff
et al. 2012, 2018; Crawford et al. 2020; Shiber
et al. 2024) were successful in matching observed
quantities with WD merger models. A common
characteristic of RCB stars is
16O/18O ratios
which are considerably lower than the solar value
of approximately 500 (e.g., Clayton et al. 2007;
Karambelkar et al. 2022; Mehla et al. 2025), with
values on the order of unity recently observed1.
There are other characteristics of RCB stars such
as their photometric variability due to dust forma-
tion, their metallicity, and 13C/12C ratio, but their
low 16O/18O ratio is the criterion at the center of
our work.
During the merger of a CO WD with a He WD,
the lower mass He WD donor will become tidally
disrupted and form an accretion disk around the
CO WD accretor (see, e.g., Longland et al. 2011;
Staff et al. 2012, 2018; Shiber et al. 2024). In addi-
tion to the accretion disk, the He will form a thin
spherical shell around the CO core, where temper-
atures well above 1 × 108 K will be reached. This
so-called shell-of-fire (SoF) is considered to be the
main production site of 18O through the reaction
14N(α, γ)18F(β+)18O, where the precise produc-
tion yields depend on factors such as progenitor
metallicity and the mass ratio between the donor
and the accretor (Staff et al. 2012).
Here, two main problems arise that make
matching models to the observations difficult. For
one, the temperatures commonly found in mod-
els of the SoF are too high to maintain 18O for
long periods of time as it will undergo fast α-
capture reactions 18O(α, γ)22Ne. In other words,
18O will be destroyed before it can build up in

Although current models have made great
strides in investigating the one-dimensional (1D)
stellar evolution (e.g., Zhang et al. 2014; Lauer
et al. 2019; Schwab 2019; Crawford et al. 2020)
of RCB stars and the merger process with three-
dimensional (3D) hydrodynamic simulations (e.g.,
Longland et al. 2011, 2012; Staff et al. 2012, 2018;
Menon et al. 2013, 2019; Shiber et al. 2024), the
18O production cycle is still not fully understood
in this context. Particularly hydrodynamic simu-
lations of the merger do not yet capture the full
picture of the dynamic process. There are many
studies that model the merger in 1D (e.g., Menon
et al. 2013; Schwab 2019; Crawford et al. 2024),
but struggle to accurately capture the asymmet-
ric nature of such events. Furthermore, current 3D
hydrodynamic simulations, although successful in
investigating mechanisms such as dredge-up, lack
detailed modeling of the microphysics involved in
this process, such as an in-situ nuclear network
to correctly capture the nuclear energy release in
the SoF (with the exception of Longland et al.
20112), an accurate equation of state, or magnetic
fields. Lastly, recent studies of the SoF (Staff et al.
2012; Crawford et al. 2020) assume uniform con-
ditions in the SoF, neglecting the dynamic range
of conditions found in this region.
In this work, we present a 3D magnetohy-
drodynamic (MHD) simulation of a merger of a
0.6 M⊙CO WD with a 0.3 M⊙He WD, con-
ducted with the Arepo code. With this simula-
tion, we aim to provide accurate initial conditions
of a merged hybrid HeCO WD that allow for a
detailed long-term study of the nucleosynthesis in
such objects and consequently determining if the
predicted 16O/18O ratios are in agreement with
observed values. We further investigate the long-
term nucleosynthesis in different locations within
the SoF, demonstrating the wide range of reaction

2 Methods
2.1 Hydrodynamic simulation
We model the hydrodynamic evolution of the
WD binary system in 3D using the code Arepo
(Springel 2010; Pakmor et al. 2011, 2016; Wein-
berger et al. 2020). Arepo solves the equations
of ideal MHD using a second-order finite-volume
approach on an unstructured moving Voronoi
mesh. Explicit refinement and de-refinement con-
ditions are employed for cells whose mass deviates
by more than a factor of two away from the target
mass of the cells (Mtarget = 5 × 10−7 M⊙). Self-
gravity is treated as Newtonian and the WD mat-
ter is modeled with a Helmholtz equation of state
(Timmes and Swesty 2000), including Coulomb
corrections (Yakovlev and Shalybkov 1989).
We couple a 55 isotope nuclear reaction net-
work to the hydrodynamics, which includes the
isotopes: n, p, 4He, 11B, 12−13C, 13−15N, 15−17O,
18F, 19−22Ne, 22−23Na, 23−26Mg, 25−27Al, 28−30Si,
29−31P,
31−33S,
33−35Cl,
36−39Ar,
39K,
40Ca,
43Sc, 44Ti, 47V, 48Cr, 51Mn, 52,56Fe, 55Co, and
56,58−59Ni. The reaction rates are provided by
the JINA REACTLIB database (Cyburt et al.
2010) and the network is active in all cells where
T > 2 × 107 K and ρ > 1 × 104 g cm−3.
The initial stellar binary system consists of
a 0.6 M⊙CO WD (50 %/50 % by mass) and a
0.3 M⊙He WD, both having an initial uniform
temperature of 1×106 K. Our simulation follows a
setup similar to the one presented in Mor´an-Fraile
et al. (2024). To ensure hydrostatic equilibrium
in the stellar models, the WDs are relaxed in iso-
lation for ten dynamical timescales of each star,
following the procedure described in Pakmor et al.
(2012); Ohlmann et al. (2017).
After the relaxation procedure, both WDs are
placed together into a simulation box with size
7.7 × 1012 cm and uniform background density of
1×10−8 g cm−3. To avoid strong tidal forces on the
WDs as they are placed in the same box, the ini-
tial orbital separation is chosen to be 7.7 ×109 cm,
which is equal to two times the separation at the
point of Roche-lobe overflow (RLOF). We artifi-
cially reduce the orbital separation following the
inspiral procedure described by Pakmor et al.
(2021) until reaching the point of RLOF, after
which we let the system evolve freely. At this point
we also enable the nuclear reaction network. The
simulation is followed for a total of 4000 s after
RLOF.
2.2 Nucleosynthesis
We perform nucleosythesis calculations in post-
processing using the NuGrid3 nuclear reaction
network (Pignatari et al. 2016; Ritter et al. 2018),
specifically the modified version described by
Jones et al. (2019). Here, we include a total of
5213 isotopes as listed in Jones et al. (2019, Table
1), in particular n, 1−3H, 3−6He, 7−9Li, 7−12Be,
8−14B, 11−18C, 11−21N, 13−22O, 17−26F, 17−41Ne,
19−44Na, 20−47Mg, 21−51Al, and 22−54Si; although
heavier isotopes are included, they are not relevant
for the conditions studied here, and the effective
network will only include up to around 300 iso-
topes. For details on the included reaction rates
and data sources, we refer the reader to Jones
et al. (2019) and references therein. We note that,
in contrast to Jones et al. (2019), we do not use
the weak reaction rates of Nabi and Klapdor-
Kleingrothaus (2004) as they are not relevant to
our study.
To obtain detailed nucleosynthetic yields of the
hydrodynamic simulation, we advect 106 equal-by-
mass Lagrangian tracer particles in the flow and
record the temperature and density along their
trajectories. This data is then used as input for the
NuGrid nuclear network calculations, from which
we obtain detailed abundances for each particle at
the end of the hydrodynamic simulation.

extended period of time, 100 years in our case. As
we are mainly interested in an exploratory study
of the nucleosynthesis under the conditions found
in the WD merger to establish potentially rel-

modeling observed data, we deem this a sufficient
approximation.
For the post-processing, we add an initial solar
metallicity utilizing the data of Asplund et al.
(2009). Here, we follow the scheme described by
Lach et al. (2020) and convert CNO isotopes to
14N in the He WD and to 22Ne in the CO WD, as
one would expect from the preceding stellar evo-
lution. In addition, we consider the presence of 1H
in the He WD. For the current study, we include
this as a homogeneous mass fraction X(1H) = 0.01
throughout the WD (similar to Staff et al. 2012)
as we are mostly interested in studying how the
presence of free protons impacts the subsequent
nucleosynthesis. Although it is not entirely clear if
a homogeneous H abundance is representative of
the dynamic merger history, we deem this a suf-
ficient approximation for this exploratory study.
It is not yet clear to what extent mixing during
the merger process could homogenize an initially
stratified donor WD (e.g., a thin H shell on top
of a He WD). This will be further investigated in
future studies.

3 Results
3.1 Hydrodynamic merger
simulation
Here, we only briefly outline the results of
the merger simulation relevant for this work; a
detailed analysis of this simulation will be given
in its own dedicated study. We start following the
merger after the inspiral phase when RLOF sets
in (we define this point in time as t = 0.0 s), that
is mass transfer from the He WD onto the sur-
face of the CO WD begins. The evolution of the
merger that follows is similar to the one outlined
by, e.g., Staff et al. (2012, 2018); Mor´an-Fraile
et al. (2024); Shiber et al. (2024). The orbital sep-
aration decreases as a result of the mass transfer
until the He WD is tidally disrupted and forms a
shell around the CO WD, as well as an accretion
disk; this process is illustrated in Figure 1. Ini-
tially, the He WD is not yet fully dissolved; see the
cold blob in the t = 2000.0 s column in Figure 1,
but eventually it forms a mostly spherical shell
over the following 1000 s around the CO core5.
This shell continuously heats up, mostly because
of the onset of nuclear burning, that is triple-α
reactions. The formation of this He burning layer,
the SoF, is illustrated in Figure 1. At t = 4000 s
the shell has settled into a quasi-equilibrium state
and He burning sustains the temperature at a
constant value of a few 108 K. At this point,the
density has also equilibrated into a steady state
profile.

is mostly depleted. After 1 year, 18O has been

In this zone, we also observe a large initial 12C

3.3.2 Zone 13
Looking at zone 13 (see the middle column of
Table 1), starting with the case without addi-
tional 1H, we find that, apart from longer reac-
tion timescales, the picture is overall similar to
zone 10. 18O is produced from 14N until 14N is
exhausted and eventually 18O is destroyed again
by α-captures to 22Ne. However, there are some
notable differences. Importantly, within the first
1 × 10−2 years there is a strong production of 15N
from 18F, 18O, and 15O. In addition, substantial
amounts of 14C are formed by 14N(n, p)14C. Gen-
erally, neutron and proton capture reactions also
show comparably large fluxes, suggesting that a
large initial abundance of neutrons and protons
remains after the initial merger phase; indeed,
the angle-averaged tracer abundances show an
increase in the proton mass fraction by up to two
orders of magnitude in zone 13 compared to zone
10. This is mostly due to the reduced abundance
of 12C and 16O in zone 13 compared to that in
zone 10, which would otherwise dominate n- and
p-capture reaction, thereby absorbing most of the
free n and p.
After around 1 × 10−2 years, most of these
reactions stop, and the largest fluxes are in the
18O and 22Ne producing reactions. Here, 18O pro-
duction accelerates as it (and 18F) is no longer
destroyed by, e.g. 18O(p, α)15N. Similarly to zone
10, 18F eventually depletes and 18O is only pro-
duced (at a comparably small rate) from 14C until
its exhaustion. The 22Ne production also slows
down significantly once 18O is burned away.
Moving to the case with X(1H) = 0.01, we
see an even larger impact of reactions involv-
ing protons than in zone 10. In the first few
10−5 years, we observe an intense burst of cold
CNO cycling, particularly the first cold CNO cycle
burning 12C to 14N and 15N. After this phase,
we still find many neutron and proton capture
reactions, but at a lower rate. Although severely
depleted by 14C(p, γ)15N, 14C is now produced
in large amounts by 17O(n, α)14C, 14N(n, p)14C,
and to a lesser extent by 13C(n, γ)14C. 18O is pro-
duced in small amounts by residual 18F, which
in turn is produced slowly from
14N. Impor-
tantly, the
18F(n, α)15N flux is comparable to
the
14N(α, γ)18F flux and thus there is barely
any buildup of 18F. Until around 1 × 10−2 years
15N(p, α)12C contributes in large parts to the
production of 12C; afterwards, its production is
dominated again by triple-α reactions. During this
phase, large amounts of 16O are produced as well
by 13C(α, n)16O.
At 1 × 10−3 years, 14C(α, γ)18O becomes the
dominant 18O production pathway, which can be
seen in Figure 3 by the resurgence of 18O that has
been previously destroyed by 18O(p, α)15N. This
is in so far interesting as previous work, e.g., Staff
et al. (2012); Crawford et al. (2020), does not con-
sider 14C and thus does not observe this second
resurgence in 18O.
As described before, these processes eventually
produce 22Ne until 18O is completely depleted.
3.3.3 Zone 16
In zone 16 (see the right column of Table 1), the
picture changes again, not only through longer
reaction timescales, but also the possible reactions
at the comparably low densities and temperatures
found here.
Without additional protons, this zone initially
mostly exhibits
18F(β+)18O with
14N(α, γ)18F
slowly beginning to produce new 18F until around
1 × 10−3 years. This reaction is, however, rela-
tively slow compared to the production of 14N
and does not significantly impact the 14N abun-
dance. In this case the reaction 18O(p, α)15N is
also relatively efficient.
After approximately 1×10−3 years, 18O is pro-
duced at the same rate as it is destroyed. Later, at
1 × 10−2 years, 14C is now produced at a notice-
able rate, further boosting 18O production. Only
after one year does the 18O abundance decrease
through 18O(p, α)15N (instead of α-captures on
22Ne) while 14C gets slowly burnt and the 14C pro-
duction stops. Overall, on the timescales simulated
here, 22Ne is barely produced as the conditions are
insufficient.

Regardless of the additional proton seed, 22Ne

3.3.4
16O/18O ratio
Lastly, we present the evolution of the 16O/18O
ratios over time in our model. Figure 3 shows
the ratios for the zones discussed in the previous
sections and Figure 4 shows the 16O/18O ratios for
all 19 zones highlighted in Figure 1.
Focusing first on Figure 3, we find that in zone
10 a minimal value of 10 is reached; adding an
initial fraction of protons increases this ratio by
almost an order of magnitude due to the destruc-
tion of 18O through 18O(p, α)15N. In both cases,
this minimum is only maintained for a very short
period of time, on the order of a few days to weeks
after the merger. Due to the high temperature and
density, 18O is rapidly burned to 22Ne. It should
be noted that due to the dredge up of 16O dis-
cussed in Section 3.2, the 16O/18O ratio in this
case is increased by this enrichment with 16O.
Zone 13 achieves a 16O/18O ratio close to unity
which would be in the range suggested by Mehla
et al. (2025) to match observed RCB stars. Similar
to zone 10, the X(1H) = 0.01 case does not reach
the same low ratio, and for both cases this phase
does not last very long, at most a few years.
However, in zone 16, the picture is slightly dif-
ferent. First, reactions seem to be slow enough
that a long phase of low 16O/18O — on the order
of centuries — could reasonably be sustained. It
is somewhat difficult to extrapolate, but if the
temperature and density are maintained for long
enough, the values ≲10 seem to be achievable for
both X(1H) cases. Second, in this zone, the pro-
ton rich case reaches lower 16O/18O ratios, at least
temporarily. In contrast to zone 13, where 16O is
produced in the initial phase due to 13C(α, n)16O,
in zone 16 a small amount is destroyed by proton
captures 16O(p, γ)17F. Third, and most impor-
tantly, in zone 16 the conditions seem insufficient
to rapidly burn 18O to 22Ne, suggesting that these
outer zones are the most favorable for reaching
RCB-like 16O/18O ratios on timescales of 102 −
103 years.
This brings us to Figure 4, where this clear
trend towards outer zones is clearly evident. Here,
it can be seen that the inner zones are clearly unfa-
vorable for reaching low 16O/18O ratios. Contam-
ination of 16O, either from dredge up or produced
during the merger, negatively impacts the ratios in
this regime. Moreover, the temperatures and den-
sities cause any 18O to burn to 22Ne too quickly.
The further out one goes, the lower the achieved
16O/18O ratios become (although the lower limit
seems to be on the order of unity) and the produc-
tion timescales seem to be increased. In general,
the addition of protons appears to increase the
overall value of the 16O/18O ratio, before 18O pro-
duction from 14C sets in. In contrast, in the outer
zones ≳15 the initial 1H fraction leads to lower
16O/18O ratios on the timescales studied here.
Although zones out to zone 16 (on the
timescales simulated here) seem to allow for
16O/18O ratios compatible with observed RCB
stars, we find that there is a wide range of
timescales on which suitable values are reached.
The inner zones are clearly too hot to prevent
18O burning to 22Ne, while the outer layers may
not produce it fast enough to explain observed
16O/18O values. The intermediate zones seem the
most likely to produce the correct amounts of 18O
and keep it from burning away for long enough.
4 Discussion
As can be expected from a merger of two WDs,
the process is highly asymmetric and cannot easily
be represented in a 1D model. Although Figure 1
suggests the resultant temperature and density
distribution is well approximated by a spherical
average (at least up to 0.74 M⊙of the total mass),
the nucleosynthetic post-processing discussed in
Section 3.2 reveals that the dynamic history is
clearly imprinted on the abundance of key species,
importantly 14N. Without further mixing, this will

directly impact the resultant yields of 18O, par-
ticularly without additional protons that enable
the production of new 14N. Recent works such
as Shiber et al. (2024); Staff et al. (2018) have
investigated the multi-dimensional properties of
the merger process and merged hybrid WD; how-
ever, they do not present detailed nucleosynthe-
sis results, but rather focus on the impact of
dredge-up. Longland et al. (2011, 2012) employ
tracer particles similar to our approach, but do
not examine isotopic yields with respect to their
asymmetry. Studies that discuss detailed reaction
pathways (e.g., Staff et al. 2012; Menon et al. 2013;
Schwab 2019; Crawford et al. 2020) only consider
a 1D initial abundance distribution. For practi-
cal reasons, this is a sufficient approximation, but
neglects the fact that the initial 14N can vary by
several orders of magnitude if protons are avail-
able. For example, our zone 10 has an initial 14N
mass fraction of a few 10−3 while Figure 1 shows
that initial abundances as low as 10−5 are possible;
the resultant 18O will likely also vary by a similar
amount. Yoon et al. (2007) and Longland et al.
(2011) suggest that the entire burning shell will
quickly become convective; however, while we find
a similar thermal timescale τth ≈109 s, we observe
a nuclear timescale of τnuc ≈104 s, much longer
than their estimated 100 s. Although these num-
bers alone do not allow for a definitive statement
for the timescale on which convection will develop,
they suggest that our model may take longer
before it becomes convective, making the outcome
more sensitive to the asymmetric distribution of
the initial fuel composition. For the purposes of
this exploratory study, we deem the assumption of
constant temperature and density sufficient, as we
are mainly interested in establishing the possible
range of relevant reaction regimes. Future studies
aiming at matching models to observed RCB stars
will need to account for the long term evolution of
the conditions in the SoF.
In contrast to recent studies such as the work of
Staff et al. (2012) (see also Staff et al. 2018; Shiber
et al. 2024), who observe only a very thin SoF with
a width of 1−2×108 cm, our SoF9 is much thicker
on the order of 1 × 109 cm. A likely explanation
for this is that since we consider the energy release
from nuclear burning, a larger area is heated up,
allowing for nuclear reactions to occur. This also
has the consequence that for our simulation a sin-
gle temperature and density average for the entire
SoF would not be a good approximation as in the
case of Staff et al. (2012). We note that although
we additionally consider the energy released from

2018; Shiber et al. 2024 who neglect this), we find
a peak temperature in the SoF of slightly above

ratio q = 0.5 scenario.
Overall, the thicker SoF has two main conse-
quences for the 16O/18O ratio. On the one hand,
compared to other studies, we observe long-term
18O formation in the outer layers and, particularly,
its production in regions too cold and low density
to burn to 22Ne. On the other hand, the dredge-up
of 16O into the accreted material is no longer as
impactful to the 16O/18O ratio. We also observe
some mixing of core material with the accreted
shell (in a roughly 108 cm thick region), but this
layer is not critically relevant to what is likely the
observable surface of the merged object. Both of
these consequences are favorable for achieving a
low 16O/18O ratio for the expected life-time of an
RCB star. However, this picture may change if
convection sets in (or any other mechanism that
changes temperature and density); depending on
the thickness of the convecting layer, parts of the
SoF, if not the entire shell, may become thor-
oughly mixed. We leave a study of the impact of
convection for future work.
Another indirect consequence of the thicker
SoF is the emergence of 14C(α, γ)18O as an addi-
tional, potentially dominant, production channel
for 18O. Other works (e.g. Staff et al. 2012; Craw-
ford et al. 2020) neglect 14C in their networks —
given the conditions considered by them, rightfully
so — and thus overlook its contribution. However,
particularly in the outer shells, where a favor-
able 16O/18O ratio is reached on long timescales
and 18O(α, γ)22Ne is suppressed, 14C becomes an
important source of 18O and should be considered.
In our study, we also find that an initial
source of protons can have a drastic impact on
subsequent nucleosynthesis because it allows, for
example, efficient CNO cycling and 14N produc-
tion. Although other works find similar abundance
fractions for the He WD as the X(1H) = 0.01
explored by us (e.g., 1.51% of Staff et al. 2012), it
is not clear if a homogeneous distribution of pro-
tons is found in nature (e.g., Cunningham et al.
2020). To what extent this surface layer will be
mixed into the SoF during the merger remains
to be seen and will be considered in future stud-
ies. Furthermore, primordial 3He (Vangioni-Flam
et al. 2003) could equally provide protons to the
fuel mixture, ultimately allowing for enhanced 14N
and subsequent 18O production.
Overall, we find that the 18O production is
highly localized. Factors such as the fuel distri-
bution after the merger phase, varying thermo-
dynamic conditions and consequentially different
production channels contribute to a spatially and
temporally varying 16O/18O ratio. In our scenario,
although observed, dredge-up of
16O does not
negatively impact the resultant 16O/18O ratios,
except in a few innermost shells, in which the tem-
peratures are too high to maintain 18O for long
periods of time in any case. For several shells, we
find 16O/18O ratios that fall within the range of
what is observed, that is, around 100 −102 (Mehla
et al. 2025). However, our study neglects the long-
term effects of, for example, convection and mass
loss over time. Therefore, it remains to be seen
whether the static stratification studied here is
representative of nature.
Lastly, it is also not entirely clear which layers
will contribute to observed spectra. Although it is
likely that the outer low 16O/18O layers will be
closer to the photosphere, we refrain from defini-
tive statements and leave an investigation of this
for future work.
5 Conclusion
In this work, we examined the localized 18O pro-
duction in a merger of a 0.6 M⊙CO WD and
a 0.3 M⊙He WD. In Section 3.1 we briefly out-
lined the merger and described the final merged
HeCO WD structure, from which we derived 1D
zones to study their long-term nucleosynthesis.
In Section 3.2, we highlighted the nucleosynthe-
sis results following the hydrodynamic simulation.
Finally, in Section 3.3, we described the detailed
long-term nucleosynthesis based on three charac-
teristic zones in the SoF that illustrate the various
burning regimes. Here, we also discussed the spa-
tially and temporally varying 16O/18O ratios of
our model.
In summary, we draw three main conclu-
sions from this study. First, we demonstrate that
although the final merged HeCO WD at the end
of our hydrodynamic simulation (at 4000 s) is
to good approximation spherically symmetric in
the regions of interest, the dynamic history of
12


## Page 13


the merger process gets imprinted in the com-
position of the SoF, particularly its inner layers,
where nuclear reaction timescales are short. This
will directly impact subsequent nucleosynthesis
unless convection or a similar process can erase
the merger history on relatively short timescales.
Ultimately, this underscores the multidimensional
nature of such mergers.
Second, our study reveals that the SoF can
host a variety of different nucleosynthetic pro-
cesses and so far unexplored reaction channels
such as 14C(α, γ)18O can become the dominant
18O production mechanism, or at least an impor-
tant side channel. Furthermore, nucleosynthesis is
highly sensitive to the initial abundance of pro-
tons, and therefore future studies should take
special care of isotopes such as 1H or 3He.
Third, and most importantly, we find that our
model can produce 16O/18O ratios well within
the observed range and maintain these values
for long periods of time. Our results indicate a
strong time and location dependence of their val-
ues. In particular, we observe a much thicker
SoF than in comparable previous studies, allow-
ing for
14C(α, γ)18O to become the dominant
18O production channel. Lower temperatures and
densities also suppress
18O(α, γ)22Ne, therefore
preventing the destruction of 18O over time.
In this study, we do not consider the impact
of convection and other dynamic effects that can
impact the stratification in the SoF. This may be
an important factor in determining the 16O/18O
ratio in the photosphere that ultimately can be
observed. Future studies should therefore focus
on establishing to what extent such mechanisms
impact the 16O/18O ratio in the observable regions
of the star.
