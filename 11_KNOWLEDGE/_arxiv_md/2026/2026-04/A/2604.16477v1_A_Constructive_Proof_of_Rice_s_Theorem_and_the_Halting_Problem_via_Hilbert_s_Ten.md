Abstract. Rice’s theorem states that no non-trivial semantic property of programs is
decidable. Classical proofs proceed by reduction from the halting problem, invoking the
law of excluded middle (LEM) twice: once through diagonalization, and once through a
case split on whether the always-diverging program ⊥satisfies the property in question. We
present a proof that is constructive relative to the undecidability of Hilbert’s Tenth Problem
(MRDP): valid in intuitionistic logic, requiring neither diagonalization nor self-reference,
and adding no classical reasoning beyond the MRDP assumption itself.
The key idea is a two-witness construction. Given a non-trivial property P, we attach to
each Diophantine polynomial D a pair of programs S0
D, S1
D that behave like the negative
and positive witnesses for P when D is solvable, and both diverge identically when it
is not. A hypothetical decider for P would therefore decide Diophantine solvability via
the difference δD = DecideP (S1
D) −DecideP (S0
D) — contradicting the MRDP theorem.
The argument is structured as two separate implications, never asserting a disjunction
about solvability, and never examining P(⊥). The undecidability of the halting problem
follows as an immediate corollary: a single application of Rice’s theorem to the Terminates
property.
A formalization in the Rocq proof assistant1 confirms both results within a step-indexed
model of computation, with the undecidability of Hilbert’s Tenth Problem as the sole
external axiom.

1. Introduction
Rice’s theorem [Ric53] states that no non-trivial semantic property of programs is decid-
able. We prove it constructively relative to MRDP undecidability: our proof is valid in
intuitionistic logic and adds no classical reasoning beyond the undecidability of Hilbert’s
Tenth Problem. The halting problem [Tur36] is the most famous specific instance: no algo-
rithm decides, for an arbitrary program and input, whether the program halts. Standard
textbook proofs of both results invoke the law of excluded middle (LEM) in essential ways.
For Rice’s theorem, LEM appears in at least two places: through diagonalization, which
requires asserting that a hypothetically constructed program either halts or diverges; and
through a case split on whether the always-diverging program ⊥satisfies the property P
in question — the so-called P(⊥) split. For the halting problem, LEM enters through self-
reference: the diagonal program halts if and only if the hypothetical decider says it does

Why a constructive proof matters. Constructive proofs of undecidability results carry
computational content that classical proofs lack. In intuitionistic type theory and in sys-
tems such as Coq and Agda that are used for program extraction, a classical proof cannot
be directly executed or extracted into a verified program. A constructive proof of Rice’s
theorem would, in principle, allow the undecidability argument to be internalized in such
systems without axioms beyond the undecidability of Hilbert’s Tenth Problem. It also con-
tributes to constructive reverse mathematics, where the goal is to identify precisely which
non-constructive principles are required for each theorem. Furthermore, this proof can be
seen as a non-branching reduction: information is encoded in the relational difference δD
between two programs rather than in a case distinction on a single program, a structural
property that may be of independent interest.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Partial Functions and Convergence. We work in a standard model of partial com-
putable functions.
For a program e and input x, we write φe(x) ↓if the computation
converges, and φe(x) ↑if it diverges. We denote by ⊥the specific program that diverges
on every input, i.e., ∀x : φ⊥(x) ↑. Note that ⊥here denotes a concrete program (e.g.,
the program that runs an infinite loop on every input), not a logical proposition; we use ⊥
exclusively in this sense throughout.
2.2. Constructive Equality of Partial Functions. A central concern in constructive
logic is the meaning of equality for partial functions.
We adopt a step-indexed notion
suitable for formalization.
Definition 2.1 (Observational Equivalence). For a step-indexed model where each program
is a function e : N →(N →option N) (input x then fuel k), two programs e and f are
observationally equivalent, written φe ≃φf, if for every input x there exists a fuel bound
N such that for all k ≥N, e(x, k) = f(x, k).
This definition is strictly constructive: it does not assert φe(x) ↓∨φe(x) ↑for any
particular x; it requires eventual agreement in the step-indexed model.

Remark 2.3 (Scope of the formalization). Proposition 2.2 is a metatheoretic statement
connecting the step-indexed model to classical computability theory.
The Rocq formal-

within the step-indexed model, with no reference to Turing machines or partial recursive
functions. What the formalization proves is: within any model of computation satisfying
the step-indexed interface (Definition 2.1), no non-trivial semantic property is decidable.

Remark 2.4. Observational equivalence is a relation, not a decision procedure. It serves as
a definition of extensional equality in the step-indexed setting. For programs that converge,
it is consistent with Kleene equality: if φe(x) ↓v and φf(x) ↓v′ for all x, then φe ≃φf
implies v = v′. Divergence is handled by requiring eventual agreement of the step-indexed
outputs rather than asserting a disjunction on convergence.

3. The MRDP Theorem and its Constructive Status
The MRDP theorem (Davis–Putnam–Robinson–Matiyasevich, 1970) states that every re-
cursively enumerable set is Diophantine: for any recursively enumerable set S ⊆N, there
exists a polynomial DS(⃗x, y) with integer coefficients such that:
y ∈S ⇐⇒∃⃗x ∈Nk : DS(⃗x, y) = 0.
The construction of DS from a program enumerating S is algorithmic and explicit. No
proof by contradiction or diagonalization is required; the theorem reduces the membership
problem to polynomial arithmetic through a finite, computable sequence of steps.
Remark 3.1 (Constructive status of MRDP). The polynomial construction in MRDP uses
induction and case analysis but no LEM on undecidable propositions; the genuinely classical
step is the undecidability argument, not the polynomial encoding.
Ruitenburg [Rui85]
established the forward direction (solvability from a halting derivation) in intuitionistic
arithmetic with countable choice. For our proof, MRDP enters exactly once via the axiom
H10C SAT undec, proved by Larchey-Wendling and Forster [LWF22] in CIC without classical
logic.
In particular, let K = {e | φe(e)↓} be the halting set. K is recursively enumerable but
not computable. MRDP yields an explicit polynomial DK(⃗x, e) such that:
φe(e)↓⇐⇒∃⃗x ∈Nk : DK(⃗x, e) = 0.
The construction of DK from a Turing machine description is algorithmic: register-machine
transitions are encoded as Diophantine constraints, and their conjunction is folded into a

Theorem 5.1 (Rice’s Theorem, Constructive Version). Let P be a non-trivial semantic
property in the sense of Definition 2.6. Then there is no program DecideP that decides
P: for every program e, DecideP (e) terminates and returns 1 if P(e) holds and 0 if ¬P(e)
holds.

6. Corollary: The Halting Problem
The undecidability of the halting problem follows as an immediate corollary of Theo-
rem 5.1. The instantiation requires ≈40 lines (defining Terminates, diverge, halt, and
Terminates NonTrivial); once those are in hand, the proof of Corollary 6.4 is one line of
Rocq. The corollary adds no new mathematical content beyond the instantiation.
Definition 6.1 (Stable termination). A program e terminates stably on input x if there
exist a fuel level k and a value v such that e(x, k) = Some v and e(x, k′) = Some v for all
k′ ≥k.
Remark 6.2 (Stable termination and the classical halting problem). In the step-indexed
model, stable termination and classical halting coincide: a program halts (stably) on input x
if and only if some fuel level returns Some v. The forward direction holds because the model
is monotone (find sol stable): once a value is returned, all higher fuel levels return the
same value. The backward direction is immediate from the definition of stable termination.
Consequently, the undecidability of Terminates established by Corollary 6.4 implies the
undecidability of ordinary halting on input 0.
Definition 6.3 (Terminates). The property Terminates is defined by: Terminates(e) ⇔
e terminates stably on input 0.
Terminates is a semantic property in the sense of Definition 2.6: if e ≃f (observational
equivalence) then Terminates(e) ⇔Terminates(f). It is non-trivial, witnessed by:
e0 = diverge := λx. λk. None
(never terminates)
e1 = halt := λx. λk. Some 0
(terminates stably at all fuel levels)

Remark 6.5 (Historical context and novelty). Turing’s 1936 proof of the halting problem’s
undecidability uses diagonalization: it constructs a program that halts if and only if a
hypothetical decider says it does not, deriving a contradiction by self-application. That
argument invokes excluded middle (the self-referential program either halts or it does not)
and is therefore not valid in intuitionistic logic.
Existing constructive proofs of the halting problem in Rocq (e.g., Ciaffaglione [CG04],
and the coq-library-undecidability [LWF22]) take the halting problem as a seed: they
prove it undecidable first (typically by diagonalization over a Turing machine model) and
then derive all other undecidability results by reduction from it.
Corollary 6.4 reverses this direction. The halting problem is derived as a consequence
of Theorem 5.1, which is itself derived from the MRDP theorem. The undecidability of
halting flows from the intractability of Diophantine arithmetic rather than from a self-

7. The Decidability Frontier for Bounded Programs
The undecidability established by Theorem 5.1 is an asymptotic result: it concerns programs
with no bound on computation time or state space. In practice, programs are often analyzed
under explicit resource bounds, which restores decidability. For programs bounded to ter-
minate within T steps over state spaces of size ≤N, semantic properties become decidable
by exhaustive enumeration — the foundation of bounded model checking.
Our reduction induces a natural degree hierarchy on Rice’s theorem via the degree of
the Diophantine witness polynomial.
Definition 7.1 (Rice degree). Let Rice(d) denote the class of non-trivial semantic prop-
erties separable by a two-witness construction (Definition 4.2) whose Diophantine witness
polynomial has degree ≤d. Write Rice(∞) for the unrestricted class. The degree measures
the minimal Diophantine complexity required to witness the undecidability of P.
Corollary 7.2 (Degree-4 saturation). Rice(4) = Rice(∞).
Proof. Matiyasevich’s MRDP encoding [Mat70] represents every r.e. set as the solution set
of a degree-≤4 polynomial. The witness polynomial in the two-witness construction for
any P can therefore be taken as the MRDP polynomial for K = {n | ϕn(n)↓}, which has
degree ≤4.
Remark 7.3 (Mechanization status). Corollary 7.2 is proved mathematically above but is
not yet mechanized in rice.v. The Rocq formalization establishes Rice(∞) without the
degree bound; the saturation result could be added as a Rocq corollary in a future revision.
We therefore have Rice(2) ⊆Rice(3) ⊆Rice(4) = Rice(∞), with the left inclusions open.
Whether Rice(3) = Rice(4) depends on whether every r.e. set has a degree-3 Diophantine
representation, an open problem in Diophantine complexity [Mat93].1
To see that the left inclusions are not trivially collapsed by a change of witnesses,
consider Rice(2): the class of properties witnessable by a degree-≤2 Diophantine polynomial.
The equation D(x, y) = x2+y2−n = 0 has degree 2, and its solvability (whether n is a sum of
two squares) is decidable — so no undecidability argument can be anchored there. A witness
polynomial for an undecidable property must encode a Turing-complete computation, which
known MRDP encodings achieve only at degree ≥4. Whether degree 3 suffices, and whether
degree 2 can ever encode an undecidable property, are the core open questions behind the
left inclusions.
Two further directions are noted briefly: the Sb

transfer to a rational-coefficient Rice theorem with no modification to the proof; and merg-

The synthetic approach to computability, developed by Forster et al. [FKS19] in Coq

Our proof falls in the weakest constructive category: valid in intuitionistic predicate
logic with the metatheoretic assumptions on MRDP stated in Section 3, and no further
classical principles.
The MRDP theorem and constructive arithmetic. The Davis–Putnam–Robinson–
Matiyasevich theorem [DPR61, Mat70] is classical in its original formulation. Its construc-
tive status was first analysed by Ruitenburg [Rui85], who showed it holds in intuitionistic
arithmetic with the axiom of choice for numbers. Cornaros and Esbelin [CE98] provide
a detailed treatment of the constructive content of Hilbert’s Tenth Problem, establishing
the precise fragment of intuitionistic arithmetic required. A full mechanization of MRDP in
Rocq has been given by Larchey-Wendling and Forster [LWF22], confirming its constructive
validity in Coq’s type theory. Our proof takes MRDP undecidable as an axiom that acts as a
pointer to that mechanization; it does not depend on the details of the constructivization be-
yond the existence of the many-one reduction from K to Diophantine solvability. Crucially,
LW H10c undec is a negative proposition (¬ ∃f, . . .); its proof in CIC proceeds by intuitionis-
tically valid reductio (deriving ⊥from the assumed decider) without invoking LEM on any
Diophantine instance. This is the precise sense in which the axiom is compatible with the
purely intuitionistic metatheory of the rest of the development.

reasoning about recursive types, and have since become a standard tool for mechanized
semantics. Our use of the step-indexed model is more elementary: we require only that

Forster, Kirst, and Smolka [FKS19] also formalize Rice’s theorem in Coq as part of that

This construction requires
two classical steps.

on a diagonalization argument. Second, the direction of the reduction depends on whether
P(⊥) holds, so their proof performs an explicit case split on P(⊥) ∨¬P(⊥), invoking LEM.
Their formalization uses the Classical library throughout.

Diophantine polynomial D and a bit b alone — no self-reference, no reference to e, no
fixed-point argument. The P(⊥) case split is avoided by design: when D is unsolvable, S0

Bounded decidability and model checking. The connection between bounded program
analysis and decidability has been studied extensively in the model checking literature.
Clarke et al. [CHVB18] survey bounded model checking and its completeness thresholds.
The relationship between the complexity of semantic properties and the parameters (T, N)
of the bounded model (termination steps and state space size) is closely related to questions
in descriptive complexity studied by Immerman [Imm99].
