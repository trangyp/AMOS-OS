ABSTRACT
Aims. The gravo-turbulent fragmentation of the interstellar medium is expected to create a hierarchical cascade of cloud structures, crossing the
scales from core to disk. We aim to predict how the currently observed top-heavy core mass function (CMF) in the massive protocluster W43-MM1
evolves due to core subfragmentation.
Methods. We used the getsf algorithm to extract sources in five ALMA images of W43-MM1 at 3 mm, with a spatial resolution ranging from
14 kau to 270 au. Then, we applied FAMILY, a graph-theory-based analysis tool, to create and characterize networks of nested sources in W43-
MM1. We compared the hierarchical fragmentation cascade of W43-MM1 to those measured in the NGC 2264 protocluster and in synthetic
images of an Orion-like protocluster simulated by magneto-hydrodynamical calculations.
Results. Assuming self-similarity, we measure a small fractality index of F3D = 1.19 ± 0.10 in W43-MM1, which means that, on average, a
cloud structure will fragment into only 1.19 fragments each time the physical scale decreases by a factor of two. We estimate an imbalanced mass
partition between siblings, with 2
3 of the mass of siblings at a given scale belonging to the dominant sibling. The mass transfer efficiency, computed
from one physical scale to another, is high and corresponds to a core formation efficiency (CFE) from 2400 au to 200 au of ∼16%.
Conclusions. Based on the fractality and efficiency values measured in W43-MM1, the gravo-turbulent model by Thomasson et al. predicts that its
fragmentation below ∼14 kau is not driven by turbulence but by gravity. Using these parameters and the measured mass partition, we demonstrate
that the fragment mass function, from which the the initial mass function (IMF) emerges, has a high-mass end which remains top-heavy. Therefore,
core subfragmentation in W43-MM1, and perhaps more broadly in massive Galactic protoclusters, plays a minimal role in the IMF origin.

1. Introduction
Understanding star formation requires addressing the challenge
of determining how the discrete distribution of stars emerges
from the continuous distribution of gas in molecular clouds.
Most analytical models that have been proposed to describe star
formation are based on the interplay between supersonic turbu-
lence, magnetic field, and gravity; they are called gravo-turbulent
models (e.g., Hennebelle & Chabrier 2008; Hopkins 2012). In
line with the fact that gravity and incompressible turbulence are
scale-free processes, studies of the interstellar medium (whether
atomic, ionized, or molecular) first showed that it exhibits frac-
tal properties (e.g., Elmegreen et al. 2001; Hennebelle & Fal-
garone 2012). Subsequent studies have revealed that the molec-
ular medium is better described by a superposition of several
hierarchies of structures that coexist at all scales, and therefore
a multifractal model (Falgarone et al. 2004; Miville-Deschênes
et al. 2007; Robitaille et al. 2020). This multifractality can be
explained by the emergence of physical processes associated
with star formation, as evidenced by the appearance of a net-
work of dense, filamentary structures coherent over a range of
physical scales down to the scale of cores (Elia et al. 2018; Ro-
bitaille et al. 2019). These processes include turbulence intermit-
tency, ambipolar diffusion, conservation of angular momentum,
changes in the equation of state, and stellar feedback, such as
heating and compression (e.g., Girart et al. 2013; Robitaille et al.
2020; Thomasson et al. 2024). The fragmentation cascade asso-
ciated with these multifractal properties could therefore change,
at small scales in the densest cloud areas where optical depth in-
creases, and rotation and magnetic fields intensify (e.g., Thomas-
son et al. 2024). It could also change close to new-born stars,
which heat their surrounding gas, drive outflows and potentially
H ii regions. The multifractal characteristics of molecular clouds
have only recently begun to be investigated (e.g., Elia et al. 2018;
Pokhrel et al. 2018; Robitaille et al. 2019; Thomasson et al.
2024), and further studies are needed to understand how these
characteristics vary during the star formation process and within
star-forming clouds.
In the fragmentation cascade associated with the multifrac-
tal structure of clouds, the gas mass reservoirs used to form in-
dividual stars or close binaries are generally called cores (e.g.,
McKee & Ostriker 2007). Their exact definition is the subject of
heated debate, particularly regarding the formation of high-mass
stars and whithin synthetic protoclusters (e.g., Smith et al. 2009;
Motte et al. 2018a; Louvet et al. 2021; Pelkonen et al. 2021). Ob-
servationally, cores are identified as dense, gravitationally bound
cloud structures with sizes ranging from ∼10 kau to ∼2 kau de-

to form high-mass stars (e.g., André et al. 2000; Motte et al.
2018b). In nearby, low-mass star-forming regions initially stud-
ied, the mass distribution of cores (the core mass function, or
CMF) resembles the initial mass function (IMF) of stars (e.g.,
Motte et al. 1998; Enoch et al. 2008; Könyves et al. 2015). Since
the shape of the CMF was not observed to vary much, the com-
munity considered that the CMF followed the canonical shape
of the IMF or, at least, its high-mass end (e.g., Offner et al.
2014; Hennebelle & Grudi´c 2024). This led to the interpreta-
tion that cores were the direct progenitors of stars (e.g., André
et al. 2000). However the putative direct link between cores and
stars is now being questioned, in particular in dynamical proto-
clusters. Two main processes have been identified that compli-
cate this link: the growth of core mass through gas inflows (e.g.,
Csengeri et al. 2014; Álvarez-Gutiérrez et al. 2024; Sandoval-
Garrido et al. 2025; Beuther et al. 2025) and the subfragmenta-
tion of cores (e.g., Brogan et al. 2016; Olguin et al. 2022; Yoo
et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2026).
More recently, the ALMA-IMF Large Program (Motte et al.
2022) discovered that young massive protoclusters, including
W43-MM1, had top-heavy CMFs, meaning they had an excess
of high-mass cores relative to low-mass cores (Pouteau et al.
2022; Nony et al. 2023; Louvet et al. 2024, Cunningham et al.
in prep.). These results are consistent with those of the ALMA-
IMF pilot program (Motte et al. 2018b) and other large programs
(Coletta et al. 2025; Morii et al. 2026), which call into question
the universality of the CMF. Regarding the IMF, for a long time
its shape has not been observed to vary much, so the commu-
nity assumed it was universal (Bastian et al. 2010; Offner et al.
2014). However, some top-heavy IMFs have recently been ob-
served in young massive clusters (see Lu et al. 2013; Maia et al.
2016; Schneider et al. 2018; Hosek et al. 2019) and some theo-
retical arguments favor the non-universality of the IMF (Kroupa
et al. 2013; Hopkins 2018). While the IMF of stars forming
in the massive protoclusters observed with ALMA remains un-
known, studying the effects of core subfragmentation and core
mass growth on the CMF is crucial. Previous studies have re-
lied on stellar multiplicity prescriptions (e.g., Swift & Williams
2008; Clark & Whitworth 2021) that may not accurately depict
core subfragmentation because stellar multiplicity is the result of
the core evolution into stars followed by stellar dynamics.
It is challenging to account for the effect of core subfragmen-
tation because it requires the use of stochastic properties mea-
sured in clouds to make deterministic predictions regarding how
many star-forming fragments a core will host and the mass of
each gas reservoir. Thus far, only a few studies have been ded-
icated to the cloud fragmentation cascade, and they have barely
constrained some of the properties of core fragmentation (e.g.,
Pokhrel et al. 2018; Thomasson et al. 2022; Beuther et al. 2025).
Concurrently, fragmentation studies conducted across two phys-
ical scales provided constraints that depend heavily on the se-
lected scales and the completeness level of the observations,
which restricts the investigation of physical effects on fragmen-
tation (e.g., Bontemps et al. 2010; Palau et al. 2013; Traficante
et al. 2023; Morii et al. 2024). The present study uses the multi-
resolution database constructed for the W43-MM1 protoclus-
ter, which has a top-heavy CMF (see Sect. 2). With the FAM-
ILY hierarchy analysis tool presented in Sect. 3, we character-
ize its fragmentation cascade, which range from above to below
2.4 kau, which is the cores’ scale in W43-MM1 (see Sect. 4).
Then in Sect. 5, we compare the characteristics of various frag-
mentation studies and predict the IMF that emerges from the top-
heavy CMF of W43-MM1. Finally, Sect. 6 summarizes the main
findings of the paper.
2. Database: images and source catalogs
The present study makes use of five images of the W43-MM1
protocluster, which trace its cloud structure from ∼14 kau to
∼0.27 kau (see Sects. 2.1–2.2). For testing purposes, four im-
ages of two synthetic protoclusters from numerical simulations
are jointly analyzed (see Sect. 2.3). Figures 1 and A.1 display
the five W43-MM1 images that we used in the present analysis.
2.1. Data reduction of the ALMA images targeting W43-MM1
We used three different datasets, each obtained with ALMA at
3 mm and covering the central 1.6 pc × 1.6 pc part of the W43-
MM1 protocluster. The first dataset comes from the ALMA-
IMF Large Program, which produced a large mosaic of the
W43-MM1 protocluster in Cycle 5, with a spectral coverage of
91.7−105.5 GHz (#2017.1.01355.L, PIs: Motte, Ginsburg, Lou-
vet, Sanhueza, see Motte et al. 2022, Paper I). The other two
datasets correspond to a single field, with a primary beam of
∼60′′, centered on the brightest ALMA-IMF cores with cov-
erage at 3 mm of 84.1 −99.9 GHz: one from ALMA Cy-
cle 6 and the other from ALMA Cycle 7 (#2018.1.01787.S and
#2019.1.00502.S, PI: Louvet). We used the 3 mm continuum im-
age of ALMA-IMF, which was reduced using CASA1 as de-
scribed in companion paper, Paper II (Ginsburg et al. 2022).
With a robust parameter of 0, the mean resulting angular reso-
lution is ∼0.43′′, corresponding to ∼2.4 kau, adopting a distance
of 5.5±0.4 kpc (Zhang et al. 2014). The other 3 mm images
produced by the ALMA-IMF consortium2 do not have signifi-
cantly different angular resolutions to be useful for our hierar-
chical analysis.
With the same attention to sensitivity, angular resolution, and
line contamination, the Cycle 6 and Cycle 7 data were processed
with IMAGER3. We used the two continuum bands (bandwidth
of 1.875 GHz centered at ∼85 GHz and ∼99 GHz) with a filter
to remove the regions of the spectrum contaminated by spectral
lines above the 1σ noise level (typically 50 mK and 4-5 K for
Cycle 6 and Cycle 7 data, respectively). We applied phase and
amplitude self-calibration and the cleaning was performed us-
ing Clark deconvolution. With natural and uniform weightings
(corresponding to robust 2 and −2 parameters), Cycle 6 data pro-
vided images with a mean spatial resolution of ∼2.6′′ and ∼1.45′′
(i.e., ∼14 kau and ∼8 kau), respectively. With the same weight-
ings, Cycle 7 images have a ∼0.12′′ and ∼0.050′′ (i.e., ∼650 au
and ∼270 au) resolution, respectively. The basic characteristics
of the five observational images, including their spatial resolu-
tion and noise levels, are listed in Table 1.

emission is largely dominated by dust thermal emission, rather
than free-free, and more likely remains optically thin than at
1 mm. Consistent with this, companion paper, Paper XX (Yoo

2.2. Compact sources in W43-MM1
Since our goal is to extract sources with different character-
istic sizes in each image, we chose to use a software pack-
age that identifies and characterizes emission peaks: getsf
(Men’shchikov 2021). The size of the extracted sources is lim-
ited by their structured background and neighboring sources.
Therefore, as discussed in Louvet et al. (2021), the size distri-
bution and thus the flux distribution of sources is directly related
to the angular resolution of the image. Using this property, getsf
has already been used to characterize the hierarchical structure of
a cloud associated with a protocluster (Thomasson et al. 2022).
We used getsf (v240325) for each of the five images of W43-
MM1 with angular resolutions of 2.6′′, 1.45′′, 0.43′′, 0.12′′, and
0.050′′.
In detail, the getsf software package employs a spatial de-
composition of the observed images to better isolate various spa-
tial scales and separate the structural components of relatively
round sources and elongated filaments from each other and from
the background. This method has a single free parameter, the
maximum size of the sources to be extracted. In order to focus
on compact sources, we kept this parameter small, that is three
times the beam of each image. The detection provides a first-
order estimate of source footprints, sizes, and fluxes. As a sec-
ond step, robust measurements of the sizes and fluxes of sources
are made on background-subtracted images. The resulting cat-
alog contains, for each source, its coordinates, the parameters
of its Gaussian Full Width at Half maximum (FWHM) elliptical
size, its peak, and integrated fluxes. getsf extracted between 3
to 71 sources in the W43-MM1 images at 3 mm (see Table 1).
The number of sources is obviously lower in the lowest angular
resolution images, that is those from Cycle 6, because all of our
input images cover the same spatial area. The number of sources
is also lower in images that were cleaned with uniform weight-
ing because the angular resolution is improved at the expense
of sensitivity. That is particularly obvious for the best-angular
Cycle 7 image, which has a resolution of 0.050′′ (see Table 1).
We will address this issue in Sect. 4. Although the W43-MM1
individual images have been observed and reduced differently,
interferometric filtering is not expected to significantly affect the
flux estimates of compact sources extracted in W43-MM1 by
getsf (see, e.g., Appendix A of Pouteau et al. 2022). Table B.1
lists the characteristics of each source extracted by getsf.
The images targeting the central part of the W43-MM1 pro-
tocluster have spatial resolutions of 14 kau, 8 kau, 2.4 kau,
0.65 au, and 0.27 au. In the following, we will call ‘clumps’
sources extracted in the two lowest angular resolution images
of Cycle 6 because their typical deconvolved sizes range from
5 kau to 30 kau is larger than typical core sizes in massive pro-
toclusters (see Motte et al. 2018a). The sources of the ALMA-
IMF 3 mm image have the size of cores, as proposed by previ-
ous ALMA-IMF papers (e.g., Papers I, III and XV, Motte et al.
2022; Pouteau et al. 2022; Louvet et al. 2024). We refer as ‘frag-
ments’ the sources extracted in the highest spatial resolution im-
ages of Cycle 7 because they have typical deconvolved size range
from 1000 au to 150 au. They are quoted as Pre/protostellar
objects (PPOs) by Yoo et al. (2025) when identified in images
at 100 −300 au resolutions. In the present paper, we assume
that protostellar fragments remain dynamically coupled to their
parental cloud hierarchy. This occurs in numerical simulations
when a cloud is forming and stellar dynamics are not yet signif-
icant (Bernard et al. 2025). This situation is highly probable in
the W43-MM1 protocluster because it is young, and its cloud is
still collapsing globally (Motte et al. 2003; Nguyen Luong et al.
2013, Veyry et al. in prep.).
The prestellar versus protostellar nature of the sources ex-
tracted in these five 3 mm images is not easily defined. In partic-
ular, the Cycle 7 dataset is not sensitive enough (see Sect. 2.1) to
conduct a thorough survey of protostellar outflows or trace many
hot core lines. We can only rely on the ALMA-IMF outflow sur-
veys (including Papers V, X, and XVII, Nony et al. 2020, 2023;
Armante et al. 2024; Valeille-Manet et al. 2025, Nony et al. in
prep.) and hot core surveys (Papers IV and XI, Brouillet et al.
2022; Bonfand et al. 2024), which were performed at the core
scale. Since the determination of the prestellar or protostellar na-
ture of the sources is incomplete in the five W43-MM1 catalogs,
we will not attempt below to use this characterization to separate
the structures according to their nature.
2.3. Synthetic database: compact source catalogs
For test and initial comparison purposes, we used source cat-
alogs derived from synthetic images of numerical simulations
designed to represent the Orion protocluster (105 M⊙within
∼(20 pc)3, Louvet et al. 2021). The formation of this protoclus-
ter was simulated by solving magneto-hydrodynamical (MHD)
and hydrodynamical (HD) equations with the RAMSES code
(see Ntormousi & Hennebelle 2019). An isothermal equation of
state was used, as well as no recipes of stellar feedback. Lou-
vet et al. (2021) simulated the 250 µm observation at 9′′ −72′′
resolutions of a cloud located at 1.75 kpc. We rescaled these ob-
servations to those of an imaginary cloud located at 550 pc. This
allows us to study smaller physical scales than in Louvet et al.
(2021) while remaining at scales larger than 5 kau, where the
ideal MHD and an isothermal equation of state are valid. We
also changed the synthetic observation frequency to remain in
the optical thinness regime assumed by Louvet et al. (2021). To
simulate the 3 mm emission of these synthetic clouds, we di-
vided their 250 µm fluxes by a factor of 1.2×103, corresponding
to the flux ratio expected for a 20 K modified blackbody with a
dust emissivity law of κ(ν) ∝ν1.5.
Using getsf, Louvet et al. (2021) extracted between 14 to 452
sources from these two synthetic images (see Table 1). The HD
simulations of Ntormousi & Hennebelle (2019) provide a larger
number of compact sources, ∼7 times more than MHD simu-
lations (Louvet et al. 2021), but they are less suited to represent
the complex physics of the W43-MM1 massive protocluster. The
synthetic source catalogs are employed here to characterize the
fragmentation cascade above the scale of cores and to verify the
reliability of W43-MM1 measurements conducted with limited
statistics. Numerical simulations solving non-ideal MHD equa-
tions, using radiative transfer, and including stellar feedback will
be necessary to probe smaller spatial scales.

4. Characterization of the hierarchical
fragmentation cascade
Here, we use FAMILY to analyze the hierarchical fragmenta-
tion cascade of the W43-MM1 protocluster and the two syn-
thetic protoclusters. We identify their multi-scale network of
sources in Sect. 4.1 and characterize their fragmentation cascade
in Sects. 4.2–4.3. Table 2 lists all the parameters derived in this
way for W43-MM1 and the synthetic protoclusters of Sect. 2.3,
along with those estimated by Thomasson et al. (2022, 2024) for
the NGC 2264 protocluster.
4.1. Networks of nested sources
We applied the FAMILY analysis tool of Thomasson et al.
(2022) to create a parental link between the compact sources
of each W43-MM1 catalog and of each catalog of the two syn-
thetic protoclusters, respectively. Table B.1 is complemented by
the source list corresponding to the ascending FAMILY tree of
each W43-MM1 source detected in Sect. 2.2.
In W43-MM1, we defined 34 structures, including nine hi-
erarchical, 18 linear, and seven isolated (see Table 2). Figures 4
and A.2 display the nine hierarchical structures of W43-MM1
and provide a representation of their source network. The hierar-
chical structures of W43-MM1 contain three to 73 sources. The
main hierarchical structure of W43 contains ∼40% of the sources
discovered in this massive protocluster and presents characteris-
tics that dominate those of the entire protocluster. Figure A.3 dis-
plays the spatial distribution of all structures identified in W43-
MM1 compared to those of the MHD synthetic protocluster. The
W43-MM1 protocluster consists of a cluster of rich, hierarchical
structures associated with the main clump, sometimes called a
ridge or hub. In numerical simulations, hierarchical structures
are located along the densest filaments.

4.3. Mass transfer through the fragmentation cascade
Beyond the fractality index measured in Sect. 4.2, characteriz-
ing the fragmentation cascade requires estimating two parame-
ters defined in Sect. 3 (see also Fig. 3): the mass transfer effi-
ciency, ϵjump2, and the mass partition, MP(Primary; Secondary).
As demonstrated by Thomasson et al. (2026), these parameters
are among the critical ones for the evolution of the CMF into an
IMF.
The source catalogs of Table B.1 provide source fluxes at
varying frequencies, rather than masses. For a theoretical cloud
with constant dust temperature and emissivity, as well as opti-
cally thin emission at the same frequency at all probed scales, the
source fluxes are proportional to the source masses. This is the
case for the synthetic catalogs of Sect. 2.3 because the radiative
transfer applied by Louvet et al. (2021) to the large-scale cloud
structures traced in the numerical simulations of Ntormousi &
Hennebelle (2019) makes these simple assumptions. Therefore,
the distributions of flux transfer efficiency and flux partition that
we measure for these simulations perfectly correspond to the dis-
tributions of mass transfer efficiency and mass partition. The sit-
uation is much more complex for real observations, such as those
of Sect. 2.2, or for synthetic simulations tracing smaller physical
scales, for several reasons. First, as shown by Yoo et al. (2025),
sources detected at 100-300 au scales in massive protoclusters
exhibit partially optically thick emission even at 3 mm. Second,
dust emissivity is expected to vary from one frequency to an-
other and from one source to another, particularly as a function
of source density and temperature. Lastly for W43-MM1, even
if source temperatures could be extrapolated from the relations
proposed by companions papers, Papers XII and XVI (Dell’Ova
et al. 2024; Motte et al. 2025), they would be highly uncertain
for fragments whose prestellar versus protostellar nature has yet
to be determined. Therefore, we consider the distributions of flux
transfer efficiency and flux partition in the fragmentation cascade
of the W43-MM1 protocluster with utmost caution.
Figure A.5 shows the distributions of mass transfer efficiency
directly computed from the flux transfer efficiency measured in
the two synthetic protoclusters. Some values of these distribu-
tions exceed ϵjump2 = 100%. Since the parental links built in
Sect. 4.1 are constructed under the assumption that at least 75%
of a child area overlaps with its parent area (see Sect. 3), flux
and thus mass efficiency values greater than one can exist. These
cases may correspond to situations in which the gas reservoirs of
children extend beyond those of their immediate parental struc-
tures, as in the clump-fed accretion scenario (see, e.g., Motte
et al. 2018a; Peretto et al. 2020). The efficiency distributions of
the MHD synthetic protocluster is fitted using a Gaussian with
ϵjump2 ≃60% ± 25%. Specifically, the mass transfer efficiency
increases when focusing on the densest sources of the synthetic
images. It also slightly increases when considering in Fig. A.5,
the population of child sources and their direct parents identified
at increasingly higher resolutions. In the W43-MM1 protoclus-
ter, we could not properly characterize the mass transfer effi-
ciency distribution. This is due to the small number of sources
over a six-times-larger scale span and the reasons given above.
The median value of the flux transfer efficiency in W43-MM1,
measured as ∼60%, is a lower limit since the W43-MM1 cata-
logs are not expected to be as complete as in numerical simula-
tions and some fluxes measured at high-resolution could be op-
tically thick, thus underestimating flux ratios of children. There-
fore, we hereafter assumed that the mass transfer efficiency is
ϵjump2 = 60% ± 25% in W43-MM1.
The 3D fractality measured in the W43-MM1 protocluster is
F3D ≃1.19 ± 0.10 (see Sect. 4.2). This means that, for every
jump in physical scales of a factor of two, one finds two children
in a parental structure one-fifth of the time. As shown in Sect. 3
and Fig. 3, the mass partition between the dominant (primary)
child and the other (secondary) child can be estimated by the
mass ratio between these two children. The median of the flux
ratios measured in W43-MM1 is ∼1.95. If we ignore tempera-
ture variations between children of the same parental structure,
the median mass ratio would then be γ ≃1.95. The mass parti-
tion of fragments in W43-MM1 could therefore be imbalanced,
MP(Primary; Secondary) ≃[ 2
3 ; 1
3], and similar to those found in
the synthetic protoclusters examined here (see Table 2).
In summary, we showed that, in W43-MM1 and for every
two-fold decrease in physical scales, parental structures should
host single children four-fifths of the time and two children the
other one-fifth of the time. Although the measured fluxes are not
directly proportional to the masses, the mass transfer efficiency is
expected to be high, and the mass partition is likely imbalanced
(see Table 2).
5. Discussion
In Sect. 4, we characterized the fragmentation cascade devel-
oping in the W43-MM1 protocluster. Section 5.1 compares this
cascade with those measured in other protoclusters. Section 5.2
then discusses the resulting constraints on fragmentation levels
and core formation efficiency (CFE), as defined below, within
the framework of values reported in the literature. Section 5.3 fi-
nally predicts the mass function of gas reservoirs for star forma-
tion in the W43-MM1 protocluster. This is done by simulating
the transfer of gas mass from cores identified at 2400 au (Nony
et al. 2023) to fragments 200 au in size.
5.1. Fragmentation cascades
This study is the second to examine the fragmentation cascade of
a protocluster in detail. Thomasson et al. (2022) applied FAM-
ILY to the intermediate-mass protocluster NGC 2264, which
was imaged at scales ranging from 26 to 1.4 kau. These scales
are 2−5 times larger than those studied in Sect. 4, 14−0.27 kau,
for the W43-MM1 protocluster. Like for synthetic protoclusters,
the fragmentation cascade of NGC 2264 traces the fragmenta-
tion down to the core scale according to the definition by, for
example, Motte et al. (2018a). Characterizing the fragmentation
cascades of both NGC 2264 and synthetic protoclusters could
in principle enable the study of turbulent fragmentation. Study-
ing the fragmentation cascade of W43-MM1, on the other hand,
could allow us to investigate two fragmentation regimes: one
mainly driven by turbulence and the other by gravity, respec-
tively at scales larger and smaller than the core scale.
As shown in Table 2, the W43-MM1 protocluster has twice
as many hierarchical structures, percentage-wise, as the syn-
thetic MHD protocluster (see Table 2). Conversely, it has less
isolated structures. The hierarchical structures of W43-MM1 are
also richer than those of the synthetic MHD protocluster; they
contain 3 −73 versus 3 −46 sources. As suggested by the
equivalently small number of hierarchical structures found in
NGC 2264 (see Table 2), this could be related to the fact that the

reason is that W43-MM1 is undergoing a starburst event, which
results in an intense star formation activity concentrated in space
and time (Motte et al. 2003; Nguyen Luong et al. 2013). Con-

to the low sensitivity of the observations, at least not at the 0.43′′
scale corresponding to the deep ALMA-IMF mosaic (Nony et al.

The 3D fractality index of W43-MM1 is smaller than that
measured in the synthetic MHD protocluster: F3D = 1.19 ± 0.10
and F3D = 1.47 ± 0.15, respectively (see Table 2). It is also
smaller than the measurement made in the intermediate-mass
protocluster NGC 2264, F3D = 1.70 ± 0.24 (Thomasson et al.
2024). To obtain similar values in W43-MM1, the analysis must
focus on the largest scales, F3D = 1.55 ± 0.09 at 5 −14 kau
scales (see Sect. 4.2), or the lowest density regions (cyan rect-
angular area in Fig. 6). This may seem contradictory to the re-
sults of Thomasson et al. (2022), which suggest that most lin-
ear structures, therefore with F3D = 1, are found in the lower-
density outskirts of the cloud. As mentioned earlier, no sources
are yet present in the outskirts of the W43-MM1 starburst pro-
tocluster. Therefore, the low fractality values we have measured
in the densest regions of W43-MM1 must reflect a change in
the physical processes, likely associated with the magnetic field
and stellar feedback. Like shown in numerical simulations as a
whole, the smaller number of total sources in the MHD synthetic
protocluster compared to the HD protocluster illustrates how the
magnetic field organizes the interstellar medium by concentrat-
ing its gas in specific areas. Although it is unclear whether the
small fractality index measured in W43-MM1, F3D = 1.19±0.10
at 0.27 −5 kau scales, reflects the suppression of fragmenta-
tion by the magnetic fields or tidal interactions, Valeille-Manet
et al. (subm) measured rather high values of the magnetic field
strength in the plane of the sky, up to 50 mG on the ∼2.4 kau
scale of the W43-MM1 cores.
The mass transfer efficiency of the MHD synthetic proto-
cluster is high, ϵjump2 ≃60% ± 25%, and tends to increase with
density or decreasing scale (see Sect. 4.3). If confirmed, this
trend would be consistent with the quasi-linear dependence of
star formation efficiency with gas density at 0.1 −1 pc scales
(Louvet et al. 2014) and the correlation of CFE with clump den-
sity (Bontemps et al. 2010; Palau et al. 2013) or accretion rate
with core mass (Wells et al. 2024; Morii et al. 2026). Regard-
ing the mass partition, MP(Primary; Secondary) ≃[ 2

measurement in W43-MM1 is consistent with those proposed
by Pouteau et al. (2022) and Yoo et al. (2025), on the basis of
flux ratios measured in high-mass core systems of massive pro-

a more isolated, massive prestellar core (Sanhueza et al. 2025).
Unbalanced mass partitions are predicted in competitive accre-
tion models, some of which are based on tidal-lobe accretion
rates (e.g., Clark & Whitworth 2021). In fact, competitive accre-
tion is a process that should develop in massive protoclusters,
like W43-MM1 and W51-IRS2, where star formation occurs in
local bursts.
5.2. Fragmentation levels, CFE, and fragmentation regime
The fractality index of Table 2 enables the computation of the
fragmentation or multiplicity level of a parental structure, de-
fined as the number of fragments it contains. Several studies of
high-mass star-forming regions have focused on fragmentation
from the clump to the core scales (e.g., Bontemps et al. 2010;
Sanhueza et al. 2019; Elia et al. 2026; Morii et al. 2026). More
recently, a couple of studies characterized the fragmentation
from the core to the fragment scales (Budaiev et al. 2024; Yoo
et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2026). To compare these studies with each
other, as well as with our own study, we must rescale the number
of fragments to a given jump in physical scales, as is done when
computing a fractality index. Using Eq. (1) and the F3D = 1.19
of W43-MM1, we estimate that, on average, a clump detected at
a scale of sclump = 30 kau will fragment into ncore ≃2.0 cores of
scale score = 2 kau. On the other hand, a core will fragment into
nfrag ≃1.8 fragments of scale sfrag = 0.2 kau. The ncore number
is, within a factor of two, equal to the number of cores detected in
most massive clumps (Bontemps et al. 2010; Palau et al. 2013;
Sanhueza et al. 2019; Traficante et al. 2023). In contrast, it is
six times smaller than those measured in infrared-dark clouds
(Sanhueza et al. 2019). The number of predicted fragments is
also generally consistent with measurements performed by Bu-
daiev et al. (2024), Yoo et al. (2025), and Luo et al. (2026). How-
ever, Yoo et al. (2025) found that one of his targeted protocluster
has three times fewer fragments than predicted. Therefore, it is
likely that there are spatial variations in the fragmentation levels
of high-mass star-forming regions but current studies are neither
robust nor statistically significant enough to reveal the physical
processes behind them. Studies based on more than two scales
are also required to be robust against detection incompleteness
and be corrected for undetected sources in the hierarchical cas-
cade.
Additionally, the mass transfer efficiency estimated in
Sect. 4.2 for the W43-MM1 fragmentation cascade predicts the
CFE within a given parental structure. According to Eq. (2) and
ϵjump2 ≃60%, the CFE of 2 kau cores within 30 kau clumps is
predicted to be CFEclump
cores ≃14%. The published CFE of Bon-
temps et al. (2010), Palau et al. (2013), and Sanhueza et al.
(2019) are in excellent agreement with this value. However, mea-
surements over more different scales or environments are ∼4
times smaller or larger (Traficante et al. 2023; Yoo et al. 2025;
Elia et al. 2026). This preliminary result suggests that the mass
transfer efficiency varies with cloud properties such as density
(Csengeri et al. 2017) and accretion flows (Wells et al. 2024;
Morii et al. 2025). To provide more robust estimates of the mass
transfer efficiency and its spatial and scale-dependent variation,
future studies must consider dust emissivity variations and tem-
perature gradients.
Compared to the Jeans instability predictions, the fragmen-
tation level of the massive cores present in the W43-MM1 proto-
cluster, around two fragments within 10−100 M⊙cores, is small.
This low level of fragmentation has already been observed in
other high-mass star-forming regions, from clump to core scales
(Bontemps et al. 2010; Palau et al. 2013; Traficante et al. 2023),
and thus appears to persist down to the 270 au scales studied here
(see also Yoo et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2026). These results prove
that gravitational fragmentation according to the Jeans criterion
is invalid and that fragmentation cascades can create massive, so-
called super-Jeans structures (Bontemps et al. 2010; Wang et al.
2011; Palau et al. 2015; Yoo et al. 2025).
This interpretation is confirmed when comparing our esti-
mates of the fractality index and mass transfer efficiency in W43-

adiabatic equation of state is assumed, the diagram of the Φ as a
function of size scale and ξ allows to distinguish the fragmenta-
tion regime associated with supersonic turbulence from the ther-
mal fragmentation regime, in which turbulence is subsonic (see
Fig. 7 of Thomasson et al. 2024). In this figure, the two points
corresponding to the W43-MM1 fragmentation cascade lie in
the second regime, in which fragmentation is driven by grav-
ity and cloud structures are primarily supported by thermal en-
ergy. Across this 0.27−14 kau range of scales, structures should
therefore be (velocity) coherent, that is, mostly devoid of turbu-
lent support (e.g., Ballesteros-Paredes et al. 2011). In low-mass
star-forming regions, the transition to coherence is reached at the
∼8 kau scale of cores (Pineda et al. 2010; Pokhrel et al. 2018;
Chen et al. 2019). In W43-MM1, however, this transition occurs
at larger scales, contrary to expectations for high-mass proto-
clusters from the large line widths observed for cores (Wienen
et al. 2012; Kauffmann et al. 2013; Cunningham et al. 2023, in-
cluding Paper VII). This result confirms a recent study showing
that the turbulent and magnetic supports of W43-MM1 cores are
substantially overestimated due to line width contamination by
organized motions such as gravitational infall (Valeille-Manet et
al. subm). Our results also suggest that the cores and fragments
with super-Jeans masses form due to the outer pressure exerted
by the infalling gas on their outskirts.

6. Conclusion
We have characterized the fragmentation cascade of the W43-
MM1 protocluster and investigated the impact of core sufrag-
mentation on its CMF, which was found to be top-heavy (Motte
et al. 2018b; Nony et al. 2023). Our methodology and main find-
ings are summarized below:
– Using getsf , we extracted compact sources in a set of five
ALMA images of the W43-MM1 protocluster, covering the
0.27 −14 kau resolution range (see Sect. 2, Figs. 1 and A.1,
and Tables 1 and B.1). For comparison, we used source cat-
alogs from a synthetic Orion-like protoclusters derived from
MHD numerical simulations (see Table 1).
– We employed FAMILY (Thomasson et al. 2022, 2024, also
described in Sect. 3), a tool defining networks of nested
sources in protoclusters (see Sect. 4.1, Figs. 2–4 and A.2–
A.3). In Sects. 4.2–4.3, we characterized the fragmentation
cascades of W43-MM1 and synthetic protoclusters, which
are at first order assumed to be scale-free.
– The resulting cascade parameters are the fractality index,
mass transfer efficiency, and mass partition between siblings
defined in Sect. 3 (see also Figs. 3, 5, and A.4–A.5). In W43-
MM1, we found a low fractality index and imbalanced mass
partition (see Table 2): F3D ≃1.19 ± 0.10 and MP(Primary;
Secondary) ≃[ 2
3 ; 1
3]. We also estimated a high transfer effi-
ciency of ϵjump2 ≃60%.
– A comparison with the fractality indices measured, only
at large scales, in the NGC 2264 intermediate-mass and
Orion-like synthetic protoclusters (see Sect. 5.1) shows that
the fractal index, and thus fragmentation level, decreases at
scales smaller than ∼5 kau: from F3D = 1.55 to F3D = 1.19
(see Sect. 5.2). Fragmentation cascade parameters are ex-
pected, but not yet firmly observed, to vary in space, scale
and during the concentrated star-formation event of the W43-
MM1 mini-starburst (see Figs. 6 and A.3).
– The fragmentation cascade developing in W43-MM1 sug-
gest, according to the predictive model of Thomasson et al.
(2024), that its structures up to ∼5 kau lie in the regime of
fragmentation driven by gravity (see Sect. 5.2).
– We used the parameters of the fragmentation cascade mea-
sured for W43-MM1 (see Table 2) and the model by
Thomasson et al. (2026) to predict the subfragmentation of
its 2.4 kau cores. The resulting fragment mass function, from
which the IMF will emerge, has a high-mass end which re-
mains top-heavy (see Sect. 5.3 and Fig. 7), demonstrating
that core subfragmentation has a minimal role in shaping the
IMF.
Combining the characterization of the fragmentation cas-
cade in a protocluster with the Thomasson et al. (2024) model
is a powerful, robust method for determining the fragmentation
regime of its cloud structures and the main physical processes
at their origin. This information is invaluable, as line and polar-
ization observations struggle to measure non-thermal support in
massive protoclusters (see, e.g., Valeille-Manet et al. subm).
Furthermore, statistical studies of the fragmentation cascade
are mandatory to investigate how the cascade varies with cloud
properties and evolutionary state. This is especially important for
determining the shape of the mass function of fragments emerg-
ing from core subfragmentation. Additionally, investigation of
the direct link between gas reservoirs devoted to star formation
and stars requires constraining the growth of fragment mass dur-
ing the protostellar collapse. In fact, a tight connection between
the fragmentation cascade of clouds and the accretion cascade of
infalling gas toward newborn stars is expected in the hierarchical
collapse model (e.g., Vázquez-Semadeni et al. 2019).
