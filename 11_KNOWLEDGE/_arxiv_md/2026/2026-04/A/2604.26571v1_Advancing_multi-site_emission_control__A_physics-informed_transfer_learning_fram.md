Abstract
Municipal solid waste incineration is increasingly central to urban waste manage-
ment, yet its sustainability benefit depends on controlling carbon emissions and
multiple air pollutants under highly heterogeneous operating conditions. Current
data-driven models are often accurate within individual plants but are difficult to
transfer across facilities, limiting their value for scalable emission-control strate-
gies. Here we show that multi-site emission behaviour can be represented through
transferable system-level structures when physical constraints, operating-regime
heterogeneity and carbon–pollutant coupling are jointly considered. We develop

1 Introduction
Municipal solid waste incineration (MSWI) has become an indispensable component of
modern waste management systems, particularly in densely populated regions where
landfill capacity is limited[1,2]. Beyond its role in waste reduction, MSWI functions
as a complex waste-to-energy system in which carbon conversion, pollutant forma-
tion, and energy recovery are tightly coupled[3,4]. Although incineration contributes
to energy supply and landfill diversion, it also represents a persistent source of atmo-
spheric emissions, including carbon dioxide (CO2), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen
oxides (NOx), sulfur dioxide (SO2), hydrogen chloride (HCl), and particulate matter
(PM)[4–6]. The environmental and health impacts of these emissions are cumula-
tive, multi-dimensional, and subject to increasingly stringent regulatory constraints,
making emission control in MSWI systems a central challenge for sustainable urban
infrastructure.
A defining feature of MSWI systems is their pronounced heterogeneity[7,8]. Dif-
ferences in waste composition, furnace configuration, air supply strategies, operating
conditions, and pollution control technologies lead to substantial variability in emis-
sion behaviors between plants and even between furnaces within the same facility[9,10].
As a result, operational strategies that achieve low emissions in one incinerator often
fail when transferred to another[11]. This heterogeneity has long constrained the devel-
opment of robust emission prediction and control models, forcing operators to rely on
site-specific tuning and empirical experience rather than transferable decision-support
tools. From a sustainability perspective, such plant-specific approaches limit the scala-
bility of best practices and hinder the rapid deployment of optimized control strategies
across expanding waste-to-energy networks.

Existing modeling approaches for MSWI emissions have addressed this challenge by
decomposing the problem into isolated pollutant-specific prediction tasks[12–15]. Sta-
tistical regression models, machine learning methods, and, more recently, deep learning
architectures have been used to predict individual pollutant concentrations based
on operational variables[16–18]. While these methods have achieved notable improve-
ments in predictive accuracy within individual plants[19–21], they are typically trained
and validated under narrow operating conditions and exhibit poor generalization to
new sites[22–24]. More fundamentally, treating each pollutant independently obscures
the coupled nature of emission formation, in which combustion efficiency, excess air,
temperature fields, and fuel characteristics jointly shape the evolution of multiple pol-
lutants[25,26]. This reductionist paradigm limits not only predictive robustness but also
the ability to reason about trade-offs, synergies, and control priorities at the system
level[27,28].
Beyond pollutant decoupling, most data-driven MSWI models implicitly assume
that sufficient data can compensate for structural differences between plants. However,
empirical evidence suggests that increasing model complexity or data volume alone
does not guarantee transferability[29,30]. Models trained purely on data correlations
tend to learn site-specific signatures rather than underlying physical relationships,
leading to unstable performance when operating conditions shift or when applied to
previously unseen facilities. This challenge reflects a deeper scientific gap: it remains
unclear whether heterogeneous MSWI systems share transferable system-level struc-
tures that govern multi-pollutant formation and controllability under physical and
regulatory constraints. Without identifying such structures, data-driven models risk
remaining sophisticated interpolators rather than generalizable tools for sustainable
operation.
Recent advances in physics-informed machine learning offer a potential pathway
to address this limitation by embedding conservation laws and processing knowledge
directly into data-driven models[31]. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), in
particular, have demonstrated the ability to improve model stability and extrapola-
tion by constraining learning with physical consistency. Yet, most existing applications
of PINNs focus on single-output prediction tasks or well-defined physical systems,
and their role in complex, regulated industrial processes remains underexplored[32,33].
Moreover, even physics-informed models may struggle to capture the diversity of
operating regimes inherent to MSWI systems, where different combustion modes and
control strategies can dominate under different conditions[34,35].
Even recent advances in deep learning and transfer learning have not fully resolved
this challenge. Although such models can improve predictive accuracy within indi-
vidual plants, they often rely on site-specific learning correlations rather than the
underlying behavior of the system[36,37]. As a result, their performance degrades when
operating conditions shift or when models are transferred to new furnaces or facili-
ties. This limitation is not primarily due to insufficient data or model complexity, but
to the absence of structural constraints that enforce physical consistency and system-
level coherence[38,39]. Without embedding conservation laws and explicitly accounting
for interactions among multiple pollutants, data-driven models lack a common refer-
ence frame through which emission behaviors can be compared across heterogeneous

sites[40]. Consequently, accurate predictions may fail to support robust operational
decisions, as they do not reveal transferable emission-control relationships or enable
consistent evaluation of trade-offs between carbon efficiency and pollutant mitigation.
In this study, we address these limitations by reframing MSWI emission model-
ing as a problem of identifying transferable system-level structures in heterogeneous,
physics-constrained environments (Fig. 1). Rather than optimizing plant-specific
predictors, we investigate whether shared emission–control relationships can be
revealed when physical conservation laws, multi-pollutant interactions, and opera-
tional diversity are jointly considered. To this end, we develop a physics-informed
mixture-of-experts framework that integrates conservation constraints with data-
driven learning to represent multiple operating regimes within and across incineration
facilities. Building on this framework, we further introduce a carbon-pollutant syner-
gistic index (CPSI) to quantify emission risk at the system level, enabling consistent
comparison of operating conditions by capturing the combined impacts of carbon
emissions and pollutant synergies. Together, these elements transform data-driven
emission modeling from plant-specific prediction to a transferable decision-support
paradigm, allowing robust evaluation and navigation of emission control trade-offs
across heterogeneous waste-to-energy systems.
2 Methods
2.1 Problem Definition and Framework
Rather than treating emission prediction as an end task, this study formulates MSWI
modeling as a system identification problem, aimed at revealing transferable emis-
sion–control relationships under heterogeneous operating conditions. The input feature
sequence consists of process parameters over the past T time steps, including 31-
dimensional features such as temperature, pressure, flow rate, and oxygen content.
The objective is to predict the concentration values of major emissions: PM, SO2,
NOx, HCl, CO, and CO2. The proposed framework achieves an end-to-end mapping
from raw process parameters to pollutant concentrations. The overall architecture of
the model is illustrated in Fig. 1b–d.
2.2 Data Preprocessing
Data preprocessing and feature engineering are designed to preserve physically mean-
ingful temporal patterns in MSWI operation, including medium-term thermal inertia,
load fluctuations, and diurnal operational cycles, rather than solely optimizing statis-
tical properties. We adopt a triple imputation strategy to handle missing values. First,
forward filling is applied to replace missing values with the previous valid observa-
tion. Missing values at the beginning of the sequence are then handled by backward
filling. Finally, any remaining missing values are filled with zeros. For outlier treat-
ment, considering the significant differences in data distribution characteristics among
different pollutants, a per-indicator adaptive percentile cleaning method is employed.
PM and HCl data are thresholded using the 5th–95th percentiles, while NOx data are
thresholded using the 2nd–98th percentiles. CO data exhibit a highly right-skewed

2.3 Carbon-Pollutant Mixture-of-Experts (CPMoE)

therefore insufficient to represent the system behavior across the full operating space.

2.4 Mechanism Consistency under Physical Constraints
Despite pronounced heterogeneity in waste composition, furnace configuration, and
operational strategies, emission formation in MSWI systems remains constrained by
shared physical principles. To ensure that the learned emission-control relationships
reflect such cross-site consistencies rather than plant-specific correlations, mechanism
consistency is explicitly enforced under physical constraints. This identifies emis-
sion–control structures that are admissible across furnaces and facilities, thereby
supporting transferable modeling and control.
A physics-informed framework is adopted to embed fundamental conservation
principles as soft structural constraints. This framework does not reproduce detailed
combustion chemistry, but defines a common solution space of emission behaviors
shared by heterogeneous MSWI systems. By restricting learning to physically con-
sistent manifolds, the model captures system-level emission-control relationships that
persist under varying operating conditions, rather than fitting site-specific statistical
patterns.

2.5 Physics-Informed Transfer Learning (PITL)
The Carbon-Pollutant Mixture-of-Experts (CPMoE) framework demonstrates a
strong capacity to capture the multi-regime emission behavior of a single waste
incineration facility. However, its direct application to a new plant with different oper-
ational characteristics typically results in significant performance degradation due
to domain shift. This shift originates from substantial heterogeneities across plants,
including variations in waste composition, furnace design and scale, combustion control
strategies, and sensor calibration.

3 Results
3.1 Experimental Settings
All experiments were implemented in PyTorch and run on a single workstation
equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Unless otherwise stated, all runs used
a fixed random seed of 123 for reproducibility. The temporal input window was
set to 24 h. The original monitored variables comprised 31 process features, which
were expanded to a 98-dimensional input representation through the unified feature-
engineering pipeline described in Section 2.2. The model directly predicted six target
pollutants, namely PM, SO2, NOx, HCl, CO, and CO2. CPSI was subsequently
calculated from the predicted pollutant concentrations and used for system-level
evaluation.
The CPMoE architecture consisted of four heterogeneous experts, namely Trans-
former, CNN, LSTM, and MLP. The shared hidden dimension was set to 352, the
Transformer model dimension to 192, and the dropout ratio to 0.15. The Transformer
expert used 4 attention heads, 1 encoder layer, and a feedforward dimension of 768.
The CNN expert used two one-dimensional convolutional layers with 176 and 352 out-
put channels, respectively, a kernel size of 3, and adaptive global average pooling. The
LSTM expert adopted a two-layer bidirectional architecture with hidden size 352. The
MLP expert used the same hidden dimension of 352. To promote expert specializa-
tion while maintaining efficient inference, the gating module adopted sparse Top-K
routing with K = 3, so that three of the four experts were activated for each sample.
During training, Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.01 was injected into
the gating logits before Softmax to prevent premature collapse to fixed expert choices
and to encourage exploration of diverse expert combinations.
To evaluate within-plant modeling performance, each of the 13 facilities was first
treated as an independent source domain and trained separately under the same pro-
tocol. For each plant, the available samples were randomly split into 90% for training
and 10% for validation. To avoid temporal leakage, the random split was performed at
the sequence-block level rather than at the individual time-window level. All source-
domain models were optimized with AdamW for up to 400 epochs, using a learning rate
of 0.001, weight decay of 0.0002, and batch size of 32. Early stopping was applied with
a patience of 50 epochs, and a 10-epoch warm-up schedule was used at the beginning
of training. This unified design enabled direct comparison of source-domain prediction
performance across facilities under the same architecture and optimization settings.
Cross-facility generalization was then evaluated via physics-informed transfer
learning. MSWI#1 in Shandong Province was used as the reference source domain, and
the pretrained model was transferred to multiple target plants, including MSWI#2–3

in Shandong Province, MSWI#4–12 in Zhejiang Province, and MSWI#13 in Hebei
Province. Across all target-domain datasets, approximately 213,000 sample points
were collected over a 3-year acquisition period. For each target plant, the model was
initialized with the pretrained weights from MSWI#1 and fine-tuned using AdamW
with a reduced learning rate of 0.00006 and weight decay of 0.0001, for up to 550
epochs with early stopping (patience 70). The same preprocessing pipeline and input
representation were reused unchanged for all target domains, ensuring that cross-site
evaluation was conducted in a consistent feature space. Detailed configurations of
the investigated facilities, including plant scale, energy recovery capacity, and APCS
architectures, are provided in Supplementary Section D.
3.2 System-level Prediction and Multiple Operating Regimes
Before evaluating cross-site transfer, we first examined whether CPMoE could recover
system-level emission behavior when each plant was modeled within its own source
domain. Across the 13 facilities, CPMoE achieved strong overall performance for most
pollutants and for the integrated CPSI metric (Fig. 2a,b). The average R2 across
pollutants ranged from 0.668 to 0.904, with the highest values observed in MSWI#4–
#6 (0.885–0.904) and the lowest in MSWI#13 (0.668). CPSI was also captured well in
most plants, with R2 values of 0.666–0.970. Particularly high CPSI predictability was
obtained in MSWI#4 (0.970), MSWI#11 (0.962), MSWI#12 (0.933), and MSWI#5
(0.923), indicating that CPMoE recovered not only pollutant-wise outputs, but also
the coupled structure underlying system-level emission risk within individual plants.
The pollutant-level results revealed a clear hierarchy in source-domain predictabil-
ity. CO2 was the most stable target across all plants, with R2 = 0.849–0.998, and
HCl also remained robust (R2 = 0.786–0.955). PM and NOx were predicted accu-
rately in most plants, reaching R2 = 0.545–0.948 and 0.636–0.969, respectively. By
contrast, SO2 and CO showed stronger plant dependence. For SO2, source-domain
R2 ranged from 0.305 in MSWI#8 to 0.835 in MSWI#12. For CO, high coefficients
of determination were obtained in MSWI#1–#6 (0.833–0.968), whereas performance
remained much lower in MSWI#7–#13 (0.438–0.509). The error metrics showed the

3.3 Transferability of System-level Structures across Plants
Having established that CPMoE captures both system-level prediction and multiple
operating regimes within individual plants, we next examined whether these learned
structures remain transferable across heterogeneous facilities. Across the 12 target
facilities (MSWI#2–13), the transferred CPMoE preserved stable multi-pollutant pre-
dictability under strong cross-site heterogeneity (Fig. 3). The average R2 across
pollutants remained within a narrow range (0.661–0.842). This indicates that most
variance can be recovered after adaptation, even when plants differ in operating
regimes, APCS configurations, and measurement baselines. The stability was not lim-
ited to a single pollutant. It extended across the emission portfolio that underlies
system-level risk aggregation.
Transferability was strongest for signals with clear physical anchoring. CO2 was
consistently captured in every target plant (R2 = 0.869–0.997). HCl also remained
robust (R2 = 0.773–0.945). PM and NOx generalized well in most domains, reaching
R2 = 0.934 and 0.956, respectively. By contrast, SO2 and CO showed larger disper-
sion. SO2 dropped sharply in a small number of plants (0.321 in MSWI#8 and 0.183
in MSWI#11), while remaining high elsewhere, including 0.799 in MSWI#12. CO
stayed in a moderate band (R2 = 0.403–0.833) and was consistently harder to trans-
fer than CO2 and HCl. These patterns are consistent with CO and SO2 being more
sensitive to site-specific combustion stability, reagent dosing, and control setpoints.
Importantly, the system-level CPSI retained comparable transferability across plants
(R2 = 0.610–0.841). This result is non-trivial. CPSI is a composite risk aggregation
over multiple pollutants. Its transferability suggests that the learned emission–control
structures persist beyond pollutant-wise fitting. The model therefore generalizes not
only pointwise outputs, but also the coupled structure that governs integrated risk.
Error magnitudes showed the same clustered-versus-outlier structure. For most
target plants, CPSI errors were low and tightly grouped, especially in MSWI#4–12,
where CPSI MAE remained within 1.63–2.51 and CPSI RMSE within 2.47–5.68. The
largest deviations concentrated in a small number of domains. MSWI#13 was the
clearest case, with CPSI MAE/RMSE of 9.62/13.22. This site also showed elevated
errors for multiple pollutants, including SO2 MAE/RMSE of 14.912/21.789 and NOx
RMSE of 31.238. A second but less severe deviation appeared in MSWI#3, where
CPSI RMSE reached 9.01. The error peaks were pollutant- and plant-specific, rather
than indicative of global transfer failure. PM exhibited a distinct spike in MSWI#6
(RMSE = 1.1467), while remaining much lower in MSWI#2–3 (0.0277–0.0686). SO2
displayed a different failure mode, with the strongest degradation in MSWI#13 and
secondary elevations in MSWI#8–9. Such localized degradation is consistent with
bounded domain shift under heterogeneous combustion regimes and APCS layouts,
rather than with a collapse of the transferred representation itself.

3.4 From Prediction to Control: Interpretable Digital Twin
and Operational Navigation
Having established that CPMoE captures system-level emission behavior within plants
and preserves transferable emission–control structures across plants, we next extended
the framework from prediction to control-oriented navigation. This extension was
motivated by two observations from the preceding sections. First, plant operation
was organized into multiple regimes rather than a single global mapping. Second,
these regime-organized structures remained identifiable after physics-informed trans-
fer. Together, these results indicate that the learned representation can support not
only emission prediction, but also regime-aware operational guidance.
On this basis, we reformulated the transferred CPMoE as an interpretable digi-
tal twin. The twin maps the recent process history to the predicted concentrations of
the six pollutants and the corresponding CPSI, while retaining the regime-dependent
structure encoded by the gating mechanism. It therefore provides more than a point-
wise prediction of future emissions. It reconstructs the local emission–control landscape
associated with the current operating state. Because the same input representation,
physical constraints, and expert-routing logic are preserved, this twin provides a con-
sistent basis for evaluating alternative operating adjustments under heterogeneous
plant conditions.
The control space was organized according to the regime modules identified in
the clustering analysis. Rather than treating plant operation as an unconstrained
search over all variables, operational navigation was defined over interpretable control
directions associated with the thermal–steam backbone, the air–grate module, and
the O2–pressure state. This regime-aware decomposition is important because the
preceding results showed that emission behavior is governed by structured coordination
among thermal load, air supply, grate progression, and flue-gas transport, rather than
by isolated variables. Operational navigation was therefore designed to move the plant
from its current regime toward a lower-risk region of the coupled emission landscape.

support. It converts transferable emission–control structures into a navigable digital

operation under heterogeneous conditions.
4 Discussion
This study reframes MSWI emission modeling from a plant-specific prediction prob-
lem into the identification of transferable system-level emission–control structures.
The results show that heterogeneous MSWI facilities share partially conserved rela-
tionships among combustion state, multi-pollutant formation, and integrated emission
risk, even though their operating regimes and plant-specific emission distributions
differ substantially. Within individual plants, CPMoE recovered both pollutant-wise
outputs and CPSI, indicating that the mixture structure captured not only isolated
concentration trajectories but also the coupled organization of system-level risk. After
cross-site adaptation, the transferred model preserved stable predictability for physi-
cally anchored signals such as CO2 and HCl, while maintaining bounded performance
for more volatile targets such as SO2 and CO. The transferability of CPSI is particu-
larly important because it suggests that the learned representation generalizes beyond
pointwise pollutant fitting and retains the coupled carbon–pollutant structure required
for integrated environmental assessment.
A noteworthy pattern emerged for CO in MSWI#4–#6. When these plants were
modeled as standalone source domains, CO exhibited disproportionately large abso-
lute errors, with MAE values of 12.4972, 11.2774, and 8.3677, and RMSE values of
136.6414, 206.5453, and 100.6793, respectively. By contrast, after physics-informed
transfer from MSWI#1, the corresponding errors decreased sharply to 1.4202, 1.4066,
and 1.7677 in MAE, and to 4.3332, 3.9570, and 4.0905 in RMSE. This contrast does not
simply indicate a generic advantage of transfer learning. Rather, it suggests that plant-
specific training can be vulnerable to unstable local correlations and extreme residual
events when the target signal is dominated by transient combustion instability, local
oxygen deficiency, feedstock fluctuation, and short-term control perturbations. In this
sense, PITL acts not only as a domain adaptation strategy, but also as a structural
regularizer. By constraining the adapted representation with excess-air consistency,
aggregated pollutant conservation, and simplified energy balance, the model suppresses
site-specific correlations that are statistically strong but mechanistically weak. The
comparison with Transformer, CNN, LSTM, and MLP baselines further supports this
interpretation: the improvement of CPMoE arises not from a single temporal encoder,
but from regime-dependent integration of complementary inductive biases.

The control-oriented digital twin extends this modeling framework from prediction
toward operational navigation, but its current role should be interpreted as a struc-
tured decision-support layer rather than a fully validated closed-loop controller. The
regime-aware navigation strategy is valuable because it decomposes the control space
into interpretable modules associated with the thermal–steam backbone, air–grate
operation, and O2–pressure state, thereby avoiding unconstrained search over all mon-
itored variables. However, the physical constraints used here are deliberately aggregate
and differentiable, rather than detailed descriptions of combustion chemistry, reagent
reactions, or equipment-level APCS kinetics. In addition, the digital twin has been
formulated for offline constrained policy evaluation, and prospective validation under
real plant interventions remains necessary before deployment in safety-critical control
settings. Future work should therefore integrate uncertainty quantification, actuator
constraints, APCS dosing information, and operator feedback, and should evaluate
whether regime-aware navigation can deliver measurable reductions in CPSI, carbon
intensity, and pollutant exceedance risk under real operating conditions.
5 Conclusion
This study developed a physics-informed transfer learning framework with a carbon–
pollutant mixture-of-experts architecture for multi-site MSWI emission modeling
and control-oriented decision support. By combining heterogeneous expert networks,
sparse regime-dependent routing, conservation-based physical regularization, domain
alignment, and CPSI-based system-level evaluation, the proposed framework captures
both pollutant-specific emission dynamics and integrated carbon–pollutant risk across
heterogeneous facilities.
Across 13 MSWI plants, CPMoE achieved robust within-plant prediction for six
major emission indicators and CPSI, while outperforming single-architecture baselines
in most source domains. Cross-site experiments further showed that physics-informed
transfer preserved stable multi-pollutant predictability across 12 target plants and
maintained the transferability of CPSI, indicating that shared emission–control struc-
tures can be identified despite substantial differences in plant configuration, operating
regime, and emission distribution. Expert-utilization patterns revealed that adaptation
occurred through structured re-weighting of operating regimes rather than complete
re-learning, supporting the central hypothesis that heterogeneous MSWI systems share
transferable system-level mechanisms.
The framework was further extended into an interpretable digital twin for regime-
aware operational navigation. This extension links predicted multi-pollutant emissions,
CPSI, and process-regime structure to candidate control directions, providing a
pathway from emission prediction to carbon–pollutant synergistic decision support.
Although future work is required to validate the digital twin under prospective plant
interventions and to incorporate more detailed equipment-level constraints, the present
results demonstrate that physically constrained multi-expert transfer learning can
provide a scalable basis for robust emission assessment and operational optimization
across heterogeneous waste-to-energy systems.
