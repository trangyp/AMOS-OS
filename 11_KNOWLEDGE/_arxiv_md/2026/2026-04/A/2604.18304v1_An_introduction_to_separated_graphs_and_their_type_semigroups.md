1. Introduction
These notes are an expanded version of the lecture notes for the course
“Introduction to separated graphs” that I delivered at the Summer School
Luis Santal´o “Structure and approximation of C∗-algebras”, held at the
UIMP, Santander, in July 2025.
Graph C∗-algebras are a central object in modern operator algebra the-
ory, connecting functional analysis, topology, dynamics, and even number
theory.
Their importance comes from how they translate combinatorial
data (graphs) into rich analytic structures (C∗-algebras), creating a power-
ful bridge between discrete and continuous mathematics. Graph C∗-algebras
associated to directed graphs generalize the important class of Cuntz-Krieger
algebras [29], and provide important models for the classification of C∗-
algebras. In turn, they have been generalized in several directions, some of
which will be considered here.
In these notes, we introduce C∗-algebras associated with directed graphs,
along with two generalizations of this concept, namely Exel-Pardo C∗-algebras
associated with a self-similar action of a group on a directed graph, and the
C∗-algebras associated with separated graphs.
These constructions have
in common that they have a dynamical behavior, being the groupoid C∗-
algebras associated to certain topological groupoids, which are built from
the combinatorial structure. As such, an important invariant one may asso-
ciate to the dynamical systems is the so-called type semigroup. This has its
origins in the work of Tarski [68,70] on paradoxical decompositions, where
the type semigroup serves to express paradoxical decompositions (or their
absence) in an algebraic language. Hence the type semigroup is a purely
dynamical device that one may try to directly compute from the combina-
torial object. We show formulas for the computation of the type semigroup
for a general self-similar action of a group on a row-finite graph E without
sources, following the recent paper [46], and for any finite bipartite separated
graph, following [11].
There is a natural map from the type semigroup to the Murray-von Neu-
mann monoid of projections of the associated C∗-algebras, which in some
cases, turns out to be a monoid isomorphism. We advocate here that this
map is a non-stable version of the well-known map H0(G) →K0(C∗(G)) that
shows up in some formulations of Matui’s Conjectures [20,53], see Remark
3.9.

We will survey two important results of Wehrung [73] concerning the type

can be represented as the type monoid of an action of a countable group

of any supramenble group on a locally compact Hausdorff zero-dimensional

powerful cancellation condition.

2. Graph C∗-algebras
2.1. Graphs. We start by fixing notation for graphs, following Raeburn’s
book [63]. We warn the reader that different notations are used in other
sources.
Definitions 2.1. A directed graph E = (E0, E1, r, s) consists of two count-
able sets E0 (the vertices) and E1 (the edges) and functions r, s: E1 →E0
giving the range and the source of an edge e ∈E1.
A path in E is given by a finite string of edges γ = e1 · · · en such that
s(ei) = r(ei+1) for all 1 ≤i < n. The integer n is called the length of the
path, denoted by |γ|. Vertices are considered as paths of length 0, and are
called trivial paths. We set s(γ) = s(en) and r(γ) = r(e1). We also will
consider infinite paths γ = e1e2 · · · . In this case, we set r(γ) = r(e1).
We will deal here with row-finite graphs, which are the graphs E such
that the adjacency matrix AE,
AE(v, w) = #{e ∈E1 : r(e) = v, s(e) = w}
has a finite number of nonzero entries at each row. This means that the
vertices in E receive only finitely many arrows from the graph. Of course,
this includes all finite graphs, which by definition are those graphs E such
that E0 and E1 are both finite sets.
A source in a directed graph E is a vertex v ∈E0 such that v does not
receive any edge, that is, such that r−1(v) = ∅. Similarly, a sink is a vertex
v ∈E0 such that s−1(v) = ∅. We denote the set of all sources of E by
Sour(E), and the set of all sinks by Sink(E).

2.6. The groupoid model. We now introduce the groupoid picture for
graph C∗-algebras. These groupoids have been introduced in a certain ad hoc
way in several instances, and this is indeed the case in the initial paper [45].
However the construction of the groupoid can be done from an algebraic
structure called an inverse semigroup, see [33] and [34] for this approach.
This is the most satisfying way to build this structure, but we will restrict
to the ad hoc method in these notes.
We refer the reader to [65] for a quick introduction to the theory of ´etale
groupoids.
A groupoid is a small category with inverses. Although this is the most
economical definition, we want to phrase it in more concrete terms. We will
deal thus with a set G, endowed with two maps s, r: G →G0, where G0 is
the set of objects of G. These maps are called the source and the range of
G. We have a partially defined multiplication
µ: G(2) →G,

“arrow” g ∈G is invertible, with s(g−1) = r(g), r(g−1) = s(g), gg−1 = r(g),
g−1g = s(g).

it is not assumed that the groupoid itself is Hausdorff.

4.3. The type semigroup of a separated graph I. We are now inter-

model of a separated graph. This semigroup can be computed in the case of

Before we give the promised description, we need to introduce the subject
of (m, n)-paradoxes.
Let G be a group acting on a set X. In the setting of the classical Banach-
Tarski paradox, one is interested in the study of equidecomposability for
subsets of X. Recall that two subsets U and V of X are G-equidecomposable
if there are decompositions
U = U1 ⊔U2 ⊔· · · ⊔Un,
V = V1 ⊔V2 ⊔· · · ⊔Vn
and elements g1, . . . , gn ∈G such that giUi = Vi for i = 1, . . . , n.
A subset E of X is called G-paradoxical if there are two disjoint subsets
E1 and E2 of E such that each Ei is G-equidecomposable to E. Tarski’s
Theorem states that a subset E of X is not G-paradoxical if and only if there
is a finitely additive invariant measure µ: P(X) →[0, ∞] such that µ(E) =
1. Tarski’s Theorem can be expressed as a result on the type semigroup
Typ(X, G) associated to the action of G on X, see [70, Theorem 11.1 and
Corollary 11.2].
This type semigroup coincides with the type semigroup
associated to the transformation groupoid of the action which, in this case,
is a discrete groupoid.
As Tarski shows in [69, Theorem 16.12], his result holds also if we replace
global actions with partial actions.
In the topological setting, that is, when G is a discrete group acting by
(partial) homeomorphisms on a locally compact Hausdorff topological space
X, we may restrict the sets considered to the family K of open compact
subsets of X. In this way, a version of the type monoid may be constructed,
see for instance [11, Section 7]. This type monoid will be denoted here by
Typ(X, G, K) in order to distinguish it from the set-theoretical type monoid
Typ(X, G). Note that Typ(X, G, K) ∼= Typ(GG,X), where GG,X is the trans-
formation groupoid of the (partial) action.
The proof of Tarski’s Theorem is based on two special properties of the
type semigroup Typ(X, G), and a general semigroup-theoretic result.
If S is a commutative monoid, we consider the so-called algebraic pre-order
on S, defined by x ≤y if and only if there is z ∈S such that y = x + z.

5. Special properties of type semigroups
In this section we will state some properties of type semigroups in certain
favorable circumstances. In particular, we review an interesting result of
Wehrung [73, Theorem 5.3.8]. This result goes far beyond the framework
of minimal actions. In the final part of the section, we will describe how to
compute the type monoid associated to any finite bipartite separated graph,

5.1. Different levels of cancellation in commutative monoids. We
will follow [10] and the recent paper [16] concerning notation and terminol-
ogy.
Let M be a commutative monoid. We say that M is cancellative if x+z =
y + z implies x = y for any x, y, z ∈M. We say that M is separative (or has
separative cancellation) if 2x = x+y = 2y implies x = y for every x, y ∈M.
We say that M is strongly separative if 2x = x + y implies x = y for any
x, y ∈M.
Observe that a cancellative monoid is necessarily stably finite.
For a commutative monoid M, separativity is equivalent to cancellation
of “two-sided small” elements: M is separative if and only if whenever we
have x + z = y + z and z ≤nx, z ≤my for some n, m ≥1, then we have
x = y, see [10, Lemma 2.1].
Similarly, strong separativity is equivalent to cancellation of “one-sided
small” elements: M is strongly separative if and only if whenever we have
x + z = y + z and z ≤nx for some n ≥1, then we have x = y, see [10, page
126].
Separativity leads to a strong form of dichotomy in the simple case, that is,
a simple conical monoid M is separative if and only if it is either cancellative
or purely infinite. If a simple conical monoid is strongly separative then it
is necessarily cancellative.

We can now compute the type semigroup of the full shift. We will state
the result only for a two-element alphabet, but an analogous result holds for

For a homeomorphism α of the Cantor set X, we will simply denote by
Typ(α) the type monoid Typ(X, G, K) of the associated action of Z on X.
If α is a minimal homeomorphism on the Cantor set X, then its topological
full group [[α]], which is the group of units of the Boolean inverse semigroup
pHomeo(X, Z), admits a dense locally finite subgrup (see [36] and [57]).

unperforated, and thus, by e.g. [15, Theorem 3.14], it is the positive cone of
a countable dimension group.
