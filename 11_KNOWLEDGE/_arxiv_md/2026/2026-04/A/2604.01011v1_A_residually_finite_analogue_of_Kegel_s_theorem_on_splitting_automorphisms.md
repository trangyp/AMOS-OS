A RESIDUALLY FINITE ANALOGUE OF KEGEL’S THEOREM
ON SPLITTING AUTOMORPHISMS
ALFONSO DI BARTOLO, KIVANC¸ ERSOY, AND GIOVANNI FALCONE
Abstract. Thompson proved that every finite group admitting a fixed-point-
free automorphism of prime order is nilpotent, and Kegel showed that the
same conclusion holds for finite groups admitting a splitting automorphism of
prime order. Motivated by these results, Sozutov asked whether a p′-group
admitting a splitting automorphism of prime order is locally nilpotent if
⟨g, gφ, . . . , gφp−1⟩
is nilpotent for every g ∈G, [7, Problem 10.59].
We prove that if G is a
periodic residually finite group admitting a splitting automorphism of prime
order p then G is nilpotent of class bounded in terms of p.
This gives an
affirmative answer, for residually finite groups, to the problem of Sozutov. We
also prove that a possible counterexample to Sozutov’s problem cannot be a
Tarski monster.
1. Introduction
Let G be a group and let α ∈Aut(G). An automorphism α is called fixed-point-
free if CG(α) = 1. For finite groups, every fixed-point-free automorphism α of order
n satisfies
xxαxα2 · · · xαn−1 = 1
for all x ∈G (see [13, 10.5.1]). This led Gorchakov to introduce the notion of a
splitting automorphism.
Definition 1.1. Let G be a group. An automorphism α of order n is called a
splitting automorphism if
ggαgα2 · · · gαn−1 = 1
for every g ∈G.
An element a ∈G is called an anticentral element of G if
aG′ = aG
(see [10] or [2]). It is easy to see that if a ∈G is anticentral, then conjugation
by a induces a splitting automorphism of G′. Moreover, if α is a fixed-point-free
automorphism of a finite group G, then α is an anticentral element of the semidirect
product
H = G ⋊⟨α⟩.
Rowley observed that every finite group admitting a fixed-point-free automorphism
is solvable [14, Theorem]. Later, Ladisch proved that every finite group containing

second author proved in [3] that a finite group admitting a splitting automorphism
of odd order is solvable. In addition, Jabara proved in [5] that every finite group
admitting a splitting automorphism of order 4 is solvable. For nilpotency, the situ-
ation is more restrictive. The existence of splitting automorphisms imposes strong
restrictions on the structure of a group. Thompson proved that every finite group
admitting a fixed-point-free automorphism of prime order is nilpotent [16], while
Kegel showed that every finite group admitting a splitting automorphism of prime
order is nilpotent [6]. Moreover, by results of Higman [4] and Kreknin–Kostrikin [9],
there exists a function f such that every finite group admitting a fixed-point-free
automorphism of prime order p is nilpotent of class at most f(p).
Motivated by these results, Sozutov asked the following question.
Problem 1.2. [7, Sozutov, Problem 10.59] Is a p′-group G locally nilpotent if it
admits a splitting automorphism φ of prime order p such that all subgroups of the
form
⟨g, gφ, . . . , gφp−1⟩
are nilpotent?
Our first main result gives a positive answer in the periodic residually finite case,
and in fact implies bounded nilpotency class.
Theorem 1.3. There is a natural valued function f such that if G is a periodic
residually finite group admitting a splitting automorphism φ of prime order p then
G is nilpotent of class less than f(p).
As an immediate consequence, Sozutov’s problem has a positive answer for resid-
ually finite groups.
Corollary 1.4. Let G be a periodic residually finite p′-group admitting a splitting
automorphism φ of prime order p such that
⟨g, gφ, . . . , gφp−1⟩
is nilpotent for every g ∈G. Then G is nilpotent.
Our second main result shows that a possible counterexample to Problem 1.2
can not be Tarski monsters. They are not locally finite, they are not residually
finite but however, they can not be Tarski monsters either.
Theorem 1.5. Let G be a p′-group admitting a splitting automorphism φ of order
p. Assume that
⟨g, gφ, . . . , gφp−1⟩
is nilpotent for every g ∈G. Then G is not isomorphic to a Tarski monster.
One of the most important results in the theory of locally finite groups is the
following:
Theorem 1.6 (Hall–Kulatilaka). Every infinite locally finite group contains an
infinite abelian subgroup.
However, existence of a periodic residually finite analogue of Hall-Kulatilaka
theorem is still unknown. In particular, A. Mann asked the following:

Problem 1.7. [7, A. Mann, Problem 11.56] Does every infinite residually finite
group contain an infinite abelian subgroup? Does every infinite residually finite
p-group contain an infinite abelian subgroup? The same questions are valid for
infinite centralizers.
If there is an infinite residually finite group whose all centralizers are finite, it
must contain no involutions by [15]. Hence, every finite subgroup is solvable. How-
ever, since periodic solvable groups are locally finite and locally finite groups have
infinite abelian subgroups, such in such a counterexample every solvable subgroup
is finite. That means if there is a periodic residually finite group with finite cen-
tralizers, there is also a non-solvable finitely generated example. Since the possible
counterexamples of both of the problems lead us to this direction, we may formulate
an intermediate problem weaker than problems by Sozutov and Mann.
Problem 1.8. Let G be an infinite periodic residually finite group whose all cen-
tralizers are finite. Does G admit a splitting automorphism?
2. Preliminaries
We collect some basic facts that will be used later.
Proposition 2.1. Let G be a group, and let φ ∈Aut(G) be a splitting automor-
phism of order n. Then CG(φ) has exponent dividing n.
Proof. The identity
x xφ · · · xφn−1 = 1
is equivalent to the equation
(xφ−1)n = 1
in the holomorph G⟨φ⟩. If x ∈CG(φ), then this implies that xn = 1.
□
Proposition 2.2. [8, 7.1.3 Lemma]
(1) If G is finite and φ is fixed-point-free, then φ is a splitting automorphism.
(2) If G is periodic and contains no nontrivial element whose order divides n,
then every splitting automorphism of order n is fixed-point-free.
The following result shows that on the contrary to finite groups, fixed point free
automorphisms and splitting automorphisms have different natures.
Theorem 2.3. [11] Let G be a periodic group with a fixed-point-free automorphism
of order 2. Then G is abelian without elements of order 2.
Remark 2.4. In infinite groups, in contrast to the finite case, the relationship
between fixed-point-free automorphisms and splitting automorphisms is far less di-
rect. For example, the free group of rank 2 admits a fixed-point-free automorphism
of order 2, whereas every group admitting a splitting automorphism of order 2 is
abelian.
Proposition 2.5. Let G be a group, let φ ∈Aut(G) have finite order n and let
M ⊴G be of finite index. Then there exists a φ-invariant normal subgroup N ⊴G
of finite index with N ≤M. In particular, φ induces an automorphism of G/N.
Moreover, if φ is a splitting automorphism of G, then the induced automorphism
on G/N is also splitting.

3. Some properties of Tarski monsters
We now collect the facts about Tarski monsters that will be needed in the sequel.
In particular, all Tarski monsters appearing in this section are periodic, hence
infinite, two generated simple p-groups. For the details of their construction, see
[12].
Proposition 3.1. Let G be a Tarski monster. There is no nontrivial automorphism
φ ∈Aut(G) such that, for every g ∈G, the subgroup
⟨g, gφ, . . . , gφ n−1⟩
is nilpotent.

Proof. Let G be a Tarski monster and suppose, for a contradiction, that there exists
a non-trivial automorphism φ ∈Aut(G) such that for every g ∈G the subgroup
Kg := ⟨g, gφ, . . . , gφ n−1⟩
is nilpotent.
Fix 1 ̸= g ∈G.
If Kg = G, then G is nilpotent, which is impossible since
a periodic nilpotent group is locally finite and G is an infinite finitely generated
group. Hence Kg is a proper subgroup of G. By definition of a Tarski monster,
every proper nontrivial subgroup of G is cyclic of prime order. Therefore Kg = ⟨g⟩,
and in particular gφ ∈⟨g⟩.
Since this holds for every g ∈G, it follows that φ is a power automorphism of
G.
By [1], every power automorphism of G is central, that is,
x−1xφ ∈Z(G)
for all x ∈G.
Since Z(G) = 1, it follows that xφ = x for all x ∈G, so φ = idG, contradicting the
assumption that φ is non-trivial.
Therefore no such automorphism exists.
□
Remark 3.2. The direct analogue of Proposition 3.1 for groups merely involving
a Tarski monster is false in general; see Example 3.4.
Remark 3.3. Unlike the finite case, infinite non-solvable groups can have splitting
automorphisms of prime order. Let G be a Tarski monster p-group. By [12], every
proper nontrivial subgroup of G has order p, where p is a suitably large prime. Fix
a nontrivial g ∈G. For every x ∈G, one has
xxg · · · xgp−1 = (xg−1)p = 1.
Thus every inner automorphism of a Tarski monster p-group induced by a nontrivial
element is a splitting automorphism of order p.
Example 3.4. Let p be a sufficiently large prime, and let H be a Tarski monster
of exponent p. Let A be a cyclic group of prime order q such that p | q −1. Then A
admits a fixed-point-free automorphism α of order p. Set G = H × A, and define
(h, a)φ = (h, aα)
(h ∈H, a ∈A).
Then φ is a splitting automorphism of order p of the infinite finitely generated
group G. Moreover, for every (h, a) ∈G, the subgroup
⟨(h, a), (h, a)φ, . . . , (h, a)φp−1⟩
is finite. However, G is not nilpotent.
