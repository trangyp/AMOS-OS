ARITHMETIC FUNCTIONS AND LEARNING THEORY
W. BURSTEIN, A. IOSEVICH, AND A. SANT
Abstract. We establish a connection between analytic number theory and computational
learning theory by showing that the M¨obius function belongs to a class of functions that is
statistically hard to learn from random samples. Let µR denote the restriction of the M¨obius
function to the squarefree integers in {1, . . . , R}. Using a recent lower bound of Pandey and
Radziwi l l for the L1 norm of exponential sums with M¨obius coefficients, we prove that
FR(µR) ≫R−1/4−ϵ
for every ϵ > 0. We then show that, for a suitable absolute constant c0 > 0, the class of
{−1, 1}-valued functions on the squarefree integers with Fourier Ratio at least c0 has Vapnik–
Chervonenkis dimension at least cR. It follows that any distribution-independent learning
algorithm that succeeds uniformly on the class HR(ηR) containing µR, where ηR →0,
requires at least Ω(R) samples. We also discuss a conditional improvement under a strong
uniform bound for additive twists of the M¨obius function, and we note that the same method
applies to the Liouville function.
1. Introduction
Let µ(n) denote the classical M¨obius function. Recall that µ(1) = 1, µ(n) = 0 if n is not
squarefree, and µ(n) = (−1)k if n is squarefree, where k is the number of prime factors of
n. The purpose of this paper is to show that the M¨obius function is difficult to learn in the
distribution-independent setting, in the sense that any algorithm that succeeds uniformly
over the hypothesis class HR(ηR) containing µR requires a large number of samples.
We show that any distribution-independent learning algorithm that succeeds uniformly
over the class HR(ηR) containing µR and achieves nontrivial accuracy must use at least
Ω(R) samples. The key analytic input is a lower bound for the Fourier Ratio of µR, which
serves as a complexity parameter that, through an appropriate thresholded hypothesis class,
yields large Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension.
The main idea is to use the Fourier Ratio, defined as the ratio of the L1 and L2 norms of
the Fourier transform (see Definition 1.1), as a measure of complexity. A lower bound on
the Fourier Ratio bounded away from zero leads to large combinatorial complexity, in the
sense that the associated hypothesis class has large Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension
and therefore requires many samples to learn.

The Fourier Ratio has recently emerged as a useful tool in a variety of settings, including
signal complexity, spectral synthesis, and approximation theory [1, 11, 12, 7].
There is a long-standing heuristic, supported by both conjectures and partial results, that
the M¨obius function behaves in many respects like a random sequence. The Chowla conjec-
ture predicts that the values of µ(n) exhibit asymptotic independence [19], while Sarnak’s
M¨obius randomness principle [18] asserts that µ(n) is asymptotically orthogonal to all deter-
ministic sequences. Even classical estimates such as P
n≤x µ(n) = o(x), and its refinement
under the Riemann Hypothesis [6], reflect cancellation consistent with random behavior.
These perspectives suggest that the M¨obius function should be difficult to predict from
partial information.
The perspective taken in this paper is different from the traditional probabilistic or dy-
namical viewpoints. Instead of studying correlations or orthogonality properties, we measure
the complexity of µ through its Fourier transform and relate this to learnability in a pre-
cise computational sense. This provides a new bridge between analytic number theory and
statistical learning theory, suggesting that classical problems about multiplicative functions
can be reinterpreted in terms of complexity and sampling.
It is useful to place our results in the broader context of bounds for exponential sums with
multiplicative coefficients. A classical consequence of Littlewood-type inequalities [13, 14] is
that trigonometric polynomials with coefficients of modulus one have L1 norm bounded below
by a logarithmic factor. In particular, for multiplicative functions taking values in {−1, 1},
one expects ∥FR∥L1 ≫log R. Recent and forthcoming work of Klurman and Bezin (preprint,
in preparation) shows that, among multiplicative functions, the condition ∥FR∥L1 ≪log R
essentially characterizes Dirichlet characters. In contrast, the M¨obius and Liouville functions
lie in a different regime, where the L1 norm grows much faster (at least R1/4−ϵ uncondition-
ally), reflecting a higher level of arithmetic complexity.
Recent work of Pandey and Radziwi l l [16] provides a complementary structural perspec-
tive on exponential sums with multiplicative coefficients.

Remark 1.9. There is an instructive contrast between different notions of complexity illus-

distribution-independent algorithm that succeeds uniformly on a hypothesis class containing
µR requires at least Ω(R) samples to achieve nontrivial accuracy. Thus, despite its low de-

complexity.
This highlights a fundamental distinction between algorithmic complexity and statistical
complexity: a function may be easy to describe but hard to learn from data.

6. Open Problems
The results of this paper raise several questions that we believe merit further investigation.
Problem 6.1 (Improve the L1 lower bound). Pandey and Radziwi l l [15] proved that ∥FR∥L1 ≫ϵ
R1/4−ϵ. Is the true order of growth R1/2? This would be consistent with the Gaussian heuris-
tic and with the conditional result under Assumption 2.3. Improving the exponent 1/4 would
require new insights into the distribution of zeros of Dirichlet L-functions.
Problem 6.2 (Determine the true growth of ∥FR∥L∞). The best known uniform bound,
due to Baker and Harman [3], is ∥FR∥L∞≪ϵ R3/4+ϵ.
Is the true exponent 1/2?
This
is a fundamental open problem in analytic number theory, closely related to the M¨obius
randomness principle.
Problem 6.3 (Connection with the Riemann Hypothesis). Assumption 2.3 is much stronger
than the Riemann Hypothesis. Is there any direct implication from the Riemann Hypothesis
(or even the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis) to a non-trivial bound on ∥FR∥L∞? Con-
versely, if the Riemann Hypothesis were false, would it force ∥FR∥L∞to be large? These
questions remain open.
Problem 6.4 (VC dimension of HR(η)). We have shown that for constant η = c0 > 0,
VC(HR(c0)) ≥cR. What is the VC dimension of HR(η) for η = ηR decaying to zero? Our
argument shows that VC(HR(ηR)) ≥VC(HR(c0)) ≥cR whenever ηR ≤c0, but this does
not preclude the possibility that the VC dimension is actually much larger for smaller ηR.
Determining the precise dependence of VC dimension on the threshold η is an interesting
combinatorial problem.
Problem 6.5 (Other arithmetic functions). The methods of this paper apply to any arith-
metic function f satisfying PR
n=1 |f(n)|2 ≍R and the Pandey–Radziwill L1 lower bound
∥Ff∥L1 ≫R1/4−ϵ. Which multiplicative functions satisfy such an L1 lower bound? The

isfy such bounds. Exploring the learnability of other arithmetic functions is a promising
direction for future work.
Problem 6.6 (Algorithmic simplicity versus statistical complexity). The M¨obius function is
algorithmically simple: given R, one can compute µ(n) for all n ≤R by factoring each integer,
so the Kolmogorov complexity of the sequence (µ(n))n≤R is O(log R). Yet our results show
that µR belongs to hypothesis classes that are statistically hard to learn in the distribution-
independent setting, requiring Ω(R) samples for uniform success. This separation between
algorithmic and statistical complexity is reminiscent of phenomena in computational learning
theory (e.g., the existence of concept classes that are easy to represent but hard to learn). Can
one construct explicit families of functions with maximal separation between Kolmogorov
complexity and VC dimension? The M¨obius function provides a natural candidate from
number theory.
