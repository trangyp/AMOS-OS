ABSTRACT
We present ALMA detections (or stringent upper limits) of the [CII] 158 µm emission line and underlying dust continuum from five
massive quenched galaxies (QGs) at 2<z<4.7. We find extreme variations in the molecular gas fractions ( fmol =Mmol/M∗), spanning
0.1%-25%, if a standard α[CII] applies. We attempt a first empirical calibration of α[CII] with respect to dust continuum in a z = 2 lensed
QG and with respect to CO(3-2) in a z = 3.1 QG, finding no evidence of strong deviations from the standard value. Dust continuum
measurements, coupled with JWST/MIRI fluxes, suggest higher dust temperatures compared to expectations from z < 2 QGs, reaching
Td ∼40 −50 K in two galaxies. Coupled with remarkably high total infrared luminosities (LIR) not explained by observed JWST
colors not by energy balance based on literature dust extinction measurements, and with [CII] deficits down to [CII]/LIR ∼2 × 10−4
typical of (Ultra)Luminous Infrared Galaxies, our findings point to additional dust-heating mechanisms other than dust-absorbed
stellar radiation. Surprisingly, JWST/NIRCam and ALMA imaging reveal widespread disturbed stellar morphologies and offsets/tails
in dust and gas, indicative of ongoing interactions. While larger samples are needed to assess how common these features are in
high-z QGs, these findings support a merger-driven origin for the phenomenology observed in these systems, with key similarities
with respect to local post-starburst galaxies where low-velocity shocks and turbulence also inject energy into the residual ISM.

1. Introduction
In recent years, a wealth of observational evidence has revealed
that massive (log(M∗/M⊙)>10.5) quenched galaxies (MQGs)
emerged remarkably early in cosmic history (Schreiber et al.
2018; Valentino et al. 2020b; Carnall et al. 2023; Tanaka et al.
2024; de Graaff et al. 2025; Weibel et al. 2025; Antwi-Danso
et al. 2025). The onset of star formation quenching is fundamen-
tally linked to profound changes in the availability or properties
of a galaxy’s interstellar medium (ISM). This can occur through
multiple, not necessarily independent, pathways: gas heating (Ji
et al. 2024), rapid consumption (Schawinski et al. 2014), gas
loss through outflows (Belli et al. 2024; Valentino et al. 2025),
galaxy-galaxy interactions (Khoram et al. 2025; D’Onofrio et al.
2025), ram-pressure stripping (Boselli et al. 2022; Xu et al.
2025; Rhee et al. 2025), stabilization against gravitational col-
lapse (Martig et al. 2009; Michałowski et al. 2024) or starvation
(Dekel et al. 2009; Feldmann & Mayer 2015). Probing the resid-

α[CII] = 31 M⊙/L⊙conversion factor from (Zanella et al. 2018,
hereafter Z18), empirically calibrated on star-forming galaxies
at 0 < z < 6 over 4 orders of magnitudes in [CII] luminosi-
ties (L[CII]) and molecular gas masses (Mmol), would also likely
apply at high redshift. This method is best suited for recently
quenched or transitioning systems, with sSFRs less than 10%
that of the coeval Main Sequence1, but with enough residual star
formation to have enough far-UV (6-13.6 eV) photons produced
by OB stars creating Photodissociation Regions (PDRs), whose
major coolant is the [CII] line2. In the local Universe, gas-rich
early-type galaxies (ETGs) from the ATLAS3D survey (Cappel-
lari et al. 2011) exhibit a power-law correlation between LCO(1−0)
and L[CII] (Lapham et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2024), extending down
to sSFR ≲3×10−11yr−1 and SFRs of a few M⊙yr−1. This empir-
ical relation supports a connection between [CII] emission and
diffuse molecular gas even in systems with strongly suppressed
star formation. If this assumption on α[CII] is correct, [CII] ob-
servations at 3 < z < 6 would probe molecular gas fraction lim-
its significantly deeper than achievable with dust continuum or
low-J CO transitions at fixed integration time (D’Eugenio et al.
2023), allowing us to access the properties of the ISM in the
early generations of MQGs. A critical step, however, is validat-
ing the applicability of a standard α[CII] for the high-z quenched
galaxy population. Thankfully, ALMA high-frequency Bands 9
and 10, allow us to observe [CII] in galaxies at 1.2 < z < 1.4
and 1.8 < z < 2.0, enabling cross-calibration of [CII]-based gas
masses against existing dust continuum and CO measurements
in spectroscopically confirmed MQGs.
In this work we present the first observations targeting [CII]
and its underlying continuum dedicated to MQGs at 2 < z < 4.7,
using ALMA bands 7, 8 and 9, including supporting imaging
from JWST. We describe our sample in Sect. 2 and our data re-
duction and analysis in Sect. 3. We provide results on the [CII]
line and continuum measurements in Sect. 4, while we constrain
the dust temperature of our galaxies through FIR SED fitting
in Sect. 5. We discuss our findings and related uncertainties in
Sect. 6. Finally, we summarize our conclusions in Sect. 7. We
assume a ΛCDM cosmology with H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1,
ΩM = 0.27, ΩΛ = 0.73, and a Chabrier (2003) initial mass func-
tion (IMF). Magnitudes are given in the AB photometric system.
2. Sample description
The galaxies analyzed in this work were selected as well-
studied and spectroscopically confirmed quiescent galaxies with
log(M∗/M⊙)>10.5 lying at redshifts compatible with the most fa-
vorable transmission windows for high frequency observations
of the redshifted [CII] line with ALMA. They pertain to two
ALMA observing programs: program ID 2023.1.00609.S (P.I.
R. Gobat) aimed at probing the cold gas budget of high-z MQGs
through [CII], thus focused on galaxies at zspec > 2.8; program
ID 2024.1.00099.S (P.I. R. Gobat) aimed at calibrating the α[CII]
in MQGs at intermediate redshifts by following-up galaxies with
publicly available gas mass measurements from detections either
in dust continuum or CO transitions. Despite only a subset of the
pointings in these two programs were observed (33% and 25%,
respectively), we still found invaluable information. We provide
an overview of the completed observations in Table 1. In what
follows we present observed targets.

3. Reduction and analysis of ALMA data
We calibrated ALMA observations with the standard CASA
script. We split individual sources after binning the original fre-
quency channels by ×2 and performed time averaging with 30s.
We then exported uvfits files for further analysis in uv-plane with
GILDAS3. We did not perform any cleaning of the data. In the
case of GS-9209, the field is empty and thus it was unnecessary.
In the case of the SSA22 field, we fitted and subtracted 6 bright
star-forming sources in the protocluster, all detected in the [CII]
line and continuum, to avoid contamination our targets’ visibil-
ities and potential biases in the uv-plane fit. In what follows we
describe the analysis done on the spectra covering the expected
frequency of the redshifted [CII] line. We extracted the spectra
of the quiescent galaxies using our custom pipeline scripts fol-
lowing the iterative procedure described in Daddi et al. (2015),
see also Valentino et al. (2018, 2020a). In the case of secure de-
tections, we allowed fine tuning of the extraction position with
small offsets from the nominal JWST positions, while for non-
detections or weak signal (at the level of SNR ≳3) we extracted

the resulting SNR, which also defines the line full width at zero

5. Constraints on the dust temperature
Motivated by the recent reports of diffuse, hot dust (Td ∼47 K)
on scales 2-3 times larger than the rest-optical stellar component
of GS-9209 by Ji et al. (2024) we here attempt to constrain the
MIR-to-FIR SED of our sources based on weak continuum de-
tections underneath the [CII] line (or their upper limits), adding
information from MIRI imaging at 7.7µm and 21µm and archival
mm and radio measurements whenever available. We thus at-
tempt a measure of their total infrared luminosities and dust tem-
peratures.
We perform SED fitting using MICHI2 (Liu et al. 2021),
which does not require imposing energy balance between the op-
tical reddening of the stellar component and the FIR dust com-
ponent, as expected for quenched sources. We adopt: BC03 stel-
lar templates (Bruzual & Charlot 2003) to only model fluxes at
λrest > 1µm as a baseline upon which dust emission is recov-
ered; a Mid-IR AGN component based on observationally cal-
ibrated AGN torus templates from Mullaney et al. (2011); dust
templates from Draine & Li (2007) (hereafter DL07) spanning
a large range of effective dust temperatures, where dust grains
are exposed to a PDR whose strength of the interstellar radia-
tion field intensity ranges from Umin to Umax=107; and, for fits
incorporating a radio constraint, we use a default radio power-
law component tied to the total infrared luminosity through the
infrared-radio correlation, using a mass- and redshift-dependent
qIR from Delvecchio et al. (2021). The latter point is relevant
only for QG2, which is detected in radio, while upper limits for
the other sources are not stringent enough to constrain the fits
in the radio regime. We convert < U > into Td using the relation
< U >= (Td/18.9 K)6.04 resulting from Magdis et al. (2012), fol-
lowing Jin et al. (2022). All results can be found in Table 3,
where, for completeness, we include outputs obtained with and
without including an AGN torus in the fit.
5.1. Evidence for warm dust over a diffuse and extended
stellar halo around GS-9209
The ALMA cube shows a weak and extended dust continuum
signal at the JWST position of the source and corresponding to
an extended, faint stellar halo visible in the F200W, F356W and
F444W filters. We perform flux extraction in the uv space at the
JWST position of the galaxy using a Sérsic profile with n=1 cor-
responding to reff=1.8±0.5 kpc measured in Ji et al. (2024), thus
not including the leftmost continuum emission visible in Fig. 1.
Thus, we make sure not to include emission offset from the main
stellar component of the galaxy. Although a fit with n=1 might
not fully grasp the exact profile of the galaxy, it has the advan-
tage of downplaying the flux contribution from the central AGN-
dominated component, as noted in Ji et al. (2024), and extending
further out of the stellar component of the galaxy. We obtain
Fcont = 0.064 ± 0.017 mJy, which we add to the FIR SED col-
lected in Ji et al. (2024) (see their Fig.7) which refers to the inte-
grated stellar profile of the galaxy (r<0.7"). We also incorporate
their 1.2 mm dust continuum upper limit in our SED fitting.
The best fitting model (without an AGN component) is
shown as a black solid curve in Fig. B.1 (see Appendix B)
and it is largely dominated by a warm dust component with
Td > 43.9 K, whereas restricting the fit to a single cold dust
component or to a combination of cold dust and AGN compo-
nents does not fit the available JWST/MIRI photometry equally
well. The associated dust mass upper limit is fairly uncertain
with log(Md,TOT/M⊙) < 7.5. Assuming a standard gas-to-dust ra-
tio δGD=100, we obtain a cold gas mass of log(Mgas/M⊙) < 9.5,
which would result in L[CII] < 0.9 × 108 L⊙, consistent with our
non detection. Our results support the presence of diffuse warm
dust in GS-9209 as reported in Ji et al. (2024).
In order to evaluate if additional dust is present beyond what
recovered based on the JWST profile, we measured the dust con-
tinuum signal-to-noise at increasingly large FWHM. We detect
a peak of SNR=5.05 at FWHM=0.4" corresponding to a flux
density of fcont,[CII] = 0.184 ± 0.028 mJy, about 2.8 times the
flux density measured at FWHM=0.2". The excess signal seem
to pertain to the diffuse stellar halo seen in the residual images
from JWST. In Fig. 2 and Fig. 4 (top left panel) we show a ta-
pered image of GS-9209 with a synthesized beam of 1" (black
contours and white dashed contours, in the two respective fig-
ures). Overall, we report an offset of 0.35" between the centroid
of the tapered image and the nominal position of the galaxy from
the JWST/MIRI F560W filter.

We extracted JWST/MIRI F770W and F2100W fluxes using au-

et al. (2022), together with our rest 158µm dust continuum upper
limit. We fit QG2 anchoring the normalization of the stellar tem-

fixed the qIR using the Delvecchio et al. (2021) relation and we
impose E(B-V)<0.12, taking the maximum value allowed from
Kubo et al. (2022). For QG2, the best fits systematically hit the
upper boundary on <U>, which arises from the maximum Umin
allowed in the DL07 models. Therefore, we quote the lower un-
certainty on our best fit value as a lower limit on <U> and on Td,
thus < U >> 497.17 and Td > 52.8K. The artificial upper bound-
ary on Td implies that the lower uncertainty on dust masses could
be lower. Therefore, whenever we have a lower limit on Td, dust
masses should be regarded as upper limits. These results do not
change if we force a substantial AGN torus component to the
MIR. However, we note that the F2100W emission in QG2 is
resolved and consistent with a Sérsic profile. This suggests that
an AGN torus does not contribute much to MIR fluxes, and that
QG2 hosts dust (and likely gas) at larger temperatures compared
to expectations from the size evolution of quiescent galaxies de-
scribed in (Magdis et al. 2021) and larger than in star-forming
galaxies at the same redshift (see Fig. 5).
As can be seen in Table 3 QG2 has very high LIR and Td
values. They stem from including the radio fluxes in the SED
fitting while imposing a qIR = 2.45, so that MICHI2 selects the
largest LIR that still allows it to properly fit the normalization
of the radio component. In this case we see a mild radio excess,
which would imply qIR < 2.30, within the scatter of the Delvec-
chio et al. (2021) relation, thus not formally requiring any radio
AGN component in the galaxy. We do not find a low qIR = 0.61
described in Kubo et al. (2022) likely due to our larger LIR upper
limit found including the MIRI detection to our fits. On the other
hand, if we remove our constraints on qIR all parameters become
unconstrained when including the AGN torus component in the
fit. As mentioned above, however, the rest-frame 3-4 µm emis-
sion is resolved and does not show evidence of a dominant cen-
tral source. Removing the AGN torus template and the constraint
on qIR still yields log(LIR/L⊙) = 12.13 ± 0.07 and Td > 49.8 K.

6. Discussion
6.1. Constraints on α[CII] and implications for gas mass
measurements for high-z QGs
In this section, we attempt to constrain α[CII] using M0138 and
QG1, the only two targets in our sample with detections in both
[CII] and an independent gas mass tracer. Although based on
just two galaxies, this represents, to our knowledge, the first
attempt to constrain α[CII] in systems with strongly suppressed
star formation activity, particularly at cosmic noon. We begin
with M0138 and then consider QG1, leveraging detections in ei-
ther dust continuum or CO(3–2). The accuracy of α[CII] depends
on the adopted conversion factors and their uncertainties (δGD
or αCO). Given the limited sample size, we do not attempt to
quantify the intrinsic scatter in α[CII] and instead adopt standard
conversion factors without accounting for their dispersion, as is
commonly done in the literature.
6.1.1. Clues from dust continuum in M0138
We constrain α[CII] for M0138 using the most accurate dust
mass and stellar metallicity measurements currently avail-
able. To derive the gas mass, we estimate δGD from the
gas-phase metallicity-dependent scaling relation of Magdis
et al. (2012). Although no gas-phase metallicity is avail-
able for M0138, spatially resolved stellar metallicity measure-
ments for Image 1 from Jafariyazani et al. (2020) provide
[Fe/H] = 0.21 ± 0.06 and [Mg/Fe] = 0.51 ± 0.02 in the core re-
gion, where dust is detected. We conservatively approximate
[O/H]≈[Fe/H]+[Mg/Fe]6, yielding 12 + log(O/H) = 9.4 ± 0.1
and a low δGD = 17. This value is ∼5 times lower than the
commonly adopted δGD=100 and significantly below predic-
tions from recent simulations (e.g., Whitaker et al. 2021b),
which invoke efficient dust destruction. Adopting the Magdis
et al. (2012) scaling implicitly assumes negligible dust de-
struction. Having derived the δGD for this galaxy, we pro-
ceed to convert the dust mass obtained by our SED fitting,
as well as the dust mass previously obtained in Gobat et al.
(2022), into gas masses that we use for our test. Using the dust
mass from Sect. 5.3, we derive µMmol = 9.3+35.4

6.2. Widespread merger signatures underlying [CII] and dust
continuum offsets
Local PSBs typically host strong tidal features and kinematics
indicative of violent relaxation due to major, late stage merg-
ers (Zabludoff et al. 1996; Yang et al. 2004, 2008). While rare
locally, they are expected to be more common at high redshift
(Tran et al. 2004; Kriek et al. 2016; Zahid et al. 2016). Visual
inspection of several of our four z > 3 targets suggests disturbed
morphologies or additional components beyond the main galaxy
emission, possibly related to offsets in ALMA emission (contin-
uum or [CII]). We thus systematically inspected the morphology
of our z > 3 galaxies by fitting and subtracting a Sérsic com-
ponent from the F444W image (we used F356W for GS-9209
due to the higher SNR) with GALFIT. Fitting the whole galax-
ies, including their extended halos, failed to converge, confirm-
ing structural complexity. We thus proceeded by constraining the
best fitting Sérsic model only on galaxy cores, and subtracting it
from the whole image with the same parameters. When the pro-
cedure revealed an additional component overlapping with the
main Sérsic core (QG2 and QG1) we fit double Sérsic models
to account for this. Strong residuals can be found in all cases, as
shown in Fig. 4.

in QG2, a candidate case of ISM heating driven by radio-mode
feedback.

6.4. Comparing z > 3 quiescent galaxies to local
post-starbursts
Given that MQGs at high redshift are observed shortly after
quenching, it is instructive to compare them with lower-redshift
PSB systems. We adopt as a reference Smercina et al. (2018)
(hereafter S18) who studied 33 local E+A PSB galaxies com-
bining infrared photometry, Spitzer IRS spectroscopy, [CII] and
[OI] emission lines from Herschel PACS. These observations en-
abled constraints on total gas and dust masses, L[CII] and Td,
and thus they are particularly suited for our study. S18 reports
high central surface densities and even more compact IR cores
of warm dust, heated by a high-intensity soft radiation field
from A-type stars. Such a radiation field boosts their LIRtot
driving strong [CII] deficits. H2 spectroscopy further indicates
widespread shock-dominated regions suggesting, overall, that
their ISM is supported against collapse by a continued injection
of turbulent or mechanical heating. Here we list their main find-
ings and highlight key differences with our sources.
1) Dust is warm: on average, SEDs in S18 peak at
λpeak ≈70 −75 µm instead of ∼100 µm of normal star-
forming galaxies, implying warmer grains and a higher
intensity radiation field, comparable to (U)LIRGs, corre-
sponding roughly to Td ∼30–35 K and < U >∼30–40,
respectively. This heating is attributed to dense stellar
regions dominated by A-type stars where a "high-soft"
radiation field is intense enough to heat dust grains without a
significant FUV contribution from on-going star formation.
In contrast, we find λpeak ≈40 −48 µm and thus even
warmer dust temperatures. Although the proximity of our
galaxies to their quenching epoch suggests a potentially
stronger soft-UV field from A-type stars, in QG2 and
GS-9209 the LIRtot expected from reprocessed stellar light
falls far short of the observed one. Additional mechanisms
that could provide such heating are widespread shocks,
heating at formation from AGB stellar envelopes or AGN
radio-mode feedback, which we explore in the following
sections;
2) The ISM in S18 is mainly distributed in compact IR
cores: typically on scales smaller than 1/3 of the optical size,
approaching (U)LIRGs-like sizes at rest 8 µm (∼500 pc,
Tacconi et al. 2006) suggestive of dense, compact star-
forming progenitors. In our case, M0138 (z = 2) shows a
compact dust core in the bulge but our data only provide
a 2σ upper limit of 0.35′′ (3 kpc) on its FIR size. Optical
source-plane morphology reveals a regular disk with no
clear signs of recent interactions (Newman et al. 2025).
For the rest of our sample, dust continuum and/or [CII]
emission is extended, on scales comparable to or larger than
the optical size, at odds with S18.
While interpreting the bulk of L[CII] as purely tracing molec-
ular gas is uncertain due to possible contributions from the
ionized phase, the extent of our detections suggests that
we might be witnessing the redistribution of gas following
the major assembly event of our galaxies, rather than the
formation of very compact IR cores as in local PSBs;
3) [CII] deficits: As can be seen in Zanella et al. (2018),
L[CII]/LIR tend to drop significantly below 0.1% for galaxies
with LIR > 1012L⊙, thus exhibiting a [CII] deficit compared
to their high LIR. [CII]/LIR ratios in S18 show a rather large
variation7, typically around 0.1–0.2%, with a few cases
reaching down to 0.01% and many upper limits indicating

6.4.1. Plausible dust heating mechanisms
In this and the following subsection, we speculate on what pro-
cesses could account for dust heating in excess of stellar radia-
tion. While a definitive identification might be difficult and be-
yond the scope of this work, it is interesting to list several mech-
anisms that can play a role in our galaxies:
A) Shocks: shocks induced by tidal interactions or mergers
—widespread in our sample— can generate turbulence and
heat dust, as observed in local PSBs and AGN hosts (Haidar
et al. 2024, 2026), without significant dust destruction.
Notably, QG2 and GS-9209, which exhibit the highest Td
and strongest [CII] deficits, also show disturbed stellar
morphologies, including lopsided structures and extended
stellar halos on ∼10–15 kpc scales. Furthermore, at high
redshift galaxies are denser and more compact so, possibly,
shocks might be even more efficient than locally;
B) AGB stars: with stellar ages of ∼200–800 Myr, our galaxies
are likely near the peak of AGB dust production. Dust in
AGB envelopes can reach temperatures > 400 K (Lagadec
et al. 2005) and its contribution to the emission from the
stellar surface is ingrained in stellar templates, thus difficult
to disentangle with simple energy balance considerations;

6.4.2. QG2: Candidate test-case of on-going radio-mode
feedback at z=3
For QG2, SED fitting of the 21 µm MIRI detection combined
with ALMA upper limits yields a robust lower limit of Td > 52
K, exceeding typical values for star-forming galaxies at similar
redshifts (Béthermin et al. 2015; Schreiber et al. 2015). MICHI2
fits rule out a dominant AGN torus component, and the spa-
tially resolved 21 µm emission indicates that the warm dust is
distributed on galactic scales, rather than in an unresolved nu-
clear component. F444W residuals reveal a lopsided morphol-
ogy consistent with a past major merger. The [CII] line profile in
Fig. 8 (blue and red contours) suggests rotation or two merging
components, although the low SNR prevents a clear assessment.
6 GHz radio contours at 3σ in Kubo et al. (2022) reveal an elon-
gated and diffuse component which we detect in continuum at
158 µm rest at 4σ, without any stellar counterpart in JWST im-
ages up to ∼7 µm. The radio power measured on the core of
QG2, AGN-like optical line ratios, and the morphology aligned
with the radio axis suggest the presence of a putative radio hot-
spot. The lack of NIRCam and MIRI counterparts at its position
disfavors jet-induced dust-obscured star formation. The absence
of a 1.1 mm counterpart for our 158 µm rest detection, how-
ever, disfavors synchrotron emission and suggests potential jet-
induced dust heating. Thus, the high L[CII] measured on QG2,
compared to weak 1.1mm dust continuum and CO(3-2) emis-
sion, suggests [CII] powering by shocks or turbulence driven by
a merger and/or a radio jet. We cannot exclude, however, a con-
tribution to L[CII] from the [OIII] ionized outflow observed in the
optical.

6.6. The origin of [CII] emission in high-z QGs
It is not straightforward to assign the origin of [CII] to a given
phase of the ISM of our galaxies. Assessing the contribution of
ionized gas to L[CII] is particularly relevant to understand the
plausibility of our assumption on α[CII] and to understand which
ISM phase are we considering when comparing our [CII] maps
to the emission from other galaxy components. Given the lower
ionization potential of carbon (11.3 eV) compared to hydrogen
(13.6 eV), [CII] is produced both in the ionized and the neu-
tral gas components. Most studies have concluded that the bulk
of [CII] emission originates in neutral regions, both in the lo-
cal universe and at high-z, with a contribution from ionized gas

typically ranging around ∼5-30% (Wolfire et al. 2022). In star-
forming galaxies, [CII] primarily arises from classical photodis-
sociation regions (PDRs), where FUV radiation from young O
and B stars heats gas and dust via the photoelectric effect.8 In
classic PDRs, the gas and dust are heated by photoelectric ef-
fect, namely by the ejection of electrons from small dust grains
and PAHs, which provides partners for collisional excitation of
fine-structure line emission (free electrons) and continuum emis-
sion (dust grains). [CII] can also originate in X-ray-dominated
regions (XDRs), where X-rays from an AGN accretion disk or
from X-ray binaries enhance gas heating and cooling efficien-
cies. However, this enhancement is limited to the immediate sur-
roundings of the heating source, with typical sizes of a few hun-
dreds of parsecs (Wolfire et al. 2022). In our sample, three of
the four [CII]-detected galaxies (M0138, QG1, QG3) are unde-
tected in X-rays, while QG2 hosts a luminous AGN but shows
extended [CII] emission on ∼3 kpc scales. Additionally, QG1
and QG3 show an extended [CII] tail far outside the stellar body
of the galaxy, likely due to an outflow or a tidal event. Although
low-level AGN activity cannot be ruled out in all cases, the avail-
able evidence suggests that XDRs do not dominate the observed
[CII] emission.
Ideally, the ionized gas contribution to L[CII] could be con-
strained via the [CII]158/[NII]205 ratio (Schreiber et al. 2021)
which, however, is unavailable for our sample. We can, how-
ever, take a look at local ETGs: Lapham et al. (2017) find low
[CII]/[NII] ratios in about 25% of their sample indicating en-
hanced gas ionization preferentially in systems with larger dust
temperatures. Our results for QG2 suggest that a significant part
of its [CII] luminosity could indeed arise from ionized gas, po-
tentially linked to the same process leading to its high dust
temperature. Dissipation of mechanical energy (turbulence and
shocks) in highly turbulent conditions –such as galaxy mergers
or AGN-driven outflows– was also proposed as a [CII] power-
ing source (Appleton et al. 2013, 2017; De Breuck et al. 2022).
While S18 argue for low-velocity shocks as a viable source of
heating in local PSBs, QG2 also hosts an [OIII] outflow. Thus,
comparing the spatial distribution of the [OIII] emission line rel-
ative to [CII] will be useful to assess any role of the outflow in
powering the [CII] line. Finally, measuring [CII]/PAH ratios will
be useful to assess any excess [CII] emission relative to what ex-
pected from classical PDRs.
7. Summary and Conclusions
We present ALMA observations of the [CII] line emission and
its underlying continuum in 5 massive quiescent galaxies at
2 < z < 4.7. Lacking a consensus on the actual ISM content
in this class of galaxies, we exploit the first harvesting of deep
high-z [CII] observations to probe their gas reservoirs with un-
precedented sensitivity to study their ISM properties and cos-
mic evolution. Complementary archival JWST NIRCam imag-
ing, and MIRI where available, enables a detailed analysis of the
host morphologies and SED modeling from the near- to mid-IR,
providing additional constraints on their dust content. We sum-
marize our results as follows:
i) We obtain the first [CII] detections in QGs in the distant
Universe: three secure (M0138, QG2, QG3), one tentative

ii) We attempt a first cross-calibration of α[CII] for QGs in the
distant Universe, finding no evidence for a strong evolution.
For QG2 we measure the CO/[CII] ratio finding it consistent
with both local QGs and star-forming galaxies. This provides
reliability to attempts at estimating the gas content of distant

a mild increase in α[CII] of a factor of 2–3 in galaxies with a

formation episodes;
v) We find further evidence for warm dust (supporting Ji et al.
2024) together with evidence of dust heating not due to stel-
lar radiation absorbed by dust. The IR of QG2 and GS-9209
violate the expectations from reprocessed radiation from dust
by over an order of magnitude;
v) The measured LIR are so large that these galaxies would be
placed in the Main Sequence, despite convincing evidence
for their quiescence from literature studies and JWST col-
ors. This implies that IR luminosities cannot be used to esti-
mate SFRs for these sources, as advocated in lower redshift
studies;
vi) Plausible heating mechanisms include merger-driven shocks
and production in AGB stellar envelopes, consistent with
their complex morphologies and their young stellar ages (< 1
Gyr) and altered grain properties;
vii) QG2 likely requires further explanation as it shows excess
gas mass from [CII] relative to its dust mass, very warm dust
temperatures, and potentially a very high gas-to-dust frac-
tion. If the presence of a radio jet is confirmed, we suggest
that there could be radio-mode feedback enhancing L[CII] and
dust heating in this galaxy, representing the first known case
to our knowledge;
viii) A careful comparison with local PSBs reveals several simi-
larities (warm dust, evidence for mergers, heating and turbu-
lence injection by shocks) but also key differences, includ-
ing potentially stronger [CII] deficits, more extended gas and
dust reservoirs, and non stellar dust heating;
ix) We discuss the origin of [CII] emission, considering con-
tributions from both PDRs and XDRs, and from molecular,
neutral and ionized gas phases.
This work highlights a number of unexpected findings that,
at the same time, raise key questions on how these could be con-
firmed and best understood. In particular, the possibility of non-
stellar ISM heating requires further confirmation. One possible
way to distinguish between shock-dominated regions and AGB
stars is assessing PAH line ratios and H2 rotational emission via
MIRI spectroscopy and to verify the presence of AGB features in
the rest-frame NIR, ideally with much larger samples. Further-
more, improved constraints on the FIR SED peak are essential
to make these results less model dependent. Lastly, significantly
larger samples are needed to determine whether these findings
are representative or whether they pertain to individual outliers.
