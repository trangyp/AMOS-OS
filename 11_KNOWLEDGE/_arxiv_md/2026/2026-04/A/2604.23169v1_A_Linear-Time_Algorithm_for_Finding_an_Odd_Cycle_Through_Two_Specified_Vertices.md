A Linear-Time Algorithm
for Finding an Odd Cycle Through Two Specified Vertices
Takumi Kano∗
Yutaro Yamaguchi†
Abstract
We present a deterministic linear-time algorithm for finding an odd cycle through two
specified vertices in an undirected graph. This is shown in a generalized form as follows:
Let Γ be any group in which every element is of order at most 2. For a given Γ-labeled
graph with two specified vertices (or edges), we can determine in linear time whether there
exist two cycles with distinct labels that are through both of the two specified vertices (or
edges), and find such cycles if yes.
Keywords: Cycle through three vertices, Group-labeled graphs, Non-zero cycle, SPQR-tree

Introduction
In this paper, we show the following:
Theorem 1.1. There exists a deterministic algorithm to find an odd cycle through two specified
vertices in a given undirected graph that runs in O(n + m) time, where n and m denote the
numbers of vertices and of edges in the input graph, respectively.
This extends a linear-time algorithm for finding a cycle through three specified vertices in
a given undirected graph [3]. To see this, let G be an undirected graph, and x, y, z be three
distinct vertices in G. We construct from G an auxiliary graph ˜G as follows: subdivide each
edge in G with exactly one extra vertex in the middle, split the vertex z into two copies z1 and
z2 with the same neighbor as the original z, and add an extra edge between z1 and z2. Then, a
cycle in ˜G is odd if and only if it is through the extra edge {z1, z2}. Thus, an odd cycle through
both x and y in ˜G corresponds to a cycle through all of x, y, and z in G.
In order to prove Theorem 1.1, we actually solve a more general problem in group-labeled
graphs, which is formally defined in Section 2.2. Our main theorem is stated as follows:
Theorem 1.2. Let Γ be a group in which every element is of order at most 2 (i.e., self-inverse).
There exists a deterministic algorithm to determine whether the label of a cycle through two
specified vertices (or two specified disjoint edges) in a given biconnected Γ-labeled graph is unique
or not in O(n + m) time. Moreover, if it is not unique, the algorithm outputs two such cycles
with distinct labels.
The assumption that every element of Γ is of order at most 2 is essential. For signed graphs,
that is, for Γ = Z2 = Z/2Z, DeVos and Nurse [1] recently gave a structural characterization of
when there exist two cycles passing through two specified edges whose signs are different. This
characterization (in a rephrased form) plays a key role in our algorithm, for which we give a
constructive proof with an efficient algorithm (see Section 4).
Theorem 1.3 (DeVos and Nurse [1] (rephrased)). Let G be a triconnected Z2-labeled graph,
and e1, e2 ∈E(G) be disjoint edges that have no parallel edges. Then, G contains two cycles
passing through both e1 and e2 with distinct labels if and only if G−{e1, e2} contains a non-zero
cycle of length at least three.
Indeed, this characterization fails already for triconnected Z3-labeled graphs, as shown in
Figure 1. In particular, even though G −{e1, e2} contains a cycle on {x1, x2, w, v, u} with label
2 −1 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 1, which is non-zero, it may still happen that every cycle through the
distinguished edges e1 and e2 has the same label 0. For precise definitions on group-labeled
graphs, see Section 2.2.
The proof of Theorem 1.3 in [1] is by a minimal-counterexample argument with an extremal
choice of a non-zero cycle. Thus, it does not provide a fast algorithm to find explicit witness
cycles even for Z2-labeled graphs. Our main contribution is showing Theorem 1.2, which gives
a linear-time algorithm to find target cycles for a more general class of group-labeled graphs.
We should remark that a polynomial-time algorithm for our problem (when |Γ| is constant)
can be derived from [9,11]. In [11], Kawarabayashi, Reed, and Wollan gave an O(mn·α(m, n))-
time algorithm for finding k vertex-disjoint paths in a given undirected graph between k specified
pairs of vertices with a specified parity of each path for any fixed k. It is not difficult to see
that, by taking k = 2 and checking all of 22 = 4 patterns of parities for each element of a fixed
generating set of Γ, we can solve our problem via this setting. In [9], Huynh also provided a
polynomial-time algorithm for finding k vertex-disjoint paths in a given Γ-labeled graph between

Preliminaries
In this section, we provide necessary definitions, related concepts, and basic facts used in this
paper. Throughout the paper, graphs may have parallel edges, but have no self-loops.
2.1
Graphs
Let G = (V, E) be an undirected graph. An alternating sequence W = (v0, e1, v1, . . . , eℓ, vℓ) of
vertices and edges in G is called a walk in G if ei = {vi−1, vi} ∈E for every i = 1, 2, . . . , ℓ. We
call ℓthe length of W, and we say that W is odd and even if ℓis odd and even, respectively.
We say that W is a path if all its vertices v0, v1, . . . , vℓare distinct, and a cycle if ℓ≥2, almost
all its vertices v1, . . . , vℓare distinct, v0 = vℓ, and e1 ̸= eℓ. In particular, a walk of length zero
is regarded as a path, which is called trivial, and a walk consisting of two distinct parallel edges
is regarded as a cycle. A path is referred to as a v0–vℓpath (or an X–Y path when v0 ∈X
and vℓ∈Y ) if the end vertices are emphasized. We denote by P[vi, vj] the subpath of a path
P from vi to vj, where the direction is flipped accordingly.
We say that G is connected if every pair of vertices, say s, t ∈V , there exists an s–t path
in G. For each integer k ≥1, a k-cut is a vertex set X ⊆V with |X| ≤k such that its removal
results in a disconnected graph G −X. For k ≥2, we say that G is k-connected if |V | > k and
it has no (k −1)-cut. In particular, it is also called biconnected when k = 2 and triconnected
when k = 3. A bridge is an edge whose removal makes the graph disconnected, and a block is a
maximal biconnected subgraph or a two-vertex subgraph with at least two parallel edges which
becomes a bridge if we merge them into a single edge. It is well-known that any connected
graph has a unique tree structure formed by the blocks and bridges that are connected by the
1-cuts, called the block-cut tree, which can be computed in linear time [7]. Any cycle is included
in a single block, and if a cycle through two specified vertices s and t exists, then any of such
cycles is included in the unique block that contains both s and t. A further decomposition of
biconnected graphs into triconnected components is also known [8], and we explain a closely
related concept, the so-called SPQR-trees, in Section 3.1.

2.2
Group-Labeled Graphs
We first introduce a general definition of group-labeled graphs. Let Γ be a group, for which
we use the multiplicative notation without assuming commutativity at first. A Γ-labeled graph
is a pair (⃗G, ψ) of a directed graph ⃗G = (V, ⃗E) and a label function ψ: ⃗E →Γ. Let G =
(V, E) denote the underlying graph of ⃗G, i.e., E := { e = {u, v} | ⃗e = uv ∈⃗E }, and define
ψ(e, uv) := ψ(⃗e) and ψ(e, vu) := ψ(⃗e)−1 for each edge e = {u, v} ∈E with the corresponding
arc ⃗e = uv ∈⃗E. The label of a walk W = (v0, e1, v1, . . . , eℓ, vℓ) is defined as
ψ(W) := ψ(e1, v0v1) · ψ(e2, v1v2) · · · · · ψ(eℓ, vℓ−1vℓ).
A walk is called non-zero if its label is not the identity element of Γ. We say that (⃗G, ψ) is
balanced if it contains no non-zero cycle.
In this paper, we focus on the case where every element of Γ is of order at most 2; that
is, for any α ∈Γ, we have α−1 = α. Then, ψ(e, uv) = ψ(e, vu) for any edge e = {u, v} ∈E,
so the orientation of the edges becomes irrelevant.
Thus, we identify the original directed
graph ⃗G with its underlying graph G, and each arc ⃗e = uv ∈⃗E with its corresponding edge
e = {u, v} ∈E. Also, in this case, Γ is automatically abelian. Indeed, for any α, β ∈Γ, we have
αβ = (αβ)−1 = β−1α−1 = βα. Thus, we use the additive notation hereafter.
Our problems are formally stated as follows (recall that any cycle through s and t is included
in the unique block that contains s and t, which enable us to assume biconnectivity):
Two Labels of Cycles Through Two Vertices
Input: A biconnected Γ-labeled graph (G = (V, E), ψ) and two distinct vertices s, t ∈V .
Goal: Determine whether there exist two cycles C1 and C2 in G that intersect both s and
t with ψ(C1) ̸= ψ(C2), and if so, find such cycles.
Two Labels of Cycles Through Two Disjoint Edges
Input: A biconnected Γ-labeled graph (G = (V, E), ψ) and two distinct edges es, et ∈E.
Goal: Determine whether there exist two cycles C1 and C2 in G that pass through both
es and et with ψ(C1) ̸= ψ(C2), and if so, find such cycles.
Theorem 1.2 states that these problems admit a deterministic linear-time algorithm.
If
Γ = Z2 = Z/2Z and ψ(e) = 1 for every e ∈E, the former problem exactly requires to determine
the possible parities of cycles in G through both s and t and to find such a cycle of each possible
parity. Thus, Theorem 1.1 (and the same statement for an even cycle) holds.
We also note that the two problems are essentially equivalent, i.e., each easily reduces to
the other. Indeed, from the latter to the former, it is enough to subdivide each of the edges es
and et with an extra vertex s and t, respectively, and to assign the original label to one of the
two subdivided edges and label 0 to the other. This operation preserves biconnectivity and the
labels of target cycles. From the former to the latter, split s into two vertices s1 and s2, add
an edge es = s1s2 with label 0, and replace each edge su by two edges s1u and s2u, both with
label ψ(su); do the same at t, obtaining t1, t2, and a new edge et with label 0. The resulting
graph is still biconnected, and contracting es and et (and merging parallel edges of the same
label into a single one) recovers the original graph, so the cycles through s and t correspond
exactly to the cycles through es and et, and the labels are preserved.
Thus, in what follows, we concentrate on the latter problem.

Lemma 2.1. Let G = (V, E) be a graph, s ∈V , T ⊆V , and Q be a path from s to a vertex of
T. Then, Q has a subpath Q′ starting at s, ending in T, and satisfying |V (Q′) ∩T| = 1.
For s ∈V and T ⊆V \ {s}, an s–T k-fan is a collection of k s–T paths such that they
only share the vertex s and each path has no inner vertex in T. By Menger’s theorem [13], any
k-connected graph contains a collection of k vertex-disjoint paths between two vertex subsets
(as well as a k-fan), which can be computed in O(k(|V | + |E|)) time using the Ford–Fulkerson
algorithm for maximum flow problem [4] (see also [15, Section 9.2]).
Lemma 2.2. There exists a deterministic algorithm running in O(k(|V | + |E|)) time that finds
for a given k-connected graph G = (V, E) and two vertex sets A, B ⊆V with |A| ≥k (or
A = {s}) and |B| ≥k, a collection of vertex-disjoint A–B paths (or an s–B k-fan, respectively)
such that all the inner vertices are disjoint from A ∪B.
It is well-known that the following elementary problems on (general) group-labeled graphs
can be solved in linear time (see [10,12] for details and more general problems):
• test whether a group-labeled graph is balanced or not, and if not, find a non-zero cycle;
• find a non-zero cycle through a specified vertex s (if any);
• find a non-zero s–t path between two specified vertices s and t (if any).

λ ∈X, an explicit s–t path Pλ with ψ(Pλ) = λ, such that |X| = min{|L|, 2}.

Reduction via SPQR-Tree
In this section, we reduce our problem Two Labels of Cycles Through Two Disjoint
Edges with the aid of the SPQR-trees to a unique nontrivial task, naming it Two Labels of
Cycles Through Two Edges in Triconnected Graphs.
3.1
SPQR-Trees
An SPQR-tree represents the decomposition of a biconnected graph into triconnected compo-
nents, which was introduced by Di Battista and Tamassia [2]. We review its definition and
properties according to [5,6].
Let G = (V, E) be a biconnected graph. The SPQR-tree is obtained by recursively decom-
posing G along split pairs, i.e., pairs of vertices that form either a 2-cut (also called a separation
pair) or a pair of adjacent vertices, and by replacing each separated part by a virtual edge. Thus,
each decomposition step splits the graph into two parts sharing exactly the corresponding split
pair, and the original graph can be recovered by expanding the virtual edges recursively.
Each node µ of the SPQR-tree has an associated graph, called the skeleton of µ and denoted
by skel(µ). There are four types of nodes. A Q-node corresponds to a single original edge of G,
an S-node has a cycle as its skeleton, a P-node has a skeleton consisting of two vertices joined
by parallel edges, and an R-node has a triconnected skeleton. In particular, each original edge
e ∈E corresponds to a unique Q-node, which we denote by Q(e). Moreover, the skeletons of the
S-, P-, and R-nodes are exactly the components in the triconnected component decomposition
of G.
Each skeleton edge is either real or virtual. A real edge is an original edge of G, while a
virtual edge is newly introduced for the decomposition. If two nodes µ and ν are adjacent in the
SPQR-tree, then skel(µ) and skel(ν) contain corresponding virtual edges with the same pair of
end vertices. This common pair of end vertices is the split pair corresponding to the tree edge
{µ, ν}. Thus, deleting the tree edge {µ, ν} splits the tree into two components, and each side
represents a subgraph attached to the other side through that split pair.
Once the tree is rooted (at a node or a connected node set), every non-root node ν has
a unique parent µ that is adjacent to ν and closer to the root(s) than ν. The virtual edge
in skel(ν) corresponding to this tree edge {ν, µ} is called the parent virtual edge of ν, whose
end vertices are called the poles of ν. The pertinent graph pert(ν) is obtained from skel(ν) by
expanding every virtual edge except the parent one. Equivalently, if eν is the parent virtual
edge of ν, then pert(ν) −eν is exactly the subgraph of G represented by the subtree rooted at
ν, and it is attached to the rest of the graph only through the poles of ν.
The SPQR-tree is unique up to isomorphism, can be computed in linear time, and the
total size of all skeletons is O(|V | + |E|). In the reduction below, we focus on the unique tree
path between the Q-nodes corresponding to the distinguished edges, together with the off-path
subtrees attached to it.
3.2
Reduction
Let (G = (V, E), ψ) be a biconnected Γ-labeled graph, and let es, et ∈E be disjoint.
Step 1. Let T be an SPQR-tree of G, and let Q(es) = µ0, µ1, . . . , µk = Q(et) be the nodes on
the unique path in T from Q(es) to Q(et). For each i = 0, 1, . . . , k, let ℓi and ri denote the left
and right interface edges of µi, namely, ℓ0 := es, rk := et, and for 1 ≤i ≤k, let ℓi be the virtual
edge of skel(µi) corresponding to the tree edge {µi−1, µi}, while, for 0 ≤i ≤k −1, let ri be the
virtual edge of skel(µi) corresponding to the tree edge {µi, µi+1}.

Step 2.
Now regard the path {µ0, µ1, . . . , µk} as the root of T. Thus every node ν outside
this path has a unique parent, namely the neighbor of ν closer to the path. Let aν, bν be the
poles of ν, and let Gν = pert(ν) −eν be the subgraph of G represented by the subtree rooted
at ν, after deleting the parent virtual edge.
Whenever a cycle through es and et uses the part represented by ν, it enters that part at
one pole and leaves it at the other. Hence, from the viewpoint of the parent, the only relevant
information carried by this subtree is the set Mν := { ψ(P) | P is an aν–bν path in Gν }. By
Lemma 2.5, in linear time we can compute a representative set Σν ⊆Mν of size min{|Mν|, 2},
together with realizing aν–bν paths.
This is sufficient because every later use of Mν only adds a fixed value to an element of
Mν. Since α ̸= β implies α + γ ̸= β + γ for any α, β, γ in a group Γ, it is enough to keep one
representative if |Mν| = 1, and any two distinct representatives otherwise.
Compute these summaries for all outside nodes ν adjacent to the path {µ0, µ1, . . . , µk}. More
precisely, when processing such a node ν, apply Lemma 2.5 to compute a representative set Σν
of the label of aν–bν paths in Gν. After that, every virtual edge corresponding to a subtree
outside the path can be replaced by one or two edges of distinct labels corresponding to the
summary of that subtree, and we keep the corresponding realizing paths for later reconstruction.
After that, every off-path virtual edge can be replaced by one or two edges of distinct labels
corresponding to the summary of the attached subtree, and we keep the corresponding realizing
paths for later reconstruction.
Step 3. For each i = 0, 1, . . . , k, let Hi be the graph obtained from skel(µi) by replacing every
virtual edge other than ℓi and ri by the already computed summary edges of the corresponding
off-path subtree. Then Hi is a Γ-labeled graph, possibly with parallel edges, with two distin-
guished edges ℓi and ri, whose labels are set to be 0. Let Li be the set of labels of cycles in Hi
through both ℓi and ri, truncated to at most two representatives together with realizing local
cycles.
Any cycle through es and et induces, for each i, a cycle in Hi through both ℓi and ri, since
each off-path subtree is attached to the rest of the graph only through its two poles and can
therefore be replaced by the corresponding summary edge. Conversely, any choice of one such
local cycle in each Hi combines along the corresponding interface edges, and after expanding
each summary edge by its stored realizing path, reconstructs a cycle through es and et in the
original graph.
Since all interface edges have label 0, the label of the resulting cycle is the sum of the chosen
local labels. Hence, if all local label sets are singletons, then the set of labels of cycles through
es and et is also a singleton, whereas if some Lj has size at least two, fixing arbitrary choices
in all other Li yields two distinct global labels. Therefore the original instance has at least two
possible labels if and only if some Li has size at least two.
For Q- and P-nodes, the only local cycle through both distinguished edges is ℓi ∪ri, so
the local label set is a singleton.
For an S-node, the underlying local cycle through both
distinguished edges is the skeleton cycle itself, so its possible labels are obtained by summing
the summaries along that cycle. Thus only the case that µi is an R-node requires a separate
argument. In that case, Hi is triconnected, possibly with parallel edges, and the local problem
is exactly the following:

Input: A triconnected Γ-labeled graph (G = (V, E), ψ) and two distinct edges e1, e2 ∈E.
Goal: Determine whether there exist two cycles C1 and C2 in G that pass through both
e1 and e2 with ψ(C1) ̸= ψ(C2), and if so, find such cycles.

4.1
Further Reduction
We first consider an easy case, where e1 and e2 share an end vertex.
Lemma 4.1. If e1 and e2 share an end vertex, then the problem can be solved in O(|V | + |E|)
time.
Proof. Let e1 = x1y and e2 = x2y. If x1 = x2, then the two edges are parallel and form a
unique cycle passing through both edges, so the problem is trivial. Suppose that x1 ̸= x2, and
set G0 := G −{y}. Then, a cycle C passing through both e1 and e2 in G corresponds to an
x1–x2 path P in G0, where ψ(C) = ψ(P) + ψ(e1) + ψ(e2). Thus, by applying Lemma 2.5 with
G = G0, s = x1, and t = x2, the problem is solved in O(|V | + |E|) time.
In what follows, we assume that e1 and e2 are disjoint.
Specifically, let e1 = x1y1 and
e2 = x2y2, where x1, y1, x2, y2 are distinct. Let G′ be the graph obtained from G by removing
the edges x1y1 and x2y2 (if there are parallel edges, remove all of them). Then, a solution to
the decision problem (deciding whether there exist two cycles passing through both e1 and e2
with distinct labels) is as follows (cf. Theorem 1.3):
The answer is yes if and only if G′ has a non-zero cycle of length at least three.
(⋆)
We show this with an O(|V | + |E|)-time algorithm for construction in the yes case, which

This case reduces to one of Cases 1–5. Take an x1–V (C) 3-fan in G by Lemma 2.2. If both e1 and
e2 are used in the 3-fan, two paths among them form a V (C)–V (C) path in G passing through
both e1 and e2 whose inner vertices are disjoint from V (C). Then, Lemma 4.4 completes the
proof. Otherwise, an x1–V (C) 2-fan in G′ is obtained from the 3-fan. This forms a V (C)–V (C)
path Q intersecting x1 whose inner vertices are disjoint from V (C). Then, by splitting C with
Q, one can obtain a non-zero cycle intersecting x1, reducing this case to one of Cases 1–5; see
Figure 9.
