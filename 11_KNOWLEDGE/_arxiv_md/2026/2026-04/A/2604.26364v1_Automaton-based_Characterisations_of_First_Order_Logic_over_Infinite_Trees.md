Abstract. We study the expressive power of First-Order Logic (FO) over (unordered)
infinite trees, with the aim of identifying robust characterisations in terms of branching-time
specification formalisms. While such correspondences are well understood in the linear-time
setting, the branching-time case presents well-known structural challenges. To this end, we
introduce two classes of hesitant tree automata and show that they capture precisely the
expressive power of two branching-time temporal logics, namely PastCTL± and CTL∗
f,
both of which have been previously shown to be equivalent to FO over infinite trees. These
results provide uniform automata-theoretic characterisations and yield a natural normal
form for the latter in terms of a new fragment of CTL∗called CTL∗
±. As a consequence, we
identify a fundamental limitation of FO in this setting: along each branch, it can express
only properties that are either safety or co-safety, thereby revealing a sharp expressive
boundary for first-order definability over infinite trees.
1. Introduction
Characterisation theorems [Flu85] are powerful model-theoretic tools that offer a principled
approach to understanding the intrinsic features of formal systems. They allow us to mark
the expressive boundaries of specification languages, compare these formalisms w.r.t. their
descriptive power on specific classes of models, and design new languages starting from
a given set of requirements, in the spirit of Lindstr¨om-style theorems [Lin69] (e.g., based
on maximality principles). They also play a central role in definability theory, guiding
the identification of expressive fragments and meaningful extensions of known logics, thus
supporting the selection of suitable languages for the specification of the correct behaviour
of systems in verification and synthesis tasks.
A foundational distinction exists between linear-time and branching-time languages [MP92,
MP95]. The former capture properties of computations viewed as totally-ordered sets of
events, while the latter account for the branching structure inherent in concurrent and
nondeterministic system behaviours.
Key words and phrases: Tree languages, first order logic, tree automata.

The linear-time case, where models are isomorphic to (finite or infinite) words, is by now
well understood. A rich and intertwined network of equivalences connects predicate logics
over linear orders with temporal logics, such as LTL [Pnu77, Pnu81] and ELTL [Wol83],
with star-free [Sch65, PP86] and ω-regular [B¨uc62] languages, and with automata-theoretic
models, including finite [Ner58, RS59] and B¨uchi [Elg61, B¨uc62, B¨uc66] automata. Within
this landscape, the safety and co-safety fragments of LTL [CMP92] occupy a central position:
they capture the two most elementary types of reactive specification, respectively enforcing
that something bad never happens and that something good eventually happens. All these
connections provide deep insights into the structure of definable properties and lead to
optimal decision procedures across different representations.
By contrast, the branching-time setting remains more fragmented. Even for First-Order
Logic (FO) interpreted over (finite or infinite) trees many fundamental definability questions
remain unsettled. A longstanding open problem posed by Thomas in the 1980s [Tho84] asks
whether it is decidable if a given regular tree language is definable in FO. This question has
been studied under various combinations of tree types (ranked/unranked, ordered/unordered)
and interpreted vocabularies (e.g., including only child, only ancestor, or both relations).
Aside from the positive result for FO over finite trees with the child relation [BS09], the
problem remains open in all other settings. Efforts to resolve this question have mainly
followed algebraic approaches [Boj21], inspired by their success in the word case (most notably
the Sch¨utzenberger theorem on star-free languages [Sch65]). These approaches rely on the
compositionality and structural insights provided by syntactic algebras. Despite significant
progress, they have provided only partial results, mostly for classes of finite trees [Pot95,
Boj04, EW10, BSW12] or topologically simple infinite trees [BI09, BIS13]. An alternative and
often complementary line of work seeks direct characterisations of FO-definable tree languages
via automata. This route, highly successful in the linear-time case, has also led to fruitful
results in the branching-time setting, including a correspondence [JW96] between Monadic
Second-Order Logic (MSO) [Rab69], Parity Tree Automata [Mos84, EJ91], and the Modal
µ-Calculus [Koz83]. More recently [BBMP24], the landscape has expanded to include the
expressive equivalence of Monadic Chain/Path Logics (MCL/MPL) [Tho84, Tho87, HT87],
their temporal Computation Tree Logic counterparts (ECTL∗/CTL∗) [VW84, EH83, EH85],
and variants of Hesitant Tree Automata (HTA) [KVW00]. Within this picture, the boundary
between MSO and FO has also been probed through a weaker form of MCL. Carreiro and
Venema [CV14] provided a syntactic and automaton-based characterisation of Propositional
Dynamic Logic (PDL) [FL79], a logic that sits strictly below ECTL∗in expressive power,
as a fragment of the modal µ-Calculus. Subsequently, Carreiro [Car15] showed that PDL
is precisely the bisimulation-invariant fragment of Weak Monadic Chain Logic (WMCL), a
variant of MSO that quantifies over finite chains, and introduced a class of parity automata
capturing the expressive power of WCL over trees.
In this work, we continue along this line of development, by providing the first complete
automaton-based characterisation, to the best of our knowledge, of first-order logic with
the descendant relation over unranked unordered infinite trees. Throughout this work, all
temporal logics are implicitly considered in their counting extensions [Fin72], which allow
formulas to express properties of the number of successors satisfying a given condition. Our
approach builds on previous results for two branching-time temporal logics, namely a fragment
of Computation Tree Logic with past, denoted PastCTL±, and the Full Computation Tree
Logic with finite path quantification, denoted CTL∗
f. In [Sch92, BBMP23] these logics
were shown to be expressively equivalent to FO when interpreted on unranked unordered

of CTL with past [Sch92]; for this reason, we provide a careful comparison of these two.

2. preliminaries
Words and trees. A finite word over an alphabet Σ is a finite sequence w = a1 . . . an of
letters from Σ. We write w(i) to denote the letter from Σ in the i-th position of w. We
denote by Σ∗the set of all finite words over Σ, which includes the empty word ε. A word
language is any subset of Σ∗. Similarly, we define an ω-word over Σ as an infinite sequence
w = a1a2 . . . of letters from Σ; we denote by Σω the set of all ω-words over Σ, and call
ω-language any subset of Σω.
We shall also work with trees, and precisely with unranked, unordered, leafless trees.
By unranked we mean that every node can have any finite number of immediate successors;
by unordered we mean that trees cannot be distinguished by just reordering the successors
of a node; by leafless we mean that every node has at least one successor. We adopt the
usual terminology for trees: ancestor, descendant, parent, sibling, root, etc. We denote by
Dom(t) the set of nodes of a tree t. Given an alphabet Σ, a Σ-labelled tree (Σ-tree for short)
is a tree t equipped with a labelling λt : Dom(t) →Σ, which associates a letter from Σ with
each node of t. By a slight abuse of notation, we also denote the resulting Σ-tree by t, and
given v ∈Dom(t), we write t(v) for the label assigned to v by λt. A tree language over Σ is
a set of Σ-trees. We denote by TΣ the language of all Σ-trees.
A path of a tree (or a Σ-tree) t is a finite or infinite sequence π = v0v1 . . . of nodes
totally ordered by the successor relation (i.e., each vi is a successor of vi−1 for all i > 0), but
not necessarily starting from the root. If the path starts from the root it is called initial. We
denote by π(i) the i-th node vi along the path π = v0v1 . . . . A maximal path is a path that
cannot be prolonged to the right; in particular, because we restrict ourselves to leafless trees,
a maximal path is necessarily infinite. We denote by tu the subtree of t rooted at node u,
namely, the tree t restricted to the set of all descendants of u, including u itself. Of course,
if t is a Σ-tree, then the labelling of the nodes of tu is inherited from t, i.e., tu(v) = t(v) for
all v ∈Dom(tu).

variables. Formulae of FO are generated by the following grammar, where p ranges over

temporal logics are often used (see, for instance, [MR03]).

this convention here, and rather assume that all temporal logics are graded.

For simplicity, we shall use |= for either |=s or |=p, depending on which type of structure and
formula we are considering. Two state (resp. path) formulae ψ1, ψ2 are full state equivalent if,
for every pointed tree (t, u), we have (t, u) |=s ψ1 iff (t, u) |=s ψ2 (resp. for every path-pointed
tree (t, π, i), we have (t, π, i) |=p ψ1 iff (t, π, i) |=p ψ2). By a slight abuse of notation, we
sometimes write t |= φ to mean (t, εt) |= φ (recall that εt is the root of t), and in this case
we also say that t is a model of φ. We denote by L (φ) the set of all models t of φ, and
accordingly say that two state formulae φ and φ′ are initially equivalent if L (φ) = L (φ′).
Note that in this case we are using a notion of equivalence, called initial equivalence, that
evaluates state formulae at the root of an arbitrary tree. In general, notably in the presence
of past modal operators, this equivalence is coarser than full state equivalence. On the one
hand, we will often use full state equivalence for comparing formulae and for performing
syntactic translations from one formalism to another – this is because full state equivalence is
naturally compatible with substitutions of subformulae. On the other hand, when comparing
the expressive power of logical formalisms, we will mostly use initial equivalence between state
formulae. For example, we say that one formalism is as expressive as another formalism if
there are effective translations of each formula from each formalism to an initially equivalent
formula of the other formalism. Similarly, we say that one formalism is less expressive than
another formalism if there is an effective translation from each formula of the former to an
initially equivalent formula of the latter, but not the other way around.

• CTL is the intersection of the previous two fragments, forbidding past operators and

Word automata. A nondeterministic B¨uchi word automaton (NBA) is a tuple A =
(Q, Σ, ∆, qI, F), where Q is a finite set of states, Σ is a finite alphabet, ∆⊆Q × Σ × Q is a
transition relation, qI ∈Q is an initial state, and F ⊆Q is a set of final states. An NBA is
deterministic if ∆can be seen as a partial function from Q × Σ to Q.
A path of A on a finite or infinite word w = σ1σ2 . . . is a sequence of states ρ = q0q1 . . .
of length |w| + 1 (if w is infinite, then the path is also infinite) such that (qi−1, σi, qi) ∈∆
for all positions i in w. Such a path ρ is called a run of A on w if it starts with the initial
state qI of the automaton. We denote by inf(ρ) the set of states visited infinitely often in
an infinite run ρ, and say that ρ is successful if there is a q ∈F such that q ∈inf(ρ). An
infinite word w is accepted by A if there is a successful run of A on w. The language L (A)
recognised by A is the set of words accepted by A. Two automata are equivalent if they
recognise the same language.
Variants of NBAs are obtained by modifying the acceptance condition. One way is to
simply turn existential nondeterminism into universal nondeterminism, that is, to declare
that the automaton accepts an infinite word w whenever every run on w is successful; we call
these models universal automata (e.g., we have universal B¨uchi word automata, abbreviated
UBA). Another way is to replace a B¨uchi acceptance condition with a coB¨uchi one: formally,
a run ρ of a nondeterministic coB¨uchi automaton (NCA for short) is successful if there is no
q ∈F such that q ∈inf(ρ); accordingly, the states of F are called rejecting states.
An automaton A is counter-free if for all states q, all finite words w, and all natural
numbers n, if A admits a path on wn that starts and ends at q, then it also admits a path
on w that starts and ends at q.
Proposition 2.7. [DG08, Theorem 1.1] The languages recognisable by nondeterministic,
counter-free B¨uchi automata are exactly those definable in FO.
3. Temporal logics equivalent to FO
In this section, we introduce and discuss two tree temporal logics that are known to be
equivalent to FO over infinite trees. Although the equivalence results are not new, we here
include a rather detailed section dedicated to these two logics, with the aim of recasting
them in a syntax closer to the community standard and of presenting brand new results
about them (including a new normal form) to shed light on their behaviour. Doing this has
already helped us better understand the behaviour of FO over infinite trees and it might
also be useful to others working on the same topic.
3.1. Polarised PastCTL
PastCTL
PastCTL. The first formalism we present is called Polarised PastCTL,
denoted PastCTL±. The logic was already introduced in [Sch92], under a different notation,
namely {U, S, Xk}, and was proved to be as expressive as FO over infinite trees under initial

4.1. Graded Tree Automata. Let X be a set. We denote by B+(X) the set of positive

the current node of the input tree, sends a set of target states to the possible children of
the current node, so as to satisfy the positive Boolean formula δ(q, a), where each atom
(♢k, q′) (resp., (□k, q′)) of the formula is considered as satisfied if state q′ is sent to at least k
children (resp., if q′ is sent to all but k −1 children). In particular, the atom (♢1, q′) (often
abbreviated (♢, q′)) requires q′ to be sent to at least one child, and can be used, for instance,
to check a formula like EXϕ; similarly, (□1, q′) (often abbreviated (□, q′)) requires q′ to be
sent to all children, and can be used to check a formula like AXϕ. Of course, the atoms
(♢k, q′) and (□k, q′) for arbitrary k are often used to simulate the counting modalities. Given

• existential, when, for all q ∈Qi and σ ∈Σ, all Qi-atoms in δ(q, σ) are of the form (♢, q′),
and there is at most one Qi-atom in each clause of the disjunctive normal form of δ(q, σ);
• universal, when, for all q ∈Qi and σ ∈Σ, all Qi-atoms in δ(q, σ) are of the form (□, q′),
and there is at most one Qi-atom in each clause of the conjunctive normal form of δ(q, σ).
Note that in the above definition, all counting modalities ♢k (resp. □k) associated with
atoms that stay in the same existential (resp. universal) component have cardinality k = 1.
This intuitively means that a HTA can traverse at most one node-to-child relationship
while remaining in the same existential component. Moreover, by the above restrictions,
every infinite path of a run of a HTA eventually gets stuck in an existential or universal
component. All together, these properties let us capture the semantics of existential and
universal path quantification of tree temporal logics by allowing the HTA to guess and check
a single path in the input tree while remaining in the same component.
4.3. Polarised Hesitant Tree Automata. A Polarised Hesitant Tree Automaton (HTA±)
is an HTA where every state q of an existential component is such that q /∈F and, dually,
every state q of a universal component is such that q ∈F (for states of transient components,
it does not matter whether they belong or not to F, as they can occur at most once along
an infinite path of a run). The polarised condition, originally introduced in the conference
version of this work [BDMM+25], is a stronger restriction than the classical weak condition
[MSS86]. The latter only requires each component of an automaton to contain exclusively
accepting states or exclusively rejecting states, without imposing any constraint on the type
of component or on the structural properties of its transitions. As the name suggests, the
polarised restriction simulates the polarisation phenomenon observed in CTL∗
±.
5. Automaton-based characterisation of PastCTL±
In this section, we first introduce a further restriction of HTA±, and then an enhancement
of their head movement capabilities. We finally show that the resulting class of automata,
which we call two-way linear HTA±, is equivalent to PastCTL±.
The restriction is simple: we require components of the hesitant partition of a HTA±
to be singletons. We call such an HTA± linear.
The head movement enhancement is instead less trivial. We allow for two-way head
movements along the input tree, meaning that the automaton can decide to visit the parent
of the currently read node, in addition to its children. This requires an adaptation of the
transition function δ. Formally, a two-way HTA± is a tuple ⟨Q, Σ, δ, qI, F⟩, where Q, Σ,
qI, and F are, as in the definition of GTA, a finite set of states, a finite alphabet, the

• upward, when, for all σ ∈Σ, q can appear in δ(q, σ) only in the form (⇑, q).

be duals of each other, meaning that complementing an automaton in one class gives an

8. Discussion
In this paper, we investigated the expressive power of FO over infinite trees. We first
considered two branching-time temporal logics, already known to be equivalent to FO, and
studied their behaviour and structural properties. We highlighted a common polarisation


## Page 35

property, relating it to recent work on Weak Chain Logic. We also provided two automaton-
based characterisations of FO over infinite trees: the first is based on a two-way automaton
whose state space can be partitioned into singletons, while the second relies on a one-way
automaton whose state space can be partitioned into components of arbitrary size, subject,
however, to strong expressiveness restrictions when viewed as word automata.
We conclude this work by discussing some consequences of our results and a number of
related open questions.
Finite trees. It is natural to investigate the implications of our results in the setting of
finite trees. To date, no automaton-based characterisation of FO over finite trees is known.
To be precise, in his Ph.D. thesis [Boj04, Theorem 2.5.7], Boja´nczyk showed that FO is
equivalent to the cascade product of aperiodic wordsum automata over finite binary trees.
This characterisation implies that every deterministic automaton recognising an FO-definable
language must admit such a decomposition. However, no general decomposition theorem for
deterministic tree automata is known, in contrast with the Krohn-Rhodes theorem for word
automata [KR65]. As a consequence, given a deterministic tree automaton, there is currently
no effective way to determine whether it admits such a decomposition and, hence, whether
it defines an FO language. Let us recall that, over finite trees, deterministic bottom-up tree
automata (i.e., automata scanning the tree from the leaves to the root) recognise all regular
tree languages, unlike the infinite-tree setting, where no class of deterministic automata
captures all regular languages. Our characterisations rely, indeed, on alternating automata
over unranked trees, suggesting that variants of our constructions could be considered over
finite trees with arbitrary branching. However, several obstacles immediately arise. Most
notably, the acceptance condition becomes problematic: in the alternating setting over finite
trees, one may define acceptance in terms of runs whose branches end in a trivial condition
⊤, making the polarised acceptance condition vacuous. For this and related reasons, it is
unclear how to obtain an alternating automata characterisation of FO over finite trees,
even though it seems reasonable to believe that such a characterisation would be just an
adaptation of the ones presented here. Nevertheless, the central challenge in the finite-tree
setting is to obtain a characterisation in terms of deterministic automata. Such a result
would have major consequences, as it would resolve the long-standing problem of deciding
whether a regular tree language definable in MSO is already definable in FO.
Simulation of the two classes of automata. In Corollary 7.8, we established the
equivalence between counter-free distinct HTA± and two-way linear HTA± via a logical
detour. A natural question is whether a direct translation between these two classes exists.
This appears to be highly non-trivial: the structural restrictions imposed on the two models
are fundamentally different, and there is no obvious way to transform, for instance, a
two-way automaton with singleton components into a one-way automaton whose components
correspond to counter-free word automata. A direct construction, although likely to be
technically involved, could provide further insight into the structural constraints underlying
FO over infinite trees.
The expressive power of PastCTL. We have already mentioned that the authors of
[MR03] proved CTL∗equivalent to MPL. In contrast, this work focuses on Weak MPL,
namely FO, which was shown by Schlingloff to be equivalent to PastCTL±. We proved
that PastCTL± is strictly less expressive than PastCTL (Theorem 3.2). Since PastCTL
is itself strictly less expressive than CTL∗(and hence MPL), it follows that PastCTL lies

corresponding to PastCTL thus emerges as a natural and interesting problem, as it would
