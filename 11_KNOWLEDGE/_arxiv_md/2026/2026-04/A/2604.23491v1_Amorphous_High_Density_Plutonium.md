Abstract
[Abstract] Metastable aluminum-alloyed δ-plutonium shrinks rapidly and pure α-
plutonium swells rapidly at 4 K. At ambient temperature alloyed δ-plutonium
slowly swells, but its bulk density decreases more slowly than would be inferred
from the increase in its lattice parameter. These results might be explained as the
result of ingrowth of the opposite phases, but they have not been found in X-ray
diffraction. The cryogenic results may be explained by ingrowth of an amorphous
phase with density intermediate between those of α and δ. This phase rapidly but
not instantaneously anneals to its parent phases at temperatures ⪆100 K.
Keywords: plutonium, cryogenic, amorphous phase
1. Introduction
The equilibrium phase of plutonium at room temperature is the dense (ρ =
19.86 g/cm3) monoclinic α phase. The thermodynamically unstable low density

it is stabilized kinetically (and partially thermodynamically) by alloying with a
few atomic percent of aluminum or gallium.
All isotopes of plutonium are radioactive, the most important ones by α-decay.
Widely studied isotopic mixtures, typically 94% 239Pu, introduce helium at a rate
of about 40 ppm (atomic) per year. The ingrowth of helium, and of 241Am from
β decay of traces of 241Pu, are predicted to produce very slow swelling, initially
at a volumetric rate of about 5 × 10−5/y [1]. The α particle and recoiling uranium
nuclei resulting from these decays damage the plutonium lattice at a rate of about
0.1 displacement per atom (dpa) per year in 239Pu, and at higher rates in material
doped with highly radioactive (t1/2 = 87.8 y) 238Pu. This damage disorders the
lattice and changes its properties in ways that are not fully understood and that are
sensitive to temperature.
The low-temperature behavior of plutonium undergoing radioactive decay and
self-irradiation has long been known to be unusual. At temperatures > 15 K [2],
but not at 4 K [3], the damaged material anneals at temperature-dependent rates.
Annealing is also inferred from the presence of transients in δ-Pu at ambient tem-
perature [4, 5], the reversibility of its rapid shrinkage at 4 K upon annealing at 97
K and the reversibility of the rapid swelling of α-Pu at 4 K upon annealing at 92
K [6].
In this letter we follow [7] in suggesting that radiation damage produces a dis-
tinct, metastable, non-equilibrium phase, with density intermediate between that
of the α and δ phases, disordered enough that it does not show X-ray diffrac-
tion peaks. At ambient temperature in δ-Pu it relaxes to the crystalline δ-phase
almost as fast as it is formed, manifesting itself as a small discrepancy between

K, where kinetic barriers preclude relaxation, over times of thousands of hours

2. Cryogenic Behavior
Most measurements of density changes as plutonium self-irradiates have been
made at room temperature, but studies at 4 K [6, 7, 8, 9] were reviewed by [10]1.
At such low temperature relaxation and annealing are expected to be negligible
(a report [9] that the behavior of α-Pu 1.5 K differs from that at 4 K was later
disputed [6]).
At 4 K δ-Pu initially shrinks rapidly; in 0.5 year a sample shrank by about
1.4% in length at a gradually decreasing rate that would extrapolate to an asymp-
totic volumetric shrinkage of about 15% after a few years [6, 8]. This is nearly
2000 times as fast as it swells at ambient temperature [4]. Most of these data
describe length shrinkage; there are a few crystallographic data [6] at very early
time (< 0.01 dpa) that appear approximately consistent with the rate of length
contraction.
Less attention has been paid to α-Pu, but at 4K [6, 7, 10] reported an initial
linear growth rate of about 3% per year, decaying with an e-folding time of about
a year. This is comparable in magnitude, but opposite in sign, to the shrinkage
rate found for δ-Pu [6]. It extrapolates to almost the same asymptotic density as
does δ-plutonium [6, 7, 10]. Both shrinkage and expansion were almost entirely

reversed upon annealing at about 100 K [6, 9], consistent with the formation of a
metastable state of density intermediate between those of the δ and α phases.
The rates of density change of both phases of Pu gradually decrease, suggest-
ing convergence to a phase of intermediate density in a time of about a year. This
is explained if the material, whether initially δ or α, is largely converted to the
intermediate phase, disordered enough not to show X-ray diffraction peaks, after
that time, and that further radiation damage does not change it.
After the roughly half year of irradiation at 4K in the experiments reported
in [6, 8] much of the δ or α remained, and annealing returned the material to its
original phase. The remaining δ or α may have nucleated regrowth of the original
phase. The experiments did not extend long enough for conversion of all the
material to the intermediate phase. It is unclear to which phase annealing would
return after complete conversion, if that occurs.
Measurements of the swelling of α-U doped with 238Pu to achieve a level of α
activity about 3/2 of that of 239Pu were also reported [6], yet it expanded only about
40% as fast as did 239Pu.This implies that less than half the expansion of α-Pu may
be attributed directly to radiation damage (which occurs for both elements); the
remainder is specific to Pu, which we attribute to conversion of the dense α-Pu
phase to a phase of intermediate density.
These results might be explained if radiation damage at 4 K converted δ-Pu
to α-Pu and α-Pu to δ-Pu, with saturation at a few tenths of a dpa. Complete
conversion of δ to α would increase the density by 25%, consistent with the ex-
trapolation of the early-time cryogenic density increase to longer times. However,
conversion of α-Pu to δ-Pu by radiation damage is implausible; α-Pu is stable at
low temperatures, while δ-Pu is only kinetically stabilized. Conversion of δ-Pu to

## Page 6


α-Pu is thermodynamically possible, if radiation damage overcomes the kinetic
barrier, but this is inconsistent with reversal of the contraction upon annealing—
α-Pu cannot anneal to δ-Pu below the γ-δ transition temperature around 600 K.
Radiation damage might prefer one phase to the other, but it is implausible
that it would always prefer the “other” phase; having converted one to the other,
this would require that radiation damage would then begin to reverse the process.
There have been no reports of α structure in the X-ray diffraction of contracted
δ-Pu, or vice versa, so we reject this hypothesis.
These experimental results should be compared to theoretical predictions of
swelling as a result of the ingrowth of helium and (at ages of ⪅20 years) of
decay (t1/2 = 14.4 y) of 241Pu to larger 241Am atoms [1]. This theory predicts a
volume growth rate (at zero age) of about 0.005% per year, about three orders of
magnitude less, and of the opposite sign, than what was observed for δ-plutonium
at 4 K [6] where no annealing is expected.
Ab initio calculations [11] of void swelling predict that it is small in δ-Pu but
large in α-Pu. This is consistent with ambient temperature measurements of δ-
Pu, but no ambient temperature data are available for α-Pu. It does not explain
the shrinkage of δ-Pu at 4 K (which is why we hypothesize a denser disordered
phase); it may be qualitatively consistent with the rapid swelling of α-Pu at 4K
([11] do not quantify this) but may not predict the reversal of this swelling around
100 K.
3. Amorphous Phase?
We follow [7] in suggesting that another phase, sufficiently disordered not to
show X-ray diffraction peaks, with density intermediate between those of α and δ,
5


## Page 7


is formed by radiation damage under cryogenic conditions. It would not show the
crystallographic signature of either the α or the δ phase, but partial transformation
to it would shrink δ and expand α, as observed.
Molecular dynamic calculations of radiation damage cascades in unalloyed
δ-Pu indicate the formation of such a glassy phase as radiation damage relaxes
[12]. At 300 K this amorphous phase persisted for the entire 2 ns duration of the
calculation; it is impossible to say if it would persist on laboratory time scales,
either at 300 K or at 4 K. These authors did not report its density, but if it were
intermediate between those of the α and δ phases it could explain the observed
contraction of δ-Pu at 4 K and the discrepancy between lattice parameter and
dilatometric densities discussed in Sec. 4. At 180 K these calculations indicated
complete transformation of the computational domain to the glassy phase. This
has not occurred in any experiment, perhaps because experiments on the δ phase
at room or cryogenic temperatures necessarily involve alloy-stabilized plutonium
rather than the pure element studied in [12].
Such an amorphous phase would be metastable, and expected to anneal to the
equilibrium α phase at temperatures comparable to those at which later EXAFS
studies reported annealing of radiation damage in δ-Pu at temperatures 15–150 K
[2]. Annealing at temperatures in the range 90–100 K was reported [6] to reverse
the density changes both α- and δ-Pu underwent at 4 K, supporting this hypothesis.
Annealing of expanded α-Pu to the density of the undamaged α phase is easy
to understand—α is the equilibrium phase. The annealing of contracted δ-Pu back
to its undamaged density is more difficult to understand. It cannot be explained
if contraction is the result of conversion to the equilibrium α phase, because α
cannot spontaneously convert to any other phase at temperatures below ∼400

K. The annealing of δ-Pu irradiated at 4 K back to δ can only be explained if
radiation damage to δ-Pu at 4 K produces another metastable phase that is even
more thermodynamically disfavored than is δ-Pu.
That leaves the question of why the hypothesized amorphous phase formed
from radiation damaged δ anneals to δ, rather than to the thermodynamically pre-
ferred α. A possible explanation is that recrystallization nucleates on the remain-
ing δ in contact with the amorphous phase, and as a result assumes its crystallo-
graphic symmetry. Conversion of δ to α is kinetically inhibited at these tempera-
tures, although it does occur at somewhat higher temperatures [13]. This question
could be addressed by long-term X-ray diffraction studies of cryogenic δ-Pu (and
particularly accelerated-aged δ-Pu, spiked with 238Pu to increase the rate of ra-
diation damage [14]), when nearly complete conversion to the amorphous phase
might be expected.
4. Transients in Room Temperature Expansion
At room temperature δ-Pu very slowly swells, following somewhat faster ini-
tial transients [4, 5]. The fact that the rate of swelling decreases implies that some
of the radiation damage is reversed by annealing with a relaxation time O(1 y)
(0.1 dpa). An irreversible part produces a long term trend approximately consis-
tent with theoretical predictions for the ingrowth of helium and americium [1].
X-ray diffraction indicates the δ-Pu lattice expands about three times more
than the change in dimensions measured by dilatometry [4, 5, 15, 16]. This discor-
dance can be explained if some of the low density crystalline δ phase transforms to
a higher density phase. Were this higher density phase crystalline α-Pu, it would
show its distinctive signature in X-ray diffraction, but that has not been reported.

This is explained if the higher density material is sufficiently disordered. Alter-
natively, [17] suggested that the transient increase of lattice parameter at ambient
temperature results from radiation-induced diffusion of Ga to Pu3Ga (ζ′ phase),
with increasing lattice parameter of the δ-Pu phase as it is gradually depleted of
Ga; this saturates at about 0.1 dpa because of radiation-induced disordering. The
lesser growth of dimension is then explained by ingrowth of Pu3Ga.
The transient relaxes to slower growth at about 10−3 lattice expansion in nor-
mally aging material [5], but at about 2 × 10−4 linear dilation in accelerated-aged
material [4], in both cases at about 0.1 dpa (unfortunately, these experiments dif-
fered in two variables—the quantities measured and the rate of radiation damage,
clouding direct comparisons). Again, the greater expansion of lattice parameter
is partly compensated by collapse of some portion of the (expanded) δ phase to
denser material, that we suggest was amorphous. After the initial relaxation time,
further production of denser material is balanced by annealing of existing denser
material to δ-Pu, and the subsequent slow swelling results from helium and ameri-
cium ingrowth. The fact that the transients relax at similar damage, measured
in dpa, rather than at similar physical ages (the accelerated-aging material accu-
mulates damage 15–20 times faster, depending on the unstated 241Am content)
indicates that either the relaxation processes or the nature of damage depend on
the damage rate, calling into question the equivalence of accelerated and natural
aging.

6. Discussion
The recovery of the δ-Pu aged at 4 K to its unaged lower density upon anneal-
ing implies that the denser component induced by radiation damage cannot be the
α phase because thermodynamics precludes spontantaneous transformation of α
to δ under these conditions. Recovery can only be explained if the higher density
phase is a nonequilibrium phase, even further from equilibrium than δ-Pu. Such
a phase may store energy sufficient to drive it back to the δ phase once warmed
enough to overcome kinetic barriers. We suggest that contact with surrounding δ-
Pu nucleates the transformation back to δ-Pu, despite the stronger thermodynamic
drive to the equilibrium α phase.
Many of the experimental results discussed here are quite old (e.g. [6, 8, 9]),
and it would be desirable to repeat these cryogenic experiments, extending them
to times of several years but with particular attention paid to the first months and
year. X-ray diffraction data should be obtained along with dilatometry to test the
inference that dimensional changes are not the result of transformation of δ-Pu to
α-Pu, and vice versa, but rather imply the existence of a disordered phase.
The behavior of normally and accelerated aging material should also be di-
rectly compared, in order to test the assumption that accelerating aging with 238Pu
faithfully accelerates the clock without changing aging in any other way. The
rapid shrinkage of δ-Pu and swelling of α-Pu at 4 K enable accurate measure-
ment and comparison of their rates in both unaccelerated and accelerated aging
material, and quantitative tests of this assumption. At the high rate of radiation
damage of pure 238Pu several different crystal phases co-exist [22], implying that
such extreme rates of radiation damage do more than accelerate the aging clock.
The formation of a disordered phase in plutonium as a result of autoirradiation
10


## Page 12


may be analogous to the formation of amorphous phases of silica as a result of
applied pressure [23, 24]. In each case an external source of free energy drives an
equilibrium crystalline phase to a non-equlibrium disordered phase.
The interpretation of the density changes of α- and δ-plutonium at 4 K as the
formation of an amorphous phases may be tested empirically. Metallic glasses
show [25] low temperature specific heat excesses analogous to those of network
and molecular glasses, but measuring this in plutonium may be difficult. Amor-
phous metals have electrical resistivities, straightforward to measure, greater than
those of crystalline phases [26]. Resonant ultrasound spectroscopy can provide
accurate measurements of elastic constants [27] to test the hypothesis of ingrowth
of an amorphous phase with self-irradiation.
