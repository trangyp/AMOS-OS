Abstract
In our previous work: Adv. Math. 455 (2024), no. 109880, solubility
of solutions was introduced as an extension of solubility of skew braces
in the classification context of non-degenerate solutions of the Yang-
Baxter equation. One of our main results (Theorem C) proved that a
skew brace is soluble if, and only if, its associated solution is soluble. A
minor step depending on the definition of homomorphism of solutions
was overlooked. In this work, proof of Theorem C is repaired by means
of a new class of homomorphisms of solutions: i-homomorphisms of
solutions. The importance of this new class is twofold: indecomposable
solutions are characterised by means of i-simplicity of solutions, and i-
kernels of i-homomorphisms generate ideals in structure skew braces of
solutions. Hence, solubility of solutions is redefined as an opposite class
of indecomposable solutions. The results obtained with this definition
improve our previous outcomes: every soluble solution is proved to
have a soluble structure skew brace, and consequently, Theorem C still
holds. Several results stemming from this new analysis are outlined.

posable solutions, simple solutions.

Introduction
In this article, every (set-theoretic) solution (X, r) of the Yang-Baxter
equation (YBE) is denoted as r(x, y) = (λx(y), ρy(x)) for every x, y ∈X, and
is considered non-degenerate, i.e. λx and ρx are bijective for every x ∈X.
There is a wide consensus that the classification problem of solutions goes
hand in hand with the development of a structural theory of skew braces. It
is well-known that every skew brace provides a solution, and every solution
(X, r) has associated two fundamental skew braces that encode its behaviour:
the structure skew brace G(X, r) and the permutation skew brace G(X, r)
of (X, r) —(X, r) is said to be injective if X is embedded in G(X, r) through
an injective map ι: X →G(X, r).
One of the main open problems is the classification of indecomposable
solutions, i.e. solutions which can not be written as a union of two proper
subsolutions. This is an outstanding question which is a long way off to be
solved (see [3, 6, 7, 13]). A possible approach consists of analysing solutions
that do not admit any non-trivial epimorphic image —a homomorphism of
solutions (X, r) and (Y, s) is a map f : X →Y which makes compatible
the λ and ρ components of r and s. These are called simple solutions, and
structural analysis of associated structure and permutation skew braces have
provided significant insight into the classification and construction of such so-
lutions (see [5, 8]). In particular, the study of simple solutions naturally leads
to consider simple skew braces, i.e. those ones that do not admit non-trivial
quotients. One can check that injective solutions (X, r) with simple structure
skew braces G(X, r) are simple (see Proposition 2.5), and simple solutions
(X, r) with |X| ̸= 2 are indecomposable. Nevertheless, the converses of these
implications are not true in general: see Example 2.6 for the former, and for
the latter, every indecomposable solution (X, r) with finite free-square car-
dinality is not simple (see [6]). Hence, new tools are required to characterise
indecomposability.
Following [1], in order to classify minimal simple skew braces, solubility
of skew braces was defined as an opposite class of simplicity, i.e. skew braces
with a rich ideal structure. Similarly, we proposed a definition of solubility
of solutions as an antithesis of simple solutions: (X, r) is soluble at xt ∈X
if there exist Xt ⊆. . . ⊆X1 ⊆X0 = X with Xt = {xt} such that, for every
1 ≤i ≤t, there exists a homomorphism fi : (X, r) →(Yi, si) satisfying
• Xi ∈X/ ker fi, here ker fi is the equivalence relation given by: x(ker fi)y
if, and only if, fi(x) = fi(y);
• fi(Xi−1, r|Xi−1×Xi−1) = (Zi, si) is a twist subsolution of (Yi, si), i.e.
si(u, v) = (v, u) for every u, v ∈Zi.

Our main result in this sense was to prove that solubility of skew braces
characterises the soluble character of a solution associated with a skew brace
(see [1, Theorem C]).
The motivation of this work comes from [9], where the authors “have spot-
ted an irreparable mistake in the proof of Theorem C” in [1]. We observed
that a minor step depending on the definition of homomorphisms of solu-
tions was overlooked. Thus, the main goal of this addendum/corrigendum is
to repair the mistake in the proof of Theorem C by revisiting our previous def-
inition of soluble solutions. To this end, we introduce and study what we call
i-homomorphisms of solutions. They turn out to be essential to understand
indecomposable solutions since i-simplicity is equivalent to indecomposabil-
ity (Proposition 3.3), and i-kernels associated with i-homomorphisms induce
ideals in the structure skew brace of a solution (see Theorem 3.9). Then, solu-
bility of solutions naturally emerges as a class of multidecomposable solutions
opposed to indecomposable/i-simple ones (see Definition 4.1 in Section 4).
Furthermore, with this revision we improve our previous outcomes: every
soluble solution has a soluble structure skew brace (see Theorem 4.2). As a
corollary, we extend our previous Theorem C (see Corollary 4.3): for every
skew brace B the following statements are equivalent
• the associated solution (B, rB) is a soluble solution;
• B is a soluble skew brace;
• the associated structure skew brace G(B, rB) is soluble;
• the associated permutation skew brace G(B, rB) is soluble.
We also show that the converse of Theorem 4.2 is not true in general (see
Example 4.7), but we prove a sufficient condition for the converse: see Theo-
rem 4.4. This result yields a new version of Theorem D in [1]. In particular,
every non-degenerate solution (X, r) with a soluble skew brace structure, and
an element x ∈X such that λx = ρx = idX, is a soluble solution.
We shall adhere here to our previous notation and preliminaries of [1].

On i-homomorphisms of solutions
Previous results show that simplicity of solutions exhibits a rigid be-
haviour that is not reflected in the ideal structure of the structure skew
brace associated with a solution. Therefore, it is natural to consider a more
comprehensive notion of homomorphism that enables us to get a tighter grip
over the associated structure skew brace of a solution.
Definition 3.1. Let (X, r) and (Y, s) be solutions. We call a homomorphism
of solutions f : X →Y an i-homomorphism, if there exists an equivalence
class X0 ⊆X of ker f such that for every equivalence class Z of ker f, it
holds r(X0, Z) = Z × X0 and r(Z, X0) = X0 × Z. We say X0 is an i-kernel
of f, and f is said to be an i-epimorphism if f is also surjective.
The importance of i-homomorphisms of solutions is twofold: on the one

be associated with ideals of structure skew braces of solutions.
Recall that a solution (X, r) is said to be decomposable if there exist
proper subsets Y, Z ⊆X such that X = Y ∪Z, and r(Y, Y ) = Y × Y and
r(Z, Z) = Z × Z. Otherwise, if no such decomposition can be considered,
(X, r) is said to be indecomposable. It is well-known that the only decom-
posable simple solution is the twist solution on two elements, and out of
it, simplicity of solutions is a stronger property that does not characterise
indecomposability.

posable solutions in terms of an associated i-simplicity.

Theorem 4.4 opens the door to explore the relation between soluble solu-
tions and multipermutation solutions. Let (X, r) be a solution, and consider
the equivalence relation ∼on X defined as x ∼y if λx = λy and ρx = ρy. The
so-called retraction solution Ret(X, r) = (X/ ∼, ¯r) arises, where ¯r is defined
by
¯r([x], [y]) = ([λx(y)], [ρy(x)]),
for all [x], [y] ∈X/ ∼.
We can iterate this process and define inductively
Ret1(X, r) = Ret(X, r),
Retn+1(X, r) = Ret(Retn(X, r)),
for all n ≥1.
A solution (X, r) is said to be a multipermutation solution of level m, if m is
the smallest natural such that Retm(X, r) is a singleton solution. A singleton
solution is multipermutation of level 0. It is well-known that a solution is mul-
tipermutation if, and only if, GX and G(X, r) are right nilpotent of nilpotent
type, and in addition, right nilpotent skew braces are soluble (cf. [1]).
Corollary 4.5. Let (X, r) be a solution with soluble structure skew brace GX.
If there exists x ∈X such that λx = ρx = idX, then (X, r) is a soluble
solution.
Proof. Observe that if λx = ρx = idX, then by equations (5) and (6), it turns
out that λGX
ι(x) = ρGX
ι(x) = idGX. Thus, ι(x) ∈Soc(GX), and Soc(GX) is an
abelian ideal of GX. Theorem 4.4 yields the result.
Corollary 4.6 ([1, Corollary 3]). Let (X, r) be a multipermutation solution.
If Soc(GX) ∩ι(X) ̸= ∅, then (X, r) is a soluble solution.
The following example shows that condition ι(x) ∈I, with I an abelian
ideal of GX is essential, as some multipermutation solutions can be simple.
Example 4.7 ([1, Example 4]). Consider the set X = {1, 2, 3} and the solu-
tion (X, r) of previous Example 2.6. Since it is a Lyubashenko solution, (X, r)
has multipermutational level 1, as Ret(X, r) is a singleton solution. There-
fore, GX is right nilpotent, and therefore, a soluble skew brace. Nevertheless,
we have seen that (X, r) is a simple solution which can not be soluble.
