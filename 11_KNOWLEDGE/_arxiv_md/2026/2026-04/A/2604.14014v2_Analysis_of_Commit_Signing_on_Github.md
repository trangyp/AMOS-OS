Abstract
Commit signing is a principal mechanism for verifying the origin
of code in software supply chains. Security frameworks treat it as a
core trust signal, assuming developers sign their commits
consistently with keys they control and keep those keys in good
standing over time. Whether this assumption holds in practice has
not been evaluated at ecosystem scale. This study addresses this
gap. We present the first developer-centric, ecosystem-scale
measurement of commit signing on GitHub, covering the
platform’s full history, spanning 71,694 active developers, 16.1
million commits, and 874,198 repositories. To summarize our
findings: (1) overall signing adoption rates are misleading, as most
signed commits come from automatic platform signing rather than
deliberate developer action; (2) developers who do sign locally
rarely keep it up consistently across repositories or over time; (3)
signing lapse rates rise alongside account age rather than falling,
making
sustained
coverage
structurally
unlikely;
and
(4)
developers manage their signing keys poorly, leaving expired keys
unrevoked and letting credential debt grow over time. Our
findings show that the assumptions underlying supply-chain
security frameworks do not hold in practice, establishing that
progress requires either signing credentials redesigned to travel
with developer identity while remaining outside platform control,
or frameworks built on defenses that do not depend on every
developer managing their own keys.

Introduction
Software supply chain attacks increasingly succeed by bypassing
cryptography rather than breaking it. In the tj-actions
compromise (CVE-2025-30066), stolen credentials were used to
silently poison over 23,000 repositories [9, 45, 57]. Alarmingly, any
attacker with account access can replicate this through a forge’s
web interface. Because platforms automatically sign web commits
to authenticate the session rather than the developer’s identity,
attackers can easily generate maliciously poisoned commits that
display a "verified" badge.
To mitigate this credential-theft loophole, supply-chain security
frameworks mandate local commit signing as an essential defense
layer [1, 54]. While stronger authentication like 2FA makes
accounts harder to compromise [34, 36], local commit signing
limits the blast radius if a breach occurs: a stolen login alone
cannot produce a signature the developer never made [23, 56].
Consequently, cryptographic signatures have become the primary
mechanism
across
the
stack
for
verifying
component
origins [26, 49, 54].
However, for local commit signing to deliver these guarantees
in practice, three conditions must hold: 1 developers must sign
with locally controlled keys rather than platform-generated
signatures;
2
those who adopt signing must sustain it
consistently across repositories and over time so that an unsigned
commit from a known signer stands out; and 3 the keys behind
those signatures must be actively maintained so that platform
verification signals today still reflect a key the developer controls.
Crucially, it remains unknown whether these conditions hold at
ecosystem scale because prior measurements evaluate repositories
rather than developers [23, 51]. By conflating individual security
practices with project-level governance, existing research makes it
impossible to determine if the theoretical protections of commit
signing actually hold true across the platform.

minority who do sign locally, very few sign consistently across all

rather than falling. Key management follows the same pattern:
virtually all expired keys go unrevoked, rotation is reactive rather
than proactive, and dead-key accumulation grows monotonically
with account tenure, reaching a majority among accounts older
than a decade.
In summary, we make the following contributions:
(1) We introduce the concept of platform-mediated signing
inflation and show it renders ecosystem-wide adoption
statistics misleading for supply-chain security purposes.
(2) We provide the first characterization of signing lapse and
cross-repository inconsistency as structural ecosystem
properties, showing they persist regardless of developer
tenure or activity level.
(3) We show that platform tenure does not predict signing
adoption, ruling out the hypothesis that ecosystem-wide
coverage improves naturally over time.
(4) We introduce and operationalize a dead-key metric for
credential hygiene, enabling systematic measurement of
key lifecycle failures at ecosystem scale.

a specific fix; they establish that the assumptions underlying a
large body of supply-chain security work need to be revisited with
ecosystem-scale evidence in hand.
2
Background and Related Work
2.1
Git Commit Signing
Git represents history as immutable objects, where each commit
includes author and committer identity as unverified text [7, 35]. As
a result, anyone can create commits under arbitrary identities [25],
enabling impersonation on reputation-based platforms [23, 41].
To address this, Git supports cryptographic signing, which binds
commit contents and metadata to a private key via OpenPGP [8, 56],
SSH-based signing [53, 55], or X.509/CMS [24, 33].
In practice, platforms act as interpretation layers over Git.
GitHub assigns a “Verified” badge based on both cryptographic
checks and platform-side identity binding [23, 51]. This creates a
key distinction: in developer-managed signing, the developer
controls the private key and the platform verifies it against a
registered public key; in platform-mediated signing, GitHub
generates and signs commits created through its web interface,
producing verified commits without any developer-held key [2].
Prior work shows that developers often misunderstand these
differences and underestimate impersonation risks [58], yet no
study has examined whether this distinction manifests at
ecosystem scale.
Modern supply-chain security frameworks rely on commit
signing as a foundational provenance signal. The SLSA model [54]
defines build integrity levels that assume verifiable source origins,
while systems such as AStRA [1] treat source provenance as a core
security property. Commit signing therefore serves as a
foundational signal for anchoring provenance across the supply
chain [22]. If the conditions these frameworks assume do not hold
in practice, their guarantees break at the earliest stage of the
pipeline.
2.2
Empirical Studies of Signing Practices
Empirical studies of commit signing have focused on repositories
rather than developers. Sharma et al. [51] studied 60 popular
repositories and found that roughly 10% of commits are verified,
but their design conflates developer behavior with project-level
policy and excludes web-based commits, the dominant signing
mechanism. Holtgrave et al. [23] analyzed critical open-source
projects and found widespread vulnerability to contributor
spoofing, but their study remains project-centered and cannot
track individual developer behavior across repositories. Because
these studies sample repositories rather than developers, they
cannot separate individual practice from project governance, nor
determine whether observed patterns generalize across the
broader ecosystem. Neither study measures platform-generated
and developer-managed signatures separately; both simply exclude
platform signatures, making it impossible to determine whether
observed signing activity reflects developer intent or ability. We
address this gap following the Tree of Validity framework [43],
which models experiments as combinations of choices across
problem, domain, method, data, and analysis. By varying the
sampling unit from repositories to individual developers tracked
across all their repositories and over time, we isolate developer

2.3
Adoption Barriers and Key Lifecycle
Management
Even when signing is available, adoption is limited by usability
and workflow friction. Studies show that developers rely on
ad-hoc security practices [30], and that even widely understood
protections such as two-factor authentication suffer from
inconsistent adoption due to usability costs [32]. Platform nudges
alone are insufficient without reducing friction in developer
workflows [10, 12, 44]. Broader studies of software signing
adoption spanning package registries and practitioner perspectives
further confirm that usability friction and organizational factors
constrain signing adoption consistently across contexts [26–28].
The effectiveness of signing further depends on key lifecycle
management. Standards such as NIST SP 800-57 and SP 800-131A
emphasize key rotation, expiration, and revocation as essential
practices [4, 5], while FIPS 186-5 defines approved cryptographic
primitives [40]. Prior audits of GitHub keys focus primarily on
algorithm strength and configuration [6], leaving lifecycle
properties such as expiration and revocation underexplored.
Whether developers treat signing keys as living credentials or
static fixtures has not been measured, and poor key hygiene can
weaken provenance guarantees even when signatures verify
correctly.

3.2.2
Stratified Sampling Strategy. Rather than enumerating users
sequentially from GitHub’s inception, we sampled uniformly
across the entire 230 million user ID range. We targeted a 1%
sample based on practical constraints identified in the full user
enumeration attempt. Given that full traversal would require
approximately 2.3 million API requests (230M IDs at 100 users per
call), an equivalent 1% sampling rate would require roughly 23,000
requests. To achieve this, we divided the ID range into 23,000 equal
intervals. For each interval, we used the starting ID as a sampling
point for the since parameter. Because each API request returns a
block (or bucket) of 100 consecutive available users, we capture
clusters of users at regular intervals across the entire ID space.
This approach mitigates the risk of missing specific account
distributions that might occur if we had sampled only individual,
isolated IDs. Querying the API at each sampling point (returning
up to 100 accounts per request) theoretically yields a maximum of
2.3 million user profiles. However, we ultimately retrieved
complete metadata for 1,294,214 accounts. The remaining queries
returned “NOT FOUND”. To understand these cases, we manually
inspected a subset by visiting the corresponding profile URLs and
confirmed that these accounts were inaccessible—likely deleted,
suspended, or otherwise removed from public view, or enterprise
managed
users
[14],
whose
profiles
are
only
visible
to
authenticated members of the associated organization.

API rate limit. GitHub’s API rate limits prevented exhaustive
enumeration of all users. To address this, we used progressive

Sampling constraints. We acknowledge that bucket based
stratified sampling might miss account distributions. While

the stability observed across all three strata suggests that our
results are unlikely to be artifacts of sample size or identifier gaps,

Scope of visible activity. Our analysis is limited to public
repositories because commits in private repositories are not
accessible through GitHub’s public APIs. As a result, behaviors in
private or enterprise environments remain unobserved. Our

only

signing requirements. Our goal is to understand patterns across

Identity ambiguity. Commits made under different usernames
by the same individual were treated as separate entities because

may affect interpretations at the individual level, but broad trends
are likely reliable.
4
Research Questions
Our review of the literature (§2.1,2.2,2.3) reveals gaps, which we
seek to address by asking the following questions:
RQ1: Which mechanisms and workflows produce signed commits
on GitHub, and what cryptographic configurations do they
use?
RQ2: What factors undermine commit signatures as reliable
provenance signals in software supply chains?
RQ3: How consistently do developers adopt and sustain commit
signing across their repositories and over time?
We present our results in the next sections organized around
these research questions. §5 addresses RQ1 by characterizing the
mechanisms and workflows that produce signed commits, including
the distinction between platform-mediated and developer-managed
signing, as well as the underlying cryptographic configurations.
§5 and §7 jointly address RQ2. §5 analyzes verification outcomes,
workflow-dependent failure modes, and cryptographic hygiene
(e.g., legacy algorithms and misconfigurations), while §7 examines
key lifecycle management, including expiration, revocation, and
rotation practices. §6 addresses RQ3 by measuring the adoption
and sustainment of developer-managed signing, including cross-
repository consistency, temporal persistence, and signing lapse.
5
Findings: Signing Mechanisms
Platform Dominance. We stratify signing activity by whether
commits were created via GitHub’s web UI (the web-flow path) or
via non-UI workflows. Because GitHub automatically signs

a rate of 96.41% because GitHub signs most web-interface commits
automatically using a platform-controlled key. In stark contrast,

work, have a signing rate of only 5.24% (𝜒2(1) = 12,002,263.50,
𝑝< 0.001). Consequently, 82.69% of all signed commits originate
from the GitHub UI, implying that verifiable provenance is largely
a byproduct of the interface used rather than a deliberate
developer practice. At the user level, this dominance is even
stronger: 94.29% of users who have ever produced a signed commit
do so exclusively via the GitHub UI, with no observable evidence
of developer-managed signing outside the platform.

the public key corresponding to the private key used to sign

percentage-point gap is practically meaningful for provenance

expected to provide end-to-end integrity, yet roughly one in eight
such signatures does not surface as a valid trust signal on GitHub.

them. To assess whether GitHub’s cryptographic provenance can
be sustained in practice, we shift from commits to the key

sign to whether they manage signing-capable credentials as living
security artifacts, with expiration, rotation, and revocation, or as
static remnants that accumulate over time.
Our key-only dataset (Table 2) contains 208,546 unique public
keys across 122,050 users, spanning SSH authentication keys, SSH
signing keys, and OpenPGP keys. We evaluate lifecycle state at the
most recent snapshot in our collection (Dec 31, 2025), and interpret
expiry and revocation relative to that point. This lens is deliberately
conservative: we do not infer intent from commits or repository
policies, but instead measure what the platform’s registry records
about key validity and maintenance.
Rarity of Expiration. If expiration functioned as a widely
adopted hygiene mechanism, expiry metadata would be expected
across all key types. However, only 2.81% of all keys (5,858 out of
208,546) possess an expiry date. Expiry is also unevenly
distributed: 35.45% of OpenPGP keys include an expiration
timestamp, whereas expiry metadata is essentially absent for SSH
authentication and SSH signing keys in this registry snapshot.
This indicates that explicit lifecycle configuration is primarily
associated with OpenPGP workflows and is not reflected in SSH
key material on the platform. This pattern aligns with
expectations, as SSH public key material typically does not support
expiration timestamps; thus, the absence of expiry metadata
reflects the key format and registry semantics rather than a
deliberate user decision to avoid expiration.
Long Key Lifetimes. Even among the minority of keys that adopt
expiration, expiry date does not typically represent short rotation
cycles. For expiring keys, the median configured lifetime (created
→expires) is 730 days, with an interquartile range of 365 to 1,460
days. The tail is long: the 95th percentile lifetime is 3,650 days (10
years). These values are difficult to reconcile with expiration as
a routine safeguard; instead, expiration appears to function as an
infrequent maintenance boundary.

subset of keys that have an expiration configuration (𝑁= 5, 858),
more than half had already expired by our snapshot date (3, 096, or
52.85%). In a managed lifecycle, we would expect these lapsed keys
to be formally revoked to prevent future misuse. However, we
observe the opposite: 99.81% of expired keys remain unrevoked.
This confirms revocation is effectively non-existent in this
ecosystem and that even among users who configure expiration, it
functions primarily as a passive timestamp rather than a trigger
for active credential cleanup.
Rotation is uncommon and largely reactive. To probe whether
expiration triggers replacement, we measure a conservative
registry-level replacement signal: whether the key owner adds
another key to their GitHub account within 30 days before or after
the expiring key’s expiration date. We use a 30-day window to
capture actions plausibly prompted by the expiration boundary
while minimizing accidental matches to unrelated key uploads.
This signal indicates that a new credential was uploaded near its
expiration date, but it does not, by itself, establish that the new key
was adopted for signing or that the expiring key was retired.
Overall, only 8.42% of expiring keys exhibit such a replacement.
The timing further suggests reactive maintenance. Among keys
that are not expired at snapshot, the replacement signal is
essentially absent at 0.14%, whereas among keys already expired it
rises to 15.79%. This difference is highly statistically significant
(𝜒2(1) = 461.82, 𝑝< 0.001), and indicates that users typically do not
rotate keys before expiration; when replacements occur, they are
disproportionately associated with keys that have already lapsed.

8
Discussion
We identified three conditions necessary for commit signing to
function as a supply-chain defense: 1 developers must sign with
locally controlled keys, 2 signing must be sustained consistently
across repositories and over time, and 3 keys must be actively
maintained. Our findings show all three fail together rather than
independently, and that the failures compound in a way that is
structural rather than behavioral.
8.1
The Provenance Paradox
Platform-mediated signatures confirm only that a commit passed
through GitHub’s web interface. Developer-managed signatures
confirm that a private key held outside the platform was used
directly. Only the latter protects against the credential-theft
attacks that motivate commit signing in the first place. Yet 94% of
apparent signers have never produced a signature outside the
browser, meaning the badge reflects interface usage rather than
deliberate security practice for the vast majority of users. Any
downstream system that consumes GitHub’s verification status
without distinguishing its source will systematically overestimate
provenance coverage, and supply-chain tools that build integrity
guarantees on top of this signal inherit that overestimate.
8.2
Why the Failures Are Structural
Low adoption might suggest a need for developer education or
platform nudges. The data complicates that response. Lapse rates
rise with account age rather than falling, dead-key accumulation
grows monotonically with tenure, and consistent local signing
concentrates almost exclusively among the lowest-activity
developers. None of these patterns fit a knowledge gap that
improves with experience. The productivity penalty makes this
concrete: developers who sign every commit have a median of just
11 total commits, versus 308 for inconsistent signers. This is not a
marginal difference. It reflects friction that compounds with
commit volume and environmental heterogeneity (multiple
machines, repositories, and CI pipelines) rather than carelessness.
Nudges that do not absorb that underlying cost are unlikely to
shift behavior among the high-activity contributors who matter
most for supply-chain risk.
8.3
Recommendations
For
platform
designers. The
structural
argument
shifts
responsibility toward the platform, whose design goal should be a
signing mechanism that provides end-to-end provenance without
requiring developers to manage key material across environments.
Candidate approaches include hardware-backed or passkey-rooted
credentials that travel with developer identity, platform-side
lifecycle automation for rotation and revocation, and splitting the
Verified badge into separate signals for platform-attested and
developer-attested commits. The last is actionable near term
without changes to cryptographic infrastructure; the distinction
already exists in the data and in the API and only needs to be
surfaced. On key hygiene, dead-key debt is heavily concentrated
among long-tenured accounts: 53.46% of accounts older than ten
years carry at least one dead key. Targeted outreach to this cohort,
with automated expiration reminders and prompted revocation at
expiry, would address the majority of accumulated risk without
platform-wide intervention, consistent with NIST cryptoperiod
guidance [4].
For practitioners and auditors. Organizations using commit
signing as a pipeline verification step should not treat the Verified
badge as equivalent to developer-attested provenance without
filtering by verification source. Raw verified counts overstate
developer-managed coverage by more than an order of magnitude.
Auditing workflows should operate on non-UI signing rates and
key lifecycle state rather than aggregate verification statistics. The
dead-key metric we introduce is computable from publicly
available key registry data without access to private repositories
and can be incorporated into existing supply-chain risk assessment
tooling.
