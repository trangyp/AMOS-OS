ABSTRACT
(Sub)millimeter dust polarization in protoplanetary disks has revealed the presence of large (∼
100 µm) dust grains that are aligned along their long axis following the azimuthal direction of the
disk. The novel Badminton Birdie-like Aerodynamic Alignment predicts large grains to align with
their long axes following the direction of gas flow experienced by the dust, denoted as the A-field.
With 3D streaming instability (SI) simulations, we find that the A-field is predominantly in the
radial direction in regions of low dust-to-gas ratio, but in the azimuthal direction in regions of high
dust-to-gas ratio. Through polarized radiation transfer, we find that the resulting polarization angle
indeed follows the disk azimuthal direction in the high dust density regions. Therefore, the azimuthal
dust polarization pattern, as observed in an increasing number of disks, especially at relatively long
millimeter wavelengths, offers evidence of ongoing SI in protoplanetary disks.

The origin of aligned grains in protoplanetary disks is
currently a mystery, but we list a few key empirical con-
straints on the geometrical properties of grains. First,
the fact that grains can scatter millimeter wavelength ra-
diation requires grain sizes to be at least ∼100 µm (e.g.
A. Kataoka et al. 2015; H. Yang et al. 2016a; Z.-Y. D.
Lin et al. 2020), which is significantly larger than the
typical ∼0.1 µm interstellar medium grains. Second,
while grains are generally triaxial, polarization infers
grains aligned predominantly by the long axes making
them effectively act like prolate grains for polarization.
Third, the alignment of the grain’s long axis is in the
azimuthal direction around the disk. These constraints
come simply from the axisymmetry of disks which de-
termines the azimuthal variation of polarization fraction
and polarization angle (H. Yang et al. 2019; T. Mori &
A. Kataoka 2021). An addition to the third constraint
is that in some cases, there are reported deviations to
alignment in the azimuthal direction by 4.5◦for AS 209
(T. Mori et al. 2019), 7.3◦for GG Tau (Y.-W. Tang
et al. 2023), and 4◦for HL Tau (Xu et al. in prep.).
Relative to the disk rotation directions, all are trailing
spirals.
Explaining how and why the large grains are az-
imuthally aligned and effectively prolate in shape has
proven difficult. Historically, grains in the interstellar
medium (ISM) are shown to be aligned to the magnetic
field (B-field) via RAdiative Torques (RAT) (e.g. B. T.
Draine & J. C. Weingartner 1996; A. Lazarian & T.
Hoang 2007a; B.-G. Andersson et al. 2015). In short, as
a grain spins, the axis of maximum inertia (i.e., the short
axis) becomes aligned to its angular momentum vector
(e.g. E. M. Purcell 1979) and both become aligned to
the B-field. Over an ensemble, which determines the
polarization, the population appear as effectively oblate
grains whose short axes are aligned to B-field. Appli-
cations of RAT to protoplanetary disk geometry with
the expected toroidal B-field structure produces radi-
ally oriented polarization (e.g. J. Cho & A. Lazarian
2007; H. Yang et al. 2016b). The observed azimuthally
aligned, effectively prolate grains immediately rule out
the conventional picture applied to the ISM and calls
for new physics to the grain alignment problem of pro-
toplanetary disks.
Recently, Z.-Y. D. Lin et al. (2024a) proposed the
Badminton Birdie-like Aerodynamic Alignment mecha-
nism (hereafter, birdie-like alignment) where an offset in
the grain center of mass to the general geometric center
along the long axis of large, millimeter-sized grains can
produce the necessary effectively prolate grains simply
due to gas drag from gas motion relative to the grain14.
Dust polarization traces the alignment direction of the
grain following this relative motion, which we refer to
as the field of aerodynamic flow — the A-field — de-
fined as the gas velocity relative to the dust velocity.
Grains are aligned of order the stopping time, which is
usually a fraction of the Keplerian time for millimeter
grains (e.g. T. Birnstiel 2024). The speed of alignment
is a major advantage of this mechanism, especially in
high-density disk environments where other mechanisms
might be damped.
Although birdie-like alignment can readily satisfy the
polarization requirements on the shape and sizes of
grains, the observed alignment direction requires an A-
field that is in the azimuthal direction which is unex-
pected. Assuming dust grains experience aerodynamic
drag in a gaseous disk with the typical negative pressure
gradient in steady state, the grains with Stokes number
≪1 (such as the expected 100 µm grains) should drift
inward relative to the gas (T. Takeuchi & D. N. C. Lin
2002). As a result, the A-field should point in the out-
ward radial direction, which would produce radially ori-
ented polarization under birdie-like alignment (Z.-Y. D.
Lin et al. 2024a). This is inconsistent with the obser-
vations of azimuthal polarization patterns, highlighting
the tension between simple equilibrium dust-gas dynam-
ics and the observations.
One potential avenue is to incoporate dust feedback
where local gas dynamics within a concentrated dust
layer can be significantly altered by dust drag (Y. Nak-
agawa et al. 1986).
Furthermore, the time-dependent
evolution of the coupled gas and dust dynamics are regu-
larly studied in simulations of Streaming Instability (SI;
e.g., A. Johansen & A. Youdin 2007). First studied by
A. N. Youdin & J. Goodman (2005) in its linear regime,
SI has long thought to be a promising avenue for form-
ing planetesimals directly from relatively small grains,
involving significant clumping of dust through collec-
tive aerodynamic effects (e.g. A. Johansen & A. Youdin
2007; E. Chiang & A. N. Youdin 2010). Since SI nec-
essarily involves aerodynamic interactions between dust
and gas, it begs the question: how do the flows align
grains through birdie-like alignment? As explained in
J. Squire & P. F. Hopkins (2020), inward gas motion is
deflected to the azimuthal direction due to the Coriolis
force, making it a likely candidate for explaining the ob-
served inferred azimuthal alignment direction. Recent
advancements in SI simulations with greatly improved

2.1. Streaming Instability Simulation
We analyze existing streaming instability simulations
from J. Lim et al. (2025b) and only briefly describe the
methodology and scope (see also J. Lim et al. 2024,
2025c). To solve for the coupled dynamics of gas and
dust, the simulations used the ATHENA code (J. M. Stone
et al. 2008) to model the isothermal, unmagnetized gas,
while utilizing the Lagrangian particle module from X.-
N. Bai & J. M. Stone (2010) to numerically solve the
equation of motion for dust particles.
We do not as-
sume external turbulence and ignore self-gravity of solid
particles.

approximation, which models a co-rotating patch of a

4.2. The Grain Alignment Problem of Protoplanetary
Disks
As mentioned in Sec. 1, the observed effectively pro-
late grains have convincingly ruled out the conventional
RAT grain alignment paradigm adopted for the ISM.
For grains smaller than ∼1 µm, we generally expect
internal energy dissipation, which aligns the short axes
of grains to the angular momentum, also called “inter-
nal alignment" (E. M. Purcell 1979). How the popula-
tion aligns as an ensemble depends on the external field.
Under strong RAT conditions, the short axes of grains
with helicity are expected to be aligned to the direction
of radiation anisotropy (A. Lazarian & T. Hoang 2007a;
R. Tazaki et al. 2017). Under the MEchanical Torques
(MET) framework, the short axes of grains with helicity
are aligned to the aerodynamic flow due to gas bombard-
ment (A. Lazarian & T. Hoang 2007b; T. Hoang et al.
2018; M. Lietzow-Sinjen et al. 2025). In both cases, if
there exists a B-field, the short axes become aligned to
the B-field since spinning, paramagnetic grains gain a
magnetic moment through the Barnett effect (A. Z. Dol-
ginov & I. G. Mytrophanov 1976; E. M. Purcell 1979).
In any case, the issue lies in the alignment of the short
axes, which creates effectively oblate grains. Perhaps it
is not too surprising, since in disk environments, where
the grains responsible for millimeter polarization are at
least 100 µm and gas densities are drastically higher, in-
ternal relaxation is no longer faster than the gas damp-
ing time making internal alignment an invalid assump-
tion (T. Hoang & A. Lazarian 2009; T. Hoang et al.
2022).
How large grains can be aligned to external vector
fields in the limit of slow internal relaxation is currently
unclear. The birdie-like alignment adopted in this work
is one approach utilizing gas bombardment in this limit.
Experimentally, G. Wurm & J. Blum (2000) showed that
grains grown in a cluster-cluster type of aggregation are
naturally elongated and that they preferentially align
their long axes along the flow direction, which is consis-
tent with birdie-like alignment. The long-to-short axes
ratio in that direction is ∼1.2 which is intriguingly con-
sistent with that inferred from polarization of HL Tau
(I. W. Stephens et al. 2023; Z.-Y. D. Lin et al. 2024b).
Another candidate is the so-called “wrong" internal
alignment with B-RAT where the short axis of a grain
is perpendicular to its angular momentum J and J is
aligned to B at low-J attractors in the limit of slow in-
ternal relaxation (T. Hoang 2022; T. Hoang et al. 2022).
As a result, the polarization is parallel to the B-field.
Under a toroidal B-field for a disk, the polarization be-
haves as azimuthally aligned, effectively prolate grains
that can satisfy the azimuthal variation in the polar-

ization degree and direction in a disk (N. T. Thang
et al. 2024). While the two candidates cannot be distin-
guished by polarization morphology, it can be differen-
tiated by the damping time. Short damping time due to
high gas densities benefits the birdie-like alignment by
stopping all spin, and puts B-RAT at a disadvantage,
since grains need to spin to maintain a magnetic mo-
ment from the Barnett effect. Several theoretical works
have shown that the gas damping time is usually shorter
than Larmor precession time in midplanes of disks (e.g.
R. Tazaki et al. 2017; H. Yang 2021; T. Hoang et al.
2022).
Observationally, A. Fourkas et al. (2026) demon-
strated with Class I BHB 07-11 that the damping
timescale tends to be shorter than the Larmor preces-
sion time for a wide range in potential grain structure,
offering one case that favors birdie-like alignment. In-
triguingly, L. W. Looney et al. (2025) showed that the
aligned grains in the Class 0 L1448 IRS 3 follows the
Stokes I spiral in the high density regions, but their di-
rections and polarization fraction change drastically in
the low density, outer regions. The two cases here hints
at a gas density dependence where B-RAT operates in
the low gas density regime while birdie-like alignment
takes over in the high gas density regime. However, in
a low-density gas environment such as that of a debris
disk, C. L. H. Hull et al. (2022) found that grain align-
ment is inconsistent with magnetic alignment.
Given
the upper limit on polarization of approximately 1%,
any alignment that does occur would be extremely poor,
even if B-RAT were the underlying mechanism. Several
questions about alignment processes remain and more
theoretical clarification is necessary.
At the same time, given the multitude of potential
grain structures, observational tests are invaluable. Af-
terall, the grain shape and orientation are deduced im-
mediately from the azimuthal variation in the polariza-
tion expected from axisymmetric disks and are relatively
immune from assumptions of any alignment theory (H.
Yang et al. 2019; T. Mori & A. Kataoka 2021). Search-
ing for aligned grains and testing its dependence on the
environment, for example, the gas density, evolutionary
stage, or the B-field morphology (constrained by other
means), can provide tremendous insights.
4.3. Observational Implications
The SI has been proposed as the main candidate
for planetesimal formation (E. Chiang & A. N. Youdin
2010), but the observational evidence remains scant
for such an important step in planet formation.
D.
Nesvorný et al. (2019) identified that the prograde mu-
tual inclinations of binaries in the cold classical Kuiper
Belt is consistent with expecations from the SI. The size
distribution of the Asteroid and Kuiper Belt objects can
also be compared to the resulting planetesimal popula-
tion from SI simulations with gravitational collapse of
solids (e.g. J. B. Simon et al. 2016; R. Li & E. Chi-
ang 2025). The density dichotomy of Kuiper Belt Ob-
jects can also be explained by SI (M. H. Cañas et al.
2024). The obvious downside of these approach is that
the constraints come entirely from the Solar System. As
we have demonstrated, birdie-like alignment and the az-
imuthally aligned prolate grains provide another line of
evidence for SI. Therefore, we believe that dust polar-
ization opens a new window to directly study the mil-
limeter grains experiencing ongoing SI in protoplanetary
systems other than the solar nebula.
HL Tau is currently the disk with the best set of po-
larization data, ranging from λ = 0.87 mm to 7 mm
(I. W. Stephens et al. 2014; A. Kataoka et al. 2016a;
I. W. Stephens et al. 2017; Z.-Y. D. Lin et al. 2024b)
along with the highest angular resolution (5 au) at
λ = 0.87 mm (I. W. Stephens et al. 2023). The exis-
tence of azimuthally aligned, prolate grains, even within
the gaps, has been well established in this Class I/II disk
accounting for optical depth effects and, thus, suggests
ongoing SI.
Several other sources, while studied in less detail, also
show the azimuthally oriented polarization pattern, like
HL Tau, that is consistent with azimuthally aligned, pro-
late grains, including Class I IRS 63 (S. I. Sadavoy et al.
2019), other Class II sources, like AS 209 (T. Mori et al.
2019; R. E. Harrison et al. 2021), Haro 6-13 (R. E. Har-
rison et al. 2019, 2024), DG Tau (R. E. Harrison et al.
2019), V892 Tau (R. E. Harrison et al. 2024), GG Tau
(Y.-W. Tang et al. 2023), and even a high mass star
GGD 27 MM1 (J. M. Girart et al. 2018). Several bi-
naries or triples also show azimuthally oriented polar-
ization patterns, including L1448 IRS3 (L. W. Looney
et al. 2025), BHB 07-11 (A. Fourkas et al. 2026), and
HD 142527 (S. Ohashi et al. 2025). However, several
other sources do not show similar polarization patterns
(e.g. C. L. H. Hull et al. 2018; W. R. F. Dent et al.
2019). It is unclear if the contrast is due to a lack of SI
or if the signature of aligned grains are hidden by scat-
tering. Given how we can infer the presense of SI or the
magnetic field (or large τs grains in steady state, though
unlikely) through polarization, all of which have signif-
icant importance in understanding disk evolution and
planet formation, further discovery awaits from using
instruments sensitive to millimeter wave polarization,
as exemplified by ALMA, the Very Large Array (VLA),
and eventually ngVLA.

5. SUMMARY
The grain alignment problem of protoplanetary disks
is built on three lines of empirical evidence, namely that
the grains are large (at least 100 µm in size), effectively
prolate, and aligned in the disk azimuthal direction,
which conventional alignment theories cannot explain.
The novel birdie-like alignment allows large grains to be
aligned as effectively prolate grains in the direction of
the gas velocity relative to the dust velocity (A-field).
Through state-of-the-art 3D streaming instability (SI)
simulations from J. Lim et al. (2025b), we find that in
regions with high dust-to-gas ratio, dust predominantly
experiences azimuthal A. We conduct polarized radi-
ation transfer and find that the polarization angle χ
follows the disk azimuthal direction in the regions of
high dust-to-gas ratio. The azimuthal χ depends on the
Stokes number (τs) and the initial dust content rela-
tive to the gas (Z). With low dust-to-gas ratios, dust
experiences radial A resulting in radially oriented po-
larization.
Therefore, the observed large, azimuthally
aligned, prolate grains of protoplanetary disks could be
a signature of ongoing SI. The significance of this find-
ing is two-fold. First, it solves the long mysterious origin
of aligned grains in protoplanetary disks. Second, it of-
fers new evidence to the long anticipated SI currently
favored to form planetesimals. The seemingly different
lines of work are natural results of aerodynamic interac-
tions between gas and dust.

A. VERTICAL STRUCTURE OF THE A-FIELD
Here we show the vertical structure of the two example simulations (τs, Z) = (0.01, 0.03) and (0.03, 0.005). We
average ϵ, Ax, Ay, and Az along the y-axis for visualization. Fig. 6 shows ϵ and the normalized components Ax/|A|,
Ay/|A|, and Az/|A| to show the relative strength. In both cases, the dust is highly settled in that ϵ drops below 1
just beyond |z|/H ∼0.01 (Fig. 6a,b). For (τs, Z) = (0.01, 0.03), Ax/|A| is generally positive throughout, while Ay/|A|
is positive only in the midplane and turns negative when at regions away from the midplane (Fig. 6c,e). Az/|A| is
positive where z > 0, but switches sign where z < 0 giving Az/|A| ∼0 as mentioned in Sec. 3. It is the smallest among
the three components. The (τs, Z) = (0.03, 0.005) shows more complex structure. Within −0.2 < x/H < 0.05 where
the midplane ϵ > 1, Ax is generally positive and we see a stratified Ay/|A| where Ay < 0 in the midplane, but Ay > 0
away from the midplane. The two features are similar to the previous example. However, at x where the midplane
ϵ < 1, A is completely dominated by Ax, while Ay/|A| ∼0. The vertical component Az/|A| shares a similar change
in sign across the midplane like the previous example.
The trailing spiral of polarization χ is a result of the stratification of Ay for both cases. In the midplane where
ϵ > 1, Ay > 0 and since Ax is also slightly positive, the A-field in the midplane is a leading spiral. However, above
the midplane, where Ay < 0 while Ax > 0, the A-field is a trailing spiral. Although the midplane has a higher dust
density, the layer with Ay > 0 is smaller in its vertical extent compared to the layer where Ay < 0. Polarization from
aligned grains in these two layers counter each other, and it is the trailing spiral that wins for these simulations.
