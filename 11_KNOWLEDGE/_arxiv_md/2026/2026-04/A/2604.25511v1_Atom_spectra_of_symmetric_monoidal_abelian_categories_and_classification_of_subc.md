ATOM SPECTRA OF SYMMETRIC MONOIDAL ABELIAN CATEGORIES AND
CLASSIFICATION OF SUBCATEGORIES
SHUNYA SAITO
Abstract. We extend the classification results for torsion classes and torsion-free classes in the category
of finitely generated modules over a commutative noetherian ring to suitable symmetric monoidal closed
noetherian abelian categories.
Our main tool is the orbit atom spectrum, defined as the quotient of Kanda’s atom spectrum by
the action induced by tensoring with invertible objects. We prove that, under natural tensor-theoretic
assumptions, several classes of subcategories collapse to Serre subcategories or torsion-free classes.
Moreover, torsion-free classes compatible with the tensor structure are classified by arbitrary subsets of
the orbit atom spectrum.
As applications, we recover the classical classifications for commutative noetherian rings and obtain
analogues for graded modules, coherent sheaves, and dg modules.

1. Introduction
1.1. Background. The classification of subcategories of abelian categories is a classical problem in rep-
resentation theory, algebraic geometry, and commutative algebra. Many natural classes of subcategories
encode structural features of abelian categories. For example, Serre subcategories control the localization
theory of abelian categories. Torsion classes and torsion-free classes arise as the two halves of torsion
pairs, and hence describe ways of decomposing objects in an abelian category. Wide subcategories are
extension-closed subcategories which are abelian in their own right. These subcategories are all defined
by being closed under basic operations in an abelian category, such as extensions, subobjects, quotients,

full subcategories satisfying such natural closure conditions.
One of the most classical classification theorems is due to Gabriel [Gab62]: for a noetherian scheme
X, localizing subcategories of the category Qcoh X of quasi-coherent sheaves are classified in terms of
specialization-closed subsets of the underlying topological space of X. In particular, for a commutative
noetherian ring R, Serre subcategories of the category mod R of finitely generated R-modules are clas-
sified by specialization-closed subsets of Spec R. This result has served as a prototype for many later
classification theorems.
Another fundamental classification result is due to Takahashi [Tak08]: for a commutative noetherian
ring R, torsion-free classes of mod R are classified by arbitrary subsets of Spec R. Subsequent results
showed that, over a commutative noetherian ring, many apparently different closure conditions collapse to
one of two types. More precisely, torsion classes, wide subcategories, ICE-closed subcategories, and CE-
closed subcategories coincide with Serre subcategories [Tak08, SW11]; similarly, IKE-closed subcategories
and IE-closed subcategories coincide with torsion-free classes [IMST24, Eno]. For the precise definitions
of the subcategories appearing above, see Definition 2.1. See also [Kra08, ˇCˇS20, IK24, KS24, Sai25, KS]
for related developments and extensions.
These results reveal a remarkable rigidity in the commutative noetherian case. In many other settings,
the subcategories mentioned above usually do not coincide and exhibit much richer behavior. For ex-
ample, over finite-dimensional algebras, such subcategories are closely related to tilting theory, τ-tilting
theory, and silting theory. Through these theories, they are connected with noncrossing partitions, cluster
combinatorics, and other combinatorial structures [IT09, AIR14, MS17, Asa20, Eno22, ES22]. In con-
trast, many natural closure conditions on subcategories of mod R collapse into only two familiar types:
Serre subcategories and torsion-free classes. Moreover, both types are controlled by the same topological
space Spec R: Serre subcategories correspond to specialization-closed subsets, and torsion-free classes
correspond to arbitrary subsets.
The purpose of this paper is to show that these phenomena are not special to commutative rings.
Rather, once the tensor product is taken into account, they are instances of a general phenomenon in
symmetric monoidal abelian categories.
1.2. Main results. We now explain our main results.
The basic setting of this paper is a locally
noetherian Grothendieck cosmos, that is, a locally noetherian Grothendieck category A equipped with
a symmetric monoidal closed structure. Thus, it is equipped with a tensor product, a unit object I,
and internal Hom objects [M, N], characterized by the adjunction between −⊗M and [M, −]. In what
follows, let A be a locally noetherian Grothendieck cosmos. We write Anoeth for the full subcategory of
noetherian objects. The basic examples of locally noetherian Grothendieck cosmoi include the following
categories:
• the category Mod R of modules over a commutative noetherian ring,
• the category ModG S of graded modules over a commutative graded noetherian ring,
• the category Qcoh X of quasi-coherent sheaves on a noetherian scheme,
• the category dgMod A of dg modules over a skew-commutative dg noetherian ring.
In an abelian category equipped with a symmetric monoidal structure, it is natural to study sub-
categories which are compatible with the tensor product. A subcategory X is called a tensor ideal if
X ⊗M ∈X for all X ∈X and all objects M ∈A. Tensor ideals are especially natural from the viewpoint
of localization. A localizing subcategory which is also a tensor ideal is called a tensor localizing subcat-
egory. If a localizing subcategory X is a tensor ideal, then the quotient category A/X again inherits
a symmetric monoidal closed structure, and the quotient functor is monoidal. Thus, tensor localizing
subcategories provide the localization theory compatible with the tensor product.
However, the condition of being a tensor ideal involves tensoring with all objects of A, and is therefore
difficult to control directly. There is a weaker and more tractable condition obtained by considering only
invertible objects. Recall that an object L is invertible if the functor −⊗L is an autoequivalence. We
say that a subcategory X of A is L-closed if X ⊗L ∈X for all X ∈X and all invertible objects L of
A. This condition is natural in many examples. An additive subcategory of mod R closed under direct
summands is automatically L-closed, whereas an additive subcategory of modG S closed under direct
summands is L-closed if and only if it is closed under grading shifts.

Setup A (cf. Setup 4.1). For a locally noetherian Grothendieck cosmos A, assume that:
• The unit object I is finitely presented.
• The Grothendieck category A is generated by a small set of invertible-filtered objects.

We now state the first main result.
Theorem B (cf. Corollary 5.3). Let A be a locally noetherian Grothendieck cosmos satisfying the as-
sumptions in Setup A. Let X be an L-closed subcategory in Anoeth.
(1) The following are equivalent in Anoeth:
• X is a Serre subcategory.
• X is a torsion class.
• X is a wide subcategory.
• X is an ICE-closed subcategory.
• X is a CE-closed subcategory.
(2) The following are equivalent in Anoeth:
• X is a torsion-free class.
• X is an IKE-closed subcategory.
• X is an IE-closed subcategory.
Thus, under Setup A, among L-closed subcategories, torsion classes, wide subcategories, ICE-closed
subcategories, and CE-closed subcategories are precisely Serre subcategories. Likewise, IKE-closed sub-
categories and IE-closed subcategories are precisely torsion-free classes.
The proof of Theorem B is based on the theory of atom spectra developed by Kanda [Kan12]. For an
abelian category A, the atom spectrum is a topological space Spec A that generalizes the prime spectrum
of a commutative ring. A fundamental result in the theory of atom spectra says that, when A is a locally
noetherian Grothendieck category, localizing subcategories of A are classified by open subsets of Spec A.
Thus Spec A controls the ordinary localization theory of A.
In the symmetric monoidal case, however, the relevant localizations are those compatible with the
tensor product. Recall that the Picard group Pic A is the group of isomorphism classes of invertible
objects of A under the tensor product. It acts on A by tensoring with invertible objects, and hence on
Spec A. We therefore define the orbit atom spectrum by
LSpec A := Spec A/ Pic A.
As in Kanda’s theory, one can define subsets LSupp M and LAss M of LSpec A for each object M ∈A,
analogous to the support and associated primes of a module.
The ordinary atom spectrum Spec A controls ordinary abelian localizations, whereas the orbit atom
spectrum LSpec A controls monoidal abelian localizations. More precisely, under Setup A, tensor localiz-
ing subcategories are exactly L-closed localizing subcategories. Hence, tensor localizations are classified
by open subsets of LSpec A. Using this, we can define the localization of A at a point x ∈LSpec A,
which produces a locally noetherian Grothendieck cosmos Ax that behaves like a local category in the
following sense: its simple objects are unique up to tensoring with invertible objects. This local nature
is the key ingredient of the proof of Theorem B.
By Theorem B, under Setup A, the subcategories of Anoeth considered above collapse to two types:
Serre subcategories and torsion-free classes. The former are already classified by the orbit atom spectrum:
L-closed Serre subcategories of Anoeth correspond to open subsets of LSpec A. Thus, it remains to classify
the L-closed torsion-free classes.

support and associated primes over a commutative noetherian ring: every prime in the support of a

Theorems B and C generalize the classical classifications of subcategories of mod R [Tak08, SW11,
IMST24, Eno]. However, the proofs are new even in the case of commutative noetherian rings. In the
classical commutative setting, one can use a lifting argument: an Rp-linear map Mp →Np can be lifted
to an R-linear map M →N, up to a unit of Rp. This argument is not well suited to the monoidal
abelian setting considered here. Our proofs avoid it and instead use only global constructions available
in a symmetric monoidal closed category, namely the tensor product and the internal Hom. Thus, our
proofs give new conceptual proofs of the classical classifications.
In concrete examples, the orbit atom spectrum is often computable and recovers familiar spaces:
the ordinary prime spectrum for commutative noetherian rings, the homogeneous prime spectrum for
commutative graded noetherian rings, and the underlying topological space for noetherian schemes.
These computations allow Theorem B and Theorem C to recover the known classifications for mod R
and to produce graded and geometric analogues.
In the dg setting, we obtain a new computation of atom spectra. For a bounded non-positive right
dg noetherian dg ring, not necessarily commutative, we determine the atom spectrum of the category
of dg modules itself in terms of the atom spectrum of the degree-zero part. In particular, in the skew-
commutative case, the relevant orbit atom spectrum is isomorphic to the ordinary prime spectrum of
the degree-zero part. This yields dg analogues of the classification results above.
We also discuss examples outside the scope of Setup A in §6.5. For representations of finite acyclic
quivers with the pointwise tensor product and for modules over cocommutative Hopf algebras, tensor
torsion classes are still Serre subcategories, although these categories are not generated by invertible-
filtered objects in general. These examples suggest that the equality between tensor torsion classes and
tensor Serre subcategories should hold beyond the framework of Setup A.
However, to prove such a general statement, one may need a different kind of spectrum. The orbit atom
spectrum used in this paper is constructed as a quotient of the atom spectrum by the autoequivalences
induced by invertible objects, and is well suited to the framework of Setup A. To treat more general
symmetric monoidal abelian categories, it seems more natural to construct the theory directly from
objects such as “prime tensor localizing subcategories”, as in Balmer’s tensor triangular geometry [Bal05].
Even if such a theory were developed, the results of this paper would still be of interest, since orbit atom
spectra are often computable in concrete examples.
Organization. This paper is organized as follows. In §2, we review basic facts on atom spectra and
symmetric monoidal categories. In §3, for an abelian category A equipped with an action of a group G, we
introduce the orbit atom spectrum SpecG A, defined as the quotient space of the atom spectrum Spec A
by G. For a symmetric monoidal abelian category, if we take G = Pic A, then SpecG A = LSpec A, which
was introduced in the introduction. We also investigate basic properties of SpecG A and the localization
of A at a point of SpecG A. In §4, we apply the theory of orbit atom spectra developed in §3 to symmetric
monoidal abelian categories. We show that, under suitable assumptions, monoidal abelian localizations
can be controlled by the orbit atom spectrum, and that the localization at a point of SpecG A behaves
well with respect to the monoidal structure. In §5, we prove Theorems B and C by applying the theory
developed in §3 and §4. In §6, we describe the orbit atom spectrum for several concrete Grothendieck
cosmoi satisfying Setup A and discuss consequences of Theorems B and C. In §6.5, we give examples
of Grothendieck cosmoi that lie outside the scope of Setup A but nevertheless have the property that
tensor torsion classes are Serre subcategories.
Conventions. Throughout this paper, we fix a Grothendieck universe. A set is said to be small if it
belongs to the Grothendieck universe. For a category C, the collection Ob C of objects and the collection
Mor C of morphisms are sets. For any objects M, N ∈C, the set C(M, N) of morphisms from M to N is
assumed to be small. All rings, modules, schemes, and so forth are assumed to be small. A category is
said to be essentially small if there exists a bijection between the set of isomorphism classes of objects
and a small set.
All subcategories are supposed to be full subcategories closed under isomorphisms. Thus, we often
identify the subcategories with the subsets of the set of isomorphism classes of objects.

Remark 2.6. Let A be a locally noetherian Grothendieck category. The atom spectrum is closely related
to the classical Gabriel spectrum. Indeed, the Gabriel spectrum of A is the set of isomorphism classes of
indecomposable injective objects. The injective hull of a monoform object is an indecomposable injective
object, and this construction gives a homeomorphism from Spec A to the Gabriel spectrum, equivalently
to the Ziegler spectrum (see [Kan12, Section 5]). Under this identification, the classification of localizing
subcategories in Fact 2.5 recovers the corresponding classification by Herzog and Krause [Her97, Kra97].
The advantage of using the atom spectrum here is that it is defined purely in terms of monoform
objects. In particular, it can be defined for noetherian abelian categories without referring to injective
objects. Moreover, monoform objects may be viewed as categorical analogues of the R-modules R/p,
where p is a prime ideal of a commutative noetherian ring R. This point of view allows us to use the
atom spectrum with the same intuition as the prime spectrum.

2.3. Symmetric monoidal categories. In this subsection, we review some basic facts on symmetric
monoidal categories. For details, see [Kel82, Mac98]. Throughout this subsection, let A be a symmetric
monoidal closed category. We write ⊗A for the tensor functor and IA for the unit object. For objects
M, N ∈A, the internal Hom from M to N is denoted by A[M, N]. If there is no ambiguity, we simply
write ⊗, I, and [M, N]. As (−⊗M, [M, −]) is an adjoint pair, the functor −⊗M preserves colimits and
[M, −] preserves limits.
An object D ∈A is dualizable if there is E ∈A such that −⊗E is a right adjoint of −⊗D. In this
case, E is called the dual of D. The dual E is isomorphic to D∨:= [D, I] by the following:
A(−, [D, I]) ∼= A(−⊗D, I) ∼= A(−, I ⊗E) ∼= A(−, E).
An argument analogous to the one above yields a natural isomorphism −⊗D∨∼= [D, −]. Dualizable
objects are stable under direct sums, tensor products, internal Hom, and extensions. That is, if D and
E are dualizable objects, then so are D ⊕E, D ⊗E, and [D, E] (see [HO23, Lemma 6.7]). For an exact
sequence 0 →D →X →E →0 such that D and E are dualizable, the middle term X is also dualizable
(see [Bak20, Section 4, Lemma 7]).

A Hopf algebra is a bialgebra H with an antipode, that is, an algebra homomorphism S : H →Hop
satisfying certain conditions. For any M, N ∈Mod H, the k-vector space Homk(M, N) can be viewed as
an H-module via the antipode S (cf. [Kas95, Section III.5]). If H is a cocommutative Hopf algebra, then
the internal Hom in Mod H is given by Homk(M, N). The dualizable objects of Mod H are precisely the
H-modules that are finite dimensional over k. Therefore, if H is a finite-dimensional cocommutative Hopf
algebra, then Mod H is a locally noetherian Grothendieck cosmos satisfying (C1) and (C2). However,
in general, it does not satisfy the assumptions in Setup 4.1.
Nevertheless, we obtain the following
observation.
Proposition 6.24. Let H be a cocommutative Hopf algebra that is right noetherian as a ring. Then
every tensor torsion class of mod H is either zero or mod H. In particular, every tensor torsion class is
a Serre subcategory.

Proof. Let X be a nonzero tensor torsion class of mod H. Take a nonzero object X ∈X. Since the
evaluation map Homk(X, k) ⊗k X →k is a nonzero H-linear map, it is surjective. Since X is a tensor
torsion class, we have k ∈X, and hence X = mod H.
□
Finally, we give examples of noetherian cocommutative Hopf algebras.
Example 6.25. Let k be a field.
(1) For a finite group G, the group algebra kG is a finite-dimensional cocommutative Hopf algebra
whose coproduct, counit, and antipode are given by
∆(g) = g ⊗g,
ϵ(g) = 1,
S(g) = g−1
(g ∈G).
(2) For a finite-dimensional Lie algebra g, the universal enveloping algebra U(g) is a noetherian
cocommutative Hopf algebra whose coproduct, counit, and antipode are given by
∆(x) = x ⊗1 + 1 ⊗x,
ϵ(x) = 0,
S(x) = −x
(x ∈g).
For the fact that U(g) is noetherian, we refer to [MR01, Chapter 1, §7.4], for example.
