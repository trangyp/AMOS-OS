Kartick Ramakrishnan and Phani Motamarri∗

Abstract
Accurate large-scale Kohn–Sham density functional theory (DFT) calculations are
essential for modeling material systems, including interfaces, defects, nanoclusters,
twisted two-dimensional heterostructures, and more. However, achieving desired ac-
curacy at the scale of 104–105 electrons for these complex systems, with practical
time-to-solution, remains a significant challenge for existing DFT implementations.
We present GPU-centric computational methods and algorithmic innovations within a
finite-element (FE) discretized projector augmented-wave (PAW) based density func-
tional theory formulation (PAW-FE), for accurate, efficient, and scalable electronic-
structure calculations on modern exascale computing systems.
The FE discretiza-
tion of the PAW governing equations, derived in this work within a collinear spin
formalism, naturally accommodates generic boundary conditions and employs multi-
resolution quadrature approach for accurate evaluation of atom-centered PAW inte-
grals on coarse FE grids. The underlying FE-discretized PAW generalized Hermitian
eigenproblem is solved using the Residual-based Chebyshev Filtered Subspace Itera-
tion (R-ChFSI) procedure. In particular, exploiting R-ChFSI’s tolerance to inexact
matrix–multivector products, we employ an approximate inverse PAW overlap matrix,
reduced-precision arithmetic (FP32/TF32) and low-precision nearest-neighbour com-
munication (BF16) during Chebyshev-filtered subspace construction, combined with a
block-wise computation–communication overlap strategy to reduce the computational
cost without sacrificing the numerical robustness.
These strategies yield an overall
CPU–GPU speedup of 8× and 20× on Intel and AMD GPU architectures, respec-
tively.
Relative to widely used plane-wave PAW implementations, PAW-FE achieves
about 8× reduction in minimum wall time to solution on system sizes of approximately
10,000 electrons using NVIDIA GPUs, with greater gains at larger system sizes, and
around 6× relative to norm-conserving pseudopotential-based FE implementations.
We further demonstrate practical scalability on material systems comprising 130,000
electrons, establishing PAW-FE as an exascale-ready method for large-scale, chemically
accurate first-principles simulations.

Introduction
Electronic-structure calculations have played a pivotal role in accurately predicting diverse
material properties and today stand as the primary workhorse in modern materials discovery.
Applications of electronic-structure calculations based on Kohn–Sham density functional the-
ory (DFT)1–3 have found remarkable success in identifying viable materials for thermoelectric
devices, predicting dopants and alloy compositions that enhance electrical, mechanical, and
optical properties, and providing quantitative insight into the mechanisms that govern charge
transport, defect chemistry, and catalytic reaction pathways. This ubiquitous adoption is
largely due to the computational tractability afforded by DFT, which enables simulations
of complex material systems while retaining a close connection to the underlying quantum-
mechanical description of electronic interactions. Widely used plane-wave implementations
such as VASP,4 Quantum ESPRESSO,5 and ABINIT6 have further enabled high-throughput
materials screening and the generation of reference datasets for training machine-learning
interatomic potentials, including MACE,7 Allegro8 and NEP.9
Despite this success, many scientifically important problems now demand simulations at
length scales that significantly exceed those accessible with conventional approaches. Com-
puting spin textures such as magnetic skyrmions, modeling catalytic reactions on substrate-
supported metal nanoparticles, studying defect formation and migration in crystalline solids,
and describing electrode–electrolyte interfaces in solid-state batteries all require large simu-
lation cells to eliminate finite-size artifacts, capture charge transfer and strain effects, avoid
spurious defect–defect interactions, and represent chemically complex environments span-
ning several nanometers and involving thousands of atoms. In all these settings, an accurate
electronic-structure description is essential, calling for simulations of 10000-100000 electrons
at chemical accuracy. Such large-scale calculations are equally indispensable for validat-
ing machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) at length scales beyond those sampled
during training.
Although the asymptotic cubic scaling of Kohn–Sham DFT is unavoidable for complex
heterogeneous material systems, the practical barriers to routine large-scale calculations are
of a different character: high time-to-solution, large memory footprints arising from the
need for many basis functions per atom to achieve desired accuracy, poor parallel scala-
bility on modern multi-node architectures, and eigensolver algorithms ill-suited to exploit
the latest GPU hardware. The Projector Augmented-Wave (PAW) method introduced by
Bl¨ochl10 addresses a few of these limitations by reformulating the all-electron problem, char-
acterized by rapidly oscillatory valence wavefunctions near atomic cores, into an equivalent
problem involving smooth pseudo-wavefunctions augmented by localized atom-centered cor-
rections. Within the frozen-core approximation, this transformation allows valence states
to be represented with significantly fewer basis functions while preserving all-electron accu-
racy, thereby substantially reducing both the dimensionality of the discretized Kohn–Sham
problem and its associated memory footprint. Plane-wave implementations such as VASP,4
Quantum ESPRESSO,5 and ABINIT6 have widely adopted the PAW method,10,11 mak-
ing them the predominant tools in the materials science community. However, plane-wave
implementations inherently enforce periodic boundary conditions and rely on global fast
Fourier transforms (FFTs), which incur substantial all-to-all communication. On modern
GPU-accelerated supercomputers, where floating-point throughput has grown dramatically

relative to memory bandwidth and inter-node communication capacity, FFT-based imple-
mentations are increasingly bandwidth-bound and communication-limited, posing a funda-
mental obstacle to scalable large-scale DFT on exascale systems.
Real-space discretizations provide an alternative route that is better suited to leverage
multi-node computing architectures. In addition to improved parallel scalability, real-space
methods allow the use of generic boundary conditions, which provide a more realistic compu-
tational setup for modelling slabs, surfaces, and nanoclusters.12 Real-space methods include
finite difference approaches such as Octopus,13 GPAW,14 PARSEC,15 SPARC,16 wavelet
based approaches in BigDFT17 and finite-element approaches in DFT-FE,18–20 FEMTECK.21,22
However, with the exception of GPAW, most existing real-space implementations rely on the
norm-conserving pseudopotential (ONCV) approximation to solve the DFT problem. Al-
though systematically transferable, ONCV pseudopotentials are hard, particularly for first-
row and transition-metal elements, and therefore require a substantially larger number of
basis functions to achieve chemical accuracy. This leads to a substantial increase in com-
putational cost and memory footprint. In contrast, the projector augmented-wave (PAW)
method reconstructs the true all-electron wavefunctions from smooth pseudo-wavefunctions
through an explicit linear transformation. This formulation enables accurate recovery of the
all-electron charge density and wavefunctions in the vicinity of atomic nuclei, allowing reliable
evaluation of quantities sensitive to core regions such as forces, hyperfine interactions, and
electrostatic potentials, while retaining the efficiency of a smooth basis representation. Fur-
thermore, by relaxing the strict norm-conservation constraint, PAW typically achieves faster
basis set convergence and improved transferability compared to norm-conserving pseudopo-
tentials.
Real-space finite-element (FE) discretization for the projector augmented-wave method
(PAW-FE23) has only recently been explored, where high-order spectral finite elements are
employed to solve the PAW governing equations. This approach demonstrates a natural
synergy between the smooth fields arising from the PAW formalism and the adaptive resolu-
tion and systematic improvability of finite-element discretizations. In PAW-FE, higher-order
finite-element basis functions with compact support are used to discretize the governing equa-
tions, enabling systematic polynomial convergence while naturally accommodating periodic,
semi-periodic, and non-periodic boundary conditions. The locality of these FE basis func-
tions induces sparse operator structure requiring only nearest-neighbour communication19,23
to evaluate its action, thereby enabling fine-grained parallelism well suited for large-scale
simulations on exascale architectures. The FE-discretized PAW governing equations derived
in our previous work23 lead to a generalized Hermitian eigenvalue problem (GHEP) of the
form H eΨ = S eΨΛ. To efficiently solve this problem, this previous work extended the tra-
ditional Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration (ChFSI) eigensolver, originally employed for
standard Hermitian eigenvalue problems, to GHEP. The resulting PAW-FE methodology
implemented on distributed multinode CPU architectures demonstrated excellent agreement
with plane-wave PAW implementations across representative benchmark systems with non-
periodic, semi-periodic, and fully periodic boundary conditions, including comparisons of
ground-state energies, energy variation against bond-length and lattice parameters, forma-
tion energies, surface energies, and band structures. In addition to accuracy, the proposed
computational strategies enabled substantial performance gains relative to plane-wave imple-

Motivated by the promising results of PAW-FE
on multi-node CPU architectures, this
work develops new eigensolver algorithms and numerical implementation strategies for the
PAW generalized Hermitian eigenproblem (GHEP) that are well-suited to modern multi-

Contemporary GPU hardware, which is largely optimized for
machine-learning workloads, delivers extremely high throughput through low-precision ten-
sor cores (FP16/BF16/TF32), yet continues to face bottlenecks associated with memory
bandwidth and data movement. Achieving optimal performance on such hardware, therefore,
requires minimizing data movement, maximizing arithmetic intensity, overlapping commu-
nication with computation, and leveraging reduced-precision arithmetic without sacrificing
numerical robustness. Finite-element (FE) discretizations are particularly well-positioned
to meet these demands, as the cell-structured representation of both the Hamiltonian and
the inverse PAW overlap operator enables their action to be reformulated as dense cell-level
matrix–matrix operations, naturally reducing global synchronization and facilitating com-
putation–communication overlap. Central to our approach is an extension of the recently
proposed Residual-based Chebyshev Filtered Subspace Iteration (R-ChFSI) algorithm24 to
solve the PAW-GHEP efficiently on multi-node GPU architectures. Because R-ChFSI is
tolerant to inexact matrix–vector products during Chebyshev filter subspace construction,
it admits the use of reduced-precision arithmetic (FP32/TF32) and low-precision nearest-
neighbour communication (BF16) in the Chebyshev filtering step that is used to construct
the subspace rich in desired eigensubspace. Building on these foundations, the key contri-
butions of this work are: (i) formulation of FE-discretized governing equations for the PAW
method within a collinear spin formalism; (ii) a multi-resolution quadrature scheme for accu-
rate evaluation of atom-centered integrals arising in the FE discretization of PAW matrices
on coarse grids; (iii) an eigensolver strategy based on R-ChFSI that solves the nonlinear FE-
discretized PAW generalized eigenproblem while avoiding expensive inversions of the PAW
overlap operator; (iv) mixed-precision compute (FP32/TF32) and low-precision MPI com-
munication (BF16) in the Chebyshev filtering step, leveraging R-ChFSI’s residual-based cor-
rection to recover double-precision accuracy while reducing time to solution; and (v) a com-
putation–communication overlap strategy within the Chebyshev filtered blocked subspace
construction for the PAW-GHEP. Collectively, these contributions enable PAW-FE to fully
exploit modern exascale computing architectures to advance scalable, efficient electronic-
structure calculations.
We demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves chemical accuracy across periodic
and non-periodic benchmark systems while substantially reducing time-to-solution relative
to plane-wave PAW methods at comparable accuracy. Ground-state calculations involving
approximately 104 electrons can be completed within minutes on modern multi-GPU nodes,

that PAW-FE requires close to 6× fewer compute resources and achieves 5× reduction in
computational cost. Finally, we consider twisted bilayer WTe2 comprising 130,000 electrons
(11,000 atoms) as a representative large-scale system to demonstrate the capability of the
proposed approach for realistic materials applications, which is beyond the reach of current
DFT codes that employ systematically convergent basis sets.

are solved within a periodic unit cell by invoking Bloch’s theorem. The corresponding formu-
lations for fully non-periodic and semi-periodic systems are then obtained as special cases of
the periodic framework through appropriate modifications to the boundary conditions and
Brillouin-zone sampling.

In this section, we propose various algorithmic strategies and implementation innovations
to determine the ground-state DFT solution efficiently using the computational methods
discussed in the previous section on modern GPU architectures. To this end, this section
highlights the synergy between the projector-augmented-wave (PAW) formalism and the in-
herent advantages of the finite element method that leverage extreme levels of parallelism
offered by evolving computing architectures. As discussed previously, the PAW generalised
eigenproblem (PAW-GHEP) H eΨ = S eΨΛ is solved in each self-consistent field (SCF) it-
eration using the Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration approach (ChFSI). In particular,
we resort to the residual-based reformulation of the Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration
approach (R-ChFSI),24 tolerant to approximations in matrix-multivector multiplications as
discussed previously. Recalling from section 3, the FE discretised operators H and S are
M × M sparse matrices and eΨ denotes the M × N multi-vector comprising FE-discretized
pseudo-wavefunctions where ’M’ denotes the total degrees-of-freedom(DoFs) to be solved
and ’N’ denotes the number of wavefunctions.
Furthermore, as illustrated in Algo. (2), the Chebyshev filtered space construction scales
quadratically with system size and requires the action of HbS
−1 on multivectors. Following
this, we perform the Rayleigh-Ritz step, which scales cubically with system size and involves:
(i) projecting the FE-discretised PAW GHEP onto space spanned by the columns of filtered
space ( eΨf) (ii) solving the smaller projected generalized eigenvalue problem (iii) perform a
subspace-rotation step to obtain the S-orthonormal eigenvectors ( eΨ).

4.5 Computational Strategies for Rayleigh Ritz Procedure
The Rayleigh-Ritz (RR) procedure (steps 4-6 in algorithm (2)) starts becoming the compu-
tational bottleneck for large-scale systems comprising more than 10,000 electrons. From our
benchmark calculations, we observe the decreasing cost of the various steps involved in RR
as: (i)RR-GHEP, (ii) RR-SR, (iii) RR-Projection.
RR-Projection:
We outline the computational strategy employed to construct the pro-
jected Hamiltonian (Hp) and projected PAW overlap (Sp) matrices, both of which are com-
puted using the operation X†Y, where Y = HX or Y = SX. To this end, X is partitioned
across MPI tasks t according to the finite-element domain decomposition, yielding local
blocks Xt. Similar in spirit of our previous works,19,23 the processor level Ap

computed locally in each processor after which the global matrix Ap is assembled by sum-
ming these contributions using an MPI AllReduce collective. Furthermore, to reduce the
peak memory usage, only the lower triangle of Ap

formed in blocks of size N × Bv, where Bv refers to the choice of wavefunction blocksize.
Furthermore, the local computation of Ap

communication of Ap

compute–communication overlap.
RR-GHEP:
Towards scalable and efficient evaluation of the eigenvalue prblem HpQ = SpQΛ,
the projected matrices Hp, Sp are stored in a 2D-block-cyclic format. We employ the state-
of-the-art ELPA library46,47 to solve the projected GHEP on GPUs or CPUs, with openMP
parallelisation based on system size. The use of GPU parallel GEMM in ELPA enables efficient
scaling on multi-node GPUs.
After computing the eigenpairs, we perform the subspace
rotation step to recover the eigenvectors in the full finite-element space.
RR-SR:
A mixed precision strategy, as shown in figure (6), is employed in PAW-FE without
compromising on SCF convergence. RR-SR involves the computation of XtQ, where the en-
tries Q are distributed over various MPI-tasks. The key steps in RR-SR involve the extraction
and copying of the entries of Q and the subsequent GEMM operation to get the rotated space.
As shown in figure (6), the two steps are overlapped over blocks of Q, with the extraction and
copying occurring on a communication-stream (shown in orange) and the GEMM operation
in the compute-stream. Additionally, these operations are performed in reduced precision
(FP32) for the off-diagonal entries, reducing the overall cost. This is justified by the fact that
Q approaches the identity matrix as the SCF procedure converges, so the off-diagonal entries
correspond to small mixing corrections whose evaluation in reduced precision has minimal
impact on the resulting eigenspace. Furthermore, the GEMM operation is done in batches of
DoFs to reduce the peak memory requirement.

enhances device utilization and contributes to the observed performance gains on GPUs.
Collectively, these factors enable substantially higher utilization of GPU resources compared
to CPUs, resulting in the observed order-of-magnitude speedups. The variation in speedup
between OLCF Frontier and ALCF Aurora can be attributed to differences in GPU/CPU ar-

results underscore the importance of hardware aware algorithm design in achieving efficient

Perspectives and concluding remarks
In this work, we have presented the key computational methodologies underlying PAW-FE,
a finite-element discretized projector augmented-wave DFT formulation designed for ac-
curate, efficient, and scalable electronic-structure calculations on modern multi-node GPU
architectures. The proposed approach is built around a hardware-aware eigensolver strategy
that leverages approximate inverse of PAW overlap matrix, mixed-precision arithmetic, low-
precision communication, and computation–communication overlap. Relative to established
plane-wave PAW based DFT codes, PAW-FE substantially reduces time-to-solution across a
wide range of system sizes while naturally accommodating generic boundary conditions, and
demonstrates scalability to the 104–105 electron regime otherwise inaccessible to existing
systematically convergent DFT implementations.
A central design principle of PAW-FE is the synergy between the PAW formalism and
high-order finite-element discretization. In particular, the multi-resolution quadrature rules
employed in the finite-element framework enable accurate evaluation of atom-centered PAW
integrals without increasing the number of degrees of freedom, preserving the efficiency
of the discretized representation.
Further, the locality of the FE basis induces a sparse
operator structure requiring only nearest-neighbor communication, a property that maps
favorably onto the distributed memory hierarchies of exascale architectures and naturally
accommodates periodic, semi-periodic, and non-periodic boundary conditions within a single
unified formulation.
The latter capability enables direct, artifact-free modeling of slabs,
surfaces, nanoclusters, and interfaces that would otherwise require artificial periodicity or
large vacuum padding in widely used plane-wave approaches.
To achieve high computational efficiency on modern architectures, the operations within
each SCF iteration are reformulated as high-arithmetic-intensity matrix–matrix multiplica-
tions well-suited to GPU execution. The dominant computational step, the solution of the
PAW generalized Hermitian eigenproblem (PAW-GHEP), is addressed through the Residual-
based Chebyshev Filtered Subspace Iteration (R-ChFSI) algorithm. R-ChFSI, being toler-
ant to approximations in matrix–multivector products arising in the construction of desired
eigensubspace, admits several performance-oriented strategies without compromising the ac-
curacy of the converged solution. Specifically, a computationally inexpensive FE-discretized
PAW overlap matrix inverse is introduced; reduced-precision arithmetic (FP32/TF32) is em-
ployed during Chebyshev-filtered subspace construction; nearest-neighbour data transfers are
performed in BF16 to reduce communication volume by a factor of 4× during filtering; and a
block-wise overlap strategy is developed for orchestrating computation and communication
throughout. Together, these strategies yield a 3× reduction in wall time for Chebyshev-
41


## Page 42


filtered subspace construction and an overall CPU–GPU speedup of 8×– 20× across Intel
and AMD GPU architectures. PAW-FE achieves a 8× reduction in minimum wall time to
solution relative to plane-wave PAW codes for systems of approximately 10,000 electrons,
with greater gains expected for larger system sizes and a speedup of up to 6× relative to
the ONCV-based DFT-FE implementation. Time to solution per SCF iteration of approxi-
mately 2 minutes for systems comprising 130,000 electrons further demonstrates the practical
scalability of PAW-FE to realistic large-scale simulations.
Looking ahead, the computational framework developed here opens several avenues for
further development. A natural next step is the incorporation of kinetic-energy-dependent
meta-GGA functionals such as r2SCAN61 and LAK,62 representing the next rung on Jacob’s
ladder of exchange-correlation approximations.
Incorporating solvent effects within the
present framework is also of immediate interest for catalytic and electrochemical applica-
tions involving nanometer-scale systems. Extending the current collinear spin formalism to
non-collinear magnetism and relativistic effects will be important for accurate modelling of
magnetic materials and heavy elements and will form part of future investigations. Finally,
systematic guidelines for the selection of finite-element discretization parameters p, pel, and
h, together with appropriate quadrature rules, particularly for the JTHv2.051 PAW datasets,
will be provided in forthcoming work to facilitate broader adoption of PAW-FE across diverse
materials systems.

Algorithimic innovation in filtered subspace construction
In algorithm (4), we make use of extensive dense linear algebra operations such as GEMM which
is used to compute matrix-matrix products of the form C = αAX + βC which is efficiently
implemented in mathematical libraries such as Intel MKL for Intel CPU and GPUs, cuBLAS
for Nvidia GPUs and hipBLAS for AMD GPUs. Similarly, the operation GEMMBatched per-
forms a large number of small, independent GEMM operations in a single launch, enabling
high-throughput when matrices share identical shapes but are stored as separate pointers.
While the operation GEMMStridedBatched kernel is even more efficient when matrices are
stored in memory with a fixed stride between consecutive operands, allowing the GPU to
process a batch of small GEMM operations without pointers to address the input and output
matrices.
In addition to these vendor-optimized primitives, we employ several custom kernels that
are specifically designed to exploit the data layouts and sparsity patterns arising from finite-
element discretization. In particular, the stridedBlockAxpy kernel performs a block-wise
axpby operation of the form yi = αsixi + βyi, where xi and yi are contiguous blocks
and si is the DoF-dependent scaling factor. The stridedCopyToBlock kernel performs a
gather-style operation that extracts DoFs from a processor-level block vector into a com-
pact, contiguous block for each cell. This operation is primarily employed during cell-level
extraction, where DoF associated with selected finite-element cells are copied into local
buffers for subsequent evaluations. The copy pattern depends on the processDoFToCellDoF
map (refers to E(e,t)), which encodes the correspondence between cell-level DoFs and their
indices in the processor-level block vector. To assemble contributions computed at the cell-
level back into a processor-level block vector, we use the axpyStridedBlockAtomicAdd ker-
nel, which performs a block-wise scaled addition into strided locations of the destination
vector using the processDoFToCellDoF map. On GPUs, this kernel employs atomic up-
dates, ensuring thread-safe updates when multiple cells contribute to the same DoF. Finally,
the accumulateAddLocallyOwned kernel consolidates contributions associated with DoF de-
fined on shared nodes into the locally owned DoF. Here, shared nodes refer to finite-element
nodes that lie on subdomain interfaces and are therefore replicated across neighbouring MPI
tasks in the domain decomposition. This operation involves nearest-neighbour MPI com-
munication, during which values corresponding to these shared nodes are exchanged and
accumulated to ensure consistency of the global vector across subdomains. By restricting
communication to neighbouring subdomains and operating only on shared-node data, this
kernel minimizes communication volume and latency, which is critical for achieving good
strong-scaling performance on large-scale CPU–GPU systems.
