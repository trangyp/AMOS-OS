AN ANALOGUE OF IRREDUCIBLE CUSPIDAL
REPRESENTATIONS FOR THE GROUP PGL(2) OVER A
TWO-DIMENSIONAL LOCAL FIELD
ALEXANDER BRAVERMAN AND DAVID KAZHDAN
Abstract. Let F be a local non-archimedian field of odd residue charac-
teristic and let G = PGL(2). In this paper we study an analog of irreducible
cuspidal representations of the group G(F) when F is replaced by the field
K = F((t)). The story turns out to be similar to the classical case, but also
with some differences. We present a construction of such representations es-
sentially (up to a small subtlety) starting from a quadratic extension L of K
and a character θ : L∗/K∗→C∗which is not Galois invariant. We also show
that the restriction of the representations we construct to the group P(K)
(here P is a Borel subgroup of PGL(2)) is irreducible. However, contrary to
the classical case it turns out that these restrictions are not isomorphic to
the ”standard” irreducible cuspidal representation of P(K).
In the Appendix we propose a notion of cuspidality for smooth represen-
tations of the group H(K) for an arbitrary split group H.
1. Introduction
1.1. The setup. Let F be a local non-archimedian field of odd residue charac-

denote the algebraic group PGL(2). We denote by P ⊂G the Borel subgroup.1

The following notions play the central role in this paper.
Definition 1.1.
(1) A complex representation π of dimension > 1 of groups
G(O) or G′(O) is special if its restriction to P(O) is irreducible.
(2) A representation Π of the group G(K) is special if Π is not of dimension

The main purpose of this paper is to present a construction of special repre-
sentations of G(K) (we do not know whether this constructions gives rise to all
special representations of G(K) – this is one of the questions we would like to
study in the future). To motivate both the above definition and what we want
to do with it we first review the parallel results in the ”classical” situation –
i.e. when the field K is replaced by F.
1.2. Classical results. All results of this subsection can be found in [4]. In
this subsection only we shall denote by G′ the corresponding group scheme over
OF defined similarly to the above (but we use the uniformizer ϖ of OF instead
of t).
The embedding P(F) ,→G(F) induces embeddings P(O) ,→G(OF ) and
P(OF ) ,→G′(OF ). We say that a representation π of dimension > 1 of one

Theorem 1.2.
(1) Let E/F be a quadratic extension and let θ : E∗/F ∗→

π(E, θ) of G′(OF ).
(2) Any special representation of G(OF ) or of G′(OF ) is isomorphic to some
π(E, θ).
(3) Let π be an irreducible representation of either G(OF ) or G′(OF ) and
let Π(π) denote its compact induction to G(F). Then π is special if
and only if Π(π)) is irreducible; moreover, in this case Π(π) is special.
In particular, if we set Π(E, θ) = Π(π(E, θ)) (for E and θ as above),
then Π(E, θ) is special (equivalently cuspidal and irreducible), and any
special representation of G(F) is of the form Π(E, θ).
(4) Two representations Π(E1, θ1) and Π(E2, θ2) are isomorphic if and only
if E1 = E2 and either θ1 = θ2 or θ1 = θ2.

1.4. Special and elliptic representations of G(O) and G′(O). The notion
of special representation of G(O) and G′(O) is rather inexplicit (for example,
it is not a priori clear that such representations exist). Our first result is that
the property of being special is equivalent to a much more explicit property of
being elliptic. 2
Let us explain this notion for G(O). Let G(O)(m) denote the m-th congru-
ence subgroup in G(O) (here m ≥0 and let G(O)m = G(O)/G(O)(m+1). Note
that if m > 0 then G(O)m contains a commutative subgroup G(O)m(m) iso-

For any irreducuble representation π of G(O) factorizes through some G(O)m.
If m⟩0 we denote by ω(π) ⊂g(F) the support of the restriction of π onto
G(O)m(m).
Let π be an irreducible representation of G(O) and m be minimal such that
representation π of G(O) factorizes through G(O)m.
Definition 1.3. A representation π is elliptic if
(1) m = 0 and π is a cuspidal representation of G(F) = G(O)1 or
(2) m > 0 and ω(π) ⊂g(F) is an elliptic conjugacy class.
The notion of ellipticity for representations of the group G′(O) is defined
similarly but we shall not give a precise definition in the Introduction (cf. Def-
inition 6.2(5)).
As was mentioned above, we show that an irreducible representation π of
G(O) or G′(O) is special if and only if it is elliptic.

1.5. Special representations of the group G(K). Our ultimate goal is to
construct some irreducible representation of G(K). The analog of compact in-
duction from G(O) or G′(O) (in fact, from any ”bounded” subgroup of G(K),
where ”bounded” can be thought of as an analog of ”compact” for G(F)) was
constructed in [7]. For a representation π of either G(O) or G′(O) we denote
by Π(π) the corresponding ”compact induction” to G(K). The notion of cus-
pidality for representations of G(K) can be defined in a way similar to the
case of G(F) (although some care is needed there as we work with pro-vector
spaces; we shall not discuss the details in the Introduction). Then we prove the
following
Theorem 1.5. If π is a special representation of either G(O) or G′(O) then
the representation Π(π) of G(K) is cuspidal and irreducible.
Moreover the
restriction of Π(π) to P(K) is irreducible.
Thus if we combine Theorem 1.4 with Theorem 1.5 we can essentially attach
to any character θ of L∗/K∗such that θ ̸= θ an irreducible representation
Π(L, θ) of G(K) whose restriction to P(K) is irreducible (the word ”essentially”
is necessary here because of the subtlety in the 2nd assertion of Theorem 1.4).
These represetations are isomorphic when we replace θ by θ but we do not know
whether other isomorphisms exist between them.
1.6. Remarks. Several remarks are in order here. First, if we go back to G(F)
instead of G(K), then not only the restriction of a cuspidal irreducible repre-
sentation of G(F) to P(F) is irreducible, but in addition all those irreducible
representations of P(F) are isomorphic. In fact, P(F) has unique irreducible
representation which is not of dimension 1 (and this representation is cuspidal).
The latter statement is wrong when we replace F by K. Namely, on the one
hand, we can explicitly construct certain irreducible cuspidal representation Υ
of P(K) which is a direct analog of the above (unique) irreducible cuspidal rep-
resentation of P(F) (the construction is essentially a word-by-word repetition
of a similar construction when K is replaced by F). On the other hand, we
show that Υ cannot be extended to G(K). We expect that the first statement

representations of this central extension of certain level which is a character of
the central F ∗. Usually, this character is taken to be unramified and thus it

representations of G(K) behaves quite differently depending on the level. How-
ever, the choice of level doesn’t seem to be important when we study cuspidal
representation of G(K). That is why in this paper we just restrict ourselves to
level zero (i.e. we ignore the central extension).

13. Questions
Here we present a list of questions to which we don’t know an answer and
possible directions of future research.
(1) Let π, π′ be two non-isomorphic special representations of either G(O) of
G′(O) (i.e. either these are two representations of two different groups or
two non-isomorphic representations of the same group). Can it happen
that Π(π) and Π(π′) are isomorphic?
(2) Is it true that any special representation of G(K) is isomorphic to Π(π)
where π is a special representation of either G(O) or G′(O)?
(3) Let π and π′ be two special representations of either G(O) or G′(O).
Is this true that Θ(π) is isomorphic to Θ(π′) if and only if m(π) =
m(π′)?
(Note that since the depth of any special representation of
G(O) is an integer, and the depth of any special representation of G′(O)
is not an integer, the condition m(π) = m(π′) implies that these are
representations of the same group). 5

0 < dim(W(W)) < ∞? (see Definition 9.9).
