Abstract
The Hanoi Omega-Automata (HOA) format has established itself as the definitive standard for
encoding ω-regular automata in modern synthesis tools. While HOA is widely adopted due to its
succinct symbolic representation, using Boolean formulas as transition guards and transition-based
coloring, the exact computational cost of these features has remained understudied. This paper
provides the first systematic investigation into the theoretical complexity of decision problems for
HOA-encoded automata and games. We establish that the structural features of HOA, specifically
the symbolic encoding of large alphabets, make classical problems more complex than in traditional
formats. We prove that the non-emptiness problem is NP-complete for all standard acceptance
conditions, with hardness arising directly from the Boolean transition guards. For language inclusion,
we show the problem is PSPACE-complete for most conditions but reaches EXPSPACE-completeness
for Emerson-Lei acceptance. Furthermore, we formalize Hanoi Omega-Games (HOG), where the
underlying arena is a deterministic HOA with atomic propositions partitioned into inputs and
outputs. We provide tight complexity bounds for solving HOGs, ranging from Π2-completeness
for parity and safety conditions to PSPACE-completeness for Muller and Emerson-Lei objectives.
Finally, we generalize our techniques to symbolic games where transitions are guarded by formulas
in arbitrary decidable first-order theories.

Introduction
The Hanoi Omega-Automata (HOA) format has established itself as the definitive standard
for encoding ω-regular automata. Its widespread adoption is evident in its integration into
cornerstone tools like Spot, Owl, and LTL3TELA [11, 20, 22]. We note that the foundational
paper [2] introducing the format has already earned more than 150 citations.
Automata encoded using HOA are a critical component of the temporal synthesis pipeline.
Synthesizing controllers from Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) specifications typically requires
compiling formulas into automata and then solving a two-player zero-sum game. In this
process, the HOA-encoded automaton is what ultimately determines the winner. Modern
research continues to refine the automata-theoretic pipeline to achieve real-world scalability.
Although the worst-case complexity of temporal synthesis is known to be 2ExpTime-complete
[4], the annual SYNTCOMP competition [19] highlights an active, ongoing effort to optimize
these tools and benchmarks for practical applications.

A significant gap exists in our understanding of the theoretical complexity of analyzing
HOA-encoded automata. Despite being a standard input format for SYNTCOMP, the exact
complexity of automata- and game-theoretic questions specifically for HOA-encoded automata
has not been formally studied. Personal communications with Alexandre Duret-Lutz (author
of the Spot library [11]) confirm that while these problems are routinely solved—often using
binary decision diagrams [7]—their precise complexity bounds remain open.
What is different about HOA-encoded automata?
HOA introduces unique structural
features that differ from classical set-based definitions of automata. Notably, it utilizes
transition-based acceptance rather than state-based acceptance (for example, priority-labeled
transitions, instead of states, for parity acceptance conditions). Furthermore, it employs a
symbolic alphabet encoded succinctly by labeling transitions with Boolean formulas over
atomic propositions. This encoding allows a single transition to represent all (out of a
possibly exponential set, in the number of atomic propositions) transitions on a valuation
that satisfies the formula. We note that this makes the model similar to symbolic automata
over the Boolean domain [10]. In fact, HOA-encoded automata are a concrete instantiation
of symbolic automata over infinite words with propositional logic as a fixed Boolean algebra.
These subtle structural differences have a profound impact on the computational com-
plexity of classical automata-theoretic problems. Recent work suggests that moving from
state-based to transition-based acceptance alone can change the complexity of certain ques-
tions (see [8] and references therein), and it is intuitive that the succinct encoding of large
alphabets via formulas would have similar effects.
What about Hanoi Omega-Games?
Since the 2020 edition of SYNTCOMP, a synthesis
track starting from HOA—extended with a partitioning of the the atomic propositions into
inputs and outputs—was added [19]. At the time, most synthesis solvers for the temporal-
logic specification track were translating input specifications into HOA-encoded automata
and then solving a game played on it. The new track on HOA games (henceforth shortened
as HOGs) was intended to encourage the development of tools that focus on the latter part of
the synthesis pipeline. One of the only alternative formats for games at the time was the one
used by the PGSolver collection of parity-game generators and solvers [12]. The PGSolver
format encodes a (parity) game by explicitly enumerating edges of the bipartite graph on
which the game is played. Naively translating a HOG into such an explicit representation
incurs an exponential blow-up.
While HOG-solving is practically possible using Knor [28] or ltlsynt [11], there has been
no systematic study of the complexity of solving HOGs. Moreover, while we can place HOA
within the framework of symbolic automata, we are not aware of a general framework where
HOGs fit, i.e. some sort of symbolic game framework. The closest match may be [16, 1] (and
models referenced therein). However, the referenced models all have variable valuations that
affect the state of the system while HOGs have a much more local parametric structure:
valuations are chosen to select one of finitely many successors.

Contributions.
In this work, we confirm that HOA-encoded ω-automata present harder
decision problems. We establish that classical problems such as emptiness and language
inclusion are more complex in this format. Our most important contributions, though,
concern HOGs: We provide tight complexity bounds for solving important classes of HOGs,
directly addressing the setup used in the SYNTCOMP competition. Finally, we generalize
our techniques to symbolic games (over possibly infinite data domains) as hinted at before,
e.g., to games where the controller and the environment choose values of natural vectors.

Preliminaries
We write N = {1, 2, . . .} for the set of natural numbers and B = {True, False} for Boolean
truth values. For every d ∈N, we use [d] to denote the set {1, 2, . . . , d}.
Words, Alphabets.
A word (resp. ω-word) over a (finite) alphabet is a finite (resp. infinite)
sequence of symbols from the alphabet. The set of finite (resp. ω-word) over an alphabet
Σ is denoted by Σ∗(resp. Σω). The length of a word w is denoted by |w| (it is infinite for

Polynomial Hierarchy.
We recall the definition of the polynomial hierarchy. Let Σ0 = P
be the class of languages decidable in deterministic polynomial time. For every i > 0, let
Σi = NPΣi−1, that is, the class of languages decidable by a non-deterministic polynomial-
time Turing machine equipped with an oracle for some language in Σi−1. In particular,
Σ1 = NP. Similarly, let Πi = coNPΣi−1. By standard closure properties of the hierarchy,
for every i ≥0 we have Σi = coΠi and Πi = coΣi. All these classes lie within PSPACE.

Classical Two-player Games.
A classical (turn-based) two-player game is a tuple G =
(V, Vin, Vout, E, C, C, Acc), where V is a set of vertices partitioned into Vin and Vout, the
positions of Player In and Player Out, respectively. The relation E ⊆V × V is the edge
relation. The set C is a set of colors, and C : V →C is a coloring function that assigns a
color to each vertex. The winning condition Acc ⊆Cω is defined over infinite sequences
of colors. A play in G is an infinite sequence of vertices π = v0v1v2 · · · ∈V ω such that
(vi, vi+1) ∈E for all i ≥0. If vi ∈Vin (resp. Vout), then Player In (resp. Player Out) chooses
the successor vi+1. The play induces a sequence of colors C(π) = C(v0)C(v1)C(v2) · · · ∈Cω.
Player Out wins the play if C(π) ∈Acc, otherwise Player In wins. The arena of G is the tuple
(V, Vin, Vout, E, C, C).
A strategy for Player Out is a function σ : V ∗Vout →V such that (v, σ(hv)) ∈E for
all histories hv ∈V ∗Vout. Strategies for Player In are defined analogously. A strategy for
Player Out is winning from a vertex if, no matter how Player In plays, every resulting play
satisfies the winning condition Acc. Similarly, a strategy for Player In is winning from a
vertex if every resulting play violates the winning condition Acc. The winning region of
Player Out, denoted by Wout, is the set of vertices from which Player Out has a strategy to
ensure a win. The winning region of Player In is Win = V \ Wout.

State-Based Hanoi Omega-Automaton.
An HOA A = (Q, I, P, ∆, C) is said to have a
state-based coloring function if all transitions that leave the same state are assigned the same
color, i.e., for any state q ∈Q, if (q, φ, p), (q, φ′, p′) ∈∆for some states p, p′ ∈Q and Boolean

now denotes the sequence of colors that label the states along the run. A state-based HOA is
an HOA equipped with such a state-based coloring function.

Language Inclusion of Hanoi Omega-Automaton
Given two HOAs A and B equipped with acceptance conditions, and over the same set of
atomic propositions P, the language inclusion problem asks whether every infinite word
accepted by A is also accepted by B. We show that this problem is PSPACE-complete for
all acceptance conditions discussed in Section 3, except for Emerson-Lei, where the problem
becomes EXPSPACE-complete. We start with acceptance conditions up to Streett.
▶Theorem 7. If A and B are Hanoi omega-automata with Streett, parity, Buchi, co-
Buchi, reachability or safety accepting conditions, then determining whether L(A) ⊆L(B) is
PSPACE-complete.
Proof. The PSPACE-hardness follows directly from the known PSPACE-hardness of
language inclusion for finite automata [27]. For the upper bound, consider two HOAs A and
B equipped with Streett acceptance. By virtue of Lemma 1, we can assume that A and B
are state-based HOAs. We can translate each of them into an equivalent classical Streett
automaton A′ and B′ over the alphabet Val(P), by replacing every transition labeled with a
Boolean formula φ by all transitions labeled with valuations satisfying φ. This construction
preserves the set of states unchanged but may introduce an exponential number of transitions.
Hence, explicitly constructing A′ and B′ would in general require exponential time and space,
which we avoid by simulating them on the fly.

cycling through all colors to satisfy the Muller condition.

space. So the space requirements are bounded above by O(|V |2), and hence computing
winner regions in Muller games is in PSPACE.
The above algorithm is extended to Emerson-Lei games in [18, 17], where the winning
condition F is given by an Emerson-Lei Boolean formula over colors. The only modification
is in the global check: instead of testing whether S ∈F, one checks whether S satisfies the
Emerson-Lei formula. This check is linear in the size of the formula and is performed at
most once per recursion level, yielding a polynomial space procedure overall.

Symbolic Games
HOA can be seen as a subclass of the more general class of symbolic automata [10], which
extend finite automata to infinite data domains D. In particular, the transitions of a symbolic
automaton are guarded by formulas from a decidable first-order theory over D, e.g., the
theory of natural numbers with addition (aka Presburger arithmetic). The free variables
in the guards on transitions form a (common) tuple of n variables, so that the language
recognized by such an automaton is a subset of words over alphabet Dn. It is easily seen that

HOA automata are a particular case of symbolic automata over the Boolean data domain.
Likewise, HOG can be generalized to symbolic games, whose underlying arena is given by a
deterministic symbolic automaton.
As a matter of fact, the algorithms for solving HOGs, presented in Section 5, can be used
to solve symbolic games, modulo calling some oracle for checking satisfiability of formulas
of the form ∃∗∀∗φ, for φ formulas occurring on transitions. In particular, the reduction to
solving a two-player (classical) game PG, proved in Proposition 15, carries over to symbolic
games, so that symbolic games are decidable (for ω-regular winning conditions) as soon as
this reduction is computable (see Theorem 21 below). For Streett conditions (and therefore
parity, safety, reachability, (co)Büchi conditions), a more precise complexity analysis yields
the upper-bound coNPC for solving symbolic games where the satisfiability problem of
formulas of the form ∃∗∀∗φ is in some complexity class C (see Lemma 22). For Rabin
objectives, the ideas for HOG developed in Lemma 18 still apply, allowing us to derive the
upper bound NPC (see Lemma 23). We now define symbolic automata and games and give
the main arguments to prove the aforementioned results.
First-order formulas
A signature σ = (R, F, C) consists of countable sets of relation,
function, and constant symbols respectively. Let X be a countable set of variables. The set
of terms over σ is defined inductively: every x ∈X and c ∈C is a term, and if f ∈F is

φ ::= r(t1, . . . , tn) | t1 = t2 | ¬φ | (φ ∧φ) | (φ ∨φ) | ∃x φ | ∀x φ,
