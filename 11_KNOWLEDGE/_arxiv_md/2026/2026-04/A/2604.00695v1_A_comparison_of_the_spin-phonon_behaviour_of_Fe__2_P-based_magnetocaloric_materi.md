Abstract
Magnetic refrigeration can provide an environmentally friendly technology to reduce significantly
the energy consumption of cooling devices without using harmful gases. To retain the sustainability
of the device, all parts must be made from abundant materials, excluding e.g. rare earth elements.
As such, materials based on Fe2P have shown great potential for magnetocaloric devices, partic-
ularly enhanced by the pliant tunability arising through chemical substitutions. In this study,
two magnetocaloric compounds, Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45, have been studied using magnetom-
etry, neutron diffraction, theoretical modelling and inelastic neutron scattering with the aim to
understand the ferromagnetic transition, closely related to the magnetocaloric effect. Analysis of
the diffraction data of Fe2P showed that it is the Fe3g-site that drives the magnetic transition as
the Fe3f does not have any magnetic contribution at the magnetic transition temperature. For
FeMnP0.55Si0.45, the magnetic transition is more gradual with coexistence of the para- and ferro-
magnetic phases close to the magnetic transition. In the ferromagnetic phase of FeMnP0.55Si0.45,
magnetic moments are observed for both sites. The temperature dependent magnetic structure
behaviour found from neutron diffraction are well in agreement with our first principles calculations.
Both Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 showed two distinct regions, at different length scales, in their
S(Q,ω) spectra.
The two length scales can be modelled using a different set of magnetic spin
states (S), using SFe = 2 and SMn = 2.5, consistent with the ground state of the magnetic atoms
in (Fe,Mn)2(P,Si)-compounds. QENS at low Q (Q < 0.5 ˚A) shows similar magnetic processes in
both compounds with uncorrelated magnetism below the magnetic transition temperature. The
occurrence of the uncorrelated state in both Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 highlights that the magnetic
anisotropy does not play a major role in the formation of this state. Furthermore, this emphasises
the existence of a two part system in FeMn(P,Si)-based compounds, that drives the magnetic
transition and in turn the magnetocaloric effect. This means that short range magnetic clusters
exist above and below the magnetic ordering temperature of the compound, strongly influencing
the magnetic behaviour.
Keywords: Magnetocaloric materials, Magnetism, Inelastic neutron scattering, Neutron diffraction

I.
INTRODUCTION
Magnetic refrigeration has been envisaged as an effective cooling phenomenon since the
discovery of the giant magnetocaloric effect in Gd5Si2Ge2 [1]. Several materials have been
investigated with respect to their refrigeration properties [2–4], and prototypes with the most
prominent class of materials have been made [5] often involving rare earth metals which,
in addition to being scarce, pose significant environmental and health risks. These risks
include toxic waste generation, potential for radioactive contamination, and harmful effects
on human health. As such, it is crucial that magnetocaloric devices are developed using
resources that are plentiful and not harmful [6]. Therefore, only compounds with suitable
elements from an environmental and sustainability point of view should be considered. A
class of material that meets these sustainability criteria is Fe2-xMnxP1-ySiy with Fe2P as the
basic compound with a ferromagnetic transition temperature at 220 K, that can be tuned
to a room temperature transition temperature, therefore creating a more energy efficient
material, through the introduction of Mn and Si.
Fe2P orders in a hexagonal structure (P¯62m) with two crystallographic atomic positions
for Fe (Wyckoff 3f and 3g) and two for P (Wyckoff 1b and 2c), respectively [7], visualised in
Figure 1 a) where Fe is light and dark brown and P is pink/dark purple. Upon cooling below
220 K, Fe2P undergoes a first-order ferromagnetic transition [8] where the magnetic moments
on the Fe sites align along the crystallographic c-axis [9]. Given that the Curie temperature
(TC) for Fe2P is significantly below 300 K, the usefulness in a room temperature application
is limited.
However, atomic substitutions can easily be made in Fe2P and improve the
relevant properties. Upon substitutions to Fe2-xMnxP1-ySiy, the unit cell parameters are
affected varying the strength of the magnetic moments and, importantly, enabling TC to be
tuned [10–14]. In particular, TC can be tuned from 220 K in Fe2P to 400 K in FeMnP0.5Si0.5
and the ferromagnetic spin configuration in FeMnP0.5Si0.5 changes to the ab-plane [10].
In the substituted compounds, strong structural preference is observed for the arrange-
ment of Fe and Mn, with Mn preferring to occupy the pyramidal 3g site, leaving Fe in the
tetrahedral 3f site, referred to as Fe(Mn)3g and Fe3f, respectively [10, 12]. P and Si occupy
the 1b and 2c sites with no experimental evidence for preferential occupation. The arrange-
ment is shown in Figure 1 b), which highlights the tetrahedral arrangement for Fe and the
pyramidal surrounding from Mn.

A magnetic phase transition will induce a large entropic change and this is particularly
pronounced for a paramagnetic to ferromagnetic transition at TC. The entropy (S) of a
material is strongly affected by changes in internal degrees of freedom that include phonon
modes, spin waves and phonon-electron interactions. As such, understanding the interplay
between spin, electron, and lattice degrees of freedom is essential to optimise magnetocaloric
devices.
Intrinsic spin-phonon coupling has previously been studied for magnetocaloric materials
Mn5Si3, MnFe4Si3, Fe2P and LaFe13-xSix using inelastic neutron scattering (INS) [15–18].
Common findings from these studies suggest that anisotropy plays a big role in the the
magnetocaloric response regardless of the nature of the magnetic ordering. Our previous
work on Fe2P revealed that in addition to long range magnetic correlations, and concomitant
spin waves, a weakly antiferromagnetic correlated nanoscale clustered state exists below TC
that develops into ferromagnetic correlated clusters above TC. These clustered states appear
to drive, most unusually, a gapped phonon state that may enhance electron mobility which
in turn will enhance the magnetocaloric effect [17]. In addition, the long range magnetic
spin structure shows a canting at TC.
The previous INS studies all suggest that more
research in this area are needed to understand the complex interactions responsible for the
magnetocaloric effect.
In this study, we aim to explore the magnetic transition in materials used in magnetic
refrigeration to gain insight into the magnetocaloric behaviour. This is performed by compar-
ing the properties of Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 using mainly neutron diffraction and inelastic

of the spatial and energy scales of the magnetic spin correlations and the magnon-phonon
properties close to the magnetic transitions. We study the effect of substitution on the
ferromagnetic transition and relevant magnetic exchange interactions that drives the mag-
netocaloric effect.
II.
METHODS
A.
Synthesis and sample quality
Powder samples were produced using the drop synthesis method, where the volatile ele-
ments (P, Mn) are dropped into a melt of the non-volatile elements (Fe, Si) [19]. All elements
used had purities >99.99%. The samples were heat treated in evacuated silica ampoules at
1273 K for 7-14 days, yielding polycrystalline samples. Crystalline quality was determined
using X-ray diffraction and reveal high purity samples with no detected secondary phases.
B.
Magnetometry
Magnetic characterisation of the samples was carried out using a Quantum Design MPMS
SQUID magnetometer and a Quantum Design MPMS3 SQUID magnetometer. The tem-
perature dependence of the magnetisation has been probed using an applied magnetic field
of H = 4 kA/m (µ0H = 5 mT). The field dependence was studied in the field range of
±4000 kA/m (µ0H = ±5 T) at select temperatures.
C.
Neutron diffraction
Neutron powder diffraction (NPD) experiments were carried out at the instruments Po-
laris [20, 21] at ISIS Neutron and Muon Source (Didcot, UK) and Nomad [22] at the Spalla-
tion Neutron Source (Oak Ridge, USA). The experiments were carried out at a temperature
range from 150 to 450 K (T/TC range of 0.4 to 1.2). The received diffraction patterns were
analysed using the Rietveld method [23] implemented in the software FullProf [24]. All
possible magnetic space groups were evaluated with k-SUBGROUPSMAG [25], and tested
to the diffraction data below TC.

D.
Inelastic neutron scattering
Inelastic neutron scattering studies (INS) were performed using the cold chopper spec-
trometers at Institute Laue Langevin and Heinz Maier-Leibnitz Zentrum (MLZ), IN5 [26, 27]
and TOFTOF [28]. The double partial differential scattering cross section, S(Q, ω) was mea-
sured for Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 across a temperature range 0.4 ≤T/TC ≤1.2, using
neutron wavelengths of 5 ˚A (TOFTOF) and 4.8 ˚A (IN5), which both have instrumental
energy resolutions of ∼90 µeV determined from the incoherent scattering of a vanadium
reference. The low Q region (0.3 to 0.5 ˚A−1) of the measured INS spectra was fitted to the
incoherent scattering function

The chemical disorder for P and Si was treated within the coherent potential approxima-

within the soft-core and scalar-relativistic approximations, with lmax = 3 for partial waves
and lt

points distributed exponentially on a semi-circular contour including states within 1 Ry

III.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A.
Magnetometry
Magnetisation as a function of temperature for Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 are presented
if Figure 3 a) and b). As can be observed Fe2P exhibits a magnetic phase transition at
∼220 K, while FeMnP0.55Si0.45 exhibits a transition at ∼370 K, which is in good agreement
with previous studies [8, 13].
The field dependence of the magnetisation for Fe2P and
FeMnP0.55Si0.45 are presented in Fig. 3 c) and d). For Fe2P it can be seen that the sample
has an S shaped curve at the lowest temperatures in good agreement with previous studies
which found that Fe2P is ferromagnetic at these temperatures. However, it is clear that the
sample does not fully saturate at the lowest temperatures even at 4000 kA/m (µ0H = 5 T).
This is likely an effect of the strong magnetic anisotropy (2.32×106 J/m3) in Fe2P [43], which
prevents the magnetic moments of the individual crystallites in the powder sample from fully
aligning with the field [44]. At 300 K and 400 K, the curve appears to be linear indicating
that the sample is in its paramagnetic phase. For FeMnP0.55Si0.45 the curve is more square
like and exhibits a clear plateau at larger field suggesting a smaller magnetic anisotropy
than in Fe2P. From the field dependent data it is also evident that FeMnP0.55Si0.45 has
a significantly larger average magnetic moment as compared to Fe2P, e.g. at 4000 kA/m
(µ0H = 5 T) and T/TC∼0.4 (150 K and 100 K respectively) the average moment is 2.25
µB/magnetic atom for FeMnP0.55Si0.45 and 1.25 µB/magnetic atom for Fe2P.
B.
Nuclear and magnetic structures
The magnetic phase transitions, magnetic moment size and corresponding structural
variations across the paramagnetic (PM) to ferromagnetic (FM) phase has been probed
by neutron diffraction. Figure 4 shows the neutron powder diffraction patterns, integrated
over all energies, for Fe2P (a) and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 (b) in the paramagnetic state at 305
and 470 K, T/TC ≈1.3, respectively. The ferromagnetic state was examined for Fe2P and
FeMnP0.55Si0.45 at 200 (T/TC ≈0.9) and 150 K (T/TC ≈0.4), respectively.

2.
Magnetic state
At 200 K (T/TC = 0.9) Fe2P the neutron diffraction patterns can be described using
a magnetic structure along the crystallographic c-direction (magnetic space group (MSG)
P¯62′m′), in agreement with previous findings [9]. However, the total magnetisation at this
temperature is rather low, 2.0(1) µB/f.u., in comparison to the theoretical value of 3.1 µB/f.u.
(0 K) [46].
The majority of the magnetic moment comes from the Fe3g site, refined to
1.6(1) µB in agreement with previous findings at liquid nitrogen temperatures [47]. The
refined values for both sites are listed in Table II. As the sample is heated and approaches
TC, the peculiar canting of the Fe moments towards the ab-plane [17] is again observed here.
This can be modelled using two different magnetic space groups, either P31m′ or P32′1,
meaning that the symmetry is lowered from the parent space group, P¯62m. Common to
both space groups is a large magnetic contribution along the crystallographic c-axis and
a smaller antiferromagnetic component in the ab-plane. However, the current data cannot
distinguish between the two magnetic models. The different magnetic structures of Fe2P are
shown in Figure 5. In the refinements of the magnetic moments above 200 K, the moment
of Fe3f was unstable and yielded moments close to 0 with large errors, and was therefore
omitted from the refinements. However, theoretical calculations [46, 48] suggests that the
magnetic moment of Fe3f disappears during the heating as the transition is driven by the
moment of Fe3g. The results from our NPD study supports the predictions from theory and
clearly shows that Fe3g-site drives the magnetisation in Fe2P.

C.
Inelastic neutron scattering
Figure 7 shows S(Q,ω) for Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 at two temperatures below TC and
one above. For Fe2P clear correlated excitations are observed at Q∼1.2 ˚A−1, Q∼1.8 ˚A−1
and Q∼2.15 ˚A−1 corresponding to the spin-spin and phononic dynamic correlations from
the (1 0 0), (0 0 1) and (1 1 1) reflections, respectively. In addition, a large broad feature
at low Q (Q < 0.5 ˚A), and extending high in energy transfer can be observed up to 1 meV,
which exists for T < TC and grows significantly in intensity upon approaching - and beyond
- TC. This feature is similar to the observed scattering behaviour in the magnetocaloric
compound LaFe11.8Si1.2, and was ascribed to spin waves [50].
Since the magnetic moments in Fe2P are aligned along the crystallographic c-axis, and
the neutron magnetic scattering cross section is sensitive to moments perpendicular to Q
and therefore any scattering observed at (0 0 1) cannot be magnetic. As such the observed
excitation at Q∼1.8 ˚A−1 (0 0 1) is purely phononic [51]. The excitations at Q∼1.2 ˚A−1
(1 0 0) and Q∼2.15 ˚A−1 (1 1 1) contain both phonon and magnon contributions.
In the case of FeMnP0.55Si0.45 excitations can be observed at Q∼1.8 ˚A−1 and Q∼2.15 ˚A−1
and are expected to contain both phonon and magnon contributions, due to the alignment of
the magnetic moment along the crystallographic ab-axis. Similar to Fe2P, a purely phononic

IV.
CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we have investigated the magnetic phase transitions in the compounds
Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 by using magnetometry, neutron diffraction, theoretical modelling
and inelastic neutron scattering. Close to TC in Fe2P, a canted magnetic structure with
magnetic moments solely on the Fe3g-site is observed using neutron diffraction. This clearly
shows that it is the Fe3g-site that drives the magnetisation in Fe2P, in agreement with
previous theoretical calculations. In contrast, FeMnP0.55Si0.45 show a more gradual magnetic
transition with magnetic moments on both sites. However, QENS data at low Q highlights
the similar behaviour in the magnetic process in Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 with the existence
of uncorrelated magnetism in both compounds below TC, independent of the difference
in anisotropy in the compounds. This shows that anisotropy is not the most important
parameter for the magnetisation process in Fe2P-based materials.
The inelastic neutron scattering show temperature dependent features with two distinct
length scales for both compounds. In the long range regime, the magnetic structure of both
Fe2P and FeMnP0.55Si0.45 revealed complex magnetic interactions. To fully simulate the
S(Q,ω) spectra, magnetic exchange interaction parameters up to the sixth nearest neighbour
(J1 to J6) needs to be considered.
The Ji,j’s used in the linear spin wave simulations
were directly incorporated from first principles calculations, giving good agreement with the
experimental S(Q,ω) spectra only needing to evaluate the spin states (SFe and SMn).
The low Q scattering could only be explained using a subset of the exchange parameters
(J1 and J2), highlighting the existence of a two part system. It is the two part system
that drives the magnetic transition and in turn also the magnetocaloric effect in FeMn(P,Si)
compounds. This emphasises that magnetic anisotropy is less important then believed pre-
viously. The existence of temperature dependant short range correlations - more distinct
at TC than at low temperatures - show that magnetic clusters exist upon magnetisation of
