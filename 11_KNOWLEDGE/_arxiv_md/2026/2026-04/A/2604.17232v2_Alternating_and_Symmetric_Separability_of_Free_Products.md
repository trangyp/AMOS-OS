DONGXIAO ZHAO AND QIANG ZHANG
ABSTRACT. Let F ∗G be a free product of a free group F and a LERF group G. In this note, we
provide sufficient conditions for a subgroup H of F ∗G to be A ∪S-separable, that is, for any finite
set {γ1, . . . , γn} ⊂(F ∗G) \ H, there is a surjection f from F ∗G to an alternating or symmetric
group such that f(γi) /∈f(H) for all i. As a corollary, any finitely generated infinite-index subgroup
of a free group is A ∪S-separable in the free product of the free group and an arbitrary LERF group,
generalizing a result of Wilton.
1. INTRODUCTION
Residual properties have been widely discussed on the topic of infinite discrete groups. Let C
be a class of groups. A group G is said to be residually C if for every g ∈G \ {e}, there exists a
homomorphism f : G →C onto some group C ∈C, such that f(g) is nontrivial. The property of
residual finiteness admits several generalizations, most notably the concept of LERF: a group G is
said to be locally extended residually finite (LERF) if for any finitely generated subgroup H ≤G
and any γ ∈G \ H, there is a homomorphism f from G onto a finite group such that f(γ) /∈f(H).
The LERFness of groups is a property closely related to low dimensional topology and has been
widely studied. Large classes of groups are known to be LERF: for example, finitely generated
free groups [6], closed orientable hyperbolic surface groups [13], compact Seifert fiber space groups
[13], Sol manifold groups [8] and hyperbolic 3-manifold groups [1] [19]. In fact, by [15], a compact
(with empty or tori boundary) orientable irreducible 3-manifold M with π1(M) LERF if and only
if M supports one of Thurston’s eight geometries. Moreover, by [17], limit groups are LERF, which
generalizes both the cases of free groups and surface groups. However, nontrivial graph manifold
groups are not LERF [11], noncompact arithmetic hyperbolic m-manifold groups with m > 3 and
compact arithmetic hyperbolic m-manifold groups with m > 4 (except when defined by octonions
with m = 7) are not LERF [15].
The LERFness admits a broader generalization through the restriction of its finite quotients to a
more specific class, for example, the class of alternating groups A and symmetric groups S.
Definition 1.1. Let H be a subgroup of a finitely generated group G and let C be a class of groups.
We say that H is C-separable in G if for any finite set {γ1, . . . , γn} ⊂G \ H, there is a surjective
homomorphism f from G to a group in C such that f(γi) /∈f(H) for all i.
Note that C-separability is a property of a certain subgroup, in particular, the trivial subgroup of
G is C-separable implies that G is residually C.
Notations. Throughout this paper, let Fr be a free group of rank r > 1 and let Hg = gHg−1 be
the conjugation of a subgroup H by g. For a set V , let |V | denote the cardinality of V .

In [18], Wilton showed that the finitely generated infinite-index subgroups of free groups are
A-separable. More precisely,
Theorem 1.2. [18, Theorem A] Let H be a finitely generated subgroup of infinite index in the free
group Fr and let {γ1, . . . , γn} be any finite set of F \ H. Then there is a surjection f from Fr to
some finite alternating group Ak such that f(γi) /∈f(H) for all i.
Wilton also conjectured that the same result of Theorem 1.2 holds for closed orientable hyperbolic
surface groups. In 2021, Buran [2] confirmed Wilton’s conjecture. Moreover, he generalized this
result to right-angled Coxeter groups and provided a complete characterization of the A-separability
and the S-separability of convex-cocompact subgroups.
We now investigate the residual properties and the C-separability of free products of groups. It
is well known that both of the residually finiteness and LERFness are preserved under taking free
products, see [12] and [3]. In 1984, Tamburini and Wilson [16] studied the residually alternating
property of the free products of residually finite groups.
Theorem 1.3. [16, Theorem 1] Let G be a free product of at least two nontrivial residually finite
groups. Then G is residually alternating unless G is the infinite dihedral group Z/2Z ∗Z/2Z.
Note that LERF groups are residually finite, and the trivial subgroup of G is A-separable implies
that G is residually alternating. It is natural to ask the following question.
Question 1.4. Which kinds of subgroups are A-separable or S-separable in free products of LERF
groups?
By combining the methods in [5, 18], we show that there exists a class of A ∪S-separable
subgroups in the free product of a LERF group with a free group Fr of rank r > 1.
Theorem 1.5. Let G be a LERF group and let H ≤Fr ∗G be a finitely generated subgroup. Then
H is A ∪S-separable in Fr ∗G if one of the following holds:
(1) H ∩F γ

Since every finitely generated infinite-index subgroup of Fr satisfies the hypothesis in Theorem
1.5, the following immediate corollary represents a generalization of Theorem 1.2 regarding the
A-separability of free groups.
Corollary 1.6. Any finitely generated infinite-index subgroup of Fr is A ∪S-separable in the free
product Fr ∗G, where G is a LERF group.
Notice that A ∪S-separable means the quotient is required to be either an alternating or a sym-
metric group. Since G is an arbitrary LERF group, no uniform modification method seems to exist
that would guarantee the quotient is precisely an alternating group. Moreover, the hypothesis “infi-
nite index" is essential. In fact, the following example shows there exist finite-index subgroups that
are not alternating separable in such a free product.
Example 1.7. Let F2 = ⟨a, b | −⟩, G = ⟨c | c2⟩. We consider

2. PRELIMINARIES
In this section, we review some notions and properties of LERFness and topology of graphs. Let
G = ⟨X | R⟩be a group and
X∗:= {x, x−1|x ∈X}.
2.1. Labeled graph and embedding. A graph is a 1-dimensional CW-complex consisting of two
sets V and E representing the 0-cells and 1-cells respectively and two functions E →E and E →V:
for each e ∈E, there is an element ¯e ∈E and an element ι(e) ∈V. We call edge for e ∈E and
vertex for v ∈V, the requirements of ¯e are ¯¯e = e and ¯e ̸= e. A map of graph consists of a pair of
functions preserving the structure, which maps edges to edges, vertices to vertices.
A graph Γ is called a labeled graph (with the group G = ⟨X | R⟩) if it is equipped with a labeling
function
Lab : E(Γ) →X∗
satisfying Lab(¯e) = Lab(e)−1 and the immersion condition: e1 = e2 provided Lab(e1) = Lab(e2)
and ι(e1) = ι(e2). The label of a path p = e1e2 · · · en ⊂Γ is defined by
Lab(p) ≡Lab(e1) · Lab(e2) · · · Lab(en) ∈G.
If in addition, any path p with Lab(p) = 1 is closed, then Γ is called G-based. For each vertex v
and each word g ≡x1x2 · · · xn , since Γ is a labeled graph, there is a unique path p in Γ originating
from v with Lab(p) ≡x1x2 · · · xn. The subgroup
Lab(Γ, v) := {Lab(p) | p is a loop in Γ with base point v} ≤G
is called the subgroup of G determined by the graph Γ.
Let x ∈X∗. A vertex v ∈V(Γ) is called x-saturated if there exists e ∈E(Γ) with ι(e) = v and
Lab(e) = x. If Γ is x-saturated for any x ∈X∗at any v ∈V(Γ), then Γ is called X∗-saturated.
A map π : Γ →∆of two labeled graphs is an immersion if it is injective on the set {e ∈
E(Γ)|ι(e) = v} of all vertices of Γ. In addition, if π preserves the labels of edges, then it is called a
morphism of labeled graphs. If π is again an injective morphism, then it is called an embedding or
Γ embeds in the graph ∆.
Remark 2.1. A labeled graph is also called a well-labeled graph in some literature, see [9] [10]. Any
labeled graph of a free group Fr is Fr-based.
2.2. Relative Cayley graph. For a group G = ⟨X | R⟩, we denote the Cayley graph of G with
respect to the given generating set X by Cay(G). Recall that Cay(G) is an oriented graph with the
vertex set V(Cay(G)) = G and the edge set E(Cay(G)) = G × X∗. An edge (g, x) ∈E(Cay(G))
originates at g and terminates at gx.

For any subgroup H ≤G, we denote by Cay(G, H) the Cayley graph of G relative to the

V(Cay(G, H)) := {Hg | g ∈G},

Moreover, a path p originating from Hg must end at HgLab(p). Therefore, we have the following.
Lemma 2.2. Let H ≤G be a subgroup of G. A path p originating from the base point H · 1 in
Cay(G, H, H · 1) is a loop if and only if Lab(p) ∈H. Hence,
Lab(Cay(G, H), H · 1) = H.
The following lemma shows that G-based graphs are subgraphs of the relative Cayley graphs.
Lemma 2.3. [5, Lemma 1.5] Let G = ⟨X | R⟩be a group and let (Γ, v0) be a graph labeled with
X. Denote Lab(Γ, v0) = H. Then
(1) Γ is G-based if and only if it can be embedded in Cay(G, H, H · 1);
(2) Γ is G-based and X∗-saturated if and only if it is isomorphic to Cay(G, H, H · 1).
Note that Cay(G) and Cay(G, H) are naturally labeled with the generating set X of G, and there
are natural action of G on V(Cay(G)) and V(Cay(G, H)) given by left translation. The relative
Cayley graph Cay(G, H) can be defined as the quotient of Cay(G) by the action of H. Moreover,
Cay(G, H) can be viewed as the 1-skeleton of a covering space of the representation complex of G,
so we follow the terminology of Gitik [5] and refer to the "cover" as the relative Cayley graph.
Scott [13] described the residually finite and LERF properties by the embeddability from sub-
graphs to covers. Gitik [5] rephrased Scott’s result as follows.
Theorem 2.4. [5, Theorem 1.1] Let G = ⟨X | R⟩be a group.
(1) G is residually finite if and only if for any finite tree Γ, which is a subgraph of the Cayley
graph of G, there exists a finite-index subgroup H0 of G such that Γ can be embedded in
Cay(G, H0).
(2) G is LERF if and only if for any finitely generated subgroup H of G and for any finite
connected subgraph (Γ, H · 1) of Cay(G, H, H · 1), there exists a finite-index subgroup H0
of G such that (Γ, H · 1) can be embedded in Cay(G, H0, H0 · 1).
2.3. Precover. The notion of precover was defined by Gitik in [5] and then actively employed by
Markus-Epstein to study algorithmic problems in amalgams of finite groups in [9, 10].
Let G1 and G2 be two groups with generating sets X and Y respectively such that X∗∩Y ∗= ∅,
and let A = G1 ∗G2 be the free product of G1 and G2.
Definition 2.5. Let Γ be a graph labeled with X ∪Y .
(1) A subgraph of Γ is called monochromatic if it is labeled only with X or only with Y . An
X-component (resp. Y -component) of Γ is a maximal connected subgraph of Γ labeled
with X (resp. Y ).
(2) A vertex v in V(Γ) is called X-monochromatic (resp. Y -monochromatic), if all edges
connected to v are labeled with X (resp. Y ). Otherwise, v is called bichromatic.
(3) Γ is called a precover of A, if it is A-based, and each X-component of Γ is a cover of G1
and each Y -component of Γ is a cover of G2.
There is an equivalent description of the A-based graph as follows.

In this section, we recall the specific structure of Wilton’s proof of that any finitely generated
infinite-index subgroup of a free group with rank greater than one is A-separable.

3.1. Jordan’s theorem. An action of a group H on {1, 2, . . . , n} is called symmetric (resp. alter-
nating) if the image of H →Sn is Sn (resp. An). A subgroup G ≤Sn is called transitive if it acts
transitively on {1, 2, . . . , n}, and called primitive if in addition, it does not preserve any nontrivial
partition.
The main tool for proving Lemma 3.3 is the following Jordan’s theorem, which gives a criterion
for an action to be symmetric or alternating.
Theorem 3.1 (Jordan’s theorem, [4]). For each k > 2 there exists N such that if n > N, G ≤Sn is
a primitive subgroup and there exists γ ∈G \ {e}, which moves less than k elements, then G = Sn
or An.
Note that if G ≤Sn is transitive, then the cardinality of any partition preserved by G divides
n. Therefore, if p is prime, then G ≤Sp is primitive if and only if G is transitive. We have an
immediate corollary of Theorem 3.1 as follows.
Lemma 3.2. For each k > 2, there exists N such that for any prime p > N and any transitive
subgroup G ≤Sp if there exists γ ∈G \ {e}, which moves less than k elements, then G = Sp or
Ap.
3.2. Finite cover and Wilton’s construction. Let Fr be a free group of rank r > 1 with the
generating set X = {x1, · · · , xr}. Stallings [14] showed: any finite graph labeled with X can
be embedded into a cover without increasing the number of vertices. Wilton made a modification
in the construction to force the action of Fr on the vertex set of the covering to be alternating or
symmetric, see [18, Lemma 18 and Lemma 19]. We summarize them to the following lemma and
describe Wilton’s specific construction in detail as a proof, which shall be used in our proof of
Theorem 1.5.
Lemma 3.3. Let Z be a finite, connected graph labeled with X. If Z is not X∗-saturated, then there
is a finite cover Y of Fr such that Z embeds in Y and the action of Fr on V(Y ) is alternating or
symmetric.
Proof. Suppose Z has k vertices. Then by Lemma 3.2, there exists a sufficiently large prime p for
k + 5 such that for any transitive subgroup G ≤Sp if there exists γ ∈G \ {e}, which moves less
than k + 5 elements, then G = Sp or Ap. We shall construct a finite cover Y with p vertices, such
that Fr acts on V(Y ) transitively and there is an element of Fr moving less than k + 5 vertices.
Since Z is not an X∗-saturated graph, there are (not necessarily distinct) vertices a, b and some
x ∈X such that Z is not x-saturated at a and not x−1-saturated at b. Without loss of generality, we
take x = x1.
We begin by constructing two particular labeled graphs Wn and Vs with n = p −k −4 and
4 vertices respectively, such that xi ∈X acts trivially on V(Wn) for each i ≥2 and x2 acts
nontrivially on V(Vs).
