Abstract
Boolean networks are dynamical models of disease development in which the activation levels of
genes are represented by binary variables. Given a Boolean network, controls represent mutations or
medical treatments that fix the activation levels of selected genes so that all states in every attractor (i.e.,
long-term recurrent states) satisfy a desired phenotype. Our goal is to enumerate all minimal controls,
identifying critical gene subsets in disease development and therapy. This problem has an inherent bilevel
integer programming structure and is computationally challenging.
We propose an infeasibility-based Benders decomposition, a logic-based Benders framework for
bilevel integer programs with multiple subproblems. In our application, each subproblem finds a forbidden
attractor of a given length and yields a problem-specific feasibility cut. We also propose an auxiliary IP
called subspace separation that finds a Boolean subspace that includes multiple forbidden attractors and
thereby strengthens the cut. Numerical experiments show that the resulting algorithms are much more
scalable than state-of-the-art methods and that subspace separation substantially improves performance.
Keywords: Boolean network, precision medicine, bilevel optimization, Benders decomposition

Introduction
1.1
Precision Medicine and Boolean Networks
Precision medicine is a paradigm that aims to provide tailored treatments to individuals based on their
susceptibility and response to disease. The development of disease is regulated by interactions among many
genes, biological molecules, and environmental factors; we refer to all these factors simply as ‘genes’. An
essential task of precision medicine is to identify combinations of genetic alterations that either promote
disease development or facilitate recovery (Lord and Ashworth 2017, Vargas and Harris 2016). Because this
process requires extensive experiments, an efficient computational tool is crucial to narrowing down the set
of alterations.
Recent studies have utilized Boolean networks (BNs) to model the development of diseases, demonstrating
their practical relevance (Schwab et al. 2020).
A BN is a qualitative, discrete dynamical model that
represents the activation levels of genes in a set I as binary variables. Mathematically, it is a Boolean map
𝑓: {0, 1}|I| →{0, 1}|I| between binary vectors of dimension |I| where 0 and 1 represent the low and the
high activation levels, respectively. Interactions among genes are represented as Boolean formulas, referred
to as transition formulas. To construct a dynamical system, a state is an assignment of Boolean values to
genes where 2|I| states are possible. Given a state x, the synchronous update mode updates all variables
together as the image of the state by 𝑓(i.e., x becomes 𝑓(x)). This mode has proven effective for modeling
biological systems (Mori and Akutsu 2022); other possible update modes are summarized by Pauleve and
Sene (2021), but we leave them for future work.
BNs offer several advantages over alternative modeling approaches. Unlike machine-learning models,
they explicitly represent the disease dynamics and causal interactions among genes. They are also more
computationally efficient and scalable than continuous dynamical models such as differential equations.
We are interested in the long-term recurrent sets of states, called attractors. An attractor is a mutually
reachable set of states where no transition leaves the set (Kauffman 1993). For the synchronous update,
state x is in an attractor if and only if there exists a natural number 𝑇such that x = 𝑓𝑇(x). The smallest
such number 𝑇is called the length, and the set of states { 𝑓(x), 𝑓2(x), . . . , 𝑓𝑇(x)} constitutes the attractor.
Attractors are considered to determine the long-term behavior of the dynamical system because every initial
state eventually reaches one of the attractors. A relevant concept is a trap space, a Boolean subspace where
certain variables are fixed at either 0 or 1, and no transition leaves the subspace (Klarner et al. 2015). Trap
spaces are important in this paper because every trap space includes one or more attractors as a subset,
providing a tractable over-approximation of attractors.
The biological or medical condition of each state can be evaluated by another Boolean function repre-
senting the phenotype, denoted by 𝜙: {0, 1}|I| →{0, 1}. Depending on the purpose of modeling, it may
represent either the diseased condition or the normal condition, as inferred from accumulated experiments
and clinical trials. If every state in all attractors satisfies the disease phenotype, this strongly suggests that the
disease will develop. This analysis has been widely used to identify treatments for bladder cancer (Grieco
et al. 2013, Remy et al. 2015), breast cancer (Biane and Delaplace 2019), and so on.
Figure 1 shows an example of a BN with three genes denoted as 1–3. Figure 1(a) presents the transition

1.2
The Synchronous Attractor Control Problem (SACP)
In a BN, control means permanently fixing the transition formula of some genes to either 0 or 1, and their
activation levels accordingly (Biane et al. 2026). A control may change the set of attractors, as well as the
phenotype compositions of the states in attractors. Biologists consider controls fixing fewer genes more
relevant, as they highlight the parsimonious causes of a disease and key targets for treatment (Biane and
Delaplace 2019). At the same time, more candidates are desirable to validate with detailed experiments.
Hence, the main objective is to enumerate all minimal controls, defined as follows.
Definition 1 (Minimal control). A control is feasible if all states of every attractor under the control satisfy
the phenotype. A feasible control is minimal if it is inclusion-wise minimal, i.e., unfixing any single gene
would violate feasibility.
As a recent survey of control problems in BNs shows (Biane et al. 2026), enumeration of minimal
controls is a common goal for more than 16 existing tools.
Figure 1(c) illustrates the new state transition graph of Figure 1(a) under the control fixing 𝑥2 = 1,
overriding the original transition formula 𝑓2(x) = 𝑥1 ∧𝑥3. The previous attractor {(000)}, which does not
satisfy the phenotype, completely vanishes. As a result, all states of the remaining attractor {(011), (111)}
satisfy the phenotype. The set of trap spaces also changes by the control; (000) is removed, while (∗11) and
(∗1∗) are introduced. Applying 𝑥3 = 1 in addition to 𝑥2 = 1 is also feasible, but we will not accept it as a
minimal control because 𝑥2 = 1 alone is feasible.
Let 𝑇max ∈Z+ be the maximum attractor length considered: attractors longer than 𝑇max will be discarded
when evaluating the phenotype.
We will refer to the enumeration problem of minimal controls as the
synchronous attractor control problem with the maximum length 𝑇max, denoted as SACP(𝑇max). For example,
the problem limited to length one can be denoted as SACP(1), and the unrestricted problem is denoted as
SACP(∞) (≡SACP(2|I|)).
Previous studies have mostly restricted the length of attractors to one. Hayashida et al. (2008) first
developed an exponential-time search algorithm for SACP(1). Later, a few algorithms used integer pro-
gramming (IP) models by alternately solving two problems (Akutsu et al. 2012, Qiu et al. 2014). For more
efficient enumeration, computational algebra (Murrugarra et al. 2016) and binary decision diagrams (Biane
and Delaplace 2019) have been used. Recently, Moon et al. (2022) proposed a bilevel integer programming
(Bilevel IP) formulation of SACP(1) and developed a scalable branch-and-bound algorithm. On the other
hand, research for 𝑇max > 1 is quite limited. To the best of our knowledge, only Cifuentes-Fontanals et al.
(2022) has addressed SACP(∞) using a model checking solver (Cimatti et al. 2002) with some heuristics
developed by Cifuentes-Fontanals et al. (2020). However, the method often suffers from scalability issues.

1.3
Bilevel Integer Programming and Logic-Based Benders Decomposition
Bilevel IP (Kleinert et al. 2021) involves two decision-makers, referred to as the leader and the follower, who
make decisions sequentially as follows. The leader first makes a decision anticipating the optimal reactions
of the follower, and the follower then optimally solves their own optimization problem based on the leader’s
decision. The leader’s problem is referred to as the upper-level problem (ULP) and the follower’s problem
is referred to as the lower-level problem (LLP). The main interest is to find an optimal solution to the ULP,
which is formulated as a nested optimization problem. Coupling constraints are constraints of the ULP that
involve both the leader’s and the follower’s decision variables. They are important sources of computational
challenges as they create a strong interdependence between the two problems.
SACP(𝑇max) is computationally challenging even with the restriction to attractor length one. Finding
a single minimal control in SACP(1) is Σ𝑃
2 -hard (Akutsu et al. 2012, Moon et al. 2022), which implies
that no general compact single-level reformulation is expected unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses
(Stockmeyer 1976). Hence, bilevel IP is not merely a convenient modeling choice here; it is the natural
structure of the problem. For SACP(𝑇max), the leader finds a minimal control, while the follower searches
for an attractor that violates the phenotype under that control. Existing bilevel IP solvers such as MibS
(Tahernejad et al. 2020) can be used to solve SACP(𝑇max). However, they rely on branch-and-bound and
branch-and-cut methods (Moore and Bard 1990, Xu and Wang 2014, Fischetti et al. 2017, Wang and Xu
2017, Kleinert et al. 2021), which struggle to solve large instances of SACP(𝑇max). We therefore develop a
logic-based Benders decomposition specialized to this structure.
The logic-based Benders decomposition (LBBD) (Hooker and Ottosson 2003, Hooker 2024) partitions
an (usually single-level) optimization problem into a master problem and a subproblem. First, a master
problem decides one subset of variables subject to the constraints including those variables only. Given a
candidate partial solution from the master problem, a subproblem is solved to decide the remaining variables.
If the candidate yields an infeasible or suboptimal subproblem, a corresponding Benders feasibility cut or
an optimality cut is added to the master problem to remove the bad candidate. This process repeats until an
optimal solution is found or all candidates are exhausted.

The LBBD can be used for a wide variety of nonlinear and discrete optimization problems (Hooker
2024, Rahmaniani et al. 2017). However, its application to bilevel IPs is limited. Benders binary cuts
(DeNegre 2011) and Benders interdiction cuts (Caprara et al. 2016) are LBBD-style cuts available in the
MibS solver, but they require strict structural conditions. We address this gap with an infeasibility-based
Benders decomposition (IBBD) defined as follows.
Definition 2 (Infeasibility-based Benders decomposition (IBBD)). IBBD is a variant of LBBD for bilevel
IPs, in which each subproblem is dedicated to finding a certain type of feasibility cut. The core idea is to
decompose the subproblem by the type of coupling constraint violation as follows.
(i) Master problem. The coupling constraints and the lower-level optimality condition are relaxed from
the ULP, leaving upper-level variables only.
(ii) Aggregated subproblem. Given a candidate, the LLP is solved with a lexicographic objective: the
primary objective is the original LLP objective, and the secondary objective minimizes the violation
of coupling constraints. If the optimal violation is nonzero, the candidate is certified infeasible and a
feasibility cut is derived.
(iii) (Optional) Subproblem further decomposed by violation type. If the violation type has multiple
categories, the aggregated subproblem is further split into multiple subproblems by the type. This
decomposition is valid if any candidate infeasible to ULP has at least one decomposed subproblem
producing a feasibility cut. Subproblems are prioritized by their expected computational benefit.
Here, we focus on applying IBBD to SACP(𝑇max); Appendix A discusses its generalization to other
bilevel IPs with formal mathematical models. In SACP(𝑇max), a candidate control is infeasible to ULP if a
forbidden attractor is found under that candidate. An attractor is forbidden if at least one of its states violates
the phenotype (i.e., 𝜙(x) = 0 for some state x in the attractor). The aggregated subproblem is decomposed
into smaller subproblems to detect a forbidden attractor of a specific length. We say 𝑇max is sufficiently
large when no control feasible to SACP(𝑇max) has a forbidden attractor longer than 𝑇max under that control.
With a sufficiently large 𝑇max, the SACP(𝑇max) is equivalent to SACP(∞), and thus our algorithm can solve
SACP(∞) as well.
In addition to the decomposed subproblems, we also solve an auxiliary subproblem that finds a fully
forbidden trap space where every state violates the phenotype. Since this includes one or more forbidden
attractors, the resulting Benders cut is stronger under a certain condition, which is often satisfied in practice.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
1. We extend the bilevel IP approach of Moon et al. (2022) from𝑇max = 1 to arbitrary finite𝑇max to capture
more attractors that involve critical subsets of genes to control. With a sufficiently large 𝑇max, the
resulting algorithm can also be used for SACP(∞), outperforming the state-of-the-art model checking
approach by Cifuentes-Fontanals et al. (2022).
2. We propose a new type of logic-based Benders decomposition for the synchronous attractor control
problem, which decomposes the problem using the coupling constraint violation. We demonstrate
how feasibility cuts can be strengthened by solving an auxiliary subproblem, and discuss how the
entire framework can be generalized to bilevel IPs.
3. We distribute the proposed algorithm as an open-source package optboolnet (Software Heritage
Identifier swh:1:dir:194b2539a59ee22821b6b388b653376694045adc) via CoLoMoTo Docker (Naldi
et al. 2018), a Python platform for reproducible analyses of Boolean network models. Our package is
expected to accelerate the discovery of new treatments with a reproducible computational pipeline.

4.1
Experiment Setting
We compare the following methods.
• MibS: Exhaustively solving U(𝜆, D) with a bilevel IP solver MibS (Tahernejad et al. 2020) (ver. 1.2)

• BEN/SEP: Enumerative IBBD algorithms based on Algorithm 1. BEN sets UseTScut = 0 and uses only

– (DEC): Solve the decomposed subproblems S𝑇(d) for 𝑇= 1, . . . ,𝑇max.
– (AGG): Solve L𝑇max(d), and add an AT cut if its optimal solution has 𝑝= 0. We also label MibS

Our experiments address the following questions:
(i) How do the proposed IBBD algorithms (BEN and SEP) perform compared to MibS and PBN?
(ii) Does the use of the trap space cut lead to better performance (SEP vs. BEN)?
(iii) Do the decomposed subproblems (DEC) outperform the aggregated subproblems (AGG)?
(iv) How does the choice of 𝑇max affect computational performance and feasibility for SACP(∞)?
All algorithms enumerate minimal controls in nondecreasing order of their size. IP models are solved

performance measure is the number of minimal controls found within 600 seconds, reflecting time-critical

4.3
Discussion
We highlight three extensions of the enumerative IBBD algorithm for SACP(𝑇max):
• The heuristic approach SEP for finite 𝑇max can be refined by checking subset controls of each returned
solution. This would remove surviving non-minimal controls, although it would still not yield an exact
method.
• We can remove the need for no-good cuts by using the high point relaxation, namely the master
problem augmented with lower-level feasibility constraints (Moore and Bard 1990). For 𝑇max = 1,
Moon et al. (2022) used this idea in a branch-and-bound implementation.
• In some modeling variants, controls inducing no attractor of a prescribed length may be accepted
(Biane et al. 2026). Algorithm 1 adapts easily to that setting by treating those controls as feasible and
omitting no-good cuts.
5
Conclusion
We studied the synchronous attractor control problem for Boolean networks, where the goal is to enumerate
all inclusion-wise minimal controls under which every attractor satisfies a given phenotype.
To solve
this problem, we proposed a bilevel integer programming formulation and developed an infeasibility-based
Benders decomposition (IBBD) that derives strong feasibility cuts from forbidden attractors of different
lengths.
We also introduced subspace separation, which yields a trap space cut that strengthens one or more
attractor cuts under certain conditions. This strengthening is effective when many forbidden attractors are
covered by a common fully forbidden trap space, and the computational study shows that the resulting IBBD
framework is scalable and practically effective.
17


## Page 18


Beyond this application, IBBD may be applicable to other bilevel integer programming problems if Ben-
ders feasibility cuts can be generated from infeasible solutions of the lower-level problem (see Appendix A).
Our results suggest that IBBD is useful for problems in which coupling constraint violation can be classified
into a small number of meaningful categories. If feasible solutions to the original bilevel problem are very
sparse, IBBD can be more efficient as it focuses on strong feasibility cuts.
Several directions remain for future work. First, we may develop a method to determine a valid bound
on attractor length (𝑇max) for a given Boolean network, thereby proving the exactness of trap space cuts.
Another promising direction is to combine the IBBD framework with symbolic methods, such as model
checking or constraint programming, to solve the subproblems. As noted in the survey by Hooker (2024),
this hybridization will offer more flexibility in selecting the most suitable method for the subproblems.
Finally, the trap space cut can be applied to controlling other long-term visited states such as asynchronous
attractors (Rozum et al. 2021, Beneˇs et al. 2022) or minimal trap spaces (Paulev´e 2023, Riva et al. 2023);
we refer to a recent survey by Biane et al. (2026). This is possible because trap spaces are independent of
the update mode (Paulev´e et al. 2020).
