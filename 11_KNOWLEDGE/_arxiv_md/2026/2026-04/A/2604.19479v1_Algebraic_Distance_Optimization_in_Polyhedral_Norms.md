Algebraic Distance Optimization in Polyhedral Norms
Eliana Duarte, Nidhi Kaihnsa, Julia Lindberg,
Ang´elica Torres, and Madeleine Weinstein
April 22, 2026
Abstract
We consider the distance minimization problem to a real algebraic variety X ⊆Rn when the metric
is induced by a polyhedral norm. Each point in the variety has a Voronoi cell whose geometry depends
on the normal space at the point and the inner normal fan of the polyhedral ball. For codimension-one
varieties, we decompose X into sets of points whose Voronoi cones have the same dimension, which is
the expected dimension of their Voronoi cell. We prove that this decomposition is a stratification of X
and that each strata is a semialgebraic set. We conclude by giving an algebraic description of the medial
axis, which is the locus of points whose minimal distance to X is achieved at more than one point on X.

Introduction
A distance optimization problem consists of a subset X of a metric space, representing a model, together with
an observed point, v ̸∈X. The fundamental question is then: which point of X best explains the observed
point v? Algebraic models of this type arise in disciplines such as algebraic statistics, computer vision,
machine learning, and phylogenetics, among others [3]. The complexity of this optimization problem has
been studied from an algebraic perspective for a wide range of models X and distance functions [4, 3, 10, 14].
In this article, we consider the inverse problem. Given a set X ⊂Rn and a point p ∈X, we ask which
points in Rn are best modeled by p. In other words, we study which data points have their distance to X
minimized at p. The set of all such points in Rn is called the Voronoi cell of p. The collection of all of the
Voronoi cells forms the Voronoi diagram of X.
Voronoi diagrams have been studied extensively, predominantly in the setting where X is a finite subset
of Rn equipped with the Euclidean metric. In [7], the authors extend this analysis to the case when X is
an algebraic variety, showing that for any smooth point p ∈X, the Euclidean Voronoi cell is convex and
contained in the normal space of X at p. For discrete sets and the tropical norm, the authors in [8, Theorem
6] proved that Voronoi cells are star-convex, but not necessarily convex. In this work, we study the Voronoi
diagram when X is an algebraic variety and the metric is induced by a polyhedral norm.
A primary motivation for this setting comes from optimal transport. Consider the probability simplex of
all distributions on n states. A metric d on the set of states induces a transportation distance on the simplex
known as the Wasserstein distance Wd(µ, ν). The corresponding unit ball is a centrally symmetric convex
polytope known as the Lipschitz polytope. This setting was studied in [5, 6], where it was shown that the
Wasserstein distance to a variety is a piecewise polynomial function.
Our approach follows [1, 7]. For a point p ∈X, [1] shows that its Voronoi cell is contained in a union of
polyhedral cones, which we refer to as the Voronoi cone of p. We prove that the Voronoi cone is determined
by the relative position of the normal space of X at p with respect to the inner normal fan of the unit ball.
Equivalently, the Voronoi cone can be characterized by the intersection of the dual unit ball with the normal
space at p (Theorem 3.6). Moreover, when X has pure codimension one, this perspective allows us to stratify
X according to the dimension of the Voronoi cones of its points (Theorem 4.4).
We also study the medial axis of X, the locus of points whose distance to X is optimized by more than
one point. When X consists of finitely many points, prior work examined the combinatorial structure of
such bisectors [8, 11]. We consider the case where X has codimension one. In this case, the medial axis

2.2
Polyhedral Norms
Let L be a finite set of linear functionals on Rn such that the set P = {x ∈Rn | ℓ(x) ≤1 for ℓ∈L} is
bounded and describes a full-dimensional convex polytope. Furthermore, we impose that L = −L so the
polytope P is centrally symmetric around the origin, i.e. P = −P. For the entirety of this article, we will
assume that L = −L and P = −P.
Definition 2.8. For a finite collection of linear functionals L, a polyhedral norm is a function hL on Rn
given by
hL(x) := max
ℓ∈L ℓ(x).

Definition 2.9. Given a polyhedral norm hL, the ball of radius r at an arbitrary point y ∈Rn is
Br(y) := {x ∈Rn | hL(x −y) ≤r}.
For convenience, we write the unit ball at y as B(y) and simply as B when considering the unit ball around
the origin.
For a given polyhedral norm, B is a centrally symmetric convex polytope. In the next result, we show
that there is a polyhedral norm associated with every full-dimensional centrally symmetric convex polytope.
Lemma 2.10. Let P be a full-dimensional centrally symmetric convex polytope in Rn that contains the
origin. Then there is a polyhedral norm hL such that P is the unit ball for hL.
Proof. We consider the set of linear functionals L that give supporting hyperplanes for the facets of P. This
gives the minimal H−representation of P. Since 0 ∈P and P is centrally symmetric, the set L is of the
form {a1, . . . , am, −a1, . . . , −am} with ai ∈Rn∗. By construction, P is the unit ball of the polyhedral norm
hL up to scaling.

functionals {±x, ±y} . The dual polytope P ∗is the unit ball for the dual norm and it is defined by the set
of functionals {±(x + y), ±(−x + y)} .
Example 2.7 (continued). Similar to the previous example, the cube is the unit ball for the norm defined

2.3
Differential Topology
Here, we recall some results and definitions from differential topology that we will use later to stratify
a codimension-one manifold using polyhedral norms. For a basic introduction to topological and smooth
manifolds, we refer readers to [12, 13]. Going forward, we consider a manifold X ⊂Rn with the Euclidean
topology inherited from Rn. Below, we give definitions of locally closed subspaces and stratifications of X.
Definition 2.11. A subset U of a topological space X is locally closed if U \ U is closed in X. Equivalently,
U is locally closed if it is the set difference of two open sets in X.
Definition 2.12. Let X be a manifold and I be an index set with a partial ordering. The collection {Xi}i∈I
is a stratification of X if the following conditions are satisfied:
(i) X = S
i∈I Xi,
(ii) Xi is a submanifold for all i ∈I,
(iii) Xi is locally closed for all i ∈I,
(iv) Xi ∩Xj = ∅if i ̸= j,
(v) if Xi ∩Xj ̸= ∅, then Xi ⊂Xj. Equivalently, if Xi ⊂Xj, then i ≤j.
A family {Xi}i∈I of subsets of a topological space X is locally finite if, for each x ∈X, there is a
neighborhood U of x such that U ∩Xi = ∅for all but finitely many indices i ∈I. If I is a finite set, that is,
{Xi}i∈I is a finite family, then it is trivially locally finite. This will always be the case later on.
We now recall some basic results and definitions that will be useful when constructing the stratification.

Voronoi Diagrams in Polyhedral Norms
In this section, we study the Voronoi diagrams of algebraic varieties with respect to polyhedral norms. We
state our main definitions and prove results regarding Voronoi cells of smooth points of algebraic varieties.
In what follows, we assume that B ⊂Rn is a centrally symmetric convex polytope that corresponds to the
unit ball of a polyhedral norm on Rn.
Definition 3.1. Let X ⊂Rn be a variety and let v ∈X. Given a polyhedral norm hL, the Voronoi cell of
v with respect to hL is:
VorX(v) := {y ∈Rn | d(y, X) = hL(y −v)}.
Given a smooth point v on a variety X, we define the Type of v as the collection of faces of the unit ball
B that have a supporting hyperplane whose normal vector is contained in the normal space of v, Nv.
Definition 3.2. Let X be a variety and Nv be the normal space of X at a smooth point v ∈X. We define
the type of v as:
Type(v) := {F ∈F(B) | C(P, F) ∩Nv ̸= ∅} .
We point out that the definition above differs from the definition of type in [6, Proposition 4]. In our case,
the type of a point does not depend on a second point. It depends only on the local geometry of the variety
and the polyhedral ball. In Theorem 3.11, we will refine the definition of type by defining an optimizing face
which is closer to the definition of type from [6].
Lemma 3.3. Given a ball B as defined above and a smooth point v ∈X, if F ∈Type(v), then −F ∈Type(v).
Proof. Since B is centrally symmetric, it holds that Nv ∩C(P, F) ̸= ∅if and only if Nv ∩C(P, −F) ̸= ∅.
