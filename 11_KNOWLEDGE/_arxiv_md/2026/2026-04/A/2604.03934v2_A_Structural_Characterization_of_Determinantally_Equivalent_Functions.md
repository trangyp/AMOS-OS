Abstract
Let Λ be a set and F a field. Suppose that K, Q : Λ2 →F are two functions such that
for any n ∈N and x1, x2, . . . , xn ∈Λ, the determinants of matrices (K(xi, xj))1≤i,j≤n and
(Q(xi, xj))1≤i,j≤n agree. We study to what extent K and Q must be related by two canonical
transformations corresponding to diagonal similarity and transposition.
In the symmetric case, this relation holds without further assumptions (see Stevens (2021),
[5]), while in general it fails. In Mantelos (2026), [4], it was shown that the relation remains
valid under a natural 2 × 2 determinantal condition (property D), together with the additional
assumption that both functions are nowhere vanishing.
We prove that the ‘nowhere vanishing’ assumption can be removed entirely, and that property
D alone provides the correct and complete structural mechanism governing the problem. In
particular, this shows that the nowhere-zero assumption is not intrinsic to the problem, but
rather an artefact of the specific method used in [4].
The proof is entirely combinatorial and avoids linear algebra, relying on an analysis of
permutations in the definition of a determinant as cycles in a graph; in particular, it requires
new arguments to handle the breakdown of the identities used in [4], which are crucial to the
method therein. In the ‘finite Λ’ case, this also yields a new approach to the classical matrix
problem of Loewy (1986), [3], thereby revealing an underlying combinatorial structure.

The functional formulation was first
introduced in [5], where it was motivated by determinantal point processes. However, both in that
work and in the present paper, the problem is formulated and studied in a purely deterministic
setting, independent of any probabilistic interpretation.
In [5], the problem was studied under the additional assumption that K and Q are symmetric, in
which case a transposition transformation plays no role. More precisely, it was shown that deter-
minantally equivalent symmetric functions are necessarily related by conjugation transformations.
This extends, in the finite setting (i.e., in the setting where the underlying ‘set Λ’ is finite), a
result for (real or complex) symmetric matrices with equal corresponding principal minors; cf. [1].
Related extensions to (complex) Hermitian matrices can be found in [2].

The presence of the nowhere-zero assumption raises a natural question: is it an essential part of
the phenomenon, or merely a technical device used to facilitate/enable the specific argument in
[4]? The main result of this paper shows that it is not essential – property D alone provides the
underlying structural mechanism.
Theorem 1.1 Let Λ be a set, F a field, and let K, Q : Λ2 →F be two functions. Suppose further
that K and Q are of class D. If K and Q are determinantally equivalent, then Q can be transformed
into K through only conjugation and transposition transformations.
We remark further that property D was not fully exploited in [4], where it appeared only at the
final stage of the argument. This suggests that its true structural significance is not fully captured
by that paper and goes far beyond its original use. The present work makes its structural role
precise.
While the overall strategy is inspired by [4], the techniques developed here are substantially dif-

applicable in the presence of zero values. As in that paper, and in contrast to classical approaches
to Loewy’s problem [3] relying on linear-algebraic machinery, our approach is entirely combinato-
rial: the proof is based on analyzing permutations in the definition of the determinant as cycles in
a graph. From this cycle structure, we naturally construct two explicit bivariate functions S and ˜S
(given in (14) and (15), respectively), which capture the dichotomy between the two structural cases
of the problem. The main step is to establish that all 3-cycles fall into a single category, namely
either Case 1 or Case 2 in the terminology of [4]. Under this classification, the argument splits
according to the two cases: In the Case 1 situation, we show that S satisfies the cocycle property
(defined precisely in Section 2.1), thereby yielding conjugation equivalence (2); and in the Case 2
situation, ˜S is shown to be a cocycle function, thereby yielding transposition equivalence (3), up
to conjugation. A central part of the argument is a careful analysis of the constraints imposed by
property D on 3-cycles, which ultimately reveals it as the genuine structural condition governing
the problem.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2.1, besides recalling the notations and terminology
from [4], we introduce several more together with a graphical framework adapted to the presence
of vanishing values. Section 2.2 recalls background results from [5] and [4]. Section 3 outlines the
strategy of our proof of Theorem 1.1. Section 4 develops new cycle-based identities capable of
handling vanishing values, which may be viewed as extensions of certain identities from Section 5
of [4], to the setting with zeros. Section 5 analyzes the implications of the ‘class D’ assumption,
establishing several structural properties crucial to our arguments later on. Finally, utilizing all
the tools developed in the previous sections, Section 6 contains our proof of Theorem 1.1.

This section is devoted to a detailed analysis of the ‘class D’ assumption, which plays a central role
