Abstract
We develop a qualitative-dynamics framework for general Iterated Function Systems (IFSs)
on locally compact spaces. Our approach extends to IFSs a framework recently developed in the
semiflows setting by James Yorke and the present author based on binary relations (streams)
that encode which point can be regarded as “downstream” from which. This leads to a graph
representation of the global qualitative dynamics that separates recurrent behavior from gradient-
like behavior.
1
Introduction
The main goal of the present article is to extend to Iterated Function Systems (IFSs) a framework
recently introduced by James Yorke and the present author to study and encode the qualitative
behavior of semiflows.
Historically, IFSs have been used quite successfully for the construction of deterministic frac-
tals [4] in the context of contractive maps, beginning with the seminal article of Hutchinson [19] and
the introduction of the general theory of contractive IFS by Michael Barnsley in [4]. Recently, Barns-
ley and Andrew Vince [7, 24] and, independently, the present author [11, 14, 13] started investigating
some self-projective IFSs on real and complex projective spaces whose maps are not contractions.
At the same time, Barnsley et al. started investigating properties of “general” IFSs [6, 8, 5], namely
IFSs where the only requirement is that maps be continuous, mostly from the point of view of
the chaos game. In this article, we embrace precisely this point of view but our aim is somehow
complementary: we consider general IFSs as a dynamical system and develop a framework to study
and represent the qualitative behavior of such systems.
For simplicity, we restrict our attention to locally compact metric spaces.
We also require
separability for technical reasons. We do not require completeness and we assume instead that
our IFSs have “compact dynamics”.
More precisely, we require our IFSs to have a non-empty
global attractor, namely a (unique) maximal H-invariant (i.e.
invariant under the Hutchinson
map) compact set that attracts every compact subset of the phase space. We showed in [20] that
the qualitative behavior of a semiflow with compact dynamics is equivalent to that of its restriction
to its global attractor, and here we show that the same happens in case of IFSs. This way, we
switch the focus from the topological properties of the phase space to the dynamical properties of
the IFS.
Given an IFS F with compact dynamics on a locally compact metric space X, we use “streams”
to decompose the dynamics of F into its “recurrent” and “gradient” components and represent the

qualitative dynamics of F in a graph. A stream of F is a closed and transitive binary relation on X
that establishes “which point is downstream from which”. For any given point x ∈X, every point
on the orbit of x is downstream from x but in general, due to the closure and transitivity properties,
the set of points downstream from a given point is larger than its sole orbit. Given a stream S of
F, it is natural to define “ponds” as sets of points that are both upstream and downstream from
each other. Hence, ponds generalize the concept of periodic orbit. A pond N is downstream from
another pond M if the points of N are downstream from those of M. We encode the qualitative
behavior of S into a directed graph by taking all ponds of S as the nodes of the graph and saying
that there is an edge from M to N in the stream’s graph if N is downstream from M. Due to
transitivity, the graph of any stream has no cycles.
An IFS can have infinitely many different streams, each highlighting some different aspects of
its dynamics, but the most important is arguably the chain stream, based on an adaptation to IFS
of Charles Conley’s ε-chains.
Our main results are the following.
Assume that an IFS F on a locally compact metric space X has compact dynamics and denote
by GF its global attractor. Then:
1. The chain stream of F has the same nodes and edges (and so the same graph) as the chain
stream of the restriction of F to GF (Theorem 3).
This means that F has the same qualitative dynamics as an IFS on a compact space.
2. The chain-recurrent set and each of its nodes are compact and H-invariant, i.e. invariant
under the Hutchinson map of F (Theorem 4).
In the IFS framework, H-invariance plays the role that invariance plays for semiflows.
3. If GF is connected and every point in GF has a backward orbit that asymptotes to some chain
node, then the chain graph of ΦF is connected (Theorem 5).
Connectedness is a strong topological condition for a graph. In [20], Yorke and the present
author proved that the graph of any stream of a semiflow with compact dynamics on a locally
compact space is connected.
We show with an example that this result does not hold for
IFSs but we provide a general condition, satisfied by many important cases, that grants the
connectivity of the graph of an IFS.
4. If the smallest stream of F has countably many weak nodes, then it coincides with its chain
stream (Theorem 6).
Hence, in these cases the chain stream is the smallest stream of F and so the one that is most
strictly related to its dynamics.
5. The Hutchinson map HF of F has compact dynamics and its global attractor is K(GF), the
set of all compact subsets of GF (Theorem 7). More generally, we show that HF has compact
dynamics if and only if F has compact dynamics.
The Hutchinson map of an IFS is able to reveal hidden aspects of its dynamics. This result
shows that our framework for semiflows, applied to HF, aligns nicely the framework for IFS
applied to F. Notice that, if the global attractor of F is connected, then the global attractor of
HF is connected as well and so, by Theorem 6 in [20], the graph of HF is connected.
6. Each distinct chain node of F, as a compact H-invariant set, is a chain-recurrent point of HF
and different chain nodes of F are contained in different chain nodes of HF. In particular, if

the chain graph of HF consists of a single node, then the chain graph of F has a single node

The relation between the dynamics of F and the dynamics of HF is often non-trivial. Hence,
general relations between the qualitative behavior of these two systems are particularly impor-
tant. This result shows that, when the dynamics of HF is particularly simple (namely the
whole global attractor is itself a pond), the same happens to F. The inverse result does not
hold: even for IFSs consisting of a single map there are cases (e.g. see Examples 2 and 3 in
Section 7.2) where the chain graph of F has a single node while the chain graph of HF has
many.
Since global attractors are often topologically complicated and hard to find analytically, we
provide a criterion to establish their existence: an IFS F has compact dynamics if and only if it has
a compact global trapping region (Theorems 1 and 2). Often a system has many trapping regions,
and these trapping regions are frequently topologically simple (e.g. convex open sets).
Finally, we show that the Hutchinson map of an IFS whose maps are distance-decreasing (this
includes maps that are contractions at every point but finitely many ones) has a single node (The-
orem 9). This result extends Hutchinson’s classical result on contractive IFSs (see Theorem C)
and applies to an important non-contractive self-projective IFS introduced by the author in [11]
(Example 1 in Section 7.2).
The article is structured as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the main definitions about the
dynamics of an IFS. In Section 3, we introduce global attractors and trapping regions and prove their
main properties. In Section 4, we introduce streams of IFSs and prove several of their fundamental
properties. In Section 5, we study in some detail the fundamental properties of the chain stream of
a IFS. In Section 6, we apply our framework on semiflows to the Hutchinson map and compare the
chain stream of an IFS with the chain stream of its Hutchinson map. Finally, in Section 7 we go
over several examples of non-trivial non contractive IFSs in dimension 1 and 2.

Global attractor of an IFS
Just as in case of semiflows, the IFSs with richest asymptotics are the ones whose dynamics is,

attracted by and stay within some compact region. For instance, this is precisely what happens in
case of the celebrated Lorenz “butterfly” system [22]. In this section, we give a precise definition of
the IFSs that can be studied within our framework.

Streams and graphs of an IFS
The orbit relation OF is transitive but not closed and its closure is not necessarily transitive. It turns
out that having an extension of the orbit relation which is at the same time closed and transitive
allows to define “generalized recurrent points” with nice properties: the set of these generalized
recurrent points is closed, which is not necessarily the case for RF, and there is a canonical way to
sort these points into equivalence relation classes, which we call “nodes”, and to define edges among
these nodes. Points belonging to the same node are in some sense “dynamically equivalent”, just like
points on a periodic orbit. Nodes and edges allow to encode the qualitative behavior of a system
into a directed graph. Below we go over these definitions and prove some of their fundamental
properties.
Stream of an IFS. Recall that a quasi-order on X is a binary relation on X which is reflexive and
transitive. We call stream of an IFS F a closed quasi-order S that contains OF. When (x, y) ∈S,
we say that y is S-downstream from x and we use the notation
x ≽S y.
Sometimes we use also the notation y ∈DownS(x) or x ∈UpS(y). When x ≽S y and y ≽S x,
we write
x
S= y,
and we say that x and y are S-equivalent.
We say that x is S-recurrent if x S= z for every z ∈OF(x). We denote by RS the set of
all S-recurrent points. We say that a set M ⊂RS is S-equivalent if all points of M are mutually
S-equivalent. We call nodes the equivalence classes of RS with respect to S=. If x ∈RS, we denote
by NodeS(x) the node of S containing x. Notice that, as implicitly assumed in the definition
above, S= is an equivalence relation.
The largest and the smallest stream. One way to give a concrete meaning to streams is either
to think that F is subject to some control or that F is an approximated model of some natural or
abstract phenomenon one wants to study. In this framework, x ≽S y for y ̸∈OF(x) means that y
is reachable from x by steering the orbit as the point moves under F or that y is an some orbit of x
compatible with the approximation of the model. The reader can verify the following basic results:
1. X × X is a stream, in fact the largest stream of each IFS;
2. the intersection of streams is a stream, so each IFS has a smallest stream.
The largest stream corresponds to the trivial situation where the controls (or the errors) are so large
that every point can be reached from any other point of the phase space. In this case, the dynamics
of F becomes irrelevant.
At the other end, the smallest stream is the one most faithful to the dynamics of F: x ≽S y
means that y can be reached from x by making arbitrarily small “adjustments” to the orbit of x.
For instance, consider the time-1 map f of the ODE

in (0, 1) asymptotes to 1 and every point in (1, 2) asymptotes to 2. Given any stream S of this
system, every point is downstream from 0. Indeed, every point x ∈(0, 1) has a backward orbit

The chain streams
The concept of ε-chain was introduced in the theory of dynamical systems by Charles Conley in
1972 [10] as a way to simplify the construction of generalized recurrence introduced by Joseph
Auslander a decade earlier [3]. In [17], we used this concept to define chain streams for semiflows
with compact dynamics on metric spaces. Here, we generalize ε-chains to IFSs and use them to
define analogous chain streams. We will show in this section that the infinitesimal chain stream

3. the chain-recurrent set of CF and each of its nodes are H-invariant, compact and contained in
GF; 4. the graph of CF coincides with that of the chain stream of the restriction of F to GF.
