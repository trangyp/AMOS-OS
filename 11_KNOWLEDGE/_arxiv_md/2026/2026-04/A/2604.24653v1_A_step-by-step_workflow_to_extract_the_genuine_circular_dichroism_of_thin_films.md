Circular Dichroism (CD) spectroscopy has evolved from a purely solution based method
towards a powerful tool in the analysis of chiral thin films. Although a straightforward
technique, the genuine CD signal is often accompanied by artifacts arising from optical
anisotropy and instrumental imperfections. This tutorial presents a two-step workflow
that reliably isolates the orientation invariant CD response for anisotropic thin films by
combining azimuthal sample rotation with sample flipping. For this purpose, a home-built
sample stage was developed, which enables systematic suppression of many anisotropy-
induced artifacts in commercial CD spectrophotometers. Both a detailed description of the
setup itself as well as the needed python script are provided. The reliability of the workflow
is demonstrated on two selected samples from different research fields: chiral molecules
attached to metallic surfaces as well as metal halide perovskites incorporating chiral spacer
molecules.

I.
INTRODUCTION
Chirality or handedness describes the existence of two variations - enantiomers - of a sub-
stance that are mirror opposites of each other1. Nowadays, chirality plays an important role in
a wide range of research topics ranging from biomolecules2 over organic semiconductors3 to
halide perovskites4 and chiral superstructures5,6. Distinguishing the two enantiomers is often a
challenging task. Currently, the most powerful way to differentiate enantiomers is based on their
interaction with light. Chiral materials typically exhibit chiroptical activity, which modifies the
polarization state of light. This can be probed by circular dichroism (CD) or optical rotary disper-
sion (ORD) spectroscopy. CD and ORD effects occur when left-handed (LCP) and right-handed
circular polarized (RCP) waves are absorbed and/or retarded to different extents by a chiral mate-
rial.
As the research on chiral materials has its roots in biochemistry, CD has been widely used for the
analysis of enantiomers in solution. Based, in part, on the discovery of the chirality induced spin
selectivity (CISS) effect, interest in chiral structures has also shifted towards thin film samples
and fields such as spintronics, electrochemistry and magnetic resonance7–9. This led to unex-
pected challenges in the field of CD spectroscopy. Most importantly, chiral molecules in solution
are randomly oriented, whereas in the solid state they can adopt a preferential alignment. This
reduced degree of freedom related to preferential molecular alignment gives rise to uniaxial or
biaxial optical anisotropy of the films10, leading to linear dichroism (LD) and/or linear birefrin-
gence (LB) in addition to CD10–12. The disentanglement of the CD, LD, and LB contributions
from the measured signal in CD spectroscopy is, therefore, essential but challenging and often
neglected. For example, in the field of chiral perovskite thin films the measured CD spectrum
can change sign when the sample surface is flipped by 180 ◦with respect to the light propagation
direction, which would be unexpected if the signal originated from circular dichroism only5,12,13.
Although the LD and LB contributions to the measured CD spectra have already been reported
in an earlier review article11, and have gained more attention in recent publications5,13, a simple
and broadly accessible experimental workflow for isolating reliable CD signals in thin films is
still lacking. Accurate accounting of the LD and LB artifacts is complicated because the required
sample stage and electronics are not provided as standard equipment by commercial vendors of
CD spectrometers.
In this work, we highlight the fundamental effects that can overlay the genuine CD response of

BACKGROUND ON CIRCULAR DICHROISM SPECTROSCOPY AND
MEASUREMENT ARTIFACTS
A.
Properties of thin films acting on the polarization state of light
To understand the working mechanism of our two-step measurement routine, we first intro-
duce both the microscopic and the macroscopic descriptions of circular dichroism, along with
additional macroscopic effects that can influence the measurements of CD. CD arises from the
differential absorption of LCP and RCP light in chiral media, i.e., media lacking mirror symmetry.
Microscopically speaking, it results from differences in the transition probabilities for left- and
right-circularly polarized photons during a transition from a ground state, a, to an excited state,
b. This phenomenon was formulated by Léon Rosenfeld15 and is illustrated in Figures 1 (a) and
(b). The magnitude of this effect is proportional to the rotatory strength R(a →b), defined as the
imaginary part of the scalar product between the electric dipole transition moment ⟨a|µ|b⟩and the
magnetic dipole transition moment ⟨b|m|a⟩:16
R(a →b) ∼Im(⟨a | µ | b⟩·⟨b | m | a⟩)
(1)
In essence, CD requires that an electric dipole transition is accompanied by a magnetic dipole
contribution with a non-zero relative phase. In practical terms, this leads to different absorption
probabilities of circularly polarized σ+ (RCP) and σ−(LCP) photons. For example, if we consider
a chiral molecule with a single transition, i.e., an amino acid, the transition probability for σ−is
higher for the L-enantiomer, resulting in more σ−photons than σ+ photons being absorbed17 (See

remainder of this tutorial. Within this framework, the superposition of two circularly polarized

If, instead, the refractive indices for the RCP and LCP waves differ, the two modes propagate
at different velocities, resulting in a relative phase shift. Their superposition remains linearly po-
larized, but with a rotated polarization direction relative to the original wave. This phenomenon,
known as CB (or ORD) (Figure S1 (a)), lays the historical foundation of spectroscopic CD mea-
surements and can be readily accessible in optically isotropic samples, either liquids or solids.
The above mentioned changes in the polarization state (elliptization and rotation) of light are,
however, not caused solely by chiral materials. In optically anisotropic but achiral materials, the
two normal modes are LP waves, and can produce similar effects. These "measurement artifacts"
are in fact intrinsic optical properties of the anisotropic samples. Linear dichroism (LD), Figure S1
(b), describes the light-matter interaction of anisotropic materials that absorb two orthogonal LP
waves to a different extent16,18, leading to a tilt of the polarization direction with respect to the
original direction, i.e., similar to the CB effect. In linear birefringence (LB) (Figure 1 (e)) the two
LP normal modes experience different velocities in the material and therefore, accumulate a phase
delay19. Their superposition leads to an elliptical polarization state that mimics the CD effect. In
addition, when LD and LB occur simultaneously in an oriented sample, combined LDLB effects
arise, where the material both differentially absorbs the normal light modes and introduces a phase
shift between them.
More generally, absorption and dispersion are fundamentally linked: the spectral behaviour of
these effects is linked through the Kramers-Kronig relations20. Therefore, CD, CB, LD, and LB
may co-exist in materials that are both chiral and anisotropic, requiring careful experimental pro-
cedures to isolate the intrinsic ("clean") CD response.
B.
Artifacts in CD measurements
To date, three experimental approaches have been employed to measure CD. Early measure-
ments, such as those on heme proteins, used modified double-beam spectrophotometers to directly
detect absorption differences, but these absorption differences are often too small to be reliably
detected16. The second approach measures the ellipticity induced in linearly polarized light (LPL)
passing through the sample, though it is limited by the difficulty of maintaining LPL quality and
detecting the small orthogonal components of elliptically polarized light (EPL). Third, modern
CD spectrometers most commonly use rapid polarization modulation, typically with a photoelas-
tic modulator (PEM), which significantly enhances sensitivity and enables reliable detection of
6


## Page 7


weak CD signals16.
Although PEM-based techniques are highly sensitive, the measured CD signal can still include
contributions from the optical anisotropy of the film or substrate rather than from intrinsic sample
chirality, resulting in spurious CD. CD artifacts in thin film samples arise from three main sources
- some of which can be disentangled, whereas others cannot.
The first principal source of artifacts is LDLB in the sample, which during polarization modu-
lation can produce an apparent intensity difference between LCP and RCP, generating a false CD
signal comparable in magnitude to the intrinsic signal. A second source of artifacts arises from
instrumental imperfections. Modern CD spectrometers use a PEM to rapidly switch between LCP
and RCP and synchronously detect the differential signal. The PEM induces periodic birefrin-
gence in a transparent crystal, converting LPL into alternating circularly and elliptically polarized
states at a modulation frequency ∼50 kHz16. Ideally, this enables isolation of the genuine CD sig-
nals, but in practice, residual linear polarization components can be superimposed with the sample
response, thereby producing spurious CD-like signals.
A third source of artifacts arises from wavelength-dependent scattering, reflections, or thin-film in-
terference within the sample or substrate, all of which can alter the polarization state and produce
signals unrelated to molecular chirality21. Unlike LDLB, such effects are difficult to disentangle,
as they depend on factors including surface roughness, film thickness, refractive-index contrast,
and measurement geometry, and may yield spectral features that mimic genuine CD signals; thus,
making them impossible to remove. Therefore, careful experimental design, appropriate control
measurements, and rigorous data analysis are essential to reliably disentangle the intrinsic CD
from artifacts.
C.
Understanding and mitigating linear contributions in CD: A Stokes–Mueller
perspective
To quantitatively disentangle the linear and circular contributions in CD measurements, it is
useful to describe the optical response of the sample within the Stokes–Mueller formalism, which
provides a general framework for analysing the interaction between light and anisotropic media.
In this formalism, the polarization state of light is represented by a four-component Stokes vec-
tor that encodes the total intensity as well as the degree of linear and circular polarization. The
effect of an optical element or sample on this polarization state is then described by a Mueller
7


## Page 8


matrix, which linearly transforms the incident Stokes vector into the output Stokes vector. This
approach explicitly accounts for how linear optical properties can influence the detected circular
signal through polarization modulation.
Within this framework, the experimentally measured CD signal does not necessarily correspond
solely to genuine circular dichroism. Instead, it can contain additional contributions arising from
LD, LB, and coupling terms between these effects. These coupling mechanisms originate from
the fact that the polarization state of the probing light evolves as it propagates through anisotropic
media, allowing linear optical anisotropies to convert or mix polarization components that are de-
tected as apparent circular signals.
Previous studies have applied this formalism to mitigate linear artifacts, addressing macroscopic
anisotropy contributions, identify LDLB coupling terms, and integrate microscopic LDLB mod-
elling within Lorentz oscillator models11,22–24. The Mueller matrix is a 4×4 matrix that describes
how an optical systems transforms the Stokes vector of incident light, thereby providing a com-
plete phenomenological description of linear, circular, and depolarizing effects. In principle, the
measured CD signals can be quantitatively modelled through matrix calculations (Supplementary
Note III).
In practice, however, anisotropic thin films present additional challenges. Depolarization, scat-
tering, and linear anisotropies are all embedded in the full Mueller matrix, and extracting reliable
information requires measurement of multiple matrix elements. Partial measurements are gener-
ally insufficient because no single Mueller matrix element can be uniquely assigned to CD when
LDLB are intrinsically coupled25. This limitation is exacerbated in thin film systems whereby they
are compounded by structural anisotropy, surface reflections, and scattering, that can introduce ad-
ditional polarization distortions that can complicate the interpretation of the measured signal.
Although full Mueller polarimetry can, in principle, disentangle these contributions, such ap-
proaches are experimentally demanding and not always accessible. Consequently, whereas the
Stokes-Mueller formalism provides a rigorous theoretical framework for identifying the linear ar-
tifacts in CD measurements, its full experimental implementation is often impractical for routine
measurements of anisotropic thin films. To address this limitation, we adopt a more experimentally
accessible strategy based on a two-stage workflow. Specifically, we combine front/back acquisi-
tion with rotational CD measurements to isolate and diagnose linear anisotropy contributions in the
measured spectra. These complementary measurements do not require full Mueller polarimetry,
providing a practical approach for disentangling genuine CD signals.

spectrum is recorded. The purpose of this step is diagnostic: in anisotropic thin films, the mea-

In this case, if the CD signal changes with cos2θ, especially near the excitonic peaks, that is
a strong indication of linear artifacts, not intrinsic chirality. This step allows us to diagnose
anisotropy contributions before disentangling them.

IV.
EXPERIMENTAL IMPLEMENTATION
In the following, we present our custom-built CD extension for a commercial CD spectrometer
aiming to reduce linear artifacts and provide a step-by-step description of the measurement pro-
cess.
Our CD measurements were performed on a J-1500 spectrophotometer from JASCO, for which
we designed an automated rotation mount as depicted in Figure 3 (a). It consists of a baseplate
(Figure 3 (a) D) that can be screwed to the bottom of the sample compartment of the J-1500 to
prevent movement. A step motor mount located on top of the base plate can be rotated in 45◦steps
as indicated by the black arrow. This allows the mount to be positioned reproducibly in front and

measurements (see Figure 3 (d)). The step motor mount and the base plate are connected with a

hollow shaft allows light transmission. The motor is controlled by a 2-phase SBC-MD-DM860H
driver from Joy-it run by a python script, which is provided in the Supplementary Information.
This allows the user to freely choose both the step size and the maximum azimuthal angle. The
sample is placed on a sample stage, marked as B in Figure 3 (a), consisting of an aluminum base

B.
Example 2 - chiral halide perovskite thin films
Chiral halide perovskites and perovskite-inspired structures30 have emerged as a new paradigm
for the design of chiral functional materials, owing to their tunable chemical composition and
hierarchical structures4. Their outstanding properties render them promising candidates for appli-
cations in optoelectronic devices. Moreover, the perovskite framework can accommodate a wide
variety of chiral organic ligands. By selecting appropriate enantiomeric organic cations, one can
direct the formation of diverse chiral metal-halide structures —- including 0D clusters, 1D chains,
2D and quasi-2D layered structures, as well as 3D networks31.
In this study, thin films of a one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) chiral halide per-
ovskite were fabricated using the prototypical methylbenzylammonium (MBA+) cations in its two
enantiomeric forms: (R)-(+)-MBA+ and (S)-(–)-MBA+. The use of enantiopure organic cations
allows the transfer of molecular chirality to the extended inorganic framework.
The 1D compound, (R/S)-MBAPbBr3, adopts the space group P212121, consisting of face-sharing
[PbBr6]4−octahedra forming an infinite chains oriented along the [002] direction26,32,33. These
inorganic chains are spatially separated by intercalated chiral MBA+ cations, as illustrated in Fig-
ure 6 (a). Each octahedron is structurally asymmetric, exhibiting six distinct Pb–Br bond lengths
and lacking both inversion and mirror symmetry, features that contribute directly to the material’s
chiral nature26,33,34. In comparison, the 2D perovskites, (R/S)-MBA2PbI4, adopt a layered struc-
ture composed of a corner-sharing [PbI6]4−octahedra network, with each octahedron exhibiting
17


## Page 18


six distinct Pb–I bond lengths, and with the layers separated by chiral MBA+ cations, as shown in
Figure S15 (a).
Because MBA+ cations can template both 1D and 2D perovskite phases depending on stoi-
chiometry and processing conditions, we ensured the formation of the correct phase using X-ray
diffraction (XRD) measurements for both systems. For the 1D phase, by comparison with a sim-
ulated powder XRD pattern32, we find the peaks corresponding to the (002) and (004) reflections
of the 1D phase located at 8.63◦and 17.31◦, respectively (See Figure S17). These results are
in agreement with those previously reported by Lu et al. 33 and with our observation of an exci-
tonic peak around ∼320 nm in the absorption spectra, characteristic of the 1D bromide perovskite
framework (Figure 6 (b)).
Meanwhile, for 2D (R)- and (S)-MBA2PbI4, the XRD pattern of the thin films depicted in Fig-
ure S18 matches the one reported by Soares et al. , confirming the formation of the targeted 2D
phase structure35. In addition, the excitonic peak located around ∼498 nm further corroborates the
characteristic signature of 2D iodide perovskites.
Perovskite thin films are typically polycrystalline and frequently exhibit varying degrees of
preferred orientation. As above, structural anisotropy can give rise to significant LD and LB that
corrupt the measured CD signal13. To disentangle these linear optical artifacts from the mea-
sured CD signal, we first determine the angle-dependent CD measurements over a full 0◦to 360◦
rotation with +30◦increments. Both 1D (R)- and (S)-MBAPbBr3 films exhibit CD signals that
vary systematically with rotation angle, displaying a clear cosine dependence at their respective
excitonic peaks (310.2 nm for (R)-MBAPbBr3 and 313.9 nm for (S)-MBAPbBr3; Figures 6 (e)
and S14 (b)). Next, we apply our second strategy by acquiring CD spectra from both the front
and back sides for all the measured angles of (R)- and (S)-MBAPbBr3 and applying Equation 7;
the associated LDLB terms were then calculated using Equation 8. As shown in Figure S12 (b),
1D films display substantial LDLBfb contributions. Notably, the CD front- and back-side spectra
of both systems do not match as expected (see Figures S13 (b) and S14 (a)), indicating strong
optical anisotropy. In such cases, taking the front-back average cannot fully eliminate the linear
artifacts, as averaging alone does not fully cancel these orientation-dependent effects24. Following
the workflow, performing the angle-dependent CD measurements and averaging the CD spectra
over all rotation angles diminish the propagation-dependent LDLB effects in the chiral perovskites
(See Figure 6 (f)) and yield a reliable estimation of the genuine CD13,26.
In contrast, 2D systems exhibit small or negligible linear contributions (LDLBfb) as illustrated

counterparts, the magnitude of the gCD is largely governed by the degree of structural distortion
and crystallographic orientation36–38. As reported, 1D chiral perovskite thin films exhibit moder-

VI.
LIMITATION OF THE WORKFLOW
Although the front/back flipping and azimuthal rotational CD workflow is effective in many
cases, its applicability is fundamentally limited by two factors:
Irreducible optical artifacts During CD measurements, scattering, reflection, or other optical
effects can occur from inhomogeneities in the thin film, such as surface roughness, grain bound-
aries, or thickness variations. These irregularities cause part of the incident light to deviate from
the original propagation direction and often produce signals that differ between the front and back
sides of the film. Such contributions have nothing to do with the CD and are considered as artifacts;
they cannot be removed simply by flipping or rotation; they must be considered independently.
For example, our measurements on the gold film demonstrate that strong scattering at the surface
20


## Page 21


due to high roughness produces an apparent CD signal that is purely artificial. Angle dependent
CD measurements further revealed a cosine dependence, highlighting the presence of such arti-
facts.
Even under an ideal transmission geometry, flipping only removes linear effects that reverse sign
under propagation inversion. It does not compensate for other direction-dependent optical con-
tributions, such as scattering or reflections, meaning that flipping alone cannot guarantee that the
genuine CD is free from all artifacts.
Thin film anisotropy and optical symmetry The workflow further assumes that the optical re-
sponse of the sample is symmetric under propagation reversal. This assumption fails in anisotropic
films. Consider 2D perovskites: although these materials are structurally anisotropic, the term
“anisotropy” can be misleading in the context of CD measurements. CD is typically measured
along the stacking (out-of-plane) direction, along which in-plane processes average out and ap-
pear isotropic. Consequently, the optical response probed in this geometry is largely insensitive to
in-plane anisotropy, even though the material itself remains anisotropic at the microscopic level.
This distinction is critical: the workflow’s ability to disentangle linear artifacts depends on the
optical anisotropy projected along the measurement direction, not on the intrinsic anisotropy of
the material alone.
VII.
CONCLUSION
We have developed a robust workflow which consists of a four-step protocol for reliably ex-
tracting genuine CD spectra from anisotropic chiral thin films. Central to this approach is a
motorized sample stage that enables automated azimuthal rotation and straightforward front-back
measurements. Schematic drawings and the corresponding source code are provided in the Sup-
plementary Information. The reliability of the approach was validated by CD measurements on
different samples.
Our first example highlights the importance of azimuthal rotation to eliminate artifacts arising
from reflection or scattering processes at the surface of a thin Au film. Due to our workflow, the
measured CD signals could be identified as pure artifact-based. In addition, we showed the value
of the front and back approach for extracting the genuine CD response of anisotropic films by
varying the deposition technique of Boc-L-Cysteine on Au substrates. Furthermore, we underline
how the LDLB component and the gCD value can be used for the estimation of the film quality.
21


## Page 22


Our workflow was further demonstrated and can also be applicable for chiral halide perovskite
thin films, where 1D systems exhibit moderate gCD in the range of 10-3, confirming their chi-
roptical activity but readily susceptible to obscuration by linear artifacts, which can be partially
mitigated using our approach. The remaining linear artifacts may arise from scattering or thin-
film interference in 1D structures. In contrast, 2D systems show smaller gCD values (10-4) and
negligible LDLB contributions, with linear artifacts that can be disentangled using the workflow.
Overall, this work establishes practical guidelines and best practices to reliably disentangle gen-
uine CD from the linear artifacts in thin films. The presented approach is broadly applicable
to chiral hybrid perovskites and other low-dimensional materials, enabling reliable interpretation
of CD spectra and to avoid common pitfalls associated with orientation-dependent optical artifacts.

A.
What Mueller matrices describes
Mueller matrices provides the linear relationship between polarization state of an incident light
beam and the polarization state of the light after it passes through optical elements or a sample. In
other words, they give a complete description of how a sample transforms the polarization of light.
This formalism is used together with Stokes vectors, which describe the polarization state of
light in terms of measurable intensities. The connection between Stokes vectors and Mueller
matrices is developed in the following sections.
B.
Electric-field of polarized light
Light propagating along the z-direction can be described by two orthogonal electric-field com-

Ellipsometry Modelling of Boc-Cysteine/Au/sapphire samples
Optical modelling of variable-angle spectroscopic ellipsometry (VASE) data was performed to
extract the dielectric function, film thickness, as well as thickness non-uniformity based on the
depolarization data. For details on the VASE measurements see Methods Section.
The dielectric functions of sapphire and Boc-Cysteine were modelled using a Cauchy function,
as no respectively very little absorption was observed in the measured spectral range. Au was
modelled with a general oscillator model including a Drude term to account for free carriers and
Gaussian functions to account for details in the imaginary part of the dielectric function. The
results are depicted in Figure S10. For the sapphire and Au/sapphire reference samples, no signif-
icant depolarization was observed, which is referenced in Figures S11d and S11e. For the samples
Au1 – Au3, however, depolarization partially reached 100 % as depicted in Figures S11a - c. De-
polarization is typically caused by a high degree of surface roughness or thickness non-uniformity
as well as bandwidth effects of the monochromator. Thickness non-uniformity considers thickness
variations on the lateral scale smaller than but close to the size of the measurement spot. For the
optical models of the samples Au1 – Au3, the thickness non-uniformity had to be considered to
fit the depolarization. This is done by defining a number of equally spaced points across a normal
distribution of the thickness varied by a percentile of the total film thickness. For each point the
optical model is calculated, the results get convoluted into the final model, and their deviation
yields an estimate for the degree of depolarization. The percentile of thickness variation serves as
the fitting parameter here. Surface roughness is likely present as well, but its inclusion led to no
significant improvement of the fit and yielded inconsistent values and therefore was omitted.
The average film thickness of Boc-Cysteine is 460 ± 30 nm, 340 ± 20 nm, and 190 ± 10 nm for
Au1 – Au3, respectively. The average percentile thickness non-uniformity of Au1 is 24±4 %. For
the other two samples the parameter reached 100 %, indicating more severe non-idealities than
our optical model can account for. Comparing these results to the plots of the depolarization in
Figures S11 (a)-(c) leads to a similar conclusion. The line shape is only roughly described by
the data generated from the optical model. However, for Au1 most of the sharp features in the
depolarization are at least approximated. For the other two cases the features are much broader
and offset compared to the optical model.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
A.
Sample Preparation
1.
Deposition of molecular thin films
The 30 nm Au films were sputtered on double-side polished c-plane sapphire ordered from
Siegert Wafer. The substrates were first cleaned in isopropanol in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min.
Afterwards, residual organic contaminations were removed by plasma etching in a Zepto low-
pressure plasma etching chamber by diener electronics with 100% power and 5 mbar pressure for
one minute. Following this, one of the clean substrates was transferred into the solution containing
20 mg/ml of Boc-L-Cysteine and dip coated for 1 h (Au3). The second sample was drop coated

immediately (Au1) whereas a third one was after a waiting time of 1 h with an identical solution to
the dip coating (Au2). Boc-L-Cysteine was ordered from Sigma Aldrich and the Boc-D-Cysteine
from Iris Biotech.
2.
Experimental Procedure Chiral Perovskites: Synthesis and fabrication
Reagents.
(S)-(-)-1-phenylethylamine, (R)-(+)-1-phenylethylamine, and hydrobromic acid,

crons powder) was purchased from Acros Organics. Lead(II) bromide, PbBr2 (99.998%) and
used solvents were purchased from Fisher Scientific. Hydroiodic acid, HI (57% in water, dis-
tilled, unstabilized) was purchased from Fisher Scientific. Lead(II) iodide, PbI2 (99.99%, trace
metals basis) was purchased from TCI Chemicals, Tributyl phosphate (TBP) and Dimethyl sul-

dichloromethane (DCM) were purchased from Honeywell.
(a) 1D chiral perovskites:
Synthesis of (R)-MBABr and (S)-MBABr salts. 5 g of R- or S-MBA (41.3 mmol) was dis-
solved in 40 mL of anhydrous DCM in a round-bottom flask. To this solution, 4.746 mL of HBr
was added, and the mixture was stirred vigorously at room temperature for 7 h. Afterward, cold
diethyl ether was introduced, causing a precipitate to form. The solid was collected by filtration
and dried overnight under high vacuum.
Synthesis of (R)-MBAPbBr3 and (S)-MBAPbBr3 crystals. The synthesis of crystals of (R)-
MBAPbBr3 and (S)-MBAPbBr3 used to prepare the precursor solutions for thin film deposition
was based on a reported procedure33. 223.2 mg of PbO (1 mmol) and 363.6 mg (3 mmol) of R- or
S-MBA were mixed with 6 mL of HBr solution in a glass vial. The vial was placed in a metal block
on a heating plate, and the mixture was subsequently dissolved by heating at 80◦C for 10 min. The
heating plate was turned off, and the solution was allowed to cool down to room temperature,
resulting in the formation of colourless needle-like crystals. The crystals were isolated using
vacuum filtration, washed with copious amounts of dry diethyl ether, and subsequently dried under
a high vacuum overnight.
Thin-film deposition. Precursor solutions for 1D (R)-MBAPbBr3 and (S)-MBAPbBr3 thin-

sonicated in DMSO, deionised water, acetone, and isopropanol, respectively (15 min each) and
treated with UV/ozone for 15 min prior to deposition. The films were prepared in a N2-filled
glovebox by injecting 40 µL of precursor solution and spinning for 2000 rpm at 2000 rpm/s for 20

CHARACTERISATION METHODS
Circular Dichroism Spectroscopy. The CD measurements were performed on a J-1500 CD-
spectrophotometer from Jasco equipped with the rotation stage developed in this work. The CD
spectra of molecular films on Au and the pure Au films were collected at 200 nm/min scan speed,
with an integration time (D.I.T.) of 2.0 s, a data pitch of 0.1 nm and a bandwidth of 1 nm. The
perovskite measurements were collected at 200 nm/min scan speed, with a D.I.T. of 2.0 s, a data

pitch of 0.1 nm and a bandwidth of 1 nm.
Atomic Force Microscopy. The Atomic Force Microscopy measurements were performed on
a Park systems XE100 AFM in non-contact mode under ambient conditions.
Optical microscopy. The optical microscopy was performed on a Zeiss Jenalab pol-30-G0527
optical microscope. To capture the pictures, a ToupTek E3ISPM 20000 camera was placed at the
top right ocular and controlled with the ToupView software.
Ellipsometry.
The variable-angle spectroscopic ellipsometry (VASE) was performed on a
M2000 T-Solar ellipsometer, and optical modelling was executed using the CompleteEASE soft-
ware, both by J. A. Woollam. VASE data were acquired at a minimum of three positions on each
sample at 7 different angles from 45 – 75◦in steps of 5◦and in a spectral range of 0.7 – 5.0 eV. A
spot size of 200 – 300 µm, depending on the angle of incidence (AOI), was enabled by focusing
the optics. As the M2000 T-Solar utilizes modulation of the incident light polarization by a ro-
tating compensator, it is sensitive to the degree of depolarization. In order to perform the optical
modelling of the samples Au1 – Au3, additional measurements and corresponding optical models
of the substrate, i.e. a clean sapphire sample and a sample of Au/sapphire, were implemented.
X-ray Diffraction. XRD measurements on thin films were performed at room temperature in an
ambient atmosphere on a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer with parallel beam geometry using
a Göbel mirror and CuKα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA), with an energy-dispersive one dimensional
detector (Lynxeye). The scans were performed in a coupled 2θ/θ continuous mode from 2θ =
2◦-35◦.
