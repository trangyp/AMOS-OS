ABSTRACT
Aims. We aim to understand the progenitor channels of CCSNe via a statistical study of the ages of their environments.
Methods. We compiled a large and minimally biased sample of 129 CCSNe discovered by untargeted wide-field transient surveys and
with archival VLT/MUSE integral-field-unit spectroscopy. We measured the local Hα luminosity within a 300-pc aperture centered
on the SN explosion site as an empirical proxy for the environmental age.
Results. We find that the environments of Type II(P), IIb and Ib SNe do not show a significant age difference while Type Ic SNe are
located in systematically younger environments than the other types (i.e. II ≈IIb ≈Ib > Ic). This is inconsistent with some previous
reports of monotonically younger CCSNe environments with increasing envelope stripping (II > IIb > Ib > Ic).
Conclusions. Our result suggests that Type Ic SNe have much younger and more massive progenitors than the other CCSN types
and they likely originate from a distinct progenitor channel. The distinction between Types II(P), IIb and Ib SNe is insensitive to
progenitor mass and mainly due to the different binary separation; in contrast, Type Ic SNe predominantly require much higher-mass
progenitors accompanied by close companions with large mass ratios and/or much stronger stellar wind that depends sensitively on
progenitor mass.

1. Introduction
Core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) mark the terminal explo-
sions of massive stars (MZAMS ≳8 M⊙) and play key roles
in stellar and galactic evolution. They inject ∼1051 erg of ki-
netic energy into the interstellar medium, drive turbulence and
feedback, return newly synthesized heavy elements and dust to
their host galaxies, and shape chemical enrichment and further
star formation (Smartt 2009). CCSNe also connect to multi-
messenger astrophysics as potential sources of neutrinos, cos-
mic rays, and gravitational-wave signals from compact-remnant
formation and asymmetric explosions (e.g., Mirizzi et al. 2016;
Helder et al. 2012; Ott 2009; Abbott et al. 2020).
Observationally, SNe are classified into Type I and Type II
by the presence or absence of hydrogen in their spectra, re-
spectively (Minkowski 1941; Filippenko 1997; Turatto 2003;
Gal-Yam 2017). Among CCSNe, hydrogen-rich events (e.g.,
Type IIP) typically arise from red supergiants that retain most
of their H envelopes until core collapse (Smartt 2009). In con-
trast, stripped-envelope (SE) SNe include Types IIb, Ib, and Ic:

Type IIb show hydrogen only at early phases, Type Ib lack hy-
drogen, and Type Ic lack both hydrogen and conspicuous helium
features. These spectroscopic differences are widely interpreted
as outcomes of envelope stripping, making SESNe a uniquely
sensitive probe of mass-loss processes in massive star evolution.
A key question related to SESNe is how massive stars lose
their H- and He-rich envelopes before explosion. Two lead-
ing channels are commonly invoked: a single-star pathway in
which line-driven winds produce massive Wolf-Rayet (WR) pro-
genitors, and a binary pathway in which Roche-lobe overflow
and/or common-envelope evolution strip the envelope over a
broader range of progenitor initial masses (e.g., Crowther 2007;
Vink 2017a; Smartt 2009; Yoon 2015; Podsiadlowski et al.
1992; Dewi et al. 2002; Dewi & Pols 2003). Direct identifica-
tion offers the most stringent constraints on SN progenitors, but
for SESNe this is observationally difficult, owing to distance,
crowding, extinction, and the intrinsic faintness/compactness
of the stripped progenitors. The limited sample of progen-
itors detected so far is all consistent with the binary pro-
genitor channel and does not provide unambiguous evidence
for single WR progenitors (e.g., Cao et al. 2013; Maund et al.

Environmental analysis provides a complementary avenue
for constraining CCSN progenitors, because massive stars pref-
erentially form in clustered environments whose stellar pop-
ulations share, to first order, a common age and metallic-
ity. Metallicity, Z, is particularly important because higher-
metallicity stars develop stronger line-driven winds, which can
more efficiently strip their outer envelopes, whereas at lower
metallicity binary interaction may become the dominant strip-
ping channel (Vink et al. 2001; Eldridge et al. 2008). Previ-
ous studies have investigated the environmental metallicities
of different SN types, but no consensus has yet emerged re-
garding whether their metallicity distributions are systemat-
ically distinct (e.g., Sanders et al. 2012; Galbany et al. 2018;
Kuncarayakti et al. 2018; Pessi et al. 2023). In Xi et al. (2025)
(hereafter (Paper I)), we constructed a minimally biased, spa-
tially resolved CCSN environmental sample from untargeted-
survey discoveries with archival VLT/MUSE observations. After
considering stochastic sampling effects, we found no clear type
dependence in the environmental metallicity distributions.

Some studies have reported a monotonic progression toward
younger environments with increasing envelope stripping (i.e., II
> IIb > Ib > Ic; e.g., Anderson et al. 2012; Galbany et al. 2018;
Kuncarayakti et al. 2018). By contrast, other works suggest that
SNe Ic occur in significantly younger environments, while the
other CCSN types exhibit broadly comparable environmental
ages (i.e., II ≈IIb ≈Ib > Ic; e.g., Sun et al. 2023a; Fang et al.
2019). Theoretically, a recent population synthesis predicts
an age sequence of II > IIb > Ib > Ic at Z > 0.2 Z⊙while at
lower metallicities the sequence changes into Ib > II > IIb (e.g.,
Souropanis et al. 2025). It is also important to note that most of
the observational studies of SN environments rely on samples in
the metal-rich local Universe.
Estimating ages for CCSN environments is challenging and
subject to various uncertainties. Integral-field unit (IFU) spec-
troscopy is particularly valuable for CCSN environmental stud-
ies. Compared to traditional long-slit spectroscopy, IFU provides
spatially resolved spectroscopy for the host galaxy, enabling a
two-dimensional view of the complex SN environment. In prac-
tice, this allows one to identify and extract a physically moti-
vated spectrum at (or around) the SN explosion site, to separate
the nearest star-forming clump from surrounding diffuse emis-
sion, and to quantify gradients and mixing that would otherwise
be folded into a single slit measurement. These capabilities are
essential for robustly characterizing the immediate stellar popu-
lation most plausibly linked to the progenitor.
In addition to methodology, sample-selection effects may
also introduce substantial biases into the inferred demographics
of CCSN environments. In order to maximize discovery rates,
early SN searches often target luminous galaxies with high star
formation rates. This preferentially samples younger environ-
ments and may introduce systematic biases into the resulting en-
vironmental statistics (Tremonti et al. 2004). In contrast, mod-
ern wide-field surveys such as the Zwicky Transient Facility
(ZTF; Bellm et al. 2019), the Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last
Alert System (ATLAS; Jedicke et al. 2012), and, in the near fu-
ture, the Legacy Survey of Space and Time at the Vera C. Rubin
Observatory (LSST; Ivezi´c et al. 2019) discover transients with-
out pre-selecting galaxies, thereby reducing this bias in SN sam-
ples.
Building on Paper I, we use the same minimally biased
CCSN sample discovered by untargeted surveys and observed
with VLT/MUSE IFU spectroscopy, and extend our environmen-
tal analysis from metallicity to age. We present the environmen-
tal age distributions for different CCSN types and test whether
they are statistically distinct.
This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe
the methodology, including sample construction and the adopted
environmental age indicators. In Section 3, we present the result-
ing age distributions for different CCSN types. In Section 4, we
compare our findings with previous studies and discuss the im-
plications and potential systematics. Finally, we summarize our
conclusions in Section 5.
2. Methods
2.1. Sample selection
Our sample selection and data-reduction procedures follow those
presented in Paper I and we summarize the key steps here. We
compiled a SN sample from the Transient Name Server1 (TNS)
and the Open Supernova Catalog (OSC; Guillochon et al. 2017)
and cross-matched these transients with archival VLT/MUSE
observations. To mitigate potential selection biases, we imposed
two principal requirements:
1. SN discovery: To minimize host-galaxy selection effects, we
restrict our sample to SNe discovered by untargeted, wide-
field transient surveys (e.g., ZTF, ATLAS, or other compara-
ble untargeted searches).
2. Data availability: given the wide field of view of the MUSE
IFU spectrograph, distant and low-mass galaxies with small
angular diameters are less likely to be observed; we apply
a redshfit cut of z ≤0.02, within which the sample is quite
representative of the SN population in the local Universe and
the potential bias could be small (see Fig. 2 in Paper I).
Starting from the sample presented in Paper I, we further
removed a number of events for which the SNe still remained
bright at the epoch of the MUSE observations to avoid the con-
tamination of SN radiation in the environmental analysis at the
explosion site. After this manual vetting, the final sample com-
prises 129 SNe: 84 Type II(P)[including SNe IIP and Type II
events without a secure subtype, but most of the latter should be
of Type IIP according to the SN fraction in the local Universe;
(Smartt et al. 2009; Smith et al. 2011)], 14 Type IIb, 17 Type Ib,
and 14 Type Ic. The vast majority of these events are located in
environments with metallicities of approximately 0.2–1 Z⊙.
2.2. Age indicator
A useful age indicator is crucial for comparing CCSN en-
vironments across types. Multiple age diagnostics have been
presented in the literature, including Hα equivalent width
(EW) (Leloudas et al. 2011; Kuncarayakti et al. 2013a,b, 2018;
Galbany et al. 2018; Pessi et al. 2023), Hα-based associa-
tion statistics (NCR-like measures; Anderson & James 2008,
2009; Anderson et al. 2012), and detailed modeling of the
photoionization-sensitive emission-line ratios (Sun et al. 2021,
2022, 2023b). Each of these approaches, however, has its own

limitations. Hα EW can be diluted by the continuum from older
and unrelated stellar populations (see the discussion of Sun et al.
2021). Normalized Cumulative Rank (NCR) statistics relies on
the relative rank of Hα brightness at the SN position within the
host galaxy, so it is affected by the level of star-forming ac-
tivities in other parts of the galaxy. Detailed modeling of the
emission lines can derive very accurate ages for the ionizing
stellar population, but it is computationally expensive and can
suffer from degeneracies without complementary information of
the SN environment (such as high-spatial-resolution imaging of
the ionizing stellar populations; e.g. see Sun et al. 2021, 2022,
2023). After a series of tests, we adopt the local Hα luminos-
ity, measured within a fixed physical aperture around the SN,
as an indicator of progenitor age. Specifically, we adopt a phys-
ical radius of 300 pc, motivated by the characteristic scales of
clustered massive-star formation and OB associations (Wright
2020; Garmany 1994), the extended sizes of giant H II regions
(Kennicutt & Hodge 1986; Pleuss et al. 2000), and the possi-
bility that some progenitors may be displaced by several hun-
dred parsecs before explosion as runaway or walkaway stars
(Renzo et al. 2019; Wagg et al. 2025). Statistically, higher local
Hα luminosities on average indicate younger environments and
hence higher progenitor masses, and vice versa.
Following this idea, we processed the VLT/MUSE dat-
acubes retrieved from the ESO Data Archive2 with the ifua-
nal package (Lyman et al. 2018). Each cube was first corrected
for foreground Milky Way extinction using the recalibration
of Schlafly & Finkbeiner (2011) and the Cardelli et al. (1989)
(CCM89) reddening law with RV = 3.1, and then shifted to the
rest frame using redshifts reported by TNS/OSC. We co-added
all spaxels within a circular aperture of radius 300 pc centered
on the SN position, and the stellar continuum was fitted and re-
moved with the starlight package (Cid Fernandes et al. 2005),
leaving only the nebular emission lines for subsequent analysis.

3. Results
Fig. 2 shows the cumulative distributions of the local Hα lu-
minosity at the explosion sites of different CCSN types, and
their corresponding mean and median values are summarized in
Tab. 1. The detailed SN sample and the full list of measured re-
sults are available online. The local Hα luminosities span a range
of log(LHα/erg s−1) ≈37–41. With the exception of Type Ic, the
other CCSN types display very similar distributions with compa-
rable central tendencies. By contrast, Type Ic environments are
systematically brighter in local Hα luminosity by ∼0.5 dex. We
also repeated this analysis after dividing the sample into several
metallicity bins, and found qualitatively similar results in each
bin.
To account for the stochastic sampling effect, we merged the
non-Ic types (i.e.Type II(P) + IIb + Ib) into a single reference
sample (blue curve in Fig. 2) and constructed its confidence en-
velopes via the bootstrap resampling method. Specifically, we
generated 104 mock realizations by drawing, with replacement,
N = 14 events per realization—matching the Ic sample size—
from the combined non-Ic reference distribution. The resulting
1–3σ confidence bands are shown as gray shading in Fig. 2. The
cumulative curves for Types II(P), IIb, and Ib are fully consistent
within 1σ uncertainties, while the Type Ic curve shows a modest
excursion beyond 1σ yet still within 2σ uncertainties. This pat-
tern suggests a mild preference for higher local Hα luminosities
in Type Ic SN environments.
As an alternative approach, we also bootstrap–resampled the
non–Ic reference sample while varying the draw size N and, for
each N, computed the distribution of the sample mean of local
Hα luminosity. The resulting 1σ, 2σ, and 3σ bands as a func-
tion of N are shown in Fig. 3, showing that the uncertainties
decrease with increasing N. The mean local Hα luminosities for
Types II(P), IIb and Ib are all consistent within 1σ stochastic-
sampling uncertainties, while that for Type Ic has a 2σ differ-
ence. The difference is still beyond the 1σ significance level if
we consider the measurement uncertainty of the mean local Hα
luminosity.
To assess whether the Type Ic offset is statistically mean-
ingful, we applied two–sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) and
Anderson–Darling (AD) tests and the results are summarized as
a p-value heatmap in Fig. 4. Here, the p–value quantifies the
probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as ob-
served under the null hypothesis that the two samples are drawn
from the same parent distribution. In the KS tests comparing
Type Ic against the other types, the p–values fall in the range
of p ≃0.2–0.3; by contrast, the AD tests yield lower p–values,
with p ≃0.1 for several pairwise comparisons; when testing
Type Ic against the pooled non-Ic reference sample (II(P) + IIb
+ Ib), we obtain a p-value as low as 0.07.
The different results of the two tests are readily understood.
The KS statistic is driven solely by the maximum pointwise sep-
aration between the empirical cumulative distribution functions
(CDFs) and can have limited power when two distributions dif-
fer mainly by a broad, global shift. The AD statistic, in contrast,
integrates the squared differences between the cumulative distri-
butions with variance weighting, giving additional sensitivity to
systematic offsets and tail behavior. As seen in Fig. 2, the Type Ic
distribution is systematically displaced to higher Hα luminosi-
ties relative to the other types; accordingly, the AD test reports a
more significant deviation than the KS test.

We also note that the choice of a 300 pc aperture may intro-
duce an additional source of uncertainty. This scale should be
regarded as a compromise: a smaller aperture would better pre-
serve the locality of the measurement, but may miss emission
physically associated with the progenitor because of spatial off-
sets, finite resolution, or progenitor migration; a larger aperture
would be more inclusive, but at the cost of increased contam-
ination from unrelated surrounding stellar populations and Hα
emission. Although we do not explicitly quantify this aperture-
dependent effect here, the use of a uniform physical scale for all
objects ensures that the relative comparisons across the sample
remain internally consistent.
In summary, our results suggest that Type Ic SN environ-
ments are marginally brighter in local Hα luminosity than those
of the other CCSN types, while Types II(P), IIb and Ib do not
have any significant difference. We emphasize here that the local
Hα luminosity is only an indicator instead of a precise measure-
ment of the progenitor age. The mapping between Hα luminos-
ity and progenitor age is only statistically meaningful and suffers
from uncertainties such as chance alignment, leakage of ionizing
photons, and variations in the detailed star-formation history. As
a result, the underlying difference in progenitor age and mass be-
tween Type Ic and other SN types may be even more significant.

4.2. Implications
Our work shows that the progenitor age and inferred initial mass
are statistically indistinguishable among Types II(P), IIb, and
Ib, whereas Type Ic SNe preferentially occur in systematically
younger (and hence more massive) environments. This points
to a distinct progenitor channel for Type Ic compared with the
bulk of CCSNe. Our sample is drawn from the local Universe
(z ≤0.02) and has a metallicity range of 0.2 −1.0 Z⊙. (see Pa-
per I).
Souropanis et al. (2025) investigated CCSN progenitor chan-
nels with the POSYDON binary population-synthesis frame-
work (Fragos et al. 2023; Andrews et al. 2025), spanning a broad
range of initial masses and metallicities (see also Claeys et al.
2011; Eldridge et al. 2017; Zapartas et al. 2017). Under their de-
fault assumptions, they find that most Type IIb and Type Ib SNe
originate from the initially more massive primary star in a binary
(accounting for ∼85% at solar metallicity), with a much smaller
contribution from the secondary.
In contrast, most Type II SNe arise from progenitors that
are effectively single (∼45.6%), with substantial contributions
from binary mergers (∼32.4%), consistent with the high expec-
tations from theoretical predictions (Podsiadlowski et al. 1992;
Zapartas et al. 2017; Schneider et al. 2025) and with arising ob-
servational evidence of their existence (Bostroem et al. 2023;
Niu et al. 2026). Only a minor fraction from the initially less
massive secondary in disrupted binaries (∼18.0%). These re-
sults imply that for moderately massive stars (8–25 M⊙), which

In our observations, the environmental age distributions of
Types Ib, IIb, and II show no obvious separation, whereas
Type Ic appears younger. This is not fully consistent with
Souropanis et al. (2025), who predict an IMF-weighted median
explosion-age sequence of Type Ic < Ib < IIb < II at Z >
0.2 Z⊙. A plausible explanation is that an Hα-based age in-
dicator is mainly sensitive to the most distinct, youngest Ic
population, but is not sufficiently discriminating to robustly re-
solve the much smaller offsets among Types Ib, IIb, and II. We
also note that overlapping mass ranges do not imply identical
IMF-weighted mass distributions: in Souropanis et al. (2025),
the IMF-weighted populations at solar metallicity still predict
a modest shift in the median explosion age between Types IIb
and Ib despite their strong overlap. It therefore remains unclear
whether such a small offset can be recovered with an Hα-based
environmental age diagnostic, or whether it is effectively ab-
sorbed into the systematic uncertainties of the method.
It is worth noting that the type boundaries inferred from bi-
nary population synthesis can evolve with metallicity, and the
corresponding conclusions may differ when considering a wider
metallicity baseline. In Souropanis et al. (2025), as metallicity
decreases to < 0.2 Z⊙(roughly the lower end probed by our sam-
ple), the separation thresholds dividing different SN types shift
substantially, even though the overall parameter space produc-
ing stripped-envelope SNe (IIb and Ib) remains broadly similar.
This highlights how metallicity governs both stellar winds and
binary interactions, thereby shaping the effective boundaries be-
tween different outcomes (see Souropanis et al. 2025). A salient
trend in their models is that the IIb progenitor distribution shifts
toward higher masses at lower metallicity, while the Ib progen-
itor distribution shifts in the opposite direction. Consequently, a
given progenitor mass may yield a Type Ib SN at higher metal-
licity but a Type IIb SN at lower metallicity (and vice versa),
depending on how metallicity and binary interactions. Taken to-
gether, this suggests that an apparent “progenitor-mass insen-
sitivity” across SESN types in multimetallicity samples could
partly reflect metallicity-dependent effects washing out underly-
ing population differences. While our environments extend down
to only ∼0.2 Z⊙, and the metallicity trends emphasized in their
Fig. 3 may be modest over this interval, weaker winds than
adopted in that study could render such metallicity-dependent
behavior relevant even at comparatively higher metallicities.
In addition, the inferred Type Ic progenitor channel depends
sensitively on the classification criteria adopted in theoretical
studies, particularly on how much helium can remain “hidden”
in Ib/c explosions. In Souropanis et al. (2025), the default crite-
rion is deliberately conservative: Type Ic progenitors must not
only have low helium ejecta masses, but also low surface he-
lium and nitrogen abundances. Under this assumption, Type Ic
SNe are produced predominantly by very massive progenitors,
consistent with our inference that Ic SNe favor younger, higher-
mass environments. By contrast, their alternative criterion clas-
sifies Ib/Ic events using the helium ejecta mass alone, without
imposing the additional surface He/N constraints. This leads to
a bimodal Type Ic progenitor distribution, in which Ic SNe can
arise both from very massive stars and from progenitors below
∼13 M⊙, with the lower-mass channel becoming important at
low metallicity.
Beyond binary interaction, stellar-wind mass loss may also
contribute to producing Type Ic SNe, but its magnitude re-
mains highly uncertain and can alter the terminal envelope
structure and hence the final SN type (e.g., Woosley 2019;
Fullerton et al. 2006; Vink 2017b). Fang et al. (2019) proposed
a hybrid envelope-stripping scenario in which the H envelope
is removed primarily through (largely mass-insensitive) binary
interaction, while the He-rich envelope is further eroded mainly
by (more mass-sensitive) winds from the post-interaction helium
star (see also Sun et al. 2023a). In this picture, the IIb/Ib distinc-
tion is set chiefly by binary separation, whereas the Ib/Ic dis-
tinction is driven largely by the progenitor mass through its con-
trol of helium-star wind mass loss (Vink 2017b; Dutta & Klencki
2024; Yungelson et al. 2024). This framework can also ac-
commodate our empirical result. However, the wind mass-loss
rates of helium stars remain poorly constrained observation-
ally (Pols & Dewi 2002; Vink 2017b; Moriya & Yoon 2022;
Drout et al. 2023; Götberg et al. 2023), and therefore the quan-
titative role of winds in the origin of Type Ic SNe still awaits
further investigation.
5. Conclusions
In this work, we investigate the age of environments of a large
and minimally biased sample of CCSNe, which are discov-
ered by untargeted wide-field transient surveys and with archival
VLT/MUSE IFU spectroscopy. We use the local, extinction-
corrected Hα luminosity measured at the explosion site as an
age indicator to compare the environmental age distributions of
Types II(P), IIb, Ib and Ic SNe. We find that Types II(P), IIb and
Ib SN environments have very similar local Hα luminosities and
ages while the Type Ic SN environments have brighter local Hα
luminosities and younger ages (i.e. II(P) ≈IIb ≈Ib > Ic, in terms
of age). This difference is significant at the 1σ level and cannot
be fully explained by stochastic sampling effects. Our result does
not support previous claims that CCSNe reside in monotonically
younger environments with increasing envelope stripping (i.e. II
> IIb > Ib > Ic).
Our result suggests that the progenitor age and mass are
statistically indistinguishable for Types II(P), IIb and Ib, while
the Type Ic SNe have systematically younger and more mas-
sive progenitors. This is consistent with the recent binary popu-
lation synthesis carried out by Souropanis et al. (2025), who find
that, in high-metallicity environments,the distinction between
Types II, IIb and Ib is mainly due to binary separation while
Type Ic SNe originate predominantly from more massive pro-
genitors accompanied by close companions with extreme mass
ratios so that their helium envelope can be stripped by binary in-
teraction; we emphasize that this conclusion may hold primarily
at solar and sub-solar high metallicities, and may differ in sub-
stantially lower-metallicity regimes. Our result may also be ex-
plained by a hybrid envelope-stripping mechanism as proposed
by Fang et al. (2019), in which the hydrogen envelope stripping
is dominated by the mass-insensitive binary interaction while
the helium envelope stripping is due to the mass-sensitive stellar
wind of the post-binary-interaction helium star.
