wave transmission through the vocal tract
R´emi Blandin, Martin Laabs, Rudolf von B¨unau, Bryn Lloyd, Silvia Farcito, Denys Nikolayev, Gabriela Hossu,
Peter Birkholz, and Dirk Plettemeier,
Abstract—This study experimentally validates a numerical
model of electromagnetic propagation through the human head
during the pronunciation of different vowels, with the goal of
improving our understanding of the underlying physical phenom-
ena. A realistic finite element model was created from magnetic
resonance images acquired while pronouncing the vowels /a/,
/i/, and /u/. The model was validated against scattering matrix
measurements obtained from two subjects whose geometries were
modeled. Despite several potential sources of discrepancy, the
simulations and measurements showed good qualitative agree-
ment, confirming the validity of the approach. Similar trans-
mission coefficient patterns were observed across subjects for
the same vowels. Within the investigated frequency range of (1-
6 GHz), the electric field exhibited a Mie scattering pattern. Local
minima and maxima in the transmission coefficient, character-
izing different articulatory configurations, were correlated with
local variations in the electric field amplitude. The transmission
coefficient’s shape results from an interplay between resonance
patterns and antenna placement, while the degree of mouth
opening influences the shape of scattering modes. Although
technically challenging, this numerical approach proved effective
for studying electromagnetic propagation in the human head. The

I. INTRODUCTION
Radio frequencies (RF) have proven to be an efficient tool
for investigating body structure and movements, as demon-
strated by a review on the use of RF to observe the human
body [1] and another one more specifically targeting vital signs
detection [2], such as heartbeat and respiration [3] [4]. Beyond
these applications, RF technology is also utilized in imaging
[5], [6], particularly for breast tissues to detect cancer [7].
One notable application within this domain is speech move-
ment tracking, which is the focus of the present study. The con-
cept of using an RF system to sense articulation, often referred
to as radar speech, was first introduced by Holzrichter et al.
[8], [9]. The application of this concept to the generation of a
substitute voice is represented in Fig. 1.
This innovative approach presents a promising solution for
silent speech interfaces (SSI), which aim to recover speech
in the absence of the acoustic signal. SSI systems utilize
coexistent, non-audible (bio)signals that are generated during
speech production, even when the acoustic signal is absent.
These technologies rely on a wide variety of methods, as
illustrated in several reviews [10], [11].
SSI serve a multitude of purposes, including voice restora-
tion for laryngectomy patients [12], [13], enabling private con-
versations in public areas, and facilitating communication in
noisy environments [10]. Despite their potential, most current
techniques are either invasive or impractical for real-world
applications. Consequently, practical applications outside of
research settings remain limited and largely anecdotal.
The newly developing radar speech method offers a promis-
ing technique to overcome these limitations. It is one of the
most suitable approaches for practical applications due to its
non-invasive nature and reliability, making it a viable option
for future advancements in SSI based technologies.
Most studies on radar speech have concentrated on contact-
less technologies that utilize antennas positioned at a distance
from the speaker [14]–[17]. However, these methods primarily
capture external changes in facial geometry, which can often
be detected with better signal quality, for example, using
optical depth cameras [18]. In fact, a study on contactless tech-
nologies for measuring heart rate and respiration demonstrated

To address the limitation of impedance mismatch, some
studies have explored placing antennas in direct contact with
the subject’s skin [20], [21], as illustrated in Figs. 1a and
3. This approach enhances the penetration of electromagnetic
waves into the head, allowing for the capture of information
regarding internal structures, such as the tongue. Articula-
tory movements alter the resonance patterns of the electro-
magnetic field, as shown in Fig. 1b, which in turn affects
the transmission spectrum, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. These
changes in the transmission spectrum can be utilized to train
a neural network. The network can then recognize speech
and generate a synthetic substitute voice, as demonstrated in
Fig. 1d. While there are relatively few studies on contact
radar speech, the results are promising. For instance, one study
achieved a recognition accuracy of 99.17% for a command-
word recognition task conducted across multiple sessions with
a single speaker [21]. An accuracy of 88.87% was achieved
when using sessions from two different speakers. In a sub-
sequent study involving eight subjects, similar performance
was reported –up to 97.78% for a single subject and 88.94%
across multiple subjects [22]. Another investigation focused
on continuous speech recognition for real time application,
employing phoneme recognition. This approach yielded a
phoneme error rate of 45.62% which was 15% higher than
that obtained with a benchmark acoustic feature set [23].
Additionally, one study examined the combination of radar
signals with optical palatography to enhance the robustness
of command word recognition tasks. The radar-based system
alone achieved an accuracy of 85.40%, compared to 66.99%
for optical palatography alone, while combining both modali-
ties resulted in a slightly higher accuracy of 86.55% [24]. This
technology is also the foundation of a commercial application
under development: the Promise-AI project, which funded this
work, aims to explore the technological basis for potential
speech prostheses using this technology. Therefore, our focus
is on this particular type of radar speech technology.
Before this study, the physical phenomena underlying this
technology were poorly understood and optimization efforts
were largely conducted through trial and error without a
deep comprehension of the underlying processes [21], [22],
[25]. Furthermore, there was no simulation of the physical
phenomena involved, which would enable reproducible testing
of the technology.
Therefore, the goal of this study is to develop and ex-
perimentally validate an accurate simulation framework for
the physical phenomena associated with radar speech. This
framework will enhance our understanding of the operational

principles of this emerging technology and facilitate its devel-

Numerical simulations have been extensively employed to
understand and optimize RF technologies used on or near the
body. They are particularly valuable for evaluating the user
exposure and the specific absorption rate (SAR) [27]–[30].
Additionally, these simulations are applied in more specialized
contexts, such as evaluating a concept for the early diagnosis
of Alzheimer’s disease [31] and detecting brain strokes using
an antenna array [32].
While finite difference methods have been widely used due
to their straightforward grid definition from medical images
[27], some studies have favored numerical methods that rely on
unstructured grids, such as the finite elements method (FEM).
These methods offer a more accurate description of anatomical
details [28]–[30]. Given the high accuracy requirements for
studying speech, FEM was a relevant choice for our objec-
tives. Consequently, we selected the Ansys High-Frequency
Structure Simulator (HFSS) [33] as our simulation tool.
Bow-tie dipole antennas are appreciated for their broad-
band capabilities, simple and easy-to-fabricate design, stable
directivity pattern, and consistent polarization. These features,
combined with their geometric suitability for attachment on the
body, make them a common choice for biomedical applications
[34]–[36]. For these reasons, we selected this type of antenna
design for our measurements.
We created detailed and accurate three-dimensional (3D)
models of human heads based on magnetic resonance imaging
(MRI) for three distinct vowels: /a/, /i/, and /u/. These vowels
represent the most extreme vocal tract positions, thereby
covering the full range of variability. To achieve this, we
acquired MRI scans of two subjects pronouncing these vowels.
The MRI scans were segmented to identify various tis-
sues, including gray matter, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid,
internal air, skull, mandible, teeth, and eyes. The resulting
segmentation masks were converted into 3D surface meshes,
which were importable into HFSS using a custom-made pro-
gram. From these 3D models, we constructed numerical FEM
models.
We simulated the propagation of electromagnetic waves
through these numerical models and validated the simulations
by measuring the scattering matrix with a vector network
analyzer (VNA) on the same subjects. Bow-tie antennas were
attached to each cheek, positioned near the lips, as this location
has been shown to be favorable [21], [22], [25]. The scattering
matrices obtained from the measurements were compared with
the simulated data, and the electric field was analyzed to
investigate the underlying phenomena.

B. MRI Data Collection
MRI data were acquired at a different institution for each
subject, as the second subject was added in a subsequent phase
of the study to expand the dataset.
For Subject 1 it was done in the Max Planck Institute for
Multidisciplinary Sciences Biomedical NMR research group
in G¨ottingen [26] using the method described in [37] on
a 3T system (Magnetom Prisma fit; Siemens Healthineers,
Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil.
The sequence employed was a radiofrequency (RF)-spoiled
gradient echo MRI (FLASH) with mild T1 weighting, high-
speed imaging with significant undersampling and automatic
slice shifting. The slices were oriented in the sagittal plane,
with 9 radial spokes utilized. The imaging parameters included
a repetition time of 3.33 ms, an echo time of 2.11 ms, an RF
flip angle of 10°, and an acquisition time of 30 ms. The slices
had a thickness of 2 mm with a 50% slice shift, achieving a
resolution of 1.5 mm × 1.5 mm × 1 mm. The field of view
measured 256 × 256 mm², with a matrix size of 170 × 170.
A total of 120 images/slices were acquired, covering a lateral
width of 120 mm. The total acquisition time was 120 × 30
ms, resulting in a duration of 3.6 s.
Two overlapping images were acquired for each vocal tract
configuration. The first acquisition focused on one hemisphere,
with the subject maintaining a stable articulatory position for
at least 3.6 seconds. The same process was repeated for the
opposite hemisphere.
The MRI of Subject 2 was aquired at the CHRU of Nancy,
in France using a 3T system (Siemens Prisma; Siemens,
Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil.
The sequence used was a 3D gradient echo with partial Fourier
and parallel imaging (3D VIBE). The imaging parameters
included a repetition time of 3.57 ms, an echo time of 1.33 ms,
an RF flip angle of 9°, an acquisition time of 15 s, a pixel
bandwidth of 444 Hz/pixel, an acceleration factor iPAT = 3,
reference lines 24 AP × 24 RL, a partial Fourier phase of 6/8
and a partial Fourier slice of 7/8. The slices had a thickness of
1.2 mm achieving a resolution of 1.05469 mm × 1.05469 mm
× 1.2 mm in a field of view of 253 ×
270 × 144 mm3. A
single image was acquired for each vocal tract configuration.
Subject 1 was a 54-year-old male native German speaker,
and Subject 2 was a 33-year-old male native French speaker.
The subjects began articulating the target phonemes /a, i,
u/ just before each acquisition and maintained as stable an
articulatory position as possible throughout the entire duration.
Additionally, a reference image was taken with the mouth
in a neutral position: lips closed and the tongue relaxed.
This process was designed to minimize disturbances from
involuntary movements by keeping the acquisition time as
short as possible.
C. MRI Segmentation
To generate phoneme-specific finite element models for
Subject 1 we employed a structured image segmentation

workflow to minimize inconsistencies between models. First,
high-resolution T1 and T2 MR images in a neutral mouth
position were manually segmented to create a template model,
which included anatomical structures such as gray matter,
white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, internal air, skull, mandible,
teeth (upper and lower), eyes, and a general head mask.
Manual segmentation was performed using iSEG [38].
The workflow to segment fast MRI scans acquired dur-
ing phoneme articulation was implemented in Python using
SimpleITK [39]. The first step was to pre-process the fast
MRI data, including merging of left and right hemisphere
scans, resampling to 1x1x1 mm resolution, and N4 bias
correction [40]. Next, a head mask was segmented using Otsu
thresholding [41] and morphological operations. The template
model served as the foundation for segmenting fast MRI scans
acquired during phoneme articulation, ensuring that differ-
ences between models reflected only mouth movements. Since
the registration was intra-subject, rigid alignment of bones in
the template model to the phoneme-specific images provided
a reliable method for transferring anatomical structures. First,
the skull was registered to the phoneme images using a rigid
transformation, allowing the transfer of tissues enclosed or
attached to the skull (teeth, brain and eyes). The mandible
(and lower teeth) was then registered separately to account
for potential movement relative to the skull during phoneme
articulation. The registration approach used Mattes mutual
information as a similarity metric [42].
The remaining tissues critical to articulation—such as in-
ternal air, mucosa, tongue, and velum—were then segmented
manually in iSEG. Skin was added with a uniform thickness
of 2 mm. All segmentations were reviewed by a medical
professional for accuracy and corrected as needed.
Because a significant time interval separated the processing
of Subject 1 and Subject 2, Subject 2 benefited from a
more advanced approach unavailable during the processing
of Subject 1. Subject 2 was segmented automatically using
an AI-based approach in Sim4Life that directly segmented 40
tissues from the T1 image using a UNet model described in
[45]. Since the automatic segmentation did not perform that
well in the vicinity of the teeth (teeth and air are black in MRI),
we manually corrected this region in iSEG. Similar to Sub-
ject 1, we registered one bone template mask (from phoneme
/a/) to the other phonemes to ensure they have consistent
skull/mandible geometry and thickness. Finally, we manually
segmented the velum, refined the segmentation of the air and
tongue, and mapped the 40 tissue labels to the corresponding
labels in Subject 1 to have consistent/comparable material
assignments.

model of the micro coaxial cable.

F. Surface Mesh Generation
To simulate wave propagation in the head, a geometrical
description of the various anatomical parts is required. This
is achieved by using closed surfaces that correspond to the
boundaries of specific tissues. These surfaces are represented
using surface meshes, which are collections of interconnected
triangles. The goal of this particular stage in the process was
to generate these meshes from the segmented MRI data.
Surface meshes generated using standard free tools, such
as Meshlab or FreeCAD, were not accepted by the ver-
sion of HFSS used (2022 R2). Mesh errors were detected
when importing these meshes into HFSS, which were not
found in other softwares. However, a test run using the C++
computational geometry library CGAL (version 5.4.1) [46]
demonstrated that the mesh obtained was considered error-
free by HFSS. A custom C++ program, freely available on
GitHub [47], was developed for this purpose.
Segmented MRI data were converted to surface meshes
using the CGAL, Eigen3 (version 3.4.0) [48], and Visu-
alization Toolkit (VTK) (version 9) [49] C++ libraries. A
minimalist user interface was implemented with the Qt5
C++ graphical interface library (version 5.15.3), which takes
segmentation masks in Neuroimaging Informatics Technol-
ogy Initiative (NIfTI) format as input. Users can specify
meshing parameters, including the maximal edge dimension
in millimeters, the number of smoothing iterations, and the
percentage of simplification. The surface mesh is generated
from the segmentation mask using the make surface mesh
function from the ”3D Surface Mesh Generation” package
of CGAL [50]. Typically, an edge dimension of 1 mm is
requested, but the resulting surface mesh is not smooth as
it follows the voxels of the mask. To address this, the mesh is
smoothed using the smooth shape function from the ”Polygon
Mesh Processing” CGAL package, which is based on the
curvature flow algorithm [51], [52]. Usually, six iterations are
performed, as this was found to be a good trade-off between
removing voxelization artifacts and avoiding over-smoothing.
However, the resulting surface mesh has an unnecessarily high
number of elements, which would slow down FEM simulations
without improving accuracy. To mitigate this, the number of
elements is reduced using the edge collapse function from the
”Triangulated Surface Mesh Simplification” CGAL package,

G. FEM Model Creation
Surface meshes were generated using the method previously
described in Section II-F. For this purpose, some masks
corresponding to the same material, such as bones, were
merged in order to have one surface mesh per simulated
material. The resulting surface meshes are illustrated in Fig. 4.
They were then imported via the modeler of HFSS. Initially,
the volume enclosed by the outer surface, primarily the skin,
was defined. Subsequently, surface meshes for various internal
elements, including the brain, bones, eyes, nasal mucosa,
tongue, soft palate, and air, were created. To avoid overlap,
these surface meshes were first subtracted from the other
intersecting elements using boolean operations. This process

6
created voids that matched exactly the shape of each element,
ensuring no gap at the boundary between the various materials
when the internal parts were added.
The relevant tissue properties were assigned to the volumes
enclosed by the different surface meshes. These properties
were specified in terms of frequency-dependent relative per-
mittivity and conductivity. They were obtained from a para-
metric model based on measurements provided by the web
application [53] of the Istituto di Fisica Applicata ”Nello
Carrara” (IFAC) [54]–[56]. The tissues considered included
white matter, gray matter, cerebrospinal fluid, cortical bone,
nasal mucosa, eye vitreous humor, and muscles. Initially,
fat was considered for other non-segmented tissues in the
envelope. However, initial results indicated that this did not
match the order of magnitude of the measurements. Consid-
ering these tissues as muscle in a first approximation proved
to be more realistic. Nevertheless, better estimations of the
material properties could be made in the future (see discussion
section IV).
Once the different anatomical parts were assembled, the
antennas were positioned. The antenna model was directly
imported from its computer aided design (CAD) model via
the HFSS modeler, although it was simplified compared to
the actual devices. The balun was removed, retaining only
the copper sheets and the printed circuit board (PCB), as the
influence of the balun and connecting cables was considered
minimal and unnecessarily increased the complexity of the
modeling task.
Given that the antennas were attached with adhesive to the
cheeks and the distance between the antenna and the cheek was
expected to play an important role, it was crucial to place the
antennas as close as possible to the cheek and ensure this dis-
tance was uniform. To fulfill both conditions, parallelepipeds
with slightly larger dimensions than the antennas were created
and placed at the surface of the cheeks. These parallelepipeds
were fused with the head model using a boolean operation.
This created locally perfectly flat surfaces in front of which the
antenna models could be brought as close as desired without
intersecting the head model, thus avoiding galvanic contact.
The location of the antennas was adjusted to correspond as
closely as possible to the pictures taken during the experiment.
This task was challenging because the 3D head geometry may
have varied slightly between the experiment and the MRI
acquisition, due to changes in position (supine vs. seated)
and the natural variability of phoneme production. Finding a
correspondence between the pictures and the 3D models was
also difficult.
A parametric analysis showed that a distance of 1 µm from
the head model ensured a good match with the experimental
data, specifically achieving an S11 of the same order of
magnitude (see Fig. 5).
Lumped ports were defined between the two sides of the
bow-tie antennas. Simulations were conducted from 1 GHz to
6 GHz using an interpolating sweep. The SAR was computed
following the ICNIRP guidelines (standard IEC/IEEE 62704-
4) using the CertificationSAR algorithm provided in the field
calculator in HFSS at 1 GHz, 3 GHz and 6 GHz with an input

III. RESULTS
The reflection coefficient, as shown in Fig. 5, exhibits
oscillations in the measured data due to imperfect deembed-
ding. Specifically, the effect of standing waves in the cable
connecting the network analyzer to the antenna was not fully
compensated. Despite this, the measured and simulated reflec-
tion coefficients are of the same order of magnitude. However,
the simulated reflection coefficient is slightly higher, around
–7.5 dB at 1 GHz, instead of –10 dB for the measurement.
Both simulated and measured reflection coefficients exhibit
one dip around 2 GHz and 1.5 GHz respectively. However,
the measured reflection coefficient has two prominent dips
at higher frequencies which are not seen in the simulated
one. The differences between the reflection coefficients for
various vowels are minimal, reaching up to 2 dB near the
minimum (around 1.5 GHz for experimental data and 2 GHz
for simulations). Notably, these differences are marginally
larger in the experimental data for subject 1 compared to the

can also see that the minima are more widely spaced outside
the head than inside. This aligns with the shorter wavelength in
head due to higher permittivity of the head tissues. Thus, Mie
scattering modes may underlie simplified modeling approaches
to this phenomenon.

The SAR averaged over 10 g of tissue increases with
frequency, rising from approximately 17 W/kg at 1 GHz to
a maximum of about 40 W/kg at 6 GHz, with only minor
variations between vowels and subjects. Similarly, the SAR
averaged over 1 g of tissue increases from around 51 W/kg at
1 GHz to approximately 280 W/kg at 6 GHz. These relatively
high values, compared to those observed in typical applications
such as mobile phones, can be attributed to the direct contact
between the antenna and the skin (albeit with an adhesive layer
in between). To comply with the ICNIRP limit of 2 W/kg,
the input power should remain below 0.025 W (14 dBm),
while to satisfy the more stringent U.S. limit of 1.6 W/kg,
it should remain below 0.0025 W (4 dBm). The -10 dBm
power used in the experiments is therefore well within both
safety limits. This allowable power could be further increased
by introducing a dielectric matching layer with an intermediate
permittivity between air and soft tissue, as done in [58].
IV. DISCUSSION
Differences between experimental results and simulations
are expected for several reasons. Firstly, the cables and balun
are not included in the simulations, which is anticipated
to primarily affect the reflection coefficient. Despite this
omission, the agreement for the reflection coefficient can be
considered good, as the general order of magnitude is similar.
Simulating the cables would introduce ripples akin to the
artifacts observed in the experimental data. Thus, the antenna-
skin interface appears to be accurately simulated.
Other sources of deviation between experiments and simu-
lations include differences in the imaged volume (e.g. the neck


## Page 12


12
was excluded for Subject 1, whereas the top and sides of the
head were cropped for Subject 2; see Fig. 4), segmentation
inaccuracies and/or incompleteness (e.g., neck bones were not
segmented for Subject 1), approximations in material proper-
ties, discrepancies in antenna placement between simulations
and experiments, differences in subject positioning between
MRI acquisition and scattering matrix measurement (supine
vs. seated) [60], the noise of the MRI machine potentially
affecting vowel pronunciation (Lombard effect [61]), and
natural variations in articulation.
Despite these numerous sources of deviations, the simulated
transmission coefficient closely matches the measured data up
to 3 GHz, exhibiting identical minima and maxima at similar
frequencies and magnitudes. Thus, there is surprisingly good
qualitative agreement.
However, above 3 GHz, a comparison is not feasible due
to the low signal-to-noise ratio of the experimental data.
This results from stronger attenuation, caused by increasing
dielectric and conductive losses at higher frequencies. Pos-
sible mitigation strategies include increasing the transmitted
power (within SAR limits), enhancing receiver sensitivity
through longer measurement times, reducing the intermediate
frequency bandwidth of the VNA, or using antennas with
higher sensitivity. Alternatively, shorter transmission paths
could be achieved by placing antennas inside the nose, mouth,
or ear cavities. Wavefront shaping could also be employed to
optimize the transmission path [62].
The overall intersubject difference in the simulated trans-
mission coefficient amplitude can be attributed to variations
in head size and geometry. Specifically, the average distance
between the antennas is 79 mm for Subject 2, compared to
104 mm for Subject 1. Furthermore, the average relative angle
between the antennas is 78° for Subject 2, while it is 105° for
Subject 1. As a result, the antennas in Subject 1 are positioned
farther apart and are less directly facing each other, which
explains the lower transmission coefficient observed in the
simulations. In contrast, the experimental data show that the
transmission coefficient amplitudes for the vowels /i/ and /u/
are similar for both subjects. This suggests that differences
in tissue properties—identical in the simulations—compensate
for the geometric discrepancies in the real measurements.
For Subject 1, the shift of the simulated shape toward
lower frequencies is likely caused by an overestimation of the
average relative permittivity of soft tissues. In contrast, the
opposite trend is observed for Subject 2 /i/ and /u/, probably
resulting from an underestimation of this parameter. Such
intersubject differences may arise from general anatomical
variations, differing fat-to-muscle ratios, or incomplete seg-
mentation—particularly the omission of fat and other tissues
such as blood vessels. Since variations in the average relative
permittivity of soft tissues between subjects can induce the
observed frequency shifts, including this parameter in the anal-
ysis could make speech recognition from transmission spectra
more reliable. Specifically, estimating and compensating for
this parameter may help correct frequency shifts, similar to
existing approaches in speech processing that compensate for
intersubject variations in vocal tract length. Another implica-
tion of these frequency shifts potentially related to intersubject
tissue properties variation is that broad band signals are nec-
essary to capture similar patterns. However, this trend requires
further confirmation, as it was not observed for the vowel /a/
of Subject 2. It should be noted that the agreement between
experiment and simulation was also poorer for this particular
phoneme and subject.
The reflection coefficient provides limited information re-
garding articulatory movements, as the differences between
vowels are too subtle to easily detect them. Without specific
signal processing, the reflection coefficient is of little use for
this purpose. This is further emphasized by the similarity
observed between both subjects: the anatomical differences
have little effect, indicating that it is primarily determined by
interactions with tissues located very close to the antenna.
In contrast, the relatively small variation between the trans-
mission coefficients of different repetitions, coupled with
larger differences between vowels, indicates that they can be
distinguished in a reproducible and reliable manner. Thus,
the transmission spectrum likely contains more information
about articulatory movements than the reflection spectrum.
This aligns with observations from [20], who noted that
the transmission spectrum provided the most information for
phoneme classification.
The slightly larger variation observed in one repetition of
/u/ of Subject 1 is probably due to a larger variation in
articulation compared to the other repetitions. Recording the
sound could help evaluate the relationship between articulatory
variation and vowel quality by comparing variations in the
transmission coefficient with formants variations. In speech
science, formants are peaks in the spectrum of the acoustic
signal, typically corresponding to acoustic resonances in the
vocal tract. They play a crucial role in phoneme identification.
The fact that variability mostly occurs in the minima may
be because these regions are more sensitive to small changes
in articulatory position. Therefore, they may provide accurate
information regarding small articulatory variations, while more
stable features may offer robust information about the global
configuration, aiding in phoneme identification.
The global amplitude decay with frequency can be under-
stood as a complex interplay of several factors, including the
increase in tissue conductivity with frequency, the antenna’s
radiation efficiency, and various reflections at material inter-
faces [59].
The gaps and maxima in the transmission coefficient result
from variations in resonance patterns with frequency and ar-
ticulatory configuration. When the antenna location coincides
with a minimum of a resonance pattern, it induces a gap, and
vice versa. This interaction occurs at both the receiving and
sending antenna locations.
A specific minimum or maximum in the transmission co-
efficient can be, in some cases, solely related to a resonance
pattern localized within the head (Figs. 8b, 8e-f, and 9a-c),
in other cases, solely to a resonance pattern occurring outside
the head (Figs. 8a and 9d), or to a combination of both types
of resonances (Figs. 8c, 8d and 9e-f). In the latter case, the
combination of both can induce the same effect at the same
frequency (e.g., a minimum in the case of /u/ of Subject 1
in Fig. 8c, or a maximum in the case of /u/ of Subject 2 in


## Page 13


13
Fig. 9f) or have antagonistic effects, with one being slightly
stronger than the other at certain frequencies. The combination
of antagonistic effects in the case of /a/ may explain its
smoother transmission coefficient, where, for Subject 1, the
first minimum and maximum are less pronounced and spread
over a wider frequency range, and for Subject 2, a smooth
transmission coefficient is seen until about 2.5 GHz.
From this perspective, it is clear that modifying the an-
tenna location can substantially alter the transmission coeffi-
cient, inducing minima and maxima at different frequencies.
Therefore, antenna location is a key parameter for SSI, as
highlighted by [21], [22], [25].
It is also evident that anatomical differences strongly influ-
ence the field distribution patterns, as illustrated in Fig. 10.
As expected, as a consequence of this, substantial intersub-
ject variations can be observed in the transmission spectra,
shown in Figs. 6 and 7. However, it is remarkable that,
despite the numerous sources of variation between the two
subjects, the transmission spectra of the different vowels share
common characteristics—particularly for /i/ and /u/. Although
similarities in the field distributions are more difficult to
discern, this suggests that the articulatory configuration affects
electromagnetic wave transmission in a comparable manner. In
fact, parallels can be drawn between how the acoustic signal
and the transmission spectrum encode phonemic information:
despite multiple sources of variation (anatomy, articulatory dif-
ferences, etc.), similar patterns are produced. These patterns,
though somewhat distorted relative to each other, still convey
equivalent information, allowing a trained neural network or
the human brain to recognize them. This observation explains
why proof-of-concept studies of SSI based on variations in
electromagnetic transmission spectra have proven successful
and robust to inter-subject variability [20]–[22]. This also
suggests that a speaker’s identity could be inferred from
the transmission spectra, much like from the speech signal.
Another implication is that broadband signals are necessary
to develop applications robust to inter-subject variability.
Finding a relationship between the geometrical variations
induced by articulation and the transmission coefficient is
not straightforward because the resonance patterns are very
sensitive to small geometrical changes. However, one can
discuss general hypotheses.
One notable observation is that the mouth opening seems to
affect the scattering modes. It may force some minima to be
located near the receiving antenna, as observed for the vowel
/a/, for which this effect seems more prominent. Additionally,
one can hypothesize a stronger coupling between internal
modes and scattering modes through the mouth. Some energy
transmitted through the cheek may be re-emitted and scattered
through the mouth. This may, in turn, affect the internal modes,
possibly decreasing their quality factor by increasing losses.
Note that scattering modes are also affected by changes in face
geometry, such as jaw movements.
Under these considerations, one can understand that the
torso, which is not modeled here, may have an influence. In
particular, the reflection on the shoulders may affect the shape
of the scattering modes. It may be relevant in the future to
include the torso or at least the shoulders, as is done in speech
and hearing acoustics, to simulate the diffraction by the head
and torso [63].
In the future, articulatory models such as VocalTractLab
[64] and Artisynth [65] could play a key role in clarifying how
variations in the transmission coefficient relate to articulatory
movements. By enabling more controlled, gradual, and fine-
grained articulatory changes, these models would allow for a
more precise observation of the impact of phoneme transitions.
They could also facilitate the development of simplified yet
more explanatory models. Moreover, by simulating a wider
range of vocal tract configurations, these tools could be used
for articulatory inversion, allowing one to infer the correspond-
ing vocal tract configuration from transmission coefficients.
This approach could have practical applications in speech
therapy—by providing articulatory feedback to patients—or
in foreign language education, by helping learners acquire
phonemes absent from their native language.
Articulatory motion also induces small changes in the
antennas’ location and orientation, particularly for Subject 1.
A larger distance between the antennas may result in a globally
lower transmission coefficient. For example, for Subject 1,
the vowel /i/ has the largest distance between the antennas
(114 mm) compared to /a/ (106 mm) and /u/ (91 mm). This
may contribute to the overall lower transmission coefficient
for /i/. Conversely, this may explain in part why /u/ has the
highest amplitude for Subject 1. For Subject 2 the variation of
distance with the vowel is much smaller reaching a maximum
of 4.5 mm instead of 23 mm for Subject 1. No prominent
amplitude differences are observed for this subject.
The orientation of the antennas with respect to each other
can have a similar effect due to their directivity. The directivity
pattern of the bow-tie antenna consists of two lobes located on
either side of the plane containing the antenna. Therefore, the
greater the deviation from a parallel configuration, the lower
the transmission coefficient. For Subject 1, the angle between
them is smaller (closer to parallel, which would be 0°) for
/u/: 83° compared to 114° for /i/ and 119° for /a/. This may
also contribute to the higher amplitude of /u/. For Subject 2,
similarly to the distance, the variations of orientation are
smaller than for Subject 1: maximum 8° instead of 36°.
The intersubject differences in the variation of the antennas’
relative positions can be attributed to both anatomical factors
and differences in articulation. It is possible that Subject 1
exhibited a more pronounced articulation.
V. CONCLUSION
The agreement between simulations and measurements is
satisfying, indicating that the method is promising for future
investigations. In alignment with previous work, it is confirmed
that the transmission spectrum carries most of the information
related to articulatory movements. The electromagnetic field
can be interpreted as a Mie scattering pattern, consisting in
internal resonances as well as scattering modes.
The observed minima and maxima of the transmission
coefficient can be understood as the consequence of an in-
terplay between the antenna locations and the geometry of
the resonance patterns. To a lesser degree, the small relative


## Page 14


14
displacements of the antennas are also expected to play a role
in the variations of the transmission coefficient.
Although some effects, such as the influence of the mouth
opening on scattering modes, have been discussed, relating
specific articulatory gestures to changes in the transmission
coefficient remains challenging. This may be addressed in the
future using an articulatory model (e.g., VocalTractLab [64]
or Artisynth [65]).
Despite the sensitivity of resonances to geometrical details,
similar patterns were observed for the vowels pronounced
by both subjects. This finding is consistent with the success
of previous proof-of-concept studies, which reported positive
results across multiple participants. However, as only two
subjects were included in the present study, extending this
work to a larger and more diverse sample—particularly includ-
ing female subjects—would provide a better understanding of
how inter-individual anatomical variations influence phoneme
identification. Such an expansion could also contribute to
making radar-based speech technologies more robust and less
dependent on individual anatomical differences.
