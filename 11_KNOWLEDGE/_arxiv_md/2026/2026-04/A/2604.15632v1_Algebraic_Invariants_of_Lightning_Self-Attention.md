Abstract
We study the polynomial coefficients of lightning self-attention as coordinates of an algebraic variety.
We identify linear and nonlinear families of algebraic invariants, including Chow-type, low-rank, Veronese-
type, and Sylvester resultant-based constraints.

Introduction
Self-attention is a central component of transformer architectures and, more broadly, modern sequence
models. In this paper, we study the lightning self-attention module, also called linear self-attention, obtained
by replacing the softmax nonlinearity with the identity map. In this setting, the module becomes a cubic
polynomial in the input coordinates, with coefficients which are cubic polynomials in the parameters, making
it amenable to algebraic methods.
This polynomial structure leads to a natural problem: which coefficient arrays can arise from a lightning
self-attention module? Equivalently, what algebraic relations must be satisfied by the polynomial coefficients
of the attention module? These questions are natural from several points of view. For algebraic statistics
and applied algebraic geometry, they ask for the implicit image of a structured parametrization. Algebraic
invariants are fundamental in these areas because they encode polynomial relations satisfied by the model
and certify non-realizability. For machine learning, they ask what structural constraints are built into a
basic attention mechanism. Studying the corresponding algebraic invariants makes those constraints explicit,
clarifies the expressive limits of linear self-attention, and may be useful for model comparison and verification.
Our point of view is to treat the polynomial coefficients of lightning self-attention as coordinates of a
parametrized algebraic variety, which we call the attention variety. The goal of the paper is to identify
and organize the algebraic invariants satisfied by these coefficients. We show that this coefficient geometry
is governed by a combination of linear relations and classical nonlinear structures, including Chow-type,
low-rank, Veronese-type and Sylvester resultant-type relations. To illustrate the nature of these relations,
Figure 1 shows a two-dimensional slice of one of the Veronese-type invariants arising in our analysis. Even
in this small example, the geometry exhibits nontrivial, higher-order dependencies among the coefficients,
quartic in this case.

Related work
Since the introduction of the transformer [38], self-attention has become a central object in modern ma-
chine learning. Alongside its empirical success, a growing theoretical literature has studied attention from
several mathematical points of view, including measure-theoretic formulations [40], Lipschitz and regularity
questions [23], interacting-particle and mean-field perspectives [16], and spline-theoretic interpretations of
attention modules [26]. Other recent works study the expressive power and limitations of self-attention and
transformer architectures from various perspectives, including universality and approximation theory [43],
architectural limitations of relative-position-based attention [32], structural constraints on self-attention ma-
trices [30], and learning-theoretic analyses of a single attention layer [14]. Closest to the present work is the
recent algebro-geometric study of lightning self-attention [19], which analyzes the parametrization from the
point of view of identifiability, dimension, singular and boundary points. Our perspective is complementary:
rather than studying fibers and dimension of the parametrization, we study the coefficients themselves and
the algebraic invariants they satisfy.
Algebraic invariants have long played an important role in algebraic statistics and phylogenetics, where
one studies polynomial relations that vanish on a model and thereby encode structural constraints on the

corresponding family of hypotheses [5, 6, 10, 11, 27]. In machine learning, a growing body of work has applied
algebraic, geometric, and tropical methods to neural networks, with particular emphasis on expressivity
[3, 7, 22, 25], identifiability [4, 19, 37], and optimization [24, 39]. Within this broader direction, explicit
algebraic invariants for neural network models are only beginning to emerge as objects of study in their own
right. Recent work on ReLU networks [3] shows that the piecewise-multilinear dependence of the outputs
on the network parameters already imposes nontrivial polynomial constraints on the set of possible outputs.
The present paper continues this line of inquiry for lightning self-attention.
Beyond their intrinsic geometric interest, algebraic invariants can also be useful in applications. At a
basic level, they provide certificates of impossibility: any candidate coefficient array or output pattern that
violates these relations cannot be realized by the model. More broadly, they can help organize the feasible
set of behaviors of an architecture and reduce high-dimensional questions to structured algebraic constraints.
This perspective is especially natural in formal verification [1], where one seeks provable guarantees about
the behavior of a network under perturbations.
Existing verification methods exploit a variety of other
forms of structure: satisfiability and constraint-solving methods give exact certificates for piecewise-linear
networks [13, 21], mixed-integer formulations encode verification as discrete optimization [36], abstract in-
terpretation propagates sound bounds through the network [15, 34], and convex relaxations together with
branch-and-bound provide scalable certification algorithms [9, 12, 41, 42]. While these approximate methods
can be effective in practice, high-stakes applications ultimately require exact and provable guarantees.
Recent work [2] shows that robustness verification can also be formulated as an algebraic distance mini-
mization problem to the decision boundary, and suggests architecture-dependent complexity measures such
as the Euclidean Distance degree. This points to a broader program in which algebraic invariants are used
not only to describe what a model can represent, but also to support exact certification and to quantify
the intrinsic difficulty of verification. From this point of view, identifying explicit invariants for attention
mechanisms is a first step toward algebraic approaches to safety and robustness in transformer-based models.
Organization of the paper
Section 2 introduces lightning self-attention, the coefficient parametrization, and the attention variety. Sec-
tion 3 studies the linear relations among the coefficients. Section 4 turns to the single-column coefficients
and derives Chow-type relations, together with additional determinantal constraints in the low-rank regime.
Section 5 studies the cross-column coefficients and derives low-rank and Veronese-type invariants, as well as
Sylvester resultants of quadratic forms defined by these coefficients. Throughout the paper, we include ex-
amples to illustrate the main constructions and results. Finally, Section 6 provides a catalog of characterized
invariants and lists open problems.

Single-column monomials
We now turn to the single-column coefficients, that is, the cubic forms supported in a single input column.
The key geometric feature of these cubics is that they factor as a product of a linear form and a quadratic
form. Our goal in this section is to derive algebraic equations detecting this factorization. To do so, we
use a differential criterion based on the first partial derivatives. For a generic cubic polynomial, the first
partial derivatives admit no non-trivial linear syzygy. In contrast, if a polynomial factors, then its partial
derivatives satisfy a non-trivial linear syzygy, meaning a polynomial relation with linear coefficients. This
leads to matrices whose rank conditions vanish on the single-column slice of the attention variety.

Summary
We have uncovered a rich algebraic structure governing the coefficients of the lightning self-attention map,
partitioning these invariants into those arising from single-column monomials and those from cross-column
monomials. Before cataloging these families, it is natural to ask whether there exist nonlinear “mixed”
relations, that is, invariants that involve both the single-column and cross-column coefficients. Algebraically,
we do not expect such mixed relations to appear as minimal generators of the defining ideal.
Let Ysingle and Ycross denote the sets of formal ambient variables corresponding to the single-column
and cross-column coefficients, respectively.
The single-column coefficients define a Chow variety of split
type (2, 1), as established in Proposition 17. This Chow-type structure is intrinsic to the single-column
coefficients and is already present in the single-token regime (t = 1), where Ycross = ∅.
For sequence
lengths t > 1, the cross-column coefficients introduce additional low-rank and Veronese-type determinantal
structures. Moreover, Proposition 9 establishes that these two domains are bound together by a system of
linear symmetrization relations, which we denote by Jlin. These linear relations allow us to express every
variable y ∈Ysingle as a linear combination of variables in Ycross.
Let J ⊂R[Ysingle∪Ycross] be the defining ideal of the attention variety. Because J contains Jlin, any mixed
polynomial F(Ysingle, Ycross) ∈J reduces via substitution to a polynomial eF(Ycross) ∈J ∩R[Ycross]. Thus the
study of nonlinear relations may be reduced, modulo the linear symmetrization relations, to pure relations
on the corresponding coordinate slices. Consequently, our search for the defining ideal systematically reduces
to classifying the linear relations alongside the pure nonlinear invariants of each respective coordinate slice.

Catalog of invariants
Based on the algebraic reductions established above, we isolate the following families of linear and nonlinear
invariants for the attention variety.
• Linear relations (Section 3). In a fixed output row, the ambient coordinates satisfy sequence-copy
relations that identify repeated coefficient blocks as the target column j varies and, for fixed j, as the
context column n ̸= j varies. Furthermore, the internal symmetrization relations generate the ideal
Jlin, expressing each single-column coordinate variable yj(K) as a linear combination of cross-column
coordinate variables yn,j(A, b) within a fixed output coordinate.
• Single-column Chow invariants (Section 4). The single-column coefficients parametrize a cubic poly-
nomial that strictly factors into a linear and a quadratic form, placing it on the Chow variety of split
type (2, 1). The invariants are obtained from the vanishing maximal minors of the Lie flattening matrix
MLie(fT ). Furthermore, when the attention dimension is highly bottlenecked (a < d/2), the low rank
of the attention parameter matrix A = K⊤Q strictly bounds the rank of the coefficient submatrix
N(j, j, j), generating additional determinantal invariants whenever rank(A) < d/2.
• Cross-column cubic invariants (Proposition 24). For any context column and target column, specific
linear combinations of the symmetric tensor slices have rank at most 2. The vanishing 3 × 3 mixed
minors of these slice matrices yield a family of cubic invariants.
• Attention rank constraints (Theorem 25). When the attention dimension is bottlenecked (a < d), the
unbalanced flattening matrix Fn,j has rank bounded by a. The vanishing (a + 1) × (a + 1) minors of
this matrix generate invariant polynomials of degree a + 1.
• Cross-column determinantal syzygies (Theorem 27). In the full-rank attention regime (a ≥d), dimen-
sional constraints on the target space of the matrix Fn,j force exact linear dependencies among its
maximal d × d minors. This dimensional overcrowding generates invariant polynomials of degree d.
• Veronese-type invariants (Theorem 30). For any integer r such that 2 ≤r ≤min(a, d), specific r × r
minors of the cross-column coefficients evaluate to the coordinates of the rth Veronese embedding. The
2 × 2 minors of the associated catalecticant matrices yield determinantal invariants of degree 2r.
• Sylvester resultants (Theorem 40). The quadratic forms defined by the cross-column coefficients share a
common linear factor on the attention variety. After restricting to a line, they become binary quadrics,
so their Sylvester resultants vanish and yield quartic invariants.
Open problems
While the families of invariants in this paper capture the fundamental geometric constraints of the parametriza-
tion, we do not claim that they form a complete minimal generating set for the defining ideal J. Proving
completeness would require showing that the ideal generated by our invariants is prime and defines a variety
of the same dimension as the attention variety, which appears difficult in general. Moreover, to formulate a
credible conjecture about minimal generators, substantially more computational evidence is needed. Because
symbolic methods quickly become infeasible as the parameters d, a, and t grow, it remains possible that
additional generators appear in larger dimensions. This leaves several directions for future work.
• Completeness, defining equations, and minimality. Determine whether the invariant families identified
in this paper generate the full defining ideal J. As an intermediate problem, determine which subfami-
lies already define the attention variety, at least set-theoretically, in different parameter regimes. More
modestly, characterize which invariants remain minimal generators.
• Degree bounds. Obtain effective bounds on the degrees of minimal generators of the defining ideal in
terms of d, a, and t.
• Cross-row geometry. Develop a systematic theory of cross-row invariants for d′ > 1, and study how
these relations interact with the rowwise invariant families studied in this paper.

The main body of the paper studies rowwise invariants, obtained after reducing to the case d′ = 1. For
general d′, however, the attention variety also satisfies polynomial relations coupling different output rows.
The reason is that all output rows depend on the same attention matrix A = K⊤Q, while the dependence
on the value matrix V is linear in the chosen row.

corresponding scaled coefficient is a linear form in the entries of the relevant row of V , with coefficients
determined by A alone. As a result, any collection of coefficients taken across output rows factors through
the common matrix V .
