Abstract
We study the zero-free regions of the partition function of the hard-core model on fi-
nite graphs and their implications for the analyticity of the free energy on infinite lattices.
Classically, zero-freeness results have been established up to the tree uniqueness threshold
λc(∆−1) determined by the maximum degree ∆. However, for many graph classes, such
as regular lattices, the connective constant σ provides a more precise measure of structural
complexity than the maximum degree. While recent approximation algorithms based on cor-
relation decay [SSŠY17] and Markov chain Monte Carlo [Eft26] have successfully exploited
the connective constant to improve the threshold to λc(σ), analogous results for complex
zero-freeness have been lacking.
In this paper, we bridge this gap by introducing a proper definition of the connective
constant for finite graphs based on a lower bound on the number of k-depth self-avoiding
walks. We prove that for any graph family with a lower connective constant µ, the partition
function is zero-free in a complex neighborhood of the interval [0, λ] for all λ < λc(µ). As
a direct consequence, we establish the uniqueness and analyticity of the free energy density
for infinite lattices up to the connective constant threshold, extending the known regions
derived from maximum degree bounds. Our proof utilizes a block contraction technique that
lifts the correlation decay property from a real interval to a strip-like complex neighborhood.

Introduction
The hard-core model is a fundamental model in statistical physics, dating back to the 1960s
[Run65]. It describes a gas of particles with hard-sphere interactions and is mathematically
equivalent to the problem of counting independent sets in a graph when the particle activity λ is
set to 1. Let G = (V, E) be a finite graph, and let I(G) denote the collection of all independent
sets of G. Given an activity (or fugacity) λ ∈C, the partition function ZG(λ) of the hard-core
model over G is defined as P

The study of the zero-free regions of the partition function ZG(λ) in the complex plane is of
profound significance. In statistical physics, according to the Lee-Yang theory [LY52], the dis-
tribution of zeros determines the analyticity of the free energy density fG(λ) = log ZG(λ)/|VG|,
which is a classical notion for defining (the absence of) phase transitions. For an infinite lattice
L, the analyticity of the limiting free energy fL(λ) is typically established by showing that the
partition functions of a sequence of finite subgraphs of L are uniformly zero-free in a complex
neighborhood of a real interval. [SS05, Reg18].
From a computational perspective, the location of complex zeros is closely related to efficient
approximation of the partition functions. The study of complex zeros of the partition function
ZG(λ) is also of algorithmic interest. Using the approach developed by Barvinok [Bar16] and
extended by Patel and Regts [PR17], a zero-free region C∆of ZG(λ) can be turned into a
fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS) for computing ZG(λ) on all graphs with
maximum degree ∆, given z ∈C∆. An FPTAS is a family of algorithms {Aε,δ}, where Aε,δ
is a multiplicative (1 ± ε)-approximation algorithm with running time polynomial in 1/ε and
log(1/δ) for each ε, δ > 0.

This method was originally developed by Weitz [Wei06] and independently by Bandyopadhyay
and Gamarnik [BG08] for the hard-core model. Weitz’s algorithm approximates the partition
function on a graph G by expressing it as a telescoping product of marginal probabilities, utilizing
the self-avoiding walk (SAW) tree to reduce the computation from G to a tree structure. For
all graphs of degree at most ∆, SSM holds and FPTASes exist up to the uniqueness threshold
λ < λc(∆−1). A seminal improvement was achieved in [SSŠY17], which extended the threshold
from λc(∆−1) (based on maximum degree) to λc(σ) for graphs with a connective constant at
most σ. For the square lattice, the connective constant is at most 2.64 [JSG16], which is less
than ∆−1 = 3, yielding a better threshold λc(2.64) ≈2.08, which was further extended to 2.538
with additional refinements [SSŠY17].
The other major approach is the randomized scheme based on the Markov Chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) method, pioneered by Jerrum and Sinclair for the ferromagnetic Ising model [JS93].
For the hard-core model on bounded-degree graphs, the mixing time of Glauber dynamics has
been extensively studied [LV99, HV06, EHŠ+19]. Using the spectral independence framework
[ALO20], polynomial mixing times were established up to the uniqueness threshold ∆−1 for
graphs with degree at most ∆. Moreover, the optimal O(n log n) mixing time has been proven in
a line of recent work [CLV21, CE22, CFYZ22]. For random graphs G(n, d/n), a mixing time of
n1+O(1/ log log n) on typical instances was proved [EF23]. There are also results for special graph
classes, such as bipartite graphs and claw-free graphs [CLY23, CG24, CF24, Jer24, CCC+25].
Notably, for the square lattice, Restrepo et al. [RST+13] obtained an improved mixing regime
for all 0 < λ < 2.3882 beyond the degree threshold λc(3).
In a very recent work [Eft26],
optimal mixing time was obtained for graphs with bounded connective constant σ, extending the
threshold from λc(∆−1) to λc(σ) similar to the improvements seen in correlation decay [SSŠY17],
though using a different notion of local connective constant.
While both MCMC and correlation decay methods have successfully utilized the connective
constant to surpass the uniqueness threshold λc(∆−1), zero-freeness results have largely re-
mained tied to the maximum degree ∆. This raises a natural question: can a larger zero-free
region be established in terms of the connective constant?
To answer this, we must first define a proper notion of the connective constant for finite
graphs. For infinite graphs, widely used definitions capture the exponential growth rate of self-
avoiding walks [Ham57, Lac14, GL15]. However, there are only a few different definitions for
connective constant for finite graphs.
The definition proposed in [SST14] is a log |V |-depth
connective constant depending on the graph size V , while the definition used in [Eft26] is a local
connective constant based on fixed-length SAWs. However, neither definition readily guarantees
zero-freeness, as easy counter-examples can be constructed. We introduce a slightly different
definition and use it to prove our zero-freeness results.
Our contribution
We propose the following definition of connective constants. For a graph G and a vertex v, let
N≤k(G, v) denote the number of self-avoiding walks of length no more than k starting at v, and
let µk(G) = supv∈V N≤k(G, v)1/k.
Definition 1.1 (Connective constants). For a family H of finite graphs, the k-depth connective
constant of H for a fixed k, denoted by µk(H), is supG∈H µk(G).
The lower connective constant of H, denoted by µinf(H), is infk≥1 µk(H).
For an infinite graph G, let HG denote the set of all finite subgraphs of G. We establish the
following relationship: for any infinite graph G with connective constant σ(G), µinf(HG) ≤σ(G).
In particular, if G is an infinite lattice, then µinf(HG) = σ(G). This ensures that we can derive
the analyticity of the free energy function on infinite lattices from the zero-freeness of partition
functions on their finite subgraphs.

Preliminaries
In this paper, an infinite graph is a locally finite graph.
Weitz [Wei06] introduced the self-avoiding walk (SAW) tree as a tool to analyze the hard-
core model. Given a (finite or infinite) graph G and a root vertex v, the SAW tree TSAW(G, v)
is a rooted tree that encodes all self-avoiding walks.
Fix G = (V, E), a root v ∈V . Given any ordering scheme {≺u: u ∈V } where each ≺u is a
total order on N(u), the SAW tree TSAW(G, v) is the rooted tree whose nodes are self-avoiding
walks w = (v = v0, v1, . . . , vt) in G. From w we add a child for each extension (v0, . . . , vt, x)
with x ∈N(vt) \ {v0, . . . , vt}.
If x ∈N(vt) already appeared in w (say x = vs, s < t), the extension creates a cycle-closing
leaf. Let y := vs+1 be the neighbor used when the walk first left x. If vt ≺x y, then pin this

4.1
Depth-k cutsets and k-layer block operators
The hard-core recursion is a one-step map from the occupation ratios of a vertex’s children
to the occupation ratio of the vertex itself.
In our arguments it is convenient to bundle k
successive steps into a single operator that maps the “interface” at depth k to the root. This
viewpoint is especially natural for Weitz’s SAW tree: some branches terminate early due to
boundary conditions (pinned leaves), while the vertices at depth exactly k should be regarded
as free interface nodes—beyond which the tree may continue (and will be handled by subsequent
blocks).
