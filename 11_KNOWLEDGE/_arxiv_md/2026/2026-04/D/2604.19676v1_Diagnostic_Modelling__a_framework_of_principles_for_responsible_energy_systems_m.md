Diagnostic Modelling: a framework of principles for 
responsible energy systems modelling 
I. David Elder, Juan Moreno-Cruz, Cameron Wade, Sylvia Sleep, Sara Hastings-Simon, Sean 
McCoy, Heather L. MacLean, I. Daniel Posen  
Abstract 
Energy systems optimisation models are a leading tool for informing decisions in the energy 
transition. However, these models often remain opaque, and results are frequently presented 
without a clear discussion of their epistemic limitations. We propose Diagnostic Modelling as a 
framework wherein modellers critically interrogate their models and explore uncertainties to 
uncover mechanistic explanations that offer policy-relevant insights. Mechanistic explanations 
provide fundamental understanding that remains valid despite model uncertainty and does not 
depend on detailed knowledge of a specific model. By adopting a more open and transparent 
approach to engaging with energy systems models, Diagnostic Modelling encourages the 
participation of a broader range of decision-makers, thereby building consensus in support of the 
energy transition.

Three principles for responsible energy systems modelling 
The world needs a low-emissions energy transition to address key global issues like climate change, 
energy security, and economic development1. The transition will be complex and difficult: 
economically, as we finance capital-intensive projects, incentivise behavioural changes, rapidly scale 
up new industries and phase out others; environmentally, as we exploit new critical mineral 
resources while conserving and revitalising damaged ecosystems; technologically, as we build new 
infrastructure, manage a changing electricity grid, and develop or deploy new low- or negative-
emissions technologies; and socially, as we must do this all justly and with political support. Each of 
these challenges is further complicated by deep uncertainty in how the future will unfold2–4. We 
need ways to make informed and just decisions despite these overwhelming challenges, 
complexities, and uncertainties. 
Quantitative models and their results are given a privileged position in decision processes. Among 
these, energy systems models (ESMs)—along with their grander relatives, integrated assessment 
models—have emerged as a leading decision-making tool for the energy transition5–9. These 
computer models optimise (or simulate) the design and operation of the energy system into the 
future—projecting the optimal network of energy processes to take primary energy resources 
through transportation and conversion technologies to satisfy end use demands (Figure 1). They are 
used to inform decisions about infrastructure investment, system operation, policy design, and long-
term planning under technical, economic, and environmental constraints and uncertainty. When 
misused, however, flaws of methodology, interpretation, and communication jeopardise their value 
for decision-making.

ESM studies and their audiences often neglect uncertainties and, instead, focus on deterministic 
results. Further, these results are often inappropriately assumed to provide insights about the real 
world (not just the model world), reinforced by the presentation of these results as detailed narrative 
scenarios (plausible stories) of the future, an effect which is magnified when these stories become 
familiar paradigms10,11—a form of anchoring bias. ESMs are only one of many valuable decision-

To tackle these issues, we propose a framework for energy systems modelling: Diagnostic Modelling. 
This framework builds upon a rich literature discussing scientific inference and best practices in 
modelling, by authors such as Popper13, Lempert14, Bankes15, DeCarolis7,16,17, Stirling12,18, Saltelli and 
Puy19–24, Morgan and Keith11,25, and their collaborators. Three simple principles underpin our 
framework to improve the scientific value and validity of ESM studies (Figure 2):

model results. Insights should be explained for a broad audience in plain language and 
contextualised within the epistemic limits of the study.

literature to demonstrate that specific principle. We did not find a single study in the space of energy 
systems modelling, including our own studies, that we believe comprehensively demonstrates all 
three principles together.

Explore uncertainties to find robust insights 
Energy systems models have become increasingly complex in the pursuit of model realism, but 
complex models are less interpretable, less explorable, and more uncertain22,23,26,27. Studies that 
neglect these uncertainties also neglect their epistemic limits23. To justify insights for an uncertain 
future, exploration of uncertainty—all types of uncertainty, not only quantifiable parametric 
uncertainties—should be prioritised over model completeness or complexity. 
Historically, exploration of uncertainty in many ESM studies has been perfunctory or absent, though 
this has improved in recent years with advances in uncertainty methods and practices28 (see Table S7 
for a brief review of existing uncertainty methods). Most studies that do explore uncertainty focus on 
parametric uncertainty (uncertain input data)28. Increasing recognition has also been given to 
structural uncertainties28 (uncertain choices in the structuring of the model), though methods to 
address structural uncertainty are more nascent. These are further complicated by subjectivities and 
biases in the construction of the model or study and in its interpretation by the modeller and the 
audience (including, for example, anchoring bias, logical fallacies, misinterpretation, or disagreement 
on objectives). We explore a taxonomy of ESM uncertainties in Table S6 but parameters, structures, 
and subjectivities are, in practice, often inseparable in modelling. 
A major challenge in energy systems modelling is the high dimensionality of uncertainty. The energy 
transition is taking place in an immensely complex system of systems. ESMs that model this 
transition therefore absorb many diverse uncertainties within their scope and leave many more 
uncertainties unmodelled and out of scope. Given the amount of uncertainty at play, it is vanishingly 
unlikely that our best projections of the future will be accurate. 
As a thought exercise, let us focus only on parametric uncertainty and consider the combined 
uncertainty of N independent uncertain variables. Let us assume these uncertainties combine by 
quadrature—in other words, the Euclidean distance from the centroid (our central or “best guess” 
scenario) of all N variables. So, consider an N-dimensional unit hypercube, where each of these N 
dimensions represents the distribution of a single independent uncertain variable, taking one of 
three values with equal probability: {0 (low), 0.5 (central), 1 (high)}. This is a standard approach to 
representing uncertain variables in ESMs14,28, where low, central, and high might be, for one common 
example, cheap, moderate, and expensive future natural gas prices, and “central” represents our 
best guess. Scenarios are then assembled from narratively cohesive combinations of these three 
cases for many uncertain variables, and a small ensemble of scenarios modelled to observe results 
under different “plausible” futures. Other, more-sophisticated uncertainty approaches like Stochastic 
Programming, Robust Optimisation, Monte Carlo, and Modelling to Generate Alternatives are gaining 
popularity, but scenario analysis remains the standard28. 
Let us focus on an ensemble of all our central cases (which could be interpreted as our best guess 
outcome of all future uncertainty) and the expected divergence of all 3𝑁 possible cases from this 
centre. In one dimension, a unit line with a centroid at 0.5, the mean distance of our three possible 
outcomes {0, 0.5, 1} from this central estimate is 1/3, 0.33. This increases to 0.54 in two dimensions 
and, in N dimensions, the mean distance from the centre (Figure 3.a) increases as approximately 
√𝑁/6—which is to say: the more variables we add, the further we expect to end up from our central 
best guess (the more “wrong” we should expect our central scenario to be), without limit11,22,29. Had 
we sampled each variable from a normal distribution, we should also expect an increasing 
occurrence of extreme, tail outcomes (Figure 3.b), challenging the validity of not only our central 
scenario but also the bounds of our exploration11,23,24,30. Some recent historical examples of impactful 
tail outcomes include the precipitous cost decline of solar photovoltaics31, the COVID-19 pandemic,

Beyond parametric uncertainty, complexity further increases uncertainty of interpretation (epistemic 
opacity), making insights more difficult to discover or communicate, and model operation (data entry 
mistakes, code bugs) while increasing computational intensity, limiting the feasible scope of 
exploration. This “curse of dimensionality” contradicts a common attitude in modelling where 
modellers must improve their forecast accuracies (the “goodness” of their model) by increasing 
model complexity26,28,30,33–36—adding more independent uncertain variables at the expense of 
exploration and interpretability11,28,37. This is often framed as a necessity to faithfully represent the 
system26,33—a reduction of completeness uncertainty which is given undue priority over other 
uncertainty types26,28,36. 
Certainly, there is a need for completeness—or even complexity24,27,38. In fact, it is the complexity of 
models that often produces novel insight, found in the emergent phenomena of many interacting 
assumptions39. Increased complexity can result in more-sophisticated—and so, we hope, more 
surprising and insightful—phenomena. However, these phenomena may be increasingly difficult to 
interpret. Complexity can be a source of insight but we must dispel the idea that it is a treatment for 
uncertainty when, in fact, it is a root cause23,24,40. For each model there exists some crossover point 
where the benefits of increasing complexity are overwhelmed by lost exploration (due to limits in 
compute resources, time, effort, etc.), decreased interpretability and other compounding 
uncertainties7,24. Beyond this point, it follows that we benefit from more samples, not more 
variables, meaning greater exploration of uncertainty, with a simpler, more tractable, more 
interpretable model15,21,30,41.       
Finally, we must realise that these uncertainties exist whether we model them or not. Our N (number 
of uncertain variables) is not a function of the number of variables in our model but rather the

number of independent, unmitigated uncertainties in the system we are modelling. From this follow 
three insights: (1) to completely mitigate uncertainty, we need not just a simple model but a model 
of a simple system, such that all intrinsic uncertainties can be fully defined and explored (if such a 
thing is even possible outside more-fundamental physical sciences); (2) aggregating variables may 
enable a greater exploration of uncertainty (due to reduced computational intensity) without 
changing the intrinsic uncertainties of the study; (3) for models of large, complex systems—where 
the model cannot capture all the intrinsic uncertainties of the system—a simple exploration of in-
model uncertainties (e.g., parametric sensitivity analysis), though necessary, may be insufficient. We 
discuss uncertainty further in Section S2. 
To demonstrate a thorough exploration of at least parametric uncertainty, we point to Sepulveda et 
al. (2018). They assess the role of firm low-carbon generation in the future power system. They 
model 912 scenarios, combining several possible cases along seven aggregate dimensions of 
uncertain inputs. Though we stress that it is not necessarily the scale of their exploration that should 
be emulated, but their ethos. Their high and low cases in each dimension are extreme-but-plausible 
upper and lower bounds, rather than small divergences from a central estimate. Their approach is 
exhaustive, not perfunctory. Their exploration is thoughtful, building from a Global Sensitivity 
Analysis to determine the most influential parameters. Finally, they seek insights to inform decisions 
under all this uncertainty. For example, they conclude that, under any plausible outcome of the 
uncertainties they considered, firm low-carbon resources lower decarbonized electricity system 
costs. This is an insight for all uncertain futures, not a justification of insight for a central projection of 
the future. 
As another example, we point the reader toward the work of Neumann and Brown (2023)42 who use 
multi-fidelity surrogate modelling to explore the dynamics of a widely used open-source European 
power system model, PyPSA-Eur. This surrogate approach allows them to explore over 50,000 
optimisation scenarios, combining Global Sensitivity Analysis methods with Modelling to Generate 
Alternatives to observe the structure of the model’s solution space across a range of uncertainties for 
technology capital costs. This approach explores aspects of both parametric and structural 
uncertainty. We further commend their transparent communication of study limitations. 
Brute force parametric exploration of the model is not always necessary, however, and in fact is not 
possible or appropriate for all types of uncertainty. An expansive parametric exploration may give the 
sense that uncertainty has been fully explored (which is not possible in cases of high dimensionality), 
while deeper uncertainties are ignored. The most influential uncertainties may not be parametrizable 
or within the structural scope of model, requiring qualitative discussion or, where implications are 
also deeply uncertain, simply acknowledgement43. For example, Mersch, Markides, and Mac Dowell 
(2023)44, while exploring fuel price uncertainty (an in-model parametric exploration), acknowledge a 
wide range of uncertainty types in their “Limitations of the study” section. They raise parametric 
uncertainties within the model (such as technological learning rates), structural uncertainties of the 
model (a central decision-maker with perfect foresight), and unmodelled uncertainties (political 
instability and global energy markets), with some brief discussion of their potential implications for 
insights. 
In the Diagnostic Modelling philosophy, one should, quantitatively or qualitatively, thoughtfully 
explore different types of uncertainty and their implications for the study, including those within and 
without the model—parametric, structural, and subjective. By understanding these uncertainties, we 
define the epistemic limits of the study and, within those limits, find robust insights.

Interrogate insights for mechanistic explanations 
Due to the complexity of the energy transition, no matter how broad our exploration, many 
uncertainties will likely remain unknown or unexplored. For this we need a change of paradigm. 
Heeding wisdom from the system dynamicists45,46, we should detach our conclusions from the results 
of the model and instead interrogate the model for real-world insights. We should seek mechanistic, 
cause-and-effect insights (or causal explanations) that withstand uncertainty and remain robust to 
model assumptions. These mechanisms, once discovered and analysed through the modelling 
process, can be explained in plain language, to a broad audience, justified solely by the reason of 
that explanation, without the need to explain or justify the model itself. Such insights are 
communicable and necessarily insensitive to the model used and therefore more robust to 
uncertainty. This type of analysis is diagnostic (“Why does it happen?”)—from which Diagnostic 
Modelling inherits its name—(as opposed to descriptive (“What happened?”), predictive (“What will 
happen?”), or prescriptive (“What should happen?”)—established terms in the field of data 
analytics47 (Table 1).

A model is not the real world and model experiments do not produce real-world observations25. In 
well-executed physical experiments, such as we might see in physics, chemistry, medicine, or even a 
well-designed survey, the results themselves contain a real-world observation prior to any analysis or 
interpretation. Not so in modelling, where results could be a bug or an artefact of model 
assumptions, structure, or scope. Seeking mechanistic explanations pushes us to think again in real-
world terms. 
One of the great strengths of ESMs is that, being bottom-up models, they are capable of mechanistic 
analysis, but—despite an oft-touted goal of insights-not-numbers7,48–50—the majority of ESM 
studies—especially those using large models at the national planning level1,51–53, and even those 
employing uncertainty methods16,54—have historically employed a more prescriptive or predictive 
approach55. Even model intercomparisons are often reduced to a comparison of model features and 
results rather than a discussion of the real-world decisions those results are supposed to inform56–60 
(we discuss model intercomparison further in Section S3.1). Insights from diagnostic analysis, on the 
other hand, represent real-world mechanisms. 
Mechanistic insights are not only robust to uncertainty, but also support a pluralistic approach to 
decision making, as they are easier to communicate to a broad audience and can be reproduced or 
falsified by other models or by those outside the field of energy systems modelling, leaving room in 
the decision-making process for stakeholder preferences and consensus from other studies and 
disciplines20,21,34,48,49,61. They may also be more generalisable and enduring. Mechanisms describe 
general system structures rather than outcomes for a particular instance of a system in one time and

place. They may therefore be generalisable to other times and places, or other systems. By 
accumulating these insights, we incrementally refine the collective mental models we use each day 
to interpret the messy world around us and make executive decisions under complexity43. 
To justify mechanistic insights, the modeller must reject easy conclusions and ask, ad nauseam, 
“Why?” Rather than accept the model outputs at face value, treat the model as a compulsive liar and 
interrogate it until insights emerge as explainable real-world mechanisms. Form a hypothesis (a 
possible interpretation of results) and seek, in earnest, to disprove it, using the model to test your 
intuition. ESMs suffer from two problems that make formal statistical inference unreliable. First, their 
uncertainties, and therefore probabilities, cannot be fully quantified or explored (as we have already 
argued). Second, it is impossible to compare their projections to future observations as these 
observations have not yet happened17. As a result, we often rely on an entirely subjective, heuristic 
form of Bayesian inference, based on how reasonable the hypothesis sounds (the prior), the 
apparent falsifiability of the hypothesis, and how much rigour has seemingly been applied in 
attempting to falsify it (perceived severity of testing)13,62,63. Through the process of interrogation, to 
justify our mechanistic insight, we aim to maximise its falsifiability and the strength and breadth of 
attempts to falsify it. 
Interrogation can be thought of as having two modes: discovery and testing. In the discovery mode, 
we seek novel and surprising insights. These emerge from unexpected intermediate interactions 
between model assumptions (emergent phenomena). In the testing mode, which may or may not 
follow a phase of discovery, we seek to better understand and define these emergent interactions—
leading to robust mechanistic explanations—or to test prior system intuitions (Figure 4). 
With an interesting model outcome in hand, use the model—or perhaps another, simpler, more-
interpretable model—to interrogate that result and attempt to construct a robust mechanistic 
explanation for it. Test each of these statements in turn, especially when tempted not to: 
1. “This is a result of a bug in my model. If I fix that bug, the outcome changes.” 
2. “This is a result of my own input assumptions or data. Different, reasonable assumptions do 
not reproduce this outcome.” 
3. “This is a result of how I chose to represent these systems. I can represent these systems in a 
reasonable alternative way such that a different outcome is produced.” 
4. “This is an artefact of computer modelling or hidden within a black box. I cannot reasonably 
justify or explain this outcome in terms of a real-world mechanism.” 
5. “I have not adequately considered alternatives. If I try, I can justify a different explanation.” 
Testing these statements requires diagnostic analysis of the model, which requires diagnostic tools. 
These are tools which open wide the black box to analyse and display the underlying mechanisms of 
the model, so that they can be evaluated for real-world relevance. We believe there is a need to 
develop and propagate new diagnostic tools for ESMs, and to better apply existing ones. These might 
be new modelling methods or protocols, new methods to explore uncertainty, or new methods of 
visualisation to communicate model dynamics. As a starting point, we discuss some existing tools for 
Diagnostic Modelling and some examples of their application in Section S3.

An insight that survives interrogation should be real-world relevant and explainable without the 
model that produced it. The more interrogation it withstands, the more justified the explanation 
becomes62,63. The final insight may seem so obvious and intuitive that one wonders why a model was 
needed at all. At this point, the insight has transferred from the computer model to the mental 
model—a causal description (𝐴→𝐵) becomes a causal explanation (𝐴⇒𝐵⇒𝐶⇒𝐷)—and the 
computer model may be left behind. This explanation may still be wrong but, unlike a predictive or 
prescriptive insight that can only be falsified by discrediting the model itself, a diagnostic insight can 
be falsified by discrediting its explanation, which does not require access to or knowledge of the 
original model, enabling a pluralistic debate.

reactors and thermal storage. They identify a first-order intermediate interaction—that fission plants 
with thermal storage displace more wind, batteries, and natural gas plants, but this is still a model

considering the operational behaviour of the nuclear plant, they construct a detailed mechanistic 
explanation in real-world terms: in short, the nuclear plant can oversize its turbine and use thermal

In Diagnostic Modelling, one should interrogate the model to seek causal explanations for model 
outcomes. Attempt to explain outcomes in terms of a real-world mechanism, then test that 
explanation. Share your explanation so that others might understand it and interrogate it 
themselves, with their own methods and tools. As your insight withstands more (and more-varied) 
interrogation, it becomes more justified62,63, and we can more-confidently fold it into our collective 
mental models. 
Communicate insights as explanations, within epistemic limits 
Insights should be communicated responsibly within the epistemic limits of the study—the limits of 
what can be reasonably known or justified based on the methods, scope, and limitations of that 
study. Modellers should not attempt to provide complete answers to wicked problems but instead 
leverage the strengths of their specific methods, models, or expertise to deliver robust insights to the 
wider forum, enabling transparent and pluralistic debate. 
Myriad methods have emerged to assess policies, strategies, and technologies for the energy 
transition. We discuss a broad classification of these methods in Section S1.1, discussing the roles of 
qualitative and quantitative methods, and top-down, bottom-up, and hybrid computer models. It is 
important to recognise that any decision-making method comes with inherent strengths and 
weaknesses. No method can capture all the complexity of the energy transition; every method will 
unavoidably emphasise certain factors over others and neglect numerous dimensions of the 
problem. Each method is limited but valuable—when applied responsibly, thoughtfully, and humbly. 
The result is that a combination of methods, quantitative and qualitative (mixed-methods), will likely 
offer greater insights than a single method alone5,12,19,24,65–68. In this endeavour, each individual study 
need not tackle the whole. Under the insurmountable complexity and uncertainty of the energy 
transition, truth is often more constructed than objective14,69–73, supported by consensus rather than 
by exact mathematical relationships. Independent studies may each therefore address one small 
corner of this holistic process, and it is the role of the entire field—academic, public, and private—to 
collaborate, discuss, and democratically debate to find just decisions for a plurality of interests18,20. It 
is not the responsibility of the analyst to apply, themself alone, all these methods in tandem but 
rather to be sufficiently aware of alternative methods and their contributions to the forum. To 
demonstrate our own efforts in this regard, we have reviewed some available decision-making 
methods and tools for the energy transition in Section S1.2, where we attempt to broadly classify 
some methods and review their claimed strengths and weaknesses in Tables S1-S5. 
To facilitate a pluralistic decision-making process, insights should be (1) presented in plain language 
so they can be understood and debated by a broad audience, (2) grounded in real world 
mechanisms—those discovered and tested through interrogation—so that they can be justified or 
falsified by the merit of that explanation alone, without the need to understand the model itself,

and, (3) responsibly contextualised within the epistemic limits of the study—including explored and

made with an informed understanding of what we actually know. 
As an example of this in practice, we point to Usher and Strachan (2012)74. They perform a two-stage 
stochastic optimisation of the future UK power system. Their stochastic optimisation is limited to 
nine states of world due to computational tractability—a narrow exploration of uncertainty. 
However, they readily recognise these limitations and thoughtfully discuss their implications for 
decision-making throughout. They also focus discussion on real-world insights for decision making, 
justified through thoughtful mechanistic explanation, rather than claims of model or study quality, 
and present these insights within their epistemic limits. For example, they highlight the value of 
short-lived demand technologies to quickly adapt to structural changes in energy supply, reducing 
the cost of uncertainty. A greater exploration of uncertainty would likely have yielded deeper or 
more robust insights, which they themselves admit, but the humility of their discussion exemplifies 
the Diagnostic Modelling philosophy and, notably, produced enduring insights that remain as 
relevant and valuable now as then, 13 years ago. 
The Diagnostic Modelling philosophy recognises the need for plural and democratic decision-making 
in the energy transition. No single method or tool can provide a whole answer to wicked problems 
with diverse—and often competing—objectives, so we must rely on democratic processes to find just 
solutions for a plurality of interests, from a plurality of insights. To support these processes, we need 
real-world insights, explained in plain language, responsibly framed within their epistemic limits. 
A note on pragmatism 
Modelling for policy must balance pragmatism with scientific idealism. Current practices have kept 
the lights on, but the energy transition is far behind schedule75 and objectives of cost and reliability 
speak little to justice76. Are we sure the balance is right? We would claim, only, that to find good 
balance, we must first understand the trade-offs. 
For certain objectives, under certain circumstances, safeguards exist to protect the validity of single-
scenario (or few-scenario) modelling, including: (1) deep uncertainties emerge and compound into 
the future, so uncertainties may be limited by modelling decisions for the present or near future3, (2) 
real-world decision-makers may be presented with a discrete set of real options, bounding the 
dimensionality and uncertainty of the decision space, (3) correlations between uncertainties may 
reduce the effective dimensionality of uncertainty even further77, and (4) work with Modelling to 
Generate Alternatives has shown that many diverse solutions may be adequate for a given modelled 
problem78, and so a decision based on a small ensemble of modelled scenarios may be adequate for 
a wider range of uncertain outcomes. 
Where these and other factors protect us from uncertainty, an optimal decision from a model may 
be an adequate real-world decision. However, it may not be possible to know whether sufficient 
safeguards existed until after the fact—ex-post studies have shown that cost-optimal modelling has 
not historically produced cost-optimal realities79—and we should not mistake adequate (to satisfy 
basic objectives like reliability and cost) for good (to satisfy basic objectives and justly balance a 
plurality of other interests). 
Decision-making (and decision-makers) often demand straight-forward recommendations on short 
timelines80. So, a strict application of these—admittedly idealistic—principles may not always be 
pragmatic or possible. In these cases, we do what we can, but what we can always do is

Conclusion 
We argue that energy systems models should be used diagnostically, rather than predictively or 
prescriptively. Rather than modelling a few scenarios and presenting outputs directly or interpreting 
them only subjectively, analysts should diagnose the model, exploring uncertainty and interrogating 
model behaviour to identify robust mechanistic explanations that reflect real-world insights. They 
should then communicate these explanations in plain language, responsibly contextualised within 
the epistemic limits of the study. Such explanations, communicated this way, are less bound to the 
uncertainty of the underlying model. They can be justified without the burden of justifying the model 
itself and can be understood without knowledge of the specific model or of energy systems 
modelling methods, generally. This makes the explanations transparent, communicable, and 
justifiable to decision-makers as well as to researchers in other fields, supporting an open, 
democratic, and pluralistic decision-making process to support a just and decisive energy transition.

S1. Decision-making methods and tools for the energy transition 
 
Decision-making methods can be broadly classified as: qualitative decision-making methods, 
quantitative decision-making methods, and computer models (top-down, bottom-up, and hybrid 
forms). 
 
Quantitative decision-making methods seek, through rigorous numerical analysis, to render a 
number to a decision, leaving the final recommendation to a comparison of numbers between 
alternatives. This has some perceived benefits over qualitative decision making: (1) quantitative 
analysis is seen (perhaps falsely)  as being more objective, repeatable, and credibleS1,S3,S4; (2) 
quantitative methodologies may uncover more generalisable, mechanistic insightsS4,S5; (3) it can be 
easier to make and execute a final decision when the burden of that decision has been offloaded to 
hard numbersS1–S3. Criticisms reflect these strengths: (1) any analysis, no matter how rigorous, is 
structured through the subjectivities and biases of the analysts, especially under uncertaintyS1,S6,S7; 
(2) quantitative methods narrow the scope to quantifiable dimensions, often excluding or 
minimising important but less-quantifiable dimensions of a decisionS1,S2,S8,S9; (3) decision makers may 
overestimate the authority of quantitative recommendations, taking for granted the certainty, 
quality, and relevance of underlying analysis and dataS1–S4,S6,S7,S10. 
 
The top-down versus bottom-up paradigm of computer models can be explained as a difference in 
strategy between attempting to directly fit a model to observations of macroscopic system 
behaviours (top-down) versus attempting to model the underlying mechanisms of those behaviours 
(bottom-up)S13–S15. This parallels the paradigms of qualitative versus quantitative, where top-down 
models typically capture a broader range of system behaviours—intrinsic to macroscopic trendsS13–
S15—that might be lost in a more mechanistic model, while bottom-up models capture more-granular 
details of a narrower scope of interactions, retaining greater ability to analyse the mechanisms 
themselvesS13,S16. Top-down studies are typically more “pessimistic”, implicitly accounting for the 
hidden costs and barriers of the energy transition and reporting higher emissions abatement costs.


## Page 19


Bottom-up studies are typically more “optimistic”, allowing for a greater range of abatement options 
and assuming greater efficiency than is realistic, resulting in lower or even negative abatement 
costsS13,S15–S17. Hybrid models attempt to pair these paradigms, usually coupling top-down economic 
or behavioural models with bottom-up technological, agent-based, or environmental 
modelsS13,S15,S16. As computer models are limited by available resources like processing power, 
processing time, memory, data storage, and analysts’ time and effort, analysts must carefully assess 
study priorities in crafting the scope of the modelS1,S18. 
 
Our approach for this review was simply to research methods and tools that we had encountered in 
proximity to our own work, and then to explore additional methods and tools we encountered while 
reviewing literature on those. We searched for information to fill these tables by three main 
methods: web search using Google Scholar (terms like “<method> limitations”, “<method> 
criticisms”, “<method> strengths”, or “<method> review”), following citations up and downstream 
from these articles, and conversation with an LLM (OpenAI’s o3-deep-research via ChatGPT), asking 
for peer-reviewed literature that discussed these methods and tools, and following up for more 
authoritative or relevant articles.

Several methods have emerged to explore uncertainty in ESMs (Table S7). These are each best 
suited to explore certain types of uncertainty so, to identify gaps, we explore a taxonomy of 
uncertainty for ESMs and map out the appropriate applications of existing uncertainty methods. 
 
We further follow established practice for energy modellingS251 in classifying uncertainty by 
parametric (input data) versus structural (design choices made in crafting the model), these being 
the insertion of the uncertainty—how it enters the model or study. To this category, we would again 
add a third, human insertion: subjective—that which inserts at the transition between the computer 
model or reality and the mental model of the interpreter or model builder.

S3. Methods and tools for Diagnostic Modelling 
 
S3.1. 
Tools to explore uncertainty 
 
Structural uncertainties are more challenging to explore as testing different structures often requires 
changing the mathematical formulation of the model. Though this is a topic of focus in the modelling 
communityS183,S251,S287, we cannot point to any particular tools for assessing general structural 
uncertainty, except perhaps model intercomparison. Modelling to Generate Alternatives can help to 
address one structural uncertainty, by revealing all the near-optimal solutions that might otherwise 
be hidden by single-solution optimisation. Lau et al. have made notable recent efforts to advance 
MGA methods to better explore this near-optimal spaceS260,S263. Similarly, Multi-Objective 
Optimisation has been utilised to explore trade-offs between competing alternative objectives, as 
demonstrated by Prina et al. (2020)S108. 
 
Multi-Objective Optimisation is, again, a core tool for comparing competing objectives.


## Page 32


also be difficult to meaningfully interpret, compared to insights, as we have argued in this work. We 
see potential value for some future model intercomparisons to perform blind comparison of entire 
studies aligned only on questions, comparing final insights with no alignment of methods or data. 
Though, paradigms and limited data availability can lead to unintentional alignment of parameters, 
structures, and subjectivities that will remain unexplored regardless. 
 
Tools for interrogation are those which help us to understand why the model behaves as it does, and 
whether this behaviour reflects real world systems or is just an artefact of abstraction. Sensitivity 
analysis remains a powerful tool, here. By exploring macroscopic trends of model response to 
changing inputs (sensitivity), interpretable insights can emerge. Mallapragada, Sepulveda, and 
Jenkins (2020)S291 demonstrate this with their sensitivity analysis of breakeven cost (see their Figures 
3, 6, and 7). 
 
The key bottleneck in the interrogation process is from model outputs into the mental model of the 
interpreter. That interpreter may be the original modeller or an audience of their work, whose role it 
is to challenge the proposed mechanistic explanation. In either case, visualisation plays an important 
role. A clear visualisation of model behaviour can lead to immediate intuition. Rather than overstep 
our expertise by attempting to define what a good visualisation should be, we instead enumerate a 
few examples of visualisations that we have found particularly intuitive for understanding model 
behaviour. The energy modelling field may benefit from a more comprehensive review of 
visualisation methods for model diagnosis. 
 
These same authors demonstrate in Neumann and Brown (2021)S302 the use of correlation plots to 
visualise trade-offs of investment decisions between technologies (see Figure 6). Correlation plots 
are also used by Sinha et al (2024)S262 to similar effect (see Figure 6). Of this second article, we also 
note their visualisation of near-optimal investment decisions over time in Figure 1.

system model, oemof-moea. They produce Pareto fronts to visualise the trade-off of alternative 
objectives with total cost. Their Figure 6, visualising the trade-off between CO2 emissions and total 
cost, is particularly intuitive. 
 
Finally, Ruggles et al. (2024)S304 examine the impact of modelling a greater number of weather years 
on system adequacy. They find that modelling this increased weather variability leads to higher
