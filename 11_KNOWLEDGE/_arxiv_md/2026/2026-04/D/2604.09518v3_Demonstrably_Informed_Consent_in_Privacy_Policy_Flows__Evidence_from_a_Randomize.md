Abstract
Privacy policies govern how personal data is collected, used, and
shared. Yet, in most privacy-policy consent flows, agreement is
operationalized as a single click at the end of a long, opaque policy
document. Recent privacy-law scholarship has argued for a stan-
dard of demonstrably informed consent. That is, the party drafting
and designing privacy-policy consent mechanisms must generate
reliable evidence that a person demonstrates comprehension of the
consequential terms to which they agree. To this end, we study
pedagogical friction as a design framing: minimal interventions em-
bedded within a privacy-policy consent flow that aim to support
demonstrated comprehension while keeping burden on the user
low.
In a randomized experiment, we tested pedagogical friction for
demonstrably informed consent in the context of a privacy policy
for an edtech app for young children. We recruited 293 parents of
kids ages 3-8 to review the app’s privacy policy under one of six
conditions that varied presentation format and pacing, then com-
plete a six-question comprehension quiz. Three conditions offered
a second policy review and quiz retake for participants who did not
pass this quiz on their first attempt. We find that the slide-based
condition (G3) achieved the highest first-attempt threshold attain-
ment (≥80%) (41.7%), followed by the paced, sectioned condition
(G4) (30.6%). In the retake conditions, 64.9% of participants who
completed a second attempt improved their score. Notably, in con-
ditions that did not gate consent on demonstrated comprehension,
97.3% of participants who scored below the threshold still chose to
consent, suggesting that ungated consent flows can record agree-
ment without demonstrated comprehension. Our results suggest
that pedagogical friction can strengthen the evidentiary basis of
consent and clarify what it costs in time and burden.
Keywords
demonstrably informed consent, pedagogical friction, friction in
design, consent decision

Introduction
Consent in privacy-policy flows should carry weight only if it
reflects demonstrated comprehension [22]. In many digital settings,
though, clicking “I agree” tells us little about what a person actually
read or understood [10, 46]. Privacy policies are long, technical,
and routinely ignored [40, 52]. Many interfaces treat policy review
as a formality that sits between a user and access [38, 47]. As a
result, consent can become a procedural step instead of an informed
choice. In this paper, we study demonstrably informed consent
in the specific context of privacy-policy flows for digital services,
focusing on comprehension of a small set of substantively important
terms instead of full-policy reading.
Recent work at the intersection of privacy law and technology ar-
gues for a higher standard of demonstrably informed consent [22]. In
this view, the burden lies with the consent-seeker to show that the
person giving consent both intended to consent and understood key
terms. This framing shifts attention to the interface mechanisms
that shape reading, reflection, and confirmation. It also raises prac-
tical questions–how should consent flows be designed to produce
credible evidence of comprehension?
One answer is to introduce friction-in-design. Friction has typi-
cally been viewed as usability cost, but prior work shows that small,
well-placed interventions can redirect attention and interrupt auto-
matic behavior [1, 21, 37, 55]. Recent work in psychology argues
that moderate friction can support learning and meaning, while
overly frictionless interactions can undermine these benefits [60].
This framing motivates our focus on both benefits and tolerated
costs. Timing also matters. Behavior-change research finds that
guidance is most useful when it appears at the moment a person
can act on it [18, 19]. Related research in persuasion and behavioral
science suggest that timely cues can increase attention and elab-
oration, which can support retention of key information [41, 50].
Microlearning makes a similar point in educational settings, arguing
that short, focused instruction embedded within a task can support
comprehension without separate training sessions [3, 30, 53]. We
use pedagogical friction to describe structured, minimal interven-
tions embedded in a consent flow that aim to support demonstrated
comprehension while keeping burden as low as possible.
We evaluate these trade-offs empirically in the context of pri-
vacy policy consent. In particular, we tested pedagogical friction for
demonstrably informed consent in the context of a privacy policy
for an edtech app for young children. We recruited 293 parents
of kids ages 3-8 to review the app’s privacy policy under one of
six conditions that varied presentation format and pacing: plain
text privacy policy (control; G0); privacy policy with substantive
terms highlighted in yellow (G1); privacy policy with substantive
terms highlighted in yellow and additional explanatory blurbs ap-
pearing alongside highlighted text (G2); visual presentation of key
privacy terms through a timed slideshow (G3); a paced and sec-
tioned version of G1 (G4); and, a paced and sectioned version of
G2 (G5). After reviewing the policy, all participants completed a
six-question comprehension quiz. In three conditions (G2, G3, and

Our work contributes:
A practical framing for studying demonstrably informed
consent. We show how interface choices can be organized as peda-

policy consent flow.
Evidence about which pedagogical friction designs improve
demonstrated comprehension of key privacy terms. In a six-

Related Work
2.1
Friction in Policy Consent Flows
A common pattern in policy consent flows is fast progression with
limited attention to terms [48]. More broadly, related work on proso-
cial friction treats small amounts of resistance as a tool to slow users
down at consequential moments and to support more deliberate
choices [6, 21, 27, 45]. Closely related ideas appear under different
terms, including frictional design, friction-in-design, microbound-
aries, programmed inefficiencies, seamful design, slow design, and
work on consent interface patterns. Across these lines of work, the
shared claim is that interfaces can shape what people notice and
what they do next [47].
Seamful design highlights how making system limitations visible
can prompt reflection about what the infrastructure is doing [7].
Slow design argues for interfaces that create time and space for
deliberation in settings where speed works against careful judg-
ment [26]. Cox et al. [8] describe microboundaries as small inter-
action barriers that interrupt habitual use and encourage more
mindful decision making. Frischmann and Benesch [21] argue that
well-chosen friction can support reflection and self-determination,
and propose a framework that distinguishes types of friction, direct
effects on subjects, and intended purposes. Evidence from security
and privacy research also suggests that attention matters for out-
comes. Warnings that interrupt the task and require an explicit user
action tend to have stronger behavioral effects than passive cues
that users can ignore [32, 57].
Our study builds on this body of work by applying pedagogical
friction framing to privacy-policy consent flows, shaping attention
at consequential moments, teaching users about substantive privacy
terms, and measuring comprehension.
2.2
Microlearning
Microlearning is a broad term for instructional approaches that
deliver targeted, action-oriented content in small units designed
to achieve specific objectives within a short period [9, 43]. Prior
studies suggest that microlearning can support knowledge and
skill acquisition better than some traditional training methods [9,
12]. More generally, guidance embedded inside a task can focus
attention, support immediate correction, and improve retention
better than detached training [14]. This perspective also aligns
with behavioral accounts in which timely prompts matter most
when users have both the ability to act and a reason to do so [19].
These studies suggest that comprehension support should be
delivered where and when users make decisions. Interfaces can
provide short explanations for unfamiliar terms, structure expo-
sure through chunking and pacing, and include brief checks that
encourage users to revisit relevant text when they miss key points.
2.3
Usable Privacy
Privacy policies and similar disclosures are difficult for most people
to understand in one pass [56, 58]. Usable privacy research has
explored formats that make policy terms easier to find and com-
pare, including structured presentations and standardized notice
layouts [33, 34]. Schaub et al. [54] systematize these approaches by
mapping a design space for privacy notices and identifying design
dimensions that shape what users notice and how they interpret a
notice.
Structured presentations can shape perceived privacy risk and
willingness to proceed, but users may struggle when terminology is
unfamiliar or when label information is misaligned with underlying
practices [2, 4, 28]. Related work also shows that small interface
elements can affect whether users recognize and correctly interpret
available privacy choices [29]. Additional tools aim to reduce the
burden of long disclosures by segmenting and summarizing policy
text to help readers locate relevant clauses [59].
Our study fits within this usable privacy framework by treating
structured presentation and pacing as interface choices in a privacy-
policy consent flow and by measuring the costs of friction, users’
tolerance of the resulting burden, and evidence of demonstrated
comprehension at the point of consent.

2.4
Demonstrably Informed Consent
In research ethics, informed consent is commonly described as
involving information, comprehension, and voluntariness [49]. Sev-
eral strands of work emphasize that these conditions can be sup-
ported or undermined by interface design [20, 35, 42].
The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) strengthens
informational duties and places the burden of proving consent on
controllers, but it primarily articulates consent in terms of aware-
ness and demonstrability of the act of consenting, not verified
understanding of specific terms [5, 22, 51]. This distinction moti-
vates our empirical focus on privacy-policy consent flows that aim
to produce measurable evidence of comprehension.
Demonstrably informed consent strengthens this framing by
shifting responsibility for demonstrating comprehension onto the
party seeking consent. Specifically, the party seeking consent should
be able to produce evidence that a person both intended to consent
and understood substantively important terms at the time consent
was sought [22]. A central practical issue is scope. Demonstrably
informed consent does not require the same level of demonstrated
comprehension for every clause, and prior work highlights that
which terms are “substantive,” and how much detail must be under-
stood, varies by context [22, 36, 44].

Method
We conducted a randomized experiment where participants com-
pleted a short privacy consent task as part of a multi-step study flow
(see Figure 1). We built a learning website for children by adapting
public code from KidsZone [13]. The site includes pages that teach
children basic skills, such as the alphabet, counting, and simple
math.
Participants first answered a screening survey to confirm they
had a child. Participants who reported no child were screened out.
Next, we asked the remaining participants to take the role of a
parent who is deciding whether their child can use the learning
website. Before they made this decision, they read a privacy policy
for the site. The policy explains what data the site may collect from
children, how it may use and share that data, and what choices
parents have, including consent and deletion requests (see Appendix
Figure 7 for the full terms).
After reading the policy, participants took a quiz with six ques-
tions that matched key points in the policy (Quiz questions are
provided in Appendix Table 7). We used an 80% score as a basic
threshold for demonstrated comprehension. Because the quiz has
six questions, an 80% threshold effectively requires answering at
least five questions correctly. Participants could not return to the
policy pages during the quiz, so their answers relied on what they
remembered from reading. After the quiz and quiz feedback, par-
ticipants decided whether to provide consent to the privacy policy
terms or to decline consent. They then explored the website for two
minutes to mimic a brief parent check of the site content. Finally,
they completed an exit survey and the study ended (Table 2 lists
survey questions).
To preserve study validity, we did not disclose in advance that the
privacy-policy consent flow itself was the focus of the experiment.
The study protocol received prior approval from our Institutional
Review Board (IRB).

3.1
Experimental Conditions
After enrollment and screening, participants were randomly as-
signed to one of six experimental conditions (G0-G5) for privacy
policy review. As shown in Figure 2, the conditions differed in how
the policy was presented and how the quiz was administered. No-
tably, participants in all six conditions received the full privacy
policy text. What varied were the additional forms of pedagogical
friction.
Control group (G0): Plain text policy. Participants read a stan-
dard privacy policy page in plain text and then took the quiz once.
After the quiz, they were shown which questions they answered
incorrectly and the correct answers. Participants who scored at
least 80% could choose whether to provide consent to the privacy
policy terms or to decline consent. Participants who scored below
80% were still allowed to choose whether to consent; we use this
condition to measure consent without demonstrated comprehen-
sion. Quiz accuracy is the number of correct answers divided by
the total number of questions.
Treatment group 1 (G1): Highlighted policy. Participants read
the same policy page, but key policy statements were highlighted.
The highlighted text covered the information needed to answer the
quiz questions. Apart from highlighting, all other procedures were
identical to G0, including that consent was not gated on the quiz
threshold.
Treatment group 2 (G2): Highlighted policy with explanatory
blurbs. Participants read the highlighted policy and also saw short
explanatory blurbs next to the highlighted statements. The blurbs
were designed to explain the highlighted terms in simpler language.
Participants first took the quiz once. If they scored below 80%, they
were shown which questions they answered incorrectly, but not the
correct answers. They then reviewed the policy again and retook
the quiz. After the final quiz attempt, participants who scored at
least 80% could choose whether to provide consent to the privacy
policy terms or to decline consent. Participants who scored below
80% could not provide consent.
Treatment group 3 (G3): Timed slide-based policy. Partici-
pants first read a standard privacy policy page in plain text and
then viewed the policy in a slide format. The slide format was de-
signed to visually recap the key terms in a Q&A format. A 5 second
minimum time was enforced on each slide before they could con-
tinue. Participants then took the quiz once. If they scored below
80%, they reviewed the slides again and retook the quiz. All other
procedures were identical to G2.
Treatment group 4 (G4): Extended G1. This condition extended
G1 by splitting the highlighted policy into six sections, with one
section per page. A 5 second minimum time was enforced on each
page before participants could continue. Apart from these changes,
all other procedures were identical to G1, and consent was not
gated on the quiz threshold.

3.2
Study Design
Our study consisted of a friction-based policy review phase followed
by a post-review phase.
Friction-based policy review Phase.
(1) Participants completed screening questions to confirm that they
had a child and were eligible to take part in the study.
(2) Participants were assigned to one condition (G0-G5) and re-
viewed the privacy policy under that condition’s presentation set-
tings.
(3) Participants then completed the privacy policy quiz under the
condition’s quiz settings.
Post-review Phase.
(4) After completing the quiz and the consent decision, participants
explored the website for two minutes to mimic a brief parent check
of the site content.
(5) Participants then completed an exit survey. The survey asked
about their experience with the consent process and their views of
the website and its privacy policy, and then the study ended.
3.3
Participants and Exclusions
Participants were recruited via Positly. Eligibility required that par-
ticipants be at least 18 years old and have at least one child between
the ages of 3 and 8. Sessions lasted approximately 8 minutes, and
participants were paid $18.75 per hour, prorated to their completion
time.
We excluded data from participants who did not complete the
study. The final analytic dataset comprised 293 participants, dis-
tributed across the six groups as follows: G0 (46), G1 (47), G2 (48),
G3 (48), G4 (49), and G5 (55).
We conducted an a priori power analysis in G∗Power for a
between-subjects comparison across six groups. We used an as-
sumed effect size of 0.15, 𝛼= 0.05, and target power of 0.9. We
selected 0.15 as a conservative planning value for a modest omnibus
effect, since the study compared lightweight interface interventions
across six conditions. This analysis indicated a required total sample
size of 264. Our final analytic sample (293) exceeded this target.
3.4
Demographics
Participant demographics provided by Positly are summarized in
Appendix Table 4. The sample was 54.6% female and 45.4% male, and
most participants were 31 to 40 years old (50.7%). Education levels
were high school or below (30.9%) and bachelor’s degree (30.9%),
followed by graduate or professional degree (20.3%). Household
income was broadly distributed, with most participants reporting
$60,000 to $149,999 per year (56.0%).
We used chi squared tests to check whether demographics dif-
fered across the six groups. We found no significant differences
across groups for age category, gender, or education (all 𝑝≥0.05),
which is consistent with successful random assignment.
3.5
Instrumentation and Measures
During the study, our platform recorded policy review time, quiz
completion time, timestamps, and quiz responses for each partic-
ipant. These logs let us measure engagement and objective time
costs of friction, and they let us compute policy comprehension
from quiz accuracy. We also collected exit survey responses to cap-
ture participants’ perceived demonstrated comprehension and their
experience with the consent process, including self-reported burden
and whether the time felt worthwhile.
3.5.1
Interaction telemetry. Telemetry variables help describe how
participants engaged with the consent flow. They are not the main
outcomes, but they help interpret differences across conditions. We
measure:
• Policy review duration. Time spent viewing the policy content.
We report these in seconds.
• Quiz completion duration. Time spent answering the quiz.
We report these in seconds.
3.5.2
Policy comprehension measures. Participants completed a
six-question comprehension quiz tied to specific policy statements
(see Appendix Table 7). Quiz accuracy is the number of correct
answers divided by six.
• First quiz accuracy. Mean accuracy on the first quiz attempt
within each condition.
• Second quiz accuracy. Mean accuracy on the second attempt for
the conditions that included a retake (G2, G3, and G5), computed
only among participants who took the second quiz.
• Retake accuracy. Second-attempt accuracy among participants
who went on to take the second quiz (G2, G3, and G5). This
isolates the subgroup that did not meet the threshold on the first
attempt.
• Retake gain. The change in accuracy from the first to the sec-
ond attempt for retake participants (second accuracy minus first
accuracy). We report the average gain by condition.
• Retake improvement rate. Among participants who took the
second quiz, the proportion whose second score was higher than
their first score.
• Threshold attainment. The proportion of participants who
scored at least 80%, which we treat as demonstrated comprehen-
sion in all conditions, but G0, G1, and G4 do not enforce this rule
for consenting.

Exit survey measures. The exit survey used 5-point Likert
items (Strongly agree to Strongly disagree) to measure participants’

the interface features, and self-reported burden and whether the
time felt worthwhile. Most exit survey items were positively framed,
but Item 8 was negatively framed and asked whether the consent
process felt like a burden. We use these items to summarize partici-

Results
Our results are organized in two stages. We first report inferential
comparisons for first-attempt outcomes that are defined across all
six conditions. We then report descriptive analyses for outcomes
among retakers and consent patterns under gated versus ungated
flows.
RQ1: Can added friction during privacy policy review improve
demonstrated comprehension of key terms prior to consent?
4.1
RQ1: Quiz performance by condition
We first compare performance on the initial privacy policy quiz
across conditions. Quiz accuracy is the fraction of the six questions
answered correctly. We also report the proportion of participants
who met the consent threshold on the first attempt (details are
provided in Table 1).
First-attempt mean accuracy in the control condition G0 (plain
text) was 0.52 (Figure 3), comparable to G5 (paced, sectioned with
blurbs; 0.52), and slightly lower than G1 (highlighted; 0.54) and
G2 (highlighted with blurbs; 0.54). The highest first-attempt mean
accuracies were observed in G3 (timed slide-based; 0.60) and G4
(paced, sectioned highlighting; 0.63).
A clearer separation appears when we consider the first-attempt
threshold outcome. In G0, 17.4% of participants met the threshold
on their first quiz. Threshold attainment increased in G3 (41.7%)
and in G4 (30.6%). This shows that the paced, structured designs,
especially the timed slide-based condition G3, are the conditions
most associated with higher first-attempt threshold attainment.
We ran a one-way ANOVA with condition as a between-subjects
factor to test for differences in first-attempt mean quiz accuracy
(𝐹(5, 287) = 1.77, 𝑝= .12). It indicated that differences in average
accuracy were modest relative to within-condition variability. In
contrast, first-attempt threshold attainment differed by condition
(𝜒2(5, 293) = 14.1, 𝑝= .015). Compared with G0 (17.4%), thresh-
old attainment was higher in G3 (41.7%) and G4 (30.6%). In Holm-
adjusted pairwise comparisons, the G3 vs. G5 contrast remained
reliable (𝑝= .03).

Consent Decisions
We define consent rate as the fraction of participants who selected

4.7
Demographic Patterns
We conducted demographic analysis to assess whether quiz out-
comes varied across age and education groups (Appendix Table 5).
Across age groups, first-attempt mean accuracy was similar for
participants aged 19-40 (0.54) and was higher for ages 41-50 (0.60)
and 51-64 (0.64). First-attempt threshold attainment showed a sim-
ilar pattern, increasing from 25.0% (ages 19-30) to 33.3% (51-64).
Second-attempt mean accuracy ranged from 0.51 to 0.66 across the
younger and middle-age groups.
Across education groups, first-attempt mean accuracy was simi-
lar for high school, associate degree, and bachelor’s degree (0.50-
0.52), and higher for graduate degree (0.62). First-attempt threshold
attainment followed the same direction, rising from 11.8% (high
school) to 32.1% (graduate degree).
First-attempt mean accuracy and threshold attainment were
higher in the 41-64 age groups than in the younger group. Second-
attempt threshold attainment was higher in the high school and
associate groups than in the bachelor’s and graduate groups, sug-
gesting that a second attempt may be particularly helpful for par-
ticipants who started with lower first-attempt performance.
5
Discussion
Our main outcome is whether participants demonstrate comprehen-
sion on the quiz before privacy-policy consent. Two higher friction
conditions showed the strongest first-attempt performance: the
timed slide-based condition (G3) achieved the highest first-attempt
threshold attainment, and the paced, sectioned highlighting con-
dition (G4) was next. Mean accuracy follows the same ordering,
although differences in mean accuracy are modest. For participants
who did not demonstrate comprehension on the first attempt, the
paced, sectioned format with blurbs and a retake (G5) showed the
strongest observed recovery, with the highest second-attempt accu-
racy and the highest second-attempt threshold attainment among
retakers. Our results suggest that different forms of pedagogical
friction support different stages of demonstrably informed con-
sent, either improving first-attempt demonstrated comprehension
or supporting recovery in retake.
5.1
Pedagogical friction can increase
demonstrated comprehension
In our study, designs that structured exposure and slowed pro-
gression through the policy were most associated with reaching
the 80% threshold. The timed slide-based condition (G3) produced
the highest first-attempt threshold attainment, and the paced, sec-
tioned format (G4) showed a similar direction. By contrast, lighter
friction (plain text or highlighting) produced smaller shifts. Our
results suggest that if the goal is evidence of demonstrated compre-
hension before consent, the interface must do more than present
information. It must shape how people encounter it.
5.2
Retakes support improved demonstrated
comprehension
Retakes provided a measurable path to improved comprehension
for retakers. In all retake conditions, average scores increased on the
second attempt and a majority of retakers improved. The strongest

observed retake pattern appears in G5, which combines section-
ing, pacing, and blurbs. G5 had the largest mean gain, the highest
fraction of retakers who improved, and the highest second-attempt
threshold attainment.
Answer transitions demonstrate that retakers corrected 52.1%
of previously missed questions, while backsliding on previously
correct questions was limited overall and lowest in G5. The typical
retaker fixed about one more question than they newly missed. This
pattern is consistent with targeted correction after re-exposure,
which is the intended purpose of retake design that aims to support
demonstrated comprehension.
5.3
Comprehension checks and privacy-policy
consent decisions
The privacy-policy consent analysis highlights a key limitation
of click-through consent flows in our study. When consent was
not gated on demonstrated comprehension, 97.3% of participants
still chose to consent even when they scored below threshold. This
pattern shows that recorded agreement can be present even when
demonstrated comprehension on the quiz is not. In contrast, when
the flow required demonstrated comprehension for eligibility, 87.4%
of participants who met the threshold chose to consent.
This difference is important for privacy-policy consent mecha-
nisms. A flow that records only a click can overstate how informed
a choice was. A flow that pairs consent with a demonstrated com-
prehension criterion provides a clearer record of what was demon-
strated on the quiz.
5.4
Observed costs of pedagogical friction
The main observed costs of pedagogical friction were higher time-
on-task and higher self-reported burden in some conditions. The
more structured designs required more time for policy review, and
conditions with retakes added additional quiz time. Exit survey
results also show higher burden endorsement in conditions with
more enforced exposure and retakes. These results make the trade-
off explicit that stronger evidence of demonstrated comprehension
can come with higher time costs and higher perceived burden. De-
signers must choose how much friction is justified for a given con-
text, balancing the desired strength of the comprehension evidence
against the extra time and burden.
5.5
Selecting substantive terms for
demonstrably informed consent
A practical challenge for demonstrably informed consent is decid-
ing what a user must understand [22, 23]. Privacy policies cover
many topics, and what counts as a substantively important term
can vary by context. In practice, the set of key terms is likely to
reflect what a service expects users to notice, what matters for users
making decisions, and what the service would later want to be able
to enforce. Legal regulation may also determine what terms are
substantively important. Given this background, we do not treat
our quiz as a universal taxonomy of privacy terms users must un-
derstand. Instead, it is a concrete, context-relevant set chosen to
make teaching and assessing demonstrated comprehension feasible
within a realistic consent flow.
In designing the study policy and quiz, we aimed for a stimulus
that was concise enough to be read in an experiment while still
reflecting common disclosure topics for a child-directed app. We
therefore centered the policy on GDPR- and Children’s Online
Privacy Protection Act (COPPA)-relevant categories, including the
types of personal information that may be collected, how that
information may be used, and when it may be shared [16, 17, 24] (see
Appendix Figure 7). Our quiz items operationalize these categories
as assessable terms: persistent identifiers (Item 1), deletion rights
and how parents can exercise them (Item 2), geolocation collection
and the conditions for verifiable parental consent (Item 3), the
COPPA age threshold for verifiable parental consent (Item 5), and
limits on sharing for business functions, legal compliance, and
safety (Item 6) (Appendix Table 7). We also included a dispute
resolution provision (Item 4), because arbitration is a substantively
important contractual term in many digital agreements even when
it is not treated as a standard privacy disclosure [11, 15].
Our approach illustrates that, rather than treating a policy as a
single block of text, the consent-seeker can identify a small set of
key terms that are both decision-relevant and substantively impor-
tant in the given context, then pair those terms with lightweight
teaching mechanisms and aligned assessments. In our study, the
interface interventions focus attention during policy review, while
the quiz provides a direct signal of whether participants under-
stood the selected terms. Future designs will need to choose which
terms to emphasize, but the broader point remains that demon-
strably informed consent requires not only disclosure, but also a
deliberate selection of key terms and a way to verify demonstrated
comprehension of them.
5.6
Practical implications
Our results reinforce a basic limitation of notice and choice. Peo-
ple commonly ignore privacy policies, and click-through consent
provides weak evidence of comprehension [31]. A practical impli-
cation is that privacy-policy consent flows should be designed to
produce evidence of comprehension for a small set of substantively
important terms. Our design illustrates one lightweight way to do
this by shaping exposure and attention during review and pairing
consent with a comprehension criterion. The main design decision
is calibration. Designers should use the lightest friction that reli-
ably supports the desired evidence threshold and reserve stronger
friction for higher stakes terms and contexts.
Our design can also be treated as a reusable interaction pattern
for consent critical interfaces. Interface structure can strongly steer
user behavior, including through manipulative designs [25, 39],
while well-timed, contextual cues can support better decisions when
they appear at the moment users can act [33, 54]. Our findings
suggest a simple design and evaluation template. Designers can
select context relevant key terms, guide attention with components
such as structured presentation, pacing, brief explanations, and
retake loops, and validate outcomes using a clear comprehension
criterion such as threshold attainment. This moves privacy-policy
consent beyond a single recorded click toward an interface behavior
that can be measured and iterated.

Limitations and future work
Participants in our study reviewed the privacy policy before directly
browsing the website, so the study isolates consent design under a
fixed service description instead of testing comprehension after full
product exploration. Website exploration also occurred only after
the privacy-policy consent decision and lasted two minutes. As a
result, Item 1 in Table 2 reflects a broader post-review judgment
about the app and may incorporate perceived usefulness or other
non-privacy considerations.
Our study uses a short quiz tied to a single policy and a single
session. The six-question quiz supports a clean threshold rule, but
it also makes accuracy coarse. When many participants answer
three or four questions correctly, small improvements can be hard
to distinguish from noise, and the quiz may not capture deeper
demonstrated comprehension of policy implications. In addition,
our study did not directly measure cognitive load or mental effort
with a validated scale, so the survey results should be interpreted
as self-reported reactions to the study instead of comprehensive
measures of burden. Future work should validate these findings with
longer and more fine-grained comprehension measures, including
question sets that probe both factual recall and what the terms
mean in practice.
Although our sample size exceeds the a priori power target, a
larger study would provide tighter uncertainty bounds and better
sensitivity to smaller design differences, especially for subgroup
comparisons and condition pairs that are close in performance.
Replication with additional participants and across multiple policies
and domains would strengthen confidence in the generality of
pedagogical friction effects.

Ethics Statement
Our study protocol was reviewed and approved by our IRB before
recruitment. All participants were informed about the study proce-
dure before they began and could stop at any time without penalty.
Participants were paid $18.75 per hour, prorated for time spent. We
did not collect participant images, audio, or video. Participants were
told that the website would access the types of data described in
the privacy policy, but our study did not actually collect or access
such personal data. In addition, to preserve study validity, we did
not disclose in advance that the privacy-policy consent flow itself
was the focus of the experiment.
Our study website stored study metadata on a secure server so
that we could compute accuracy measures linked only to anony-
mous participant identifiers. Identifying information remained on
the Positly platform and was not included in the analytic dataset
or shared outside the research team.
7
Conclusion
We evaluate pedagogical friction framing as a practical way to sup-
port demonstrably informed consent in privacy-policy flows. In a
six-condition experiment, we find that designs with stronger peda-
gogical friction are associated with better demonstrated comprehen-
sion, including higher first-attempt performance and clearer gains
in retakes. Answer changes among retakers are consistent with
targeted correction after re-exposure. These results suggest that
privacy-policy consent flows can be designed to produce stronger
evidence of comprehension. We provide an empirical template that
links key terms to measurable comprehension outcomes, which can
be reused to test longer policies, other domains, and more sensitive
assessments.

KidsZone Privacy Policy
KidsZone is committed to protecting the privacy of children who use our site. The U.S. Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act ("COPPA") requires parental consent for
collecting personal information from children, for all children under the age of 13 years old.
1. THE INFORMATION WE COLLECT FROM CHILDREN, HOW WE USE IT, AND HOW AND WHEN WE COMMUNICATE WITH PARENTS
KidsZone offers to its users a range of sites and applications, some targeted at children. Activities may collect information from children. We retain child-collected personal data
only as long as necessary for the activity, security, or legal requirements. If information is collected inconsistent with COPPA, we will delete it or seek parental consent.
1.1 Registration
Children can register to view content, play games, and participate in contests. During registration, we may collect parent email, child’s first name, gender, username, password,
and birth date to validate age. We don’t ask for more information than necessary for the activity.
1.2 About the collection of parent email address
Before collecting any personal data from children under 13 years old, we ask for a parent/guardian email address so we can obtain parental consent. If you believe your child’s
data was collected without notice, contact privacycontact@kidszone.com. Parent emails for consent won’t be used for marketing unless opted in.
1.3 Verifiable Parental Consent
In the event KidsZone wishes to collect personal information from a child, COPPA requires that we first seek a parent or guardian’s consent by email. In the email we will
explain what information we are collecting, how we plan to use it, how the parent can provide consent, and how the parent can revoke consent. If we do not receive parental
consent within a reasonable time, we will delete the parent contact information and any other information collected from the child in connection with that activity.
1.4 Content Generated by a Child
Certain activities on our sites and applications allow children to create or manipulate content and save it with KidsZone. Some of these activities do not require children to
provide any personal information and therefore do not require parental consent. If an activity potentially allows a child to insert personal information in their created content,
we will either pre-screen the submission to delete any personal information, or we will seek verifiable parental consent by email for the collection. Examples of created content
that may include personal information are stories or other open-text fields, and drawings that allow text or free-hand entry of information. If, in addition to collecting content
that includes personal information, KidsZone also plans to post the content publicly or share it with a third party for the third party’s own use, we will seek verifiable parental
consent by email.
1.5 Email Contact with a Child
In connection with certain activities or services, we may collect a child’s online contact information, such as an email address, in order to communicate with the child more
than once. In such instances we will retain the child’s online contact information to honor the request and for no other purpose such as marketing. One example would be a
newsletter that provides occasional updates about a site, game/activity, television show, personality/character, or feature movie. Whenever we collect a child’s online contact
information for ongoing communications, we will require a parent email address in order to notify the parent about the collection and use of the child’s information, as well as to
provide the parent an opportunity to prevent further contact with the child. On some occasions a child may be engaged in more than one ongoing communication, and a parent
may be required to “opt-out” of each communication individually.
1.6 Geolocation Data
If a child-directed KidsZone site or application collects geolocation information that is specific enough to equate to the collection of a street address, we will first seek parental
consent via email.
1.7 Persistent Identifiers
When children interact with us, certain information may automatically be collected, both to make our sites and applications more interesting and useful to children and for
various purposes related to our business. Examples include the type of computer operating system, the child’s IP address or mobile device identifier, the web browser, the
frequency with which the child visits various parts of our sites or applications, and information regarding the online or mobile service provider. This information is collected
using technologies such as cookies, flash cookies, web beacons, and other unique identifiers. This information may be collected by KidsZone or by a third party. This data is
principally used for internal purposes only, in order to:
- provide children with access to features and activities on our sites and applications
- conduct research and analysis to address the performance of our sites and applications
- generate anonymous reporting for use by KidsZone
KidsZone may share or disclose a child’s personal information as needed to provide our Service or with your consent or permission. For example, we share information with our
trusted vendors, third party service providers and individuals to provide services for us on our behalf, which may include analytics providers and hosting services. We may also
share personal information if we have a good faith belief that access, use, preservation, or disclosure of such information is reasonably necessary to (a) satisfy any applicable law,
regulation, legal process, or enforceable governmental request; (b) enforce applicable Terms of Service, including investigation of potential violations thereof; (c) detect, prevent
or otherwise address fraud, security or technical issues; (d) protect the rights, property, or personal safety of KidsZone, its users, or the public; or (e) as required or permitted
by law If KidsZone becomes involved in a merger, acquisition, bankruptcy, change of control, or any form of sale of some or all of its assets, personal information may be
transferred or disclosed in connection with the business transaction, subject to any applicable laws. We may also share aggregate or de-identified information in a manner that
cannot be reasonably used to identify an individual user.
2. PARENTAL CHOICES AND CONTROLS
At any time, parents can refuse to permit us to collect further personal information from their children in association with a particular account, and can request that we delete
from our records the personal information we have collected in connection with that account. Please keep in mind that a request to delete records may lead to a termination of
an account, membership, or other service. Where a child has registered for a KidsZone account, we use the following method to allow parents to access, change, or delete the
personally identifiable information that we have collected from their children. Parents can ask for deletion of all personal information collected in connection with their child’s
account and Kids Zone will delete this information. Any other inquiries may be directed to:
Kids Zone Phone: (000) 123-4567 Email: privacycontact@kidszone.com
In any correspondence such as e-mail or mail, please include the child’s username and the parent’s email address and telephone number. To protect children’s privacy and
security, we will take reasonable steps to help verify a parent’s identity before granting access to any personal information.
3. ARBITRATION of DISPUTES
In the event a dispute shall arise between the parties to this policy, it is hereby agreed that the dispute shall be referred to United States Arbitration and Mediation for arbitration
in accordance with United States Arbitration and Mediation Rules of Arbitration. “Dispute” includes any claim, dispute, action, or other controversy, whether based on past,
present, or future events, whether based in contract, tort, statute, or common law, between you and KidsZone. The arbitrator’s decision shall be final and binding.
