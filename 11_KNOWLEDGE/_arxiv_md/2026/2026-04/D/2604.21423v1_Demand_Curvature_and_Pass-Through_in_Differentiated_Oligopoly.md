Abstract
This paper studies cost pass-through in differentiated-product oligopoly. I derive a general
representation of the pass-through matrix that decomposes equilibrium price responses into the
roles of demand curvature, substitution, and multiproduct ownership. This extends the classic
insight in single-product monopoly to multiproduct settings in which diversion and ownership
also matter. I then develop a tractable first-order approximation that yields a sufficient-statistics
characterization for empirically relevant demand systems. Finally, I characterize the small-share
limit and show how common demand specifications impose tail restrictions that shape pass-
through. The results provide a practical framework for applied work on tax incidence, merger
analysis, and related questions in imperfect competition.
Keywords: Pass-through, demand curvature, oligopoly, differentiated products, sufficient statis-

Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Cost pass-through is a central determinant of prices, markups, and welfare in imperfectly compet-
itive markets. It governs how marginal cost shocks—arising from input prices, taxes, tariffs, or
mergers—translate into consumer prices and therefore plays a key role in competition policy. In
practice, merger evaluation and incidence analysis often rely, implicitly or explicitly, on assumptions
about pass-through. Yet, despite its importance, pass-through is often treated as a reduced-form
object, with limited attention to how it is shaped by underlying demand primitives and strategic
interactions.
A large theoretical literature has shown that pass-through depends on both conduct and demand
curvature. In perfect competition, pass-through is governed by demand and supply elasticities,
whereas under a monopoly, it also depends on curvature, reflecting how marginal willingness to pay
varies with quantity. More broadly, Weyl and Fabinger (2013) emphasize that incidence reasoning
extends well beyond taxation to settings such as procurement, price discrimination, mergers, market
design, and welfare comparisons. These insights highlight the central role of curvature in shaping
how cost shocks propagate through prices.
However, extending these insights to a general oligopoly setting is nontrivial.
Much of the
theoretical intuition has been developed in relatively tractable environments, such as single-product
monopoly or settings with strong symmetry or aggregation assumptions. While these frameworks
provide sharp intuition, they abstract from the rich substitution patterns and ownership structures
that characterize modern empirical models of markets with imperfect competition.
In empirical work, firms typically offer many products, and pass-through is naturally a matrix
capturing cross-product interactions. In these settings, pass-through is computed as the inverse of
the pricing Jacobian, a high-dimensional object that depends on demand derivatives and owner-
ship structure in a complex way. As a result, it is often evaluated numerically rather than analyzed
directly, and its dependence on economically meaningful primitives—such as curvature and substi-
tution patterns—can be difficult to interpret.
This gap in theory and practice raises a natural question: how can pass-through be character-
ized in a general oligopoly setting in a way that is both economically transparent and analytically

with limited data.
1.2
Main Findings
This paper studies cost pass-through in differentiated-product oligopoly with multiproduct firms.
The main objective is to replace the usual black-box matrix inverse with a characterization of
pass-through in economically interpretable terms.
The analysis shows how demand curvature,
substitution, diversion, and ownership jointly determine the incidence of cost shocks.
The paper makes four contributions.
First, it extends the familiar monopoly link between
curvature and pass-through to a general multiproduct setting. In a single-product monopoly, the
same curvature term governs both the second-order condition and the pass-through formula. In
a multiproduct setting, the relevant object is no longer scalar. I show that local stability can be
summarized by a directional stability margin, and that the same margin governs the magnitude of
the equilibrium price response along the realized adjustment direction. This provides a multiproduct
analog of the Bulow and Pfleiderer (1983) intuition.
Second, I characterize the pricing Jacobian itself. I show that the Jacobian of the normalized
pricing system can be decomposed into a baseline own-price term, a demand-side term that captures
curvature and substitution, and an ownership term that captures direct diversion internalization and
diversion reallocation. This decomposition gives a transparent map from primitives to pass-through
and makes clear why pass-through can be high when curvature and ownership interactions flatten
the pricing system. It also yields a Neumann-series representation of the pass-through matrix, which
interprets equilibrium incidence as a direct effect plus successive rounds of competitive feedback
across products. The resulting low-order approximations are simple to compute and perform well
in the numerical illustration.
Third, I characterize the small-share limit of the pricing system. The results show that pass-
through is governed by the local tail behavior of demand, which yields a useful classification of
demand systems. In thin-tail models such as logit and mixed logit with log-normal mixing, cross-

arises because merger incentives are endogenous to prices, and obtain a thin-tail approximation that
replaces the full pass-through matrix with a diagonal benchmark based on own-price elasticities.
Taken together, these results provide a practical framework for tax incidence, merger analysis, and
related counterfactuals in settings where full curvature information is difficult to recover.
1.3
Related Literature
This paper relates to several strands of the literature on pass-through, imperfect competition, and
differentiated-product demand.
A large theoretical literature shows that pass-through depends on demand curvature under
imperfect competition. Bulow and Pfleiderer (1983) established this insight in monopoly, showing
that pass-through depends on curvature in addition to elasticity, and Weyl and Fabinger (2013)
generalized it to a broad framework for incidence and welfare.
Related work examines welfare
and incidence (Adachi and Fabinger, 2022) and the role of curvature in other settings (Mr´azov´a
and Neary, 2017; Aguirre, Cowan and Vickers, 2010). Much of the curvature-based pass-through
literature focuses on monopoly, symmetric oligopoly, or other tractable environments in which the
relevant demand-side object can be summarized by a scalar.
This paper extends that logic to
differentiated-product oligopoly with multiproduct firms, where pass-through is naturally matrix-
valued and depends jointly on curvature, substitution patterns, and ownership.
The paper is also related to the literature on pass-through and pricing in differentiated-product

that curvature and strategic interaction shape tax incidence and merger effects, while Jaffe and
Weyl (2013) and the upward pricing pressure literature emphasize that local price effects can be
approximated using pass-through and pricing incentives (Miller, Remer, Ryan and Sheu, 2016,
2017b). Closely related is Miravete, Seim and Thurk (2023), who show that incidence predictions

A related literature studies how demand specification disciplines curvature in differentiated-
product models.
Standard models such as logit, nested logit, and mixed logit are widely used

implied by demand systems, whereas this paper studies how curvature enters the pricing system in
differentiated-product oligopoly. In particular, while their framework summarizes curvature at the
product level, the present paper emphasizes that equilibrium pass-through is governed by a pricing
Jacobian that depends jointly on curvature, substitution patterns, and ownership, and is therefore
naturally matrix-valued.
Finally, the paper relates to the empirical literature documenting substantial heterogeneity
in pass-through across products, industries, and competitive environments (Miller, Osborne and
Sheu, 2017a; Kim and Cotterill, 2008; Muehlegger and Sweeney, 2022; Flaaen, Horta¸csu, Tintelnot,
Urdaneta and Xu, 2025). Ritz (2024) shows that the relationship between competition and pass-
through depends on the underlying environment, while MacKay, Miller, Remer and Sheu (2014)
highlight the difficulty of interpreting reduced-form pass-through estimates when pass-through is
non-constant or cost shocks are only partially observed. This paper complements that literature by
providing a structural interpretation of such heterogeneity: the decomposition developed here clar-

Small-Share Limits of Pass-Through Matrix
How do demand assumptions shape curvature and pass-through, and when does pass-through admit
a tractable approximation based on limited information? This section studies small-share approxi-
mations to the pass-through matrix. When product shares are small, demand is evaluated in a tail

it simplifies the pricing system: as shares vanish, many cross-product interactions become negligi-
ble, yielding a tractable limiting approximation with relatively low data requirements. In this way,
the analysis connects familiar monopoly pass-through results to differentiated oligopoly.

5.4
Extension to Multiproduct Firms
The single-product results above show how demand assumptions restrict the limiting behavior of
the normalized pricing Jacobian through the tail behavior of own- and cross-semi-elasticities. In
multiproduct environments, this logic remains central but is no longer sufficient. With nontrivial
ownership, pass-through depends not only on the normalized pricing Jacobian, but also on the
diversion structure Λ = −Ω◦D. Moreover, by Lemma 2,
Jf = −2I + K + C,
where K collects demand-curvature and substitution terms, and C collects ownership effects through
diversion ratios and their price derivatives. Thus the multiproduct small-share problem is governed
jointly by the limits of K, C, and Λ.
This perspective clarifies what survives from the single-product analysis. The tail behavior
of demand continues to govern the limit of K: if own and cross interactions vanish in the tail,
then the demand-side system becomes asymptotically diagonal. In that case, diversion terms also
vanish, so C and Λ become negligible and ownership has no first-order effect.
By contrast, if
demand preserves first-order substitution within economically meaningful groups of products, then
the tail classification does not collapse to the diagonal benchmark. Instead, those surviving demand
interactions can keep both diversion and ownership effects first-order in the limit.
A useful benchmark is nested logit, in which cross-nest shares may vanish while within-nest
shares remain stable. In that case, the demand-side limit is block diagonal by nest rather than
diagonal. Multiproduct ownership matters when it links products within those surviving blocks,
because it then enters the limit through C and Λ. More generally, the multiproduct extension
preserves the logic of the single-product analysis: demand assumptions shape the tail behavior of
substitution and curvature, and ownership matters precisely to the extent that those demand-side
interactions survive in the small-share limit. Appendix B.4 develops this benchmark in detail.

Conclusion
This paper studies how demand curvature shapes pass-through in multiproduct differentiated
oligopoly.
While the single-product monopoly case is well understood, much less is known in
multiproduct environments with differentiated products and general ownership structures. The
paper develops a framework that extends the curvature–pass-through connection to that broader
setting.
The first contribution is to show that the second-order condition of the multiproduct pric-
ing problem imposes a directional restriction on demand curvature. This yields a differentiated-
oligopoly analog of the familiar monopoly curvature bound, while making clear that in multiproduct
settings the relevant object is directional rather than scalar. The same directional stability margin
also governs the size of direct price responses to cost and tax shocks.
The second contribution is to decompose the pricing Jacobian into terms that isolate the roles
of demand curvature, substitution, and ownership.
This decomposition clarifies how strategic
interaction modifies the monopoly intuition and provides a transparent map from demand primitives
to pass-through. Building on it, the paper derives a Neumann-series representation of the pass-
through matrix, which interprets equilibrium incidence as a direct effect plus successive rounds of
competitive feedback.
The third contribution is to characterize the small-share limit.
The results show that this
limit is informative not only for approximation but also for economic interpretation. In thin-tail
environments, pass-through becomes asymptotically diagonal and can be disciplined by own-price
elasticity alone. In heavier-tail environments, by contrast, heterogeneity can survive in the limit, so
curvature and pass-through need not collapse to the logit benchmark. These findings clarify how
functional-form assumptions shape incidence through their implicit tail restrictions.
Taken together, the results suggest that pass-through in differentiated oligopoly can be under-
stood through a small set of economically meaningful objects: slopes, curvature, diversion, and
ownership. This perspective is useful both theoretically and empirically. It links local comparative
statics to demand primitives, yields tractable approximations in high-dimensional settings, and
provides simple ways to discipline incidence, welfare, and merger calculations under limited data.

Interpretation.
The log-normal case behaves like a thin-tail model. Along the ray p(n) = λnp,
the relevant contribution to demand comes from consumers whose price coefficients are still large
enough that all conditional inside shares vanish.
As a result, substitution across products be-
comes asymptotically negligible: the off-diagonal interaction terms vanish, and the diagonal term
converges to the logit benchmark −1. In this sense, log-normal mixing preserves the basic logit
implication that small shares lead to an asymptotically diagonal pass-through matrix.

α = 0. As prices scale up, the relevant consumers are those with α ≍λ−1

effective own-price sensitivity relative to that benchmark, χj(p) captures how market interaction
changes local own-price curvature, and ξjk(p) captures the surviving first-order interaction between

relevant ray, then Cj(p) varies little with prices, and the correction terms ζj(p), χj(p), and ξjk(p)

term is approximately −1 + 1/r, and the off-diagonal terms are approximately zero.
What makes these corrections small is economically intuitive. They will be small when the
low-α consumers who remain relevant in the tail assign little conditional mass to rival inside goods.
This is more likely when rival products have relatively low mean utilities δℓ, when their prices are
high relative to pj along the ray, or when product j is effectively isolated in the rescaled choice

local geometry resembles the finite-choke-type class, while substitution remains first-order within
economically meaningful groups of products.
