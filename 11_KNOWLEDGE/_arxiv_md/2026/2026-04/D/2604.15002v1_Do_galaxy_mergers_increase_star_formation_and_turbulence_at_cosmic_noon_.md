ABSTRACT
Mergers and interactions can significantly affect the morphological and dynamical properties of galaxies, however the impact of
mergers on turbulence at 𝑧> 1 has not been observationally constrained. In this work we use the interaction strength parameter
𝑄𝑃to identify likely interacting and isolated galaxies at cosmic noon (𝑧∼1 −2) within the KMOS3D integral field spectroscopy
survey, utilising redshifts from the 3D-HST, CANDELS and UVCANDELS surveys. For 186 galaxies, we measure deconvolved
H𝛼kinematics, including velocity dispersion, using a spatially non-parametric approach to account for observational effects
in the dynamically diverse range of galaxies. We compare offsets in H𝛼flux, star formation rate (SFR), dust attenuations,
and velocity dispersion of likely interacting galaxies to isolated control galaxies matched in mass and lookback time. We find
increased H𝛼fluxes and SFRs in the likely interacting sample at the level of ∼0.1 dex, a similar enhancement to studies of local
pairs. In contrast, we find no significant increase in the level of velocity dispersion in interacting galaxies compared to their
controls. The lack of increase in dispersion may reflect a combination of physical and observational factors, including limits to
increasing turbulent motions in an already turbulent medium and spectral resolution limits.
Key words: galaxies: interactions – galaxies: kinematics and dynamics – galaxies: high-redshift
1 INTRODUCTION
According to the hierarchical Λ Cold Dark Matter (ΛCDM)
paradigm, galaxies grow through two main processes: successive
mergers and the accretion of gas. Mergers are significant events in
the life cycles of galaxies −shown to drive both morphological and
dynamical diversity across cosmic time (e.g., Dubois et al. 2016;
Sparre & Springel 2017; Lagos et al. 2018).
Interactions of massive (log(𝑀★/𝑀⊙) ∼8 −12) star-forming disc
galaxies at 𝑧≲1 are associated with substantial changes in galaxy star
formation rates (SFRs), metallicities and dynamics. Tidal disruptions
from close companions are believed to induce inflows of relatively
metal-poor gas from the outer regions of galaxies to their more
metal-rich centres. These inflows enhance star formation and dilute
metallicity throughout the whole disc and most prominently near the
galaxy centre (Ellison et al. 2013; Bustamante et al. 2020; Ferreira
et al. 2025). Studies of thousands of close pairs in the Sloan Digital
Sky Suvey (SDSS) (𝑧≲0.12) have found that pairs have up to
∼0.13 −0.25 dex higher total SFRs than isolated galaxies matched
in mass, redshift and environment (Bustamante et al. 2020; Ferreira
et al. 2025) and even stronger enhancements when considering only
the inner few kiloparsec regions, up to ∼0.2−0.3 dex (Scudder et al.
2012; Ellison et al. 2013). Metallicity dillution shows the opposite
trend, where close pairs in SDSS have total metallicity offsets of
around −0.035 dex (Bustamante et al. 2020) and −0.04 to −0.07 dex

𝑧∼0 pairs have been found to have flatter metallicity gradients than
their matched controls, likely a result of lower metallicity gas flows
to the galaxy centres (Kewley et al. 2010). Our own galaxy provides a
further example, where metallicity dilution has been observed in the
age-metallicity relation of Milky Way disc-stars (Ciucă et al. 2024),
believed to be cause by the gas-rich Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus major
merger.
Both gas and stellar kinematics are also expected to be impacted
by interactions. Observations and simulations find that mergers cause
complex, non-axisymmetric rotation and velocity dispersion profiles
in gas discs (e.g., Shapiro et al. 2008; Epinat et al. 2009; Bellocchi
et al. 2012). Mergers also increase the intrinsic turbulence of galaxies,
likely due to inducing shocks and gas inflows (e.g., Rich et al. 2015;
Fensch et al. 2017; Puech et al. 2019; Perna et al. 2022; Baron et al.
2024). Puech et al. (2019) analysed the velocity dispersion of ionised
gas in mergers at 𝑧∼0.6, and, by comparing with simulations to
determine merger time evolution, found ∼1.5 −2 × increases in ve-
locity dispersion (Δ log 𝜎∼0.18−0.3), which correlated with peaks
in star formation and gravitational potential perturbations during the
merger process.
Galaxy properties are often studied, and observed to change, as
a function of pair separation. SFR enhancements and metallicity
dilutions have been observed in pairs with separations as large as
∼100 kpc, which may include galaxies which are post-first passage
(Scudder et al. 2012; Patton et al. 2013; Bustamante et al. 2020;
Ferreira et al. 2025). Offsets become stronger as pair separation

Interaction-driven changes in galaxy properties at 𝑧≳1 are less
certain, and may change across cosmic time as galaxies evolve.
Galaxies at higher redshift are thicker, more turbulent, gas-rich sys-
tems than local galaxies (e.g., Tacconi et al. 2020; Wisnioski et al.
2025; Yu et al. 2026). As such, the primary merger type changes
from gas-rich mergers at 𝑧≳1, to gas-poor in the local universe.
In the theoretical framework of Krumholz et al. (2018), the primary
driver of turbulence also changes as galaxies evolve. Turbulence in
local discs is mostly caused by star-formation feedback, whereas tur-
bulence at higher redshift is primarily driven by gas transport within
the disc. SFR density also changes with redshift, peaking at cosmic
noon (𝑧∼1 −2), and merger rates are higher at earlier times (Puskás
et al. 2025, and references therein). The impact of mergers on the
evolution and scatter in these galaxy properties is also uncertain.
Merger-driven SFR and velocity dispersion offsets may be respon-
sible for significant scatter in a range of galaxy scaling relations
at 𝑧≳1, including the star-forming main sequence (SFR-M★), the
Tully-Fisher relation (rotational velocity-M★) and the 𝜎-SFR relation
(Puech et al. 2019). Merger-driven turbulence may also contribute to
the scatter in observed velocity dispersions at fixed mass and redshift.
Dynamical perturbations from mergers impacting rotational velocity
and dispersion profiles may also impact assumptions on the virialised
nature of discs, further increasing scatter in relations derived from
high redshift samples (Rodrigues et al. 2017).
Studies of merger-driven SFRs find a range of offsets depending
on the sample and method used to measure SFR. SFR offsets mea-
sured using H𝛼have generally been found to be larger than offsets
using SFRs measured using Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fit-
ting codes. A potential explanation for this is that if merger-driven
SFR enhancements only occur over short time periods, SED-based
SFRs may average over the SFR increases (Horstman et al. 2021).
In contrast, H𝛼emission is sensitive to SFR on shorter timescales,
∼10 Myr, and may be able to more accurately capture the merger-
driven SFR enhancement. However, SED fitting codes which use
non-parametric star-formation-histories may be able to more suc-
cessfully capture merger-driven SF increases on ∼10 Myr timescales
(Calabrò et al. 2026). Using H𝛼-based SFRs of galaxies in the MOS-
DEF survey at 2 ≤𝑧≤2.7 and log(𝑀★/𝑀⊙) ∼9 −11, Horstman
et al. (2021) find that interacting galaxies in their sample have median
SFR offsets of around 0.13−0.24 dex, depending on the offset calcu-
lation method. Whereas, when using SED-based SFR measurements
of the same galaxies, Horstman et al. (2021) measure lower median
SFR offsets of around 0.10−0.16 dex. Observations using the James
Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have enabled studies of SFR offsets
in pairs beyond cosmic noon out to 𝑧∼9. Duan et al. (2026) find
SED-based SFR offsets in close pairs of 0.25 ± 0.10 and 0.26 ± 0.11
for galaxies in redshift ranges 4.5 < 𝑧< 6.5 and 6.5 < 𝑧< 8.5 re-
spectively. We note that these enhancements are only observed in the
closest pairs (separation < 20 kpc) and no significant enhancement
was seen at wider separations. Calabrò et al. (2026) measure SFR
offsets of 0.25 ± 0.04 in log(𝑀★/𝑀⊙) ∼7 −9 interacting galaxies
at 5 < 𝑧< 8 identified using morphological indicators1. In contrast,

A range of simulations have been used to quantify the expected im-
pact of interactions on SFR in galaxies across cosmic time. Idealised
hydrodynamical simulations of equal mass (1:1) major mergers have
found that SFR offsets decrease as gas fractions increase (Scudder
et al. 2015; Fensch et al. 2017). Fensch et al. (2017) found that the
SFR enhancements in gas-rich mergers approximating 𝑧∼2 massive
galaxies (Δ log SFR ≲0.7) were less than half that of the enhance-
ments in gas-poor 𝑧∼0 mergers (Δ log SFR ≳1). Patton et al. (2020)
also found that enhancement of sSFR in close pairs at 𝑧= 0.2 −1
was weaker at higher redshifts using the IllustrisTNG cosmologi-
cal simulations TNG100 and TNG300 (Marinacci et al. 2018; Springel
et al. 2018; Pillepich et al. 2018; Naiman et al. 2018; Nelson et al.
2018), which included interactions with a range of properties includ-
ing mass ratios and gas fractions. Other works using IllustrisTNG
and SIMBA (Davé et al. 2019) cosmological simulations find only
minimal changes in SFR increase in post-mergers, out to 𝑧∼1 −2
(Rodríguez Montero et al. 2019; Hani et al. 2020).
Despite the increase in observational studies investigating the ef-
fects of mergers on star-formation at 𝑧> 1, there have yet to be
comparable studies of galaxy kinematics and turbulence. In general,
simulations find that galaxy mergers at 𝑧≳1 do increase turbulence,
though to a lesser extent than at lower redshift (e.g., Fensch et al.
2017; Mortazavi & Lotz 2019; Jiménez et al. 2023; Jiménez Hen-
riquez 2024). Merger simulations from Fensch et al. (2017) found that
gas-poor mergers (fgas = 10 percent) cause an increase in gas velocity
dispersions of Δ log 𝜎∼0.60, whereas gas-rich mergers (fgas = 60
percent) cause lower increases of Δ log 𝜎∼0.23. Jiménez Henriquez
(2024) found a similar trend when comparing turbulence in merging
and isolated galaxies in the Evolution and Assembly of GaLaxies
and their Environments (EAGLE) simulation (Schaye et al. 2015;
Crain et al. 2015). Mergers at 0.1 < 𝑧< 0.5, had median Δ log 𝜎
offsets which peaked at around 0.16 −0.22 depending on halo mass,
whereas mergers at 0.7 < 𝑧< 1.3 had weaker offsets of around
Δ log 𝜎∼0.12.
In this work we utilise ROHSA-SNAPD (Kanowski et al. 2025), a
spatially non-parametric kinematic modelling code to measure the
kinematics in a diverse set of star-forming galaxies from the KMOS3D
survey (Wisnioski et al. 2019). ROHSA-SNAPD is uniquely suited to
studying the kinematics of interacting pairs as it can account for
the effects of beam smearing without assuming an intrinsic rotation
model. The code uses regularisation to enforce smoothly varying
kinematics in the underlying model, relying on the assumption that
nearby regions of a galaxy have similar intrinsic kinematics. Com-
monly adopted tools for analysing galaxy kinematics are optimised
for regular and axisymmetric rotating disc galaxies (e.g., Davis et al.
2013; Di Teodoro & Fraternali 2015; Bouché et al. 2015; Varidel
et al. 2019; Price et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2025) and have not been de-
signed for fitting systems with clearly non-axisymmetric kinematics,
particularly merging or interacting galaxies.

2 SAMPLE AND METHODOLOGY
To study the kinematic properties of interacting galaxies at cosmic
noon, we required a large, mass-complete galaxy catalogue with
high-quality redshifts, a subset of which were observed with Integral
Field Spectroscopy (IFS). This allowed us to determine the level of
interaction between each IFS observed galaxy and the surrounding
catalogue galaxies. The 3D-HST Treasury Survey (Brammer et al.
2012; Skelton et al. 2014; Momcheva et al. 2016) was chosen as the
parent sample and the KMOS3D survey (Wisnioski et al. 2015, 2019)
was chosen as the kinematic IFS subsample.
2.1 Parent sample
We used source catalogues from the 3D-HST Treasury Survey based
on Hubble Space Telescope (HST) grism observations from five
extragalactic fields (AEGIS, COSMOS, GOODS-North, GOODS-
South and UDS), primarily of galaxies with redshifts < 3.5. As our
goal was to create a high-quality parent catalogue to compare with
the KMOS3D sample, we focused on the three southern fields, (COS-
MOS, GOODS-South and UDS), and applied a number of cuts. The
use_phot = 1 quality flag and grism selection criteria of Kodra
et al. (2023) were applied to only include high-quality photomet-
ric and grism redshifts across these three fields. To further increase
redshift accuracy, additional spectroscopic and photometric redshift
information was compiled from the CANDELS (Kodra et al. 2023),
UVCANDELS (Mehta et al. 2024) and KMOS3D catalogues. Sources
were crossmatched with 3D-HST within 0.5 arcseconds, following
Skelton et al. (2014). For sources with multiple spectroscopic redshift
measurements, we preferentially selected redshifts measured by the
KMOS3D survey. For sources with multiple photometric redshifts,
we first selected photo-zs from UVCANDELS as they include addi-
tional photometric bands, then CANDELS HB4_z_weight redshifts
as these are optimised using a combination of methods, and then the
original 3D-HST photometric redshifts. Approximately 20% of the
3D-HST galaxies across the three fields had updated redshifts that
differed by more than ±0.5 from the original 3D-HST redshifts. We
removed these from our catalogue as the stellar masses are likely to
have a higher uncertainty given the difference in redshift.
We set the magnitude limit of our sample to an observed JH-band
magnitude of 𝑚JH = 25 because both photometric and grism redshift
uncertainties increase towards fainter magnitudes (Bezanson et al.
2016). In Figure 1 we show the mass and redshift distribution of the
magnitude-limited 3D-HST sample (small grey points). For compar-
ison we also show galaxies from the KMOS3D survey (large open and
closed circles) which form our kinematic sample, discussed further
in Section 2.3. We derived the 90 percent stellar mass completeness
limit of the magnitude-limited 3D-HST sample (solid line in Figure
1) following the procedures of Marchesini et al. (2009); Pozzetti et al.
(2010). To determine the completeness limit, this approach relies on
the assumption that the deeper 26 < 𝑚JH < 25 3D-HST sample in-
cludes galaxies with a representative range of mass-to-light ratios.

3 KINEMATIC MODELLING WITH ROHSA-SNAPD
To obtain deconvolved kinematic maps of the KMOS3D galaxies,
we used an updated version of the code ROHSA-SNAPD4, “Spa-
tially Non-parametric Approach to PSF Deconvolution using ROHSA”
(Kanowski et al. 2025), which was adapted from the HI emission-
line fitting code ROHSA (Marchal et al. 2019). Briefly, ROHSA-SNAPD
optimises 2D kinematic maps of flux, 𝒇(𝒓), velocity, 𝝁(𝒓), and ve-
locity dispersion, 𝝈(𝒓), such that when converted to a 3D model and
convolved by both the Point Spread Function (PSF) and data spectral
resolution 𝜎inst, the convolved model matches well to the observed
data. The option also exists to optimise a single value of velocity dis-
persion (𝝈) instead of a full map, which assumes an isotropic velocity
dispersion across the whole model, discussed further in Section 3.2.
Kinematic regularisation is used to ensure that the underlying
model is physically meaningful by enforcing that spatial variations
in the deconvolved kinematic maps are smooth. The strength of kine-
matic regularisation is set by hyper-parameters 𝜆𝑓, 𝜆𝜇and 𝜆𝜎, which
weight the relative importance of convolved model goodness-of-fit
and kinematic smoothness in the output model. 𝜆𝜎has no effect
when optimising a single velocity dispersion value, 𝝈.

3.2 Applying ROHSA-SNAPD
To prepare the observed data for fitting with ROHSA-SNAPD, we sub-
tracted the continuum from each cube following the methodology
of Wisnioski et al. (2019), before spectrally cropping a 31 channel
wide region centred on the H𝛼emission line (around 67 Å wide at
𝑧∼1.5, corresponding to 1220 kms−1 in velocity space). We spatially
cropped the data to remove pixels with low numbers of exposures,
so that more than 50 percent of the edge pixels in the cropped area
were observed with at least 10 exposures, equivalent to 50 minutes
of on-source observation. We spectrally masked skyline-dominated
spectral channels of each cube, which could otherwise impact the
kinematic fitting. We denoted that a given channel was impacted by
skylines if more than 75 percent of the pixels in the channel had RMS
errors above the 80th percentile of the total cube’s RMS error. To
ensure the ROHSA-SNAPD optimisation algorithm could effectively
fit the data, the flux and Root-Mean-Square (RMS) error cubes were
scaled so that the integrated per-spaxel fluxes were generally between
1 and 100. After fitting the cubes were re-scaled to their original val-
ues.

3.3 Choice of regularisation hyper-parameters
When applying ROHSA-SNAPD to the data, we set the flux regulari-
sation at 𝜆𝑓= 0.0005 and tested three velocity regularisation values
𝜆𝜇= 2, 4 and 8. The choice of regularisation hyper-parameters 𝜆𝑓
and 𝜆𝜇can have significant impacts on the deconvolved model. Flux
regularisation reduces the impact of noise during fitting and enforces
spatial coherence in the model. Velocity regularisation enforces the
level of smoothness in the underlying velocity field to both reduce
the impact of noise and alleviate degeneracies between the velocity
and velocity dispersion maps. 𝜆𝑓and 𝜆𝜇values can be approximated
for each galaxy in the sample using, for example, the increase in 𝜒2
(Press et al. 2007) or the L-curve method (Hansen 1992). However,
choosing individual regularisation values is a difficult, computation-
ally expensive task, likely requiring fitting each observation with a
range of regularisation values. Therefore, we instead chose to use the
same regularisation values across the entire sample. As we studied
galaxy kinematics using a statistical approach, individual kinematic
fitting errors due to imperfect regularisation choices were likely aver-
aged out. Because velocity regularisation often has a larger influence
on the deconvolved model than flux regularisation, we chose a single
𝜆𝑓value which was appropriate for the full sample, and applied a
small number of reasonable 𝜆𝜇values to test the sensitivity of our
results to velocity regularisation.
3.4 Monte Carlo resampling
We used Monte Carlo resampling to derive uncertainties on the
ROHSA-SNAPD fits. As the RMS error of each pixel was known,

each pixel, with a median of 0 and standard deviation equal to the

the resampling, we chose not to add the generated noise cube to the
observed data itself. Instead, we assumed that the convolved model
of the ROHSA-SNAPD fit provided a good estimate of the observed
data and created Monte Carlo resamples by adding the generated
noise cubes to the convolved model. The assumption of a good con-
volved model fit is reasonable, as the reduced 𝜒2

3.5 Kinemtic quality cuts
We used the median ROHSA-SNAPD model of each galaxy in the
KMOS3D sample described in Section 2.3, obtained from fitting
100 noise resamples of each cube, to compare the kinematics of the
likely interacting and control galaxy samples. Deconvolved kinematic
maps of flux, line-of-sight velocity and velocity dispersion were
returned by ROHSA-SNAPD directly. Convolved kinematic maps were
calculated by converting the 100 deconvolved kinematic maps into
convolved models and fitting Gaussian functions to each line of sight.
A deconvolved S/N map was calculated for each galaxy by spectrally
collapsing the median deconvolved model and observed RMS noise
cubes and then dividing the two maps. A convolved S/N map was
calculated in the same way, using the convolved model cube. A single
deconvolved flux value was also calculated for each galaxy, as the sum
of all spaxels in the median deconvolved flux map with deconvolved
S/N > 1.
The kinematic and S/N maps were used to make a number of
additional cuts to ensure a high quality sample. We removed 47, 46
and 48 galaxies from the 𝜆𝜇= 2, 4 and 8 fits respectively which had
less than nine spaxels with a convolved S/N > 1. A further 37, 38 and
36 galaxies from the 𝜆𝜇= 2, 4 and 8 fits were removed through visual
inspection of the kinematic maps and model residuals, as they were
fit poorly by ROHSA-SNAPD, were significantly impacted by skyline
residuals or had low S/N despite passing the S/N cut mentioned
above. 6 galaxies were also removed because they had neighbours at
very small projected separations. At the KMOS 0".2 × 0".2 spatial
sampling it was not possible to separate the neighbouring sources
from the primary, even in the deconvolved flux maps, making it
impossible to measure kinematics of only the primary galaxy. 12, 13
and 12 galaxies that were poorly resolved in the 𝜆𝜇= 2, 4 and 8 fits
respectively were kept in the sample when fluxes and SFRs of the
interacting and control samples, but were removed when comparing
velocity dispersions. Poorly resolved galaxies were visually identified
using their convolved kinematic maps and PSF Full Width Half
Max (FWHM) sizes, and were generally smaller than twice the PSF
FWHM.

4 RESULTS
With our final sample we compare global properties of 130 galaxies
(120 well resolved) across the redshift range 0.6 < 𝑧< 2.6. To
compare a given parameter (e.g., H𝛼flux) between the control and
likely interacting samples, we calculated offsets between each likely
interacting galaxy and a sample of control galaxies with similar
physical properties, using a similar method to other merger studies
(e.g., Patton et al. 2011; Scudder et al. 2012).
For each galaxy in the high 𝑄𝑃"likely interacting" sample, we
identified galaxies in the control sample with lookback times and
stellar masses within ±0.3 Gyr and ±0.1 dex of the likely interacting
galaxy. We chose these mass and lookback time bounds as a balance
between minimising the shift in the normalisation of the star-forming-
main-sequence (≲0.1 dex) and ensuring there were sufficient control
galaxies to match to. We then randomly chose one of the matched
controls to calculate the offset in a parameter (e.g., Δ log H𝛼flux)
between the likely interacting galaxy and the control. If no matches
existed, we expanded the mass and redshift bounds by ±0.1 Gyr
in lookback time and ±0.1 dex in stellar mass until at least one
match occurred. Once calculated for all likely interacting galaxies,
we saved the median offset (e.g., median Δ log H𝛼flux). We repeated
this process 200 times, each iteration randomly choosing another
control galaxy for each 𝑄𝑃> 90th galaxy, to obtain a distribution
of median offsets of the parameter. We then jackknife resampled
the distribution, as we found during testing that the removal of single
galaxies can sometimes alter the measured offsets, though the impact
of jackknifing was generally minimal. This process was repeated to
match the control sample to itself.

not apply a specific pair mass ratio cut in our sample, galaxies with
the highest interaction strengths are generally galaxies with more
massive companions, as discussed in Appendix A2.
There have been a number of studies of SFR enhancement in in-
teracting galaxies at 𝑧> 1, including new observations with JWST
out to 𝑧= 8.5 (e.g., Silva et al. 2018; Wilson et al. 2019; Horstman
et al. 2021; Shah et al. 2022; Calabrò et al. 2026; Duan et al. 2026).
Most works find interacting galaxies have higher SFRs than isolated
galaxies, although there is substantial scatter, likely due to the range
of approaches (e.g. pair criteria, mass range, SFR measurement tech-
nique), which make it difficult to directly compare to our results.
Some works also find no increase in SFR enhancement (e.g., Silva
et al. 2018; Wilson et al. 2019). The most substantial difference
between the studies is the SFR-measurement technique, which can
generally be separated into H𝛼- and SED-based methods. Most SED-
based SFR offset measurements are lower than H𝛼-based offsets at
the same redshift.

Interaction-driven SFR offsets have also been explored in simula-
tions. Most studies find increased SF in interacting galaxies, though
the observed offsets are sensitive to parameters noted above (e.g.
mass ratio), as well as galaxy properties that evolve with time (e.g.
gas fraction). Hydrodynamical simulations of massive discs in 1:1

gas fractions increases (Fensch et al. 2017; Scudder et al. 2015).

interactions at a range of mass ratios and gas fractions, Patton et al.

significant variations over 0 < 𝑧< 1 (Rodríguez Montero et al. 2019;
Hani et al. 2020). Our results show similarities with some simulation
SFR offset trends across cosmic time, particularly findings from cos-
mological simulations which include a range of mass ratios and gas
fractions, as these merger samples are more similar to our observed
sample, as explored in Appendix A2.

6 CONCLUSIONS
We have used the interaction strength parameter 𝑄𝑃to identify
likely interacting galaxies within the KMOS3D survey at cosmic
noon. We obtained deconvolved kinematics and fluxes of 131 star-
forming galaxies (120 well resolved) using a spatially non-parametric
approach and compare the properties of likely interacting galax-
ies (𝑄𝑃> −2.4; 10 ≲𝑟𝑄𝑃kpc] ≲100) with isolated controls
(𝑄𝑃< −4.2) matched in mass and redshift. We find that the likely
interacting galaxies have higher H𝛼fluxes (Δ log 𝑓H𝛼= 0.06+0.01
−0.03)
and SFRs (Δ log SFR = 0.14+0.10
−0.12) and are dustier (Δ𝐴V=0.10+0.14
−0.06)
than isolated galaxies. Comparing to studies across a range of red-
shifts, we find no evolution in the SFR offset in 𝑧∼0 galaxies and
our sample at 𝑧∼1. In contrast to SFR, we find no measurable offset
in the velocity dispersions of the interacting sample. This suggests
that interactions do not substantially increase turbulence in our sam-
ple when averaged over 10 ≲𝑟𝑄𝑃[kpc] ≲100, at least less than a
factor of ∼2. We note that mergers at their closest interaction are
not included in this analysis (𝑟𝑄𝑃[kpc] ≲10). Our results are gen-
erally consistent with merger simulations and theoretical arguments
which suggest that high-redshift, gas-rich mergers cause proportion-
ally lower increases in velocity dispersion than gas-poor mergers.
Future observations with high spectral resolution instruments tar-
geting large numbers of interacting galaxies are required to test the
robustness of our results. These observations will provide more ac-
curate velocity dispersions measurements and increase population
statistics, facilitating the use of finer interaction strength bins and
comparisons between specific merger types, such as major vs. minor
and gas-rich vs. gas-poor.

function of ROHSA-SNAPD6, instead of the default Gaussian likeli-
