ABSTRACT
Shallow nanoindentation enables mechanical characterization of thin films, individual phases, ir-
radiated layers, and other volume-constrained materials, but measured hardness is often inflated
by the indentation size effect (ISE), contact-area errors and tip-geometry artifacts. Classical ISE
corrections such as the Nix–Gao relation require a sufficiently deep linear regime an are unreliable
when only shallow measurements are accessible. This study investigates how a small experimental
nanoindentation dataset can be used to predict a reference hardness with physics-guided feature
engineering and data augmentation.
Approximately 700 experimental indentations were collected from three steel reference specimens
covering a high-load hardness range of 2–6.5 GPa and augmented using physically motivated
variations representing instrumental noise, session-level drift, and local multiphase boundary blending.
The input space combined Oliver–Pharr quantities with mechanically informed descriptors, including
indentation work partitioning, (H/Er), and the area-invariant compliance proxy (Pmax/S2). Ridge
Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Neural Networks were evaluated using a fully quarantined
fourth steel specimen tested at staggered loads.
The shallow-to-reference hardness mapping was strongly nonlinear: ridge regression failed, whereas
nonlinear models achieved (R2 > 0.98) internally. A constrained (64–8–64) neural network gave
the best balance between accuracy and generalization, reaching RMSE = 0.184 GPa internally and
RMSE = 0.470 GPa, MAPE = 5.4%, on the quarantined steel. Unlike Nix–Gao analysis, which
required measurements above (hc ≈215) nm, the neural network produced stable estimates in the
shallow regime. SHAP and latent-space analysis showed reliance on area-invariant and energy-based
descriptors, especially (Pmax/S2) and (Wp/Wtot). The results demonstrate the feasibility of a practical
experimental workflow for shallow-indentation hardness correction in multiphase steels using small
experimental datasets and suggest a pathway toward data-efficient characterization of any other
volume constrained materials.

Introduction
Instrumented indentation testing (IIT) is the established standard for evaluating the mechanical properties of materials
at the micro- and nanoscale [1]. By continuously recording the applied load (P) and resulting displacement (h), IIT
enables the simultaneous extraction of hardness (H) and reduced elastic modulus (Er) from a single load-displacement
curve, relying on the Oliver-Pharr method to estimate the projected contact area (Ac) from the elastic unloading stiffness

[2]. The principal advantage of this technique is its exceptionally high spatial resolution: by confining the indentation
plastic zone to sub-micron volumes, IIT can resolve the intrinsic mechanical properties of thin films, functionally graded
layers, individual microstructural phases within complex alloys, and ion-irradiated surface layers that are difficult to
isolate in macroscopic testing methods [3–5].
However, this spatial confinement introduces fundamental physical tension. The very condition that makes IIT
indispensable for characterizing constrained micro-volumes, testing at low loads and shallow contact depths, is the
condition that activates a depth-dependent hardening phenomenon known as the Indentation Size Effect (ISE) [6–8].
Consequently, the apparent hardness extracted from shallow micro-volume measurements is systematically elevated
above the true macroscopic bulk hardness (H0) and cannot be directly compared to other reference values.
While the ISE is universally observed across crystalline materials, its governing mechanism is not universal. In
conventional metallic alloys and multiphase steels, the dominant mechanism is Strain Gradient Plasticity (SGP):
the geometrically self-similar Berkovich geometry imposes a plastic strain gradient scaling inversely with contact
depth, requiring the generation of Geometrically Necessary Dislocations (GNDs) beyond the Statistically Stored
Dislocation (SSD) population of the undeformed material [7, 9, 10]. In nanocrystalline metals and high-entropy alloys,
however, grain boundary plasticity and lattice distortion effects contribute additional depth-dependent strengthening
that does not reduce to a single GND density parameter [11]. In ceramics and covalently bonded materials, the ISE is
further complicated by cracking, densification, and pressure-induced phase transformations at the contact [12]. This
mechanistic diversity means that no single analytical correction framework is universally applicable; the validity of any
size-effect correction must be assessed against the dominant deformation physics of the specific material system under
investigation.
For the multiphase steels examined in this study, GND-mediated SGP governs the ISE, and the depth-dependent
strengthening is formalized by the well-established Nix-Gao scaling relation [7]:

materials impose maximum permissible contact depths well below the 200 nm threshold required for valid Nix-Gao
linearization.
Inverse Finite Element Analysis (FEA) offers a numerically rigorous alternative by iteratively updating elastoplastic
constitutive parameters until the simulated P-h response converges to the experimental curve [16]. However, two
fundamental limitations restrict its practical applicability: the inverse problem requires hundreds of non-linear finite
element evaluations per property extraction [17], and the solution is mathematically non-unique - distinctly different
combinations of yield strength and strain-hardening parameters can produce virtually indistinguishable P-h curves
[18, 19], rendering inverse FEA impractical for routine microstructural characterization.
Machine learning regression frameworks present a more tractable alternative for bypassing this iterative computational
overhead while preserving equivalent non-linear mapping capability in solid mechanics [20, 21]. Generalized non-linear
architectures have been successfully deployed to predict macroscopic mechanical behavior, demonstrating the capacity
to forecast complex phenomena like strain-life fatigue directly from simple tensile data using neural networks [22, 23],
fracture toughness of pultruded composite materials [24], or to map the hardness of multi-component alloys using
tree-based models paired with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretability [25]. Within the specific domain
of contact mechanics, supervised learning has transitioned from bulk material correlation to direct load-displacement
curve analysis. For example, regression frameworks have successfully extracted elastic moduli from atomic force
microscopy arrays without requiring traditional contact model fitting [26], while artificial neural networks routinely
isolate intrinsic flow stress and strain-hardening parameters from spherical and Berkovich nanoindentation, effectively
replacing mathematically non-unique inverse finite element simulations [27–29]. However, while these models reliably
extract elastoplastic continuous parameters under ideal geometric assumptions, their capacity to explicitly decouple
dynamic strain-gradient artifacts (the ISE) from intrinsic macroscopic hardness using shallow, size-affected experimental
data remains undemonstrated.

Closing this gap through purely data-driven means introduces a second, equally fundamental constraint: a “small
data” dilemma that limits the routine experimental application of predictive machine learning in solid mechanics [30].
Current regression frameworks for contact mechanics predominantly rely on massive synthetic datasets generated via
finite element simulations, frequently requiring tens of thousands of modeled curves to adequately constrain non-linear
architectures [28]. This computational reliance renders the approach impractical for evaluating volume-restricted
specimens or newly synthesized microstructures, where the underlying constitutive laws are intrinsically unknown
prior to testing. Conversely, compiling equivalent datasets through purely experimental means is cost-prohibitive,
while classical analytical size-effect corrections natively break down for complex multiphase microstructures [13, 31].
To transition out of this bottleneck, materials informatics must adapt to data-scarcity scenarios. Recent literature
demonstrates that the “small data” challenge can be overcome by restricting model degrees of freedom using established
physical laws [30, 32]. For instance, substituting purely data-driven methods with physics-informed architectures
or dimensional scaling laws has enabled the successful prediction of complex elastoplastic constitutive relationships
and bulk breakdown limits from exceptionally small experimental arrays, often fewer than a hundred data points
[33]. Consequently, no established protocol exists for correcting the ISE directly from low-load, purely experimental
nanoindentation data at the dataset scale routinely accessible in a standard materials laboratory, without recourse to
large-scale finite element simulation or iterative inverse.
The present study addresses this gap by developing a regression framework to extract size-effect-corrected high-load
reference hardness directly from shallow, low-load experimental nanoindentation data. Four regression architectures
are trained on approximately 700 indentations across three multiphase steel specimens, selected to provide a well-
characterized Nix-Gao baseline for rigorous benchmarking, expanded via a physically justified tabular augmentation
strategy simulating instrumental variance, and driven by physics-guided feature engineering prioritizing the area-
invariant proxies Pmax/S2 and Wp/Wtot. The framework is benchmarked against a strictly held-out blind validation
specimen, with feature attribution and latent space decomposition employed to verify that predictions reflect established
contact mechanics rather than numerical artifacts, and boundary testing on amorphous fused silica used to explicitly
delineate the physical domain of validity. Validating against a system governed by well-understood GND-driven SGP
ensures the framework can subsequently be extended to materials where equivalent analytical scaling relations are
absent, such as high-entropy alloys or nanocrystalline microstructures.

Materials and Methods
2.1
Materials Preparation and Microstructural Characterization
Four steel specimens were selected to span a representative range of elastoplastic behaviors and microstructural phase
assemblages. The primary training dataset comprises three certified steel hardness reference blocks, traceable to national
metrology standards, with calibrated Vickers hardness calibrated with a 25-gram load and with passport values of 212
±11 HV0.025, 426 ± 22 HV0.025, and 841 ± 9 HV0.025 (with corresponding hardness bulk H ≈2 −−6.5GPa).
These specimens are referred to hereafter as E212, E426, and E841, respectively. The certified supplier values were
used as verification, not as the supervised learning target variable, since the passport values were also done at shallow
depth and affected by ISE. This hardness range spans microstructures from a soft, high-plasticity ferritic/pearlitic phase,
exhibiting pronounced elastoplastic pile-up, to a hard martensitic architecture with restricted plastic flow. A fourth
independent reference block (passport value is H0 ≈6GPaat5N) was quarantined prior to any model training and
reserved as a blind verification set.
A fused silica calibration specimen was used for nanoindentation system calibration and was included as instrumental
boundary control. As an amorphous material, fused silica does not exhibit the classical GND-mediated indentation
size effect characteristic of crystalline metals [13]; any measured depth dependence is expected to be dominated by
densification, tip geometry, cracking if present, and calibration artifacts [34], allowing these instrumental contributions
to be isolated from genuine strain-gradient hardening in the steel specimens.
All metallic specimens were mechanically polished to a mirror finish, culminating in colloidal silica suspension to
remove the near-surface Beilby layer [35]. Elemental compositions of all steel grades were confirmed by X-ray
fluorescence (done on XRF Olympus Vanta C) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Nanoindentation
marks were analyzed with scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which correlated compositional variations with their
respective microstructural architectures and confirmed the phase assemblages governing the elastoplastic contact
response at each hardness level. SEM and EDS were done on ThermoFisher Quattro S in Skoltech’s Advanced Imaging
Core Facility. These characterization results are presented in Section 3.1.

2.2
Nanoindentation Protocol
All instrumented indentation experiments were performed on a Nanovea PB1000 (Nanovea, USA) mechanical tester
equipped with a calibrated Berkovich diamond indenter. Prior to each indentation cycle, a thermal equilibration hold
was maintained until the displacement drift rate fell below 0.05 nm/s, at which point the drift rate was recorded and
subtracted as a linear correction from the subsequent displacement signal [2].
Tests were conducted in load-controlled mode at a constant loading rate (dP/dt = 2 · Pmax mN per minute) to ensure
quasi-static deformation conditions. Upon reaching each target peak load (Pmax), a 15-second dwell period was applied
before unloading to allow time-dependent plastic displacement to stabilize, thereby preventing the upward curvature
(“nose” artifact) in the initial unloading segment that would otherwise distort the contact stiffness calculation (S)
[34, 36].
For the three reference steel specimens constituting the primary training dataset, a loading schedule of seven discrete
peak loads was applied: 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, and 350 mN. This range was selected to span the full physical spectrum
of the ISE, from the ultra-shallow GND-dominated regime (h_c < 100 nm at 5 mN) to the macroscopic plateau where
the ISE is almost dissipated (h_c > 1 µm at 350 mN). At each load level, a 5×6 spatial grid of indentations was
executed per specimen, yielding 30 tests per load per material. Indentations were excluded if the unloading segment
deviated from a power-law fit (P
= α (h −hf)m) with a coefficient of determination below R² = 0.98, or if a
discontinuous load drop exceeding 5% of Pmax was present in the loading curve, indicative of cracking or surface
debris contact. This criterion retained approximately 25–28 valid indentations per load level per specimen.
The independent fourth steel specimen was tested at a staggered set of peak loads (3, 7, 15, 35, 75, 150, 275, and 375
mN) deliberately offset from the discrete training load levels. This design ensures that model evaluation on the hold-out
specimen requires continuous inter-load interpolation across the feature space and extrapolation beyond the space,
rather than direct recall of training-set load conditions, providing a more stringent test of generalization.
The fused silica calibration standard was tested at five indentations per load level using the same protocol. As established
in Section 2.1, the purpose of this evaluation is to characterize the depth dependence of purely instrumental geometric
artifacts, tip-apex rounding and compliance calibration residuals, in the absence of any dislocation-mediated plasticity.
The measurements for training dataset directly inform the physical interpretation of the shallow-depth augmentation
strategy described in Section 2.4.

inflating apparent H by an amount that is additive to, and physically distinct from, the depth-dependent GND-driven ISE.

(2) Random Forest (Nestimators = 100, unconstrained depth, min samples leaf = 2)) reduces variance through bootstrap

reflect the same loading history at different integration levels, constraining the representation to 8 latent dimensions is
designed to force compression of collinear load-scaling information, retaining only features that distinguish intrinsic
material strength from depth-dependent geometric artifacts. How the latent activations of this layer are analyzed by
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is described in Section 2.6.
All hyperparameters were selected by manual sweep over the held-out internal test partition; no automated search was
performed.
As mentioned in Section 2.4, the 90/10 split was applied to the original experimental dataset prior to augmentation,
yielding approximately 2,520 augmented training rows and 80 purely experimental test rows. Because the internal split
contains indentations from materials present during training, it quantifies interpolation within the training material
manifold. Generalization is assessed primarily using the fourth steel specimen. The held-out fourth specimen was
excluded entirely from this phase. Given the single partition and small test set, reported internal metrics should be
interpreted alongside the independent out-of-sample verification in Section 3.4. Predictive performance was quantified
by R², root mean square error (RMSE, GPa), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE, %).
2.6
Feature Attribution and Latent Space Analysis
To verify that model predictions reflect contact mechanics rather than dataset-specific numerical structure, a two-
component interpretability framework was applied.
SHapley Additive exPlanations feature attribution [50] was computed for both the Random Forest and Neural Net-
work models, using the augmented training dataset as the background reference distribution. The resulting additive
decomposition of per-feature marginal contributions [51] was used to evaluate whether predictions are anchored to the
area-invariant proxies Pmax/S2 and Wp/Wtot, or whether geometrically derived parameters carrying systematic pile-up
errors (hc, Ac) dominate the decision hierarchy.
Latent space was analyzed with PCA [52], since SHAP does not reveal how inputs are transformed within hidden
layers. To evaluate the physical structure of the 64-8-64 network’s compressed representation, the 8-neuron bottleneck
activations were extracted for the full experimental dataset by analytical forward propagation through the first two weight
matrices with ReLU activations, yielding an (n × 8) activation matrix. PCA was applied retaining two components, and
the resulting projection was examined against three coloring variables: material class, high load reference hardness
Href and contact depth hc. This evaluated two specific questions: (1) whether PC1 organizes the latent space along
a continuous intrinsic hardness gradient independent of discrete class boundaries; and (2) whether hc variation, the
geometric signature of the ISE, maps orthogonally to PC1, indicating successful decoupling of depth-dependent
geometry from the underlying mechanical state.

the Random Forest. Rather, it must operate at the level of internal representation, the bottleneck layer must filter the

passing information to the output layers. Whether this filtering is achieved is the specific question addressed by the
latent space analysis in Section 3.5.2.

observed in Section 3.4.2. The Random Forest relies on bounded dimensionless ratios that are insensitive to absolute
load magnitude, conferring stability within the training distribution but limiting extrapolation capacity. The Neural
Network’s reliance on absolute-magnitude variables (Pmax, Wtot) that scale continuously with load enables stable
extrapolation beyond training load boundaries, at the cost of requiring the bottleneck architecture to prevent overfitting
to their shared collinear load-scaling structure.

3.6
Broader Implications for Materials Characterization and Theory
The framework’s broader relevance lies in applicability to systems where an equivalent analytical baseline does not exist.
In high-entropy alloys, nanocrystalline metals, and irradiated microstructures, ISE deviations from classical Nix-Gao
scaling arise from deformation mechanisms, lattice distortion, grain boundary plasticity, defect cluster interactions,
that do not reduce to a single GND density parameter [55–57], and where inverse FEA is constrained by solution
non-uniqueness and computational cost [19]. The present framework offers an experimentally grounded alternative
capable of extracting size-effect-corrected hardness estimates (RMSE ≈0.5 GPa on blind specimens), without prior
knowledge of the governing analytical scaling form. The methodology transfers to new alloy systems, while the trained
model does not; retraining on a representative experimental dataset is required whenever the alloy class, dominant
deformation mechanism, or hardness range differs substantially from the training distribution.
The consistent emergence of Pmax/S2 and Wp/Wtot as predictive invariants across both the Random Forest and Neural
Network, two architectures with fundamentally different learning strategies, suggests these quantities retain physical
relevance as contact mechanics descriptors beyond the specific GND framework used to motivate their inclusion.
Whether their primacy holds in systems governed by different deformation physics is a testable hypothesis: applying the
same feature engineering and SHAP attribution protocol to nanocrystalline or high-entropy alloy datasets would reveal
whether these descriptors remain sufficient or whether additional invariants are required, providing a concrete pathway
by which the interpretability analysis can inform the development of new analytical scaling relations for emerging
structural materials.
4
Conclusion
This study developed a physics-guided machine learning framework for estimating a size-effect-reduced high-load
hardness reference from a small dataset of shallow nanoindentation responses in multiphase steels. The approach
combines experimentally accessible Oliver–Pharr quantities with mechanically motivated descriptors, especially the
area-invariant compliance proxy (Pmax/S2), the plastic work fraction (Wp/Wtot), and (H/Er), allowing the regression
models to exploit information contained in shallow, size-affected indentation curves.
The classical Nix–Gao linearization provided an accurate benchmark only after excluding shallow contacts below
approximately (hc ≈215) nm. When the full depth range was used, shallow-depth curvature caused systematic
overestimation of the high-load hardness reference. This confirms that analytical extrapolation remains reliable when a

deep linear regime is available but is unsuitable for constrained-volume cases where only shallow indentation data can
be collected.
Nonlinear regression models successfully captured the mapping between shallow indentation features and the high-load
hardness reference. Ridge regression failed to generalize, confirming the nonlinear nature of the correction. Among
the evaluated models, the constrained (64–8–64) neural network gave the best balance between accuracy and stability,
achieving low internal error and an RMSE = 0.47 GPa and MAPE = 5.4% on a fully quarantined steel specimen tested
at staggered loads.
Model interpretation showed two complementary correction mechanisms. The random forest relied primarily on
area-invariant descriptors, suppressing features directly affected by contact-area error. The neural network accepted
area-dependent inputs for extrapolation and separated hardness-related and depth-related variance within its bottleneck
representation, reducing sensitivity to indentation depth and pile-up artifacts. Application to fused silica produced
systematic failure, showing that the trained model is not universal.
The present study therefore should be interpreted as a validation of the workflow on a well-established material class,
rather than as a universal trained predictor. By applying the framework to crystalline steels, where the indentation
size effect is already understood in terms of dislocation-mediated plasticity and can be benchmarked against Nix–Gao
scaling, the results demonstrate that small experimental datasets can be expanded with physics-guided augmentation to
perform practical ISE correction. The same workflow can be repeated for other material classes, including systems for
which no reliable analytical size-effect model is yet available, provided that representative calibration data are collected
over the relevant hardness range and deformation regime.
