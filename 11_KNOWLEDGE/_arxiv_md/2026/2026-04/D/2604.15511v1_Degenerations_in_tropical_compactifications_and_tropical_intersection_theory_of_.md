DEGENERATIONS IN TROPICAL COMPACTIFICATIONS AND TROPICAL
INTERSECTION THEORY OF M0,n
SEAN T. GRIFFIN, JAKE LEVINSON, ROHINI RAMADAS, AND ROB SILVERSMITH
Abstract. The main result of this paper is a formula for the limit cycle of a 1-parameter family of subva-
rieties of a tropical compactification, expressed in terms of tropical intersections. Our theorem generalizes
results of Dickenstein-Feichtner-Sturmfels and Katz to the case of tropical compactifications.
In the second part of the paper, we apply our formula to the moduli space M0,n of stable marked rational
curves. We describe the tropicalization of the Kapranov maps M0,n →Pn−3, whose hyperplane pullbacks
are the ψ-classes, with respect to a suitable choice of torus. We introduce tropical ψ-hypersurfaces (in genus
zero). These are different from the standard definition of Mikhalkin and Kerber-Markwig, and may be of
independent interest. We demonstrate our main result by giving a “firework algorithm” that computes limits
of intersections of ψ-hypersurfaces.
1. Introduction
This paper has two parts. In the first part, described in Section 1.3 of the introduction, we generalize
theorems of Dickenstein-Feichtner-Sturmfels and Katz [DFS07, Kat09] for computing limit cycles in toric
varieties via tropical intersection theory.
We prove Theorem A, which computes limit cycles in tropical
compactifications as introduced by Tevelev [Tev07]. Our generalization is analogous to Osserman-Payne’s
generalization of tropical intersection theory [OP13], from intersections in tori to intersections in subvarieties
of tori.
In the second part, described in Section 1.4 of the introduction, we introduce a notion of tropical ψ-
hypersurfaces on the moduli space Mtrop
0,n
of genus-zero tropical curves, which is different from, and in
a sense ‘dual to’, the standard definition of Mikhalkin and Kerber-Markwig [Mik07, KM09].
We apply
Theorem A to compute limit cycles of intersections of these hypersurfaces. We will see that in this class of
computations, the underlying tropical geometry is intricate, while still being well-behaved enough to allow
us to conclude all the geometric information we need from the tropical picture. In this case, the formula for
the limit cycle is combinatorially described by a “firework algorithm” in Mtrop
0,n , described in Section 1.4.3.

1.1. Background: Tropical intersection theory. Tropical intersection theory was developed in the early
2000s, with the goal of computing intersections in algebraic geometry (e.g. in the Chow ring of a variety)
using the geometry of polyhedral complexes in Rn [FS97, Mik06, BJS+07, GKM09, Kat09, AR10, OP13]. The
theory has been subsequently applied in many examples, see e.g. [Mik05, KM09, HK12, BS15, BLM+16,
Gro18, Gol21, Rau23, CM23, PMPR24].
To an m-dimensional subvariety X ⊆T of an n-dimensional
algebraic torus, one associates an m-dimensional weighted polyhedral complex Trop(X) ⊆Rn called its
tropicalization, and one can recover information about the intersections of subvarieties in T by intersecting
their tropicalizations.

1.2. Background: Tropical intersection theory in the context of degenerations onto boundary
strata. In [DFS07, Thm. 2.2], Dickenstein-Feichtner-Sturmfels introduced a rather different way to use
tropical intersection theory. In their setup, one wishes to compute the limit of a family of m-dimensional
subvarieties Xt ⊆Pn, parametrized by some variable t, such that as t →0, Xt degenerates to a subscheme
X0 ⊆Pn supported on the union of the coordinate m-planes. As a cycle, [X0] is a sum of coordinate m-
planes, each with a nonnegative integer coefficient, and [DFS07, Thm. 2.2] expresses these coefficients via
tropical intersection theory; specifically, a coordinate m-plane Lσ corresponds to a codimension-m cone σ
in the fan Σ of Pn, and the coefficient of [Lσ] in [X0] is the tropical intersection number of Trop(Xt) with
the (complementary-dimensional) cone σ, computed using the same tropical intersection multiplicities as
in Example 1.1. That is, [X0] is entirely determined by the way Trop(Xt) intersects the “m-dimensional
skeleton” Σm of the fan Σ.

1.4.4. Relation of this paper to [GGL23a]. It is straightforward (using standard techniques for working with
the Chow ring of M0,n, see Section 1.6) to express products of classes of the form π∗
S(ψi) as sums of boundary
strata of M0,n in various ways. This calculation in Chow does not, however, imply that any particular such
expression is “geometric” in the strong sense of arising as the cycle of a complete intersection of hypersurfaces,
each representing one π∗
S(ψi) factor. In [GGL23a], Gillespie–Griffin–Levinson give expressions for products
of ω-classes (certain special classes of the form π∗
S(ψi)) as sums of boundary strata, which are geometric in
the weaker sense of arising as cycles of limits of complete intersections. Explicitly, they find degenerating 1-
parameter families of hypersurfaces in M0,n, each representing one ω factor, such that the generic intersection
is transverse, and such that the limit cycle of the 1-parameter family of intersections is the corresponding
sum of boundary strata. This paper arose as an attempt to make a precise connection between their results
and tropical intersection theory. Specifically, we realized that
(1) the degenerating hypersurfaces chosen by [GGL23a] could be used to define tropical ψ-classes with
non-trivial valuations, as in Section 1.5,
(2) the choices made in [GGL23a] were essentially tropical in nature, and implied that r such tropical
hypersurfaces intersected “nicely” along the r-skeleton Σr of Mtrop
0,n (in the sense of Theorem B),
(3) the algorithm in [GGL23a] admitted an elegant metric/tropical augmentation that computed the
finitely many intersection points of the r tropical hypersurfaces with Σr, and
(4) everything generalized without issue from ω-classes to all classes of the form π∗
S(ψi).
Carrying out this calculation led us to develop the general tropical technique of Part 1 (Theorem A) to
efficiently prove algebraic results analogous to those in [GGL23a], in particular to conclude Theorem C from
Theorem B.

1.6. Intersection theory of M0,n, ψ-classes, and combinatorics. The intersection theory of M0,n is
well-studied, and is an important ingredient in many enumerative problems involving rational curves, see
e.g. [KV07]. Keel and Kontsevich-Manin gave presentations [Kee92, KM94] for the Chow ring A∗(M0,n), as
a result of which any intersection product is computable via certain basic combinatorial rules. Nonetheless,
A∗(M0,n) has an extraordinary combinatorial richness, and decades later, its combinatorics are still of signif-
icant interest. The specific case of intersection numbers of pullbacks of ψ-classes, or Kapranov degrees, have
appeared in many recent works [GGS20, CGM21, Gol21, Sil22, GGL23a, GGL23b, Sil24, RS24, BELL25],
with connections to many well-known combinatorial objects (rigid graphs, parking functions, matching the-
ory of bipartite graphs, triangulations of polygons, mixed volumes, chromatic polynomials of graphs).

2.1. Polyhedral geometry. We review some of the basic terminology in polyhedral geometry. (See [MS15,
Sec. 2.3].) A polyhedron σ in Rn is a (possibly unbounded) subset that is the intersection of finitely many
closed half-spaces. A polyhedron has faces of various dimensions. The affine span of σ is the smallest
affine subspace of Rn containing σ — it is a translate of the linear space parallel to σ, which we denote
LR(σ). The dimension dim(σ) of σ is the dimension of LR(σ) as a real vector space. The relative interior
relint(σ) of σ is the interior of σ inside its affine span. A polyhedral complex in Rn is a set Σ of polyhedra
in Rn that are “glued along faces”; precisely, a set such that (1) Σ is closed under taking faces, and (2) if
σ1, σ2 ∈Σ are polyhedra, then σ1 ∩σ2 is empty or is a face of both σ1 and σ2. The elements σ ∈Σ are
called cells (or d-cells, where d = dim(σ)), and the maximal cells with respect to inclusion are called facets.
Σ is called pure dimension m if all facets have dimension m, and in this case we write dim(Σ) = m. The
r-skeleton of Σ is the polyhedral complex Σr = {σ ∈Σ : dim(σ) ≤r}. The union |Σ| ⊆Rn of all cells
σ ∈Σ is called the support of Σ. For polyhedral complexes Σ1, Σ2, we say Σ1 refines Σ2 if |Σ1| = |Σ2|
and every cell σ ∈Σ2 is a union of cells of Σ1. The union of the relative interiors of all facets of Σ is called
the relative interior relint(Σ) of Σ. The lineality space of Σ is the largest linear subspace of Rn with
respect to which Σ is translation-invariant. If all cells of Σ are cones (i.e. stable under multiplication by
R≥0), then Σ is called a fan.
