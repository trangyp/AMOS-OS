Abstract
DEKL 2.0 is a dependent type-theoretic framework for trace-indexed knowledge evolu-
tion. Its central claim is that the proof calculus remains monotone under standard struc-
tural rules, while non-monotonic behavior arises semantically from trace extension. Finite
and infinite traces are first-class objects in the computational universe; knowledge is in-
terpreted as a presheaf over the finite-trace category; and proposition-level reasoning is
handled categorically with fixed-point support. We establish trace–reachability correspon-
dence and completeness, characterize non-monotonicity by non-surjective restriction maps,
and present a semantic interpretation based on the free category generated by a transition
system. The framework unifies executable traces, typed witnesses, and knowledge revision
in one dependent language.
Keywords.
Dependent type theory, trace semantics, presheaf semantics, knowledge evolution,
non-monotonicity, categorical semantics.

Introduction
Many dynamic systems require reasoning about facts that depend on execution history.
A
statement may hold now, then fail after a new event.
Standard dependent type theory is
monotone at the level of derivability (weakening, substitution, subject reduction); therefore,
this kind of knowledge invalidation is not represented as a proof-theoretic rule.
DEKL resolves this tension by separating proof-theoretic monotonicity from semantic dy-
namics:
• The proof system remains ordinary dependent type theory.
2


## Page 3


• Traces are represented as first-class terms in the computational universe.
• Knowledge is indexed by traces and interpreted contravariantly.
Hence “non-monotonicity” is not a non-monotonic logic rule; it is the behavior of trace-indexed
knowledge under trace extension.
The main technical contributions are:
• a layered syntax for computational objects, trace-indexed knowledge, and fixed-point rea-
soning at the proposition layer;
• a bidirectional correspondence between finite traces and constructive reachability wit-
nesses;
• a semantic characterization of non-monotonicity via failure of surjectivity of restriction
maps;
• a categorical interpretation combining CwF syntax with free trace categories and presheaf
semantics.
Paper organization follows a definition–theorem–proof-sketch style. Section 2 gives intu-
ition; Section 3 fixes syntax and judgments; Section 4 presents typing and operational rules;
Section 5 proves the presheaf characterization of non-monotonicity; Section 6 gives full categor-
ical semantics; Section 7 states metatheory; Section 8 provides application templates; Section 9
discusses related work; Section 10 discusses current limitations; and Section 11 concludes.
2
Informal Overview
Consider an execution:
σ0 →σ1 →· · · →σn.
A finite trace from σ0 to σn is a term
t : FinTrace(σ0, σn).
This term is a constructive typed witness that σn is reachable from σ0.
2.1
A simple τ/τ ′ example
Let τ be a trace prefix and τ′ = τ · e its one-step extension. Suppose
Γ ⊢p : Kf(τ)
for a knowledge family Kf. In general, it does not follow that
Γ ⊢p : Kf(τ ′).
The knowledge witness p does not disappear; rather, its type index changes. So invalidation is
an index mismatch, not inconsistency of the core logic.
2.2
Intuition for non-monotonicity
Knowledge is modeled as a presheaf over traces:
Kf : T op
f
→Type.
For each extension ϵ : τ →τ′, there is a restriction map
restrict(ϵ, −) : Kf(τ ′) →Kf(τ).
Contravariance means we move backwards along extensions. If this map is not surjective, some
old knowledge at τ has no extension witness at τ′, which is exactly non-monotonic behavior.

2.3
Running intuition: revocation under trace extension
To make the index shift concrete, consider a credential lifecycle with events Issue(c), Use(c),
and Revoke(c). Let
Auth(c, τ) : Typeℓ
denote that credential c is valid on trace τ. On a prefix containing only issue/use events, one
may construct
q : Auth(c, τ).
If the trace extends to
τ ′ = τ · Revoke(c),
the original typed witness q generally does not inhabit Auth(c, τ′). This illustrates the central
point used throughout the paper: typed witnesses are stable as terms, but their typing index
changes with trace evolution.

Theorem 4.1 (Trace–Proof Correspondence). For arbitrary states σ0, σn ∈State, a term
t : FinTrace(σ0, σn)
is a constructive typed reachability witness for σ0 ⇝σn.
Proof sketch. By induction on the structure of finite traces. The base constructor nil witnesses
reflexive reachability. The extension constructor step composes a previously witnessed prefix
with one legal transition step. Hence every inhabitant of FinTrace(σ0, σn) encodes a finite typed
witness of σ0 ⇝σn.
Theorem 4.2 (Trace Completeness). For arbitrary states σ0, σn ∈State, if σ0 ⇝σn, then
there exists
t : FinTrace(σ0, σn).
Proof sketch. Induct on the length of a typed reachability witness. Length 0 yields the empty
trace. For length k+1, decompose the run as σ0 ⇝σk followed by σk →σn; apply the induction
hypothesis to obtain a prefix trace and then extend it using step.

Non-monotonicity via Presheaf Semantics
5.1
Trace category
Definition 5.1 (Finite-trace category). Fix a transition system (State, Event, Step). Let Tf be
the category whose objects are finite traces generated by this system and whose morphisms are
trace extensions ϵ : τ →τ ′.
5.2
Knowledge presheaf
Definition 5.2 (Trace-indexed knowledge). A constructive knowledge system is a functor
Kf : T op
f
→Type,
assigning each trace τ a type Kf(τ) and each extension ϵ : τ →τ ′ a restriction map
restrict(ϵ, −) : Kf(τ ′) →Kf(τ).
5.3
Restriction maps
Lemma 5.3 (Restriction Functoriality). Restriction maps satisfy:
restrict(idτ, k) = k,
restrict(ϵ1 ◦ϵ2, k) = restrict(ϵ1, restrict(ϵ2, k)).
Proof sketch. These are exactly the identity and composition laws of the functor Kf : T op
f
→
Type, transported to element-level notation for restriction maps.

5.4
Characterization theorem
Theorem 5.4 (Non-monotonicity Characterization). Let Kf : T op
f
→Type be a knowledge
presheaf. Then the following are equivalent:
• Kf is non-monotone under trace extension.
• There exists ϵ : τ →τ ′ such that
restrict(ϵ, −) : Kf(τ ′) →Kf(τ)
is not surjective.
Proof. (⇒) Assume non-monotonicity. Then for some extension ϵ : τ →τ′ there exists a witness
k ∈Kf(τ) such that no k′ ∈Kf(τ ′) satisfies restrict(ϵ, k′) = k. Hence k is not in the image of
restrict(ϵ, −), so that map is not surjective.
(⇐) Assume there exists ϵ : τ →τ′ such that restrict(ϵ, −) is not surjective. Choose k ∈
Kf(τ) outside its image. By construction there is no k′ ∈Kf(τ ′) with restrict(ϵ, k′) = k, i.e.,
no extension witness of k along ϵ. Therefore knowledge does not persist under trace extension,
which is exactly non-monotonicity.
5.5
Conceptual theorem
Theorem 5.5 (Conceptual Consequence). Non-monotonicity of trace-indexed knowledge is a
direct effect of contravariance in presheaf semantics, not a failure of monotonic structural rules
in the dependent proof calculus.
Proof sketch. By Theorem 5.4, non-monotonicity is equivalent to non-surjectivity of some re-
striction map. Such maps are induced by contravariant functorial action along extensions in
Tf. Therefore the source of non-monotonicity is semantic contravariance, while proof-theoretic
monotonicity remains intact.
5.6
Stability criterion and monotone fragment
Proposition 5.6 (Prefix-Stability Criterion). Let Kf : T op
f
→Type be a knowledge presheaf.
If every restriction map
restrict(ϵ, −) : Kf(τ ′) →Kf(τ)
is surjective, then knowledge is extension-stable in the sense that every witness at τ is the
restriction of some witness at any extension τ ′.
Proof sketch. Fix ϵ : τ →τ ′ and k ∈Kf(τ). Surjectivity gives k′ ∈Kf(τ ′) with restrict(ϵ, k′) =
k. Since this holds for arbitrary ϵ and k, extension-stability follows.
Proposition 5.6 identifies a monotone semantic fragment inside the general DEKL setting
and complements Theorem 5.4.

Full Categorical Semantics
6.1
Model decomposition
DEKL is interpreted using two connected categorical components:
• a CwF-style interpretation for dependent syntax (Γ ⊢A : U, Γ ⊢t : A);
• a trace semantics where a transition system generates a free trace category.

Theorem 6.1 (Free Trace Category). For a fixed transition system (State, Event, Step), states
and finite traces form the free category generated by the underlying transition graph: objects
are states, morphisms are traces, identity morphisms are empty traces, and composition is trace
concatenation.
Proof sketch. Category laws follow from properties of concatenation (associativity) and empty
traces (identities). Freeness follows because morphisms are generated only by primitive transi-
tions and finite composition, with no additional equations beyond category axioms.
6.2
Proposition layer interpretation
The proposition layer Prop is interpreted via subobject-style structure in the presheaf setting,
with fixed-point operators available at proposition level. This supports temporal and recursive
property specifications while preserving substitution stability.
6.3
Interpretation of K
The knowledge layer is interpreted in
c
Tf = [T op
f , Set],
with Kf as a presheaf object. Syntactic restriction operations correspond exactly to functor
action on extension morphisms. Thus:
syntax of trace-indexed knowledge ⇐⇒categorical action of Kf.
6.4
Adequacy perspective
Lemma 6.2 (Adequacy, soundness direction). For any derivable term t : FinTrace(σ0, σn), its
interpretation JtK is a morphism σ0 →σn in the free trace category.
Proof sketch. By induction on trace constructors. The base case maps nil to identity, and the
step case maps step to composition with a generating transition. Domain and codomain are
preserved by typing premises.
Lemma 6.3 (Adequacy, completeness direction). For any morphism m : σ0 →σn in the free
trace category, there exists a term t : FinTrace(σ0, σn) such that JtK = m up to trace equations.
Proof sketch. By induction on the generation of m in the free category. Identity morphisms
are represented by nil; composite morphisms are represented by iterated step-construction with
transition witnesses.
Theorem 6.4 (Trace Adequacy). For finite traces, syntactic reachability witnesses and seman-
tic morphisms in the free trace category are mutually representable, up to definitional equality
of trace terms and category equations.
Proof sketch. Immediate from Lemma 6.2 and Lemma 6.3. Concretely, the forward map sends
a term to its interpreted morphism, and the backward map reconstructs a term from a free-
category decomposition. These two maps are inverse up to the definitional equality on trace
terms and category equations induced by associativity/identity.

Meta-theory
7.1
Soundness
Theorem 7.1 (Semantic Soundness). For any judgment Γ ⊢t : A derivable in DEKL, its
interpretation is valid in the CwF/presheaf model and preserves substitution and computation.
Proof sketch. We proceed by induction on the derivation of Γ ⊢t : A.
Step 1 (contexts and substitutions). Interpretation of contexts and substitutions fol-
lows CwF structure. Well-formed context extension is mapped to comprehension objects, and
substitution composition is respected by definition.
Step 2 (core dependent rules). Rules for variables, conversion, Π-formation, introduc-
tion, and elimination are preserved by the corresponding universal properties in the semantic
model. Beta-equality is validated by functorial substitution.
Step 3 (trace constructors). Trace terms are interpreted in the free trace category: nil as
identities and step as composition with generating transitions. Typing premises ensure domain
and codomain compatibility for each interpreted morphism.
Step 4 (knowledge-indexed rules). Restriction operators are interpreted as action of
Kf : T op
f
→Type.
Functoriality yields identity and composition equations, matching the
syntactic restriction laws.
Step 5 (compatibility and conclusion). By Lemma B.1, interpretation commutes with
substitution; by construction of each rule case above, computation clauses are preserved. There-
fore every derivable judgment has a valid semantic interpretation, establishing soundness.
7.2
Consistency
Theorem 7.2 (Consistency). There is no closed term ⊢t : ⊥.
Proof sketch. In the semantic model, ⊥is interpreted as the initial/empty subobject. A closed
inhabitant would give a global element of the empty object, impossible by set-theoretic seman-
tics. Soundness transfers this impossibility to syntax.
Proof-obligations checklist (consistency).
For a fully expanded proof, one discharges:
• Initial-object interpretation. J⊥K is initial in each semantic fiber.
• Global section exclusion. There is no section 1 →J⊥K.
• Syntactic transfer.
By Theorem 7.1, semantic emptiness implies absence of closed
inhabitants in syntax.
7.3
Normalization
The computational layer retains the normalization behavior of standard dependent type theory.
Trace extensions and knowledge indexing do not introduce non-terminating computational re-
dexes into the core term calculus. Together with Theorem 7.1 and Theorem 7.2, this keeps the
logical kernel standard while relocating dynamic effects to trace-indexed semantics.
Proof-obligations checklist (normalization).
For mechanized normalization, the main
obligations are:
• Reduction closure. Every reduction rule preserves typing.
• Measure decrease. Core computational reductions decrease a reducibility measure.

• Trace conservativity. Trace constructors do not introduce new non-terminating redex
patterns in the core calculus.
• Index-erasure compatibility. Erasing trace indices preserves the reducibility argument
used by the base normalization theorem.
7.4
Subject reduction and canonicity perspective
Beyond consistency, two standard quality checks are relevant for publication-level metatheory:
• Subject reduction under computational rules.
Reduction in the computational
layer preserves typing and does not break trace indices.
• Canonicity in closed base types. Closed normal forms at base computational types
(e.g., naturals, finite traces) are canonical constructors.
In DEKL, these properties are inherited from the underlying dependent calculus and preserved
by conservative addition of trace constructors and presheaf-indexed knowledge families.
7.5
Explicit structural metatheorems
For completeness, we state the usual structural results in a form directly usable for mechaniza-
tion.
Theorem 7.3 (Weakening). If Γ ⊢t : A and Γ ⊢B : U, then
Γ, x : B ⊢t : A.
Proof sketch. Induction on the derivation of Γ ⊢t : A. Variable cases are handled by context
extension; all constructors commute with added assumptions by standard admissibility lemmas.
Theorem 7.4 (Substitution). If Γ, x : A ⊢t : B and Γ ⊢s : A, then
Γ ⊢t[s/x] : B[s/x].
Proof sketch. By induction on typing derivations of t. The critical cases are dependent prod-
ucts and trace-indexed constructors; both follow from substitution closure of the underlying
dependent calculus and stability of trace indices under term replacement.
Theorem 7.5 (Subject Reduction (Explicit Form)). If Γ ⊢t : A and t →t′, then
Γ ⊢t′ : A.
Proof sketch. Induction on reduction steps. Beta-reduction follows from Theorem 7.4. Compu-
tational rules for trace constructors are typed by preservation of the corresponding eliminator
branches.

8.1
Runtime monitoring and violation localization
Let
Safe : FinTrace →Typeℓ
encode “no policy-violating access has occurred on this prefix.” Assume
Γ ⊢p : Safe(τ).
If monitoring observes
τ ′ = τ · eviol,
the system may fail to derive
Γ ⊢p : Safe(τ ′).
The diagnostic content is constructive: the edge τ →τ′ localizes the exact event where validity
is lost.
Typed derivation snippet.
Γ ⊢τ : FinTrace(σ0, σ1)
Γ ⊢eviol : Event
Γ ⊢π : Step(σ1, eviol, σ2)
Γ ⊢step(τ, eviol, π) : FinTrace(σ0, σ2)
The monitor reports failure precisely at this extension step, yielding a typed localization witness.
8.2
Credential revocation in security protocols
Reuse the family
Auth(c, τ) : Typeℓ.
On issuance-only prefixes we derive witnesses
Γ ⊢q : Auth(c, τ).
After extension by revocation,
τ ′ = τ · Revoke(c),
one generally has no derivation of Γ ⊢q : Auth(c, τ′). The term q remains well-formed, but no
longer at the updated trace index. This is the intended form of semantic non-monotonicity for
dynamic authorization.
Typed derivation snippet.
Γ ⊢q : Auth(c, τ),
Γ ⊢ρ : Extf(τ, τ · Revoke(c)).
The system still admits the backward restriction shape
restrict(ρ, −) : Auth(c, τ · Revoke(c)) →Auth(c, τ),
while forward transport of q to the revoked index generally fails.
8.3
Defeasible defaults via trace-conditioned validity
Let
CanAccess(u, r, τ) : Typeℓ
represent access validity of user u to resource r on trace τ. A default witness
d : CanAccess(u, r, τ)
may become inapplicable after a risk event erisk:
τ ′ = τ · erisk.
Again, the framework does not derive a contradiction. Instead, it changes the typing index and
thereby models exception handling as index-sensitive validity.

Typed derivation snippet.
Γ ⊢d : CanAccess(u, r, τ)
and
Γ ⊢ϵ : Extf(τ, τ · erisk).
Whether d : CanAccess(u, r, τ·erisk) remains derivable depends on updated policy side conditions,
not on failure of structural rules.
Proposition 8.1 (Application-level index shift principle). For any knowledge family K :
FinTrace →Typeℓ, if policy predicates depend on new events introduced by τ →τ ′, then there
may exist a : K(τ) such that a is not typable at K(τ ′).
Proof sketch. Instantiate K by one of the families above and choose an extension event that
changes the policy side condition. Typability fails at the new index for semantic reasons, not
because the structural rules cease to hold.

Related Work
DEKL builds on Martin-Löf dependent type theory [8] and categorical semantics through cat-
egories with families [4, 11, 6, 9, 1]. It is also related to temporal/modal verification traditions
(LTL, CTL, µ-calculus) [10, 5, 3, 7, 2] and trace/event semantics in concurrency [12].
Compared with temporal logics, DEKL does not primarily target model-checking expres-
siveness classes; instead, execution evidence is internalized as typed terms and used as proof-
relevant reachability witnesses. Compared with type-theoretic effect systems, the focus is not
effect tracking in programs, but trace-indexed validity of knowledge witnesses under system
evolution. The distinctive contribution is not to replace existing logics, but to connect traces,
proofs, and knowledge revision in one dependent framework where non-monotonic behavior is
semantic (presheaf-theoretic) rather than inferential.
Four-dimensional comparison.
The distinction from nearby lines of work can be summa-
rized along four dimensions:
• Primary object. Temporal logics reason over path formulas; DEKL reasons over typed
trace witnesses.
• Methodological core. Program logics and effect systems center on operational effects;
DEKL centers on dependent typing indexed by execution traces.
• Proof obligation style.
Model-checking emphasizes satisfaction over transition sys-
tems, while DEKL emphasizes construction and transport of typed witnesses under trace
evolution.
• Semantic carrier. Classical modal semantics use Kripke/transition frames; DEKL uses
free trace categories with presheaf-indexed knowledge fibers.
This comparison clarifies that DEKL is complementary to temporal verification: it provides a
proof-relevant, type-theoretic account of history-sensitive knowledge rather than a replacement
for automata- or fixpoint-based model checking.
Finer technical contrasts.
• Counterexample form. Model checkers return violating paths; DEKL can represent
violating extensions as typed index-shift boundaries.

Limitations and Scope
The present manuscript prioritizes coherence of the core framework over maximal coverage. The
main limitations are:
• Finite-trace emphasis. Most formal adequacy and characterization results are stated
for finite traces; infinite-trace extensions are outlined but not fully developed.
• Proof granularity. Several results remain at proof-sketch level and should be expanded
into full derivation-level proofs for final publication.
• Mechanization status.
Rule schemas and proof roadmaps are now provided, but a
completed Coq/Agda artifact is not yet included.
• Case-study depth. Application sections currently provide formal templates rather than
full industrial-scale evaluations.
These limitations define a concrete extension path rather than a conceptual gap in the frame-
work.

Conclusion
DEKL 2.0 separates monotone proof theory from non-monotone knowledge dynamics.
The
core calculus remains dependent type theory; traces are first-class terms; knowledge is trace-
indexed via presheaf semantics; and non-monotonicity is characterized by non-surjective restric-
tion maps.
This reorganization clarifies both formal structure and conceptual message: proof objects
can carry execution history, and knowledge evolution in dynamic systems can be modeled con-
structively without adopting non-monotonic proof rules. In particular, Theorem 5.4 and The-
orem 5.5 isolate the source of non-monotonicity, while Theorem 7.1 and Theorem 7.2 preserve
the expected metatheoretic guarantees.
Future work includes mechanized formalization in proof assistants, a refined account of
fixed-point interaction with trace-indexed knowledge at the proposition layer, and larger case
studies in runtime monitoring and security policy evolution.
Short abstract (submission form draft).
DEKL 2.0 is a dependent type-theoretic frame-
work for trace-indexed knowledge evolution. The proof calculus remains monotone, while non-
monotonic behavior is explained semantically through presheaf restriction along trace exten-
sions. Finite and infinite traces are first-class typed objects; finite traces correspond construc-
tively to reachability witnesses; and the full semantics combines CwF interpretation with the free
trace category and its presheaf category. Non-monotonicity is characterized by non-surjective
restriction maps. The framework unifies execution evidence, proof objects, and knowledge re-
vision without introducing non-monotonic proof rules.

Proof Roadmap for Full Soundness
To scale from the present proof sketches to a full journal proof, one can adopt the following
dependency order:
1. Define interpretation on contexts, substitutions, and type formers.
2. Prove substitution compatibility (Lemma B.1).
3. Prove trace constructor preservation and restriction coherence (Lemmas B.2–B.3).
