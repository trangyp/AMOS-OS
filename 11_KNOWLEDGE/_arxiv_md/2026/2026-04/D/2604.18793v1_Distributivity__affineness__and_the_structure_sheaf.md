Abstract. We observe that for a quasi-compact and quasi-separated scheme
the structure sheaf generates the perfect complexes if and only if the lattice of
thick subcategories is distributive if and only if the affinization map is 0-affine.
Examples are discussed, including an example of a quasi-projective scheme
which is not quasi-affine but for which these equivalent conditions hold.
1. Introduction
Hypercohomology is a fundamental invariant of a complex of OX-modules on a
scheme X. It is thus a natural, and seemingly innocent, question to ask exactly how
much of the unbounded derived category D(X) of OX-modules with quasi-coherent
cohomology it sees. In other words, how large is the kernel of RHom(OX, −)? In
this paper we address the extreme case that this kernel is trivial; for such a scheme
X hypercohomology witnesses the non-triviality of every complex in D(X) that is
not acyclic. This is equivalent to D(X) being generated by the structure sheaf OX
under coproducts and to Dperf(X) being classically generated by OX.
There is an obvious class of schemes for which this is the case, namely the
affine schemes. As we all know in this case RHom(OX, −) gives an equivalence of
D(X) with the derived category D(OX(X)) of modules over the global sections. A
moments consideration leads one to notice that hypercohomology must also detect
vanishing on quasi-affine schemes, i.e. quasi-compact open subschemes of affine
schemes. An equivalent characterization of the quasi-affine schemes are as those
schemes such that OX is ample. We know that the powers of an ample line bundle
will always generate D(X) and so one is tempted to ask if cohomology detects
vanishing precisely for the quasi-affine schemes.
It turns out that this would be a bad instinct to follow (as the reader no doubt
already gathered from the abstract). In Examples 6.1 and 6.4 we exhibit a pair
of schemes which are not quasi-affine and for which the structure sheaf generates:
the first is non-separated and the second is quasi-projective. In particular, this
shows that a line bundle can generate the derived category without having to be
ample (even if there is an ample line bundle around).
Our main theorems, 4.2 and 5.4 give an abstract characterization of generation
by OX in terms of two rather different conditions.
The first shows that OX
generates if and only if the lattice Thick(OX) of thick subcategories of Dperf(X) is
distributive, which is a very natural condition from the point of view of tt-geometry.
The second theorem shows that generation by OX is equivalent to the derived

3. Tricks for manipulating subcategories
We begin with some general observations that apply for arbitrary commutative
2-rings and then apply these in the context of algebraic geometry. In fact, these
are all things that take place in the homotopy category and are valid for plain
tt-categories.
Lemma 3.1. Let K be a 2-ring and L a thick subcategory of K. If M is a thick
ideal of K such that 1 generates K/M then L ∨M is an ideal.
Proof. We have poset isomorphisms
{J ∈Thick⊗(K) | M ⊆J} ∼= Thick⊗(K/M)

precisely the thick subcategories containing M.

4. Distributivity
In this section we make the connection between generation by OX and distribu-
tivity of the lattice of thick subcategories. It is a straightforward combination of
standard facts to see that generation by the structure sheaf implies the lattice of
thick subcategories is very well behaved.
Proposition 4.1. If Dperf(X) is generated by OX then Thick(Dperf(X)) is a
coherent frame, and so in particular is distributive.
Proof. By [3, Theorem 3.1.9] the lattice of radical tensor ideals of Dperf(X) is a
coherent frame and so a fortiori distributive. Because Dperf(X) is rigid every tensor
ideal is radical, and because Dperf(X) = thick(OX) every thick subcategory is a
tensor ideal. Hence the lattices of radical tensor ideals and of thick subcategories
are identified and the claim follows.
□
Surprisingly, distributivity is also a sufficient condition for generation. The orig-
inal argument for the following theorem had an unnecessary noetherian hypothesis;
we are grateful to Martin Gallauer for suggesting that it could be removed.

5. 0-affine morphisms
We now give another abstract characterization of generation by the structure
sheaf. As above we work with ∞-categorical avatars of the derived category, but
for what follows it is really crucial.
The key notion is the following definition of Bhatt and Halpern-Leistner [2,

Definition 5.1. A morphism f : X →Y of schemes is 0-affine if f∗induces an
equivalence

between the derived category of X and the category of modules over the sheaf of
commutative ring spectra f∗OX.
Example 5.2. Suppose that i: U →Y is a quasi-compact open immersion and
let Z denote the complement of U.
Then i is 0-affine.

More generally, suppose that f : X →Y is quasi-affine. Then f is 0-affine as
everything in sight satisfies descent.
Actually being 0-affine is a condition we already know and love.
Proposition 5.3. A quasi-compact and quasi-separated morphism f : X →Y of
quasi-compact and quasi-separated schemes is 0-affine if and only if f∗is conserva-
tive.
Proof. If f is 0-affine then f∗is identified with the forgetful functor and so is
conservative. On the other hand, suppose that f∗is conservative. Because f is
quasi-compact and quasi-separated the functor f∗preserves colimits. Thus the
hypotheses of the ∞-categorical Barr-Beck theorem [4, Theorem 3.4.5] are (more
than) satisfied and we learn that D(X) is the category of modules over the monad
f∗f∗on D(Y ). By the projection formula this is the same as the monad induced
by the sheaf of rings f∗OX and so f is 0-affine.
□
This already allows us to come to the point as far as generation by the structure
sheaf is concerned. We call this a theorem because it is important; it is already
known to experts and the proof is quite cheap.

Example 6.8. Let G be a unipotent group scheme of finite cohomological dimension
over a field k, then BG is a 0-affine geometric stack (hence perfect). The reason is

A quasi-geometric stack with finite cohomological dimension has a compact

of finite cohomological dimension).
Now let us discuss a case where there are no non-trivial 0-affine morphisms.
Theorem 6.9. Suppose that f : X →Y is proper and Y is noetherian. Then f is
0-affine if and only if f is finite.
Proof. It is clear that if f is finite it is 0-affine (since it is just affine). For the
converse it is enough to check being 0-affine implies that the fibres of f are finite
by [9, Lemma 02OG]. So let Spec k →Y be a point and let f′ : π0(Xk) →Spec k
be the classical fibre. The map f′ is also 0-affine.
Let Z be a connected component of π0(Xk).
Then, because f′ is 0-affine,
Dperf(Z) is equivalent to a Dperf(A) where A = f′
∗(Oπ0(Z)) is a proper coconnective
dg algebra over k with H0(A) = k. Because k is a field and A is coconnective we
may model A by a dg algebra such that A<0 = 0 and A0 = k.
Without loss of generality we can replace Z by its reduction. Thus by virtue
of being reduced and of finite type over a field there is a regular point z ∈Z.
Hence the dg algebra A has a perfect module Hom(A, k(z)) which has cohomology
concentrated in degree 0. It follows from [7, Corollary 6.2] that A is quasi-isomorphic
to k and hence Z ∼= Spec k is a point. Thus the fibre π0(Xk) is 0-dimensional as
claimed.
