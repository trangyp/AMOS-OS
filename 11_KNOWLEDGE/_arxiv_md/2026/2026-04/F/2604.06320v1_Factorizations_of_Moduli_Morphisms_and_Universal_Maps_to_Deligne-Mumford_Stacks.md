ALBERTO LANDI
Abstract. Let X be an algebraic stack admitting a moduli space Xmod. We study the factorizations of the
moduli space morphism X →Xmod to construct intermediate stacks that simplify the stacky structure of X
while retaining more structural information than Xmod. Under mild assumptions, we prove the existence of
a universal morphism from X to stacks satisfying well-behaved ‘modular properties’ (such as being Deligne-
Mumford, having finite inertia, or being uniformizable), and show that this universal map is itself an
adequate moduli space morphism. We achieve this by proving that ascending chains of adequate moduli
space morphisms from a Noetherian stack stabilize if they are cohomologically affine or with target Deligne-
Mumford stacks. Finally, we demonstrate that stabilization completely fails for general adequate moduli
space morphisms. We construct a simple Noetherian, Deligne-Mumford stack admitting an infinite, non-
stabilizing chain of adequate moduli space morphisms, whose limit is a non-algebraic fpqc stack.

1. Introduction
A fundamental problem in algebraic geometry is the classification of isomorphism classes of objects of a
given type, such as curves or vector bundles. Traditionally, this is achieved by constructing a moduli space—a
scheme or algebraic space whose closed points are in bijection with these isomorphism classes. Historically,
such spaces were constructed using Geometric Invariant Theory (GIT), as developed by Mumford [MFK94].
The modern approach is to construct an algebraic stack X that parametrizes families of these objects,
thereby retaining more structural information than a coarse moduli space. Nevertheless, recovering a moduli
space from X remains geometrically significant. When X has finite inertia, this was first achieved by Keel and
Mori via coarse moduli spaces ([KM97, Con05, Ryd07]). For the general case, Alper and others introduced
the notions of good and adequate moduli spaces [Alp13, Alp14, AHLH23, AHR25]. This framework provides
a well-behaved morphism πX : X →Xmod that is universal among morphisms to algebraic spaces, a theory
that has since proven highly successful.
However, given an algebraic stack X admitting a moduli space Xmod, it is natural to ask whether there exist
intermediate stacks that retain more of the structural information of X than Xmod, yet remain geometrically
simpler than X itself. This amounts to studying all the possible factorizations of the moduli morphism πX ,
which can be organized in a 2-category CX . This parametrizes adequate moduli space morphisms X →Y,
that are relative versions of moduli spaces; see Definition 2.2.
We can then ask whether any of these
simplifications of the stacky structure of X has some universal properties.
The most immediate example is a morphism ΦDM : X →XDM that is universal among morphisms to
Deligne-Mumford stacks. In this paper, we establish the existence of such intermediate stacks under relatively
weak assumptions.

Theorem 1.1 (Deligne-Mumford Reduction). Let X be a quasi-separated locally-Noetherian algebraic stack
admitting an adequate moduli space πX : X →Xmod. Then, there exists an adequate moduli space morphism
ΦDM : X →XDM that is universal among morphisms to Deligne-Mumford stacks with affine diagonal.
See Corollary 4.6 for a slightly more general statement and its proof. Notice that XDM has automatically
finite inertia by [Alp14, Theorem 8.3.2]. More generally, we address the following question.
Question 1.2. Given a property P of algebraic stacks, does there exist a morphism ΦP : X →XP that is
universal among morphisms from X to stacks satisfying P?
We answer Question 1.2 in Theorems 4.1 and 4.4, for a class of well-behaved properties, that we call
modular properties (Definition 3.5). We also need to assume that πX is a good moduli space, or that P
implies the property of being a Deligne-Mumford stack. In §5 we will see how things can go very wrong
without the above assumptions, even for stacks that are arguably simple.
Beyond the case of Deligne-Mumford stacks, we specialize this discussion to morphisms to stacks with
finite inertia (Corollary 4.5), as well as to uniformizable stacks (§4.2)—algebraic stacks admitting a finite
´etale cover by an algebraic space. The latter case is tightly connected to the ´etale fundamental group of X,
making the existence of a universal map to uniformizable stacks a valuable tool for studying π1(X).
Unfortunately, the formation of XP does not commute with base change along morphisms to XP or Xmod,
even when the morphisms are fppf or open immersions. This is in stark contrast with what happens for
moduli spaces, and shows that the construction of XP cannot be local in nature. For X of finite type over
an algebraically closed field, in §4.3 we completely characterize when XDM commutes with base change, and
similarly for other properties P. As an application, we recover [ER17, Proposition B.2] over an algebraically
closed field, see Corollary 4.20.

1.2. Inadequacy of Adequate Moduli Space Morphisms. The results we mentioned above show that
CX is very well-behaved when we restrict to good moduli space morphisms or require the targets to be
Deligne-Mumford stacks. In contrast, if we require neither of these properties, we open the door to wild,
pathological examples.
For instance, it is known that in general adequate moduli space morphisms do not yield a surjection
between geometric stabilizers (see for example [RRZ18, §4.5]). This undermines the intuition that moduli
space morphisms simplify stabilizers. However, this intuition does hold true when considering good moduli
space morphisms or Deligne-Mumford targets; see Lemma 2.9.
Similarly, the stabilization property fails in general. The core issue is that adequate moduli space mor-
phisms are not generally determined by their relative inertia, see [Ryd20, §A.2.3]. In §5, we construct an
example of an ascending chain in CX that does not stabilize even if X is Noetherian and has a very simple
structure, showing that the assumptions in the key Proposition 1.5 are necessary. This surprising example
is essentially an infinite iteration of the one in [RRZ18, §4.5], and it is inspired by Nagata’s famous example
([Nag69],[Kol97, Example 6.5.1]) of a Noetherian ring with non-Noetherian ring of invariants under a finite
group action.

X be the Deligne-Mumford stack X = B(Z/pZ)k(y)[ϵ]/(ϵ2), which is a trivial gerbe over its non-reduced
moduli space Xmod = Spec k(y)[ϵ]/(ϵ2). Because Z/pZ is not linearly-reductive, the moduli space morphism
πX : X →Xmod is adequate, but not good.

2.1. Good and Adequate Moduli Spaces. The following results are likely well-known to specialists;
however, we include their proofs here for completeness and the reader’s convenience.
We start by recalling what are good and adequate moduli space morphisms.
We use the convention
of [AHR25, Definition 1.13], where cohomologically and adequately affine morphisms are imposed to be
stable under base change.
Definition 2.1. Let X be an algebraic stack.
A morphism A →B of quasi-coherent OX -algebras is
universally adequate if every section s of B over a smooth morphism Spec R →Y has a positive power that
lifts to a section of A.
Definition 2.2. A quasi-compact and quasi-separated morphism f : X →Y of algebraic stacks is cohomo-
logically affine (resp. adequately affine) if:
(1) f∗is exact on the category of quasi-coherent OX -modules (resp. for every surjection A →B of
quasi-coherent OX -algebras, f∗A →f∗B is universally adequate); and,
(2) property (1) holds after arbitrary base change Y′ →Y.
We say that f is a good (resp. adequate) moduli space morphism if f is cohomologically affine (resp. adequately
affine) and f∗OX ≃OY. If further Y is an algebraic space, we simply call it a good (resp. adequate) moduli
space, and denote it by πX : X →Xmod.
All properties above commute with flat base change Y′ →Y, and are fpqc-local on the target, see [Alp13,
Proposition 4.7] and [Alp14, Proposition 5.2.9]. In particular, X →Y is a good (resp. adequate) moduli
space morphism if and only if for every flat morphism U →Y from an algebraic space X ×Y U →U is a good
(resp. adequate) moduli space. Good moduli space morphisms commute with arbitrary base change ([Alp13,
Proposition 4.7]), while adequate moduli space morphisms do so only up to adequate homeomorphism.

2.2. Fpqc Stacks. In this subsection, we briefly introduce the notion of an fpqc stack. Since this material
is only used in §5, the reader may safely skip it until then.
Let P be a property of morphisms of algebraic spaces that is stable under base change.
Recall that
a representable morphism f : X →Y of (not necessarily algebraic) stacks is said to satisfy P if for all
morphisms U →Y from an algebraic space U the pullback morphism X ×Y U →U has property P.
Definition 2.10. Let Y be a stack in the fpqc topology. We say that Y is an fpqc stack if there exists an
algebraic space U and a representable fpqc morphism π : U →Y. We call π an fpqc presentation. We say
that an fpqc stack Y is quasi-algebraic if the diagonal is representable by algebraic spaces.
Example 2.11. An example of a quasi-algebraic stack is the classifying stack of an fpqc group scheme over
a base scheme S. It is algebraic if and only if it is also finitely presented over S, see [Sta26, Tag 06PL].
Remark 2.12. Recall that if Y admits a representable fppf morphism from a scheme, then its diagonal is
automatically representable, and indeed Y is an algebraic stack by Artin’s Theorem [Art74, Theorem 6.1]
(see also [Sta26, Tag 06DC]). In contrast, not all fpqc stacks are quasi-algebraic. Indeed, having an fpqc
presentation only guarantees the diagonal to be representable by fpqc spaces, that is, fpqc stacks that are
equivalent to a sheaf. Since fpqc spaces are not algebraic spaces in general, as shown in [dJ26], the diagonal
is not guaranteed to be representable.

2.2.1. Properties of Morphisms of Fpqc Stacks. Recall that given a morphism ϕ : X →Y of algebraic stacks,
by definition, ϕ is of finite type (respectively, finite presentation) if and only if for all fppf presentations
Y →Y and X →X ×Y Y , the morphism X →Y is of finite type (respectively, of finite presentation). This
is well-defined and independent of the choice of fppf presentations because being of finite type (respectively,
of finite presentation) is fppf-local on the target and the source.
Although these properties are also fpqc-local on the target, locality on the source generally fails. Conse-
quently, for fpqc stacks, one should talk about finiteness properties only for morphisms that are representable
by algebraic stacks. This motivates the following natural definition.
Definition 2.13. Let X and Y be fpqc stacks, let ϕ : X →Y be a morphism representable by algebraic
stacks. Let P be a property of morphisms of algebraic stacks that is stable under base change along fpqc
morphisms, and is fpqc-local on the base.
We say that ϕ has property P if for any fpqc presentation
π : U →Y by a scheme, the base change X ×Y U →U has property P.
This definition is independent of the choice of U, which can also be taken to be an algebraic stack.
Moreover, the extended property P is again stable under base change along fpqc morphisms, and is fpqc
local on the base. It is stable under composition and arbitrary base change if the same holds for P when
regarded as a property of morphisms between algebraic stacks.
Notice that flatness is fpqc-local on the source; thus, to define flat morphisms of fpqc stacks, there is no
