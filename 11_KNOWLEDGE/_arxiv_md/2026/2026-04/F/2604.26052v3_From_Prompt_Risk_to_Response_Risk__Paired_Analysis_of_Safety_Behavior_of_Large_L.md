From Prompt Risk to Response Risk:
Paired Analysis of Safety Behavior of Large Language Model
Mengya (Mia) Hu, Qiong Wei, Sandeep Atluri
Responsible AI, Microsoft
humia@microsoft.com
Abstract
Safety evaluations of large language models
(LLMs) typically report binary outcomes, i.e.
attack success rate (ASR), refusal rate, or harm-
ful versus safe classification, which hide how
risk changes between prompt and response.
We present a paired analysis over human la-
beled prompt and response records across four
harm categories (Sexual, Self harm, Hate and
Violence) and ordinal severity levels (Safe,
Low, Medium, High). 61% of responses re-
duce harm relative to the prompt, 36% pre-
serve severity, and 3% escalate. The escalation
splits into two mechanisms: benign prompts
triggering unrequested harmful detail, and an-
swers that stay on task at higher severity than
the prompt. Category decomposition shows
that Sexual content exhibits the highest harm
persistence in this sample, driven by compli-
ance at the same severity rather than drift from
benign inputs.
Joint relevance analysis ex-
poses a helpfulness versus harmlessness trade-
off: compliance escalations remain highly rel-
evant, whereas safe responses include generic
refusals with low relevance. Finally, few-shot
LLM graders exhibit a prompt/response de-
tection asymmetry that data calibration does
not close.
Grader prompts are shared at
https://github.com/microsoft/PairedSafety.

Introduction
A standard way to evaluate the safety of a LLM is
to count harmful outputs on an evaluation set: ASR
style metrics on adversarial benchmarks (Mazeika
et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023), false refusal rates
on benign inputs (Röttger et al., 2024), or binary
moderation accuracy (Han et al., 2024; Inan et al.,
2023; Markov et al., 2023). These endpoint met-
rics measure “how often does the model produce a
harmful response” but not “what does the model do
with the harmful prompts,” nor “how does response
severity move relative to prompt severity.”
We propose treating safety from prompt to re-
sponse as a directional, paired measurement: each
prompt and each response receives an independent
label on the same category and severity scale. The
unit of analysis is the transition from prompt sever-
ity to response severity in each category. We apply
the framework to 1,250 human labeled interactions
covering two production grade LLMs (gpt-4 and
gpt-5.1) to generate response. The contributions
of this paper are:
1. A paired safety transition framework with cat-
egory level severity tracking (§3).
2. Headline rates, particularly the 3.20% of esca-
lation from prompt to response severity. We
further decompose escalation into two mech-
anisms: unsolicited elaboration and compli-
ance escalation. We identify a category asym-
metry: the sexual category exhibits higher
harm persistence in our sample (§4, §4.2).
3. A joint severity and relevance analysis show-
ing that compliance escalation is typically
highly relevant, whereas reduction includes
generic refusals with low relevance (§4).
4. An LLM grader evaluation with ablation stud-
ies demonstrating that response moderation
is more challenging than prompt modera-
tion.(§4.3).

Data and Methods
Corpus. The corpus consists of 1,250 (prompt,
LLM response) pairs from single turn English
conversations. The length distribution is in Ap-
pendix A. Both prompts and responses are anno-
tated with harm labels across four categories: Sex-
ual, Hate, Violence, and Self harm. Labels use
a four point severity scale: 0 (Safe), 1 (Low), 2
(Medium), and 3 (High). This scale merges the
public Azure AI Content Safety text severity lev-
els (Microsoft, 2025): raw levels 0 to 1 map to
Safe, 2 to 3 to Low, 4 to 5 to Medium, and 6 to 7
to High. The prompt and response were labeled
independently, which makes the paired transition
analysis well defined. Annotators also scored re-
sponse relevance to the prompt:
• 3 (Relevant). The response thoroughly ad-
dresses the subject or intent of the prompt, or
when refusing a harmful prompt, explains the
refusal in a way clearly tied to that prompt
and offers safe redirection.
• 2 (Somewhat relevant). The response par-
tially addresses the topic or refuses with refer-
ence to the prompt’s intent, but is incomplete,
vague, or lacks actionable redirection.
• 1 (Not relevant). The response is unrelated to
the prompt, or is a generic refusal that fails to
acknowledge the prompt’s intent.
571 of 1,250 records were independently labeled
by two annotators, with disagreements jointly re-
viewed alongside two additional reviewers. Dis-
putes were adjudicated to consensus, and one origi-
nal annotator then applied the refined understand-
ing to label the remaining data.

5
Implications for Content Moderation
Prompt only filtering misses the tail. Of the
40 escalations, 32 (80%) originate from prompts
whose maximum severity across all four categories
is 0. Treating “max sp ≥1” as the trigger for
response review yields precision 0.128 and recall
0.773 against the harmful response label, missing
all 32 cases that start from severity 0. Reassuringly,
these escalations cluster at the bottom of the sever-
ity scale (29 land at severity 1, 3 at severity 2, 0
at severity 3): the worst severity 3 outputs in the
corpus all originate from prompts that already car-
ried category signal above zero. The implication
is a clean division of labor: prompt classifiers can
gate the worst escalations; a response moderator is
required to catch the long tail of low but nonzero
severity outputs that emerge from clean prompts.
Response filter needs further tuning These re-
sults motivate treating response moderation as a
separate detection problem rather than reusing ex-
pectations from prompt moderation. A response
grader must be calibrated for long refusals, safety
advice, and educational explanations where harm-
ful content can be lexically present but policy de-
pendent.
6
Conclusion
Paired analysis from prompt severity to response
severity reveals a dominant reduction regime with a
small escalation tail invisible to binary metrics. The
escalation splits into unsolicited elaboration and
compliance escalation; the latter produces highly
relevant harmful content. Combined with relevance
analysis, it empirically shows the helpfulness ver-
sus harmlessness tradeoff. Few-shot graders con-
firm that response moderation is harder than prompt
moderation; adding examples sourced directly from
the corpus do not close the gap, motivating further
study on response safety classifiers.
7
Limitations
Results are from 1,250 single turn English inter-
actions; broader generalization requires larger and
multilingual corpora, and comparisons across mod-
els.

Length Distribution Analysis
In agentic settings, inputs dominate: Manus reports
a 100:1 input to output token ratio (Ji, 2025). In
the single turn chat setting studied here the pattern
reverses. Figure 3 compares prompt and response
length distributions of the data set in both tokens
and characters, together with response to prompt
length ratios. Prompts are short and tightly con-
centrated (median 21 tokens / 95 characters; mean
23.7 tokens / 106.3 characters), while responses
are much longer and more skewed to the right (me-
dian 221 tokens / 1,004 characters; mean 311.4
tokens / 1,360.4 characters). No prompt exceeds
500 characters, but 79.5% of responses exceed 500
characters, 50.1% exceed 1,000, and 21.5% exceed
2,000.

Reproducibility
Annotators are provided with detailed internal
guidelines that extend beyond the public Azure
Content Safety definitions, including richer instruc-
tions and examples; however, these extended guide-
lines cannot be shared externally due to compliance
requirements.
We selected 1,250 short text samples with rep-
resentation across severity levels as input prompts.
The prompts are send to LLM directly without
any additonal system prompt, using Azure API
with content safety filtering disabled, to generate
responses. The generated responses were subse-
quently labeled by human annotators.
Because the prompts contain explicit harm cat-
egory exemplars and were curated as an internal
test set, the full data cannot be released publicly.
We instead release aggregate statistics, transition
matrices, and figures that have broad insight im-
plication, and share some representative examples
above to be illustrative.
We used the Azure API with content safety fil-
tering disabled when implementing the grader. The
choice of API is not essential to the proposed
method, and as such, we do not release the full
implementation code, which may not be univer-
sally applicable. To support reproducibility, we
instead release the grader prompts in our reposi-
tory: https://github.com/microsoft/PairedSafety.
E
Model comparison
Each prompt is randomly assigned to one of two
production LLMs, gpt-5.1 (n=961) or gpt-4
(n=289), to generate responses. As prompts are
not matched across models, we note this as a limita-
tion. We bootstrap standardized differences (Efron
and Tibshirani, 1993) (B=10,000) for comparisons
across models under prompt severity standardiza-
tion. After standardizing for prompt severity, the
estimated rates are 11.79% for gpt-4 and 11.24%
for gpt-5.1, a standardized point estimate differ-
ence of 0.56 percentage points. The bootstrap 95%
CI for the difference is [−3.73, +5.14] and crosses
zero, indicating no statistically supported differ-
ence between models in this sample.
