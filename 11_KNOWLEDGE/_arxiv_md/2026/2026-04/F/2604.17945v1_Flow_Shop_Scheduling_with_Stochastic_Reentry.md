Abstract
We study flow shop scheduling with stochastic reentry, where jobs must complete mul-
tiple passes through the entire shop, and the number of passes that a job requires for
completion is drawn from a discrete probability distribution. The goal is to find policies
that minimize performance measures in expectation. Our main contribution is a reduction
to a classical parallel machine scheduling problem augmented with machine arrivals. This
reduction preserves expected objective values and enables transferring structural results and
performance guarantees from the auxiliary problems to the reentrant flow shop setting. We
demonstrate the usefulness of this reduction by proving the optimality of simple priority
policies for minimizing the makespan and the total completion time in expectation under
geometric and, more generally, monotone hazard rate distributions. For minimizing the total
weighted completion time, we derive an approximation guarantee that depends only on the
squared coefficient of variation of the underlying distributions for a simple priority policy.
Our results constitute the first optimality and approximation guarantees for flow shops with
stochastic reentry and demonstrate that established scheduling policies naturally extend to
this setting through the proposed reduction.

Introduction
The machine environment flow shop with reentry is closely related to the well-understood
flow shop and job shop environments. In classical flow shops, each job must traverse each
machine exactly once; in contrast, in this model, jobs may require several passes through
the entire flow shop to be completed. We call each pass through the flow shop a loop and
allow jobs to require a different number of loops for completion. After completing a pass
through the flow shop, the job may either be scheduled again on the shop’s first machine
immediately or reenter later. Although general job shops can model this behavior, job shop
scheduling policies may not exploit its inherent cyclical structure.
The reentry pattern can be used to model manufacturing processes in which multiple
layers of material are applied individually, e.g., textile dyeing or mirror fabrication (Choi
and Kim 2007). The most important application arises in semiconductor manufacturing,
where dozens of layers of insulators and conductors are individually deposited onto a wafer
through a series of processes on specialized machines (Pfund, Mason, and Fowler 2006,
M¨onch, Fowler, Dauz`ere-P´er`es, Mason, and Rose 2011).
Since wafers pass through the
entire flow shop for each layer and require a different number of layers depending on the
architecture or function of the desired semiconductor chip, this problem naturally motivates
studying the flow shop model with reentry.
Real-world manufacturing problems are commonly subject to uncertainty, for example,
due to processing errors or malfunctions. To ensure that computed schedules are robust
against such uncertainties, we consider a general model in which the numbers of required
loops are randomly distributed and aim to develop policies that are optimal in expectation.

1.1
Model & Definitions
We are given a flow shop consisting of m machines {1, 2, . . . , m} that must be traversed
in increasing order and multiple times by n jobs. For a job j ∈{1, 2, . . . , n}, the discrete
random variable Yj denotes the number of required loops through the flow shop. In what
follows, we use the short-hand notation [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n} to denote the set of positive
integers up to n. The loops of each job must be completed in sequence. Furthermore, each
machine can process at most one job at a time. Throughout this paper, we assume that
each operation of any job and any loop on any machine takes unit time (i.e., pijk = 1 for
all i ∈[m], j ∈[n], k ∈[Yj] in standard scheduling notation). Additionally, we assume that
the Yj’s are independent. When minimizing a weighted objective function, each job j ∈[n] is
also assigned a positive weight wj. For a given objective function γ, the goal is to compute a
scheduling policy that minimizes γ in expectation. In this paper, we focus on the objectives
of minimizing the makespan, the total completion time, and the total weighted completion
time, i.e., γ ∈{Cmax, P Cj, P wjCj}.
We refer to this problem as Flow Shop Scheduling with Stochastic Reentry. In
the standard three-field-notation, it may be denoted as F|rntr, pijk = 1, Yj ∼stoch|E [γ].
Because all processing times are unit, each loop can, without loss of generality, be sched-
uled contiguously, and we can restrict our attention to schedules in which all operations
start at integer times. The scheduling policies we consider in this paper are dynamic and
non-anticipatory. Dynamic, non-anticipatory policies may use the realized completion times
of already completed jobs to make a decision, but their decisions may not depend on the
realization of still unfinished jobs. In particular, a policy checks at each time step if a job
has completed a loop through the flowshop without being fully processed. If so, it adds it
back to the set of uncompleted jobs. Then, it chooses an uncompleted job (or no job at
all, denoted as ⊥) to start processing on machine 1. For the jobs currently being processed
on a machine—we call these the active jobs—we keep track of when they were added to
machine 1 so that we know when they will complete their current loop (m units later). The
policy has no knowledge of the exact number of loops a job requires, but knows only the
expected number.
Formally, a policy π is a function that takes as input an instance I, the current time t ∈N,
the set of uncompleted jobs J0 ⊂J, and, the set of active jobs Ja ⊂J × N (including their
start times); and gives as output a job j ∈J0 ∪{⊥} to be scheduled next. In general, such a
policy may require exponential space to encode or exponential time to evaluate due to the
size of the state space. We are particularly interested in simple priority policies that assign
a fixed priority value to each job and always schedule the job with the largest priority value
next.
Let OPT denote an optimal policy. We call π an α-approximation or an α-approximate
policy if E[γ(OPT(I))] ≤E[γ(π(I))] ≤αE[γ(OPT(I))] for all instances I, where E[γ(π(I))]
denotes the expected objective value under policy π on instance I. We call α the approxi-
mation ratio or performance guarantee under policy π.

Flow shops with reentry were originally introduced by Graves, Meal, Stefek, and Zeghmi
(1983), and various heuristics have since been developed (e.g., Mason, Fowler, and Matthew Car-

Polynomial-time solvable

2003, Yu and Pinedo 2020, von Aspern et al. 2025). Other reentrant flow shop models, in
which jobs reenter only some of the flow shop’s machines, were also studied (e.g., Middendorf
and Timkovsky 2002, Emmons and Vairaktarakis 2012).
While the literature on flow shops with reentry has so far focused on purely determin-
istic problems, there is a large body of results on stochastic scheduling in traditional flow
shops (e.g., Emmons and Vairaktarakis 2012, Pinedo 2022) and on parallel machines. The
optimality of simple priority policies for the makespan and total completion time objectives
when scheduling jobs with geometrically (or exponentially) distributed processing times
on parallel machines was established by various authors (Pinedo and Weiss 1979, Bruno,
Downey, and Frederickson 1981, Gittins 1981, van der Heyden 1981, Weber 1982, K¨ampke
1987, Chang, Nelson, and Pinedo 1991). Most relevant to the problem described in this
paper are the results by Glazebrook (1979) and Weber (1982) on parallel machine models,
in which the number of machines increases monotonically over time. The authors showed
that simple priority policies are optimal for a broad class of random processing time dis-
tributions, including geometric and deterministic processing times. For the total weighted
completion time objective, the first policy with a parametrized approximation ratio for arbi-
trary distributions was given by M¨ohring, Schulz, and Uetz (1999). Its approximation ratio
was later improved by J¨ager and Skutella (2018).

Outline & Results
In Section 2, we present our reduction to an auxiliary parallel machine scheduling prob-
lem. Specifically, we give sufficient conditions for the existence of policies with bounded
performance guarantees in the flow shop with reentry setting. Section 3 contains several ap-
plications of our reduction. We state simple priority policies for the makespan and the total
completion time objective and show that they are optimal under certain assumptions. For
the total weighted completion time objective, we show that an equally simple priority policy
achieves a parametrized approximation ratio. Technical details of the analysis are contained
in Section 4. We extend our results to practically motivated distributions in Section 5 and
conclude with a discussion in Section 6.

Reduction to Stochastic Scheduling with Machine Ar-
rivals
In this section, we present our main technical contribution: a reduction to an auxiliary
problem, which we shall refer to as Stochastic Scheduling with Machine Arrivals.
This is a classical scheduling problem on identical parallel machines augmented with machine
arrivals.
A machine i ∈[m] arrives at time ai ∈Z and must not be used before this
time. Once a machine arrives, it remains available until all jobs have been completed. A
random variable Xj denotes the processing time of job j. One may denote this problem
as P|ai, Xj ∼stoch|E [γ] using the standard three-field notation.
The idea behind this
reduction is as follows. Since each job requires m time units per loop, we partition time
into periods of length m, interpreting each as m parallel processing steps. This yields a
view in which a loop corresponds to one unit of processing on one of m parallel machines.
To preserve the sequential structure of the flow shop, machines must become available at
staggered times 0, 1/m, 2/m, . . . , (m−1)/m. These staggered arrivals break standard inductive
arguments that rely on simultaneous preemption of multiple jobs. To overcome this difficulty,
we instead study a family of instances with binary machine arrival times whose average
behavior matches that of the original system.

Optimal and Approximately Optimal Priority Poli-
cies
In this section, we present some applications of Theorem 3. That is, we derive optimal-
ity results or approximation guarantees for certain policies for Flow Shop Scheduling
with Stochastic Reentry by leveraging results for Stochastic Scheduling with
Machine Arrivals. Specifically, we consider the class of priority policies, which are of
particular interest due to their simple design. Given a predefined static priority value for
each job j, priority policies schedule at any time the job j with the largest priority value
among all available jobs. In particular, priority policies for Flow Shop Scheduling with
Stochastic Reentry are non-interruptive, i.e., whenever a job reenters the system after
being processed on machine m, it is immediately scheduled on machine 1.

5.1
Monotone Hazard Rate Model
In this section, we present the monotone hazard rate model introduced by Weber (1979)
and state a more general version of Corollary 6. Let hj : N0 →[0, 1] denote the probability
that job j is completed after loop k + 1, conditioned on it not being completed after loop k.
We say that {Y1, Y2, . . . , Yn} have a monotone hazard rate if the following hold:

1. There exist ν functions f1, . . . , fν : N0 →[0, 1] that are either all non-increasing or all

Weber (1979) shows that the preemptive LEPT and SEPT policies minimize the expected
makespan and expected total completion time, respectively, for Stochastic Scheduling
with Machine Arrivals if {Y1, Y2, . . . , Yn} have a monotone hazard rate.
Inspired by this result, we introduce the dynamic and interruptive priority policies Most
Expected Remaining Loops first (MERL) and Least Expected Remaining Loops first (LERL).
These policies are defined by the same priority values as their static counterparts from
Section 3, but the priority value is updated each time a loop is completed. In particular, if
job j reenters the flow shop after completing loop k, its priority value is E[Yj−k|Yj > k] under
the MERL policy, or E[Yj −k|Yj > k]−1 under the LERL policy. Analogously to Lemma 4
and Corollary 5 it can be shown that the MERL and LERL policies induce the preemptive
LEPT and SEPT policies, respectively. Here, we apply the arguments of Lemma 4 to the
start time of each individual loop rather than focusing only on the start time of the first
loop. Combined with Theorem 3, this gives the following result.
Corollary 13. Let {Y1, Y2, . . . , Yn} have a monotone hazard rate. For Flow Shop Schedul-
ing with Stochastic Reentry, the LERL policy minimizes the expected total completion
time, and the MERL policy minimizes the expected makespan.
The monotone hazard rate model generalizes several different models (Weber 1982).
The following two are of particular interest because they capture the results in Corollary 6
and in (Yu and Pinedo 2020) and (von Aspern et al. 2025).
If all Yj are geometrically
distributed with parameters qj, then we have that hj(k) = qj for all k ∈N0. Appropriately
choosing constant functions fi and setting xj = 0 for all j shows that {Y1, Y2, . . . , Yn} have
a monotone hazard rate. By the memorylessness property of the geometric distribution,
MERL and LERL are equivalent to the non-interruptive policies MEL and LEL in this case.
Thus, Corollary 13 is a strict generalization of Corollary 6. If all Yj are deterministic, then
we can choose a single function f by defining f(Ymax) = 1 and f(k) = 0 for k ̸= Ymax, where
Ymax := maxj Yj. Now we can set xj = Ymax −Yj and we have hj(k) = f(xj + k). In this
case, MERL and LERL are equivalent to the deterministic scheduling rules Most Remaining
Loops first (MRL) and Least Remaining Loops first (LRL), which were previously studied
by Yu and Pinedo (2020) and von Aspern et al. (2025).

Discussion
In this paper, we establish the first optimality results and approximation guarantees for
Flow Shop Scheduling with Stochastic Reentry. These results are achieved by
introducing a framework that connects the problem to a variant of a well-studied parallel
machine scheduling problem. This framework enables extensions and, in some cases, direct
transfers of classical optimality results and approximation guarantees from the literature.
At the same time, the established connection provides insights into the structure of the
reentrant flow shop that have inspired the development of our approximate policies. We
hope that our framework will facilitate future work on designing and analyzing policies—
whether optimal, approximate, or heuristic—for the reentrant flow shop model under various
stochastic models that capture real-world uncertainty in production environments.
In Section 5.2, we introduce a family of practically motivated probability distributions
that we believe are particularly relevant for future research.
Our result in Corollary 7
immediately implies that the WLEL policy is a
√
2-approximation for the total weighted
completion time objective under these distributions. While we show in Section 5.2 that
MERL is no longer optimal under these distributions, it would be interesting to establish
general worst-case performance guarantees in general, or in settings where either the number
of loops or the success probability is constant across all jobs, or to develop new policies
specifically tailored to these distributions.
Finally, it is important to note that our reduction heavily depends on the assumption
of unit processing times.
For future research, we propose establishing structural results
on flow shops with reentry—both deterministic and stochastic—where this assumption is
relaxed. Such results would allow us to study more complex manufacturing settings, such
as reentrant flow shops with bottleneck machines.
