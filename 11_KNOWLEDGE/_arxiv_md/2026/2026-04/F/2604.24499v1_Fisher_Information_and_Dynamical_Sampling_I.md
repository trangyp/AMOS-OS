Abstract
Information theory is a powerful framework to capture aspects of dynamical systems
with multiple degrees of freedom. Mathematically, the dynamics can be represented as
a continuous curve C on a suitable hyperplane in flat space and the Fisher information
provides the norm of an infinitesimal displacement along this curve. In many applications,
however, we do not have direct access to C. Instead, we have to reconstruct the latter from
a time-series of measurements (obtained as samples of size n), which are represented by an
ordered set of points bC on the same hyperplane. In this work, we calculate the bias of the
Fisher information for large n, which provides a quantitative estimation for how accurately
the dynamics of a system can be reconstructed from a given set of sampled data. Based
on this result, we show that a clustering of the degrees of freedom reduces the bias and
thus improves the accuracy with which the new system can be described with the same
data. Inspired by a concrete proposal for such a clustering in [1], we provide a quantitive
assessment of the loss of information, which allows to estimate how much information
about the dynamics of a system can reliably be extracted based on a given set of data. We
illustrate our findings in the case of a simple compartmental model. Although the latter is
inspired by epidemiology, the results of this work are applicable to very general dynamical
models with multiple degrees of freedom.

Introduction
Analysing dynamical systems from an information theoretical perspective has a long standing
history: notably in the context of communication theory [2–6], questions such as the maximal
rate at which information can be transmitted by a channel [7, 8] or the optimal compression
of data [9, 10] have been studied using a variety of mathematical tools [11, 12]. The latter, in
fact, are applicable to a large number of different systems in electrical engineering, physics (e.g.
statistical mechanics [13–15]), computer science or economics. In most of these examples, the
2


## Page 3


state of a system with a general number of degrees of freedom, is measured at periodic time
intervals, from which a(n effective) model is inferred to describe the future dynamics. It is of
particular practical interest to understand the influence of noise on the predictive power of this
model, i.e. the fact that the measurements generally only allow to reconstruct an approximate
state of the system at any given moment in time. Information theoretical and statistical tools
allow to make statements about the maximal information that can be obtained about a system
from such measurements.
A particular branch of information theory that we shall rely on in this work is information
geometry [16–20], i.e. the application of elements of (Riemannian) geometry to describe and
analyse probabilistic systems: indeed, the general object of study in this paper is a statistical
model C as a system of N dynamical degrees of freedom, which are encoded in a time-dependent
probability1 distribution, namely a set of functions pµ(t) ∈[0, 1] (with µ = 1, . . . , N + 1), such
that PN+1
µ=1 pµ(t) = 1 for all times t ∈Ξ ⊂R. As we shall review (and extend) in this paper,
there are two different geometric notions that can be studied in this framework: on the one
hand, the Fisher information [22] gtt associated with C can be understood as the metric on a
Riemannian manifold (see e.g. [18] for an excellent review). While in the current setup, this
simply means that gtt is a smooth function of time, this notion becomes more sophisticated
for probability distributions that depend on several parameters. On the other hand, C can be
understood as a (continuous) curve on a hyperplane of RN+1, which we shall call the simplex
∆N+1.
A notion of distance on ∆N+1 is provided by the Shahshahani metric gµν [23] (see
also [24]). These two geometric notions are, in fact, related: the norm (with respect to the
Shahshahani metric) of an infinitesimal displacement along the curve C is given by the Fisher
information gtt (see e.g. [25–29]). Thus, the Fisher information is an indispensable tool for
studying the dynamics of the model C and describes ’how much’ the system changes in an
infinitesimal time interval. In fact, in the case of models with a single degree of freedom (i.e.
N = 1), it was demonstrated in [30] that the entire dynamics can be re-organised in terms of gtt.
As mentioned above, in many applications, we try to determine the model C through time-
series sampling in the form of measuring the probability distribution at certain time intervals
dt.
These measurements produce a discrete statistical model bC which consists of sampled
probabilities bpµ ∈[0, 1] at discrete points in time. These probabilities are generally subject
to noise and therefore differ from the pµ(t) that we wish to determine.
This prevents us
from accurately capturing the dynamics of the system: the difference between the (sampled)
probabilities at two different times is now superimposed by the statistical noise. Therefore,
in this work, we first quantify the impact of sampling on the Fisher information: we assume
that bp(t) at any time t has been obtained as a sequence of n ∈N independent and identically
distributed (i.i.d.) random data points (based on the probability distribution p(t)). In other
words, we assume that the probability for a specific sampling to be returned is a multinomial
distribution, which for large enough n can be analysed using methods in information theory
and statistics (such as the method of types [31–33, 21] or large deviation theory [34–36, 15]).
Indeed, from a geometric perspective, the probability to find bp is strongly localised around p(t)
and depends on the distance to the latter (in the sense of the Shahshahani-metric). Following
a proposal in [1] (see eq.(3.1)), we extract gtt(t) from the discrete sampling data bp(t+dt/2) and

In order to illustrate our theoretical computations, we demonstrate them at the hand of
a simple dynamical system. Inspired by epidemiological applications, we consider a simple
compartmental model [38, 39] that groups a fixed population into different classes with regards
to their epidemiological status with respect to a number N + 1 of pathogens. Their infectivity

with the overall status of the population (concretely the number of healthy individuals) the
couplings dµ for each variant. Despite its simplicity, the model allows to numerically verify our
theoretical computations and demonstrates that clustering is indeed an effective way to improve
the accuracy of reproducing the Fisher information extracted from time-series samplings. The
numerical model also allows us to address another question, namely how to choose the clustering
itself directly from the sampled data. Indeed, we provide numerical indications that an optimal
clustering can reliably be formed when combining sampled data from more points in time in the
discrete model bC. This suggests that even more information can be extracted from the latter
by making better use of the time series sampling of the data. We will discuss highly effective
techniques of this type in the companion paper [37].
This paper is organised as follows: Section 2 introduces and reviews important notions of
information theory and in particular builds upon geometric notions that have been scattered in
the literature to link distances between points of a statistical model C to the Fisher information,
in particular in the presence of statistical noise. Section 3 quantifies this notion by calculating
the expectation value (and variance) of the Fisher information computed from the discrete,
sampled statistical model bC. Section 4 introduces the clustering of degrees of freedom as a
means of reducing the bias for the Fisher information obtained from a time-series sampling
procedure, while minimising the loss of information.
It notably calculates the expectation
value of the Fisher information for a clustered version of the statistical model bC and provides
further results related to the expectation value of the sampled change of information per degree
of freedom. These results are relevant in gauging whether clusterings that minimise the loss of
information can be obtained directly from the sampled data. Section 5 illustrates our finding
for a simple compartmental model and provides first numerical indications that the clustering
of sampled data can be improved by taking into account more than two sampling points of
bC. Section 6 contains our conclusions and an outlook for future work, notably the companion
paper [37]. This paper is supplemented by two appendices, which contain details on technical
computations, as well as further background material on statistics.

Before closing this Section, we would like to stress an important aspect of our results com-
pared to other approaches in the literature. From a certain perspective, we are comparing
the sampled statistical model bC with an underlying theoretical model C. There are numerous
discussions of mutual information and mutual entropy between two probability distributions
(see e.g. [47–49] for a direct application in neurology and [50] for a general review). A broader
framework for capturing mutual information in this sense is the method of sieves (see [51]).
Such approaches establish how different bC is from C at a given time t. More concretely, such
approaches discuss estimators that provide certain static distances between these models (sim-
ilar to our discussion in Section 2.3.2). In the current Section, we have provided a way to
estimate the difference of the dynamics described by the statistical models. That is, rather
than analysing a (Riemannian) distance between to points of the curves bC and C, we have
analysed the Fisher information as a way to describe how far one is moving along the respec-
tive curves after an infinitesimal shift in time t →t + dt. This requires to compare the two
curves at (at least) two different points and therefore average over multiple samplings. This
also explains, why our results are not a trivial application of the Central Limit Theorem (see
Appendix A). Notice, however, that our final results (3.12) and (3.15) show the same order of
magnitude, since in many examples the bias and variance of (mutual) entropies is found to be
of order O(1/n) (see e.g. [52, 53]).
4
Clustering
Given the bias (3.12) for the Fisher information as a measure for the dynamical change along the
model C, reconstructing the latter from a time-series of samples can be difficult depending on
the available data. Indeed (3.12) (along with (3.15)) allows to quantify how much information
that is recovered from the sampling is due to statistical noise of the data-taking process and
unrelated to the actual dynamics of the underlying system. Geometrically, since gtt(t) measures
how far the system moves along the curve C during the time evolution t →t+dt, the result (3.12)
determines how much this motion is superposed by statistical noise from the measuring process.
In situations where dynamical processes are reconstructed from analysing a finite amount of
data, it is important to understand how much information can actually be recovered: how much
of the model C can be reconstructed from a given discrete model bC?
In this Section, we shall analyse this question from the perspective of effective degrees of
freedom that can (reliably) be described from bC. Indeed, if the bias (3.12) is too large, it is not
possible to correctly capture the temporal evolution of all N + 1 degrees of freedom. However,
the fact that the leading term in (3.12) is proportional to N, suggests that this bias can be
lowered if the dynamics of a smaller number of effective degrees of freedom is considered. From
an information theoretical perspective, this corresponds to the clustering of the probability
distribution.
In the following, we shall describe such a ’coarse-graining’ approach from an
information geometric perspective.

Conclusions
In this paper, we study the impact of statistical noise on the sampled time-series of dynamical
systems.
Using the framework of information geometry, we interpret statistical models (as

where N denotes the number of degrees of freedom of the system. A key quantity in describing

reduction in the bias due to the clustering of the dynamical system. Moreover, we are able to link
the changes in the (sampled) Fisher information to quantities pertaining either to the underlying
physical system or the sampling process respectively. This allows to gauge in concrete situations
what level of coarse graining of the system is required to describe the dynamics adequately in
view of a finite amount of data.
