Abstract
We obtain better algorithms for computing more balanced orientations and degree splits
in Local. Important to our result is a connection to the hypergraph sinkless orientation
problem [BMN+25, SODA’25]. We design an algorithm of complexity O(ε−1 · log n) for
computing a balanced orientation with discrepancy at most ε·deg(v) for every vertex v ∈V .
This improves upon a previous result by [GHK+20, Distrib. Comput. 2020] of complexity
O(ε−1·log ε−1·(log log ε−1)1.71·log n). Further, we show that this result can also be extended
to compute undirected degree splits with the same discrepancy and in the same runtime.
As an application we show that (3/2 + ε)∆-edge coloring can now be solved in O(ε−1 ·
log2 ∆· log n + ε−2 · log n) rounds in Local. Note that for constant ε and ∆= O(2log1/3 n)
this runtime matches the current state-of-the-art for (2∆−1)-edge coloring in [GK21].

Introduction
In this work, we present improved distributed algorithms for degree splitting problems in the
LOCAL model. As a corollary we also obtain faster algorithms for classic edge coloring problems.
The LOCAL model [Lin92].
We work in the standard LOCAL model of distributed com-
putation.
The network is represented as a graph, where each node hosts a processor and
communication proceeds in synchronous rounds. In each round, nodes can exchange arbitrarily
large messages with their neighbors and perform unbounded local computation. The complexity
of an algorithm is measured solely by the number of communication rounds.
Degree splitting.
Degree splitting problems are among the most basic problems in dis-
tributed graph algorithms that resist straightforward greedy solutions. Despite their simple
formulation, they have received considerable attention due to both their intrinsic combinatorial
interest and their applications, e.g., [BFH+16, GS17, BGK+19, GHK+20, BKK+23, HMN22,
Dav23, BKP25, BMN+25]. In the undirected degree splitting problem, the task is to color the
edges of a graph with two colors, red and blue, such that the discrepancy between the number
of incident red and blue edges at every node is minimized. The objective in the directed degree
splitting problem is to orient the edges of a graph such that for each node the difference between
its number of incoming and outgoing edges is minimized.
Which discrepancy is possible?
From a centralized perspective, the problem admits a
simple solution via an Euler tour argument, and more generally it connects to classical discrep-
ancy theory [Cha00]. By adding a dummy vertex to connect odd-degree nodes and traversing
an Euler tour, alternating the colors along the tour and removing the dummy vertex yields a
discrepancy of at most 2. In the directed variant, orienting edges along such a tour even gives
a discrepancy of at most 1. In the distributed setting, however, the problem becomes substan-
tially more challenging. The global structure exploited by standard centralized arguments is not
directly accessible, and known distributed approaches rely on more involved techniques such as
augmenting paths [GS17] or bootstrapping techniques [GHK+20] for amplifying balanced ori-
entations.
Degree splittings in the bigger scope of distributed complexity theory.
Although
degree splitting problems are easy to state and well understood centrally, they capture a fun-
damental source of difficulty in distributed computation.
The necessity of global coordina-
tion inherent in these problems can be formalized: each admits a logarithmic lower bound
on the round complexity, which holds even for constant-degree graphs [CKP19, BGK+19]. In
contrast to the classical ’big four’ problems—maximal independent set, maximal matching,
(∆+ 1)-vertex coloring, and (2∆−1)-edge coloring—which admit O(log∗n)-round algorithms
on constant-degree graphs and whose partial solutions can always be locally extended to full
ones [Lin92, PR01], degree splittings fall into a qualitatively different regime. They are closely
tied to the Lov´asz Local Lemma (LLL) [CP19, BGK+19, HMN22, Dav23, BKP25], which cap-
tures the task of simultaneously avoiding a family of locally dependent bad events—for in-
stance, that a node’s discrepancy exceeds a prescribed threshold. The distributed complexity
of problems in the LLL regime is central to the landscape of locally checkable labeling problems
and underlies many recent advances in sublogarithmic and randomized distributed algorithms
[GKM17, BHK+18, GHK18, CKP19, CP19, Cha20, BBOS20, BBOS21, GRB22]. At the same
time, obtaining a sharp understanding of the distributed complexity of LLL-type problems
remains a major open direction in the field.

Degree splitting and the logarithmic time complexity barrier.
The logarithmic barrier
already appears in the sinkless orientation problem, which can be interpreted as a directed
degree splitting with discrepancy max(deg(v) −1, 2). The problem is known to have a Θ(log n)
round complexity on general graphs [CKP19, GS17], making it one of the few natural problems
with a tight intermediate complexity on general graphs. The same logarithmic lower bound
extends to weak (undirected) degree splitting [BGK+19] where the goal is to color edges red
and blue so that each node has at least one edge of each color.
Thus, the Θ(log n) barrier already arises for the largest meaningful discrepancy. Conse-
quently, the goal is not to circumvent this barrier but to understand the trade-offs within it,
namely how the round complexity depends on the allowed discrepancy. This dependence is
crucial in recursive divide-and-conquer applications, where one requires discrepancy ε · deg(v)
with ε = o(1) and the overall efficiency hinges on how the runtime scales with ε.
1.1
Our Contributions
Our main contribution is a faster and conceptually simpler algorithm for computing both di-
rected and undirected degree splittings.
Theorem 1. For every ε > 0, there is a deterministic Local algorithm with round complexity
O(ε−1 · log n) that computes an (un)directed degree splitting of any n-vertex graph such that the
discrepancy at each vertex v of degree deg(v) is at most ε · deg(v) + 1 if deg(v) is odd and at
most ε · deg(v) + 2 if deg(v) is even.
Our algorithm is conceptually simpler than the augmenting path-based approach of [GS17]
and the bootstrapping framework of [GHK+20]. As a consequence, we obtain a clean runtime
bound of O(ε−1 · log n), improving upon the O(ε−1 log ε−1(log log ε−1)1.71 · log n) bound of
[GHK+20]. For ε = 1/∆our algorithm runs in O(∆· log n) time and obtains an almost optimal
discrepancy. Lowering the discrepancy further cannot be expected from an efficient algorithm,
as computing a perfect split for even-degree nodes already incurs an Ω(n) lower bound via a
reduction from 2-coloring on cycles.
Theorem 2. For every ε > 0, there is a randomized Local algorithm with round complexity
O(ε−1 log ε−1 · log log n) that computes an (un)directed degree splitting of any n-vertex graph
such that the discrepancy at each vertex v of degree deg(v) is at most ε · deg(v) + 1 if deg(v) is
odd and at most ε · deg(v) + 2 if deg(v) is even.
The improved dependence on the discrepancy is particularly useful in applications with a
recursive divide-and-conquer structure. As a corollary, we obtain the following result on edge
coloring with few colors. The algorithm repeatedly applies Theorem 1 to partition the graph
into subgraphs of progressively smaller maximum degree, assigning disjoint color palettes to
each part. Once the degrees become sufficiently small, a separate procedure is used to complete
the coloring.
Corollary 3. For every ε > 0, there is a deterministic Local algorithm with complexity
O(ε−1 · log2 ∆· log n + ε−2 · log n) to edge color any (multi)-graph with maximum degree ∆with
(3/2 + ε)∆colors.
Note that 3/2∆colors is a natural threshold for coloring multi-graphs—according to Shan-
non’s Theorem [Sha49] this is an upper bound on the chromatic index of any multigraph. This
bound is tight as witnessed by the family of Shannon multigraphs [Viz65]. Our result improves
upon the previous state-of-the-art of O(ε−2 · log2 ∆· poly log log ∆· log n) rounds in [BMN+25].
Note that for constant ε and ∆= O(2log1/3 n) it even matches the state-of-the-art for the greedily
solvable (2∆−1)-edge coloring problem [GK21].

Limitations for Degree Splitting Algorithms.
We have already discussed the inher-
ent logarithmic barrier for degree splitting problems.
Our algorithm achieves a runtime of
O(ε−1 log n) for a discrepancy of ε · deg(v) at every vertex v ∈V . The first distributed algo-
rithm for degree splitting [GS17] was based on augmenting paths. We prove that the O(ε−1 log n)
bound is tight for na¨ıve augmenting-path–based approaches, even though our own algorithm
does not rely on augmenting paths. Formally, we show a mending radius lower bound. This
quantity measures, when extending a given partial solution to a new element of the graph,
whether the extension can always be performed without changing the partial solution far from
where it is being extended [BHM+22].
Theorem 4. Computing a directed degree splitting with discrepancy at most ε·∆at each vertex
has mending radius Ω(ε−1 · log n).
Open Problem I: Optimality of the runtime.
Our algorithms achieve a round complexity
of O(ε−1 log n) for a local discrepancy of ε · deg(v). Is this dependence optimal? In particular,
can one improve the ε−1 factor, or obtain a bound in which the discrepancy-dependent term
appears only additively in the runtime? More generally, does a matching lower bound hold
beyond the limitations of augmenting path-based techniques?
Open Problem II: Multi-way splitting.
Our edge-coloring results rely on recursively ap-
plying Theorem 1 in a divide-and-conquer fashion, yielding a k-way partition at a cost of
O(ε−1 log2 k log n) rounds. Is there an O(log n/ε)-round algorithm that directly partitions the
edges into k = ω(1) parts such that each node v has deg(v)/k±ε∆edges in each part? Achieving
such a bound would remove the additional log2 k overhead incurred by recursive splitting.

Related Work
Degree splitting
The first authors to consider degree splits in the distributed setting were
Han´ckowiak, Karo´nski and Panconesi [HKP01]. They used degree splits as a subroutine for
their breakthrough result of designing the first polylogarithmic deterministic distributed max-
imal matching algorithm.
However, to achieve this result they had to work with a relaxed
version of the problem, allowing a 1/poly log n fraction of nodes to have arbitrary splits. The
first algorithm solving the degree splitting problem in full generality only came much later, when
Ghaffari and Su released their influential paper [GS17]. The paper is best-known for its seminal
sinkless orientation algorithm. Nevertheless, it also shows how to compute more balanced orien-
tations, as well as undirected splits. Surprisingly, their splitting algorithms do not use sinkless
orientation as a subroutine—instead, they rely on an augmenting-path based approach. As a
result, they obtain a deterministic directed degree splitting algorithm in O(log7 n/ε3) rounds
and a randomized counterpart in O(log4 n/ε3) rounds. For the undirected setting, they obtain
a deterministic algorithm of complexity O(log7 n/ε3) for a sufficiently large ∆and randomized
algorithm of complexity O(∆2 log4 n/ε2), which works for all ∆. All of their algorithms com-
pute a balanced split with discrepancy at most ε · ∆. The next improvement, and the current
state-of-the-art, comes from [GHK+20], which leverages the sinkless orientation algorithm by
[GS17] to amplify balanced orientation.
Their deterministic algorithm relies on a recursive
bootstrapping procedure and computes both directed and undirected splits with a discrepancy
of ε·deg(v) in O(ε−1 log ε−1(log log ε−1)1.71 ·log n) rounds. Most recently, [BKP25] further gen-
eralized the above work by providing an algorithm for mixed directed and undirected problem
with the same discrepancy. Moreover, they reduce the additive constant in the discrepancy for
undirected splits from 4 down to just 1 for odd-degree vertices and 2 for even-degree vertices.
In [HMN22] a different kind of splitting is considered. They partition the vertices of a graph
into k parts V1, . . . , Vk such that for each i ∈[k], each node v ∈V has (deg(v)+ε∆)/k neighbors

(Hypergraph) sinkless orientation.
The sinkless orientation problem is arguably the sim-
plest problem of complexity Θ(log∆n) in the Local model of distributed computing, on ∆-
regular graphs.
A lower bound of Ω(log∆log n) on the randomized complexity was shown
in [BFH+16] and an algorithm matching the corresponding lower bound was promptly given in
[GS17]. The Ω(log∆n) lower bound for deterministic algorithms for sinkless orientation (and
hence also for directed degree splitting) was later simplified [BKK+23] and extended to the
stronger supported LOCAL model, in which the communication graph G is a subgraph of a
graph H known to all nodes. This bound on the randomized complexity was later extended to
an Ω(log∆n) lower bound on the deterministic complexity of the problem [CKP19] matching
the complexity of the deterministic algorithm in [GS17].
Recently, it has been shown that the generalization of the sinkless orientation problem to
hypergraphs, i.e., the hypergraph sinkless orientation (HSO) problem, also has an Θ(log n)
intermediate complexity [BMN+25]. In this work, we will use HSO as a major subroutine to
design efficient degree splitting algorithms with small discrepancies. In the HSO problem, one is
given a hypergraph, and the goal is to orient each hyperedge toward one of its incident vertices
so that no vertex becomes a sink. The deterministic HSO algorithm from [BMN+25], works
under the condition that the minimum degree δ of the hypergraph exceeds its maximum rank
r by a constant factor. More detailed, it runs in time O(logδ/r n).

1.3
Our Algorithm in a Nutshell
For simplicity’s sake, suppose G = (V, E) is a ∆-regular graph and focus on the directed version.
Split and orient.
For each vertex v ∈V , we create ⌊∆/2⌋virtual copies of degree 2 and pos-
sibly one degree-1 node if ∆is odd. For directed degree splitting from a centralized perspective
we can then simply orient all paths and cycles consistently. The discrepancy at a vertex v ∈V
can then be bounded by the number of paths originating at a copy of v. Hence, the discrepancy
will be 0 for nodes of even degree and 1 for nodes of odd degree.
From centralized to distributed
The main bottleneck with this approach is that con-
sistently orienting paths—for the sake of this overview assume that the split graph has no
cycles—in a distributed manner has an overhead that is proportional to the length of the path.
The natural solution is to chop each very long path into shorter ones.

Why is standard symmetry breaking not enough?
A commonly used tool to break
long paths into short ones in distributed models are ruling sets [CP19, Cha20, Cha24]. Ruling
sets are a relaxed notion of maximal independent sets and come with a parameter k, which
prescribes the maximal distance that any vertex can have to the ruling set. Hence, by chopping
the path at every ruling set vertex, we can obtain a sequence of shorter paths of maximum
length k. However, these chops come at a price. At every chopping point we introduce new
start/endpoints of shorter paths and as a result there is no coordination between the orientation
of these paths. As a result, the discrepancy of the vertex of G hosting the split may increase
by an additive two for every chopping point that it hosts. Since the classic symmetry breaking
tools do not come equipped with any kind of load balancing functionalities, it may happen that
we are left with a very imbalanced split for some unlucky vertices.
Balancing chopping points via HSO
Ensuring that we do not chop at too many virtual
copies of the same vertex can be seen as a resource allocation problem. The players in this
problem are the nodes in the ruling set R, which want to chop the path in their vicinity. The
resources are the vertices V of G that offer the chopping points. In order to limit the discrepancy
at each node, each vertex v ∈V divides its virtual copies into x buckets of approximately equal
sizes. In each bucket, we allow at most one chop. This yields a discrepancy of at most 2x for
even-degree vertices and 2x + 1 for odd-degree vertices. To each node r in the ruling set, we
associate a subpath P(r) of length ℓ. Now we set up a hypergraph sinkless orientation instance
H. Each node r ∈R will model a vertex in H. Each bucket B1(v), . . . , Bx(v) of each vertex
v ∈V will model a hyperedge. The hyperedge corresponding to a bucket B contains all ruling
set nodes, where the subpath P(r) contains at least one node of the bucket. The maximum
rank of H is then easily bounded by ∆/x. Controlling the minimum degree is a bit more tricky,
since a subpath might contain multiple nodes from the same bucket aka hyperedge. Hence, we
will need to construct subpaths containing nodes from at least 2∆/x different buckets. Under
this condition, HSO can be solved efficiently to determine where to chop paths such each vertex
of the graph is at the start/end of few paths effectively limiting the discrepancy.
Towards optimal discrepancy.
The outlined approach results in a discrepancy of 2x + 1.
This is perfectly satisfactory if we set x = ε · ∆. However, for very precisely balanced splits,
where we want to set x = 1, we would like to get the discrepancy for odd nodes down to just
the optimal value of one. To achieve this, we compute a sinkless orientation on another graph,
where we merge the three degree-one copies of odd-degree vertices into one virtual node of
degree three. A sinkless orientation on this graph finally then the discrepancy from three down
to one.
Extension to undirected splitting.
For the unbalanced splitting problem we do not aim to
orient paths consistently but to color them red/blue in an alternating manner. Here, additional
care has to be taken with short cycles, e.g., a cycle of length three does not allow for a red/blue
coloring that is balanced for all of its vertices. Hence, we perform a preprocessing on the graph
that effectively changes the way the graph is split to remove all short cycles. Long cycles are
not a problem because we will chop them in the middle anyway and thus we can deal with them
just like long paths.

2.2
Problem Definitions and Subroutines
Definition 5 (Directed Degree Splitting). Given a graph with maximum degree ∆, orient each
edge such that each node has in-degree and out-degree at most (1 + ε)∆/2, for some ε ≥0.
Definition 6 (Undirected Degree Splitting). Given a graph with maximum degree ∆, color each
edge red or blue such that each node has at most (1+ε)∆/2 edges in each color, for some ε ≥0.
Definition 7 (Sinkless Orientation). In the Sinkless Orientation problem, the goal is to orient
all edges such that every node has at least one outgoing edge. Put differently, the graph is free
of sinks.
Sinkless orientation stated in this form can actually be potentially unsolvable, or solvable
with a very high complexity, on some graphs (notably paths and cycles). As such, the following
problem is often considered instead.
Definition 8 (min-degree-δ Sinkless Orientation). In the min-degree-δ Sinkless Orientation
problem, the goal is to orient all edges such that every node of degree δ or higher is not a sink.
For δ ≥3, min-degree-δ Sinkless Orientation has deterministic complexity Θ(logδ n) and
randomized complexity Θ(logδ log n) on general graphs in Local. In particular, Sinkless Ori-
entation has complexity Θ(log∆n) and Θ(log∆log n) on the graph class of ∆-regular graphs,
for ∆≥3.
Lemma 9 (Sink- and Sourceless Orientation [GHK+20, Corollary 3.2]). A min-degree-δ sink-
and source-less orientation for δ ≥3 can be computed in O(logδ n) rounds of deterministic
Local and with high probability in O(logδ log n) rounds of randomized Local.
Definition 10 (Hypergraph sinkless orientation (HSO) [BMN+25]). The objective of the hy-
pergraph sinkless orientation problem (HSO) is to orient the hyperedges of a hypergraph H such
that every vertex has at least one outgoing hyperedge, where a hyperedge is outgoing for exactly
one of its incident nodes and incoming for all others.
Theorem 11 (deterministic HSO [BMN+25, Theorem 1.4]). There is a deterministic Local
algorithm of complexity O(logδ/r n) for computing a hypergraph sinkless orientation on any
n-vertex multihypergraph H with maximum rank r and minimum degree δ > r.
Definition 12 ((α, β)-ruling set). For two integers α, β ≥1, an (α, β)-ruling set is a set of
nodes S ⊆V such that the distance between any two nodes in S is at least α and any node
v ∈V has a distance of at most β to the closest node in S.
Ruling sets generalize maximal independent sets, which are (2, 1)-ruling sets. In this paper,
we will use the following ruling set algorithm obtained by combining results from [SEW13] and
[GV07].

Directed Degree Splitting
In this section we describe our improved Local algorithm for balanced directed degree splits
that is obtained through a connection with the hypergraph sinkless orientation (HSO) problem.
Theorem 1. For every ε > 0, there is a deterministic Local algorithm with round complexity
O(ε−1 · log n) that computes an (un)directed degree splitting of any n-vertex graph such that the
discrepancy at each vertex v of degree deg(v) is at most ε · deg(v) + 1 if deg(v) is odd and at
most ε · deg(v) + 2 if deg(v) is even.
Setting ε = 1/∆we obtain the following corollary.
Corollary 14. There is a deterministic Local algorithm of complexity O(∆· log n) that com-
putes an orientation of the edges in any graph G on n nodes such that the discrepancy at each
vertex v is at most 2 if deg(v) is even and at most 1 if deg(v) is odd.
This improves on a previous result of complexity O(∆· log ∆· (log log ∆)1.71 · log n) for the
same problem by [GHK+20].
3.1
High-Level Overview
Before we describe our algorithm, it is useful to revisit a simple corollary about balanced
orientations from sink- and sourceless orientations. The proof of this statement can be found
in [GHK+20], but it is simple enough to restate it here.
Corollary 15. Let s ≥3 be an integer. An orientation where each node v of degree deg(v)
has at least ⌊deg(v)/s⌋outgoing and incoming edges can be computed in O(logs n) rounds of
deterministic Local and with high probability in O(logs log n) rounds of randomized Local.
Proof. For each vertex v of degree deg(v) we create ⌊deg(v)/s⌋copies and divide the edges
incident to v evenly among the copies.
The virtual graph obtained this way has minimum
degree s. Hence, we can apply Lemma 9 to obtain a sink- and sourceless orientation of this
virtual graph within O(logs n) rounds deterministically or O(logs log n) rounds randomized.
Lifting this orientation back to G, we get that each vertex v has at least ⌊deg(v)/s⌋incoming
and outgoing edges.
Since sinkless orientation only guarantees an outgoing edge for nodes of degree at least three,
the discrepancy achievable with this approach is capped at deg(v)/3. Hence, in order to go below
this barrier, we need to adopt a more aggressive splitting strategy. We split each vertex into
virtual copies of degree 2 (with one additional degree-one copy for odd-degree vertices). Our
splitting strategy comes with the clear upside that it is now very easy to compute a near-perfect
split from a centralized perspective. All nodes on the same path/cycle decide on a consistent
orientation of the path/cycle. This procedure clearly produces an orientation where no node
of degree two is a source or a sink.
Since the edges of the split graph are in a one-to-one
correspondence with the edges of G, we can immediately lift the orientation back to G. All
even-degree vertices are thus perfectly split, while for odd-degree vertices we get an unavoidable
discrepancy of one.
However, there is also a very clear downside of this approach.
Without any additional
modifications, it is a very inefficient distributed algorithm. The maximum length of a path in
the split graph might be linear in the number of edges and even the weak diameter in G might

such a split graph might take up to Ω(n) rounds in Local.
Therefore, we need to construct a split graph, where each path or cycle has a small weak

merging together, they form a rooted tree if we put a directed edge from C to C′ in the set
whenever C′ = Tar(C). Executing the merge operations sequentially, from root to leaves, we
progressively grow the root cycle with each merge until a single cycle remains, spanning the
virtual nodes of all cycles in the rooted tree.
Next, we argue that the weak diameter of New(S) is at least d(S).
This is clear, if
Ck = Max(S). If Ck ̸= Max(S), let i⋆< k be the smallest i such that Max(Si⋆) ̸= Max(S).
Since Max(Si⋆) beats Max(S) either in terms of true length or tiebreaks we must have that
dist(Max(Si⋆), S) > ℓ. Since f is monotonically increasing along the chain, this implies that
the chain can never terminate at a cycle C with dist(S, C) ≤ℓ. Hence, the weak diameter of
New(S) is at least ℓ≥d(S).
If we are in the second case, we are already done, as the weak diameter is always a lower
bound for the true length of a cycle. Now suppose there still exists a short cycle S, where the true
length of New(S) is less than ℓ. Unless the entire graph itself has small diameter (trivializing
all our problems), there must be two cycles C and Ci such that dist(C, Ci) = 0 such that
Ci ⊆New(S) and but C ̸⊆New(S), i.e., C has not been merged with S. Since Tar(C) ̸= Ci and
Tar(Ci) ̸= Tar(C), we have that Max(Ci) = Max(S). Since dist(Max(S), C) ≤2ℓ, this implies
that dist(S, Max(Ci)) > 2ℓ. Hence, after joining New(S) with New(C), we get a cycle with
weak diameter at least ℓ(or a path, if New(C) is a path). Importantly, since dist(S, Si) < ℓ,
this implies that New(C) already had a weak diameter of at least ℓon its own. Since this step
only joins cycles of true length less than ℓto cycles of weak diameter at least ℓ(or paths), we
get that our graph of cycle joins remains acyclic and we never execute a cycle join on two virtual
nodes that are part of the same cycle.

Mending Radius Lower Bound
Before defining the mending radius, let us formally define what we mean by a partial solution
to a problem.
For simplicity, we only give the definition of a partial solution for directed
splitting. For the more general definition that applies to all locally checkable labeling problems,
see [BHM+22].
For a graph G = (V, E), we represent a partial orientation of the edges of that graph as an
assignment ψ : E →{−, +, ⊥}. For an edge e ∈E, ψ(e) = ⊥corresponds to e being unoriented.
For an edge e = uv ∈E, with u < v (i.e., v has a larger identifier than u), ψ(uv) = + represents
that the edge is oriented from u to v. Conversely ψ(uv) = −indicates that the edge is oriented
in the opposite way, from v to u. When ψ(e) ̸= ⊥for all e ∈E, ψ is said to be a full orientation.
We say that a partial solution ψ′ extends another partial solution ψ if for each e ∈E, either
ψ′(e) = ⊥=⇒ψ(e) = ⊥or ψ(e) ̸= ⊥=⇒ψ(e) = ψ′(e). For two solution ψ and ψ′, their
difference ψ△ψ′ is the set of edges {e ∈E | ψ(e) ̸= ψ′(e)}.

lower or greater than that of v.
