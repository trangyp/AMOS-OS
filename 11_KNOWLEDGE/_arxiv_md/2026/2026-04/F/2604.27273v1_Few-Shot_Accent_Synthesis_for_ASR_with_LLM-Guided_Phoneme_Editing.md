Abstract
Accented automatic speech recognition (ASR) often de-
grades due to the limited availability of accented training data.
Prior work has explored accent modeling in low-resource set-
tings, but existing approaches typically require minutes to hours
of labeled speech, which may still be impractical for truly scarce
accent scenarios. We propose a pipeline that adapts a text-to-
speech (TTS) decoder to a target-accent speaker using fewer
than ten reference utterances and employs large language model
(LLM)-based phoneme editing to generate accent-conditioned
pronunciations. The resulting synthetic speech is used to fine-
tune a self-supervised ASR model. Experiments demonstrate
consistent word error rate (WER) reductions on real accented
speech, including cross-speaker evaluation and ultra-low data
regimes. A matched-rate random phoneme baseline shows that
phoneme-space perturbation itself is a strong form of augmenta-
tion, while LLM-guided edits provide additional gains through
accent-conditioned structure.
Index Terms: accented ASR, synthetic dataset, few-shot adap-
tation, LLM-based phoneme editing
1. Introduction
Modern automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems perform
well on high-resource speech varieties but often degrade on
regional and non-native accents. Accent-related performance
gaps have been documented in bias analyses and benchmarks,
motivating the development of more robust and inclusive speech
technologies [1, 2, 3]. Existing approaches to accent robust-
ness broadly fall into two categories. Model-centric methods
incorporate accent embeddings, adversarial objectives, or meta-
learning to improve cross-accent generalization [4, 5, 6, 7].
Data-centric approaches instead augment training data using
voice conversion or text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis [8, 9, 10].
While these methods improve robustness, they typically rely on
minutes to hours of accented speech to model a target accent
effectively [11, 12, 10]. In many practical scenarios, however,
only a handful of accented utterances may be available. Mod-
eling accents in such few-shot settings is challenging because
accent cues are distributed across multiple linguistic and acous-
tic levels and are entangled with speaker identity [13, 14, 15].
Accent variation affects segmental phoneme realizations as well
as suprasegmental and acoustic patterns. Prior work has ap-
proached this problem in different ways.
Multi-accent TTS
systems explicitly learn local and global accent representations
[16]. Other approaches incorporate explicit pronunciation rules
or phoneme-level variation [17, 18].
Alternatively, multilin-
gual TTS systems induce accented English by leveraging cross-
lingual phonetic and prosodic structure [19, 20]. However, these
strategies often require substantial accented data, carefully de-
signed rules, or multilingual resources, which may not be avail-
able for low-resource regional or sociolectal varieties.
In this work, we investigate whether a few-shot synthetic
data generation strategy can support accented ASR adaptation.
We propose a pipeline that adapts a phoneme-conditioned TTS
decoder, pretrained on Standard American English, to a target-
accent speaker using fewer than ten reference utterances, al-
lowing the decoder to learn accent-related acoustic biases that
influence the realization of phoneme–prosody inputs. A large
language model (LLM) performs accent-aware phoneme edit-
ing to generate accent-conditioned pronunciations, which are
rendered into synthetic speech and used to fine-tune a self-
supervised ASR model. To disentangle accent-conditioned edit-
ing from generic phoneme perturbation, we also introduce a
matched-rate random phoneme baseline. Evaluated on Indian
and Korean English, the proposed synthetic data improves WER
on real accented speech under cross-speaker evaluation and
ultra-low data regimes, while the random baseline shows that
phoneme-space perturbation is a strong but less accent-faithful
augmentation signal. Audio samples and additional materials
are available on the project page.1
2. Proposed Method
2.1. System Overview
An overview of the proposed pipeline is shown in Fig. 1. The
system takes a source speech waveform (Standard American
English in our experiments), its transcript, and a small set of
target-accent reference utterances.
We obtain phonemes via
grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) conversion and extract phoneme-
level prosody (duration, log-F0, energy) from the source wave-
form. An LLM edits the phoneme sequence to match target-
accent pronunciations while preserving phoneme–prosody
alignment; in-context examples consist of paired source and
target-accent phoneme–prosody sequences (Sec. 2.3). Target-
accent reference speech is encoded into speaker and style vec-
tors that are summed into a global conditioning vector for both
the phoneme encoder and acoustic decoder. The adapted de-
coder generates a mel-spectrogram, which a neural vocoder
converts to waveform audio.
2.2. Acoustic TTS Backbone and Few-Shot Adaptation
Our system builds upon the phoneme-conditioned TTS acous-
tic model proposed in [21].
We remove the learned local
prosody predictor and condition the decoder on externally ex-
tracted phoneme-level prosody (duration, pitch, energy) from
source speech, increasing prosodic variability and broadening

the synthetic data distribution. Learned speaker embeddings
are replaced by zero-shot speaker embeddings from reference
speech combined with utterance-level style embeddings; they
are projected, summed, and used to FiLM-condition both the
phoneme encoder and acoustic decoder. While these embed-

tics that correlate with accent realization. The original system
[21] relies on precomputed per-speaker pitch and energy statis-
tics, which are unavailable at inference time for unseen speakers
with limited reference data. We therefore normalize phoneme-
level prosodic features using statistics estimated from a small,
dynamically sampled subset of reference utterances per speaker
during training, reducing train–test mismatch. Speaker embed-
dings are computed from the same subset and averaged to obtain
a stable speaker representation, emphasizing speaker identity
over utterance-level variation.
The acoustic model is trained in two stages: pretraining and
few-shot accent-speaker adaptation. During pretraining, we re-
tain the reconstruction, adversarial disentanglement, and FiLM
regularization losses from [21], while removing losses associ-
ated with the original prosody prediction module. To ensure
adherence to externally provided prosody, we introduce frame-
level pitch and energy consistency losses. Target energy and
pitch contours are computed using the same extraction proce-
dures as in the prosody extraction block, without phoneme-
level aggregation.
Energy is derived directly from the pre-
dicted mel-spectrogram, while pitch is predicted by an auxiliary
network trained to estimate frame-level log-F0 from the mel-
spectrogram. A frame-level ℓ2 loss aligns predicted and target
contours in both cases.
In few-shot adaptation we fine-tune the decoder on the tar-
get speaker using mel reconstruction losses only and disable
auxiliary losses to stabilize low-data adaptation. After adap-
tation, the decoder is specialized to the target speaker and no
longer preserves zero-shot speaker generalization.
2.3. LLM-Based Pronunciation Editing
The LLM operates on phoneme sequences with aligned
phoneme-level prosody and edits pronunciations to reflect the
target accent while preserving phoneme–prosody correspon-
dence. Each input consists of a phoneme sequence together
with aligned duration (d), pitch (p), and energy (e) vectors at
the phoneme level.
Edits are constrained to insertion, dele-
tion, splitting, and merging under an alignment constraint to
ensure the output remains a valid, aligned phoneme sequence.
When structural edits modify sequence length, the correspond-
ing prosody is adjusted solely to maintain alignment; other-
wise, prosodic values are copied directly from the source. For
example, the word will, represented in ARPAbet as W IH1 L
| d:10,7,7 | p:5.3,5.3,5.2 | e:0.8,3.6,3.1 for a
Standard American realization, may be edited to the Indian-
English pronunciation V IH1 L, while retaining the same
(d, p, e) sequences because no structural edit occurs. We do
not attempt to transform prosody toward the target accent, but
only to preserve consistency with the edited phoneme sequence.
In-context examples are paired source and target-accent realiza-
tions represented as phoneme and prosody sequences: source
phonemes from G2P and source prosody from source speech,
target phonemes from user phonetic transcriptions and target
prosody from target-accent speech. Although target prosody is
included in the examples, prosody is not edited at inference ex-
cept when required to preserve alignment. To select informative
in-context examples, we rank candidate source–target pairs by
the ratio of target-to-source pitch standard deviation and prefer
pairs where the target exhibits greater pitch variability relative
to the source, as these examples tend to encode stronger accent
cues. To improve consistency in few-shot settings, the LLM is
instructed to explicitly reason about each phoneme-level change
it proposes. We also compute the overall phoneme change rate
in the examples and incorporate it into the prompt, instructing
the LLM to approximately match the observed level of phoneme
substitution while remaining free to deviate when required to
satisfy alignment and validity constraints.
3. Experimental Setup
3.1. Data and Speaker Roles
The backbone TTS model is pretrained on LJSpeech [22] and
the English subset of ESD [23], both Standard American En-
glish only. No accented speech is used during backbone pre-
training. Accented speech is drawn from L2-ARCTIC [24]; the
CLB speaker from CMU Arctic [25] provides matched Stan-
dard American source utterances. For LLM in-context prompt-
ing, target-accent phoneme sequences are obtained from the L2-
ARCTIC perceived canonical label (PCL) annotations, which
provide human-transcribed accented pronunciations. We evalu-
ate Indian English (speakers TNI, RRBI, SVBI) and Korean
English (speakers HKK, YDCK, YKWK). For each accent, one
adaptation speaker (TNI for Indian, HKK for Korean) provides
reference utterances for embedding extraction, decoder adap-
tation, LLM prompting, and synthetic data generation. Unless
otherwise stated, we use 10 utterances (about 36 seconds) se-
lected from a ranked pool of 15, and all reference utterances are
disjoint from evaluation data. All speakers within each accent
read the same set of prompts in L2-ARCTIC, enabling content-
matched cross-speaker evaluation.

3.2. Evaluation Setup
Acoustic evaluation uses 50 held-out synthesized utterances per
speaker (150 per accent). ASR fine-tuning uses 500 synthetic
utterances from the adaptation speaker and is evaluated on 500
real utterances per speaker with disjoint text. For mixed train-
ing (Real+Synth), we combine 500 synthetic utterances with the
corresponding number of real fine-tuning utterances.
3.3. Evaluation Metrics
Intelligibility of synthetic speech is measured using WER from
a fixed Whisper small model [26] as a proxy (not used for ASR
training). Naturalness is measured with UTMOS [27]. Accent
similarity (AccSim) uses a pretrained wav2vec2-based accent
classifier from SpeechBrain [28]: we compute speaker-specific
centroid embeddings from real evaluation utterances and aver-
age cosine similarity between each synthesized embedding and
all centroids.
3.4. System Variants
We compare American TTS (no adaptation or editing), Adapt-
only, Adapt+LLM, Adapt+Random phonemes, Adapt+GT
(oracle) using ground-truth accent phonemes and prosody,
Real speech, and Real+Synth, which combines real accented
speech with synthetic utterances generated by Adapt+LLM; the
American TTS condition is used only for acoustic evaluation.
Adapt+Random phonemes is a matched-rate control for LLM
editing: phoneme positions are sampled uniformly and replaced
with uniformly sampled phonemes from the inventory. The edit
rate is matched to the average LLM edit rate for each accent,
19% for Indian English and 35% for Korean English, isolat-
ing whether improvements come from accent-structured edits
or from generic phoneme perturbation.
3.5. Experiments
Experiment 1: Acoustic Accent Realization. We compare
acoustic quality across system variants for both accents, pooling
results across the three speakers of each accent.
Experiment 2: ASR Scaling.
For each accent, ASR mod-
els are fine-tuned on varying numbers of utterances (N
∈
[1, 500]). In the Real+Synth condition, N real utterances are
combined with a fixed set of 500 synthetic utterances generated
by Adapt+LLM. Evaluation is performed on the correspond-
ing adaptation speaker (TNI for Indian, HKK for Korean). For
each N, real utterances are randomly sampled from a fixed
pool, and results are averaged over 7 runs.
In addition, for
a subset of N = 96 utterances with available ground-truth
accented phonemes and prosody, we include an oracle com-
parison to contextualize the effect of random phoneme pertur-
bations. Specifically, we evaluate (i) ground-truth phonemes
with aligned source prosody (alignment handled using the
same LLM-based procedure without phoneme editing), and (ii)
ground-truth phonemes with ground-truth prosody obtained via
forced alignment. This subset is used only for analysis and is
not part of the main training pool.
Experiment 3: Cross-Speaker ASR Generalization.
For
each accent, ASR models fine-tuned on 500 synthetic utterances
from the adaptation speaker are evaluated on all speakers of the
same accent. Results are reported per speaker and averaged over
multiple runs.

4. Implementation Details
Acoustic Features and Vocoder. Mel-spectrograms use 80 mel
bins at 22,050 Hz, a 1024-point FFT, hop length 256, and mel
range 0–8 kHz. Waveforms are synthesized using the universal
HiFi-GAN vocoder [29].
Backbone TTS Training. The backbone is trained for 72k iter-
ations with Adam (batch size 128). The mel reconstruction loss
consists of ℓ1 and ℓ2 terms, each weighted by 1.0. Pitch and
energy consistency losses use weights λp = 1.0 and λe = 0.2,
and the FiLM regularization weight is set to 10−3. Pitch con-
sistency uses a lightweight 3-layer 1D convolutional network
(kernel size 3, hidden size 256) that predicts frame-level log-F0
from mel-spectrograms. Dynamic speaker statistics are com-
puted from randomly sampled subsets of M ∈{1, . . . , 15} ut-
terances per speaker and refreshed every 2500 training steps.
Few-Shot Accent-Speaker Adaptation. We fine-tune the en-
tire acoustic decoder for 600 steps (learning rate 0.001) using
mel reconstruction loss only; all auxiliary losses are disabled.
G2P, Alignment, and Prosody Extraction. Text-to-phoneme
conversion and phoneme durations use Montreal Forced
Aligner (MFA) [30] English resources with CMU Pronouncing
Dictionary lookup and MFA’s pretrained English G2P model as
fallback. Frame-level pitch is extracted from waveforms using
REAPER [31], and energy is computed from mel-spectrogram
magnitude.
Duration, pitch, and energy are aggregated to
phoneme level for conditioning.
LLM Configuration. For Adapt+LLM, phoneme edits and any
required prosody alignment adjustments are generated using an
OpenAI GPT-5.1 model [32] at inference time via prompt de-
sign and in-context examples. For oracle aligned-prosody anal-
ysis, the same LLM-based alignment procedure is used without
phoneme editing.
ASR Model and Training.
All ASR experiments use the
wav2vec 2.0 Base model pretrained on 960 hours of English
speech. Models are fine-tuned with CTC loss for 15 epochs
using AdamW (3 × 10−5); the convolutional feature extractor
is frozen while the remaining parameters are fine-tuned, and
SpecAugment is disabled.

5. Results
5.1. Acoustic Accent Realization
Table 1 summarizes acoustic results. Adaptation substantially
increases accent similarity (AccSim) relative to the American
TTS baseline: from 0.27 to 0.69 for Indian and from 0.32 to
0.61 for Korean. This indicates that adaptation induces accent-
specific acoustic biases that influence both global spectral char-
acteristics and the local realization of phoneme–prosody inputs.
The trade-off is reduced UTMOS, which may reflect both adap-
tation artifacts under limited data and potential bias of natural-
ness predictors toward standard-accent speech.
Effect of phoneme editing. For Indian English, LLM-based
phoneme editing further increases AccSim (0.69→0.72) with
a moderate increase in synthetic-speech WER. For Korean En-
glish, however, Adapt+LLM does not increase AccSim and sub-
stantially degrades acoustic WER. Inspection of the edits re-
veals a higher phoneme substitution density for Korean, reflect-
ing the change patterns present in the selected in-context exam-
ples. Under these more aggressive symbolic edits, the adapted
decoder struggles to maintain intelligibility. The matched-rate
random baseline further shows that arbitrary phoneme perturba-
tions severely degrade synthetic-speech intelligibility, reaching
47.2% WER for Indian English and 93.4% for Korean English.
Consistently, the oracle condition, which provides ground-truth
accented phonemes and prosody, only modestly exceeds Adapt-
only in AccSim. Together, these results suggest that the adapted

5.2. ASR Fine-Tuning with Synthetic Speech
Figure 2 reports ASR WER as a function of fine-tuning budget
N. Training on synthetic speech improves ASR across bud-
gets. However, the matched-rate random baseline shows that a
substantial part of this gain comes from phoneme-space per-
turbation itself: Adapt+Random phonemes often approaches
Adapt+LLM, especially for Indian English at larger N. At the
same time, Adapt+LLM remains consistently better than the
random baseline, with a larger average gap for Korean English,
indicating that accent-structured edits provide additional ben-
efit beyond generic symbolic noise. This reframes the role of
LLM editing: the ASR gains are not solely due to acoustically
faithful accent synthesis, but to a combination of pronunciation
variability and accent-conditioned structure.

for Korean at N=5). Mixing also substantially stabilizes train-
ing: at N=3, cross-run standard deviation decreases from 3.11
to 0.49 for Indian and from 2.71 to 0.09 for Korean. In this
regime the mixed WER remains close to the synthetic-data floor
(∼16–17%), as the 500 synthetic utterances dominate the train-

few real examples. As N increases, however, real-only fine-
tuning improves at different rates across accents. Indian English
adapts rapidly and surpasses the mixed condition around N≈8,
where the fixed synthetic budget begins to dilute the influence
of newly added real data, whereas Korean improves more grad-
ually with crossover near N≈25. We hypothesize that Indian
English is acoustically closer to the wav2vec2 pretraining distri-
bution, enabling faster adaptation from limited real data. Over-
all, synthetic augmentation is most beneficial in the few-shot
regime, while the optimal balance between real and synthetic
data becomes increasingly important as more real speech be-
comes available.
5.3. Cross-Speaker ASR Generalization
Table 2 reports per-speaker WER when ASR models are
fine-tuned on 500 synthetic utterances generated from a sin-
gle adaptation speaker.
Despite training exclusively on one
speaker’s synthetic speech, WER improves for all other speak-
ers of the same accent. For Indian English, Adapt+LLM re-
duces WER from 25.3% to 14.6% (RRBI) and from 26.1% to
17.1% (SVBI). For Korean English, improvements are similarly
consistent (YDCK: 20.5%→13.2%; YKWK: 21.5%→13.9%).
These results indicate that Adapt+LLM synthetic data trans-
fers beyond the adaptation speaker and captures useful accent-
related variation rather than only speaker-specific idiosyn-
crasies.
5.4. Few-Shot Data Efficiency
Figure 4 analyzes the effect of varying the number of reference
examples K on Indian English (TNI). The ICL (LLM) curve
varies the number of in-context examples, Speaker Emb and
Style Emb vary the number of utterances used to compute the
corresponding embeddings, Decoder FT varies the number of
utterances used for decoder fine-tuning, and Joint Adaptation
(All) varies decoder fine-tuning and both embeddings jointly.
Under joint adaptation, system quality improves sharply from
K=1 (AccSim 0.67, UTMOS 1.85) to K=3 (0.74, 2.46) and
then remains stable across larger K, indicating that the full
system reaches near-plateau performance with only a few ref-
erence utterances.
Decoder fine-tuning is the most sensitive
component, but even in isolation performance at K=3 is al-
ready close to the plateau observed at larger K. In contrast,
ICL (LLM) remains nearly constant from K=0 to 15, indicat-
ing that pronunciation editing is largely robust to the number of
in-context examples, while speaker and style embeddings vary
only slightly across K. Overall, the synthesis pipeline stabi-
lizes from K≥3, with only K=1 showing clear degradation,
suggesting that 3–5 reference utterances are sufficient for gen-
erating synthetic speech usable for ASR adaptation.
6. Conclusion and Future Work
We presented a few-shot pipeline for generating accented syn-
thetic speech using LLM-guided phoneme editing and speaker-
adapted TTS. With fewer than ten reference utterances, the ap-
proach produces synthetic data that improves ASR performance
on real accented speech, particularly in ultra-low-resource set-
tings and under cross-speaker evaluation. A matched-rate ran-
dom phoneme baseline shows that phoneme-space perturbation
itself is a strong form of augmentation, while LLM-guided ed-
its provide additional gains through accent-conditioned struc-
ture and improved accent fidelity. These results suggest that
effective accented ASR adaptation benefits from both increased
phonological variability and linguistically structured pronunci-
ation changes.
While effective, the current system inherits prosody from
source speech rather than explicitly modeling accent-specific
prosodic variation and adapts to a single reference speaker
without disentangling accent and speaker identity.
Future
work will explore explicit prosody modeling, disentangled ac-
cent–speaker representations to enable multi-speaker accent
generation, improved strategies for balancing real and synthetic
data during ASR adaptation, and evaluation across a broader
range of accents.
