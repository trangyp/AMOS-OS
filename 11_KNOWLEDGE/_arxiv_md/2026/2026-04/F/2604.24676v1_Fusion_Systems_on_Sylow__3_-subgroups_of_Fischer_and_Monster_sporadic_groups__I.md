FUSION SYSTEMS ON SYLOW 3-SUBGROUPS OF FISCHER AND
MONSTER SPORADIC GROUPS: I
PETE GAUTAM
Abstract. We classify all corefree fusion systems on a Sylow 3-subgroup of the sporadic
groups Fi22, Fi23 and B. We show that the 3-group in each case does not support any exotic
fusion systems. This is the first of two papers that will complete the classification of all corefree
fusion systems on Sylow p-subgroups of sporadic groups for p odd.
1. Introduction
A fusion system F is an algebraic structure on a p-group S that generalises conjugation by an
overgroup G. We are particularly interested in saturated fusion systems, which include all fusion
systems on S where S ∈Sylp(G), in which case we say that F is realized by G. Much interest is
in the case where a saturated fusion system is not realized by any finite group G, called exotic
fusion systems (for a discussion, see [AKO11, III.7.4]).
For a given p-group S, it is quite unlikely that S supports exotic fusion systems (see, for
example, [PS21]). On the other hand, many of the known exotic fusion systems have been found
on Sylow p-subgroups of finite simple sporadic groups. In fact, the first examples of exotic fusion
systems for odd primes were found in [RV04] on the extraspecial group 71+2
+
, which is a Sylow
7-subgroup of the sporadic groups O′N, He and Fi′
24. Later work has resulted in further exotic
fusion systems being found on Sylow p-subgroups of sporadic groups in [Oli14, COS17, PS18,
vB25]. Moreover, the smallest exotic fusion system on a 2-group is known to be supported on a
Sylow 2-subgroup of Co3 (see [Sol74, Ben98]).
Looking at [vB25, Table 1], to complete the classification of fusion systems on Sylow p-
subgroups of sporadic groups for p odd, it remains to consider the Sylow 3-subgroups of large
sporadic groups. These are the Fischer groups Fi22, Fi23, Fi′
24, the Baby Monster B and the
Monster M. We will finalise this classification over the course of two papers. We note that a
Sylow 3-subgroup of the Fischer groups Fi22 and Fi23 are also isomorphic to Sylow 3-subgroups
of other (almost) simple groups. As such, this work is valuable in characterising fusion systems
on other interesting groups.
The first result we prove in this paper pertains to a Sylow 3-subgroup of Fi22. We note that
a Sylow 3-subgroup of Fi22 is also isomorphic to a Sylow 3-subgroup of O7(3) and 2E6(2). The
following result is proven in Theorem 5.17.
Theorem 1.1. Let F be a fusion system on a Sylow 3-subgroup of Fi22. If O3(F) = 1, then F
is realized by an almost simple group G, where O3′(G) = F ∗(G) is isomorphic to O7(3), Fi22 or
2E6(2).
A Sylow 3-subgroup S of Fi22 supports plenty of saturated fusion systems. As such, it is not
practical to classify all saturated fusion systems on S. We shall only focus on fusion systems F
containing no normal 3-subgroups, called corefree fusion systems and denoted O3(F) = 1.
The outer automorphism groups of the simple groups O7(3) and Fi22 have order 2. On the
other hand, 2E6(2) has an outer automorphism group isomorphic to Sym(3).
In particular,
a Sylow 3-subgroup of Aut(2E6(2)) is not isomorphic to a Sylow 3-subgroup of 2E6(2).
We
extend the work on a Sylow 3-subgroup of Fi22 to classify all corefree fusion systems on a Sylow
3-subgroup Aut(2E6(2)). This result can be found in Theorem 6.13.

The sporadic groups Fi22 and Fi23 are closely related to groups of Lie type in characteristic
3. Indeed, Fi22 and O7(3) share the same Sylow 3-subgroup, and the same holds for Fi23 and
Aut(O+
8 (3)). In [PS20, Conjecture 2], Parker and Semeraro conjecture that if G is a group of
Lie type in defining characteristic, not isomorphic to Sp4(q) or a small number of exceptional
cases, all corefree fusion systems on a Sylow p-subgroup of G can be realized by a subgroup
G ≤H ≤Aut(G). These two 3-groups are certainly exceptional cases. For p = 2, all exceptional
cases are believed to be classified following the recent work by Stroth in [Str26].
In [vB25], van Beek stated that a major obstacle in completing the classification for all sporadic
groups would be computing with such large 3-groups. The process of finding all fusion systems
on a p-group S starts with finding the proto-essential subgroups of S. Prior to this work, the
state-of-the-art algorithm to compute this was the Parker-Semeraro MAGMA package [PS20].
This code was indeed quite valuable in their work on [PS21] that classified fusion systems on
groups of small order. The code computes all the proto-essential subgroups of S by first finding
all subgroups of the p-group S/Z(S).
We were able to implement in GAP an equivalent code that computes the proto-essential
subgroups of a p-group S. This is more efficient than the code in MAGMA since the subgroups
are computed up to Aut(S)-conjugacy, not S-conjugacy. While this is enough to work with the
Sylow 3-subgroup of the sporadic groups Fi22, Fi23 and B, this approach does not work for the
Sylow 3-subgroups of Fi24 and M. In a sequel paper, we will unveil an algorithm to compute
the proto-essential subgroups of a p-group without requiring all subgroups of S. This will make
the process of finding essential subgroups for a particular group tractable for relatively large
p-groups. We highlight that the Parker-Semeraro package cannot typically be used to work with
groups of order ≥310, but our package can be used when looking at a Sylow 3-subgroup of
the Monster group M, of order 320. Appendix A gives a brief description of the version of the
algorithm we make use of here, and lists how it performs with the four 3-groups we consider in
this paper.
After computing the proto-essential subgroups, much of the classification involves identifying
the relevant normal subsystems that correspond to the 3-local subgroups we see in each simple
group. This makes use of techniques in group theory such as coprime action. To prove that
there are no exotic fusion systems supported on each group, we mostly make use of techniques
already present in the literature. In particular, this comes from identifying parabolic systems
using [Ono11, Proposition 7.5] and showing that constrained subsystems are equal using [Oli23,
Proposition 2.11].
Because the 3-groups we are considering are relatively large, we will make use of coding
throughout the paper. This code can be found in the github repository [Gau26]. If a proof
requires some code, we make an explicit reference to the relevant file. We remark that most of
the results in this paper can be proven solely using code. Nonetheless, we try to prove most of
them theoretically, relying on code as little as we can.

Lemma 2.1 (Three subgroups lemma). Let X, Y, Z ≤G be such that [X, Y, Z] = 1 and

(2) If J(S) ≤P ≤S, then J(P) = J(S).
Proof. This is [KS06, 9.2.8].

We shall make use of the following result throughout the paper without explicit reference.
Lemma 2.4 (Coprime Action). Let G act coprimely on A, and let B be a G-invariant subgroup
of A. Then the following hold:
(1) CA/B(G) = CA(G)B/B;
(2) if G acts trivially on A/B and B, then G acts trivially on A; and
(3) if G acts trivially on A/Φ(A), then G acts trivially on A.
Proof. (1) and (2) are given in [KS06, 8.2.2]. (3) is proven in [KS06, 8.2.9].
□
We can strengthen (3) from the previous lemma by the following result of Burnside.
Lemma 2.5 (Burnside). Let S be a finite p-group. Then CAut(S)(S/Φ(S)) is a normal p-subgroup
of Aut(S).
Proof. See [Gor07, Theorem I.5.1.4].
□
Lemma 2.6. Let E be a finite p-group, with A ⊴E. Let G be a subgroup of Aut(E) such that
A is G-invariant. If CE(A) ≤A, then CG(A) ≤Op(G).
Proof. Let r ∈G be a p′-element that centralizes A. Then [r, A] = 1, so that [r, A, E] = 1. Since
A ⊴E, we also find that [A, E, r] ≤[A, r] = 1. By the Three Subgroups Lemma, we deduce
that [E, r, A] = 1. In that case, [E, r] ≤CE(A). By assumption, we know that CE(A) ≤A,
meaning that r centralizes E/A. We deduce that r centralizes E by coprime action. But r is an
automorphism of E, so we conclude that r = 1.
Now consider K := CG(A). Since A is G-invariant, we know that K is a normal subgroup
of G. Moreover, we showed above that if r ∈K is a p′-element, then r = 1. We deduce that
K ≤Op(G), as desired.
□

for all x ∈G with Ax ≤H, we have that Ax = A.

Theorem 3.8 (Model Theorem). Let F be a fusion system on a finite p-group S. Let Q ⊴S be

(2) If G1 and G2 are models for F, then there exists an isomorphism ϕ: G1 →G2 such that
ϕ|S = 1.

Appendix A. Algorithm for computing all proto-essential subgroups
In this section, we give a brief description of the algorithm in GAP to check whether a subgroup
E of S is proto-essential. Here, we check every subgroup E of S (up to Aut(S)-conjugacy). In
the next paper, we will provide an optimisation of the algorithm that allows us to check only
certain subgroups of S.
The code is loosely based on the MAGMA algorithm by Parker-Semeraro in [PS20].
In
particular, for a subgroup E of S, we perform the following checks in the given order:
(1) Centric Test Check that CS(E) ≤E.
(2) Rank Test Check that OutS(E) is a Sylow p-subgroup of a group containing a strongly
p-embedded subgroup. Moreover, if this is the case, then use [Sam14, Theorem 6.9] to
ensure that the rank of E is sufficiently large.
(3) Frattini Test Check that [NS(E), E] ≰Φ(E) and that CNS(E)(E/Φ(E)) ≤E.
(4) Radical Test Check that OutS(E) ∩Op(Out(E)) = 1.
(5) Lifting Test If OutS(E) is elementary abelian of order pn > p and Aut(E) does not
have a section isomorphic to Alt(2p) (if p ≥5), check that Aut(NS(E)) has an element
of order
1
(2,pn−1)(pn −1).
We briefly justify these steps. Steps (1) and (4) follow from the definition of a radical subgroup.
If E < CNS(E)(E/Φ(E)) =: A, then
Inn(E) < AutA(E) ≤CAutS(E)(E/Φ(E)) ≤Op(Aut(E))
by Lemma 2.5, meaning that E cannot be S-radical.
Performing this check before step (4)
ensures that we do not compute Aut(E), an expensive process. As an optimisation, we first
compute whether [NS(E), E] ≤Φ(E) before computing the centralizer itself. If E is elementary
abelian, then it passes step (4). But if E is not proto-essential, then it typically does not pass
step (5). The following result justifies step (5).
