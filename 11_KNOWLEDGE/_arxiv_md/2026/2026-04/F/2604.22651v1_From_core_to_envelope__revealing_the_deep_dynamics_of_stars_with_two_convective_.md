ABSTRACT
On the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, F-type solar pulsators connect the Sun to intermediate mass stars located on the instability
strip. With respect to lower mass stars, they are structurally peculiar in the sense that they are constituted of three distinct dynamical
layers: a small convective core, a deep radiative interior, and a shallow convective envelope. Current asteroseismic techniques only
provide limited information on the interior dynamics of these stars. Indeed only gravity modes (g modes), for which unambiguous
characterisation is lacking, are able to probe the deep stellar layers. A better understanding of the excitation and behaviour in F-type
solar pulsators is therefore necessary in order to consider their detection. In this work, we simulate for the first time the global stellar
structure of an F-type star (core, radiative interior, envelope). We show that the contribution of the core strongly affects the spectrum
of excited g modes, with low-order high-degree modes unable to form due to their interaction with the turbulent convection of the
core. Finally, by computing the disc-integrated signature of the modes, we are able to demonstrate that they preserve their integrity up
to the top of the convective envelope, which is a strong argument in favour of their detectability with spaceborne photometry.

1. Introduction
With their peculiar structural properties, F-type stars populate
the region between the Sun and intermediate-mass stars (see
e.g. Fig. 1 from Breton et al. 2023). Their radiative interior is
surrounded both by a shallow convective envelope and a small
convective core, making them fascinating test-beds to explore
the non-linear excitation of internal waves by decoupled convec-
tive sources. When they are able to form standing modes, these
internal waves give rise to stellar oscillations (e.g. Unno et al.
1989; Aerts et al. 2010). In particular, direct constraints on the
core properties and on the stellar deep dynamics can be obtained
only through the observation of gravity modes (g modes, e.g.
Christensen-Dalsgaard et al. 1985; García et al. 2007), that is the
standing modes of oscillation related to the propagation of in-
ternal gravity waves (IGWs) in the stellar interior. However, due
to their low surface amplitude when a convective envelope is
present, the detectability of such g modes is unclear (Belkacem
et al. 2009, 2022; Breton et al. 2023) below the γ Dor instabil-
ity strip (e.g. Kaye et al. 1999; Warner et al. 2003; Dupret et al.
2004).
Indeed, F-type main-sequence stars with effective tempera-
ture below 6500 K exhibit stochastically excited acoustic modes
of oscillation (p modes, e.g. Belkacem et al. 2008; Chaplin et al.
2011; Mathur et al. 2014; Lund et al. 2017) and are therefore
still considered solar-like from this point of view. They are of
paramount importance for our comprehension of stellar object
below 1.5 M⊙, as a significant part of the upcoming Planetary
Transit and Oscillation of Stars mission (PLATO, Rauer et al.
2025) core sample will be constituted with such main-sequence
F-type solar-like pulsators (Montalto et al. 2021; Goupil et al.
2024). Although p-mode asteroseismology allows putting some
constraints on the extent of their convective core, this requires
the implementation of model-dependent fitting of observational
frequencies on a stellar evolutionary track (e.g. Deheuvels et al.
2016), or the application of complex inversion techniques (e.g.
Bétrisey & Buldgen 2022). Moreover, p modes alone are unable
to provide constraints on the rotational dynamics of the deep ra-
diative layers (e.g. Thompson et al. 1996, 2003; García et al.
2007; Benomar et al. 2015). On the contrary, the core extent is
directly encoded in the g-mode asymptotic period spacing (Tas-
soul 1980), and g modes are extremely sensitive to the rotation
of such regions (Deheuvels et al. 2012; Li et al. 2020). The main
issue for the application of g-mode asteroseismology on main-
sequence solar-type stars stems from the fact that, unlike γ Dor
stars which exhibit g modes with large amplitudes due to the ex-
istence of non-adiabatic destabilising mechanisms (e.g. Dupret
et al. 2005), g modes in solar-type stars are stable and can only
be excited by the stochastic energy input from turbulent con-
vective motions (e.g. Kumar et al. 1996; Lecoanet & Quataert
2013; Pinçon et al. 2016; Augustson et al. 2020). Previous 2D
and 3D hydrodynamical simulation in polar and spherical con-
figurations demonstrated that IGWs are excited more efficiently
in F-type stars (Breton et al. 2022a) than in the Sun (e.g. Rogers
& Glatzmaier 2005; Brun et al. 2011; Alvan et al. 2014, 2015).
In main-sequence solar-type stars, g modes are low-
frequency (ν ≲500 µHz) modes with absolute radial order n
increasing with decreasing frequency. In F-type stars, low-order
high-frequency modes are in principle able to mix with low-
order p modes (Breton et al. 2023) to form mixed modes while
high-order low-frequency g modes will become gravito-inertial
modes under the effect of the Coriolis force (Lee & Saio 1997;
Townsend 2003; Van Reeth et al. 2015, 2018), with possible ad-
ditional couplings between the eigenmodes of the core, the ra-
diative interior, and the envelope (Ouazzani et al. 2020; Tokuno

In this work, we present the first 3D simulations of a stellar
interior including both a convective core and a convective enve-

the core in their properties. The modes maintain their coherence
up to the highest regions of the convective envelope. With respect
to models accounting only for envelope excitation, we find that
the core plays a crucial role by filtering out high-frequency high-
degree modes. Constructing observational time series from our
simulations, we are able to unambiguously identify the signature
of the modes at the top of the convective envelope. This should
justify and encourage future efforts for their identification and
characterisation in observational time series. The layout of the
article is as follows. In Sect. 2, we present the anelastic numer-

spectrum of the g modes excited in the simulation in order to
analyse the different behaviour exhibited by the setups depend-

computation of pseudo-observational time series and the discus-

of our work in Sect. 5.
2. Simulation setups
In order to assess the importance of the convective core in wave
internal dynamics of an F-type stars, we develop and we anal-
yse three 1.3 M⊙spherical models with the Anelastic Spherical
Harmonics code (ASH, Clune et al. 1999; Brun et al. 2004). The
first one includes the full extent of the stellar structure (convec-
tive envelope surrounding the radiative interior and the convec-
tive core, the model is named hereafter Full). The second one
includes only the convective envelope and the radiative interior
(model named hereafter EnvCZ), and the third one only the ra-
diative interior and the convective core (model named hereafter
CoreCZ). We note that, despite their similar configuration, the
background structure of the EnvCZ model slightly differ from
the model presented in Breton et al. (2022a). Each simulation
is initialised with small perturbations in the unstable convective
layer to allow for the growth of the convective instability, and
evolved until it statistically reached a relaxed steady state. More
details on the numerical setup used for the the simulations are
provided in Appendix A.
A volume representation of the radial velocity field, 3r, for
the Full model is shown in Fig. 1. To allow visualising both the
convective structure and the IGW pattern, 3r is normalised by
the root-mean-square value, 3rms, computed on the radial shell
each cell belongs to. In the convective envelope, large scale up-
wards and downards flow are visible. In particular, downwards
motions can be seen interacting with the top of the radiative in-
terior through plumes penetrating the stably stratified layers and
exciting the IGWs. Given the more limited extent of the core, the
typical scale of the convective motions close to the centre of the
star is smaller than for the envelope. As shown in Fig. A.3 and
expected from a mixing-length argument (Böhm-Vitense 1958),
the convective motions are also slower in the core than in the en-
velope. In the upper layers of the radiative interior, the signal is
constituted by the superposition of progressive IGWs and stand-
ing g mode of all degrees. Close to the convective core, the wave
signal is dominated by high-amplitude, high-frequency g modes.
For comparison with the Full model, the volume display of the
EnvCZ and CoreCZ models are shown in Fig. 2. We note that,
due to the absence of the convective core, the superposition of
mode patterns in the lower half of the radiative interior differs
between the Full and the EnvCZ cases. Finally, the wave struc-
ture we distinguish in the CoreCZ case has a large scale even
close to the top of the radiative interior, suggesting that the sig-
nal is dominated by low-order modes with few radial nodes.
We show in Fig. 3 the profile of the Brunt-Väisäla frequency,
N, of the reference stellar structure. We have N = 0 in the con-
vective layer and N > 0 in the radiative layers. Given that N
imposes the maximal frequency of IGWs propagating in the lo-
cal medium, waves in the radiative interior are expected to have
frequencies up to 400 µHz. As the ray trajectory of an IGW is
frequency dependent (Gough 1993), it is possible to filter the
temporal signal to disentangle the signature of waves at distinct
frequencies (Alvan et al. 2015). We illustrate this in the right
panel of Fig. 1 where we show the result of this filtering in the
radiative interior for a frequency 121 µHz ≤ν ≤123 µHz and
ν ≤5 µHz. In the former case, the ray travels from inner caus-
tic to outer caustic, with a reflection geometry that creates the
typical rosetta-pattern of IGWs in this range of frequency. In the
latter case, at very low frequency, the rays are converging to-
wards the centre in a spiral pattern with winding angles so low
that they appear as concentric circles.
3. Mode power spectrum
Once the simulations are relaxed, we compute the power spec-
trum of the radial velocity at given depths for our different se-
tups, considering time series of lengths 104 days.
3.1. Power spectrum computation
The power spectrum are computed by constructing time series
with the simulation outputs (Breton et al. 2022a). Considering
the radial grid of the simulations, a subsample of spherical shells
with radial coordinates r are selected. The corresponding (θ, ϕ)
velocity maps, with θ the latitude and ϕ the longitude, are out-

This underlines the fact that the convective envelope alone is
able of exciting these progressives waves for a large range of
frequency and degrees.

3.3. Interpreting the convective core role on mode formation
As highlighted by the dotted line in Fig. 4, the modes that are
unable to form in the Full case while being excited to large am-
plitudes in the EnvCZ case have a frequency ν ≳150 µHz. Below
this frequency, the lower boundary of the mode resonant cavity
approximately coincides with the interface between the core and
the radiative interior (see Fig. 3). We suggest that the distinct be-
haviour observed for these modes in the Full and EnvCZ cases
can be interpreted in terms of mode asymptoticity. Indeed, we
argue that the waves corresponding to an asymptotic mode will
perceive the νnℓ= N boundary, that is, in this case, the top of the
convective core, as a transmission-reflection interface. It may be
demonstrated through the use of the Werner, Jeffreys, Brillouin,
and Kramers (WKBJ, e.g. Unno et al. 1989) method that their
radial eigenfunction is oscillatory within the bounds of the cav-
ity thus defined and evanescent outside. Under this asymptotic
configuration, in the EnvCZ case, the inner boundary condition
imposes complete reflection at r = 0.07 R⋆(it is the bottom of
the simulation domain), while in the Full case a sufficient frac-
tion of the wave energy is reflected and a standing mode is still
able to form.
By contrast, the WKBJ approximation is not valid anymore
for non-asymptotic modes. A travelling wave will not perceive
the νnℓ= N boundary as a transmission-reflection interface and
it will propagate beyond this point. In the EnvCZ case, the wave
will therefore reach the bottom of the simulation domain and will
be reflected there. The situation is different when the dynamical
picture includes the convective core. The wave would need to be
refracted close to the centre of the star to form a coherent stand-
ing mode. However, as it enters the overshoot region, the wave
starts experiencing viscous damping from the convective eddies,
which adds to the radiative damping. In this picture, the wave is
damped while travelling in the convective core, and is therefore
unable to form a mode. In the asymptotic picture, the damping
efficiency increases with the degree ℓ, and the distribution we
obtain in the power spectrum for the Full case supports that this
should still be valid as only low-degree non asymptotic modes
are able to form. We illustrate our argument with Fig. 7, where
we compare the eigenfunctions computed with the GYRE code
(Townsend & Teitler 2013, see Appendix B) for the simulation
reference states, for two ℓ= 15 modes. The first mode, with
n = 5 and ν = 290.8 µHz, is excited in the EnvCZ case but not in
the Full case. The second mode, with n = 29 and ν = 100.1 µHz,
is excited in both cases. In particular, as visible from the loca-
tion of the vertical dashed lines, the oscillatory behaviour of the
eigenfunction in the asymptotic case is confined inside the whole
extent of the radiative interior, while there is no clear distinction
between an oscillatory component and an exponential one in the
non-asymptotic case.
4. Mode detectability
We now turn to the issue of the mode detectability. Obser-
vationally, the main issue stems from the fact that the low-
surface amplitude of g modes makes it difficult to disentangle
their contribution from the incoherent turbulent noise dominat-
ing the Fourier spectrum in this range of frequency (e.g. García
et al. 2007; Breton et al. 2023). In order to examine this issue
with our simulation, we develop a process to compute pseudo-
observational time series.

4.2. Observational PSD
By integrating the projected velocity signal on the stellar disc,
as would be seen by an observer, we were therefore able to con-
struct a set of time series that allows us to model how the disc-
integrated observable g-mode signature evolve with the stellar
depth.
In Fig 10, we show the corresponding PSD obtained after ap-
plying a DFT on these time series. It is expected that, in this ob-
servational PSD, the contribution from increasing degree ℓwill
be gradually filtered out by the disc-integrated spatial integration
(Dziembowski 1977). Moreover, by temporally filtering the low-
frequency contribution to the time series, we are able to unam-
biguously isolate the g-mode signal in the power spectral density
(PSD) even in the upper layers of the convective envelope. Here
again, the signature differs importantly between the Full case and
the EnvCZ model. In both cases, the main g-mode signature con-
tribution comes from the ℓ= 1 and ℓ= 2 modes. As visible, the
EnvCZ case exhibits a few peaks related to the contribution from
modes at higher degree (this is also the case for the mode with
the lowest frequency in the Full spectrum).

5. Conclusion
In this work, we presented the first 3D simulations of the dy-
namics of a star with two convective zones. Indeed, F-type stars

the envelope in the excitation of g modes, we compared a full-
extent model (Full) with a coreless model (EnvCZ) and a model

Appendix B: Power spectrum comparison with
linear eigenfunctions
The linear eigenfunction shown in Fig. 7 were computed with
the GYRE code (Townsend & Teitler 2013), considering the 1D
reference profile of our 3D setup. The implemented GYRE for-
malism closest to the anelastic approximation used in ASH is the
γ formalism (Ong & Basu 2020), as it isolates the g-mode buoy-
ancy cavity from the acoustic cavity, similarly to the anelastic
approximation where acoustic waves are filtered out. The oscil-
lation equations are solved both with and without the Cowling
approximation, which neglects the perturbation of the gravita-
tional potential (Cowling 1941).
In Fig. B.1, we compare the GYRE frequencies for modes
with νnℓ> 100 µHz and ℓ≤20 to the Eℓdistribution computed
with the ASH simulations. As it is visible in Fig. B.1, the agree-
ment between the GYRE eigenfunctions and the 3D-simulation
power spectrum is very good. We note that the first ℓ= 1 mode
of the ASH spectra, visible at νnℓ≈190 µHz, is present in the
GYRE predictions including the Cowling approximation, but ab-
sent otherwise. It corresponds to a n = 0 dipolar mode that is un-
physical for a self-gravitating spherical object (that would mean
the object is oscillating around its centre of mass) but permitted
in the context of the Cowling approximation and in ASH se-
tups, where the gravitational potential is defined by a fixed back-
ground term with neglected fluctuations.
