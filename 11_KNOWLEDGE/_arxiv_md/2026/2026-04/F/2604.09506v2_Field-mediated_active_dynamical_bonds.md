Abstract
Active matter systems typically exhibit a trade-off between structural robustness and dynamical freedom, limiting
independent control over structure and motion. Here, we show that encoding interactions in a shared field
overcomes this constraint, enabling continuous tuning between stable architectures and dynamically active states.
Using droplets on a vibrated fluid bath as a minimal realization, we demonstrate that individually unstable units
can collectively self-stabilize through field-mediated dynamical bonds. Arising from wavefield interference,
these bonds form persistent, self-healing connections that preserve architecture while sustaining motion. Droplet
size sets the symmetry of the interactions, with identical droplets forming rigid σ-like frameworks that enforce
triangular packing, while smaller droplets enable π-like coordination that supports higher-order symmetries. The
resulting assemblies exhibit both stability and sustained collective dynamics, including spontaneous rotation
and controlled migration. This work establishes a general route to programmable active matter in which shared
fields reconcile structural robustness with dynamical freedom.
Keywords Dynamical bonds · Active matter · Self-assembly · Wave-mediated interactions · Programmable materials
Active matter exhibits striking collective behaviours, from
flocking and swarming [1] to motility-induced phase separa-
tion [2] and active turbulence [3]. Despite this diversity, achiev-
ing and controlling structural robustness together with sustained
collective motion remains a central challenge [4, 5]. Systems
with sufficiently strong pairwise interactions can form ordered
assemblies, but such interactions often suppress mobility or lead
to dynamically arrested states [6]. Conversely, highly motile
active agents typically assemble only transient or fluid-like ag-
gregates without persistent, reconfigurable internal bonds [2].
As a result, synthetic active systems typically separate into ei-
ther coherently moving assemblies with little internal rigidity or
ordered structures whose strong interactions constrain dynami-
cal freedom. By contrast, biological systems reconcile stability
and motion through complex regulatory mechanisms, including
coordinated cell-cell interactions and adaptive mechanical feed-
back [7, 8, 9]. However, a minimal physical mechanism that
produces durable architecture while preserving dynamical free-
dom, without embedding complexity in the particles themselves,
has remained elusive.
Here we demonstrate that this tension can be overcome
when interactions are encoded in a shared field rather than in
static pairwise forces. Using wave-generating droplets on a vi-
brating fluid bath as a minimal realization, we find that units that
are individually unstable can collectively self-stabilize through
field-mediated dynamical bonds. These bonds arise from wave
interference patterns sampled over the droplet’s local contact
region, producing connections that preserve geometry while en-
abling collective motion. Bond symmetry and stiffness depend
on droplet size, yielding rigid σ-like frameworks for identical
large droplets and π-like coordination when smaller droplets are
introduced. Shared fields thus provide a flexible mechanism for
assembling active, self-healing architectures in which stability
and mobility emerge simultaneously and can be tuned.
Collective self-stabilization
Walking droplets on a vertically vibrated fluid bath pro-
vide a well-established model system in which particles in-
teract through a self-generated, memory-bearing wavefield
[10, 11, 12, 13].
In this system, a droplet repeatedly im-
pacts the liquid surface, exciting localized subharmonic Faraday
waves [14] whose gradual decay imparts memory to the inter-
face. The droplet subsequently is guided horizontally by the
local wave slope from its self-generated wavefield, establishing
a feedback loop between particle and field and forming a sta-
ble, self-propelled, wave-particle entity on the liquid surface.
Owing to the wave-particle coupling, remarkably, these walking
droplets exhibit several hydrodynamic quantum analogs [15, 16].
Through this wave-mediated interaction, droplets can form
a remarkable spectrum of bound states, including orbiting
pairs [17, 18, 19], promenading pairs [20, 21], chasing pairs [12],
ratchets [22, 23], and self-organized droplet lattices and small
spinning clusters of droplets [24, 25, 26, 12]. Moreover, solid
floating bodies at air-fluid interface, when vibrated internally
or externally, can also form bound states and self-organized
structures through wave-mediated interactions [27, 28, 29, 30]
Under single-frequency forcing, walking droplets are typi-
cally small (radii ∼0.3-0.5 mm) and slow (up to 15 mm/s) [31].

primary frequency and its subharmonic, droplets enter the super-

We perform experiments on a circular bath driven simulta-

using a subwoofer-based shaking system (Fig. 1a, Methods).
Unlike walkers or superwalkers, we find that droplets with radii
larger than about 1.2 mm are not stable in isolation, even under
two-frequency driving. Such large droplets rapidly lose phase
synchrony with the vertical oscillations of the bath and coa-
lesce (Fig. 1b; Movie 1). This loss of synchronization reflects a
breakdown of the self-sustained bouncing mechanism at large
droplet size. Remarkably, this instability is reversed in the col-
lective setting. When several such large droplets are generated
in close proximity, they do not coalesce. Instead, they undergo
synchronized deformation cycles and spontaneously assemble
into long-lived, tightly bound, crystal-like clusters (Fig. 1c). We
refer to these large, individually unstable droplets as field-bound
droplets, emphasizing that their persistence arises only through
mutual wave-mediated coupling rather than individual stability.
Wavefield patterning and the dynamical bond
The collective self-organization described above is medi-
ated by wave interactions between field-bound droplets. Each
deforming droplet on the vibrated bath generates a localized
standing Faraday wave that interferes with those of its neigh-
bours. The resulting interference pattern is imprinted onto the
liquid surface and determines both the magnitude and direction
of the forces acting on the droplets. Binding is therefore not
governed by a fixed pairwise potential, but emerges dynamically
from the superposition of self-generated wavefields.

The wavefield interference described above defines a new
interaction entity between field-bound droplets: the dynamical
bond. Unlike static attractive forces, dynamical bonds are con-
tinuously sustained by the refreshed wavefield and exhibit three
defining properties. First, they possess a capture separation,
determined by the zero-crossing of F(r) (Fig. 2a), within which
droplets are drawn toward a stable equilibrium separation. Sec-
ond, they are self-healing: clusters remain intact across a wide
range of driving accelerations, and directed perturbations trigger
restoring dynamics that rapidly re-establish both separation and
orientation (Movies 4-6). Third, they are modular: multiple
bonds coexist without destructive interference, enabling capture
of additional droplets into existing clusters (Movie 3).
Programmable architectures
The symmetry and function of assemblies formed from
dynamical bonds depend strongly on droplet size composition.
Interactions between two field-bound droplets (R ≳1.2 mm)
are dominated by strong radial confinement and behave as rigid
struts that resist both compression and angular distortion. We
refer to this regime as σ-like bonding, in analogy with σ bonds
between atoms, to emphasize its role in fixing pairwise separa-
tions and imposing geometric constraints. Under homogeneous
σ coupling, clusters of N = 2-6 droplets invariably converge
to triangular packing (Fig. 3a). This triangular arrangement
is not a geometric accident but a dynamical consequence since
bonds that fix equal separations without introducing angular con-
straints naturally favor triangular configurations, which provide
mechanically robust structures under such interactions. Because
dynamical bonds require each droplet to access the surrounding
wavefield, compact uniformly bonded clusters remain robust
up to N = 6, while linear chains persist to larger sizes before
destabilizing (Movie 7; see Movie 8 for the enhanced stability
of the pyramidal six-droplet cluster and the chain-to-pyramid
transition under perturbation).
Figure 3b demonstrates the self-healing nature of assem-
bilies formed using dynamical bonds through a perturbation-
recovery sequence. When one droplet in a five-droplet cluster is
displaced (white arrow), the affected bond breaks (red dashed).
The shared wavefield, however, continuously refreshes the inter-
action landscape, and the broken bond spontaneously reforms
(blue dashed) as the droplet returns to its equilibrium position.
This recovery occurs without external intervention, highlighting
the robustness of field-mediated binding.
Introducing droplets of smaller size (R ≈0.5 mm) qualita-
tively alters the bonding symmetry. A small droplet confined
within a cluster of larger ones does not act as a rigid strut; in-
stead, it responds to the superposed wavefield from multiple
neighbours and modifies the local interference landscape experi-
enced by the cluster. This gives rise to a π-like coordination bond
that shifts the equilibrium arrangement of the larger droplets
beyond the triangular packing imposed by pure σ bonding. For
example, placing a small droplet at the centre of four large
droplets arranged in a rhombus transforms the configuration into
a stable square (Fig. 3c; Movie 9). Similar centrally mediated
coordination enables higher-order rotational symmetries in five-

Emergent chirality
Dynamical bonds do not merely stabilize structure; they
also sustain collective motion. A minimal realization is the three-
droplet cluster shown in Fig. 4a. Three identical field-bound
droplets bind into a triangular configuration that can rotates
persistently, choosing either clockwise or anticlockwise motion
with equal probability (Movie 10). The rotating cluster behaves
as a single active chiral unit whose rotational motion emerges
from non-reciprocal wave-mediated forces.
The rotation rate of the chiral unit is continuously tunable
through the amplitude of the driving acceleration of the bath
vibrations. As the vibration amplitude increases, the angular ve-
locity remains negligible at low forcing (passive regime), before
crossing a threshold into a chiral regime where persistent rota-
tion emerges (Fig. 4a). Within this regime, the angular velocity
increases approximately linearly with driving acceleration. This
linear region indicates that the strength of the wave-mediated
forcing scales proportionally with the driving, allowing continu-
ous control of rotational speed without altering the underlying
structure. Importantly, this mechanism decouples structure from
dynamics: the triangular architecture remains intact throughout
the linear increase in rotation rate, demonstrating that geometry
is preserved while speed is modulated. At lower accelerations,
the system lacks sufficient wavefield strength to sustain coher-
ent rotation, while at higher accelerations the rotational state
becomes unstable. Beyond this upper threshold, the chiral unit
can no longer support faster rotations and coalesces (Movie 11).

of the dimensionless wave amplitude α and memory parameter
M for droplets of radius R = 1.7 mm. In the model, we use a
slightly larger effective radius than the experimentally measured
droplet radius of R ≈1.5 mm (when it just lifts-off) to account
for droplet flattening upon contact with the bath, which increases
its lateral extent. Three distinct dynamical regimes are identified.
At low α and M, the system resides in a stationary phase, where
droplets remain locked in a triangular configuration without
net rotation. As α and M increase, the system transitions into a
rotating phase characterized by persistent chiral motion, with the
cluster maintaining a stable geometry while exhibiting sustained
rotation. At sufficiently large values of α and M, this ordered
state breaks down into an unstable regime, where droplets either
remain loosely bound while undergoing irregular, fluctuating
motion or the cluster fully disintegrates.
Within the rotating phase, the color map indicates that the
angular velocity is comparable with experimental values and
increases monotonically with increasing memory. Since higher
M corresponds to higher driving accelerations in experiments,
this trend is consistent with the monontonic increase of rotation
rate observed in Fig. 4a. Together, these results demonstrate
that both the existence and the speed of chiral motion are con-
trolled by the interplay between wavefield memory and forcing
amplitude.
Hierarchy of dynamical freedoms
Beyond rigid triplets, the same wave-mediated bonding
mechanism supports a broad spectrum of collective behaviours,
giving rise to a hierarchy of dynamical freedoms that is primarily
controlled by droplet size (Fig. 5a).
Figure 5b shows experimental realizations of these dynam-
ical states for clusters of 2–4 droplets. Reducing the droplet
size effectively lowers the confining barrier imposed by the
self-generated wavefield, thereby sequentially unlocking addi-
tional degrees of freedom. Large droplets generate strong radial
confinement and form stationary, rigid clusters. As the droplet
size decreases, this confinement weakens, first permitting rota-
tional motion while preserving the underlying bonded structure
(Movie 12). Upon further reduction in size, translational degrees
of freedom are released, allowing clusters to move collectively
across the bath while remaining bound (Movie 13). This progres-
sion reflects a continuous weakening of dynamical bonds: from
rigid, geometry-preserving configurations to flexible, mobile
assemblies.
At smaller droplet sizes (R ≲1.1 mm), a richer set of
weakly bound states emerges, consistent with observations in
superwalker systems [12]. Two-droplet systems exhibit a variety
of dynamical modes, including chasing (Movie 14), orbiting
of a smaller satellite around a larger droplet (Movie 15), prom-
enading pairs (Movie 16), and outward spiralling trajectories
(Movie 17). Three-droplet clusters, by contrast, robustly adopt a
plane-like walking configuration, with two droplets leading and
one trailing (Movie 18).
Across all cases, droplet size acts as the primary control
parameter governing the accessible degrees of freedom, while
the bath driving amplitude tunes the rate at which these degrees
are explored. The shared wavefield thus encodes not only equi-
librium separations but also the structure of collective dynamics.
Stability and motion are not competing attributes; rather, both
emerge from a balance between wavefield-imposed confinement
and the droplet’s ability to access and exploit dynamical free-
doms.

Outlook: Field-mediated active matter
We have identified field-mediated dynamical bonds as a
mechanism by which individually unstable units can collec-
tively self-stabilize. These bonds are persistent, self-healing,
and composition-dependent: large droplets form rigid σ-like
frameworks, while smaller droplets mediate π-like coordina-
tion that tunes symmetry. The resulting architectures combine
structural robustness with sustained motion, distinguishing this
field-based mechanism from conventional particle-centric ap-
proaches to active matter.
The central conceptual shift is one of interaction encoding.
In most active systems, the rules of assembly are embedded
directly in particle-particle interactions. Here, by contrast, com-
plexity resides in the shared field that couples otherwise simple
units. Wavefield interference encodes preferred separations and
symmetries, while the droplet’s local footprint converts these
patterns into forces and torques. Structure and motion therefore
emerge from a distributed medium rather than from fixed local
rules.
This inversion expands the design space of active mate-
rials. Hierarchical architectures can be constructed in which
σ-bonded frameworks provide rigidity, π-mediated coordina-
tion enables symmetry tuning, and residual dynamical freedoms
permit controlled motion. Such combinations of stability and
mobility are difficult to achieve through static pairwise poten-
tials alone. More broadly, any system in which agents generate
and respond to a persistent field such as optical [36, 37], elec-
trical [38], acoustic [39], or chemical [40, 41], could exploit
analogous strategies.
Field-mediated droplets thus provide a minimal physical
realization of a broader design principle: interactions can be
programmed globally in a field and decoded locally by simple
agents. This approach reconciles structural robustness with
dynamical freedom, offering a route to programmable, self-
healing, and reconfigurable active materials. More generally,
it suggests a shift in perspective—from designing interactions
between particles to designing the fields that mediate them.

Methods
Experimental setup
Experiments were performed on a circular bath of diameter
120 mm and fluid depth 10 mm, filled with silicone oil of kine-
matic viscosity 20 cSt (density ρ ≈950 kg m−3). The bath was
mounted on a subwoofer-driven shaking system and driven by
a dual-frequency vertical forcing signal consisting of 80 Hz at
100% amplitude and 40 Hz at 25% amplitude. The waveform
was generated using a RIGOL DG1032Z function generator and
amplified by a SA-PA003 power amplifier. The peak vertical
acceleration γ was measured using an accelerometer attached
to the bath and calibrated relative to the Faraday instability
threshold of γF = 4.2 g where g is the gravitational acceleration.
Droplets were manually deposited using a syringe with
a 2 mm nozzle. Three characteristic size classes were stud-
ied: field-bound droplets (R > 1.2 mm), ordinary superwalk-
ing droplets (R ≈0.7-1.1 mm), and smaller mediator droplets
(R ≈0.5 mm). Droplet radii were measured from high-speed
images during airborne phases when droplets are approximately
spherical.
Horizontal motion was recorded from above at 1200 frames
per second using a Highspeed Vision 300c-U3 camera. Back-
illumination from an LED panel provided high contrast for cen-
troid tracking. Droplet positions were extracted using custom
image-processing routines in MATLAB.
