Finite-Difference Zeta Function Regularisation and Spectral Weighting in Effective Actions
Keisuke Okamura
Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, Tokyo, Japan∗
(Dated: April 14, 2026)
Standard zeta function regularisation enforces a scale-independent prescription for spectral aggregation,
effectively fixing the relative weight of spectral contributions. We relax this constraint by replacing the derivative
at 𝑠= 0 with a finite-difference construction based on 𝜁𝐴(0) and 𝜁𝐴(𝑞−1). In finite systems, it gives rise in
the macroscopic limit to Tsallis-type quantities and a 𝑞-controlled information-geometric structure. In infinite
dimensions, it yields an effective action whose variation 𝛿Γ𝑞= Tr(𝐴−𝑞𝛿𝐴) realises scale-dependent spectral
weighting. Within this framework, zeta function regularisation, effective action, nonextensive scaling, and
information geometry emerge as manifestations of a common principle of finite-difference spectral aggregation.

provides a systematic link between spectral data and physically
meaningful quantities.
Such spectral-to-physical correspondences appear more
broadly across different contexts. In the Casimir effect, spec-
tral differences associated with boundary conditions yield fi-
nite energy shifts [9, 10], while in the spectral action principle
the action itself is defined as a function of the spectrum [11].
It has also been suggested that entropy may admit a spectral
interpretation [12]. In all these cases, physical quantities are
determined by the manner in which spectral data are aggre-
gated.
Despite this broad applicability, the structure of this aggre-
gation is usually taken for granted. In the standard formulation,
it is implicitly fixed by the logarithmic prescription, without
an explicit principle that uniquely selects it.
As a conse-
quence, the relative contribution of different spectral scales is
not treated as an independent degree of freedom. This limi-
tation becomes particularly relevant in systems where spectral
contributions are expected to be scale-dependent.
This raises a fundamental question: is logarithmic aggrega-
tion intrinsically required, or does it represent a special case
within a broader class of admissible spectral constructions?
Relaxing this constraint allows for a broader class of aggre-
gation rules. We replace the local derivative at 𝑠= 0 with a fi-
nite difference between the distinct evaluation points 𝑠= 0 and
𝑠= 𝑞−1. This defines a deformation that probes the spectral
zeta function at a finite separation from 𝑠= 0, thereby cap-
turing spectral structure across scales. This finite-difference
structure can be expressed in terms of the 𝑞-logarithm [13, 14],
yielding a 𝑞-parametrised deformation with the standard 𝑞= 1
case recovered as the undeformed limit.
This formulation yields a controllable spectral weighting,
in which the aggregation procedure determines how different
regions of the spectrum contribute to effective quantities. Reg-
ularisation is thereby reinterpreted as a spectral aggregation
operation rather than merely a technical device for removing
divergences. The choice of aggregation rule thus becomes a
physical degree of freedom, rather than a fixed assumption.
This viewpoint also differs from existing formulations of
non-additive statistics.
The Tsallis-type 𝑞-deformed theory
has been extensively studied in nonextensive statistical me-
chanics [14], leading to generalised distributions and thermo-
dynamic structures [15], axiomatic characterisations [16–18],
and structural classifications of entropy functionals [19, 20],
as well as deformed algebraic frameworks [21, 22]. These
approaches have been successfully applied to systems with
long-range correlations and memory effects, but typically take
𝑞-generalised entropy as their starting point.
In contrast, the present framework starts from microscopic
spectral aggregation, shifting the focus from phenomeno-
logical generalisation to structural origin.
It yields, in the
macroscopic limit, Tsallis-type quantities and a 𝑞-controlled
information-geometric structure, and, in infinite dimensions,
an effective action exhibiting a covariance structure and scale-
dependent spectral weighting. These results establish finite-
difference spectral aggregation as a unifying principle link-
ing zeta function regularisation, effective action, nonextensive
scaling, and information geometry.

Conclusion—In this work, we have reformulated zeta func-
tion regularisation as a finite-difference spectral aggregation
rather than a derivative-based prescription.
This yields a
𝑞-deformed determinant and an effective action exhibiting
a covariance structure and scale-dependent spectral weight-
ing.
In the macroscopic limit, it gives rise to Tsallis-type
quantities and a 𝑞-controlled information-geometric structure.
These results establish finite-difference spectral aggregation
as a unified framework linking zeta function regularisation,
effective action, nonextensive scaling, and information geom-
etry. Rather than constituting independent constructions, they
emerge as distinct manifestations of a common spectral prin-
ciple.
This perspective suggests a new approach to effective ac-
tions in systems with non-uniform spectra, including frac-
tal media, anomalous diffusion systems [30], and operators
such as Laplacians on fractal geometries or non-local effective
Hamiltonians, as well as to spectral regularisation in quan-
tum field theory [4]. More broadly, the present framework
provides a unified spectral interpretation of deviations from
Boltzmann–Gibbs statistics and Shannon entropy. Nonexten-
sivity in systems with long-range correlations and memory
effects, scaling behaviour in fractal media, and spectral dif-
ferences in Casimir-type phenomena may be understood as
responses to deformations of spectral aggregation.
At the same time, the present construction should not be
regarded as the only possible deformation of standard zeta
function regularisation. Alternative replacements of the local
derivative structure may lead to different deformed algebras,
effective actions, determinant-like quantities, and induced ge-
ometries. What distinguishes the present finite-difference con-
struction is that these elements form a coherent framework that
recovers the standard case at 𝑞= 1.
