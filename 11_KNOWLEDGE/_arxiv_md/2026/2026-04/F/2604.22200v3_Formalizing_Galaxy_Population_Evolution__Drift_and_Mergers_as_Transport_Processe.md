Abstract. Galaxy evolution is commonly described through the time evolution of observa-
tional statistics such as luminosity functions and stellar mass functions. However, these quan-
tities are projections of an underlying multivariate galaxy state space rather than fundamental
dynamical variables. We develop a unified framework in which galaxy evolution is formulated
as the time evolution of a probability measure on the galaxy manifold. Representing galaxy
states by latent variables θ ∈M and the population by a density ρ(θ, t), the evolution is gov-
erned by a general equation containing continuous transport and nonlocal jump processes. By
reinterpreting manifold learning as the pushforward of measures, we distinguish observational,
representation, and physical measures, and emphasize that manifold coordinates themselves
need not carry direct physical meaning. In this picture, luminosity functions and stellar mass
functions arise as projected observables of a single underlying dynamics, and generally do
not form closed equations in observational space. The framework contains existing models as
limiting cases: reduction to a single mass variable yields continuity-equation models, while
additive post-merger states recover the Smoluchowski coagulation equation. We further show
that luminosity-function evolution is naturally described within the Schechter family, whose
apparent stability is interpreted as an effective consequence of projection. Since observables
are projections of measures, inference of galaxy evolution becomes a statistical inverse prob-
lem of recovering manifold dynamics from data. This framework shifts the focus from fitting
observed statistics directly to inferring the underlying state-space dynamics, thereby bridging
manifold learning and physical theory.

Introduction
A fundamental challenge in galaxy formation theory lies in identifying the essential state
variables that govern galaxy evolution and, more crucially, the physical laws dictating their
trajectories. Modern surveys characterize galaxies through an extensive suite of observables,
ranging from stellar mass and luminosity to chemical abundances and gas fractions. While
these parameters offer a multifaceted view, their high dimensionality often obscures the under-
lying physical simplicity. Observations reveal galaxies not as a uniform filling of multivariate
space, but as structured point clouds with tight mutual dependencies. Such correlations im-
ply that the effective state space is constrained to a lower-dimensional manifold, M. While
representation learning now provides the numerical tools to extract this geometric structure,
a deeper theoretical question remains: what fundamental evolution equation governs the dy-
namics of galaxy populations upon this inferred manifold?
The objective of this study is to characterize galaxy populations as probability densities
on a manifold M, formulating their temporal evolution through a stochastic framework that
accounts for both continuous internal development and discrete merger events.
By doing
so, we bridge several disparate theoretical pillars within a unified mathematical architecture.
The first is the hierarchical paradigm, where galaxy statistics are fundamentally rooted in the
gravitational growth of dark matter halos. The second is the kinetic theory of mergers, which
utilizes coagulation equations to explain the emergence of mass and luminosity functions from
nonlinear interactions. The third is the phenomenological continuity approach, often employed
to decouple the observational impacts of star formation, quenching, and assembly. Our aim

is to transcend these individual perspectives by deriving a single, self-consistent evolution
equation defined directly on the galaxy manifold.
The foundational premise of galaxy formation theory resides in the gravitational am-
plification of primordial density fluctuations, which dictates the emergence of dark matter
halos. A seminal early synthesis was provided by [1], who integrated primordial instabili-
ties, galaxy clustering, and the mapping between multiplicity and luminosity functions into
a singular conceptual framework. In this classical view, the statistical properties of galaxy
populations are treated primarily as a direct mapping of initial conditions, with mass and
luminosity functions appearing as subsequent deterministic transformations. This lineage was
further solidified by [2] and [3], whose development of the Press–Schechter formalism and its
extensions provided the rigorous basis for describing hierarchical halo growth. Their work es-
tablished the theoretical bedrock for merger trees and semi-analytic models, framing galaxy
assembly as a structured history derived from the underlying cosmic web.
A representative expansion of this paradigm into the realm of baryonic physics is the
semi-analytic formulation by [4]. Their work synthesized halo assembly, radiative gas cooling,
disk formation, and feedback mechanisms into a comprehensive modeling pipeline. In this ar-
chitecture, galaxies evolve as baryonic components within hierarchically merging dark matter
halos, where gas cools to form stars and galaxies coalesce following the mergers of their host
halos. However, these semi-analytic models were primarily structured as a modular assembly
of subgrid prescriptions for disparate physical processes, rather than a singular, self-consistent
statistical equation governing the global distribution of the galaxy population.
Parallel to the hierarchical lineage, another theoretical tradition has focused on the
nonlinear stage of galaxy evolution itself, proposing that mergers are the primary drivers in
shaping mass and luminosity functions. Early foundations were laid by [5] and [6], who in-
vestigated collisions among protogalaxies within nascent clusters. These studies argued that
such dynamical processes fundamentally influence not only mass and luminosity functions
but also observable properties such as colors, metallicities, and the enrichment of the intr-
acluster medium. They described the evolution of the number density N(M, t) through a
Smoluchowski-type coagulation equation with gain and loss terms [7]:

Galaxy Mergers on the Galaxy Manifold: A Smoluchowski-Type Jump
Process
From the discussion in the previous sections, galaxy evolution is described as the temporal
evolution of a probability density ρ(θ, t) on the manifold M.

star formation, gas inflow, feedback, and chemical enrichment, is represented as continuous
transport on the state space. However, galaxy evolution also contains processes that cannot
be expressed solely by such continuous deformations. The representative example of this is

single system appears in a significantly different state. Accordingly, this process should be
described not as a local flow on state space, but as a nonlocal jump from a two-body state

5.2
A Geometric Interpretation of the Downsizing Phenomenon
In the previous subsection, we established that the evolution of observables is described as
the projection of dynamics on the galaxy manifold. This projection is generally not closed
but can be represented through an effective equation. Specifically, the Schechter form of the
luminosity function emerges as an effective description when the manifold evolution equation
becomes approximately self-closing for that specific observable. From this viewpoint, another
fundamental observational feature of galaxy evolution, namely, downsizing, should also be
understood not as an a priori physical law, but as a geometric consequence of projecting
manifold dynamics into observational space2.

that move rapidly across the manifold also cross the “boundary” out of the star-forming region
more quickly. In this sense, the empirical findings of Peng et al. can be understood as a special
case of the present master equation, specifically where the transport term T[ρ] and the loss
term L[ρ] are dynamically coupled through the velocity field.
Our approach also shares conceptual ground with previous theoretical efforts to treat
galaxy population evolution through continuity equations. For instance, Neistein et al. pro-
posed analytic frameworks where the time evolution of the mass function is described by
one-dimensional continuity equations [9, 10]. While those models can describe mass function
evolution once growth and merger rates are specified, the present framework distinguishes
itself by extending the study from a single mass coordinate to the higher-dimensional latent
manifold M spanned by the full physical state of galaxies. By doing so, the evolution of mass
functions, luminosity functions, and color distributions are understood in a unified manner
as multiple projections of a single transport process.
However, as emphasized in the previous subsection, the essential caveat remains: ob-
servables are merely projected quantities. Downsizing appears as an effective phenomenon
resulting from the projection of manifold dynamics, and one cannot uniquely determine the
underlying evolution equation from it alone. For example, the observed evolution of a char-
acteristic mass or luminosity scale could originate from mass-dependent transport, mass-
dependent source/sink terms, or the cumulative effect of mergers. These distinct physical
drivers cannot, in general, be distinguished by examining a single projected observable.
Therefore, even in understanding a phenomenon as ubiquitous as downsizing, the essen-
tial problem is not merely to describe the time evolution of observational statistics, but to
determine how the latent manifold dynamics can be rigorously inferred. Downsizing serves
as both a natural consequence of our framework and a cautionary example of the problems
of projection and non-identifiability. In the following section, we address this challenge by
developing the standpoint of an inverse problem, discussing how the individual components
of the galaxy manifold evolution equation may be inferred from the collective structure of
observational distributions.

Formulation as an Inverse Problem and Implementation for Observa-
tional Estimators
In the preceding sections, we developed a framework describing galaxy evolution as the tem-
poral evolution of a density distribution ρ(θ, t) on the galaxy manifold M. We demonstrated
that observational statistics are obtained as projections of this latent dynamics. Using the
luminosity function as a primary example, we explored the projection of manifold dynam-
ics onto a one-dimensional observable, revealing that the inherent information loss and the
property of invariant families (such as the Schechter form) prevent a unique reconstruction
of the underlying dynamics from a single observable alone. The logical structure of this tran-
sition from latent dynamics to observable data, and the corresponding inverse problem, is
summarized schematically in Fig. 3.
In practical applications, however, we are not presented with idealized distribution func-

errors, incompleteness, and finite-sample fluctuations. Consequently, to translate our theory
into an implementable form, we must formalize the relationship between the manifold evolu-
tion equation and empirical observables as a general inverse problem. This formulation is a
prerequisite for any specific observational analysis.
In this section, we organize the inverse problem by detailing the forward map, the

7
Conclusion and Outlook
In this work, we have constructed a theoretical framework in which galaxy evolution is formu-
lated as a stochastic process on the galaxy manifold M. Within this framework, the physical
state of a galaxy is represented by a latent variable θ ∈M, and the statistical state of the
galaxy population is described by a density distribution ρ(θ, t) on the manifold. By reinter-
preting manifold learning as the pushforward of measures, we have clearly distinguished the
observational measure µ, the representation measure ν = Ψ#µ, and the physical measure
ρ. This positioning allows the theory to treat the time evolution of measures as its primary
object. From this standpoint, the manifold is understood not merely as a physical entity,
but as a coordinate system for representing measures, and the essence of galaxy evolution is
formulated as the dynamics of the measure distributed upon it.
Through this lens, galaxy evolution is described by the general equation:

As a result, observational statistics such as luminosity functions and mass functions are

other words, observational statistics are not independent objects; instead, they are unified as
quantities generated by observational maps acting on the measure ρ. The explicit example

At the same time, the projection-based viewpoint calls for an important reconsideration
of observational interpretation. Classifications that appear static on observational planes do
not necessarily represent the dynamical speed in latent state space directly. The downsizing
phenomenon discussed in this work is a typical example: the fact that massive galaxies
appear red and dead at the present epoch is not inconsistent with the fact that, in the
past, they evolved more rapidly along evolutionary trajectories and reached the quenched
population at earlier times. Consequently, static configurations in observational diagrams
must be distinguished from temporal evolution on the manifold. This point demonstrates
that the appearance of galaxy populations changes depending on the chosen observational
projection, such as the color—magnitude diagram, the stellar mass–star formation rate plane,
or morphological classification diagrams. It implies that a proper understanding of galaxy
evolution requires an explicit treatment of the dynamics in latent state space.
Because projection loses information, it is generally impossible to reconstruct the mani-
fold dynamics uniquely from observational statistics alone. This irreversibility arises naturally
once manifold learning is understood as a pushforward of measures. In this work, we formu-
lated this issue explicitly as an inverse problem and connected theoretical quantities with

observations and theory.
The principal contributions of this work are summarized as follows. First, we formu-
lated galaxy evolution as a stochastic dynamics on the manifold and introduced a unified
equation that incorporates both continuous transport and discrete jump processes. Second,
by reinterpreting manifold learning through the pushforward of measures, we clarified the
three-layer structure consisting of the observational measure µ, the representation measure
ν, and the physical measure ρ. Third, we expressed galaxy mergers as general jump kernels
and systematized their relationship to observational statistics using projection operators.
This framework provides a fundamental methodological reorganization of galaxy evolu-
tion studies. While conventional studies have often modeled the time evolution of observa-
tional statistics, such as luminosity and mass functions, as separate entities, our formulation
understands all such statistics as different projections of a single density distribution ρ(θ, t) on
the galaxy manifold. Accordingly, the evolution of individual statistics is interpreted not as
a set of independent phenomena, but as disparate observational images derived from a com-
mon underlying dynamics. This viewpoint implies a significant methodological shift, moving
from theories that explain the evolution of statistics to theories that infer the fundamental
dynamics on the latent state space.

Several directions are identified for future work. The first is the challenge of estimating
the observational measure µ and its pushforward ν from data, which involves statistically
identifying the density ρ on the manifold alongside the velocity field v and the merger kernel
K. This can be formulated as a statistical inverse problem that integrates manifold learn-
ing with Bayesian inference and machine learning methods. The second direction involves
the physical modeling of the merger kernel K, where it will be necessary to derive concrete
forms incorporating gravitational interactions, environmental dependencies, gas physics, and
chemical enrichment. The third is the connection with numerical simulations; by interpret-
ing galaxy distributions from N-body simulations and semi-analytic models as measures, it
becomes possible to infer the effective parameters of the present framework directly. Further-
more, application to real observations, accounting for time-dependent selection effects and
light-cone effects, remains a vital subject for future study.
More broadly, the framework presented here is not restricted to galaxy evolution, but can
be generalized as a dynamical statistical theory for multivariate data. The perspective of the
time evolution of measures and their subsequent projections provides a universal structure
linking data science and physical theory. In this sense, the present work serves as both a
new formulation of galaxy evolution theory and a step toward a general methodology for
constructing physical theories directly from high-dimensional observational data.
