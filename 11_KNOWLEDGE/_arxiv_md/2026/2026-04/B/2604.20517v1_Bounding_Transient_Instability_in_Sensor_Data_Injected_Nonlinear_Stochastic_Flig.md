Transient instability in nonlinear stochastic dynamical systems constitutes a fundamental
and often overlooked limitation in safety-critical aerospace applications, particularly during
powered descent and landing where failure is driven by finite-time excursions rather than
asymptotic divergence. Consequently, classical notions of mean-square or asymptotic stability
are insufficient for certification and design.
This paper develops a logarithmic-norm-based framework for finite-time transient stability
analysis of nonlinear Itô stochastic differential equations. By extending matrix measures to
nonlinear mappings in a Lipschitz sense, the proposed approach provides a computationally
efficient characterization of instantaneous perturbation growth without requiring local lin-
earization. Application of Itô calculus to the logarithmic perturbation norm yields explicit
bounds on the mean and variance of transient growth, establishing sufficient conditions for
non-positive finite-time mean growth together with probabilistic bounds on transient instability
events.
The analysis reveals a fundamental distinction between mean and sample-path behavior,
showing that stability in expectation does not imply pathwise finite-time safety and that almost-
sure transient stability cannot, in general, be guaranteed in the presence of stochastic diffusion.
The framework is further extended to data-constrained and projected stochastic dynamics
arising in navigation and estimation, revealing a structural trade-off between estimation
consistency and transient robustness induced by continuous data injection.
The approach is demonstrated using flight-like lunar lander descent telemetry. Results show
that trajectories with similar mean behavior can exhibit markedly different transient stability
characteristics, and that mission failure correlates with the accumulation of transient instability
over a short critical time interval. These findings establish transient instability as a structural
property of stochastic, data-driven systems and motivate the use of probabilistic finite-time
stability metrics for the analysis and design of safety-critical autonomous systems.

I. Introduction
Transient instability in nonlinear stochastic dynamical systems constitutes a fundamental limitation in safety-critical
aerospace applications, particularly during powered descent and landing, where failure is driven by finite-time excursions
rather than asymptotic divergence. Classical stability analysis of stochastic systems has largely focused on asymptotic or
moment-based notions of stability, typically established through Lyapunov methods or second-moment bounds [1, 2].
Although mathematically well developed, these notions are insufficient for certification and design in safety-critical
settings, as they do not capture finite-time amplification of perturbations along individual sample paths.
Recent developments in control theory have begun to address finite-time behavior in stochastic systems. Finite-time
stability has been investigated using Lyapunov-based approaches, particularly in discrete-time settings, where conditions
for stability in probability and optimal finite-time stabilization have been established [3]. In parallel, finite-time and
fixed-time stabilization have been studied in nonlinear deterministic systems [4]. However, continuous-time nonlinear
stochastic systems remain significantly more challenging to analyze, and explicit characterizations of finite-time
perturbation growth are still limited.
A complementary perspective is provided by contraction and incremental stability analysis, which characterize
stability through trajectory-wise perturbation growth rather than equilibrium-based arguments. The foundational results
on incremental stability [5] and contraction analysis [6] have been extended to nonlinear systems using differential and
Lyapunov-based frameworks [7, 8]. Extensions to stochastic systems have also been developed, including discrete-time
contraction formulations and differentially positive systems [9, 10]. Recent work has introduced stochastic logarithmic
Lipschitz constants to analyze the contractivity of stochastic differential equations [11] in a limited sense. However, these
approaches primarily establish contractivity conditions and do not provide a finite-time probabilistic characterization of
transient perturbation growth.
Recent developments have explored contraction-based methods for nonlinear systems [12] and finite-time stability
for stochastic systems Itô [13]. While these approaches provide important insights into stability properties, they do not

explicitly capture the finite-time stochastic evolution of perturbations in continuous-time nonlinear systems.
Despite substantial progress in stochastic stability analysis, a critical gap remains in the development of computa-
tionally tractable methods that explicitly characterize finite-time perturbation growth in continuous-time nonlinear Itô
systems. Existing approaches primarily provide asymptotic or moment-based guarantees [1, 2] and do not quantify the
finite-time probabilistic behavior of perturbations, particularly in the presence of stochastic diffusion and data-driven
corrections. In particular, contraction-based and Lyapunov-based methods ensure stability in expectation or in moments
but do not capture transient amplification along individual sample paths over finite horizons, which is often the dominant
mechanism of failure in safety-critical systems.
From an application perspective, modern navigation and estimation architectures introduce additional complexity
through continuous data injection, filtering, and constraint enforcement. In aerospace systems such as powered planetary
descent, guidance strategies based on convex optimization [14] and data-driven sensing approaches [15] operate in
the presence of stochastic disturbances and measurement noise. These systems are more accurately described as
stochastic hybrid or data-constrained dynamical systems, for which stability properties are significantly influenced by
the interaction between intrinsic dynamics and observation-driven corrections [16–18]. In particular, projection and
constraint enforcement mechanisms may introduce non-smooth corrections that alter local contraction properties and
can induce transient amplification even when the underlying dynamics are locally contractive. The impact of such data
injection mechanisms on finite-time transient stability remains insufficiently understood, especially in continuous-time
nonlinear settings.
In strongly transient mission phases such as spacecraft entry, descent, and landing, the primary safety requirement
is the avoidance of unsafe excursions during a finite-duration maneuver rather than asymptotic convergence. In this
regime, systems that are stable in expectation or in moments may nevertheless exhibit significant transient amplification
along individual sample paths [2]. Such events, although probabilistically rare, can have catastrophic consequences
and must therefore be explicitly characterized in analysis and certification. Transient stochastic stability has also been
investigated for linear systems using stochastic logarithmic norms [19], highlighting the importance of finite-time
behavior. Extension of [19] to Lipschitz-type coefficients and Stratonovich formulations is found in [11]. However,
these early versions of stochastic logarithmic norm require the weak derivative of the Wiener process, a restriction on
the growth bound of the perturbation.
To address this gap, this paper develops a logarithmic-norm-based framework for finite-time transient stability
analysis of nonlinear Itô stochastic differential equations without requiring weak derivative assumptions on the Wiener
process. Logarithmic norms, also known as matrix measures in the linear setting, characterize infinitesimal perturbation
growth [20, 21]. By extending this concept to nonlinear mappings in a Lipschitz sense, the proposed formulation
characterizes perturbation dynamics directly at the level of nonlinear stochastic flows without relying on variational
linearization. Unlike classical approaches, the method avoids both local linearization and Lyapunov function construction,

A key outcome of the analysis is a decomposition of transient stability behavior into a mean growth component and
a diffusion-induced variability component, characterized by the quantities 𝛼and 𝛽, respectively. This decomposition
provides a direct link between deterministic contraction properties and stochastic variability in finite-time stability.
Unlike contraction-based approaches, which provide sufficient conditions for incremental stability, the present work
establishes sufficient conditions for non-positive mean transient growth together with probabilistic bounds on transient
instability events. In particular, the analysis shows that stability in expectation does not guarantee pathwise finite-time
safety and that stochastic diffusion fundamentally induces a nonzero probability of transient growth over arbitrarily small
time intervals. This establishes a structural limitation of stochastic stability that is particularly relevant for safety-critical
systems.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
• A logarithmic-norm-based framework for nonlinear Itô stochastic differential equations that characterizes
instantaneous perturbation growth in a Lipschitz sense without requiring local linearization.
• Explicit finite-time bounds on the mean and variance of logarithmic perturbation growth, yielding sufficient
conditions for non-positive mean transient growth together with probabilistic bounds on transient instability events.
• A fundamental limitation result showing that stability in expectation does not guarantee pathwise finite-time
safety, and that stochastic diffusion induces a strictly nonzero probability of transient growth over arbitrarily small
time intervals.
• Extension to data-constrained and projected stochastic dynamics, revealing a structural trade-off between estimation
consistency and transient robustness induced by continuous data injection.
• Demonstration on flight-like lunar lander descent telemetry, showing that systems with similar mean behavior
can exhibit markedly different transient stability characteristics, with failure driven by accumulated transient
instability.
These results indicate that transient instability in nonlinear stochastic systems is not merely a design artifact but a
structural consequence of stochastic diffusion and continuous data injection. This motivates a shift from asymptotic
stability analysis toward probabilistic finite-time stability metrics for the analysis and certification of safety-critical
autonomous systems.

III. Logarithmic Norm in the Lipschitz Sense
This section introduces a logarithmic norm for nonlinear mappings in a Lipschitz sense, extending classical matrix
measures to nonlinear and stochastic settings. The proposed construction provides an efficient characterization of
instantaneous perturbation growth and serves as the central analytical tool for the transient stability analysis developed
in subsequent sections.
Logarithmic norms, also known as matrix measures in the linear setting, were originally introduced in numerical
stability analysis [20] and later adopted in nonlinear systems theory [21]. Throughout this paper, stochastic differential
equations are interpreted in the Itô sense following standard conventions [1].
The constructions in this section are formulated in a finite-difference and upper Dini derivative sense, rather than
requiring classical Fréchet differentiability. This allows the framework to remain valid for locally Lipschitz nonlinear
mappings without invoking variational linearization.
In this setting, the logarithmic norm quantifies the instantaneous worst-case rate of perturbation growth along
admissible perturbation directions, with positive values indicating local expansion and negative values indicating
contraction.

The results reveal a fundamental trade-off in data-driven systems: while data injection improves estimation

Consequently, system design must explicitly balance estimation accuracy and transient robustness, rather than
relying solely on mean stability guarantees. This motivates the use of probabilistic finite-time stability metrics for the
analysis and certification of safety-critical navigation and estimation systems.
Furthermore, these results provide a theoretical explanation for empirically observed instability phenomena in
high-gain or aggressively corrected estimation systems, where improved accuracy can paradoxically increase transient

VII. Discussion and Design Implications
This section distills the practical implications of the transient stability framework developed in Sections III–VI for
the analysis, certification, and design of safety-critical autonomous systems operating under stochastic uncertainty and
continuous data injection.
A. Finite-Time Safety versus Mean Stability
The analysis indicates that finite-time safety cannot, in general, be reliably inferred from asymptotic or mean stability
criteria alone. For safety-critical missions operating in strongly transient regimes, certification objectives may therefore
be formulated in terms of bounded finite-time excursion probabilities rather than asymptotic convergence or moment
stability.
This perspective reframes transient instability as a risk management problem rather than a purely stabilization
problem, aligning stability analysis with operational safety requirements in mission-critical phases such as entry, descent,
and landing.
B. Structural Impact of Data Injection and Projection
Continuous data injection and projection-based correction mechanisms, while essential for estimation consistency
and constraint enforcement, structurally modify the intrinsic contraction properties of the system dynamics and introduce
additional sources of transient amplification. This effect arises from the geometry of the constrained dynamics and is
largely independent of specific algorithmic implementations under the considered modeling assumptions.
Consequently, navigation and estimation architectures should be analyzed at the level of their induced projected
dynamics rather than solely through nominal system models. This observation suggests a trade-off between estimation
consistency and transient robustness that must be explicitly managed in safety-critical systems.
C. Implications for Navigation System Certification
The results motivate a shift in certification philosophy for autonomous navigation systems. In particular:
• certification criteria should incorporate probabilistic finite-time excursion bounds rather than relying solely on
deterministic asymptotic stability guarantees,
• safety margins should be formulated in terms of allowable transient amplification probabilities,
• navigation and estimation architectures should be evaluated under projection-modified dynamics rather than
nominal models,
• online monitoring of transient stability indicators should be integrated into safety supervision layers.
27


## Page 28


Such criteria more accurately reflect the stochastic and data-driven nature of modern navigation systems operating
under continuous data injection.
D. Relation to Contraction-Based Design
Classical contraction theory provides powerful sufficient conditions for incremental stability under deterministic
dynamics [6, 12], and stochastic extensions characterize mean contraction properties [22]. The present framework
complements these approaches by explicitly characterizing finite-time transient amplification mechanisms induced by
stochastic diffusion and observation noise.
This suggests that contraction properties in stochastic systems can be interpreted as average or probabilistic stability
notions rather than guarantees of pathwise behavior in safety-critical regimes.
E. Design Guidelines
From a design standpoint, the analysis motivates the following structural guidelines:
• it is beneficial to prefer bounded and smooth projection operators to mitigate curvature-induced transient
amplification,
• tune data injection gains with explicit consideration of transient risk rather than steady-state estimation accuracy
alone,
• integrate transient stability indicators as real-time risk monitors during critical mission phases,
• couple projection-based estimation with risk-aware control or supervisory logic to attenuate finite-time excursions.
These guidelines identify structural design considerations rather than prescribing specific algorithms and are
applicable across navigation, estimation, and control architectures subject to continuous data injection.
Fundamental Implication
Transient instability in nonlinear stochastic systems can arise as a structural consequence
of stochastic diffusion and continuous data injection over finite-time horizons. Consequently, the objective of analysis
and design shifts from elimination of transient instability to the principled quantification, monitoring, and management
of transient risk in safety-critical autonomous systems.
F. Practical Use of Logarithmic Norm Metrics in GN&C Design
The quantities 𝛼(𝑡) and 𝛽(𝑡) derived in Section V are not intended to provide tight numerical estimates of failure
probability. Instead, they serve as computationally efficient indicators of transient instability risk over finite-time

These quantities correspond directly to the mean and variance bounds on logarithmic perturbation growth developed

These observations support the central insight of the paper: non-positive mean growth does not guarantee

also on variability-driven transient behavior. Consequently, transient instability must be quantified and managed using
finite-time, probabilistic stability measures rather than relying solely on asymptotic or expectation-based criteria.

IX. Conclusion
This paper develops a logarithmic-norm-based framework for finite-time transient stability analysis of nonlinear
Itô stochastic differential equations, with direct relevance to safety-critical autonomous navigation and estimation. By
extending classical matrix measures to nonlinear mappings in a Lipschitz sense, the framework provides a computationally
efficient characterization of instantaneous perturbation growth and enables finite-time analysis without reliance on
Lyapunov constructions or local linearization.
Explicit bounds on the mean and variance of logarithmic perturbation growth are derived, yielding sufficient
conditions for non-positive mean transient growth together with probabilistic bounds on transient instability events. The
analysis establishes a fundamental distinction between mean and pathwise behavior: stability in expectation does not
guarantee pathwise finite-time safety, and almost-sure transient stability cannot, in general, be ensured in the presence
of stochastic diffusion. The quantities 𝛼and 𝛽provide a natural decomposition of these effects, characterizing mean
contraction and diffusion-induced variability, respectively.
The framework is further extended to data-constrained and projected stochastic dynamics arising in navigation
and estimation. The results show that continuous data injection structurally modifies contraction properties and
introduces additional diffusion, thereby increasing the likelihood of transient amplification. This reveals an inherent,
algorithm-independent trade-off between estimation consistency and transient robustness.
Numerical results using flight-like lunar lander descent telemetry demonstrate that finite-time perturbation growth
provides early indicators of instability mechanisms not captured by conventional mean stability metrics, and that failure
correlates with the accumulation of transient instability over a short critical time window.
Practical Implications for GN&C Design
The results suggest several actionable considerations for guidance,
navigation, and control system design:
• monitor the transient stability indicator 𝛼(𝑡) as a real-time measure of finite-time instability risk,
• limit aggressive data injection or high-gain corrections during critical mission phases to reduce projection-induced
diffusion and transient amplification,
• use cumulative transient instability as a diagnostic metric for identifying high-risk trajectories,
• incorporate transient stability screening as a pre-processing step to reduce Monte Carlo simulation burden.
Final Implication
Transient instability in nonlinear stochastic systems is not merely a modeling artifact or controller
deficiency, but a structural consequence of stochastic diffusion and continuous data injection over finite-time horizons.
Consequently, the objective of analysis and design for safety-critical autonomous systems must shift from eliminating
transient instability to quantifying, monitoring, and managing transient risk.
Future work will focus on embedding the proposed transient stability metrics into real-time risk-aware navigation,
