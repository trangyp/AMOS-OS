Introduction
The purpose of this paper is to construct a biquantization of the necklace Lie bialgebra of
quivers. Let Q be a quiver and Q be its double, which means doubling the edges of Q but
with an opposite orientation. It was proved by Ginzburg [4] and independently by Bocklandt
and LeBruyn [1] that the cyclic paths of Q, called the necklaces, have a Lie algebra structure.
Later Schedler showed in [7] that the necklaces also have a Lie cobracket, which is a Lie
cocycle, and thus form a Lie bialgebra. Moreover, Schedler constructed a Hopf algebra which
quantizes such a Lie bialgebra, which is inspired by the quantization of the Lie bialgebra of
loops (i.e., the homotopy classes of closed paths) on Riemann surfaces (see Turaev [8] with
some preceding work due to Goldman [5]).
Recall that, given a Lie bialgebra g, its symmetric product S(g) has an induced Lie bracket
and cobracket, which is in fact a bi-Poisson bialgebra. We thus may first deform the algebra
structure of S(g) in the direction of the Lie bracket, and then deform the coalgebra structure
on the resulted algebra in the direction of the Lie cobracket, or vice versa. A priori, these
two algebras may not be the same, and if they are, we say such an algebra is a biquantization
of L.
In this sense, Schedler’s Hopf algebra is only a (co)quantization of the necklaces in the
direction of the Lie cobracket, and is different from the common definition of quantization of
Lie bialgebras in the literature (see, for example, [3] and §2 below). It is natural to expect
that the quantizations of necklaces in both directions exist; moreover, they are compatible in
some sense.
In a series of papers [3], Etingof and Kazhdan constructed a quantization of (both fi-
nite and infinite dimensional) Lie bialgebras, by applying the Drinfeld associator on the
corresponding vector spaces. Later in [6], Kassel and Turaev continued to construct a bi-
quantization of finite dimensional Lie bialgebras. Of course, we can apply these two results
to necklace Lie bialgebras and obtain their biquantization; the only issue is probably to gen-
eralize Kassel-Turaev’s biquantization to infinite dimensional Lie bialgebras, which seems not
so difficult.
In this paper, we construct a biquantization of the necklace Lie bialgebra, via Schedler’s
construction. The advantage, compared to the above proposal, is that his construction is
quite explicit and combinatorial, and does not use Drinfeld’s associator, which remains a
mystery to us.
1.1
Main result
For a finite quiver Q, the set of necklaces is the commutator quotient space (kQ)♮:=
kQ/[kQ, kQ], where kQ is the path algebra of Q over a field k of characteristic zero. The Lie
bracket is obtained by removing the crossing edges of two necklaces and then re-connecting
the rest, and the Lie cobracket is realized by cutting a necklace in its self-crossing edges and
then reconnecting the two components (see Definition 2.3 below for more details). Denote
by L the necklace Lie bialgebra of Q.
According to Turaev [8], there are two bialgebras εℏ(Lℏ) and Vh(Lh) that coquantizes and
quantizes the symmetric product S(L) of L. We introduce the two canonical morphisms

1.2
Organization of the paper
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 is a review of the necessary background on the
necklace Lie bialgebra, and provides definition for the related quantization, coquantization
bialgebras Vh(Lh) and εℏ(Lℏ). Next, the two-parameter algebra N(Q)h,ℏis constructed in

synthesize the preceding contents and prove the above theorem.

2.1
Lie bialgebra and its symmetric products
Recall the definition of Lie bialgebras.
Definition 2.1. A Lie coalgebra over k is a k-module g equipped with a linear morphism
ν : g →g ⊗g such that
(1) (Skew-symmetry) Perm ◦ν = −ν, and
(2) (Co-Jacobi identity) (τ 2 + τ + 1) ◦(id ⊗ν) ◦ν = 0,
where Perm(a ⊗b) = b ⊗a and τ : a ⊗b ⊗c 7→c ⊗a ⊗b for all a, b, c ∈g. The morphism ν is
called the Lie cobracket in g.
The Lie algebra structure, together with the Lie coalgebra structure, defines a Lie bial-
gebra structure if the cocycle condition is satisfied.
Definition 2.2 (Drinfeld [2, Section 3]). Suppose that g is a k-module. Then g is called a
Lie bialgebra over k if it is provided with a Lie bracket [−, −] and a Lie cobracket ν so that
for any a, b ∈g they satisfy the cocycle condition,
ν([a, b]) = aν(b) + ν(a)b,
where the action of a ∈g on b ⊗c ∈g ⊗g is given by a(b ⊗c) = [a, b] ⊗c + b ⊗[a, c].

The Poisson and co-Poisson structures
Lie bialgebras naturally arise from Poisson-Lie groups (see [2]).
We next describe some
algebraic structures on the symmetric algebra of a Lie bialgebra.
Definition 2.4. (1) A Poisson algebra is a commutative associative algebra equipped with

(2) A co-Poisson coalgebra A over a field k is a cocommutative coassociative coalgebra

Remark 6.3 (Why is the quantization so complicated?). Our proof of the quantization in
Theorem 4.4 is quite long and complicated. It is inspired by Turaev’s technical work (see [8,
§12-15]). The necklaces are not completely identical to the loops on Riemann surfaces. Our
situation is even more complicated in the sense that, for loops on surfaces, we may assume
they are in generic position, but this is not the case for necklaces: as in Section 4.3 (right
after Step 1), for an edge ei ∈TX,Y ∩PX, its reverse e∗
i ∈Q may appear multiple times in PY .
Concretely, in the above example, the edge a in α corresponds to two a∗’s in β; this makes
∆(Y ) not so easy to compute. We had to deal with such a situation carefully, especially in
Steps 2 and 4 in Section 4.3. We thus have worked out all the details for completeness.
