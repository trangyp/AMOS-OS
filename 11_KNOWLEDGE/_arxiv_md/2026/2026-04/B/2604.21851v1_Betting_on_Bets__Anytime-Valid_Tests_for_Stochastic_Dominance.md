Abstract
How can we monitor, in real time, whether one uncertain prospect has any upside over
another? To answer this question, we develop a novel family of sequential, anytime-valid
tests for stochastic dominance (SD; also known as stochastic ordering), a classical and popular
notion for comparing entire distribution functions. The problem is distinct from the pop-
ular problem of testing for dominance in means, which would not capture distributional
differences beyond the first moment. We first derive powerful, nonparametric e-processes
that quantify evidence against the null hypothesis that one prospect is dominated by an-
other. For first-order SD, these e-processes are constructed as a mixture of asymptotically
growth-rate optimal e-variables and yield a test of power one. The approach further gen-
eralizes to sequential testing for SD beyond the first order, including any higher-order SD.
Empirically, we demonstrate that the resulting sequential tests are competitive with existing
non-sequential SD tests in terms of power, while achieving validity under continuous moni-
toring that existing methods do not. Finally, we sketch the complementary and challenging
problem of testing the non-SD null hypothesis, which asks whether a prospect has a definite
upside, and describe the conditions under which we can derive a nontrivial anytime-valid
test.

Introduction
Stochastic dominance (SD), also known as stochastic ordering, is a classical framework for comparing an
arbitrary pair of random variables (see, e.g., Müller and Stoyan, 2002; Shaked and Shanthikumar, 2007).
In its simplest form, first-order stochastic dominance (FSD) states that the cumulative distribution func-
tion (CDF) of one random variable lies entirely below that of another random variable; higher-order SD
notions generalize this idea to integrals of the CDFs. Yet, SD is far more complex and nonparametric
than dominance in means; in fact, no finite number of moment inequalities implies FSD or higher-order
SD (Brockett and Kahane, 1992). SD is a cornerstone in economics and decision theory (e.g., Fishburn,
1970; Levy, 1992; Müller and Stoyan, 2002; Whang, 2019), where a decision maker (DM) is tasked with
comparing uncertain prospects under general classes of utility functions.
Recent advances in sequential inference, particularly in the theory of sequential, anytime-valid in-
ference (SAVI) based on e-processes (Shafer et al., 2011; Ramdas et al., 2020, 2023; Grünwald et al., 2024),
motivate us to revisit the problem of SD testing. While most classical SD tests are fixed-sample tests with
asymptotic validity (e.g., McFadden, 1989; Barrett and Donald, 2003; Linton et al., 2010), the modern
framework of SAVI warrant a new look at SD testing because the framework is particularly well-suited
to SD testing. First, SD testing is popular in applications where the data is inherently sequential—after
all, the goal is to compare and order uncertain prospects in the future based on past data. This makes
it desirable to use SAVI methods, which provably control the type I error under optional stopping (at
adaptively chosen sample sizes). Second, e-processes are natural candidates for handling the composite
and nonparametric nature of SD testing problems; as we shall see in this paper, these methods do not
require any parametric assumptions on the distributions being compared. We will also see, particularly
in Section 6, that SD testing itself poses unique challenges to the existing SAVI stack, as it will require
both constructing new e-processes and rethinking the roles of null and alternative hypotheses.
In this paper, we develop anytime-valid tests and e-processes for various types of SD testing problems
where the data arrives sequentially over time. To ground our discussion in real-world applications, we
now introduce our first motivating example.
Example 1 (Testing out a new treatment: Is there an upside?). Consider the problem of deploying a
new treatment 𝑌into the market. This could be an app piloting a new subscription plan; an employer
testing out a new technology stack; a sports team manager scouting a new player to trade for; and so
on. The DM wants to know whether the performance of 𝑌, as measured by a metric of interest, has any
upside over an existing treatment 𝑋(e.g., the baseline subscription plan, the most popular tech stack,
or the candidate replacement player). As the new treatment is deployed, the DM wants to monitor the
data over time and collect evidence on this upside. If there is sufficient evidence, the DM can decide to
keep or adopt 𝑌as an option; otherwise, the DM can decide to drop 𝑌and stick with the existing ones.
Setting aside the practical nuances of each application, the core statistical problem common to all
scenarios in Example 1 is to test for an “upside” of one uncertain outcome over another. We may for-

such as Barrett and Donald (2003); Linton et al. (2010). In Section 5, we include multiple simulation

particular, the resulting tests are empirically more powerful against the SD null than baseline methods

the aforementioned classical SD tests that do not come with anytime-valid guarantees.

this case, the null and alternative hypotheses are swapped, making the null much “larger” and thus the
inference problem far more challenging (Davidson and Duclos, 2013). We will first discuss the difficulty
of this problem and then derive conditions under which we can still derive non-trivial anytime-valid
tests using existing tools. Finally, Section 7 concludes with a discussion of extensions and limitations,
and the Appendix contains proofs, additional results, and further extensions.

Related Work on (Testing) Stochastic Dominance
The literature on SD is vast, dating back at least to Hardy et al. (1929) and Karamata (1932) who studied
the problem of comparing two nonnegative vectors in ℝ𝑑. In statistics, some classical methods study
testing problems (Mann and Whitney, 1947; Lehmann, 1951) are related to, but distinct from, fully
nonparametric SD testing. Many modern testing methods for SD comes from the broader economics
and decision theory literature. There, the focus is on the conditions under which decision makers,
equipped with a partially specified utility function, would prefer one uncertain prospect (or a risky
asset) over another, under the classical expected utility hypothesis (von Neumann and Morgenstern,
1947; Savage, 1951; Fishburn, 1970). See, e.g., Hadar and Russell (1969); Hanoch and Levy (1969); Levy
(1992); Mosler and Scarsini (1993); Müller and Stoyan (2002); Shaked and Shanthikumar (2007) for
general characterizations of SD in this context.

Sequentially testing for definite upside: The non-SD null hypothesis
Up until now, we have focused on testing the SD null hypothesis (1), the rejection of which informs
the DM that 𝑌has an upside over 𝑋. However, in some contexts, the DM may need a stronger form
of evidence that 𝑌is definitely better than 𝑋in a given order, such that rejection implies 𝑌is definitely
superior to 𝑋in that order. This happens when there is an “incumbent” option 𝐴and a “challenger”
option 𝐵over a single slot, as illustrated in the following example:
Example 3 (Online A/B testing: Should I replace the incumbent design?). The experimentation team at
a large tech company is running an online A/B test to compare a new interface 𝐵against the incumbent
design 𝐴. It aims to evaluate the two designs on a user metric of choice, say, the level of user engagement

that 𝐵is definitely better than (i.e., stochastically dominates) 𝐴. The company proceeds to randomly
assign users to either design 𝐴or 𝐵and collect their scores.
This is a topical problem and anytime-valid methods have found practical use in online A/B testing

an unnecessary deployment of a suboptimal design (e.g., Johari et al., 2022; Lindon and Malek, 2022;
Maharaj et al., 2023). Unlike Example 1, here the rejection event has to affirm the SD relation, as the

Summary and Discussion
We presented a comprehensive study of anytime-valid testing procedures for stochastic dominance
based on e-processes. The resulting sequential tests remain valid under optional stopping for first-
and higher-order SD as well as for more general integral stochastic orders, such as the increasing con-
vex order and infinite-order SD. For first-order SD, we derived growth-rate optimal e-processes and
established an asymptotic power-one guarantee by predictably mixing over thresholds. Methodologi-
cally, this construction draws on techniques from online learning where we employ adaptive weighting
strategies such as exponential weighting allowing to allocate betting capital adaptively across thresh-
olds where violations of SD may occur. For higher-order SD, we leverage recent advances on universal
portfolio-based betting strategies that asymptotically achieve the log-optimal growth rate. Empirical re-
sults from a range of simulation settings illustrate that the resulting procedures perform well in practice,
detecting violations of SD efficiently even in challenging scenarios.
A central feature of our approach is that it is entirely distribution-free. In particular, we impose
no parametric assumptions on the marginal distributions of 𝑋and 𝑌, nor do we require independence
between them. Unlike several classical approaches that rely on strong distributional assumptions, our
methods remain valid even under maximal dependence between the samples, as confirmed by our
simulation experiments including strongly negatively correlated settings.
We view the present work as a first systematic step toward sequential testing procedures for SD.
Several important directions remain open for future research. In Section 6, we briefly sketched how
our methods extend to the more challenging task of affirming SD, which is particularly important as
it more directly guides the decision maker. However, it was shown that this problem is fundamen-
tally difficult and that tests for affirming SD typically cannot achieve power against the full alternative
without additional assumptions. This difficulty is one of motivations for earlier works on relaxed SD
notions, such as almost stochastic dominance (Leshno and Levy, 2002; Baıllo et al., 2024) and restricted
stochastic dominance (Davidson and Duclos, 2013). Developing powerful, anytime-valid procedures
for such relaxed notions of SD remains an interesting direction for future work. Furthermore, while
SD admits well-established multivariate generalizations (Marshall, 1991; Shaked and Shanthikumar,
2007), we restricted attention to the univariate case for clarity.
In closing, we refer the reader to Appendix H for additional extensions of our methods. First, we
provide a brief sketch of how our methods extend to multivariate stochastic orders and leave a more
comprehensive treatment for future work. We furthermore discuss sensible choices of the SD null for
data with time-varying marginal distributions based on the notion of a time-varying CDF, inspired by
Mineiro and Howard (2023), and how our methods readily extend to settings when the data are no
longer observed in pairs.
