The bulk and surface electronic structure of MoAlB(010) is studied by a combination of angle-
resolved photoemission spectroscopy and density functional calculations. The observed bulk Fermi-
level crossings agree with the previously reported bulk Fermi surface of the material. Additionally, we
find several surface states in the wide projected bulk band gaps around the Fermi energy. The surface
states differ in their stability under residual-gas exposure in the vacuum system and in the magnitude
of their Rashba-type spin–orbit splitting. We explain this in terms of their elemental and orbital
character. A surface state arising from Al dangling bonds is sensitive to surface contamination,
whereas a mainly Mo-derived surface state exhibits the stronger spin–orbit splitting. The surface
states show symmetry-enforced crossings near the ¯S point of the surface Brillouin zone. These are
protected by the mirror-symmetry elements of the p2mm wallpaper group.
INTRODUCTION
MAB phases are ternary compounds composed of tran-
sition metals (M), an A-group element (typically from
groups 13 or 14, e.g. Al or Si), and boron (B) [1].
They form a family of layered materials closely related
to the MAX phases [2], where X denotes carbon or ni-
trogen.
Like MAX phases, MAB compounds combine
metallic properties—such as good electrical and ther-
mal conductivity—with ceramic characteristics, includ-
ing a high Young’s modulus, high melting point, and
resistance to oxidation; these make them attractive for
a range of applications [1].
As in MAX phases, MAB
materials crystallize as natural heterostructures in which
strongly bonded planar MB units are interleaved with
more weakly bonded A layers. This layered bonding hi-
erarchy not only underlies the mechanical and transport
properties of MAB phases, but also gives rise to a rich
electronic structure. Variations in synthesis conditions
allow control over the stacking of the structural units.
The chemical difference between the A and MB layers
can be exploited to transform MAB crystals into two-
dimensional MBenes via etching [3].
The crystal structure of the MAB phase MoAlB is
shown in Fig. 1(a) and (b).
MoAlB crystallizes in
the non-symmorphic Cmcm space group [4]. The crys-
tal structure is conventionally described using the or-
thorhombic unit cell indicated by the black lines in the
figure. Alternatively, a unit cell of half the size can be
chosen (red lines).
We refer to these unit cells as the
conventional cell and the primitive cell, respectively, and
we use the conventional cell unless stated otherwise. The
MoAlB structure consists of layers of side-sharing BMo6
trigonal prisms that alternate with double Al square lat-
tices along the crystallographic b axis.
In contrast to
many MAX carbides and nitrides, MoAlB exhibits a pro-
nounced in-plane structural anisotropy arising from zig-
zag B chains running along the a axis. Together with the
out-of-plane anisotropy imposed by the layered structure,
this leads to a strong three-dimensional anisotropy in the
electronic, vibrational, and transport properties [5]. Den-
sity functional theory calculations indicate a predomi-
nantly covalent character of the Mo–B and B–B bonds,
whereas the Mo–Al and Al–Al bonds are largely metallic
in nature. The electronic states forming the Fermi sur-
face are mainly derived from Mo and Al orbitals, and the
structural anisotropy is reflected in a highly anisotropic,
largely two-dimensional Fermi surface comprising many
sheets according to both calculations [5–8] and quantum
oscillations [9, 10].
An additional important aspect of MoAlB is its non-
symmorphic Cmcm space group symmetry, which has di-
rect consequences for both the bulk and surface electronic
structure. The non-symmorphic character of MoAlB de-
rives from the presence of glide planes and screw axes
defined when considering the primitive unit cell.
The
common choice to adopt the conventional unit cell in-
troduces additional symmetries, which are combinations
of a point-group symmetry and fractional translations
along the b axis. We stress that these symmetry oper-
ations are, however, not proper non-symmorphic oper-
ations, since the involved translations can be described
by the translation vectors spanning the primitive lattice.
Overall, this leads to a situation where the use of a non-

In this paper, we present a comprehensive investi-
gation of the bulk and surface electronic structure of
MoAlB(010) using a combination of angle-resolved pho-
toemission spectroscopy (ARPES) and first-principles
calculations.
The measurements confirm the strongly
metallic character of MoAlB and are in agreement with
previous calculations and experimental studies of its
Fermi surface. A central result of this work is the iden-
tification of strongly surface-localized electronic states
within wide projected bulk band gaps and the charac-
terization of these states with respect to their elemental
and orbital character, as well as their symmetry.
METHODS
Millimeter-sized MoAlB single crystals were synthe-
sized using a flux method via the following procedure.
Mo powder (300 mesh), Al powder (300 mesh), and amor-
phous B powder were used as starting materials. The
raw materials were mixed according to a molar ratio of
Mo:Al:B = 3:21:1.
The excess Al and Mo were intro-
duced to act as an Al and Mo–Al alloy flux. The mixed
powders were loaded and heated in a furnace under an
Ar atmosphere. The temperature was first increased to
1550 ◦C at a rate of 5 ◦C/min and maintained for 4 hours,
followed by cooling to 1200 ◦C at 1 ◦C/min, and then fur-
ther cooling to room temperature at 10 ◦C/min. After
synthesis, the obtained solidified product was placed in
3 mol/L HCl and heated to 60 ◦C to dissolve most of the
flux. The remaining residual flux was then rapidly re-
moved using 9 mol/L HCl. Finally, the resulting MoAlB
crystals were alternately washed with deionized water
and acetone, and then dried.
The resulting crystals
are brittle and typically exhibit large, flat facets with
a metallic luster.
Micro-ARPES experiments were performed at the
I05 beamline of Diamond Light Source. Samples were
mounted using silver epoxy, top-posted, and cleaved in
situ at a temperature of approximately 25 K. ARPES
data were acquired at 25 K using a focused light spot
with a diameter of approximately 5 µm. The light po-
larization was linear horizontal. The angular and energy
resolutions were 0.2◦and 30 meV, respectively. The pres-
sure during the measurements was 1.0·10−10 mbar.
Electronic structure calculations were performed us-
ing density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in
the GPAW code [11]. The exchange–correlation potential
was treated within the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof approx-
imation [12]. Both bulk and slab geometries were initially
constructed using the Atomic Simulation Environment
[13], employing lattice parameters taken from Ref. [4].
The slab model consists of two out-of-plane layers of the
conventional bulk unit cell. Both bulk and slab struc-
tures were fully relaxed and subsequently symmetrized
according to the Cmcm and P21/m space groups, re-
spectively.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Surface termination
The weakest bonding in the MoAlB structure occurs
between adjacent Al layers, suggesting that the natural
cleavage plane lies between these layers, perpendicular to
the crystallographic b axis. Cleaving the crystal to form
the MoAlB(010) surface is thus expected to result in a
single surface termination, as opposed to, e.g., a mixture
of Al and Mo terminations. To verify this, we examine
the spatial homogeneity of the Al 2p and Mo 4p core
level spectra, as well as the valence-band signal, across
the sample surface. Over lateral distances of several hun-
dred µm, the spectra show only minor variations, indi-
cating a largely homogeneous surface. Throughout the
scanned area, the Al 2p core level signal is systematically
much more intense than the Mo 4p signal, with an inten-
sity ratio of ≈500 (for the core level spectra see the on-

Bulk electronic structure
The surface BZ of MoAlB(010), sketched in Fig. 1(c), is
a rectangle with nearly equal side lengths; its dimensions
are ¯Γ −¯X = 1.013 ˚A−1 and ¯Γ −¯Y = 0.978 ˚A−1. First-
principles calculations predict a bulk Fermi surface com-
posed of six sheets with predominantly two-dimensional
character, extending mainly along the Γ–X direction of
the conventional bulk Brillouin zone [5, 7]. Figure 2(a)
displays the photoemission intensity at the Fermi energy
EF throughout the surface BZ, and Fig. 2(b) shows a
zoom-in of the bulk Fermi surface structures measured
with a lower photon energy. The data confirm the pres-
ence of the predicted manifold of bulk Fermi-level cross-
ings near ¯Γ and along the ¯Γ–¯X direction. In contrast,
the circular features observed around the ¯S and ¯Y points

do not follow the predicted bulk dispersion [5, 7]. The
simplest explanation for this discrepancy is to attribute
these features to electronic surface states formed after
sample cleavage. This scenario is discussed in the follow-
ing subsection.
Here we focus on the bulk electronic structure. Fig-
ures 2(c) and (e) present an overview of the electronic dis-
persion along the ¯Γ–¯Y and ¯Γ–¯X directions, respectively,
over a broad energy range. Zoomed-in details around ¯Γ
measured with lower photon energies are shown in pan-
els (d) and (f). Several well-defined quasiparticle bands
can be seen, including various hole-like bands crossing
EF along ¯Γ–¯X (see panel (f)), and an apparent Dirac-
like band crossing at the ¯X point near EF (panel (e)).
Other features are substantially broader, making it diffi-
cult to unambiguously trace the dispersion of the visible
bands and to identify the potentially numerous Fermi-
level crossings. One example is a wide region of high pho-
toemission intensity at EF along ¯Γ–¯X close to ¯X (panel
(e)). A similar strong broadening can be observed for the
completely filled hole-like band at ¯Γ just below EF.
Comparing the data to DFT calculations does in prin-
ciple require knowledge of the crystal momentum k⊥per-
pendicular to the surface, which is not conserved in the
photoemission process [15] and, moreover, suffers from
a significant uncertainty due to the small inelastic mean
free path of the electrons [16]. In the present case, this is
particularly severe because the small size of the bulk BZ
in the k⊥direction (≈0.44 ˚A−1) means that even a small
k⊥broadening can correspond to an integration over the
entire BZ in that direction. In order to account for this,
we compare the data not to a calculation for a particular
k⊥but to the projection of the bulk band structure onto
the (010) surface, using only a small number of k⊥points
in order not to obscure the experimental data. Such a
calculation is overlaid on the left-hand side of the exper-
imental spectra shown in Fig. 2(c) to (f). Full bulk band
structure calculations for the primitive and conventional
unit cells are given in the SM [14].
The agreement between ARPES results and calcula-
tions near EF is very good. The Dirac-like crossing near
¯X is well described by DFT. The observed hole-like struc-
ture just below EF at ¯Γ also matches the continuum of
calculated bands extremely well, indicating k⊥smearing
across the entire BZ for this band. This k⊥smearing also
explains the high photoemission intensity at EF near ¯X,
which is due to multiple Fermi-level crossings at different
k⊥values. Some of the calculated bands, on the other
hand, show very little k⊥dispersion. These appear as
sharp bands, in contrast to the broad continua of states
elsewhere.
Note, for example, the sharp hole-like fea-
ture at ¯Γ that crosses the Fermi level at a wave vector
k ∼0.2 ˚A−1 along the ¯Γ–¯X direction. Other examples
can be found near the ¯Y point: The two bands crossing
at E −EF ≈−1.5 eV, for instance, are essentially two-
dimensional. The same is true for the band at −1 eV, but
this loses its two-dimensional character as it approaches
¯Γ. Consistent with this, in the experiment it is only seen
as an identifiable feature near ¯Y.
The dense coverage of an area in E–k space by cal-
culated bands is not necessarily due to k⊥dispersion.
It can also be caused by the presence of many nearly
two-dimensional bands in that region.
This is almost
certainly the case for the Fermi-level crossings close to ¯Γ
in the ¯Γ–¯X direction in Fig. 2(f). While the calculated
bands appear to form a broad continuum, the experimen-
tal results on the right-hand side show at least four sharp
bands, giving rise to well-defined quasi-two-dimensional
Fermi surfaces, which are also seen in Fig. 2(b). A Fermi
surface consisting of many sheets of predominantly two-
dimensional character, extended along the Γ–X direction
of the bulk Brillouin zone, is also in very good agreement
with earlier DFT calculations [5, 7]. Indeed, these cal-
culations predict all Fermi surface elements except one
to be nearly “tubes” in the Γ–Z direction of the con-
ventional zone. The only Fermi surface element with a
distinct three-dimensional character is a pocket along the
¯Γ–¯Y direction (sheet 4 in Ref. [7] and band 5 in Ref. [5]).
This pocket is also observed here in ARPES, not as dis-
tinct crossings but as a weak feature marked with a white
arrow in Fig. 2(b). Its three-dimensional character is in-
dicated by the fact that this pocket is visible when mea-
sured with 32.5 eV photon energy but is not detectable
with 62 eV light. The diffuse ARPES signal of this fea-
ture can be ascribed to k⊥smearing.
Surface electronic structure
A closer comparison of our ARPES data with bulk
band structure calculations in Fig. 2(c) reveals that not
all experimental features are accounted for by the bulk
calculations. At ¯Y, a shallow electron pocket near EF,
corresponding to the feature giving rise to the Fermi con-
tour around the ¯Y point in Fig. 2(a), and a cross-like
structure about 3 eV below EF clearly fall outside the
bulk band projection. This provides strong experimen-
tal evidence for the surface-localized character of these
states. This assignment is also supported by DFT. Fig-
ure 3(a) shows the projection of the bulk band structure
onto the (010) surface (grey), together with the calcu-
lated surface state dispersion (blue). Both calculations
include spin–orbit coupling. A similar plot over a larger
energy range is available in Fig. S5 of the SM [14]. The
calculated surface bands closely match the experimen-
tally observed dispersions shown in Fig. 3(b), confirming
that the previously discussed features are indeed surface
states.

ing the Fermi contours around the ¯S and ¯Y points in
Fig. 2(a). The calculations in Fig. 3(a) reveal that they
consist of two pairs of nearly degenerate bands. The first

From this analysis, a clear distinction between the
two surface states emerges, providing a plausible ex-
planation for their markedly different chemical stabil-
ity. Simple metal surfaces such as Al are well known to
be highly reactive, even under ultra–high vacuum con-
ditions, where residual gases—most notably water—can
lead to chemical modification of under-coordinated sur-
face sites [20, 21]. The pronounced dangling-bond char-
acter of the S2 state is therefore consistent with its rapid
degradation after cleavage. Al, being the lighter element,
S2 is also expected to show the smaller spin–orbit split-
ting. By contrast, the higher stability of the Mo-derived
S1 state suggests that, at the low temperatures of the
experiment, surface degradation does not proceed appre-
ciably beyond the Al surface termination. At elevated
temperatures, it has been suggested that MoAlB may
be protected against oxidation by the segregation of Al
atoms to the surface and the formation of a stable, passi-
vating aluminum oxide layer, analogous to the behavior
of pure aluminum [4].
Despite the reduction of symmetry at the surface, cer-
tain crystalline symmetries remain and continue to con-
strain the surface state dispersion.
In particular, the
(100) and (001) mirror symmetries of the p2mm wallpa-
per group of the MoAlB(010) surface protect the cross-
ings of the surface states along the ¯S–¯X and ¯S–¯Y high-
symmetry lines, as shown in the magnified dispersions in
Fig. 3(a). For wave vectors along these directions, the
little group is isomorphic to the Cs point group. Surface
states S1 and S2 belong to the A′ and A′′ irreducible rep-
resentations of this group, respectively; thus, the Hamil-
tonian matrix elements between them vanish, and a band
crossing is allowed. Along the ¯S–¯Γ direction, by contrast,
only the identity operation remains in the little group,
so that both states belong to the same trivial represen-
tation and a small gap opens due to hybridization be-
tween the surface-state branches. Notably, the avoided-
crossing gap remains very small, which we attribute to
the markedly different elemental and orbital character of
the involved states, as discussed above. The dispersion in

CONCLUSION
We have presented a combined experimental and theo-
retical investigation of the bulk and surface electronic
structure of MoAlB(010).
The bulk exhibits a clear
metallic character and Fermi surfaces in agreement with
our calculations, previous theoretical work, and exper-
imental results from quantum oscillations.
The (010)
surface supports metallic surface states within wide pro-
jected bulk band gaps. At the Fermi energy, two such
surface states, S1 and S2, are observed around the ¯S and
¯Y points. A detailed analysis of their orbital character
reveals that S1 and S2 are mainly derived from Mo and Al
atoms near the surface, respectively. This is consistent
with their different sensitivity to residual-gas exposure
in the vacuum chamber and with the magnitude of their
Rashba spin–orbit splitting. The surface-state degener-
acy along certain high-symmetry directions is protected
by the residual mirror symmetries of the (010) surface
lattice.
