Abstract – Finite-amplitude gravity waves at the air-water interface induce net fluid
and particle transport, known as Stokes drift. While this mechanism is well understood
for steady waves, transport under unsteady, evolving conditions remains poorly char-
acterized. Here, we investigate Lagrangian transport in freely decaying waves using
high-resolution two-phase simulations and a perturbative analytical model. Wave de-
cay modifies the classical Lagrangian drift by introducing both first- and second-order
corrections in the wave amplitude expansion, and generates a net vertical transport,
governed by the balance between inertia and viscosity.
These effects alter particle
trajectories and enhance anisotropic mixing, with implications for interpreting field
observations and modeling surface transport processes.
Introduction.
–
More
than
two-thirds
of
Earth’s surface is covered by oceans, which are es-
sential to sustaining life and regulating the climate
system. At the ocean-atmosphere interface, the in-
teraction between winds, currents, and surface waves
drives complex exchanges of mass, momentum, and
energy, generating motions across a broad range of
spatial and temporal scales.
On a global scale,
oceanic currents shape continental climates [1], while
extreme atmospheric events can transport marine
aerosols and particulates inland [2]. At more local
scales, weak winds give rise to Langmuir circulations,
near-surface convective cells that accumulate floating
material in convergence zones [3]. At the microscale,
gradients in local viscosity induced by microbial ac-
tivity and phytoplankton can influence solute trans-
port and the motility of microorganisms [4].
Surface waves of all wavelengths propagating at the
air-water interface [5] play a key role in the trans-
port of sediments [6] and particulate matter [7, 8].
When waves break, they inject momentum into un-
derlying currents [9–11], enhancing the transport and
dispersion of suspended materials.
They also fa-
cilitate mass, momentum, and energy exchange be-

on both the characteristics of the sustaining forcing,
such as local wind intensity, and on viscous dissipa-

the absence of continuous forcing, wave energy de-

In this Letter, we investigate the net Lagrangian
transport induced by freely decaying surface gravity
waves in deep water. Using fully nonlinear direct nu-
merical simulations, supported by a perturbative ana-
lytical framework, we characterize the transport gen-
erated by a monochromatic wave propagating with-
out external forcing and subject to viscous decay.
Unlike the idealized inviscid case, where Stokes drift
yields purely horizontal motion, we thus observe an
additional vertical migration of particles (figure 1).
Even small vertical displacements cause substantial
changes in drift velocity, leading to trajectories that
deviate markedly from classical Stokes-drift predic-
tions. In the vanishing-viscosity limit, our analytical
model recovers the classical result.

Simulations are

Results. –
The classical Stokes drift associ-
ated with inviscid, periodic surface waves predicts
purely horizontal (longitudinal) particle motion, with
a depth-dependent drift velocity given by ud

speed. In contrast, under decaying waves, we observe
an additional, systematic vertical drift. While ver-
tical displacement is a documented effect when par-
ticles have inertial properties [27, 29], interact with
an Eulerian return flow [22, 23], move over an in-
clined bed [34], or in shallow-water waves [35], here
we discuss a novel and purely kinematic phenomenon
arising in freely decaying conditions. The magnitude
and direction of the vertical motion depend on the
initial phase ϕp0 of particles beneath the wave, as
shown in figure 1a. This effect is quantified in fig-
ure 1b, where the vertical displacement saturates af-
ter approximately 50 wave periods, following an ini-
tial transient.
Simultaneously, the horizontal drift
(figure 1c) exhibits nonlinear evolution, indicative of
a time-dependent drift velocity. Once the particles
reach an approximately constant depth, their hori-
zontal displacement becomes nearly linear in time,
recovering a classical Stokes-like drift despite the on-
going decay of the wave amplitude.
A physical in-
terpretation of the vertical drift is as follows.
For
infinitesimal-amplitude, non-decaying waves in deep
water, fluid particles follow closed circular orbits [5].
At finite, steady amplitude, these orbits become tro-
choidal, leading to a net horizontal displacement as
a nonlinear effect. Finite viscosity alters the parti-
cle response. In this case, the wave amplitude decays
over time, causing the particle orbits to progressively
shrink.
As a result, a particle moving upward (or
downward) and downstream (or upstream) will re-
turn along a shorter path in the opposite direction,
due to the reduced wave amplitude. Since the wave
period remains unchanged, this asymmetry accumu-
lates over successive cycles, producing a net drift in
both vertical and horizontal directions, even at in-
finitesimal amplitude. The direction of these drifts
is determined by the particle’s initial phase beneath
the wave. As shown below, viscosity modifies the La-
grangian drift by introducing a linear contribution in
A and altering the classical Stokes drift.

This seemingly simple mechanism has profound im-
plications for particle dynamics, due to its nonlinear
interaction with the depth-dependent velocity field
beneath the wave.
In particular, it strongly influ-
ences the mixing properties of the flow, which we now
examine. Based on our direct numerical simulations,
we consider fluid particles initially released along a
vertical line beneath the air-water interface (e.g., ar-
bitrarily choosing an initial phase ϕp0 = 0). Since
all particles share the same initial phase, they drift
coherently in the same vertical direction (i.e., down-
ward, for the choice of ϕp0 = 0 shown in figure 3a).
However, due to the exponential decay of velocity
with depth, particles closer to the interface initially
move faster, as seen in figure 3b.
This spatial de-
cay interacts nontrivially with the temporal decay of
the wave. A particle near the surface descends more
rapidly at first, but quickly enters a region of weaker
flow. Conversely, a particle released slightly deeper
experiences slower vertical initial motion but sustains
it longer, ultimately undergoing a larger vertical net
displacement. This counterintuitive behavior is illus-
trated by the overlapping trajectories in figure 3a and
reveals a novel mixing mechanism, which we quantify
using pair dispersion.

Discussion.
–
We conjecture that the drift
mechanism identified in this Letter may have escaped
detection in previous studies because most field and
wave-tank measurements focus on tracers under pass-
ing wave packets. Such configurations do not capture
the cumulative effect of wave decay highlighted here,
which instead emerges when tracers remain exposed
to the same wave field as it decays in time, as oc-
curs in our simulations owing to the use of periodic
boundary conditions. To isolate this mechanism ex-
perimentally, we propose wave-tank studies in which
tracers are subjected to incident waves with gradually
decreasing amplitude. The drift reported here may be
particularly relevant in older sea states, where waves
are no longer actively forced, and should therefore
be considered when interpreting oceanic observations
and modeling transport processes in the near-surface
layer.
Importantly, the vertical displacement identified in
this study originates solely from the temporal decay
of the wave field and is thus distinct from other verti-
cal drift mechanisms discussed in the literature, such
as inertia-driven tracer effects [27] or wave-induced
Eulerian return flows [23].
Plastic residues from industrial activity and mis-
managed waste enter the ocean in vast quantities ev-
ery day, contaminating the global water cycle. Con-
trary to popular depictions of plastic pollution as
floating debris fields, the reality is dominated by a
diffuse suspension of microscopic particles [7], which
pose severe risks to marine organisms and ecosystems
[43].
The long-term fate of these microplastics re-
mains poorly understood: they may fragment into
nanoplastics, settle into sediments, or be carried to
coastal regions. The kinematic drifting mechanism
analyzed in this Letter might offer new insight into
these open questions.

I.
THE NUMERICAL SIMULATIONS
A.
Numerical methods
We perform direct numerical simulations in a two-dimensional domain of length λ in the longitudinal (x)
direction, aligned with the wave propagation, and height λ in the vertical (y) direction. The domain is half-
filled with water and subject to gravity g = (0, −g) acting along the negative y axis. The top and bottom
boundaries are modeled as a solid wall with no-slip and no-penetration conditions.
Periodic boundary
conditions are imposed in the longitudinal direction. The origin of the Cartesian coordinate system is placed
at the undisturbed interface between the two fluids when the system is at rest.
The flow is governed by the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations:
∂u
∂t + ∇· (uu) = 1
ρf
(−∇p + ∇· τ+fI) + g,
(1)
∇· u = 0,
(2)
where u and p denote the velocity and pressure fields, respectively, and ρf is the local fluid density—ρw in
water and ρa in air. The stress tensor is denoted by τ. The interfacial force fI accounts for surface tension
effects at the interface between the two fluids, where the velocity field is continuous but the stress tensor
exhibits a jump. This surface tension force is modeled as a volumetric force:
fI = σkδIn,
(3)
where σ is the surface tension coefficient, k is the local curvature of the interface, n is the unit normal vector
at the interface, and δI is a Dirac delta function nonzero only at the interface.
The motion of Lagrangian tracers is governed by:
dxp
dt = u(xp(t), t),
(4)
where xp(t) denotes the tracer position at time t, and u(xp(t), t) is the interpolated fluid velocity at that
position and time.
By doing so, particles are “blind” to the fluid-phase they lay in, and might drift away
from the interface even if initially released on it. Our numerics is thus unable to track particles constrained
to remain on the surface (indeed, here we always considered particles at a finite submergence depth). The
problem of floating particles bounded to the interface is nevertheless relevant and physically backed, e.g.,
by applications to floating debris in the oceans: future works could address it introducing a penalty term in
equation 4 to constrain particle motion at the surface.
Simulations are initialized using the inviscid, linear, deep-water wave solution:
u(x, y, t) = Ueκy cos(κx −ωt),
v(x, y, t) = Ueκy sin(κx −ωt),

B.
Energy decay
Given the sharp contrast between the simplicity of the assumptions in Landau’s theory [5] and the complexity
of our simulated system, we assess its implications measuring the temporal decay of the total mechanical
energy. The fall-off in the simulations is slightly faster than the theoretical prediction (figure 1, left). Yet, the
good agreement between our measurements with Fujin and former simulation results [6, 7] (validated in [4]
and reported for completeness in the left inset of figure 1) points towards the presence of further dissipative
mechanisms compared to theory, rather than a fault of the numerics. The simulated energy decay remains
nevertheless exponential (with good approximation), not affecting in principle the qualitative comparison of
the theoretical predictions in the following with our measured trends.
To better understand the dissipative process, we compute the dissipation rate in the flow fields at different
times (one of them is reported in the right of figure 1). Dissipation within the fluid bulk always appears
negligible, while most of it occurs in a thin air layer immediately above the surface. The reasonable agreement
of Landau’s decay prediction with our simulations is therefore impressive, noting that it completely overlooks
the fact that the most dissipative process occurs in the air [7, 8].

Boundary effects

B.
Numerical integration
In this section, we present results obtained integrating numerically the dimensional version of equations (16),
with the goal of verifying the analytical predictions derived above and assessing their robustness with respect
to the expansion parameter ϵ.
We fix the parameters to λ = 1, g = 1, νw = 1/1000, ω = 2π, and vary the initial wave amplitude A
to explore different values of ϵ. To evaluate the accuracy of our predictions, we begin analyzing the time-
dependent tracer velocity up and the period-dependent drift velocity ud, see equations (29) and (32). The
numerical estimate of ud is computed by substituting the integrated tracer positions at times tn and tn−1
into equation (32).
Figure 3 shows that the analytical predictions agree well with the numerical results when the expansion
parameter ϵ remains small. As expected, for ϵ > 0.1, the agreement deteriorates due to the breakdown of
the perturbative approximation. However, as the wave amplitude decays over time and the instantaneous
value of ϵ decreases, the agreement improves significantly.
Furthermore, in figure 4, we compare the asymptotic tracer displacement as a function of the Reynolds

λ = 1, g = 1, and ϵ = 0.1. At early times, the agreement is good: for both up and vp, the signals overlap
closely, with the theoretical model slightly underpredicting the peak amplitudes. At later times, however,
discrepancies emerge. While vp continues to be well captured, the model prediction for up deviates from
the DNS results, resulting in a systematic underestimation of the longitudinal drift velocity at large times
at several Re (not shown). Since the agreement is strong during the initial oscillation periods—with the
drift velocity closely matching between simulations and model—we attribute the growing deviation to a
progressive divergence between the simulated flow field and that assumed in the Landau-based model, which
is shown in panel (c) of figure 5. Even small differences between the predicted and simulated tracer velocities
induce significant divergence of their trajectories over long times.

In conclusion, while the theoretical model and the simulations remain in qualitative agreement, quantitative
differences become apparent at long times, being particularly evident in the asymptotic behavior for t →∞.
These discrepancies in the flow field likely account for the model’s reduced accuracy in predicting long-time
