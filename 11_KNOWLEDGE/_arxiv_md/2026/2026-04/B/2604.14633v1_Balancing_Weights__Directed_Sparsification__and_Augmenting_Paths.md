Balancing Weights, Directed Sparsification, and Augmenting Paths

Abstract
We present a randomized augmenting paths-based algorithm to compute the maximum flow
in a directed, uncapacitated graph in almost m + nF time, matching the algorithm of Karger
and Levine for undirected graphs (SICOMP 2015). Combined with an initial √n rounds of
blocking flow to reduce the value of F, we obtain a maximum flow algorithm with running time
mn1/2+o(1). For combinatorial, augmenting paths-based algorithms, this is the first improvement
over Dinic’s algorithm for moderately sparse graphs.
To obtain our algorithm, we introduce a new technique to re-weight the edges of a strongly
connected directed graph so that each cut is approximately balanced: the total weight of edges
in one direction is within a constant factor of the total weight in the other direction.
We
then adapt Karger and Levine’s technique of sampling edges from the newly weighted residual
graph, ensuring that an augmenting path exists in the sampled graph with high probability.
One technical difficulty is that our balancing weights have to be dynamically maintained upon
changes to the residual graph. Surprisingly, we can black box the dynamic data structure from
the recent interior point method-based flow algorithm of van den Brand et al. (FOCS 2024).

Introduction
In the (uncapacitated) maximum flow problem, we are given a directed graph and two vertices
s and t, and the goal is to find the maximum number of edge-disjoint paths from s to t.
It
is one of the oldest problems in combinatorial optimization [Dan51], and many influential tech-
niques have been developed to tackle the problem, including augmenting paths [FF56], blocking
flows [Din70], push-relabel [GT88], and random sampling [KL15]. More recently, techniques based
on continuous optimization have become widely successful, culminating in the first almost-linear
time algorithm for maximum flow and its generalizations [CKL+25]. However, it remains an open
question whether a more traditional approach based on augmenting paths can match this recent
breakthrough. Two very recent lines of work have obtained almost quadratic-time algorithms for
maximum flow [BBST24, BBL+25] and bipartite matching [CK24a, CK24b], showing that combi-
natorial techniques still have room for future development.
In this work, we present a new approach based on sampling edges from an appropriately weighted
residual graph. Our main result is the following:
Theorem 1. There is a randomized, augmenting paths-based algorithm that computes the maximum
flow on a directed, uncapacitated graph in time (m+nF)·no(1), where F is the value of the maximum
flow.

1.1
Our Techniques
Our starting point is Karger and Levine’s algorithm on undirected graphs [KL15], which finds each
augmenting path in a random sample of the residual graph. At a high level, Karger and Levine
show that if the algorithm has sent a (1 −ϵ)-fraction of the maximum flow, then the residual graph
is O(1/ϵ)-balanced in the following sense: for any cut, viewed as a bipartition (S, V \ S) of the
vertex set, the number of edges going from S to V \ S is within a multiplicative O(1/ϵ)-factor of
the number of edges going from V \ S to S. Then, they employ the random sampling method of
Bencz´ur and Karger for undirected graphs [BK15], accounting for the balance of O(1/ϵ) by over-
sampling at a rate of O(1/ϵ). Overall, they show that sampling ˜O(n/ϵ)1 edges is sufficient with
high probability2, so the next augmenting path can be found in time ˜O(n/ϵ). Since most of the
augmenting paths are found when ϵ is still constant, the amortized running time per augmenting
path is ˜O(n), establishing their running time of ˜O(m + nF).
Unfortunately, the balance guarantee no longer holds if the input graph is directed. For example,
suppose the input graph has an unbalanced cut (S, V \ S) with s ∈S and t ∈V \ S, with only
a single edge going from S to V \ S but many edges in the opposite direction. Since Karger and
Levine’s algorithm treats the residual graph as undirected for the purpose of sampling, the single
edge from S to V \ S cannot be distinguished from the edges in the opposite direction, and is
therefore unlikely to be sampled. If the edge is not sampled, then there cannot be a path from s
to t, invalidating the approach.
Our main idea is to re-weight the residual graph so that any cut is balanced in terms of weight:
the total weight of edges in one direction is within a constant factor of the total weight in the other
direction. In the example above, the single edge from S to V \ S must have much higher weight
than the edges in the opposite direction. We then treat the graph as undirected but weighted when
sampling edges, so that the single edge from S to V \ S is likely to be sampled.
One technical difficulty is that our balancing weights have to be dynamically maintained upon
changes to the residual graph. More precisely, when an augmenting path is found, the residual
graph is updated by flipping each edge along the path. Since Karger and Levine treat the graph
as undirected for the purpose of sampling, flipping an edge does not change their sampling prob-
abilities. However, our balancing weights are sensitive to edge flips, and a dynamic algorithm is
required to maintain them. Surprisingly, we show that the minimum ratio cut framework from the
recent interior point method-based flow algorithm of van den Brand et al. [VDBCK+24] can be
adapted to compute balancing weights. In particular, we completely black box their data structure
for maintaining approximate minimum ratio cuts. We remark that this data structure is purely
combinatorial and is comparable in complexity to the data structures in [CK24a, CK24b].

1.2
Related Work
The concept of balance for directed graphs is introduced by Ene et al. [EMPS16], who show that
existing techniques for undirected flow-based problems can be translated to directed graphs that are
approximately balanced. For graphs that are perfectly balanced, namely the Eulerian graphs, Cohen
et al. [CKK+18] use directed graph sparsification to obtain fast algorithms for solving directed
Laplacian systems. Sparsification for approximately balanced graphs is systematically studied by
Cen et al. [CCPS21], who obtain tight upper and lower bounds for various types of sparsification.
All of these results are restricted to balanced graphs, and to the best of our knowledge, our work
is the first to employ techniques from balanced graphs in the context of general directed graphs.
1.3
Preliminaries
Throughout the paper, all graphs are uncapacitated, either weighted or unweighted, and either
directed or undirected. We refer to directed edges as arcs and undirected edges as edges. The input
directed graph to maximum flow is assumed to be G = (V, E), with vertices s, t ∈V . We define
a flow from s to t as a set of arc-disjoint paths from s to t in G, and the value of the flow is the
number of paths in the set. The residual graph Gf is obtained from G by flipping each arc along
each path in f. The standard augmenting paths framework attempts to find a path from s to t in
Gf, called an augmenting path. If a path is found, then each arc along the path is flipped, and the
flow f is updated accordingly. Otherwise, if no path exists, then f is a maximum flow, and the
algorithm terminates.
A cut is a bipartition (S, V \ S) of the vertex set into two non-empty subsets, represented as
one of the sides S ⊆V . The boundary ∂S of the cut is the set of arcs/edges with exactly one
endpoint in S. For a directed graph, the out-boundary ∂+S is the set of arcs with only the tail in
S, and the in-boundary ∂−S is the set of arcs with only the head in S. For a weighted graph with
weight function w, let w(∂S) denote the total weight of arcs/edges in ∂S, and define w(∂+S) and
w(∂−S) similarly. For an unweighted graph, let |∂S| denote the number of arcs/edges in ∂S, and
define |∂+S| and |∂−S| similarly.
1.4
Preprocessing the Graph
We first preprocess the graph as follows. First, add m many parallel arcs (t, s), which does not
affect the maximum flow from s to t. Then, compute the strongly connected components of the new
graph. If s and t do not belong to the same strongly connected component, then the existence of
arc (t, s) implies that there is no path from s to t, and the algorithm concludes that the maximum
flow from s to t has value 0. Otherwise, discard all strongly connected components except the
one containing s and t, and solve the maximum flow problem on the remaining component. This
operation is safe because there is no path from s to t that contains vertices outside the strongly
connected component containing s and t.

3.3
Running Time
Outside of the calls to ToggleCut, the algorithm spends n1+o(1) time on each augmenting path:
the algorithm computes a sparsifier, finds an augmenting path, flips the path, and updates the data
structures. To bound the number of calls to ToggleCut, it suffices to bound the total energy
increase over the course of the algorithm, since the total energy starts at m ln(M +1) and decreases
by Ω(α3) = 1/no(1) on each call to ToggleCut (Lemma 14).
Let E be the current total energy. By Lemma 15, the energy increase is at most y(t)−y(s)+(n−
1) ln(M +1) after flipping the next augmenting path. If y(t)−y(s) ≥0, then we bound y(t)−y(s)
as follows. Each of the arcs (t, s) added during preprocessing has energy ln(M + 1) + y(t) −y(s) ≥
y(t) −y(s). There are Ω(m) such arcs, so their total energy Ω(m) · (y(t) −y(s)) is at most E. We
conclude that y(t) −y(s) ≤O(E/m), and the total energy after flipping the augmenting path is at
most (1+O(1/m))·E+(n−1) ln(M+1). The total number of augmenting paths is F ≤m, so the total
energy increase is at most (1+O(1/m))F ·(m ln(M+1)+F(n−1) ln(M+1)) ≤O((m+nF) ln(M+1)).
At this point, we can also justify the assumption maxu,v |y(u) −y(v)| ≤M from Lemma 15
for M = nO(1) as long as the residual graph is strongly connected. For the sake of argument,
re-define M as the largest value of maxu,v |y(u) −y(v)| over the course of the algorithm, and
suppose for contradiction that M is too large. Consider the moment of the algorithm at which
y(u) −y(v) = M for some u, v ∈V . Consider a path from u to v of at most n −1 arcs in the
current residual graph. There is an arc (u′, v′) on the path with y(u′) −y(v′) ≥M/(n −1), which
has energy ln(M + 1) + y(u′) −y(v′) ≥M/(n −1). In particular, the total energy is at least
M/(n −1). However, by the previous energy bound, the total energy at this point is at most
O((m + nF) ln(M + 1)). For large enough M = nO(1), we obtain a contradiction.
We conclude that the algorithm has total running time (m+nF)·no(1) while the residual graph
is strongly connected. When the flow is maximum, the residual graph Gf is no longer strongly
connected, and the algorithm may have undefined behavior. However, we can impose a running

Given the graph H, the algorithm
first adds between m/n and 2m/n self-loops to each vertex in H. This initial pre-processing has

5
Conclusion
We conclude with some future directions.
The first question is whether our weight function
w(u, v) =
1
max{y(v)−y(u), 0}+1 is the “right” function. A more natural function is
1
y(v)−y(u)+1, which
induces a “barrier” that enforces y(v) ≥y(u) −1 in the same way the push-relabel algorithm re-
quires d(v) ≥d(u)−1 on arcs (u, v) in the residual graph [GT88]. However, our algorithm may flip
an “upward” arc (v, u) with y(v) ≪y(u) −1, causing the new weight
1
y(v)−y(u)+1 to be undefined.
On the other hand, the push-relabel algorithm never sends flow along upward arcs, so perhaps the
algorithm should only flip downward arcs. We leave as an open question whether our algorithm
can be adapted to behave like a “continuous” push-relabel algorithm.
Another question is whether the balancing weights technique can be applied to other directed
graph problems. Balanced directed graphs behave similarly to undirected graphs, so the method
13


## Page 14


can be viewed as a way to apply the powerful techniques for undirected graphs to the more difficult
setting of directed graphs. Indeed, the recent interior point method-based flow algorithms [CKL+25,
VDBCK+24] all adopt this strategy to great success: each iteration reduces to a problem on an
undirected graph, which is solved quickly by dynamic data structures.
Finally, one limit to the current balancing weights approach is the requirement that the graph
is strongly connected. We leave as an open question whether the technique can be extended to
general directed graphs. One difficulty in handling cuts with no arcs in one direction is that the
arcs in the other direction may have their weights approaching 0, which means that y(v) −y(u)
becomes arbitrarily large. One possible approach is to employ a “softer” weight function to control
the potential differences as the weights tend to 0.
