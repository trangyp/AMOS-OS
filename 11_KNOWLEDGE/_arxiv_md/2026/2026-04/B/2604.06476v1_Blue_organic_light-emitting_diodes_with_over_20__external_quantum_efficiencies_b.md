Abstract 
The realization of blue electroluminescence with high efficiency and lifetime remains a 
long-standing hurdle for OLED technology to overcome. Divalent Europium 
[Europium(II)] complexes offer a fundamentally distinct pathway toward this goal, as their 
atomic 4f–5d transitions yield single-Gaussian, spectrally pure emission with theoretical 
100% exciton utilization and no involvement of fragile organic bonds in the emissive 
process. However, their true potential has never been fully demonstrated before. In this 
work, we design a rigid aza-crown europium(II) complex (Eu5NHCrown) that achieves 
near-unity photoluminescence quantum yield with bright, pure-blue emission. The 
complex sublimes without decomposition and can be vacuum-deposited into a bottom-
emitting, single-host OLED architecture, delivering an external quantum efficiency (EQE) 
of 20.7% with minimal roll-off (19.3% at 1000 cd m −2 ) and a narrowband 
electroluminescence with Commission Internationale de L’Eclairage coordinates of (0.12, 
0.25). These results reveal the true potential of Eu(II) 4f–5d transitions for high-efficiency 
blue OLEDs, establishing a molecular design concept that bridges atomic-transition 
efficiency with the processability of organic materials. 
1 
Introduction 
Organic light-emitting diode (OLED) displays are inseparable from modern life.[1] Yet 
despite their commercial success, OLEDs continue to face intrinsic challenges in achieving 
long operational lifetimes and high efficiencies—particularly for blue emission, which 
remains the bottleneck for full-color display technology.[2–4] Over the past decades, 
multiple emitter concepts have been explored, including phosphorescent, thermally 
activated delayed fluorescent (TADF), and conventional fluorescent materials.[5,6] Each has 
achieved critical milestones, but none meet all performance requirements simultaneously. 
Phosphorescent emitters offer high exciton utilization yet suffer from instability due to 
bond cleavage, while fluorescent emitters exhibit excellent stability but are intrinsically 
limited in their ability to harvest triplet excitons.[2–4] These issues are mainly rooted in 
molecular orbital transitions, which inherently couple emissive states to chemical bonds.


## Page 2


Lanthanide-based emitters offer a fundamentally different strategy. Their emission 
originates from atomic 5d-4f transitions that are decoupled from molecular bonding, 
eliminating bond cleavage as a degradation pathway and enabling full exciton utilization 
independent of spin statistics. Among these, Eu(II) stands out as a particularly promising 
candidate due to its zero orbital angular momentum (𝐿= 0) ground state, which yields a 
single-peak emission band and an intrinsically short excited-state lifetime characteristic of 
a parity-allowed 5d→4f transition. Despite the conceptual appeal of Eu(II) emitters, 
progress toward highly efficient blue OLED has remained challenging.

Over the past 30 years, numerous studies have explored Eu-based emitters to demonstrate 
the theoretical advantage in the device. At one of the earliest reports, Shipley et al. in 1999, 
described an orange-emitting Eu(II) borate complex, though with poor electroluminescent 
performance.[8] In 2018, Allen and co-workers reported a Eu(II) azacryptand complex in 
which methyl substitution at the NH positions exhibited narrow, deep-blue emission (450 
nm, PLQY ≈100%). However, the resulting complex demonstrated salt-like character and 
poor volatility, rendering it unsuitable for vacuum deposition OLEDs.[9] Later in 2020, Liu 
et al. reported a related azacryptand complex that enabled OLED fabrication, achieving a 
high external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 17.7% and confirming that Eu(II) complexes 
can effectively harvest triplet excitons.[10] However, in this case, the specific coordination 
environment led to a broad emission spectrum with a double-band feature in the green–
yellow region, rather than the narrow blue Gaussian profile characteristic of 4f–5d 
transitions.[10] More recently, in 2024, Liu et al. reported a series of Eu(II) complexes 
employing bulky tert-butylated tris(pyrazolyl)borate ligands, achieving improved emission 
color and sublimation properties, with the best-performing device reaching a maximum 
EQE of 15.7%.[11] Most recently, Reineke et al. introduced two new classes of Europium-


## Page 3


based emitters incorporating a crown ether and carborate anions, revealing this 
combination provides an effective strategy to stabilize Eu(II) core while maintaining strong 
4f-5d emission and excellent volatility, and maintaining deep blue emission.[12]  
Building on the foundation of the previous work, in this work, we introduce Eu5NHCrown, 
a Eu(II) complex with an advanced crown-ether ligand design that stabilizes the divalent 
center, improves the energy level while preserving its atomic-transition emission 
characteristic. The design enables decomposition-free sublimation and near-unity 
photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY, ΦPL). OLEDs incorporating Eu5NHCrown 
exhibit a bright blue emission with a record EQE of 20.7% and 19.3% at 1000 cd m−2. 
These results establish Eu5NHCrown as the first europium-based emitter to approach the 
theoretical efficiency limit in OLEDs, setting a new benchmark for blue divalent Europium 
devices based on atomic-transition emitters. 
2 
Results and Discussion 
2.1 
Design and Synthesis 
To enhance the photophysical properties, tune the energy levels, and achieve better 
sublimation behavior, we developed a new Eu(II)-based emitter complex featuring an all-
aza-20-crown-5 ether as the neutral ligand and two carborate anions. The different size and 
type of this neutral ligand are the main differences to the previously introduced Eu-based 
emitters EuCrown and EuCovCrown.[12] Specifically, instead of an 18c6-type crown with 
two nitrogen and four oxygen donors, Eu5NHCrown comprises a larger 20-ring with five 
nitrogen donors connected through propylene bridges, while it uses the same carborate 
(CB11H12) anions to neutralize Eu(II). The differences in the ring-size and donor groups 
have two important consequences: Firstly, being larger than the 18-ring, the 20-ring folds 
in on itself to accommodate the Eu(II) cation, which improves the steric shielding of the 
Eu(II) center (cf. Figure 1). This folding effect is further enhanced by the more flexible 
propyl-linking groups and the kinks introduced at the triangular [–Pr–N(H)–Pr–] nitrogen 
donors, which is in contrast to the ethylene-linked and mostly linear [–Et–O–Et–] oxygen 
donors of the 18c6 crowns. Secondly, polarized through the coordination of Eu(II), the NH 
groups help to bind the carborate anions via dihydrogen bonds [13], which is evident from 
four short (< 2.2 Å) non-covalent H-H contacts in the structure displayed in Fig. 1 as 
dotted red lines. With this new approach, we manage to combine the good steric shielding 
of EuCovCrown with the deep energy levels of EuCrown. 
The complex Eu5NHCrown was synthesized by reacting equimolar amounts of Eu 
(CB11H12)2·5THF and the macrocyclic amine 5NH5Pr in toluene under inert conditions, 
affording a colorless powder in high yield. The product was purified by sublimation at 
10−6 mbar to give analytically pure material suitable for vacuum deposition. Detailed

The photoluminescence spectrum of Eu5NHCrown exhibits an intense single-peak blue 
emission centered at  482 nm (≈2.57 eV) with a full width at half maximum of 58  in 
toluene solution. Time-resolved measurements reveal a single-exponential decay with a 
lifetime of approximately 1 µs, which together with the single peak emission profile, is 
consistent with the 4f7 (8𝑆7/2, 𝐿= 0) ground state of Eu(II) and a spin-allowed 5d→4f 
transition, confirming that the emission originates from the Eu(II) center rather than from 
ligand or charge-transfer states. Compared with previously reported Eu(II) complexes such 
as EuCrown and EuCovCrown, Eu5NHCrown exhibits a red-shifted emission by ≈
0.18 eV while maintaining excellent spectral purity, reflecting the influence of the stronger 
ligand field and the more rigid coordination environment provided by the ligand for Eu(II). 
As shown in Figure 2A and reported in Table 1, the electroluminescence (EL) spectrum of 
Eu5NHCrown is narrower than its photoluminescence in toluene solution. This difference 
can be attributed to the more rigid environment of the emissive layer in the OLED, where 
the surrounding host matrix restricts vibrational and degrees of freedom of the ligand and 
Carborate anions. The reduced structural relaxation in the excited state leads to a more


## Page 6


confined emissive geometry, resulting in a narrower 5𝑑–4𝑓 emission band and enhanced 
color purity compared to the solution phase. Consequently, the electroluminescent 
emission exhibits a CIE𝑥 coordinate of 0.12 (𝑐𝑓. Table 2), shifted from 0.17 in solution, 
reflecting the stronger spatial confinement and reduced vibronic coupling in the solid-state 
environment. 
The photoluminescence quantum yield of Eu5NHCrown reaches 95% in toluene, 
demonstrating almost complete suppression of nonradiative decay. Such high efficiency 
places this complex among the best Eu(II) emitters reported to date, matching the near-
unity yields of rigid azacryptate and pyrazolylborate systems.[14] This performance arises 
from the optimized ligand field of 20-crown-5 architecture, which confines the Eu(II) 5𝑑 
orbital while minimizing vibrational coupling and quenching pathways. This structural 
property provides excellent steric shielding of the Eu(II) core and effectively localizes the 
excited state and restricts intramolecular relaxation, leading to high and stable 
luminescence. 
The excited-state lifetime of Eu5NHCrown was determined to be 1078 ns, which falls 
within the characteristic range for Eu(II) 5 𝑑–4 𝑓 emission.13 Using the measured 
photoluminescence quantum yield (ΦPL = 0.95), the radiative and nonradiative rate 
constants were estimated as 𝑘r = 8.8 × 104 s−1 and 𝑘nr = 4.6 × 103 s−1, respectively. 
This dominance of radiative processes confirms that the emission originates from an 
efficiently confined 5𝑑–4𝑓 transition with minimal vibronic coupling. The slightly longer 
lifetime compared to the typical range likely reflects subtle differences in ligand-field 
strength and coordination rigidity, which stabilize the 5 𝑑 excited state without 
compromising radiative efficiency. These results demonstrate that the 20-crown-5 
architecture of Eu5NHCrown provides a rigid yet electronically balanced coordination 
environment that maximizes radiative efficiency while maintaining excellent 
photostability. The combination of near-unity photoluminescence quantum yield and 
spectrally pure blue emission highlights the excellent optical performance of 
Eu5NHCrown. The complex also exhibits outstanding sublimation and resublimation 
behavior (see Supporting Information), confirming its exceptional volatility and suitability 
for vacuum processing. Together, these properties make Eu5NHCrown an ideal emitter for 
vacuum-deposited devices. They directly translate into efficient electroluminescence, as 
demonstrated in the following section on device performance. 
2.3 
Vacuum-fabricated OLEDs 
To evaluate the electroluminescent performance of Eu5NHCrown, bottom-emissive 
OLEDs were fabricated using a conventional multilayer architecture: ITO / MoO3 (2 nm) 
/ SimCP2 (70 nm) / SiDBFCz:Eu5NHCrown (30 wt%, 20 nm) / mSiTrz (5 nm) / 
PPO21:Yb (1 vol%, 25 nm) / Al (120 nm). This simplified configuration was chosen to 
highlight the intrinsic emissive efficiency of the Eu(II) complex without the aid of co-hosts 
or exciplex-forming layers.


## Page 7


The current density–voltage–luminance (J–V–L) and external quantum efficiency (EQE) 
characteristics are presented in Figure 3(A,B), and the key device parameters are 
summarized in Table 2. The Eu5NHCrown OLED achieves a maximum EQE (EQEmax) 
of 20.7% with a turn-on voltage of 4.2 V, representing a new performance benchmark for 
lanthanide-based blue emitters (Supporting Information Section IV). Since Eu(II) emission 
arises from an atomic orbital 5d–4f transition rather than molecular orbitals, the transition 
dipoles are inherently isotropic. The emission terminates on the 4𝑓7 (8𝑆7/2) ground state, 
which possesses eight energetically equivalent spin sublevels and zero orbital angular 
momentum (L = 0), leading to a single-band Gaussian emission with an isotropic 
orientation-independent emission. Thus, this efficiency essentially reaches the theoretical 
limit expected for 100% exciton utilization efficiency, where the EQE is constrained only 
by the optical out-coupling efficiency (𝜂out ≈18–22%). A detailed analysis of this limit 
is provided in the Supporting Information (Section III). Remarkably, the device maintains 
an EQE of 19.3% at 1000 cd m−2, indicating negligible roll-off and highly balanced charge 
transport. Such stability at elevated luminance confirms that non-radiative decay channels 
are effectively suppressed, consistent with the spin- and parity-allowed 5𝑑–4𝑓 transition 
of Eu(II) that enables full exciton harvesting without the need for intersystem crossing or 
triplet-management processes. 
3 
Discussion and Outlook 
Eu5NHCrown represents a major advance among Eu(II)-based OLED emitters, combining 
near-unity photoluminescence quantum yield, clean vacuum sublimation, and record-
breaking device efficiency for a europium-based OLED compared to previously reported 
emitters. The optimized device reaches an external quantum efficiency of 20.7% with 
negligible efficiency roll-off, reaching the near-theoretical maximum EQE for isotropic 
emitters. This establishes Eu5NHCrown as the first Eu(II)-based emitter to achieve the 
near-theoretical maximum in an OLED device, while maintaining the photophysical and 
thermal properties required for vacuum-deposited OLEDs. Taken together, these results 
demonstrate that Eu5NHCrown bridges atomic-like transition efficiency and organic 
processability, establishing a new benchmark for blue OLEDs based on lanthanide 
emitters. 
Beyond benchmarking against Eu-based systems, Eu5NHCrown also stands out when 
compared to conventional organic emitters. Fluorescent blue OLEDs use simple 
architectures but are fundamentally limited in efficiency. Phosphorescent and TADF-based 
blue OLEDs can reach higher efficiencies but generally require complex co-host structures. 
In sharp contrast, Eu5NHCrown achieves near-theoretical efficiency in a simple bottom-
emitting architecture with a single host without a co-host, no exciplex layer, and no 
orientation engineering—demonstrating that atomic 5d–4f emission can unify the structure 
simplicity of FlOLEDs with the efficiency of PhOLEDs.


## Page 8


Future development will focus on tuning the emission energy and improving operational 
stability. Subtle modifications to the crown geometry and donor strength offer a rational 
handle to shift the 5d–4f transition toward deeper blue while maintaining high radiative 
efficiency. This strategy positions Eu(II) emitters as strong candidates with high potential 
for next-generation OLED display technology.

Experimental Section 
Materials: All materials were obtained from commercial suppliers and further purified by 
resublimation prior to use. The purity of the emitter and host materials was verified by 
HPLC and elemental analysis to ensure high analytical quality before OLED fabrication. 
Other Commercial materials or reagents were used as received unless otherwise noted. 
Fabrication: OLEDs were fabricated on pre-patterned and cleaned ITO glass substrates in 
a Kurt J. Lesker vacuum deposition system under high vacuum (< 1 × 10−7 mbar). 
Organic and metal layers were sequentially deposited with precise rate control (0.5–
1.0 Å s −1  for organics, 0.2 Å s −1  for MoO 3 , and 2 Å s −1  for Al). OLEDs were 
encapsulated inside a nitrogen glovebox using a glass cavity cap, UV-curable epoxy, and 
a getter. Measurements: Photoluminescence and PLQY measurements were performed 
under an inert atmosphere using an Edinburgh Instruments spectrometer and a Hamamatsu 
Quantaurus-QY. Steady-state PL spectra were recorded at 310 nm excitation, and excited-
state lifetimes were obtained by TCSPC using 310 nm pulsed excitation. Emission was 
detected with a monochromator–photomultiplier setup at the Eu(II) peak wavelength (5–
10 nm bandwidth) to selectively probe emitter emission. Current–voltage–luminance (IVL) 
characteristics and electroluminescence spectra were recorded using a calibrated 
McScience Inc IVL robot.

Thermal stability 
The thermal stabilities of Eu5NHCrown was investigated by thermogravimetric analysis 
(TGA, Figure 2). The materials were measured in an 40µl Al crucible without lid under 
vacuum with a heating rate of 10K/min and a typical sample size of 2-10 mg. The onset of 
volatilization, corresponding to 1% weight loss, was found at 𝑇= 255.3 °𝐶 and is in 
accordance with the experimental sublimation temperature around 𝑇= 260 °𝐶. The slow 
mass reduction up to 𝑇≤ 330.5 °𝐶 can be mainly attributed to evaporation of the material, 
implying thermal stability to this point. The following significant reduction in mass to 8% 
residual mass indicates thermal decomposition and is consistent with observations in 
sublimation experiments.

Theoretical Limit of External Quantum Efficiency for Randomly Oriented 
Emitters in Planar Bottom-Emissive OLEDs 
 
The external quantum efficiency (EQE) of an OLED can be expressed as 
𝜂EQE = 𝛾× 𝜂ST × 𝑞× 𝜂out 
where 𝛾 is the charge-balance factor, 𝜂ST is the exciton-utilization efficiency, 𝑞 is the 
intrinsic photoluminescence quantum yield under device operation, and 𝜂out is the optical 
outcoupling efficiency. In the ideal limit where charge balance, exciton utilization, and 
photoluminescence quantum yield all approach unity, the EQE becomes equal to 𝜂out. 
In a conventional planar bottom-emissive OLED (glass/ITO/organic/metal), the maximum 
optical outcoupling efficiency is fundamentally constrained by total internal reflection at 
the glass/air interface, waveguiding in the high–refractive-index organic layers, and 
coupling to surface-plasmon modes at the metal electrode. The magnitude of these losses 
depends sensitively on the orientation of the emitter’s transition dipole moment: emitters 
with predominantly horizontal dipole orientation couple less strongly to waveguided and

The blue emission arises from Eu(II) emitters fall into the random-dipole category because 
their electroluminescence arises from an atomic 5d–4f transition that terminates on the 4f7

by molecular design or host–guest interactions. Consequently, Eu(II) complexes cannot 
benefit from dipole-orientation engineering, and the random-dipole outcoupling  limit of

The Eu(II) deep-blue OLED reported in this work exhibits an EQE of 20.7 %. Given the 
isotropic nature of the Eu(II) transition dipoles and the absence of strong optical cavity 
effects, this value is effectively at the theoretical maximum for a randomly oriented emitter 
with near-unity internal quantum efficiency in a conventional bottom-emitting OLED 
stack.
