for Poverty and Inequality Estimation

Abstract
The estimation of inequality and poverty measures is frequently constrained by a lack of
individual data. Many countries, including China, continue to report income data in the
form of aggregated income shares. In this context, the Beta Lorenz curve, introduced by
Kakwani (Econometrica, 48, 1980), has become a standard tool for reconstructing income
distributions at both academic and institutional levels. Notably, alongside the General
Quadratic (GQ) Lorenz curve, it represents the primary specification used by the World
Bank to construct its official poverty estimates when microdata is unavailable. In this
paper, we demonstrate that Kawani’s model fails to satisfy the formal requirements of a
genuine Lorenz curve. To address this, we identify the specific constraints that ensure the
theoretical validity of this model and introduce a new family of Lorenz curves derived from
the corrected parametric space. Our analysis, conducted across more than 2,000 datasets,
reveals that our proposed four-parameter specification provides highly accurate estimates
of several poverty and inequality measures. Our results show that this model consistently
outperforms the GQ Lorenz curve, which we find tends to underestimate poverty in over
80 percent of the analyzed cases.
Key Words: Grouped data, parametric models, Gini index, poverty gap, Watts index

Introduction
At the core of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, the eradication of poverty and
the reduction of inequality are central objectives of the global development agenda. To mon-
itor progress toward these goals, it is essential to measure poverty and inequality on a regular
basis using high-quality data. In this sense, international initiatives have been developed to
increase the availability of data and methodological harmonization across countries and over
time. This is the role played by the Poverty and Inequality Platform (PIP) developed by the
World Bank, which offers regularly updated indicators for cross-country comparisons and in-
tertemporal analyses of the evolution of poverty and inequality. Complementary to this ini-
tiative, the World Inequality Lab has developed the World Inequality Database, which offers
detailed distributional estimates through the systematic integration of available data from sur-
veys, tax records, and national accounts.
These efforts, however, are often undermined by the limited availability of individual-level
data. Even today, no microdata on individual incomes are available for several countries,

In practice, distributional estimates in the World Inequality Database are frequently con-
structed using the methods proposed by Blanchet et al. (2022), which rely on flexible models
from the generalized Pareto family. Generalized Pareto models are an effective tool for char-
acterizing distributions that exhibit a power-law tail (Arnold, 2008). Consequently, they are an
attractive candidate for modeling income distribution above the median. However, their appli-
cation may be less effective for the lower half of the distribution. As a result, using this model
to estimate poverty and inequality would likely lead to significant biases.
By contrast, the World Bank’s official poverty estimates rely on parametric Lorenz curve
specifications when individual-level data are unavailable. The estimation of their parameters
is often simpler compared to more traditional statistical distributions, especially because these
models only require the estimation of shape parameters. While several specifications exist,3
PIP relies on two primary models: the General Quadratic (GQ) Lorenz curve developed by
Villaseñor and Arnold (1989) and the Beta Lorenz curve introduced by Kakwani (1980). Al-
though these models have been found to be particularly suitable for estimating poverty indica-
tors (Minoiu and Reddy, 2009; Bresson, 2009), they are prone to reporting inconsistent esti-
mates. These inconsistencies are predominantly caused by the restrictive parameter constraints
that characterize these models as valid Lorenz curves. When these conditions are violated, the
model can produce non-increasing concave curves or even negative values, which do not meet
the basic regularity conditions that a theoretically valid Lorenz curve should satisfy.4
The Beta Lorenz curve has been particularly problematic in this regard, as Kakwani (1980)’s
original proposal does not capture a true Lorenz curve except for one particular configuration of
the parameter space. This lack of theoretical robustness is particularly important given the in-
stitutional weight of this model within the World Bank’s poverty estimates. It is also a frequent
choice for approximating income distributions, even in recent literature (see Bresson (2009);
Kobayashi et al. (2022); Ravallion and Chen (2022); Wilson et al. (2022); Aron et al. (2024)).
However, as we demonstrate in this paper, these applications rely on a functional form that
can become mathematically inconsistent, a limitation that has remained largely undetected in
contemporary research until now.

In the most comprehensive secondary databases reporting distributional statistics, the sam-
pling design typically dictates that the proportion of observations within each group is pre-
determined. Consequently, the population shares (pj) are treated as fixed constants, whereas
the income shares (sj) are the realized random variables. Under this specific data structure,
the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) framework based on a multinomial specification,
as proposed by (McDonald, 1984), is misspecified, as group frequencies are non-stochastic by
construction. Furthermore, implementing this MLE approach requires information on the in-
come group boundaries (hj), which are rarely included from secondary sources. Given these
data constraints, researchers have traditionally relied on minimum distance estimators to iden-
tify the vector of parameters of interest.

retical functional form of the Lorenz curve. Let X be a random variable defined on the support

curve, denoted as L(u; θ), is a function solely of the shape parameters θ ⊆λ.

Consequently, when the research objective is centered on relative inequality, this estimation
strategy circumvents the requirement for mean income data. Conversely, if the objective in-
volves the estimation of poverty measures or absolute welfare, the scale of the distribution can
be recovered by incorporating an external estimate of the mean income.
Although EWMD estimators yield consistent estimates of θ, they are generally asymptot-
ically inefficient. This estimator fails to account for two critical features of the data structure.
First, the elements of the moment vector M(θ) may exhibit heteroskedasticity (unequal vari-
ances). Second, these moments are inherently correlated because the sum of income shares
is, by definition, constrained to one. Consequently, any welfare indicators or indices derived
from these parameters, including poverty and inequality measures, will also inherit this loss of
efficiency, resulting in wider confidence intervals and reduced statistical power compared to an
optimal weighting scheme.
Since our main objective is to provide point estimates for poverty and inequality measures,
the EWMD approach remains a tractable and consistent choice. However, if the research objec-
tive extends to statistical inference, it should be noted that deriving valid confidence intervals

Evaluating parametric Lorenz curves for poverty and in-
equality measurement
In this section, we evaluate the performance of the proposed models in estimating poverty and
inequality measures. We consider the model introduced by Ortega et al. (1991) given in (3.1),
the only particular case of Kakwani’s proposal that is actually a Lorenz curve (2.4), and curves
L2 and L3 in (3.3) and (3.6) respectively. We also consider the GQ Lorenz curve introduced by
Villaseñor and Arnold (1989), as it remains one of the most robust frameworks for estimating
income distributions across a diverse set of countries (Bresson, 2009). Furthermore, the GQ
Lorenz curve serves as a primary alternative specification used by the World Bank’s PIP to
approximate these measures when microdata are unavailable.
Our analysis relies on data from the PIP, currently the most comprehensive global database
compiling harmonized poverty and inequality estimates. The database contains 2,556 datasets,
constructed from microdata, synthetic distributions, grouped data, or internal imputations. For
this analysis, we restrict our attention to datasets based on individual-level microdata, resulting
in a final sample of 2,346 datasets.
While the World Bank maintains access to individual-level microdata, PIP exclusively re-
ports aggregated statistics. Specifically, the platform provides estimates for three indices from
the Foster-Greer-Thorbecke class: the poverty headcount ratio, the poverty gap, and the poverty
severity index. Data on the Watts index, widely regarded as the first distribution-sensitive
poverty measure, is also available. In addition, two inequality indicators are included: the Gini
index, which is more sensitive to changes around the center of the income distribution, and the
mean log deviation (MLD), which is more responsive to redistributions affecting the lower tail
of the distribution.
The database also reports ten points of the Lorenz curve, which are used to evaluate the
performance of the various models introduced in this paper. While the Ortega specification
and the Kakwani special case consistently produced valid Lorenz curves, the proposed L2 and
L3models proved non-genuine for 741 and 775 datasets. Additionally, the conditions of the GQ
model were not satisfied in 308 datasets.

This final finding calls for a critical observation about the geometric properties of the Gini
index. Because it is defined as a measure proportional to the area between the Lorenz curve and
the line of equality, the Gini index is prone to an error compensation effect. In particular, when
the estimated Lorenz curve lies closer to the line of equality than the observed curve below
the median, but farther from it above the median, these deviations can offset each other in the
integration process. As a result, a small absolute error in the Gini index does not necessarily
indicate a better fit of the functional form, especially in the tails of the distribution.

most prominent specifications for this purpose. Its significance extends well beyond academic
research. The model serves as a cornerstone of institutional poverty monitoring, being used by
the World Bank to produce official global poverty estimates when microdata are unavailable.
In this paper, we demonstrate that the functional form originally proposed by Kakwani
does not generally satisfy the mathematical properties of a genuine Lorenz curve. We identify
the unique case within this family that remains theoretically consistent across the entire unit
interval. Given the popularity of the original model, we propose a new family of alternative
specifications based on Kakwani’s parametric framework that ensure adherence to the funda-
mental mathematical axioms of a Lorenz curve. The performance of these models is evaluated
through a combination of goodness-of-fit analysis across more than 2,000 empirical datasets
and Monte Carlo simulations to assess their finite-sample properties.
Our results indicate that the four-parameter L3 curve is the best choice when prioritizing
point-estimate accuracy. It achieves the lowest average RSS and the smallest finite-sample bias
for nearly all poverty measures, with an error distribution centered at zero. In contrast, the GQ
model, despite being the current standard, exhibits a systematic downward bias, significantly
underestimating poverty and inequality. However, the GQ model compensates for this lack of
accuracy with the lowest standard errors among all analyzed models. In practice, while the
GQ model seems to be “systematically erroneous” in the lower tail, its estimates are the least
sensitive to sampling noise.
Ultimately, the choice of a Lorenz specification is far more than a technical exercise. It
has profound implications for the global monitoring of poverty and inequality. Our findings
suggest that relying exclusively on the GQ model may lead to an over-optimistic assessment of
poverty reduction. In contrast, the L3 model provides a more conservative and potentially more
accurate reflection of deprived populations, with a more precise representation the lower tail of
the income distribution. For practitioners, this highlights the necessity of triangulating results
across these two functional forms to ensure that distributional changes are correctly identified
and addressed.
