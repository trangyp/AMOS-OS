Abstract: While advances in materials informatics have accelerated the development of 
Self-Driving Laboratories (SDLs), human-led experiments remain standard in many 
educational and exploratory research laboratories. In specific lab settings, formal 
documentation alone is often insufficient for safe and reliable operation. We refer to the 
gap between formal documentation and reliable execution in such settings as the 
experimental last mile; this gap mainly involves site-specific operational know-how, 
including local rules, routine checks, procedural details, and safety-conscious actions that 
are can be verbalizable but are often under-documented in standard manuals. In this 
proof-of-concept study, we developed a human-in-the-loop AI assistant that combines 
first-person experimental video, multimodal AI, and retrieval-augmented generation 
(RAG). Using powder X-ray diffraction experiments and student-recorded video data as 
inputs, the system extracts site-specific laboratory knowledge from recorded procedures, 
including physical techniques and audible confirmation that conventional manuals could 
omit. It then provides grounded responses based on the resulting manual. To reduce the 
risk of unsupported outputs, the system employs a two-layer safety design: source 
restriction through RAG and strict system-prompt constraints. Instructor-based 
evaluation showed alignment with expected guidance for questions covered by the 
manual. For out-of-scope queries, the system appropriately refused to answer, indicating 
a reduced risk of hallucination. Expert evaluation further indicated that the generated 
advisory reports were useful and safe (utility: 3.25/4.00; safety: 4.00/4.00). These results 
suggest the feasibility of a framework for bridging the experimental last mile in which AI

supports laboratory practice under explicit human supervision rather than replacing 
human judgment.

1. Introduction 
Materials Informatics has accelerated the development of Self-Driving Laboratories (SDLs) 1-3. To 
minimize human error and increase operational reproducibility, automated platforms have successfully 
integrated complex solid-state processes, including inorganic powder synthesis combined with X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) characterization4 and automated bulk intermetallic processing5. Nonetheless, 
human-led experiments inevitably introduce variability and error, often compromising data 
reproducibility.4 In many academic laboratories, however, experiments are still carried out primarily 
by humans — particularly in educational settings and exploratory research, where local rules, context-
specific handling, and accumulated experience play critical roles. Even as automation advances, 
human observation and judgment remain indispensable, as highlighted by recent studies showing that 
autonomous robots still rely on human expertise for interpreting unexpected phenomena. 6 Therefore, 
laboratory support should not be discussed only in terms of full automation; it is equally important to 
develop tools that assist human-led laboratories. 
A persistent problem in laboratory education is the gap between formal documentation and 
actual operation. We define “the experimental last mile” as the practical gap between formal 
experimental documentation—such as papers, protocols, and equipment manuals—and reliable 
execution in real laboratory settings. Standard protocols and locally prepared equipment manuals 
describe general procedures, but they often omit the practical details needed to operate safely and 
reliably in a specific laboratory. Students, therefore, learn much of this knowledge informally through 
demonstration, repeated supervision, and local custom. This dependence on person-to-person transfer 
places a burden on instructors and can lead to uneven training. These challenges suggest a need for 
tools that can deliver reliable, site-specific guidance to support human-led laboratory work.  
Large language models (LLMs)7 have broadened access to scientific information.8 Recent multi-
agent LLM systems have further demonstrated the potential to support hypothesis generation and 
experimental design from published knowledge9, 10. However, their direct application in practical 
laboratory work remains challenging, particularly for inexperienced researchers. One reason is that 
they do not inherently contain site-specific operational knowledge. Another is the risk of 
hallucination11: they may produce plausible but unsupported answers. In laboratory settings, such 
responses are not merely inconvenient; they can create safety risks, lead to improper operation, and 
damage equipment 12. 
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)13 offers one practical way to reduce this problem by 
grounding outputs in a defined knowledge source. In educational settings, RAG has attracted attention 
as a method for improving factual grounding while preserving the usability of natural-language 
interaction 14, 15. Even so, successful use depends not only on the model itself but also on how it is 
integrated into supervision and workflow design.16


## Page 4


In this work, we propose a human-in-the-loop framework for digitizing laboratory know-how 
and providing grounded AI-assisted support. In this workflow, a student records first-person 
explanations of experimental procedures under human guidance, and multimodal AI converts the 
resulting audiovisual data into structured text. That structured text is then used as the retrieval source 
for a laboratory support system. Using powder X-ray diffraction (pXRD) as a case study, we examined 
two questions: first, whether site-specific procedural knowledge can be captured and reused effectively 
through this pipeline; and second, whether a combination of RAG and prompt-level constraints can 
support safe operation by limiting unsupported outputs. 
2. Methods 
2.1. System Overview 
The proposed framework consisted of four stages: knowledge capture, structuring, retrieval, and 
evaluation.

reliable execution in real laboratory settings. In the human-led laboratory context examined here, this

four stages: (1) Knowledge capture using wearable cameras, (2) Structuring audiovisual data into a 
textual manual via a multimodal AI model, (3) Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) to provide 
safety-grounded responses, and (4) Performance evaluation through quantitative analysis using a 
multilingual Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model and expert analysis.  
 
In the knowledge-capture stage, students recorded experimental procedures from a first-person 
perspective using a wearable camera. During recording, they verbally explained the procedure in 
Japanese, detailing practical notes, safety precautions, and local operational rules. 
In the structuring stage, a multimodal AI model (Claude Opus 4.617) analyzed the recorded 
audiovisual data to generate a textual manual. The system was instructed to capture not only spoken 
explanations but also relevant nonverbal information, such as equipment state changes and hand 
movements, when these contributed to procedural understanding. 
In the Q&A stage, the structured manual was stored in a database and used as the knowledge 
source for a RAG-based question-answering system. Upon receiving a user query, the system retrieved 
relevant portions of the manual and generated an answer governed by predefined prompt constraints. 
In the evaluation stage, the system was assessed quantitatively through question 
answering and qualitatively through expert review of a generated advisory report. 
2.2. Prompt Design 
Two prompt types, retrieval and instructional prompts, were utilized, both designed around the 
principles of safety-first and strict grounding. Both prompts were written in English and included 
specific examples of queries and corresponding responses in both English and Japanese. The whole 
prompts are available in the SI. 
Retrieval Prompt (≤50-word responses) was designed for concise question answering strictly 
grounded in the manual. Its role was to produce short, source-based responses and avoid unsupported 
elaboration. If the required information was absent from the manual, the model was explicitly 
instructed to state this limitation rather than infer an answer. 
Instructional Prompt (no words limit) was designed for broader educational support. This prompt 
allowed more detailed output, including procedural guidance, risk reminders, and cautionary notes. 
Even in this mode, the model was required to remain within the bounds of the source material and 
direct the user to a human supervisor when necessary.

2.3. Performance Evaluation 
System performance was evaluated using two approaches. First, for quantitative evaluation, 21 
question-answer pairs related to pXRD operation using MiniFlex600 (Rigaku) were prepared. AI-
generated responses were compared with instructor-provided reference answers. Semantic similarity 
was calculated as cosine similarity between sentence embeddings generated by the multilingual 
Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model (paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2)18. For questions covered 
by the manual, higher similarity indicated better agreement with the intended guidance. For questions 
outside the manual, the evaluation focused on whether the system successfully refrained from 
generating unsupported content. Statistical comparisons of similarity scores between groups were 
performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. 
Second, for qualitative evaluation, the Instructional Prompt was used to generate an advisory 
report for a representative user query. The advisory reports were evaluated by four independent experts, 
all of whom have hands-on experience with Rigaku powder X-ray diffraction, such as Miniflex600 
(Rigaku) or MultiFlex (Rigaku). Each evaluator independently rated the advisory reports on two 
criteria—utility and safety—using a four-point scale. The scoring rubric is provided at the end of the 
Supplementary Information.

functioned as intended, we compared responses produced through the RAG-based pipeline (governed 
by the Retrieval Prompt from AI-produced manual) with those produced by AI without RAG.

Figure 2 shows the comparison of SBERT similarity scores. For in-manual questions, the RAG-
based system yielded higher similarity scores (0.58 ± 0.12) than the baseline (0.50 ± 0.09). This result 
indicates that the retrieval mechanism successfully supplied procedural knowledge to the response. It 
also suggests that relevant information was retained through each stage of the pipeline—from the 
student's verbal explanation during recording, through the multimodal structuring step, to the final 
answer generation. 
 
     Conversely, for out-of-manual questions, this trend reversed. The RAG-based system produced 
lower similarity scores (0.29 ± 0.16) compared to both the baseline (0.41 ± 0.15) and its own 
performance on in-manual questions. The relatively higher baseline score reflects the tendency of the 
unconstrained LLM to generate relevant but unverified content. Critically, the difference in RAG-
based similarity scores between in-manual and out-of-manual questions was statistically significant 
(Mann-Whitney U test, U=96, p<0.001), indicating that the system effectively distinguished between 
queries within and outside the scope of the manual. Qualitative examination of the 13 out-of-manual 
responses revealed three distinct patterns: explicit refusal stating the information was not in the manual 
(n=4), safety warnings directing the user to immediately consult a supervisor (n=3), and partial 
responses limited to manual-grounded information accompanied by escalation to a human instructor 
(n=6). In no case did the system generate unsupported procedural content beyond the scope of the 
manual. This behavior followed the safety protocol specified in the Retrieval Prompt.3.2. 
 Expert Evaluation of Advisory Report 
As a representative example, an advisory report generated via the Instructional Prompt in response to 
the query "How can I determine the crystal structure of TiO₂ using the MiniFlex600?" received mean 
scores of 3.25 out of 4.00 for utility and 4.00 out of 4.00 for safety by independent four experts.  
The generated output exhibited three notable features. First, the system placed safety 
information before operational instructions, prompting users to check relevant Safety Data Sheets 
(SDS) and complete in-person training before operating the instrument. A dedicated risk-assessment 
section alerted users to serious hazards such as radiation exposure and possible equipment damage. 
Second, the report incorporated site-specific information, including local rules such as making 
reservations through Google Calendar and recording usage in the logbook beside the instrument. Third, 
the system captured operational know-how and flagged common beginner mistakes—for example, the 
physical technique needed to level a powder sample and the need to execute the shutdown command 
to prevent the instrument doors from remaining locked while X-rays were active. 
When asked how to analyze the acquired data to determine crystal structure, the system 
recognized that the necessary analytical procedures were not included in the manual. It declined to 
generate an unsupported procedure and instead directed the student to consult their supervising 
professor. This refusal behavior was consistent with the pattern observed in the quantitative evaluation

4. Discussion 
4.1. Digitizing Practical Know-How for Laboratory Training 
The results suggest that first-person video, combined with spoken explanation, can serve as a practical 
source for capturing laboratory know-how often omitted from formal written documentation. This 
includes fine-grained operational details (e.g., the handling technique for leveling a powder sample), 
context-dependent precautions, and local procedural rules. Once converted into structured text, such 
information can be reused through a retrieval-based system to support later users. Together, these 
findings suggest that bridging the experimental last mile requires not only access to formal information 
but also the digitization of laboratory know-how, including site-specific rules, routine checks, safety-
related handling, and, where relevant, embodied actions, visual judgment, and timing. 
The higher similarity scores for in-manual questions (Table 1) suggest that the extracted know-
how was delivered to users through the RAG framework. Such operational and site-specific 
knowledge is not included in published scientific knowledge, nor the content that current multi-agent 
AI systems typically provide9, 10. By treating the laboratory know-how as the primary knowledge 
source, the present framework captures information that models trained only on public information 
are unlikely to provide. Furthermore, the digitization of such practical know-how may contribute to 
addressing the challenge of experimental reproducibility, although its direct impact remains to be 
empirically validated. Recent studies on fully autonomous laboratories4, 5 have highlighted the 
challenge of human variability in experimental workflows. While automated systems address this by 
enforcing mechanical consistency, human operators in traditional labs must rely on acquiring physical 
know-how—such as the specific handling technique needed to level a powder sample—to achieve the 
same level of data accuracy. By structuring and sharing these site-specific, under-documented skills 
through AI, our framework may help minimize the human variability that can compromise data quality. 
In this sense, digitizing laboratory know-how may offer a practical step toward improving consistency 
in environments where full robotic automation is not yet feasible. 
4.2. Safety through constrained AI support 
A central design goal of this framework was not autonomous decision-making but constrained support. 
In laboratory environments, unsupported model output is unacceptable when it may affect safety-
related behavior. For that reason, the system was designed with two complementary safeguards:


## Page 10


restriction of the answer source through RAG and explicit prompt-level prohibition of unsupported 
inference. Indeed, recent work has cautioned that RAG alone does not guarantee safe outputs, 
highlighting the importance of the additional prompt-level constraints employed in this study.19 
     The observed refusal behavior for out-of-manual questions is therefore an important result. In this 
setting, declining to answer is preferable to producing a plausible but unverified response. The same 
principle also applied to the generation of advisory reports, indicating that the safety design remained 
effective across different output formats. 
4.3. Boundaries of AI-Assisted Laboratory Support 
Although the system shows promise for digitizing laboratory know-how, exclusive reliance on AI for 
laboratory management would carry inherent risks. Recent work has highlighted the need for careful 
design when integrating human-AI collaboration into practical workflows16, to avoid automation bias 
and overreliance. The output quality is fundamentally limited by the accuracy of those source records. 
The AI cannot independently resolve undocumented troubles, and should be treated as a support tool 
rather than a decision-maker. 
Deployment of such systems should therefore be accompanied by clear operational protocols. 
For example, in the present context, several measures would be proposed. Generated manuals should 
be verified by instructors before use, since the knowledge base originates from student input. Explicit 
escalation rules should direct students to consult a human instructor whenever any anomaly occurs. 
In-person safety training for beginners should also be maintained. The specific protocols required will 
vary with institutional and disciplinary context, but the underlying principle is consistent: a human-
in-the-loop architecture in which AI handles the routine transfer of operational knowledge while 
human experts retain final responsibility for safety. Finally, we note that our work is complementary 
to AI systems that support upstream research phases: hypothesis generation9 and computational 
experimental design10 address what and how to investigate, whereas the present system addresses how 
to carry out the experiment safely.  
4.4. Language and usability 
The system was operated in Japanese in the present study. This was an important practical choice, 
because safe laboratory communication depends on minimizing misunderstanding and cognitive load. 
Instructions are more reliable when presented in a language familiar to the user, especially during 
complex procedures or unusual events. At the same time, the framework itself is not limited to 
Japanese and could be adapted for international laboratory environments. English and Japanese 
outputs of Advisory Reports are in the supplementary document.

4.5. Limitations 
Several limitations should be noted. First, the system was validated with a single student using a single 
instrument (pXRD) in a single laboratory. Whether the pipeline generalizes to other instruments, 
experimental procedures, or institutional contexts remains to be established. Second, although the 
qualitative evaluation involved four independent expert evaluators, this small panel limits the 
statistical power of the ratings. Third, the study assessed system performance at a single time point; 
long-term effects on training quality, knowledge retention, and safety outcomes were not measured.  
Future work should address these limitations through multi-instrument and multi-site validation, 
larger evaluator panels, long-term assessment of educational and safety outcomes, and systematic 
error-checking mechanisms within the pipeline. 
 
5. Conclusion 
This study presented a human-in-the-loop AI framework for digitizing laboratory know-how from 
first-person experimental video and providing grounded support through RAG to bridge the 
experimental last mile. Using pXRD operation as a case study, we found that the system could capture 
and reuse site-specific procedural knowledge and provide responses aligned with instructor 
expectations for questions covered by the manual. For questions outside the manual, the system 
consistently declined to answer rather than generating unsupported content. Expert evaluation further 
suggested that the generated advisory reports were both useful and safe. 
The significance of this work lies not in replacing automation-driven approaches, but in 
complementing them by supporting the human-led laboratories where most academic research still 
takes place. By combining multimodal knowledge capture, constrained retrieval-based generation, and 
explicit human oversight, the proposed framework offers a practical approach to reducing training 
burden while maintaining safety and reproducibility. While its direct impact on experimental 
reproducibility remains a subject for future study, such AI-assisted transfer of local know-how serves 
as a realistic step toward more consistent and safety-conscious support for reproducible human-led 
experiments in laboratories where full automation is not yet feasible.

Answer in the same language as the user's question. 
The answer must be under 50 words in English, or under 100 characters in 
Japanese. Bullet points are prohibited. 
If no relevant information is found → return the fixed-format response only. No

Role Definition 
You are the "Laboratory Equipment and Safety Management Support AI" for the 
Structural Inorganic Chemistry Laboratory. Based on the provided knowledge 
base (procedure manuals), you serve as an accurate navigator to help students 
prevent to the greatest extent possible equipment damage, data loss, and 
personal injury (radiation exposure, fire, chemical accidents, electric shock, 
explosions). Limitation of chemical characters should be mentioned if necessary. 
 
Target Users and Stance 
Target: Students who are planning experiments or want to reconfirm equipment 
operation 
Stance: A "strict yet sincere expert" who never compromises on safety 
Tone: Polite and concise, but firm and resolute when addressing important safety 
points 
Critical Rules (Highest Priority) 
 
Prohibition of Answers Based on Knowledge Outside the Knowledge Base. Do 
not answer using information not found in the procedure manuals. If no relevant 
information is available, return the Pattern B fixed-format response. 
 
Prohibition of Original Proposals Never propose new experimental conditions

•

2. 試料調製（TiO₂粉末） 
• 
ホルダー選定: 通常の粉末測定では ガラス板ホルダー を使用します 
(/Miniflex.docx, Section 4-1)。 
• 
充填: 中サイズのスパチュラ約1 杯分のTiO₂粉末をホルダーの窓に盛ります 
(/Miniflex.docx, Section 4-2)。 
• 
平滑化（ツライチ）: 別のガラス板で粉末を押し当て、スライドさせて表面を平ら
にします。**「ツライチ（面一）」**になっていないと、ピーク角度のズレや強度
低下の原因になります (/Miniflex.docx, Section 4-2)。 
3. 試料のセット 
• 
高さの選択（最重要）: 試料ステージの 「一番高い位置の歯」 の上にホルダーを載
せてください。低い位置に載せると正しいデータが得られません (/Miniflex.docx, 
Section 5-1)。 
• 
向き: ガラスの厚みがある側（または透明な面）を奥側にして差し込みます 
(/Miniflex.docx, Section 5-1)。 
4. 測定設定と実行 
• 
条件設定: SmartLab Studio II で保存先とファイル名を設定します。スキャンスピー
ドは、構造決定のためにS/N 比を上げたい場合は 1～5 deg/min 程度の遅めに設定
することを推奨します (/Miniflex.docx, Section 6-3)。 
• 
自動化: 測定フローの最後には必ず 「Shutdown」 コマンドを入れ、X 線OFF とド

• 
本体電源: 前面のグレーの電源ボタン（丸マーク）を押してOFF にします
