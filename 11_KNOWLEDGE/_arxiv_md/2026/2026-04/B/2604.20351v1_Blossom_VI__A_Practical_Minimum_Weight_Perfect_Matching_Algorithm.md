Abstract
We implement an algorithm for solving the minimum weight perfect matching problem.
Our code significantly outperforms the current state-of-the-art Blossom V algorithm on those
families of instances where Blossom V takes superlinear time. In practice, our implementation
shows almost-linear runtime on every family of instances on which we have tested it.
Our algorithm relies on solving the maximum-cardinality unweighted matching problems
during its primal phase. Following the state-of-the-art cherry blossom algorithm, we use cherry
trees instead of traditional alternating trees and cherry blossoms instead of traditional blossoms.
We shrink cherry blossoms rather than traditional blossoms into supernodes. This strategy
allows us to deal with much shallower supernodes.

We study the minimum weight perfect matching problem. Given a graph G = (V, E) with edge
weights we, one needs to compute a perfect matching of minimum total weight, i.e. a subset of
edges M ⊆E that minimizes the total weight P

The existence of a polynomial-time algorithm for solving the minimum weight perfect matching
problem is due to Edmonds [10, 11]. Since then, the problem has inspired a long line of research.
On the theoretical side, the complexity of the algorithm has been improving through [18, 15,

weight perfect matching problem is Blossom V, which has been uncontested since 2009. Following
the tradition of naming the minimum weight perfect matching algorithms, we call our algorithm
Blossom VI.

1.3
Unweighted maximum matching
The problem of finding a maximum-cardinality matching in an unweighted graph is also a widely
studied problem. First solved by Edmonds in [11], it received a lot of attention. The theoretical

## Page 3


works are [14, 24, 29, 25, 30], with the current best complexity being O(m√n) due to Micali and
Vazirani [24]. Their algorithm is famously complicated and required later clarifications [29, 30].
There also exist a lot of practical implementations, including the Boost library [28], the LEMON
library [7], the cherry-tree implementation [9] and X-Blossom implementation [13].
The cherry-tree algorithm is the closest to our work. It solves the maximum-cardinality match-
ing problem by augmenting alternating paths between unmatched nodes. It does so by growing
several cherry trees: data structures that allow one to channel an augmenting path from any tree
node marked “+” to the root. Instead of shrinking blossoms upon finding odd cycles, the authors
build cherry blossoms: sets of nodes within one tree with a special receptacle node, such that every
other node in the cherry blossom has an odd and an even alternating path to the receptacle. We
are going to discuss the cherry trees and cherry blossoms below in more detail.
Another interesting feature of the cherry-algorithm is the usage of metagraphs. To perform an
augmentation destroying a pair of cherry trees, one needs two cherry trees and a suitable unmatched
edge connecting the trees. In the general case, there is some freedom in choosing the augmentations.
A minimal meaningful example is the following: imagine four trees T1, T2, T3, T4. Suppose, the
available augmentations are (T1, T2), (T1, T3), (T2, T4). One can augment (T1, T2) immediately,
but then one would need to grow T3 and T4 further until one finds an augmentation between T3
and T4. On the other hand, with a smarter choice of augmentations, one can immediately augment
(T1, T3) and (T2, T4), destroying all the trees, and thus, the algorithm would be done. For this
reason, the authors of the cherry algorithm propose shrinking the trees (hence the name of their
paper) and finding a maximum matching recursively on the metagraph whose nodes are the trees
and whose edges are the available augmentations. A matching on the metagraph is then translated
into an augmentation pattern in the original graph. We do not use this feature in our adaptation.
1.4
Overview of our contributions
The following observation is at the heart of this paper: the primal phase of a blossom-type al-
gorithm is indistinguishable from solving a maximum unweighted matching on a graph (V, E0),
where E0 = {e ∈E | slack(e) = 0} is the set of zero-slack edges. Indeed, during the primal phase,
one does not change the dual variables or the edge slacks, and as many augmentations as possible
are performed, increasing the current size of the matching as far as possible. This observation
allows us to use the state-of-the-art practical algorithm for the maximum unweighted matching [9]
with cherry trees and cherry blossoms in our primal update phase.
After the primal update is over, we shrink the cherry blossoms into supernodes and perform
a dual update. In contrast, Blossom V shrinks traditional blossoms into supernodes. Shrinking
cherry blossoms leads to less supernode initializations and to much shallower blossoms in com-
parison to Blossom V, since instead of a single cherry blossom shrink, it would generally perform
multiple nested blossom shrinks.
In blossom algorithms, blossoms have two purposes. First, blossoms hold the dual variables
yS. Second, blossoms know how to change the matching inside themselves to keep the matching
feasible after an augmentation. It is possible to solve the unweighted maximum matching without
shrinking any vertex sets [31, 2, 14, 3, 9]. In our weighted case, we cannot avoid shrink operations
altogether, since we may need to have non-zero dual variables for some odd-sized vertex sets.
Shrinking such sets into supervertices with their own dual variables is natural. However, shrinking
cherry blossoms conceptually divides the responsibilities “hold a dual variable” and “channel an
augmenting path to the root”.
When a blossom algorithm makes several nested blossoms during a single primal phase, all the
inner blossoms hold a zero dual variable. Only the topmost blossom will have its dual variable

changed during the upcoming dual update. Thus, there is no reason for the inner blossoms to
be initialized: a zero dual variable is implicitly assumed for any odd-sized node subset anyway,
and a cherry blossom structure allows us to update the matching inside the blossom when needed.
Moreover, if such a nested blossom is expanded in the future, we would generally have a sequence of
expands for the nested blossoms in a single primal phase: these inner blossoms have zero variables
and thus may be eligible for expands in case they are minuses.
Since expands are expensive
operations, avoiding cascading expands gives a significant speedup.
Note that [9] also uses contraction operations: when the graph becomes sufficiently dense,
certain cherry trees are contracted into supernodes (which is reflected in the title of their paper).
If we followed this strategy, we would need to expand supernodes before applying dual updates.
We contract cherry blossoms instead, and thus can proceed directly with dual updates when no
more primal updates are possible.
We implemented our algorithm in C++ and performed extensive experimental comparison
with our main rival, the Blossom V algorithm. We observed a significant speedup for families of
instances where Blossom V takes superlinear time. However, for those families of instances that
are solved by Blossom V in almost linear time, our code might be up to two times slower than
Blossom V. Our code is available on GitHub: https://github.com/Paul566/blossom-vi.

Slacks and dual variables
We denote the current slack of an edge e by slack(e); it is given by
eq. 3. The following invariant is maintained throughout the algorithm: all plus- and minus-parents
in the cherry forest and in all blossoms, as well as all matched edges, have zero slack. All other
edges have a non-negative slack.
Each blossom v stores the dual variable yv. If v corresponds to a singleton node in Vorig then
yv can have any sign. Otherwise, if v corresponds to an odd-cardinality subset of Vorig with at
least three nodes, we must have yv ≥0.
The algorithm alternates between (i) primal updates, which modify the current cherry forest,
blossoms and matchings while keeping all dual variables intact, and (ii) dual updates, which modify
variables yv in the current cherry forest. Below we describe operations that are allowed in these
updates.

Dual updates
The dual update phase can be applied when there are no top ±-nodes (and hence each cherry tree
is a normal alternating tree). In this phase we find a non-negative number δT for each cherry tree
T, and then update dual variables for nodes v in T: if v is a plus-node then increase yv by δT , and
if v is a minus-node then decrease yv by δT . This will increase the dual objective by P

Main Algorithm
This section contains a description of our algorithm. Let us define several types of structures that
we use.
• An edge remembers the following information:
– endpoints
– slack
– the edge heap it belongs to (may be null)
• A node remembers the following information:

Experimental results
We have measured the performance of our C++ implementation against the performance of Blos-
som V on 9 families of instances. We used a laptop with Intel Core i7-8565U CPU (1.80GHz, 8MB

Take the dual planar graph with weights inherited from the weights of the

weight perfect matching in this graph corresponds to a max-cut in the triangulation. Solving
max-cut is also sometimes described in terms of finding a ground state of a specific Ising
model.

Delaunay triangulation of the point cloud and set the weights to edge lengths rounded to

5
Implementation remarks
Our implementation includes some optimizations not listed in the main algorithm description from
Section 2.3. Here, we give a list of important implementation details that were not stated before.
1. Initialization: we tested several initialization strategies.
First, we considered the greedy initialization. In the very beginning, we initialize the dual
variables such that all the edge slacks are non-negative. We do this by setting yv = 1

After that, we go through the nodes and greedily increase the associated dual variables as
far as we can (i.e. until some incident edge hits zero slack). The matching is also initialized
greedily: if the current node is unmatched and there is an unmatched neighbor at the end
of a zero-slack incident edge, we include such edge in the matching.
Second, we considered the fractional matching initialization. We still start by initializing yv
such that all slacks are non-negative. After that, we solve the minimum weight fractional
perfect matching problem, obtaining a half-integral solution, and then round it. This ini-
tialization was suggested in [6]. We have implemented the algorithm described in [1], with a
single alternating tree.
Third, we considered an approximate fractional matching initialization. For the sake of ini-
tialization, we do not have to solve the fractional problem to optimality. Thus, we introduced
a threshold t: if the alternating tree from the fractional matching algorithm grows to have
more than t nodes, we abandon it without performing any augmentations. We found t = 100
to be a good value that balances between a cheap initialization and a good jumpstart for
the main algorithm. We use this initialization strategy in all tests. Enabling the threshold
is especially impactful in the sparse random family of instances. Potentially, implementing
a fractional matching initialization with multiple trees could give another mild speedup, but
we did not go through with it.

was made because of the rounding). Consequently, if we quadruple all edge weights in the
very beginning, then we will have integer slacks and variables throughout the algorithm.2
3. Lazy updates for slacks and dual variables: in the dual update phase, for a tree T,
we need to increase the dual variables of the top plus-nodes by δT and decrease the dual

incident to the tree. Just like in Blossom V, we do this lazily in O(1) time by storing a
variable yT in the tree holding the sum of δT over all dual updates. A node remembers a
lazy variable yv, and the true dual variable can be calculated as yv = yv ± yT , where T is the
tree that v belongs to, and the sign is determined by the sign of v. A similar lazy calculation
goes for edge slacks.

In the edge heaps, the edges are compared by their lazy slacks, and nodes in the node
heaps are compared by their lazy variables. This is correct, because within the same type of
edges/nodes, lazy slacks/variables yield the same ordering as the true slacks/variables.
4. Lazy updates for heads and tails: every edge remembers the top nodes of its endpoints.
Whenever we shrink a set of nodes S into a supernode, the endpoints of the edges in δ(S)
change. We do not update the endpoints immediately. Rather, we allow the head/tail fields
of an edge to point to non-top blossoms. Once needed, we can find the true head/tail by
walking up the blossom parent pointers. The same is done in Blossom V.
5. Path compression for blossom parents: to calculate the top node of a given node, we
do not walk the entire path of blossom parents, but use path compression.
Every node
remembers its blossom ancestor, which is a lazily computed top node. The same is done in
Blossom V. Notice that expands may invalidate the current blossom ancestor fields for the
blossom descendants of the expanded blossom.
6. Lazy updates for queues of internal plus plus edges: after the queues are updated, all
queues are correct except for (possibly) the queues of plus-plus internal edges. This means
that the queues except for the queues of plus-plus internal edges contain precisely the set of
non-loop edges of the needed type. Queues of plus-plus internal edges are allowed to contain
plus-plus loops. Whenever we need to get the minimal slack plus plus internal non-loop edge,
we keep popping the root of the heap until it is a non-loop. This makes shrink operations
cheaper. The same is done in Blossom V.
7. No tree children needed: in Blossom V and the unweighted cherry-based solver from [9],
nodes in a tree remember their children. This way, the authors of [22] and [9] can traverse
their trees when they need to. We notice that the only case when we need to go through
all the nodes in the tree is when the tree is destroyed. Moreover, the order of the traversal
does not matter. Thus, we can just make a tree remember the list of its vertices, rather than
make nodes remember their tree children. This is useful with regard to shrink operations,
since we do not need to define the tree children for the supernodes created.
8. Heap choice: We compared two types of heaps for the edge heaps and the node heaps: the
traditional d-ary array-based heap and the pairing heap.
The traditional array-based heap allows one to push new elements to the heap and pop the
heap root in O(log N) time, where N is the size of the heap. Accessing the root is O(1).
Such heaps are attractive, because the elements are stored contiguously in a vector, making it
cache-friendlier than heaps that have tree structure. For our purposes, we need an additional
operation: given an edge, remove it from the heap that it is currently in. For this, we used
handles. Insertion returns a pointer to a handle of the inserted element, and one can use this
handle later to locate the element in the heap and remove it.
The pairing heap allows us to access the minimum element and insert a new element in
O(1), and delete elements in O(log N) amortized time. We implemented an intrusive pairing
heap, i.e. the heap-related information is stored in the edges. Intrusiveness makes the “given
an edge, remove it” operation more natural, since there is no need for handles. The latest
version of Blossom V uses pairing heaps.
We observed that pairing heaps have a better performance in our algorithm, so we use them
in the final version of our code.

9. Incorrect removes from dead heaps: removing an element from a pairing heap is the
only operation with pairing heaps that is not done in O(1) time. We observe that some edges
and nodes might be in heaps that are associated with trees that have already been destroyed.
In this case, we do not need to maintain this heap in a correct state. Thus, we can afford
doing incorrect remove operations in a straightforward way in O(1) time from such a heap.
An alternative way would be to explicitly destroy all the heaps associated with a tree upon
the tree destruction. However, the edges and nodes from these heaps might never be included
in another heap in the future, so our lazy heap destruction is more efficient.
10. Incident edges computations: we frequently need to traverse all edges incident to a node.
We tried two ways of maintaining the list of neighboring non-loop edges for supernodes.
The first way is to store the neighboring non-loop edges in a lazily computed vector. When
we need to traverse the neighbors of a given node that does not have the field computed yet,
we go through the tree of the blossom descendants until we reach nodes with the neighbors
field computed. Then, we populate the list of neighbors with the edges from the descendants’
neighbors lists, discarding loops.
The second way is to store the neighboring non-loop edges in a linked list. Upon Shrink
operations, we steal the neighbors lists from the blossom children and splice them together
to make a list of neighbors for the new vertex. Later, upon the first traversal, we discard
loops from the list. This is done in Blossom V.
The first approach stores the data contiguously and does less work in Shrink operations.
The second approach uses less memory. We use the first approach in the final version of our
code, since it showed a slightly better performance.
11. E0 maintenance: recall that E0 is the set of zero-slack edges. During our primal phase, we
need to compute a maximum matching in the graph (V, E0). It requires traversing zero-slack
incident edges for a given vertex. One can go through all incident edges of a vertex, compute
the true slack for every edge, and then do the required action only to the edges of zero slack.
We observed a performance improvement when we added the following trick. We make the
edges remember a boolean field maybe has zero slack. If maybe has zero slack=false,
then the edge has non-zero slack. Otherwise, the edge may or may not have zero slack.
When we go through incident edges of a given vertex, we immediately discard the edges with
maybe has zero slack=false. Although computing the true slack for a given edge takes
O(1) time, it requires accessing the endpoint vertices and the trees they belong to, which is
more expensive than just accessing the edge. It turns out that maintaining the extra field
maybe has zero slack improves the performance. The field is set to true after the dual
updates for those edges that became zero-slack.
However, E0 is not purely incremental,
since, for example, a minus-minus edge may increase its slack. For those edges that leave
E0, maybe has zero slack is set to false lazily.
6
Discussion of correctness
Our algorithm deviates significantly from the usual blossom algorithms with traditional alternating
trees and traditional blossoms. For this reason, we need to discuss the correctness of the algorithm.
It is enough to prove that if no progress can be made in both the primal and the dual phases
(and thus, the algorithm is stuck), then the input graph does not contain a perfect matching.
16


## Page 17


The lowest-effort proof is to show that the behavior of our algorithm can be emulated by a
traditional multi-tree blossom algorithm. Thus, it is enough to show that any cherry blossom can
be represented by a traditional blossom (possibly, with sub-blossoms). Then, the state of the trees
after the shrinking phase can be realized as a state of a traditional blossom algorithm. Thus, the
traditional blossom algorithm would also be stuck in this state.
Finally, it is left to show that for any cherry blossom B, there exists a sequence of shrinks of
traditional blossoms that also result in shrinking B. Let r be the receptacle of B. Take any node
v ∈B −r such that r is a minus-parent of v. Let P be the even alternating path from v to r.
P together with the minus-parent of v make an odd alternating cycle of zero-slack edges. Shrink
it into a traditional blossom and make the blossom the new receptacle of B. Clearly, the cherry
blossom structure of the new smaller cherry blossom is still valid. Repeat this procedure until the
entire cherry blossom is contracted. Thus, the correctness claim holds.
