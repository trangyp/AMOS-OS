BUCKET BRIGADES: UNIQUENESS OF THE FIXED POINT AND
THREE-WORKER ASYMPTOTICS
YASSER ALGHOUASS, ABDERHAMANE DRIOUCH, MOHAMMED LAGMAH,
AND FR´ED´ERIC MEUNIER
Abstract. A standard organization of production lines exhibiting self-balancing behavior is
given by bucket brigades. Their study in operations research was initiated by the foundational
work of Bartholdi and Eisenstein (Operations Research, 1996), where a simplified version
of the model is considered. Their main result shows that when workers are ordered from
the slowest to the fastest, the system is stable and converges to a “fixed point,” where each
worker oscillates between two limiting positions. They also observe that the dynamics can
become highly complex when this ordering condition is not satisfied. The no-station setting,
in which work is distributed continuously and uniformly along the production line, is given
special attention in their work. In a subsequent paper with Bunimovich (Operations Research,
1999), they characterize all stable behaviors of this setting for up to three workers.
In this work, we extend their analysis for three workers beyond the stable regime, providing
a complete description when workers are ordered from the fastest to the slowest. We also
show that, due to their restrictive notion of stability, some of their conclusions must be
revisited. Finally, for an arbitrary number of workers, we prove that the fixed point is always
unique in the no-station setting.
1. Introduction
1.1. Context. “Bucket brigades” form a way to organize workers on a production line. In
its simplest form, such an organization can be described as follows. Assume that the workers
are numbered from 1 to n according to their sequence on the line. In the ideal version of the
organization, the workers follow two rules:
Forward rule. Worker i remains devoted to a single item, processes it along the production
line until the item is taken over by worker i + 1 (or until worker i reaches the end of the
production line in case i = n), and then applies the backward rule. If worker i catches up
with worker i + 1, then worker i adjusts his velocity to that of worker i + 1.
Backward rule. Worker i walks back to workers i −1 and takes over its item (or walks back
to the start of the line and begins a new item in case i = 1), and then applies the forward
rule.
Moreover, no overtaking is possible.
Bucket brigades are used in a variety of industries [1,6] (often with some flexibility in the
way the idealized organization described above is applied). They have been studied from
various points of view; see, e.g., [2,5,7,9,10]. Bartholdi and Eisenstein [3] have provided the
first theoretical study, which remains the reference work on this topic. They show that even
though their model is deterministic, it already captures a substantial part of the essence of

elusive).
In the original model of Bartholdi and Eisenstein [3], the production line is moreover
subdivided into “stations,” whose presence further complicates the system’s dynamics. As
noted in their paper, the special setting where the stations are of negligible length, which we
call no-station, is already of great interest. This is actually the situation considered in the
paper by Bartholdi, Bunimovich, and Eisenstein [4].
Our contributions focus on the no-station setting and are twofold. First, we show that in
this setting the fixed point is always unique, even when the workers are not ordered from
the slowest to the fastest; the proof is remarkably short. Second, we complete the analysis
of Bartholdi, Bunimovich, and Eisenstein in the three-worker case. We provide proofs for
several behaviors they identified, and we also investigate non-stable behaviors. This broader
perspective shows that their neat conclusions do not necessarily extend beyond the stability
assumption. Moreover, we point out a limitation in their treatment of stability. They write
at the very beginning of their paper:
By “stable behavior” we mean qualitative structure that persists, even in
the presence of perturbations. This is the behavior that will assert itself in
practice.
However, their analysis is restricted to periodic behaviors, whereas we show that other
qualitative structures may persist in a robust way without being periodic.
1.2. Model.
1.2.1. No-station setting. We describe the model of Bartholdi and Eisenstein [3] directly in the
no-station setting. The state of the system can be represented as a vector x = (x1, x2, . . . , xn),
with 0 ⩽x1 ⩽x2 ⩽· · · ⩽xn ⩽1. The quantity xi represents the position of workers i along
the production line identified with the interval [0, 1]. Each worker i comes with a function
vi : [0, 1] →R>0: when worker i has not caught up worker i + 1, his velocity at position
x is vi(x). Otherwise, his velocity is equal to the velocity of worker i + 1. The following
assumptions are made for vi:
• vi is continuous almost everywhere;
• there exist numbers b and B such that 0 < b < vi(x) < B < +∞for all x ∈[0, 1].
Finally, the workers are assumed to move backward at an infinite velocity. This means that
when worker n reaches the end of the production line, then at the same instant, worker n
takes over from worker n −1, who takes over from worker n −2, and so on, and worker 1 are
sent back to the start of the production line to begin a new item. This corresponds to a reset.

3.2. Region 1. Bartholdi, Bunimovich, and Eisenstein [4] claim that the system always
converges to the fixed point when the speeds lie in the “Region 1,” which is the set of
speeds satisfying simultaneously v1 < v3 and v2 < v1 + v3. This is also confirmed by our own
experiments. However, even though this is proved in the paper by Bartholdi and Eisenstein [3]
when v1 < v2 < v3, they do not provide any proof for the other values in this region. This
deserves future work.
3.3. Four workers and beyond. Even though we are able to describe quite precisely the
asymptotic behavior of bucket brigades with three workers in many situations, we are far
from providing a complete answer. Describing the asymptotic behavior of bucket brigades for
four workers and more (still assuming constant velocities and instantaneous walk-balks) seem
to be a challenging open problem.
