Glassy Arrest Behind the Apparent Second Liquid in
Water

1SISSA – Scuola Internazionale Superiore di Studi Avanzati, 34136 Trieste, Italy

The origin of water’s anomalous behavior remains a central open problem in
the physical sciences and is often attributed to a liquid–liquid transition (LLT)
between high- and low-density liquid states deep in the supercooled regime. Exper-
imental access to this region has been challenging due to rapid crystallization, leav-
ing atomistic simulations as a major source of supporting evidence. Using extensive
machine-learning–accelerated first-principles simulations in direct comparison
with spectroscopic, structural, and dynamical experimental measurements, we
show that features commonly interpreted as signatures of two-liquid behavior
emerge at the onset of dynamical arrest. Specifically, we find that two-state fluc-
tuations previously associated with an LLT reflect a transformation from a high-
density liquid to a kinetically arrested low-density glass. By mapping equilibrium
dynamics across pressure and temperature, our results suggest a reassessment of
water’s metastable landscape, in which apparent two-state behavior may reflect a
relatively high glass-transition temperature of ambient-pressure low-density wa-
ter, 189±8 K—remarkably close to the temperature previously associated with the
LLT.

Water defies the expectations of a simple liquid. Upon cooling, it expands rather than contracts,
while its compressibility and heat capacity exhibit pronounced extrema. These anomalies have long
hinted at an underlying organizing principle that remains unresolved (1, 2). A leading hypothesis
attributes them to fluctuations associated with a hidden liquid–liquid critical point (LLCP), buried
below the freezing line at elevated pressure. Support for this view emerged from the observation
of a first-order–like transition between two amorphous states in experiments (3), and later between
two liquid states in simulations (4), giving rise to the liquid–liquid transition scenario.
Experimental access to the LLCP has proven extremely challenging because it lies within
the crystallization-prone no-man’s-land. Only very recent experiments have managed to penetrate
this regime, probing various physical quantities on ultra-short time scales before ice formation
intervenes (2,5–7). As a consequence, much of the support for the LLCP hypothesis has relied on
information coming from molecular dynamics simulations.
Very recently, atomistic simulations using machine-learning based potentials trained on first-
principles data (8,9) seem to confirm the original hypothesis of the existence of an LLT that were
based on the ST2 potential (4), while studies based on the classical point-charge model TIP4P/2005
have produced conflicting conclusions on the presence of an LLT (10–12). Although these simula-
tions have pushed the boundary to microsecond timescales, comparable to the ultrafast experiments
that briefly access deeply supercooled water before crystallization intervenes, equilibration at these
low temperatures becomes exceedingly challenging due to the very long structural relaxation times.
At these conditions, the dynamics becomes glassy and the inference of thermodynamic quantities
from simulations becomes difficult. This challenge also translates to experiments deriving thermo-
dynamic support for the presence of an LLCP (7), since they involve measurements limited to the
microsecond timescales before the onset of crystallization.
In fact, one of the open questions in the physics of supercooled water is the exact location of
its glass transition temperature (13). The commonly cited value of 136 K hinges on experiments of
hyper-quenched and vapor-deposited samples whose relationship to slowly-supercooled pure water
is uncertain. These measurements show only a faint calorimetric signal at this temperature, small
enough to be interpreted as a ”shadow” transition (14, 15), suggesting that the true liquid–glass
transformation may occur at substantially higher temperatures. While moderately concentrated salt
solutions often exhibit a 𝑇𝑔around 136 K, recent experiments using minimal solute content find a

These observations raise an intriguing possibility: if deeply supercooled water becomes glassy
around the temperature of the LLCP, could signatures often attributed to a liquid–liquid transition
also be consistent with a transition between a fluid high-density liquid (HDL) and a kinetically
arrested low-density glass? To tackle this question, we explore how the equilibrium dynamics of
deeply supercooled water evolve as a function of temperature and pressure, comparing state-of-
the-art machine-learning–accelerated first-principles simulations with experimental measurements
in regimes where crystallization can be suppressed. This allows us to identify the conditions
under which liquid water remains ergodic and when it instead becomes kinetically arrested—
distinguishing genuine liquid behavior from glassy non-equilibrium states. By establishing the
location of the glass-transition boundary relative to the proposed liquid–liquid transition, we suggest
that signatures attributed to an LLT may not necessarily reflect an equilibrium transformation
between two liquids, but could instead be consistent with a crossover between a liquid and a glass.

Discussion and Conclusions
The hypothesis of a liquid–liquid transition (LLT) has shaped modern understanding of water’s
anomalies. Yet direct experimental proof remains elusive, leaving molecular simulations as a
primary source of supporting evidence. By combining extensive machine-learning–accelerated
first-principles simulations with direct comparison to multiple experimental measurements, we
identify a common constraint affecting both approaches: in the deeply supercooled regime near
the proposed LLT, structural relaxation slows dramatically with small reductions in pressure or
temperature. Systems commonly interpreted as liquid may therefore reside in a kinetically arrested
glassy state, biasing inferred thermodynamic properties. We propose that many signatures attributed
to a liquid–liquid transition can emerge at the onset of dynamical arrest, where equilibration fails
and glassy behavior mimics liquid–liquid coexistence.
This proximity between vitrification and the putative LLT motivates a re-examination of prior
interpretations. In simulations, fluctuations between high- and low-density states appear to be
associated with trapping in low-energy configurations with vanishing molecular mobility, consistent
with transitions between a diffusive high-density liquid and a dynamically arrested low-density
glass. Mobilities in the arrested state for all three models in Fig. 1—including DNN@MB-pol,
currently the most accurate model for neutral bulk water—fall within the range reported for water
in low-density amorphous (LDA) ice (34). Free-energy estimates may be sensitive to equilibration
in regions of low mobility, where incomplete sampling can bias the apparent stability of the low-
density state and give rise to an apparent coexistence between two liquid phases. Similarly, abrupt
experimental signatures may reflect observations on timescales too short for full equilibration,
consistent with the formation of an amorphous low-density state rather than a distinct second liquid.
Apparent LLT or Widom-line signatures (i.e., maxima in thermodynamic response functions) may
therefore emerge near the boundary between ergodic and non-ergodic behavior, where rapidly
increasing relaxation times hinder equilibration.

aqueous solutions, with a glass-transition temperature near 136 K, consistent with previous reports.

surprisingly high glass-transition temperature, 𝑇|𝜏=100s = 189 ± 8 K, in the DNN@SCAN and

structure (17,18). Also, experimental diffusion coefficients, inferred from crystal growth rates, show
a clear kink in their temperature dependence around 189 K (38). This feature can be interpreted
as the transition from crystallization advancing via structural relaxation at higher temperatures
to proceeding via secondary (𝛽) relaxation below 𝑇𝑔(see SM, Fig. S7 for more details). These
experiments are therefore consistent with probing the onset of vitrification, supporting a scenario
in which dynamical arrest can be regarded the dominant organizing feature of deeply supercooled

Our results suggest that an abrupt, first-order–like transition between high- and low-density
states can emerge when at least one of these states becomes dynamically arrested. Under equilibrium
conditions, by contrast, the density varies smoothly with pressure and temperature, as the system
remains able to adapt to the imposed state point. Although we cannot exclude the existence of
a liquid–liquid transition (LLT) in regions inaccessible to current simulations, our perspective is
consistent with earlier suggestions that a relatively high glass-transition temperature could obviate
the need for an LLT to explain water’s anomalies (39), as well as with models in which a liquid–liquid
critical point lies below the Kauzmann temperature (40).
Our results are also consistent with recent coexistence simulations, in which a low-mobility,
14


## Page 15


low-density phase is brought into contact with a high-mobility, high-density phase (37). In these
simulations, the interface persists for tens of nanoseconds—timescales comparable to those associ-
ated with structural relaxation and aging toward lower-energy states in deeply supercooled systems
(Fig. 2). Similar front-like melting dynamics have been reported in glassy systems (41), and model
glass formers can exhibit first-order–like coexistence between ordered glassy and disordered liquid
phases (42).
Taken together, our results indicate that a crossover from an high density liquid to a glass
may rationalize the origins of the apparent two-state behavior. In this regard, these findings hint at
the possibility that the singularity-free scenarios for water’s anomalies (43), in which anomalous
thermodynamic behavior arises from the continuous evolution of local structure rather than from
a hidden critical point, should be reconsidered. Indeed, several models reproduce many of water’s
anomalies in the absence of a liquid–liquid critical point, including the coarse-grained mW model
(44). Signatures of a Widom line—potentially arising either from nonequilibrium effects or from
an LLT—are also expected under equilibrium conditions within the singularity-free scenario for
substances such as water, which expand upon cooling (43). More broadly, the phenomenology
attributed to a liquid–liquid transition appears to emerge naturally from the rapid onset of dynamical
arrest. From this perspective, the longstanding debate over supercooled water may reflect not a
critical point, but the boundary between liquid and glass, which gives rise to phenomena that mimic
a liquid–liquid transition.

Materials and Methods
Three different water models were used for the simulations in this study, for which two-state
fluctuations have previously been reported and attributed to a liquid–liquid transition: the classi-
cal TIP4P/2005 model (45), and two deep neural network potentials trained either on DFT data
(DNN@SCAN (21)) or on MB-pol (DNN@MB-pol (22)).
All temperatures reported in this work for simulations employing the DNN models are shifted
by the difference in melting temperature between experiment and the respective model (−40 K
for DNN@SCAN (21) and +10 K for DNN@MB-pol (22)) in order to facilitate comparison with
experiment. It is well known that, after applying such a shift, the dynamics obtained from DNNs
and experiment are in good agreement (46,47).
Simulations with the DNNs are performed using LAMMPS (48), interfaced with the DeepMD-
kit (49), on systems of 512 water molecules. For all simulations from which correlation times 𝜏are
deduced, great care is taken to equilibrate the system prior to production runs, as follows. First, NPT
simulations are performed, starting from the final configuration of the preceding higher-temperature
run, for at least 10𝜏with a time step of 0.5 fs. The density obtained at the end of this simulation
is then used for a subsequent NVT run, again with a 0.5 fs time step. After at least 10𝜏, eight
independent configurations, separated by at least 1𝜏, are extracted and used as starting points for
eight production runs in the NVE ensemble, with a time step of 0.25 fs and a duration of at least
10𝜏.
Simulations with the TIP4P/2005 model are performed in GROMACS on systems of 1000
water molecules, using the same settings as in the study reporting two-state fluctuations (10). The
same NPT–NVT–NVE protocol described above is employed, but with a time step four times larger.
At the lowest temperatures (220 K and below), the temperature could not be maintained within

performed in the NVT ensemble using the v-rescale thermostat (50).

Influence of the barostat on two-state fluctuations
In the main text, we showed that the two-state fluctuations reported in the literature, which were
previously assigned to a liquid–liquid transition, instead reflect intermittent trapping in a kinetically
arrested, low-energy glassy state that is not accessible in equilibrium at the corresponding state
point. Since large pressure fluctuations in the small simulation box (192 molecules) are presumed to
facilitate trapping in an energy state corresponding to lower pressures, we investigated how different
barostat settings influence the observed two-state fluctuation behavior. To this end, we employed
the SCAN@DNN model with identical settings and procedures (3200 bar, initialized in the high-
density regime) as used in the literature (8) to detect the purported liquid–liquid fluctuations. In
addition, we performed a second simulation in which all parameters were kept identical, except
that the damping constants of both the barostat and thermostat were increased by a factor of
10. Both simulations were run for 1 𝜇s, i.e., twice as long as in the original work. The potential
energy, density, and time-resolved mean square displacement are shown in Fig. S1, with the original
settings on the left-hand side and the modified settings on the right-hand side. While the simulation
performed with the original settings again exhibits trapping in a low-energy, low-density kinetically
S2


## Page 24


arrested state for a significant fraction of the trajectory (here, ∼0.5 𝜇s), in agreement with Fig. 1,
the behavior is markedly different for the modified settings. In this case, no long-lived trapping
in the low-energy state is observed; instead, only very short excursions occur, with the longest
appearing at around 0.75 𝜇s and lasting for approximately ∼50 ns. These results demonstrate that
the previously reported two-state fluctuations are highly sensitive to barostat parameters and may
therefore not be indicative of an underlying equilibrium liquid–liquid transition, but instead from
non-equilibrium trapping effects.

Relation of 𝑇𝑔to other characteristic temperatures.
Diffusion coefficients extracted from crystal growth measurements have been used to support
the notion of a glass transition in water around 136 K, since significant crystal growth (and thus
molecular mobility) has been observed in this temperature regime (38). The temperature dependence
of the inferred diffusion coefficients follows Arrhenius behavior below ∼150 K and crosses over
to a much stronger temperature dependence above ∼180 K (38). Such an apparent change in slope
is commonly observed when secondary (𝛽) relaxation processes, which typically exhibit Arrhenius
behavior, separate from the structural (𝛼) relaxation at 𝑇𝑔.
Indeed, it is well established for various liquids that crystallization can proceed via molecular
mechanisms distinct from the structural relaxation, allowing crystallization to occur even in the
glassy state (55). To test this scenario, we compare the experimental diffusion coefficients from
Ref. (38), inferred from crystallization rates, with the diffusion coefficients obtained from our
simulations in Fig. S7. The shaded area denotes the range of structural relaxation times 𝜏from
the different models, as shown in Fig. 3C. The average glass transition temperature 𝑇𝑔= 189 K
determined in the main text is indicated by a vertical line.
The experimental diffusion coefficients below 189 K are well described by an Arrhenius fit. Most
importantly, above 189 K, the experimental data deviate from this Arrhenius behavior, reflecting the
pronounced change in temperature dependence discussed above. Thus, the experimental diffusion
coefficients are fully consistent with a glass transition around ∼189 K, with crystallization below
𝑇𝑔proceeding via secondary relaxation processes rather than structural relaxation. We note that a
similar scenario has been proposed for water under confinement (19), but also that the 𝛽-relaxation
scenario at the lowest temperatures is challenged by diffusivity measurements on layered H2O-D2O
films (56).
