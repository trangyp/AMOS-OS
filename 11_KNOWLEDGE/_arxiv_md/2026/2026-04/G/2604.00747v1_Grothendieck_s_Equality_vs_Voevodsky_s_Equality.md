THOMAS ECKL
Abstract. We discuss how canonical and universal constructions, properties
and characterizations interact with equality in the framework of Homotopy
Type Theory, comparing it with Grothendieck’s use of equality and shedding
further light on (efficient) formalisation of mathematics. This is achieved by
investigating examples that range from monoids, groups, rings and modules to
cohomology theories in the category of modules over commutative rings and
culminate in a cohomological criterion of flatness.
0. Introduction
Unsurprisingly, the ongoing massive collaborative efforts to formalize mathemat-
ics, especially in the Mathlib [Mat] based on the interactive theorem prover Lean
[Lea] and the projects derived from it, reveal glitches in the standard ways devel-
oped in the past century to ensure mathematical rigour. Not only the soundness
of certain practices is scrutinized, but also their efficiency when it comes to formal-
ization – the very name of Lean alludes to considerations of the latter sort, and the
trend away from set-theoretic foundations like the ZFC axioms towards type theory
can be motivated along those lines. But the jury is still out on that matter, and is
also so on the question which flavour of type theory is best suited for formalization
(see e.g. [BPL22]), and further investigations are needed.
In his recent paper [Buz24] (see also [BHL+22]) Buzzard reflects on his experi-
ences when formalising more and more advanced chunks of mathematics belonging
to very specific theories, culminating in the construction of Scholze’s perfectoid
spaces [BCM19] (but by no means stopping there [Buzb, Fermat’s Last Theorem]).
His most striking case study, however, looks at a rather basic topic in Commutative
Algebra: the localisation of rings. It already exhibits several problems which will
be important in the formalisation of many parts of mathematics, as they seem to
require a deviation from well-established proof practices, and thus tie the discussion
back to foundational issues:
• Formal proofs of properties of objects satisfying a universal property, like
the localisation of a ring in a multiplicatively closed set, are inefficient or
even impossible by just using the universal property. Instead, a specific
construction of the object is needed.
• If a property is shown for a specifically constructed object with a universal
property it can be difficult to transfer it to other objects satisfying the same
universal property.
The standard way of using the unique isomorphism
between these objects induced by the universal property often requires a
lot of supporting technical lemmas.

the same universal property, calling the unique isomorphism between them

1. Equalities in Homotopy Type Theory
In many type theories, not just dependent type theories or Homotopy Type The-
ory, types can be introduced by inductive constructions (see [HoTb, 5.1]). They
consist of constructors postulating the existence of objects and determining how
objects can be produced from objects of other types or recursively from objects of
the same type, as long as the recursion does not create infinite loops. From these
constructors, a recursion resp. induction principle telling how to produce a func-
tion from the inductively defined type to a (dependent) type can be automatically
extracted, together with the calculation rules showing the evaluation of such func-
tions on the constructed objects. Note that the function values can be propositions,
and that in dependent type theories like HoTT, their type may depend on objects
of the inductively defined type.
The easiest inductive definitions just contain constructors postulating the exis-
tence of an object of the type. For example, the type 1 has one constructor ∗: 1,
whereas the type 2 has two constructors 02 : 2 and 12 : 2. Functions on 1 and 2 are

1.1. Identity Types. In Homotopy Type Theory, identity types a =A b of two
objects a, b : A are inductively defined: For given a, only for a =A a we can
construct an equality, called reflexivity or for short, refl. This seems to restrict a
lot the possible equalities, but already the constructor comprises more than visible
at first glance: In Homotopy Type Theory, or Type Theory in general, objects (or
terms) are judgmentally equal if they can be reduced to the same terms when using
the calculation rules of inductive types (or function evaluation).
In that sense,
2 + 2, 2 × 2 and 4 are all judgmentally equal, and the equalities are all identical to
refl(4). We sometimes denote judgmental equalities by 2 + 2 ≡4 etc.
Furthermore, the existence of only one constructor does not prevent an identity
type to have different inhabitants, so that there are more than one way that two
objects of certain types can be equal, at least if we assume univalence – we will
discuss this in Sec. 1.3 in more details. In particular, Homotopy Type Theory does
not postulate that equalities always are unique, or a special kind of “sort”, different
from types, as does the “logic enriched” type theory underlying the Lean Mathlib
[Mat].
The characterisation of identity types between arbitrary objects of a given type
requires some work. By induction, the only identity type of objects in 1 to consider
is ∗=1 ∗, and it is inhabited by refl(∗). With univalence (see Sec. 1.3), we can
show even more: ∗=1 ∗and 1 are equivalent, hence equal, so there is only one
equality of type ∗=1 ∗. For the type 2, we can use the encode-decode-method to
determine equalities: The code maps (02, 02) and (12, 12) to 1 and (02, 12) and
(12, 02) to O. By double induction, we can encode equalities a =2 b by mapping
them to ∗: 1 and decode codes to equalities by mapping ∗to refl(02) and refl(12),
respectively, or deduce anything from n : O. Encoding and decoding are inverse to
each other, so n =2 m and code(n, m) are equivalent, hence by univalence equal,
and furthermore, equalities between two objects of type 2, if they exist, are equal.
The encode-decode method and its more explicit variants like the structure iden-
tity principle (see [Rij22, 11.6]) can be applied to every inductively constructed type
(see [HoTb, 2.13] for the natural numbers). The case of dependent pairs, or Σ-types,
shows how to proceed when constructors have different types as arguments: De-
pendent pairs (a1, b1), (a2, b2) : Σa:AB(a) over a type family B : A →U, a 7→B(a)
are equal if, and only if, we have an equality p : a1 =A b1 and b1 : B(a1) transported
to B(a2) is equal to b2. The transport in type families B : A →U over equalities
p : a1 =A a2 can be defined by induction on the equality p since b1 should be
transported to itself if p ≡refl(a1). Since most (algebraic) structures are nested
Σ-types, this criterion allows to prove their equalities iteratively.

The name “proposition” is sometimes misleading because it alludes to only one
of the possible uses of propositions: as statements which can have a proof or not,
but the exact nature of the proof is irrelevant. Another use of propositions is as
types that encode uniquely determined choices.
In Homotopy Type Theory, propositions are the natural kind of types on
which we can perform logical operations: The negation of a proposition P is a
function not P :≡P →False, and we can connect two propositions P, Q to
P and Q :≡P ×Q, P or Q :≡P +Q and P implies Q :≡P →Q. If we introduce
not, and, or and implies on arbitrary types, univalence will cause “paradoxa”, for
example a contradiction to the Law of Excluded Middle (see [HoTb, Cor.3.2.7]).
Of course, even the Law of Excluded Middle for propositions cannot be deduced
from the basic type theory, but we can introduce it as an axiom, without causing
inconsistencies:
LEM−1 : For all propositions P, P + ¬P.
For arbitrary types, it is possible to cut off all the higher equalities by proposi-
tional truncation. This is actually a first example of a higher inductive construction:
For each type A, we construct a type ∥A∥with a constructor t : A →∥A∥and a
higher constructor of t(|a|) = t(|b|) for each |a|, |b| : ∥A∥, making ∥A∥into a propo-
sition by truncating the higher equalities. The recursion principle delivers a map
g : ∥A∥→B for each proposition B with a map f : A →B such that g(t(a)) = f(a),
for all a : A. If we have an object a : ∥A∥we say that there merely exists an object
of type A.
From an object of a propositionally truncated type we cannot construct an object
of the type itself, unless it is a proposition. But if we want to construct an object
of a proposition and another object of a propositionally truncated type is available,
we can apply induction on propositional truncation and continue to work with an
object of the type, without truncation, and without checking independence from
the choice. These kind of arguments will be at the core of our treatment of choices
in the constructions of Sec. 4. The problem solved there is not that we do not know
how to construct objects of a specific type (which may or may not be the case),
but that we do not know (or do not want to decide) which of the many possibilities
to choose — and we do not need to specify our choice as long as we only want to
prove a proposition, or construct another uniquely determined object.

1.3. Univalence and Function Extensionality. Up to now, we mainly discussed
equalities in inductively defined types. This does not tell us how to construct and
characterise equalities of types themselves, or of functions between types.
Univalence deals with equalities A = B of arbitrary types A, B in the same
universe U. Univalence postulates the equivalence of identity types and equivalence
types, for short:
UA : (A =U B) ≃(A ≃B).
One way to define equivalences A ≃B are as pairs of functions f : A →B and
g : B →A inverse to each other, checked by equalities
(f ◦g)(b) = b for all b : B and (g ◦f)(a) = a for all a : A,
together with additional higher equalities between these equalities (see [HoTb,
Def.4.2.1] for the details).
For practical uses of univalence, it is most of the times enough to present the
inverse maps f and g, as adjointification ([HoTb, Thm.4.2.3]) allows to construct
a proper equivalence from these data without changing f and g (but possibly some
of the equalities checking inverseness). If the types A and B are sets, the higher
equalities are irrelevant (since uniquely determined) anyway, so the inverseness of
f and g is sufficient. Therefore, univalence completely characterizes equalities of
sets:
Proposition 1.3. Equalities p : A = B of sets A, B are in one-to-one correspon-
dence with bijective maps f : A →B.
□
Thus, there always will be a multitude of equalities between sets of the same
cardinality > 1. This concept certainly contrasts with the definition of equality of
sets in Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory, by the Axiom of Extensionality: Two sets are
equal if they contain the same elements. In Homotopy Type Theory, on the other
hand, the equivalences = bijective maps between sets allow to jumble the elements
freely and still produce an equality. This in turn seems to contradict mathematical
instincts when applied for example to the set of natural numbers: An equality of
N with itself should better lead to an equivalence identifying 0 with itself.
However, the mathematical instinct here reacts to the additional semiring struc-
ture on natural numbers, not present when we only discuss N as a set. Objects

Univalence and in extension Homotopy Type Theory is usually promoted as a
tool to transport structures and properties from one type to another type equal to
it, without need of further constructions or proofs. While this claim is certainly
true, the untrained eye may exaggerate its benefits: For example, if we want to
compare the transported structure with another one already present on the second
type, we need to execute the checks we thought to have avoided, namely that the
equivalence induced by the equality preserves the structures.
In [VMA21, 2.1], a telling example of this problem can be found (see also [HoTb,
5.2]): The inductive definition of natural numbers by introducing 0 and the succes-
sor function is very efficient when proving the laws of calculations collected in the
semiring structure for N. On the other hand, it does not allow efficient calculations
— for this purpose, binary (or alternatively decimal, . . . ) numbers, call them BinN,
are much more suitable; however, proving the calculation laws for these representa-
tions of natural numbers is very tedious. In many arguments involving calculations
with natural numbers you may want to use both laws and actual calculations, so to
do this efficiently we would need to switch back and forth between the two repre-
sentations of natural numbers. Now, it is easy to show that N and BinN are equal
as sets, and we could construct many such equalities. But for the aim outlined
above, you actually need the (unique) equality preserving addition and multiplica-
tion, together with 0 and 1, so you end up with constructing an isomorphism of
semirings. We will further discuss this example at the end of Sec. 2.
In the end, univalence makes equalities of structures and structural isomorphisms
literally and in a very precise technical sense equivalent, so it is to be expected that
using univalence instead of isomorphisms may not necessarily simplify arguments.
In the example above, univalence will become really beneficial to calculations if it
produces a judgmental equality between the different presentations of the natural
numbers and their semiring structures because then the switch between N and BinN
will be performed automatically as a term reduction. Cubical Type Theory provides
such a construction, turning the Univalence Axiom UA into a constructively prov-
able statement, and its effects are further described in [VMA21]. Apart from some
remarks in the final section, we will however not pursue these developments further
in this article.

1.4. Categories. Following [HoTb, Ch. 9] we call a type of objects together with
a family of homomorphism sets between two objects, provided with identity ho-
momorphisms and a composition of homomorphisms satisfying the usual laws, as a
precategory. If we relax the requirement of homomorphisms between two given ob-
jects being a set to allowing higher equalities, we obtain 2- or higher (pre)categories.
When objects have underlying sets, and homomorphisms have an underlying map
of sets, homomorphisms usually form sets, as maps between the underlying sets
are a set again. For example, this happens for categories of algebraic objects like
monoids, groups and rings. Thus, this definition of categories should not be seen as
restrictive, but as a useful starting point already covering many interesting exam-
ples, and allowing a systematic extension to more complicated kinds of categories.
A precategory is called a category if isomorphisms and equalities between two
given objects are equivalent. Sometimes, such a category is also called a univalent
(1-)category, because the equivalence between isomorphisms and equalities can be
seen as a generalisation of univalence. If objects of the category have underlying
sets and homomorphisms have an underlying map of sets, the equivalence is usually
indeed derived from univalence. As with univalence, the identification of equali-
ties with isomorphisms should not be seen as destroying the structure given by
isomorphisms, but actually as providing equalities with this structure.
2. Universal properties from inductive constructions
The standard ingredients of an inductive construction hide another important
feature of an inductively defined type (which always can be proven using the induc-
tive principle): The constructors freely generate the objects of the type. First of
all, this means that every object is the result of exactly one of the constructors, and
in a unique way. But if some constructors are recursive, even more is true: there
is a unique way to produce an object by iteratively applying constructors.
We
discuss how to turn this into characterisations of inductive types on two examples,
Cartesian products of two and more factors, and the natural numbers.
2.1. Cartesian products. The only constructor of the Cartesian product A × B
of two types delivers a pair (a, b) from two given objects a of type A and b of type
B. The recursion principle then associates a function g : A × B →C to a function
f : A →B →C (that is, a function that gives a function B →C for each object
a of type A), and the calculation rule states that g((a, b)) = f(a)(b) (and similarly
for the induction principle). This may look less familiar than induction on N, first
of all because the difference between g((a, b)) and f(a)(b) seems insignificant. Even
worse, we are used to the universal property of a Cartesian product delivering a
(unique) map ϕ from a type C to A×B, for given maps f : C →A, g : C →B and
factorising f, g by the (inductively defined) natural projections pA : A × B →A,
pB : A × B →B, not the other way round. But how do we prove this universal
property? We use the free generatedness of the Cartesian product, that is, each
p : A × B is equal to the pair (pA(p), pB(p)), and for all a : A, b : B there is exactly
one object p : A × B such that pA(p) = a and pB(p) = b!
We will spell this out by first using the free generatedness of its constructor as
a characterisation of Cartesian products of types:
Definition 2.1. A type P is a Cartesian product of two types A and B if
(i) there is a function f : A →B →P (called pair constructor), and

2.2. Natural types. Cartesian products are rather simple inductive types, with
just one constructor and no recursion. The paradigmatic example of a recursively
defined type are the natural numbers N, with two constructors: One postulates the
existence of 0 in N, and the other constructor is the recursive successor operation
succ : N →N. The inductive principle guarantees a (unique) function value for
each natural number, if we know the value of 0 and the value of the successor given
the function value of any natural number. The calculation rules simply state what
the function value of 0 is and how to determine the function value of the successor
from the value of any given natural number.
Because of the recursive constructor, free generation means more than just every
n : N being either 0 or the successor of a uniquely determined m : N. If we only
assume that condition it would also cover the disjoint union of N and the integers Z,
with the standard successor function on Z. Instead, free generatedness prescribes
a unique number of iterations of the successor function to produce a given number
n : N from 0, and thus leads to the following characterisation:
Definition 2.6. A type N is natural if
(i) there is an object n0 : N and a function s : N →N, and
(ii) for each n : N there is a unique natural number i : N such that n = s◦i(n0),
the function s applied i times on n0.
It is straightforward to show that the natural numbers N form such a natural
type, and that natural types satisfy the analogue of the induction principle for
natural numbers. This leads to a categorical characterisation identifying a natural
type with an initial object (see [HoTb, 5.4]):

3. Universal Properties from Higher Inductive Constructions
The results of the previous section, as long as they did not use univalence, are
valid for any foundations in dependent type theory with inductive constructions.
But for more advanced concepts than the natural numbers and Cartesian products
(of sets), and especially for algebraic ones, using a central feature of Homotopy
Type Theory can pay off immensely: the so-called higher inductive types, for short
HITs.
3.1. Set quotients. We start discussing HITs on one of the basic examples, set
quotients. They are used for the fundamental construction of identifying all the
elements in the same equivalence class of an equivalence relation on a set, with one
element in a new set, the set quotient. The formalisation of this construction, and
especially its efficiency, are a difficult issue in many formal proof systems (see for
example [Set]).
In HoTT, relations are given by maps R : A →A →Prop associating pairs of
objects of a set A to propositions, that are types whose objects, if they exist, are all
equal. Equivalence relations require that R is reflexive, that is, R(a, a) is a true (or
inhabited) proposition, and that R is symmetric and transitive. The equivalence
class of a : A is the (dependent pair) type of all objects b : A such that R(a, b) is
true.
It is straightforward to characterize a set quotient of an equivalence relation:
Definition
3.1. A set Q is a set
quotient of an equivalence relation
R : A →A →Prop on a set A if
(i) there is quotient map q : A →Q,
(ii) the map q is surjective, that is, for all a : Q there (merely) exists a : A such
that q(a) = a, and
(iii) for all a, b : A, q(a) = q(b) if, and only if R(a, b) is true.
Let us first clarify once again the meaning of “merely” in Def. 3.1. It weakens
the requirement of having a proper function s : Q →A such that q(s(a)) = a for
all a : Q (in other words, a section of q). Instead, it only postulates the existence
of an a such that q(a) = a, without further specifying how to obtain this a. This
is enough to state that the map q is surjective. As noted in 1.2, postulating the
“mere existence” instead of outright existence is mainly done to avoid a choice,
which would not matter anyway, and not because choices cannot be made. We just
need to be aware that we only can use an explicit preimage if we want to show a
proposition or construct a uniquely determined object.

tients directly.

which produces for each a, b : A with true R(a, b) an equality q(a) = q(b).

equalities q(a) = q(b) are at least not a priori equal to any other equality in the
identity type q(a) = q(b). For example, R(a, a) is always true, hence we obtain
a second equality besides the reflexive equality refla produced by Martin-L¨of’s
inductive construction of equality. If we do not want these equalities to differ, we
must introduce a further higher constructor, which for any objects a, b : Q and
equalities r, s : a = b produces an equality r = s. In other words, this constructor
makes the set quotient into a set, by truncating all the higher equalities. Therefore
it is called the set truncation.

with the same structure a function from the HIT to the type, also mapping the
constructors to that structure. If we denote the HIT constructed above from a set

qR(a) = qR(b). We need that B is a set because of the set truncation constructor,
and thus the last property of g above is automatically satisfied.

3.2. Subsets. Before we continue to use HITs for constructing types with algebraic
structure we need to discuss subsets of sets, as the underlying type of algebraic
subobjects.
Usually subsets are described as predicates A →Prop, reflecting the fact that
(a propositionally resized) Prop is a classifier in the category of sets (see [HoTb,
Thm.10.1.12]). But alternatively, a subset can be described as a set B together with
an injective map ι : B →A, which corresponds to the definition of subobjects in
category theory. This point of view pays off when algebraic structures get involved,
as I will explain below.
Before showing that both definitions are coinciding, let us remove possible con-
cerns about the second one: Of course, there are different ways to embed a set
into another set without changing the image. However, two injections ι : B →A,
ι′ : B′ →A will have the same image if, and only if there is a bijection f : B →B′
such that ι = f ◦ι′, and f is uniquely determined by ι and ι′. The bijection therefore
induces a unique equality between the pairs (B, ι) and (B′, ι′), by univalence.

us to denote a subset B of a set A by B ⊆A, and for a : A the element relation
a ∈B checks whether the proposition B(a) is true or equivalently, whether a is in
the image of ι.
3.3. Monoids. As a first algebraic structure defined on sets M we will discuss
monoids. They are sets provided with
• a composition map · : M →M →M;
• a neutral element eM : M;
• associativity: for all m1, m2, m3 : M, we have
(m1 · m2) · m3 = m1 · (m2 · m3);
• neutrality: for all m : M, we have m · eM = eM · m = m;
A monoid is commutative if furthermore,
• m · n = n · m, for all m, n : M.
The most basic example of a (commutative) monoid is the unit monoid 1, whose
only object will be identified with the neutral element.
A homomorphism between monoids M, N is a map f : M →N of the underlying
sets satisfying
• f(eM) = eN, and
• f(m1 · m2) = f(m1) · f(m2) for all m1, m2 : M.
There always is the trivial homomorphism u : M →N between two monoids,
mapping each element of M to the neutral element eN : N.
It is straightforward to show that the identity map is a homomorphism, that ho-
momorphisms f : N →M, g : M →L between monoids N, M, L can be composed
to a homomorphism g ◦f : N →L, and that the composition of (identity) homo-
morphisms satisfies the usual laws: This makes monoids into a precategory, in the
sense discussed in 1.4. The additional property turning it into a category, namely

3.4. Groups. We now extend the algebraic structure of monoids to that of a group:
A monoid G is a group if it is furthermore provided with
• an inverse g−1 : G, for each g : G;
• inverseness: for all g : G, we have g · g−1 = g−1 · g = eG.

3.5. Rings and Modules. Commutative rings with one are sets R provided with

r : R by −r;
• multiplication: a commutative monoid structure, with composition results
denoted by r × s for r, s : R and the neutral element by 1;
• a distributive law asserting (r + s) × t = r × t + s × t, for all r, s, t : R.

An element u : R is a unit of the commutative ring R with one if there exists a
v : R such that u × v = 1. The inverse v is actually uniquely determined.
A homomorphism f : R →S between two commutative rings R, S with one is a
group homomorphism on the underlying additive groups of R and S, which is at the
same time a monoid homomorphism on the underlying multiplicative monoids of
R and S. As before, equality of ring homomorphisms is decided by equality of the
underlying set maps. Thus, commutative rings with one and their homomorphisms
can be realized as a concrete category over the category of abelian groups, with
fibers consisting of commutative monoid structures.
A module over the ring R, for short R-module is a set M provide with
• a commutative group structure, with composition results denoted by m+n
for m, n : M, the neutral element by 0 and the element negative to m : M
by −m;
• scalar multiplication: a function R →M →M, composition results de-
noted by r · m for r : R, m : M.
Furthermore, there are several laws ensuring the compatibility of addition and
multiplication in the ring and scalar multiplication:
• r · (s · m) = (r × s) · m for all r, s : R, m : M;
• (r + s) · m = r · m + s · m for all r, s : R, m : M;
• r · (m + n) = r · m + r · n for all r : R, m, n : M;
• 1 · m = m for all m : M.
A homomorphism f : M →N of R-modules M, N is a group homomorphism
compatible with the scalar multiplication:
f(r · m) = r · f(m) for all r : R, m : M.
As a group homomorphism, an R-module homomorphism is completely determined
by its underlying map of sets. It is straightforward to check that the identity map
M →M is always an R-module homomorphism, and that the composition of
two R-module homomorphisms is an R-module homomorphism. This gives the R-
modules together with their homomorphisms the structure of a category, with a
faithful forgetful functor to the category of sets and their maps.

5. Calculating with Cohomology Classes
The constructions and considerations of the previous section show that in HoTT,
“calculations” with arbitrary cohomology groups will be different from calculations
with numbers or more advanced algebraic objects like polynomials: The cohomol-
ogy classes with which we calculate are usually chosen by induction on proposi-
tional truncation, and therefore we can only show proper theorems (propositions in
HoTT terminology). In particular, for such purposes cohomology classes need not
be explicitly constructed, but in special cases, an explicit construction, or even a
particular choice of a construction, may nevertheless be possible and useful, without
the theorem depending on the choice.
A typical application of cohomology groups is to deduce properties from their
vanishing – generations of students have wondered why to introduce such a com-
plicated machinery just to show that it produces nothing else than 0. We present
one instance of such an application by proving a criterion for flatness, a subtle
commutative-algebraic concept capturing how “smoothly” modules vary over the
spectrum of a ring, and use it for a specific example:
Proposition 5.1 ([Eis95, Ex.6.4]). Let R be a Noetherian ring, S = R[x1, . . . , xr]
the polynomial ring in r variables over R and f a non-zerodivisor. Then S/(f) is
a flat R-module if, and only if the coefficients of f generate the unit ideal in R.
Corollary 5.2. Let k be a field. Then k[x, y]/(1 + xy) is a flat k[x]-module.
Proof. Apply Prop. 5.1 on R = k[x], S = k[x, y] and f = 1 + xy: The cofficients of
f in k[x] are 1 and x and generate the unit ideal in k[x].
