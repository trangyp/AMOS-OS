Geometry- and topology-controlled synchronization phase transition on manifolds∗
Yang Tian1, †
1Infplane Computing Technologies Ltd, Beijing, 100080, China
In this work, we explore how the geometry and topology of the underlying manifold shape the
synchronization phase transition of a system. To do so, we extend the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi model
from spheres to compact, connected, orientable, and homogeneous Riemannian manifolds of arbi-
trary dimension. Starting from the mean-field kinetic equation on the manifold, we derive a local
response equation for the order parameter near the incoherent state and separate the geometric and
topological contributions to the phase transition out of the incoherent state. The manifold geometry
determines a coefficient κ (M) to control the critical coupling for the linear loss of stability of the
incoherent state. The manifold topology constrains the cubic term of the response equation through
the Euler characteristic χ (M). Under a local sign condition on the cubic term, topology does not
allow a generic continuous or tricritical synchronization phase transition to occur when χ (M) ̸= 0,
and it imposes a non-zero net defect charge on the incipient ordered texture. When an additional
local stabilization condition holds in that nonzero-Euler class, topology further selects a discontin-
uous phase transition. When χ (M) = 0, topology does not impose that obstruction, so continuous,
discontinuous, and tricritical local branches are all allowed. We verify these findings on representa-
tive families including hyperspheres, equal even-sphere products, complex Grassmannians, complex
projective spaces, flat tori, real Stiefel manifolds, rotation groups, and unitary groups. Our frame-
work recovers the classical hyperspherical parity law and extends it to a broad class of non-spherical
state spaces. It therefore identifies the geometric origin of the linear instability threshold and the
topological origin of the accessible phase transition scenarios in diverse synchronization phenomena.
I.
INTRODUCTION
The Kuramoto model serves as a prototypical frame-
work for investigating low-dimensional synchronization
phenomena [1–4]. Its generalized form, the Kuramoto-
Sakaguchi model, also plays a crucial role in study-
ing both synchronization and asynchronization in low-
dimensional systems [2, 3, 5].
However, to accurately
describe a broader range of biological, chemical, and
physical systems [6–8], it is necessary to consider high-
dimensional settings (D
≥
3), in which the origi-
nal low-dimensional formulations (D ≤2) of the Ku-
ramoto model and the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi model be-
come inadequate [9–11]. Moreover, the phase space of
a high-dimensional system need not be a standard D-
dimensional sphere. In that case, high-dimensional ex-
tensions built specifically on the hypersphere may fail to
represent the underlying state space [12–16].
In this study, we extend the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi
model to compact, connected, orientable, and homoge-
neous D-dimensional Riemannian manifolds, which may
possess non-trivial topology or an arbitrary number of
holes [17]. This class already contains the principal ge-
ometric examples relevant to synchronization, including
spheres, flat tori, Stiefel manifolds, rotation groups, and
unitary groups [9, 12–14, 18–22]. Related Lohe-type and
unitary-group models provide a complementary matrix-
valued route to manifold synchronization [14, 23–33].

E.
Topology-conditioned onset scenarios
Within the local reductions of Appendices F and G,
the cubic and quintic coefficients determine the local
statistical-physics scenario at onset. The important point
is not that topology changes the numerical values of the
generic mean-field exponents in this one-dimensional lo-
cal theory. The important point is that topology deter-
mines which branch carrying those scalings can actually
appear near threshold.
If χ (M) ̸= 0 and the sign condition in Appendix E
holds, then Λ3 < 0. The generic supercritical continuous
branch in Appendix F is therefore not allowed to occur
by topology. The tricritical continuous branch discussed
in Appendix G is also not allowed to occur by topology,
because it would require Λ3 = 0. Under the additional
analytic condition Λ5 > 0, the onset is locally discontin-
uous, and the local hysteresis width and jump amplitude
are then determined by Appendix G.
If χ (M) = 0, topology imposes no sign constraint
on Λ3.
Topology therefore removes the obstruction to
a continuous onset. A generic continuous branch is al-
lowed when Λ3 > 0. The same zero-Euler condition also
allows a discontinuous branch, which is allowed when
Λ3 < 0 < Λ5.
It also allows a tricritical continuous
branch, which is allowed after additional tuning to Λ3 = 0
with Λ5 > 0. In this sense, topology acts as a selection
rule for onset scenarios: a non-zero Euler characteris-
tic does not allow certain branches to occur by topology,
whereas a zero Euler characteristic permits them without
selecting which one is actually realized. The local expo-
nents are still fixed by the reduced normal form itself.

I.
Summary of the case studies
The case studies all support the same split between
geometry and topology. In every model, the chosen em-
bedding and order-parameter sector determine the coef-
ficient κ (M) through the averaged tangent projection.
This coefficient multiplies the linear growth term of the
order-parameter perturbation near the incoherent state.
More precisely, κ (M) fixes the critical coupling Kc at
which the incoherent state first loses linear stability, but
it does not decide which onset scenario is allowed.
The nonzero-Euler cases identify the regime in which
topology has direct predictive power. In the odd hyper-
sphere sector, in the equal even-sphere product family,
in the complex Grassmannian family, and in the complex
projective family, topology fixes the sign-accessibility of
the cubic term in the reduced response equation through
χ (M) ̸= 0, the locality reduction of Appendix D, and
the sign condition of Appendix E. As a result, topology
imposes a nonzero net defect charge on the onset texture,
gives the lower bound Ndef ≥|χ (M) | on generic simple-
defect branches, and does not allow generic continuous
or tricritical onset to occur.
When Λ5 > 0, topology
therefore selects a discontinuous onset.
The zero-Euler cases show the complementary role of
topology. In the even hypersphere sector, in the torus
family, in the real Stiefel family, in the rotation group,
and in the unitary group, topology removes the obstruc-
tion to continuous onset and allows a defect-free texture.
At the same time, the same zero-Euler condition also al-
lows discontinuous and tricritical local branches. In that
class, topology acts as a permission rule rather than a se-
lection rule. The actually realized onset scenario is then
decided by the analytic coefficients of the reduced normal
form.
V.
CONCLUSION
In this work, we extended the Kuramoto-Sakaguchi
framework from spheres to compact, connected, ori-
entable, and homogeneous manifolds of arbitrary dimen-
sion. We derived the continuum equation on the man-
ifold, identified the linear and nonlinear terms in the
order-parameter response equation near the incoherent
state, and connected those terms with the geometry and
topology of the state manifold. For the chosen embedding
and order-parameter sector, the averaged tangent pro-
jection determines the coefficient κ (M). This coefficient
multiplies the linear growth term of the order-parameter
perturbation and therefore fixes the critical coupling Kc
at which the incoherent state first loses linear stability.
The manifold topology constrains the cubic term of the


## Page 15


15
reduced response equation through the Euler character-
istic and the corresponding cubic coefficient. Within the
one-mode local reduction of Appendices D and E, topol-
ogy does not allow generic continuous or tricritical onset
to occur when χ (M) ̸= 0. It also imposes a non-zero
net defect charge on the onset texture. If the additional
stabilization condition Λ5 > 0 also holds in that nonzero-
Euler class, topology further selects a discontinuous on-
set. When χ (M) = 0, topology does not impose that
obstruction, so continuous, discontinuous, and tricritical
local branches all remain allowed. In that class, topology
does not select the branch by itself.
We then tested the theory on representative homo-
geneous manifolds.
The hypersphere case recovers the
classical parity law and shows how the odd and even sec-
tors fall on the two topological sides of the theory. The
family S2m × S2m demonstrates that the same topolog-
ical selection mechanism extends beyond a single sphere
and survives while the geometric threshold factor varies.
The complex Grassmannian and complex projective fam-
ilies show that the nonzero-Euler mechanism also persists
on genuinely non-spherical complex homogeneous spaces.
The torus, real Stiefel, rotation-group, and unitary cases
show the complementary zero-Euler side: topology allows
continuous onset and defect-free textures, but it does not
rule out discontinuous or tricritical local branches. Col-
lectively, the case studies support the main message of
our theory: the averaged tangent projection of the cho-
sen embedding sector fixes the critical coupling for the
linear loss of stability of the incoherent state, while topol-
ogy constrains which synchronization-onset scenarios can
occur.
Several concrete statistical-physics directions now be-
come accessible within the present framework:
1. Finite-size scaling near topology-conditioned onset.
One can study how the finite-N order-parameter
fluctuations, Binder-like cumulants [49], and pseu-
docritical couplings depend on whether χ (M) van-
ishes. The most direct comparison is between odd
hyperspheres or CPm, where continuous onset is
not allowed by topology, and zero-Euler cases such
as Td or U (d), where it is allowed [50].
2. Noise-induced
switching
and
metastability
on
nonzero-Euler manifolds. By adding intrinsic noise
to the kinetic equation, one can derive the station-
ary order-parameter distribution near the subcrit-
ical branch and compute switching times between
incoherent and synchronized states.
The present
quintic normal form gives a direct starting point
for studying how topology-conditioned discontinu-
ous onset affects activation barriers and residence
times [50].
3. Defect kinetics near synchronization onset.
The
theory predicts a net defect charge and a lower
bound on the number of simple defect cores when
χ (M) ̸= 0.
A natural next step is to simulate
the onset texture on manifolds such as S2m × S2m,
CPm, and Grk (Cn), and then measure defect cre-
ation, motion, annihilation, and coarsening as K
crosses the threshold [45–47].
4. Quenched disorder in the intrinsic drifts.
The
present theory already contains the drift field
through the measure h (V ).
One can study how
the width and symmetry of that disorder mod-
ify the linear threshold, the cubic coefficient, and
the accessibility of the continuous branch.
This
is especially relevant for matrix-valued models on
St (p, n), SO (n), and U (d) [51–53].
5. Topology
versus
interaction-network
structure.
The present analysis is mean-field in character. A
concrete next problem is to place the same man-
ifold state spaces on sparse random graphs, mod-
ular graphs, or low-dimensional lattices, and ask
whether the topology-conditioned onset scenarios
survive when the coupling network itself is no
longer complete [41, 54–61].
6. Nonequilibrium hysteresis under ramps and exter-
nal forcing. Appendix F and Appendix G give lo-
cal static scaling and hysteresis scales.
One can
now study slow and fast parameter ramps, periodic
driving, and weak external fields to determine how
the loop area, jump point, and lag depend on the
manifold topology. This direction is especially nat-
ural for the nonzero-Euler cases, where topology
already selects the discontinuous side of the local
bifurcation structure [7, 62–64].
