General-Purpose Technology and Speculative Bubble Detection
Abstract
We show that the leading bubble test suffers severe size distortion when fundamentals incor-
porate general-purpose technology adoption. Embedding a hump-shaped technology shock in the
Campbell-Shiller present-value model, we prove that the fundamental price becomes locally explo-
sive during adoption, contaminating the test’s limit distribution with a non-centrality parameter
proportional to the shock’s peak. We propose a fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition that
projects prices onto observable technology proxies and applies the test to the residual. Empirically,
the decomposition eliminates evidence of speculation in the 2020–2025 AI rally while confirming a
speculative peak confined to December 1999–March 2000 in the dot-com episode.
Keywords: general-purpose technology, speculative bubbles, bubble detection, artificial intelli-
gence, present-value model
JEL classification: C12, C22, G12, O33

Introduction
Since the release of large language models in late 2022, equity markets have experienced a dramatic
rally concentrated in artificial-intelligence (AI)-related firms. The “Magnificent Seven” (Alphabet,
Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA, and Tesla) accounted for a majority of the S&P 500’s
gains during 2023–2024 (Basele et al., 2025), and the NASDAQ Composite nearly doubled from
its October 2022 trough. Corporate AI investment has surged in parallel, with aggregate compute
expenditure, related patenting, and research and development (R&D) intensity all rising rapidly
(McElheran et al., 2024; Pairolero et al., 2025; Aldasoro et al., 2026), while productivity effects
of generative AI are beginning to materialize in firm-level data (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025). The
pattern of a concentrated technology rally amid rapid but uncertain technological change echoes
the late-1990s NASDAQ run-up, whose subsequent crash destroyed trillions of dollars in market
value. The central question for researchers, regulators, and investors is the same now as it was then:
are these price dynamics a speculative bubble, or a rational repricing of the economy’s productive
capacity?
This paper asks a question that recurs with every technological revolution: are standard bubble
tests systematically mistaking fundamental repricing for speculative excess? The most widely used
real-time bubble detector is the seminal right-tailed unit root test of Phillips et al. (2015a,b) (here-
after PSY), now embedded in the surveillance frameworks of central banks worldwide.1 Applied to
the current rally, PSY detects explosive dynamics. Yet its null hypothesis is a pure random walk
and abstracts entirely from structural change in fundamentals. Precisely during periods of general-
purpose technology (GPT) adoption, when the bubble question is most pressing, technology-driven
fundamentals themselves generate locally explosive price dynamics that are observationally indis-
tinguishable from speculation. The risk is not merely a statistical artifact: if policymakers treat
a technological repricing as a bubble, premature intervention can stifle the very adoption process
that drives long-run growth.
Our first contribution is new empirical evidence on the AI rally and the late-1990s dot-com
episode. We show that the 2020–2025 AI rally leaves no evidence of speculative behavior at any

peak from December 1999 to March 2000: the technology-driven run-up was fundamental, and
only the terminal phase was speculative. These findings speak to the long-standing debate over

Our second contribution is methodological. The size distortion we document is a specific in-
stance of a broader identification problem: deterministic structural change in fundamentals can
lead the PSY test to reject the null of no bubble even when no speculative component is present.
Existing work has examined related sources of misspecification, including time-varying discount
rates (Cochrane, 2011), rational bubble dynamics (Blanchard and Watson, 1982; Diba and Gross-
man, 1988; Evans, 1991), and structural breaks in unit root inference (Phillips et al., 2011, 2015b;
Harvey et al., 2015, 2016; Monschang and Wilfling, 2021). The technology channel is distinctive:
the hump-shaped, nonlinear adoption profile of GPTs produces locally explosive dynamics that
standard linear detrending cannot remove. Formally, the absence of a bubble does not guaran-
tee the absence of explosive dynamics once fundamentals incorporate technology-driven structural
change.
Our third contribution is a fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition that removes the
estimated technology component before applying PSY, restoring correct size while preserving power
against genuine bubbles. The decomposition uses three observable technology proxies—total factor
productivity (TFP), information technology (IT) investment, and patent grants—each grounded in
a production-side microfoundation and each separable from the speculative component of prices.
A stochastic technology extension nests a simplified version of the P´astor and Veronesi (2009)
uncertainty-resolution framework and shows that the deterministic results are a conservative lower
bound on the true size distortion.
The analysis is embedded in the Campbell and Shiller (1988) log-linear present-value framework.
A GPT shock, defined as a hump-shaped, deterministic component in dividend growth that reflects
technology adoption and maturation (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg, 1995; Jovanovic and Rousseau,
2005), generates a technology present-value term in the fundamental price. During the adoption

Empirically, we estimate the technology component via a cointegrating regression of prices on

The quantitative magnitude of the distortion is large. In Monte Carlo simulations calibrated
to postwar United States equity data and containing only technology-driven fundamentals with no
speculative component, PSY rejects the null of no bubble in 93 percent of samples for detrended
log prices and 100 percent for the price-dividend ratio at the 5 percent nominal level. Removing
the technology component restores the rejection rate to its nominal level. In a controlled overlap
experiment in which a genuine bubble coexists with a technology shock, the unadjusted test fires
prematurely during the technology-only phase, while the technology-adjusted procedure correctly
delays detection until genuine speculative activity begins. For the NASDAQ dot-com episode, the
unadjusted test detects two explosive episodes: a brief signal in May 1996 and the main episode
from November 1999 to March 2000. Our decomposition eliminates the early signal and narrows
the genuine speculative peak to December 1999 through March 2000. For the 2020–2025 AI rally,
the technology adjustment eliminates the explosive signal entirely: conditional on the technology-
adjusted framework, there is no evidence of speculation at any conventional significance level.
Firm-level analysis of the Magnificent Seven confirms this pattern: explosive signals in Microsoft,
Alphabet, and NVIDIA disappear after technology adjustment.
The results are robust across four functional forms for the technology shock (triangular, Gaus-
sian, Beta, and Gamma-like profiles); across dynamic ordinary least squares (DOLS), fully modified
ordinary least squares (FMOLS), and first-difference specifications of the cointegrating regression;
and across 121 alternative training windows for the AI era. A battery of identification diagnostics—
Granger causality tests, placebo tests with non-technology covariates, principal component analysis
(PCA) of firm-level price gaps, and training-window stability analysis—supports the separability
of the technology proxies from the speculative component.

2.5
Local Explosiveness of Fundamentals
The drift representation of Proposition 3 shows that the sign and magnitude of µt −c are governed
by (1 −ρ−1)δt + (ρ−1 −1)Tt−1. Because ρ−1 > 1, the first term is negative in δt while the second is
positive in Tt−1; during the early adoption phase, Tt−1 rises faster than δt (forward-looking agents
price in all still-unrealized future shocks), so the positive second term dominates and the funda-
mental process exhibits a drift that is strictly above its unconditional mean c. The next proposition
records this heuristic formally: along the adoption–maturation cycle, the fundamental price passes
through four qualitatively distinct regimes—a near-random-walk regime before adoption, a super-
normal-growth regime early in adoption, a deceleration regime late in adoption, and a new-level
random-walk regime after maturation. The key statistical implication is that the price-dividend
ratio is strictly convex on an initial subinterval of the adoption window, which is precisely the
property that drives the spurious-explosiveness result of Theorem 1.

3

The preceding sections establish theoretically and via simulation that technology-driven fundamen-
tals can generate locally explosive price dynamics that are observationally indistinguishable from
speculative bubbles. We now apply the fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition alongside
the standard PSY test to two episodes that bookend the digital age: the late-1990s NASDAQ
dot-com run-up and the AI-driven rally of the 2020s.

5.1
The Identification Challenge
A fundamental obstacle in empirical bubble detection is that the econometrician observes only the
market price pt, the sum of the fundamental value ft and any bubble component bt:
pt = ft + bt.
During a potential bubble period, ft is not directly observable: it cannot be “read off” from prices,
because prices themselves are potentially contaminated by the bubble. The result is a circularity—
testing for a bubble requires knowing the fundamental, but knowing the fundamental requires
knowing whether there is a bubble.
Our theoretical framework (Section 2) shows that the fundamental price is a function of divi-
dends, technology-driven growth, and discount rates:
ft = dt + C + Tt,
where Tt = P∞
j=0 ρjδt+1+j aggregates the future path of technology-driven dividend growth. In
principle, if we could observe the technology component δt and the discount factor ρ, we could
construct Tt directly. In practice, the technology path is uncertain, agents’ information sets are
richer than the econometrician’s, and the mapping from observable technology indicators to δt is
imprecise.
We address this challenge by adapting the counterfactual estimation approach of Hsiao et al.
(2012). The key insight is that during non-bubble periods, the observed price equals the funda-
mental (up to stationary noise), so one can use these periods to estimate the relationship between
the fundamental and observable covariates.
During the potential bubble period, the estimated
relationship is used to construct a counterfactual fundamental, and PSY is applied to the residual.

5.3
Endogeneity and Identification
The key identification assumption is that Xt is not directly affected by the speculative bubble bt.
The production-side microfoundation of Section 2.3 provides the theoretical basis for this assump-
tion: the bubble enters as an additive component in the secondary-market valuation (pt = ft + bt)
but does not appear in the production block that determines Xt (Remark 9). This separability
implies that the technology variables load on the fundamental ft but not on bt. We reinforce this
structural argument with four empirical strategies.
Structural separability.
The production economy of Section 2.3 implies that the technology
observables Xt = (TFPt, log ITt, log Patt)′ are determined by the real side of the economy (pro-
ductivity, capital accumulation, and innovation), not by speculative dynamics in equity markets.
In particular: (i) patent grants reflect the latent innovation quality qt−ℓwith ℓ≥1, making them
predetermined with respect to contemporaneous market conditions because successful R&D must
be undertaken, filed, examined, and only later granted; (ii) TFP is a production-side measure of
technology and factor utilization; and (iii) IT investment is pinned down by the optimality condi-
tion (A.7), which equates the cost of IT capital to its expected marginal product, a fundamental
object, not to speculative resale value. Even if stock-market valuations affect future innovation or
IT adoption through financing constraints, the resulting variation operates through future produc-

5.6
The Artificial-Intelligence Era Rally
We now turn to the most pressing contemporary application: the AI-driven equity rally of the
2020s. The release of large language models in late 2022 triggered a sustained surge in technology-
related equity prices, raising the same fundamental question our framework addresses: whether the
observed price dynamics reflect a rational fundamental response to a GPT shock or speculative
excess.
5.6.1
Motivation and Background. The AI rally shares structural parallels with the dot-com
episode. Both feature a GPT whose productivity implications are uncertain ex ante (Acemoglu,
2025; Brynjolfsson et al., 2025), a concentration of price gains in technology firms, and a rapid ex-
pansion of technology investment. Between October 2022 and early 2025, the NASDAQ Composite
nearly doubled, with the “Magnificent Seven” stocks (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft,
NVIDIA, and Tesla) accounting for a disproportionate share of market-capitalization gains (Basele
et al., 2025). Corporate capital expenditure on AI compute infrastructure has surged, AI-related
patent filings have accelerated (Pairolero et al., 2025; McElheran et al., 2024), and the financing of
AI investment has shifted from internal cash flows toward external debt (Aldasoro et al., 2026).
Basele et al. (2025) apply the standard PSY procedure to the Magnificent Seven and the NAS-
DAQ Composite during this period and find evidence of explosive behavior. Our theoretical and
simulation results show that such findings can reflect fundamental repricing induced by the AI
technology shock; the framework developed in Sections 4–5 separates the technology-driven and

5.6.7
Discussion. The AI-era results, combined with the dot-com findings, yield three conclu-
sions. (1) The technology-adjustment methodology is structurally identical across both episodes,
yet it produces asymmetric outcomes.
In the dot-com case, both tests reject: the adjustment
sharpens dating but confirms speculation. In the AI era, the standard test rejects at 10% while
the adjusted test eliminates the signal entirely. This divergence addresses the identification prob-
lem: within the technology-adjusted framework, the explosive dynamics in the AI-era price are
consistent with fundamental repricing rather than speculation. (2) The AI rally differs from dot-
com: (i) substantial IT investment and patenting growth; (ii) less extreme price-to-fundamental
acceleration; (iii) concentration in profitable incumbents. (3) The contrasting results across the
two episodes suggest a practical surveillance protocol. Central banks and regulators applying PSY
should routinely apply the technology-adjusted procedure in parallel to obtain a more granular di-
agnosis of explosive dynamics. If both tests reject, as in the dot-com case, there is strong evidence
of a speculative component above and beyond technology-driven fundamentals. If the standard test
rejects but the technology-adjusted test does not, as in our AI-era application, the explosive dy-
namics are attributable to technology-driven fundamental repricing rather than speculation. This
two-test protocol directly addresses the identification problem that arises during periods of rapid
technological change.

5.8
Robustness of the Identification Strategy
The identification strategy rests on the separability of technology proxies from the speculative
bubble component. Appendix F reports five complementary diagnostics that assess this assump-
tion. First, Granger causality tests confirm that stock returns do not predict technology proxies
during speculative periods (p > 0.47 throughout), directly supporting separability; bidirectional
causality during non-speculative periods is consistent with fundamentals driving prices in normal
times. Second, placebo tests using non-technology covariates (consumer sentiment, VIX, industrial
production) fail to replicate the bubble-elimination effect, confirming that the technology proxies
capture specific fundamental content rather than generic macro variation. Third, PCA of the Mag-
nificent Seven price gaps reveals a dominant common factor (60.0% of variance) with no explosive
behavior in any linear combination, providing cross-sectional evidence consistent with fundamental
repricing rather than speculation. Fourth, training-window stability analysis shows that the AI-
era non-rejection finding holds across all 121 training windows (GSADF ∈[−0.30, 0.84], all below
CV0.10 = 1.176); the dot-com results are more sensitive, driven primarily by the patent coefficient.
Fifth, the Hansen (1992) Lc test rejects parameter constancy in both eras, qualifying the DOLS
estimates as reflecting an average equilibrium, a limitation that the training-window robustness,
particularly for the AI era, mitigates.

Conclusion
This paper develops a fundamental-versus-speculative decomposition framework for asset prices
during general-purpose technology adoption. The central finding is that technology-driven struc-
37


## Page 39


tural change in fundamentals generates locally explosive price dynamics that the standard PSY
bubble test systematically misclassifies as speculative, a misidentification that matters most pre-
cisely when the bubble question is most pressing. Applying the framework to two bookend episodes
of the digital age, the 2020–2025 AI-driven rally and the late-1990s dot-com run-up, we reach
sharply different conclusions. Conditional on the technology-adjusted framework, no evidence of
speculative behavior remains in the AI era at any conventional significance level once technology
fundamentals, including total factor productivity, information technology investment, and patent
grants, are accounted for. In contrast, the dot-com episode was partially speculative: while the
technology adjustment eliminates an early spurious signal, it confirms the presence of genuine
speculation from December 1999 to March 2000. Firm-level evidence from the Magnificent Seven
largely reinforces these aggregate findings: five of the seven firms show no explosive behavior after
adjusting for firm-specific fundamentals, although Tesla and Apple retain residual signals that may
reflect idiosyncratic speculative dynamics. These results support the view that the current AI era
reflects a structural shift in productivity rather than a repeat of the late 1990s exuberance.
The framework makes a broader methodological point: standard bubble tests applied during
technological transitions risk a paradigm mistake, confusing fundamental repricing for speculative
excess. The toolkit for bubble detection has remained largely statistical. Just as risk management
evolved from the static Value-at-Risk (VaR) threshold to the more informative tail-risk measures of
Conditional VaR (CVaR) and the forward-looking, market-consistent Mark-to-Market VaR (MM-
VaR) of Chen et al. (2019), the econometrics of bubble detection must evolve from detecting sta-
tistical explosiveness to diagnosing its economic source. Our framework is a step in that direction:
technology-driven structural change in fundamentals can generate locally explosive dynamics that
satisfy all the statistical signatures of a bubble without any speculative component being present,
and the decomposition restores correct test size while preserving power against genuine bubbles.
The results are robust across alternative shock shapes (triangular, Gaussian, Beta, and Gamma-
like), estimation methods (DOLS, FMOLS, and first-differencing), and 121 alternative training
windows for the AI era.
These findings suggest a two-test surveillance protocol for central banks and financial regulators
conducting real-time bubble monitoring. During periods of rapid technological transition, regulators
should run the standard and technology-adjusted PSY tests in parallel. If both reject the null of no

test rejects but the adjusted test does not, as in the recent AI case, the explosive dynamics are
more consistent with fundamental repricing than with speculation. This protocol offers a more
nuanced diagnostic for policy, helping avoid premature interventions that could stifle technological
adoption and growth.

Extensions
This appendix presents the three extensions summarized in Section 2.6: time-varying expected

B.1

The first extension asks whether the explosive pattern survives when expected returns respond to
the technology state. High-valuation regimes are typically associated with low expected returns

to ft.

Distinguishing Technology from Speculation: An Overlap Experiment
The preceding experiments establish that PSY produces spurious rejections when fundamentals

Robustness Tests: Identification and Specification
This appendix reports five complementary diagnostics that assess the separability of technology
proxies from the speculative bubble component, which is the paper’s main identification assumption.
While Proposition 2 provides a structural justification for this separability, the tests below evaluate
its empirical validity.
F.1
Granger Causality Tests
Under the separability assumption, stock returns should not Granger-cause the technology proxies
during speculative periods: if the bubble component bt is orthogonal to fundamentals, explosive
price dynamics should not feed back into real technology variables. During non-speculative peri-
ods, however, bidirectional causality between technology and returns is expected, since technology
fundamentals drive prices and stock market valuations affect corporate investment.
We test this prediction by estimating bivariate Granger causality regressions between NASDAQ
returns and each of the three technology proxies (IT-investment growth, TFP growth, and patent
growth), separately for speculative and non-speculative regimes. The PSY-detected bubble periods
are too narrow (5–6 months for the dot-com episode) to support meaningful inference, so we define
broader speculative periods based on the run-up phase: January 1997 to March 2000 for the
dot-com era (n = 39) and January 2023 to December 2025 for the AI era (n = 33–35).
Lag
lengths are selected by the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust p-
values could not be computed because of near-singular covariance matrices in the small speculative-
period samples; we therefore report both standard F-test and wild bootstrap p-values, which yield
consistent conclusions throughout.
Table F.1 reports the results. During both speculative periods, no technology proxy is predicted
by lagged returns in the Granger sense: all p-values exceed 0.70 for the dot-com episode and 0.54 for
the AI era. The reverse direction is also insignificant: technology variables do not predict returns in
the Granger sense during speculative periods, which is consistent with speculative dynamics reflect-
ing sentiment rather than fundamentals. This pattern holds uniformly across all three technology
proxies and both eras, providing direct support for the separability assumption.
The non-speculative periods tell a complementary story. In the dot-com non-speculative sample

(n = 136–141), only one marginally significant relationship emerges: returns Granger-cause IT
investment at the 10% level (F = 2.476, p = 0.062), consistent with an accelerator channel whereby
equity market valuations affect IT spending with a lag. No other direction is significant. In the AI
non-speculative sample (n = 36), the results are richer: IT investment, TFP, and patent grants all
Granger-cause returns at the 5% level or better (p = 0.019, 0.015, and 0.005, respectively), while
returns also Granger-cause TFP (p = 0.016). This bidirectional causality during normal periods is
expected and economically sensible: technology fundamentals drive asset prices, while stock market
wealth effects and Tobin’s q channels feed back into productivity. Crucially, this bidirectional link is
absent during the speculative regime, which is precisely what the separability assumption requires.
F.2
Placebo Tests: Non-Technology Covariates
If the technology-adjusted test eliminates explosive signals specifically because the technology prox-
ies capture genuine fundamentals, then replacing these proxies with economically irrelevant covari-
ates should fail to eliminate the signal. We construct “placebo” counterfactuals by regressing log
NASDAQ prices on three non-technology variables (consumer sentiment (University of Michigan
Index), the VIX implied volatility index, and industrial production (INDPRO)) via DOLS over the
same training windows, then apply PSY to the resulting price gaps. Table F.2 reports the results
alongside the technology baseline.
For the AI era, all three placebos yield GSADF statistics well below their respective 10 percent
critical values (−0.223, 0.712, and 0.869 against CV0.10 = 1.334), consistent with the baseline
technology adjustment (test statistic = −0.275). This is expected: when no explosive dynamic
exists in the data, neither the correct nor incorrect covariates will produce a rejection.
The dot-com results are more nuanced. Industrial production does not reject (test statistic =
1.460 < CV0.10 = 1.768; R2 = 0.743), confirming that a generic macroeconomic variable cannot
replicate the technology adjustment’s bubble-elimination effect. The VIX could not be tested for
the dot-com era because the CBOE VIX series begins in January 1990, providing only 12 training
observations, which is insufficient for reliable DOLS estimation.
However, consumer sentiment
rejects at both the 10 and 5 percent levels (test statistic = 2.283, CV0.10 = 1.768, CV0.05 = 2.025).
The result requires careful interpretation. The sentiment-based DOLS specification achieves a low
R2 of only 0.308, indicating that sentiment is a poor predictor of log price levels; the resulting price

F.4

A potential concern with the technology-adjusted test is that results depend on the training period
used to estimate the DOLS coefficients. We systematically vary the training window end-date: from
January 1985 to December 1994 for the dot-com application (with a fixed start of January 1975) and
from January 2010 to December 2019 for the AI application (with a fixed start of January 2006).
We reestimate the DOLS coefficients on each subsample and apply PSY to the resulting price gaps.
Table F.4 reports representative windows; Figure F.2 displays the complete trajectories.
The two eras present a clear contrast. For the AI era, the GSADF statistic ranges from −0.30
to 0.84 across 121 training windows, never approaching the 10 percent critical value of 1.176. The
“no AI bubble” conclusion is entirely insensitive to training window choice. This robustness holds
despite substantial variation in the DOLS coefficients: the IT-investment coefficient ˆβIT ranges
from 0.35 to 2.65, and the patent coefficient ˆβPat spans −0.16 to 1.19, yet the adjusted price
gap never produces a rejection. The R2 remains high throughout (0.82–0.91), indicating that the
technology proxies explain a large and stable share of price variation regardless of the specific
training window.
For the dot-com era, the picture is more complex.
The test statistic varies from approxi-
mately 1.30 (training ending September 1987) to 2.76 (training ending June 1985), a range of 1.46.
With the 10 percent critical value at 1.745, early training windows (ending before mid-1987) yield

dows ending from late 1988 through early 1992 return to rejection, before settling below the critical
value for windows ending after mid-1992. The source of this instability is primarily the patent coef-
ficient, which varies from −0.39 (January 1985) to +1.04 (December 1994), while the IT coefficient
is comparatively stable at 0.42–0.49. This sensitivity reflects the changing role of patenting in the
technology-growth relationship: in the early 1980s, patent grants were a poor proxy for innovation
(Kogan et al., 2017), while their informational content improved substantially through the 1990s.

F.5
Hansen (1992) Parameter Instability Test
The training-window analysis reveals coefficient variation, but does not formally test whether the
cointegrating relationship is stable within each training period. We apply the Hansen (1992) Lc
test for parameter instability in cointegrating regressions, which tests the null hypothesis that the
DOLS coefficients are constant against the alternative of a martingale parameter process. Table F.5
reports the results.
The Lc test rejects parameter stability in both eras and both specifications. For the dot-com
baseline (technology-only), Lc = 1.845 (p = 0.009); for the AI-era baseline, Lc = 3.994 (p < 0.001).
The extended specifications including the term spread yield similar conclusions (Lc = 1.780, p =
0.018 for dot-com; Lc = 3.941, p < 0.001 for the AI era). All statistics far exceed the 1% critical
values (1.160 for baseline, 1.278 for extended), indicating strong evidence of parameter instability.
This finding has important implications.
The cointegrating relationship between log prices
and technology proxies is not stable over the training periods. For the dot-com era (1975–1990),
this is unsurprising: the technology-growth nexus evolved dramatically as IT transitioned from
mainframes to personal computers to networked systems. For the AI era (2006–2019), instability
reflects the transition from mobile and cloud computing to the nascent AI paradigm, with the
patent coefficient (capturing innovation composition) absorbing most of the structural change, as
the training-window analysis of Section F.4 confirms.
The Hansen rejection does not invalidate the technology-adjusted test, but it qualifies the inter-
pretation. The DOLS estimates represent an average technology-price relationship over the training
period, and the adjustment is valid insofar as this average captures the typical equilibrium. The
training-window analysis provides reassurance: despite parameter instability, the qualitative con-
clusions are robust. In particular, the AI-era non-rejection finding holds across all 121 training
windows, even as individual coefficients vary substantially. The dot-com results are more fragile,
with the GSADF statistic crossing the critical value as the training window shifts, though the qual-
itative interpretation (a genuine speculative peak in 1999–2000) is supported across the majority
of specifications. Future work could address parameter instability more formally through time-
varying coefficient approaches, such as the Bierens and Martins (2010) time-varying cointegration
framework or state-space models with Kalman-filtered parameters.
