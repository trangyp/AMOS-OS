Governing the Agentic Enterprise: A Governance
Maturity Model for Managing AI Agent Sprawl in
Business Operations
Vivek Acharya
Independent Researcher, Dallas, TX, USA
Email: vacharya@bu.edu · ORCID: 0009-0002-0860-9462
Abstract: The rapid adoption of agentic AI in enterprise business operations—autonomous
systems capable of planning, reasoning, and executing multi-step workflows—has created an
urgent governance crisis. Organizations face uncontrolled agent sprawl: the proliferation of
redundant, ungoverned, and conflicting AI agents across business functions. Industry surveys
report that only 21% of enterprises have mature governance models for autonomous agents,
while 40% of agentic AI projects are projected to fail by 2027 due to inadequate governance and
risk controls. Despite growing acknowledgment of this challenge, academic literature lacks a
formal, empirically validated governance maturity model connecting governance capability
to measurable business outcomes. This paper introduces the Agentic AI Governance Matu-
rity Model (AAGMM), a five-level framework spanning 12 governance domains, grounded in
NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 standards. We additionally propose a novel taxonomy of
agent sprawl patterns—functional duplication, shadow agents, orphaned agents, permission
creep, and unmonitored delegation chains—each linked to quantifiable business cost models.
The framework is validated through 750 simulation runs across five enterprise scenarios and
five governance maturity levels, measuring business outcomes including cost containment,
risk incident rates, operational efficiency, and decision quality. Results demonstrate statisti-
cally significant differences (p < 0.001, large effect sizes d > 2.0) between all governance
maturity levels, with Level 4–5 organizations achieving 94.3% lower sprawl indices, 96.4%
fewer risk incidents, and 32.6% higher effective task completion rates compared to Level 1.
The AAGMM provides practitioners with an actionable roadmap for governing autonomous
AI agents while maximizing business returns.
Keywords: agentic AI; governance maturity model; agent sprawl; enterprise AI governance;
NIST AI RMF; ISO/IEC 42001; multi-agent systems; AI-driven business strategy; autonomous
agents; AI risk management
1. Introduction
The emergence of agentic artificial intelligence (AI) represents a paradigm shift in enterprise
technology. Unlike traditional AI systems that respond to queries or classify inputs, agentic AI
systems autonomously plan multi-step strategies, select and invoke tools, and execute com-
plex business workflows with minimal human intervention [1, 2]. These systems have rapidly

This adoption trajectory has generated immense business value. Google Cloud’s 2025 ROI
Report documented that 74% of executives deploying AI agents achieved return on invest-
ment within the first year, with 39% reporting doubled productivity in specific workflows [5].
Organizations across financial services, healthcare, retail, and manufacturing are deploying
autonomous agents for customer service, fraud detection, supply chain optimization, and op-
erational decision-making [6, 7].
However, this rapid adoption has simultaneously created a governance crisis that threatens
to undermine the very value it generates. Three converging problems define this crisis:
First, agent sprawl. As low-code and no-code platforms make agent creation accessible
to anyone in the organization, enterprises face the uncontrolled proliferation of redundant,
fragmented, and ungoverned agents across teams and functions [8]. McKinsey identifies this
as a risk analogous to the early days of robotic process automation: agents multiply across
teams, duplicate efforts, and operate without oversight [9].
Second, the governance gap. Despite 74% of companies planning agentic AI deployments,
only 21% of leaders surveyed have a mature governance model for autonomous agents [3].
Gartner projects that 40% of agentic AI projects will fail by 2027 due to escalating costs, unclear
business value, and inadequate risk controls [10]. The disconnect between adoption velocity
and governance readiness is striking: 42% of organizations report still developing their agentic
strategy roadmap, with 35% having no formal strategy at all [6].
Third, the ROI measurement vacuum. While industry reports document impressive re-
turns, they notably lack frameworks for how governance contributes to or detracts from these
returns. Google Cloud’s comprehensive ROI report, based on 3,466 executives, contains no
discussion of agent governance models—how agents should be monitored, overridden, or au-
dited [11]. Traditional ROI models are too narrow for agentic AI, focusing solely on direct cost
savings while overlooking governance-dependent outcomes [12].
Academic research has begun addressing individual facets of this problem. Recent work
includes the Enterprise Agentic Mesh (EAM) architecture for network-layer governance [13],
the Governance-as-a-Service (GaaS) framework for runtime compliance filtering [14], compre-
hensive TRiSM reviews [15], and multi-expert analyses [16]. However, none provide a holistic
governance maturity model that (a) defines progressive capability levels, (b) maps controls to
business outcomes, and (c) empirically validates the governance–value relationship.
This paper addresses this gap with the Agentic AI Governance Maturity Model (AAGMM).
Our contributions are fourfold:
C1. The AAGMM framework: a five-level model spanning 12 governance domains, grounded
in NIST AI RMF 1.0 [17] and ISO/IEC 42001 [18].
C2. A governance-to-ROI mapping connecting maturity levels to four business outcome dimen-
sions.
C3. A novel taxonomy of five agent sprawl patterns with quantifiable cost models.

2. Related Work
2.1. Agentic AI in Enterprise Business Operations
Agentic AI represents a fundamental evolution from generative AI. While generative AI excels
at content production, agentic AI systems combine LLMs with tool access, memory, and plan-
ning to autonomously pursue complex goals [1, 2]. The 2025 MIT Sloan Management Review
study found that agentic AI complicates traditional management boundaries: these systems
plan, learn, and adapt as organizational actors [19].
Deloitte’s 2026 report documents expanding use cases across customer support, supply
chain, R&D, and cybersecurity [3]. KPMG reported 67% of leaders will maintain AI spending
even during recession, with $124M projected per organization [7]. Agentic AI is projected to
add $2.6–4.4T to global GDP by 2030 [10].
2.2. AI Governance Frameworks
NIST AI RMF 1.0 provides the most widely referenced structure (Govern, Map, Measure,
Manage) [17]. NIST AI 600-1 extends this to generative AI [20]. ISO/IEC 42001 establishes
certifiable AI management requirements [18]. The EU AI Act introduces risk-tiered obliga-
tions [21]. However, these were designed for traditional deployments—not for environments
with dozens of autonomous agents delegating tasks and making decisions without oversight.
2.3. Maturity Models in Technology Governance
CMMI defines five process maturity levels widely adopted in software engineering [22]. CO-
BIT provides IT governance maturity [23]. Recent AI maturity models [24, 25] address adop-
tion readiness but not autonomous multi-agent governance specifically.
2.4. Agent Governance and Multi-Agent Systems
The EAM proposed a Semantic Control Plane and Governance Sidecar [13]. GaaS introduced
runtime compliance filtering with trust scores [14]. TRiSM reviews cataloged trust/risk/security
challenges [15]. Manager Agent research formalized orchestration as a POSG [26]. The LOKA
Protocol proposed decentralized governance [27]. OpenAI published agentic governance prac-
tices [28].
Despite this work, no framework provides a prescriptive maturity model mapping gov-
ernance capabilities to business outcomes with empirical validation. The AAGMM fills this
gap.

4. The AAGMM Framework
The AAGMM defines five progressive capability levels across 12 governance domains, grounded
in NIST AI RMF 1.0 and ISO/IEC 42001.
4.1. Design Principles
Four principles guide the AAGMM: Progressive capability (each level builds on the previ-
ous); Business-outcome orientation (levels defined by outcomes, not just controls); Domain
completeness (12 domains cover the full agent lifecycle); Standards alignment (every domain
maps to NIST/ISO clauses).

7. Discussion
7.1. Practical Implications
Level 3 is the minimum viable governance standard (+0.155 NBV, d = 5.90 from L2). Auto-
mated sprawl detection is the highest-leverage control (74.4% SI reduction at L4). L5 delivers
an automation dividend (GCR decreases while outcomes improve). Reactive governance is
inadequate for security (0.3% NBV improvement L1→L2 in adversarial scenario).
7.2. Comparison with Existing Models
The AAGMM differs from CMMI [22] (no agent provisions), COBIT [23] (no real-time controls),
and Gartner/Microsoft models [24, 25] (adoption-focused). Among academic work, EAM [13]
addresses network governance and GaaS [14] focuses on output filtering—neither provides
maturity levels linked to business outcomes. The AAGMM is the first to combine progressive
levels, 12 standards-mapped domains, and empirical validation.
7.3. Alignment with Industry Findings
Deloitte’s leadership-driven governance finding [3] aligns with monotonic NBV increase. KPMG’s
complexity-barrier finding [7] is addressed by our sprawl taxonomy. McKinsey’s RPA anal-
ogy [9] is validated: S2 L1 SI of 0.746 means three-quarters of agents are problematic.

7.4. Limitations
Validation uses simulation (not production deployments). Task/violation models are parame-
terized from reports. Governance costs are fixed per-level. NBV weights reflect one prioritiza-
tion. Binary controls simplify continuous spectrums. Sensitivity analysis and case studies are
future work.
7.5. Ethical Considerations
The AAGMM embeds ethics via Human Oversight (GD-06, HITL at L3+), Risk Classification
(GD-10, EU AI Act-consistent tiers [21]), and Audit & Compliance (GD-08, decision traceabil-
ity).
8. Conclusions and Future Work
This paper introduced the AAGMM, validated through 750 simulation runs. Key results: SI
decreased 94.6%; RIR decreased 96.5%; ETCR improved 33.0%; DSR improved 60.2%→99.2%.
All comparisons p < 0.001, d > 2.0.
Three practical findings: (1) L3 is minimum viable governance; (2) automated sprawl de-
tection is highest-leverage; (3) L5 delivers an automation dividend.
Future work: case study validation, self-assessment instrument, platform-specific guidance
(LangChain, AutoGen, CrewAI), dynamic cost modeling, and MCP integration.
As agentic AI becomes the enterprise backbone, governance determines whether agent
ecosystems generate compounding value or compounding risk.
The AAGMM provides a
structured, evidence-based path to the former.
