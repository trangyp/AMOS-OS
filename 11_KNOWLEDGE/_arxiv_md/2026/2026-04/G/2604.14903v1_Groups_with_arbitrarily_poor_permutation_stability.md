Groups with arbitrarily poor permutation
stability
Henry Bradford

Abstract
We propose a quantitative notion of permutation stability for finitely
generated groups. Our notion is related to, but distinct from, the “stabil-
ity rate” introduced by Becker and Mosheiff (which is valid within the class
of finitely presented groups). We construct a family of finitely generated
stable groups which exhibit, quantitatively, arbitrarily “bad” permutation
stability. This means that any application of a “sample-and-substitute”
algorithm will be very slow in ascertaining whether a given tuple of per-
mutations satisfy the defining relations of our groups.

Introduction
A group Γ is permutation stable (henceforth simply stable) if every almost-action
of Γ on a finite set is a small perturbation of an honest action. In this paper we
study a quantitative notion of stability. Intuitively, the group Γ is “very stable”
if an almost-action which is more than a tiny perturbation of an action must
fail the defining properties of a group action at very many points. Conversely,
the stability is “poor” if there are almost-actions on finite sets which come very
close to satisfying the defining properties of an action but must be perturbed
a great deal to yield a genuine action. The main result of this paper is that
the class of finitely generated stable groups contains groups whose stability is
arbitrarily poor.

Background
Determining, for a given π ∶F ↠Γ, whether ρ ∶F →Sym(Ω) is close to
some homomorphism factoring through π, is an example of a “property-testing”
problem: we are interested in some parameter of a huge object, and seek a
fast (possibly randomised) algorithm which will, in a small number of queries
(“small” meaning, for example, of a number which is bounded independent of
the size of the object), give an approximate evaluation of the parameter. In
our context, the object in question is a tuple (ρ(x))x∈X of permutations of a
large finite set Ω, and the parameter we wish to evaluate is whether or not
the ρ(x) satisfy the defining relations between a given ordered set of generators
(π(x))x∈X for the group Γ. The algorithm which implements the test described
in Subsection 1.1 above is named “Sample-and-Substitute” in [5]. The number
of queries required by Sample-and-Substitute is then the number n of points ωi
which are sampled from the permutation domain Ω. If the group is stable, then
there exists a way of running the algorithm with a number of queries which is
independent of ∣Ω∣, such that the outcome gives reasonable confidence as to
whether the ρ(x) are close in the Hamming metric to permutations satisfying
the defining relations of Γ.
For more on this perspective on group stability
coming from theoretical computer science, see [4].
From this point of view it is very natural to ask for an estimate of the
computational costs associated to running Sample-and-Substitute, of which the
stability growth function, as defined above, is a sensible measure. Theorem 1.3
tells us that there are groups for which implementing Sample-and-Substitute
is viable on very large permutation domains Ω, in the sense that the number
of queries is independent of ∣Ω∣, but for which the cost of running Sample-
and-Substitute grows very quickly as a function of the proximity ϵ to an action
of Γ which we demand. Currently the class of stable groups for which we have
good bounds on the stability growth function remains extremely limited. Becker
and Mosheiff [6] quantify the stability of finitely generated abelian groups (the
stability of such groups being due to Arzhantseva and Paunescu [2]). In our
terminology, their main result implies the following.

The same basic strategy outlined above has already been fruitfully employed
by Dogon, Levit and Vigdorovich in [12], and applied to the same groups from
[8], to prove a result analogous to our Theorem 1.3, but with respect to Hilbert-
Schmidt stability rather than permutation stability, that is with finite sym-
metric groups equipped with the Hamming metric being replaced by unitary
groups of finite-dimensional complex Hilbert spaces, equipped with the nor-
malised Hilbert-Schmidt metric. It is interesting to note that HS-stability of
the groups from [8] follows from a general result, also proved in [12], on the
HS-stability of so-called diagonal product groups. Such a result is not known in
the setting of permutation stability, so we make use of more ad hoc arguments.
It should also be noted that Dogon, Levit and Vigdorovich state their result
only for the “stability radius growth” function: this is a variant of stability
growth function, according to which only the maximal word length of elements
of the finite set E appearing in Definition 1.1 contributes to the “cost”, with the
parameter δ playing no rˆole. Nevertheless, their proof should also be relevant to
the analogue, for the Hilbert-Schmidt metric, of the stability growth function
as we have defined it.
Having established bounds on RΓ and LΓ for our groups Γ, it remains to
prove that the Γ are indeed stable. The original B.H. Neumann groups were
proven stable by Levit and Lubotzky [16]; our strategy follows theirs: first, since
the Γ are amenable groups, the criterion of Becker, Lubotzky and Thom [3] for
stability of Γ in terms of the cosoficity of the invariant random subgroups (IRS)
of Γ is available to us (see Theorem 4.1 below). To prove cosoficity of many of
the IRS of Γ, we avail ourselves of the technique of Weiss approximation used in
[16] (see Theorem 4.3 below). Finally, the structure of Γ as a diagonal product
of marked groups which converge to a “limiting group” ˜Γ (which is hence a
quotient of Γ) allows us to lift restrictions on the structure of the IRS of ˜Γ to
those of Γ. In our setting, ˜Γ is always the ternary lamplighter group C3 ≀Z,
whose IRS were studied by Bowen, Grigorchuk and Kravchenko [7].

which weigh the costs associated to the parameters E and δ appearing in Def-

The paper is structured as follow. In Section 2 we prove the basic properties

of Becker and Moshieff, proving Theorem 1.4 in the process. We also recall the

For Γ and ∆groups and A ⊆Γ a subset, a function ϕ ∶A →∆is called a

Invariant random subgroups
For Γ a countable group, let Sub(Γ) ⊆{0, 1}Γ be the space of subgroups of Γ,
equipped with the Tychonoff topology. There is a continuous action of Γ on
Sub(Γ) by conjugation, and an invariant random subgroup (or IRS for short) of
Γ is a Γ-invariant Borel probability measure on Sub(Γ). By IRS(Γ) we denote
the space of all IRS of Γ. This is a compact metrizable space under the weak∗
topology, and forms a Choquet simplex within Prob(Γ). An IRS of Γ is ergodic
if it is an extreme point of this simplex: that is, if it cannot be written as a
proper convex combination of other elements of IRS(Γ).
Thus there are no
proper closed convex subspace of IRS(Γ) containing all ergodic IRS of Γ.
An IRS of Γ is called finite-index if it is an atomic probability measure
supported on finite-index subgroups of Γ, and is called cosofic in Γ if it is the
weak∗-limit of finite-index IRS of Γ.
Theorem 4.1 (Theorem 1.3 of [3]). Let Γ be a finitely generated amenable
group. If every ergodic IRS of Γ is cosofic in Γ then Γ is stable.

Further questions
In the light of the connections to property-testing problems, it is especially
natural to attempt to quantify stability for finite sets of relations. Alas, the
generalised B.H. Neumann groups are typically not finitely presented. For those
groups in the family for which the epimorphism to C3 ≀Z from Subsection 3.2
is defined, this follows immediately from the main result of [13], which implies
that no finitely presented amenable group admits an epimorphism to a wreath
product A ≀B, with B infinite and A nontrivial. More generally, any finitely
generated group for which the full residual finiteness growth is not eventually
equal to the LEF growth is not finitely presented (Proposition 2.18 of [9]). As
such, the following remains open, where we refer to the group Γ as polynomially
stable if F π

II.16 of [4]).
Question 5.1. Does there exist a finitely presented group Γ which is stable but
not polynomially stable?
Similarly, one wishes to have more examples of polynomially stable groups.
In this vein, we amplify and generalise a suggestion from Section 6 of [6].

What about every polycyclic group?

Finally, recall that for a group Γ to be stable means that the “sample-and-
substitute” algorithm described in the Introduction is capable of recognising
tuples of permutations which are close to a solution for the family of defining
relations for Γ. A priori, the property that proximity to a solution is recognised
by “sample-and-substitute” (stability) is stronger than the property of recog-
nition by some algorithm. Groups satisfying the latter, weaker property are
known as testable groups [4]. Not every testable group is stable: for instance
every finitely generated amenable group is testable (Theorem 1.14 of [5]). It is
natural to ask of a testable group Γ for bounds on the complexity of algorithms
witnessing the testability of the group. Remarkably, there exists a “universal”
algorithm, called in [4] the Local Statistics Matcher algorithm, which witnesses
testability for any finitely generated testable group. Therefore, one could define
a notion of testability growth function for a finitely generated testable group Γ
in one of two ways: either as a measure of the complexity of the most efficient
algorithm for recognising which tuples of permutations are close to a solution for
the relations of Γ, or as a measure of the complexity of Local Statistics Matcher
when applied to Γ. The following questions may be interpreted in either sense.
Question 5.3. Does there exist a finitely generated group which is not stable but
which is polynomially testable? Does there exist a finitely presented example?
Question 5.4. Does there exist a finitely generated group which is testable but
not polynomially testable? Does there exist a stable such group? Does there
exist a finitely presented such group? Do there exist finitely generated groups
with arbitrarily large testability growth functions? Do there exist such groups
which are stable?
The tools that we have employed in this paper to give obstructions to effec-
tive stability (specifically large full residual finiteness growth) do not seem so
relevant to Question 5.4, because sofic testable groups need not be residually
finite (for instance, there exist many finitely generated amenable groups which
are not residually finite). One therefore seeks other group-theoretic properties,
which are obstructions to testability, and which may be quantified.
