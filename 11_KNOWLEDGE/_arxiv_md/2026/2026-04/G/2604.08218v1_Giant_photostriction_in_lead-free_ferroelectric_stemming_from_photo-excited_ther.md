Abstract
Ferroelectrics are polar materials whose polarization can be switched by applying electric fields;
they offer unique opportunities to develop performant photostrictive materials, i.e., materials that can
deform under visible light illumination. Naturally devoid of inversion symmetry, they exhibit original
photogalvanic effects such as the Bulk Photovoltaic Effect, which relies on “hot” photoexcited carri-
ers. It has long been thought that the electric field generated by this effect may couple to the natural
piezoelectric abilities of ferroelectrics to provide large photoinduced deformations. However, due to
competing effects, such as thermal dilatation, deformation potential, polarization, or depolarizing-
field screening by thermalized carriers, it remains unclear which microscopic phenomena govern the
photoinduced deformations in classical ferroelectric materials. Here, we demonstrate the largest pho-
toinduced deformation measured in a ferroelectric thin film. Reaching 1%, this giant photostriction
likely originates from the contribution of thermalized photoinduced carriers.
INTRODUCTION
Ferroelectrics form a distinct class of functional materials due to their inherent switchable elec-
trical polarization. This particular feature leads to strong electron-lattice couplings as manifested
by their piezoelectric nature, the generation of ultrafast, strong, coherent acoustic waves1,2, and
large mechanical deformations3–5 under visible or near-visible optical excitation. Therefore, fer-
roelectrics have been deemed extremely promising to generate large photoinduced mechanical de-
formations, a phenomenon known as photostriction. Potential applications cover optically-driven
micro- or nanorobots6, optical switches and actuators7, or optomagnetic memories9.
Several studies have explored the photostriction of ferroelectrics, starting with PbTiO33, and
PbTiO3-based solid solutions10 that gathered interest due to a peak in their piezoelectric response
at the morphotropic phase boundary. Photostriction of multiferroic BiFeO3 has also been thor-
oughly investigated at ultrafast1,2,4,5 and quasistatic11 time scales. The photoinduced strain in such
inorganic ferroelectric materials is typically of the order of 0.1%; meanwhile, their effective pho-
tostriction (defined as the total displacement divided by the incident light surface power), which
translates to the efficiency of the conversion of light into a mechanical displacement, is remarkably
low (below 10-20 m3/W). Meanwhile, other (non-ferroelectric) inorganic materials have already

Surprisingly, photostriction in BaTiO3 (BTO), the prototypical ferroelectric material, has been
far less explored, except for a few recent works on free-standing membranes7, transient response8,
or first-principle calculations12. In contrast to many inorganic ferroelectrics exhibiting significant
photostrictive response, such as lead- or bismuth-based compounds10,11,13, barium titanate is non-
toxic and environmentally friendly. Thus, there is an urgent need to assess the specific potential of
barium titanate for photostrictive applications and to better understand the dominant mechanism
underlying photostriction in ferroelectrics. In this work, we demonstrate a giant photostriction in
barium titanate thin films reaching 1%, and discuss its potential origin.
Photostriction in ferroelectrics involves multiple, concomitant microscopic mechanisms that
are depicted in Figure 1a. Some of them are not related to the ferroelectric polarization, such as
thermal expansion (which is due to the photogenerated heat), and deformation potential (which is
due to the semiconductor nature of the material, and involves the bandgap pressure sensitivity14).
Ferroelectricity offers additional conversion mechanisms of absorbed optical photons because of
the natural asymmetry endowed by their spontaneous electric polarization and its strong coupling
with mechanical degrees of freedom. For instance, our first-principles calculations have predicted
that thermalized photo-excited carriers could screen the electric polarization, resulting in a piezo-
electric deformation of the lattice12,19. In thin films, experimental and theoretical reports indicate
that these photo-excited carriers in quasi-equilibrium mitigate depolarizing-field effects by migrat-
ing to the interfaces, thereby inducing a piezoelectric-related deformation17,18 characterized by the
d coefficient. Additionally, the hot, non-thermalized portion of photo-excited carriers can generate
strong photovoltages in excess of the bandgap16 by means of the anomalous Bulk Photovoltaic Ef-
fect (BPVE) in noncentrosymmetric materials15 such as ferroelectrics. Such large photovoltages
(and those created by more conventional photovoltaic effect related to Schottky contacts20) can
also couple with the piezoelectric effect (d constant) to induce a photoinduced mechanical defor-
mation. Thus, it is unclear whether (i) thermalized or non-thermalized photo-excited carriers and
(ii) interfacial or bulk-like effects dominate over the photoinduced mechanical response of ferro-
electrics. Here, we demonstrate a photoinduced strain exceeding 1 % in a BaTiO3 thin film and
an effective photostriction of 3×10−15 m3/W, constituting the largest photostrain in ferroelectrics,
and the largest effective photostriction in perovskite oxide and semiconductor thin films (see Fig-
ure 1b). Furthermore, our analysis reveals that the photostrictive strain measured in BaTiO3 does

the photoinduced mechanical deformation of a 100 nm BaTiO3 thin film, epitaxially grown by

Structurally, high-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy images show columnar
growth of the BaTiO3 films in Figure 2a and d, with little to no interface intermixing based on
Electron X-ray Dispersive Spectroscopy. The relative intensities of the (103) peaks shown on the

consists of about 98 % a-domains and 2 % c-domains whereas the thinner 55 nm film (directly
deposited onto SrTiO3) only displays a-domains.
The real and imaginary parts of the refractive index were determined by ellipsometry on both
samples, for wavelengths ranging from 200 nm to 1,700 nm, in Figures 2c and f. As expected for
BTO and STO, the extinction coefficient is low in the visible range, but can be increased due to
defects (which is especially the case in the thicker BTO film, as can be seen from the growth in
Figure 2a).
The photoresponse of our films was investigated under illumination with a 405-nm continuous
wave “pump” laser whose intensity is modulated sinusoidally at a frequency of 10 Hz. The result-
ing photoinduced displacement was measured with sub-nanometer sensitivity by interferometry,
employing a second (“probe”) laser operating with a 594-nm wavelength (see Methods). Photoin-
duced displacements of a 50-nm SRO electrode grown on STO and of a bare STO substrate were
also quantified to serve as references and, later on, to isolate the individual response of the BTO
films.
The photoinduced displacement of the 100-nm BTO/SRO/STO sample increases linearly with
the amplitude of the sinusoidally modulated intensity of the pump laser (see Figure 3a). The
maximum surface displacement is 1.66 nm at 26.5 W.cm-2 and is limited only by the pump laser’s
maximum power as no saturation is observed. In contrast, the photoinduced surface displacement
of the SRO/STO system (i.e., without the BTO film) reaches a maximum photoinduced surface
displacement of only 0.63 nm (i.e., ≈38 % of the BTO/SRO/STO system) and saturates, while
the bare STO substrate exhibits an even smaller photoinduced displacement of 0.23 nm (≈14 %
of the BTO/SRO/STO system) at a comparable laser fluence. Therefore, each component of the
BTO/SRO/STO sample contributes to the measured photoinduced displacement, including the
conducting SRO21. Still, a significant portion of the photostrictive response actually originates
from the polar barium titanate layer.

DISCUSSION
Having isolated the contributions from the SRO electrode (for the 100-nm film) and from the
STO substrate, we discuss hereafter the relative contributions of the various phenomena (thermal
expansion, photovoltaic effects, couplings with piezoelectricity, or thermalized photoexcited car-
riers effects, illustrated in Figure 1) that may be at the origin of the photo-generated deformation
in the BTO film alone
As for SRO and STO, an obvious contribution to the deformation under optical excitation is
the thermal expansion of the BTO film. In previous reports11,25 this contribution has been ruled
out due to dynamical response considerations, i.e., the thermal expansion is expected to be much
slower than the observed photostriction. However, as mentioned previously, the characteristic
thermal response time τth ≈10 ns in our BTO thin films is much shorter than the modula-
tion time of the pump laser (typically 0.1 s). From the measured surface temperature increase
∆TBTO(z = 0) = 6 mK under 33 W.cm-2, we modelled the temperature profile ∆TBTO(z) in
the 55 nm BTO thick film (see Supplementary Information). Consistent with our hypothesis, the
temperature in the BTO film can be considered uniform as the temperature difference through-
out the film is only 0.002 mK (i.e., 0.03 %). The corresponding thermal expansion displace-
ment contributing to the photoinduced displacement of the BTO film amounts to dtemperature

ate electric fields strong enough that they would induce a sizable mechanical deformation through
the reverse piezoelectric effect and, therefore, would contribute to the photoinduced surface dis-
placement. To estimate this contribution, we performed transport measurements under illumina-
tion, and we investigated the piezoelectric response (see Methods and Supplementary Information)
on the 100-nm BTO film (the one with a bottom SRO electrode). The obtained open-circuit photo-
voltage saturates with light intensity at about 1 V. Such photovoltage is of the same order of mag-
nitude as the giant photovoltaic response reported in thinner barium titanate films by Zenkevich
et al.26. The direct piezoelectric response was measured from the interferometric measurement of

This would produce a photoinduced strain of the a-domains of ∼1.4×10−7, i.e., orders of magni-

reports, our analysis indicates that, in the present case, the contribution from photovoltaic effects,
whether macro- or micro-scopic, conventional or related to “hot” (non-thermalized) photo-excited
carriers, is negligibly small.
The photostrictive response thus likely stems from the actions of thermalized photo-excited
carriers, which include the polarization screening32 and deformation potential33 mechanisms. The
polarization screening mechanism is illustrated in Figure 1: in barium titanate, the polarization
stems from the hybridization of the O-2p and Ti-3d orbitals. These orbitals are respectively in-
volved in the valence and conduction bands so that, under illumination, an electron is transferred
from the oxygen to the titanium atom. This decreases the off-centering of the titanium ion and in-
duces an overall modification of the structure through the piezoelectric effect characterized by the
g constant. The second action of thermalized photo-carriers is the deformation potential mecha-
nism that relies on the bandgap pressure sensitivity of any semiconductor, triggering an anisotropic
volume change when carriers are photogenerated.

position of the peak from 519 cm-1 to 514 cm-1 corresponds to an increase in the lattice constant
perpendicular to the polarization, as evidenced by Raelijariona et al.23 and confirmed by DFT
calculations reported in the Supplementary Information. Note that, as the surface powers during
Raman measurements are much higher than in interferometric measurements (by three orders of
magnitude), the saturation observed in Figure 3c and d is not expected to happen during interfero-
metric measurements.
As a conclusion, our results show that giant photoinduced strains reaching 1 % can be achieved
in BTO films. The photoinduced strain in the BTO film is driven by thermalized photo-excited
carriers, in agreement with Hoang et al.8, rather than “hot” carriers. This study opens new av-
enues to design more efficient lead-free photostrictive materials and devices based on the well-
controlled BaTiO3 material platform. Among others, co-doping to enhance optical absorption37
and the thermalized carrier generation, epitaxial strains, and domain engineering are worthy pur-
suits to strengthen further the photostrictive performance of barium titanate-based ferroelectrics.

METHODS
Thin film growth & characterization. The BaTiO3 (BTO) and SrRuO3 (SRO) thin films have
been grown with pulsed laser deposition using a KrF excimer laser (wavelength 248 nm) on (001)-
oriented SrTiO3 substrates. Both layers were grown at 4 Hz pulse frequency, a fluence of 2 J/cm²,
while a temperature of 740°C and oxygen pressure of 0.05 mbar were used for BTO (respectively
0.2 mbar and 690°C for SRO). The reciprocal space maps were measured with the PANanalytical
X-pert PRO MRD, the incident beam contains the CuKα1 and CuKα2 wavelengths. The acquisition
is performed in terms of 2θ −ω scans centered on the (103) peak of the BTO film, lasting a total
of eight hours and thirty-five minutes for both samples.
Photostriction measurements. Photostriction measurements are performed with a homemade
interferometer38,39, using a 594nm continuous-wave diode-pumped laser from the Cobolt 04-01
series that emits up to 50mW as a probe. The interference signal is collected by a photodiode
(Thorlabs PDA36A2) and is treated by a lock-in amplifier (Zurich Instruments MFLI 500kHz).
The interferometer is maintained at its detection maximum using a feedback loop, relying on a
microcontroller (RedPitaya STEMlab 125-14), driving a piezoelectric stack (Thorlabs PA25LEW)
through a voltage amplifier (Pendulum A600). Feedback is performed with the PyRPL software40.
The pump laser is from the Cobolt 06-01 series and emits at 405nm, with a maximum output of
120mW. Its intensity is modulated following a sine wave by an arbitrary signal generator (Tek-
tronix AFG1022), which also serves as a reference signal for synchronous detection. The incident
surface power is calculated based on the frequency component (measured via synchronous detec-
tion) of the laser output and the spot size. The sample holder has an aperture larger than the beam
size, allowing the transmitted beams to exit the setup without back-reflecting in the sample and in

The measurements are performed at a frequency of 10 Hz, the magnitude is decreased in ten
steps between the maximum and 0. The pump incidence angle with the sample surface normal
is 30°. Due to a change in the experimental configuration, the maximum power is 33 W.cm-2 for
the BTO/STO and STO samples; and 26.5 W.cm-2 for the BTO/SRO/STO and SRO/STO samples.

propagated through the classical interference formula, and the 99% confidence interval is evaluated
by taking 2.5 times the calculated error. The confidence intervals vary between 5 and 80pm.

Ellipsometry measurements. All the films have been characterized by Variable Angle Spectro-
scopic Ellipsometry (VASE) in the ultraviolet to near-infrared range (UV-vis-NIR, from 0.7 eV to
5.9 eV) using a J. A. Woollam M2000XI. The ellipsometric angles Ψ and ∆were measured at the

supported by the J. A. Woollam Company. The ellipsometric measurements, the procedure and
the optical models developed in this work to take into account the different layers are described in
details in the Supplementary Information.
Transport measurements. Semi-transparent Pt electrodes (8 nm thick) are deposited on the
thicker 100 nm BTO film. Photocurrent and photoconductivity measurements are performed with
a Keithley 2635B sourcemeter on 50 µm x 50 µm square electrodes. The light source is the same
than the one used for photostriction, in static mode.

Piezoelectric measurement. The probe beam of the interferometer is focused on one of the
50 µm x 50 µm Pt electrode with the help of a x10 objective. The arbitrary signal generator
(Tektronix AFG1022) is used to apply a sinusoidal voltage between the top (Pt) electrode and the
bottom (SRO) electrode. The out-of-plane surface displacement is measured via interferometry.
Temperature measurement. An infrared camera (FLIR A35) is used to measure the temperature
elevation on the sample surface. The temperature is monitored in three points: under the laser
spot, on the sample far from the laser spot and on the sample holder. This ensures the filtering of
additional changes of temperature that are not due to the illumination. The laser is modulated with
a 10 Hz frequency (to match the experimental conditions of photostriction measurement). The
temperature under the laser spot increases, with a static and a dynamic component. Only the latter
is considered for the analysis, since all of the values are taken after synchronous detection.
Raman spectroscopy. Raman spectroscopy is performed with a Horiba Labram Soleil instru-
ment at a wavelength of 405 nm. An objective x100 (NA=0.9, WD=1mm) is used to reduce the
spot size to .5 µm, and the laser power is varied from 0.84 mW up to 11 mW to record the Raman
spectra at different power intensities.
Density Functional Theory calculations. We performed DFT calculations of tetragonal bar-
ium titanate using the plane-wave ABINIT software package35. We employ PAW pseudopotentials
from the PseudoDojo repository41. The PBESol exchange-correlation functional42 was selected.
A plane wave cut-off of 35 Ha was used alongside a 20 × 20 × 20 sampling of the first Brillouin
zone. The electronic self-consistent field cycle was converged until the differences on the forces
between two iterations was smaller than 10−7 Ha/Bohr. Structural relaxation of the ions and lat-
tice vectors, performed using the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno algorithm, was considered
converged when all forces were smaller than 5 × 10−6 Ha/Bohr. A Fermi-Dirac smearing with
a smearing temperature of 0.025 eV was applied. To mimic photo-excitation, we employed the
constrained occupation number implemented in Ref.34 to constrain nph electrons (resp. holes) in
the conduction (resp. valence) bands in the unit cell. Phonon calculations at the Γ-point were also
performed using Density Functional Perturbation Theory under different out-of-plane lattice con-
stant. Throughout these calculations, the effect of the STO substrate was mimicked by clamping
