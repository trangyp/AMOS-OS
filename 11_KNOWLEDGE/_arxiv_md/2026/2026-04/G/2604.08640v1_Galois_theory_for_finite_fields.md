Galois theory for finite fields
Askold Khovanskii
April 13, 2026
Abstract
This note presents Galois theory for finite fields. It was written as
a handout for the MAT401 course “Polynomial equations and fields”
taught at the University of Toronto in Spring 2026. We use without
proofs some basic properties of finite fields and of finite field extensions
which we already covered in class. Firstly, we describe an extension
K ⊂F of a finite field K of a given degree n. We show that the set of
all intermediate fields for this extension is in one-to-one correspondence
with the set of all divisors k of the degree n. Then we describe the
Galois group of this extension which is the cyclic group of order n.
The set of subgroups of this group also is in one-to-one correspondence
with the set of all divisors k of the degree n. It allows us to prove
the Galois correspondence for that extension. In the last section, we
state basic theorems of Galois theory for arbitrary fields which will be
proven later in the course.
1
Introduction
Below, we present Galois theory for finite fields. We use without proofs some
basic properties of field extensions which we study in class. In particular,
it includes basic properties of finite fields, listed in the section 2. We use
splitting fields of polynomials over a given field. We also use the following
elementary but very useful Degree formula: if K ⊂B ⊂F is a chain of
finite extensions, then the degree of the extension K ⊂F is the product of
degrees of the extension K ⊂B and of the extension B ⊂F.
We recall some simple properties of cyclic groups. In the theory of finite
fields, cyclic groups appear in two different ways: on the one hand, the
multiplicative group of a finite field is a cyclic group, on the other hand, the
Galois group of finite extension of any finite field is a cyclic group.
In the section 3, we recall the famous Chinese Remainder Theorem and
its versions.

Preliminaries on finite fields
In this section, we list simple results on finite fields which we studied in
class. We will use these results without proof. Such proofs can be found in
many places, see for example a textbook [1] which we use in the course.
1. Each finite field F has characteristic p, where p is some prime number,
i.e., F contains the subfield field Zp. The field F is a finite extension
of the field Zp.
The degree n of this extension together with the
characteristic p totally determines the finite field F which is called
the Galois field Fq, where q = pn. The field Fq can be considered as
an n-dimensional vector space over the field Zp. It contains q = pn
elements.

2. A field K contains the field Fq if and only if K has characteristic p
and the polynomial xq −x has exactly q = pn roots in K. Moreover,
the set of elements in the subfield Fq ⊂K is equal to the set of all
roots in K of the polynomial xq −x.
3. For any field K of characteristic p the map Φ : K →K, which sends
x to xp, is called the Frobenius homomorphism. The homomorphism
Φ may not be an one-to-one map: some elements of K could have no
preimages under the map Φ. But if K is a finite field, then Φ : K →K
is an onto map.
4. The statement 2) means that the Fq belongs to K if and only if the
q-th power Φq : K →K of the Frobenius homomorphism has exactly
q = pn fixed points, and the set Fq ⊂K is equal to the set of all fixed

Around Chinese Remainder Theorem
In this section, we recall the famous Chinese Remainder Theorem and its
versions.
Let m1, . . . , mn ∈Z be a collection of natural numbers such that any
two different numbers mi, mj from the collection are relatively prime. Let
π : Z →Z/m1Z⊕. . .⊕Z/mnZ be a ring homomorphism from Z to the direct
sum of the factor rings Z/miZ which sends n ∈Z to (n mod m1, . . . , n
mod mn).
Lemma 3.1. The kernel of the homomorphism π is the principal ideal (d) ⊂
Z, where d = m1 · . . . · mn.
Proof. The principal ideal (mi) ⊂Z of the number mi is the kernel of the
factorization map Z →Z/miZ. The kernel of π is the ideal ∩1≤i≤n(mi) ⊂Z.
Since the numbers mi are relatively prime, we have ∩1≤i≤n(mi) = (m1 · . . . ·
mn) = (d).
Corollary 3.2. If the natural numbers m1, . . . , mn ∈Z are relatively prime,
then the ring Z/dZ, where d = m1 · . . . · mn, is isomorphic to the direct sum
⊕1≤i≤nZ/miZ of the rings Z/miZ.
Proof. By Lemma 3.1, the homomorphism π provides an embedding of the
finite ring Z/dZ to the direct sum ⊕1≤i≤nZ/miZ of the finite rings Z/miZ.
Since the rings Z/dZ and ⊕1≤i≤nZ/miZ contain the same number d of ele-
ments, the embedding π is an isomorphism.

In this section, we explore finite extensions K ⊂F of a finite field K of
characteristic p. We show that there is a unique such extension of any given
degree n and describe all intermediate fields for that extension.
First of all, any finite field F of characteristic p contains its finite subfield
Zp. The degree n of the extension Zp ⊂F completely determines it: the
field F is the splitting field of the polynomial xq −x, where q = pn over the
field Zp. The extension Zp ⊂F is unique in the following sense: if Zp ⊂F1 is
another splitting field of the polynomial xq−x, then there is an isomorphism
π : F →F1 whose restriction to the field Zp is the identity map.
Similar result holds for an extension of degree n of the finite field Fq,
where q = ps.

Automorphisms of finite fields
In this section, we study automorphisms of finite fields. In particular, we
show that any automorphism of a finite field F of characteristic p is a power
of the Frobinius map Φ : F →F.

Recall that the Frobenius map
Φ : F →F is the map which sends an element a ∈F to the element
Φ(a) = ap. The map Φ is a field homomorphism, i.e., it is a one-to-one map

map.

Galois theory
In this section, we present, without proof, basic theorems of Galois theory
which widely generalize the results on extensions of finite fields which we
discuss above. Proofs of these theorems will be presented in the course later.
Definition 11.1. An element a ∈E is called a primitive element for a finite
field extension K ⊂E if E can be obtained by adjoining a to the field K.
Theorem 11.2 (Primitive Element Theorem). For any separable finite field
extension K ⊂E there exists a primitive element a ∈E.
Theorem 11.2 widely generalized Corollary 6.2 which plays a similar role
for extensions of finite fields.
Above we defined normal field extensions and their Galois group (see
Definition 9.1).
Theorem 11.3. A finite field extension K ⊂F is normal, if and only if it is
separable and the field F is a splitting field of some polynomial over K. The
Galois group G(K, F) of the normal extension is well defined, i.e., there is
a unique group of automorphisms G(K, F) of F with fixes an element a ∈F
if and only if a ∈K.
Let K ⊂F be a field extension of degree n. For an intermediate field
B we denote by k and m degrees of the extensions K ⊂B and B ⊂F,
respectively. Then k and m are divisors of n, and n = km. However, in
general, it is not true that for every divisor k of n there exists a unique
intermediate field B such that the degree of the extension K ⊂B is equal
to k. There may be several such subfields, or none at all.

Similarly, the Galois group of a normal extension of degree n has order
n, and, by Lagrange’s theorem, orders of its subgroups divide n. Yet, in
general, for a given divisor k of n, there need not be a unique subgroup of
order k; there may be several such subgroups, or none.
Nevertheless, the following Galois Correspondence Theorem holds for
every normal field extension.
Theorem 11.4 (Galois correspondence). Let K ⊂F be a normal extension
of degree n. Then the Galois correspondence provides a bijection between
the set of all subgroups of the Galois group G(F, K) and the set of all inter-
mediate subfields of the extension.
Moreover, for an intermediate field B the extension K ⊂B is normal if
and only if B corresponds to a normal divisor G of the Galois group G(K, F).
In that case, there is a natural action of the factor group G(F, K)/G on the
intermediate field B which fixes all elements of the field K.
It provides
an isomorphism between the factor group G(F, K)/G and the Galois group
G(K, B) of the extension K ⊂B.
