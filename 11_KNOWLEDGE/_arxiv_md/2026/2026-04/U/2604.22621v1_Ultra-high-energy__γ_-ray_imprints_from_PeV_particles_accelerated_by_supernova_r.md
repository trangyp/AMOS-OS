Ultra-high-energy 𝜸-ray imprints from PeV particles
accelerated by supernova remnants
Authors and affiliations appear at the end of the paper.
The quest for the origin of cosmic ray (CRs) is a fundamental issue in astro-
physics (1,2). Shocks of supernova remnants (SNRs) have been considered as the
dominant contributors to Galactic CRs below the spectral knee near ∼3 petaelec-
tronvolt (PeV). Whether SNRs are efficient accelerators of particles beyond PeV
energies has long been debated (3, 4). Here we report observations of very-high-
energy 𝜸-ray emission up to hundreds of TeV from two middle age shell-type SNRs,
G150.3+4.5 and 𝜸-Cygni, with the Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory
(LHAASO). Two (or three) distinct morphological/spectral components with con-
vex spectral shapes are observed in both sources, with the low-energy one being
more extended than the high-energy one. The likely association of the high-energy
component with molecular clouds at similar distances, and the weakness/absence
of pulsar wind nebulae (PWNe) inside these SNRs (5, 6) clearly indicate for the
first time that the highest energy emission is produced by collision of hadronic
CRs up to PeV energies with the clouds. These results are compatible with the
classic model prediction that PeV particles accelerated near the end of the free
expansion phase of SNR evolution can illuminate nearby molecular clouds (MCs)
to produce strong 𝜸-ray emission (7,8).
It is generally accepted that SNRs shocks provide sufficient energy to sustain the observed GeV
CR fluxes (9). Evidence of a neutral pion decay signature in several SNRs accompanied with MCs
suggests that SNRs are hadronic particle accelerators (10,11), supporting the scenario of SNR origin
of Galactic CRs. However, there is no direct evidence that SNRs act as PeV particle accelerators

the Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory (LHAASO) (12) offers a unique opportunity to

In an earlier study, ultra-high-energy 𝛾-ray emission was reported from the compact SNR W51
with a powerful PWN inside (13). Here we report observations of two SNRs, G150.3+4.5 and
𝛾-Cygni, with LHAASO. The SNR G150.3+4.5 was first identified by radio observations, showing
a radius of about 1.3◦(15). Recent CO observations show that the distance from the SNR is about
740 pc and that the age is estimated to be (1 −18) × 104 yr (16). Using the statistical diameter-age
relation (17), an age of 26 kyr can be derived. The overall GeV 𝛾-ray spectrum is hard, with a photon
index of −1.6 at 9 GeV with a significant spectral curvature toward high energies (18). Detailed
analysis of the Fermi data showed that the 𝛾-ray spectra have spatial variations, suggesting a hybrid
radiation process (6). In the first catalog of LHAASO, an extended source associated with the radio
shell around TeV energies and a more compact component at higher energies were detected (19).
SNR 𝛾-Cygni (G78.2+2.1 or DR4) has been extensively studied in the radio, X-ray, and 𝛾-ray
bands (5, 20–23). The age of this SNR is estimated to be about 7 kyr, and the distance is ∼1.7
kpc (24). Its radio shell has an angular radius of about 0.5◦, which corresponds to a scale of ∼15
pc. Strong 𝛾-ray emission has been detected from the pulsar PSR J2021+4026, which is a bit off to
the east of the SNR center and might be associated with the SNR (25). However, the PWN of PSR
J2021+4026 is very weak in radio and X-ray bands (5), which implies weak TeV emission around
the pulsar. This source was reported in the first LHAASO catalog (19), and was associated with
VER J2019+407 as detected by VERITAS (21).
LHAASO observations
Using more than three years of data recorded by LHAASO, we study these two SNRs in detail.
The 𝛾-ray spectrum and morphology are fitted simultaneously using a 3-dimensional likelihood
fitting method (two for the spatial distribution and one for the spectrum). We assume a two-
dimensional Gaussian morphology and power-law with exponential cutoff (PLEcut) spectrum to
describe the target source. In total, there are 6 free parameters for one source. To characterize the

Figure 3 gives the spectral energy distributions (SEDs) of different source components of both
SNRs. For each component the spectral shape is assumed to be a power-law with an exponential

Interpretation
We consider a hybrid model containing both leptonic (inverse Compton scattering) and hadronic
(𝜋0 decay) 𝛾-ray emission processes. We assume that high-energy CR protons and electrons were
accelerated at an early stage of the SNR and were then injected instantaneously into the interstellar
medium. These particles then diffuse out and interact with the ambient matter and fields, and
produce multi-wavelength emission (30,31). A broken power-law with an exponential high-energy
cutoff function (32,33) of the injection spectra for both electrons and protons is assumed. Efficient
cooling of electrons results in a cutoff energy smaller than that of protons at present. The spatial and
spectral distributions of electrons and protons are detailed in the Supplementary Materials (28).

SNR shocks have been considered to be the dominant agent for the acceleration of CR (1). The
maximum acceleration energy of SNRs has been unclear for a long time. Although SNR W51 has
been detected with LHAASO above 100 TeV (13), it is compact with a strong X-ray pulsar wind
nebula inside, implying leptonic contributions to TeV emission. Ultra-high-energy 𝛾rays produced
by accelerated particles when hitting nearby molecular clouds, as illustrated by the detection of
distinct high-energy components here by LHAASO, offer a direct probe of this problem. The
highest energy of 𝛾-ray photons from dense cloud sites exceeds 100 TeV, strongly supporting the
view that SNR shocks can serve as PeVatrons to accelerate CR ions to PeV energies. However, the
spectral cutoff of the soft injection spectrum, characterized by an exponential cutoff energy 𝐸cut, is
found to be below PeV, indicating that SNR may not be dominant contributors to CR well above the
knee. The acceleration of CR by other sources, such as the center of the Milky Way galaxy (35),
massive star clusters (36), and/or microquasars (37,38), is possibly relevant to the high-energy end
of Galactic CR spectra.

LHAASO experiment.
LHAASO is a hybrid, large area, wide field-of-view observatory for CRs and 𝛾rays in a wide
energy range. It is located at Haizi Mountain (100◦.01E, 29◦.35N; 4400 m above the sea level),
Daocheng, Sichuan province, China (12). LHAASO consists of three detector arrays, the square
kilometer array (KM2A), the water Cherenkov detector array (WCDA), and the wide field-of-view
Cherenkov telescope array (WFCTA). The KM2A consists of 5195 electromagnetic detectors (EDs)
placed on a 15 m spacing grid and 1188 muon detectors (MDs) placed on a 30 m spacing grid. An
outer ring of EDs with 30 m spacing is built to separate the in-array and out-array showers. The total
area of KM2A is about 1.3 km2. The KM2A gives the most sensitive survey of the northern sky
in ultra-high-energy 𝛾rays (39). The WCDA has 3120 water Cherenkov detectors placed evenly in
three pools with a depth of 4.4 m and a total area of 78,000 m2. Each detector unit has a dimension
of 5 × 5 m2, covered with non-reflecting black plastic curtains. Two photo-multipliers are equipped
in each unit to record the Cherenkov light produced by charged particles in the pure water. The
WCDA is sensitive to 𝛾rays from 100 GeV to 30 TeV (40). The WFCTA consists of 18 telescopes,
each with an area of 4.7 m2 and a field-of-view of 16◦×16◦. The telescopes are placed in the center
of the KM2A, which are used to image the showers to precisely measure the composition of CRs.

We use the direct integral method (42) to estimate the background. This method assumes that
the spatial distribution of the detection efficiency is stable in a reasonably short time period. The
background is then determined by the observed events in adjacent time bins. A time step of 4 hours
is adopted, and at each step a time window of ±5 hours is used to estimate the background.
The 3D-likelihood method, a joint analysis of the morphology and spectrum, is employed to
simultaneously fit the data from the WCDA and KM2A detectors. The sky around the target source
is binned into cells with a size of 0◦.1 in both the right ascension (R.A.) and declination (Dec.).
A likelihood-ratio test is performed, with the test statistic (TS) defined as TS = 2 ln(L𝑠+𝑏/L𝑏),
where L𝑠+𝑏is the maximum likelihood for the signal plus background hypothesis and L𝑏is the
likelihood for the background only hypothesis. In the background only case, the TS value obeys
a 𝜒2 distribution with 𝑛degrees of freedom, where 𝑛is the number of additional parameters in
the signal plus background model. The statistical significance of the detection of a source can be
obtained from the Wilks’s theorem (43).

Multi-wavelength observations.
G150.3+4.5 is a shell-type SNR. The eastern part of the shell was initially discovered by Gerbrandt
et al. (2014) (15), showing faint, extended non-thermal radio emission, which was named as
G150.8+3.8. Gao and Han (2014) (51) reported the total shell-like morphology with a size of
2.5◦× 3.0◦using the 𝜆6 cm survey data. The non-thermal radio spectral indices for the eastern
and western shells of G150.3+4.5 are ∼−0.4 and ∼−0.7, respectively (51). The hard power-law
𝛾-ray emission of G150.3+4.5 was discovered by (52) through analyzing seven years of Pass 8 data
recorded by the Fermi-LAT. The 𝛾-ray morphology was found to be extended with a radius of about
1.4◦. Then in Fermi-LAT catalogs (2FHL (53), FGES (54), 4FGL (55)), an extended source with
about 1.5◦radius was reported together with a hard spectrum in GeV band with a power-law spectral
index of about 1.9. Devin et al. (2020) (18) used a two-dimensional symmetric Gaussian model
or a disk model, spatially coincident with the radio emission, to describe the 𝛾-ray emission using
more than 10 years of Fermi-LAT data, and obtained a hard spectral index of Γ = 1.62 at 𝐸0 = 9.0
GeV with a spectral curvature toward high energies. An unidentified source with a steep spectrum
in the east-southern part of the shell, 4FGL J0426.5+5434, was present in the 4FGL catalog (55).
This source is a point-like source positionally coincident with LHAASO source B. No pulsation
is reported for this source. We regard it as a background source, due to its distinct spectrum and
morphology from the LHAASO source. The ROSAT X-ray observation of this region gave only an
upper limit of the non-thermal component assuming a power-law spectrum with Γ = 2 (18). We
re-analyze the data and derive an unabsorbed non-thermal 0.1 −3.0 keV flux upper limit (95%)
