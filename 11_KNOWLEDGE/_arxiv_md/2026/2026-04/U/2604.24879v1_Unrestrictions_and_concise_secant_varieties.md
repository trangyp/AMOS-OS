UNRESTRICTIONS AND CONCISE SECANT VARIETIES
JAKUB JAGIEŁŁA AND JOACHIM JELISIEJEW
Abstract. We introduce the concise secant varieties, which are, informally speaking, modular
partial desingularisations of secant varieties to Segre embeddings. More precisely, they are projec-
tive and birational to the abstract secant varieties, yet each of their points corresponds to a concise
tensor of appropriate border rank (that is, to a minimal border rank tensor).
We discuss implications throughout the theory of tensors, including a characterisation of border
rank ≤r tensors as unrestrictions of minimal border rank r tensors (also in the Veronese and
Segre-Veronese cases), a characterisation of tensors with cactus rank ≤r, concise versions of border
apolarity including the fixed point theorem, concise Varieties of Sums of Powers, counting points
on the second secant variety, connections to defectivity and identifiability in the Segre case, to the
Salmon conjecture etc.

1.1. Unrestrictions. For a tensor [T] ∈PV1,...,d, a central problem is to determine its border rank
brk(T) that is, the smallest integer r such that [T] = limt→0[Tt] for some tensors Tt of rank ≤r.
When all coordinate spaces V• have dimension r, Buczyński observed [Buc22] that brk(T) ≤r
exactly when T is a restriction of a minimal border rank tensor (see §1.4.2, §3.2 for definitions).
This is important, since restriction is a linear-algebraic operation, while passing to the limit is
much more involved.
Based on Buczyński’s observation, we show that the operation of passing to the limit [T] =
limt→0[Tt] can be split into two steps:
(a) the minimal border rank limit eT = limt→0 Tt,
(b) a restriction from eT to T.

1.4. Applications of concise secant varieties.
1.4.1. Resolutions of singularities. We pass to analysing the singularities of secant varieties. The
two main goals here are to understand the singular locus and resolve singularities. When the variety
is embedded positively enough, there are many positive results [Ull16, ENP20, CLPS25, AP25].
In our setup, without positivity, the situation is more complicated. We are not aware of results
about resolution of singularities. For describing the singular locus, partial results appear in [Han18,
FH25]. A full answer is known only for r = 2 and r = d = 3. The paper [MOZ14] describes σ2
for every d. For d = 3, Buczyński and Landsberg [BL14] give normal forms of tensors in σ3 and
equations are known here, from which one can describe the singular locus.
In contrast, our method works for every d ≥3 and yields resolution of singularities in the Segre
case for r = 2, 3, 4, and, conjecturally, for other values of r. Importantly, we do not perform any
case-by-case analysis, but rather recast the problem as a question of singularities of Hilbert and
Quot schemes, so that the results follow from known ones. The order d ≥3 of tensors is not
important throughout the argument.
Theorem 1.1 (Theorem 3.29).
(a) The variety cσr is nonsingular for r ≤3.
(b) The variety cσ4 is singular, with singular locus of codimension 9. It is Cohen-Macaulay
with rational (in fact, canonical) singularities. The singular locus is smoothly equivalent to
the vertex of the affine cone over Gr(2, 6) ,→P14. Its singularities are resolved by a single
blow up of the singular locus.
(c) Assume that ρ is birational (this happens when dim V• = r and, in fact, in much greater
generality, see §4.5). For r = 2, 3 the map ρ: cσr →σr is a resolution of singularities of
σr. For r = 4 the map BlSing(cσ4) cσ4 →cσ4 →σ4 is a resolution of singularities.
We have a similar description also for cσ5, which we will discuss elsewhere, see Remark 3.31.
Also for general r, the geometry of cσr is better than the geometry of σr. The secant variety
suffers because σr−1 forms a “black hole” inside: at any point [T] ∈σr−1 ⊆σr, the tangent space
T[T]σr is the whole tangent space to PV1,...,d and there is little hope for a meaningful geometry. In
contrast, for any r, the geometry of cσr at any point is smoothly equivalent to the geometry of
σr ⊆PW1,...,d at a concise point, where W1,...,d = W1 ⊗. . . ⊗Wd and dim W• = r, see §3.6.

1.4.4. Varieties of sums of powers. Theorem 1.5 has immediate consequences for the varieties of
sums of powers. Recall that these varieties were introduced by Ranestad and Schreyer [RS00] as
closures of
(1.4)
VSP(T, r) = {[Z] ,→Segre | Z zero-dimensional smoothable Gorenstein, deg Z = r, T ∈⟨Z⟩}.
These varieties exhibit very interesting global geometry, especially in the defective cases, see for
example [IR01, RV17, RS13, JRS25], yielding hyperkähler varieties etc. However, as in the case
of secant varieties, the need for closure is a source of major technical problems, as it is very hard
to prove that the closure is smooth, for example, or to describe it, see the discussion in [JRS25].
The need of closure is also the same as existence of wild tensors, as in [BB15].
One solution for this problem was recently introduced using border apolarity [BB21]. There, one
considers V SP(T, r) which, roughly speaking, consists of all “good” multigraded ideals I with fixed
Hilbert function and such that T is apolar I. The word “good” above technically means that [I]
lies in the Slip component of a multigraded Hilbert scheme of Haiman-Sturmfels [HS04]. Border
apolarity has led to dramatic improvements in border rank estimates, see [CHL23a, CHL23b,
MV24].
Working with V SP(T, r) poses two challenges:
(a) For a given ideal I, it is notoriously hard to determine if it lies in the Slip component. This
is because the multigraded Hilbert scheme, apart from containing the Slip component,

1.4.5. Concise version of border apolarity. As discussed above, border apolarity has been a suc-
cessful tool for obtaining lower bounds on the border rank. In this section we explain that the
same can be achieved with cσr. To compare the two, we can say that:
(a) Border apolarity produces [BB21, Theorems 1.2, 4.3] a Borel-fixed ideal I inside the apolar
ideal of the tensor T. The main question is whether I is the Slip component. The obstacles
for proving that are discussed in §1.4.4 above.
(b) The procedure below produces a Borel-fixed unrestriction eT ≥T. The unrestriction is
a concise tensor in W1,...,d, where dim W• = r, so that brk eT ≥r. The main question is
whether eT has border rank r.
In our view, there are two advantages of (b) over (a). First, the procedure below requires no
apolarity, no multigraded Hilbert schemes etc. Second, more importantly, the output eT is a tensor
and can be tested using all existing tools of the theory: Koszul and Young flattenings [OO13],
centroids [JLP24], etc.
Let us finally describe the procedure more carefully. Let [T] ∈PV1,...,d and let ρ: cσr →σr.
First, almost directly from the definitions, we have the following:
(a) brk T ≤r if and only if ρ−1([T]) is nonempty,
(b) the cactus rank of T is ≤r if and only if ρ−1([T]) contains a 1-generic tensor (see §4.3),
(c) the smoothable rank of T is ≤r if and only if ρ−1([T]) contains a 1-generic tensor with
smoothable centroid,
(d) the rank of T is ≤r if and only if ρ−1([T]) contains a 1-generic tensor with reduced centroid.

B-fixed tensor eT restricting to T.
To understand Borel-fixed tensors, we need to carefully see how the action of B lifts to an action
on W1,...,d. This is discussed formally in §3.3.1 and concretely on a small, but illuminating example

1.5. Open problems. Miraculously, concise secant varieties seem to apply to a range of tensor
problems of various flavours. We sketch three possible applications. None of them is currently
worked out, so below we mostly illustrate a possible approach.
1.5.1. Lower bounds for border ranks, including matrix multiplication. This problem is a straight-
forward continuation of the discussion in §1.4.5. It is also quite mature: it is clear what to do, it
only leaves the question on how effective it will be.
Namely, consider the matrix multiplication tensor Mn ∈V1 ⊗V2 ⊗V3, where dim V• = n2. It is
stabilised by the Borel subgroup B of upper-triangular n × n matrices.
To prove, for example, that brk M3 > 17, one can enumerate possible B-fixed concise tensors eT
in ρ−1(M3) with dim Cen eT ≥17, to prove that none of them has minimal border rank tensor. The
code provided by Austin Conner for the paper [CHL23a] can be adapted to this purpose. Then,
one has to rule out the possible candidates, showing that none of them has minimal border rank.
This part is currently unexplored, but it is tempting to employ the suite of already existing tools:

Before stating and proving Proposition 2.4, we discuss the intuitive notion of a degeneration of
tensors. There are a few closely related ways to formally define it. Let us list a few.
(a) For k = C, a degeneration of tensors Tt to a tensor T can be thought of as a sequence
of tensors in Sν1V1 ⊗. . . ⊗SνdVd converging to T in the usual topological sense. In this
interpretation, the general tensor Tt̸=0 is general tensor from this sequence.
(b) For k = C, a degeneration of tensors Tt to a tensor T can be thought of as a curve germ
∆→Sν1V1⊗. . .⊗SνdVd, where ∆⊆C is a small disk around 0, such that 0 ∈∆is mapped
to T. In this interpretation, the general tensor Tt̸=0 is the image of general point of ∆\{0}.

of Spec k[[t]] is mapped to T. In this interpretation, the general tensor Tt̸=0 is the image of
the generic point of Spec k[[t]].
(d) For general k, a degeneration of tensors Tt to a tensor T can be thought of as an single
tensor Tt ∈(Sν1V1 ⊗. . . ⊗SνdVd)[[t]] such that Tt=0 ∈V1,...,d coincides with the tensor T.
