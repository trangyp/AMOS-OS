Abstract
In a binary groupoid (G, ∗), a Fibonacci sequence is a recurrent sequence defined by
f1 = a, f2 = b, . . . , fn = fn−2 ∗fn−1. A universal Fibonacci sequence (UFS) is a singly or
doubly infinite sequence whose set of suffixes coincides precisely with the set of all Fibonacci
sequences in the groupoid.
This paper studies UFS-groupoids, i.e., groupoids that admit a universal Fibonacci se-
quence. It is shown that every nontrivial UFS-groupoid is at most countable, locally cyclic,
and non-power-associative; that the right cancellation property and the right quasigroup
property hold for all pairs of elements except possibly one and two, respectively; that no
neutral element or zero element exists; and that there is at most one idempotent element. It
is proved that any UFS-groupoid whose universal Fibonacci sequence is not doubly infinite
strictly preperiodic is cyclic. It has also been proved that the class of UFS-groupoids is
closed under taking subgroupoids and homomorphic images, but is not closed under finite
direct products. The structure of subgroupoids of UFS-groupoids is described.
A complete classification of UFS-groupoids is given in terms of the cardinality of G and
the periodicity of the universal Fibonacci sequences. Finite UFS-groupoids are described
combinatorially via de Bruijn sequences. The number of distinct UFS-groupoids on a finite
set is determined, and explicit constructions are provided for both finite and infinite cases
across all periodicity classes, including embeddings of UFS-groupoids as subgroupoids into
other UFS-groupoids and infinitely generated UFS-groupoids.

Introduction
Fibonacci sequences in arbitrary binary groupoids (magmas) (G, ∗) provide perhaps the most
natural example of nontrivial recurrent sequences. Such a sequence is specified by the recursion
f1 = a, f2 = b, . . . , fn = fn−2 ∗fn−1,
where ∗denotes the binary operation of the groupoid.
A systematic investigation of these
sequences was initiated by Donald Dines Wall in his 1960 paper [1], which focused on Fibonacci-
type recurrences in the cyclic groups (Zn, +). Several particular instances, however, had been
examined much earlier. Notably, already in 1774 Joseph-Louis Lagrange studied the periods
π(n) of the classical integer Fibonacci sequence
F1 = 0, F2 = 1, . . . , Fn = Fn−2 + Fn−1,
when taken modulo n; these periods are now known as the Pisano periods [2].
Since then, Fibonacci sequences, together with their properties and applications, have been
studied intensively across a wide range of algebraic structures, including groups [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9],
quasigroups [10, 11, 12], semigroups [13], and more general groupoids [14]. A central focus in
much of this literature has been the period of a Fibonacci sequence and various characteristics
derived from it.
This paper introduces the concept of a universal Fibonacci sequence (UFS) in a groupoid
(G, ∗). It is a sequence—either singly or doubly infinite—of the form
bu = (u1, u2, . . .)
or
bu = (. . . , u−1, u0, u1, . . .),
whose set of suffixes {(uk, uk+1, uk+2, . . .)} is precisely the set of all Fibonacci sequences in (G, ∗).
By definition, its terms satisfy the same recurrence relation that defines the Fibonacci sequence
in (G, ∗). We refer to groupoids that admit a universal Fibonacci sequence as UFS-groupoids.
For a Fibonacci sequence
(f1, f2, . . . , fn = fn−2 ∗fn−1, . . .),
every suffix
(fk, fk+1, fk+2, . . .)

satisfies the same recurrence and is therefore itself a Fibonacci sequence. However, most pairs
of Fibonacci sequences in an arbitrarily chosen groupoid are, as a rule, not suffixes of one an-
other. As established in 3.16, the existence of a universal Fibonacci sequence is equivalent to
the condition that for every pair of Fibonacci sequences, one is a suffix of the other. Thus,
UFS-groupoids form an extremal class where the degree of nesting of Fibonacci sequences within
each other is maximal, and the structure of all Fibonacci sequences reduces to the behavior of a
single universal Fibonacci sequence.
Universal Fibonacci sequences also admit a natural geometric interpretation. As we move
along a Fibonacci sequence, we pass from (a, b) to (b, a∗b). This is equivalent to moving through
the Cayley table of the groupoid: the row index is replaced by the column index, and the column
index is replaced by the entry in the Cayley table corresponding to their product under the
operation.
A universal Fibonacci sequence thus provides a complete traversal of the cells of
the Cayley table according to this rule. In particular, periodic universal Fibonacci sequences
correspond precisely to Hamiltonian cycles in the Cayley table.
On the other hand, any recurrence function f defining a recurrent sequence ak = f(ak−2, ak−1)
of two arguments can be interpreted as a binary operation. Under this interpretation, UFS-
groupoids describe exactly those recurrence relations f that, regardless of the choice of the two
initial points, always follow the same large common sequence.
As shown in Chapter 3, a universal Fibonacci sequence bu, if it exists, is unique up to a
cyclic shift of its cycle in the periodic case. This allows us to regard a UFS-groupoid together
with its uniquely determined universal Fibonacci sequence. The sequence itself encodes structural
information about the operation, and its combinatorial properties are closely tied to the algebraic
properties of the UFS-groupoid. The periodicity of the universal Fibonacci sequence plays a
central role in this connection.
The article is divided into several chapters.
In Chapter 2 we collect the definitions and known results used throughout the paper.
In Chapter 3 we introduce the main concepts, establish a number of auxiliary technical
lemmas, and prove the basic properties of UFS-groupoids and universal Fibonacci sequences.
In particular, we prove Lemma 3.7 on the at most countable cardinality of the class of UFS-
groupoids; Theorem 3.8, which provides criteria for a sequence to be a universal Fibonacci
sequence of some groupoid and shows that the groupoid can be recovered uniquely from the
sequence; Theorem 3.14, which characterizes universal Fibonacci sequences via the non-repetition
of substrings of length 2; and Theorem 3.15, which establishes the uniqueness of a universal
Fibonacci sequence.
Chapter 4 contains a detailed description of finite UFS-groupoids. In particular, Theorem
4.1 shows that the finite UFS-groupoids are precisely those whose universal Fibonacci sequence

yielding a natural bijection between UFS-groupoids with periodic universal Fibonacci sequences
and de Bruijn sequences. Theorems 4.3 and 4.5 show that the UFS-groupoids whose universal
Fibonacci sequence is singly infinite and strictly preperiodic arise uniquely from UFS-groupoids

In Chapter 5, in Theorems 5.1, 5.8, 5.12, 5.13, 5.14 and Lemmas 5.2, 5.3, we establish various

according to their cardinality and the periodicity type of the universal Fibonacci sequence.

of all possible types, classified both by their cardinality and the periodicity type of the universal
Fibonacci sequence. In particular, we construct inductively mutually optimally extendable UFS-
groupoids with periodic universal Fibonacci sequences as well as UFS-groupoids containing an
idempotent element. Constructions are given for embedding any UFS-groupoid with a singly
infinite strictly preperiodic, periodic, or singly infinite aperiodic universal Fibonacci sequence
into other UFS-groupoids. Infinitely generated UFS-groupoids are constructed.

Definitions and known results
For the reader’s convenience, this chapter collects the main definitions and concepts used in
the paper. Most standard notions from algebra and the theory of Fibonacci sequences can be

2.1
Basic algebraic concepts
Definition 2.1. A groupoid or magma is a pair (G, ∗) consisting of a set G together with a
binary operation ∗: G × G →G on it.
For groupoids and their elements, a number of standard properties can be formulated.
Definition 2.2. In a groupoid (G, ∗), the following elements may exist:
1. Neutral element: an element e ∈G such that a ∗e = e ∗a = a for all a ∈G is called a
neutral element.
2. Zero element: an element 0 ∈G such that a ∗0 = 0 ∗a = 0 for all a ∈G is called a zero
element.
3. Idempotent element: an element i ∈G such that i∗i = i is called an idempotent element.
4. Inverse element: if a neutral element e ∈G exists, then an element a−1 ∈G satisfying
a ∗a−1 = a−1 ∗a = e is called an inverse element of a ∈G.
Definition 2.3. In a groupoid (G, ∗), the following properties may hold:

Definition 2.4. An abelian groupoid is a commutative groupoid.
Definition 2.5. A semigroup is an associative groupoid.
Definition 2.6. A monoid is a semigroup with a neutral element.
In groupoids, one can consider the equations a ∗x = b and x ∗a = b for x. The solvability of
these equations gives rise to definitions of various algebraic structures.
Definition 2.7. A groupoid (G, ∗) is called a left cancellative groupoid if for any a, b ∈G
the equation a ∗x = b has at most one solution; that is, a ∗x1 = a ∗x2 =⇒x1 = x2.
Definition 2.8. A groupoid (G, ∗) is called a right cancellative groupoid if for any a, b ∈G
the equation x ∗a = b has at most one solution; that is, x1 ∗a = x2 ∗a =⇒x1 = x2.
Definition 2.9. A groupoid (G, ∗) is called a cancellative groupoid if it is both left and right
cancellative.
Definition 2.10. A groupoid (G, ∗) is called a left quasigroup if for any a, b ∈G the equation
a ∗x = b has a unique solution.
Definition 2.11. A groupoid (G, ∗) is called a right quasigroup if for any a, b ∈G the equation
x ∗a = b has a unique solution.
Definition 2.12. A groupoid (G, ∗) is called a quasigroup if it is both a left and a right
quasigroup.
Many of these properties can also be formulated elementwise. For instance, for a specific
pair of elements (a, b) ∈G2 in a groupoid (G, ∗), the right cancellation property is said to
hold if the equation x ∗a = b has at most one solution x, and the right quasigroup property
holds if the equation x ∗a = b has exactly one solution x. Note that a quasigroup is a special
case of a cancellative groupoid. For finite groupoids, these notions coincide [15]: a finite left
(right) cancellative groupoid is a left (right) quasigroup, and a finite cancellative groupoid is a
quasigroup.
Definition 2.13. A loop is a quasigroup with a neutral element.
Definition 2.14. A group is an associative groupoid with a neutral element and inverses for
all elements.

Definition 2.15. A subgroupoid of a groupoid (G, ∗) is a nonempty groupoid (A, ∗) with A ⊂G
such that for all a, b ∈A we have a ∗b ∈A, where the operation ∗is restricted to the elements
of A.
Definition 2.16. A subgroup of a group (G, ∗) is a subgroupoid (A, ∗) that contains inverses
for all its elements.
Definition 2.17. A proper subgroupoid/subgroup of a groupoid/group (G, ∗) is a subgroupoid
(A, ∗) with A ̸= G.
A groupoid is a group if and only if it is both a semigroup and a quasigroup [18].
Definition 2.18. A generating set of a groupoid (G, ∗) is a subset A ⊂G such that every
element of G can be expressed as a finite product of elements of A.
Definition 2.19. A generating set of a group (G, ∗) is a subset A ⊂G such that every element
of G can be expressed as a finite product of elements of A and their inverses.
The fact that A is a generating set of a groupoid/group (G, ∗) is denoted by ⟨A⟩= G. If
A = a1, a2, . . . , an, we also write ⟨a1, . . . , an⟩= G.
Definition 2.20. A groupoid/group (G, ∗) is called k-generated if there exists A ⊂G such that
⟨A⟩= G and |A| = k.
Definition 2.21. A groupoid/group (G, ∗) is called finitely generated if (G, ∗) is k-generated
for some natural number k.
Definition 2.22. A groupoid/group (G, ∗) is called infinitely generated if it is not finitely
generated.
Definition 2.23. A groupoid/group (G, ∗) is called cyclic if there exists a ∈G such that ⟨a⟩= G.
Definition 2.24. A groupoid/group (G, ∗) is called locally cyclic if every finitely generated
subgroupoid/subgroup is cyclic.
The structure of cyclic groups and cyclic semigroups (semigroups considered as cyclic groupoids)
is well known [16, 19]; we state it explicitly. Arbitrary cyclic groupoids can have a more complex
structure.
Theorem 2.25. Any cyclic group (G, ∗) = ⟨a⟩satisfies:
1. if |G| = n, then (G, ∗) is isomorphic to the additive group of residues (Zn, +);
2. if G is infinite, then (G, ∗) is isomorphic to the additive group of integers (Z, +).
Theorem 2.26. Any cyclic semigroup (G, ∗) = ⟨a⟩satisfies:
1. if |G| = n, then (G, ∗) consists of a tail T = {a, a2, . . . , am−1} and a cyclic group K =
{am, . . . , am+r−1}, where n = m + r −1. The number m is called the index of (G, ∗), and
r is called the period of (G, ∗). Moreover, am+x = am+y if and only if x ≡y mod r.
2. if G is infinite, then (G, ∗) is isomorphic to the additive semigroup of natural numbers
(N, +).
Remark 2.27. Every cyclic group is locally cyclic [16]. However, a cyclic groupoid need not be
locally cyclic. For example, (N, +) = ⟨1⟩, but its subgroupoid ⟨2, 3⟩is not cyclic.

Many classical algebraic objects are varieties, such as semigroups (satisfying the associativity
identity) and abelian groupoids (satisfying the commutativity identity).
There is a classical theorem on varieties due to Birkhoff.
We present it in a form that
characterizes when a class of groupoids is a variety.
Theorem 2.34. [20] A class K of groupoids is a variety if and only if:
1. If (G, ∗) ∈K, then every subgroupoid of (G, ∗) also belongs to K.
2. If (Gi, ∗i) ∈K for all i ∈A, then the direct product of the family (Gi, ∗i), i ∈A, also
belongs to K.
3. If (G, ∗) ∈K and a homomorphism f : G →G′ into an arbitrary groupoid (G′, ∗′) is given,
then the homomorphic image f((G, ∗)) also belongs to K.

2.4
Fibonacci sequences
For an arbitrary groupoid (G, ∗) without any additional structure, the most natural way to
construct a recurrent sequence is to apply the operation sequentially to the preceding terms.

The extreme case of embedding Fibonacci sequences in a groupoid is the embedding of all of

Definition 3.2. A universal Fibonacci sequence or UFS in a groupoid (G, ∗) is an infinite

1. Any suffix

Theorem 5.1. In a UFS-groupoid (G, ∗), there exists at most one idempotent element. If an

Main classification theorem
A number of theorems in this paper can be combined into a final classification theorem.
Theorem 5.27. UFS-groupoids (G, ∗) are locally cyclic, non-power-associative, and contain no
neutral or zero elements. Moreover, depending on the number of elements and the periodicity of
the universal Fibonacci sequence bu, they can be divided into:
1. Finite UFS-groupoids, |G| = n:
(a) UFS-groupoids with periodic bu with cycle (u1, . . . , un2): these are finite cyclic right
quasigroups without idempotent elements and without proper subgroupoids. Such groupoids
are naturally bijective to the set of de Bruijn sequences B(n, 2); the de Bruijn sequences
are precisely (u1, . . . , un2).
(b) UFS-groupoids with singly infinite strictly preperiodic bu with preperiodic part (u1, . . . , ul)
and periodic part (ul+1, . . . , un2): they satisfy u1 = ul+1, are finite cyclic groupoids in
which the right cancellation property fails only for (ul+1, ul+2), and the right quasi-
group property fails only for (ul+1, ul+2) and (u1, u2). Such groupoids are uniquely
obtained from UFS-groupoids with periodic bu, and hence from de Bruijn sequences, by
arbitrarily changing the operation on an arbitrary pair of elements.
2. Countable UFS-groupoids, |G| = ∞:
(a) UFS-groupoids with singly infinite aperiodic bu = (u1, u2, . . .): these are infinite cyclic
groupoids with right cancellation, without idempotent elements and without proper
subgroupoids, in which the right quasigroup property fails only for (u1, u2).
(b) UFS-groupoids with doubly infinite aperiodic bu: these are infinite cyclic right quasi-
groups without idempotent elements, in which there can be at most one proper sub-
groupoid, and any such subgroupoid must be infinite.
(c) UFS-groupoids with doubly infinite strictly preperiodic bu with periodic part (ul+1, . . . , un2):
these are infinite groupoids in which both the right cancellation property and the right
quasigroup property fail only for (ul+1, ul+2), and all proper subgroupoids are finite.
Proof. The first properties in the statement follow from Lemmas 5.2 and 5.3, Theorems 5.8 and
5.12. The cardinality is at most countable by Lemma 3.7. The first part of the classification
for finite groupoids follows from Theorems 4.1, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5, 5.1, 5.13. The remaining properties

Conclusions and hypotheses
The UFS-groupoids introduced in this paper are a highly interesting type of algebraic struc-
ture. UFS-groupoids are uniquely described by their uniquely determined universal Fibonacci
sequence, whose combinatorial properties provide an alternative descriptive language for the
algebraic properties of the UFS-groupoid itself.
Moreover, by Theorem 5.12, no UFS-groupoid other than the trivial one-element groupoid can
be even power-associative, let alone associative. This makes many classical methods of algebra
inapplicable to UFS-groupoids. For finite UFS-groupoids, one can measure the extent to which
they deviate from associativity, following [26, 27, 28].
Definition 7.1. The associativity index a((G, ∗)) of a finite groupoid (G, ∗) is the number of
its associative triples, that is,
a((G, ∗)) = |{(a, b, c) ∈G3|(a ∗b) ∗c = a ∗(b ∗c)}|.

By Theorem 5.12, for UFS-

of interest to find explicit constructions of those groupoids that are maximally associative or
maximally non-associative.
Unlike associativity, by Theorem 5.13, for any UFS-groupoid the right cancellation property
holds for all pairs except possibly one. There are many examples where it holds for all pairs; in
particular, all UFS-groupoids with periodic universal Fibonacci sequences have this property and
are also right quasigroups. On the other hand, examination of the Cayley tables of specific UFS-
groupoids that are right quasigroups, such as those given as examples in the section on periodic
universal Fibonacci sequences, shows that the left cancellation property often fails dramatically.
This leads to the following natural questions:
Problem 7.3. Does there exist a finite (with more than one element) or infinite UFS-groupoid
with the left cancellation property?
Problem 7.4. Does there exist a finite (with more than one element) or infinite UFS-groupoid
that is a left quasigroup?
Problem 7.5. Does there exist a finite (with more than one element) or infinite UFS-groupoid
that is a quasigroup?
These three problems are successive strengthenings of one another; a positive answer to the
conjecture in Problem 7.5 implies a positive answer to the conjecture in Problem 7.4, and a
positive answer to the conjecture in Problem 7.4 implies a positive answer to the conjecture in
Problem 7.3. For finite UFS-groupoids, Problems 7.3 and 7.4 are equivalent.
For finite UFS-groupoids, we can consider a more general question about the cancellation
index.
