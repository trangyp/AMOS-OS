Abstract. We show that a finitely generated soluble group is virtually nilpotent if and only if
the diameter of its finite coset spaces admits a uniform polynomial lower bound in terms of their
size. We obtain the same conclusion for certain finitely generated abelian-by-cyclic groups under
the weaker assumption that the diameters of their finite quotients are uniformly bounded below by
a polynomial in their size. This extends the previous work of the author with Tointon.

1. Introduction
The relationship between a group’s algebraic structure and its geometry is a central theme in
geometric group theory. A foundational result is Gromov’s Theorem, which states that a finitely
generated group has polynomial growth if and only if it is virtually nilpotent. Prior to this, Wolf
showed that a polycyclic group of subexponential growth is virtually nilpotent, while Milnor proved
that a finitely generated soluble group of subexponential growth must be polycyclic. Together, these
results form the Milnor–Wolf Theorem: a finitely generated soluble group either has polynomial
growth (and is thus virtually nilpotent) or has exponential growth.
In [9], Tointon and the author introduce two notions of uniform flatness for residually finite
groups—two properties conjectured to be equivalent to virtual nilpotency. Inspired by the devel-
opment of Gromov’s theorem, this paper addresses the conjecture in the soluble case. We begin by
recalling some definitions. A sequence (Nk)k>0 of finite-index subgroups of a group G is called a
filtration if it is strictly decreasing, that is, Nk+1 < Nk for all k. If, in addition, each Nk is normal
in G, we call (Nk)k>0 a normal filtration. Throughout this paper, we denote by G an infinite group
generated by a finite subset S, and we assume that G admits a filtration. In particular, a group
G is residually finite if it admits a filtration with trivial intersection. It is straightforward to see
that every infinite finitely generated soluble group admits a filtration.

The reader will notice that we generalize the notion of uniform flatness from [9] to groups that
admit a filtration. This is motivated by the fact that a finitely generated soluble group has polyno-
mial growth if and only if all its residually finite quotients have polynomial growth; we will examine
this further in Section 2.1.
Here is our main theorem:
Theorem 1.2. A finitely generated soluble group has the u.c. property if and only if it is virtually
nilpotent.
In the Milnor–Wolf Theorem, the finitely generated soluble case is reduced to the abelian-by-
polycyclic case, and the abelian-by-polycyclic case is handled via a straightforward counting argu-
ment (see, for example, [5, Theorem 14.31]). In contrast, the proof of Theorem 1.2 is significantly
more involved. We will refine the reduction process further by reducing the problem to the abelian-
by-abelian case where the extension splits. We then divide the analysis into two classes of soluble
groups: those with finite subgroup rank and those with infinite subgroup rank. Here and through-
out this paper, we say a group G has finite subgroup rank if there exists a positive integer r such
that every finitely generated subgroup of G can be generated by at most r elements. Conversely, G
is said to have infinite subgroup rank if no such bound exists.
In the finite subgroup rank case, although the group structure is well understood, constructing
a sequence of finite coset spaces that violates (1) is highly delicate. This relies, for instance, on a
deep result of Breuillard, Green, and Tao on the structure of approximate groups[2]. In contrast, for
groups of infinite subgroup rank, the structure is more complicated, but producing such a sequence
is considerably more straightforward; in fact, we obtain a sharp bound on the diameter of finite
coset spaces in terms of their index.

2. Some group theory
2.1. The core of a group. We begin by addressing a point mentioned in the introduction section:
a finitely generated soluble group is not necessarily residually finite, yet whether it is virtually
nilpotent can be deduced from its residually finite quotients.
Proposition 2.1. A finitely generated soluble group G is virtually nilpotent if and only if every
residually finite quotient of G is virtually nilpotent.
Remark. Note that this property does not hold for all finitely generated groups. For instance, there
exist infinite finitely generated simple groups that exhibit exponential growth. A more elemen-
tary example is the wreath product A5 ≀Z. A straightforward exercise shows that this group has
exponential growth, while all its residually finite quotients are cyclic.
We will prove a slightly stronger statement than that of Proposition 2.1. Recall that the core
Core(G) of a countable group G is the intersection of all subgroups of G of finite index. A group
G is residually finite if and only if Core(G) = {1}.
Lemma 2.2. Let G be a finitely generated group. Suppose G/Core(G) is virtually polycyclic. Then
Core(G) is perfect.
Proof. By the Roseblade–Jategaonkar result [15, 12], finitely generated abelian-by-polycyclic groups
are residually finite. Since a group is residually finite if and only if it is virtually residually finite, it
follows that abelian-by-(virtually polycyclic) groups are also residually finite. Consider the following
quotient:
G/[Core(G),Core(G)]
Core(G)/[Core(G),Core(G)] ≅
G
Core(G).
The subgroup Core(G)/[Core(G),Core(G)] is abelian, and by hypothesis, the quotient G/Core(G)
is virtually polycyclic. Thus, G/[Core(G),Core(G)] is an abelian-by-(virtually polycyclic) group,
which implies it is residually finite. Therefore we must have [Core(G),Core(G)] = Core(G).

3. Finite subgroup rank
We begin by investigating the u.c. property in finitely generated soluble groups with finite sub-
group rank. An important class of such groups is the class of polycyclic groups, where the u.c.
property was studied in preceding work [9]. On the one hand, there are similarities in the approach,
as we develop tools from linear algebra and number theory before applying the strengthened ver-
sion of Gromov’s Theorem by Breuillard, Green, and Tao [2]. Indeed, while the study of polycyclic
groups involves understanding general linear matrices with integer entries, the study of finitely
generated soluble groups with finite subgroup rank will involve rational entries instead. On the
other hand, we encounter a distinct situation due to the existence of infinitely generated subgroups.
For example, the polycyclic case reduces to the abelian-by-cyclic case (see [9, Lemma 8.21 ]), this
reduction is not generally possible for finitely generated soluble groups with finite subgroup rank
(see the proof of Proposition 3.18).
We will study finitely generated groups of the form G = A ⋊Zm, where A is an abelian group of
finite rank. Since Tor(A) is a finite characteristic subgroup of G, we may assume without loss of
generality that A is torsion-free. For convenience, we define the class of groups Af.r.(n) as follows:
A ∈Af.r.(n) if A is a torsion-free abelian group of finite rank n such that it can be extended to a
finitely generated group G = A ⋊Γ, where Γ is polycyclic.

must have [Fit(G),Fit(G)] = 1, which proves that Fit(G) is abelian.

As outlined in Section 1, our strategy is to reduce to the case of abelian-by-abelian groups with

Proposition 5.1. Let G be a finitely generated abelian-by-nilpotent group that is not polycyclic.
Then there exists Γ ∈C(G) such that Γ ≅A ⋊Zm, where A is an abelian group that is not finitely
generated.
In the following proof, we will make use of the terms just non-polycyclic (JNP) group and primitive
just non-polycyclic (PJNP) group. Recall a group G is a JNP group if G is not polycyclic, but every
proper quotient of G is polycyclic. Such groups have been studied extensively by Robinson and
Wilson [14] using techniques from group cohomology [13] and the theory of modules over polycyclic
group rings [10]. A PJNP group is a JNP group satisfying additional structural conditions (see [14,
p.208]); we shall primarily utilize the structural properties of PJNP groups.
Proof. Since G is not polycyclic, there exists Q ∈C(G) that is PJNP by [14, p.208, (4.4)]. The
Fitting subgroup Fit(Q) is abelian by [14, p.197, (2.3)] and cannot be finitely generated since Q
is not polycyclic. We first show that the Fitting quotient of Q is nilpotent. Since G is abelian-by-
nilpotent, Q inherits this property by Corollary 2.9; thus, there exists an abelian normal subgroup
K ⊴Q such that Q/K is nilpotent. In particular, this implies that K ⊆Fit(Q). By the Third
Isomorphism Theorem, Q/Fit(Q) ≅(Q/K)/(Fit(Q)/K), which implies that Q/Fit(Q) is a quotient
of a nilpotent group and is therefore nilpotent. By [14, p.210, (2.4) & (5.1)], Q has a free abelian
subgroup X ≅Zm such that Fit(Q)X is a finite-index subgroup of Q and Fit(Q) ∩X = {1}. We are
done by Theorem 2.7.
