Phase-Coherent Distributed MIMO Systems

Abstract—We consider uplink frugal simultaneous localization
and mapping (SLAM) in phase-coherent distributed MIMO (D-
MIMO) systems, where a network of spatially separated single-
antenna access points (APs) coherently receives narrowband,
single-snapshot pilot signals from a single-antenna user equip-
ment (UE). In contrast to existing phase-coherent localization
and SLAM methods that rely on wideband measurements and/or
multi-antenna APs, the proposed frugal setting operates with the
minimum possible localization resources: a single subcarrier and
a single snapshot at each single-antenna AP. In this paper, we
formulate phase-coherent frugal SLAM as a coherent imaging
problem, constructing a spatial image over a region of interest by
treating the distributed AP observations as coming from a large
synthetic aperture. Based on the coherent image, we develop a
detection and localization framework that jointly identifies the
UE, reflective surfaces, and scatterers. Simulation results validate
the proposed framework and provide insight into the impact of
grid resolution and off-grid error on detection and localization
performance.
Index Terms—Radio SLAM, D-MIMO, phase-coherent, nar-
rowband, coherent imaging.
I. INTRODUCTION
In 5G and future 6G systems, localization and sensing are
evolving from auxiliary features to fundamental capabilities
[1]. Combined with the emergence of integrated sensing
and communication (ISAC) as a unifying vision for future
networks, this drives a paradigm shift from communication-
centric radio infrastructure toward systems that can simulta-
neously localize users and sense objects in the environment
[1], [2]. Within this trend, a key ISAC approach is radio
simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), which jointly
estimates the position of a user equipment (UE) and builds a
map of the environment [3], [4].
Conventional radio SLAM systems treat multipath not as
interference to be suppressed, but as evidence of physical
structures (walls, corners and reflective surfaces) that can be
localized alongside the UE [5], [6]. Starting from early ultra-
wideband (UWB) demonstrations and progressing through
millimeter-wave and 5G New Radio implementations, the
field has matured considerably [3], [5], [7]. The dominant
architecture employs a co-located, multi-antenna base station
(BS) transmitting downlink signals to the UE, from which
delay and angle measurements are extracted and fused to
estimate the UE state and an environmental map at the UE
side [3], [8]. The performance of such systems is, however,
fundamentally limited by the aperture and geometry of a single
BS, which constrains the angular resolution and the ability to
resolve specular multipath from distant surfaces [8].
Distributed multiple-input multiple-output (D-MIMO) sys-
tems, comprising a network of spatially separated access
points (APs) that observe the same UE from geometrically
diverse viewpoints, offer a natural remedy to these limitations
[9], [10]. Unlike co-located arrays, D-MIMO provides spatial
macro-diversity that mitigates the impact of shadowing and
enhances coverage [9], while the large effective aperture
formed by the AP network enables high-resolution imaging of
the environment [10], [11]. In particular, when the distributed
APs operate in a phase-coherent fashion (sharing a common
timing and phase reference), the received signals can be coher-
ently combined across the entire network, forming a synthetic
aperture of unprecedented size [10], [12]. In uplink wideband
D-MIMO systems, this phase coherence property has been
leveraged for direct, single-snapshot localization of UEs in
both single-antenna APs [13], [14] and multi-antenna APs
[15]. The idea of phase-coherent SLAM has been introduced
in [16], where joint UE localization and environment mapping
has been performed using phase-coherent uplink observations.
In light of the literature on phase-coherent localization and
SLAM, three major research gaps stand out: existing methods
(i) rely on wideband (or, multiple subcarriers) measurements
[13]–[17], and/or (ii) employ multi-antenna APs [15], [16],
and/or (iii) perform localization of only the UE [13]–[15], [17]
or the UE and scatterers [16] without investigating reflective
surfaces. Hence, the potential of phase-coherent D-MIMO
based SLAM with single-antenna APs and narrowband/single-
subcarrier observations for joint detection and localization of
(i) UEs, (ii) large reflective surfaces, and (iii) point scatterers
has remained unexplored.
To fill these research gaps, this paper studies the problem
of uplink frugal SLAM (i.e., narrowband and single-snapshot
measurements at single-antenna APs) in phase-coherent D-
MIMO systems. Specifically, we consider a single-antenna
UE transmitting narrowband uplink pilot signals that are
received coherently by a network of distributed phase-coherent
single-antenna APs. Treating the collection of AP observations
as coming from a large synthetic aperture, we formulate
the problem of phase-coherent single-snapshot SLAM as an
imaging problem that involves constructing a coherent spatial

II. SYSTEM MODEL
In this paper, we consider a multi-access point (AP) and
single UE scenario, as described in Fig. 1, where the UE
sends uplink signals to all APs via complex propagation
environment. In this section, the models for the fixed APs and
the UE state, the state of the landmarks in the propagation
environment, and the received signals are introduced.
A. State models
In the considered environment, there are N ≫1 APs, and
all APs are fixed over time. Each AP is equipped with a
single antenna with its position at xn
AP in the global reference
coordinate system. All AP positions are globally known, and
all APs are assumed to be perfectly phase synchronized among
themselves. There is a single UE that moves over time in the
considered environment, which is also equipped with a single
antenna. As we consider a specific snapshot, we denote the
position of the UE as x.
In the considered environment, there are also multiple fixed

C. Discussion
When the hypothesized location ˜x coincides with the UE
or a landmark location, the phases corresponding to the
hypothesized location (i.e., the steering vector a(˜x)) align with
those of the true signal component, leading to a constructive
combination. This alignment can potentially lead to a large
value of I(˜x), as long as the corresponding path is not weak,
i.e., (ρm)Hρm is not too small. In contrast, when ˜x deviates
from the true UE or the landmark location, the phases become
misaligned, causing destructive interference and a lower value
of I(˜x). As illustrated by the blue and red curves in Fig. 2,
I(˜x) attains higher values at the true UE and SP locations,
while remaining lower elsewhere. Therefore, when aggregating
over all components, locations corresponding to the UE and
landmarks are expected to yield higher values of I(˜x) in
principle. However, due to variations in path strengths, this
behavior is not always guaranteed.
It is important to note that the location-dependent steering
vector a(˜x) can be used to detect UEs, reflecting surfaces,
and small objects within a unified framework. This is because
a(˜x) is governed by the propagation delay τ n(˜x), which
determines the phase differences across APs. For both LoS
paths and NLoS paths from reflecting surfaces, the delay
has a similar structure, being determined by the UE position
or the VUE associated with the reflector. Consequently, the
hypothesis location ˜x can correspond to either the UE or
a VUE, and the resulting spatial image exhibits peaks at
these locations. Although the VUE is geometrically related to
the UE, introducing some correlation, the corresponding peak
remains at the VUE location. For small objects with location
xSP, the delay consists of two components: the propagation
time from the UE to the SP and from the SP to each AP.
Since the former is common across all APs, it does not affect
the phase differences. Therefore, the spatial correlation is
determined by the latter component, and the corresponding
peak appears at xSP.
IV. SNAPSHOT PHASE-COHERENT SLAM
In this section, we discuss the spatial ambiguity function,
and introduce the details about the proposed snapshot phase-
coherent SLAM algorithm.
A. Spatial ambiguity function and resolution
To characterize the intrinsic resolution of the system in-
dependently of noise, we study the ambiguity function for
distributed antenna arrays [18]. The ambiguity function is
conceptually defined between a hypothesized location and the
true location of the object. We evaluate the ambiguity function
between a hypothesized location ˜x and a target x, and define
the normalized spatial ambiguity function as

The ambiguity function quantifies how distinguishable two
candidate locations are in terms of their spatial signatures. A
narrow mainlobe corresponds to high localization resolution,
while high sidelobes indicate a higher risk of ambiguities
which would lead to false alarms for the considered SLAM
problem. Therefore, we always prefer the mainlobe as domi-
nant as possible, and sidelobes as low as possible. However,
(9) consists of M + 1 weighted components corresponding to
different propagation paths, each with a distinct amplitude.
Due to these unequal weights, the resulting spatial image
may exhibit mainlobes with significantly different magnitudes.
In particular, some mainlobes associated with weaker paths
may be lower than the sidelobes generated by stronger paths.
For example, in Fig. 2, the sidelobes of the LoS path can
exceed the mainlobe corresponding to the SP, since the LoS
component is typically stronger than the NLoS component
from the SP. Consequently, simply selecting the M + 1
dominant local maxima of the imaging function as detections
often leads to misdetections and false alarms.

V. RESULTS
A. Simulation Environment
We consider an indoor scenario with room dimensions
20 m × 10 m × 2.9 m (length, width, height). The coordinate
origin is located at the center of the ceiling. A total of 50
APs are uniformly deployed on the ceiling with 2 m spacing,
covering x ∈[−4, 4] m and y ∈[−9, 9] m. The environment
contains one UE, one reflecting surface generating a VUE, and
one SP. The reflecting surface is fixed at y = 10 m, while the
UE and SP locations can be different for different simulations.
The heights of the UE and SP are fixed at 1.5 m and assumed
to be known, so that only a 2D grid search is required.
However, the extension to 3D is straightforward. The reflection
attenuation factor is set to 0.5, and the radar cross section is
10 m2. The carrier frequency is 3 GHz, the transmit power is
10 dBm, the noise spectral density is −174 dBm/Hz, and the
receiver noise figure is 8 dB. The total bandwidth is 30 MHz,
and 30 kHz is used for SLAM. The grid spacing is 1 cm in
each coordinate. As an initial study, the number of detectable
targets is assumed known for the proposed iterative algorithm,
which therefore runs for three iterations, although alternative
stopping criteria could be easily incorporated. Performance is
evaluated in terms of the detection probability of each object,
defined as the fraction of successful detections over 500 Monte
Carlo simulations. A detection is declared successful if the
estimated position lies within 0.2 m of the ground truth.
B. Results and Discussion
We first evaluate the on-grid performance of the proposed
snapshot SLAM algorithm, where the UE and landmarks
lie on the search grid. Fig. 3 shows the imaging function
over the first three iterations under the considered scenario.
As observed, the proposed phase-coherent SLAM method
accurately detects the UE and the landmarks, with sharp
peaks aligned with the ground-truth locations. In Fig. 3(a), the
mainlobe corresponding to the UE is dominant, while those of
the VUE and SP are not visible. This is because their paths
are significantly weaker than the LoS paths, and their peaks
are masked by the sidelobes of the UE component. A similar
effect is observed in Fig. 3(b), where the NLoS paths from
the SP are weaker than those from the reflecting surface, and
the SP is thus masked by the sidelobes of the VUE. These
observations motivate the proposed iterative procedure, where
the signal contribution of a detected target is removed prior
to detecting subsequent targets. In terms of peak amplitudes,
the UE yields the strongest peak, followed by the VUE due
to reflection attenuation, while the SP peak is significantly
weaker as its path loss accumulates over two propagation
segments. Finally, the VUE exhibits a wider peak than the UE
and SP. This is because the VUE lies outside the antenna array
aperture, leading to stronger far-field characteristics, whereas
the UE and SP are within the array aperture and exhibit more
pronounced near-field behavior.
We next evaluate the off-grid performance of the proposed
algorithm for detecting the UE, VUE, and SP. Fig. 4 shows
the detection probability of the three targets. As observed,
finer grid resolutions lead to higher detection probabilities.
Specifically, at a resolution of 0.01 m, the detection proba-
bilities reach 100% for the UE and 47% for the VUE, which
decrease to 43.2% and 4.2%, respectively, when the resolution
is reduced to 0.1 m. This performance degradation is due to
increased off-grid error at coarser resolutions, which leads to
residual interference. Notably, an error of 0.05 m in the prop-
agation distance already corresponds to approximately half a
wavelength, resulting in a π phase offset. In addition, weaker
targets exhibit lower detection probabilities. In particular, the
detection probability of the SP is nearly zero, as its NLoS
paths are significantly weaker than both the LoS path and
the reflected paths from the VUE. Due to off-grid errors, the
signal contributions of detected targets (UE or VUE) cannot
be perfectly removed, and the residual interference may still
dominate the SP signal, making it difficult to detect.
However, if we assume that the signal contributions of
previously detected targets can be perfectly removed, the
detection performance improves significantly, as illustrated
by the dashed lines in Fig. 4. Under this assumption, the
detection probability of the SP becomes comparable to that of
the UE, since both targets exhibit near-field characteristics. In
contrast, the detection probability of the VUE is less sensitive
to resolution, as the VUE exhibits far-field behavior and
produces a broader peak that spans multiple resolution cells.

VI. CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we propose a phase-coherent snapshot SLAM
framework for joint localization and mapping in a D-MIMO
system. By exploiting phase information and an iterative
interference cancellation strategy, the proposed method enables
accurate detection of the UE and prominent environmental
landmarks from a single snapshot. Numerical results demon-
strated that the proposed approach achieves high localization
accuracy under on-grid conditions and provides valuable in-
sights into the impact of off-grid errors. In particular, grid
resolution and signal strength were shown to play critical
roles in detection performance, with weaker targets being more
sensitive to residual interference. Future work will focus on
mitigating off-grid effects through gridless or super-resolution
techniques, as well as extending the framework to multi-UE
cooperative phase-SLAM.
