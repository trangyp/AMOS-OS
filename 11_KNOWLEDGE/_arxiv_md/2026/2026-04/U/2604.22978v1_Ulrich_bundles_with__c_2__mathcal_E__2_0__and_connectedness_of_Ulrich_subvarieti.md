ULRICH BUNDLES WITH c2(E)2 = 0 AND CONNECTEDNESS OF ULRICH
SUBVARIETIES
VALERIO BUTTINELLI*, ANGELO FELICE LOPEZ AND ROBERTO VACCA**
Abstract. We give an almost complete classification of Ulrich bundles E with c2(E)2 = 0 on a variety
X of dimension n ≥4. Moreover, we show that there are strong constraints on the geometry of X and
we study disconnected Ulrich subvarieties.
1. Introduction
Let X ⊂PN be a smooth irreducible variety of dimension n and let E be an Ulrich bundle on X, that
is such that Hi(E(−p)) = 0 for 1 ≤p ≤n. It is nowadays well-known that, in the presence of an Ulrich
bundle, several geometric consequences can be deduced about X, see for example [Be, ES, CMRPL, LS].
A classical way to study vector bundles is through their degeneracy loci, see for example [Ful, §14].
In the spirit of the Hartshorne-Serre correspondence, in [CFK, Thm. 3.1], [LR1, Thm. 1], [Vac, §2.2]
was considered a family of subvarieties, called Ulrich subvarieties, having several special properties and
whose existence is equivalent to have an Ulrich bundle. As Ulrich subvarieties are codimension two
degeneracy loci of an Ulrich bundle E, hence their class in the Chow group of X represents c2(E), it is
not a surprise that their geometrical properties are related to vanishing of suitable Chern polynomials.
For example, as seen in [BLV, Cor. 1], their connectedness is controlled by the non-vanishing of c3(E).
The leitmotif of our work is to show that non-connectedness of Ulrich subvarieties poses very severe
restrictions on X, to the point of reaching classification results. As it will turn out, an important family
of Ulrich bundles E to be studied is the one with c2(E) ̸= 0 and c2(E)2 = 0. In the latter case, Ulrich
subvarieties move on a smooth divisor on X (as shown in [BLV, Lemma 4.5]) and very often cover X,
see Lemma 4.4. Moreover, we will be able to give several geometrical information about them.
In order to study Ulrich bundles E on X with c2(E)2 = 0, the condition being obvious for n ≤3, the
first case to be considered is when n ≥4 and c1(E)4 = 0. The latter, in fact, turns out to be stronger
than c2(E)2 = 0, as shown in the following classification. We will use the notion of linear Ulrich triple,
see Definition 5.2.
Theorem 1.
Let X ⊂PN be a smooth irreducible variety of dimension n ≥4 and let E be a rank r bundle on X.
If n ≥5, then E is Ulrich with c1(E)4 = 0 if and only if (X, OX(1), E) is one of the following:
(i) (Pn, OPn(1), O⊕r
Pn ).
(ii) A linear Ulrich triple over a variety of dimension b with 1 ≤b ≤3 and c2(E)2 = 0.
Now assume that n = 4 and E is Ulrich with c1(E)4 = 0. Then c2(E)2 = 0 and:
(iii) If c2(E) = 0 and r ≥2, then (X, OX(1), E) is either (P4, OP4(1), O⊕r
P4 ) or a linear Ulrich triple
over a curve.
(iv) If c2(E) ̸= 0, then (X, OX(1), E) is one of the cases (ii1) and (iii)-(x) of [LMS1, Thm. 1].
For examples of linear Ulrich triples as in the above theorem, see [Lo, LMS1] and Example 8.1. An
example of (iv) in Theorem 1, such that (X, OX(1)) is not a linear Pk-bundle, is given in 8.2.
Theorem 1 is also of independent interest, as it contributes to the study of non-big Ulrich bundles
[LM, LMS1, LMS2], because when n ≥4 and c1(E)4 = 0, then E is not big by [LM, Rmk. 2.2].

Next, to consider the general case in which c2(E)2 = 0. Since the case c2(E) = 0 has been already
analyzed in [BLV, Thm. 2], we suppose that c2(E) ̸= 0. As we will see below, Ulrich bundles with
c2(E)2 = 0, c2(E) ̸= 0 do not occur if ρ(X) = 1 and give several restrictions on the geometry of X.
When c3(E) ̸= 0, we can actually classify them as follows.
Corollary 1.
Let X ⊂PN be a smooth irreducible variety of dimension n ≥4 and let E be a rank r Ulrich bundle
on X. Assume that c2(E) ̸= 0 and c2(E)2 = 0. Then ρ(X) ≥2 and one of the following occurs:
(i) det E is not (n −4)-ample, E is not (n −4)-ample and X contains a linear space M ⊂PN with
dim M = n −3 such that E|M is trivial.
(ii) det E is (n −4)-ample, ci(E) = 0 for i ≥3 and E is not (n −4)-ample if r ≥3. Moreover,
X has a morphism with connected fibers onto a smooth curve, and a general Ulrich subvariety
associated to E is a complete intersection of two divisors on X.
Also, if c3(E) ̸= 0, we have:
(i1) If n ≥5, (X, OX(1), E) is a linear Ulrich triple over a smooth threefold.
(i2) If n = 4, (X, OX(1), E) is as in one of the cases (ii1), (iv), (v1), (v3) and (vi)-(x) of [LMS1,
Thm. 1] with r ≥3.
For an example that is not linear Ulrich triple but X has a morphism with connected fibers onto
a smooth curve, see [BLV, Ex. 10.1] (or Lemma 7.3). On the other hand, in Example 8.2, we have
that (X, OX(1)) is not Pk-bundle over another variety, but there are several linear spaces M ⊂X as in
Corollary 1.
Next, we consider the case of an Ulrich bundle with c3(E) = 0 (this case always occurs when c2(E)2 =
0, c1(E)4 ̸= 0 by Lemma 4.6(ii)). Except for case (a) below, we do not have a classification result here,
but we can show that the pair (X, OX(1)) belongs to some well-known classes of varieties covered by
lines.
Theorem 2.
Let X ⊂PN be a smooth irreducible variety of dimension n ≥3, degree d ≥2 and let E be a rank
r ≥3 Ulrich bundle on X. If c3(E) = 0, then (X, OX(1)) is one of the following:
(a) A linear Pn−1-bundle over a smooth curve.
(b) A linear Pn−2-bundle over a smooth surface.
(c) A hyperquadric fibration over a smooth curve.
If c2(E)2 = c3(E) = 0 and n ≥4, we have:
(a1) In case (a), either (X, OX(1), E) is a linear Ulrich triple over the curve or E is as in Lemma
7.3(i).
(b1) In case (b), either (X, OX(1), E) is a linear Ulrich triple over the surface or n = 4 and det E is
not ample.
(c1) In case (c), n = 4, B ∼= P1 and det E is not ample.
Putting together the above results, we have the following almost complete classification of Ulrich
bundles with c2(E)2 = 0.
Theorem 3.
Let X ⊂PN be a smooth irreducible variety of dimension n ≥4, degree d ≥2 and let E be a rank
r ≥2 Ulrich bundle on X with c2(E)2 = 0. Then we have:
(i) c2(E) = 0 if and only if (X, OX(1), E) is a linear Ulrich triple over a curve.
Now assume that c2(E) ̸= 0. Then:
(ii) If c1(E)4 = 0 then (X, OX(1), E) is as in Theorem 1(ii) and (iv).
(iii) If c1(E)4 ̸= 0 and c3(E) ̸= 0, then (X, OX(1), E) is as in Corollary 1(i1)-(i2).
(iv) If c1(E)4 ̸= 0, c3(E) = 0 and r ≥3, then either (X, OX(1), E) is as in Lemma 7.3(i), or is a
linear Ulrich triple over a smooth surface, unless possibly n = 4 and det E is not ample.
We point out that, while a triple (X, OX(1), E) is as in Lemma 7.3 exists even when r = 2, 2 ≤n ≤4
(see [BLV, Ex. 10.1]), we have no examples in the two cases excluded in (iv) above.

First, Theorem 2 allows to show that Ulrich subvarieties are often irreducible.
Corollary 2.
Let X ⊂PN be a smooth irreducible variety of dimension n ≥3 and let E be a rank r ≥3 Ulrich

(1) (X, OX(1)) is not one of the following:
(a) A linear Pn−1-bundle over a smooth curve.
(b) A linear Pn−2-bundle over a smooth surface.
(c) A hyperquadric fibration over a smooth curve.
(2) c2(E)2 = 0, n ≥4 and
(a1) (X, OX(1)) is as in (a) and E is not as in Lemma 7.3(i).

3. Definitions and preliminary results
3.1. q-ample bundles. We recall, and we will often use, the following definition and fact.
Definition 3.1. Let q ≥0 and let L be a line bundle on X. We say that L is q-ample if for every
coherent sheaf F on X, there exists an integer m0 > 0 such that Hi(F(mL)) = 0 for m ≥m0 and
i > q. Let E be a vector bundle on X. We say that E is q-ample if OP(E)(1) is q-ample.

3.2. Varieties covered by lines. We study here some families of varieties covered by lines. Even
though this subject has been widely studied, we could not locate in the literature some of the ensuing

A tool that we will use is the Fano variety of lines in X.
Definition 3.3. Let X ⊂PN and let x ∈X be a point. We denote by F1(X, x) the variety of lines
