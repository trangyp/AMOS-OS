Unreasonable Effectiveness of Physics in Biology
Alexey Burov∗
Fermilab, PO Box 500, Batavia, IL 60510-5011, USA
Alexei Tsvelik
Brookhaven National Laboratory, Upton, NY 11973-5000, USA
We demonstrate that the system of fine-tuning constraints for life is, in a sense, overdetermined:
the a priori probability of its feasibility is extremely low, especially in the chemical sector. This
entails that the structure of the physical laws is even more ”unreasonable” than Eugene Wigner
envisaged.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Fine-tuning of the universe for life refers to the deli-
cate dependence of the physical possibility of life on the
fundamental characteristics of the universe, “notably on
the form of the laws of nature, on the values of certain
constants of nature, and on aspects of the universe’s con-
ditions in its very early stages” [1]. This formulation of
the fine-tuning problem has a venerable history, tracing
back to early design arguments; see, e.g., [1, 2] for his-
torical discussion. Among the three fundamental char-
acteristics of the universe listed above—the form of the
laws, the values of the fundamental constants, and the
initial conditions of the Big Bang—the least attention
has so far been devoted to the first, namely the structure
of the laws themselves. To the best of our knowledge,
this attention has largely been limited to discussions of
the necessity of the weak interaction for life, a question
that remains under debate [3–5]. In this note, we propose
a new perspective on the role of the structure of physical
laws in the fine-tuning problem.
Discussions of fine-tuning usually tacitly assume that
the underlying structure of the laws admits a possibility
of such tuning in principle; that is, the system of con-
straints under consideration is feasible.
At first sight, problematizing such an assumption may
seem strange: after all, our very presence in this world is
the best evidence for existence of the solution. The ques-
tion, however, is not whether but how the fine-tuning
constraints are satisfied. More precisely, is the satisfac-
tion of fine-tuning requirements achieved solely by an ap-
propriate choice of the fundamental constants—the ad-
justable “knobs” of the laws and initial conditions of the
Universe—or is it possible only thanks to a highly spe-
cial structure of the laws, implying that this structure is
somehow selected for this purpose?
We approach this question in several steps. First, in
Sec. II we provide a brief overview of the mathematical
theory of the feasibility of systems of inequalities, demon-
strating that Cover’s estimate for the feasibility thresh-
old [6] is a reasonable upper bound for a broad class of

such systems. Estimates of this kind, though necessarily
rough, allow one to grasp the “scale of the problem,” i.e.,
the order of magnitude of the a priori probability that
a system of a large number of inequalities admits a so-
lution. This, in turn, opens the possibility of estimating
the order of magnitude of the probability of mathemat-
ical feasibility for a large set of fine-tuning constraints
imposed on the give number of dimensionless fundamen-
tal parameters of physics.
Next, in Sec. III, we apply Cover’s probabilistic for-
mulas to the set of nontrivial fine-tuning constraints that
can be found in the literature. As a result, we conclude
that the a priori probability of feasibility of this system
of inequalities is extremely low.
After that, in Sec. IV, we consider separately the sec-
tor of chemical constraints.
Narrowing the focus to
the bonds of the biologically most important quartet
of atoms—hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen—we
find that, to satisfy on the order of a hundred constraints,
we have at our disposal essentially only a single scale pa-
rameter.
In this situation, it is hardly meaningful to
speak of fine-tuning of parameter “knobs”: there are vir-
tually none.
The analysis carried out leads us to a conclusion that
may, in the spirit of Eugene Wigner, be expressed by the
very title of this work. Finally, in Sec. V, we propose an
integrated view of the character of the laws of nature, in
which the possibility of fine-tuning of constants for life
and Wigner’s “unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics
in the natural sciences” are brought together within a
unified framework.

This points to the conjecture that the Cover capacity,

However, this threshold can be exceeded when the sys-

straints become nearly redundant, being approximately
implied by others. In such cases, the effective number of
independent constraints is reduced, and the formally de-
fined capacity can, in principle, become arbitrarily large.
In our analysis of the feasibility of fine-tuning con-
straints for life, we assume that such degeneracy or re-
dundancy effects are negligible. This assumption is jus-
tified by the fact that authoritative articles and mono-
graphs on fine-tuning typically discuss only nontrivial re-
quirements that do not follow from one another. In most
cases, each requirement is associated with a distinct phys-
ical process, often rather specific to that requirement, so
that any substantial logical dependence between different
constraints would appear unlikely. As for the possibility
that the capacity of physical laws is reduced relative to
the Cover limit, this is, on the contrary, to be expected
to some extent, at least for reasons analogous to those
illustrated in the examples discussed above.
Thus, we conclude that the Cover model provides a
well-motivated upper bound on the number of fine-tuning
constraints for which feasibility of the system can be a
priori expected.

III.
FINE-TUNING: EVERYTHING BUT
CHEMISTRY
In this section, we estimate the probability of the joint
feasibility of conventional fine-tuning requirements aris-
ing from all areas of physics and cosmology, with the ex-
ception of chemistry, which will be discussed separately
in the next section.
A representative example of apparent overdetermina-
tion is shown in Fig. 1, taken from Ref. [4]. The figure
displays nine viability constraints on the masses of the
two lightest quarks, which constitute protons and neu-
trons. The physical meaning of these constraints is ex-
plained in the cited literature. The central green region
indicates where all nine constraints are simultaneously
satisfied; its smallness suggests that the very existence of
a potentially viable domain is fragile.
Note that, in addition to the two quark masses, the rel-
evant fundamental parameters underlying the nine curves
include four additional key constants of the Standard
Model: three interaction couplings and the electron mass.
If these four parameters were effectively fixed for some
reason, the Cover approach would suggest that the sys-
tem is most likely overdetermined:
for Nv = 2 and
Nc = 9, Eq. 2 gives the feasibility probability P = 0.035.
By contrast, if these four parameters are actually free,
then Fig. 1 represents only a two-dimensional slice of a
higher-dimensional parameter space with Nv = 6. In this
case, the Cover threshold is Nc = 12, and the system is
more likely to be feasible than not.
Let us now turn to a more general analysis of fine-
tuning in particle physics and cosmology. To begin with,
of the approximately thirty dimensionless fundamental
constants, only about a dozen, Nv = 12, currently enter
the known fine-tuning constraints [4, 9, 10]. Although no
single canonical list of life-permitting conditions exists,
our reconstruction based on the analyses in [2, 4, 9, 10]
suggests that the number of independent constraints sat-
isfies Nc >∼60.
Thus, the number of constraints sub-
stantially exceeds the Cover threshold, rendering the sys-
tem highly overdetermined, with P <∼6 · 10−7. There is
every reason to expect that the number of constraints
will continue to grow: the previously identified require-
ments will remain in force, while new ones will be added.
One is thus led to conclude that not only are the dozen
key fundamental constants finely tuned for life, but that
the very structure of the physical laws is remarkably
specific—effectively arranged so as to permit such fine-
tuning with only a small set of relevant constants.
IV.
ALMOST NOTHING TO TUNE:
CHEMISTRY
Let us first determine how many relevant dimensionless
constants—the “knobs” of fine-tuning—are present in the
chemical sector of the life-permitting constraints.
The chemical properties of atoms and molecules are
governed by the Schr¨odinger equation and the parame-
terless Pauli principle. The former is essentially the non-
relativistic wave equation for electrons in the Coulomb
fields of one another and of one or more nuclei. If one
adopts atomic units, in which the proton charge e, the
electron mass me, and Planck’s constant ¯h are set equal
to unity, then, to leading order in the small relativistic
parameter αe ≡e2/¯hc (where c is the speed of light) and
in the small electron–proton and electron–neutron mass
ratios, the Schr¨odinger equation contains no free param-
eters whatsoever.
Consequently, to leading order in these small parame-
ters, all chemical properties—bond energies and lengths,
as well as molecular dipole moments—are fixed up to
universal scaling factors.
In this sense, dimensionless
quantities—such as ratios of bond energies, relative sizes
of atoms and molecules together with bond angles and
other form factors—do not scale at all; they are fully de-
termined by the mathematical form of the dimensionless
nonrelativistic Schr¨odinger equation and the Pauli prin-
ciple. As Ref. [11] puts it, ”The solutions to the scaled
electronic Schr¨odinger equation are pure numbers inde-
pendent of the fundamental constants.” It is difficult to
imagine a more striking example of an overdetermined
system, with a lot of requirements and almost no knobs
to tune.

• The possibility of life requires that the energy scale
of chemical bonds, set by mec2α2

with the ambient temperature T, giving rise to the
dimensionless parameter τ = T/(mec2α2

• The Schr¨odinger equation for electrons in the
Coulomb fields of one another and of the nuclei ne-

effects give rise to fine structure—the splitting of
Coulomb energy levels due to spin–orbit interac-
tion. For light atoms (H, C, N, O), this splitting is
negligible.
• To leading order, chemical calculations can be car-
ried out in the approximation of infinitely heavy

rections for some moderately heavy nuclei, where
vibrational degrees of freedom may play a role.
Such effects introduce corrections governed by the
electron-proton mass ratio, β = me/mp ≪1.
The proton-neutron mass ratio is fixed by nuclear
physics to be close to unity with such accuracy that
any deviations are insignificant. The requirements
that the fine–structure and the mass ratio be small,
αe, β ≪1, are imposed by cosmology, nuclear and
atomic physics, see Ref. [12], pp. 167, 168.

straints in the chemical sector. Modern molecular biol-
ogy identifies 20 or more elements involved in the vital
processes of higher animals [13]. However, for the pur-

Within the quartet of H, C, N and O, one finds twelve
biologically essential covalent bonds [14]: (N–H, O–H,
C–H, C–C, C=C, C–N, C=N, C≡N, C–O, C=O, N–O,
O–O), six hydrogen bonds [15] (N–H· · · H, O–H· · · H, C–
H· · · N, C–H· · · O, N–H· · · N, N–H· · · O), and 4 · 5/2 =
10 van der Waals interactions, for a total of 28 distinct
bonds.
Biological requirements impose one- or two-sided con-
straints on each of these bonds—on their energies, equi-
librium lengths, dipole moments, and the angles they
form.
The resulting number of inequalities associated
with these bonds appears to be of order one hundred. For
the probability of joint feasibility of a system of roughly
a hundred inequalities not to be negligibly small, one
would require at least several tens of adjustable variables,
whereas here there is only one, the overall scaling factor
τ.
Note that the elements H, C, N, and O, which consti-
tute more than 99% of the atoms in the human body,
belong to four different chemical groups. Therefore, one
does not expect a significant fraction of the inequalities
associated with their various bonds to be trivial, i.e., the
system is not expected to be significantly degenerate.
In the previous section, we have shown that, without a
certain degree of life–friendliness in the structure of cos-
mology and particle physics, the roughly dozen available
free constants would most likely be insufficient to satisfy
more than five dozen known constraints. Consideration
of the chemical sector leads even more compellingly to
the same conclusion, demonstrating that approximately
Nc ≃100 nontrivial constraints in its lightest part are
satisfied with a single adjustable parameter, Nv = 1.
The only plausible explanation for such a remarkable
possibility is a highly special character of the physical
laws, allowing a plenitude of life–permitting constraints
to be satisfied with a small number of tunable free pa-
rameters. In other words, a new property of the structure
of physical laws is revealed, which, in the spirit of Wigner
[16], may aptly be called the “unreasonable effectiveness
of physics in biology.”
V.
CONCLUSION
The known laws of physics exhibit two fundamental
and widely discussed features. The first is their math-
ematical simplicity and structural elegance, underlying
Eugene Wigner’s celebrated “unreasonable effectiveness
of mathematics in the natural sciences” [16], i.e., the dis-
coverability of the Universe across an enormous range of
parameters and with extraordinary precision; see also its
further development by Mark Steiner [17]. The second,
no less striking feature is the fine-tuning of the constants
of these laws for life in all its richness, including intelli-
gent life, see e.g. [2, 9, 10, 12, 18, 19].
The structure of the laws, understood as the mathe-
matical principles of physics, has traditionally been as-
sociated with their discoverability, whereas the theoret-
ically free, empirically determined constants of the laws
have been considered in the context of enabling life; sym-
bolically, structure ⇒discoverability, constants ⇒viabil-
ity.
In addition to these two relations, Robin Collins has
proposed a crosswise hypothesis, namely that the fun-
damental constants are fine-tuned not only for life, but
also for the discoverability of the laws themselves [20];
symbolically, constants ⇒discoverability.
In the present work, we aim to demonstrate the second,
hitherto unnoticed cross-linking: structure ⇒viability.
Namely, we argue that the very possibility of fine-tuning
the constants within a mathematically simple and elegant
structure of the laws appears, in a purely mathematical
sense, highly implausible, owing to the tension between
the large number of constraints and the disproportion-
ately small number of tunable free parameters available
to satisfy them.
This idea was put forward by one of us (A.T.) more
than a decade ago, giving rise to the title of the book
”Life in the Impossible World” [21]; here we attempt to
formulate that idea as a quantitative argument.
The principle unifying all the above features of the laws
may be expressed as a minimax of complexity required
for their discoverability [22]. Were the laws simpler, life-
supporting fine-tuning would be unlikely—indeed, even
at the present level of complexity, such fine-tuning ap-
pears to be a mathematically unsolvable problem. Were
they more complex, we, emerging living sapient beings,
would likely be unable to uncover them—already at the
current level, the laws seem to have been discovered at
the very limits of the capabilities of the most gifted pio-
neers.
