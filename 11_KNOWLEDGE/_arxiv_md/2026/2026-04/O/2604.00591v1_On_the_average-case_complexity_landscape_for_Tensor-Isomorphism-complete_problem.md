Tensor-Isomorphism-complete problems over finite fields
Tiange Li∗

Abstract
In Grochow and Qiao (SIAM J. Comput., 2021), the complexity class Tensor Isomorphism
(TI) was introduced and isomorphism problems for groups, algebras, and polynomials were
shown to be TI-complete.
In this paper, we study average-case algorithms for several TI-
complete problems over finite fields, including algebra isomorphism, matrix code conjugacy, and
4-tensor isomorphism.
Our main results are as follows. Over the finite field of order q, we devise (1) average-case
polynomial-time algorithms for algebra isomorphism and matrix code conjugacy that succeed
in a 1/Θ(q) fraction of inputs and (2) an average-case polynomial-time algorithm for the 4-
tensor isomorphism that succeeds in a 1/qΘ(1) fraction of inputs. Prior to our work, algorithms
for algebra isomorphism with rigorous average-case analyses ran in exponential time, albeit
succeeding on a larger fraction of inputs (Li–Qiao, FOCS’17; Brooksbank–Li–Qiao–Wilson,
ESA’20; Grochow–Qiao–Tang, STACS’21).
These results reveal a finer landscape of the average-case complexities of TI-complete prob-
lems, providing guidance for cryptographic systems based on isomorphism problems. Our main
technical contribution is to introduce the spectral properties of random matrices into algorithms
for TI-complete problems. This leads to not only new algorithms but also new questions in ran-
dom matrix theory over finite fields. To settle these questions, we need to extend both the
generating function approach as in Neumann and Praeger (J. London Math. Soc., 1998) and
the characteristic sum method of Gorodetsky and Rodgers (Trans. Amer. Math. Soc., 2021).

1.1
Background
1.1.1
Graph Isomorphism: average-case algorithms and cryptography
An isomorphism problem asks whether two combinatorial or algebraic structures are essentially
the same. The family of isomorphism problems has a long history in theoretical computer science.
A flagship isomorphism problem is the Graph Isomorphism (GraphIso) problem, which asks if two
graphs are the same up to permuting vertices. For a long time, GraphIso has been known to be easy
in practice thanks to software such as McKay’s Nauty [McK80, MP14], while the best algorithm
for GraphIso with worst-case analyses ran in time 2 ˜O(√n) for n-vertex graphs due to Babai and
Luks [BL83]. Of course, the situation regarding the worst-case complexity of GraphIso changed
dramatically by Babai’s quasipolynomial-time algorithm in 2016 [Bab16].
Before Babai’s breakthrough, the best theoretical justification for GraphIso being potentially
easy was through average-case algorithms. An average-case algorithm for GraphIso requires that
one graph is drawn from a probability distribution of graphs, such as the Erdős–Rényi model,
and the other graph is arbitrary. Babai, Erdős and Selkow [BES80] presented a polynomial-time
algorithm that works for almost all graphs drawn from the Erdős–Rényi G(n, 1/2) model, with
improvements by Babai and Kučera [BK79]. More recently, average-case algorithms for GraphIso
received renewed attention: in a breakthrough [AKM25], Anastos, Kwan, and Moore conducted a
smoothed analysis of GraphIso and an average-case analysis for canonical labeling for random graphs
from G(n, p) covering all p, concluding a line of research [BES80,BK79,Bol82,CP08,LM17].
In the 1980’s, there were some speculations that GraphIso for some graphs could be hard, and
hence amenable for use in cryptography [BC86, BY90]. Furthermore, a digital signature can be
designed based on GraphIso following the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson zero-knowledge protocol for
GraphIso [GMW91] and the Fiat–Shamir transformation [FS86]. However, such speculations could
not be realized because of the difficulty in identifying such hard-for-isomorphism graphs. Further-
more, Babai’s algorithm [Bab16] implies that they are at most quasipolynomial-hard.
1.1.2
Tensor Isomorphism: complexity and algorithms
While cryptography based on graph isomorphism seems unlikely, many cryptographic constructions
based on graph isomorphism, such as the Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson zero-knowledge protocol for
GraphIso, can be adapted to other isomorphism problems. This is an important motivation for the
recent active research on tensor isomorphism. In this subsection, we provide an overview of the
complexity theoretic and algorithmic aspects of tensor isomorphism, and then turn to cryptography
based on tensor isomorphism in Section 1.2.
Tensors are multilinear maps, and in algorithms, they are stored as multidimensional arrays.
For now, let us focus on 3-tensors (i.e. trilinear forms or bilinear maps). Let T(ℓ× m × n, F) be the
linear space of ℓ× m × n 3-way arrays over a field F. That is, A = (ai,j,k) ∈T(ℓ× m × n, F) is an
ℓ× m × n cuboid of numbers ai,j,k ∈F.
Tensor isomorphism can be defined by resorting to elementary operations on tensors, in analogy
with elementary operations on matrices. First, note that we can slice A along any of the three
directions to obtain a tuple of matrices. For n ∈N, we set [n] := {1, . . . , n}. The horizontal slices
of A are Ah

three directions, multiply one slice by a non-zero scalar, permute two slices, or add one slice to

another. Then, two tensors are defined to be isomorphic if they are the same up to elementary

Since elementary operations (for matrices) can be represented as generators of the general linear
group, two 3-tensors A, B ∈T(ℓ× m × n, F) are isomorphic if and only if there exist T ∈GL(ℓ, F),
S ∈GL(m, F) and R ∈GL(n, F) such that (T ⊗S ⊗R)A = B. In other words, A and B are in the
same orbit under the natural group action of GL(ℓ, F) × GL(m, F) × GL(n, F) on T(ℓ× m × n, F).
Complexity. In a series of papers [GQ19, GQT22, GQ23, GQ24, CGQ+24, GQ25, GQSS25], the
Tensor Isomorphism (TI) complexity class was introduced, and isomorphism problems for several
algebraic structures, including (families of) groups, polynomials, and algebras, are shown to be TI-
complete. A key observation is that these algebraic structures can be encoded by 3-way arrays (with
possibly some structural conditions). Therefore, their isomorphism problems can be formulated as
equivalences of 3-way arrays under different group actions.
Formally, let U, V, W be vector spaces such that U ∼= Fℓ, V ∼= Fm, and W ∼= Fn. Let U∗be
the dual space of U. Let ⊗, ⊙and ∧denote the tensor, symmetric, and exterior products between
vector spaces, respectively. Then we have the following list of problems.
• Tensor isomorphism (TensorIso), U ⊗V ⊗W: The tensor isomorphism problem asks whether
A, B ∈U ⊗V ⊗W are in the same orbit under the natural action of GL(U) × GL(V ) ×
GL(W) [GQ23].
• Group isomorphism (GroupIso), U ∧U ⊗V : A long-standing bottleneck for finite group iso-
morphism is p-groups of class 2 and exponent p. Testing the isomorphism of this family of
groups is equivalent to asking whether A, B ∈U ∧U ⊗V are in the same orbit under the
natural action of GL(U) × GL(V ) with F = Fp [Bae38] (see also [GQ17, Fact 4.3]).
• Polynomial isomorphism (PolyIso), U ⊙U ⊙U: Over fields of characteristic not 2 or 3, the
cubic form isomorphism is equivalent to the orbit problem for GL(U) acting on U ⊙U ⊙
U [Pat96,AS06].
• Algebra isomorphism (AlgIso), U ⊗U ⊗U ∗: Algebra Isomorphism asks whether two (possibly
associative or Lie) algebras are isomorphic. Recall that the multiplication of an algebra is
recorded as a bilinear map f : U ×U →U. To test if two algebras are isomorphic is equivalent
to the orbit problem for GL(U) acting on U ⊗U ⊗U ∗. For commutative, associative, or Lie
algebras, we can restrict ourselves to certain varieties of U ⊗U ⊗U ∗[AS05,AS06,KS06,Gro12].
We shall not assume these conditions in the following.
• Matrix code conjugacy (MatCodeConj), U ⊗U∗⊗V : Matrix code1 conjugacy asks whether
two subspaces A, B of M(n, F) are conjugate, i.e. ∃T ∈GL(n, F) such that A = TBT −1 :=
{TBT −1 | B ∈B}. This is equivalent to the orbit problem of GL(U) × GL(V ) acting on
U ⊗U∗⊗V [GQ23,CL25].
Algorithms.
There has been nice progress in algorithms for Tensor Isomorphism and related
problems over finite fields over the past few years. To state the results, it is convenient to introduce
the length of a tensor as the sum of its side lengths: a tensor in U ⊗V ⊗W with U ∼= Fℓ, V ∼= Fm,
and W ∼= Fn is of length ℓ+ m + n.

isomorphism of cubic forms, algebras, and multilinear forms [GQT22]. Average-case algorithms for
TensorIso over R or C under orthogonal or unitary group actions were recently studied in [CEMQ26].
1.2
Motivation: TI-complete problems in cryptography
A direct motivation for our work comes from the practice of TI-complete problems in cryptogra-
phy. Recall in Section 1.1.2, TI-complete problems include isomorphism problems for polynomials,
groups, and algebras. Despite some nice progress, all the known worst-case and average-case algo-
rithms still run in exponential time. These justify the use of TI-complete problems in cryptography.
The first use of TI-complete problems in cryptography dates back to the 1990s. In [Pat96],
Patarin proposed using polynomial isomorphism to realise a digital signature scheme based on the
celebrated Goldreich–Micali–Wigderson zero-knowledge protocol [GMW91] and the Fiat–Shamir
transformation [FS86].
This work led to much research on practical algorithms for polynomial
isomorphism, usually using the Gröbner basis technique, cf. [MPG13, BFV13, BFP15]. Note that
polynomial isomorphism is in fact a family of problems, depending on the choices of degrees, a
single polynomial or a polynomial system, and so on. Therefore, some polynomial isomorphism
instance is actually not TI-complete, such as the so-called isomorphism of polynomials with one
secret problem, which turns out to be polynomial-time solvable [IQ19].
Recently, there has been a renewed interest in TI-complete problems in cryptography in the con-
text of group-action-based cryptography [BY90,JQSY19,AFMP20]. In 2023, the National Institute
of Standards and Technology of the US (NIST) issued an additional call for post-quantum digi-
tal signature schemes2. In response to this call, two round-1 candidates are based on TI-complete
problems, one called MEDS [CNP+23a,CNP+23b] and the other called ALTEQ [TDJ+22,BDN+23].
MEDS is based on tensor isomorphism (that is, U ⊗V ⊗W), while ALTEQ is based on alternating
trilinear form isomorphism (that is, U ∧U ∧U).
MEDS and ALTEQ motivated new practical algorithms for TI-complete problems from the
cryptanalysis perspective [Beu23,NQT24,RS24,CL25]. Some works led to the suspicion that
Tensor Isomorphism-complete problems seem not equally hard in practice.
For example, Couvreur and Levrat commented in [CL25, Section 2.3] that
. . . the equivalence problem seems to become much easier when reducing from general
matrix code equivalence (i.e. arbitrary P, Q) to matrix code conjugacy (i.e. m = n and
P = Q). It is interesting to observe that from a complexity theory point of view, the two
problems are polynomially equivalent . . .
Couvreur and Levrat’s main justification of the above comment lies in their algorithms for matrix
code conjugacy that work for 1/Θ(q) fraction over a random choice of matrix codes.

receive such a weak public key.
1.3
Our results: efficient average-case algorithms for some TI-complete prob-
lems
In Section 1.2, we reviewed recent progress in cryptanalysis of schemes based on TI-complete prob-
lems, which indicates that TI-complete problems could have different difficulty levels in practice
despite their worst-case polynomial-time equivalence. This calls for a study of average-case algo-
rithms for TI-complete problems that are efficient for 1/Θ(q) fraction of the inputs, while previous
work [LQ17,BLQW20,GQT22] focuses mainly on algorithms that work for 1 −1/qΩ(n) fraction of
the inputs. Note that if q is viewed as a constant, 1/Θ(q) means a constant fraction of the inputs.
Of course, with the relaxation of the requirements for the fraction of inputs that the algorithm
works on, we expect the running time to be substantially improved.

1.4
Overview of the techniques
For algebra and matrix code isomorphism, our main technical contribution is to introduce spec-
tral properties of random matrices over finite fields into average-case algorithms for TI-complete
problems.
Note that spectral properties of matrices over finite fields behave quite differently from those
of matrices over C. Let A ∈M(n, q) be an n × n matrix over Fq. The roots of det(A −xIn) as
a polynomial in Fq[x] are the eigenvalues of A. Note that it is possible that eigenvalues of A lie

1.4.1
Algorithmic techniques
Review of previous techniques. In the literature, there are two main techniques for average-case
algorithms for TI-complete problems.
The first one, used in [LQ17, BLQW20, GQT22], is inspired by the classical individualization
and refinement technique for graph isomorphism (see e.g. [BES80]). Briefly speaking, the idea there
is to examine the effect of guessing a constant number of columns of the transformation matrix,
which usually leads to determining the full matrix. The guessing of these columns contributes to
the qO(n) running time of these algorithms in [LQ17,BLQW20,GQT22].
The second one, used in [CL25] and originated from [Sen02], is to start with some global iso-
morphism invariant. After this step, one may employ either polynomial solving or linear algebraic
structures for the algorithm design. The global isomorphism invariant is non-trivial with 1/Θ(q)
fraction of the tensors which goes into the average-case analysis.
The main innovation in our algorithms. We now briefly explain our algorithm for algebra
isomorphism. Suppose we wish to test if two algebras A, B ∈U ⊗U ⊗U ∗, U ∼= Fn
q , are isomorphic.
It is natural to start with the global isomorphism invariant in [CL25], which gives a distinguished
matrix DA ∈U ⊗U∗from A over 1/Θ(q) fraction of A.
Following the individualization and refinement strategy as in [LQ17,BLQW20,GQT22], we wish
to extract more information from the canonical matrix DA. From the experience in [LQ17,BLQW20,
GQT22], one canonical matrix does not produce enough constraints on the underlying transforma-
tion matrices, and we need constantly many.
The goal becomes to deduce another “canonical”
matrix in U ⊗U ∗.
This is where our main innovation, the spectral properties of DA, comes to aid. Suppose DA has
a unique eigenvalue in Fq of algebraic multiplicity 1. The 1-dimensional eigenspace associated with
DA is then another isomorphism invariant, from which we can extract further information, leading
to relations in the matrix tuple conjugacy form of TAiT −1 = Bi for Ai, Bi ∈M(n, q), i ∈[c] as
in [LQ17, BLQW20, GQT22]. This allows for individualization-and-refinement idea to match up a
constant number of pairs of matrices.

with this unique eigenvalue would be preserved under matrix conjugation.

that any potential algebra isomorphism T must satisfy T(span{v}) = span{u}.

1.4.2
Average-case analysis
As briefly explained in Section 1.4.1, a key technical result for the average-case analysis of our
algorithm is to show that when the hull is 1-dimensional spanned by A ∈M(n, q), A has a unique
eigenvalue in Fq with algebraic multiplicity 1 with constant probability. To the best of our knowl-
edge, this question had not been studied before in random matrix theory over finite fields.
Some works on enumerating eigenvalue-free matrices. The first evidence that this happens
is a classical result from random matrix theory over finite fields. In [NP98] Neumann and Praeger
studied eigenvalue-free matrices in GL(n, q), which are invertible matrices with no eigenvalues in
Fq; such matrices are linear algebraic analogues of derangements, namely those permutations with
no fixed points. They exhibited the generating function of the number of eigenvalue-free matrices
in GL(n, q). To achieve this, one can utilize the primary decomposition to count the number of
matrices in GL(n, q), and establish a relationship between the generating function of the number of
such matrices with the generating function of the number of unipotent matrices, the latter of which
has been extensively studied (see e.g. [FH58] and [Ste68, Thm. 15.1]).
Generalizing to enumerating matrices with fixed eigenvalue multiplicity profile. Our
first technical contribution is to generalize the result of Neumann and Praeger to count the number
of matrices in M(n, q) with a given eigenvalue multiplicity profile. Namely, let ϕ : Fq →N be
a function that assigns to each element in Fq a non-negative integer. We say A has eigenvalue
multiplicity profile ϕ if for each λ ∈Fq, λ is an eigenvalue of A with algebraic multiplicity ϕ(λ).
Utilizing the generating functions of the number of eigenvalue-free matrices and the number of
unipotent matrices, we explicitly compute the generating function of the number of matrices in
M(n, q) with eigenvalue multiplicity structure ϕ (Theorem 5.3).
We then use such a counting result to prove that, with a constant probability, a random matrix
A has a unique eigenvalue in Fq with algebraic multiplicity 1 (Corollary 5.4). To see this, note that
such eigenvalue multiplicity structure corresponds to the function ϕ such that ϕ(λ) = 1 for some
λ ∈Fq, and ϕ(µ) = 0 for any µ ∈Fq \ {λ}. Therefore, we explicitly count the number of matrices
with such eigenvalue multiplicity structure, and lower bound the proportion of such matrices in
M(n, q) by some absolute constant.
While we only used a special profile in this work, we hope that the generalization to arbitrary
profiles in Theorem 5.3 would be handy in other settings.
Equidistribution of trace powers with eigenvalue conditions. However, for A to be in the
hull, a priori condition is Tr(A2) = 0. We call such a matrix A self-dual. The self-dual condition
creates a technical difficulty that seems beyond the generating function method as in [NP98] or
the cycle index method as in [Kun81,Sto88,Ful02]. Note that the probability of a random matrix
A ∈M(n, C) that satisfies certain spectral and trace power properties has been extensively studied in

random matrix theory over complex fields (see, e.g., [Vu21,JL21,DS94] and the references therein).
It is natural to expect that random matrices over finite fields behave similarly as those over real or
complex fields. However, powerful tools like the method of moments and the representation theory
of GL(n, C) are missing in the finite field setting.
Fortunately, inspired by the work of Diaconis and Shahshahani [DS94], Gorodetsky and Rodgers
[GR21] and Gorodetsky and Kovaleva [GK24] studied the equidistribution of trace powers over finite
fields using the characteristic sum method from analytical number theory. Our second technical
contribution on the average-case analysis is to extend the characteristic sum method introduced by
Gorodetsky and Rodgers [GR21] to compute the desired probabilities of matrices with both spectral
and self-dual conditions.
Note that in the literature on random matrix theory over finite fields, the results are usually
stated in the range of q →∞, or if q is fixed for n →∞. The most precise results are often taken
in the form of the convergence probability for fixed q, and the error term between the convergence
probability and fixed n. All our results on random matrices over finite fields are of this precision.
Some technical aspects of the characteristic sum method. Our first step is to show that,
in order to study Tr(A2) where A is a matrix with a unique eigenvalue in Fq, we can investigate
matrices with no eigenvalues in Fq. This reduction is presented in the first part of Theorem 5.11,
while we look into matrices with no eigenvalues in Fq in Theorem 5.12. Our goal is to show that
for these matrices A, Tr(A2) is equidistributed. This is achieved via the characteristic sum method
following Gorodetsky and Rodgers in [GR21].
