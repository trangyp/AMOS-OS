OVER FIELDS OF CHARACTERISTIC ZERO
YUTARO NAITO
Abstract. In this paper, we study complete simplicial toric varieties
admitting faithful actions of large symmetric groups. First, we correct
a recent classification result by Esser, Ji, and Moraga concerning 4-
dimensional toric varieties with S6-actions over the complex numbers
C, providing the complete list of such varieties.
Second, we extend
the study of maximal symmetric group actions to non-closed fields k of
characteristic zero satisfying a certain arithmetic condition (such as Q
or R). Over such fields, we reveal a striking rigidity in dimensions n ̸= 2,
where the maximal symmetric action uniquely restricts the variety to
the projective space Pn
k. In sharp contrast, for dimension n = 2, we
discover and classify an infinite family of split and non-split toric surfaces
admitting faithful S4-actions by utilizing the equivariant Minimal Model
Program and Galois descent.

The study of finite group actions on algebraic varieties is a classical and
central topic in algebraic geometry. Among various algebraic varieties, toric
varieties provide a rich testing ground because their geometric properties
can be completely translated into the combinatorial data of fans and lat-
tices. Recently, Esser, Ji, and Moraga [EJM25] systematically studied finite

n-dimensional complete simplicial toric variety over the complex numbers
C, and they classified the varieties realizing these maximal actions for many

However, their classification over C contains a gap in dimension n = 4.

When we consider toric varieties over non-closed

realized over non-closed fields. This motivates us to correct the classifica-

actions over general fields.
In this paper, we focus on a base field k of characteristic zero satisfying
a specific arithmetic condition ((⋆) in Section 4), which requires that the
equation a2 + b2 = −1 has no solutions in k. Under this natural condition,
we completely classify the n-dimensional complete simplicial toric varieties
over k admitting the maximal symmetric group actions. We show that while
the geometry is extremely rigid for dimensions n ̸= 2, the 2-dimensional case
exhibits a surprisingly rich structure, yielding an infinite family of non-split
toric surfaces.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Toric Varieties and Cox Rings over C. Let N ∼= Zn be a lattice
of rank n, and let M = Hom(N, Z) be its dual lattice. Let T = Spec C[M]
be the algebraic torus over C associated with N. A toric variety X over C
is defined by a fan ∆in NR = N ⊗Z R. We assume that X is complete and
simplicial.
Let ∆(1) be the set of 1-dimensional cones (rays) in ∆. For each ray
ρ ∈∆(1), we denote by vρ ∈N its primitive generator, and by Dρ the
corresponding torus-invariant prime divisor on X. Since X is simplicial, the
divisor class group Cl(X) is a finitely generated abelian group. The Cox
ring of X is defined as the polynomial ring
R(X) = C[xρ | ρ ∈∆(1)],
which is naturally graded by Cl(X) via deg(xρ) = [Dρ]. The toric variety
X can be recovered as a geometric quotient of an open subset of Spec R(X)
by the action of the characteristic quasitorus G = Hom(Cl(X), Gm).

2.3. Toric varieties over an arbitrary field.
Definition 2.2. Let k be a field. An algebraic group T over k is called
an algebraic torus if Tk ∼= Gn
m,k after base change to an algebraic closure k,
where n is the dimension of the torus. A toric variety X over k is a normal
variety over k containing an algebraic torus T as an open dense subset, such
that the action of T on itself extends to an algebraic action T × X →X.
Remark 2.3. When the base field is algebraically closed (i.e., k = k), we al-
ways have T ∼= Gn
m. As is well known, a toric variety X over an algebraically
closed field is completely determined by a fan ∆in the cocharacter lattice
N = Hom(Gm, T) ∼= Zn.
Definition 2.4. If T ∼= Gn
m,k over k, T is called a split torus.
A toric
variety X equipped with an action of a split torus is called a split toric
variety if every irreducible component of the torus-invariant divisor on X is
geometrically integral over k. For a general algebraic torus T, there exists a
finite Galois extension L/k such that TL := T ×k Spec L is a split torus over
L. In this case, the Galois group Gal(L/k) acts on the cocharacter lattice
N (see, for example, [Vos98, Chapter 3, §8.2]). Such a field L is called a
splitting field for T.
Example 2.5. Consider the circle group S1 = Spec R[x, y]/(x2 + y2 −1)
over the field of real numbers R. Upon base change to the splitting field C,
letting u = x + iy and v = x −iy yields uv = 1, which implies S1
C ∼= Gm,C.
Thus, S1 is a torus over R. However, considering the topological properties
of the R-rational points, S1(R) is compact, whereas Gm(R) ∼= R× is not
compact. Therefore, S1 ̸∼= Gm,R, which means S1 is a non-split torus over
R. In this case, the action of Gal(C/R) ∼= Z/2Z on the lattice N ∼= Z is
given by the inversion v 7→−v.
From the above facts, a toric variety X over k is completely characterized
by the following pair of data:
(1) A split toric variety XL over a splitting field L (which is equivalent
to a fan ∆in the lattice N).
(2) A faithful action of the Galois group Gal(L/k) on the lattice N that
leaves the fan ∆invariant.

2.4. Equivariant Minimal Model Program for Toric Surfaces. In
this subsection, we focus on complete simplicial split toric surfaces (i.e.,
dimension n = 2). Let G be a finite group acting on a split toric surface
X via algebraic automorphisms. We study the birational geometry of X
through the G-equivariant Minimal Model Program (G-MMP). The general
framework of MMP can be found in [KM98], and the equivariant surface
case is thoroughly detailed in [DI09].
Recall from [CLS11, Theorem 4.1.3] the standard short exact sequence of
real vector spaces associated to X:
0 →MR →R∆(1) →Pic(X)R →0,
where R∆(1) is generated by the torus-invariant prime divisors. Since G acts
on X, it naturally acts on this sequence. Taking the G-invariant part, which
is an exact functor because we are working over R and G is finite, we obtain
the exact sequence:
0 →(MR)G →(R∆(1))G →Pic(X)G
R →0.
If the G-action on the lattice N fixes no non-trivial vectors, we have NG =
{0}, which implies its dual (MR)G = {0}. Consequently, the G-invariant
Picard number ρG(X) := dimR Pic(X)G
R is exactly equal to dimR(R∆(1))G.
Since R∆(1) is a permutation representation of G on the set of rays ∆(1), the
dimension of its invariant subspace exactly equals the number of G-orbits
in ∆(1).
During the G-MMP, any G-equivariant extremal contraction ϕ: X →Y
preserves the toric structure.
This is because the algebraic torus T and
the finite group G generate a group isomorphic to T ⋊G acting on X, and
the contraction of a T-invariant (and G-invariant) extremal face naturally
induces a toric structure on the target variety Y (see e.g., [CLS11, Chapter
15] or [Mat02, Section 14-1]).
In the specific case of toric surfaces, a step of the G-MMP consists of
the simultaneous G-equivariant contraction of a G-orbit of KX-negative ex-
tremal curves. Since X is a toric surface, the contraction of a curve is a
divisorial contraction. According to the general theory of the Toric MMP
(see [CLS11, Section 15.4] and [Mat02, Theorem 14-1-5]), a toric divisorial
contraction of a torus-invariant prime divisor Dρ corresponds precisely to
removing its associated ray ρ from the fan ∆and replacing the adjacent
maximal cones with their union.
Therefore, a divisorial step of the G-MMP on X translates purely combi-
natorially into removing a G-orbit of rays from ∆(1). Running the G-MMP
terminates with a G-Mori fiber space, where ρG is 1 or 2. In Section 4, this
combinatorial constraint will strictly bound the possible fan structures of
X.
