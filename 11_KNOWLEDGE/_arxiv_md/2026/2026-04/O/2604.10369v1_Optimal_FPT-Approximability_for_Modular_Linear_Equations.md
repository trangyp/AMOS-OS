Abstract
We show optimal FPT-approximability results for solving almost satisfiable systems of
modular linear equations, completing the picture of the parameterized complexity and FPT-
approximability landscape for the Min-r-Lin(Zm) problem for every r and m.
In Min-r-Lin(Zm), we are given a system S of linear equations modulo m, each on at most
r variables, and the goal is to find a subset Z ⊆S of minimum cardinality such that S −Z is
satisfiable. The problem is NP-hard, and UGC-hard to approximate within any constant factor for
every r ≥2 and m ≥2, which motivates studying it through the lens of parameterized complexity
with solution size as the parameter. From previous work (Dabrowski et al. SODA’23/TALG and
ESA’25) we know that Min-r-Lin(Zm) is W[1]-hard to FPT-approximate within any constant
factor when r ≥3, and that Min-2-Lin(Zm) is in FPT when m is prime and W[1]-hard when m
has at least two distinct prime factors. The case when m = pd for some prime p and d ≥2 has
remained an open problem. We resolve this problem in this paper and prove the following:
• FPT algorithm for prime powers: we prove that Min-2-Lin(Zpd) is in FPT for every
prime p and d ≥1. This implies that Min-2-Lin(Zm) can be FPT-approximated within a
factor of ω(m), where ω is the number of distinct prime factors of m.
• Matching lower bound: we show that, under the ETH, Min-2-Lin(Zm) cannot be
FPT-approximated within ω(m) −ε for any ε > 0.
Our main algorithmic contribution is a new technique coined balanced subgraph covering,
which generalizes important balanced subgraphs of Dabrowski et al. (SODA’23/TALG) and
shadow removal of Marx and Razgon (STOC’11/SICOMP). In a nutshell, we relax Min-2-
Lin(Zpd) by allowing ambiguous assignments, which may map variables to subsets of values
(as opposed to only individual values). We then apply balanced subgraph covering to impose
additional constraints on the relaxation, solve it by a reduction to MinSat, and use the additional
constraints to convert any ambiguous assignment into a proper value assignment of the same cost.
For the lower bounds, we develop a framework for proving optimality of FPT-approximation
factors under the ETH. We consider a variant of st-MinCut where the edges of the input graph
are partitioned into bundles of size ℓand each bundle can be removed at cost 1. Starting from a
recent inapproximability result of Bafna et al. (STOC’25), we prove that st-MinCut with edge
bundles of size ℓ≥2 cannot be FPT-approximated within ℓ−ε under the ETH, and then reduce
the latter problem into Min-2-Lin(Zm) with ω(m) = ℓ.

Introduction
Systems of linear equations are omnipresent in computer science and mathematics [Grc11]. In
particular, equations and congruences over the finite ring of integers modulo m (Zm) are of central
importance in number theory, but also have many applications in computer science, see e.g. [Bla72,
DGH+13, DPS96, Yan02]. An example is the ongoing effort to understand the approximability of
finite-domain constraint satisfaction problems, where the approximability of equations over Zm plays
a key role. For instance, the unique games conjecture (UGC) [Kho02] and its underlying theory is
intrinsically based on linear equations over Zm (see the book by Widgerson [Wid19, Section 4.3.2]).
Linear equations over a modular ring Zm can be solved in polynomial time by methods such as
Gaussian elimination, but such methods are not suited for handling inconsistent systems of equations.
We consider the Min-r-Lin(R) problem, where one wants to find an assignment to a system of linear
equations over the ring R that violates the minimum number of equations, and where each equation
contains at most r distinct variables. We note that Min-r-Lin(R) for r ∈N and finite ring R is a
special case of MinCSP(Γ) for a finite constraint language Γ. This, and the more general problem
Valued CSP, have been widely studied from various perspectives [BEM16, DJO+23, KKPW25,
KKR17, KPSW26, OW23, Rag08].
The problem Min-r-Lin(R) is NP-hard even when r = 2 and R is the simplest nontrivial
ring Z2 [KKR17]. Two ways of coping with NP-hardness are approximation and parameterized
algorithms, but neither of them seem sufficient for Min-r-Lin(Zm). Even Min-2-Lin(·) over finite
fields such as Z2 is conjectured to be NP-hard to approximate within any constant factor under
the UGC: see Definition 3 in [KM16] and the discussion that follows. The natural parameter for
Min-r-Lin(R) is the cost of the optimal solution, i.e. the number of violated equations. Under
this parameterization, the Min-3-Lin(R) problem is W[1]-hard for every nontrivial ring [DJO+25a,
Theorem 44]. The situation is slightly better for Min-2-Lin(Zm): it is fixed-parameter tractable
when m is a prime [DJO+25a, Theorem 33] (and Zm is a field), but W[1]-hard when m is not
a prime power [DJO+25a, Section 6.2].
However, the parameterized complexity of seemingly
simple cases such as Min-2-Lin(Z4) is not settled by previous work, and this is a recurring open
question [DJO+25a, KPSW26, PAC24].
This has motivated the use of parameterized approximation for studying Min-2-Lin(Zm) [DJO+24,
DJO+25b]. Parameterized approximation is an approach that has received rapidly increasing
interest (see, for instance, [ERW22, GLR+24b, GRS24, LMR+21, LRSZ20, OPW24] and the sur-
veys [FKLM20, Mar08]). Informally speaking, FPT-approximability gives more time to compute
the output (compared to polynomial-time approximation) and the output may be an approximate
solution (unlike exact FPT algorithms). Let c ≥1 be a constant and assume, as usual, that the
notation O∗(·) hides polynomial factors in the input size. A factor-c FPT-approximation algorithm
takes an instance (I, k), runs in O∗(f(k)) time for some computable function f, and either returns
that there is no solution of size at most k or returns that there is a solution of size at most c · k.
A decision c-approximation procedure for Min-2-Lin(Zm) can be turned into an algorithm that
returns a c-approximate solution using self-reducibility; simply note that we have access to equations
x = a for all elements a in Zm. Clearly, a problem is in FPT if and only if it is FPT-approximable
within a factor of c = 1.
The currently best known bounds for FPT-approximability of Min-2-Lin(Zm) are scattered in
the literature and we give a summary below. Let m ≥2 be a natural number and let ω(m) denote
the number of distinct prime factors of m.
1. If r ≥3, then Min-r-Lin(Zm) is W[1]-hard to FPT-approximable within any constant
factor [DJO+25b, Theorem 16].

2. Min-2-Lin(Zm) is FPT-approximable within a factor of 2ω(m) [DJO+25b, Theorem 1].
3. If m is prime, then Min-2-Lin(Zm) is in FPT [DJO+25a, Theorem 33].
4. If m is not a prime power, then Min-2-Lin(Zm) is not FPT-approximable within a factor of
2 −ε for any ε > 0, unless the ETH is false [DJO+24, Theorem 45].
We obtain a full picture of constant-factor FPT-approximability of Min-r-Lin(Zm) (under the
ETH) by proving the following.
Theorem 1. Let m ≥2 be a natural number. Min-2-Lin(Zm) is FPT-approximable within a factor
of ω(m) and it is not FPT-approximable within ω(m) −ε for any ε > 0 unless the ETH is false.
We are not aware of any other example in the literature where tight lower and upper bounds for
FPT-approximability have been obtained for such a wide range of natural problems. In particular,
our result settles the FPT-status of the aforementioned Min-2-Lin(Z4) problem.
Technical overview.
Let us give a brief overview of our tools and techniques; more details
are found in Sections 1.1–1.3. We begin with the positive result. Let m ≥2 be a constant. An
instance of Min-2-Lin(Zm) consists (somewhat informally) of a set of variables V , a set S of binary
equations over Zm, and an integer k, and the question is whether there is an assignment φ: V →Zm
which satisfies all but at most k equations. Here, a generic binary equation over Zm takes the form
au + bv = c (mod m) where a, b, c ∈Zm and u, v ∈V are variables.
We simplify the situation in several ways. By [DJO+25b, Proposition 4], it is sufficient to
prove that Min-2-Lin(Zpd) (for d ≥2 and an arbitrary prime p) is in FPT. Furthermore, it is
known from [DJO+25b, Lemma 5] that we can restrict our attention to instances with two types of
constraints, a crisp (unbreakable) constraint s = 1 and constraints u = av for u, v ∈V and a ∈Zpd.
For simplicity, we further separate the latter into equations u = av where a mod p ̸= 0 (i.e. a is
a unit in Zpd) and equations u = pv. We refer to these as special instances. Of these equation
types, u = av induces a permutation of Zpd, while u = pv acts like a “coarsening” of the domain
(e.g. for m = 9, u = 3v maps v ∈{1, 4, 7} to u = 3, v ∈{2, 5, 8} to u = 6, and v ∈{0, 3, 6} to
u = 0). While the FPT literature contains many methods for dealing with systems of permutation
constraints [CCH+16, IWY16, IYY18, Wah17], their interaction with constraints u = pv causes
significant complications.
For our solution to Min-2-Lin(Zpd), we first show a support statement, starting from the
formulation of equation systems as biased graphs used in previous work [DJO+25a, Wah17] and
generalize it into a statement we call balanced subgraph covering; see Section 1.1. Informally, this
result takes an inconsistent system G of permutation constraints, with a hidden almost consistent
induced subsystem H, and returns a consistent subsystem G′ of G, with some annotations, such
that with sufficient probability G′ captures the hidden system H. We thus generalize the shadow
covering, (a.k.a. shadow removal) technique of Marx and Razgon [MR14] into the setting of biased
graphs.
With this tool in hand, our algorithm has two aspects. First, we formulate a Boolean relaxation
of the problem, where we reduce the instance to a Boolean bijunctive formula, in order to use the
powerful FPT-algorithm for MinSat by Kim et al. [KKPW25] as a backend solver. This formulation
is not precise, but it is an overapproximation of the solution space, whose solution gives partial
information about a variable value φ(v). Technically, it assigns a coset of Zpd for every value φ(v);
e.g. in the simplest interesting case of Z4 such a solution may encode φ(v) = 1 (mod 2) without
settling on either φ(v) = 1 or φ(v) = 3. This will handle constraints u = pv, but fail to resolve

permutation constraints such as u = 3v. To address this, we apply the balanced subgraph covering
tool to a collection of auxiliary biased graphs that capture the structure of these permutation
constraints. The solutions returned by this tool can then be used to add additional constraints to
the Boolean formula, in a way that allows a disambiguation step – a guarantee that every assignment
to the Boolean formula can be fully resolved into an assignment φ: V →Zpd without increasing
the number of violated constraints. Thus the MinSat solver, in the presence of these additional
constraints, acts as a complete FPT algorithm for Min-2-Lin(Zpd).
For the negative result, we first note that Zm is a direct sum of ω(m) different rings Zpd by
the Chinese Remainder Theorem. We show that Min-2-Lin(R) for a ring R that is the direct sum
of p rings Ri does not have an FPT-approximation with ratio p −ε for any ε > 0 unless ETH
is false. In the course of the proof, we define an auxiliary problem p-Split Min Cut and show
that it cannot be FPT-approximated within p −ε for any ε > 0. p-Split Min Cut is a natural
generalization of the problem Paired Min Cut which has frequently been used in W[1]-hardness
proofs (e.g. [ACd+25, KKPW25, OW23, DJO+23, MR09]). To show hardness of p-Split Min Cut,
we start from a recent result of Bafna et al. [BKM25] that Max-2-CSP parameterized by number
of variables is hard to approximate within any constant factor under the ETH, even on complete
instances, i.e. where every pair of variables is bound by a constraint. To reduce to p-Split Min
Cut, we construct dense instances of Max-2-CSP where the constraints can be partitioned into
p-cliques using Tur´an’s theorem, transfer inapproximability from Bafna et al. to this case, and then
reduce the case to p-Split Min Cut.
1.1
Balanced Subgraph Covering
Our algorithm builds on a combinatorial statement about so-called biased graphs. Biased graphs were
studied by Zaslavsky [Zas89, Zas91] and are an important topic in matroid theory; see e.g. [Oxl11,
Wal26, Zas12]. They have also seen applications in parameterized complexity [DJO+25a, Wah17].
Before we give the definitions, let us review an example.
Let G be a graph, and consider a
simple cycle C in G to be balanced if it has even length. A subgraph H of G is balanced if every
cycle in H is balanced – i.e. if H is bipartite. The problem of deleting at most k edges from
G so that the remaining graph is balanced is now effectively Edge Bipartization, which has
a long history in parameterized complexity (along with its vertex deletion variant, Odd Cycle
Transversal) [PPW19, GGH+06, RSV04, KMRS20].
Biased graphs provide a rich generalization of these notions: a biased graph is a pair (G, B)
where G is a graph and B is the set of cycles in G that are considered balanced (provided via
oracle access). B must satisfy a closure property: for any cycle C in G that is not balanced, and
any chord path P that connects two vertices of C, at least one of the two resulting new cycles in
C + P is also unbalanced. Both the edge- and vertex-deletion versions of the above “cleaning task”
(i.e. remove at most k edges/vertices from G such that the resulting graph is balanced) are FPT,
with a running time of O∗(4k), even with oracle access to B [Wah17, DJO+25a]. As a prominent
application, let Γ be a group, and let G be a graph. Refer to an oriented edge in G as a triple
(e, u, v) where e ∈E(G) and u and v are the endpoints of e, representing orienting e from u to v.
Let γ be a function labelling the oriented edges of G by Γ, where for every edge e = uv ∈E(G)
we have γ(e, u, v) = γ(e, v, u)−1. Let a cycle C be balanced if its edge labels, taken in the order
and orientation of C, multiply to identity in Γ. Then this defines a biased graph (G, B), known
as a group-labelled graph or Γ-gain graph, and the corresponding cleaning task serves as a “meta
algorithm” that captures many problems such as Edge Bipartization/OCT, Multiway Cut,
Subset Feedback Edge/Vertex Set and more [Wah17]. The setting also provides linear-time
FPT algorithms [IYY18] and approximation results [LW20].

However, we are looking at a more general task. Let (G, B) be a biased graph and let H be a
subgraph of G. For a set of vertices S ⊆V (G), let δ(S) be the set of edges in G with one endpoint
in S, and define the cost of H to be
cost(H) = |δ(V (H))| + |E(G[V (H)] \ E(H)|
i.e. the number of edges that need to be deleted to create and “cut off” H from the rest of G. We
define a domination order on balanced subgraphs of G: given two balanced subgraphs H1 and H2,
we say that H2 dominates H1 if V (H1) ⊆V (H2) and cost(H1) ≥cost(H2), and strictly dominates
if one of the inequalities is strict. We say that H is an important balanced subgraph of G if no
balanced subgraph strictly dominates H. Note that distinct subgraphs can be domination equivalent,
i.e. mutually dominate each other. Thus, we lift the definition: for a subset S ⊆V (G), we define
cost(S) as the minimum cost of a balanced subgraph H of G with V (H) = S, and say that S is an
important subset if there is an important balanced subgraph H with V (H) = S. It is known that
for every vertex v ∈V (G) there are at most 4k important connected subsets (i.e. G[S] is connected)
containing v of cost at most k, and they can be enumerated in O∗(4k) time [DJO+25a]. However, if
S (respectively H) is not required to be connected, then the situation is much more intangible.
Now the setting is as follows. Given a graph G, with an unknown balanced subgraph H of cost
at most k, not necessarily connected, we wish to cover H by another balanced subgraph H′, such
that V (H) ⊆V (H′) and H′ (in some sense) captures the cost of H. We show the following.
Theorem 2. There is a randomized algorithm that takes as input a biased graph (G, B) and an
integer k, with oracle access to B, and in O∗(4k) time outputs a vertex set S ⊆V (G) and edge set
F ⊆E(G) such that the following hold.
1. δG(S) ⊆F.
2. (G −F)[S] is balanced.
3. For every (not necessarily connected) balanced subgraph H of G of cost at most k, with
probability at least 1/2O(k2) we have (1) V (H) ⊆S and (2) there are at most k edges in F
with an endpoint in V (H).
For a set U ⊆V , refer to a cleaning set for U as FU ⊆E(G) such that G[U] −FU is balanced.
Returning to bipartite graphs, the theorem then informally says: for any bipartite subgraph H
of G, with cost(H) ≤k, with probability 1/2O(k2) the set F that is returned contains an optimal
cleaning set for V (H). The result can be derandomized by using cover-free families [BG17]. In the
case when H is assumed to be an important balanced subgraph, we can summarize Theorem 2 in a
simpler way.
Corollary 3. There is a randomized algorithm that takes as input a biased graph (G, B) and an
integer k, and in O∗(4k) time outputs a balanced subgraph GB of G such that for any important
balanced subgraph H of cost at most k, with probability at least 1/2O(k2) every connected component
of H is domination equivalent to a connected component of GB. In particular, in this case we have
V (H) ⊆S and NG(V (H)) ∩S = ∅.
This result is a generalization of shadow covering (a.k.a. shadow removal) for edge cuts in undi-
rected graphs, unifying the concept with the important connected balanced subgraphs of Dabrowski et
al. [DJO+25a]. The shadow covering procedure was originally created by Marx and Razgon, under
the name random sampling of important separators, for an FPT algorithm for Multicut [MR14],
and has become an important part of the toolbox for FPT algorithms for graph separation problems

1.2
Algorithm for Min-2-Lin(Zm)
Recall from the technical overview that it is sufficient to prove the following result in order to prove
the positive part of Theorem 1.
Theorem 4. Min-2-Lin(Zpd) is in FPT for every prime p and d ≥1.
When d = 1, the ring Zp is a field, and FPT algorithms for Min-2-Lin over a field are known
(see e.g. [DJO+25a]). The first interesting case for us is when d = 2 and p = 2, i.e. Min-2-Lin(Z4),
which has been an open problem resisting attacks with the existing FPT techniques. We concentrate
on this example to illustrate some ideas behind our algorithm. As pointed out in the technical
overview, we can focus on special instances. Hence, we only consider instances of Min-2-Lin(Z4)
where all binary equations are of the form u = 2v mod 4 and u = 3v mod 4, and all unary equations
are crisp and of the form s = 1. It is worth noting that the equation u = 3v mod 4 is symmetric, i.e.
3x = y mod 4 is equivalent to x + y = 0 mod 4, which is in turn equivalent to x = 3y mod 4.
Non-bipartite obstructions.
Before diving into the algorithm, let us give some examples that
hint at the connection between Min-2-Lin(Z4) and balanced (in fact, bipartite) subgraph covering.
We will make the link more concrete in the next subsection. It is not hard to see that Min-2-Lin(Z4)
encodes Edge Bipartization, i.e. the problem asking whether k edges can be removed from an
undirected graph G to make it bipartite. Given a graph G, define a system of equations IG over Z4
with the vertices of G as the variables and equations 3u = v mod 4 for every edge uv in G. Force
all variables of IG to take values in {1, 3}, e.g. by adding equations 2v = 2 mod 4 for all v in k + 1
copies (where 2v = 2 can be implemented as 2v = w, w = 2s, s = 1 in the restricted instances we
consider). The only allowed assignments to the endpoints of an edge are (1, 3) and (3, 1). Thus, any
assignment α : V (G) →{1, 3} of cost k to IG can be viewed as a 2-colouring of G (with the two
colours being 1 and 3) that is proper on all but k edges. However, Min-2-Lin(Z4) encodes more
complicated problems. Start again with a graph G and an instance IG, as described above. Pick an
arbitrary subset U of the vertices in G and add equations 2u = 2 mod 4 for all u ∈U to IG. Now
the algorithm may “opt out” of making some parts of G bipartite and use values 0 and 2 on those
variables. However, this requires cutting that part of the graph away from the bipartite region, and
also deleting all unary constraints 2u = 2 mod 4 for u that fall outside the bipartite region.
For a concrete example, see Figure 1. It depicts an instance IG for a graph G consisting of a
5-cycle with a chord joined to a 6-clique by the two horizontal edges. The set U are the black nodes.
One possible solution to the instance in the illustration is to delete all unary equations 2u = 2 for
all u ∈U, which costs five. There is a better solution of cost four, where the vertices of the 5-cycle

the other hand, the 5-cycle with a chord is almost bipartite (one edge-deletion away), and cutting it
from the rest of the graph only requires removing the two horizontal edges. Intuitively, balanced
subgraph covering helps us by discovering this information about the graph. However, there are

MinCSP relaxation.
To solve Min-2-Lin(Z4), we leave the realm of linear equations and consider
a more general problem called MinCSP. Let A be a set of values called the domain, and A be a set
of relations over A. An instance of MinCSP(A) is a triple (V, C, k), where V is a set of variables, C
is a (multi)set of constraints, and k is an integer. Each constraint is of the form R(x1, . . . , xr), where
R ∈A is a relation of arity r, and x1, . . . , xr are (not necessarily distinct) variables from V . An
assignment α : V →A satisfies a constraint R(x1, . . . , xr) if (α(x1), . . . , α(xr)) ∈R, and violates it
otherwise. The question is whether (V, C) admits an assignment that violates at most k constraints.
To cast Min-2-Lin(Z4) as a MinCSP, let A4 = {0, 1, 2, 3} and A4 = {R3, R2, U1} where

Biased graphs are combinatorial objects of importance especially to matroid
theory [Zas89]. To introduce biased graphs, recall that a theta graph is a graph consisting of two
distinct vertices connected by three internally vertex-disjoint paths. A theta graph is illustrated in
Figure 4. Note that a theta graph contains three cycles, each formed by the union of two out of the

cycles in G, called balanced cycles, with the following property: for any theta subgraph of G, if two
of its cycles are balanced, then so is the third cycle. For example, the set of even-length cycles in
any graph satisfies this property: if two even cycles C and C′ are part of a theta graph, then the

this property: if two odd cycles C and C′ forming a theta graph share one edge, then the third
cycle C△C′ has even length. Note that this is equivalent to the form given in the introduction: for
any unbalanced cycle C, and any path P whose endpoints u, v are in C and internal vertices (if
any) are disjoint from C, at least one of the two new cycles in C + P is also unbalanced.

Parameterized Complexity.
In parameterized algorithmics [DF99, FG06, Nie06] the runtime of
an algorithm is studied with respect to the input size n and a parameter p ∈N. The basic idea is to
find a parameter that describes the structure of the instance such that the combinatorial explosion
can be confined to this parameter. In this respect, the most favourable complexity class is FPT
(fixed-parameter tractable), which contains all problems that can be decided by an algorithm running
in f(p) · nO(1) time, where f is a computable function. Problems that can be solved within such a
time bound are said to be fixed-parameter tractable (FPT).
A parameterized problem is, formally speaking, a subset of Σ∗× N where Σ is the finite input
alphabet. Reductions between parameterized problems need to take the parameter into account.
To this end, we will use parameterized reductions (or FPT-reductions). Let L1 and L2 denote
parameterized problems with L1 ⊆Σ∗
1 × N and L2 ⊆Σ∗
2 × N. A parameterized reduction from L1 to
L2 is a mapping P : Σ∗
1 × N →Σ∗
2 × N such that (1) (x, k) ∈L1 if and only if P((x, k)) ∈L2, (2) the
mapping can be computed by an FPT-algorithm with respect to the parameter k, and (3) there is a
computable function g : N →N such that for all (x, k) ∈L1 if (x′, k′) = P((x, k)), then k′ ≤g(k).
One may, for instance, note that if L1 is in FPT and L2 FPT-reduces to L1, then L2 is in FPT, too.
Parameterized Approximation.
Let c ≥1 be a constant. Formally, a factor-c approximation
algorithm takes an instance (I, k) of a minimization problem Π, accepts if (I, k) is a yes-instance
and rejects if (I, c · k) is a no-instance. The running time of an FPT-approximation algorithm is
bounded by f(k) · ∥I∥O(1) where f : N →N is some computable function. We use the following
simple observation several times in the sequel. Assume Π1 and Π2 to be minimization problems and
Π1 is FPT-approximable within factor c. If there is an FPT-reduction from Π2 to Π1 that does not
change the parameter, then Π2 is FPT-approximable within factor c, too.

Balanced Subgraph Covering
Let (G, B) be a biased graph and let H be a subgraph of G. The cost of H is defined as the number
of edges in G incident with V (H) but not present in H, i.e.
cost(H) = |δG(V (H))| + |E(G[V (H)]) \ E(H)|.

We show the following balanced subgraph covering result, which we recall from the introduction.
Theorem 2. There is a randomized algorithm that takes as input a biased graph (G, B) and an
integer k, with oracle access to B, and in O∗(4k) time outputs a vertex set S ⊆V (G) and edge set
F ⊆E(G) such that the following hold.
1. δG(S) ⊆F.
2. (G −F)[S] is balanced.
3. For every (not necessarily connected) balanced subgraph H of G of cost at most k, with
probability at least 1/2O(k2) we have (1) V (H) ⊆S and (2) there are at most k edges in F
with an endpoint in V (H).
We also provide a derandomization with essentially the same performance; see Theorem 14.
If the procedure is successful for a given H, we say that S covers H. As an example, consider
the bipartization case where (G, B) is a biased graph such that B contains all cycles of even length.
Then a subgraph H as in Theorem 2 is any subgraph such that (1) G[V (H)] is nearly bipartite and
(2) δG(V (H)) is small, which is captured by cost(V (H)) ≤k. For any such H, if S covers H, then
F contains an optimal cleaning set for H. This fits the applications of Theorem 2 in the algorithm
for Min-2-Lin(Zpd); see Section4.3.

3.1
Important Balanced Subgraphs and Vertex Subsets
To state the result we are using, we need additional terminology. Let H and H′ be balanced subgraphs
of G with respect to B. We say that H′ dominates H if V (H) ⊆V (H′) and cost(H) ≥cost(H′),
and that H′ strictly dominates H if additionally at least one of these two conditions is strict. If two
balanced subgraphs H and H′ mutually dominate each other, we call them domination equivalent.
A balanced subgraph H of G is important if no balanced subgraph in G strictly dominates H.
Dabrowski et al. show the following [DJO+25a].
Theorem 9 (Theorem 5 in [DJO+25a]). Let (G, B) be a biased graph. There is an algorithm that
takes as input (G, B), a vertex v ∈V (G) and an integer k, and in O∗(4k) time computes a family
H of connected important balanced subgraphs of G containing v such that the following holds: for
every connected balanced subgraph H of G containing v with cost at most k, there is H′ ∈H that
dominates H.
As an illustrative example, we relate this to the notion of important separators due to
Marx [Mar06]. Let G be a graph and s, t ∈V (G) be vertices. Define a biased graph from G
by attaching W ≥k + 1 internally disjoint cycles to t, and let every cycle except these be balanced.
As Dabrowski et al. [DJO+25a] note, this defines a biased graph where a balanced subgraph H (of
cost at most k) is precisely a subgraph H such that t /∈V (H). Now apply Theorem 9 to (G, B) with
starting vertex s. Since there are no unbalanced cycles except those on t, the important connected
balanced subgraphs are induced subgraphs H = G[X] with s ∈X, t /∈X and G[X] connected, such
that |δ(X)| is minimized and X is maximized.

Marx [Mar06] defined important subgraphs as follows (for the case of edge cuts). Let G be a
graph and X, Y ⊆V (G) disjoint subsets. An (X, Y )-separator is an edge set S ⊆E(G) such that
X and Y are separated in G −S, i.e. no component of G −S contains vertices of both X and Y . It
is inclusion-wise minimal if no strict subset of S is an (X, Y )-separator. For an (X, Y )-separator
S, let R(X, S) denote the vertices reachable from X in G −S. An important (X, Y )-separator is
an inclusion-wise minimal (X, Y )-separator S such that there is no (X, Y )-separator S′ such that
R(X, S) ⊂R(X, S′) and |S′| ≤|S|. By Marx [Mar06] and Chen et al. [CLL09] we know that there
are at most 4k important separators of cost at most k and they can be enumerated efficiently.
This reduces back to the application of Theorem 9: indeed, for undirected edge cuts we can
contract X and Y into single vertices s and t, and as a result the important (X, Y )-separators
correspond directly to the cuts δ(V (H)) for the connected important balanced subgraphs H computed
by Theorem 9. This connection is developed more in Section 3.4.
We stress one difference between the result of Marx [Mar06] about important separators and
Theorem 9: the former enumerates all important separators of cost at most k, while the latter only
computes a dominating family. There is a good reason for this: the number of important balanced
subgraphs of cost at most k is not necessarily bounded by a function of k. For example, if G is a
cycle of length n and B is empty, then (G, B) admits n important balanced subgraphs of cost 1,
each obtained by deleting one edge from G. Observe that in this example all important balanced
subgraphs of cost 1 share the same vertex set. Indeed, the vertex set of an important balanced
subgraph seems to be a more robust object than the subgraph itself. This leads us to the following
definitions.
An edge subset F ⊆E(G) is a cleaning set if G −F is balanced. The cost of a vertex set
X ⊆V (G) is defined as
cost(X) = |δG(X)| + min{|FX| : FX is a cleaning set for G[X]}.
Note that cost(X) is equal to the minimum cost of a balanced subgraph H of G with V (H) = X.
For a pair of vertex sets X, Y ⊆V (G), we say that X dominates Y if X ⊇Y and cost(X) ≤cost(Y ),
and that X strictly dominates Y if at least one of these two conditions is strict. A vertex set X is
an important subset if it is not strictly dominated by another vertex set.
Proposition 10. Every important subset is the vertex set of an important balanced subgraph.
Proof. Let X be an important subset. By definition, there exists a balanced subgraph H of G with
V (H) = X and of the same cost as X. To see that H is important, suppose a balanced subgraph
H′ strictly dominates H. Then its vertex set V (H′) strictly dominates X.
With this terminology, we derive the following consequence of Theorem 9.
Lemma 11. Let (G, B) be a biased graph, v ∈V (G) be a vertex and k be an integer. Let Xv be

Let us now take a closer look at the shadow covering result of Marx and Razgon [MR14].
We note some more terminology; note that the following is written for vertex cuts. Let G be a
graph and W ⊆V (G) a set of terminals. For S ⊆V (G), the shadow of S is the set of vertices
not reachable from any vertex of W in G −S. A set U ⊆V (G) with W ⊆U is W-closest if
there is no set U′ ⊂U with W ⊆U′ such that |N(U′)| ≤|N(U)|. Note that this is the opposite
direction from how important separators are defined; indeed, Marx and Razgon show that if U is a
W-closest set, then for every vertex v ∈V (G) \ N[U], N(U) contains an important (v, W)-vertex
separator [MR14][Lemma 3.10]. In this scenario, V (G) \ N[U] is the shadow of N(U).
Their formulation of the result that came to be known as shadow covering is then the following.

hold.
1. N(U) ∩Z = ∅, and
2. V (G) \ N[U] ⊆Z.

We focus on the above basic form here.
To connect this to important balanced subgraphs, recall the construction discussed in Section 3.1.
Let G be a graph and W ⊆V (G) a set of terminals. Attach a sufficiently large number of unbalanced
cycles to every vertex of W and define all other cycles of the graph to be balanced. Let (G, B) be
the resulting biased graph. Then a balanced subgraph H of G of cost at most k is any subgraph H
(of cost at most k) such that W ∩V (H) = ∅.
To state the connection precisely, let us redefine W-closest sets to refer to edge cuts, so that a set
U ⊆V (G) with W ⊆U is W-closest if for any set U′ ⊂U with W ⊆U′ we have |δG(U′)| > |δG(U)|.
Then it is easy to see that U is a W-closest set if and only if V (G) \ U is an important vertex subset

for (G, B), i.e. H = G −U is an important balanced subgraph (of cost |δG(U)). Thus Corollary 3
implies Theorem 15 for the case of edge cuts.
We note in passing that collections of unbalanced cycles for biased graphs are closed under union;
i.e. if (G, B1) and (G, B2) are biased graphs then so is (G, B1 ∩B2). Thus, the above construction
can if desired be combined with other biased graph constructions, resulting in balanced subgraphs
being defined as both being disjoint from W and balanced in a second biased graph sense.
However, we briefly note a difference in application of Theorem 15 and Theorem 2. Marx and
Razgon use Theorem 15 to solve Multicut parameterized by the solution size, by applying a greedy
reduction to the shadowless case; i.e. they show, at a certain stage of their algorithm, that the
solution S they are looking for can be assumed to have the property that V (G) minus S and the
shadow of S is a W-closest set (for a known set of terminals W). They then apply a reduction
rule to simplify the graph by removing the shadow of S from G, assuming that Z in Theorem 15
has been guessed correctly. This greedy aspect has been highly successful for many other FPT
graph separation algorithms (see e.g. [CHM13, CCHM15, HJL+23]), but as has been noted, it
fails to apply in certain situations, e.g. in a setting of weighted graph cuts. Indeed, Weighted
Multicut was shown to be FPT only more recently, via an application of the flow augmentation
procedure [KPSW22]. A particularly interesting case is Directed Multiway Cut which, along
with a few related problems, is FPT in the unweighted setting, using shadow covering techniques,
but is W[1]-hard in the weighted case [CHM13, HJL+23].
In our algorithm for Min-2-Lin(Zpd), our main application builds on Theorem 2 rather than
Corollary 3, and our main interest is the cleaning set F rather than the exact balanced subgraph GB
itself. In particular, we use no greedy “shadow bypass” step, and our algorithm could in principle
be extended to the weighted case. We omit this, but see discussion in Section 6.

There are two randomized ingredients in the algorithm in Figure 6: the first is the procedure

combine perfect hash families with exhaustive enumeration. Perfect hash families are a standard
tool in derandomizing FPT algorithms.
Definition 30. An (n, κ)-perfect hash is family H of functions h : [n] →[κ] such that, for all
subsets S ⊆[n] of size κ there is a function h ∈H that is injective on S, i.e. h(i) ̸= h(j) for all
distinct i, j ∈S.
We will need the following result.
Theorem 31 (Naor et al. [NSS95]). For any n, κ ≥1, one can construct an (n, κ)-perfect hash
family of size eκκO(log κ) · log n in time eκκO(log κ) · n log n
Now let D be a set. There are |D|κ functions from [κ] to D. By composing an (n, κ)-perfect
hash with all functions f : [κ] →D, we obtain the following.
Lemma 32. For any n, κ ≥1 and any set D of size d, one can construct a family F of functions
f : [n] →D such that for all subsets S ⊆[n] with |S| = κ and every function g : S →D, there is a
function f ∈F that agrees with g on S, i.e. f(x) = g(x) for all x ∈S. Moreover, the size of F is
(ed)κκO(log κ) · log n and it can be constructed in time (ed)κκO(log κ) · n log n.

Hardness of FPT Approximation
The aim of this section is to prove that, under the ETH, Min-2-Lin(Zm) is not FPT-approximable
within ω(m)−ε for any ε > 0 (Corollary 42). From now on, we will formulate approximation problems
as gap problems so that we can view them as decision problems. Hence, given a minimization
problem P, the equivalent Gapc problem distinguishes between two cases, for an input k and an
instance x of problem P:
(YES)
OPTP (x) ≤k.
(NO)
OPTP (x) > c · k.
If the optimum value is between the thresholds, then there is no requirement on the output. Note
that c-approximating the solution to an instance of P is at least as hard as solving the c-gap version
of P.
Our starting point is the connection between Min-2-Lin(Zm) and graph separation problems
already observed in [DJO+24]. In particular, Dabrowski et al. [DJO+24] showed that Gap2−ε
Min-2-Lin(Zm) is not in FPT for any ε > 0, unless the ETH is false; note that this is equivalent to
saying that Min-2-Lin(Zm) is not FPT-approximable within 2 −ε for any ε > 0, unless the ETH
is false. Crucially, Dabrowski et al. [DJO+24] employed the well-known Pair Min Cut problem as
an intermediate problem, where given a graph G, two vertices s and t of G, a set P of disjoint edge
pairs (or bundles), and a parameter k, the aim is to decide whether there is an st-mincut Z ⊆E(G),
which is the union of at most k bundles in P. They showed that Gap2−ε Pair Min Cut is not in
FPT for any ε > 0, unless the ETH is false, even for split instances, i.e. where G −{s, t} is the
disjoint union of two graphs G1 and G2 and every bundle contains at most one edge from each Gi.

Conclusion
We prove that Min-2-Lin(Zm) is FPT-approximable within factor ω(m) of the optimum, and show
that FPT-approximation within ω(m) −ε for any ε > 0 is ruled out under ETH. The core part
of the algorithm is an FPT algorithm for Min-2-Lin(Zpd) for every prime p and d ≥1. For the
lower bound, we define a structurally simple problem q-Split Min Cut and show that it cannot be
FPT-approximated within a factor of q −ε for any ε > 0 under ETH.
We remark that the algorithmic result is slightly stronger, and it follows that Min-2-Lin(Zpd)
is in FPT parameterized by k, p and d. Furthermore, the algorithm can be extended to handle
the weighted variant of the problem, where the input comes with a function w : C →Z+ assigning
positive integer weights to every equation, and a weight budget W, which is not part of the
parameter; the goal is to find a solution Z such that |Z| ≤k and w(Z) ≤W. Such variants of other
FPT problems have been considered previously [KKPW25, KPSW22] and often require additional
algorithmic insights. In particular, the FPT algorithm for Multicut by Marx and Razgon [MR14],
where the shadow covering procedure was introduced, fails to handle weights due to a greedy aspect
in the “shadow removal” step. We note that our algorithm, despite using a generalization of shadow
covering, has no such greedy aspect.
The running time of our algorithm is O∗(2kO(1)) where the exponent degree is non-trivial to
analyze but we know it is at least 4. Can this be improved? The current bottleneck is the algorithm
for MinSat that we rely upon; thus one improvement would be to replace this algorithm by a
special-purpose FPT algorithm for our problem. Apart from this, the next bottlenecks are 2O(k2)
from balanced subgraph covering and 2O(k) for everything else (assuming that p and d are constants).
The following is a related question: can the success probability of the balanced subgraph
covering procedure be improved? The success probability of shadow covering, which is a special
case, was recently improved to 1/2O(k log k) by Chu et al. [CLL+26], using a result of Korhonen
and Lokshtanov [KL23] that important separators have small blocking sets, i.e. for any graph
G and disjoint vertex sets X and Y in G, there is a set of size k that intersects all important
(X, Y )-separators of size at most k. A possible generalization to balanced important subgraphs
would be the following question. Given a biased graph (G, B) and a vertex v ∈V (G), is there a set Z
of O(k) edges such that every important connected subset S in (G, B) with v ∈S has δ(S) ∩Z ̸= ∅?
This is an intriguing possibility, which could improve the success probability of balanced subgraph
covering to 1/2O(k log k).
For a much more ambitious research direction, the problems we study are instances of the problem
class MinCSP(Γ) for finite-domain constraint languages Γ. It is natural to ask for which languages
Γ the problem MinCSP(Γ) admits an FPT algorithm, or a constant-factor FPT-approximation.
While this is fully settled in the Boolean domain [BELM16, KKPW25], in the more general setting
the question is very much open; only preliminary or ad-hoc cases are known (see [KPSW26] for an
overview). For constant-factor FPT-approximation, it would be particularly interesting to obtain
matching upper and lower bounds for broader classes of problems than those studied in this paper.
With the MinCSP motivation in mind, perhaps the easiest extension of Min-2-Lin that our
algorithm does not cover is the conservative, a.k.a. list case of Min-2-Lin(Zm), where in addition
every variable v has a list of values Dv ⊆Zm that it is allowed to take. These can be treated as
unary constraints, or as a separate part of the problem formulation; this makes no difference to the
problem complexity. Is List Min-2-Lin(Zpd) FPT?
Finally, our results for p-Split Min Cut can be used to prove optimality of constant factors
achieved by FPT-approximation algorithms, as exemplified by the case of Min-2-Lin(Zm). For
example, they directly imply that the approximation factor of 2 in [DJO+23, Theorem 10] cannot be
improved under the ETH. Many more W[1]-hardness reductions in the literature can be rethought as
