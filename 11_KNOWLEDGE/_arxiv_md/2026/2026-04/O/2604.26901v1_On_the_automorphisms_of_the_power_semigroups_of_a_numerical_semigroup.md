ON THE AUTOMORPHISMS
OF THE POWER SEMIGROUPS OF A NUMERICAL SEMIGROUP
SALVATORE TRINGALI AND KEROU WEN
Abstract. If H is a numerical semigroup (that is, a cofinite subset of the non-negative integers closed
under addition), then the non-empty subsets of H form a semigroup P(H) under the sumset operation
induced by addition in H. Moreover, if 0 ∈H, then P(H) is a monoid with identity element {0}, and
the family P0(H) of all subsets of H containing 0 is a submonoid of P(H).
We show that the automorphism group of P(H) is trivial, and the same holds for P0(H) when 0 ∈H.
The proofs blend ideas from combinatorics and semigroup theory.
1. Introduction
Let H be a multiplicatively written semigroup (see the end of this section for notation and terminology).
Endowed with the (binary) operation of setwise multiplication induced by H, the collection of all non-
empty subsets of H forms a semigroup in its own right, hereinafter denoted by P(H). The family Pfin(H)
of all non-empty finite subsets of H is a subsemigroup of P(H). More explicitly, the operation is defined
by
XY := {xy: x ∈X, y ∈Y },
for all X, Y ∈P(H).
We call P(H) and Pfin(H) the large power semigroup and the finitary power semigroup of H, respectively.
If, in particular, H is a monoid with neutral element 1H, then P(H) and Pfin(H) are themselves
monoids, their neutral element being the singleton {1H}. Furthermore, the families
P1(H) := {X ⊆H : 1H ∈X}
and
Pfin,1(H) := P1(H) ∩Pfin(H)
are submonoids of P(H) and Pfin(H), respectively. We refer to P1(H) and Pfin,1(H) as the reduced large
power monoid and the reduced finitary power monoid of H, respectively.
Remark 1.1. If the semigroup H is written additively, we adopt the same convention for any subsemi-
group of its large power semigroup P(H). This amounts, in practice, to the fact that the operation on
P(H) maps a pair (X, Y ) of non-empty subsets of H to their sumset
X + Y := {x + y : x ∈X, y ∈Y },
as opposed to the setwise product of X by Y that would be used in the multiplicative setting.

For more details, let a numerical semigroup be a cofinite subsemigroup H of N (the additive monoid
of non-negative integers). If 0 ∈H, we call H a numerical monoid. Being among the simplest examples
of finitely generated cancellative commutative semigroups, numerical semigroups have been the subject
of extensive research: they are intertwined with classical questions in number theory (e.g., the Frobenius
coin problem); they play an important role in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry (e.g., in
connection with the study of coordinate rings); and they provide a natural testing ground for conjectures
and techniques in factorization theory and semigroup theory. For additional information, the reader may
consult the monograph [3], where, however, the term “numerical semigroup” is reserved for what we call
a numerical monoid.
In the aftermath of their proof [25, Theorem 2.5] of a conjecture by Bienvenu and Geroldinger [5,
Conjecture 4.7], Tringali and Yan have established [24, Theorem 3.2] that the automorphism group of
the reduced finitary power monoid Pfin,0(N) of the additive monoid of non-negative integers is cyclic of
order two. As a follow-up, Tringali and Wen [22, Theorem 3.5] have shown that the automorphism group
of the finitary power monoid of Z (the additive group of integers) is isomorphic to the direct product of
a cyclic group of order two and the infinite dihedral group, in stark contrast with the fact that the only
non-trivial automorphism of the same group is the map x 7→−x. In a remarkable feat, Rago [16, Theorem
13] has subsequently proved that, for a finite abelian group G, the automorphism group of Pfin,1(G) is
(naturally) isomorphic to Aut(G), except when G is the Klein four-group, in which case Aut(Pfin,1(G))
is isomorphic to the direct product of two copies of the symmetric group of degree three [16, Example
2]. Lastly, Wong et al. [26, Theorem 1.3] have recently established that the automorphism group of the
finitary power semigroup Pfin(S) of a numerical semigroup S is trivial unless S = Jk, ∞K for some k ∈N,
in which case Aut(Pfin(S)) is cyclic of order 2.
The present article adds to this line of research. One of its main novelties is that we investigate, for
the first time, automorphisms of infinitary power semigroups, that is, power semigroups involving both
finite and infinite sets. This setting gives rise to many new challenges, as several arguments (e.g., of a
combinatorial nature) that work in the finitary framework break down in a critical way.
Plan of the paper. Sec. 2 contains some preliminaries that may be of independent interest. In particu-
lar, we obtain in Theorem 2.3 that, for all cancellative monoids H and K, every isomorphism from P1(H)
to P1(K) restricts to an isomorphism from Pfin,1(H) to Pfin,1(K). Furthermore, we show in Theorem 2.5
that, for all cancellative commutative semigroups H and K, every isomorphism from P(H) to P(K)
restricts to an isomorphism from Pfin(H) to Pfin(K).
Secs. 3 and 4 are devoted to our main results. We use Theorem 2.3 to show that the automorphism
group of the reduced large power monoid of a numerical monoid is trivial (Theorem 3.3), and Theorem 2.5
to show that the same holds for the large power semigroup of a numerical semigroup (Theorem 4.3).
Finally, Sec. 5 offers some directions for future research. Most notably, we propose a couple of conjec-
tures on how to extend the results of the present work far beyond the numerical setting.
The proofs blend ideas from combinatorics and semigroup theory, and we have striven to make them
mostly self-contained. The only exceptions are Lemma 3.1 and Lemma 4.1.

Notation and terminology. We use N for the additive monoid of non-negative integers, and Z for the
additive group of integers. For a, b ∈Z∪{±∞}, we let Ja, bK := {x ∈Z : a ≤x ≤b} and Z≥a := Ja, +∞K.
The Frobenius number of a numerical semigroup H is the maximum element of the complement of H
in Z (this maximum exists because H is, by definition, a cofinite subset of N).
We refer to [13] for background on semigroups and monoids. In particular, we denote by Aut(H) the
automorphism group of a semigroup H and, unless H is a numerical semigroup or the power semigroup
of a numerical semigroup (see also Remark 1.1), we write H multiplicatively. An element a ∈H is right
(resp., left) cancellative if the map H →H : x 7→xa (resp., H →H : x 7→ax) is injective. The semigroup
itself is cancellative if each of its elements is right and left cancellative.
Further notation and terminology, if not defined, is standard or should be clear from the context. In
particular, every homomorphism throughout this work is a semigroup homomorphism (even if its domain
and codomain are monoids), unless otherwise specified.
2. Preliminary results
Below, we collect some preliminary results that will be needed in Secs. 3 and 4 to prove the main
theorems of the paper.
We begin with a couple of lemmas implying that any isomorphism between
the reduced large power monoids of two cancellative monoids restricts to an isomorphism between the
corresponding reduced finitary power monoids (Theorem 2.3).
Lemma 2.1. Let A and B be non-empty subsets of a semigroup H. If A contains a right cancellative
element a ∈H and B is finite, then the equation XA = B has finitely many solutions X over P(H).
Proof. If XA = B for some X ∈P(H), then Xa ⊆B. On the other hand, the map P(H) →P(H): X 7→
Xa is injective, because a is a right cancellative element of H, and hence {a} is right cancellative in P(H)
(see [21, Remark 1] for details, if necessary). Since B is finite (by hypothesis) and a finite set has finitely
many subsets, it follows that the equation XA = B has finitely many solutions X over P(H).

Theorem 2.3. If H and K are cancellative monoids, then every isomorphism f : P1(H) →P1(K) maps
Pfin,1(H) bijectively onto Pfin,1(K), and hence restricts to an isomorphism Pfin,1(H) →Pfin,1(K).
Proof. Let A ∈Pfin,1(H). Since H is cancellative, we gather from Lemma 2.1 that the equation XA = A3
has finitely many solutions X over P1(H). Because f is an isomorphism from P1(H) to P1(K), it follows
that the equation Y f(A) = f(A)3 has finitely many solutions Y over P1(K). On the other hand, Lemma
2.2 guarantees that the same equation has at least 2|f(A)|−1 solutions Y over P1(K). Consequently, |f(A)|
is finite, and hence f(A) ∈Pfin,1(K). In other words, we have that f[Pfin,1(H)] is contained in Pfin,1(K),
and for the reverse inclusion it is now sufficient to apply the same argument to the (functional) inverse
f −1 of f and to consider that f −1 is an isomorphism from P1(K) to P1(H).
In fact, since K is also a cancellative monoid, we can apply the previous argument to f −1. As a result,
we find that f −1[Pfin,1(K)] ⊆Pfin,1(H), and therefore Pfin,1(K) ⊆f[Pfin,1(H)].

We conclude the section by establishing sufficient conditions under which an automorphism of P(H)
or P1(H), for an arbitrary monoid H, is trivial. We begin with a simple but crucial observation that
allows us to convert a (purely) set-theoretic condition into an algebraic one.
Remark 2.6. Let H be a monoid and E be a subset of H such that 1H ∈E = E2. If an element y ∈H
lies in E, then E ⊆{1H, y}E ⊆E2 = E. Conversely, if {1H, y}E = E, then y = y1H ∈{1H, y}E = E.

3. The automorphisms of P0(H) when H is a numerical monoid
In this short section, we prove the first main result of the paper. Before turning to the proof, we need
two more lemmas, the first of which relies, via the work of Tringali and Yan [25], on a classical theorem
of Nathanson [15], sometimes referred to as the Fundamental Theorem of Additive Number Theory.
Lemma 3.1. If H is a numerical monoid and f is an automorphism of P0(H), then {0, x} is a fixed
point of f for every x ∈H.
Proof. Since numerical monoids are cancellative, Theorem 2.3 guarantees that f restricts to an automor-
phism of Pfin,0(H). By [25, Lemma 2.4], this implies the existence of an automorphism g of H such that
f({0, x}) = {0, g(x)} for all x ∈H. However, it is folklore that the automorphism group of a numerical
semigroup is trivial [12, Theorem 3]. Hence, g is the identity map on H, which in turn implies that {0, x}
is a fixed point of f for each x ∈H.

4. The automorphisms of P(S) when S is a numerical semigroup
In this final section, we prove the second main result of the paper. To begin, we establish a couple
more lemmas, the first of which relies on a theorem of Tringali [21, Theorem 1] that applies, in particular,
to the large power semigroup of any cancellative commutative semigroup.
Lemma 4.1. If H is a numerical semigroup and f is an automorphism of P(H), then the singleton {x}
is a fixed point of f for each x ∈H.
Proof. Numerical semigroups are cancellative and commutative. Hence, we gather from [21, Theorem 1]
that f maps one-element sets bijectively onto one-element sets, that is, there exists an automorphism g
of H such that f({x}) = {g(x)} for all x ∈H. Since the automorphism group of H is trivial (as already
noted in the proof of Lemma 3.1), it follows that every one-element subset of H is fixed by f.

The last ingredient we need is provided by the definition of a certain (binary) relation that makes it
possible to link the automorphism group of the large power semigroup of a numerical semigroup to that
of the reduced large power monoid P0(N) of the numerical monoid N.
More precisely, let Q be a commutative semigroup (written multiplicatively), and let T be a subsemi-
group of Q. Given a, b ∈Q, we say that a is a T-conjugate of b (in Q) if either a = b or ax = by for some
x, y ∈T. Denote by ∼T the relation on Q defined by declaring a ∼T b if and only if a is a T-conjugate
of b. It is routine to check that ∼T is a semigroup congruence on Q, hereby referred to as the relation of
T-conjugacy on Q. Accordingly, we call the factor semigroup Q/∼T the T-conjugate quotient of Q, and
the set {y ∈Q : x ∼T y} the ∼T -class of an element x ∈Q.
When Q is a (commutative) monoid and T is the unit group of Q, the quotient Q/∼T is a familiar
object of study in factorization theory [10, p. xiii]. Here we focus instead on the case when Q is the large
power semigroup of a numerical semigroup and T is the set of its cancellative elements. (It is a basic fact
that the cancellative elements of a semigroup form a subsemigroup.)

5. Prospects for future research
We have found that the automorphism group of the reduced large power monoid of a numerical monoid
is trivial (Theorem 3.3), and that the same holds for the automorphism group of the large power semigroup
of a numerical semigroup (Theorem 4.3). There is some evidence that such “rigidity phenomena” may
extend far beyond the numerical setting. In this direction, several of the results established in Sec. 2
appear likely to play a useful role in future investigations.
More concretely, let H be a semigroup, and let Φ be the function Aut(H) 7→Aut(P(H)) that sends
an automorphism g of H to its augmentation, namely, the automorphism g∗of P(H) given by
g∗(X) := {g(x) : x ∈X},
for all X ∈P(H).
It is easily verified that Φ is an injective group homomorphism, see, for instance, [9, Sec. 1]. However,
in general, Φ need not be an isomorphism. Accordingly, we call an automorphism f of P(H) inner if it
is the augmentation of an automorphism of H (that is, if f belongs to the image of Φ), and we say that
Aut(P(H)) is canonically isomorphic to Aut(H) when Φ is surjective.
If, on the other hand, H is a monoid, then the augmentation of an automorphism of H restricts to
an automorphism of P1(H), and hence Φ induces an injective group homomorphism Φ1 : Aut(H) →
Aut(P1(H)). Again, we say that an automorphism of P1(H) is inner if it belongs to the image of Φ1.
Conjecture 5.1. The automorphisms of the large power monoid P(Z) of Z (the additive group of
integers) are all inner, and so are the automorphisms of the reduced large power monoid P0(Z) of Z.
In particular, it would follow from Conjecture 5.1 that Aut(P(Z)) and Aut(P0(Z)) are both cyclic
groups of order two, since the only non-trivial automorphism of Z is the map x 7→−x.
There is in fact some evidence that Conjecture 5.1 holds in much greater generality for large classes
of cancellative commutative monoids. A key step in this direction would be provided by the following:
Conjecture 5.2. If H is a cancellative monoid, then every automorphism of P(H) maps P1(H) bijec-
tively onto itself and therefore restricts to an automorphism of P1(H).
