ORE’S THEOREM FOR THICK SUBCATEGORIES
SIRA GRATZ AND GREG STEVENSON
Abstract. We characterize those finite groups for which the bounded derived cate-
gory of finite dimensional representations over an algebraically closed field of character-
istic p has distributive lattice of thick subcategories: they are precisely the p-nilpotent
groups. Along the way we give necessary and sufficient criteria for the bounded derived
category and perfect complexes of a finite dimensional k-algebra to have distributive
lattices of thick subcategories.
1. Introduction
Given an object one can consider its collection of subobjects. In general, these subob-
jects form a lattice and it is natural to wonder how properties of this lattice reflect the
properties of the original object. Many instances of this question have been considered
over the years. For instance, in 1938 Ore proved that the collection of subgroups of a
finite group G is a distributive lattice precisely when G is a cyclic group [13].
The purpose of this note is to consider what we can learn from distributivity in the
derived setting. We replace the group G by one of the bounded derived category Db(kG),
the perfect complexes Dperf(kG), or the stable category mod kG, where k is an algebraically
closed field of characteristic p, and we study the lattice of thick subcategories in each case.
It turns out that these lattices are distributive more often than the lattice of subgroups,
but it is still a quite restrictive condition. The analogue of Ore’s theorem is:
Theorem (Corollary 5.3). Let G be a finite group and k an algebraically closed field of
characteristic p. Then the following are equivalent:
(1) G is p-nilpotent;
(2) The lattice of thick subcategories of Db(kG) is distributive;
(3) The lattice of thick subcategories of Dperf(kG) is distributive;
The situation for mod kG is more involved and we give some examples and prove that the
lattice of thick subcategories is distributive in several examples which are not p-nilpotent.
The theorem follows from an analysis of distributivity for finite dimensional algebras.
This turns out to be an extremely restrictive characterization as the following theorem,
which summarises the results of Corollaries 3.2 and 5.2, shows.
Theorem. Let A be a finite dimensional k-algebra. Then the lattice of thick subcategories
of Db(A) (respectively Dperf(A)) is distributive if and only if A is Morita equivalent to a
product of local algebras Ai such that each Db(Ai) (respectively Dperf(Ai)) has a distributive
lattice of thick subcategories.
The article also includes a number of examples and we pose several questions.

2. Notation
For a triangulated category T we denote by Thick(T) the complete lattice of thick
subcategories of T. The meet ∧is given by intersection and the join ∨by the smallest
thick subcategory containing the union of the thick subcategories in question. For X ∈T
we let thick(X) be the smallest thick subcategory containing X. Further details can be
found in various places, for instance [10].
Throughout we work over an algebraically closed field k (often of characteristic p where
p divides the order of some finite group G). We denote by A a finite dimensional k-algebra
which, when convenient, we assume is basic as we are only concerned with properties that
are Morita-invariant. Thus A can be represented as a quotient of a path algebra. We
denote by J the radical rad(A). We use Db(A) to denote the bounded derived category
of finite dimensional A-modules and Dperf(A) to denote the thick subcategory of perfect
complexes.

4. An application to modular representation theory
In this section we fix a field k of characteristic p and use Theorem 3.1 to characterise
those finite groups G for which Thick(Db(kG)) is a distributive lattice. In order to prove
our result we need a little preparation concerning block theory.
A group G is said to be p-nilpotent if it has a normal p-complement, i.e. G is of the form
N ⋊P where N has order prime to p and P is a p-group (and hence a p-Sylow subgroup
of G). We can understand the blocks of kG as follows using [4, Lemma 3.1]. If V is a
simple module for the semisimple algebra kN with corresponding central idempotent e
the situtation is controlled by the inertial subgroup
IG(V ) = {g ∈G | V g ∼= V }
where G acts on N and hence on mod kN by conjugation. The idempotent e gives a block
b of kIG(V ) which is Morita equivalent to a corresponding block B of kG. If IG(V ) = G
then b = B is Morita equivalent to kP.
If IG(V ) is a proper subgroup of G then we need to understand the block b. In order
to do this, it’s enough to observe that IG(V ) = N ⋊Q is itself p-nilpotent with the same
p-complement N as we obviously have N  IG(V ). Applying the same argument, for the
simple kN-module V with idempotent e, to kIG(V ) we are in the case where the inertial
group is all of IG(V ) so b, and hence B, is Morita equivalent to kQ.
In summary, every block of kG is Morita equivalent to a group algebra of a p-group.
From this we immediately deduce the following result.
Proposition 4.1. If G is p-nilpotent then Thick(Db(kG)) is distributive.
Proof. By the discussion above kG decomposes, up to Morita equivalence, into a product
of group algebras kQi where each Qi is a p-group. In particular, kQi is local and so
Db(kQi) is generated by its trivial module. We know Db(kQi) is a rigid tt-category and
so the lattice of thick tensor ideals is a coherent frame and a fortiori distributive. Because
the trivial module, which is the unit for the tensor product, is a generator it follows that
every thick subcategory is an ideal and so Thick(Db(kQi)) is distributive.

Thick(Db(kG)) is distributive if and only if G is p-nilpotent.

Proof. The proposition we just proved gives one direction. So suppose, on the other hand,
that Thick(Db(kG)) is distributive. By Corollary 3.2 it follows that each block of kG is
Morita equivalent to a local ring and so, in particular, this is true for the principal block

It follows that if G is p-nilpotent then the stable category
mod kG ∼= Db(kG)/Dperf(kG)
also has distributive lattice of thick subcategories. However, the converse is not true:
distributivity of Thick(mod kG) is a weaker condition and can hold without G being p-
nilpotent.
Example 4.3. Let k be an algebraically closed field of characteristic 3 and consider the
group algebra kS3. This algebra is connected and has two simples and so by Corollary 3.2
the lattice of thick subcategories of Db(kG) is not distributive. This is also visible from
Theorem 4.2 as S3 is not 3-nilpotent.
On the other hand kS3 has finite representation type and the indecomposable non-
projective modules form a single orbit under taking syzygies. Thus the trivial module
k generates mod kS3 and so the lattice of thick subcategories is distributive (or, if one
prefers, it is distributive for the more pedestrian reason that the only thick subcategories
of mod kS3 are 0 and itself).
Remark 4.4. For any finite group G we know that Thick⊗(Db(kG)), the lattice of
thick tensor ideals, is distributive (this is in some sense the fundamental theorem of
tt-geometry). So the obvious way in which Thick(Db(kG)) can be distributive is for every
thick subcategory to be an ideal. This happens precisely when kG is local i.e. when G is
a p-group.

Corollary 5.3. Let G be a finite group and k an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p. The following are equivalent:
(1) G is p-nilpotent;
(2) Thick(Db(kG)) is a distributive lattice;
(3) Thick(Dperf(kG)) is a distributive lattice.
Proof. We have already shown in Theorem 4.2 that (1) and (2) are equivalent. It is clear
that (2) implies (3). If Thick(Dperf(kG)) is distributive then Corollary 5.2 tells us that
the principal block is local and hence, as in the argument for Theorem 4.2, the group G
is p-nilpotent.
□

6. Aside I: Regularity
It’s also interesting to look at the structure of individual thick subcategories and how
this reflects or controls properties of the ambient category. For example, the following
result yields another (well-known) characterisation of p-nilpotent groups.

have T = thickn(G) for some G ∈T and n ≥0.

7. Aside II: Commutative rings
We mentioned the result of Ore that the subgroups of a finite group form a distributive
lattice precisely for the cyclic groups. The analogous condition has also been studied in
commutative algebra: a commutative ring is called arithmetical if its lattice of ideals is
distributive. These rings seem to have been introduced by Fuchs [8] in 1949. In the case
of domains these are precisely the Pr¨ufer domains and there are myriad characterizations
of such rings.
So, as we were motivated by Ore, one should also be motivated by Fuchs.
Let R
be a commutative ring. The case of the perfect complexes is interesting, but does not
characterise any special class of rings. The following theorem is well-known to a certain
crowd.
Theorem 7.1. The lattice Thick(Dperf(R)) is distributive.
Proof. We are essentially in the situation of Remark 4.4. There is a symmetric monoidal
structure compatible with the triangulated structure on Dperf(R) with unit object R and
Dperf(R) = thick(R). It follows that the lattice of thick subcategories coincides with the
lattice of radical tensor ideals and hence is distributive. (See [10] for further details and
references.)
□
Distributivity for thick subcategories of the bounded derived category on the other hand
is not at all well understood (see the discussion at the start of the next section) but we
know it can fail as in Example 8.3.

Turning now to the stable category, Example 4.3 also raises the question of which class
of finite groups is characterised by distributivity of Thick(mod kG).
Question 8.5. For which finite groups G is it true that Thick(mod kG) is a distributive
lattice?
Of course we know that this holds for p-nilpotent groups since already Thick(Db(kG))
is distributive. It turns out the S3 example is also typical.
Proposition 8.6. If k is an algebraically closed field of characteristic p and G has cyclic p-
Sylow subgroups, i.e. kG has finite representation type, then Thick(mod kG) is distributive.
Proof. Blocks with cyclic defect group are stably equivalent to symmetric Nakayama al-
gebras (see [9]) i.e. to algebras of the form Λ = kZe/ rad(kZe)em+1 where Ze is the cyclic
quiver with e vertices and m ≥1. In this case the stable Auslander-Reiten quiver is the
tube ZAme/τ e as a translation quiver. For any symmetric algebra we have τ = Σ−2 and
so if X ∈mod Λ is non-zero then thick(X) = mod Λ as one easily sees from the Auslander-
Reiten triangles using that thick(X) is closed under suspensions and hence also under the
Auslander-Reiten translation. Thus Thick(mod kG) is a product of copies of the distribu-
tive lattice {0 ≤1}, one for each block with non-trivial defect group, and so is distributive
as claimed.
□

Actually one can also deduce these examples, and more, from beautiful work of Benson,
Carlson, and Robinson [2] and Benson [1]. By [1, Theorem 1.1] if G is a finite group and
k has characteristic p then provided the centralizer of every element of order p in G is
p-nilpotent we have thick(k) = mod B0 where B0 is the principal block of kG. So provided
B0 = kG we see that the lattice of thick subcategories of mod kG is distributive. This
applies, for instance, to PSL(2, 7) in characteristic 2.
Example 8.8. Let us conclude with an example where mod kG does not have distributive
lattice of thick subcategories; this is inspired by [3, Example 4]. Let G = C3 × S3 and
k an algebraically closed field of characteristic 3. Then the principal block is the only
block and there are two simples k and ε. We denote by L the projective cover of k as a
kS3-module viewed as a kG-module. Then we have
thick(L) ∩(thick(k) ∨thick(ε)) = thick(L) ∩mod(kG) = thick(L)
but
thick(L) ∩thick(k) ⊊thick(L) and thick(L) ∩thick(ε) = 0.
We sketch the argument; one finds essentially everything one needs to deduce this in [3].
It is easy to check that thick(k ⊕L) contains ε (for instance by working over kS3 cf.
Example 4.3) and so must be all of mod kG. So if L ∈thick(k) it would follow that k
generated the principal block and it doesn’t as there are modules with trivial cohomology.
One can easily calculate that ε and L are orthogonal to one another, i.e. there are no
maps between any of their syzygies, and so thick(L) ∩thick(ε) = 0 as claimed.
