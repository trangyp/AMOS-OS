A B S T R A C T
Periods of low dissolved oxygen concentration–hypoxia and anoxia–threaten the health of aquatic
ecosystems and the services they provide. Hypoxia is strongly influenced by temperature, but the dif-
ferent sensitivities and response functions of oxygen removal and production processes to temperature
are not regarded in most models. Here we present OxyPOM—Oxygen and Particulate Organic Matter,
a nuanced temperature-aware process-based biogeochemical model. OxyPOM incorporates nuanced
temperature sensitivities for the key oxygen-related processes photosynthesis, re-aeration, respiration,
mineralization, and nitrification. Further sensitive variables like optimal light intensity, winter grazing
inhibition, and pathogenesis are also represented. Our model was tested in an idealized water column
experiment, representing a typical estuarine seasonal low-oxygen environment. Differences between
nuanced and uniform temperature sensitivities affect seasonal patterns of oxygen-related processes,
resulting in under- or overestimation during different times of the year, particularly with higher
differences in summer. While these changes may balance in the overall annual oxygen budget, uniform
sensitivities underestimate particulate organic carbon production by up to a factor of four along
the year and overestimate nutrient concentrations. This nuanced approach to temperature sensitivity
allows us to explore and test new hypotheses related to climate warming and heatwaves, addressing
the ecosystem changes demanded by climate change models.
1. Introduction
Dissolved oxygen is a fundamental indicator of water
quality and the health of aquatic ecosystems (European
Commission, 2006; Kannel et al., 2007). Reduced oxy-
gen concentrations, specially hypoxia (below 4 mg L-1) and
anoxia (below mg L-1), adversely affect aerobic organisms
and promote the release of greenhouse gases, nutrients, and
toxins from sediments (Bastviken et al., 2011; Salk et al.,
2016).
The oxygen budget in aquatic systems is governed by
biogeochemical (BGC) processes that in model applications
act as sources (e.g., photosynthesis) or sinks (e.g., organic
matter mineralization, respiration, nitrification, and other
biotic and abiotic reactions) of oxygen (Yakushev et al.,
2013; Holzwarth and Wirtz, 2018). In addition, air-water
oxygen exchange (re-aeration) functions as a source or sink
depending on the temperature-dependent saturation state of
the water (Wanninkhof, 2014; Carter et al., 2021). These
controlling variables exhibit pronounced seasonal variability
due to their temperature sensitivity, leading to shifts in the
dominant processes throughout the year. Substantial changes
in oxygenation and deoxygenation can also occur due to
modifications in particulate matter loads and, consequently,
water turbidity, light availability, nutrient concentrations,
and organic matter levels (Graham et al., 2024; Bernal
et al., 2025), thereby influencing, for example, photosyn-
thetic rates, and particulate organic matter (POM) produc-
tion.

Water temperature plays a particularly critical role, as
both long-term warming trends and short-term heat waves
are closely associated with observed deoxygenation rates
(Blaszczak et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the relative effect
of temperature changes in the process leading to oxygen
dynamics remain poorly constrained in current model appli-
cations. Most existing water quality and ecosystem models
describe oxygen dynamics primarily as a byproduct of more
or less complex biotic and abiotic interactions, with only a
few focusing explicitly on the processes that directly govern
the oxygen budget (Table 1). Moreover, many models that
are used to study oxygen dynamics lack the necessary tem-
perature sensitivities to assess the effects of temperature on
oxygen responses, either because the relevant processes are
absent or because their temperature dependencies are poorly
represented or entirely missing. Additionally, the recogni-
tion of previously neglected processes, such as pathogenesis
(Weitz, 2015; Wirtz, 2019; Krishna et al., 2024), adds further
complexity to current models, limiting their applicability for
accurate oxygen modeling.
Here we present the biogeochemical model OxyPOM
(Oxygen and Particulate Organic Matter), which include
individual temperature sensitivities in oxygen-related pro-
cesses as well as in other biogeochemical rates of lower
trophic levels. Our objective is to highlight the impact of
specific temperature sensitivities in ecosystem dynamics,
we compared two cases of temperature sensitivity formu-
lations (1) all process share a unique sensitivity parameter
and (2) each process have its own parameter. We coupled
OxyPOM to a one-dimensional, depth-resolving hydrody-
namical setup in GOTM to simulate oxygen dynamics in an
idealized case, representing a typical estuarine situation with
seasonal hypoxia. Our primary goal is to introduce OxyPOM

2. Methodology
2.1. OxyPOM model description
A predecessor version of OxyPOM was initially imple-
mented by Holzwarth and Wirtz (2018) in the context of
the closed-source Unstructured Tidal Residual Model–Delft
Water Quality (UnTRIM DELWAQ) system used by the Ger-
man Federal Waterways Engineering and Research Institute
(BAW). This implementation lacked many of the processes
introduced here, but also faced technical deficiencies regard-
ing portability and FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible,
Interoperable, Reusable Wilkinson et al., 2016) and Good
Modeling Software Practices (Lemmen and Sommer, 2024).
Our implementation as open source utilizes the Frame-
work for Aquatic Biogeochemical Models Application Pro-
gramming Interface (FABM API, Bruggeman and Bolding,
2014), thereby ensuring accessibility, interoperability with
other aquatic process models, reusability across different
hydrodynamic models, and zero- to three-dimensional do-
mains. Beyond the original implementation, we incorpo-
rated vertically-explicit formulations for re-aeration in rivers
and estuaries (Raymond and Cole, 2001), primary produc-
tion, and light attenuation; for better resolving effects of tem-
perature variations we included additional mortality terms
for phytoplankton, accounting for viral infections (Wirtz,
2019) and temperature-sensitive loss rates (Scharfe et al.,
2009).

3. Results
3.1. Ecological variables
Dissolved oxygen, total phytoplankton, DOC, total POC,
and nutrient concentrations show important differences in
their spatial and temporal distributions depending on the
assumed temperature sensitivities (Fig. 2, see the vertical-
averaged values in Fig. A.1). Dissolved oxygen concentra-
tions are similar in both cases (i.e., uniform-sensitivity and
reference) with an overestimation of 50–100 mmol O2 m-3
in Jul–Oct in the bottom layer in the uniform-sensitivity case
(Figs. 2a-c). The reference case shows more pronounced
phytoplankton blooms in summer than the uniform case
(Figs. 2d and e). Total phytoplankton is underestimated in
the top 10 m in Jul-Aug by 5–10 mmol C m-3 (Figs. 2f). DOC
is slightly underestimated in the uniform case in all seasons
and depths, with DOC accumulation in the bottom in Jul-
Oct with values 10–40 mmol C m-3 (Figs. 2g-i). Total POC
is understimated in the uniform case; higher overestimation
starts below 15 m in Apr-May, up to Oct in the bottom layer
with values 100–200 mmol C m-3 (Figs. 2j-l).
Contrary to carbon-related variables (i.e., total phyto-
plankton, DOC, and total POC), concentrations of ammonia,
nitrate, and phosphate are overestimated in the uniform
sensitivity case most of the time (Figs. 2m-u). Ammonia is
slightly overestimated by approximately 0.1 mmol N m-3,
except for high underestimation during the phytoplankton
bloom in the top 10 m and in the bottom layer (Figs. 2o).
Nitrate is overestimated in the uniform case before the phyto-
plankton bloom by approximately 2 mmol N m-3. It increases
above 5 mmol N m-3 after the bloom (Figs. 2r). Phosphate
shows a pattern similar to nitrate (Figs. 2s-u): before the
phytoplankton bloom, phosphate is higher by approximately
0.25 mmol P m-3 and increases above 0.5 mmol N m-3
after the bloom in the uniform case (Figs. 2u). Ammonia,
nitrate, and phosphate, as well as carbon-related variables,
are underestimated in the bottom layer in Jul-Oct, the same
time and place where oxygen is overestimated (right column
in Figs. 2).

oxygen-related processes

The absolute oxygen flux leading oxygen dynamics

uniform sensitive case in Jul and Aug. Uniform and reference

oxygen, re-aeration is an oxygen sink, and respiration is the
dominant sink for oxygen. Mineralization and nitrification
became more important from late August, when the role of
re-aeration became a source.
4. Discussion
Our results show a strong effect of temperature sensitiv-
ity in the ecological variables and seasonal variability of the
processes explaining oxygen dynamics, both in relative and
absolute terms. In OxyPOM we consider well-established
temperature dependencies for several key processes: re-
aeration (in the Sc number, after Wanninkhof (2014)),
maximum growth rates of phytoplankton (Eq. 6) (Coles and
Jones, 2000; Sherman et al., 2016; Anderson et al., 2021),
optimal light responses (Eq. 8) (Coles and Jones, 2000; Ed-
wards et al., 2016), grazing and respiration (Eqs. 11 and 12)
(Verity et al., 2002; Scharfe et al., 2009; Ferreira et al., 2022),
biochemical rates (Eqs. 23 and 27) (Krishnan et al., 2014;
Velthuis and Veraart, 2022), and viral infections (Eqs. 39
and 41) (Padhy and Singh, 1977; De Paepe and Taddei,
2006). These are incorporated through a 𝑄10 rule (Eq. 1)
with specific sensitivities for each process. The observed
𝑄10 values span a wide range across different processes
(Table 2) and thus constitute a major source of uncertainty
and variability in model applications, significantly affecting
model responses and results interpretations (e.g., Maar and
Hansen, 2011; Olonscheck et al., 2013).
The wide range of 𝑄10 highlights two key points about
our modelling approach: First, both the uniform and refer-
ence cases are equally realistic—there is not a “good” and
a “bad” parametrization—because we employ 𝑄10 values
that in either case fall within the range observed in nature.
Second, capturing this variability requires a model that
explicitly incorporates temperature-sensitivity parameters.
Depending on the intended application, one can either use a
single, unified parameter for all processes or assign distinct,
process-specific parameters (e.g., Soetaert et al., 2001; Le
Quéré et al., 2016). OxyPOM is capable of supporting either
strategy.
4.1. Temperature-dependent biases in carbon,
nutrient, and virus pools
Temperature sensitivities modify the entire ecosystem
response and are not limited to effects in oxygen concen-
tration. We observed this across most of the simulated year,
the uniform-sensitivity runs produced modest overestimates
of dissolved oxygen in the bottom water column during Jul–
Oct. This excess in oxygen coincides with the underestima-
tion of carbon-related pools (phytoplankton, DOC, and total
POC) and overestimation of nutrients (ammonia, nitrate,
and phosphate) in the same spatial and temporal window
(Fig. 2). This pattern suggests that a uniform temperature re-
sponse may dampen the coupling between primary produc-
tion, organic matter mineralization, and subsequent nutrient
regeneration. This coupling is critical for understanding ex-
periments with temperature manipulation (Biermann et al.,
2014).
Surprisingly, increasing the 𝑄10,Phy from 1.7 in the ref-
erence case to 2.5 in the uniform case did not increase the
phytoplankton concentration in summer (Fig. 2f) and instead
reduced it. Previous modelling efforts have highlighted the
importance of temperature-dependent phytoplankton growth
rates for reproducing seasonal bloom dynamics (e.g., Maar
and Hansen, 2011; Behrenfeld et al., 2013). Our results
agree with these observations, since a uniform-temperature
sensitivity not only suppresses the magnitude of the bloom
(the reference case exhibits a higher phytoplankton peak
concentrations) but also propagates downstream to affect
the balance of oxygen and nutrients. The underestimation
of phytoplankton carbon in the uniform case reduces graz-
ing, respiration and mineralization, thereby limiting oxygen
consumption and nutrient release.
Despite having an explicit temperature sensitivity, patho-
genesis, here in form of viral infections, had only a small
response to a uniform parameterization (Fig. 3a-c). Virus-
mediated mortality of diatoms showed only minor differ-
ences between the two configurations, with the largest devi-
ations occurring in Jul–Oct and occasional peaks in April.
Relative differences become apparent only within a nar-
row temperature band (10–14 ◦C), while remaining near
zero elsewhere. This apparent limited sensitivity aligns with
experimental observations that viral infection rates often
display weaker temperature dependence than host growth
(Middelboe et al., 2001). The reduced temperature-driven
variation observed here could nevertheless influence cascade
effects on carbon export and nutrient cycling.

sensitivity in oxygen budget
Denitrification, mineralization, and nitrification all ex-
hibited clear seasonal trends that follow temperature (Fig. 3d-
l). Mineral denitrification and mineralization share a com-
mon 𝑄10 value in our model configuration. Notably, the pa-
rameterization with uniform temperature sensitivity tended

leading to a 2-to-5-fold reduction in absolute oxygen flux

the importance of assigning process-specific 𝑄10 values: a

nitrifying bacteria (Sherman et al., 2016; Mundim et al.,

Oxygen production by phytoplankton and consump-

and nutrient concentrations may limit photosynthesis rates,
regardless of the temperature response (Edwards et al.,
2016; Holzwarth and Wirtz, 2018). While temperature is
the most reported predictor of respiration, organic carbon
supply (from primary production) shows similar importance,
suggesting their complex interactive relationship (Wikner
et al., 2023). Photosynthesis and respiration peaked in
summer, yet their relative differences were just weakly
temperature-dependent. This suggests that factors beyond
temperature, such as light availability, nutrient limitation,
grazing, and viral infections may play a dominant role in
governing net community metabolism during the bloom
period (Edwards et al., 2016; Wirtz, 2019).
4.3. Temperature sensitivities: a source of
uncertainty
Temperature sensitivities reported in the literature are
highly variable: For example, the estimated 𝑄10 values
for photosynthesis depend on many factors and degrees of
aggregation. For specific species, Coles and Jones (2000)
report 𝑄10 for photosynthesis rates of cyanobacteria and
diatoms of 1.79–2.67 in the range of 2–25 ◦C, while Ferreira
et al. (2022) report values as high of 3.16 for dinoflag-
ellates. In contrast, community aggregated 𝑄10 values for
phytoplankton growth rate are highly variable, e.g., 2.2–
2.5 (Verity et al., 2002) and 1–7 (Robinson and Williams,
1993), the later for Antarctic communities. Meta-analysis
of experimental data show taxa and habitat dependent 𝑄10
ranging from 1.42 for marine coccolithophores to 4.9 for
marine cyanobacteria (Anderson et al., 2021), while field
experiment estimations of global sampling are more conser-
vative with a mean of 1.47 ± 0.08 (Sherman et al., 2016).
Carbon loss rates show similar variability: 𝑄10 values for
respiration rates of dinoflagellates and protozooplankton are
1.1–1.85 for temperatures in the range 16–22 ◦C (Ferreira
et al., 2022). However, other planktivorous groups show
higher values, for example 2.15–4.6 for copepods (Hirche,
1987) and 3.0 for cod larvae (Peck and Buckley, 2008).
Phytoplankton respiration 𝑄10 at the community level are
in the range 1.45–5.59 (Robinson and Williams, 1993),
and in temperatures approaching zero growth above 10 are
possible but uncommon (Pomeroy and Wiebe, 2001). Values
lower than one, indicating that respiration rates reduce with
temperature, have been also observed (Wikner et al., 2023).
Verity et al. (2002) report size-dependent 𝑄10 for grazing
rates ranging 2.2–2.9, yet higher ranges have been observed
and used in modelling 2.2–4.8, the higher values correspond-
ing to copepods (Maar and Hansen, 2011)
Turnover rates for organic carbon and nitrogen also have
wide range. Hansen and Bendtsen (2014) report 𝑄10 for
mineralization ranging 1.5–5.0. Lower 𝑄10 values, approxi-
mately 1, are observed for DOC mineralization (Johannsson
et al., 2025). 𝑄10 for nitrification rates ranging 0.2–2.9 (Kr-
ishnan et al., 2014) and 0.8–1.3 in Arctic has been observed
(Baer et al., 2014). Other nitrogen related processes, such as
denitrification range a little higher 2.0–2.5 (Krishnan et al.,
2014) and 2.3 ± 4.7 (Velthuis and Veraart, 2022).
Temperature dependence of the optimal light intensity
for photosynthesis is not often included in models, how-
ever 𝑄10 values ranging 1.62–1.85 have been calculated
experimentally Edwards et al. (2016). Values in this range
can be estimated for low-temperature adapted species (1.8
calculated from Tilzer et al., 1986), and for cyanobacteria
and diatoms (1.5 calculated from Coles and Jones, 2000).
As including virus in plankton models is still uncommon,
available parameterizations for temperature are uncommon.
Burst size—the amount of virus copies produced by infected
host—show a strong variability with temperature: Padhy
and Singh (1977) reports smaller burst size with increasing
temperature, indicating 𝑄10 < 1, while other report larger
burst size with increasing temperature (e.g., Woody and
Cliver, 1995; Nagasaki et al., 2003; Maat et al., 2017). These
variations might be related to the lytic/lysogenic virus cycle
(Parada et al., 2006). In model applications, burst size is
often kept constant (e.g., Krishna et al., 2024). Virus life-
time–related to inactivation rates–have been observed in the
range 1.3–2.7 (estimated from activation energies reported
by Yap et al., 2021).
4.4. Room for further development
Despite its oxygen-centered philosophy, OxyPOM ne-
glects several biogeochemical processes involved—either
directly or indirectly—in oxygen dynamics. For example,
mineralization and nitrification are the only biochemical
pathways of oxygen consumption. Other biochemical redox
reactions, however, such as sulphur, iron, and manganese
kinetics, are necessary to model oxygen consumption, espe-
cially in hypoxic and anoxic waters, and have been partially
considered in other models, e.g., ERGOM, BROM, and
AQUATOX (Yakushev et al., 2017; Neumann et al., 2022;
Park et al., 2002).
Additional limitations appear from the simplified lower-
trophic levels. For example, OxyPOM prescribes phyto-
plankton with a fixed stoichiometry, assuming a nutrient-
replete system which may not be adequate for all ecosystems
(Neumann et al., 2022). Additionally, a single temperature-
dependent mortality rate affects all trophic interactions,
when in reality different predators may present different
temperature niches, thus different temperature-dependent
factors (Le Quéré et al., 2016; Anderson et al., 2021).

Temperature dependence can have responses different
than the 𝑄10 rule included in OxyPOM, e.g., linear temper-
ature response for growth rates as suggested by Montagnes
et al. (2003). Moreover, temperature dependencies can be
weakened based on observed acclimation or even reverted in
high temperature. For example, Menden-Deuer et al. (2018)
suggest that time scale-dependent temperature sensitivities
have been observed for photosynthesis and grazing, and
Edwards et al. (2016) calculate 𝑄10 values specific for
photoinhibition. Furthermore, temperature sensitivities have
been weakened below to established ranges based on model
re-calibration, for example 𝑄10 = 1.1049 for zooplankton
grazing (Olonscheck et al., 2013).
Other important aspects of oxygen budget, such as sed-
iment and benthic process (Yakushev et al., 2013; Salk
et al., 2016), are adjacent to pelagic oxygen dynamics and
not explicitly included. These limitations can be overcome
by coupling OxyPOM with other models via the FABM
interface. Moreover, OxyPOM is an open-source project;
modifications and extra functionality can be added as re-
quired by any user or developer.
Despite these shortcomings, our model includes the most
relevant processes that are required to explain oxygen dy-
namics in pelagic systems while offering flexibility in the
parameterization of temperature-dependent rates. OxyPOM
is thus a suitable model for studying oxygen dynamics in a
warming world and during extreme physical–oceanographic
events such as heatwaves (Achterberg et al., 2025).
