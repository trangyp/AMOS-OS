One-dimensional non-Hausdorff manifolds
and CW complexes
Igor Vlasenko, Sergiy Maksymenko
Abstract. This paper studies one-dimensional non-Hausdorff manifolds that are similar to “graphs
with split vertices”. It is shown that if M is a connected one-dimensional non-Hausdorff mani-
fold such that the set of its “non-Hausdorff” points is locally finite, and each component of its
complement has a countable base, then there exists a quotient map π: M →Γ onto an open
one-dimensional CW complex, which maps the non-Hausdorff points of M to the vertices of Γ.
Moreover, Γ is the minimal Hausdorff quotient of M, that is, for every continuous map f : M →
N into a Hausdorff space N, there exists a unique continuous map ˆf : Γ →N such that f = ˆf ◦π.
1. Introduction
A manifold is usually defined as a locally Euclidean space M that is also Hausdorff and has
a countable base. The latter two properties are equivalent to the metrizability of M and to the
possibility of embedding it into Euclidean spaces.
Although non-metrizable, and in particular non-Hausdorff, manifolds have been studied rela-
tively little, they often appear implicitly as quotient spaces of certain decompositions of spaces, in
particular of foliations on manifolds. In most cases they are rather “pathological” and do not carry
substantial information. However, under certain assumptions on the “regularity” of the behavior
of leaves of foliations, their leaf spaces sometimes allow one to “encode” certain combinatorial
properties of the decompositions themselves.
Let us briefly list known publications on non-metrizable manifolds, interest in which has grown
significantly in recent years. In [7], the existence of smooth structures on the non-Hausdorff letter
Y was proved. The so-called long line X and the Pr¨ufer manifold were studied in [21, Appendix A].
In [18], it was shown that X admits non-diffeomorphic Cω (real-analytic) structures, but it remains
unclear whether X admits non-diffeomorphic C∞structures. In [5], groups of homeomorphisms
of non-Hausdorff manifolds were studied. In particular, it was proved that for every group G
there exists a (possibly non-Hausdorff) manifold whose homeomorphism group is isomorphic to
G. A systematic study of non-metrizable manifolds was carried out in the monograph [6]. In [19,
20], vector bundle structures over non-Hausdorff manifolds were investigated, and in [13, 14],
classifications of all differentiable structures on the non-Hausdorff line with two origins L and on Y
were obtained.
General properties of non-metrizable manifolds, in particular their homogeneity and groups of
homeomorphisms, were studied in [2, 3, 1].

1.1. Main result of the paper. Let M be a “traditional” one-dimensional manifold, that is,
a topological space locally homeomorphic to [0, 1). It is well known and easy to prove that if M is
connected, Hausdorff, and has a countable base, then it is homeomorphic to one of the following
manifolds: [0, 1], [0, 1), (0, 1), or S1.
If M is a non-Hausdorff manifold, then one can distinguish points at which it “fails to be
Hausdorff.” Namely, we say that points x, y ∈M are inseparable (by open neighborhoods) if every
neighborhood of x intersects every neighborhood of y.
A point x ∈M that is inseparable from some other point is called a branch point of M (see
Subsection 2.3). If x is separable from all points y ∈M \ {x}, then x is called a Hausdorff point of
M.
Clearly, the relation of being inseparable on M is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive.
Therefore, consider its transitive closure: points x, y ∈M are called chain inseparable if there exists
a sequence
x = x0, x1, . . . , xk = y ∈M
such that xi is inseparable from xi+1 for all i = 0, 1, . . . , k −1.
This is an equivalence relation on M, which we denote by ≈.

M 0 := Mbr ∪∂M,

1.3. Examples of non-graph-like manifolds.
Example 1.3.1. Let A = {(0, 0)} ∪{(0, 1/n) | n ∈N} be the origin together with a sequence
on the Oy-axis converging to it. In this case, each point (0, c) ∈A divides the line y = c into two
open intervals, which give a pair of inseparable points in the manifold MA. It follows that the set
Mbr of branch points of MA consists of such pairs, which “accumulate” near the pair corresponding
to the splitting of the Ox-axis by the origin. In particular, Mbr is not locally finite, and therefore
MA is not graph-like.
Note that the projection of the plane onto the Oy-axis induces a homeomorphism from the
quotient space Γ = MA/ ≈onto the real line R. Thus Γ is an open topological graph. On the other
hand, when we identify Γ with R, the image π(Mbr) is the convergent sequence Γ0 = {1/n}n∈N∪{0}
together with its limit, so there is no cell decomposition of R for which Γ0 would be the set of
vertices.

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Quotient mappings. Recall that a mapping f : X →Y between topological spaces is
called a quotient mapping if it has the following property: a subset B ⊂Y is open if and only if its
preimage f −1(B) is open. It follows from the definition that every quotient mapping is continuous.
The following well-known and elementary lemma contains several properties of quotient map-
pings that will be used in this paper.
Lemma 2.1.1. Let f : X →Y be a quotient mapping between topological spaces. Then the
following statements hold.
(a) Let ξ : Y →Q be a mapping into an arbitrary topological space. Then ξ is continuous if and
only if the composition ξ ◦f : X →Q is continuous.
(b) Let B ⊂Y be a subset of Y such that its preimage is non-empty.
Then the restriction
f|f−1(B) : f −1(B) →B is also a quotient mapping. In particular, if f|f−1(B) is a bijection then
it is a homeomorphism.
2.2. Locally finite families of sets. Let M be a topological space, A ⊂M a subset, and
x ∈M a point. An open neighbourhood U of x is called isolating with respect to A if U ∩A = {x}.
Clearly, x is isolated for A if and only if it has an isolating neighbourhood with respect to M.
Recall that a subset A ⊂M is called
• locally finite if the family of all singletons from A is locally finite, i.e. each point x ∈M has a
neighbourhood whose intersection with A is finite;
• discrete if it is discrete in the induced topology, i.e. each point a ∈A has a neighbourhood Ua
containing no other points of A;
• strongly discrete if for every point a ∈A one can choose a neighbourhood Ua such that Ua∩Ub = ∅
for a ̸= b ∈A.
Also recall that a family of subsets β = {Bi}i∈I of a topological space M is called
• locally finite if every point x ∈M has a neighbourhood that intersects only finitely many elements
of β;
• discrete if each Bi is clopen in the induced topology B, i.e. each Bi has an open neighbourhood
that does not intersect the other elements of β;
• strongly discrete if for each i ∈I there exists an open neighbourhood Ui of Bi such that Ui∩Uj = ∅
for i ̸= j.
Let B ∈β. An open neighbourhood U of B is called isolating with respect to β if U does not
intersect the other elements of β. Clearly, the family β is discrete if and only if each of its elements
has an isolating neighbourhood with respect to β.
Let us note the following three elementary statements.
Lemma 2.2.1 (e.g. [16, Lemma 2.3.1]). Let M be a T1 space. Then a subset A ⊂M is locally
finite if and only if A is closed and discrete.

2.4. One-dimensional manifolds. A topological space (M, τ) is called a one-dimensional
manifold if for every point x ∈M one of the following equivalent conditions hold:
• there exists an open neighbourhood U of x homeomorphic either to (−1, 1) or to [0; 1); such
neighbourhoods will be called standard;
• there exists an open neighbourhood U of x and an open embedding ϕ : U →[0; 1); such embed-
dings are called (coordinate) charts.
The following elementary lemma is the one-dimensional case of the Brouwer invariance of
domain theorem. This statement has a “homological” nature, but in the one-dimensional case it
reduces to connectedness. For the reader’s convenience, we give a short proof.
Lemma 2.4.1. Let M be a one-dimensional manifold, U ⊂M an open subset, x ∈U, and
ϕ, ψ : U →[0; 1) two open embeddings. Then
• either ϕ(x) = ψ(x) = 0, in which case x is called a boundary point,
• or ϕ(x), ψ(x) ∈(0; 1), in which case x is called an interior point.
Proof. Note that the point 0 ∈[0; 1) is characterised by having a basis of open connected
neighbourhoods V such that V \ {0} is connected. On the other hand, every point t ∈(0; 1) has
a basis of open connected neighbourhoods V such that V \ {0} is disconnected. The statement of
the lemma now follows because the above properties are preserved under the homeomorphisms ϕ
and ψ.
□
