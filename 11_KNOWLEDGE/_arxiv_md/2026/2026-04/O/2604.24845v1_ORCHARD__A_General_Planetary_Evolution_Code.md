ABSTRACT
We present ORCHARD, a publicly available planetary evolution code based on the gas giant evolution
code, APPLE, capable of modeling the evolution and structures of terrestrial, super-Earth, sub-Neptune,
Neptune, and gas giant planets and exoplanets from 0.5 M⊕to 10 MJ. It supports not only the inho-
mogeneous and non-adiabatic evolution of gas giants and sub-Neptunes, but also the solidification of
the mantles and cores of terrestrial planets, sub-Neptunes, and super-Earths. ORCHARD incorporates a
state-of-the-art hydrogen-helium equation of state, “metal” equations of state (water, ice mixtures, en-
statite/perovskite, olivine/forsterite, iron), and atmospheric boundary conditions ranging from detailed
non-gray radiative transfer models for Solar System giants to irradiated sub-Neptune atmospheres and
bare rocky surfaces. The purpose of ORCHARD is to provide the scientific community with a flexible, uni-
fied tool for modeling planetary structures and evolution across the entire mass continuum of general
astrophysical and planetary interest.

1. INTRODUCTION
Nearly sixty years ago, building on earlier equation-of-
state (EOS) work from E. Wigner & H. B. Huntington
(1935); W. C. Demarcus (1958), W. B. Hubbard (1968)
developed the first fully convective models of Jupiter’s
interior to explain the then-recent thermal emission of
Jupiter (E. J. ¨Opik 1962; F. J. Low 1966). This work
was expanded to include Saturn (W. Hubbard 1969),
and later extended to the evolution of Uranus and Nep-
tune (W. B. Hubbard 1978; W. B. Hubbard & J. J.
Macfarlane 1980). After the discovery of the first extra-
solar planet, 51 Pegasi b (M. Mayor & D. Queloz 1995),
A. Burrows et al. (1995); D. Saumon et al. (1996); T.
Guillot et al. (1996), and A. Burrows et al. (1997), built
upon this early work to create the first gas giant exo-
planet and brown dwarf evolutionary models.
Over the past decade, Juno (S. J. Bolton et al. 2017)
and Cassini (D. L. Matson et al. 2003) have revealed
that both Jupiter and Saturn likely harbor extended, or
“fuzzy,” cores (J. Fuller et al. 2014; J. Fuller 2014; S. M.

arevalo@princeton.edu
Wahl et al. 2017; F. Debras & G. Chabrier 2019; N. Net-
telmann et al. 2021; C. R. Mankovich & J. Fuller 2021;
B. Militzer et al. 2022; B. Militzer & W. B. Hubbard
2023, 2024).
Taken together, these observational ad-
vances underscore the need for evolutionary models that
are self-consistent across the planetary mass range of in-
terest. Beyond the Solar System, JWST has ushered in
a new era of atmospheric characterization for exoplan-
ets and brown dwarfs, delivering transit and emission
spectra that have begun to constrain atmospheric abun-
dances and thermal structures across a wide range of
planetary masses (e.g., E. M. Kempton et al. 2023; N. F.
Wogan et al. 2024; E.-M. Ahrer et al. 2025; S. Barat
et al. 2025; N. Madhusudhan et al. 2025). These obser-
vations have also motivated new questions about interior
composition. For instance, the muted spectral features
of sub-Neptunes such as GJ 1214 b (E. M. Kempton
et al. 2023) and the debated atmospheric detections of
K2 18 b (B. Benneke et al. 2019; N. F. Wogan et al.
2024; N. Madhusudhan et al. 2025) highlight the need
for self-consistent interior/atmosphere models that con-
nect interior composition to observable spectra. Near-
future missions, including Ariel (G. Tinetti et al. 2022;

The methodological tradition of planetary interior
evolution codes, such as CEPAM (T. Guillot & P. Morel
1995), APPLE (A. Sur et al. 2024, referred to as “Paper I”
hereafter when referenced individually), and now MESPA
(MESA for planets; R. Helled et al. 2025), has heritage
in stellar evolution codes (R. Kippenhahn & A. Weigert
1990; R. Kippenhahn et al. 2012), such as GARSTEC (A.
Weiss & H. Schlattl 2008) and MESA (B. Paxton et al.
2011, 2013, 2018). These evolution codes, however, are
complex to develop, so much of the work on planetary
interior evolution has relied on models that assume adia-
batic and homogeneous interiors to explore mass-radius
relations (A. Burrows et al. 1997, 2001; J. J. Fortney
et al. 2007; E. D. Lopez et al. 2012; E. D. Lopez &
J. J. Fortney 2014) and mass-metallicity relations (D. P.
Thorngren et al. 2016; Y. Chachan et al. 2025), and some
of these adiabatic models, such as GASTLI (L. Acu˜na
et al. 2024), are publicly available. Although this latter
class of models has been widely applied, the assumptions
of these codes preclude them from adequately modeling
the thermal and compositional evolution of planet in-
teriors via thermal-diffusive processes in an inhomoge-
neous context, making them unable to properly model
the interior structure of the Solar System planets and,
perhaps by extension, exoplanets.
To adequately model the non-adiabatic and inhomo-
geneous evolution of planetary interiors, one must ac-
count for energy, thermal, and compositional transport.
Planetary interior evolution codes that incorporate the
Henyey relaxation method (L. Henyey et al. 1964), a
technique inherited from stellar evolution codes, include
CEPAM (T. Guillot & P. Morel 1995), MESPA (R. Helled
et al. 2025) and APPLE (Paper I) for gas giants, and
CMAPPER4 for super-Earth interior evolution (J. Zhang
& L. A. Rogers 2022, “Z22” hereafter). Such codes have
been harnessed to model the likely inhomogeneous evo-
lution of Jupiter and Saturn (A. Vazan et al. 2016, 2018;
S. M¨uller et al. 2020a; S. Howard et al. 2024; R. Te-
jada Arevalo et al. 2025; H. Knierim et al. 2025; A. Sur
et al. 2025b; Y. Su et al. 2026), Uranus and Neptune
(A. Vazan & R. Helled 2020; R. Tejada Arevalo 2025; S.
Howard et al. 2025), and gas giant exoplanets (S. Muller
& R. Helled 2021; H. Knierim & R. Helled 2024, 2025;
A. Sur et al. 2026). However, these existing codes are
individually limited in mass range, challenging to use,
or are not public.

This paper presents ORCHARD5, an extended and public
version of APPLE, written entirely in Python 3. ORCHARD
covers the terrestrial/rocky mass ranges from 0.5 M⊕up
to 10 MJ gas giants. ORCHARD can model the interior
thermal evolution of rocky terrestrial planets, super-
Earths, sub-Neptunes, and Neptunes, as well as the
thermal and compositional evolution of gas giants. Like
APPLE, ORCHARD is built with the flexibility to choose
among the most current H-He EOSes, metal (Z) EOSes,
H-He-Z mixtures at arbitrary Z fractions, choices of at-
mospheric boundary conditions, and transport quanti-
ties, such as Rosseland mean opacities and conductivi-
ties. ORCHARD offers the capability to model extended
compositional gradients in gas giants as well as sub-
Neptunes with convective mixing and erosion of the
compositional gradients over time.
As with other planetary evolution code papers (e.g.,
L. Acu˜na et al. 2024; A. Sur et al. 2024; R. Helled et al.
2025), this particular paper does not set out to provide
novel scientific results, but instead to present and de-
scribe ORCHARD. This paper is organized as follows: Sec-
tion 2 describes in detail the structural and evolution
equations, energy transport methods, available EOSes,
and atmospheric boundary conditions. Section 3 pro-
vides example calculations for a wide variety of planet
masses, and Section 4 describes future upgrades and sci-
entific applications. We provide concluding remarks in
Section 5. Details on the code algorithm and structure,
as well as a flowchart, can be found in the Appendices.

2.5. Atmospheres
The first atmosphere models for evolutionary calcula-
tions were published by H. C. Graboske et al. (1975).
W. B. Hubbard (1977, 1978), and J. B. Pollack et al.
(1977) applied them to the first thermal cooling mod-
els of Jupiter and Saturn coupled to boundary condi-
tions. As discussed in Paper I, A. Burrows et al. (1997)
computed the first non-gray 1-D atmospheric models for
gas-giant and brown-dwarf thermal evolution. ORCHARD
inherits this method, wherein tables from a radiative
transfer code, such as CoolTLusty (M. S. Ummah 1995;
D. Sudarsky et al. 2003, 2005; A. Burrows et al. 2008;
I. Hubeny 2011), are interpolated to derive internal flux
temperatures (Tint) and surface losses. We incorporate
such flux boundary conditions for all planet masses of in-
terest and describe these in the following sections, sum-
marized in Table 4.

4. AREAS FOR FUTURE IMPROVEMENT
Many 1-D planetary evolution capabilities are yet to
be included in a public version of ORCHARD. One of these,
already applied in TA26, is silicate miscibility. They are
not included here due to large uncertainties in the im-
miscibility of silicate and water. With improved testing,
however, both silicate and water immiscibility might be
fully deployed in later public versions of ORCHARD. In
this section, we describe our remaining priorities.

the inclusion of rotational effects using the entrainment

impact on the survivability of Jupiter and Saturn’s fuzzy
core interiors, and of those in gas giants generally. Such
an effect has already been implemented in MESA and
shown to slow convective velocities (P. Agrawal et al.
2026).

4.2. Mass Loss
Atmospheric and envelope mass loss can be an im-
portant evolutionary process for low-density, highly ir-
radiated sub-Neptune and hot Jupiter exoplanets (J. E.
Owen & Y. Wu 2013; H. Chen & L. A. Rogers 2016;
J. E. Owen & Y. Wu 2016; S. Ginzburg et al. 2016;
J. E. Owen & Y. Wu 2017; S. Ginzburg et al. 2018; A.
Gupta & H. E. Schlichting 2019; A. Gupta et al. 2022;
J. E. Owen & H. E. Schlichting 2024). Recently, evo-
lutionary calculations by Y. Tang et al. (2024) found
that “core-powered” mass loss contributions are negli-
gible on evolutionary timescales, finding that the “boil-
off” phase (J. E. Owen & Y. Wu 2016) is most signif-
icant at lower masses (≲4 M⊕) and high irradiation
(≳100F⊕). Nevertheless, mass loss due to a combina-
tion of photoevaporation, interior heat release (e.g., A.
Gupta & H. E. Schlichting 2019), or boil-off has promise
for explaining the radius valley among close-in super-
Earth and sub-Neptune exoplanets (B. J. Fulton et al.
2017). Early internal tests with ORCHARD show that this
process could be implemented in a future public release
to investigate the effects of mass loss, perhaps following
the scheme discussed in MESA (B. Paxton et al. 2011; B.
Paxton et al. 2015, 2019).

4.3. Core Erosion and Core Compositional Exchange
Gas giant planets like Jupiter and Saturn are believed
to have formed with large, compact central masses (P.
Bodenheimer & J. B. Pollack 1986; J. Pollack et al.
1996). A possible cause of Jupiter’s fuzzy core is the
gradual erosion of this central core over its evolution
(T. Guillot et al. 2004). However, this process has been
found to be thermodynamically disfavored (see Section
4.1 in R. Helled et al. 2022b) and may thwart the mix-
ing of the core (J. R. Fuentes et al. 2025). Nevertheless,
the gradual erosion of the core needs to be incorporated
into future versions of ORCHARD to enable more detailed
studies of core erosion.
Beyond Jupiter and Saturn, compositional exchange
between the cores and envelopes of sub-Neptunes is cur-
rently an active topic of research (e.g., M. C. Nixon et al.
2025; A. Gupta et al. 2026). Moreover, chemical reac-
tions between the mantle and initial envelope compo-
sitions can affect the thermal and compositional evolu-
tion of sub-Neptune interiors (e.g., H. E. Schlichting &
E. D. Young 2022; A. Werlen et al. 2025). To properly
model these exchanges, calculations of element equilib-
rium partitioning and an expanded suite of equations
of state are required. Moreover, the associated chem-
ical reaction rates need to be density-, pressure-, and
temperature-dependent in order to inform the composi-
tional state of the interior structure from one timestep
to the next. Since these effects can likely affect the radii
and atmospheric compositions of sub-Neptunes, incor-
porating this capability is another feature to include in
future versions of ORCHARD.

5. CONCLUSION
Modeling the thermal and structural evolution of plan-
ets across the full mass spectrum, from rocky super-
Earths to massive gas giants, has never before been at-
tempted using a single evolution code. This paper in-
troduces ORCHARD, a unified planetary evolution frame-
work that synthesizes the methodologies of APPLE (Pa-
per I) and CMAPPER (Z22), incorporating the ice giant

This section describes the implementation and discretization of the equations discussed in Sections 2.1, 2.2, and
2.3. ORCHARD , like APPLE , uses an operator-split approach in which the hydrostatic equilibrium and thermal and
compositional flux updates are computed separately within the same timestep. Section A.1 describes the Henyey
relaxation method we deploy to calculate hydrostatic equilibrium (Equations 1—2). Then, Section A.2 describes how
we solve the energy and compositional transport equations (Equations 3—6). A detailed algorithmic flowchart of
ORCHARD is included in Appendix B (Figure 12), and a detailed algorithmic description, Algorithm 1, is provided in
the same Appendix.
A.1. Henyey Relaxation Method
Stellar evolution codes have long relied on the Henyey relaxation scheme (L. Henyey et al. 1964), which solves the
full set of structural equations simultaneously on a one-dimensional grid. We refer the reader to Chapter 12.2 of R.
Kippenhahn et al. (2012) for a general overview.13 The Henyey Newton-Raphson iteration linearizes the residual of
the discretized equations and inverts the resulting block-tridiagonal Jacobian, achieving quadratic convergence near
the solution.
The 1-D planetary structure in ORCHARD is defined from the outside-in and is divided into three structural regions
indexed by mass coordinate m, denoted in ORCHARD by kcore and kcore fe. The mass grid index kcore is the envelope-
mantle boundary, and kcore,Fe is the core-mantle boundary. Users can specify coreless gas giants (kcore = N), planets
with no iron core (kcore,Fe = N), and bare-rock super-Earths (kcore = 0), where N is the number of mass shells. The
latter two cases are automatically defined, respectively, by setting the iron core mass to 0 or setting the total mass
equal to the core mass. This layered assumption will be relaxed in the future to allow compositional exchange between
the mantle and the envelope (see Section 4.3).

B.2. ORCHARD code structure
This section describes the main evolution files in the ORCHARD GitHub repository. All of these files are described in
detail in the repository. For clarity, we also provide detailed documentation in each file. The following Python scripts
comprise the core structure of ORCHARD :
• initial.py: Builds the specified initial thermal and compositional structure, initializes the EOSes described in
2.4, and builds the mass grid.
• atm bc.py: Implements the atmosphere boundary conditions to provide Tint, Teff, and their associated derivatives
as described in 2.5.
• rk4 initial.py: Initializes the user parameter model with a Runga-Kutta integrator to fourth order, improving
initial hydrostatic equilibrium steps for higher mass or rocky planet models. The user needs to set rk4 initial
= True to use. If rk4 initial = False, then the initial structure is a 9 kB baryon−1 isentrope that works for
most gas-giant planet models.
• hydrostatic.py: Updates the radius, pressure, density, and temperature from the current compositional/entropy
state using Equations 1–5 with a Henyey relaxation solver, incorporating the optional rotational (rotation =
True) terms using theory of figures to fourth order using ToF4 (N. Nettelmann 2017), found in TOF.py. We set
a default matrix inversion convergence tolerance of 10−10 (tol hydro = 1e-10) in the hydrostatic solver, which
is editable via the user configuration file.
• transport.py: Contains the global Newton-Raphson matrix solver that advances the entropy and composition
(S, Y , Z) by computing the convective, radiative, conductive, and compositional fluxes as described in Section 2.2,
with optional miscibility of helium or metals (he rain = True, metal rain = True), and calculates the surface
losses, all implicitly. We set a default matrix inversion convergence tolerance of 10−10 (tol NR = 1e-10) for the
Newton-Raphson matrix solver, which is editable via the user configuration file.
• evolution.py: This is the top-level driver that updates the thermodynamic and structural variables (S, Y , Z,
T, ρ, P, R, etc.). This module executes the operator-split evolution loop with transport.update SYZ(...),
then hydrostatic.hydrostatic equilibrium(...)), and manages adaptive timesteps according the relative
errors of S, Y , Z, T, ρ, χ (melt fraction). Finally, this module saves the evolution data in either .txt or .h5 file
format, set by the user (save hdf5 = True).
We provide a few auxiliary modules that set user-defined initial S, Y , and Z profiles (profiles.py) and define
mantle properties, including electrical and thermal conductivities and viscosities (mantle core props.py). These and
additional auxiliary files are housed in the utils folder within the orchard folder. We now provide a detailed graphic
and algorithmic workflow in Figure 12. This is a recapitulation of Algorithm 1.
