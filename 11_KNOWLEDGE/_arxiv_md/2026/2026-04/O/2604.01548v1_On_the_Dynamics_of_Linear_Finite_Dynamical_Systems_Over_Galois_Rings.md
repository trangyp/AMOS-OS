Abstract
Linear finite dynamical systems play an important role, for example, in coding theory and simulations.
Methods for analyzing such systems are often restricted to cases in which the system is defined over a field
or lack practicability to effectively analyze the system’s dynamical behavior. However, when analyzing
and prototyping finite dynamical systems, it is often desirable to quickly obtain basic information such as
the length of cycles and transients that appear in its dynamics, which is reflected in the structure of the
connected components of the corresponding functional graphs. In this paper, we extend the analysis of the
dynamics of linear finite dynamical systems that act over cyclic modules to Galois rings. Furthermore, we
propose algorithms for computing the length of the cycles and the height of the trees that make up their
functional graphs.

finite dynamical systems, Galois ring, algebraic algorithms

1. Introduction
Linear Finite Dynamical Systems (LFDSs) represent a fundamental entity in various fields of research.
They are employed, for example, in coding theory [1], in the design and analysis of linear feedback shift
registers [2], in the characterization of involutions relevant to cryptographic applications [3], or in simulations
of regulatory genetic networks [4] and multi-agent networks over finite fields [5, 6]. In this paper, we focus on
a subclass of LFDSs that act on cyclic modules. A recent application of such LFDSs uses finite uniform linear
Cellular Automata (CAs) as dynamical reservoirs within the reservoir computing framework for machine
learning-based time-series processing [7].
When investigating LFDSs, it is essential to identify the system’s dynamical behavior. A common
approach is to decompose their dynamics into cycles and trees. Information about the set of cycle lengths
and the height of the trees provides crucial insights into their dynamics. Extensive academic research on the
dynamics of LFDSs has been conducted, for example, in [3, 8–12].
In practical applications, it is often desirable to quickly obtain information about the system’s general
behavior, i.e., identifying the cycle and transient lengths present in its functional graph. However, existing
approaches either target systems defined over finite fields and lack generalization toward finite commutative
rings, or they rely on the complex computation of minimal polynomials and do not leverage the specific
structure of the target module, resulting in algorithms with high runtime complexity that hinder the analysis
and undermine the practical applicability of theoretical results.
In this work, we analyze the structure of the connected components in the functional graphs of LFDSs
that act on cyclic modules over Galois Rings (GRs). This includes the set of cycle lengths and the maximal

2.1. Background in Abstract Algebra
Let p be a prime number and fix some monic polynomial h ∈Z[t] of degree d = deg(h) such that h is
irreducible modulo p. If ⟨h⟩is the principal ideal generated by h, then the ring of truncated polynomials
with degree less than d over Z can be constructed as a quotient ring
R := Z[t]/⟨h⟩.
The following lemma shows that the ring R, as well as certain quotient rings of the form R[x]/⟨m⟩,
m ∈R[x], are torsion-free as Z-module (i.e. nr = 0 with n ∈Z, n ̸= 0 implies r = 0). Throughout the paper,
all rings are assumed to be commutative with unity.
Lemma 1. Let S be a ring and let g ∈S[x] be a monic polynomial. If S is torsion-free, then S[x]/⟨g⟩is
also torsion-free. In particular, R = Z[t]/⟨h⟩is torsion-free.
Proof. Let a ∈Z and r ∈S[x]. We have to prove that if ar(x) ≡0 (mod g(x)) with a ̸= 0 then r(x) ≡0
(mod g(x)). The congruence above can be written as ar(x) = g(x)k(x) for some k ∈S[x]. If a ∤k(x) we
can write k(x) = axm+1k0(x) + bxm + k1(x) with a ∤b and deg(k1) < m. Then, all the coefficients of
bxmg(x) + k1(x)g(x) = a(r(x) −xm+1k0(x)) are divisible by a. However, since g is monic and deg(k1) < m,
the leading coefficient of bxmg(x) + k1(x)g(x) is b, which is not divisible by a. This yields a contradiction.
In particular, since h ∈Z[t] is monic and Z is torsion-free (as Z-module) we have that R is free as a
Z-module.
We denote the ring of integers modulo p as Zp. Recall that Zp is isomorphic to the finite field of
characteristic p, denoted by Fp. An extension field of order q = pd can be constructed as
Fq ∼= Zp[t]/⟨h(t)⟩∼= Z[t]/⟨h(t), p⟩∼= R/⟨p⟩.
GRs are generalizations of finite fields. For any e ∈Z+, a Galois ring of characteristic pe and extension
degree d can be defined as
GR(pe, d) ∼= Zpe[t]/⟨h(t)⟩∼= Z[t]/⟨h(t), pe⟩∼= R/⟨pe⟩.
Clearly, if e = 1, then we have GR(p, d) ∼= Fq. We call Fq the residue field of GR(pe, d). To simplify notation,
for fixed p and d, we define Re := GR(pe, d). In the remainder of this work, we follow the convention of using
uppercase letters for polynomials over R1 = Fq, and lowercase letters to denote polynomials over general
rings.

2.2. Finite Dynamical Systems
FDSs are typically defined as tuples (X, Γ), where X is a finite set forming the state space, and Γ : X →X
is the system function that defines how the system transitions from one state to the next [8]. Hence, Γ defines
the overall dynamical behavior of the system. Two FDSs (X, Γ1) and (Y, Γ2) are equivalent, denoted by
(X, Γ1) ∼= (Y, Γ2), if there is a bijection φ: X →Y such that Γ2 = φ ◦Γ1 ◦φ−1.
Starting at any state x ∈X, the repeated application of Γ to x defines a path through the state space,
called the orbit of x. We denote the k-times repeated application of Γ to x as Γk(x). Since the state space is
finite, any orbit eventually stabilizes in a periodic pattern. This leads us to the definition of transients and
cycles in the orbits of an FDS.
Definition 2. (Transients and Cycles) For any x ∈X, let τ be the least non-negative integer and ℓbe the least
positive integer satisfying Γτ+ℓ(x) = Γτ(x). Then the set {x, Γ(x), . . . , Γτ−1(x)} is the transient in the orbit of
x, and pper(Γ, x) := τ the transient length (or preperiod). Moreover, the set {Γτ(x), Γτ+1(x), . . . , Γτ+ℓ−1(x)}
is the cycle in the orbit of x, and per(Γ, x) := ℓthe cycle length (or period).

The dynamics of an FDS (X, Γ) can be represented as a graph [8] in which the vertices are the elements
in X, and for any pair of states x1, x2 ∈X, there is a directed edge from x1 to x2 if and only if Γ(x1) = x2.
More precisely, we define such graphs as follows.
Definition 4. (Functional Graph) To any FDS (X, Γ) we associate a graph G(Γ) = (V, E), where V = X
is the set of vertices, and E = {(x, Γ(x)) : x ∈V} is the set of directed edges. We call G(Γ) the functional
graph of (X, Γ).
The functional graph of an FDS is a complete characterization of the structure of its state space and
dynamical behavior, and can thus be used to identify the corresponding FDS. Equivalent FDSs correspond
to isomorphic functional graphs, and the sums and products of systems translate to sums and products
of their functional graphs [8, 11]. Therefore, we reuse the same notation as introduced in Definition 3 for
functional graphs. There are two basic building blocks in the functional graphs of FDSs, namely cycles and
trees. Cycles in graphs correspond to the cycles in the orbits of FDSs.
Definition 5. (Cycles) For any ℓ∈Z+, a cycle is a directed and weakly connected graph with ℓvertices
and ℓedges such that the indegree and outdegree of every vertex are both equal to one. We denote such a
cycle by Cℓ, where ℓis the length of the cycle.
Cycles of length ℓ= 1 are called fixed points. In what follows, gcd and lcm denote the greatest common
divisor and the least common multiple, respectively.
Proposition 1. [17], [8, Proposition 3.1] The product of (a · Ci) by (b · Cj) is given by
(a · Ci) ⊗(b · Cj) ∼= (a · b · gcd(i, j)) · Clcm(i,j).
On the other hand, trees correspond to the transient parts in the orbits of FDSs.
Definition 6. (Trees) A tree is a directed, weakly connected, acyclic graph with exactly one vertex with no
successors, referred to as the root. Vertices with no predecessors are leaves. A tree is directed from its leaves
to the root. From each vertex in the tree, there exists exactly one path to the root. The height of a tree
is the maximum number of edges on the path from a leaf to the root. A tree of height zero is trivial and
consists of the root and no other vertices.
It has been shown that the functional graph of an FDS is the sum of weakly connected components,
where each component consists of a cycle whose vertices are roots of (possibly trivial) trees [8]. A notable
form of such components is given by a single tree rooted at a fixed point, which we refer to as a looped tree.

2.3. Related Work
Fundamental work in the theory and analysis of LFDSs over finite fields was done in [17], which was
extended and generalized in [8]. Several papers build upon this work and generalize towards finite rings
[9, 10, 12, 22, 23]. The authors in [3, 11, 21] developed a polynomial-based approach for analyzing LFDSs.
Apart from research on general LFDSs, numerous studies target more specific forms of LFDSs or specific
application domains. One branch considers finite linear CAs with periodic boundary conditions, corresponding
to LFDSs acting on cyclic modules [19, 24–27]. Many of these studies yield results that align with other, more
general results presented in papers on LFDSs, for example, [19]. Another notable field is systems biology,
which focuses not only on LFDSs but also on (nonlinear) polynomial finite dynamical systems [28–30]. In
[31], a software tool called Analysis of Dynamic Algebraic Models (ADAM) has been proposed and applied
to gene regulatory networks, analyzes the cyclic behavior of polynomial FDSs using Gröbner bases.

There exist other algorithms that can be used to compute the set of cycle lengths. The algorithm proposed

runtime observed.
The strength of our approach lies in avoiding costly matrix-based operations for LFDSs and leveraging
polynomial factorization, which scales more favorably than O(n3). A limitation is that our method does not
construct every explicit cycle. Nevertheless, in Lemma 8 and Proposition 3, we showed a simple method to
explicitly construct a representative cycle for each cycle length that appears in the system’s dynamics.
The software tool ADAM [31] computes all concrete cycles for a given length by solving systems of
polynomial equations using Gröbner bases. However, without prior knowledge of available cycle lengths, one
must attempt every candidate length and check for solutions in the associated system.

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we analyzed the dynamics of LFDSs that act on cyclic Re[x]-modules. Based on the
f-decomposition of the defining ideal and the corresponding decomposition into nilpotent and bijective
subsystems, we developed a method to construct the set of cycle lengths. An optimization of the computation
of L(Γe(f, m)) is achieved by grouping irreducible divisors of the defining polynomial of the bijective
subsystem over Fq via an equivalence relation on their polynomial order. Furthermore, we developed and
analyzed practical and applicable algorithms to compute the set of cycle lengths and the system height. With
runtime complexities of O∼ n1+log(log(n)) + n2e2 + (n2 + e2 + ne) · Me(n)

for the cycle length analysis and
O∼(n2 · e2 · M(n)) for the system height analysis, the proposed algorithms enable the practical analysis of
the dynamics of such types of LFDSs.
