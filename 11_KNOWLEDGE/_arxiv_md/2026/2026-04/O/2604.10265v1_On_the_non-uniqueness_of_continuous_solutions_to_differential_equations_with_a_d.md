On the non-uniqueness of continuous solutions to differential
equations with a discrete state-dependent delay
Alexander Rezounenko1
Kharkiv National University, 4 Svobody sqr., Kharkiv, 61022, Ukraine
rezounenko@gmail.com
We discuss the non-uniqueness of continuous solutions to differential equations with a
discrete state-dependent delay and continuous initial functions. We are interested not only in
the fact (conditions) of non-uniqueness, but in additional information on the number of non-
unique solutions and discuss an approach to classify them. We provide a few explicit (easy to
verify) examples of the non-uniqueness of continuous solutions and propose an approach to
their classification. This partial classification may be illustrated by using just three collors
and their simple and intuitive combination. We recognize that this initial classification is
not exhaustive, and further study is necessary to build a complete picture.
Keywords: non-uniqueness; uniqueness; state-dependent delay;
Mathematics Subject Classification 2020: 34K43; 34A12; 34K05.

Introduction
The study of state-dependent delay equations has a long history and constantly attracts
attention of many researchers due to big number of applied problems [5].
We are interested in solutions of initial valiue problems (Cauchy problems) for a delay
equation with a discrete delay argument. Let us consider the problem in details.
Let 𝐷be a domain in R𝑛. Consider the following state-dependent delay (SDD) differential
equation (system)
˙𝑥(𝑡) = 𝐹(𝑥(𝑡), 𝑥(𝑡−𝑔(𝑥(𝑡)))).
(1)
with SDD functional 𝑔: 𝐷→[0, ℎ] and 𝐹: 𝐷× 𝐷→R𝑛, both continuous. Here ˙𝑥(𝑡)
denotes the right-hand derivative of 𝑥(𝑡).
Along with (1), we consider a simplified equation
˙𝑥(𝑡) = 𝑓(𝑥(𝑡−𝑔(𝑥(𝑡))),
(2)
with a continuous 𝑓: R →R (or 𝑓: R𝑛→R𝑛in case of systems).
Basic properties of delay equations one may find in classical monographs [1, 4]. For more
details on SDD differential equations we refer to review [5]. In the last review [5] one may
find a number of real-world applications.
We are mainly interested in properties of uniqueness / non-uniqueness of solutions to (1)
and (2). As will be discussed below, we will distinguish an immediate non-uniqueness and
waiting-time non-uniqueness. Since our focus is on the immediate non-uniqueness (for the
clarity of the essential properties), we are mainly consider small 𝑡≥0.

If one is interested in dynamical properties of solutions, even more restrictions on the

uniqueness of solution to (1), (3) and even to (2), (3). It is clearly illustrated by the example

Let us explain our main ideas.
In addition to the standard view of the non-uniqueness of solutions, when it is sufficient
to prove the existence of more than one solution, we are interested in the following questions:
(A) How many solutions does the Cauchy problem have?
(B) In the case of many solutions, are all the solutions ”the same”, and if not, can they
be classified in some way?
We offer for discussion partial answers to both questions (A) and (B).
A clear example of the Cauchy problem with an infinite (uncountable) number of solutions
demonstrates the enormous variety of possible answers to question (A), even in the simplest,
scalar case (see below).
Discussing question (B), from our point of view, non-unique solutions are not ”the same”.
It seems quite natural to pay attention to the delay argument of each solution and classify
the solutions according to the behavior of this argument.
For a more intuitive (visual)
understanding, we propose to ”paint” the solutions in different colors (blue, red, yellow),
depending on the behaviour of the delay argument.
Since any continuous solution to (1), (3) is continuously differentiable for 𝑡> 0, the (local)
𝐶1 property of SDD 𝑔implies the 𝐶1 property of the delayed argument 𝑠(𝑡) ≡𝑡−𝑔(𝑥(𝑡)).
A classical connection between the sign of ˙𝑠(𝑡) and the monotonicity of 𝑠(𝑡), advises to look
closer on solutions with a monotone delay argument. Another theorem from Calculus says

Concluding remarks
1) We distinguish an immediate non-uniqueness and waiting-time non-uniqueness. Solutions
(13) and (14), (15) with 𝜏= 0 represent immediate non-uniqueness, while (14), (15) with
𝜏> 0 represent waiting-time non-uniqueness.
2) Remind the question (B). Which properties of an equation and an initial function are
”responsible” for the non-uniqueness of continuous solutions? It looks that
a) the behaviour of delay argument 𝑠(𝑡) is important.
b) Different non-Lipschitz properties of 𝜙on both half-neighbourhoods of 𝑠0 (point of
non- Lipschitz) may give birth to different families of solutions. See example 3.
c) Waiting-time non-uniqueness may occur only in cases when either the red solution
exists or the delay argument 𝑠(𝑡) leaves and latter returns to the point 𝑠0.
