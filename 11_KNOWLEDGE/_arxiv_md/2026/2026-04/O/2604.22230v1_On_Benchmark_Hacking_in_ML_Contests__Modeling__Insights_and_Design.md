Abstract
Benchmark hacking refers to tuning a machine learning model to score highly on cer-
tain evaluation criteria without improving true generalization or faithfully solving the
intended problem. We study this phenomenon in a generic machine learning contest,
where each contestant chooses two types of effort: creative effort that improves model
capability as desired by the contest host, and mechanistic effort that only improves the
model’s fitness to the particular task in contest without contributing to true general-
ization. We establish the existence of a symmetric monotone pure strategy equilibrium
in this competition game. It also provides a natural definition of benchmark hacking
in this strategic context by comparing a player’s equilibrium effort allocation to that
of a single-agent baseline scenario. Under our definition, contestants with types be-
low certain threshold (low types) always engage in benchmark hacking, whereas those
above the threshold do not. Furthermore, we show that more skewed reward structures
(favoring top-ranked contestants) can elicit more desirable contest outcomes. We also
provide empirical evidence to support our theoretical predictions.
Keywords— contests, benchmark hacking, gaming, machine learning

Introduction
With the pervasive application of machine learning techniques today, machine learning (ML) con-
tests become a very popular way for firms or individuals to elicit high-quality ML methods from
the machine learning community. Notable examples include platforms like Kaggle and AIcrowd.
Moreover, ML contests are also popular among academics as a way to advance ML methodology
development. The most notable example perhaps is the ImageNet Challenge (Deng et al., 2009),
which attracted many computer vision researchers to compete on the common problem of image
classification and sparked the revolutionary era of deep learning, started by the development of a
novel ML model called deep convolutional neural networks that significantly beats all other ML
models at the time (Krizhevsky et al., 2012). Finally, ML content also happens in industry, though
often implictly, such as the recent race in LLM model development (including the reported Llama
4’s benchmark hacking (Ndzomga, 2025)).
Unlike other avenues of ML methodology development, machine learning contests raise the
concern of benchmark hacking — the practice of optimizing only for contest rules through trial-
and-error approaches but not truly improving a model’s generalization ability or faithfully solving
the underlying problem. From the perspective of the contest host (henceforth, the principal), she
would want to elicit a model that delivers meaningful improvements, novel solutions, or even entirely
new approaches to a problem that could potentially be applied to future problems.1 However, in
contests where prizes are awarded based on model performance on the current data set (due to
the lack of perfect evaluation metrics), the contestants’ direct incentives are often not the model’s
true capability, but rather how it fits the particular evaluation metric specified by the contest.
This can be achieved not only through genuine model innovation but also through trial-and-error
tactics, such as repeatedly tweaking hyperparameters and making numerous submissions to find the
configuration that yields the highest score. Hence, compared to a single data analyst’s ML model
development, what is fundamentally different in ML contests is the mis-aligned incentives between
the principal and contestants. As long as the principal cannot distinguish genuine innovation from
benchmark hacking — a task typically infeasible given the high cost of manually reviewing hundreds
or even thousands of scripts — this misalignment persists.
To study the above issue of benchmark hacking in machine learning contests, this paper puts
forward a game-theoretic model for analyzing the potentially mis-aligned incentives between prin-
cipal and contestants. Our model distinguishes two types of effort that a ML contestant could
exert: (1) creative effort, which often requires human judgment or critical thinking, and delivers
meaningful improvements and innovative solutions, thereby benefiting the principal; for instance,
creative effort could include the time a contestant spent on developing foundational innovation (e.g.,

Let us use the famous ImageNet Challenge as an example contest to help readers better under-
stand the two types of effort. From the very beginning, the goal of this challenge was clearly set as
to advance computer vision methodology and achieve vision-level intelligence (Deng et al., 2009).
The challenge tasked researchers with classifying objects (e.g., animal species) from a standardized
dataset, the ImageNet, using accuracy as the common evaluation metric. In this challenge, mech-
anization efforts include hyperparameter tuning of known models (e.g., adjusting learning rates,
batch sizes, regularization terms, or other hyperparameters for existing algorithms), combining off-
the-shelf methods (e.g., using standard classification techniques such as random forests and logistic
regression and combining their outputs via simple ensembling, without developing new architec-
tures), and modifying image features superficially (e.g., resizing and rotating images). While these
tactics earned top performers recognition, they contributed little to major innovations and fell short
of the challenge’s goals.
A turning point came in 2012, when Krizhevsky et al. (2012) introduced a highly original deep
convolutional neural network (CNN) that boosted ImageNet accuracy to 85%, over 10% higher
than the second-best of that year on the leaderboard (ImageNet, 2012). This type of effort that in-
volves creativity and more generalizable is what we call creative effort. During the entire ImageNet
Challenge spanning 8 years from 2010 to 2017, there were multiple milestones of novel methodolog-
ical advances that overcame fundamental limitations of previous methods and led to more effective
computer vision technologies, as the contest organizers had aimed for.
Developing a contest model to analyze benchmark hacking in ML competitions faces two main
challenges. First, we need a realistic yet tractable game-theoretic framework to capture contestants’
allocation of effort between creation and mechanization. Second, we must formalize benchmark
hacking and then study whether it arises at equilibrium. This is particularly subtle since benchmark
hacking is a relative concept — some mechanization is natural and organic, but excessive focus on

We develop a contest model for machine learning where contestants compete on the performance
of their developed models for a prediction task.4 Each player allocates effort between creation and
mechanization.
While both efforts can improve contest performance on the specific prediction
task and thus rewards, only creation drives innovation and generalizability. For this reason, the
principal only benefits from the creation. Therefore, the principal faces a moral hazard problem
(Grossman and Hart, 1992; Holmstr¨om, 1979) — that is, she cannot distinguish between the two
types of contestant efforts and can only observe a signal about the overall fitness of the model to
the particular contest task, which is an aggregated outcome of both efforts.
Each contestant has a private type, interpreted as the capability for model development. While
mechanization improves any contestant’s contest reward in the same way, creation improves a
contestant’s reward differently for different types. In particularly, under the same level of creation,
more capable contestants develop a model with higher quality. This heterogeneous capability in
model development leads to different effort allocation strategies for different types of contestants
at the contest’s Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Under reasonable assumptions, we prove the existence
of a symmetric monotone pure strategy Nash equilibrium.
We then apply our model to study benchmark hacking in machine learning contests.
Our
fundamental hypothesis is that every contestant is mature enough such that if the learning task
is appointed solely to this contestant alone, then this contestant’s optimal choice of creation and
mechanization will be an organic baseline with no benchmark hacking. This hypothesis is based
on the fact that most contestants on platforms like Kaggle and AIcrowd are mature data scientist
and machine learning researcher, hence their optimal effort allocation should be appropriate if they
were assigned to complete a task alone in absence of any competition. We say a contestant engages
in benchmark hacking if he mechanizes more creates less in the contest setting compared to the
baseline.
Under this new definition, we find that contestants with types below certain threshold (low
types) always engage in benchmark hacking, whereas those above the threshold don’t.
As the
contest reward skews towards top-ranked contestants, low types mechanize more while high types
create more.
We also inform ML contest design, suggesting that a more skewed (favoring top
performers) reward structure can incentivize the development of winning models that are not only
better-fitted to the contest task but also more innovative and generalizable to future applications.
We are motivated by the concern of benchmark hacking in ML contests. Hence our model and
analysis tightly reflect this fundamental question. While some economic settings also involve two
types of efforts — e.g., a manager choosing between efforts toward short-term and long-term goals

Literature Review
Manipulation in machine learning is a widely studied problem in AI. However, to our knowledge,
most AI research thus far looks to detect benchmark hacking (also called reward hacking and speci-
fication gaming) behaviors of AI agents (e.g., Deshpande et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), or design
models robust to strategic behavior by downstream users or data providers (Ahmadi et al., 2021;
Bj¨orkegren et al., 2020; Freeman et al., 2020; Hardt et al., 2016; Perdomo et al., 2020). While shar-
ing this goal, we address a different problem — analyzing ML contests — and focus on contestants’
incentives rather than those of AI agents and end users.
Contests are widely used to deliver innovative products and solutions (e.g., Chesbrough et al.,
2006), and prior research has examined their design features, such as prize structures (Clark and Riis,
1998), number of contestants (Bhattacharya, 2021), allocation rules (Fullerton and McAfee, 1999;
Moldovanu and Sela, 2001), and information disclosure (Lemus and Marshall, 2021; Rieck, 2010),
to optimize outcome quality.
Our paper departs by analyzing players’ effort allocation across
multiple tasks and, uniquely, formalizes the notion of benchmark hacking arising from misaligned
principal-agent incentives in a game-theoretic framework.
As for modeling, our model relates to the classic literature on multi-tasking, where agents allo-
cate effort across multiple tasks (e.g., B´enabou and Tirole, 2016; Holmstrom and Milgrom, 1991).
Multi-tasking has been studied in contexts such as firm management (Narayanan, 1985), academic
publishing (Ellison, 2002), and pharmaceutical innovation (Bryan et al., 2022). Unlike this litera-
ture, which typically examines a single agent deciding between tasks that all benefit the principal, we
study a multi-agent contest where only one type of effort benefits the principal. Our division of effort
between creation and mechanization is partly motivated by the rise of generative AI and its rapid,
large-scale deployment in the workplace (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Massenkoff and McCrory,
2026), including its growing role in machine learning tasks (e.g., Chan et al., 2024). However, this
is the first paper to formalize which ML techniques are readily mechanizable and automatable
by AI agents and which are not—an essential distinction for understanding how AI tools are re-
shaping skill demands and competitive dynamics in machine learning competitions. Our model
also shares similarities with moral hazard problems in principal-agent theory (Grossman and Hart,
1992; Holmstr¨om, 1979), but differs in allowing agents to choose the levels of two distinct efforts
rather than a single action.
Our paper also contributes to the literature on gaming behaviors. Brodeur et al. (2020) stud-
ies “p-hacking” in data manipulation,5 while Ball (2022) and Frankel and Kartik (2019) examine
gaming in signaling games, and Perez-Richet and Skreta (2022) and Li and Qiu (2023) analyze
fraud-proof information design and gaming in contests.

Creative vs Mechanistic Effort
In Section 1, we have defined creation as creating new tools or fundamentally adapting methods
and mechanization as using existing tools or methods in the context of ML contests. To clarify the
distinction between the two, we pick a specific Kaggle competition, labeling the main techiques in
its winning team’s solution as either creative or mechanistic and providing rationales for it.
We start by defining machine learning contest data pipeline as the end-to-end workflow that
turns raw competition data into a final submission. Such a data pipeline typically consists of four
stages: data pre-processing and feature engineering, model training, hyperparameter tuning and
ensembling, and model evaluation. Table 1 summarizes the purpose and practices of each stage.
Having formalized the data pipeline, we now consider the Kaggle competition Predicting Heart
Disease, in which contestants are provided with a tabular dataset including features of age, sex,
chest pain type, blood pressure etc., and a label indicating whether the patient has heart disease,

with the goal of predicting the likelihood of heart disease.6 We map the solution of the first-place
team, Masaya Kawamata, to each of the four stages of the data pipeline and classify the techniques
used at each stage as either creative or mechanistic. Table 2 presents the techniques used in the
data preprocessing and feature engineering stage and provides classification and rationale for each of
them. For example, DVAE is a well-known technique in generative modeling designed to compress
high-dimensional data (e.g., images) into a compact, typically nonlinear representation. Recogniz-
ing that this tool was originally developed for a different purpose, the contestant repurposed and
adapted it to generate additional input features for tabular data. This constitutes a creative and
non-obvious adaptation of an established tool and is therefore classified as creative. In contrast,
genetic programming is a standard and widely used feature engineering technique that automati-
cally searches the space of possible symbolic transformations and can be readily implemented using
the Python package gplearn. As it is often applied without domain-specific customization, it is
therefore classified as mechanistic. The classification of techniques in model training and model
evaluation is provided in the Appendix A. In general, the contestant applied a mix of creative
and mechanistic techniques across all stages of the data pipeline, with emphasis on creation in the
model training and evaluation stages.
The division of effort into creative and mechanistic categories is motivated by both practical
and conceptual considerations. First, the two types of effort are qualitatively distinct in nature:
mechanistic effort is largely mechanical and procedural—applying established tools, running stan-
dard pipelines, and tuning parameters within known frameworks—while creative effort demands
creativity, domain insight, and the ability to reframe problems in non-obvious ways. A data sci-
entist who invests heavily in creation, as Kawamata did by repurposing DVAE for tabular feature
generation or deliberately constructing diverse feature sets to support ensembling, will tend to pro-
duce solutions that are not only higher-performing but also more robust and generalizable, since
such innovations often capture structure in the data that standard techniques miss. Second, the
distinction has become increasingly consequential in the era of AI-assisted development: many
mechanistic tasks—encoding categorical variables, running cross-validation, tuning hyperparam-
eters of standard models—can now be automated or substantially accelerated by tools such as
AutoML systems, large language model-based coding assistants, and automated feature engineer-
ing libraries (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Massenkoff and McCrory, 2026).7 By distinguishing the two
types of effort, we gain a sharper lens through which to assess how AI tools are reshaping the skill
requirements and competitive dynamics of machine learning contests: if AI increasingly substitutes
for mechanistic effort, the returns to creation may rise, and understanding what drives a contes-
tant’s allocation between the two becomes all the more important. These motivations inform the
game-theoretic model we develop in the next section, where creation and mechanization enter the
performance function in structurally distinct ways.

Intuitively, the incremental gain from increasing the skewness of the prize vector is greater when a

vector disproportionately rewards top performers. Hence, a more skewed prize vector incentivizes

Model Validation with Kaggle Data
In this section, we validate our model predictions using public data on featured competitions hosted
by a popular ML contest platform Kaggle (Lemus and Marshall, 2021).11
In a typical Kaggle
competition, there is a prediction task and players compete on the performance of their designed
ML models for it. Players are given a training dataset containing both features and labels, based
on which they build an ML model. After that players submit the model to test its performance on
the preliminary test dataset that is sampled from the same distribution as the training dataset but
is not disclosed to the players. Rather, players are only told about the prediction accuracy of their
submitted model on this test set. After seeing it, players may refine their model and submit again.
At the end of the contest, each player is required to submit a final model, whose performance will
be evaluated on the main test dataset that is from the same distribution as the training set and
preliminary test set but contains different observations. The prediction accuracy on the main test
set determines the rankings and the winner(s).
Data.
To ensure that the prediction accuracy is comparable across contests, we select contests
in which the prediction task is binary classification and the prizes are in U.S. dollars. Eventually,
our dataset contains 26 contests. The unit of observation is a contest-player-submission pair, with
contest indexed by j, player by i and submission by k. For each contest, the variables we observe
include number of players (usually around 1000∼3000), number of prizes (usually around 1∼4),
prize values (usually within 10K∼70K), contest year (within 2010∼2020) and duration (usually
spanning 1∼3 months), etc. At player level, Kaggle has a feature called Notebook which counts
how many upvotes a player receives. The number of upvotes is independent of the player’s contest
performance and is the best proxy for his expertise type (categorical from 0 (lowest) to 4 (highest)).
In addition, we observe the date and prediction score (typically between 60% and 95%) for every
submission made by each player in each contest.
Detailed data statistics are reported in the
appendices.

Conclusion
This paper (1) develops a game-theoretic model to study misaligned incentives between contest
hosts seeking high-quality ML solutions and contestants aiming to win prizes by strategically al-
locating creative and mechanistic effort, (2) formally defines benchmark hacking in ML contests
and examines its presence in equilibrium, and (3) tests the model’s predictions using Kaggle com-
petition data.
Under this new definition, our results suggest that low-type contestants engage
in benchmark hacking while high types do not. A more skewed prize structure encourages more
creation, less mechanization, and higher model fitness. These findings feature the heterogeneity
among contestants regarding their tendency to benchmark hacking and the degree of mis-aligned
incentives with contest hosts. We view this work as a step toward a more concrete characterization
of optimal ML contest design that could better addressing misaligned incentives and producing
more desirable outcomes for contest organizers.
