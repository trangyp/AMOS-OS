Abstract
Enterprise adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) is constrained by hallucination,
domain drift, and the inability to enforce regulatory compliance at the reasoning level. We
present a neurosymbolic architecture implemented within the Foundation AgenticOS
(FAOS) platform that addresses these limitations through ontology-constrained neural rea-
soning. We introduce a three-layer ontological framework—Role, Domain, and Interaction
ontologies—grounding LLM-based enterprise agents. We formalize asymmetric neurosym-
bolic coupling: current enterprise systems constrain agent inputs (context assembly, tool
discovery, governance thresholds) but not outputs, and we propose mechanisms extending
this coupling to output-side validation (response checking, reasoning verification, compli-
ance enforcement). A controlled experiment (1,800 runs across five industries and three
LLMs: Claude Sonnet 4, Qwen 2.5 72B, Gemma 4 26B) finds ontology-coupled agents
significantly outperform ungrounded agents on Metric Accuracy (p < .001) and Role Con-
sistency (p < .001) across all three models with large effect sizes (Kendall’s W = .46–.64).
Improvements are greatest where LLM parametric knowledge is weakest—particularly in
Vietnam-localized domains, where ontology lift is 2× that of English domains. Contribu-
tions: (1) a formal three-layer enterprise ontology model; (2) a taxonomy of neurosymbolic
coupling patterns; (3) ontology-constrained tool discovery via SQL-pushdown scoring; (4) a
proposed framework for output-side ontological validation; (5) empirical evidence for the in-
verse parametric knowledge effect—ontological grounding value is inversely proportional to
LLM training-data coverage of the domain; (6) cross-model replication establishing model-
independence; (7) a production system serving 22 industry verticals with 650+ agents.

1.1
Enterprise Grounding
Enterprise AI agents face a unique variant of the grounding problem [Harnad, 1990]. Unlike
general-purpose assistants, enterprise agents must:
(i) Speak the domain language: Use industry-specific terminology correctly (e.g., “com-
bined ratio” in insurance, “net revenue retention” in SaaS, “average length of stay” in
healthcare).
(ii) Reason within regulatory bounds: Ensure outputs comply with applicable regulations
(SOX, Basel III/IV, HIPAA, GDPR, EU AI Act [European Parliament and Council, 2024]).
(iii) Follow organizational workflows: Respect handoff protocols, approval chains, and
escalation paths defined by the organization.
(iv) Adapt to role-specific perspectives: A CFO and a product manager examining the
same data should receive analyses framed through their respective decision-making lenses.
Current approaches address these requirements through prompt engineering [Wei et al., 2022,
Wang et al., 2023], retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [Lewis et al., 2020, Gao et al., 2024],
and fine-tuning [Hu et al., 2022]. While effective for individual dimensions, none provides a uni-
fied formal framework for enterprise grounding. Prompt engineering is brittle and unverifiable;
RAG retrieves but does not reason; fine-tuning is expensive and domain-locked.

Background and Related Work
2.1
Neurosymbolic AI
Neural–symbolic integration has been a persistent research theme since the connectionist-symbolic
debates of the 1980s [Fodor and Pylyshyn, 1988], catalyzed recently by deep learning matura-
tion, knowledge representation standards (OWL, RDF, SKOS), and the emergence of LLMs
as general-purpose reasoning engines.
Garcez and Lamb [2023] distinguish symbolic[neural],
neural[symbolic], and neural:symbolic (bidirectional) integration; our work falls primarily in
neural[symbolic] with proposals toward neural:symbolic. A systematic review of 167 NeSyAI
papers [Colelough and Regli, 2025] confirms the convergence. Hitzler and Sarker [2022] argue
that ontologies provide a natural formalism for this integration—type hierarchies, property con-
straints, and inference rules that can bound neural computation; we extend the argument to
the enterprise domain, where ontologies must also capture organizational roles and interaction
protocols.
2.2
Knowledge-Grounded Language Models
Knowledge grounding for LLMs has been pursued through four main mechanisms. Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) augments LLM context with retrieved documents [Lewis
et al., 2020, Gao et al., 2024], addressing factual grounding but not structural or regulatory
constraints. Knowledge Graph Integration [Pan et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2022] provides re-
lational grounding but typically lacks the formal semantics of ontological reasoning. Ontology-
Enhanced Prompting uses ontological definitions to structure prompts [Babaei Giglou et al.,
2023]; Liu et al. [2025a] extend this with OntoTune (WWW 2025), aligning LLMs through in-
context learning on SNOMED CT. Constrained Decoding restricts output tokens to gram-
matical or structural constraints [Willard and Louf, 2023]—syntactic rather than semantic con-
trol. Broader surveys of tool-augmented LLMs [Mialon et al., 2023] and hybrid architectures
[Marcus, 2020] argue for neural–symbolic combination; Kambhampati et al. [2024] demonstrate
that LLMs struggle with planning without external symbolic scaffolding.
Empirical work has begun to validate these approaches quantitatively: Agrawal et al. [2024]
survey KG-augmentation for hallucination reduction; Venkatesh and Radha Krishna [2026] show
ontology-grounded LLMs exhibit reduced digression in industrial conversation; da Cruz et al.
[2025] find ontology-guided KG construction outperforms vector retrieval on specialized domains;
and Sansford et al. [2024] propose a KG-based framework for triple-level hallucination measure-
ment. Our work synthesizes these within a unified multi-layer ontological framework and targets
enterprise-specific tasks that test the boundaries of parametric knowledge.
2.3
Ontology-Grounded RAG and Hallucination Mitigation
Recent work demonstrates dramatic improvements from ontological grounding over baseline
RAG: Sharma et al. [2025] propose OG-RAG (EMNLP 2025), achieving +55% fact recall and
+40% response correctness across four LLMs via ontology-anchored hypergraph retrieval; Ali
et al. [2026] report 98% clinical QA accuracy (vs. 37% for ChatGPT-4) through RDF/OWL
knowledge graphs, reducing hallucination from 63% to 1.7%; Amayuelas et al. [2025] ground each
reasoning step in KG data for +26.5% over chain-of-thought. These results validate ontological
structure over unstructured retrieval, but all three use single-layer domain ontologies.
Our
three-layer framework extends this by encoding not just what to know (domain), but how to
reason (role) and when to delegate (interaction).

2.4
Neurosymbolic Integration Taxonomies
Yang et al. [2025b] (IJCAI) propose a three-way taxonomy: Symbolic→LLM, LLM→Symbolic,
and LLM+Symbolic; Hakim et al. [2026] provide a PRISMA-based survey of 178 neurosymbolic
agentic AI papers. Our work falls primarily in Symbolic→LLM at L1–L3 with proposals toward
LLM+Symbolic at L4–L5. Neither survey distinguishes input-side from output-side coupling—a
distinction central to our asymmetric coupling analysis (Section 4).
2.5
Parametric Knowledge and Contextual Interference
A growing literature documents the complex interaction between parametric and injected knowl-
edge.
Lin et al. [2026] demonstrate LLMs become “overly reliant on external information,
suppressing internal parametric knowledge” (contextual interference); their Knowledgeable-R1
achieves +22.89% by explicitly resisting this displacement. Du et al. [2025] show that even with
perfect retrieval, performance degrades 13.9–85% as context length increases—context volume
imposes a cost independent of content quality. Tang et al. [2025] show parametric representa-
tions operate at deeper feed-forward layers than token-level context; Zhao et al. [2025] (NeurIPS
spotlight) demonstrate parametric and contextual knowledge route through distinct attention
heads, with aligned knowledge accumulating across layers—the architectural basis for why in-
jection can displace rather than supplement parametric knowledge. These findings collectively
ground our Inverse PKE: grounding helps most where parametric knowledge is weakest, and can
actively harm performance where it is strong—a non-obvious prediction our 5-industry experi-
ment confirms.
2.6
Enterprise Ontologies
Enterprise ontology engineering has a rich history, from the Toronto Virtual Enterprise (TOVE)
[Fox et al., 1996] to the Financial Industry Business Ontology (FIBO) maintained by the EDM
Council [Bennett, 2013]. Upper ontologies such as the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) [Arp et al.,
2015] and DOLCE [Borgo et al., 2022] provide foundational categories for domain ontology
alignment.
However, existing enterprise ontologies were designed for data integration and semantic in-
teroperability, not for constraining AI agent behavior. A recent industry effort, the Agentic
Ontology of Work (AOW) [Skan AI, 2026], defines eight entity types (Agents, Skills, Intents,
Contexts, Policies, Memory, Confidence, Outcomes) for enterprise agent orchestration—the clos-
est industry parallel to our framework, though AOW focuses on workflow automation rather than
neurosymbolic grounding of LLM reasoning. We extend the enterprise ontology tradition with
three additional layers: role ontologies (how domain actors reason), interaction ontologies (how
actors coordinate), and governance constraints (what regulatory bounds apply).
2.7
Multi-Agent Systems and Coordination
The multi-agent systems (MAS) literature provides formal frameworks for agent coordination
[Guo et al., 2024]. However, classical MAS assumes agents with well-defined utility functions
and communication protocols. LLM-based agents introduce stochastic behavior that challenges
these assumptions. Concurrent work addresses this gap from different angles: Zhou et al. [2025]
propose Metagent-P, a neuro-symbolic planning agent that uses symbolic verification to ensure
reasoning correctness before execution, while Peer and Stabinger [2025] decouple agent architec-
ture into offline symbolic knowledge base ingestion and online deterministic execution (ATA).
Our work addresses the gap by using ontological constraints to bound the stochastic behav-
ior of LLM agents within formally defined operational envelopes, with a focus on enterprise
multi-industry deployment rather than open-world planning.

Three-Layer Enterprise Ontology Framework
We define an enterprise ontology O as a triple:
O = ⟨R, D, I⟩
(1)
where R is the Role Ontology, D is the Domain Ontology, and I is the Interaction Ontology.
Each layer serves a distinct function in grounding agent behavior.
3.1
Layer 1: Role Ontology (R)
The Role Ontology encodes how specific organizational roles think, decide, and communicate.
Formally:
R = {r1, r2, . . . , rn}
(2)
where each role ri is defined as a tuple:
ri = ⟨id, δi, µi, σi, ϵi, αi⟩
(3)
• δi: Decision patterns — a set of reasoning strategies (e.g., {strategic, data-driven, risk-averse})
• µi: Metrics focus — domain-specific KPIs the role prioritizes (e.g., {ARR, NPS, churn_rate})
• σi: Communication style — output framing (e.g., executive, technical, compliance-
oriented)
• ϵi: Expertise domains — knowledge areas the role is authoritative in
• αi: Approval authority — decisions the role can authorize
Example: Role Definition (Product Manager in SaaS)
product_manager:
decision_patterns: [strategic, user-centric, data-driven]
metrics_focus: [ARR, NPS, feature_adoption, churn_rate]
communication_style: executive
expertise_domains: [product_strategy, user_research]
approval_authority: [feature_releases, roadmap_changes]
The Role Ontology enables perspective-aware reasoning: the same query, posed to agents
grounded in different roles, produces analyses framed through the role’s decision patterns and
metric priorities—ontologically grounded persona specification with formal properties:
Property 1 (Role Consistency). For query q and role ri, if q invokes metric-relevant or decision-
relevant reasoning, the response a must reference metrics m ∈µi and apply decision patterns
d ∈δi.

Layer 2: Domain Ontology (D)
The Domain Ontology captures industry-specific concepts, their relationships, and associated
metrics. Formally:
D = ⟨V, E, M, G⟩
(4)
• V: Verticals — industry segments organized hierarchically (e.g., fintech.payments.
card_networks)

3.3
Layer 3: Interaction Ontology (I)
The Interaction Ontology formalizes organizational workflows as typed handoff patterns between
roles, I = ⟨H, A, P⟩: H is the set of handoff patterns (directed edges between roles with
trigger conditions, required artifacts, and approval flags); A is the set of approval chains
(ordered sequences of roles that must authorize decisions, with timeout constraints); P is the
set of escalation paths (fallback routing when conditions exceed an agent’s authority). Each
handoff h = ⟨rfrom, rto, τ, ϕ, β⟩specifies trigger τ, required artifacts ϕ, and approval requirement
β—e.g., a design-to-development handoff from ux_designer to senior_developer triggered
on design_complete, requiring UI designs, design specs, and flows, with approval by product
manager as escalation path.
3.4
Ontology Composition Across Industries
A key property is composability: the same structural schema supports radically different in-
dustries by varying per-layer content, e.g., Ofintech = ⟨Rfintech, Dfintech, Ifintech⟩vs. Ohealthcare =
⟨Rhealthcare, Dhealthcare, Ihealthcare⟩. The FAOS platform currently instantiates this framework
across 22 industry verticals (banking, healthcare, fintech, manufacturing, insurance, software,
retail, and 15 others),1 demonstrating that the three-layer schema is industry-invariant while
the content is industry-specific.

Process-side coupling constrains the reasoning process itself. In FAOS, this is partially imple-

• Autonomy gates: A block-first approval model where sensitive operations require human

• Quality judge verification: A post-reasoning verification node in the agent execution

• Escalation mechanisms: When agent confidence falls below thresholds, the system
escalates to a supervisory agent or human operator.
4.3
Output-Side Coupling (Proposed)
Output-side coupling would constrain LLM outputs against ontological definitions after gener-
ation. This is the primary research frontier and is not yet implemented in FAOS. We formalize
the requirements in Section 7.

System Architecture
The Foundation AgenticOS (FAOS) is a multi-tenant enterprise platform (Python/FastAPI,
LangGraph orchestration, PostgreSQL, Redis, Qdrant) comprising 300+ modules organized into
seven bounded contexts with Anti-Corruption Layers between domains following DDD [Evans,
2004]: Ontology Engine (three-layer resolution, validation, context injection), Skill Registry
(unified registry with semantic discovery), Agent Orchestration (LangGraph StateGraph with
parallel branching and quality verification), Outcome Tracker (month-partitioned immutable
event store with provenance chains), Tenant Manager (five-layer isolation: database, cache,
events, API, configuration), Context Engine (priority-ordered assembly with token-budget
management), and Governance (autonomy gates, cost tracking, approval workflows).

7
Toward Closed-Loop Neurosymbolic Reasoning
The current FAOS architecture implements input-side neurosymbolic coupling (L2–L3). We
propose extensions toward output-side validation (L4) and closed-loop reasoning (L5), formalized
architecturally but not yet implemented.
7.1
Output-Side Validation and Constraint Checking
We propose an OntologyValidator component that checks LLM outputs against Domain On-
tology constraints:
Definition 1 (Ontological Compliance). An agent response a is ontologically compliant with
domain d ∈D if:
(a) All domain terms in a are defined in d.E (terminological consistency).
(b) All metric references in a cite values within defined ranges in d.M (metric validity).
(c) All workflow references in a follow handoff patterns in I.H (interaction compliance).
(d) All regulatory claims in a reference frameworks in d.G (governance alignment).

For industries with formal regulatory requirements, lightweight OWL reasoning can verify
output compliance:
valid(a, O) ⇐⇒O ̸|= ¬a
(11)
That is, an output is valid if and only if the ontology does not entail its negation—requiring
translation of relevant Domain Ontology portions into OWL axioms executed by a description
logic reasoner (e.g., HermiT, ELK). A complementary decoding-time approach: Luo et al. [2025]
propose Graph-Constrained Reasoning (ICML 2025), using a KG-Trie index to constrain LLM
decoding to valid knowledge graph paths. GCR operates at the token level, our proposed valida-
tor at the semantic level—the two are complementary. Shen et al. [2025] achieve 99.5% schema
accuracy through model-agnostic post-processing (SLOT), providing syntactic structuring that
could serve as a preprocessing stage for semantic validation.
7.2
Ontology Evolution from Agent Experience
A more ambitious L5 extension is ontology evolution: agents encounter concepts outside the cur-
rent ontology (discovery), the system proposes extensions from recurring unrecognized concepts
(proposal), domain experts review and approve (validation), and approved extensions are merged
with provenance tracking (integration). This creates a learning ontology that improves with use,
closing the loop between neural experience and symbolic knowledge. The architectural impli-
cation generalizes an emerging neurosymbolic intuition: hallucinations arise at perception, not
generation—so L1–L3 coupling constrains agent perception via grounding, and L4–L5 extensions
would additionally constrain generation via validation.

Experimental Design
Conditions.
We compare four grounding conditions corresponding to maturity levels in our
coupling taxonomy (Section 4):
1. C1 – Ungrounded (L0): System prompt only; no domain context.
2. C2 – RAG-Only: Unstructured text chunks extracted from ontology blueprint docu-
ments (8 curated paragraphs per industry, ∼2,000 tokens total, injected as flat reference
text).
3. C3 – Ontology-Coupled (L2): Structured three-layer injection (Role + Domain +
Interaction) via the FAOS PromptInjector format (∼2,800–3,200 tokens depending on
industry, vs. ∼2,000 for C2, reflecting structural overhead from section headers, typed
property-value pairs, and metric range definitions).
4. C4 – Ontology+Process (L3): C3 plus a post-generation quality judge that scores
output quality and flags sub-threshold responses for escalation.
Task Set.
50 tasks across five regulated industries—FinTech (BSA-AML), Insurance (state-

RAG vs. Ontology
C2 (RAG) is competitive with—and on TF superior to—C3 (Ontology): on the primary model,

9
Discussion
9.1
Why Ontologies, Not Just RAG?
A natural question is whether formal ontologies add value beyond well-organized RAG. On the
significant metrics (MA, RC, RS), structured ontology injection (C3) and RAG (C2) achieved
comparable scores, and on the primary model’s grand means C2 scores at or above C3 on TF,
MA, and RC; only RS shows a consistent ontology advantage. Our claim is therefore not that
ontologies uniformly outperform RAG at the lexical level, but that they provide categorically
different capabilities that a document-chunk retrieval pipeline cannot:
1. Structural constraints: Ontologies define relationships (hierarchies, handoff patterns,
approval chains) that flat documents cannot express. Full governance enforcement requires
machine-readable ontological definitions.
2. Composability: The three-layer framework allows independent evolution of roles, do-
mains, and interactions. Adding a new role does not require rewriting domain documen-
tation.
3. Verifiability: Ontological definitions are machine-readable and can be checked program-
matically. RAG documents are opaque to formal verification—critical at coupling levels
L4–L5, where output-side validation requires structured definitions.
Cross-model replication provides empirical support: on Gemma 4, the C2→C3 comparison
for Role Consistency was significant (pcorr = .006)—the only metric×model combination where
ontology achieved pairwise significance over RAG. This suggests the structural value of ontolo-
gies manifests most clearly on metrics requiring relational reasoning rather than lexical recall.
Our findings participate in a broader convergence: Ali et al. [2026] report 98% clinical QA accu-
racy via RDF/OWL knowledge graphs, Amayuelas et al. [2025] improve reasoning by +26.5%
through step-level KG grounding, and Gaurav et al. [2025] propose a Governance-as-a-Service
framework complementary to our ontology-driven approach. Table 5 contrasts RAG, fine-tuning,
and ontological grounding on enterprise-deployment dimensions.
9.2

9.3
Why C3 and C4 Are Indistinguishable
A notable non-result is the absence of significant C3→C4 improvement on any metric (pcorr >
.07). We attribute this to experimental design: the C4 quality judge scored outputs and flagged
sub-threshold responses for escalation, but did not alter the response itself—by design, for fair
within-subject comparison. In production deployment, escalation routes low-quality interactions
to supervisory agents or human operators, producing a qualitatively different user experience
not captured by single-turn scoring. Future evaluation should measure C4’s value through user-
facing metrics (escalation rate, downstream task success).

9.5
Threats to Validity
1. Ontology completeness: If a domain concept is missing, grounding is incomplete. Main-
tenance cost scales with regulatory change velocity.
2. OWL reasoning practicality: Proposed output-side validation via description logic rea-
soners (§7) requires translating free-text LLM outputs into OWL-compatible representations—
itself an error-prone process.
3. Single-system analysis: Findings are based on one platform (FAOS); cross-model repli-
cation (Section 9.4) addresses model-dependence but replication on CrewAI, AutoGen, or
standalone LangGraph is future work.
4. LLM-as-judge reliability: We rely on an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4) rather than
human experts. Schroeder and Wood-Doughty [2024], Gu et al. [2024], and Szymanski
et al. [2025] document LLM-judge failure modes; the latter specifically report SME–LLM
agreement of only 64–68% on expert tasks. We mitigate with three repetitions, tempera-
ture 0.0, and structured JSON output, but have not yet validated inter-rater reliability (κ)
against domain experts. As a sensitivity envelope: under a pessimistic 35% disagreement
rate, our MA grand-mean effect (∆C1→C3 = +.37; C1= .372, C3= .740) would compress

Conclusion and Future Work
We have presented a neurosymbolic architecture for enterprise AI agents that uses a three-layer
ontological framework (Role, Domain, Interaction) to ground LLM reasoning in formal domain
knowledge. Our primary contribution is the formalization of asymmetric neurosymbolic cou-
pling—the observation that current enterprise systems constrain agent inputs but not outputs—
and the proposal of a closed-loop neurosymbolic framework extending ontological constraints to
output validation and ontology evolution. Our framework distinguishes implemented mech-
anisms (L1–L3: three-layer ontology, context injection, tool discovery, governance thresholds,
19


## Page 20


autonomy gates, quality judge—deployed across 22 industry verticals with 650+ specialized
agents) from proposed extensions (L4–L5: output-side validation, OWL constraint checking,
supervised ontology evolution—formalized architecturally but not yet empirically evaluated).
Our empirical evaluation across 1,800 runs (600 per model) spanning five regulated industries
and three LLM architectures (Claude Sonnet 4, Qwen 2.5 72B, Gemma 4 26B) provides strong,
model-independent evidence for ontological grounding. Ontology-coupled agents significantly
outperformed ungrounded agents on Metric Accuracy (p < .001, W = .460) and Role Consis-
tency (p < .001, W = .614) across all three models; Regulatory Compliance was model-sensitive
(significant on Claude and Qwen, p = .051 on Gemma). The strongest improvements appear
where LLM parametric knowledge is weakest: Vietnamese industries showed 2× the improve-
ment of English-language domains (∆= +.29 vs. +.12), replicating across all three models. This
pattern supports the claim that ontological grounding is operationally important for non-English
enterprise domains where parametric coverage is structurally insufficient. The multi-tenant na-
ture of FAOS introduces an additional neurosymbolic challenge (ontological polymorphism): the
same three-layer schema must support distinct domain instantiations per tenant—a direction
for continued architectural work.
Future work targets four areas:
1. Layer ablation: factorial design isolating the contribution of each ontology layer (R, D,
I) to each metric.
2. Implementing L4 validation: building and evaluating the OntologyValidator, focus-
ing on latency–quality trade-offs; our companion work [Luong Tuan and Sanyal, 2026]
provides the simulation and certification framework.
3. Adaptive context injection: selective injection based on estimated parametric knowl-
edge κ(d) to address the TF regression on well-known concepts. Self-RAG [Asai et al.,
2024] uses reflection tokens to decide when to retrieve; analogous when to inject mecha-
nisms are formalized as an information-theoretic optimization in [Luong Tuan, 2026a].
4. Full-scale evaluation: human-expert validation (κ inter-rater reliability), noisy RAG
baselines, and additional industry verticals.
Neurosymbolic architectures are not merely beneficial but necessary for enterprise AI agents
in regulated industries. The question is not whether to integrate symbolic and neural reasoning,
but how to do so with minimal latency, maximal coverage, and provable guarantees.
