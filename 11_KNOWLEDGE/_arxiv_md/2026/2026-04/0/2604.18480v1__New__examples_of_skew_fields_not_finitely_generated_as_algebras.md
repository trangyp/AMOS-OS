‘NEW’ EXAMPLES OF SKEW FIELDS NOT FINITELY
GENERATED AS ALGEBRAS
K. R. GOODEARL AND E. S. LETZTER
The second author dedicates this paper to the memories of his daughter Eliana
Risman Letzter (1995–2024) and sister Dr. Gail Rebecca Letzter (1960–2024).
Abstract. An associative division algebra D is said to be affine over a central
subfield k if D is finitely generated as a k-algebra. In 1956 Amitsur famously proved
that, when k is uncountable, D cannot be k-affine unless D is algebraic over k. In
this paper we consider affineness – and nonaffineness – for certain naturally occur-
ring classes of division algebras over arbitrary fields. The primary applications are
to division algebras of fractions of suitably conditioned iterated skew polynomial
rings over k, including many examples naturally arising in Lie theoretic and quan-
tum group settings. Many transcendental division algebras are thus verified to be
nonaffine over k. Division algebras of fractions of Weyl algebras and quantum affine
spaces are determined to be affine over their centers exactly when they are finite
dimensional over their centers.
1. Introduction
For the general class of (associative) division algebras D over a central subfield
k, the question of whether or not D can be both infinite k-dimensional and k-affine
(i.e., finitely generated as a k-algebra) remains one of the oldest unsolved problems in
noncommutative algebra. The seemingly more specialized (but still open) question
of whether or not D can be infinite k-dimensional, k-affine, and also algebraic over
k is the well known Kurosh Problem for Division Algebras, first appearing in 1941
[12]. (The reader is referred, e.g., to [18] and [21] for brief historical overviews.)
Notice that there is no loss of generality in this discussion if we assume further
that D is finitely generated as a skew field over k; that is, there is a finite subset
S ⊆D such that D is the smallest division k-subalgebra of itself containing S. (We
will use the terminology finitely generated skew field to help distinguish our use of
this concept from that of affine algebra.)

The most important fundamental result on affiness of division algebras is Amitsur’s
famous proof [2], for uncountable k, that if D is not algebraic over k then D cannot
be k-affine.
Our aim in this paper, then, is to prove that, even when k is countable, the skew
fields in various wide classes of noncommutative transcendental division algebras,
finitely generated over k as skew fields, cannot be k-affine. To our knowledge, the
analysis below is the first to provide verified examples of this type. These examples
are not new as skew fields, but their nonaffineness is newly verified (hence explaining
our use of ‘new’ in the title). We also address the question of when the skew fields
in these classes can be affine over their centers.
To compare the above with the commutative case, recall that the weak Nullstel-
lensatz (Zariski’s Lemma) [20, Statement Hn
3] ensures that a (commutative) field
extension K of k is affine as a k-algebra if and only if K is finite dimensional over
k, if and only if K is affine and algebraic over k. In particular, the rational function
field k(x), for any choice of field k, is not k-affine.
We note that questions of affineness over a base field versus affineness over a center
are closely related, due to the following: If a k-division algebra D is affine over
k and finite dimensional over Z(D), then D is finite dimensional over k. Namely,
under these conditions the Artin-Tate Lemma (e.g., [14, Lemma 13.9.10]) implies
that Z(D) is affine over k. The weak Nullstellensatz then ensures that Z(D) is finite
dimensional over k, and therefore D is finite dimensional over k. Consequently, if D
is a class of division algebras for which it is known that (affine over the center =⇒
finite dimensional over the center), then the implication (affine over k
=⇒finite
dimensional over k) holds within D.
1.1. Organization. Our study below is composed of three parts. In §2, we show
that the quotient division ring of a hereditary noetherian domain cannot be affine if
the domain has infinitely many distinct isomorphism classes of simple modules. In §3
we show that the theoretical results of §2 apply to suitably conditioned iterated Ore
extensions (skew polynomial rings). In §4, the work of §3 is then applied to specific
examples arising in Lie theory and quantum groups. In particular:
Theorem A. [Theorem 4.3] Let Dn(k) be the Weyl skew field Fract An(k) for a field
k and n ∈Z>0. Then Dn(k) is affine over its center if and only if Dn(k) is finite
dimensional over its center, if and only if char k > 0. However, Dn(k) is never affine
over k.
Theorem B. [Theorems 4.2, 4.7]
Let R = Oq(kn) be a quantum affine space over a field k. Then Fract R is affine
over its center if and only if Fract R is finite dimensional over its center, if and only
if all entries of q are roots of unity. However, Fract R is never affine over k.

1.2. Notation and Conventions. Throughout, k denotes an arbitrary (commuta-
tive) field, not necessarily countable. All rings in this paper are associative with unit.
A division algebra D over a field k is finite over k if D is finite dimensional over k.
The Goldie quotient ring (semisimple ring of fractions) of a (right or left) semiprime
Goldie ring R is denoted Fract R, or Q(R) for short.
We write R[y; τ, δ] and R[y±1; τ] for skew polynomial and skew-Laurent polynomial
rings, respectively, and R(y; τ, δ) and R(y±1; τ) for their Goldie quotient rings. For
all skew polynomial rings in this paper, τ is assumed to be an automorphism. When
R is an algebra over a field k, we assume that τ and δ are k-linear, so that R[y; τ, δ],
R[y±1; τ], R(y; τ, δ) and R(y±1; τ) are also k-algebras.
2. Localizing Hereditary Domains
We make use of the torsion-theoretic localizations of HNP (hereditary noetherian
prime) rings developed in [8].
Recall that if R is an HNP ring and I is an essential right ideal of R, then R/I
has finite length (e.g., [14, Proposition 5.4.5]). Given any family X of simple right
R-modules, let SX denote the collection of those essential right ideals I of R such
that R/I has no composition factors isomorphic to any of the modules in X, and set
RX := {s ∈Q(R) | sI ⊆R for some I ∈SX}.
We first collect the required tools.
2.1. Theorem. [8] Let R be a non-artinian HNP ring and W a set containing exactly
one member of each isomorphism class of simple right R-modules.
(a) Every ring T between R and Q(R) is HNP, with Q(T) = Q(R).

2.2. Theorem. Let R be a hereditary noetherian domain which has infinitely many
isomorphism classes of simple right modules. Then Q(R) cannot be generated as a
ring by R together with finitely many elements of Q(R). In particular, Q(R) is not
affine as a Z(R)-algebra.
Proof. Let F ⊆Q(R) be finite and T the subring of Q(R) generated by R∪F. Let W
be as in Theorem 2.1, and let X be the collection of those modules in W which do not
appear as composition factors in any of the R-modules (R + fR)/R for f ∈F. Then
F ⊆RX and so T ⊆RX. Since W is infinite (by hypothesis) and F is finite, X must
be infinite. Choose a proper subset Y ⊂X. By Theorem 2.1, RX ⊊RY ⊆Q(R),
and therefore T ̸= Q(R).
□
2.3. Corollary. Let R be a hereditary noetherian domain which is an algebra over a
field k. If R has infinitely many isomorphism classes of simple right modules, then
Q(R) is not affine as a k-algebra.
□
We give two immediate applications.
2.4. Corollary. Let D[z] be a polynomial ring over a division ring D and k := Z(D).
Then D(z) is not affine over k.
Proof. First, recall that the center of R := D[z] is k[z] and that every two-sided
ideal of R is generated by an element of Z(R); see, e.g., [9, Proposition 17.1]. Next,
let f1, f2, . . . be infinitely many pairwise nonassociate, irreducible polynomials in
k[z].
We see that the ideals Rfi must be pairwise distinct maximal ideals of R.
Choose a simple right (R/Rfi)-module Ai for each i; then A1, A2, . . . are pairwise
non-isomorphic simple right R-modules. It therefore follows from Corollary 2.3 that
D(z) = Q(R) is not affine over k.
□
A second application concerns the first Weyl skew field, D1(k) = Fract A1(k), over
a field k. We deal with Dn(k) for n > 1 later (Theorem 4.3).
2.5. Example. If k is a field then D1(k) is not affine over k.
Proof. Assume first that k has characteristic zero. Then the domain A1(k) is HNP,
and by [13, Proposition 5.6], A1(k) has infinitely many isomorphism classes of simple
right modules. Thus the desired conclusion follows from Corollary 2.3.
Now assume that k has characteristic p > 0. Then A1(k) is not HNP. Instead,
view D1(k) = Q(R) where R := k(x)[y; d/dx] is a PID. Then R is a finite rank free
module over the central subalgebra S := k(xp)[yp]. Choose an infinite sequence of

The converse of Corollary 2.3 does not hold in general, as follows.
2.6. Example. Let p be a prime, K an algebraic closure of k := Fp, and τ the Frobe-
nius automorphism of K. The k-algebra R := K[y±1; τ] has only one isomorphism
class of simple right modules, but Q(R) is not affine over k, which is its center.
Proof. Cozzens [6, Theorem 2.3] and Osofsky [15, Proposition 4] showed that R has
only one isomorphism class of simple right modules. That k = Z(Q(R)) follows from,
e.g., [9, Exercise 6I].
Let F be the collection of finite subfields of K, an upward directed family whose
union is K. Each F ∈F is perfect, hence invariant under τ, and so R contains
TF := F[y±1; τ|F] as a k-subalgebra. These subalgebras form an upward directed
family whose union is R, and (Q(TF))F∈F is an upward directed family whose union
is Q(R).
If Q(R) is affine over k, then Q(R) = Q(TL) for some L ∈F. Let pn := card(L);
then τ pn|L = IdL and so ypn centralizes L. But then ypn is central in Q(TL), contra-
dicting the fact that Z(Q(R)) = k.
□
2.7. Remark. Again consider the algrebra R := K[y±1; τ] from Example 2.6, and
choose any element q ∈Q(R) \ R. It follows from Theorem 2.1 that Q(R) can be
generated as a k-algebra by R ∪{q}. In particular, while Q(R) is not affine over a
central subfield, it does hold that Q(R) can be generated as a ring by q, y, y−1 and
the (noncentral) subfield K.
In contrast to the above non-affineness results, we present one case in which affine-
ness does extend. Recall that an algebra R over a field k is matrix algebraic over k
provided all of the matrix algebras Mn(R) are algebraic over k. The question posed
by Jacobson in [10, p.695] of whether or not algebraicity implies matrix algebraicity
is still open in general; Amitsur gave a positive solution over uncountable fields in
[2].
2.8. Proposition. Let D[z] be a polynomial ring over a division ring D. If D is affine
and matrix-algebraic over its center K, then D(z) is affine over its center K(z).
Proof. Set E := K(z) ⊗K D, and note that Z(E) = K(z) ⊗1. We know that E
is a simple ring, since K(z) is simple and D is central simple over K, and so the
natural map η : E →D(z) must be injective. Therefore, E is a domain. Also, E
and η(E) are clearly K(z)-affine. Next, since D is matrix-algebraic over K, it follows
from [9, Theorem 17.2] that E is a division algebra. But η(E) is generated as a
division algebra over K by D and z, and so η(E) must equal D(z). It follows that
Z(D(z)) = η(Z(E)) = K(z) and that D(z) = η(E) is affine over K(z).
□

3. PIDs with infinitely many simple modules
In order to apply Theorem 2.2 and/or Corollary 2.3 to skew fields of the form
D(y; τ, δ), where D is a division algebra, we would like to have infinite families of
simple modules over the PIDs D[y; τ, δ].
This can be obtained from information
about eigenvalues of τ or δ, as follows.
3.1. Lemma. Let R = D[y; τ, δ] where D is a division algebra over a field k.
(a) Let a, b ∈D. Then R/(y −a)R ∼= R/(y −b)R if and only if there is some
nonzero v ∈D such that av −τ(v)b = δ(v).
(b) Assume that δ = 0, and let α, β ∈k×. Then R/(y −α)R ∼= R/(y −β)R if and
only if αβ−1 is an eigenvalue for τ.
(c) Assume that τ = IdD, and let α, β ∈k. Then R/(y −α)R ∼= R/(y −β)R if
and only if α −β is an eigenvalue for δ.
Proof. For any c ∈D, the right R-module R/(y −c)R is simple, being 1-dimensional
over D on the right.
(a) If R/(y −a)R ∼= R/(y −b)R, there is a nonzero element x ∈R/(y −a)R such
that x(y −b) = 0. We may write x = u + (y −a)R for some nonzero u ∈D, and
then u(y −b) = (y −a)v for some v ∈R. Since u(y −b) has degree 1, v lies in
D. Comparing coefficients, we find that u = τ(v) and −ub = δ(v) −av, whence
av −τ(v)b = δ(v). The converse is obtained by reversing the above steps.
(b) In view of part (a), the isomorphism occurs if and only if there is some nonzero
v ∈D such that αv = τ(v)β, that is, τ(v) = αβ−1v.
(c) In this case, the isomorphism occurs if and only if there is some nonzero v ∈D
such that δ(v) = (α −β)v.
□
3.2. Corollary. Let R = D[y; τ, δ] where D is a division algebra over a field k.
(a) Assume that δ = 0, and let Eτ be the subgroup of k× consisting of the τ-eigen-
values of the τ-eigenvectors in D. If k×/Eτ is infinite, then R has infinitely many
isomorphism classes of simple right modules.
(b) Assume that τ = IdD, and let Eδ be the subgroup of (k, +) consisting of the
δ-eigenvalues of the δ-eigenvectors in D. If (k, +)/Eδ is infinite, then R has infinitely
many isomorphism classes of simple right modules.
Proof. (a) If α, β ∈k× lie in different cosets of Eτ in k×, then by Lemma 3.1(b), the
simple right R-modules R/(y −α)R and R/(y −β)R are not isomorphic.
(b) This follows similarly from Lemma 3.1(c).
□

3.4. Theorem. [3] Let D be a division algebra over a field k, and assume that the

(a) If τ is a k-algebra automorphism of D, then the set of eigenvalues of τ is a

(b) If δ is a k-linear derivation of D, then the set of eigenvalues of δ is a finitely

3.5. Remark. The class of division algebras D over a field k for which D ⊗k Dop is
noetherian contains the skew fields of fractions of the following algebras: iterated
skew polynomial rings over k (e.g., [9, Theorem 17.25]), enveloping algebras of finite
dimensional Lie algebras over k [3, Corollary 2.5], rings of differential operators on
smooth irreducible affine varieties over k when char k = 0 [3, Corollary 2.4]. This
class also contains the stratiform division algebras introduced by Schofield [16] (c.f.
[19, Proposition 1.8]).
3.6. Theorem. Let R be either D[y; τ] or D[y; δ], where D is a division algebra over
an infinite field k. If D⊗kDop is noetherian, then R has infinitely many isomorphism
classes of simple right modules. Consequently, Q(R) is not affine over k.
Proof. The groups Eτ and Eδ of Corollary 3.2 are finitely generated by Theorem 3.4.
In view of Lemma 3.3, the quotients k×/Eτ and (k, +)/Eδ are infinite, and therefore
the theorem follows from Corollaries 3.2 and 2.3.

4.6. Lemma. If T = Oq((k×)n) is a quantum torus over a field k, then Z(Q(T)) =
Q(Z(T)).
Proof. Give T its natural Zn-grading, and observe that T is graded-simple. Conse-

a nonzero element u ∈I ∩Z(T). Then zu ∈Z(T) and so z = (zu)u−1 ∈Q(Z(T)).

Z(Q(R)) ⊆E in view of Lemma 4.6.
Since E = Q(S′) where S′ is the quantum affine space (over k) corresponding to
S, the algebra E ⊗k Eop is noetherian (Remark 3.5).

If some nonzero power of τ is inner on E, then by [11, Theorems 0,1], there exist
a nonzero element u ∈E and a positive integer n such that τ(u) = u and τ n =
u−1(−)u. It follows that uyn ∈Z(E[y±1; τ]) ⊆Z(Q(R)), which is impossible because
Z(Q(R)) ⊆E. Therefore no nonzero power of τ is inner on E.
Theorem 4.1 now implies that Q(R) is not affine over its center.
□
We conclude with the following general question: Given a k-division algebra D,
equipped with a k-linear automorphism τ and τ-derivation δ, must D(y; τ, δ) be
nonaffine over k?
