Computing Homomorphisms of Poset Representations with
Applications to Multiparameter Persistence
Jan Jendrysiak
April 14, 2026
Abstract
We present algorithms to compute the vector space of homomorphisms Hom(X, Y ) between
finitely generated representations of the partially ordered set Zd. Our results generalise to any
partially ordered set.
Our main theoretical contribution is a uniqueness result for lifts of homomorphisms along
free resolutions, which we use to obtain an algorithm running in O(n4(tY + tΩ1Y )2 + Tker(d, n))
time, where tY denotes the maximal pointwise dimension of Y and Tker is the time it takes
to compute the kernel of a map between projective Zd-modules. We also apply and analyse a
classical approach due to Green, Heath, and Struble (J. Symbolic Comput., 2001), achieving
O(n3tY 3 + n4). Both improve on the naive O(n6) bound when tY is small.
Applied to the decomposition algorithm aida (Dey–J–Kerber, SoCG ’25), the classical approach
improves the asymptotic runtime the most, strengthening the result of Dey and Xin (J. Appl.
Comput. Topology, 2022) for uniquely graded modules. We implement all algorithms in the
Persistence Algebra C++ library and benchmark them on the persistent homology of density-
alpha bi-filtrations of immune-cell locations. The classical approach has the best worst-case
complexity, yet for 2-parameter modules, the lifting algorithm is fastest in practice.

Poset Representations and Multiparameter Persistence.
Representations of partially
ordered sets are a classical subject that has been studied since the 70’s [42, 44, 30, 35]. In the past
decade, poset representations have become a central tool in applied topology through their appearance
as Persistent Homology Groups.
Persistent Homology [21] is a standard tool in Topological Data Analysis to estimate the homology
groups of a space from a finite point sample, eg. via the Vietoris-Rips or Cech complex. One of its
problems is that these constructions are sensitive to outliers. The most promising ansatz to solve
this problem [8] is to filter the complex along a second density parameter, leading to Multiparameter
Persistent Homology [13]. Its result is a Multiparameter Persistence Module (or just persistence
module) – a functor Rd →Vectk. Since persistence modules are in practice mostly finitely presented,
we can, for algebraic manipulation, assume that they are finitely presented functors Zd →VectK
instead. Let A := k[x1, . . . , xd], then these are exactly the finitely generated Zd-graded A-modules.
As with persistence modules, any finitely presented representation of a poset can be seen as a
representation over a finite poset P, which in turn can be faithfully embedded into Zd. Therefore any
statement about Zd-graded A-modules is almost verbatim true for general finitely presented poset
representations. We will therefore, without loss of generality, state all definitions and theorems in this
paper for Zd-graded A-modules. This has multiple advantages:
Most importantly, they appear in classical commutative algebra [41] and have been studied for a
long time [26]. For us, who have the application to the Persistent Homology of bi-filtrations in mind,
there is another reason. Many algebraic computations are known to be faster when d is small, so we
want to state results in relation to d. For example, when d = 2 or d = 3, this includes the computation
of kernels [33, 6] and projective resolutions of chain-complexes consisting of upset-modules [4]. Zd-
graded A-modules also have additional structure that is relevant to us; the vector space Hom(X, Y )
can be enriched to a graded A-module.
At last, we remark that this generality implies that our results hold in particular for Zig-Zag
modules (representations over ZZ - two adjacent anti-diagonals in Z2) [12] or representations over
Z × ZZ ([18].
Motivation: Decomposition of Modules.
Multiparameter persistence modules do not de-
compose into nice summands like their 1-parameter counterparts, which admit a barcode. To use
them for data analysis, one therefore uses invariants that are statistically well behaved and easy to
interpret. These are only useful if they can be computed in a practical time-frame, and this requires
fast algorithms for fundamental algebraic operations.
By Krull-Schmidt-Azumaya, finitely generated persistence modules still have a decomposition into
indecomposable summands that is unique up to permutation. Computing such a decomposition can
accelerate the computation of additive invariants. This can make otherwise intractable invariants
practically computable for large modules. We have shown this for the fibred barcode [17, Table 3]
and the Skyscraper Invariant [24]. To decompose any module X over a finite dimensional algebra,
classical algorithms [14, 36] compute the Endomorphism-algebra End(X) = Hom(X, X) and then
decompose this algebra. Algorithms specialised for persistence modules have a different strategy: the
generalized persistence algorithm [20] performs an iterative matrix reduction and works for uniquely
graded modules. In [17], we realised that this matrix reduction is equivalent to computing the set of
homomorphisms between indecomposable submodules of certain projective covers ([16, Section 4]) of
X. This allowed the development of the faster and generally applicable decomposition algorithm aida
[16, Section 5].
For both types of algorithms, the computation of homomorphisms is the bottleneck. Since our focus
is on improving aida there is an emphasis on computing homomorphisms between indecomposable
modules.

Other Applications in Topological Data Analysis.
The need to compute homomorphisms
arose in two other computational problems.
• Bjerkevik introduces the Pruning of a module in [7] which stabilises decompositions with respect
to the interleaving distance. To compute it, one needs to compute the shifted endomorphisms

Related Work.
In [19, 4.1 Algorithm Interleaving], Dey and Xin explain a strategy to compute
the set of homomorphisms between interval modules in linear time for 2 parameters and in quadratic
time in the general case. For representations over finite-dimensional algebras, optimised algorithms for
homomorphism computation are described by Lux and Szőke [37] and by Green, Heath, and Struble
in [27], the latter of which we adapt to our special case. Implementations are also found in GAP [25]
and MAGMA [10].

Duality.
In Asashiba’s construction [3] it is not the target module, but the domain which has
low thickness. Therefore, we also need to be able to compute homomorphisms faster in this case. We
utilised the ideas and results of Bauer, Lenzen, and Lesnick [5], Miller [40, 39] and Lenzen [32] to
dualise Algorithm A and Algorithm B resulting in the algorithms Algorithm A* and Algorithm B*.
Implicitly, we also get a dual version of Algorithm A-1/2.
Sparsity and Thickness.
The algorithms we will present often run faster in practice than the
theoretical bounds would suggest because the persistent homology group of classical filtered simplicial
complexes has a sparse presentation in practice. We store the presentation matrix in a list-of-columns
format, and this is why we generally use column-reduction whenever possible.
To improve the analysis of our algorithms, we wanted to quantify the sparsity of presentation
matrices in relation to the modules that they represent. In [16, Section 6] we used that interval
modules have a minimal presentation with at most 2 non-zero entries per column to show that interval
decomposable modules can be decomposed fast. We extend this observation ([16, Proposition 6.13])
to all persistence modules.
Proposition 1.1. Every persistence module X has a presentation with at most tX + 1 non-zero
entries per column.

Sparsity.
All linear systems constructed by the algorithms are as sparse as M and N, so by
Proposition 1.1 their number of entries are roughly bounded by tX and tY , too. Because of possible
fill up, this does not change the worst case run-times, but gives a heuristic about the practical run-time
of Algorithm A and B.
Intervals.
In [19], the authors sketch an algorithm which computes the set of homomorphisms
for interval modules in O(n) for d = 2 and O(n2) for d > 2. This is much faster than the algorithms
we have presented. We are thus wondering if there is an algorithm which exploits pointwise low-
dimensionality of both domain and target. On top, these algorithms for interval-modules have not
been implemented yet and so we cannot compare their practical run-time against our algorithms.
Open Problems and Future Work.
• Find a way to measure how far away a module is from having thickness k in an algebraic sense.
• Question 2: Can the idea of Algorithm A be extended to compute presentations of the persistence
module Hom(X, Y ) similar to how this is possible for Algorithm B?
• Implement the algorithms for interval-modules from [19].
• Is there an algorithm that benefits from both X and Y having low thickness?
• We conjecture that by subdividing Zd, there is a faster way to compute all cokernels of M≤g
for g ∈G in one sweep with runtime in O(dn3), instead of calling Algorithm 5 each time. This
would cut the runtime of Algorithm B down to O(mωt(Y )ω + dn3) and would similarly effect
the runtime of aida.
Summary
We have presented 3 algorithms, and their duals, to compute the vector space of degree-0 homomor-
phisms.
The classical approach in Algorithm B achieves the best worst-case complexity and is independent
of the number of parameters d. We use it to accelerate aida, thereby improving on the O(n2ω+1)
runtime of Dey and Xin [20] for decomposing modules with uniquely graded relations. The new
21


## Page 22


Algorithm A also improves on the standard computation, at least in the important case d = 2; however,
for higher d its runtime additionally depends on the complexity of computing kernels of graded
matrices, which remains unknown for d ≥3.
On large indecomposable modules coming from relevant real-world data (point sets in R2 from
immune-cell locations [46]), Algorithm A and its simpler version Algorithm A-1/2 fare substantially
better than the classical approach (Algorithm B) and the direct computation (Algorithm 1), even if
we omit the reduction to a basis in the latter (Figure 12).
Our results directly accelerate the computation of indecomposable decompositions with aida,
the computation of Bjerkevik’s Pruning [7] to stabilise decompositions (with Håvard Bjerkevik and
Fabian Lenzen 3), and the algorithm in [24] that we devised with Marc Fersztand to compute the
Skyscraper Invariant because it uses aida heavily. Because the computation of homomorphisms is such
a fundamental operation, our results promise to improve many future algorithms for Multiparameter
Persistence Modules.
At last, we want to remark again that our theorems and algorithms do not depend on Zd. Every
statement is valid for finitely presented modules over any partially ordered set.
