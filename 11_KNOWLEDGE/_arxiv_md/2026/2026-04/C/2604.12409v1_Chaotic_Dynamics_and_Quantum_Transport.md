Abstract
This chapter gives an overview of transport problems where chaotic dynamics of the system plays a crucial role. We begin with
single-particle transport problems and then come to conservative and then dissipative systems of identical particles, which follows
the historical way of developing the theory of Quantum Chaos over the past 40 years. We also include brief descriptions of key
laboratory experiments on the discussed transport problems.
1 Introduction
The field of quantum transport is too diverse to be reviewed in a single book, not talking about a single publication. Thus, to keep the chapter
length to a reasonable limit one has to narrow the subject. As it follows from the chapter title, the main criterion for choosing problems to
discuss is their relevance to Quantum Chaos [1, 2, 3]. Unfortunately, criterion of relevance to chaos alone is also not sufficient because there
are many different formulations of the transport problem depending on the considered physical system and the abilities of the laboratory
experiment. However, from the theoretical viewpoint one can suggest devision of different transport problems into three groups. The first
group includes problems of the single-particle quantum mechanics where the knowledge of the single-particle wave-function suffices to
explain results of a laboratory experiment. The second group is the many-body transport problems where inter-particle interaction is the
key ingredient. Now the wave function obeys the many-body Schr¨odinger equation and the analysis is usually done by using the second
quantization formalism. Needless to say that here one should distinguish between Bose and Fermi quantum statistics. Finally, the largest
group of transport problems refers to systems which admit description in terms of the reduced density matrix. This group includes, in
particular, the so-called boundary driven systems where the system of interest, for example, one-dimensional chain of spins is coupled at
its ends to thermal reservoirs [4, 5]. In what follows we shall provide examples of quantum transport problems from each of the above
mentioned groups and build the discussion of the related problems around these examples.
It should also be stated from the very beginning what is not considered in this chapter, i.e., the scope boundaries. First of all, we do
not review sophisticated theoretical approaches to quantum transport like the Green’s functions methods, diagram techniques, etc. and do
not address general aspects of the transport theory like fluctuation-dissipation theorem or universal conduction fluctuations. These aspects
and methods of the transport theory can be found, for example, in the books [6, 7]. In the chapter we always stay on the elementary level
and use the straightforward (analytical or numerical) methods to solve the quantum equations of motion. Finally, when discussing systems
of identical particles, we restrict ourselves to the case of Bose particles. The main reason behind this choice is that bosonic systems have
well-defined classical counterparts and, thus, we can appeal to classical mechanics to get some intuition.

3.2 Quantum Chaos in the Bose-Hubbard model
First of all we note that the Bose-Hubbard model is integrable in the case J = 0, where the system eigenstates are the number Fock states,
and in the case U = 0, where the system eigenstates are the quasimomentum Fock states given by the symmetrized product of the single-
particle Bloch states. In the general case, basing on the results of classical analysis, the Bose-Hubbard system is expected to be chaotic
in the sense of Quantum Chaos. In the other words, its energy spectrum and eigenfunctions should possess universal properties similar to
those of random matrices of the proper symmetry. For details of statistical analysis of the energy spectrum of the Hamiltonian (20) we refer
the reader to Refs. [31, 32, 33, 34]. Here we only mention that transition to chaos, which is manifested in the transition from the Poisson
to the Wigner-Dyson spectrum statistics (see Fig. 6), is a crossover over the interaction strength U. In the case ¯n ∼1 this requires U ∼J
11 and in the semiclassical case ¯n ≫1 the region of chaos in the system parameter space spanned by the energy E and the ratio g/J can be
well identified by using the Lyapunov analysis of the classical system (22).

3.6 Concluding remarks
Through the Chapter we consider only the Bose-Hubbard model. Of course, there are other models of the many-body Quantum Chaos in
the physical literature, among which the most popular with respect to quantum transport are spin chains and interacting spinless fermions
in one-dimensional lattices [4]. As compared to interacting bosons, the case of Fermi particles is simpler from the numerical viewpoint
because of smaller dimension of the Hilbert space. However, Fermi systems have no well-defined classical counterparts and, thus, they
require pure quantum methods of analysis. The spin systems place themselves between fermionic and bosonic systems. Indeed, for 1/2-
spin chains we have one to one correspondence with spinless fermions in one-dimensional lattices, while spin chains with a large spin are
closer to bosonic systems. We also would like to express that numerical proof that one or the other many-body system is chaotic in the
sense of Quantum Chaos is mainly of academic interest and it is more important to find physical manifestation of this fact, which can be
detected in a laboratory experiment. As concerns the Bose-Hubbard model, we have at least one clear manifestation – the irreversible decay
of Bloch oscillations of interacting Bose atoms. Also, signatures of chaos can be seen in the quench dynamics of the system where one
follows the time evolution of the initial state which is not the eigenstate of the Bose-Hubbard Hamiltonian. This problem was analyzed in
the theoretical work Ref. [45] which also contains references to laboratory experiments on the quench dynamics of the cold Bose atoms in
optical lattices.
4 Two-terminal transport
In this and the remaining sections we study quantum transport in the two-terminal setup. The term ‘two-terminal’ means that we have two
reservoirs of quantum particles which are connected by a lattice of the length L. We are interested in the particle current across the lattice.
Some questions to ask are dependence of the current on the strength of inter-particle interaction, on the lattice length L, on the intensity of
the decoherence process which may be present in the system, and so on. Until quite recently these questions were addressed exclusively
with respect to electrons in a solid crystal but nowadays they are readdressed with respect to cold atoms in optical potentials [46, 47] and
the microwave photons in superconducting circuits [48, 49]. It is understood that in a brief review it is impossible even to mention all
theoretical approaches to the two-terminal transport. Because of this we restrict ourselves by the single approach – the master equation for
the reduced density matrix of the bosonic particles in a lattice.

4.2 The case of fermionic carriers

operators. Then, because of different commutation relation, the prefactor (¯ni + 1) in the Lindblad operator (43) is replaced by the prefactor

6 Many-body dissipative systems with periodic driving
In the previous section we analyzed the boundary driven Bose-Hubbard model by using the Markovian master equation. Validity of this
equation implies a high temperature of reservoirs, where the Bose-Einstein distribution is almost flat, and large values of the thermalization
rate γ. In the pseudoclassical approach this situation corresponds to excitations of the edge oscillators by a broad-band noise where the
band width is larger than the width 2J of the conductance band of the lattice. In the opposite limit of low temperatures and small values of
the thermalization rate one has to use the non-Markovian master equation, which in the pseudoclassical approach corresponds to excitation
of the edge oscillators by a narrow-band noise with some characteristic frequency ω. The limiting case of the narrow-band noise is the
periodic driving of the system, where the first oscillator is excited by a monochromatic force with the frequency ω while the last oscillator
is usually considered to be pure dissipative. One meets this situation, for example, in experiments with super-conducting circuits where the
first transmon in the chain of coupled transmons is excited by the microwave generator and the transmitted signal is measured by the vector
analyzer attached to the last transmon [48, 49]. In the other words, microwave photons are injected into the first site of the Bose-Hubbard
chain and withdrawn from the last cite. An important advantage of super-conducting circuits as compared to cold Bose atoms in optical
lattices is that experimentalists can easily realize quasi one-dimensional chains like, for example, the diamond lattice or the flux rhombic
lattice. 20

6.2 Conduction transition in the flux rhombic lattice
To further highlight the power of the pseudoclassical approach we consider the conduction transition in the flux rhombic lattice. This system
is of vivid theoretical and experimental interests due to the phenomenon of the Aharonov-Bohm caging [63, 68, 69]. The rhomb lattice is
constructed by connecting L rhombs, whose vertices/sites we shall label by A, B, D,C, into a chain where the D site of the first rhomb is
identified with the A site of the second rhomb and so on. A particular interest is the case where the hopping matrix elements between A and
B sites and A and C sites have opposite signs. Then, because of complete destructive interference, the quantum particle cannot reach the D
site and, thus, the rhombic lattice is isolating independent of the magnitude of the driving force ϵ. This conclusion, however, is valid only
for non-interacting particles and for a finite g there is a critical ϵcr above which the lattice becomes conducting. We want to find this critical
strength of driving.

relevant initial conditions where at t = 0 all four sites are almost empty. The system parameter are g = 0.5, γ = 0.2, Ω−ω = −0.5, and we

7 Conclusion
We briefly summarize the reviewed problem of chaotic transport in quantum systems, highlighting the main results and ideas.
In Sec. 2 we considered chaotic dynamics of the single-particle systems. The main message of this section is that there is no need to
seek for chaotic systems because chaos can be found almost in every system. A typical example is the quantum particle in a lattice which is
subject to AC and DC fields Eq. (11). This problem attracted much attention since the eighties and was analyzed by using the single-band
approximation in dozens of papers, without saying a word about chaos. However, the single-band approximation imposes severe restrictions
on the system parameters and, if we go in the parameter region where the single-band approximation is not justified, we immediately face
chaos. Moreover, in many systems we meet chaos even if we remain within the frame of single-band approximation. The bright example is
the Bose-Hubbard model considered in Sec. 3.
It was shown in Sec. 3 that in bosonic systems chaos is induced by a weak inter-particle interaction and, in this sense, it is a very common
phenomenon. The results of Sec. 3 indicate that usually chaos causes decoherence that leads, in particular, to appearance of the dephasing
operator Eq. (38) in the master equation for the single-particle density matrix. In the two-terminal transport problem, Sec. 4 and Sec. 5,
this dephasing is responsible for the crossover from the ballistic transport regime to the diffusive regime, where the particle current across
the Bose-Hubbard chain is greatly suppressed. Thus, in this example chaos plays a destructive role. However, the master equation with
dephasing Lindblad operator is also the starting point for studying a bunch of phenomena known under the name environmentally assisted
transport [70, 71, 72, 73], where chaos plays a constructive role.
In Sec. 6 we paid considerable attention to the periodically driven chain of dissipative nonlinear oscillators, which is the classical
counterpart of the driven dissipative Bose-Hubbard model. The key ingredients of this classical system are external driving, nonlinearity,
and dissipation. It is known from the theory of dynamical systems that these ingredients, if they are taken together, are responsible for variety
of phenomena, including the phenomenon of synchronization [74]. Some results on quantum synchronization in the driven dissipative Bose-
