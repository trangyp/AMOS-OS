Contact-Dependent Ion Gating Explains Directional Asymmetry in the Bacterial
Flagellar Motor
Jiading Zhu,1 Yongnan Hu,1 Yuhai Tu,2 and Yuansheng Cao1
1Department of Physics, Tsinghua University, Beijing, 100084, China
2Center for Computational Biology, Flatiron Institute, New York, New York 10010, USA
The bacterial flagellar motor (BFM) is a rotary molecular machine driven by the ion electro-
chemical potential across the cell membrane.
Recent cryo-EM structures reveal a cogwheel-like
architecture in which multiple stators engage a large rotor. A longstanding puzzle is the directional
asymmetry of its torque–speed relation: concave in counterclockwise (CCW) rotation but nearly
linear in clockwise (CW) rotation. Here, we develop a stochastic mechanochemical model that ex-
plicitly incorporates rotor–stator coupling and detailed ion translocation kinetics. By integrating
physiological torque–speed data with recent measurements of rotor–stator relative motion, we show
that under physiological conditions the motor operates in a tight engagement regime, rendering the
torque–speed relation largely insensitive to the specific form of mechanical interactions. This finding
rules out differences in rotor–stator mechanics as the origin of CW–CCW asymmetry. Guided by
cryo-EM structures, we propose a contact-dependent gating mechanism in which the MotA–FliG
interaction modulates the ion release rate of the MotB subunit proximal to the FliG ring. Molecular
dynamics simulations indicate tighter MotA–FliG contact in the CW motor, implying a reduced
ion release rate compared to CCW. Our model demonstrates that differential gating strength ac-
counts for the observed asymmetry: stronger gating in CCW shortens torque-free waiting phases,
enhances torque generation, and produces a concave torque–speed curve, whereas weaker gating in
CW yields lower torque and a linear relation. This structure-based framework quantitatively links
molecular asymmetry to motor function and identifies specific interfaces for targeted perturbation
and mutational studies.
INTRODUCTION
The bacterial flagellar motor (BFM) is a complex
molecular machine that powers the swimming of many
motile bacteria. It functions as a rotary engine embedded
in the bacterial cell wall and cytoplasmic membrane and
is driven by an ion motive force (IMF; e.g., H+ or Na+),
an electrochemical potential difference of ions across the
membrane [1–4]. In Escherichia coli, the BFM rotates
bidirectionally, enabling two distinct modes of bacterial
movement [5]. During counterclockwise (CCW) rotation,
multiple flagellar filaments form a coherent bundle that
propels the bacterium forward in a motion known as a
“run.” In contrast, clockwise (CW) rotation disrupts the
bundle, causing the bacterium to undergo a “tumble,”
which randomly reorients the cell. Alternating between
runs and tumbles allows bacteria to navigate chemical
gradients and move toward favorable environments or
away from harmful ones, a process known as chemotaxis.
Torque in the BFM is generated through interactions
between rotor and stator components [5–11]. The rotor
consists of the membrane-embedded MS ring and a cy-
toplasmic C ring composed of multiple copies of the pro-
teins FliG, FliM, and FliN. Each stator unit, embedded
in the membrane, contains a MotB dimer surrounded by
a ring of MotA subunits [6, 12–16]. Recent cryo-EM stud-
ies revealed that the MotA ring forms a pentamer [17, 18].
Each stator contains two ion channels associated with
the two MotB subunits. Ion translocation (e.g., proton
H+ flow) through these channels drives the rotation of
the MotA ring, which in turn exerts torque on the rotor
through interactions with FliG.
The cogwheel-like architecture of the motor and struc-
tures captured in different rotational states have revealed
an elegant mechanism for directional switching. The sta-
tor complexes rotate unidirectionally, while conforma-
tional changes in FliG reverse the direction of rotor mo-
tion. In particular, FliG interacts with the inner edge
of the stator during CCW rotation but changes confor-
mation to interact with the outer edge during CW rota-
tion [19, 20] (Fig. 1A).
A key functional property of the BFM is its torque–
speed relation. A longstanding puzzle is the asymmetry
between CW and CCW rotation.
During CCW rota-
tion, the motor exhibits a concave torque–speed curve:
torque remains nearly constant over a broad range of
speeds and drops only at high speeds [21, 22]. This be-
havior enables the motor to maintain high torque across
a wide range of loads during swimming.
In contrast,
the torque–speed relation for CW rotation is approx-
imately linear, with torque decreasing roughly linearly
with speed [23], reminiscent of linear molecular motors
such as kinesin [24, 25]. Despite this difference in shape,
both rotational states share the same stall torque and
maximum speed [22, 26, 27].
These observations raise a natural question: can recent
structural insights into the BFM reveal the mechanistic
origin of this CW–CCW asymmetry? In this work, we
develop a mechanochemical model of the BFM [28] based
on cryo-EM structures of the stator complex and the dis-
tinct conformational states of FliG associated with CW
and CCW rotation [17–19, 29–31]. Using this structure-

THE TORQUE-SPEED CURVE IN THE STRONG
COUPLING REGIME
The above analysis indicates that the rotor and sta-
tor are strongly coupled under physiological conditions,
leading to identical stall torques for CW and CCW rota-
tion, consistent with experiments. However, the exper-
imentally observed torque–speed relations differ in the
intermediate-load regime. What gives rise to this CW–
CCW asymmetry? Within our framework, two factors
could in principle be responsible: the stator–rotor inter-
action potential Vr or the ion-driven switching (jumping)
rate k(θs).
We first examined the role of the stator–rotor inter-
action using the model shown in Fig. 1C, in which ion
translocation occurs only near the bottom of the stator
potential (the “no-gating” model). As shown in Fig. 3A,
the resulting torque–speed curve is largely insensitive to
the specific form of Vr provided the system remains in
the strong-coupling regime with sufficiently large hr. In
this regime the torque–speed relation is linear, consistent
with that observed in CW motors. Changing the jump-
ing rate k simply shifts the maximum motor speed with-
out altering the linear dependence (Fig. 3B). Thus, the
no-gating model can account for the CW torque–speed
relation but cannot explain the concave curve observed
for CCW rotation.

partition each stator cycle into two phases: a moving

What may shorten the waiting phase in CCW motors?
One possible way is through a chemical “gating” mecha-
nism [35]. In this scenario, a gating region (red region in
Fig. 3C, bottom) located on the downhill part of the sta-
tor potential allows ion-triggered switching to the next
cycle before the stator reaches the “waiting” region near
the potential minimum (green region in Fig. 3C, bottom)
where it does not generate any net torque. By enabling
early switching, the gate suppresses the waiting phase
and maintains a higher average torque at high loads.
At low loads, however, the stator rotational speed is
fast and the stator can pass the gating region quickly
without switching (jumping). As a result, the effect of
gating diminishes, the torque drops steeply with increas-
ing speed, and the torque–speed curve converges to ap-
proximately the same maximum-speed point determined
by the transition rate at the potential minimum as in
the no-gating case. This mechanism naturally produces
a concave torque–speed relation as illustrated in Fig. 3D
(the red line).
Our analysis thus suggests an appealing explanation
for the CW–CCW asymmetry:
an additional gating
mechanism may assist ion translocation in CCW mo-
tors, while being much weaker in CW motors.
How-
ever, such a mechanism is difficult to reconcile with a
single-step chemical transition driven by only one ion. In
particular, if the stator switches in the middle of the po-
tential ramp, it is unclear how further torque could be
generated before the cycle completes, since no additional
torque-generating pathway would be available. Remark-
ably, these difficulties are naturally resolved by recent
structural results, which reveal that two ions alternately
drive the stator. This picture provides a coherent mecha-
nism for gated transitions while maintaining torque gen-
eration, as we discuss in the next section.
A CONTACT-DEPENDENT GATING
MECHANISM FOR ION RELEASE
We first describe a detailed model for torque generation
in a stator unit consisting of a MotB dimer surrounded by
a MotA pentamer, based on recent cryo-EM structures
of the stator [17, 18]. As shown in Fig. 4A, each MotB
subunit forms an ion channel together with neighboring
MotA subunits. Ion translocations through the two chan-
nels occur alternately and together drive rotation of the
flagellar motor.
Each ion translocation cycle consists of two steps—ion
binding and ion release—associated with two conforma-
tional states of MotB. When a MotB channel aligns with
the channel of its neighboring MotA subunit (θs = 0 in
Fig. 4A), an ion from the periplasm binds to MotB in
its periplasm-facing conformation (P-state). Ion binding
triggers a conformational switch to the cytoplasm-facing
state (C-state), which raises the stator potential (Vs) to
its peak value (“jumping”), powered by the ion motive
force. During the subsequent power-stroke phase, relax-
ation of this interaction potential down the gradient of
Vs generates torque and drives a half-period rotation of
the MotA ring (θs : 0 →π/5).
After the power stroke, the stator potential reaches its
minimum and remains flat during the subsequent half-
period (θs ∈[π/5, 2π/5]). During this phase the bound
ion must be released from the MotB C-state to the cyto-
plasm before the next translocation cycle can begin. Im-
portantly, while one MotB subunit is in this flat phase,
the other MotB subunit can bind an ion at θs = π/5 and
initiate its own power stroke, thereby sustaining contin-
uous torque generation over a full cycle.
This alternating, out-of-phase operation of the two
MotB channels is therefore crucial for continuous torque
generation. Here we assume that ion binding is extremely
rapid when a MotB channel aligns with its corresponding
MotA channel (gray bars in Fig. 4B,C), so that the rate-
limiting step is ion release. Because the release rate de-
pends on the size of the release channel formed by MotB
and neighboring MotA subunits, it may be modulated by
interactions between the relevant MotA subunit and the
FliG subunit of the rotor.
Consequently, the ion release rates of the two MotB
subunits can differ significantly. For the distal MotB1,
which is located away from the FliG ring, the channel
does not contact the rotor and ion release occurs rapidly
with rate ka in the flat regime (π/5 < θs < 2π/5) follow-
ing the power stroke (Fig. 4B). In contrast, the proximal
MotB2, which faces the stator–rotor interface, may ex-
perience steric interactions between MotA and FliG that
partially occlude the release channel, reducing the release
rate to kg. For simplicity, we place the release channel of
MotB2 near θs = 0 and 2π/5, allowing a possible gated
early release before the end of the flat regime (θs = 2π/5,

This contact-dependent gating mechanism (kg) for
the proximal MotB2 channel creates two distinct ion-
translocation pathways. In the upper pathway (Fig. 4A),
the bound ion is released early, between θs = 0 and
θs = π/5, via gating. As a result, a new ion can bind
immediately at the start of the next cycle (θs = π/5), en-
abling continuous torque generation without delay. For
simplicity, we assume that gating occurs immediately af-
ter torque generation by MotB2 is completed at θs = 0.
In contrast, if gating fails, the system follows the lower
pathway. The ion remains bound until the end of the flat
regime (θs = π/5), blocking the binding of a new ion.
This leads to a torque-free waiting phase, represented by
the V ′ state (shaded in Fig. 4A) near θs = π/5.
No
torque is generated during this interval, which persists
until the ion is eventually released through the slower
baseline pathway at rate k.
The strength of the gating mechanism therefore de-
termines how the cycle is partitioned between these two
pathways. For larger values of kg (strong gating), the sta-
tor spends most of the cycle in torque-generating states,
suppressing the waiting phase and driving the motor
toward the upper-limit behavior described in Eq.(11),
which produces a concave torque–speed relation. In con-
trast, when kg is small (weak gating), early release rarely
occurs and the system frequently dwells in the torque-
free waiting state (state V ′ in Fig. 4A), leading to the
linear torque–speed relation described in Eq.(10).

ASYMMETRY
Our model immediately suggests that the distinct
torque–speed curves observed for CCW and CW motors
arise from different strengths of the gating mechanism,
with kg,CCW ≫kg,CW. To test this hypothesis quantita-
tively, we performed numerical simulations of the model.
Figures 5A and 5B show representative instantaneous
torque traces for the two directions at high load, using
kg,CW = 0 and kg,CCW = kg ≫k.
During CCW rotation, strong gating keeps the sta-
tor predominantly in the positive-torque region. MotB1
and MotB2 alternately drive the motor, producing torque
that switches between τ+ and 0 (Fig. 5A). In contrast,
during CW rotation, where effective gating is absent (or
weak), the stator spends a significant fraction of time
fluctuating near its equilibrium position. In this regime
MotB1 generates positive or zero torque, while MotB2
produces zero or negative torque. The resulting “tug-of-
war” between the two MotB subunits traps the stator in

a waiting state (Fig. 5B, purple arrows) with nearly zero
net torque.
These microscopic move–wait dynamics give rise to the
macroscopic torque–speed relations shown in Fig. 5C: the
curve is concave when kg is large and becomes linear
when kg is small, with the concavity increasing as kg in-
creases. An analytical expression for the torque–speed
relation is provided in the SI. We also examined the de-
pendence of the torque–speed curve on the ion motive
force (IMF) by scaling the stator potential Vs with IMF.
The resulting curves show excellent agreement with ex-
perimental measurements [22] (Fig. 5D and Fig. S3–S4).
What is the mechanistic origin of the different gating
strengths in CCW and CW motors? Cryo-EM studies
have revealed pronounced structural differences between
the two rotational states, most notably at the MotA–FliG
contact interface [19]. In the CW state, large conforma-
tional changes rotate the FliG torque helix inward, bring-
ing MotA into close contact with the C-ring. In the CCW
state, by contrast, the torque helix points outward, leav-
ing a more open MotA–FliG interface [29–31]. Because
the MotB–MotA ion-release pathway lies near this inter-
face, these structural differences suggest that ion release
may be modulated by the MotA–FliG contact and could
be faster in the more open CCW configuration than in
the more constrained CW state, i.e., kg,CCW > kg,CW.
To quantify this structural asymmetry, we performed
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations based on recent
high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the stator–rotor
interface in the CCW and CW states [29–31].
While
cryo-EM provides high-quality structural snapshots, it
does not directly capture thermal fluctuations and hy-
dration at the interface. MD therefore enables ensemble-
averaged characterization of cavity geometry and con-
tact networks, and provides a practical framework for
assessing mutational perturbations.
We find that the
cavity volume at the MotA–FliG interface near the ion-
release region decreases substantially from 915.97 ˚A3 in
the CCW state to 508.42 ˚A3 in the CW state (Fig. 6A&B;
see SI for details). Consistent with this observation, we
identify 185 hydrogen bonds near the ion-release region
in the CCW structure, compared with 123 in the CW
structure (SI). The higher hydrogen-bond count in CCW
is consistent with increased hydration and a more con-
nected polar network. Such hydrogen-bond connectivity
can facilitate proton translocation by supporting contin-
uous pathways for rapid transfer (e.g., proton hopping
along hydrogen-bonded chains [36, 37]). Together, these
changes indicate a narrower and more constrained ion-
release pathway in the CW state, supporting our hypoth-
esis that ion release is slower in CW than in CCW, i.e.,
kg,CCW > kg,CW.

SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION
Guided by extensive experimental data—including
cryo-EM structures of the bacterial flagellar motor
(BFM), direct observations of stator rotation [32], and
the distinct torque–speed relations measured for CW and
CCW rotation—we developed a theoretical framework
to uncover the mechanochemical principles underlying
torque generation in the BFM. Our approach combines
stochastic modeling of motor dynamics with molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations of key protein–protein inter-
faces to connect molecular structure with motor function.
We first developed a coarse-grained stochastic model
describing the gear-like rotation between stator and ro-
tor inspired by recent cryo-EM structures of the sta-

The model shows that the macroscopic
torque–speed relation is governed by two factors: the
stator–rotor interaction potential and the kinetics of ion
translocation through the stator. Comparison with ex-
periments across different ion motive force (IMF) con-
ditions and recent measurements of stator rotation indi-
cates that the motor operates in a tight rotor–stator en-
gagement regime under physiological conditions. In this
regime, slipping between rotor and stator occurs only
through rare thermal fluctuations. Such tight coupling
ensures high energetic efficiency, as little of the IMF is
dissipated through slippage.
A key insight from our analysis is that, within this
strong-coupling regime, the torque–speed relation is
largely insensitive to the detailed form of the rotor–stator
interaction potential. Instead, the overall shape of the
torque–speed curve is determined primarily by the kinet-
ics of ion translocation. Guided by cryo-EM structures of
the motor complex, we propose a contact-dependent ion
release mechanism in which interactions between MotA
and the rotor protein FliG modulate the ion release rate
of the MotB subunit that is proximal to the rotor. Our
MD simulations reveal that the MotA–FliG interface is
significantly more constrained in the CW state than in
the CCW state. This structural asymmetry suggests that
ion release through the proximal MotB channel occurs
more readily in CCW rotation than in CW rotation. Our
stochastic model shows that this difference in ion release
kinetics naturally explains the distinct torque–speed re-
lations observed experimentally: a linear torque–speed
curve for CW motors and a concave torque–speed curve
for CCW motors.
More broadly, our work highlights several mechano-
chemical design principles underlying efficient torque
generation in the BFM. First, tight rotor–stator coupling
prevents loss of IMF through mechanical slippage. This
design principle likely extends beyond the rotor–stator
interface to other mechanical linkages in the motor, such
as the FliG–FliM/N and rotor–hook connections. Indeed,
cryo-EM structures show that the concentric rings of the
rotor (including the LP and MS rings) form a highly rigid
assembly [38]. Second, efficient torque generation criti-
cally depends on the timing of ion translocation events.
In particular, a gating mechanism that enables early ion
release can suppress torque-free waiting phases and in-
crease average torque output. At first sight such early
release might appear problematic, since no torque would
be generated between ion release and the next binding
event. Remarkably, this challenge is resolved by the ar-
chitecture of the stator itself: the MotB dimer forms two
ion channels that operate alternately and out of phase
during each stator rotation cycle (2π/5). This alternat-
ing operation enables continuous torque generation and
naturally explains why stronger gating leads to higher
torque output in CCW motors compared with CW mo-
tors.
Our model also leads to several experimentally testable
predictions.
First, mutations that weaken the elec-
trostatic contacts at the MotA–FliG torque transmis-
sion interface should reduce the effective coupling depth
hr, leading to lower stall torque and reduced mo-
tor efficiency [39, 40].
For example, residues such as
MotA Asp86 (Chain E), Glu94 (Chain F), and FliG
Asp241/Asp284/Asp289 form key charged contacts at
the stator–rotor interface; mutations at these sites are
expected to reduce hr and increase slippage under high
load or high IMF, resulting in a measurable decrease
in stall torque.
Second, perturbations that alter the
MotA–FliG interface or the MotA–MotB ion release
pathway should change the effectiveness of early ion re-
lease and thereby modify the concavity (or knee speed)
of the CCW torque–speed curve. For instance, charge-
neutralizing substitutions (D/E→N/Q/A) or steric re-
placements (A→Y) could shift the knee speed by altering
the probability of early ion release (see SI for details).
Finally, our study suggests several directions for fu-
ture theoretical and computational work.
Our cur-
rent MD simulations reveal structural asymmetry at the
MotA–FliG interface but do not yet resolve the detailed
ion translocation process or the corresponding kinetic
rates.
It would therefore be valuable to extend these
simulations to quantify ion release kinetics using ex-
plicit free-energy and rate calculations (e.g., enhanced-
sampling MD or QM/MM approaches).
Such stud-

ies could determine which physical factors at the in-

likely undergo conformational fluctuations. Incorporat-

such as load adaptation [11, 33, 41–44].
Finally, this

Extending the framework to motors containing multi-

TORQUE AND SPEED DEPENDENCE ON ION-MOTIVE-FORCE (IMF)
How IMF affects motor dynamics remains unclear and requires more detailed analysis based on the structural data.
Nevertheless, experiments [3] have measured how the torque-speed curves change with IMF, which helps us to choose
the dependence of τ+ and k on IMF to fit the data.
Specifically, it was found that the motor’s maximal speed (Fig. S3A) and stall torque (Fig. 2D in main text) both
linearly correlates with IMF. At zero load, the maximal speed primarily depends on the chemical transition rate and
is not limited by the physical motion or mechanical engagement of the rotor and stator (Fig. S2 C). For this reason,
we take k = γ1 · IMF −c (Fig. S3A).
The stall torque, on the other hand, is sensitive to the rotor-stator interactions. For simplicity, we still assume
a V-shaped Vs and examine how τ+ affects stall torque. We consider two scenarios: (i) τ+ = γ2 · IMF, and (ii) τ+
is a constant. We simulated the stall torque under both stochastic and deterministic slipping conditions for each
assumption and compared the results with experimental data. As shown in Fig. S3B, only when τ+ = γ2 · IMF and
slipping is stochastic, the stall torque scales linearly with IMF. Parameters γ1,2 and c are chosen to fit the experimental

MOLECULE DYNAMICS SIMULATION FOR MOTA-FLIG CONTACT INTERFACE
Model Preparation and System Setup
We selected the PDB structures 8UCS, 8UMD, and 8UMX as initial models to represent the FliG of stator–C-
ring interfaces in the CCW and CW rotation[4]. We focus on the differences in the interaction patterns between
the stator units and the FliG region in the two rotational modes, and on how these differences may influence the
efficiency of proton-transfer within the channel. The structural alignment of MotA/B and FliG was performed using
ChimeraX(version 1.10.1)[5]. Only the MotA/MotB/FliG components were retained for subsequent modeling, and
all missing amino acid residues were rebuilt accordingly. Protonation states of titratable residues were then assigned
using PROPKA 3.0 at pH 7.0[6], consistent with experimental conditions.
Each system was parameterized with the CHARMM36m force field[7] and solvated in an octahedral water box using
the TIP3P water model. A minimum distance of 15 ˚A was maintained between the protein and the edges of the box.
The appropriate numbers of Na+ and Cl−ions were added to neutralize the system. Explicit solvent was included to
capture the local hydration structure and hydrogen-bond networks surrounding the ion-channel region.
Molecular Dynamics Simulation Protocol
All MD simulations were performed using NAMD 2.14[8] under periodic boundary conditions. Long-range elec-
trostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with a grid spacing < 1 ˚A, and
electrostatic and van der Waals interactions used a 12 ˚A cutoff. Covalent bonds to hydrogen atoms were constrained
via the SHAKE algorithm, enabling a 1 fs timestep.
Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm. During minimization, heating, and
equilibration, positional restrictions were applied to the protein backbone atoms.
The system temperature was
maintained at 300 K using a Nose–Hoover chain thermostat, and the system was heated from 100 K to 300 K using
the Langevin piston method. After 1 ns of equilibration, five independent 10 ns production simulations were performed
for each system to sample solvent configurations around the protein and characterize hydration effects on channel
properties.
An averaged structure was generated from the final frames of the five independent molecular dynamics simulations,
and this representative equilibrated conformation was used for subsequent structural characterization and evaluation
of key interactions, with a particular focus on analyzing the cavity formed between the structure of MotA and FliG.
Structural analysis based on molecular dynamics simulations reveal pronounced geometric and dynamic differences
at the MotA–FliG interface in the CCW and CW rotational modes. First, the cavity volume at the MotA–FliG
interface in the CCW state is 915.97 ˚A3, whereas in the CW state it decreases to 508.42 ˚A3. This reduction indicates
that during the direction switch from CCW to CW, the cavity contracts significantly, narrowing the ion-conduction
pathway. These observations are consistent with the reported PDB structures 8UCS, 8UMD, and 8UMX, where in
the CCW state the H chain of FliG maintains a more relaxed arrangement relative to the MotA E/F chains, resulting
in a wider cavity, while in the CW state, tighter contacts between FliG and MotA lead to a more restricted channel
geometry.
Second, analysis of hydrogen-bond networks within 3.5 ˚A of the cavity further supports this conclusion. In the
CCW state, 185 hydrogen bonds are observed, compared to 123 hydrogen bonds in the CW state. The reduction in
hydrogen bonding reflects both the contraction of the cavity and a tighter interaction network, which is likely to slow
ion translocation. Combining these results with the structural features of 8UCS, 8UMD, and 8UMX suggests that
the cavity constriction is directly associated with conformational changes in the FliG H chain: in the CCW state,
helices and loops of FliG adopt a more relaxed arrangement, facilitating smooth proton or ion passage; in the CW
state, FliG helices compress and establish tighter contacts with MotA, constricting the channel and compressing the
hydrogen-bond network, potentially creating local “energy bottlenecks” that reduce the efficiency of proton transfer.
Ensemble averaging over five independent trajectories reveals a persistent difference in the MotA–FliG interfacial
cavity between the CCW and CW modes, accompanied by distinct contact dynamics (Fig.
S7).
Residue–level
interfacial contacts between MotA and FliG were quantified from all-atom molecular dynamics simulations using
MDAnalysis.
Chains E and F of the MotA chains were analyzed separately against FliG (Chain H). For each
trajectory frame, the distances Calpha–Calpha were calculated between all MotA and FliG residues, and a contact
was defined when the distance was less than 10.5 ˚A. The contact frequencies were accumulated over all frames and
normalized by the total number of frames, yielding a time-averaged contact probability matrix for each trajectory.

the local charge distribution and alter the energetic contribution of electrostatic interactions, thereby modulating
the probability, lifetime, and overall stability of the interfacial contact network in the CCW and CW states. Given
that residue pairs involving these positions exhibit pronounced ∆Pcontact between the two states, these residues are
more likely to participate in state-selective interfacial registration and coupling control rather than merely forming
nonspecific surface contacts.
With respect to mutational effects, charge-neutralizing substitutions (D/E→N/Q/A) are expected to weaken inter-
actions mediated by charged side chains and reduce the occupancy of state-selective contacts, consequently decreasing
effective stator–rotor coupling. At the macroscopic level, this may manifest as a reduced low-speed torque plateau
and measurable shifts in the characteristic features of the torque–speed relationship, such as the knee position and the
onset of torque decay. In contrast, charge-inversion substitutions (D/E→K/R) may introduce larger perturbations
to the electrostatic potential and change the sign and directionality of interfacial interactions, potentially leading to
overly strong interactions, interfacial mis-registration, or altered relative stability of the two states. As a result, these
mutations are anticipated to exert more pronounced effects on the maximum rotation rate, high-speed efficiency, and
CCW/CW directional asymmetry, potentially accompanied by changes in switching-related kinetic parameters.
To assess the sensitivity of the MotA–FliG interface to steric constraints, we performed a series of computational
site-directed mutations. Specifically, alanine residues in MotA chain E (A231, A233, A235, A239, A245) and the
corresponding alanines in FliG (A278, A279, A283, A285) were mutated to tryptophan to introduce substantial
side-chain steric bulk and simulate a highly crowded interfacial environment.

geometry(Fig S9). Although the number of mutated residues is identical on both sides, the volume of the interface
cavity in the FliG mutant is only 262.66 ˚A3, markedly smaller than that of the MotA mutant (458.24 ˚A3). This
asymmetric reduction in cavity volume suggests that the FliG side of the interface exhibits greater structural sensitivity
to changes in side-chain size.
These observations imply that this segment of FliG may constitute a critical physical interface for torque transmis-
sion in the BFM. In this region, even minor structural perturbations could substantially alter the geometric properties
of the interface, modulating MotA-FliG interactions and ultimately influencing the load-dependent response of the
motor.
