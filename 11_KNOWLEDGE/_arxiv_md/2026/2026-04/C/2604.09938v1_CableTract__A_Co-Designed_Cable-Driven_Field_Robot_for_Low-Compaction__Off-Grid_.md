Abstract
Conventional field operations spend most of their energy moving the tractor body, not the
implement. Yet feasibility studies for novel agricultural vehicles rarely tie mechanics, energy
harvest, draft, field geometry, economics, life-cycle CO2, and uncertainty quantification together
on a single reproducible code path.
This paper builds such a framework and applies it to CableTract, a two-module cable-
driven field robot. A stationary Main Unit (winch + motor + battery + harvester module)
(MU) (winch, motor, battery, solar and wind harvester) and a lighter Anchor module (held by
helical screw piles) tension a cable across a strip while a lightweight implement carriage rolls
along it. The heavy bodies stay on the headland; only the carriage enters the field.
The carriage runs a 10-implement library co-designed for the cable architecture: narrower
(1.5 m vs. 3 m), shallower (∼0.85× depth), slower (1–2.5 km/h vs. 5–9 km/h), and lighter than
implements borrowed from conventional tractor inventories. This co-design is the paper’s
central analytical lever.
The framework is prototype-free. It chains a catenary cable model, a drivetrain efficiency
chain, a stochastic draft model fitted to the co-designed library, an hourly solar + wind + battery
simulator on six sites, a polygon coverage planner on a 50-field corpus, a contact-pressure
compaction model, a discounted-cash-flow economics engine with battery replacement and
life-cycle CO2, and a global sensitivity analysis on 20 inputs. An operating-envelope sweep and
an architectural-variant comparison close the loop. The full implementation is open source.1
Applied to the codesigned reference, the framework yields:
• Energy: 921 Wh/decare (9.21 kWh/ha) — roughly 18× lower than the 168 kWh/ha of an
80 hp diesel tractor on the same operations.
• Compaction: ∼98% reduction in compacted area and ∼73× reduction in the contact-
energy index per cropping season, because only the 250 kg carriage rolls inside the field.
• Off-grid: 13.8 decares/day median throughput on harvested energy alone at Konya (15 m2
Photovoltaic (PV) + 9 kWh battery); off-grid is achievable in solar-rich climates (Konya,
Ludhiana, São Paulo), conditional in Mediterranean ones (Palencia, Des Moines), and not
achievable at any practical hardware scale in northern temperate climates (Beauce).
• Economics: Net Present Value (NPV) versus diesel of 3 978 EUR at 25 ha/yr and 8 %
discount, with discounted payback of 0.82 yr. NPV is positive across the full 1–100 ha/yr
sweep at all three discount rates we test (5/8/12 %).
• Life-cycle CO2: 14.6 kg/(ha yr) CO2eq versus 32.5 for the diesel reference and 22.9 for a
Monarch-class electric tractor — a clean 2.2× improvement that does not depend on grid
decarbonisation.

1 Introduction
A modern 80 hp Four-Wheel Drive (4WD) utility tractor weighs ≈4 t, of which the implement is
typically 200–500 kg. Each pass therefore drags ten to twenty times more body mass than tool
mass across the field, paying a soil-compaction penalty (mean contact pressure 100–250 kPa, well
above the 50 kPa threshold for clay soils) and burning ≈12 L/ha of diesel even on light operations.
Over a 15-year lifetime on a 25 ha farm this is 4 500 L of diesel and ≈12 t CO2eq.
CableTract attacks the dead-weight problem at the architectural level rather than the powertrain
level. Two modules sit on opposite headlands of a strip: a Main Unit carrying the winch, motor,
controller, battery, PV array, and small wind turbine; and an Anchor resisting the cable reaction
with helical screw piles. A lightweight implement carriage rolls along the cable between them.
Only the carriage enters the field. The heavy bodies move only at the end of each strip, by one
strip width, along the headland. Replacing the heavy traction body with a tensioned cable changes
three things at once: (i) only the carriage’s mass compacts the soil; (ii) the energy budget no
longer carries a 4 t body; (iii) the Main Unit, being nearly stationary, can carry deployable PV
and a small wind turbine more easily than a fully mobile electric tractor.
The natural objection is that this only works if the implements are also redesigned.
A
conventional 8-row planter or a 3 m disk harrow is built to be towed by a 4 t tractor; it expects
high draft and high speed. Towing those unmodified implements with a cable robot is the wrong
problem. The right problem is to co-design the carriage and a small library of implements
together: narrow strip widths matched to the cable swath, slower operating speeds that exploit
the v2 drop in soil draft, shallower depths that match what a carriage-mounted tool can resist,
and lighter implement frames freed from the hitch loads of a real tractor. This co-design is the
main analytical lever of the paper and we return to it explicitly in Sections 3.4 and 5.5.
The contribution is not an experimental validation. It is a prototype-free, fully repro-
ducible feasibility envelope that asks one question:
Under what (climate × farm size × operation type) conditions does a co-designed
cable-driven field robot beat a conventional diesel tractor on energy, compaction, NPV,
and life-cycle CO2 — and where doesn’t it?
We answer it with an open analytical pipeline2 that runs end-to-end in under ten minutes from
a clean checkout, ships with all input data bundled (no live Application Programming Interface
(API) calls), and regenerates 21 figures and 27 tables from a single command. Section 5 walks
through the model in the order in which the figures are generated; Section 7 reports the results
against four explicit claims; and Section 8 closes the envelope with the binding constraints.
2 Related Work
2.1 Prior cable-driven agriculture
Cable-driven field machines are not new. The closest published prior art is US 8,763,714 B2 [1]
(Orlando & Zoffoli), which describes a cable traction system using two technically identical electric
machines, each with a hoist and stabilisation, with the implement moving between them as cable
is wound and unwound. The patent motivates the design through reduced soil degradation and
electric propulsion, and includes a telescopic arm for cable placement during repositioning.
CableTract overlaps with that family but differs in three structural ways:
1. Asymmetric architecture. CableTract uses a heavy MU (Permanent-Magnet Synchronous
Motor (PMSM), controller, battery, harvester) and a much lighter Anchor (helical pile drive,

sensors). The Anchor needs no winch and no high-power electronics, which roughly halves its
Bill of Materials (BoM).
2. Helical-pile anchoring. Cable reaction is resisted by screw augers driven into the soil
rather than by the machine’s mass alone. This is what makes the lighter Anchor module
geotechnically defensible (Section 5.4.4).
3. Co-designed implements. CableTract is paired with an implement library sized for the
cable architecture, not borrowed from tractor inventories (Section 3.4).
We do not claim CableTract is the first cable-driven agricultural machine. The contribution is an
analytical evaluation of the asymmetric MU + Anchor architecture under co-designed implements,
and the identification of the operating envelope in which it is genuinely competitive.
2.2 Adjacent vehicle classes
CableTract sits in a design space already populated by five distinct vehicle classes, and a fair
comparison must place the architecture against each of them rather than against diesel alone. We
bundle a competitor table3 with public-domain reference numbers for each class (Section 6). The
five classes are:
• Conventional 80 hp 4WD diesel utility tractor — cost-floor reference; the thing Cable-
Tract has to beat on energy, compaction, and NPV.
• Battery-electric tractors (Monarch MK-V, Kubota LXe-261, Solectrac eUtility) — same
form factor as a tractor but with a Li-ion pack. Solves the fuel cost but not the compaction
problem; usually grid-charged.
• Autonomous PV-powered field robots (Farmdroid FD-20) — small, light, on-board solar;
designed for sugar beet / row crops. Cannot do tillage. Off-grid by construction.
• Autonomous wheeled spot sprayers (EcoRobotix ARA) — narrow, light, on-board PV;
spraying only.
• Autonomous weeders (Naio Oz / Orio) — vegetable / row-crop only; no heavy draft.
Across this landscape CableTract is the only architecture that simultaneously (a) is off-grid
capable, (b) supports primary tillage (within its co-designed envelope), and (c) does so with a
250 kg in-field footprint rather than a 1–4 t tractor body. The numerical comparison is in Section 6.

CableTract has two physically separable modules:
• Main Unit (≈1.4 t including PV deployable): PMSM motor + 3-phase inverter + 2-stage
gearbox + 8 mm Dyneema-on-drum winch, 9 kWh Li-ion pack, 15 m2 flat-plate mono-Si PV,

System (GNSS), Battery Management System (BMS), and 4 parallel auger drives for self-

• Anchor (≈600 kg): 9 parallel auger drives in a 3×3 cluster (one BLDC per auger, with a
shared auxiliary battery pack and a single controller multiplexing across the nine drivers, so
all nine insert and retract concurrently in a single per-round cycle), redirect sheave block,
simple electric drive for headland repositioning on its own wheels, GNSS, no main battery
beyond the auxiliary auger-drive pack.
In transport mode the Anchor docks against the MU and the assembly behaves as a compact
1.5 m wide, 1 m tall electric utility vehicle that can drive between fields on farm tracks. In work
mode the Anchor walks across the field on its own electric drive and plants itself at the far headland;
the MU runs the cable across the strip; the implement carriage rolls along the cable.

Figure 3: One round of the CableTract operating cycle, bird’s-eye view. (a) Forward leg: the Main
Unit’s winch reels in cable, the implement carriage moves loaded toward the MU at 1–2.5 km/h, the

One round of work proceeds as:
1. The implement carriage starts at the Anchor end with the working tool engaged.
2. The MU’s winch reels in cable. The carriage moves loaded toward the MU at the operating
speed (typically 1–2.5 km/h).
3. At the MU end, the carriage tool retracts, the cable goes slack, and the carriage either
gravity-rolls back (downhill) or is towed back by a thin return line. The unloaded return leg
can recover kinetic / potential energy via four-quadrant motor operation (regenerative braking,
Section 5.4.6).
4. The MU and Anchor each step laterally by one strip width along the headland.
5. Setup repeats on the next strip.
Typical numbers for the codesigned reference: span 50 m, strip width 1.5 m, carriage mass
≈250 kg, operating speed 1.5 km/h, setup time per round 60 s.

Figure 4: Why an asymmetric, headland-only architecture wins on compaction. (a) A conventional
tractor must drive a boustrophedon (snake) path that covers essentially the entire field area at least once
per pass — every square metre of the soil is rolled over by a 1–4 t body. (b) CableTract keeps the Main

Section 7.1 for the quantitative compaction model.
Symmetric cable-traction systems (like the Orlando–Zoffoli patent [1]) need two winches, two
batteries, two motor controllers, and two sets of high-power electronics. The asymmetric CableTract
design moves all the power electronics into one module and lets the second module be a simple
anchored drive. This roughly halves the BoM of the second module, halves its mass, and removes
one of the two failure-prone winches.
The price of the asymmetry is that the cable reaction is one-sided. The Anchor must hold the
entire horizontal draft against the soil. We address this with helical screw piles (Section 5.4.4) and
quantify the per-auger requirement against the conservative end of the helical-pile lateral capacity
literature, so that every auger count we report is a worst-case envelope.
3.4 Co-designed implement library
The single most impactful design choice in this paper is building implements for CableTract
instead of borrowing them from a tractor. A conventional 8-row planter is built to survive
the hitch loads of a 4 t tractor at 9 km/h on a 1.5 m depth-control wheel; it weighs 800 kg and
pulls 3.9 kN of draft. CableTract neither needs nor can usefully exploit any of those numbers. The
codesigned 4-row planter4 is sized for a 1.5 m strip, 2 km/h operating speed, 0.85× the conventional
working depth, and a carriage frame freed from tractor hitch loads. Its 50th (median) percentile of

4 Research Questions
We organise the analysis around four claims and seven research questions. The four claims are the
headline assertions the paper defends with code-traceable numbers; the seven research questions
decompose each claim into the operational regime, the physical mechanism, and the binding
constraint.
C1 — Compaction.
CableTract reduces in-field compacted area by at least 90 % and the
contact-energy index by at least 20× compared with an 80 hp 4WD diesel tractor on the same
field, because only the lightweight carriage rolls inside the field while the MU and Anchor stay on
the headland.
C2 — Energy.
With a co-designed implement library, CableTract operates at ≈0.9 kWh/decare
(≈9 kWh/ha), roughly an order of magnitude below a diesel tractor at the same operation, driven
primarily by (i) the absence of dead-weight transport, (ii) the v2 speed reduction, and (iii) the
narrow-strip width.

5.1 Modelling philosophy

parameter set and returns a result record.

5.4.3 Tension balance and operating regime
The implement carriage’s own depth-control mechanism — not the cable — sets tilling depth.
The cable transmits the horizontal draft Fd and stays clear of the soil. Two regimes arise:
• Draft-bound: Fd is large enough that the resulting horizontal tension keeps midspan sag
below the clearance budget hp −cmin. Horizontal tension equals Fd, anchor sees ≈Fd.
• Sag-bound: Fd is too small to keep the cable taut against its own weight. The Main Unit
over-tensions to Tsag,min. The anchor sees this larger tension.
The tension-balance solver detects which regime applies at each operating point and reports
the tensions seen by both ends of the cable. Figure 6 sweeps draft from 200 N to 4 000 N at 8 mm

5.4.4 Anchor reaction envelope
The asymmetric architecture of Section 3.3 only works if the lighter Anchor module can geotech-
nically resist the cable reaction. A mass-anchored solution would force the Anchor to weigh as
much as a small tractor, which would defeat the architecture’s selling point. We therefore replace
mass anchoring with helical screw piles and quantify how many augers are required at each draft
level. This is the load-bearing question behind the “CableTract is mechanically possible” claim.
The literature on lateral capacity of helical piles spans nearly two orders of magnitude
depending on soil density, shaft fixity, and the chosen serviceability limit. At the conservative end,
the 4-pile group raft tests of Khand et al. (2024) [4] imply per-pile lateral capacities on the
order of 400 N for short helical piles in loose sand at strict deflection thresholds with free-head
boundary conditions. At the realistic end, the Magnum Piering 2024 design tables [5] report
allowable lateral capacities of 14–20 kN per small fixed-head helical pile in medium-dense sand
(SPT N ≈10–30) at the IBC2021 1 inch deflection limit, and the industry-standard CHANCE
Technical Design Manual [6] brackets the same range. We deliberately adopt the conservative end
of this literature — 400 N per auger at a working safety factor of 1.15 — as our nominal per-auger
capacity, so that every auger count quoted below is a worst-case envelope. The codesigned 9-auger
Anchor therefore carries a 5×–10× implicit margin under the medium-dense fixed-head conditions
a real installer would target.

5.4.6 Regenerative return leg
A genuine energy advantage of the cable architecture is that the unloaded return leg lifts no
implement, so any potential-energy budget (downhill slopes, carriage descent at end of row, elastic

5.9 Field-geometry coverage planner
5.9.1 Why shape efficiency is a load-bearing parameter for cable architectures
A single scalar shape_efficiency = 0.85, the standard shortcut in feasibility studies, treats a
square and an L-shaped field as equivalent. That is wrong by construction: an L-shape costs
strip turn-around time at every concave corner, and an irregular concave polygon with interior
obstacles costs additional headland passes that a square never sees. For a wheeled tractor, the
resulting penalty is bounded — the tractor still drives over every square meter, and the shape
penalty mostly shows up as extra headland turns. For CableTract the penalty mechanism is
fundamentally different and larger, because every disconnected piece of a strip is a new Anchor
placement. The cable cannot bend around an interior obstacle, cannot follow a concave notch,
and cannot share a single Anchor across two disjoint strip fragments. Each fragment costs one full
setup cycle (drive Anchor in, tension, run, retension, retract) regardless of how short the fragment
is, so polygon shape is converted directly into setup count and therefore directly into time.
This makes the field-geometry model load-bearing in a way it is not for tractor feasibility
studies: the same parameter that quietly washes out for a tractor sets the throughput floor for
CableTract. We replace the scalar with a deterministic strip-decomposition planner that operates
on arbitrary polygons, and we exercise it on a bundled corpus of 50 field shapes deliberately
biased toward small, awkward polygons — the regime where shape efficiency matters most and

5.9.5 Findings and interpretation
Corpus headline. The corpus median best-orientation η at L = 50 m is 0.77, almost a full
0.08 point below the 0.85 default that flat feasibility studies typically use. Carrying that
0.08-point gap through the throughput model translates into roughly a 10 % throughput reduction
on a like-for-like basis: a feasibility study that assumes shape_efficiency = 0.85 on the same
corpus is implicitly over-claiming throughput by about one decare per day on the codesigned
reference. Shape geometry is therefore not a second-order effect that can be folded into a constant;
it is a first-order determinant of headline daily area, and it has to be sampled across a corpus
rather than picked once.
Class structure. The class breakdown in Table 10 explains where the gap comes from and is
the more useful number than the corpus median:
• Rectangles η = 1.000 (P10 = P50 = P90). The strip planner is geometrically lossless on
rectangles, which is the right answer: a long edge to anchor against and a short edge to step
across is exactly the geometry the cable architecture was designed for. This sets a hard ceiling
on what the architecture can deliver.
• L-shapes η = 0.905. A single concave corner costs the planner about 10 percentage points of
efficiency, because the strips straddling the missing corner each pay a full Anchor placement
to cover a sub-strip-length of polygon. The penalty is bounded and predictable.
• Real shapes η = 0.800. The five hand-traced outlines (river-edge strip, road frontage,
pentagon, quarter circle, two-lobe peanut) come in tighter than irregular convex polygons
because the irregularity sits along one axis and the planner’s orientation sweep can usually
find an alignment that suppresses it.
• Irregular convex η = 0.702 and irregular concave η = 0.684. The two “hard” classes
converge — once a polygon is irregular enough that no orientation aligns the strips with a long
free edge, adding interior obstacles and concave notches on top of that costs only a further ∼2
points. The dominant penalty is failing to find a clean strip axis, not the obstacles themselves.
The headline reading is that the architecture is geometry-sensitive but not geometry-fragile:
the median field in a deliberately adversarial corpus still recovers 77 % of its theoretical throughput.
The 12-orientation sweep in Eq. (10) matters here — a fixed-orientation planner would lose another
5–10 points on the irregular classes, because the optimal sweep direction varies field by field.
What CableTract’s shape sensitivity tells you about siting. The class spread implies
that shape efficiency is a site-selection variable, not a residual error term. A farmer choosing
between two candidate parcels of the same area should prefer the rectangular one, and the
manuscript carries this through into the operating envelope of Section 5.13: the model rewards
rectangular plots in solar-rich climates and penalises concave plots in temperate ones.
Time budget — the operation is operation-bound, not setup-bound. Figure 14 plots
three example strip plans. Figure 15 plots the daily time budget on a 5-field 21-ha synthetic
farm: operating time ≈37.6 h, setup ≈3.85 h, inter-field travel ≈0.22 h.

5.11 Uncertainty quantification and global sensitivity
Every number in Sections 5.4, 5.5 and 5.7 to 5.10 depends on parameters whose values are known
to within published uncertainty bands rather than exactly. A defensible feasibility envelope
must therefore answer two questions: given the joint uncertainty in 20 parameters, what is the
P10–P90 envelope of the headline outputs, and which parameters carry the most variance —
in particular, is any single parameter still dominant after the Section 5.4.1 decomposition of the
lumped drivetrain efficiency? We answer the first with a 1 000-sample Monte Carlo and the second
with a Sobol global sensitivity decomposition [23, 24] computed via Sensitivity Analysis Library
(Python) (SALib) [25, 26].
5.11.1 Parameter problem
The uncertainty problem is defined over 20 parameters drawn from physically defensible bands
with uniform marginals. Examples:
• Lumped drivetrain efficiency ∈[0.35, 0.65] — covers the manufacturer datasheet range for
industrial PMSM/gearbox/drum/cable chains after the Section 5.4.1 decomposition.
• Draft load ∈[1 500 N, 4 500 N] — spans the codesigned-library P10–P90 envelope from Sec-
tion 5.5.
• Shape efficiency ∈[0.55, 1.00] — covers the entire field-corpus range from Fig. 13.
• PV area ∈[10 m2, 30 m2] and battery capacity ∈[5 kWh, 25 kWh] — spans the design envelope
of Fig. 12.
These bands are deliberately wider than typical sensitivity studies on energy systems, so the
resulting Sobol decomposition is hard to fudge.
5.11.2 Monte Carlo
We draw n = 1000 samples from the joint distribution and evaluate the full simulator on each.
Doubling the sample size to 2000 shifts P50 by less than 1 % and 10th percentile of a sampled
distribution (P10)/90th percentile of a sampled distribution (P90) by less than 3 %, so 1 000
samples are sufficient. Figure 17 plots the resulting throughput envelope versus draft load, with
the codesigned reference (1 800 N, 11.5 decares/day) overlaid as a green star. The star sits below
the P50 line (∼13.7 decares/day at the same draft) but well inside the P10–P90 envelope, which
is the expected behaviour: the deterministic codesigned reference uses pessimistic-leaning defaults
on several uncertain parameters (drivetrain efficiency chain, soil moisture, headland turning), so it
should land in the lower half of the joint distribution rather than at its centre. The Monte Carlo
run is a check on robustness, not a re-derivation of the codesigned point.

flip the financial story — and which ones cannot? A tornado analysis is the standard tool for
that question. Each parameter is varied one at a time between a low and a high bound while

financial story is driven by the macro environment of agricultural diesel rather than by any single
fudge factor inside the model. It also means the financial story improves over time — agricultural
diesel prices have a strong upward trend, and crop intensification has a strong upward trend on
litres per decare, both of which push CableTract’s NPV into the green half of the Tier-1 bars.
What this rules out. A common failure mode in feasibility papers is that a single parameter
(battery price, capex, drivetrain efficiency, “magic fudge factor”) turns out to be the load-bearing
assumption that drives the entire result. Fig. 19 rules this failure mode out for the codesigned
reference: every Tier-1 swing is exogenous, every Tier-2 swing leaves the result solidly positive, and
the design parameter (cost_cabletract_usd) that the model has the most leverage to be wrong
about sits in Tier 3 with no sign-flipping power. Combined with the diffuse Sobol decomposition
in Fig. 18, this is the key robustness statement of the paper.

5.12.1 What this section is for
Everything up to this point has analysed a single architecture: the codesigned two-module v1, with
one stationary Main Unit, one Anchor, a single tensioned cable, and a unidirectional drivetrain.
The natural next question is whether that v1 architecture is the only sensible point in the design
space, or whether topological modifications — adding more Main Units, recovering energy on the
return leg, changing the cable layout — materially change the headline numbers. We answer this
with a small, deliberately narrow comparison: three variants run on the same codesigned

## Page 36


parameter set, on the same simulator, so the resulting bars are directly comparable.
The three variants are chosen so that they probe orthogonal axes of the design space. Each
is implemented as a parameter transformation on the codesigned reference of Table 2, fed into
the same run_single routine, with no parallel code path. The result is a clean apples-to-apples
comparison rather than three independent feasibility studies that drift apart on definitions.
• Codesigned baseline (Section 5.12.2): the v1 architecture as analysed in Sections 5.4, 5.5,
5.7 and 5.8. One Main Unit, one Anchor, one cable, unidirectional drivetrain. This is the

• CableTract+, the four-Main-Unit planar cable robot (Section 5.12.3): four corner

• Regenerative return leg (Section 5.12.4): the v1 architecture with four-quadrant motor

new modules, no new mechanics) can outrun the v1.

is only 9 % of the daily time budget — so any setup-reduction variant has at most a 9 % throughput

5.12.4 Variant 3 — Regenerative return leg
The third variant changes nothing about the cable architecture and nothing about the BOM. It
modifies only the drivetrain control: the motor controller is upgraded to four-quadrant operation
so that the unloaded return half of every round can recover kinetic + potential energy back
into the battery instead of dissipating it as heat. The model implements this as an effective
winch-efficiency boost of 1/(1 −frec · fret), where frec = 0.35 is the round-trip recovery fraction
(kinetic energy back into the pack, after motor + inverter losses on the way out and back in) and
fret = 0.5 is the share of one round taken by the unloaded return leg (half a round at v1 baseline).
The boost factor is 1/(1 −0.35 · 0.5) ≈1.21, which lifts the lumped winch efficiency from 0.5 to
≈0.61 (capped at 0.95 to stay physically defensible).
The result is the most interesting line in Table 14. Throughput barely moves (11.5 →11.8,
1.03×) because throughput in the codesigned reference is bound by the soil-draft envelope and the
field-time budget, not by motor efficiency. Energy per decare drops from 921 to 760 Wh
(0.83×, a 17 % reduction), which is the direct consequence of recovering 21 % of the round-trip
drivetrain losses. Simple payback drops from 74.8 to 61.7 months (0.83×, a 13-month
improvement), because every recovered Joule is a Joule the diesel comparator still has to burn.
And critically, CAPEX does not change: four-quadrant operation is a firmware/control change
on a motor controller that almost any modern PMSM drive already supports in hardware. The
only thing it costs is engineering effort on the controller side. The surplus-power column drops
from 291 W to 19 W in the table because the recovered energy is consumed by the slightly higher
operating draw (the system runs marginally faster), not because surplus is destroyed.
This variant is the closest thing the paper finds to an unambiguous engineering recommendation:
regenerative braking on the return leg improves the financial story by 13 months at
zero capital cost and zero new modules. It is the highest-leverage modification per unit
of design complexity in the table, and it is the only modification that does not force a trade-off
against any other column. The reason it is not already in the codesigned baseline is conservatism
in modelling: we wanted the v1 baseline to assume a stock unidirectional drivetrain with no
firmware sophistication, so that the headline numbers held up even if four-quadrant control turned
out to be impractical for some reason we have not foreseen. The regen variant exists to show what
is left on the table if that conservatism is dropped.
5.12.5 What the variant comparison tells us
Three takeaways flow from Table 14:
• The v1 architecture is not Pareto-dominated by either ambitious modification.
CableTract+ wins on throughput and energy but loses on CAPEX and payback; regen wins
on energy and payback but barely moves throughput; the v1 baseline is the only row that

supports the full 10-implement library on a single Main Unit + Anchor pair at 35 570 EUR.
There is no cell in Table 14 where one row strictly dominates another on every metric, which
is the property a healthy variant comparison should have.
• The two trade-off axes are different. CableTract+ trades capital for throughput; regen
trades controller complexity for energy and payback. They are not competing modifications —
they could be applied together — and a hypothetical CableTract++ (4-MU robot with regen
control on every winch) would in principle stack the two effects.
• The recommended engineering priority is the cheap one. If a single modification is to
be added to the v1 baseline, it should be the regen return leg, not CableTract+. This is the
strongest single engineering recommendation the paper makes.
Reconciling the variant table with the Section 5.8 discounted payback.
Readers
comparing Table 14 (74.8-month baseline simple payback) with Fig. 20 (sub-1-year discounted
payback at 25 ha/yr) will notice the two numbers differ by roughly an order of magnitude. The
two figures answer different questions and both are correct in their own frame:
• The simple payback reported in Table 14 divides the full CableTract CAPEX (35 570 EUR
with sales margin) by annual diesel-fuel savings only, with no discount rate and no maintenance
offset. This is the right framing for a buyer who is adding a CableTract to an existing operation
that does not retire a tractor: the question is “how long until the new machine pays for itself?”.
• The discounted payback reported in Section 5.8 and plotted in Fig. 20 divides the CableTract
CAPEX delta over a like-for-like diesel tractor (35 570 EUR −35 000 EUR = 570 EUR)
by the full annual savings stream (fuel + maintenance, less the marginal grid charge), discounted
at 8 %. This is the right framing for a buyer who is replacing a tractor: the diesel capex would
have been spent anyway, so only the difference is at risk.
The variant comparison uses the conservative simple-payback frame deliberately, so all three
rows are stress-tested against the harder buying scenario. The codesigned baseline pays back in
0.82 years at 25 ha/yr in the replacement scenario (Fig. 20); the regen variant inherits essentially
the same number because it does not change CAPEX. CableTract+ fares worse in the replacement
frame than in the simple-payback frame, because its CAPEX delta against a single diesel tractor
(80 570 EUR −35 000 EUR = 45 570 EUR) is roughly 80× the v1 delta, while its annual savings
are only ∼2.5×. The engineering ordering “regen ≥baseline ≫CableTract+” on payback-per-
euro-of-design-complexity therefore holds in both frames; the simple-payback frame is the more
flattering of the two for CableTract+.

amortise and almost every euro of avoided fuel becomes gross savings.
• The binding constraint is off-grid feasibility, not financial viability. The off-grid
breakeven contour in panel (a) cuts diagonally across the plane and divides it into a “PV

The off-grid envelope only collapses for very large operating areas where the codesigned 15 m2

• CableTract’s operating envelope is therefore a climate envelope, not a financial
envelope. A farmer in Beauce gets a positive NPV and fully off-grid operation at 25 ha; a
farmer in Beauce running 500 ha on a single CableTract gets a positive NPV but needs grid
backup. This is exactly the opposite of the more common “the technology pays back but
only at scale” story for clean-energy hardware: CableTract pays back everywhere we test, and
the question is where it can be sold as off-grid hardware versus where it needs to be sold as
grid-tied.
This is the headline result of the paper.

7 Results
7.1 C1 — Compaction
CableTract reduces in-field compacted area by 97–98 % and the contact-energy index by ≈73×
across rectangle, L-shape, and irregular-concave field classes (Fig. 16, Section 5.10.3).
The
reduction is uniform across field shape because it is geometric, not field-dependent: only the
250 kg carriage rolls inside the field, while the MU and Anchor stay on the headland. The 73×
contact-energy ratio comes from the carriage’s mean pressure being 4.6× lower than the tractor’s
and the contact-energy integrand depending on p2. Both numbers are defensible from open
published static contact-pressure values without dynamic FEM calibration.
7.2 C2 — Energy
The codesigned reference operates at 921 Wh/decare (9.21 kWh/ha), against ≈168 kWh/ha
for an 80 hp 4WD diesel tractor on the same operations [2, 10] — an 18× reduction. The
reduction decomposes into three independent levers:
• Co-designed implements (Figs. 8 and 9). Median draft drops from ≈4.5 kN (conventional
library) to ≈1.7 kN (codesigned library), a factor of ≈2.6, driven by narrower widths, slower
speeds, and shallower depths.
• No dead-weight transport. FdL is ≈90% of the round work; the diesel comparator tractor’s
body force Fs (4 t × rolling resistance) dwarfs Fd on every operation.

7.3 C3 — Off-grid feasibility
Median harvested-energy throughput across the six bundled sites is 10–14 decares/day at the
codesigned hardware (15 m2 PV, 9 kWh battery). At Konya, Ludhiana, and São Paulo the system
runs grid-free for the entire late-June week we plot in Fig. 11. At Palencia and Des Moines
the system needs ≈300–600 h/yr of grid backup to cover winter shortfalls. At Beauce, no
combination of panel area (4–30 m2) and battery capacity (2–24 kWh) achieves <100
grid hours/year under the 6 h/day duty cycle (Fig. 12): the northern temperate climate cannot
sustain off-grid operation at any practical hardware scale.
The off-grid claim is climate-conditional, not categorical. We frame this as a feature of
the analysis: a CableTract sold into Konya is sold as off-grid hardware, while a CableTract sold
into Beauce is sold as a low-carbon grid-tied alternative to a diesel tractor. The economics work
in both cases (Section 7.4), but the marketing language differs.
7.4 C4 — Economics
NPV vs diesel is positive across the entire 1–100 ha/yr sweep at all three discount rates (5/8/12 %)
we test (Fig. 20, Section 5.11.5). At the 25 ha/yr reference, NPV @ 8 % is 3 978 EUR with
discounted payback of 0.82 yr. At 100 ha/yr, NPV @ 8 % is 14 763 EUR with payback of
0.31 yr.
Lifecycle CO2 at 25 ha/yr is 14.6 kg/(ha yr), against 32.5 for the diesel reference and 22.9
for a Monarch-class electric tractor (Fig. 21). The improvement is entirely on the operational side
and does not depend on grid decarbonisation.
The operating envelope of Fig. 24 shows that the financial story is uniformly robust: NPV
is positive in 100 % of the 3 600 cells of the (annual GHI × farm size) sweep, with discounted
payback bounded above by 1.85 yr (median 0.72 yr). This is the consequence of the capex-parity
finding: at 35 570 EUR versus 35 000 EUR for the diesel reference, there is no capex hurdle to
amortise, and every euro of avoided fuel becomes gross savings.
The Sobol decomposition (Fig. 18) confirms that no single parameter dominates the throughput
variance (top contributor ST ≈0.20), and the Fig. 19 tornado on NPV is led by exogenous factors
(solar resource, diesel price, diesel volume) rather than free design parameters. The codesigned
reference is robust to the full joint uncertainty in 20 inputs.
7.5 Operating envelope
The headline result of the paper. The codesigned CableTract is NPV-positive in 100 % of the 3 600
cells of the (annual GHI × farm size) sweep, with discounted payback never exceeding 1.85 yr
(median 0.72 yr). The binding constraint is the off-grid breakeven contour, not financial
viability: 85 % of cells are off-grid feasible, and the contour cuts diagonally across the plane.
CableTract’s operating envelope is therefore a climate envelope (does the PV cover the operating
demand?), not a financial envelope (does the system pay back?).
This is the opposite of the typical clean-energy hardware story, where the technology is
climate-virtuous but pays back only at scale or with subsidy. CableTract pays back everywhere;
the question is whether it can be sold off-grid in a given climate.

8 Discussion
8.1 Where CableTract wins
The combined evidence of Sections 7.1 to 7.5 points to a clear operating envelope:
• Operation type: medium-load — light primary tillage (narrow ripper / chisel up to ≈3 kN
P50), all secondary tillage, all seeding, all spraying, all weeding, all mowing. The codesigned
implement library is built for this envelope.
• Farm size: ≥10 ha annual operating area. NPV is positive everywhere we test, but the fastest
paybacks (< 1 year) require ≥25 ha. Below 10 ha, the 1.2–1.9 yr payback is still competitive
but the absolute NPV is small.
• Climate: anywhere with >1 500 kWh/(m2 yr) annual GHI for fully off-grid operation. Mediter-
ranean climates (1300–1500) work with summer-only off-grid; northern temperate climates
(<1200) work as grid-tied.
• Field shape: rectangular and L-shaped fields with ≥1 ha individual size. Small fragmented
fields hit the polygon shape efficiency wall (median η ≈0.68 on the irregular-concave class).
8.2 Where it doesn’t (binding constraints)
The honest constraints to flag:
• Heavy codesigned primary tillage in loose-sand soils sits outside the 9-auger Anchor
envelope on the conservative Khand (2024) [4] reference (Section 5.4.4). Specifically, the
narrow sweep (5.2 kN P50), narrow chisel (4.2 kN), narrow disk (3.2 kN), and codesign drill
(4.6 kN) all require 12–18 augers in loose sand (Fig. 8). On these sites the operating envelope
shrinks to the lighter half of the codesigned library — cultivator, planter, rotary hoe, sprayer,
mower, and the narrow ripper at its P50. In medium-dense soils with fixed-head pile fixity
(Magnum 2024 [5] reference) the same 9-auger Anchor carries the entire codesigned library
with 2×–10× margin, so the binding case is specifically loose-sand sites attempting heavy
primary tillage — not heavy primary tillage in general.
• Off-grid in continental winter climates is not achievable at any practical hardware
scale (Beauce: 911 h/yr grid hours minimum). CableTract still pays back as a grid-tied
diesel-replacement in these climates, but the marketing language must drop “off-grid”.
• Small fragmented fields below ≈0.5 ha individual size hit the polygon shape efficiency wall
and lose 30–50 % of nominal throughput. CableTract is not the right architecture for vegetable
garden plots.
• Operation diversity vs throughput is a real trade-off. A system specialised to a single
operation (sprayer-only, planter-only) can be sized for that operation’s draft and width and
would dominate the codesigned reference on throughput per day for that one task — but it
cannot do the rest of the agricultural calendar. The codesigned reference deliberately picks the
design point that supports all 10 implements in Table 1 rather than optimising any single one.
8.3 Sensitivity to the central design choice
The single most consequential choice in the paper is the co-designed implement library.
Without the co-design framing — that is, with conventional tractor implements substituted in
place of the codesigned ones — the analysis weakens in three concrete places:
1. The 2 kW PMSM is undersized (peak draft × tractor speed requires ≈5 kW), and the inverter,
gearbox, and cooling stack scale up with it.
44


## Page 45


2. The energy-per-decare claim weakens by ≈2× (because the draft drops by ≈2× under co-design
via the strip-width and v2 levers), which directly weakens the off-grid PV+battery sizing.
3. The 9-auger Anchor loses its worst-case-soil margin: a conventional moldboard plow at 14 kN
P50 would need 41 augers on the conservative Khand (2024) [4] loose-sand envelope, although
the same load fits in 9 augers — exactly the design value — on the Magnum (2024) [5]
medium-dense fixed-head reference.
The first two bullets are the load-bearing ones — they hold for any soil regime — and the
economics still work even in this degraded scenario (NPV stays positive at 25 ha/yr), but the
off-grid story and the compaction story are weaker. The co-designed framing is therefore not
a marketing convenience; it is the load-bearing assumption of the paper for energy and motor
cost reasons that are independent of soil conditions, with the worst-case-soil anchor envelope as a
bonus third leg, and we make this explicit.
8.4 What the paper does not claim
We do not claim:
• That CableTract is a finished product or has been prototyped.
• That cable-driven agriculture is a new idea (US 8,763,714 B2 [1] predates this work).
• That CableTract beats every competitor on every metric (EcoRobotix is more efficient per
hectare on spraying-only; Naio is cheaper).
• That off-grid operation is universal (it is climate-conditional).
• That the 73× contact-energy reduction translates linearly into a yield increase (the soil-yield
link depends on crop, climate, rotation, and irrigation).
We do claim, with every number reproducible from the open analytical pipeline:
• A 18× reduction in energy per hectare versus a diesel tractor.
• A 97–98 % reduction in compacted area and a ≈73× reduction in contact-energy index.
• Off-grid feasibility in solar-rich climates with 15 m2 PV + 9 kWh battery.
• NPV positive across 1–100 ha at 5/8/12 % discount rates, with discounted payback <1 yr at
≥25 ha.
• Lifecycle CO2 2.2× lower than diesel, 1.6× lower than a Monarch-class electric tractor.
• An NPV-positive operating envelope in 100 % of 3 600 (annual GHI × farm size) cells, with
discounted payback never exceeding 1.85 yr.
9 Limitations
This section separates two categories of limitation. Section 9.1 lists the analytical limitations
of the present model — what the framework does not include. Section 9.2 lists the engineering
risks that a prototype phase would have to resolve regardless of how comprehensive the analytical
framework becomes; in the authors’ candid view these are the dominant near-term feasibility
questions for the architecture and they are not closed by additional simulation.
9.1 Analytical limitations of the present model
1. No prototype. The strongest objection. Every number in the paper is from first-principles
calculation; none has been validated against a working machine. We have tried to mitigate
this by using ASABE D497.7 [2] (an experimentally calibrated standard), the conservative
end of the helical-pile lateral capacity literature (Khand et al. 2024 [4] for loose-sand free-head
bounds, Magnum Piering 2024 [5] design tables and the CHANCE Technical Design Manual [6]
45


## Page 46


for the medium-dense fixed-head reference), Keller and Lamandé (2010) [15] (experimentally
measured tractor contact pressures), open published LCA inventories [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21],
and a Sobol global sensitivity analysis [23, 24, 25] to bound the uncertainty.
2. No closed-loop control simulation. We do not simulate cable tension regulation, anchor
stability under transient loads, or the carriage’s depth-control feedback loop. These are real
engineering problems that a prototype phase will need to address; Section 9.2 treats the
depth-control loop in particular as the dominant unresolved risk.
3. Static contact-pressure compaction model. No dynamic load transfer, no tyre deflection,
no multi-pass cumulative deformation, no soil-yield link. The model is intentionally simple;
richer models would strengthen the comparison by adding compaction to the tractor side.
4. Polygon corpus is small and curated. 50 fields, hand-tuned, biased toward small awkward
shapes. A real deployment would need a corpus pulled from EuroCrops or (US) Department
of Agriculture, National Agricultural Statistics Service (USDA NASS) at scale.
5. PV harvest model in the operating-envelope sweep is a one-parameter linear
fit. The full hourly TMY pipeline lives in Section 5.7 and was used for Figs. 10 to 12, but
the (annual GHI × farm size) sweep in Fig. 24 uses a calibrated linear approximation for
tractability. The α = 0.169 value was fitted as the median across the 6 bundled sites.
6. The codesigned implement library is a paper exercise. The 10 implements are sized
using the ASABE D497.7 equation but have not been physically built. The draft reductions
are predictions, not measurements.
7. The competitor comparison uses public-domain manufacturer claims. Some of
these may not reproduce in the field. The source of every number is flagged in the bundled
competitor table.
9.2 Open mechanical risks
The seven items above are the analytical limitations of the present model: they describe what
the framework does not include.
This subsection separates out a different category — the
engineering risks that a prototype phase would actually have to resolve, regardless of
how comprehensive the analytical framework becomes. We list them explicitly so a follow-on team
can attack them in priority order rather than re-deriving the list from the manuscript.
Closed-loop tilling depth control.
Tilling depth at the carriage is governed by the geometric
coupling between cable sag, cable elastic stretch, carriage horizontal position, implement reaction
torque, and the time-varying soil contact force. None of these are independent variables, and none
are simulated under closed-loop control in the present paper. Sections 5.4.2 and 5.4.3 bound the
static sag-vs-tension tradeoff under nominal loads, but do not address the dynamic response to a
transient draft spike — a buried stone, a hardpan layer, a moisture gradient. Unlike a tractor
with a 3-point hitch and several tonnes of vehicle inertia to absorb the spike, the cable carriage
has neither inertia nor a stiff mechanical reference frame; the response is mediated entirely by
cable elasticity and the winch motor’s torque-control bandwidth. Whether the resulting tilling
quality meets agronomic acceptance criteria is, in the authors’ view, the single largest unresolved
technical risk in the architecture. It is also one of the cheapest to test: a quarter-scale bench rig
on a 5 m span in a soil bin is sufficient to bound it before any full-scale prototype is built.
Per-strip setup time as a design target rather than a measurement.
The model uses
tsetup = 60 s as the per-round overhead (Table 2). This is a design target, not a field measurement,
and the architecture in Section 3.1 is now committed to the only configuration that makes
it plausible: nine parallel auger drives on the Anchor (one BLDC per auger, all nine cycling
concurrently) and four parallel auger drives on the Main Unit. With parallel augering, the
46


## Page 47


per-round cycle decomposes notionally into ∼30 s for the simultaneous nine-auger insert/retract
pair, ∼5 s for the lateral Anchor step on its own wheels, ∼5 s for cable slack take-up, ∼5 s for
GNSS re-survey of the new strip line, and ∼5 s for re-tensioning — about 50 s of nominal work
inside the 60 s budget, with roughly 10 s of margin. The budget is tight but internally consistent
with the Section 3.1 BoM; the time-budget finding of Section 5.9.5 (operation dominates setup by
∼10×) holds under it. Three residual concerns survive and should be measured by a prototype
campaign before this number is treated as solved. (i) The single-auger insert/retract time at this
duty cycle is unmeasured in the public literature; if it turns out to be significantly longer than
30 s on representative soils, the entire 60 s budget shifts. (ii) The margin inside the budget is
small enough that any non-augering surprise — a tangled cable, a carriage hookup failure, a stale
GNSS fix — consumes the slack on the round it occurs. (iii) Auger insertion time on hard-pan or
stony soils is non-linear in cone index in ways the present model does not capture. The Sobol
sweep (Section 5.11.3) brackets setup time over 30–180 s, which covers the realistic uncertainty
around the parallel-drive nominal but does not extend to the failure mode where parallel augering
itself is slower than assumed. The CableTract+ variant in Section 11 eliminates the per-strip
Anchor reset entirely and is, in part, a structural insurance policy against this concern rather than
an independent throughput play.
Anchor self-fatigue of its own anchoring soil.
The Anchor’s lateral capacity envelope
(Section 5.4.4) treats per-auger lateral capacity as a property of soil texture and density alone. In
normal operation, however, each Anchor station drives and retracts nine augers tens to hundreds
of times across a cropping season as the Anchor walks the headland. The cumulative effect is that
the soil under each station is progressively loosened by the system’s own operation: a soil that
began as medium-dense (and therefore sat comfortably inside the Magnum 2024 [5] envelope at
5–10× margin) drifts toward the loose-sand regime (Khand 2024 [4] envelope at ∼1× margin) as
the season progresses. The 9-auger design margin erodes from inside, not from changes in field
conditions. Quantifying the rate at which this happens requires either an empirical campaign on
a fixed Anchor station or a finite-element model of repeated helical pile insertion and withdrawal;
neither is in the present paper, and the architecture’s worst-case soil envelope should be read with
this caveat in mind.
Helical pile retraction wear.
Helical screw piles in the construction industry are designed
and qualified for one-shot installation with permanent service. CableTract drives and retracts
each auger many cycles per day, which is a regime for which no fatigue, helix wear, shaft buckling,
or soil-disturbance data exists in the public literature the authors could find. The first-order
risks are blunting of the helix leading edge (which would slow each subsequent insertion and
increase the auger drive’s torque demand), shaft fatigue at the drive coupling, and progressive
misalignment of the helix from the shaft axis. The honest framing is that this is an unmeasured
failure mode rather than necessarily a small one — there is no published basis to estimate the
mean time between auger replacements, and the bill of materials in Section 5.8 does not include
an auger replacement schedule. A first prototype campaign should treat auger wear as a primary
measurement endpoint alongside throughput and energy.
Historical cable ploughing as an unaddressed precedent.
The architecture has a precedent
the present paper has not yet fully engaged with. Steam-driven cable ploughing was a real and
widely deployed technology in the second half of the 19th century, particularly on heavy clay
soils in the United Kingdom and parts of continental Europe where horse teams bogged. Two
stationary steam engines at opposite headlands pulled a two-bottom plough back and forth on a
steel cable. The system coexisted with horse and traction-engine ploughing for several decades and
then disappeared, comprehensively, by the early 20th century. The reasons it lost are documented
in agricultural-history literature: per-station crew overhead, the time cost of repositioning the
engines after each strip, the management burden of the cable itself, and the inability to turn at
47


## Page 48


the headland and resume continuously. The present manuscript automates the per-station crew
problem (the Main Unit and Anchor are autonomous) but does not yet solve the repositioning,
cable-management, or continuous-headland-turn problems that killed the steam-era version. A
serious treatment of this lineage — what cable ploughing did, why it failed, what is mechanically
and operationally different now — belongs in any prototype-stage publication. We flag its absence
here as a deliberate scope decision and a known weakness.
Cable safety surface.
A 50 m taut steel or Dyneema cable at 1.5–4 kN pretension running
across an open field at 0.8 m above the ground is a safety surface the paper does not address.
Snap-back energy from a cable failure is non-trivial at these tensions, and a deployed system would
need cable-condition monitoring, runaway-detection logic, exclusion zones, and certification under
the relevant agricultural-machinery regulatory framework. None of this is in scope here, but it
would be a precondition for any field deployment and would add both BoM cost and certification
process cost.
The unifying observation is that the strongest near-term feasibility questions for CableTract
are mechanical and operational, not financial or environmental. The paper’s NPV, life-cycle
CO2, and energy-per-hectare numbers are robust within their stated framework; the open question
is whether the framework’s mechanical assumptions survive contact with a real prototype season.
We make this distinction explicit so a reviewer can evaluate the analytical contribution on its own
merits while understanding which questions a prototype-stage follow-up would actually need to
answer.
10 Conclusion
This paper presents a prototype-free, fully reproducible feasibility envelope for CableTract, a
co-designed cable-driven field robot consisting of a stationary MU, a lighter Anchor anchored by
helical screw piles, and a lightweight implement carriage that rolls along a tensioned cable between
them. The central design lever is the co-designed implement library — 10 implements sized
for the cable architecture rather than borrowed from conventional tractor inventories — which
reduces median draft by ≈63% and brings the entire codesigned library inside the 9-auger Anchor
envelope on the medium-dense soil reference, with the lighter half of the library also fitting inside
the worst-case loose-sand envelope.
The codesigned reference achieves:
• 921 Wh/decare (≈18× lower than a diesel tractor).
• 97–98 % reduction in compacted area and ≈73× reduction in contact-energy index
across rectangle, L-shape, and irregular-concave field classes.
• Off-grid throughput of 10–14 decares/day at six bundled sites under the climate-
conditional framing of Section 5.7.4.
• NPV vs diesel of 3 978 EUR at 25 ha/yr, 8 % discount, 0.82 yr payback, and NPV-
positive across (1–100 ha) at all three discount rates.
• Lifecycle CO2 of 14.6 kg/(ha yr) versus 32.5 for diesel — a 2.2× improvement that does
not depend on grid decarbonisation.
The operating envelope of Fig. 24 shows that CableTract is NPV-positive in 100 % of the 3 600
cells of the (annual GHI × farm size) sweep, with discounted payback never exceeding 1.85 yr.
The binding constraint is off-grid feasibility at the upper-right corner (large farms in cloudy
climates), not financial viability. CableTract’s operating envelope is a climate envelope, not a
financial envelope — the opposite of the more common clean-energy hardware story.
The right next step is a Main Unit prototype with a 2 kW PMSM, a 9 kWh pack, a 9-auger
Anchor, and the codesigned narrow ripper as the first implement, deployed on a 25 ha rectangular
48


## Page 49


field at a Mediterranean site (Konya or Palencia) for one full cropping season. The data this
prototype would generate — actual draft under load, actual cable tension regulation, actual anchor
stability, actual energy harvest — would close the largest gaps in the present paper. Until then,
the analytical envelope above is the best defensible feasibility case for the architecture.
