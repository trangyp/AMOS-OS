CARTESIAN PRODUCTS OF SIERPI ´NSKI CARPETS DO NOT
ATTAIN THEIR CONFORMAL DIMENSION
RIKU ANTTILA AND SYLVESTER ERIKSSON-BIQUE
Abstract. It is a long-standing open question to determine whether the
Sierpi´nski carpet attains its conformal dimension or not.
While this prob-
lem remains unresolved, we prove that Cartesian products Sk, where S is the
Sierpi´nski carpet and k ≥2, do not attain their conformal dimension. Our
approach is based on the Sobolev spaces and energy measures on S – con-
structed by Shimizu, Kigami, and Murugan and Shimizu – together with a
certain singularity result of energy measures from the theory of analysis on
fractals. This work formulates a general non-attainment result of conformal
dimension for product metric spaces Xk for k ≥2 in terms of self-similarity
and energy measures of the factor X. It applies, in particular, to the cases
where X is the Sierpi´nski carpet, the Sierpi´nski gasket, the Menger sponge,
and the Laakso diamond.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background. The Ahlfors regular conformal dimension of a metric space X is
the infimum of Hausdorff dimensions of all metric spaces Y that are Ahlfors regular
and quasisymmetrically homeomorphic to X. This notion originates in the work of
Bourdon and Pajot [14] and is a variant of the conformal dimension introduced by
Pansu [47]. Precise definitions are given in Section 2, and a more comprehensive
treatment can be found in the book by Mackay and Tyson [45]. For brevity, we
refer to the Ahlfors regular conformal dimension simply as the conformal dimension
throughout.
A central concept in the literature on conformal dimension is the attainment
problem, which asks whether the infimum of Hausdorff dimensions of Y , as described
in the previous paragraph, is a minimum. In fact, this problem turns out to be
related to a remarkably large number of questions in different fields. To name a few
notable ones, Bonk and Kleiner showed that the well-known Cannon’s conjecture
can be reformulated in terms of an attainment problem of conformal dimension for
the visual boundary of hyperbolic groups [12].

tence problems for exotic metrics and measures. In the work of the second author
and G. C. David [21], it was observed that the attainment for a certain Pillow

1.2. Main result. A notorious example for which the attainment of the conformal
dimension remains open is the Sierpi´nski carpet [12, Problem 6.2]. The value of the
conformal dimension is also unknown [27, Open problem 15.22] but quite impressive
estimates are available due to Kwapisz [43]. The hypothetical realization of the
attainment was recently studied by Murugan and Shimizu [46, Section 9].
While the present work does not resolve the attainment problem for the Sierpi´nski
carpet, our main result is a proof of the following non-attainment result.
Theorem 1.1. If S is the standard Sierpi´nski carpet and k ≥2, then the Cartesian
product Sk does not attain its conformal dimension.
Kleiner had conjectured in [40, Conjecture 7.5] that approximately self-similar
and combinatorially Lowner metric spaces attain their conformal dimension (see [20]
for a definition that is revised from [13]). By [13, Section 4.3] or [5, Remark 4.5
and Theorem 8.3], the Cartesian product Sk satisfies the combinatorial Loewner
property. Theorem 1.1, therefore, provides new counterexamples to Kleiner’s con-
jecture [40, Conjecture 7.5].
While the conjecture was first resolved in [6], all
counterexamples therein are homeomorphic to the Menger curve.

work provides the first counterexamples of arbitrary topological dimension.
Our methods are not restricted to the Sierpi´nski carpet, and apply to many
self-similar spaces. See the end of the introduction for a detailed discussion.

not have a product structure arising from X.

Up to this point, each step in the proof would work if, for instance, S is replaced
by the unit interval [0, 1], for which the conclusion of the theorem is obviously false.
But now, we use a certain singularity property carried by energy measures on many
fractal spaces, including the Sierpi´nski carpet. More specifically, if Λ is a minimal
energy dominant measure, then Λ ⊥µS. See Section 5 for more detailed discussion
about the singularity. This together with absolute continuity in the previous display
implies Λ = 0, which clearly contradicts (1.2). The attaining structure therefore
cannot exist, and this completes the proof of Theorem 1.1.
1.5. Further examples. As discussed above, the proof of Theorem 1.1 relies on
two main techniques: blow-ups and energy measures. The existence of blow-ups are
ensured by rather mild self-similarity; essentially all self-similar sets in Euclidean
spaces admit them. The existence of suitable energy measures is far more non-
trivial. Nevertheless, there is by now a substantial body of literature in analysis on
fractals that provides such examples. See, for instance, [8,30,38,39,49], as well as
the recent survey [36]. Below, we provide a list of examples of metric spaces X for
which Theorem 1.1 holds if we replace S with X. A precise generalized statement
is provided in Theorem 6.1, and see Section 6.3 for more detailed discussion.
(1) X is the Menger sponge, or more generally any generalized Sierpi´nski carpet
in the sense of [49], where also the relevant energy form is constructed.
(2) X is the Laakso diamond space, where the energy is constructed in [8].
More generally, if X is any Laakso type space in the sense of [8], assuming
that for every p ∈(1, ∞) its p-walk dimension is strictly larger than p.
(3) X is the Sierpi´nski gasket, with the energy constructed in [38].
Finally, we note that the construction of the energy measures on S discussed
above generalizes to every p ∈(1, ∞). For our application to the non-attainment,
however, it is essential that we consider the Q-energy where Q is the conformal
dimension of S2. For instance, the “conformal invariance” of the Q-energy, stated
in Proposition 4.10 and Corollary 4.15, should be false when Q is some other value.
This, nevertheless, does not mean that p-energies for other values of p are not
interesting. In fact, our method for the non-attainment demands a study of the
p-energy on the factor S, which is not conformally invariant in the sense above; Q
is the conformal dimension of the product S2 but not of the factor S. The results of
the work, therefore, indicates that the aforementioned studies of energy measures,
for general p ∈(1, ∞), should be applicable in the future studies on conformal
dimension.
Organization of the paper. Section 2 covers the necessary material on metric
spaces and conformal dimension. In Section 3, we introduce the general framework
of p-Dirichlet spaces that were developed in [23,24].
In Section 4, we establish a connection between the attainment problem with

5. Singularity of energy measures
This section regards the following singularity result of energy measures.
Proposition 5.1. Suppose that Assumption 4.1 holds, and let Λ be any minimal
energy dominant measure of (X, d, µ, E, F, Γ). If additionally (X, d) is geodesic and
β > p, then Λ ⊥µ.
The singularity of energy measures has its origins in the theory of diffusion on
fractals. The first result to this direction was obtained by Kusuoka on the Sierpi´nski
gasket [42], and later Hino generalized it to more general fractals [31]. In the full
generality of Dirichlet forms on metric spaces, the singularity result was settled
by Kajino and Murugan [33]. By using a similar argument, Yang extended the
singularity to p-energies [53]. In fact, [53] essentially covers Proposition 5.1, but we
decided to provide a detailed proof since therein assumptions slightly differ from
ours. For instance, we do not assume a Clarkson’s inequality.

very simple special case of Proposition 5.1 which seems to provide quite accurate
intuition for the general case. Suppose that Γ⟨f⟩∼Cµ for f ∈F and C > 0.

to show that f is α-H¨older continuous for α = β/p > 1. Then, by composing f

6. Non-attainment of conformal dimension
This section proves the main result, Theorem 1.1, about the non-attainment of
conformal dimension. It is generalized into the following, and see Remark 6.9 for
a detailed explanation on how it implies Theorem 1.1. Uniform scalability is given
in Definition 6.3.
Theorem 6.1. Let Q > 1, β ≥Q, and (X, dX, µX, EX, FX, ΓX) be a (Q, β)-
Poincar´e–Dirichlet space satisfying Assumption 4.1. Also let k ∈N with k ≥2,
and suppose k · dH = β where dH is the Hausdorff dimension of (X, dX). Finally
let (Z, d) be k-fold Cartesian product of (X, dX) where d is the ℓ∞-metric. Then Q
is the conformal dimension of (Z, d). Moreover, if β > Q and (X, dX) is geodesic
and uniformly scalable, then (Z, d) does not attain its conformal dimension.
Theorem 6.1 is essentially a special case of Theorem 6.2 where Y = Xk−1.
Theorem 6.2. Let Q > 1 and β ≥Q.
Suppose that (X, dX, µX, EX, FX, ΓX)
and (Y, dY , µY , EY , FY , ΓY ) are (Q, β)-Poincar´e–Dirichlet spaces, both satisfying
Assumption 4.1. Further, suppose that β = dH,X + dH,Y where dH,X and dH,Y are
Hausdorff dimensions of (X, dX) and (Y, dY ), respectively. Finally, let (Z, d) be
the Cartesian product of (X, dX) and (Y, dY ) where d is the ℓ∞-metric. Then Q is
the conformal dimension of (Z, d). Moreover, if β > Q and (X, dX), (Y, dY ) are
geodesic and uniformly scalable, then (Z, d) does not attain its conformal dimension.
Taking Theorem 6.2 as given, we prove Theorem 6.1 as follows.
Proof of Theorem 6.1. By repeatedly applying Theorem 3.5, we obtain a (Q, β)-
Poincar´e–Dirichlet space (Y, dY , µY , EY , FY , ΓY ) so that (Y, dY ) is the (k −1)-fold

and that the Hausdorff dimension of (Y, dY ) is equal to dH,Y := (k −1)·dH. Hence,

Remark 6.9. This remark explains how to verify the hypotheses of Theorem 6.1
for the Sierpi´nski carpet S. This, in particular, proves Theorem 1.1. First, S is
bi-Lipschitz to a geodesic metric space, and the maps in the uniform scalability
condition are provided by the natural similarity maps. Thus, the geometric con-
ditions have been checked. Next we verify the existence of the required Dirichlet
space and its properties.
By the work of Murugan and Shimizu [46], for every
p ∈(1, ∞) there is a (p, βp)-Poincar´e–Dirichlet space (S, dS, µS, ES, FS, ΓS) that sat-

same explanation as in the remark below, we could also refer to [38, 49]. What
remains is to check is that βQ = k · dH for some Q > 1, and that βQ > Q. First,
we have βp > p by [49, Theorem 2.27]. In order to find Q, it suffices to show
βp = dH for some p ∈(1, ∞) and p 7→βp is continuous in p ∈(1, ∞).
We then find Q by a continuity argument because βk·dH > k · dH as noted above.

to be independent of p when p varies on some compact subset of [1, ∞). For the
other condition in the display, note that βp = dH when p is equal to the conformal
dimension of S. See [46, Remark 9.17]. Finally, the continuity can also be deduced
from [37, Proposition 4.7.5].

spaces constructed in [8], such as the Laakso diamond space.
Remark 6.11. This remark shows that the hypotheses of Theorem 6.1 are satisfied
by the 3-dimensional Menger sponge (M, dM). The argument is essentially the same
