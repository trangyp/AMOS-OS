Abstract
Can contagion be inferred from aggregated default data? We study this as a problem of identifi-
ability, asking whether contagion generates components in default count distributions that remain
distinct from those induced by macroeconomic fluctuations. We compare three dependence struc-
tures: cumulative contagion in the Lo–Davis model, threshold-type contagion in the Torri model,
and common-factor dependence in the Vasicek model. Under an i.i.d. specification, the Vasicek
model provides the best overall fit, especially in the tail, indicating that a smooth mixture structure
captures annual default clustering more effectively than threshold-type contagion at the aggregate
level. We then allow the default probability to vary across years through a hierarchical specifica-
tion. Under this extension, most of the variation in annual default counts is explained by cross-year
movements in default conditions rather than by within-year contagion. What remains, however,
depends on the interaction mechanism. In the Torri model, threshold-type contagion does not leave
a stable component that can be separated from macroeconomic heterogeneity after aggregation.
In the Lo–Davis model, by contrast, a small but persistent component remains visible in both the
variance decomposition and the tail behavior. These results clarify when contagion can still be
inferred from coarse-grained data and when it is effectively absorbed into macroeconomic variation.
I.
INTRODUCTION
In econophysics and sociophysics, macroscopic phenomena such as price fluctuations and
collective behavior are often described as emergent consequences of interactions among het-
erogeneous agents [1–9].
However, similar aggregate patterns can arise by qualitatively
different microscopic mechanisms. This makes the identification of the microscopic origin of
observed fluctuations a central challenge, particularly when only aggregate observations are
available [10].
This issue is particularly relevant in credit risk, where default clustering is widely ob-
served [11, 12]. Firms are connected through economic and financial networks, and defaults
are therefore unlikely to occur independently. Two broad classes of mechanisms have been
proposed: contagion, where the default of one firm directly affects others, and macroe-
conomic fluctuations, where common shocks simultaneously increase default probabilities
across firms [13–15]. Various extensions of contagion-based models, including threshold-

type and exchangeable formulations, have also been proposed [16–18]. A key difficulty is
that empirical observations are often limited to aggregated data, such as annual default
counts, in which the underlying transmission channels are not directly observable. As a
result, distinguishing contagion from macroeconomic effects is difficult even with firm-level
information, and becomes still more challenging after aggregation [12, 19].
A large body of literature addresses default clustering using firm-level data or event-time
models. In particular, Hawkes-type processes incorporate the timing and propagation of de-
faults [20–23]. Such approaches are well suited to tracing contagion pathways when detailed
micro-level data are available. However, these data are often incomplete or unavailable,
especially over long historical periods. By contrast, many empirical datasets consist only of
aggregated default counts, without information on inter-firm linkages or precise event tim-
ing. In that setting, the relevant question is not how contagion propagates across a known
network, but rather what can be identified about contagion and macroeconomic effects from
coarse-grained observations. Even if contagion exists at the micro level, it may not remain
identifiable after aggregation. In this sense, aggregation acts as a coarse-graining procedure
that may suppress some interaction effects while preserving others.
In this paper, we study this identification problem using annual default-count data and
three representative dependence structures: cumulative contagion in the Lo–Davis model,
threshold-type contagion in the model of Torri, Giacometti, and Farina, and common-factor
dependence in the Vasicek model. We first examine these models under an i.i.d. specification
and then allow the default probability to vary across years through a hierarchical extension.
As a complementary distributional benchmark, we also use KL projections for synthetic
hierarchical distributions to examine whether contagion signatures are absorbed by smooth
macroeconomic mixtures as the variance contribution of time-varying default probabilities
increases.
The main result is straightforward. Once cross-year variation in default conditions is
taken into account, most of the variation in annual default counts is explained by shifts in
default probability rather than by within-year contagion. What remains depends on the
interaction structure. In the threshold-type model, contagion does not leave a stable com-
ponent that can be separated from macroeconomic heterogeneity at the level of aggregated
counts. In the cumulative-contagion model, by contrast, a small but persistent component
remains visible.

INFECTIOUS DEFAULT MODELS AND STRUCTURAL DIFFERENCES
In this section, we introduce three representative models of default count distributions:
the Infectious Default Model of Lo and Davis (hereafter, the Lo–Davis model)[13], the
Infectious Default Model with Immunization of Torri, Giacometti, and Farina (hereafter,
the Torri model)[17], and the Vasicek common-factor model[14]. While these models differ
in their underlying mechanisms, they can all be viewed as inducing dependence among
individual default events through binomial-mixture structures.
To facilitate comparison, we focus on their implications for the distribution of the total
number of defaults. In particular, we examine their moment generating functions, expected
values, and pairwise dependence structures. Our goal is to highlight the qualitative differ-
ences in how default clustering arises in each model.
The key distinction lies in the mechanism by which dependence is generated. In the
Lo–Davis model, contagion is cumulative, with the probability of additional defaults in-
creasing smoothly with the number of prior defaults.
In the Torri model, contagion is
triggered by the existence of at least one infectious default, leading to a threshold-type,
OR-based mechanism. In contrast, the Vasicek model generates dependence through a con-
tinuous common factor, resulting in a smooth mixture of binomial distributions. As we show
below, these differences lead to distinct patterns of clustering and tail behavior.

III.
EMPIRICAL FIT UNDER THE I.I.D. SPECIFICATION
We examine the empirical performance of the three models under the i.i.d. assumption,
where all parameters are constant over time. In this setting, each year is treated as an
independent realization from a stationary distribution of default counts, allowing us to
compare how different dependence structures reproduce the observed distribution without
incorporating time-varying macroeconomic effects.
The three models differ in how they generate dependence. The Torri model produces
threshold-type contagion, the Lo–Davis model allows contagion to accumulate across de-
faults, and the Vasicek model generates dependence through a continuous common factor.
As we show below, the Vasicek model provides the best overall fit under the i.i.d. spec-
ification, both in likelihood and in tail behavior. This result suggests that, at the level of
aggregated annual counts under the i.i.d. specification, a continuous mixture mechanism
captures default clustering more effectively than discrete contagion. At the same time, it
raises an important question: whether this advantage reflects a genuine structural feature
or the model’s ability to absorb unobserved heterogeneity through a latent factor.
In the next section, we address this question by extending the models to hierarchical
specifications in which the parameter p varies across years through an unobserved latent
factor.

V.
CONCLUSION
This paper examined the identifiability of contagion in aggregated annual default counts
by comparing three representative frameworks: the Vasicek common-factor model, the Lo–
Davis cumulative-contagion model, and the Torri threshold-type contagion model.
Under the i.i.d. specification, the Vasicek model provides the best overall fit, particularly
in reproducing the observed tail behavior. This performance, however, is associated with a
substantial common-factor component that attributes much of the observed variability to a
single source of dependence.
To separate cross-year heterogeneity from within-year contagion, we introduced hierar-
chical extensions in which the baseline default probability varies across years. The results
show that fluctuations in the annual default probability are the dominant source of ag-
gregate variability, while within-year contagion plays a secondary role. The hierarchical
Lo–Davis model retains a small but persistent infection component, whereas the hierarchi-
cal Torri model behaves almost like a purely heterogeneous model once cross-year variation
is introduced.
The comparison of tail behavior further highlights this distinction. While the Vasicek
model performs best under the i.i.d. specification, it tends to overestimate tail risk relative to
the hierarchical specifications, and the hierarchical Torri model produces excessively heavy
tails. In contrast, the hierarchical Lo–Davis model provides the most balanced fit for the
ALL and SG classes, although the IG class remains difficult for all specifications.
These results suggest that default clustering in aggregated annual data is driven primarily
by time-varying credit conditions rather than strong contagion effects. Contagion, if present,
appears to refine the tail shape rather than serve as the main source of variance. From the
perspective of identifiability, threshold-type contagion does not generate a distinct compo-
nent beyond macroeconomic variation in aggregated data, whereas cumulative contagion
leaves a small but persistent contribution that remains distinguishable after aggregation.
Equivalently, the three models can be viewed as different binomial-mixture represen-
tations of aggregated default counts. In the Lo–Davis and Torri models, the mixture is
generated by contagion mechanisms [18], whereas in the Vasicek model it is induced by a
continuous latent factor. This mixture-based view is consistent with the de Finetti represen-
tation of exchangeable Bernoulli systems [24]. Once time variation in the default probability
25


## Page 26


is introduced, these mixing effects are superposed: the Lo–Davis mechanism remains partly
distinguishable, while the Torri mechanism is largely absorbed by cross-year heterogene-
ity. This suggests that identifiability depends on whether a mechanism leaves a detectable
structural signature after aggregation.

Activation probability and tail-risk trade-off in the Torri model
To illustrate the residual degree of freedom in the Torri model, we examine how variation
in the parameter p along the iso-(m, ρ) manifold affects both the activation probability πn
and the resulting tail risk. Although the activation probability
πn = 1 −(1 −pv)n
is increasing in p for fixed v, the constraint of constant (m, ρ) induces a compensating
decrease in v, leading to a decreasing relationship between p and πn along the iso-curve.
Figure 7 summarizes this mechanism. The left panel shows how πn varies with p along
the iso-(m, ρ) manifold. The right panel shows how the corresponding values of πn translate
into tail-risk measures, specifically VaR0.99 and ES0.99.
Within the Torri family, both VaR0.99 and ES0.99 decrease over the range shown as πn
increases. Hence, smaller values of πn correspond to rarer but more severe contagion events,
whereas larger values of πn correspond to more frequent but milder cascades.
The Lo–Davis and Vasicek models appear as single points in this diagram, since their
dependence structures are fully determined once (m, ρ) is fixed. By contrast, the Torri model
generates a continuous range of tail risks even under identical first and second moments.
Together, the two panels provide a compact representation of the frequency–severity trade-
off underlying the Torri mechanism.

2.
Variance decomposition by subperiod
We therefore turn to the variance decomposition, which is central to the present paper.
Figure 11 decomposes the empirical variance into contributions from i.i.d. shocks, contagion,
and time variation in the default probability pt.
A clear structural difference emerges across periods. In the early period (1950–1979),
the contagion component plays a major role in several cases. In particular, for the SG class
under the Torri specification, the infection term dominates the empirical variance, indicating
a strong threshold-type clustering mechanism.
However, this dominance is not consistent across classes. In the same period, the ALL
results do not exhibit a comparable contribution, despite ALL being the aggregate of SG and
IG. This discrepancy suggests that the mechanism extracted by the threshold-type contagion
model is less stable at the aggregate level.
By contrast, the Lo–Davis model yields more coherent decompositions across classes.
