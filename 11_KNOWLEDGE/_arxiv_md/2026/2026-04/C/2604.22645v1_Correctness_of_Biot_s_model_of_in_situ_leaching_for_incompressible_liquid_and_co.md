We study a mathematical model of in situ leaching of rare metals, in which the joint
filtration of two liquids is governed by the microscopic model Aε. A key difficulty is the
unknown (free) boundary Γ(r) between solid and liquid components, determined by an
additional condition on Γ(r); no standard methods exist for this nonlinear problem. To
resolve it we apply the fixed point theorem. For a given function r(x, t) from a set
M(0,T ) of sufficiently smooth functions describing the skeleton structure, we consider
the auxiliary problem Bε(r): an elliptic system for displacements of the liquid and solid
components coupled with parabolic equations for the acid concentration. Selecting the
weak solution of minimal smoothness, we apply the homogenization method to pass
from the microscopic to the macroscopic description. The resulting macroscopic model
H(r) contains a homogenized boundary condition that expresses the normal boundary
velocity VN = ∂r/∂t as a linear function of the acid concentration c. Since c depends on
r via an operator F: M(0,T ) →M(0,T ), we prove that F is Lipschitz continuous and, by
Banach’s theorem, possesses a unique fixed point r∗, which yields the unique solution
H = H(r∗).
Keywords: free boundary problem; diffusion; structures with special periodicity; homog-
enization.

1. Introduction
In the proposed manuscript we consider a mathematical model of in situ leaching of
rare metals and prove existence and uniqueness theorems for the classical solvability
of the initial-boundary value problem for the corresponding system of differential
equations at the macroscopic level.
The extraction of rare metals by leaching is a very important task for our na-
tional economy. Natural deposits of uranium, nickel and other rare metals are com-
plex geologically heterogeneous objects. Inhomogeneity means that the properties
of ore bodies vary in space. Analyses of wells and cores show that the geological
properties (porosity, permeability, etc.) of ore bodies are heterogeneous even within
a single deposit. Very often the insufficient consideration of the consequences of in-
homogeneities at the stage of operation planning becomes apparent too late, when
the acid solution injected into the soil through injection wells appears far from the
intended location. In addition, an important role is played by the concentration of
the injected acid, the injection modes of acid solutions and other factors.
Hence, understanding the dynamics of fluids in heterogeneous porous media
and the mechanism of dissolution of rocks by acids is of fundamental importance
for the effective management of rare metal mining. This is achieved by creating
a Prototype of a hydrodynamic simulator for the ore body based on the
corresponding mathematical models and a set of computer programs that visualise
the physical process and determine the dynamic changes in the main characteristics
of the mathematical model, which allows one to optimize the entire technological
process. A Hydrodynamic simulator of the ore body is a prototype of the
hydrodynamic simulator supplemented with digital characteristics of the geometric
and physical properties of the solid skeleton. The aim of this manuscript is to
create a mathematical model for a prototype hydrodynamic simulator describing
ore deposits with a given structure of the deposit’s solid skeleton.
Currently, there is a wide range of mathematical models for describing the dy-
namics of in-situ leaching that can describe the physical processes under considera-
tion, but only at the Macroscopic Level (see Refs. 1–3 and references therein). Un-
like Microscopic models (the characteristic size is approximately tens of microns)
in Macroscopic models the characteristic size is decimeters or tens of decimeters.
Because of this, the aforementioned models do not account for the microstructure
of a continuous medium, since in such a model at each point the medium contains
both a solid skeleton and liquid in the pores or cracks of this skeleton.
All these models are built on the same principle. Fluid dynamics is usually con-
trolled by Darcy’s system of filtration, or some modification of it. The equations
describing the migration of acid and chemical reaction products are simply pos-
tulated and, roughly speaking, are some modifications of the diffusion-convection
equations for the corresponding concentrations. The main thing in these postulates
is the type of coefficients of the equations.
It is here that there is a wide variety of models, depending on the tastes and

models.
The authors of such models simply do not have an exact method for describing
physical processes at the microscopic level based on the fundamental laws of classical
mechanics of continuous media and chemistry, nor do they have the ability to take
the microstructure into account in their macroscopic models. Therefore, they are
forced to limit themselves to certain speculative considerations.
Due to this, a natural question arises. If there are several macroscopic models
describing the same physical process under the same conditions, which of them
most adequately reflects this process? Where is the criterion of adequacy here? It
does not make sense to talk about an experiment, since each model has enough free
parameters that are not related to the reservoir geometry (e.g., porosity), or to the
physical characteristics of the process (such as the viscosity of the filtered liquids,
or the physical properties of the solid skeleton and the like). So, with the variation
of these free parameters, one may get a match with any experiment.
R. Burridge and J. B. Keller4 and E. S´anchez-Palencia5 were the first to explain
that the exact description of filtration of liquids and seismic waves in rocks at the
macroscopic level is possible if and only if:
(a) the physical process under consideration is described at the microscopic level
by equations of Newtonian classical continuum mechanics (exact model);
(b) a set of small dimensionless parameters is selected.
(c) the macroscopic mathematical model is an exact asymptotic limit (homoge-
nization) of exact mathematical models at the microscopic level, when the selected
small parameters tend to zero.

them was not an easy task requiring considerable effort and ingenuity. Everything
changed after the appearance of G. Nguetseng’s paper,14 where the author proposed
the Method of two-scale convergence in periodic structures. What used to be

The integral identities equivalent to the dynamic Stokes and Lam´e equations,
as well as the integral identities for the diffusion equation with standard boundary
conditions are well known.
But the equivalent expression of differential equations in the form of integral
identities is a general and rather difficult challenge for the free boundary prob-
lems. The happy exception was the Stefan problem (Refs. 23, 24) describing phase
transitions in pure (without impurities) media. Such, for example, as ”water-ice”
or chemically pure metals. The authors of the cited works O. A. Oleinik and S.
L. Kamenomostskaya (S. Kamin) managed to reformulate the problem so that the
equivalent formulation in the form of an integral identity, in the case of the exis-
tence of a classical solution, contained both the equation of thermal conductivity
outside the free boundary and the condition itself on the free boundary. This ap-
proach allowed them to prove quite simply the existence and uniqueness of the weak
solution to the integral identity under minimal conditions for the smoothness of the
solution. At the same time, in the case of the existence of a classical solution of the
Stefan problem, the latter must coincide with the weak solution. The question of
the existence of a classical solution globally in time to the one-phase Stefan problem
remained open until 1975.20 The existence of a classical solution to the two-phase
