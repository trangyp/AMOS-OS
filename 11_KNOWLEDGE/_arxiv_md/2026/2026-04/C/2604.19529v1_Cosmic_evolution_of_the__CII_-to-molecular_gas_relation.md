ABSTRACT
The [C ii] 158 µm line is widely used to trace star formation and the gas contents of high-redshift galaxies. However, it remains unclear
under which physical conditions it reliably traces the molecular reservoir, and whether a unique conversion factor α[C ii] can be applied
across cosmic time. We investigate the evolution of the relation between the [C ii] luminosity and molecular gas mass from z ≃10
to z ≃0.2 using the Vintergatan simulation, a high-resolution cosmological zoom-in of a Milky Way–like galaxy. We post-process
the snapshots with the Skirt radiative transfer code to generate synthetic [C ii] data cubes. We measure global and spatially resolved
(100 pc) relations between [C ii] luminosity (L[C ii]), star formation rate (SFR), and molecular gas mass (Mmol). We follow the redshift
evolution of the [C ii]–to–molecular gas conversion factor α[C ii], and link these trends to the evolution of the interstellar medium
(ISM) phases. The global L[C ii]–Mmol and L[C ii]–SFR relations evolve from a steep, [C ii]-deficient regime at very low metallicity to
an almost linear behaviour, similar to calibrations at z ≈2, once the ISM reaches Z ≳0.05–0.1 Z⊙at z ≲5. Over this evolution, α[C ii]
spans nearly three orders of magnitude, from ≳104 down to ≈10 M⊙L−1
⊙, even though the [C ii] emission remains spatially correlated
with the molecular gas. We then provide practical prescriptions for α[C ii] based on integrated and local quantities. The latter recovers
the total molecular mass of Vintergatan within factors of order unity, whereas global relations depending only on L[C ii] and/or the
integrated metallicity fail by up to an order of magnitude during major and minor merger epochs. A unique, redshift-independent α[C ii]
therefore cannot recover molecular gas masses across the regimes we explore. [C ii] remains a viable tracer of molecular gas at very
high redshifts, but only when used with conversion factors that explicitly account for metallicity, ISM phase mix, and merger events.

1. Introduction
A central goal of galaxy evolution studies is to understand
how baryons cycle between gas and stars across cosmic time
(e.g. Madau & Dickinson 2014; Walter et al. 2020). The cosmic
star formation rate (SFR) density rises rapidly from the earliest
epochs, peaks around z ≈2, and subsequently declines, reflect-
ing a complex interplay between gas accretion, star formation,
and feedback (e.g. Bouché et al. 2010). In this context, the small-
scale physics of the interstellar medium (ISM), its density struc-
ture, phase mix, together with dynamics regulate how efficiently
galaxies can convert their gas reservoirs into stars at different
epochs and in different environments (e.g. Krumholz et al. 2012;
Hopkins et al. 2012; Krumholz et al. 2018). Limited sensitivity,
coarse spatial resolution, and heterogeneous samples still pre-
vent us from establishing whether early galaxies simply follow
the same relations for star formation as in the local Universe,
or instead host a qualitatively different star-forming regime (e.g.
Genzel et al. 2010; Tacconi et al. 2013; Freundlich et al. 2013).
At low redshift, the most widely used molecular gas tracer is
low-J CO, calibrated via a CO-to-H2 conversion factor αCO that
depends on environment, in particular metallicity and dynamical
state (e.g. Bolatto et al. 2013). In nearby galaxies, αCO increases
sharply below Z ≈0.3 −0.5 Z⊙as CO becomes confined to the
densest, best-shielded regions, and variations in depletion time
correlate more strongly with dynamical conditions than with in-
stantaneous SFR (e.g. Renaud et al. 2019). These results already
indicate that, even in the conditions of the local Universe, com-

The [C ii] 158 µm fine-structure line is typically among the
brightest far-infrared lines in high-redshift star-forming galax-
ies and is redshifted into the ALMA bands for z ≳4, making it
a cornerstone tracer (e.g. Carilli & Walter 2013; Le Fèvre et al.
2020; Fudamoto et al. 2022). The line is primarily excited in
photodissociation regions (PDRs) and at the surfaces of molecu-
lar clouds, and acts as a major coolant of the cold, neutral ISM. It
is thus widely used as a tracer of both star formation and the cold
gas reservoir (e.g. Pabst et al. 2022; Wolfire et al. 2022). How-
ever, [C ii] can also be emitted from more diffuse atomic and
ionised gas, with relative contribution of depending on metallic-
ity, radiation field, and gas surface density, which complicates
any simple conversion between L[C ii] and either the SFR or the
molecular gas mass (e.g. Pineda et al. 2014; Vallini et al. 2015;
Accurso et al. 2017). By analogy with CO, one can expect the
[C ii]–to–gas conversion factor to depend not only on metallic-
ity but also on the local ISM structure, pressure, and radiation
field. Calibrations derived in relatively metal-rich, star-forming
galaxies may not extrapolate straightforwardly to more extreme,
low-metallicity regimes found at high redshifts.
Large ALMA programs such as ALPINE (Le Fèvre et al.
2020; Béthermin et al. 2020) and REBELS (Bouwens et al.
2022), along with targeted follow-up campaigns (e.g., ALPINE-
CRISTAL; Herrera-Camus et al. 2025), have now produced size-
able samples of [C ii]-detected galaxies at z ≳4. These data
enable measurements of gas kinematics, sizes, and dynami-
cal masses, and allow for the first attempts at derived re-

resolution studies increasingly use [C ii] as their primary kine-

expensive or inaccessible. Coherent dynamical analyses must,
for practical reasons, transition from CO-based to [C ii]-based
tracers (e.g. Rizzo et al. 2024; Roman-Oliveira et al. 2024). This
shift underscores the need to understand how faithfully [C ii]
traces the molecular gas, both spatially and dynamically, in or-
der to place [C ii]-based kinematic studies on the same footing
as lower-redshift CO surveys. While these efforts have estab-
lished that [C ii] can broadly trace star formation and cold gas
at high redshift (e.g. De Looze et al. 2014; Zanella et al. 2018),
they also reveal substantial scatter and systematic offsets relative
to local [C ii]-based SFR and gas calibrations (e.g. Schaerer et al.
2020; Accard et al. 2025). In particular, a [C ii] deficit (i.e. a drop
in L[C ii]/LIR or an apparent underluminosity of [C ii] at fixed
SFR) appears to emerge in compact starbursts and some high-z
main-sequence galaxies, raising questions on how reliably [C ii]
can be used as a quantitative tracer in the early Universe and
whether a single, locally calibrated α[C ii] is adequate over a broad
range of redshifts and ISM conditions (e.g. Malhotra et al. 2001;
Lagache et al. 2018; Ferrara et al. 2019). A physically grounded
revision of these conversion factors must explicitly track how
the physical conditions of the [C ii]-emitting gas, and the (typ-
ically minor) contributions from non-molecular phases, evolve
with redshift and with global galaxy properties such as metallic-
ity and dynamical state (e.g. Narayanan & Krumholz 2017).
Addressing these issues requires information that is diffi-
cult or impossible to obtain from observations alone, especially
below kiloparsec scales in the early Universe. High-resolution
cosmological zoom-in simulations resolve the internal struc-
ture of the ISM, the clustered nature of star formation, and the
time-variable impact of feedback, providing a self-consistent
laboratory for exploring how [C ii] and other lines respond to
changing physical conditions in galaxies (e.g. Hopkins et al.
2014; Somerville & Davé 2015). However, accurately modelling
the emergent line emission with full three-dimensional radia-
tive transfer is computationally expensive, particularly at the
resolution of state-of-the-art cosmological simulations. It is
then often applied in post-processing rather than evolved self-
consistently on the fly (e.g. Vallini et al. 2015; Olsen et al. 2021).
Recent radiation-hydrodynamical efforts such as the MEGA-
TRON simulations (Katz et al. 2025; Choustikov et al. 2026)
and non-equilibrium thermochemistry schemes for metal ions
(e.g. Lupi et al. 2020) take important steps towards on-the-fly
line predictions, but they still neglect dust scattering and re-
emission. As a result, detailed dust radiative transfer is currently
more practical to perform in post-processing than to evolve self-
consistently. In this work we adopt such a post-processing ap-
proach, applying three-dimensional radiative transfer to simula-
tion snapshots. This strategy is computationally more tractable
than performing full radiation–hydrodynamics at the same res-
olution, while still capturing the geometry, dust distribution,
and radiation fields that shape the emergent [C ii] emission (e.g.
Baes et al. 2011). This approach enables both a decomposi-
tion of [C ii] into its contributing ISM phases and the deriva-
tion and validation of spatially resolved versus global [C ii]-to-
gas conversion recipes tailored to high-redshift conditions (e.g.
Ferrara et al. 2019).
In this work, we combine the high-resolution cosmologi-
cal zoom-in simulation Vintergatan (Agertz et al. 2021) with
the three-dimensional Monte Carlo radiative transfer code Skirt
(Camps & Baes 2020). We analyse both global and spatially
resolved [C ii]–SFR and [C ii]–gas relations to establish under
which conditions [C ii] remains a straightforward tracer of the
total molecular gas reservoir. To assess the accuracy of global
versus resolved α[C ii] prescriptions, we provide updated, physi-
cally grounded guidelines for interpreting [C ii] measurements at
high redshift.
2. Methods
2.1. The VINTERGATAN simulation
We analyse the Vintergatan project, a high-resolution cosmo-
logical zoom-in simulation of a Milky Way–mass galaxy (M⋆≈
6 × 1010 M⊙at z = 0.2). A detailed description of the simula-
tion setup, and sub-grid physics implementation is provided in
Agertz et al. (2021); here we briefly summarise the aspects that
are most relevant to the present work.
The simulation is performed with the adaptive mesh refine-
ment (AMR) code Ramses (Teyssier 2002). The adopted cosmol-
ogy is a flat ΛCDM model with H0 = 70.2 km s−1 Mpc−1, Ωm =
0.272, ΩΛ = 0.728, and Ωb = 0.045. A key feature of Vin-
tergatan is its high numerical resolution, achieved through a
pseudo-Lagrangian refinement strategy that follows the densest
structures in the ISM. The simulation reaches a spatial resolution
of ∆x ≈20 pc in the highest-density regions. The dark matter
mass resolution in the zoom-in region is 3.5 × 104 M⊙, while the
initial gas mass resolution is 7070 M⊙.
Star formation is modelled in gas above a density thresh-
old n > 100 cm−3, with a local efficiency per free-fall time
that depends on the turbulent properties of the gas. Stellar
feedback includes the combined effects of stellar winds, radi-
ation pressure, and Type Ia and Type II supernovae. The evo-
lution of the galaxy structure and its chemical enrichment his-
tory is discussed in detail in the companion papers Renaud et al.
(2021a,b); Segovia Otero et al. (2022); Renaud et al. (2025).
To associate the [C ii] emission with specific ISM compo-
nents, we classify the gas into distinct phases using temperature
and density thresholds. We define the cold atomic phase as gas
with T < 2 × 104 K and 0.1 < n < 10 cm−3, and the molecular
phase as gas with T < 2 × 104 K and n > 10 cm−3. Gas at lower
densities (n ≤0.1 cm−3) or higher temperatures is grouped into
a warm–hot diffuse phase in and around the galaxy.
In this work, we compare the synthetic [C ii] emission with
the SFR of the simulation. The SFR surface density maps (ΣSFR)
are directly computed from the stellar particle population on a
grid of 100 pc resolution, chosen for the reasons discussed in
Sect. 2.2. To explore how the correlation between [C ii] emission
and star formation depends on the averaging timescale, and con-
sequently on different stages of the star formation process, we
construct ΣSFR maps over three timescales: τSF = 10, 30, and
100 Myr. We adopt τSF = 10 Myr as our fiducial choice, as it
corresponds to the timescale on which feedback regulates the lo-
cal ISM. A comparison with the other timescales, for a subset of
the results in Sect. 3, is provided in Appendix A.
Throughout this work, global galaxy properties (SFR, gas
mass, [C ii] luminosity, etc.) are first extracted within a three-
dimensional spherical aperture of radius 3 rH, where rH is the
stellar half-mass radius. The resulting quantities are then pro-
jected onto the face-on and edge-on view of the galaxy.

In the following, we describe how we generated synthetic obser-

To prepare the input medium, we extract gas cells and star
particles from the Ramses snapshots using an octree grid struc-
ture. This ensures that the high spatial resolution of the dense
regions in Vintergatan is preserved, with the grid refinement
levels defined dynamically based on the simulation resolution.
We enforce a maximum refinement level such that the finest grid
cells in Skirt match the highest-resolution gas cells in the hy-
drodynamical snapshot.
For each snapshot, we treat differently the evolved stellar
component (>10 Myr) and the younger stars. The former is mod-
elled using the Bruzual & Charlot (2003) library with a Chabrier
(2003) inital mass function. The young star-forming regions are
modelled using the toddlers library (Kapoor et al. 2023, 2024),
which provides age- and metallicity-dependent SEDs including
nebular and PDR line emission. In Skirt, both stellar compo-
nents are represented as smoothed particle sources; we adopt
the standard cubic-spline smoothing kernel, with the smoothing
length of each particle set equal to twice its local cell size (i.e.
of order the native RAMSES resolution, ∼20–100 pc depending
on refinement level), to map the particle luminosities onto the
radiative-transfer grid.
The dust distribution is derived directly from the gas cells,
from which we extract positions, metallicities (Z), masses
(Mgas), and temperatures (Tgas). Gas metallicities Z are taken di-
rectly from the simulation and correspond to the total metal mass
fraction tracked by Ramses, assuming a solar abundance pattern;
for stellar particles we additionally compute the metallicity from
their Fe and O abundances, Z⋆∝1.06 Fe + 2.09 O, to match the
toddlers input requirements. We compute the dust mass in each
cell assuming a constant dust-to-metal ratio, fdust = 0.2, such
that Mdust = fdust Z Mgas. The choice of fdust is motivated by the
Skirt post-processing of the NewHorizon and NewCluster sim-
ulations at z ≃5 presented in Ismail et al. (2026), where varying
the dust-to-metal ratio by a factor of ≈2.5 only has a modest im-
pact on [C ii] luminosities compared to optical tracers, and leaves
the main [C ii]–SFR trends unchanged. We restrict the dust dis-
tribution to regions where the gas temperature is below 106 K, as
dust grains are expected to be destroyed by thermal sputtering in
hotter gas, (e.g. Draine & Salpeter 1979). We adopt the Themis
dust mix (Jones et al. 2017), which models the physical condi-
tions of dust grains varying between diffuse and dense ISM, with
15 grain size bins for both silicate and hydrocarbon populations.
Stochastic heating of dust grains is fully taken into account to
accurately model the mid-IR emission.
We account for Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)
heating, which is crucial at high redshifts where TCMB becomes
comparable to dust temperatures, potentially affecting far-IR lu-
minosity measurements (da Cunha et al. 2013). Secondary emis-
sion is calculated iteratively until convergence.
We follow the photon-statistics prescription of Kapoor et al.
(2024, their Appendix A) to estimate the optimal number of pho-
ton packets required to achieve a target signal-to-noise ratio in
the synthetic images. In practice, we impose a fixed physical res-
olution of 100 pc per pixel at all redshifts and adopt Nph = 1010
photon packets for all radiative transfer runs, as a compromise
between numerical convergence, signal-to-noise ratio, and com-
putational cost. The wavelength grid is discretised into a master
logarithmic grid covering 0.01–3000 µm, supplemented by high-

2.3. Extraction of the [CII] line measurements from
integrated and resolved SEDs
For each snapshot, we derive integrated [C ii] properties directly
from the spatially integrated SEDs produced by Skirt. For the
[C ii] 158 µm line, we convert the rest-frame line wavelength to
the observed frame using the snapshot redshift and define a ve-
locity window, typically ±2000 km s−1, to enclose the line profile
and neighbouring continuum.
Within this spectral window, we estimate the underlying con-
tinuum by fitting a linear function to line-free channels on both
sides of the line centre, excluding the region where the line emis-
sion dominates. After subtracting this continuum model, we fit
a single Gaussian profile to the residual line spectrum using
standard least-squares minimisation to obtain the line centroid,
width, and peak amplitude. The integrated line flux is then com-
puted analytically from the best-fitting Gaussian parameters and
converted to a line luminosity as

2.4. Projection and aperture size
All the steps described above are applied independently to dif-
ferent viewing angles of the same galaxy. For each snapshot, we
compute the total stellar angular-momentum vector and define a
“face-on” view by aligning the simulation z-axis with this vector,
and an “edge-on” view by rotating the line of sight by 90◦. Be-
cause the [C ii] 158 µm line lies in the far-infrared regime, dust
attenuation and viewing-angle effects have a negligible impact
on the total [C ii] luminosity. We have further verified that re-
stricting the three-dimensional aperture from 3 rH to 1 rH only
weakly changes the inferred α[C ii], owing to the modest vari-
ations in enclosed molecular fraction and metallicity over this

3. Results
3.1. Global scaling relations and consistency with literature
To benchmark our simulation against observational constraints,
we compare the total SFR and molecular gas mass (Mmol) to the
[C ii] luminosity (L[C ii]), integrated within three stellar half-mass
radii. In addition, we consider the ratio between [C ii] and in-
frared luminosities (integrated between 8–1000 µm), L[C ii]/LIR.
The left panel of Fig. 1 presents L[C ii] as a function of Mmol
at different redshifts. At z ≲5, Vintergatan lies close to the em-
pirical, observation-based L[C ii]–Mmol relation of Zanella et al.
(2018), derived for main-sequence galaxies at z ≈2 with
log(M⋆/M⊙) ≈10 −11. In their calibration, Mmol is inferred
from CO(1–0) and dust continuum measurements, converted
to molecular gas masses using standard, metallicity-dependent
CO–to–H2 and dust–to–gas conversion factors. Around z ≃
2, our points overlap their relation particularly well in the
L[C ii]–Mmol plane, while at lower redshift they remain within
the scatter of the low- and intermediate-redshift samples com-
piled by Madden et al. (2020) and Vizgan et al. (2022). The lat-
ter works combine nearby star-forming galaxies and higher-
redshift systems into L[C ii]–Mmol and L[C ii]/LIR benchmarks,
against which Vintergatan reproduces the same [C ii] output of
chemically evolved, main-sequence galaxies. A fit of the form

In this early phase, the simulated L[C ii]/LIR ratios are re-
duced by up to two orders of magnitude relative to the typical
late-time plateau around 10−3. These values are therefore lower
than those measured in the most [C ii]-deficient local ULIRGs
and in current high-z [C ii]-bright samples, which typically show
L[C ii]/LIR ≳3 × 10−4 (e.g. Luhman et al. 2003; Harikane et al.
2020). In our simulation this extreme [C ii] deficit appears when
the global gas metallicity is very low (Fig. 2), and coincides with
the regime where the global L[C ii]–Mmol and L[C ii]–SFR relations
are steeper than local Universe, nearly linear calibrations. To-
gether, these trends show that the [C ii]–to–molecular–gas con-
version factor, α[C ii], varies by several orders of magnitude in
Vintergatan, and cannot be represented by a single constant
value.
The same deviation from linearity is obtained when replacing
molecular gas by SFR, as shown in the right panel of Fig. 1. At
z ≳5, when Vintergatan is still a low-mass, metal-poor system,
with M⋆≈3 × 107 M⊙and log10(Z/Z⊙) ≲−1.3, the galaxy
follows a very steep sequence: L[C ii] rises by nearly five orders
of magnitude while the SFR increases by only ≈3 dex. This
behaviour reflects the rapid global chemical enrichment shown
in Fig. 2: the mass-weighted metallicity starts from very metal-
poor regimes (log10(Z/Z⊙) ≃−2.5 at z ≈10) and only exceeds
log10(Z/Z⊙) ≃−1.3 at z ≈5, where the change of regime appears
in both panels of Fig. 1. The resolved metallicity distribution
shown by the coloured density map in Fig. 2 closely tracks this
global trend, with the 16th–84th percentiles forming a narrow
band of ≈0.2 dex, indicating that the ISM is well-mixed.
Taken together, these results suggest that, at early times when
the global gas metallicity is still low, the evolution of the [C ii]
luminosity is primarily driven by the changing ISM conditions
and, in particular, by the enrichment of metals. Studies based on
FIRE-2 zoom-in simulations at z ≳5 likewise find an approx-
imately linear L[C ii]–SFR relation at above a few M⊙yr−1 that
matches current ALMA samples (Liu et al. 2026), but do not
probe the low-SFR regime (SFR ≲1 M⊙yr−1) where our Vin-
tergatan results reveal a steeper, strongly evolving L[C ii]–SFR
relation. Once the gas is sufficiently enriched, with log10(Z/Z⊙)
settling on a plateau around −1 to −0.5 after z ≈4, the [C ii]–SFR
and [C ii]–Mmol relations approach the widely used, nearly linear
relation discussed above for z ≲5.
To contextualise this evolution, we compare our results with
literature calibrations. In the right panel of Fig. 1, we include the
“entire sample” L[C ii]–SFR relation of De Looze et al. (2014),
derived from a heterogeneous set of over ∼500 low-redshift
systems ranging from dwarf galaxies to ULIRGs. We note that
using the other De Looze et al. (2014) relations restricted to spe-
cific subsamples (e.g. starbursts or metal-poor dwarfs) does not
alter the qualitative comparison: the low-metallicity, i.e. high-z,
Vintergatan points consistently fall below these local observa-
tional benchmarks.
We also compare with the metallicity-dependent theoretical
model of Vallini et al. (2015), showing tracks evaluated at two
fixed metallicities that roughly bracket the Vintergatan data: a
low-metallicity benchmark (log10(Z/Z⊙) = −1.95) and an en-
riched benchmark (log10(Z/Z⊙) = −0.5). In the low-Z, high-
redshift regime, the Vintergatan points lie systematically be-
low the De Looze et al. (2014) relation and the high-metallicity
Vallini et al. (2015) track. However, the apparent offset from the
Vallini et al. (2015) relation is largely driven by the low metal-
licity of Vintergatan at these epochs. If instead we evaluate the
Vallini et al. (2015) prescription using, for each snapshot, the 10
Myr-averaged SFR and the gas metallicity measured in VIN-
TERGATN, the resulting model still lies systematically below
the [C ii] luminosities obtained from our radiative transfer post-
processing with Skirt. This demonstrates that, for fixed physi-
cal conditions (SFR, Z), the simulated [C ii] emission is brighter
than predicted by the analytical model. We caution, however,
that this discrepancy likely arises from the calibration of the
Vallini et al. (2015) relation. Their fitting formula is derived for
metallicities Z ≳0.05 Z⊙, and applying it to the extremely metal-
poor regime of Vintergatan at high redshift requires extrapolat-
ing well outside its domain of validity.

full range of local conditions sampled within the galaxy.

[C ii] emission populates density–temperature phase space and
how mergers and rapid enrichment episodes redistribute [C ii]
between molecular, atomic, and ionised gas phases.
3.4. Physical origin of [CII] across cosmic time
To interpret the behaviour of the [C ii]–to–gas conversion factors,
we now examine how the phase structure of the ISM evolves
over cosmic time, and then relate this to the origin of the [C ii]
emission. At the earliest times (z ≳8), both the atomic and
molecular reservoirs remain modest according to our phase def-
initions (Fig. 5). This reflects the combined effects of very low
metallicity and inefficient or absent dust shielding, which pre-
vent the gas from cooling and settling into the high-density, low-
temperature regime required for abundant H2 formation. As the
galaxy evolves towards z ≈6, both the atomic and molecular
gas masses grow substantially. At z = 7.5, the atomic phase be-
comes a significant, and for ≈250 Myr even slightly dominant,
fraction of the cold gas reservoir, while the molecular compo-
nent increases but still occupies a relatively small volume and
fraction of the total mass. The phase diagram shows that a large
fraction of the gas has cooled below 2×104 K but remains below
the molecular density, i.e. predominantly atomic.

molecular-gas distribution, corresponding to the compact re-
gions where the metallicity is the highest. Quantitatively, how-

From z ≈5 down to z ≈0, the molecular phase remains
the dominant origin of the [C ii] emission, with the molecu-
lar contribution staying at ≳80% (Fig. 7). Over this redshift
range, the multiphase structure of the ISM and the local condi-
tions under which [C ii] is emitted keep evolving, but the dom-
inant [C ii]-emitting phase does not change. In this regime, an
increasing fraction of the molecular gas resides in environments
where [C ii] is relatively faint, and a small part of the molecular
mass becomes effectively [C ii]-dark (blue pixels outside the or-
ange contours in the second row of Fig. 6). The fractional [C ii]
contributions from molecular and atomic gas vary only weakly
with time (Fig. 7), indicating that mergers and starburst episodes
primarily modulate the excitation conditions and α[C ii], rather
than modifying the dominant [C ii]-emitting phase. The earli-
est major merger at z ≃6 coincides with one of the largest
excursions in the fractional [C ii] contribution from the molec-
ular and atomic phases (Fig. 7). Around this epoch the galaxy
is still rapidly assembling its cold gas reservoir and undergo-
ing strong chemical enrichment; the merger compresses the gas
and accelerates the build-up of dense, enriched regions, which
amplifies the [C ii] output of the molecular phase. We also note
that, at this epoch, a rotationally supported disc emerges (around
z ≃5; Segovia Otero et al. 2022, their Fig. 5). Although one
might expect a sharp change in dust attenuation or UV shield-

discussed in Sect. 3.3. At very high redshift, large α[C ii] values do

arise because [C ii] is confined to dense, metal-poor regions. Dur-
ing the build-up phase, [C ii] increasingly traces the total neutral
envelope rather than just the molecular cores, global calibrations
that implicitly assume a fixed phase mix begin to misassign part
of the [C ii] luminosity to H2. Once the ISM is enriched and [C ii]
emission is largely dominated by the molecular phase (Fig. 7),
mergers and rapid chemical evolution between 2 ≲z ≲5 drive

4. Discussion
4.1. Caveats and limitations
Our analysis is based on a single high-resolution cosmological
zoom-in of a Milky Way-like progenitor, and therefore follows
one specific mass assembly and merger history rather than a
statistical population of galaxies. While the ISM conditions in
Vintergatan broadly resemble those inferred for main-sequence
systems of comparable mass at z ≲3 (e.g. Tacconi et al. 2018),
the galaxy is substantially less massive and more metal-poor
than the observed luminous, [C ii]-bright systems that dominate
current high-redshift samples (e.g. Herrera-Camus et al. 2025).
The present work should thus be viewed not as a universal cal-
ibration, but as a detailed example of how [C ii] traces the ISM
in a galaxy that evolves from a low-mass, metal-poor progeni-
tor into a present-day disc. The mechanisms we identify, which
link α[C ii] to phase mix, metallicity, and assembly history, are
expected to operate in a broad range of systems; the resulting
trends and caveats should therefore remain relevant well beyond
this particular Milky Way analogue.
A second limitation arises from the adopted sub-grid and ra-
diative transfer prescriptions. The Skirt/Toddlers implementa-
tion relies on pre-computed photoionisation and PDR models,
and assumes a fixed dust-to-metal ratio as a function of metal-
licity. At very low metallicity, both the dust contents and the
covering fraction of dense PDRs are poorly constrained, which
could introduce systematic uncertainties in the predicted [C ii]
emissivity and phase mix, especially at z ≳8. Likewise, our def-
inition of the molecular phase is based on gas density and tem-
perature cuts rather than an explicit chemical network, thus the
precise separation between atomic and molecular gas (and be-
tween different molecular sub-phases) should be regarded as ap-
proximate. Physically motivated prescriptions for the H2 fraction
that account for shielding and metallicity (e.g. Krumholz et al.
2009) and detailed non-equilibrium networks for CO and related
species (e.g. Glover et al. 2010) provide more physical treat-
ments of the atomic-to-molecular transition and CO chemistry
than our simple density- and temperature-based phase defini-
tion. However, they involve large reaction networks with many
density- and dust-dependent channels and typically require un-
resolved clumping factors and other sub-grid assumptions that
remain uncertain at the resolution of galaxy-scale simulations.
Given these uncertainties, and our focus on [C ii] rather than CO,
we regard our simple density- and temperature-based phase def-
inition as a pragmatic choice, and expect our qualitative conclu-
sions about the association between [C ii] emission and dense,
predominantly molecular gas to be robust, even though the exact
partition between atomic and molecular components depends on
the precise definition adopted.
Despite these caveats, several of our conclusions are qualita-
tively robust against plausible variations in the sub-grid physics
and radiative transfer details. The close correspondence between
global and resolved α[C ii], the near-unity recovered molecular
mass fractions after the transition epoch, and the strong redshift
evolution of global α[C ii] all persist when varying the spatial
aperture used to extract the emission, projection, and possible
[C ii] self-absorption within reasonable bounds (Appendix A).
These features reflect generic changes in ISM structure, metal-
licity, and surface density along the Vintergatan track, rather
than being driven by a small subset of extreme regions or by a
specific choice of modelling parameters. Extending this analysis
to suites of zoom-in simulations spanning a wider range of stel-
lar masses and enrichment histories will be essential to quantify
how the characteristic transition redshift and the normalisation
of α[C ii] scale with mass and metallicity.
4.2. Implications for [CII] as a molecular gas tracer
From an observational perspective, our results emphasise both
the strengths and the limitations of [C ii] as a molecular gas
tracer at high redshift. In Vintergatan, [C ii] is almost always
co-spatial with the molecular gas once the galaxy has reached
metallicities typical of z ≈2 −5 main-sequence systems, and
resolved prescriptions based on Σ[C ii] and Z can recover the total
molecular mass to within factors of order unity. In this regime,
[C ii] can therefore be used as a practical tracer of the molec-
ular reservoir and, with appropriate resolved calibrations, as a
quantitative proxy for Mmol. At earlier times (6 ≲z ≲8), during
the merger-dominated assembly phase, α[C ii] evolves rapidly and
[C ii] traces more and more accurately the total neutral gas reser-
voir rather than only the densest molecular cores. Our results
caution against applying low-redshift, globally calibrated α[C ii]
relations to low-mass systems at high redshift without account-
ing for their lower metallicities and rapidly evolving conditions.
For current ALMA observations of massive z ≈4 −7 galax-
ies, which typically probe systems more massive and more [C ii]-
luminous than Vintergatan at the same redshift, our findings
support the use of [C ii] as a molecular gas tracer, but empha-
sise that the choice of α[C ii] or scaling relations should be in ac-
cordance with the regime for which it is calibrated (in particular
in stellar mass, metallicity). In this context, spatially resolved

Looking ahead to less massive systems and to future fa-

galaxies and forthcoming deep [C ii] surveys will be crucial to
precisely quantify the evolutionary patterns identified here in the
case of other systems.
5. Conclusions
We combine the high-resolution cosmological zoom-in simula-
tion Vintergatan with Skirt radiative transfer code to follow the
joint evolution of [C ii] emission and the molecular gas reservoir
in a Milky Way–like progenitor from z ≃10 to z ≃0.2.
The L[C ii]–Mmol and L[C ii]–SFR relations integrated over the
entire galaxy evolve strongly with redshift, primarily driven by
chemical enrichment and changes in the distribution of cold
gas within the galaxy. At very early times, when the galaxy
is still low-mass and metal-poor, the system follows a steep,
[C ii]-deficient sequence: L[C ii] and L[C ii]/LIR lie well below lo-
cal and high-redshift calibrations, and the conversion factor α[C ii]
reaches ≳103 M⊙L−1
⊙. Once the ISM reaches Z ≳0.05–0.1 Z⊙
at z ≲5, these relations become nearly linear and match with the
empirically calibrated relations of chemically evolved galaxies,
with α[C ii] ∼10–102 M⊙L−1
⊙in broad agreement with existing
observational and theoretical estimates.
The large range in α[C ii] does not arise because most of
the molecular gas becomes [C ii]-dark; instead, it reflects how
efficiently a given amount of molecular gas radiates in [C ii]
under different metallicities, phase mixes, and merger events.
Throughout the evolution, the molecular surface density from
[C ii]-emitting pixels still accounts for ∼90–100 per cent of the
total Mmol at all redshifts, with larger deviations only at the lat-
est times when a small fraction of the molecular gas becomes
effectively [C ii]-dark. In the extremely metal-poor regime, low
molecular masses combined with metal-enriched outflows re-
duce the carbon abundance in the cold ISM, leading to faint
[C ii] emission per unit molecular mass even when Mmol is non-
negligible.
Both global and spatially resolved estimates of α[C ii] carry
different information. The global conversion factor, obtained
from the ratio of total Mmol to total L[C ii], tracks the median of the
resolved pixel distribution at z ≳6, indicating a coherent shift
of the entire distribution of local α[C ii] values with redshift. At
later times however, the global α[C ii] becomes increasingly dom-
inated by a small number of bright [C ii] regions with moderate
local conversion factors, while numerous fainter pixels populate
a high-α[C ii] tail. The difference between global and median of
the resolved values therefore encodes how [C ii] and the molecu-
lar gas are redistributed within the galaxy as it forms a metal-rich
disc. Across the full history of Vintergatan, the effective α[C ii]
spans nearly three orders of magnitude, and no single, redshift-
independent global value can represent the underlying local con-
ditions.
We provide practical prescriptions to use [C ii] as a molecu-
lar gas tracer. Resolved relations of the form α[C ii](Σ[C ii], Z), cal-
ibrated on 100 pc pixels, recover the total molecular mass within
factors of order unity over most of the redshift range, with de-
viations that correlate primarily with major mergers and rapid
enrichment episodes. In contrast, global prescriptions depending
only on L[C ii] and/or integrated metallicity can under- or over-
estimate Mmol by up to an order of magnitude during merger-
dominated phases, despite the important spatial overlap between
[C ii] and H2. These results imply that spatially resolved anal-
yses, which exploit Σ[C ii] together with metallicity information,
are substantially and fundamentally more reliable than unique
global conversion factors.
Examining the phase diagram provides a coherent physical
explanation for the behaviour of α[C ii]. At very high redshift,
[C ii] emission is limited to dense, metal-poor peaks that host
small amounts of molecular gas, leaving most of the cold atomic
reservoir untraced. During the build-up of molecular gas, [C ii]
progressively extends over the entire neutral gas envelope and
draws an increasing contribution from non-molecular gas, while
the galactic molecular fraction and metallicity grow. Around the
transition epoch at z ≃6–5.7, when dust shielding becomes ef-
ficient and a rotationally supported disc emerges, [C ii] becomes
an almost complete tracer of the dominant molecular phase, and
the global α[C ii] remains close to the median of the resolved
distribution. At later times (z ≲4–3), the ISM becomes more
structured: the global α[C ii] is increasingly set by a small number
of bright [C ii] regions with moderate conversion factors, while
numerous fainter pixels populate a high-α[C ii] tail. The differ-
ence between global and median resolved values thus encodes
how [C ii] and the molecular gas are redistributed within the
galaxy as it becomes more metal rich. This evolution explains
why global prescriptions α[C ii](L[C ii], Z) or α[C ii](Z) calibrated at
later times can fail when extrapolated into the low-metallicity,
merger-dominated regime.
Finally, our analysis clarifies the conditions under which
[C ii] can be used as a quantitative tracer of molecular gas
across cosmic time. Once metallicities and masses compara-
ble to z ≈2 −5 main-sequence galaxies are reached, and
particularly in massive systems that are more enriched and
more [C ii]-luminous than VINTERGATAN, appropriately cal-
ibrated, regime-dependent α[C ii] prescriptions should allow re-
liable estimates of Mmol from [C ii] alone, in broad agreement
with existing low-redshift calibrations. At earlier times and in
lower-mass, less metal-rich galaxies, the strong dependence of
α[C ii] on metallicity, ISM phase mix, and mergers implies that
blindly applying a unique, conversion factor derived from low-
redshift data can introduce order-of-magnitude biases. In prac-
tice, our results provide a physically motivated template for
Milky Way–mass progenitors, and a framework for constructing
mass- and metallicity-dependent [C ii]-to-gas conversion func-
tions that can be confronted with deeper [C ii] observations
jointly analysed with JWST rest-frame optical spectroscopy,
which now delivers robust gas-phase metallicities and spatially
resolved abundance gradients in high redshift galaxies.
