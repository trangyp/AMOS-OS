The graph of the specific heat of an ideal Bose gas as a function of temperature exhibits
a characteristic Λ-shaped behavior near the critical temperature of Bose-Einstein condensa-
tion. While there is an explicit formula for the specific heat below the critical temperature, I
failed to find that above the critical temperature in standard textbooks. In 2004, I presented
a derivation for an approximate formula in this Journal.[1] In 2025, Princeton University
Press published The Essential Einstein,[2] a collection of Albert Einstein’s most important
works including an English translation of Einstein’s groundbreaking 1925 paper,[3] and from
it I learned that Einstein himself set a procedure for the formula I was searching for. In
this Comment I will guide readers to execute the calculations that Einstein outlined, correct
some numerical errors in the paper, and compare his formula with mine. The history of the
acceptance of Einstein’s theory will be summarized.
In 1924, Einstein published “Quantum Theory of the Monoatomic Ideal Gas” (Ref. [2],
Chapter 24) which laid the foundation for quantum statistical mechanics. Einstein’s interest
was triggered by the request to translate (to German) and publish a paper in Zeitschrift
f¨ur Physik, previously rejected by the Philosophical Magazine, by the then unknown Indian
physicist Satyendranath Bose.[4] Bose used an innovative way to count photons and ob-
tained the Planck distribution;[4] Einstein generalized Bose’s counting method for identical
particles, and established what is now known as the Bose-Einstein distribution. In 1925,
Einstein wrote a second paper with the same title (Ref. [2], Chapter 25) and predicted the
phenomenon of “Das ges¨attigte ideale Gas” (the saturated ideal gas) which is now referred
to as a Bose-Einstein condensate. He ended the paper with the equation of state of the
unsaturated ideal gas (see Figure 1) which is the focus of this note.

Using this result, the specific heat of an ideal Bose-Einstein gas as a function of temperature
is shown in Figure 3. In this plot, it is visually indistinguishable whether one uses Einstein’s
Eq. (5) or my Eq. (6) for the specific heat above Tc, but the two formulas are fundamen-
tally different. Einstein made the expansion around y = 0, which is the limit T →∞,
while I made the expansion around T = Tc. Einstein’s specific heat tends to 3/2 R as T
tends to infinity, while my specific heat and the discontinuity of the first derivatives with
respect to temperature (∂cv/∂T) at Tc are exact in terms of ζ(3/2), ζ(5/2), and π. Co-
incidentally, Einstein’s high-temperature formula works fine near the critical temperature,
although one cannot ascertain where or not the specific heat is continuous at Tc, and my
critical-temperature formula is satisfactory at high temperature.
After Einstein’s 1925 publication, “the degeneracy of the Bose-Einstein gas has rather got
the reputation of having only a purely imaginary existence”[6] and scientists overlooked this
work for years. In 1938, P. Kapitza and independently J. F. Allen and D. Misener published
results of the superfluidity of liquid helium. The same year Fritz London wrote a letter to
Nature[6] with a plot of the specific heat similar to Figure 3; presumably he used Eq. (8)

In his book Einstein and the Quantum,[10] Douglas Stone wrote, “The generosity of the
‘Bose-Einstein’ designation is not widely appreciated, as few physicists realize that Bose said
not a word about the quantum ideal gas in his seminal paper. The paper that does predict
quantum condensation belongs to Einstein alone.” Bose’s 1924 paper has been translated
back to English in this Journal,[4] and in the quote above Stone is referring to Einstein’s
1925 paper.[3] The readers can assess Stone’s statement based on the original publications.
The term “Bose-Einstein condensation” first appeared in the title of London’s 1938 Physical
