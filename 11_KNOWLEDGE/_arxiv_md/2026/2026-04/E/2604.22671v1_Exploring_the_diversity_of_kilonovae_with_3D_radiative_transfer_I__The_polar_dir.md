ABSTRACT
We present 3D kilonova radiative transfer simulations for a series of binary neutron star merger models. The masses of the neutron
stars are varied as well as the total mass of the system and two different equations of state were used (SFHO and DD2), producing a
range in dynamical ejecta masses and elemental abundance patterns. In this paper, we focus on the bolometric light curves and spectra
in the polar direction for comparison with observations of the kilonova AT2017gfo. We calculate line-by-line opacities and include
new calibrated lanthanide atomic data. All of the simulated spectra show strong features from Sr ii, La iii, Gd iii and Ce iii, which
appear to correspond to features identified in AT2017gfo, although the simulated features are generally more blueshifted. The models
with the lowest lanthanide fraction in the polar direction also show a Y ii feature. Ce iii, Ce ii, Nd iii and Nd ii play an important role in
shaping the spectral continuum. While the bolometric luminosities in the polar direction vary with the ejecta mass of each model, we
find only little sensitivity of the spectral properties to the merger configuration. Our study demonstrates that dynamical ejecta alone
can reproduce (although at earlier times) many spectral properties of AT2017gfo, suggesting dynamical ejecta may have a strong
impact on the early spectral evolution. However, future simulations are needed to also elucidate the role of other ejecta components
for shaping the kilonova spectrum.

1. Introduction
In 2017, the kilonova AT2017gfo (e.g., Coulter et al. 2017;
Cowperthwaite et al. 2017; Drout et al. 2017; Smartt et al. 2017;
Villar et al. 2017, see Margutti & Chornock 2021 for a re-
view) was detected following the gravitational wave signal
GW170817 (Abbott et al. 2017), resulting from the merging of
binary neutron stars. Since this detection there have been nu-
merous efforts to interpret the observations of AT2017gfo. A
number of spectral features have been tentatively identified in
the spectra of AT2017gfo, including Sr ii (Watson et al. 2019),
La iii and Ce iii (Domoto et al. 2022), Y ii (Sneppen & Watson
2023) and Gd iii (Gillanders et al. 2024; Rahmouni et al. 2025).
Domoto et al. (2025) have also shown that Th iii may be de-
tectable. Additionally, Te iii has been suggested as a spectral
feature in the nebular spectra of AT2017gfo (Hotokezaka et al.
2023; Gillanders et al. 2024) as well as AT2023vfi (associ-
ated with GRB230307A Levan et al. 2024; Gillanders & Smartt
2025). Pognan et al. (2025) and Jerkstrand et al. (2025) have
suggested some additional features could be detectable in the
nebular phase. It has been suggested that the feature identified
as Sr ii could be He i (Tarumi et al. 2023; Perego et al. 2022), al-
though Sneppen et al. (2024) argue that the rapid emergence of
the feature is inconsistent with He i and they favour the Sr ii in-
terpretation, at least at the early epochs. Arya et al. (2026) and
Chiba et al. (2026) also discuss the contribution of He i to this
feature.
The atomic data for r-process elements still remains incom-
pletely known. Tanaka et al. (2020) provide theoretical atomic
data for r-process elements, which contains many energy levels
and transitions required to simulate the total opacity in kilono-
vae. However, these theoretically calculated data are uncertain
and not calibrated to experimentally known values. As a result,
the wavelengths and strengths of the transitions are not accurate
enough to predict individual spectral features. This has been dis-
cussed by Tanaka et al. (2020) and Domoto et al. (2021), and the
impact on the Sr ii feature has been described by Shingles et al.
(2023). Recently, Flörs et al. (2026) have provided calibrated
atomic data for singly and doubly ionized lanthanides. In this pa-
per, we make use of the atomic data from Tanaka et al. (2020),
but replace them where available with data from Flörs et al.
(2026). Most kilonova simulations have assumed local thermo-
dynamic equilibrium (LTE), since the atomic data required to
carry out non-LTE simulations are even more incomplete. LTE
may be a reasonable assumption for the early phases of the
kilonova, although Brethauer et al. (2025) have shown that non-
thermal ionisation is important already during the first few days
of the kilonova. Some studies have carried out 1D non-LTE sim-
ulations for the nebular phase where LTE is no longer a valid
assumption (Pognan et al. 2023, 2025; Jerkstrand et al. 2025).
The aim of this work is to connect kilonova signatures
with ejecta properties (e.g. mass and composition) and with
merger parameters (e.g. binary masses and the still unknown
nuclear equation of state). We carry out these simulations self-

consistently by basing our radiative transfer on hydrodynamical
simulations of neutron-star mergers. Detailed radiative transfer
kilonova calculations performed on multidimensional simulation
models are still rare in the literature (e.g. Tanaka & Hotokezaka
2013; Kasen et al. 2015; Miller et al. 2019; Kawaguchi et al.
2021, 2022; Shingles et al. 2023), in part due to the complex-
ity of including the necessary physics ingredients (e.g. neutrino
transport and small-scale turbulence) in hydrodynamic simula-
tions and running them long enough to capture secular ejecta
launched from the merger remnant. For this reason, many stud-
ies focus on the early ejecta component, called dynamical ejecta
(e.g. Bauswein et al. 2013; Wanajo et al. 2014; Radice et al.
2018; Just et al. 2022; Foucart et al. 2023; Neuweiler et al. 2023;
Combi & Siegel 2023; Magistrelli et al. 2024; Rosswog et al.
2025), which tend to be relatively fast and may therefore sur-
round a large fraction of the post-merger ejecta. In AT2017gfo,
it is likely that the dynamical ejecta component was not the dom-
inant one in terms of mass (Siegel 2019; Nedora et al. 2021;
Just et al. 2023; Shingles et al. 2023).
In this paper, we follow on from the work of Shingles et al.
(2023), who carried out 3D kilonova radiative transfer sim-
ulations based on dynamical ejecta from a single hydrody-
namic neutron star merger model. They calculated spectra us-
ing line-by-line opacities, and found that the spectra in the po-
lar direction (near the orientation from which AT2017gfo was
observed; Abbott et al. 2017; Perego et al. 2017; Dhawan et al.
2020; Mooley et al. 2022) show a good match to the spectra ob-
served for AT2017gfo, although at earlier times than observed.
Shingles et al. (2023) demonstrated the importance of 3D radia-
tive transfer simulations for kilonovae, showing that a 1D model
did not reproduce the synthetic observables in any direction of
the 3D model. In this work, we expand on Shingles et al. (2023)
to simulate the kilonova arising from a range of neutron star
merger simulations with different neutron star masses and as-
suming different equations of state. This allows us to explore the
sensitivity of the light curves and spectral properties to different
merger configurations and to make predictions for the variation
that may be observed in future kilonovae. Here we focus only on
the spectra simulated from dynamical ejecta in the polar direc-
tion, near the observed viewing angle of AT2017gfo.
2. Methods
2.1. Radiative transfer
We carry out 3D radiative transfer simulations using the time-
dependent Monte Carlo radiative transfer code artis (Sim 2007;
Kromer & Sim 2009; Bulla et al. 2015; Shingles et al. 2020,
2023) (based on the methods of Lucy 2002, 2003, 2005), in the
same way as Shingles et al. (2023). Radioactive decays are fol-
lowed time-dependently by coupling a nuclear decay network to
artis. This includes a Monte Carlo γ-ray transport scheme (Sim
2007) and a time-dependent treatment of α and β−particle ther-
malisation (see Shingles et al. 2023 for details). Shingles et al.
(2023) showed that the nuclear decay network coupled to artis
is in good agreement with the full network calculation. Line-by-
line opacities are calculated using the Sobolev approximation.
This line-by-line approach enables a treatment of fluorescence
and also allows the direct association of spectral features with
the atomic transitions. We assume local thermodynamic equilib-
rium (LTE).
Each simulation propagates 1.44 × 108 Monte Carlo pack-
ets and is carried out between 0.1 and 80 days, with 64 loga-
rithmically spaced time steps. Escaping packets of radiation are
binned into 100 uniformly spaced angle bins in cos(θ) and ϕ di-
rections to generate angle-dependent light curves and spectra. In
this paper we focus on the synthetic observables in the region
θ < 37◦(averaging over all ϕ angles), which is the region close
to the pole (chosen to be the −z axis). This averaging reduces the
Monte Carlo noise from the simulation. North to south asymme-
tries are present in the models of ∼10s of percent, as previously
discussed by Collins et al. (2024). This leads to quantitive differ-
ences at opposite poles, but qualitatively similar spectra.
2.1.1. Atomic data
As in Shingles et al. (2023), we source atomic data for low mass
elements from the CMFGEN compilation1 (Hillier 1990) (up to
Z = 28). Most of the rest of our atomic data is sourced from the
Japan-Lithuania Opacity Database for Kilonova2 (Tanaka et al.
2020). We replace lanthanide atomic data with calibrated data
from Flörs et al. (2026)3. Flörs et al. (2026) have calibrated the-
oretical energy levels to experimentally known values where
possible. This includes La ii–iii, Ce ii–iii, Pr ii–iii, Nd ii–iii, Pm ii,
Sm ii–iii, Eu ii–iii, Gd ii–iii, Tb ii–iii, Dy ii, Ho ii–iii, Er ii–iii,
Tm ii–iii and Yb ii–iii. We also replace Sr i–v, Y i–ii, Zr i–iv and
Ba i–ii with atomic data from Kurucz (Kurucz 2018, 2006), as
well as neutral species where available, including La i, Ce i, Pr i,
Nd i, Sm i, Eu i, Gd i, Dy i, Er i, Tm i, Yb i, Lu i, Hf i, Ta i, W i,
Re i, Os i, Ir i, Pt i, Au i, Hg i, Pb i and Bi i.
2.2. Neutron star merger simulations
The hydrodynamical neutron star merger simulations we con-
sider were presented by Vijayan 2024 and Vijayan et al. (in
prep.; see also Vijayan et al. 2026). These were simulated us-
ing a 3D general relativistic smoothed-particle hydrodynamics
(SPH) code (Oechslin et al. 2002; Bauswein et al. 2010) and
included a leakage-plus-absorption neutrino treatment (ILEAS
Ardevol-Pulpillo et al. 2019). The key properties of the mod-
els are summarised in Table 1. The suite of models tests vary-
ing neutron star masses with different binary mass ratios and
different total masses. Two equations of state are employed;
SFHO (Steiner et al. 2013) and DD2 (Typel et al. 2010). SFHO
is a relatively soft equation of state, while DD2 is a relatively
stiff equation of state. SFHO typically leads to higher ejecta
masses than DD2 (Bauswein et al. 2013; Sekiguchi et al. 2015;
Radice et al. 2018; Kullmann et al. 2022). The inferred proper-
ties for GW170817/AT2017gfo are also shown for comparison.
The merger simulations were carried out for 20 ms after the
stars merged, and therefore the simulations only capture dynami-
cal ejecta. Mass ejection is expected to continue beyond the time
these simulations ended (for a discussion of secular ejecta see,
e.g. Fernández & Metzger 2013; Perego et al. 2014; Just et al.
2015; Fujibayashi et al. 2018; Siegel & Metzger 2018). In fu-
ture studies, we will investigate the impact of later ejecta com-
ponents, however in this work we carry out radiative transfer
simulations for the dynamical ejecta component only. Owing to
this, the model ejecta masses listed in Table 1 are all relatively
low, and smaller than the mass inferred from AT2017gfo.
The merger simulations were mapped to a 503 Cartesian grid
for the radiative transfer simulation using the same approach as
in Collins et al. (2023), except that we do not assume any fur-

2.3. Nucleosynthesis
Nucleosynthesis calculations were carried out in the same
way as described by Collins et al. (2023) for each SPH parti-
cle trajectory, using the same nuclear reaction network as in
Mendoza-Temis et al. (2015) together with the set of nuclear
reactions labelled ‘FRDM’. The elemental abundance profiles
are mapped onto the merger ejecta at 0.1 days and we initialise
Monte Carlo energy packets with the energy released up to this
time as calculated from the nuclear network (accounting for en-
ergy lost to the expansion of the ejecta). After 0.1 days, energy
deposition is calculated from the radioactive decays by artis.
In Figure 1 we show the mass fractions in the polar region
(defined as the region within 37◦around the polar axis) for each
element synthesised in each of the merger models. In the po-
lar region, the greatest difference between the models is in the
lanthanide mass fractions, where DD2 1.375–1.375 and SFHO
1.3–1.3 show relatively low abundances of these elements. The
Ye distribution for the models with the highest and lowest lan-
thanide fraction in the polar region are shown in Figure A.1 in
Appendix A.
The entire ejecta show on average very similar abundance
patterns between each of the models (see Figure A.2 in Ap-
pendix A), with relatively small differences across all models.
The spherically averaged, mass weighted lanthanide fractions
and Ye for these models are listed in Table 1. The ejecta near
the poles tend to have lower lanthanide fractions since these di-
rections are irradiated by neutrinos, increasing the Ye of the ma-
terial. The ejecta in the plane of the merger tend to have higher
lanthanide fractions and a lower Ye, and also tends to have more
mass ejected in these directions. The DD2 models show a larger
variation in the spherically averaged, mass weighted lanthanide
fractions and Ye than the SFHO models.
We also show the mean density in this region in Figure 2,
as well as the density of select elements. When the ejecta are
optically thick a significant fraction of the radiation reaching an
observer near the poles should come from ejecta in the polar line
of sight, however, we caution that as the ejecta become optically
thin it is expected that radiation would be able to travel further
across the ejecta and radiation reaching an observer would come
from broader ranges of ejecta than just this line of sight (as has
been discussed by Collins et al. 2024).

3.1. Bolometric Light curves
The bolometric light curves in the polar directions are shown
for each of the merger models in Figure 3. For ease of compar-
ison, we show the light curves at one pole (calculated by bin-
ning all escaping Monte Carlo packets of radiation escaping in
the directions < 37◦from the pole, in all azimuthal directions).
As expected from the low ejecta masses (since only dynamical
ejecta is included in these simulations), the model light curves
are fainter than AT2017gfo and they fade faster than the obser-
vations.
Similarly to Collins et al. (2023), we find that all of the mod-
els decline in bolometric luminosity from very early times, and
the peak of the bolometric light curve is before the start of the
simulation (0.1 days). The simulated light curve brightness gen-
erally increases with increasing ejecta mass. The light curve de-
cline rate also slows down for more massive models, such that
the more massive models remain brighter for longer. As is shown
in Table 1, the ejecta mass increases with increasing initial neu-
tron star mass, but also increases for unequal mass binaries (see
e.g. Bauswein et al. 2013). The models using the SFHO equa-
tion of state eject more mass than the models using DD2. The
most massive ejecta model is SFHO 1.15714–1.54286. At times
less than ∼1 day this model is not the brightest, but it does be-
come brightest at ∼1.1 days, as it rises to a secondary peak. This
suggests that relatively more energy is trapped for longer by the
ejecta in this model at early times compared to other models.
The most massive models show this rise to a peak after ∼1 day,
while the less massive models show a declining light curve right
from the start of the calculation (0.1 days) and exhibit a shoulder
where the decline rate increases. The shoulder is earlier for the

3.2.1. Epoch 1
The epoch 1 spectra are shown in Figure 6. The ion emission
contributions are shown beneath each spectrum (similarly to Fig-
ure 5, except the ion contributions are not stacked).
At this epoch, the greatest contributions to the emission
come from Nd iii, Zr iv and Ce iii, with the exception of SFHO
1.15714–1.54286 and DD2 1.4–1.4, where Ce ii dominates the
emission ≲9000 Å, indicating a lower ionisation state in these
models. SFHO 1.15714–1.54286 and DD2 1.4–1.4 have among
the highest densities of Ce in the outer ejecta. Pr iii, Tb iii and
Nd ii also contribute significantly at this epoch. In some models,
Dy iii also has a relatively strong contribution around 3700 Å,
such as in DD2 1.11428-–1.48571, DD2 1.375–1.375 and DD2
1.3–1.3. However, note that Dy iii is uncalibrated in our atomic
data set used for the radiative transfer calculations (see discus-
sion by Flörs et al. 2026). The emission from Nd iii and Zr iv ap-
pear to shape the continuum emission ≲9000, in addition to the
Sr ii feature in those models where it appears. Nd iii is the great-
est contribution to the region around 5000 Å, which appears as a
peak in some models, even those with weaker contributions from
Sr ii, such as SFHO 1.4–1.4, DD2 1.3–1.3 and DD2 1.11428—
1.48571.
At epoch 1, some of the spectra resemble the observations
of AT2017gfo at wavelengths between 4000 – 7500 Å, includ-
ing SFHO 1.3–1.3, SFHO 1.35–1.35 and DD2 1.45–1.45. These
models show very weak contributions from Sr ii at this epoch
that are not distinguishable in the spectrum (see also Figure 5).
This is likely because of the low density of Sr (Figure 2) in the
polar region in these models in the outer ejecta.
As discussed above, some models already show an absorp-
tion trough between ∼5000 and 7000 Å. These models show
larger contributions from Sr ii at these wavelengths, in partic-
ular SFHO 1.15714–1.54286 and SFHO 1.2–1.6, which have
the highest Sr densities in the polar region in the outer ejecta
(above ∼0.45c). In all of these cases, the Sr ii contributions are
at higher velocities (i.e. lower wavelengths) than the inferred ab-
sorption feature in AT2017gfo around 8000 Å, attributed to Sr ii
(Watson et al. 2019). Most models show some emission from
La iii λ14096, Ce iii λ16128 and Ce iii λ20691, although these
are very weak at this epoch and are not yet distinguishable in
the spectrum (see discussion below on the emergence of these
features).
None of the models match the SED of AT2017gfo at wave-
lengths ≳7500 Å, showing much fainter spectra at these redder
wavelengths. In the simulations, Ce iii is the greatest contribu-
tion to emission at these wavelengths. The models also do not
reproduce the spectrum of AT2017gfo at wavelengths ≲4000 Å,
showing too much flux at these wavelengths.
At this epoch, there are also contributions from other triply
ionised species in addition to Zr iv, including Hf iv and Os iv
(with additional more minor contributions including Pt iv, Xe iv
and Ce iv). These species however are not calibrated to exper-
imental values, and therefore the wavelengths and transition
strengths are likely inaccurate in our simulation. To properly test
the impact of these species, accurate triply ionised lanthanide
atomic data are required.

In addition to the ion emission contributions shown in Figure 6,
Figures 7–9 (showing the spectra at epochs 2–4) also show the

At epoch 2 (Figure 7), all models now show a spectral feature
from the Sr ii triplet, as was previously found in the simulation by
Shingles et al. (2023), with lines from Sr ii λ10914, λ10327 and
λ10036. Note that while only the emission components are plot-
ted here, these lines also show an absorption component, form-

Sr ii absorption by these lines).

The Gd iii λ14332 line however only shows a very minor absorp-
tion component (Figure B.1). This blended La iii and Gd iii fea-
ture was not present in the simulation by Shingles et al. (2023)
because the atomic data used in their simulations for these ele-
ments was not calibrated to experimentally known energy levels
(Tanaka et al. 2020). This blended feature now appears since we
use the atomic data from Flörs et al. (2026).
In some models this blend of La iii and Gd iii features is
clearly distinguishable in the simulated spectra, e.g., SFHO
1.35–1.35 and SFHO 1.375–1.375, while in other models the re-
gion around these features remains continuum-like. The Gd iii
λ14332 line was identified by Gillanders et al. (2024) and
Rahmouni et al. (2025) as the strongest Gd iii feature that may
form in kilonovae spectra, and they suggested that this would af-
fect the La iii feature. The results of our simulations agree with
this. The next strongest Gd iii line identified by Rahmouni et al.
(2025) is λ17474, although this contribution is only minor in our
simulations and is unlikely to be observable. It would also form
at the same wavelengths as the strong Ce iii λ16128 line in our
simulations, so would be unlikely to be identifiable even if it
were stronger.
The models all show contributions from Ce iii. The strongest
lines of Ce iii are λ16128, λ20691 and λ19524. In some mod-
els these can be seen as clear emission features in the simulated
spectra, particularly the Ce iii λ16128 and λ20691 lines. At this
epoch, these lines contribute between ∼40–50% of the total
Ce iii emission, with the Ce iii λ16128 contributing ∼20–25%,
λ20691 contributing around ∼15% and λ19524 contributing
∼10% of the total Ce iii emission. Blueward of the blended
La iii and Gd iii feature, an emission contribution can be seen
from Ce iii in most of the models which is a blend where Ce iii
λ12756 and λ12821 are the strongest contributions (each typi-
cally a few percent of the total Ce iii flux). Domoto et al. (2022)
have previously identified Ce iii features in their spectra, and
Gillanders et al. (2024) also identify Ce iii as a candidate for
many features observed in AT2017gfo.

3.2.3. Epoch 3
At epoch 3 (Figure 8), generally the strengths of the features that
appeared at epoch 2 have become stronger. All models now show
clear spectral features from Sr ii, blended La iii and Gd iii and
from Ce iii. The blueshifted velocities of these features gener-
ally have decreased compared to epoch 2 (see Figure 10), as the
line forming region has receded further inwards to lower ejecta
velocities.
DD2 1.375–1.375 still shows a strong Y ii feature and
SFHO 1.3–1.3 now also shows a clear Y ii λ7881 feature. Like
in DD2 1.375–1.375, Y ii shows multiple emission peaks in
SFHO 1.3–1.3, shaped by many lines of Y ii, which appear to
be boosted by fluorescence, with many Y ii lines absorbing in
the UV. Like DD2 1.375–1.375, SFHO 1.3–1.3 has a low Y
density as well as a low lanthanide fraction in the polar region
(although the lanthanide fraction is not as low as DD2 1.375–
1.375). In this model, spectral features also form at relatively
low velocities, likely because the low lanthanide fraction in the
line of sight leads to lower optical depths in the outer ejecta.
The rest of the simulations do not show an observable fea-
ture, although other models do show minor contributions from
Y ii λ7881. This would suggest that if the feature at 0.75µm in
AT2017gfo is Y ii (Sneppen & Watson 2023), then this would
imply that AT2017gfo also had a relatively low lanthanide frac-
tion in the line of sight.
The Nd iii contributions are further reduced at epoch 3, and
almost entirely gone from some models. Nd ii is now the domi-
nant contribution to wavelengths around 7000 Å, although Ce ii
dominates the continuum emission across most of the spectrum.
Ce iii remains important beyond ∼15000 Å.
At this epoch, additional emission-like features have
emerged in the AT2017gfo spectrum, which are indicated in Fig-
ure 8. In some of the models, the Ce iii λ16128 and λ20691
features appear to align with the observed features at 1.52µm
and 1.99µm, respectively, for example in DD2 1.4–1.4 and
DD2 1.45–1.45. Ce iii has previously been suggested as a
possible identification for these features (Domoto et al. 2022;
Gillanders et al. 2024). However, in a number of models, the ve-
locities of these features are too high, and in some models the
velocity is even too low, such as DD2 1.375–1.375. The range in
peak emission velocity of these features is shown in Figure 10.
Generally, the models with higher ejecta masses show spectral
features at higher velocities than the models with lower ejecta
masses.
In our simulations, the emission-like feature around 0.75µm
is still predominantly composed of Nd iii and Nd ii, in differ-
ing ratios for different models. The Ce ii continuum like emis-
sion is also a large contribution at these wavelengths. Pr iii also
contributes in some models. The emission-like feature around
1.06µm is mostly explained by Sr ii emission in our simulations,
but many of the models show a broadened red wing to this fea-
ture from Nd iii and Ce iii (as well as Pr iii in some models) in
addition to the continuum-like Ce ii emission. Generally the sim-
ulations show this peak at higher velocities than AT2017gfo,
but in some models the velocity matches AT2017gfo, such as
DD2 1.375–1.375 and DD2 1.4–1.4. The simulations still strug-
gle to reproduce the velocity of both this emission-like peak and
the peak at 0.75µm simultaneously. As with previous epochs,
wavelengths ≲5000 Å are too strongly line blanketed compared
to AT2017gfo.
3.2.4. Epoch 4
At epoch 4 (Figure 9), the simulated spectral features have de-
creased to lower velocities. For SFHO 1.3–1.3 some lines even
have a peak emission blueshift velocity of slightly less than zero.
It is likely that this is due to the redward drift described by
Gillanders et al. (2024) and McNeill et al. (2026), where scat-
tered photons can be redshifted due to the reverberation effect
across the ejecta. The strong lines discussed for previous epochs
continue to be present at epoch 4. In addition to these lines, some
models show a feature due to Lu i λ24177, as well as continuum-
like emission from Lu i, however, it is possible that this is arti-
ficially strong here due to the LTE approximation assumed in
our calculations, which may underestimate the ionisation state
by this epoch since non-LTE effects may prevent recombination
to neutral species at such early times.
Ce ii dominates the continuum emission, indicating a lower
ionisation state in the line forming region. Ce iii remains a sig-
nificant contribution at wavelengths beyond ∼15000 Å, in par-
ticular the Ce iii λ16128, λ20691 and λ19524 lines.
The emission-like peak around 0.79µm in the simulations is
now mostly shaped by Nd ii and Ce ii, with some models still
showing a contribution from Nd iii. In SFHO 1.3–1.3 and DD2
1.375–1.375, a Y ii feature is still present around these wave-
lengths, to the blue side of this emission-like peak. Sr ii still con-
tributes to wavelengths around the 1.07µm emission-like peak,
although at this epoch it appears the Sr ii emission mostly con-
tributes to the blue side of this peak, while the red wing tends to
be broadened by the Ce ii continuum-like emission. Nd ii, Nd iii,
Ce iii and Pr iii also contribute to the red side of this emission-
like peak in differing strengths across the models. Unlike pre-
vious epochs, at this epoch some of the models form the two
emission-like peaks at 0.79µm and 1.07µm, matching the veloc-
ities of these peaks in AT2017gfo, including SFHO 1.35–1.35,
SFHO 1.375–1.375 and DD2 1.375–1.375. However, the relative
strength of the 0.79µm peak tends to be too strong compared to
the 1.07µm peak in AT2017gfo.
At this epoch, an emission-like feature at 1.215µm emerged
in AT2017gfo. In some of the simulations, this feature aligns
with the blended La iii and Gd iii feature, such as the two 1.2–
1.6 models, SFHO 1.15714–1.54286, SFHO 1.17857–1.57143

velocity of this feature is also too high compared to AT2017gfo,
and not the cause of the 1.215µm feature. In models showing
features with lower velocities, there is Ce iii emission around
1.215µm, or blueward of this feature. While our simulations do

4. Discussion and conclusions
We have calculated kilonova light curves and spectra from a
range of dynamical ejecta models, simulated from hydrodynam-
ical neutron star merger simulations (Vijayan 2024) with nu-
clear network calculations. We adopt the same methodology
as Shingles et al. (2023), who considered only a single merger
model. Here we consider a range of neutron star masses and two
different equations of state. In the radiative transfer simulations,
we have included new calibrated atomic data for singly and dou-
bly ionised lanthanides (Flörs et al. 2026), allowing lanthanide
spectral features to be predicted which were not present in the
simulations of Shingles et al. (2023) using the uncalibrated lan-
thanide data from Tanaka et al. (2020).
We presented bolometric light curves and spectra in the di-
rection of the poles, near the observed viewing direction of

## Page 15

AT2017gfo. The simulated light curves are fainter and fade faster
than was observed for AT2017gfo, likely due to the low ejecta
mass in the models resulting from including only dynamical
ejecta. Models with higher ejecta masses generally show brighter
light curves and slower decline rates than models with lower
ejecta masses.
Despite the low ejecta masses, the simulated spectra go
through phases where they resemble the observed spectral se-
ries of AT2017gfo. We have compared the simulated spectra to
AT2017gfo at times where the simulated SED shows the clos-
est match to the observed SED. These times, however, are much
earlier than the observed times of AT2017gfo.
All the models show a similar overall evolution, with an
SED initially peaking at blue wavelengths and evolving to redder
colours with time. The models share the same strong features.
These include a Sr ii P-Cygni feature from the triplet λ10914,
λ10327 and λ10036 lines, a blended La iii and Gd iii feature from
La iii λ14096, λ13894 and Gd iii λ14332, as well as Ce iii features
from λ16128, λ20691 and λ19524.
We show that these simulated features appear to corre-
spond with identified emission-like features in AT2017gfo. In
the epoch 4 spectrum of AT2017gfo, these features form at
0.79µm, 1.07µm, 1.215µm, 1.38µm, 1.53µm and 2.01µm. We
find that in our simulations, similar features to these form, al-
though often at bluer wavelengths. Our simulations suggest that
the 1.07µm feature is from the Sr ii triplet, the 1.38µm feature is
blended La iii and Gd iii, the 1.53µm feature can be explained by
Ce iii λ16128 and the 2.01µm is best described by Ce iii λ20691
and λ19524. The best candidate in our simulations to match the
1.215µm feature is also Ce iii emission. The emission-like fea-
ture at 0.79µm may be partly shaped by Y ii fluorescent emis-
sion, of which λ7881 is the strongest line. However, only two of
the models show a Y ii feature and in most models this region is
primarily shaped by Nd iii or Nd ii and Ce ii at the blue wing of
the Sr ii P-Cygni feature.
The two models that show a clear Y ii feature are DD2 1.375–
1.375 and SFHO 1.3–1.3. These models do not have particularly
high Y abundances, but they do have low lanthanide densities,
suggesting that the outer ejecta may be less optically thick in
these models, allowing spectral features to form at lower veloc-
ities. It may be that at these lower velocities, the radiation field
is bluer, and more UV photons are available to be absorbed and
fluoresce as Y ii emission features. Fluorescence appears to be
an important process in our kilonova simulations, in particular
for forming the Y ii feature.
We find that Ce iii and Ce ii show important contributions
to shaping the continuum, as has previously been discussed by
Shingles et al. (2023). We also find that at epoch 1 Nd iii and
Zr iv show large contributions. By epoch 2, Zr iv no longer sig-
nificantly contributes, but Nd iii remains important, as well as
Nd ii, Pr iii and Tb iii. These remain the greatest contributions
until epoch 4, where Ce ii and Nd ii tend to dominate across all
wavelengths, indicating that singly ionised is likely the dominant
ionisation stage by this epoch. Again, the ionisation state may be
artificially low in our simulations due to the LTE approximation.
The improved atomic data for singly and doubly ionised lan-
thanides from Flörs et al. (2026) has enabled lanthanide features
to be predicted in our simulations that are in good agreement
with observed features of AT2017gfo. In particular, this new data
has allowed the blended La iii and Gd iii feature to form, as well
as individual Ce iii features. During the times at which the sim-
ulated spectra resemble the observations, both triply ionised and
neutral species contribute to the spectra. Triply ionised species,
in particular, are present at the first epoch. Neutral species pri-
marily contribute at epoch 4, but there are minor contributions
from Lu i as early as epoch 2. We therefore require additional
atomic data with calibrated energy levels for neutral and triply
ionised species (in addition to the singly and doubly ionised data
from Flörs et al. 2026) to accurately assess if observable features
could form for these ionisation states.
We note that we do not find as significant a difference when
comparing models that use the atomic data from Flörs et al.
(2026) and those that do not (i.e. the models of Shingles et al.
2023) as reported by Gillanders et al. (2025). In the simula-
tion by Shingles et al. (2023), the singly and doubly ionised
lanthanide data was sourced from Tanaka et al. (2020), which
already contained many lines and therefore the lanthanides
already contributed significant opacity to the simulations by
Shingles et al. (2023). When using the lanthanide data from
Flörs et al. (2026), Gillanders et al. (2025) find that their models
require a lanthanide fraction of 2.5 × 10−3. While we do not find
perfect agreement between our models and the observations of
AT2017gfo, the lanthanide fractions in our models appear to be
compatible with the observations. In the line of sight, the highest
lanthanide fraction in our models is 1.7×10−2 (the mass weighted
mean lanthanide fraction), and the highest spherically averaged
mass weighted mean lanthanide fraction is 3.3 × 10−2.
The simulations deviate from the observed spectra of
AT2017gfo at the bluest wavelengths (≲4000 Å). The models
tend to show too strong line blanketing at these wavelengths
compared to the observations. The blue end of the spectrum is
best matched at epoch 1, but by epoch 2 all models are much too
faint in this region. However, at epoch 1, all of the models are
too faint at wavelengths ≳7500 Å compared to AT2017gfo. By
epoch 2 the simulations show better agreement at these wave-
lengths. The better agreement of the blue end of the spectrum at
epoch 1 may be because the ionisation state is highest at epoch
1 but becomes too low by epoch 2, which may be a result of the
LTE approximation. Non-LTE simulations may lead to a higher
ionisation state which could reduce the level of line blanketing,
and may also reduce the appearance of neutral spectral features,
particularly at epoch 4. Brethauer et al. (2025) have shown that
accounting for non-thermal ionisation in kilonovae leads to a
higher ionisation state and reduced line blanketing.
We have shown that dynamical ejecta from a range of dif-
ferent binary neutron star merger simulations with different bi-
nary configurations can produce spectral features that resemble
those observed in AT2017gfo. However, as previously found by
Shingles et al. (2023), the times at which the simulations resem-
ble the spectra are too early. Across all of the models, we find
that the same strong spectral features persist, including the Sr ii
triplet, a blended La iii and Gd iii feature and Ce iii features. The
exception to this is the Y ii feature, which only appears in two of
the models. This similarity in the spectra for a relatively broad
range of parameters suggests that the kilonova arising from the
dynamical ejecta component in the polar region could be a rather
robust prediction. This could suggest that future observations
of kilonovae in this direction may also show the same strong
spectral features as were observed in AT2017gfo, and the early
spectral evolution of future kilonovae may appear similar. In the
future, the impact of later ejecta components on these spectral
predictions should be tested.
