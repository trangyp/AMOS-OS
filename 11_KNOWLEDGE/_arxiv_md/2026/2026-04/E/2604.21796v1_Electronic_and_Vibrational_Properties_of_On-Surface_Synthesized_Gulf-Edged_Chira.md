Abstract
On-surface synthesis enables the fabrication of graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) with atomic precision,
allowing their electronic, optical, and magnetic properties to be tuned by engineering edge structure
and width. Progress on the synthesis of chiral GNRs has nevertheless remained limited, largely because
existing precursor designs rely on laterally fused acene units and cannot access edge topologies beyond
armchair and zigzag. Here, we introduce a new on-surface synthesis motif that yields a gulf-edged chi-
ral GNR. The growth steps are monitored by scanning probe microscopy, and the atomic structure is
confirmed by non-contact atomic force microscopy. Scanning tunneling spectroscopy combined with the-
oretical simulations identifies the gulf-edged chiral GNR as a closed-shell semiconductor with a bandgap
of 1.8 eV. Raman spectroscopy reveals vibrational properties, including a distinctive mode that may
serve as a fingerprint for chiral GNRs. The Raman analysis further uncovers ambient instability despite
the large bandgap and non-spin-polarized edges, consistent with prior reports linking GNR stability to
zigzag edge features. This work establishes a rationally designed synthesis motif for chiral GNRs and
provides a combined structural, electronic, and vibrational characterization, offering guidelines for future
synthesis strategies.
Introduction
Graphene nanoribbons (GNRs) are narrow strips of graphene with nanometer-scale widths. Unlike pristine
graphene, which is gapless, GNRs typically exhibit a bandgap due to quantum confinement, and their
electronic, optical, and magnetic properties are highly tunable through structural modifications [1]. This

degree of tunability within a pure carbon framework makes GNRs promising candidates for next-generation
electronic [2], thermoelectric [3], spintronic [4, 5], and optical [6] devices.
Various types of GNRs have been established based on their edge topology. Armchair GNRs (AGNRs)
feature pure armchair edges and behave as semiconductors with width-dependent band gaps. They are clas-
sified into three families, 3p, 3p + 1, and 3p + 2, based on the number of carbon atoms across their width,
where p is an integer. Within each family, the band gap decreases as the ribbon width increases [7, 8]. Zigzag
GNRs (ZGNRs), characterized by zigzag edges, exhibit spin-polarized edge states that couple ferromagneti-
cally along each edge and antiferromagnetically between opposite edges [9]. Chiral GNRs (chGNRs) combine
armchair and zigzag segments, offering additional flexibility for tuning band gaps and carrier mobility [10]
and serve as platforms for exploring phenomena such as topological phase transitions [11] and magnetic
exchange coupling [12]. Beyond these structures, heteroatom-doped GNRs [13], GNR heterojunctions [14],
and porous GNRs [15] provide further opportunities to tailor functional properties through structural and
chemical modifications [16].
Realizing these structures with atomic precision requires a bottom-up approach.
On-surface synthe-
sis fulfills this requirement by depositing rationally designed molecular precursors onto a catalytic metal
substrate under ultra-high vacuum (UHV) conditions, followed by annealing-induced polymerization and
cyclodehydrogenation [17].
Beyond delivering atomic precision, this method is directly compatible with
UHV high-resolution characterization techniques, including scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy
(STM/STS), and non-contact atomic force microscopy (nc-AFM) [18], enabling in-situ access to both the
structure and electronic properties of the resulting GNRs [19].
For device integration, however, GNRs
must typically be transferred from UHV to ambient conditions and non-metallic substrates [20]. Raman
spectroscopy is routinely employed to monitor their structural integrity and ambient stability under such
conditions, as it probes highly structure-sensitive vibrational modes and remains compatible across a wide
range of substrates and environmental conditions. [21, 22].
Despite significant progress in GNR research, on-surface synthesis motifs for chGNRs remain relatively
scarce [11, 23–27]. This limitation stems primarily from the lack of precursor designs and synthetic method-
ologies necessary to integrate the required edge structures in a regular and controlled manner. To date,
most chGNR precursors have been designed as halogen-functionalized, π-extended acene oligomers in which
multiple acene units are laterally fused in a regular fashion [11, 24, 26, 27]. Precursors designed using this
strategy can only yield chGNRs with a mixture of armchair and zigzag edges and cannot access other edge
topologies such as gulf or cove edges. Achieving chGNRs with edge topologies beyond armchair and zigzag,
therefore, requires a new synthetic motif.
In this work, we introduce such a motif and use it to realize a gulf-edged (4,2,7)-chGNR by on-surface
synthesis. We designed a novel monomer 5 (Figure 1), in which biphenyl substituents are incorporated at
the central naphthalene unit of a trisnaphthalene moiety, to impose conformational constraints that suppress
undesirable side reactions. The strategic positioning of iodo-groups on the biphenyl moiety ensures a smooth
polymerization reaction during on-surface synthesis, while the expanded π-conjugated area, enhances surface
anchoring and thereby minimizes desorption and decomposition up to 673 K. This rational design enables
the direct synthesis of a well-defined (4,2,7)-chGNR, with controlled regiospecificity, width uniformity, and
edge purity, and establishes a new precursor design methodology for chGNRs more generally.
The on-surface synthesis of (4,2,7)-chGNR, was characterized by STM, which monitors the polymeriza-
tion and dehydrogenation steps, and by nc-AFM, which confirms the atomically precise structure. STS
measurements combined with density functional theory (DFT) were used to resolve the (4,2,7)-chGNR band
structure, while Raman spectroscopy probed the vibrational modes and ambient stability of the ribbon.
Despite its relatively large band gap and non-spin-polarized edges, the (4,2,7)-chGNR exhibits poor ambient
stability, consistent with previous findings that zigzag edge segments can drive ambient instability even in
GNRs with predominantly closed-shell electronic structure [28].

Electronic structure characterization of (4,2,7)-chGNR
To determine the valence band (VB) and conduction band (CB) positions of the (4,2,7)-chGNR, STS mea-
surements were performed on a 5-unit (4,2,7)-chGNR segment. The Shockley surface state on Au(111),
visible near 0.4 V, served as an indicator of the tip quality [31]. By comparing the differential conductance
(dI/dV) spectra acquired on the (4,2,7)-chGNR and the Au(111) substrate (Figure 3b), we identify the VB
maximum at −0.5 V and the CB minimum at 1.3 V, corresponding to an experimental band gap of 1.8 eV.
The asymmetric alignment of these bands with respect to the Fermi level is attributed to the high work
function of the Au(111) substrate, consistent with a previous study that investigated Fermi level pinning of
GNRs on Au(111) [32].
To further validate this assignment, we acquired spatially resolved dI/dV maps of a 5-unit (4,2,7)-chGNR
segment at sample biases corresponding to the VB maximum and CB minimum (Figure 3d,f). These exper-
imental maps agree well with simulated local density of states (LDOS) distributions obtained from periodic
DFT (see Methods), supporting the assignment of the VB and CB energies. The simulation converges to
a closed-shell configuration with a bandgap of 1.1 eV, indicating that the (4,2,7)-chGNR does not host any
spin localized state, despite the presence of zigzag edges (Figure 3c).
The discrepancy between the experimental 1.8 eV and the DFT-simulated 1.1 eV band gaps can be
attributed to two factors. First, defects at the ends of the 5-unit (4,2,7)-chGNR segments used for STS
and dI/dV measurements can introduce quantum confinement, resulting in a larger experimental bandgap
compared to the intrinsic value. Second, while DFT reliably captures orbital shapes and energy ordering, it
is known to systematically underestimate GNR band gaps [33]. Additional STS measurements and dI/dV
maps of VB-1 and CB+1, provided in the supporting information, show good agreement with DFT-simulated
LDOS distributions and further support the assignment of the electronic structure of the (4,2,7)-chGNR

Raman characterization of (4,2,7)-chGNR
The Raman spectrum of GNRs typically consists of two main regions: a low-frequency region, including the
longitudinal compressive mode (LCM) and the radial breathing-like mode (RBLM), and a high-frequency
region, characterized by the CH/D modes and G mode. In the low-frequency region, the LCM appears around
100 cm−1 and is sensitive to the GNR length [34], while the RBLM serves as a unique fingerprint of the GNR
width. For example, the RBLMs for 5-, 7-, 9-, and 17-AGNRs are reported at 531 cm−1, 399 cm−1, 314 cm−1,
and 170 cm−1, respectively [35, 36]. In the high-frequency region, CH/D modes located in the 1100 cm−1 to
1500 cm−1 range are associated with intrinsic backbone vibrational modes (zone-folded LO/TO/LA phonons)
along with contributions from C-H vibrations [21, 37, 38]. The most prominent Raman-active feature, the G
mode, appears around 1600 cm−1; it is present in all sp2 carbon-based materials and originates from in-plane
bond stretching vibrations [21, 37].
To characterize the vibrational properties of pristine (4,2,7)-chGNR, Raman spectroscopy was performed
under UHV on a high-coverage (4,2,7)-chGNR sample deposited on a 4 mm × 4 mm Au/mica substrate
using a 532 nm excitation wavelength. A large-scale STM image of this sample is provided in the Supporting
Information. The experimental spectrum represents an average over a 50 µm × 50 µm area, sampled at 50
× 50 points. Peaks in the low-frequency region are too weak to be resolved, whereas the high-frequency
region shows pronounced features in both the CH/D and G mode ranges (Figure 4a red spectrum; the
full Raman spectrum is provided in the supporting information). Compared with AGNRs, (4,2,7)-chGNR
exhibits substantially more peaks in these regions, reflecting the additional symmetry breaking of its complex
structure. Peak deconvolution is therefore required to assign the vibrational modes.
To guide the deconvolution, we performed a non-resonant Raman simulation based on periodic DFT
under conditions approximating the experimental, assuming randomly oriented (4,2,7)-chGNRs on a two-
dimensional plane (see Methods). All modes in the high-frequency range exhibit Ag symmetry. The simu-
lation does not account for substrate effects or resonance contributions, which may cause deviations in peak
intensities and frequencies [35]. The simulated spectrum was generated by summing Lorentzian functions of
9 cm−1 broadening centered at the calculated vibrational frequencies (Figure 4a, blue spectrum). The full
simulated Raman activity spectrum is provided in the Supporting Information.

Beyond identifying the vibrational modes, Raman spectroscopy also provides a sensitive probe of ambient
stability, a critical requirement for the practical implementation of GNR-based devices. Zigzag-edge segments
of GNRs have been shown to exhibit high reactivity when exposed to oxygen or air, even when the structures
have a predominantly closed-shell character. [28]. For instance, teranthene and hexanthene, both with a
high zigzag-to-armchair edge ratio, show pronounced broadening of the D, G, and CH modes after only
five minutes exposure [40]. (3,1)-chGNR similarly degrades under oxygen exposure, with STM and AFM
revealing that oxidation is regioselective to its zigzag segments [28], and the zigzag termini of 5AGNR also
undergo structural changes under oxygen exposure [41]. In contrast, AGNRs with low zigzag-to-armchair
edge ratios or purely armchair edges, such as 7- and 9-AGNR, exhibited excellent long-term stability in air,
with no significant broadening of their Raman peaks even after several months of exposure. [22, 42].

The Raman response of (4,2,7)-chGNR upon air exposure is consistent with this picture.Figure 4a (in

short exposure severely degraded the D and G peaks, rendering peak deconvolution and FWHM analysis
highly challenging due to the absence of well-resolved features. Direct comparison between pristine and air-
exposed spectra reveals that the CH/D peaks are far more suppressed than the G peaks. These observations
confirm that ambient instability can persist in GNRs containing zigzag edge segments, even when the overall
electronic structure is predominantly closed-shell [28].
Conclusion
We have demonstrated a new precursor design strategy for the on-surface synthesis of a gulf-edged chiral
GNR, the (4,2,7)-chGNR. By introducing biphenyl substituents at the central naphthalene unit of a trisnaph-
thalene core, the monomer imposes conformational constraints that suppress undesired side reactions and
enhances surface anchoring through its extended π-conjugation, thereby enabling clean polymerization and
cyclodehydrogenation up to 673 K. STM and nc-AFM, confirm that the resulting ribbons are long, regiospe-
cific, and atomically precise, with the expected gulf-edged chiral structure. STS measurements combined
with DFT simulations identify the (4,2,7)-chGNR as a closed-shell semiconductor with an experimental band
gap of 1.8 eV. Raman spectroscopy further characterizes the vibrational properties of the (4,2,7)-chGNR,
revealing a distinctive C-H vibration at the gulf edge that can serve as a structural fingerprint for this edge
topology. Air-exposure measurements show, however, that the (4,2,7)-chGNR is unstable under ambient
conditions despite its large bandgap and closed-shell electronic structure, consistent with previous reports
that zigzag edge segments can drive ambient degradation independently of the overall spin configuration.
Methods
Monomer synthesis
Detailed descriptions of the reaction steps and characterization of precursor molecules including single-crystal
analysis (CCDC 253729) of compound 5 are provided in the Supporting Information.
On-surface synthesis
On-surface synthesis steps were performed in a UHV chamber, directly connected to a low-temperature
scanning tunneling microscope (Scienta Omicron). Au(111) thin films epitaxially grown on mica (Phasis
Sàrl, Geneva, Switzerland) were used as growth substrates in this work. Au(111) on mica was cleaned by
two cycles of argon ion sputtering at a pressure of 8×10−6 mbar for 10 min, followed by annealing to 723 K for
10 min. Before surface deposition, the precursor 5 in powder form was thoroughly degassed at its sublimation
temperature of 573 K, with the deposition rate monitored with a quartz microbalance. During deposition,
the Au(111) substrates were kept at room temperature, around 298 K. After deposition, the samples were
heated to 438 K for 30 min and 673 K for 30 min to induce polymerization and cyclodehydrogenation. The
temperature of the Au(111) on mica was measured with an infrared pyrometer (Optris, Berlin, Germany).
Scanning probe microscopy measurements
STM, STS, and nc-AFM were performed in commercial low-temperature STM/nc-AFM systems from Scienta
Omicron, operating at a temperature of 4.5 K and a base pressure below 2 × 10−11 mbar. All STS spectra,
dI/dV maps, and the nc-AFM image were acquired with a CO-functionalized tungsten tip. In situ cold
deposition of CO molecules was performed to obtain a CO-functionalized tip. STS spectra and dI/dV maps
were acquired using a lock-in technique with a modulation frequency of 681 Hz. A modulation amplitude
of 10 mV was applied for measurements for all the STS spectras and 20 mV for all the dI/dV maps. The
nc-AFM image was taken with a qPlus tuning fork sensor [43] (resonance frequency, ∼25.4 kHz; quality
factor, ∼14 000). A CO-functionalized tungsten tip was used, and measurements were conducted in the
constant-height mode.

Raman spectroscopy
To measure the Raman spectra of pristine (4,2,7)-chGNRs, a high-coverage sample was first prepared and
characterized by STM, then transferred into a home-built vacuum chamber equipped with optical access for
the Raman microscope. The chamber was pumped by an ion pump and maintained at a pressure below
1 × 10−6 mbar, and was connected directly to the fast-entry lock of the STM system, allowing the sample to
remain under UHV conditions throughout the transfer and throughout the Raman measurement. Following
the UHV measurement, the sample was removed, exposed to ambient air for 30 min. and transferred to
a separate vacuum chamber at a pressure of approximately 10−6 mbar for Raman characterization of the
air-exposed (4,2,7)-chGNRs.
Raman measurements were carried out in a backscattering geometry using a Witec Alpha 300 R confocal
Raman microscope equipped with a 532 nm laser line operating at a power of 30 mW and an 1800 g/mm
grating. A 50× LD objective (Zeiss, NA = 0.55) was used to focus the laser beam onto the sample and to
collect the scattered light. For each spectrum, a Raman mapping approach was employed: 2500 individual
spectra (1 s integration time) were recorded over a grid of 50 × 50 points (50 µm × 50 µm), and the average
of these spectra was used for analysis. The Raman data were processed in OriginPro.
Computational methods
The band structure and Raman spectrum calculations were performed with the DFT code QUANTUM
ESPRESSO 7.4 [44–46] via AiiDAlab [47], using the AiiDAlab-QE application [48] together with the vi-
broscopy plugin AiiDAlab-qe-vibroscopy. This plugin integrates automated AiiDA-based [49] workflows for
phonon and Raman calculations based on finite-displacement and finite-field approaches (aiida-vibroscopy) [50].
The calculations employed the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange–correlation functional [51]. Plane-
wave cutoffs of 100 Ry and 400 Ry were used for the wavefunctions and charge density, respectively. The
ionic cores were described using pseudopotentials from the SSSP PBE precision library (version 1.3) [52].
The Brillouin zone was sampled using a 5 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack grid.

## Page 15


Chiral graphene nanoribbons (chGNRs) are generally defined by a chiral vector Ch(n, m) (n >
m), where n and m are indices of the graphene unit vectors [1–7]. However, the chiral vector
alone does not fully characterize the structure of chGNRs. For example, chGNRs with different
widths can share the same chiral vector.
As more distinct chGNRs have been synthesized, it has become necessary to refine the
nomenclature to more clearly distinguish different structures. The ribbon width is therefore now
included in the chGNR designation. To date, most chGNRs have been constructed by fusing
nanographene building blocks along their armchair edges, with defined lateral shifts between
adjacent blocks. In such cases, the width w is defined perpendicular to the zigzag edges [8,
9]. Figure S1 illustrates how the name of a (4,2,5) chGNR is defined, following the (n, m, w)
notation where w denotes the ribbon width. In this work, we follow the same convention.
However, the building block of the gulf-edged (4,2,7) chGNR contains an additional ben-
zenoid ring on each side, so that the measurable width varies along the ribbon. To keep the
nomenclature unambiguous, we define it at the position where the width reaches its maximum.
Figure S2 shows how we assign the name of the gulf-edged (4,2,7) chGNR in this work.
Note that the “building block” referred here denotes only the geometric unit used to de-
fine the nomenclature and is not equivalent to the molecular precursor used in the on-surface
synthesis of chGNRs.
