Eliminating Illusion in Directed Networks
Sougata Jana, Sanjukta Roy
Indian Statistical Institute
sougatajana_r@mail.isical.ac.in, sanjukta@isical.ac.in
Abstract
We study illusion elimination problems on directed social networks where each vertex is colored either
red or blue. A vertex is under majority illusion if it has more red out-neighbors than blue out-neighbors
when there are more blue vertices than red ones in the network. In a more general phenomenon of
p-illusion, at least p fraction of the out-neighbors (as opposed to 1/2 for majority) of a vertex is red.
In the directed illusion elimination problem, we recolor minimum number of vertices so that no vertex
is under p-illusion, for p ∈(0,1). Unfortunately, the problem is NP-hard for p = 1/2 even when the
network is a grid. Moreover, the problem is NP-hard and W[2]-hard when parameterized by the number
of recolorings for each p ∈(0,1) even on bipartite DAGs. Thus, we can neither get a polynomial time
algorithm on DAGs, unless P=NP, nor we can get a FPT algorithm even by combining solution size and
directed graph parameters that measure distance from acyclicity, unless FPT=W[2]. We show that the
problem can be solved in polynomial time in structured, sparse networks such as outerplanar networks,
outward grids, trees, and cycles. Finally, we show tractable algorithms parameterized by treewidth of the
underlying undirected graph, and by the number of vertices under illusion.

Introduction
Social networks influence opinions in both practice and theory [8, 16, 19]. Our perceptions are heavily filtered
by those we follow rather than the objective global truth. This vulnerability is often exploited by political
parties or marketing brands through majority illusion [28], a social phenomenon where a rare opinion appears
mainstream because a few highly connected agents adopt it.
The implications of illusion extend significantly into strategic communication, particularly within the
realms of political campaigning, digital advertising, and the optimization of influence [34, 3]. Furthermore,
the perceptual distortions due to illusion impact voting behavior [32, 12] and various collective decision-
making processes [4, 8], as well as the equitable distribution of resources through participatory budgeting
[26]. Johnson et al. [24] showed the effects of majority illusion in social network in the decision for getting
vaccinated. Schrama et al. [30] showed that majority illusion can push the entire population from an abundant,
high-welfare state to a depleted, low-welfare one. Both works highlight the need for network analysis and
network-based policies to prevent misconception driven convergence to a undesirable state.
We study the complexity of eliminating illusion in social networks by modifying minimum number of
agents’ opinions. The computational complexity of illusion elimination depends heavily on network topology.
Fioravantes et al. [18] studied networks as undirected graphs and considered the problem of eliminating
majority illusion. However, in social networks, usually, the influence between the agents are not symmetric,
i.e., the edge relations are not symmetric. Thus, we model a network as a directed graph G where the vertex
set V are the set of agents and an edge from agent i to agent j implies that i can be influenced by j. An agent’s
opinion is represented by a coloring function f : V 7→{B,R}, where B (resp. R) denotes blue (resp. red).

The DIFR Problem:
Input: G = (V,E), a directed graph, a coloring function f : V →{B,R}, and a nonnegative integer k.
Question: Does there exist a (recoloring) function f ′ : V →{B,R} of f such that G is illusion-free
under the coloring f ′, where |{v | f(v) ̸= f ′(v)}| ≤k?
Although the majority illusion is often introduced via two-party elections, applications like vaccination
provides a more compelling motivation to look beyond majority. Consider a contact network where each
agent is colored according to whether they are vaccinated. For many contagious diseases, achieving herd
immunity requires a high global vaccination rate (often well above 50%, and in some cases exceeding 90%)
[17, 2]. This corresponds to a super-majority i.e., we would require almost 90% of a neighborhood should be
vaccinated. Conversely, when the goal is accurate perception of underrepresented groups, it is relevant to
consider the status of a subset of neighborhood that is strictly smaller than majority. Here, network structure
and sampling biases can systematically distort individuals’ beliefs about the prevalence of minority attributes
(e.g., race, sexual orientation, disability status), leading to misperceptions that are well documented in studies
of social networks and the majority illusion [28, 15]. These applications require a definition of illusion more
general than majority illusion. We define illusion based on a fraction p that takes value > 1/2 for the former
example or a value < 1/2 for the later example.
We define a vertex to be under p-illusion if strictly less than p fraction of its out neighbors are blue (see
Figure 1(b)). We study elimination of p-illusion by editing the vertex colors in directed graphs where we
do not impose the assumption that the blue vertices form majority, and ask if it is possible to ensure that
every voter has at least p-fraction of blue out-neighbors, where p ∈[0,1] is a rational number. The p-illusion
problem by editing edges in undirected graphs was studied in [11]. A graph G is p-illusion free if each vertex
in the graph is p-illusion free.

1.1
Our Contribution.
Our contribution is two fold, we generalize the question eliminating majority illusion to eliminating p-illusion
and analyze their computational complexity in directed graphs. It is not clear apriori whether it would require
more recolorings to eliminate p-illusion, as opposed to the majority illusion in undirected version, due to the
non-symmetric edges.
– We show that the problem of eliminating p-illusion is NP-complete and W[2]-hard with respect to
k, the number of recolorings, even on directed acyclic bipartite graphs (DAGs) for each rational value of
p ∈(0,1). Thus, we cannot hope to obtain a fixed parameter tractable (FPT) algorithm parameterized by
k, i.e., it cannot be solved in time f(k)|I|O(1), where f is any computable function solely depending on the
argument, and |I| denotes the input size, under the complexity theoretic assumption W[2]̸=FPT.
– Surprisingly, eliminating majority illusion seems to be a harder subcase. DIFR is NP-complete even
on grids. As the influence between the agents can come from their residential neighborhood, or can be
constrained by spatial or local interactions, it is natural to assume that the graph between them can be a planar
graph or simply a grid.
– In all of our hardness reductions the vertices under illusion need exactly one blue out-neighbor to
be illusion free, i.e., we say that the maximum deficiency of any vertex is at most one. Thus, p-DIFR is
paraNP-hard 2 with respect to maximum deficiency of any vertex. Moreover, since it is hard on DAGs, it
is paraNP-hard with respect to distance from acyclicity, including many directed graph parameters such as
directed feedback vertex set, feedback arc set, directed treewidth, directed cutwidth, digraph bandwidth, or
degreewidth, to name a few.
To understand the true causes of the intractability results, we explore the line between easy and hard
graph topologies.
– We identifying orientations of grid graphs where eliminating p-illusion is solvable in polynomial
time. We call them as outward grid graphs, where the edges are oriented from left to right and from top to
bottom. Outward grids naturally model hierarchical or layered information flow, where influence propagates
directionally from central sources to peripheral nodes.
Similarly, a directed tree or out-tree (also known as arborescence) models hierarchical influence, such
as mentorship, organizational chains, or follower cascades, where influence flows outwards from central
sources. We show that p-DIFR is polynomial time solvable not only on out-trees but also when the underlying
undirected graph is a tree or a cycle.
– In search of tractable cases, we further explore structural parameters. Strengthening our results on
trees, we obtain fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) algorithm for p-DIFR parameterized by treewidth of the
graph when the maximum deficiency of any vertex is bounded.
– Finally, we use a simple ILP based formulation to show that p-DIFR is FPT parameterized by the
number of vertices under illusion, also, paramaterized by the treedepth of the primal as well as the dual.

Related Works.
Different variants of majority illusion elimination problem has been studied in a graph-theoretic perspective
by [31]. Grandi et al. and Dipple et al. [21, 11] studied the computational questions of eliminating majority
illusion, in undirected graphs by editing edges (i.e., adding and/or deleting edges). Grandi et al.[21] showed
the problem of eliminating illusion for q fraction of vertices is NP-complete in general and W[1]-hard when
parameterized by the number of modified edges (i.e., solution size). They show the decision problem of
existence of majority illusion for a q fraction of agents, is NP-complete for every rational number q ∈(1
2,1]
even for planar graphs, bipartite graphs as well as graphs of bounded maximum degree. [11] showed that
eliminating majority illusion for all agents is solvable in polynomial time.
Eliminating illusion by editing edges in directed graphs is trivial in the following sense: It does not utilize
the topology of the network and only depend on the ‘deficiency’ of the vertices. For example, when we are
adding edges to eliminate illusion, the number of edges we need to add is sum of ‘deficiencies’ of the vertices
under illusion, where deficiency of a vertex is the number of extra red out-neighbors over blue ones. Thus,
the solution size is fixed and is given by the total deficiency. Moreover, in directed networks, it is natural to
assume that agents are influenced by their out-neighbors and may change opinion due to lobbying or bribery
[6, 14].
The article by [18] is most relevant to us as it addresses the elimination of majority illusion by recoloring
vertices albeit, in undirected graphs. They show W[2]-hardness by solution size, FPT with respect to
treewidth plus the solution size, NP-hardness on planar graphs, and obtain PTAS for planar graphs, analogous
to our results. For directed graphs, we show a stronger NP-hardness on grids, and explore graph topologies,
and parameters to design efficient algorithms.
Conditions for existence of Majority illusion in directed networks and how they differ from the undirected
case have been studied [27, 1, 7]. Majority illusion also affects information diffusion, an extremely well
studied problem, where a target set is elected to maximize influence. The target set selection problem [25]
finds a minimum set of vertices to assign a color to maximize the spread of the given color. The color spreads

2
Preliminaries
This section introduces our social network model and establishes the notations used throughout the paper. We
also present several key observations that provide the foundation for the subsequent analysis and results.
A social network is represented by a directed graph G = (V,E) where vertices are the agents and each
vertex has a labeling (or color) f : V(G) →{B,R}, where B (R resp.) denotes blue (red resp.).
Notation. We use [n] to denote the set {1,2,...,n}. Given a directed graph G = (V,E), we use (v,u) to
denote the edge from v to u, for v,u ∈V. Let R(V) = f −1(R) and B(V) = V \R(V) denote the set of red and
blue vertices, respectively. For any vertex v ∈V, we denote its sets of out-neighbors and in-neighbors by
N+(v) = {u ∈V : (v,u) ∈E} and N−(v) = {u ∈V : (u,v) ∈E}, respectively. Let rv and bv be the number of
red and blue out-neighbors of v, respectively, defined as: rv = |N+(v)∩R(V)| and bv = |N+(v)∩B(V)|.
A vertex v is considered to be under illusion if it possesses a surplus of red out-neighbors; that is, if rv > bv.
We define the deficiency of a vertex v, denoted by def(v), as: def(v) = max {0,rv −bv}. Consequently, the set
of illusion vertices, denoted by X, consists of all vertices with positive deficiency: X = {v ∈V : def(v) > 0}.
The p-deficiency of a vertex v, denoted by def p(v), is defined by def p(v) = max{0,⌈p·|N+(v)|⌉−bv}.
A vertex v is under p-illusion if def p(v) > 0. Consequently, a vertex v is p-illusion free if bv ≥⌈p·|N+(v)|⌉.
Let Xp denote the set of vertices under p-illusion, called as the p-illusion vertices.
We say that a recoloring f ′ of f produces a solution to the DIFR (p-DIFR, resp.) problem on G if G
has no illusion (p-illusion, resp.) vertex under the coloring f ′; the solution is the subset of vertices that are
recolored, i.e., {v ∈V(G) : f(v) ̸= f ′(v)}, and solution size k is the size of this set, that is, the number of
recolorings.

3
Hardness Reductions
Since given a recoloring, the non-existence of majority illusion or p-illusion can be verified in polynomial
time, the problems are in NP. We provide two hardness results in this section for eliminating majority illusion
and p-illusion. Specifically, we prove that DIFR remains NP-complete when restricted to grid graphs. Thus,
we cannot hope to design polynomial time algorithms for acyclic or planar graphs, unless P=NP. Furthermore,
we reduce HITTING SET to p-DIFR to show hardness even on bipartite DAGs. We start with the reduction on
grids.
3.1
NP-completeness of DIFR on Grid Graphs
We study the DIFR problem on planar graphs, especially, on directed grids. Directed grid is a undirected grid
with arbitrary edge directions. The DIFR problem is NP-complete for directed grids. The hardness reduction
is from PLANAR MONOTONE RECTILINEAR 3-SAT problem, a restricted variant of 3-SAT. In MONOTONE
3-SAT, each clause is monotone, that is, it consists exclusively of either positive or negative literals. An
incidence graph of a logical formula (specifically a Conjunctive Normal Form or CNF formula) is a bipartite
graph representing the relationship between variables and clauses. Variables and clauses form the two distinct
vertex sets, with an edge connecting a variable to a clause if that variable (or its negation) appears in the
clause. In a standard rectilinear embedding of the incidence graph of PLANAR MONOTONE RECTILINEAR
3-SAT, variable vertices are arranged along a central horizontal axis; positive clauses are positioned above
this axis, while negative clauses are placed below. We follow this definition of the rectilinear embedding.
Each clause is depicted as a horizontal segment connected to its corresponding variables via three vertical
segments, often referred to as legs (see Figure 3(a)). The clauses can be nested such that the graph is still
planar.
PLANAR MONOTONE RECTILINEAR 3-SAT.
Input. A 3-CNF formula ϕ, such that every clause of ϕ is monotone and consists of three literals and the
incidence graph of ϕ has a rectilinear embedding.
Output. Does there exist a satisfying assignment for ϕ?
This problem is well known to be NP-complete [10]. We also assume that formula ϕ consists of distinct
clauses.

Although the problem is NP-complete on DAGs, we present a dichotomy by showing that the problem is

Observation 6. The p-DIFR problem on directed cycles can be solved by recoloring all red vertices.

Parameterized Algorithms
This section establishes the fixed-parameter tractability of p-DIFR under two different parameterizations. We
first present an FPT algorithm parameterized by the treewidth of the graph and the maximum deficiency. We
present a PTAS for the p-DIFR problem on planar graphs in Appendix A.1. Subsequently, we provide an
FPT algorithm parameterized by the number of vertices under p-illusion in Appendix 5.2.

6
Conclusion
In this work, we introduce and study majority illusion elimination on directed graphs, by recoloring a
minimum number of vertices so that no vertex remains under illusion. We establish that the problem is
NP-complete on grids. We extend the concept to p-illusion and show it remains W[2]-hard even on DAGs.
Thus, we cannot get a parameterized algorithm with respect to feedback arc set (fas) or directed feedback
vertex set (dfvs), beckoning to explore other parameters for tractability. On the algorithmic side, we identify
graph topologies where efficient algorithms can be designed. Our work opens several directions for future
research. First, can the problem admit FPT algorithm or polynomial kernels with respect to treewidth?
Second, what are the best possible approximation algorithms in general graphs? Finally, which additional
structures can yield more efficient algorithms?
