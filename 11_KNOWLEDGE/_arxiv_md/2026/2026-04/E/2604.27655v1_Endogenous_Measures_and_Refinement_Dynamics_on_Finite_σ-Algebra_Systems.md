Endogenous Measures and Refinement Dynamics on Finite σ-Algebra
Systems
Paul Baird
Laboratoire de Math´ematiques de Bretagne Atlantique,
Universit´e de Bretagne Occidentale, Brest, France
We study systems of σ-algebras ordered by refinement and introduce the notion of an endogenous
probability measure, invariant under admissible refinement transformations. We prove existence
and structural properties of such measures on finite systems and show how refinement operators
induce a natural dynamical structure on the lattice of σ-algebras.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Systems of σ-algebras on a finite set, ordered by refinement, form a natural partially ordered
structure closely related to the lattice of partitions of the set. Such systems arise whenever one
considers progressively finer resolutions of a state space.
We study probability measures that
remain invariant under admissible refinement transformations. When this invariance condition is
imposed, the refinement structure and the measure become mutually constrained. This leads to
the notion of an endogenous measure: a probability measure that is stable under the refinement
system that it itself supports.
The main result establishes the existence and structural properties of endogenous measures on
finite refinement systems. In particular, compatible families of refinements admit self-consistent
invariant measures that are minimal with respect to the refinement order on σ-algebras (Theo-
rem III.4).
Logical and combinatorial approaches to physical structure have a long history. The lattice-
theoretic formulation of quantum propositions was introduced by Birkhoff and von Neumann [1].
Discrete causal structures have been investigated in causal-set models of spacetime [2], while the
consistent-histories approach studies compatible families of quantum events [3]. The framework
developed here is different in spirit: it is based on directed systems of refining σ-algebras together
with the existence of self-consistent invariant measures on such systems. Background on invariant
measures and ergodic theory can be found in [4].
This perspective brings together ideas that normally appear in different contexts.
The re-
finement order on σ-algebras provides a natural notion of progressively finer distinctions, while
compatibility of refinements gives rise to a partial ordering reminiscent of causal structures. At
the same time, families of mutually compatible refinements play a role analogous to consistent
histories in quantum theory [3]. Within this framework probability measures are not specified
externally but arise as invariant structures determined by the refinement dynamics itself.
The paper is organised as follows. Section II introduces commuting refinements and the associ-
ated notion of compatibility. Section III develops the concept of endogenous measures and proves
the existence and structural properties of self-consistent invariant measures on finite refinement
systems. Section IV studies refinement dynamics and shows how partial ordering and causal in-
terpretation emerge from the structure of refinements. Section V presents a minimal toy model
illustrating the basic mechanisms. Section VI outlines a general theory of refinement dynamics
and discusses how sequences of refinements generate larger-scale structures.

A.
Distinctions as σ-algebras
A σ-algebra F on Ωis a family of subsets closed under complements and countable unions. An
atom of F is a nonempty set A ∈F such that no proper nonempty subset of A lies in F. The
atoms, when they exist, form a partition of the atomic part of Ω, i.e. of
Ωat :=
[
{A ⊆Ω: A is an atom of F}.
In general σ-algebras (for example the Borel σ-algebra on R) may have no nonempty atoms; the
σ-algebra can be atomless. Because Ωis finite, however, every σ-algebra F is uniquely determined
by its partition into atoms.
Thus the refinement order on σ-algebras corresponds exactly to
the refinement order on partitions of Ω. Throughout the paper we freely move between these
equivalent viewpoints.
B.
Events as refinements
An event is modelled as a refinement
F −→F′
where F′ has strictly finer atoms than F. Intuitively
• An event “carves” one or more of the existing atoms into smaller pieces.
• A chain of refinements
F0 ⊂F1 ⊂· · · ⊂Fn
records the growth of distinguishability in the universe.
• Such chains will later be interpreted as temporal sequences: time is nothing but the accumu-
lation of distinctions.
This notion of an event contains the seed of both classical measurements and quantum mea-
surements, but without invoking Hilbert spaces or operators.
C.
Choice and measure extension
We attach to each σ-algebra F a probability measure µ defined on F. At this stage the measure
is introduced formally, as if externally specified, in order to define consistent and inconsistent
refinements. In Section 3, this assumption will be relaxed: the measure itself will be required to
arise endogenously from the refinement structure, becoming the fixed point of a self-consistency
condition between events and probabilities.
A refinement F −→F′ requires extending µ to a new measure µ′ on F′. This extension may
be:
• unique–classically deterministic;
• non-unique–genuinely indeterministic, corresponding to intrinsic indeterminancy;
• impossible–indicating that F′ is incompatible with F (a key precursor to contextuality and
non-commutativity).
Thus:
Definition II.1. (Consistent Measure Extension). A refinement F −→F′ is consistent if there
exists at least one probability measure µ′ on F′ whose restriction to F equals the prior measure µ.
The number of allowed extensions encodes the degree of determinacy. A refinement for which
many extensions are possible represents a genuine choice – a point where the universe’s informa-
tional unfolding is not predetermined.
In Section 3 we will show that chains of consistent measure extensions naturally form causal
(time-like) orderings, whereas incompatible σ-algebras give rise to space-like separability or its
failure.

D.
Commutativity and the emergence of independence
The σ-algebras on a finite set Ω, ordered by inclusion form a lattice in which joins correspond
to joint refinements and meets to common coarsenings (i.e. σ-algebras obtained by merging atoms
of a finer partition). Given two relations R and S on a set Ω, we define their composition R ◦S
by (x, z) ∈R ◦S if and only if there exists y ∈Ωsuch that (x, y) ∈S and (y, z) ∈R. Every finite
σ-algebra determines an equivalence relation on Ωby declaring two elements equivalent when they
lie in the same atom.
Definition II.2. (Commuting Refinements) Let A and B be σ-algebras on the same finite Ω, and
let x ∼A y (resp. x ∼B y) denote the equivalence relation defined by membership in the same atom
of A (resp. B). We say that A and B commute, or are compatible, if
∼A ◦∼B=∼B ◦∼A .
In this case the corresponding refinements are called commuting.
Note that the composition of two equivalence relations need not be an equivalence relation, but if
they commute then it is.
In what follows, we will use the term commuting refinements for pairs of σ-algebras whose
associated equivalence relations commute. In physical language we may also call such refinements
compatible, as they represent distinctions that can coexist within a single informational state.
Example II.3. Consider the three σ-algebras A, B, B′ defined on the set Ω= {1, 2, 3, 4} by A =
{{1, 2}, {3, 4}}, B = {{1, 3}, {2, 4}} and B′ = {{1, 3, 4}, {2}}. Then one can easily check that A
and B commute. On the other hand 2 ∼A 1 and 1 ∼B′ 3, hence 2 is related to 3 in ∼B′ ◦∼A.
For ∼A ◦∼B′ we need y with 2 ∼B′ y and y ∼A 3. But 2 ∼B′ y forces y = 2 but 2 ∼A 3 is false.
So no such y exists and (2, 3) is not in ∼A ◦∼B′. So ∼A ◦∼B′̸=∼B′ ◦∼A, and A and B′ are
non-commuting refinements.
Commutativity represents the possibility of making both sets of distinctions simultaneously. This
is the purely measure-theoretic precursor to:
• commuting observables in quantum mechanics,
• space-like separated events in relativity,
• local independence in statistical physics.
Non-commutativity implies contextuality:
distinctions acquire meaning only relative to the
σ-algebra in which they are realised.
Thus, non-commutativity signals contextuality, order-
dependence, or causal entanglement.
In the next section we study refinement sequences and their associated measure structures.
These will lead naturally to a partial ordering of refinements and to the notion of endogenous
probability measures.
III.
ENDOGENOUS MEASURES AND DYNAMICAL SELF-CONSISTENCY
In classical probability theory the measure is simply given – either as an objective frequency
assignment or as an agent’s prior. If the σ-algebra encodes distinctions that “exist” only insofar
as they can be stably made, then a measure should not be an externally imposed weighting of
possibilities, but rather the fixed point of a self-consistency condition that couples:
1. the σ-algebra of meaningful distinctions, and
2. the measure that makes those distinctions dynamically stable.
Our goal in this section is to formalise this idea.

Given a σ-algebra F, a probability measure µ assigns probabilities to the measurable sets A ∈F,

meaningful only if:
• it corresponds to distinctions that can persist,
• it does not generate non-commutating refinements, and
• it is recognised by a measure that renders those distinctions dynamically coherent.
Thus the measure and the σ-algebra restrict one another.
Definition III.1. (Self-consistency of a measure and σ-algebra). A pair (F, µ) is self-consistent
if:
1. (Compatibility) The refinements generating F satisfy the commutativity condition of Defini-
tion II.2.
2. (Invariance under F-preserving transformations) The measure µ is invariant under transfor-

T −1A ∈F
for all A ∈F,

µ(T −1A) = µ(A)

The last condition is the heart of the endogenous viewpoint: refinements exist only when the
measure can meaningfully distinguish the refinements. If it cannot, they are not physically realised
distinctions.

Stability criteria for endogenous measures
To make this concept operational, we need a way to test whether a candidate measure µ is

Axiom A (Commutativity domain). There exists a nonempty set C ⊂F of σ-algebras such that
any two elements of C commute in the sense of Definition II.2.
Moreover, C is closed under
coarsening: if F ∈C and G ≺F, then G ∈C.
Axiom B (Invariant symmetries). For each F ∈C there exists a nontrivial measure-preserving
transformation T : Ω→Ωsatisfying
T −1A ∈F

µ(T −1A) = µ(A)

Axiom C (Refinement stability). Let F ∈C and let µ be a measure on F. If F′ ⪰F is a
refinement such that F′ /∈C, then µ admits no extension to a measure on F′ that preserves the
invariance conditions of Axiom B.
This latter axiom will later translate into spatial compatibility and temporal ordering: refine-
ments that preserve commutativity correspond to spacelike separation, while refinements that
violate it generate timelike or causal relations.
Definition III.3. A probability measure µ on a finite σ-algebra F is called degenerate if it is
supported on a single atom of F, that is, if there exists an atom A ∈F such that µ(A) = 1. We
call µ nondegenerate if µ(A) ∈(0, 1) for every atom A.

A.
Refinements as elementary operations
A refinement step is a map
F −→G
such that F ⊊G and the atoms of G form a strict refinement of the atoms of F.
Definition IV.1. (Atomic refinement). A refinement F →G is atomic if exactly one atom of F
is subdivided, and all others remain unchanged.
Atomic refinements therefore generate the directed system of σ-algebras under consideration.
Any finite refinement can be decomposed into a finite sequence of such elementary steps.
Proposition IV.2. Every finite refinement can be expressed (non-uniquely) as a finite sequence
of atomic refinements.
Proof. Every refinement of finite partitions can be obtained by successively subdividing atoms.
Equivalently, if P(F) and P(G) are the atom partitions with P(G) refining P(F), one may split
the atoms of P(F) one at a time until P(G) is obtained.
Each atomic refinement corresponds to the creation of a new distinction. Time will emerge from
the partial order generated by these refinement relations.

C.
Commutativity and independence
Compatibility of refinements is determined by commutativity of the associated partitions. Com-
patible refinements can be performed in either order and lead to the same joint refinement.
We now formalise the central idea: spacelike relations arise from commutative refinements, and
timelike relations from non-commutative refinements.
Let RA : F →FA and RB : F →FB be two atomic refinements of the same σ-algebra.
Definition IV.5 (Commutativity of refinements). Refinements RA and RB are commutative (or
compatible) if their associated equivalence relations ∼A and ∼B on Ωcommute:
∼A ◦∼B = ∼B ◦∼A
as in Definition II.2. Equivalently, there exists a joint refinement FAB = σ(FA∪FB) whose atoms
are the non-empty intersections of atoms of FA and FB,
Atoms(FAB) = { Ai ∩Bj ̸= ∅},
and for which a consistent measure µAB exists. If these relations fail – i.e. if ∼A and ∼B do not
commute or no consistent joint measure exists – the refinements are non-commutative.
Definition IV.6 (Spacelike vs. timelike relation). Two proto-events RA, RB are:
• spacelike-related if they are commutative (Definition II.2),
• timelike-related if they do not commute and therefore cannot be realised simultaneously within
a single refinement.
This aligns with the conceptual structure of quantum theory:
• In quantum mechanics: commutativity ↔commuting observables.
• Here: commutativity ↔refinements whose distinctions can be made simultaneously.

G.
Summary: Time as the Growth of Distinctions
We may now summarise:
1. events correspond to atomic refinements,
2. refinement chains generate a partial order,
3. compatibility corresponds to commuting refinements,
4. incompatible refinements induce ordering constraints.
Thus time, causality, and quantum behaviour all arise from the dynamics of making distinctions
in an initially undifferentiated informational continuum.

In this section we construct a minimal explicit example illustrating the refinement framework
developed above. The example demonstrates:
1. how distinctions arise through successive refinements,
2. how non-unique extensions of measures appear,
3. how compatibility and incompatibility of refinements arise,
4. how repeated compatible refinements generate stable structures.
Because Ωis finite, each σ-algebra corresponds to a partition of Ω, and refinements correspond to
refinements of partitions.
A.
The undifferentiated initial state
Let the initial universe be the trivial measurable space:
(Ω, F0),
Ω= {1, 2, 3, 4},
F0 = {∅, Ω}.
At this stage no distinctions are present: the entire space forms a single atom of F0. We assign
the trivial measure:
µ0(Ω) = 1.
No additional structure is present.
B.
First distinctions: a binary refinement
Consider the first possible event:

partition is:
FA = σ(PA) = {∅, {1, 2}, {3, 4}, Ω}.
A measure extension from µ0 assigns:
µA({1, 2}) = p,
µA({3, 4}) = 1 −p,
p ∈[0, 1].
The extension is not unique: any p ∈[0, 1] yields a valid measure on FA.
The refinement:
F0 ≺FA
is the first step in the refinement chain. Time is nothing more than the existence of this ordered
refinement.

The distinction introduced by B does not force or forbid any distinction in A; their atoms com-

Within this freedom lies the universe’s second moment of “choice.”

• The joint σ-algebra σ(FA ∪FB′) exists but introduces additional atoms beyond those ap-

• the sequence A →B′ is allowed,
• the simultaneous refinement A ∼B′ is forbidden.

framework this incompatibility induces an ordering constraint between the corresponding refine-

Summary of the toy universe
This simple example illustrates several structural features of the refinement framework:
• successive refinements generate a partial ordering of distinctions,
• compatible refinements admit joint realisations,
• incompatible refinements cannot be realised simultaneously,
• measure extensions need not be unique.
VI.
GENERAL REFINEMENT THEORY
In the preceding sections we introduced refinements of σ-algebras on a finite set and the com-
patibility relations governing such refinements. We now give a more systematic description of
refinement dynamics: the space of allowed refinement operations, the algebraic structure they
generate, and the behaviour of sequences of refinements.
Because refinements act on individual atoms of a partition but may affect subsequent refine-
ments, a general theory raises three natural questions:
1. What is the space of allowed local refinement moves?
2. How do these moves compose, commute or obstruct one another?
3. Under what conditions do refinement sequences converge to stable or universal structures?
We treat each in turn.
A.
Refinement operators
Let F be a σ-algebra on the finite set Ω. A refinement operator is a map
R : F −→G
where G is obtained by subdividing one or more atoms of F.
Elementary refinement operators correspond to atomic refinements, i.e. operations that subdi-
vide a single atom while leaving all others unchanged.
For each atom A of F we denote by
RA = {R(i)
A : i ∈IA}
the collection of allowed atomic refinements of that atom. The global refinement semigroup is
then
R = ⟨RA : A ∈Atoms(F)⟩,
consisting of finite compositions of atomic refinement operators.
B.
Compatibility relations
Two refinement operators RA and RB acting on atoms A and B commute whenever A and B
are disjoint atoms of the current partition. In this case
RARB = RBRA.
If the refinements act on overlapping or dependent atoms, the resulting operators need not
commute.
Non-commuting refinements therefore introduce ordering constraints on refinement
sequences.

C.
Constraint propagation
Refinements performed at one stage may influence the availability of subsequent refinements.
For example,
• subdividing an atom may introduce new atoms on which further refinements may act;
• certain refinement patterns may prevent subsequent refinements that would violate compat-
ibility constraints.
Thus sequences of refinements generate a constrained dynamical system on the lattice of σ-
algebras.
D.
Stability of refinement sequences
Given a sequence of refinement operators
R1, R2, . . . ,
the resulting sequence of σ-algebras
Fn = RnRn−1 · · · R1(F0)
forms a chain in the refinement poset. Such sequences may exhibit several types of behaviour:
1. stabilisation, when no further admissible refinements exist;
2. periodic behaviour in the pattern of refinements;
3. indefinite growth of the refinement structure.
These possibilities illustrate the range of dynamical behaviours that can arise within the refine-
ment framework. In particular, the structure of admissible refinements determines both the causal
ordering of events and the evolution of the endogenous probability measure associated with the
system.
Taken together, the results of this paper suggest a general picture in which distinctions, causal
structure, and probability arise together from the dynamics of refinement of σ-algebras. In this
sense, probabilistic structure is not imposed externally but emerges from the progressive resolution
of distinctions.
