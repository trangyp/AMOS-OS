abstract:We analyze a canonical extension of the Stackelberg duopoly to a sequen-
tial framework, where each ﬁrm strategically anticipates the reactions of all subsequent
players. In a triopoly (three-ﬁrm) settings, we obtain existence and uniqueness of mar-
ket equilibrium via a reformulation of the equilibrium conditions that draws on coupled
ﬁxed-point theory. Even with linear demand, convergence of myopic best-response dy-
namics is not guaranteed. A recursive equilibrium formulation enables the analysis of
the limiting case as the number of participants grow.
keywords: Stackelberg triopoly, market equilibrium, response functions, ﬁxed point
theory.

Introduction
Oligopoly markets are deﬁned by strategic interdependence: each ﬁrm’s decision de-
pends on expectations about competitors’ behaviors. This interdependence is explicit
when ﬁrms act sequentially rather than simultaneously, allowing earlier decisions to
shape the strategic environment faced by later movers. Such sequential interactions
arise in industries with capacity commitments, supply-chain ordering, infrastructure
investment, and platform ecosystems, where ﬁrms make decisions in stages and later
participants respond to earlier commitments.
While the classical Stackelberg model focuses on a leader–follower duopoly, many
markets feature more than two dominant ﬁrms and exhibit sequential play.
In the
multi-ﬁrm markets, the equilibrium analysis is more intricate than in the duopoly case
because the equilibrium properties depend on nested best-response structures, and con-
vergence of myopic best-response dynamics is not guaranteed even under simple demand
speciﬁcations. This paper develops an analytical frameworks capable of capturing the
interdependence of sequential decisions and the stability of equilibrium outcomes.
The main technical ingredient of the proposed framework is a characterization of the
ﬁrst order equilibrium conditions as a tuple-ﬁxed point problem of reaction functions.1
This characterization is one-to-one (Theorem 2).

of solution of the ﬁrst order conditions but also for the second order conditions, and
thus equilibrium.
In the ﬁrst part of the paper, section 2, we introduce our framework for a tripodal
market. We consider both Cournot and Stackeberg equilibria via an illustrative exam-
ple. In section 3, we discuss the drawbacks and limitations of the traditional approach
based on optimization and, as an alternative approach, introduce the coupled-ﬁxed
point technology together with our main technical tools that will later deliver results
on existence and uniqueness of market equilibria (Theorem 1). We also obtain condi-
tions for convergence of the Picard iteration and bounds on the rate of convergence.
In section 4, we circle back with a characterization of equilibrium via tuple-ﬁxed point
(Theorem 2). In the context of our tripodal application, we demonstrate that contrac-
tive conditions guarantee that both the ﬁrst order and second order conditions are met
by a single equilibrium. Finally in section 5, we analyze the large market dynamic and
compare Cournot and Stackelberg equilibria for linear and quadratic costs, generaliz-
ing the results in [3]. To summarize, linear costs amplify ﬁrst-mover advantage and
strategic diﬀerentiation in Stackelberg markets, creating more pronounced output and
proﬁt hierarchies. Non-linear (quadratic) costs dampen these eﬀects by penalizing high
output, leading to more balanced outcomes. In very large markets, both cost structures
yield the same competitive benchmark, and the diﬀerences vanish. Importantly, for ﬁ-
nite and intermediate sized markets, linear costs open the Stackelberg-Cournot gap in
outputs while quadratic costs lead to more balanced Stackelberg outputs across ﬁrms
and the Cournot–Stackelberg gap in output and price is smaller.
Broadly speaking, this paper develops an approach to canonical Stackelberg oligopoly
models grounded in two fundamental elements: response functions and mixed tripled
ﬁxed points. By reformulating equilibrium conditions as ﬁxed-point problems, we derive
suﬃcient conditions for the existence, uniqueness, and convergence of market equilib-
rium under transparent and economically interpretable assumptions. In doing so, the
paper bridges classical oligopoly theory with modern ﬁxed-point methods, oﬀering a

1.1
Literature
Oligopoly markets occupy a central position in economic theory, as they describe in-
dustries in which a small number of ﬁrms dominate production and strategic decision-
making [1, 8, 11, 14, 18, 21, 24, 30, 34, 39, 40, 49, 50]. Unlike perfectly competitive mar-
kets [46, 44, 12], where ﬁrms act as price takers, or monopolistic markets [12, 45], where
a single agent dictates outcomes, oligopolistic structures are characterized by strategic
interdependence: each ﬁrm’s decision depends explicitly on expectations about competi-
tors’ behavior. Such market structures arise naturally in industries such as telecommu-
nications, energy, transportation, and digital platforms, where limited competition and
informational asymmetries play a decisive role [19, 20, 24, 34, 45, 46].
The Stackelberg model introduces asymmetry into the oligopoly framework, thereby
enriching the analysis of strategic behavior. In its original formulation, due to von Stack-
elberg [48], ﬁrms are not strategically symmetric, and one participant enjoys a strategic
advantage. In a large part of the literature, this advantage is commonly interpreted in
terms of sequential decision-making, particularly in models of price competition, where
ﬁrms act in stages and later decisions respond to earlier commitments [29, 34, 45]. Ac-
cording to this interpretation, the leader moves ﬁrst by announcing a price or a quantity,
while the follower observes this decision, or has knowledge of the leader’s policy and
subsequently responds optimally. Such a timing-based view is especially prevalent in
price competition and dynamic oligopoly models [11, 15, 32].
Several academic papers speciﬁcally analyze Stackelberg oligopolies with three or
more ﬁrms, often comparing them to the Cournot model. [13] analyze the linear cost
case and demonstrate that in the limit the aggregate outcome of the Stackelberg market
converges to the perfectly competitive outcome. Closer to our study, [3] is a seminal
paper that compares an m-ﬁrm Cournot model with a hierarchical Stackelberg model
where ﬁrms choose outputs sequentially. It demonstrates that while the ﬁrst mover has
an advantage in a duopoly, this is not necessarily true when there are more than two
ﬁrms. In contrast to our focus on the canonical (sequential) Stackelberg settings, the
subsequent literature analyzed multiple leaders and followers [4, 33, 42].
Another strand of the related literature is that in the ﬁxed point analysis. Con-
cretely, the use of ﬁxed point methods in applied mathematics has a long tradition,
beginning with the Banach contraction principle [7] and extending to various general-
izations such as Chatterjea, Hardy–Rogers, and Kannan-type contractions [16, 23, 27].
More recently, coupled [10, 22], tripled [9], and n-tupled ﬁxed point [41] have been intro-

the connection between ﬁxed points and coupled ﬁxed points. Theories have been de-

The monograph [11] provides a foundational and comprehensive analysis of non-
linear oligopoly dynamics, addressing Cournot and generalized oligopoly models under
capacity and nonnegativity constraints, with extensive local and global stability results.
Extending this dynamic viewpoint, [24] introduces generalized tripled ﬁxed points of
response functions as an alternative to payoﬀmaximization, demonstrating that stabil-
ity properties of market equilibria can be ensured even when payoﬀfunctions or price
mechanisms are imperfectly known.
Similar equilibrium-existence concerns arise in
stochastic and diﬀerential settings, where [18] establishes open-loop Nash equilibria via
forward-backward stochastic diﬀerential equations and applies the theory to capacity
expansion in oligopolistic markets.
In [24], the Cournot triopoly is reformulated via generalized tripled ﬁxed points,
yielding equilibria equivalent to payoﬀmaximization under diﬀerentiability, but with
superior dynamic stability. Hierarchical competition is further explored in energy and
electricity markets, where [49] and [50] develop multi-cluster Stackelberg-Nash frame-
works incorporating Cournot competition at the lower layer. Both studies establish
existence and uniqueness of equilibria and propose distributed algorithms for equilib-
rium computation.

2.2
The Canonical Stackelberg Triopoly
Within the Stackelberg framework [48], ﬁrms interact in a sequential rather than simul-
taneous manner. In contrast to the Cournot setting, where each ﬁrm treats competitors’
output levels as ﬁxed, the Stackelberg model assumes that one ﬁrm (the leader) com-
mits to its production level ﬁrst, while the other ﬁrms (followers) choose their outputs
after observing this decision.
As a result, the leader takes into account the reaction functions of the followers
when making its decision, whereas the followers optimize given the leader’s announced
output. This introduces an asymmetry between players, not necessarily in terms of
information, but in terms of timing and strategic advantage.

Main Result
It may be diﬃcult or even impossible to solve the ﬁrst-order conditions explicitly, both
in the Cournot model and in the Stackelberg framework. For instance, if the inverse
demand function is given by P = 10 −arctan(x + y + z) and the cost functions take
the form C(t) = tet, then explicit closed-form expressions for the response functions
generally cannot be obtained.
For these cases we can adopt the static approach to Cournot in [6, 19], in which the
structure of the ﬁrst order conditions are used to re-cast the optimality conditions into
a ﬁxed point problem.

A
Background on Tupled Fixed Points
A.1
Fixed Points for Ordered Triples of Maps
The Banach ﬁxed point concept [7], despite being over 100 years old, has numerous
extensions and implementations. The generalizations can be classiﬁed in diﬀerent ways.
We are only interested in the recommended generalization [22], which alters the concept
of ﬁxed points. Coupled ﬁxed points, introduced in [22], are signiﬁcant in this context.
By considering a map of two variables F : A × A →A ([10, 22]) the notion of a
coupled ﬁxed point (x, y) ∈A × A of F in A is deﬁned as x = F(x, y) and y = F(y, x).
The concept of exploring ﬁxed points for their existence and uniqueness in cyclic
maps, i.e., T : A →B and T : B →A, verses self-maps was initially presented in [28].
By combining the ideas of cyclic maps and coupled ﬁxed points the meaning of ﬁxed
points was broadened to coupled ﬁxed points for cyclic maps of two variables in [43]
by investigating of maps F : A × A →B, F : B × B →A, and the search of suﬃcient
conditions that will ensure the existence of an ordered pair (x, y) so that x = F(x, y)
and y = F(y, x).
However, the equations x = F(x, y) and y = F(y, x) that deﬁne the linked ﬁxed
points [10, 22] frequently result in an ordered pair (x, y) such that x = y. This limitation
was solved in [51], wherein a modiﬁed coupled ﬁxed point for an ordered pair of maps
(F, G) was developed. It is suggested in [51] to combine two maps F : A × B →A,
G : A × B →B and deﬁning an ordered pair (x, y) as a coupled ﬁxed point for
(F, G) if x = F(x, y) and y = G(x, y). The deﬁnition from [10, 22] is obtained when
G(x, y) = F(y, x), and A = B.
According to [22], it has been suggested to seek for an ordered triple (x, y, z) fulﬁlling
x = F(x, y, z), y = F(y, x, y), and z = F(z, y, x), [9]. In [24], generalizations based
on the aforementioned have been presented, i.e. x = F(x, y, z), y = G(x, y, z), and
