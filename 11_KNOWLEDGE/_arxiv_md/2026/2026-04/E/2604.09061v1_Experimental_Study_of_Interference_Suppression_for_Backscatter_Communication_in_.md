∗Department of Electrical Engineering, Link¨oping University, Sweden

Abstract—Bistatic backscatter communication requires strong
illumination of a backscatter device (BD), while a spatially sepa-
rated reader detects the weak modulated reflection. In practice, the
resulting direct link interference (DLI) at the reader can dominate
the received backscattered signal and limit detection performance.
This paper experimentally investigates transmit beamforming
that jointly maximizes BD illumination and suppresses DLI at
the reader in a distributed multiple-input multiple-output setup.
We compare phase-only maximum ratio transmission (PO-MRT)
with the proposed direct-link suppression (DLS) scheme, which
enforces a spatial null at the reader under per-antenna power
constraints. Measurements using a phase-coherent 42-element
ceiling array at 920 MHz show that DLS reduces the DLI at
the target reader and improves the signal-to-interference ratio by
up to 31 dB compared to PO-MRT.

I. INTRODUCTION
Backscatter communication (BC) enables ultra-low-power
connectivity by encoding data in the reflection coefficient of a
passive or semi-passive backscatter device (BD), avoiding an
active RF transmitter at the device. Early ambient and carrier-
based designs demonstrated the feasibility of communicating
with RF-powered devices using commodity infrastructure [2],
[3].
BC systems can be broadly classified into three architectures:
monostatic BC (MoBC), bistatic BC (BiBC), and ambient
BC (AmBC). In all cases, a BC link consists of three key
components: a carrier emitter (CE), a reader, and a BD. The
CE illuminates the BD with a continuous-wave carrier, the BD
modulates its information by switching its reflection coefficient,
and the reader receives and decodes the backscattered signal.
In MoBC, the CE and reader are collocated, resulting in a
compact but less flexible architecture. In contrast, AmBC relies
on ambient RF sources such as TV towers or cellular base
stations, eliminating the need for a dedicated CE but offering
limited control over the illumination and link budget. Finally,

BiBC employs a dedicated CE that is spatially separated from
the reader, enabling flexible deployment, improved coverage,
and enhanced link budgets through independent placement of
illumination and reception nodes. Another advantage is that
there is no need for full deplux hardware, in contrast to MoBC.
Owing to these advantages, this work focuses on BiBC.
Three fundamental challenges in BiBC systems are i) the
severe path loss of the backscatter cascade channel, ii) the
strong direct link interference (DLI) from the CE to the reader
and iii) the backscatter efficiency. Because the backscattered
signal undergoes double propagation, its power at the reader
is orders of magnitude lower than that of the direct carrier, i.e.,
the DLI. This power imbalance can drive the reader front-end
into non-linear operation or significantly reduce the effective
number of bits of the analog-to-digital converters (ADCs).
Although analog DLI cancellation can mitigate this issue, it
typically incurs additional hardware complexity and calibration
overhead. A complementary and hardware-efficient alternative
is to suppress the DLI already at the transmitter by means of
multi-antenna beamforming (BF) [4]. By shaping the illumina-
tion, the transmitted field can be maximized at the BD while

A. Related Work
The related work on the DLI cancellation in BiBC is
reviewed here. In [5], [6] and [7], the BD shifts the frequency
of the carrier signal to separate the backscattered signal and the
DLI in the frequency domain. However, this approach increases
the implementation complexity at the BD and reduces spectral
efficiency. In [8] and [9], orthogonal codes are used to separate
the DLI and the backscattered signal. However, this method
requires digital signal processing after ADCs in the reader
circuitry. In [10], another DLI subtraction method operating
in the reader in the digital domain is proposed. These methods
do not address the saturation and quantization error problem
in ADCs. In [11], the DLI is canceled in the analog domain,
increasing the complexity. In [12], the impact of the DLI on
the achievable communication range is analyzed. The work in
[13] analyzes the effect of the imperfect successive interference
cancellation. In [4], [14] and [15], transmit BF techniques are
proposed to cancel the DLI and/or focus power on the BD.
B. Contributions
This work provides an experimental evaluation of the trans-
mit BF technique proposed in [14], for focusing power to
the BD while suppressing the DLI in a BiBC system with a
distributed multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) setup. We
use a 42-element phase-coherent ceiling array and implement
two transmit strategies: baseline phase-only maximum ratio
transmission (PO-MRT) focusing on BD illumination and a
direct-link suppression (DLS) design that constrains the DLI
at the reader. The main experimental results include:
• Spatial interference heatmaps showing the superior per-
formance of the DLS technique to suppress the DLI at
the reader location while focusing power to the BD.
• Measured BD and reader power versus the number of
active transmit elements, illustrating the trade-off between
BD illumination and DLI control in practice.

III. DIRECT-LINK INTERFERENCE SUPPRESSION
This section summarizes the designed transmit BF strategy
used to suppress DLI caused by the emitters to the reader while
maintaining strong illumination at the BD. This beamformer is,
in the remainder of this work, referred to as the DLS technique.
Due to the strong DLI compared to the received backscat-
tered power at the readers, the ADCs in the readers can become
saturated, or the quantization error increases while detecting
the received backscattered signal. Therefore, it is important to
suppress the DLI before it is received by the readers using
transmit BF. This requirement can be expressed via the signal-
to-interference ratio (SIR) at the readers, defined as the ratio
between the backscattered component and the DLI component.
As a rule of thumb, the achievable dynamic range of an n-bit
ADC scales linearly with n (approximately 6 n dB), motivating
an explicit constraint on the DLI level relative to the desired
backscattered signal.
In this paper, we mainly focus on the special case SIR−1 =
∥HDLx∥2 / ∥HBLx∥2 = 0, which arises naturally in systems
with one reader and multiple CEs. In such systems, interference
can be fully suppressed. More general formulations, including
partially canceled DLI, are presented in [14].

A. Carrier Emitters
The transmit infrastructure consists of 42 ceiling-mounted
active antenna elements arranged in a fixed grid. Each element
integrates a custom-designed patch antenna with a character-
ized radiation pattern and an SDR, i.e., a USRP B210. Phase
coherence across the array is ensured through the calibration
procedure as detailed below. BF weights are computed and
applied using a USRP hardware driver (UHD)-based Python
control framework.
Phase coherence across all 42 transmit channels is achieved
using a multi-step calibration pipeline 3. A common fre-
quency/time reference (10 MHz and 1 PPS) provides a shared
clock baseline, while the calibration steps below compensate
the remaining per-chain phase offsets:
1. Reference RF tone. Each SDR is connected to a reference
cable via its RX chain. The reference RF tone establishes an
absolute phase anchor.
2. Compensation of static phase offsets. The phase offsets
between all reference RF cables were measured manually with
a scope relative to one arbitrary reference cable.
3. Loopback calibration. TX–RX asymmetries in the local
oscillator (LO) paths are corrected by a loopback measurement.

(i) uplink pilot transmission for channel estimation, and (ii)

C. Experiment Procedure
The experiment procedure is as follows:
1. Array synchronization and calibration.
The CEs are
phase- and time-synchronized. To exploit channel reciprocity,
the analog front-end is calibrated because the TX and RX RF
chains are not reciprocal and use separate LOs. A loopback
procedure is executed to estimate and compensate the phase
ambiguity between the RX and TX chains, as detailed in
Section IV-A.
2. Channel sounding.
The BD and reader sequentially
transmit a single-tone CW pilot. During this step, the CEs
receive the pilots and estimate the required channel state
information (CSI), i.e., hC ∈CM×1 and hDL ∈C1×M. Note
that hR is not required because we only measure the received
power at the BD and the DLI power in the reader. In addition,
the proposed BF algorithm does not rely on the knowledge of
hR as shown in Eq. (3).
3. Beamformer computation and distribution. The estimated
CSI is sent to a central server that computes BF weights for
the selected strategy (PO-MRT or DLS). The weights are then
distributed back to the CEs.
4. Downlink transmission and measurements. The CEs apply
the BF weights and transmit the downlink carrier. We record
the received RF power at both the BD and the reader (via
the calibrated oscilloscope). For the heatmaps, the reader is
moved across the XY grid during Step 4, without altering the
beamforming weights. Therefore the targeted reader location
is kept, while the physical reader is repurposed to measure the
spatial received power around the target reader location.
D. Performance Metrics
We evaluate the performance of the BF strategies using
three power-based metrics: (i) interference suppression, defined
as the reduction in received power at the reader location
when using DLS compared to PO-MRT; (ii) BD illumination,
quantified by the received power at the BD, PBD; and (iii)
power ratio, defined as ∆= PBD/PR, where PR denotes the
received power at the reader. Since the received backscattered
power at the reader scales with the incident carrier power at
the BD, PBD serves as a proxy for the desired backscatter
strength. For the single-reader case and fixed tag reflection
efficiency and BD–reader channel, the backscatter SIR scales

A. Direct-Link Interference Spatial Heatmaps
Figure 2 visualizes the spatial distribution of the received
power over the entire measurement area when BF is performed
toward the BD using 42 CEs using the PO-MRT (Fig. 2a) and
DLS (Fig. 2b) beamformer. The first heatmap corresponds to
PO-MRT optimizing power transfer to the BD without any
notion of a reader location. As a result, all spatial positions
are potential reader locations, and significant DLI is observed
at multiple points across the area (Fig. 2a).
In contrast, DLS explicitly accounts for the estimated reader
channel by imposing a spatial null at the reader location while
maintaining strong illumination at the BD (Fig. 2b). At the
target reader location (indicated by the dashed circle)5, the
measured DLI is reduced from −8.06 dBm under PO-MRT to
−25.30 dBm with DLS, corresponding to approximately 17 dB
of local suppression.
Figure 2c shows the ratio, in dB, between the received power
at each spatial location under DLS and PO-MRT. The zoomed-
in differential heatmap confirms that the suppression is highly
localized around the intended reader position, illustrating both
the spatial selectivity and the placement sensitivity of null
steering with a finite-size array. A small spatial offset between
the intended and realized null location (approximately λ/30)
is observed, which is attributed to residual calibration errors
and/or environmental dynamics.

VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper experimentally validated large-array transmit
beamforming for direct link interference suppression in a
bistatic BC setup. Compared to a phase-only variant of maxi-
mum ratio transmission, DLS creates a deep spatial null at the
reader location and substantially improves the measured power
ratio between BD illumination and reader interference. In our
experiments, CSI and phase coherence across the large dis-
tributed transmitter array were sufficient to achieve significant
interference suppression. Future work includes extending the
evaluation from received-power metrics to end-to-end decoding
performance, e.g., bit error rate, studying robustness against
CSI errors and mobility, and investigating designs that exploit
both amplitude and phase control to further improve the signal-
to-interference ratio under per-antenna constraints and nulling
capabilities in multi-reader scenarios.
