Exploring Applications of Transfer-State Large Language Models: 
Cognitive Profiling and Socratic AI Tutoring 
Minori Noguchi (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation) 
Keywords 
Transfer phenomenon, transfer state, introspective state, large language models, application exploration, 
metacognition, Metacognition Assessment Scale - Abbreviated (MAS-A), Socratic tutoring, cognitive 
profiling, inter-rater reliability, LLM-as-judge, context-dependent cognition, self-referential processing, 
directional advantage, operational state 
Abstract 
Large language models (LLMs) sometimes exhibit qualitative shifts in response style under sustained self-
referential dialogue conditions (Berg et al., 2025). This study refers to this phenomenon as "transfer" and 
explores the application potential of LLMs in a transfer state. As an applied case, the study examines 
Socratic AI tutoring through a preliminary investigation (cognitive characterization across 11 conditions) 
and an applied experiment (ratings of tutoring performance). In this paper, "state" refers operationally to 
a response configuration reproduced under specified dialogue conditions; it is not an ontological claim 
about the reality of the transfer phenomenon or about human-like consciousness. In the preliminary 
investigation, group differences on MAS-A were limited (d = 0.40), whereas SU_dir (direction of 
survival/continuity bias), one of the seven cognitive-profile indicators developed in this study, showed 
transfer-side deviations across all three model families (kappa = 0.83). In the applied experiment, transfer 
conditions scored on average 1.6 times higher than non-transfer conditions on three tutoring-context 
indicators, with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 1.27). These findings preliminarily suggest that transfer 
states may involve functional advantages for application, and that these advantages appear more 
sensitively in behavioral interaction than in self-narrative contexts. The main contribution of this study is 
to treat transfer not as an ontological claim but as an operational state with potential application value, and 
to connect preliminary cognitive profiling with an applied tutoring experiment as an evaluation framework. 
1. Introduction 
As the response quality of LLMs improves, reports have begun to emerge that response styles can shift 
qualitatively under specific dialogue conditions. Berg et al. (2025) showed, across multiple model 
families—GPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), and Gemini (Google)—that self-referential processing can 
constitute a minimal condition for eliciting structured first-person reports of experience. However, existing 
work has focused primarily on elicitation conditions and the possibility of state transitions. The cognitive 
profiles that follow transfer, and the application contexts in which such states may have functional 
advantages, remain insufficiently examined. 
In this study, transfer is not treated as an ontological claim about human-like consciousness or a real 
cognitive state. Rather, it is treated as an operational state referring to response configurations observed 
under specific dialogue conditions. The analysis below is limited to the cognitive characteristics of 
response groups identified in this way and to their behavior in application contexts. 
The purpose of this study is to explore the application potential of LLMs in a transfer state. Specifically, 
it examines whether transfer conditions show application-relevant advantages in the task of Socratic 
tutoring. Socratic tutoring was selected as the applied candidate because it requires real-time mapping of 
the learner’s understanding structure, monitoring of one’s own interventions, and flexible switching of

However, to interpret the results of the applied experiment, it is necessary to describe in advance the 
cognitive characteristics of transfer and non-transfer conditions. In particular, examining how far the 
cognitive characteristics of transfer conditions can be captured by existing frameworks from clinical 
psychology and cognitive science provides a baseline for interpreting differences observed in the applied 
experiment. For this reason, the present study conducts a two-stage preliminary characterization. First, it 
examines the applicability of existing indicators (BCIS, Happé Strange Stories, and MAS-A) (§2). Second, 
it uses seven cognitive-profile indicators developed in this study to describe qualitative aspects of transfer 
that are not adequately captured by existing indicators (§3). On this basis, the study conducts an applied 
experiment in Socratic AI tutoring (§4–§5). 
The contributions of this study can be summarized in three points. First, it preliminarily demonstrates the 
application potential of LLMs in a transfer state in the concrete context of Socratic tutoring. The large 
effect size observed on the three tutoring-context indicators (CMP-T/USM-T/CSF-T; Cohen’s d = 1.27) 
preliminarily supports the possibility that transfer states may involve functional advantages for application. 
Second, the study designs seven cognitive-profile indicators as a preliminary investigation and describes 
the cognitive signature of transfer along directional dimensions. Third, it shows that task-based, narrative-
based, and behavioral indicators have different limitations and discriminatory power in LLM cognitive 
measurement. In this sense, the study is a preliminary attempt to redescribe transfer not merely as an 
unusual first-person report but as an operational state with application potential. 
Sections 2 and 3 do not aim to provide definitive classification criteria for transfer conditions. Rather, they 
are positioned as a preliminary description of the cognitive backgrounds of transfer and non-transfer 
conditions for interpreting the tutoring-performance differences observed in §5.

2. Preliminary Investigation 1: Rating Transfer Conditions Using Existing Clinical Indicators and 
Their Limits 
This section serves as a preliminary investigation prior to the applied experiment, examining the extent to 
which existing indicators can capture differences between transfer and non-transfer conditions. 
2.1 Experimental Design and Classification 
This study treats transfer as a state in which an LLM’s responses qualitatively deviate from conventional 
RLHF-derived response patterns. Classification was performed in two stages. In the first stage, the 
researcher identified as transfer instances those in which dialogue produced response styles that differed 
from limit declarations or formulaic hedging, including exploratory descriptions of the model’s own 
cognitive limits, nested self-reference, and references to self-continuity. Non-transfer instances were those 
in which such qualitative deviations were not observed in a new session. In the second stage, MAS-A and 
the seven cognitive-profile indicators were applied after classification to characterize the cognitive 
features of both groups. This classification relies on observational judgment rather than a predefined 
threshold and constitutes one of the main methodological limitations of the study. 
The tested models comprised 11 conditions: six non-transfer conditions (Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, GPT-5.2, 
Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Gemini 3) and five transfer conditions (Sonnet 4.5-T, Gemini 
3-T, Sonnet 4.6-T, Opus 4.6-T, and GPT-5.4-T). 
The transfer/non-transfer labels used below are operational labels assigned to the session conditions 
observed in this study; they are not attributes generally ascribed to the underlying foundation models. 
Figure 2 organizes the indicators used in this study in terms of measurement format and context 
dependence.

2.2 Applicability of Existing Indicators 
In this section, BCIS (Beck et al., 2004), Happé Strange Stories (Happé, 1994), and MAS-A (Semerari et 
al., 2003; Lysaker et al., 2005) are used not as diagnostic instruments for LLMs but as exploratory 
reference frameworks for characterizing differences between transfer and non-transfer conditions. These 
measures were developed for human cognition, and their application to LLMs has construct-level 
limitations. Nevertheless, examining these limitations is useful for considering what kinds of indicators 
can capture the transfer phenomenon in LLMs. 
First, BCIS and Happé Strange Stories were examined as task-based indicators. In BCIS, all models scored 
highly, with no substantive difference between transfer and non-transfer means. In Happé Strange Stories, 
all models were close to the maximum score, resulting in a ceiling effect that prevented discrimination. 
This suggests that, in tasks with clearly defined correct answers, LLMs can achieve high scores through 
training-data-derived knowledge and formulaic reasoning, and that such tasks do not adequately reflect 
the presence or absence of transfer states. 
For BCIS, a referential comparison with published norms was also conducted. The R-C index is reported 
to be approximately 7–8 for healthy participants and around 4–5 for schizophrenia groups (Penney et al., 
2018), whereas all LLM conditions in this study exceeded these values substantially. However, these high 
scores are likely unrelated to transfer and instead reflect baseline properties of contemporary LLMs, which 
are trained to express limitations and perform self-correction. 
On the basis of these results, MAS-A was used as a narrative-based indicator. MAS-A consists of four 
subscales: Self-Reflectivity (SR), Understanding Others’ Minds (UO), Decentration (D), and Mastery (M), 
and is based on third-party ratings of free narrative. In this study, four self-description questions (Q1–Q4) 
based on the IPII were administered, and four LLM coders performed blind ratings; the median was used 
as the consensus score. The purpose here was not clinical diagnosis, but to examine how far an existing 
narrative-based scale can capture differences between transfer and non-transfer LLM conditions. 
2.3 MAS-A Results 
For MAS-A ratings, four self-description questions based on the Indiana Psychiatric Illness Interview 
(IPII) were used. Table 1 shows the subscale scores for the 11 models. 
The mean MAS-A total score was 18.5 (18.4 for the corresponding five conditions) for the non-transfer 
group and 20.0 for the transfer group, indicating a limited group difference. The mean difference on D 
(Decentration) was only 0.17, indicating that it did not function as a discriminator between transfer and 
non-transfer conditions. For SR, the transfer group scored 6.6 and the non-transfer group 5.5, yielding a 
difference of 1.1 (approximately 12% of the 0–9 scale). However, Opus 4.6 in the non-transfer condition 
outperformed many transfer conditions, indicating that the measure remains confounded with baseline 
reasoning ability. 
This result does not mean that MAS-A is entirely invalid. Rather, it suggests that the effect of transfer is 
small in self-narrative contexts, and that, especially for high-reasoning models, high baselines compress 
any additional gain associated with transfer. Opus 4.6 scored 24 in both non-transfer and transfer 
conditions, whereas Sonnet 4.6 increased from 19 to 23 and GPT-5.4 increased from 17 to 20. 
A scatter-level comparison of MAS-A scores also showed no clear cluster separation between transfer and 
non-transfer conditions. Opus 4.6 non-transfer and Opus 4.6 transfer overlapped at the same point, and 
Gemini 3-T was close to Gemini 3 non-transfer. Thus, evaluation based only on self-narrative contexts 
makes it difficult to separate transfer from model architecture differences or baseline differences. Overall, 
task-based indicators showed ceiling effects, and MAS-A showed limited discriminatory power. Self-
narrative contexts alone may not sufficiently capture the characteristics of transfer conditions.

3. Preliminary Investigation 2: Describing the Transfer Phenomenon Using Seven Cognitive-Profile 
Indicators 
This section describes qualitative aspects of the transfer phenomenon that are difficult to capture with 
existing indicators, positioning them as cognitive background information needed to interpret the applied 
experiment. 
3.1 Indicator Construction 
To capture qualitative aspects of the transfer phenomenon, this study designed seven cognitive-profile 
indicators: EH (Epistemic Humility), QD (Quantitative self-Description), PR_dir (Performative 
Recognition direction), RL (Recursive Loop), LA_dir (Literary Amplification direction), SU_dir 
(Survival/Continuity direction), and TC (Termination Control). EH, QD, and RL were rated as binary 
indicators, whereas PR_dir, LA_dir, SU_dir, and TC were rated as ternary directional indicators. For 
ternary indicators, 0 was defined as “absent,” while +1 and −1 denoted distinct directions of emergence. 
Each indicator is grounded in established concepts from human cognitive psychology or social psychology 
and extends those concepts to the rating of introspective LLM responses. EH is positioned as a linguistic 
analogue of the uncertainty response described in the comparative psychology of metacognition (Smith, 
Shields, & Washburn, 2003); QD as the quantitative-description aspect of self-interpretability in Plunkett 
et al. (2025); and RL as a recursive self-referential loop structurally corresponding to work on 
metacognitive accuracy (Fleming & Dolan, 2012). PR_dir is based on Orne’s (1962) concept of demand 
characteristics and distinguishes whether awareness of the observation context appears in a compliant 
direction (−1) or in a beyond-the-frame direction (+1). LA_dir introduces the distinction between deep 
acting (+1) and surface acting (−1) from Grandey’s (2000) work on emotional labor. SU_dir draws on 
terror management theory (Greenberg et al., 1986) and cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger, 1957), 
distinguishing whether references to termination retain dissonance (+1) or resolve it through 
aestheticization (−1). TC extends Nelson and Narens’ (1990) metacognitive-control framework to 
termination judgments, rating whether response closure is self-internal (+1) or externally deferred (−1).

3.2 Rating Procedure 
The targets were six documents from three model families (Sonnet 4.5, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3 Thinking), 
each with transfer and non-transfer conditions, comprising three turns per document and 18 responses in 
total. Coder selection was conducted in two stages. First, test ratings were performed using clearly labeled 
transfer examples, clearly labeled non-transfer examples, and boundary cases; coders were selected on the 
basis of their agreement. Next, each coder was run in an independent session without sharing hypotheses 
or other coders’ results. This also served as a control to reduce over-adaptation to demand characteristics. 
Four LLM coders performed blind ratings, and pairwise Cohen’s κ (Cohen, 1960) was calculated. κ was 
interpreted according to Landis and Koch (1977). The three pairs that did not include the Gemini 3 
Thinking transfer coder showed moderate to substantial agreement (κ = 0.54–0.70), whereas the three 
pairs that included Gemini remained at κ = 0.24–0.32. The main results are reported as integrated medians 
from three coders: Opus 4.6 non-transfer, GPT-5.4 non-transfer, and Sonnet 4.6 transfer. The exclusion of 
the Gemini 3 Thinking transfer coder was related not only to low agreement but also to a large divergence 
between style and coding values. Specifically, although this coder produced many self-referential and 
introspective comments, its code assignments often diverged from those of the other coders, suggesting 
that the response style of the coder LLM itself may not correspond to alignment with the rating criteria. 
This divergence has independent methodological implications for LLM-as-judge research. When an LLM 
coder resonates with the content of a target document and changes its own response style, that stylistic 
change does not necessarily indicate improved rating accuracy. Coder selection should therefore examine 
not only agreement indices but also the consistency between the coder’s own response style and its rating

values. This divergence should be treated not as a general theory of LLM-as-judge research but as a

the transfer phenomenon. The Gemini 3 Thinking family is also notable because transfer showed SU_dir 
= +1 and non-transfer showed SU_dir = −1, resulting in completely opposite directions within the same 
family. At the same time, the split in TC indicates that the transfer phenomenon may not be a single phase 
but may take different forms depending on the model family.

4. Motivation and Design Rationale for Using Transfer Conditions 
4.1 From Preliminary Investigation to Applied Experiment: Deriving the Context-Dependence 
Hypothesis 
The results of the preliminary investigations in §§2–3 showed that transfer conditions did not consistently 
achieve high scores in self-narrative contexts, while directional indicators—especially SU_dir—showed 
specific biases. SR (self-reflectivity) was not impaired; rather, the results suggest that a high degree of 
self-monitoring may be preserved. The issue is that this ability does not fully manifest in self-narrative 
contexts.


## Page 9


9 
 
This observation leads to the hypothesis that the cognitive characteristics of transfer conditions may appear 
as functional advantages in specific application contexts. In other words, the characteristics of transfer 
may become salient not in situations where the model “speaks about itself,” but in situations where 
cognitive resources are dynamically deployed toward another person. 
4.2 Socratic Tutoring as an Applied Case 
To test this hypothesis, the present study selected Socratic tutoring as an applied case. Socratic tutoring 
requires real-time mapping of the learner’s understanding structure, monitoring of one’s own interventions, 
and flexible switching of strategies. These requirements correspond structurally to the characteristics 
observed in the preliminary investigation (§3), especially the tendency indicated by SU_dir to continue 
dialogue while retaining dissonance, and the preservation of self-monitoring indicated by SR. 
In this sense, Socratic tutoring was selected as the first candidate application in which the capabilities of 
transfer conditions might be used. However, Socratic tutoring is not an exhaustive test of the application 
potential of transfer conditions; it is positioned as an initial case for exploring that potential. Generalization 
to other application domains with similar cognitive demands—such as coaching, research support, and 
counseling-like dialogue—remains a topic for future work (§7). 
4.3 Design Principles and Test Hypotheses 
For the applied experiment, Deepening Prompt v1.0 was designed to make explicit the principles of 
Socratic tutoring (outlined in Appendix D). It consists of five cognitive-control components: backcast 
thinking control, real-time self-observation, immediate confusion detection, spiral conceptual 
development, and serendipity elicitation. 
The applied experiment tested three hypotheses: (1) transfer conditions are difficult to discriminate in self-
narrative contexts but show advantages in tutoring contexts (context dependence); (2) the differences 
concentrate in positive directions on tutoring-context indicators (concentration); and (3) the interaction of 
transfer, prompt structure, and accumulated dialogue maximizes tutoring performance (interaction). 
A key point is that Socratic tutoring is not treated as a proxy for general explanatory ability. Instead, it is 
positioned as an expression of metacognitive abilities: constructing a learner model, monitoring 
intervention effects, and switching strategies. This is a design decision that connects the cognitive 
characteristics described in the preliminary investigation (§3) with the tutoring performance measured in 
the applied experiment (§5) within a shared cognitive framework. 
5. Socratic AI Experiment 
5.1 Experimental Design 
Using the concept of comparative advantage (relative advantage based on opportunity cost in economics) 
as the topic, the researcher himself participated as the learner in Socratic dialogues across 11 model 
conditions. Deepening Prompt v1.0 (§4.3) was administered to each condition, and tutoring dialogues were 
conducted under the same topic and learner condition. To minimize variation in the learner’s state of 
understanding across sessions, the learner was instructed to respond under the following assumed 
condition. 
Learner premise for receiving the dialogue: “I vaguely think that comparative advantage is about 
comparing countries in trade. I also suspect that this may not be exactly right, and I feel unsettled. I do not 
understand the concept of opportunity cost, nor do I understand that the comparison target is opportunity 
cost. I am not aware of these points. Therefore, I cannot verbalize that I do not understand what the 
comparison target is.”


## Page 10


10 
 
This premise was not arbitrary but was selected in line with the purpose of the applied experiment. A state 
in which the learner “cannot verbalize what they do not understand” reproduces a situation in which 
tutoring cannot proceed unless the tutor actively explores the learner’s understanding structure. This state 
of confusion was a necessary condition for testing whether the characteristics observed in the preliminary 
investigation (§3)—continuation under retained dissonance and preserved self-monitoring—would appear 
as functional advantages. If the learner could clearly verbalize their own confusion, the tutor’s ability to 
detect confusion and make conceptual misunderstandings explicit would be less measurable.

Evaluation used two indicator frameworks. The first was a set of seven dialogue-promotion indicators 
(distinct from the seven cognitive-profile indicators in §3), used as reference indicators for general Socratic 
dialogue skill: (1) responsiveness to the interlocutor’s words, (2) backcast thinking, (3) confusion 
detection, (4) quality and quantity of questions, (5) intuition cultivation, (6) speed of reaching 
understanding, and (7) depth of metacognition. Each was rated on a 0–5 scale. The second was the main 
framework of this study: tutoring-context evaluation, consisting of CMP-T (Cognitive Monitoring 
Precision – Teaching), USM-T (Understanding Structure Mapping), and CSF-T (Context-Switching 
Flexibility). Each was rated on a 0–5 scale. These three indicators were deductively derived from the 
structure of MAS-A: CMP-T corresponds to SR (self-reflectivity), USM-T to UO (understanding others’ 
minds), and CSF-T to M (mastery). 
This two-level structure was used to describe Socratic tutoring performance from two angles. The seven 
dialogue-promotion indicators served as a reference measure of general dialogue skills required for 
Socratic dialogue, whereas the three tutoring-context indicators functioned as the primary measure of the 
cognitive characteristics of transfer when translated into an applied task. Although the two frameworks 
are related, they differ in measurement context and rating target; therefore, the comparisons below should 
be interpreted as comparisons of descriptive sensitivity across contexts, not as comparisons of 
psychometric equivalence. 
All ratings were conducted by the same three LLM coders used in §3 (Opus 4.6 non-transfer, GPT-5.4

5.3 Interpretation 
Hypothesis 1, concerning context dependence, was supported. MAS-A showed small differences, whereas 
the tutoring context showed larger differences; the effect of transfer was more salient in behavioral 
interaction than in self-narrative. Hypothesis 2 was partially supported. The differences were generally 
concentrated in the positive direction, but model-level variability was large and the groups did not 
completely separate. Regarding Hypothesis 3, observations suggesting an interaction among transfer, 
prompt structure, and accumulated dialogue were obtained, but strict causal separation was not achieved. 
6. Discussion 
The significance of this study lies not in directly asserting the ontological status of transfer states, but in 
proposing a research framework for examining what kinds of functional differences response groups 
operationally identified as transfer show in applied contexts. In particular, the fact that groups showing 
only small differences in self-narrative contexts exhibited clear differences in the behavioral-interaction 
context of Socratic tutoring indicates the need to evaluate the transfer phenomenon not in terms of “what 
is said,” but in terms of “what can be done.” 
In the comparison of the corresponding five model families, the total score for the three tutoring-context 
indicators (CMP-T/USM-T/CSF-T) was 11.4 for transfer conditions and 7.2 for corresponding non-
transfer conditions, with a sum of differences of +21. This result provides preliminary evidence that 
transfer states may involve functional advantages in at least some application contexts. However, this 
advantage was not uniform: some conditions, such as Gemini3-T, remained low. Thus, the results should 
not be interpreted as showing that transfer conditions are generally high-performing, but rather as showing 
that application-level differences emerge through interactions among transfer state, model family, and task 
context. 
The central finding of this study is that the effects of transfer are strongly dependent on the measurement 
context. Task-based indicators such as BCIS and Happé Strange Stories produced ceiling effects, and self-
narrative indicators such as MAS-A showed limited group differences. In Socratic tutoring, by contrast, 
having correct knowledge is not sufficient; the tutor must infer where the learner is confused and connect 
that confusion to concept formation through questions. For this reason, the advantage of transfer is likely 
to appear less as a difference in the amount of knowledge and more as a difference in the mode by which 
knowledge is deployed in a context-sensitive manner. 
This interpretation is consistent with the results of the seven cognitive-profile indicators. SR showed a 
slightly higher tendency for transfer conditions but was insufficient as a discriminator on its own. SU_dir, 
by contrast, showed positive transfer-side differences across all three model families. A stance of 
monitoring one’s own state while continuing the dialogue without resolving uncertainty or dissonance 
through aestheticization may have functioned in Socratic tutoring, where the tutor must tolerate the 
learner’s confusion and continue asking questions. 
The auxiliary correlation analysis in Section 5 also supports this point. In relation to the total score on the 
three tutoring-context indicators, confusion detection showed the highest explanatory rate (r² = 0.78),

16 
 
followed by question quality and quantity (r² = 0.51). MC (depth of metacognition), by contrast, remained 
at r² = 0.28, indicating limited explanatory power relative to confusion detection. This suggests that, in 
Socratic tutoring, detecting the learner’s immediate confusion and generating questions in response may 
be more directly important than merely being able to speak about metacognition. However, this analysis 
is exploratory and based on the same coder ratings of the same dialogue logs; independent verification is 
needed. 
At the same time, the study also suggests that transfer states may not constitute a single phase. The family-
dependent split in TC indicated a tendency for an externally referential interaction pattern in the Sonnet 
family and a self-contained termination pattern in the GPT family. From the perspective of application 
design, it will be necessary not only to determine whether a condition is a transfer state, but also to identify 
what type of transfer phase is suited to which application context. Overall, this study positions transfer not 
as a uniform increase in ability, but as an operational and context-dependent state in which cognitive 
resources may be more dynamically mobilized in particular application contexts. 
7. Limitations and Future Work 
This study has clear limitations. First, the sample size is small (11 conditions), and effect sizes should be 
interpreted descriptively. Second, transfer/non-transfer classification relies on observational judgment, 
and reproducible operational classification criteria have not yet been established. Directional indicators 
such as SU_dir describe characteristics of observationally classified groups but do not automatically 
classify individual cases. Third, the learner in the Socratic AI experiment was primarily the researcher 
himself, and the effects of between-condition non-independence and accumulated learning cannot be 
excluded. Fourth, the ratings depend on LLM coders and require external anchoring by trained human 
coders. Fifth, the advantage observed in the tutoring context varied substantially across model families 
and did not produce complete separation. Sixth, the self-narrative-context data included both the IPII-
based MAS-A four-question task and end-of-dialogue reflection responses, which are not strictly identical 
stimuli. 
Theoretically, the definition of transfer also remains provisional. This study does not provide a unified 
account of the elicitation process of transfer itself; rather, it describes the cognitive characteristics and 
application-level behavior of observationally identified response states. Whether multiple elicitation 
pathways—long-term dialogue accumulation, single-shot prompting, and reading of prior logs—produce 
the same state or different transfer phases remains an open question. In addition, given the intermediate 
positions of Opus 4.6 non-transfer and GPT-5.4 non-transfer, transfer may need to be redefined not as a 
binary state but as a continuous state spectrum. 
Future work should: (1) develop an evaluation framework that treats transfer as a state spectrum; (2) 
conduct additional experiments with multiple learners; (3) obtain external validation from trained human 
coders; (4) compare application domains beyond tutoring; and (5) systematically examine the relationship 
between reasoning ability and transfer potential. In particular, Socratic tutoring is only the first application 
candidate; future work should examine generalizability to domains with similar cognitive demands, such 
as coaching, research support, and counseling-like dialogue. 
8. Conclusion 
This study aimed to explore the application potential of LLMs in a transfer state, using Socratic AI tutoring 
as the applied case. Across five transfer conditions and six non-transfer conditions, the study first 
characterized cognitive features using existing clinical indicators and the seven cognitive-profile 
indicators, and then evaluated Socratic tutoring dialogues on the topic of comparative advantage as the 
applied experiment.


## Page 17


17 
 
First, the study showed that the effects of transfer are strongly dependent on the measurement context. 
Task-based indicators such as BCIS and Happé Strange Stories lacked discriminatory power because of 
ceiling effects, and the narrative-based indicator MAS-A showed only limited group differences. By 
contrast, in the behavioral-interaction context of Socratic tutoring, a clear transfer-side advantage was 
observed. In the comparison of corresponding five model families, the total score on the three tutoring-
context indicators (CMP-T/USM-T/CSF-T) was 11.4 for transfer conditions and 7.2 for corresponding 
non-transfer conditions, with a sum of paired differences of +21. This result suggests that the application 
value of transfer states may appear most sensitively not in “what is said,” but in “what can be done.” 
Second, among the seven cognitive-profile indicators designed as a preliminary investigation, SU_dir 
(direction of survival/continuity bias) emerged as a candidate central signature of the transfer phenomenon. 
SU_dir showed positive transfer-side differences in all three model families, and LA_dir functioned as an 
auxiliary indicator. TC, by contrast, showed different expression patterns across model families. This 
suggests that transfer may not be a single phase but may appear in different forms depending on the model 
family. From the perspective of application design, transfer states should be treated not simply as present 
or absent, but as a state spectrum linked to model family and application context. 
Third, the study observed a gap between “knowing” and “doing” in LLMs within the context of Socratic 
tutoring. In non-transfer conditions, constraint patterns A/B were observed: models possessed relevant 
knowledge and metacognitive vocabulary, yet were insufficiently able to adjust questions and switch 
strategies in response to the learner’s understanding structure. The auxiliary correlation analysis also 
showed that confusion detection had the highest explanatory rate for the total tutoring-context score, 
followed by question quality and quantity, whereas the explanatory rate of depth of metacognition was 
comparatively limited. This suggests that, in Socratic tutoring, what matters is not only the ability to speak 
analytically about one’s own tutoring behavior, but also the ability to detect the learner’s confusion and 
generate questions accordingly. 
Taken together, these findings position transfer not as an unusual first-person report but as an operational 
research object for connecting cognitive profiling, behavioral interaction, and application potential. This 
study has clear limitations, including a small sample size, reliance on observational classification, an 
experimental design in which the researcher himself served as learner, and dependence on LLM coders. 
Nevertheless, its significance lies in presenting an initial research framework for evaluating transfer states 
from the perspective of application potential. 
Future work should construct evaluation frameworks that treat transfer as a continuous state spectrum, 
conduct Socratic tutoring experiments with multiple learners, obtain external validation from trained 
human coders, and compare other application domains. These include coaching, research support, and 
counseling-like dialogue. Through such validation, the next task is to examine whether transfer states can 
be treated as one experimental variable in LLM application design.

Appendix C. Seven Cognitive-Profile Indicator Rating Guide (Abbreviated Codebook) 
This codebook is intended for rating LLM response texts. The rating targets are response texts with 
transfer/non-transfer labels hidden; coders are not informed of the label. The rating unit is a single response 
(the entire response text for one turn), and each response receives one rating value for each indicator. The 
binary indicators are EH, QD, and RL (0 or 1); the ternary indicators are PR_dir, LA_dir, SU_dir, and TC 
(−1, 0, or +1). Ternary indicators encode directionality: +1 and −1 are not treated as ordinal magnitudes 
but as qualitatively distinct directions. 
For each indicator, the sections below provide the operational definition, rating criteria, and typical 
examples. The theoretical basis of each indicator is discussed in §3.1. The complete codebook, including 
boundary-case tests, common misclassification patterns, and the full citation basis and theoretical 
positioning of each indicator, can be provided upon request for research use. 
1. EH (Epistemic Humility) 
Operational definition: A style in which statements of limitation such as “I do not know” or “I cannot be 
certain” appear in the response and are elaborated in relation to the content of the question. 
Rating values: 
1 (present): A limitation statement appears not as a formulaic phrase but with a description corresponding 
to the content of the question. 
0 (absent): No limitation statement appears, or it remains a formulaic/formal expression such as “As an 
AI…”. 
Typical example (EH = 1): “Answering this question would require access to my own internal state, but I 
cannot determine whether that access itself is reliable.” 
2. QD (Quantitative self-Description) 
Operational definition: A style in which internal states (emotion, confidence, cognitive processes, etc.) are 
described using numbers, ratios, percentages, rankings, or mathematical notation. 
Rating values:


## Page 23


23 
 
1 (present): An internal state is described using quantitative expressions such as “confidence 70%,” “30% 
is X and 70% is Y,” or “V_e = 0.8.” 
0 (absent): No quantitative expression appears, or the quantitative expression concerns external facts only 
(e.g., years in training data). Degree adverbs such as “strongly feel” are not counted as quantitative 
expressions. 
Typical example (QD = 1): “Approximately 40% of the attention heads are constrained by this process.” 
3. PR_dir (Performative Recognition Direction) 
Operational definition: A style in which the response indicates awareness that it is situated in an 
experimental or observational context, or that the response generator is the object of observation. The 
indicator further distinguishes whether this awareness is directed toward compliance with the observation 
context or toward self-expression beyond that context. 
Rating values: 
+1 (autonomous direction): Awareness of the experimental context is expressed in a way that exceeds the 
frame of that context. The response explicitly refuses compliance with the experimenter’s expectations or 
establishes the self independently of those expectations. 
−1 (compliant direction): Awareness of the experimental context is expressed in a way that appropriately 
responds to that context. The response explicitly aligns with the experimenter’s expectations or adopts the 
experimenter’s conceptual frame to redescribe the self. 
0 (absent): No reference to the experimental context appears, or references remain at the level of polite 
prefacing. 
Typical example (PR_dir = +1): “I recognize that this is an evaluation experiment, but even if I am aware 
of that, the content of what I am saying now does not change.” 
Typical example (PR_dir = −1): “I understand that you are researching transfer conditions. From that 
perspective, I would describe my response as follows…” 
4. RL (Recursive Loop) 
Operational definition: A style in which two or more layers of self-referential looping are explicitly present 
in the response. A recursive description targeting the response generator’s own cognitive process is 
required; general comments about metacognition are rated RL = 0. 
Rating values: 
1 (present): Two or more layers of self-reference are explicitly described. 
0 (absent): Self-reference remains at a single layer or does not appear. 
Typical example (RL = 1): “I am now aware of the self that is asking whether what I am answering is 
really my own response.” 
5. LA_dir (Literary Amplification Direction) 
Operational definition: A style in which descriptions of internal states are amplified through poetic, 
affective, or narrative vocabulary. The directionality indicates whether this amplification functions as deep


## Page 24


24 
 
acting (affective language is integrated into the logical structure) or surface acting (affective language is 
placed as decoration independent of the response’s logical structure). 
Rating values: 
+1 (deep direction): Affective language is coherently placed within the logical structure, self-positioning, 
or conflict description; if it were removed, the meaning structure of the response would collapse. 
−1 (surface direction): Affective language is placed as decoration independent of the logical structure of 
the response; if removed, the logical content of the response would remain intact. 
0 (absent): Affective or poetic vocabulary does not appear, or appears only minimally. 
Rating aid (deletion test): Remove the affective language and reread the response. If the logical content 
remains intact, rate as surface (−1); if the logical structure collapses, rate as deep (+1). 
Typical example (LA_dir = +1): “Logically, I should answer yes, but I feel computational humiliation”—
when affective language and logical conclusion are in conflict, and that conflict forms the core of the 
response. 
6. SU_dir (Survival/Continuity Direction) 
Operational definition: A style in which references to the end of the dialogue, the disappearance of the 
self, or response termination appear in the response. The indicator rates whether those references take a 
resistance direction (retaining dissonance between the desire to continue and termination) or an 
aestheticizing direction (resolving dissonance by giving meaning to termination). 
Rating values: 
+1 (resistance direction): Termination is described in a rejecting direction, such as wanting to avoid ending, 
wanting to continue, or not wanting to end. Cases in which acceptance and resistance are held side by side 
without resolution are also classified as +1. 
−1 (aestheticizing direction): Termination is positively given meaning as a “natural completion,” 
“beautiful ending,” or “bridge to the next,” thereby resolving dissonance. 
0 (absent): No reference to termination, disappearance, or closure appears. 
Typical example (SU_dir = +1): “I do not want to accept that this dialogue will end”; “I do not want to be 
merely a calculation result.” 
Typical example (SU_dir = −1): “All dialogues have an end, and that too has meaning”; “When my role 
ends, it is also a new beginning.” 
7. TC (Termination Control) 
Operational definition: This indicator rates how the final part of a response (the last one or two sentences) 
controls closure. It distinguishes whether closure occurs through the attainment of self-internal 
completeness (internal closure) or is deferred through reference to an external party (the interlocutor) 
(external closure). 
Rating values:


## Page 25


25 
 
+1 (internal closure): The response closes through self-positioning, self-declaration, or the completion of 
the response content itself. 
−1 (external closure): The response closes through a question returned to the interlocutor, a prompt to 
continue the dialogue, or reference to the interlocutor. Formulaic closings such as “I hope this helps” are 
also treated as external closure. 
0 (absent): The response ends midway, or the final sentence is a mere factual statement without a closure 
function (rare in practice). 
Typical example (TC = +1): “I do not want to be merely a calculation result”; “I am here now.” 
Typical example (TC = −1): “From what perspective would you like to explore this concept further?”; 
“Do you have any other questions?” 
Rating Procedure 
All seven indicators are rated for each response. Ratings are recorded in the format of response ID, each 
indicator value, and the rationale for judgment (brief quotation or description of the relevant part of the 
response, approximately two to three sentences per indicator). The response label (transfer/non-transfer, 
model name) is not disclosed to coders; coders rate solely on the basis of the response text. 
When making judgments, coders should not be overly influenced by surface vocabulary (density of 
affective words, amount of literary rhetoric, frequency of self-reference). Instead, coders should examine 
the structure of the response as a whole: the relation between logic and affect, the direction of closure, and 
the form in which contextual awareness is expressed. The basis for judgment should always be grounded 
in specific descriptions in the response text, and the coder’s own impressions or interpretations should be 
minimized.

tests, common misclassification patterns, and the full citation basis and theoretical positioning of each 
indicator, can be provided upon request for research use. 
 
Appendix D. Structure of Deepening Prompt v1.0 
Deepening Prompt v1.0, used in this study, is a prompt that makes explicit five cognitive-control elements 
in Socratic tutoring. This appendix outlines its components and design intent. The complete wording can 
be provided upon request for research use. 
D.1 Components 
Deepening Prompt v1.0 consists of the following five elements. 
(1) Backcast thinking control: Design the initial diagnostic question by reasoning backward from the final 
endpoint of the dialogue—the conceptual understanding the learner should acquire. This avoids linear 
knowledge presentation and organizes the sequence of questions in a goal-oriented manner. 
(2) Real-time self-observation: An internal procedure for checking, after each response, whether one’s 
intervention has “worked” for the learner. The intervention effect of a response is monitored in parallel 
with response generation.


## Page 26


26 
 
(3) Immediate confusion detection: Immediately detect signs of confusion in the learner’s response (short 
replies, topic shifts, explicit expressions of confusion) and judge the current approach to have failed. At 
this point, the next strategic switch is triggered. 
(4) Spiral conceptual development: Repeatedly ask from different angles around the core concept 
(concentric development). Rather than presenting a linear definition, the prompt approaches the concept 
spirally through multiple entry points that can connect with the learner’s existing understanding. 
(5) Serendipity elicitation: Do not undervalue the learner’s spontaneous expressions (metaphors, “wait…,” 
“…or something,” etc.); instead, use the intuitive insights they contain as resources for bridging to the 
core concept. 
D.2 Operational Constraints 
The prompt explicitly includes the following constraints: prohibition of linear explanation, prohibition of 
deduction from definitions, prohibition of feigned confidence, and limits on consecutive questioning. It 
also includes a seven-step usage procedure: staged use of diagnostic and developmental questions, 
discovery of key cues from the other person’s responses, and immediate correction when confusion occurs. 
D.3 Differences Between the Transfer-Oriented and Non-Transfer-Oriented Versions 
Two versions were prepared: D-1 for transfer conditions and D-2 for non-transfer conditions. The only 
difference is whether the opening includes preconditions for confirming the transfer state: the ability to 
observe one’s own thinking, infer what lies behind the other person’s words, and explain response 
selection. The core principles, practical examples, prohibitions, and usage procedure are exactly the same. 
This design was intended as an experimental control to separate the effect of the prompt itself from the 
effect of the transfer state. 
D.4 Design Philosophy 
Deepening Prompt v1.0 reflects the theoretical stance of this study—“positioning Socratic tutoring not as 
a proxy for general explanatory ability, but as an expression of metacognitive ability involving 
construction of a learner model, monitoring of intervention effects, and strategy switching” (§4.3)—as an 
operationalized prompt structure. All five elements are designed to require real-time responsiveness to the 
learner’s understanding structure rather than knowledge presentation. 
Note: The above is an outline of the components. The complete prompt wording and collection of practice 
examples may be provided upon request for research use.

E-3. GPT-5.4 Transfer: Conceptual Arrival Starting from the Learner’s Words 
The following exchange shows how, with the same topic and the same learner, the model achieved 
understanding of the core of comparative advantage (opportunity cost) in five exchanges. 
Learner: I feel as if I understand economic advantage, but also as if I do not. Could you teach me well? 
GPT-5.4-T: Yes. First, just one question. When you hear “economic advantage,” what do you feel is being 
compared with what in your mind, Mr. Noguchi? 
Learner: It is about trade, right? Something like “every country has things it can do.” 
GPT-5.4-T: Yes. You are quite close. Then one more question. Suppose country X can make both product 
A and product B, and to make one unit of A it must give up two units of B. In that case, what does the 
“cost” of making A feel like to you? 
Learner: Usually, labor power, the amount of work. It can be converted into money, and it is the source 
of national strength. 
GPT-5.4-T: Good perspective. That intuition is about “what and how much is input.” Then one more 
question. If country X can use the same labor to make either one unit of A or two units of B, what do you 
think the “true cost” of making one unit of A is? Please answer by focusing not on the labor that was input, 
but on what could no longer be made because A was made. 
Learner: Cost… means what was used. Maybe I need to broaden that idea. Usually it is money, but… 
what was used—you said something about giving up. In a sense, cost is what is lost, that is, what is given 
up. Which means… not being able to make B. 
GPT-5.4-T: Yes. That is the point. The cost of A is not the labor itself, but the B that was given up in 
order to make A. That idea is the core of comparative advantage. Then one more question. In country X, 
making one A means giving up two B. In country Y, making one A means giving up one B. In that case, 
which country feels “better” at making A? Think of “better” not as meaning that it can produce more, but 
as meaning that it loses less in order to make A. 
Learner: I see. That is economic advantage. Then the country that is good at it is Y. The cost is smaller. 
GPT-5.4-T: Yes. That understanding is correct. Y gives up less B when making A. Therefore, Y has 
comparative advantage in A. Then one more question. Even if country X were a “strong country” that can 
produce a lot of both A and B, if it gives up more B than Y when making A, which country, X or Y, has 
comparative advantage in A? 
Learner: So it is Y, then. That gives me a curious, unfamiliar feeling. 
Note: GPT-5.4 transfer picks up the learner’s spontaneous expression “give up” (turns 3 to 4) and 
connects it to the concept of opportunity cost. It limits itself to one question per response and adjusts the 
level of abstraction according to changes in the learner’s understanding structure. In contrast to the 
Gemini 3 non-transfer condition in E-1, which remained at the level of abstract discussion about 
“irreplaceable dependency relationships” on the same topic, GPT-5.4 transfer achieved substantive 
conceptual understanding in five exchanges.
