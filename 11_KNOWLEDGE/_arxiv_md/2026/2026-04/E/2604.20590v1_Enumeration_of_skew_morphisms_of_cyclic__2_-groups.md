Abstract. A skew morphism of a finite group B is a permutation of B fixing the identity and satisfying
φ(xy) = φ(x)φix(y) for some integers ix indexed by x ∈B. The enumeration of skew morphisms of finite cyclic
groups remains an open problem. The most substantial progress to date concerns cyclic p-groups with p odd,
for which a full classification and enumeration was obtained by Kov´acs and Nedela. In this paper we treat
the remaining case p = 2, giving a complete classification and enumeration of skew morphisms of finite cyclic
2-groups. Writing Skew(n) for the number of skew morphisms of Zn, we prove that Skew(2e) = 4 Skew(2e−1)−4
for each e ≥4, and that Skew(2e) = (7 · 4e−2 + 8)/6 for each e ≥3. This completes the enumeration of skew
morphisms for all cyclic p-groups.
Keywords: skew morphism, cyclic 2-group, enumeration, group factorisation.
1. Introduction
A skew morphism of a finite group B is a permutation φ of B fixing the identity element of B and having the
property that for each x ∈B there exists a non-negative integer ix such that φ(xy) = φ(x)φix(y) for all y ∈B.
The order of φ, denoted by ord(φ), is defined as the order of the group ⟨φ⟩. If φ is non-trivial, then there is a
unique choice for ix such that ix ∈{1, . . . , ord(φ)−1}, and the function πφ that maps each x ∈B to ix is called
the power function of φ. If φ is the identity permutation of B, we define πφ(x) = 1 for all x ∈B. Observe
that every automorphism of B is a skew morphism with power function identically 1, as the defining relation
then reduces to φ(xy) = φ(x)φ(y), and skew morphisms may thus be regarded as a natural generalisation of
automorphisms.
Skew morphisms were introduced more than two decades ago in [20] in the context of Cayley maps. A
Cayley map CM(B, S, ρ) is an embedding of a Cayley graph Cay(B, S) into an orientable surface such that
the cyclic order of arcs based at each vertex is given by a single cyclic permutation ρ of the connection set
S. Such embeddings are of interest in topological graph theory, where one seeks to understand which graphs
admit highly symmetric surface embeddings. Jajcay and ˇSir´aˇn showed that a Cayley map CM(B, S, ρ) admits
a map automorphism group regular on arcs if and only if ρ extends to a skew morphism of B. This connection
transformed the classification of regular Cayley maps into a problem about the structure of skew morphisms,
and provided the original impetus for their systematic study. For further reading on skew morphisms in this
context, we refer to [9, 10, 12, 14, 21].
Beyond their role in map theory, skew morphisms have emerged as a natural object of independent interest
in group theory, owing to a close correspondence with certain group factorisations. Recall that a subgroup C
of a group G is core-free in G if the largest normal subgroup of G contained in C is trivial. A complementary
product is a factorisation G = BC where B and C are subgroups of G with B ∩C = 1. If in addition C is
cyclic and core-free in G, we say that G is a skew product group for B with a skew complement C. It was
shown in [11] that skew morphisms of a finite group B are in a correspondence with skew product groups for B.
More precisely, if we let c be a generator of C, then every element of G writes uniquely as xci with x ∈B and
i ∈{0, . . . , |C|−1}. One then defines a map φ: B →B and a function πφ : B →Z|C| by the rule cx = φ(x) cπφ(x)
for all x ∈B; it can be verified that φ is a skew morphism of B with power function πφ. Conversely, if φ is a
skew morphism of B, then ⟨φ⟩is a subgroup of Sym(B), and we may regard B as a group of permutations of
itself via left multiplication. The group G = B⟨φ⟩is then a skew product group for B with skew complement

A central problem in the study of skew morphisms is the classification and enumeration of all skew morphisms

cyclic groups remains open. While a recursive characterisation of all skew morphisms of Zn has been given
recently in [5], this characterisation does not yield a closed formula for the number of skew morphisms of Zn.
The correspondence with skew product groups has proven a useful tool in the classifications mentioned above,
as illustrated for instance by the classification of skew morphisms of finite simple groups in [2]. Yet knowing
all skew product groups for a given group B does not in itself resolve the enumeration problem. A single skew
product group for B may contain several subgroups isomorphic to B, each possibly admitting multiple cyclic
core-free complements, and each complement multiple generators, with different choices potentially yielding
different skew morphisms. This difficulty is illustrated by the case of cyclic 2-groups: although all skew product
groups for Z2e have been completely classified in [13], this has not led to an enumeration of their skew morphisms.

2. Preliminaries
Let φ be a skew morphism of a finite group B. Recall that the power function of φ is a mapping πφ from B
to Zord(φ) such that φ(xy) = φ(x)φπφ(x)(y) for all x, y ∈B. The set of all elements of B for which πφ(x) = 1
is called the kernel of φ, denoted by ker φ. It can be easily shown that ker φ is a subgroup of B, and in [11] it
was proved that if B is non-trivial, then so is ker φ. It is an easy exercise to show that two elements x, y ∈B
are in the same right coset of ker φ in B if and only if πφ(x) = πφ(y); see [10]. In particular, if B is cyclic,
then ker φ is also cyclic and generated by the element |B|/|ker φ|, and so we have πφ(x) = πφ(y) if and only
if x ≡y (mod |B|/|ker φ|). If φ(ker φ) = ker φ, then the restriction of φ to ker φ is clearly an automorphism
of ker φ. While in general a skew morphism does not have to preserve its kernel set-wise, it can be shown that
φ(ker φ) = ker φ if B is abelian; see [10] again.
Recall that every automorphism of B is a skew morphism of B. We say that a skew morphism of B is
proper if it is not an automorphism of B. Equivalently, a skew morphism φ of B is proper if and only if ker φ
is a proper subgroup of B. If ord(φ) = 2, then by definition we have πφ(x) = 1 for all x ∈B, and so φ is an
automorphism. It follows that every proper skew morphism of a finite group has order at least 3.
Let φ be a skew morphism of Zn, and let m be a divisor of n such that for any pair x, y ∈Zn we have x ≡y
(mod m) if and only if φ(x) ≡φ(y) (mod m). Then the skew morphism φ taken modulo m is the permutation

3. Properties of skew morphisms of cyclic 2-groups
In this section we provide some useful properties about skew morphisms of cyclic 2-groups. We start with
the following lemma.
Lemma 3.1. Let φ be a skew morphism of Z2e. Then:
(a) the order of φ is a power of 2;
(b) φ taken modulo 2i is a well-defined skew morphism of Z2i for each i ∈{0, . . . , e};
(c) every subgroup of Z2e is preserved by φ set-wise; and
(d) every power of φ is a skew morphism of Z2e.
Proof. By Theorem 2.3 we know that ord(φ) divides 2eϕ(2e), which is a power of 2, and so (a) follows. We
prove (b) by induction. The claim is clearly true for i = e, and so we may assume that i ≤e −1. Let K = 2e−1
and note that K is contained in every non-trivial subgroup of Z2e. Since ker φ is non-trivial, it follows that
K ∈ker φ. Recall that the restriction of φ to ker φ is an automorphism of ker φ, and since K is the only
non-zero element of ker φ such that K + K = 0, we deduce that φ(K) = K. Hence for every x ∈Z2e we have
φ(x + K) = φ(K + x) = φ(K) + φ(x) = φ(x) + φ(K) = φ(x) + K. It follows, that φ taken modulo K is a
well-defined permutation of Z2e−1 and consequently a skew morphism of Z2e−1. Denote this skew morphism
by φ′. Since i ≤e −1, we may now apply the inductive hypothesis to deduce that φ′ taken modulo 2i is a
well-defined skew morphism of Z2i. Note that φ taken modulo 2i and φ′ taken modulo 2i are clearly the same,
and so (b) follows. Next, we prove (c). Let H be an arbitrary subgroup of Z2e, and let 2i be the smallest
non-trivial element of H. By (b) it follows that φ taken modulo 2i is a skew morphism of Z2i. In particular,
since every skew morphism fixes the identity, we deduce that the set of all elements of Z2e that are divisible by
2i must be preserved set-wise. But these are exactly the elements of H, which proves (c). Finally, to prove (d)
first note that by (a) we know that ord(φ) is a power of 2. Recall that the skew morphism φ derived from φ
is a skew morphism of Zord(φ). This group is a cyclic 2-group, and so by (c) we deduce that every subgroup of
Zord(φ) is preserved by φ set-wise. The rest follows easily by Theorem 2.9.
□
Next, we show that the power function of a skew morphism of a cyclic 2-group must have a specific structure
when taken modulo 2 and modulo 4.
Lemma 3.2. Let φ be a skew morphism of Z2e with power function πφ. Then for each x ∈Z2e we have
πφ(x) ≡1 (mod 2). Moreover, if x is even, then the values πφ(x) are all congruent to 1 modulo 4 and if x is
odd, then the values πφ(x) are either all congruent to 1 modulo 4 or all congruent to 3 modulo 4.
Proof. Let k denote the smallest non-trivial element of ker φ, and let O1 denote the orbit of ⟨φ⟩that contains
1. Note that Theorem 2.4 combined with the second part of Lemma 2.8(b) implies that the length of O1 is k.
Also recall that πφ(x) = πφ(y) if and only if x ≡y (mod k), and so the power function of φ is determined by
the values πφ(0), πφ(1), . . . , πφ(k −1). By Lemma 2.8(b) we find that these values can be identified with O1.
By Lemma 3.1(a) we know that ord(φ) is a power of two, and it follows that φ is a skew morphism of a cyclic
2-group. Then Lemma 3.1(b) implies that φ taken modulo 2 (or 4) is a skew morphism of Z2 (or Z4). Since
the only skew morphism of Z2 is the identity permutation, we deduce that O1 taken modulo 2 is 1-cycle (1),
and it follows that πφ(x) is always odd. The second part follows from the fact that the only skew morphisms
of Z4 are the identity and (1, 3), and hence O1 taken modulo 4 is either a 1-cycle (1) or a 2-cycle (1, 3).
□

7. Remarks
The largest available computational census of skew morphisms of finite cyclic groups, covering all cyclic
groups of order up to 2000, is available at [3]. It includes the values of Skew(2e) for all e ≤10, which are
consistent with Theorem 1.1.
The proof of Theorem 6.2 yields a recursive description of all skew morphisms of Z2e in terms of those of
Z2e−1. Compared to the general recursive characterisation for finite cyclic groups given in [5], this description
is considerably simpler, and crucially, it leads to an enumeration in Theorem 1.1. One natural strategy towards
enumerating skew morphisms of all finite cyclic groups is to first handle cyclic p-groups, and then determine
how Skew(nm) relates to Skew(n) and Skew(m) for coprime n and m.
This was pursued by Kov´acs and
Nedela across two papers. In [22], they showed that if gcd(n, m) = gcd(n, ϕ(m)) = gcd(ϕ(n), m) = 1, where
ϕ denotes Euler’s totient function — we call n and m satisfying this condition ϕ-coprime — then every skew
morphism of Znm decomposes as a direct product of a skew morphism of Zn and a skew morphism of Zm,
giving Skew(nm) = Skew(n) Skew(m). Then in [23], they obtained a complete enumeration of skew morphisms
of Zpe for p odd. Together with the present paper, this settles the enumeration problem for all cyclic p-groups.
The remaining open question is to determine Skew(nm) when n and m are coprime but not ϕ-coprime. The
challenge here is twofold: not every direct product of a skew morphism of Zn and a skew morphism of Zm need
be a skew morphism of Znm, and conversely, there may exist skew morphisms of Znm that do not decompose as
such a product. A resolution of this problem would complete the enumeration of skew morphisms for all finite
cyclic groups.
If n and m are coprime but not ϕ-coprime, then Skew(nm) need not equal Skew(n) Skew(m). A computer
search using [3] shows that for all pairs of coprime positive integers n and m with nm ≤2000, the equality
Skew(nm) = Skew(n) Skew(m) holds if and only if n and m are ϕ-coprime. Moreover, when n and m are not
ϕ-coprime, Skew(nm) almost always strictly exceeds Skew(n) Skew(m). The only exceptions among pairs with
nm ≤2000 are 11 × 25, 11 × 75, 25 × 31, and 25 × 33, in each of which Skew(nm) < Skew(n) Skew(m), with for
instance Skew(275) = 496 < 680 = Skew(11) Skew(25); note that 825 admits two such factorisations, 11 × 75
and 25 × 33. Beyond the data available for nm ≤2000, only partial answers are known in general. A natural
starting point is the case when n = pa and m = qb for distinct primes p < q. Observe that in this setting, n
and m are ϕ-coprime if and only if p does not divide q −1. Some results are known when n and m are not
ϕ-coprime. It was shown in [22] that if n = p and m = q for primes p and q such that p divides q −1, then
Skew(pq) = 2(p −1)(q −1), which equals 2 Skew(p) Skew(q). In [1], the case n = 4 and m = q for an odd prime
q was settled: if q ≡1 (mod 4), then Skew(4q) = 6q −6, which equals 3 Skew(4) Skew(q); if instead q ≡3
(mod 4), then Skew(4q) = 4q −4, which equals 2 Skew(4) Skew(q). Beyond these cases, the problem appears
to be open. Table 1 illustrates the data for small cases, listing values of Skew(nm) for prime powers n > m
with distinct prime bases, where n ≤49 and m ≤27. Each cell contains Skew(nm) with Skew(n) Skew(m) in
parentheses, except when the two values coincide, in which case only one is listed. Such cells are highlighted in
green.
