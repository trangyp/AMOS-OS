Efficient classical simulation has matured to a critical component of the quantum computing
stack, driving hardware validation, algorithm design, and the study of structured quantum dy-
namics. Lie-algebraic simulation (g-sim) is a compelling approach: it replaces exponentially large
Hilbert-space evolution by dynamics in a reduced adjoint space whose dimension is set by the dy-
namical Lie algebra (DLA) of the circuit, enabling efficient simulation whenever the DLA grows
only polynomially with system size. In practice, however, existing applications of g-sim have been
confined to free-fermionic (matchgate) regimes, and it has been unclear how to extend the paradigm
to other structured circuits whose generators may have large Pauli expansions.
Here we enable
Lie-algebraic classical simulation beyond free fermions by identifying additional non-trivial fami-
lies of polynomial-dimensional DLAs and introducing symmetry-adapted basis representations that
make the adjoint space mapping tractable. In particular, we develop an explicit Pauli orbit basis for
permutation-equivariant dynamics, supporting cubic-dimensional algebras despite exponential Pauli
support, and a subspace-adapted (modified) generalized Gell–Mann basis for bounded Hamming-
weight (U(1)-equivariant) dynamics, yielding polynomial costs on fixed excitation sectors. Together
with streamlined routines for free-fermionic Pauli algebras and translation-invariant variants thereof,
these constructions significantly broaden the practical scope of g-sim as a unifying simulation tool
for structured quantum dynamics. Numerical benchmarks confirm favorable preprocessing scaling
and validate large-scale proof-of-concept simulations.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Efficient classical simulation has long served as a use-
ful lens for understanding quantum advantage: identify-
ing restrictions under which quantum dynamics become
classically tractable directly informs our understanding of
the computational power of quantum devices. This per-
spective remains timely today, as classical simulation has
evolved from a foundational probe of quantumness into
an essential companion tool for the practical deployment
of quantum technology.
Today, classical simulation is
crucial to most aspects of the quantum computing stack,
including benchmarking, validation, and verification of
near-term hardware [1–3], designing meaningful quantum
algorithms [4–11], studying structured quantum many-
body dynamics [12–17], data-loading and state prepara-
tion [18–27], efficient measurement and read-out [28–32],
circuit synthesis and quantum compilation [33–35], quan-
tum error mitigation [36–39], and quantum error correc-
tion [40–44]. As classical simulation matures from a the-
oretical complexity guide to a load-bearing tool in actual
end-to-end quantum computing workflows, the criteria
for its success have fundamentally shifted. For practition-
ers relying on these tools, the most practically relevant
boundary is not “which circuits are simulable in prin-
ciple” under asymptotic complexity theory, but rather
which structured computations admit simulation with
useful constant factors and with faithful access to ex-

pectation values that underlie many near-term routines.
Consequently, a pressing need has emerged to make clas-
sical simulation fast, broadly applicable, and accessible
to the masses. In this work, we pursue these three goals
for quantum computations strongly constrained by sym-
metries.
A broad ecosystem of classical simulation paradigms
has emerged, each exploiting a different notion of struc-
ture: the discrete-group structure of the Clifford group
enables stabilizer simulations for low-magic circuits [40],
the transformation to simple non-interacting fermion
models enables simulation of matchgate circuits [45–47],
limited entanglement structure leads to efficient tensor-
network simulations [48, 49], and Hamiltonian stoquas-
ticity enables scalable Monte-Carlo methods [50, 51],
among others [52–57].
While these approaches appear
disparate, most can be reinterpreted through an under-
lying symmetry or restriction that confines dynamics to
a subspace of manageable effective dimension.
Lie-algebraic simulation makes one confining princi-
ple explicit. Early work in Hamiltonian complexity and
quantum control showed that if state preparation, gates,
and observables are restricted to a compact Lie algebra
of dimension polynomial in system size, then relevant
expectation values can be computed efficiently by work-
ing in a faithful low-dimensional representation of that
algebra, irrespective of the exponentially large Hilbert
space [58, 59]. In modern language, the computationally
relevant degrees of freedom live in an invariant operator
subspace whose dimension is governed by the associated
dynamical Lie algebra (DLA) [60].
This viewpoint has gained renewed relevance through

This convergence of ideas motivates a unified simu-
lability narrative: if a VQA is provably free of barren
plateaus, then the very structure used to prove the special
structure of gradients may also confine its dynamics to
a polynomially-sized effective model that admits efficient
classical evaluation. This intuition has been crystallized
in a prominent conjecture relating provable trainability
and efficient classical simulation [76] and has spurred re-
newed interest in Lie-algebraic simulation as both a di-
agnostic and a computational primitive.
A concrete instantiation of Lie-algebraic simulation for
quantum mean values is the g-sim framework introduced
by Goh et al. [60]. At a high level, g-sim replaces evolu-
tion in a 2n-dimensional Hilbert space by evolution in the
dim(g)-dimensional adjoint space induced by the DLA g,
enabling exact expectation values and gradients when-
ever the relevant operator subspace is of polynomial size.
While the evaluation stage is then efficient by construc-
tion, the practical bottleneck is often preprocessing: con-
structing a suitable basis representation of the DLA (or
an invariant subspace) and computing the adjoint data
(structure constants) required for adjoint-space propaga-
tion.
A broad range of practical applications of the g-sim
framework have been proposed and explored, including
warm-starting of variational training [60, 77], privacy
vulnerabilities in QML [78], reducing gradient estima-
tion costs [79], quantum metrology [80, 81], and quan-
tum circuit synthesis [60]. However, at present there are
two intertwined gaps that limit the perceived scope of
Lie-algebraic simulation in practice: First, essentially all
explicit, large-scale demonstrations of quantum circuits
simulated with g-sim to date have been in settings that
are free-fermion equivalents [60, 77, 79]. This has con-
tributed to a common impression that Lie-algebraic sim-
ulation is mainly an alternative formulation of fermionic
linear optics, rather than a distinct paradigm applicable
in additional regimes [82]. Second, even when the DLA
dimension is polynomial, it is unclear a priori whether
preprocessing remains efficient: generators and basis el-
ements may have exponentially large Pauli expansions
such that naive commutator and inner-product primi-
tives may become intractable. This concern has recently
been articulated as a conjectured additional requirement
beyond dim(g) = poly(n), namely that basis elements ad-
mit “slow Pauli expansions” with only polynomial Pauli
support [78].
If true, such a condition would exclude
highly symmetric sum generators (despite their polyno-
mial DLA dimension) from practical Lie-algebraic simu-
lation.
The purpose of this work is to show that Lie-
algebraic simulability is meaningfully broader than the
free-fermion/matchgate regime, and that the correct per-
spective is representation-theoretic: polynomial DLA di-
mension is the structural starting point, but efficient sim-
ulation hinges on choosing a basis adapted to the alge-
braic symmetries of the system. Concretely, we develop
symmetry-adapted operator bases and efficient primitives
that make g-sim preprocessing practical for several fami-
lies of polynomial-dimensional DLAs that go beyond free
fermions.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
a.
Beyond free fermions:
two further polynomial
regimes with explicit bases.
Building on the sharp di-
chotomy for Pauli Lie algebras generated by single Pauli
strings [82–84], we emphasize that this dichotomy does
not extend to general Hermitian generator sets. We iden-
tify and treat two broad classes of non-free-fermionic
polynomial DLAs arising from structured sum gener-
ators: (i) permutation-equivariant (collective) dynam-
ics, whose ambient commutant algebra has cubic dimen-
sion scaling, and (ii) Hamming-weight preserving (U(1)-
equivariant) dynamics restricted to fixed-weight sectors,
whose effective algebra on the relevant subspace has poly-
nomial dimension for bounded weight k and can exceed
the quadratic and cubic regimes.
b.
Symmetry-adapted representations that remove ex-
ponential Pauli support as an obstacle.
For translation-
invariant free-fermion equivalents, we introduce Pauli
cycles as a symmetry-adapted representation that re-
duces commutator costs by exploiting cyclic twirling. For
permutation-invariant systems, we develop the Pauli or-
bit basis and derive efficient inner-product and commuta-
tor primitives that close within the orbit representation
with polynomial resources, despite exponential Pauli ex-
pansions of individual orbit elements. This directly re-
futes the necessity of slow Pauli expansion assumptions
in this setting. For bounded Hamming-weight sectors, we
propose a modified generalized Gell–Mann basis for u(dk)
and provide the analytic commutation relations enabling
efficient structure-constant generation.

Efficient preprocessing with explicit complexity and
benchmark evidence.
Across these regimes, we focus on
the preprocessing bottleneck of g-sim: Lie closure (ba-

worst-case scaling bounds in the relevant representations

PRELIMINARIES
Lie-algebraic methods have long provided a structural
route to classical simulation and exact solvability of re-
stricted quantum dynamics. Early work by Somma et al.
showed that quantum computational models whose gates

B.
Lie-algebraic simulation of expectation values
The Lie-algebraic structure captured by the DLA not
only underpins recent analyses of expressivity and train-
ability of variational circuits, but also enables efficient
classical simulation in regimes where the induced alge-
bra remains “small” (i.e., polynomially growing in sys-
tem size); e.g., the discussion around the trainability–
simulability conjecture in [76].
A particularly general
and practically oriented framework is g-sim [60] which
builds on earlier ideas in quantum control and Hamilto-
nian complexity [58, 59, 101]. We briefly review the basic
mechanism here.
Given an initial state ρin and an observable O, the
simulation task is to evaluate the expectation value af-

arbitrary (irreducible) invariant subspaces of the adjoint

C.
Notions of classical simulability
Throughout this paper, we discuss classical simulabil-
ity of quantum circuits, with a particular emphasis on
Lie-algebraic simulation as outlined above.
Since sev-
eral notions of “simulability” coexist in the literature,
we briefly fix terminology here and explain where our
setting fits.
Consider a uniform family of n-qubit quantum circuits
Un. For a given U ∈Un acting on an input state |ψ0⟩,
followed by a computational-basis measurement, the out-
come distribution is
p(x) = |⟨x|U|ψ0⟩|2,
x ∈{0, 1}n.
(20)
A standard distinction is between strong and weak clas-
sical simulation [102]. We say that Un is strongly simula-
ble if one can compute probabilities of specified measure-
ment events (e.g., p(x) for a given x, or more generally
marginal probabilities) in time polynomial in n (and in
the requested output precision). It is weakly simulable
if one can sample x from the distribution p(x) in time
polynomial in n. Strong simulation is at least as demand-
ing as weak simulation: standard sequential sampling re-
duces weak simulation to computing a polynomial num-
ber of marginal probabilities, and more recent reductions
show alternative routes from probability/amplitude eval-
uation to sampling that can improve constants depend-
ing on the available primitive [103]. In contrast, physical
quantum devices are weak simulators, natively providing
samples from p(x) and thus estimating expectation values
only up to statistical error via repeated measurements.
The notion of Lie-algebraic simulability [59] studied
in this work is most naturally aligned with the strong
simulation task of computing expectation values of the
form (1) exactly (up to classical arithmetic precision) for
specified observables O and gate-evolved input states ρin.
This setting is especially relevant for variational quan-
tum algorithms, where objective functions are expecta-
tion values by design, motivating renewed interest in clas-
sical methods that can (approximately) evaluate such
quantities for certain classes of circuits [60, 104, 105].
Finally, we use the terms “efficient” and “polynomial
time” interchangeably throughout. This should not be
conflated with practical performance, which depends on
constant factors and typical-instance behavior; assessing
these aspects is an additional focus of this work.

The framework of Lie-algebraic simulation [59, 60] (re-
viewed in Section II B) exploits a simple but far-reaching
observation:
in many structured circuit families, the
physically reachable dynamics are confined to a proper
invariant operator subspace whose dimension grows only
polynomially with the system size n. When this happens,
expectation values of the form (1) can be computed by
propagating in a low-dimensional adjoint representation
of the underlying DLA, rather than in the 2n-dimensional
Hilbert space.
This perspective shifts the central question from “how
large is the Hilbert space?” to two algebraic questions:
(i) when does a circuit family generate a DLA g ⊆su(2n)
with dim(g) = O(poly(n)), and (ii) in which basis (or
representation) can the basic preprocessing primitives of
g-sim (i.e., linear-independence checks and commutator
evaluations for computing the adjoint data) be imple-
mented with polynomial resources?
A common baseline for discussing (i) is the Pauli string
regime.
If the generators are individual Pauli strings
(i.e., one considers Pauli Lie algebras in the sense of
Eq. (5)), recent classification results imply a strong di-
chotomy: aside from essentially free-fermionic families
(yielding Θ(n2)-dimensional algebras) and a few degener-
ate low-dimensional cases (commuting or effectively one-
body structures), connected Pauli Lie algebras are typi-
cally exponentially large in n [82–84]. In this setting, effi-
cient classical simulation is already well understood: free-
fermionic circuits admit specialized techniques such as
fermionic linear optics and Wick/Pfaffian methods [45–
47], and g-sim reproduces these as a Lie-algebraic simu-
lation paradigm (cf. Section IV).
However, Pauli string generators are not the generic
case in variational quantum algorithms.
Many prac-

are free-fermion equivalents: although their generators

requires constructing and analyzing a graph on Pauli
terms (and, depending on the case, identifying symmetry
sectors), whereas g-sim only needs Lie-closure primitives
(commutators and linear-independence checks) in what-
ever operator representation one chooses.

VII.
NUMERICAL EXPERIMENTS
We now illustrate the practical use of the Lie-algebraic
simulation framework for poly-DLA systems developed
above through a set of representative numerical exam-
ples. In each subsection, we revisit a concrete application
from the literature whose numerical study was previously
limited to relatively small system sizes by conventional
state-vector simulation, and show how the same setup
can be reproduced and extended to substantially larger
sizes using g-sim together with the algebra-specific prim-
itives introduced in this work.
The goal of these experiments is mainly methodolog-
ical:
they are intended to demonstrate the computa-
tional feasibility of our theory across the three classes
of systems considered in this paper. To this end, we in-
clude one representative example for each setting, namely
a free-fermionic benchmark for the 1D transverse-field
Ising model in Section VII A, a permutation-equivariant
quantum neural network for graph classification in Sec-
tion VII B, and a fixed-Hamming-weight amplitude en-
coder for q-Gaussian state preparation in Section VII C.
Rather than providing exhaustive application studies,
these examples serve as proof-of-principle demonstra-
tions that the Lie-algebraic formalism can be turned into
concrete large-scale simulation routines.
Broader per-
spectives on potentially impactful applications of g-sim
are discussed in Section VIII A. The code for all exper-
iments is included in the accompanying software pack-
age [85]; see the Code Availability statement for details.

marks, large-scale proof-of-concept simulations, and an
open-source software implementation.
Concretely, we showed that g-sim can be made prac-
tical beyond matchgate/free-fermion settings by con-
structing symmetry-adapted representations of the un-
derlying dynamical Lie algebra (or of a suitable in-
variant operator subspace) in which the key prepro-
cessing primitives become efficient.
Across the three
regimes treated in this paper—free-fermionic Pauli al-
gebras and symmetry-restricted free-fermion equivalents,
permutation-invariant algebras, and bounded Hamming-
weight (U(1)-invariant) subspace algebras—we provided
explicit bases, derived efficient commutator and inner-
product primitives, and gave complexity bounds for Lie-
closure and structure-constant generation.
Leveraging
an accompanying software implementation [85], we fur-
ther verified through runtime benchmarks and large-scale
proof-of-concept experiments that in these regimes, pre-
processing is not merely polynomial in principle but also
practical in wall-clock time. This enables adjoint-space
evaluation of mean values, and gradients, at system sizes
far beyond the reach of direct state-vector simulation.
These advances substantially broaden the practical
scope of Lie-algebraic simulation. On the one hand, they
provide the foundation to strengthen and extend exist-
ing applications of g-sim [60, 77–81]. On the other hand,
they open the door to entirely new applications across
the quantum computing stack, in settings where other
classical simulation paradigms have traditionally played
an enabling role [1–44].
Beyond enabling these additional use cases, our results
clarify the role of representation in Lie-algebraic simula-
bility. The main obstacle in moving beyond free fermions
is often not the DLA dimension itself, but rather the cost
of representing basis elements and evaluating commuta-
tors and inner products when naive Pauli expansions are
prohibitive.
The Pauli orbit and modified generalized
Gell–Mann constructions provide explicit counterexam-
ples to the notion that efficient g-sim requires slow Pauli
expansions: although individual generators may have ex-
ponential Pauli support, there exist compact symmetry-
adapted bases in which the adjoint representation can
still be computed with polynomial resources.
In this
sense, choosing a basis aligned with the underlying sym-
metry removes what might otherwise appear to be an
exponential preprocessing barrier.
We expect that the same principle will apply beyond
the examples studied here. Taken together, these findings
motivate the following working conjecture, which we view
as a useful organizing principle for future work.
Conjecture 1 (Working conjecture: Efficient g-sim from
polynomial DLA dimension). Let g ⊆u(2n) be a compact
Lie algebra arising as the dynamical Lie algebra (or an
invariant operator subspace for the adjoint action) of a
family of circuits, with dim(g) = poly(n). Then there ex-
ists a basis/representation of g in which the preprocessing
steps required by g-sim (basis construction and structure
constant computations) can be carried out with resources
polynomial in n and dim(g).
The examples developed here can be viewed as con-
crete evidence for this conjecture in symmetry-driven
regimes: whenever exponential Pauli support obstructs
naive preprocessing, one may instead search for a repre-
sentation aligned with the symmetry responsible for the
polynomial closure.
Establishing a general theorem of
this kind would substantially strengthen the conceptual
status of g-sim as an overarching simulability paradigm.
A.
Outlook
Many exciting directions for future research are evi-
dent. As detailed in Section I, efficient classical simula-
tion has long been a key enabler of a broad range of tools
across the quantum computing stack [1–44], with appli-
cations ranging from hardware verification and bench-
marking to state preparation and measurement. With
the new Lie-algebraic methods for classical simulation
in hand, it is natural to revisit old problems and ask


## Page 22


22
where they can be strengthened, generalized, or made
more scalable through g-sim, particularly in light of the
additional symmetry classes treated here.
Naturally, this broadening of scope and practicality of
g-sim stands to enhance its existing applications.
For
example, the HELIA protocol of Ref. [79] uses g-sim to
reduce variational circuit training costs, but so far this
has only been explored for models with free-fermionic
components.
The methods developed here widen the
class of problems for which such savings may be real-
ized. Similarly, g-sim has recently been shown to under-
pin privacy breaches in quantum machine learning [78];
hence, extending the practical scope of g-sim to addi-
tional symmetry-restricted models has direct implica-
tions for structured QML architectures [115, 133, 134].
Practically, the ability to compute exact mean val-
ues and gradients at larger system sizes also enables a
different mode of investigation: using g-sim as a diag-
nostic tool to probe scaling phenomena in variational
workflows. This includes testing whether claimed barren-
plateau-free architectures induce polynomial DLAs (or
reduce to known solvable families), studying robustness
to finite-shot noise, and identifying effective invariants
or conserved quantities via Lie closure without requiring
a bespoke mapping (e.g. the frustration-graph construc-
tions used to certify free-fermion solvability [107]).
On the theoretical side, it is natural to follow the
thread of Conjecture 1 and ask how broadly symmetry-
adapted
basis
constructions
extend.
Beyond
Sn-
equivariance and bounded Hamming-weight sectors, one
may consider other group actions (e.g. translation, di-
hedral symmetry, stabilizer-like symmetries, or combina-
tions of locality and global constraints) and ask which
induce polynomial-dimensional DLAs with efficient com-
mutator primitives. A related question is whether one
can systematically characterize polynomially growing
DLAs arising from mixed generator types (i.e., single
Pauli strings, structured sums, and collective terms) and
determine when their closure escapes the free-fermion
and permutation-equivariant regimes.
Finally, the symmetry-adapted preprocessing devel-
oped here does not only enable fast simulation via g-sim
but also provides explicit adjoint representations for
structured systems.
This opens the possibility of us-
ing adjoint-space data directly for other tasks, such as
analyzing controllability within constrained sectors, de-
signing structured ans¨atze with targeted expressivity, or
building fast routines for correlators and response quan-
tities.
Overall, our work serves as evidence that g-sim is not
merely a reformulation of free-fermionic methods, but a
flexible framework with substantially broader applicabil-
ity. We sincerely hope that the tools introduced in this
work will provide a strong foundation for the commu-
nity to continue working on creative applications of these
methods.
