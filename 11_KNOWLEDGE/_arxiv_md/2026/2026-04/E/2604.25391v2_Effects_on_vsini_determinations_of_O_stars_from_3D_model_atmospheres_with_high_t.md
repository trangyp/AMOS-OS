ABSTRACT
Context. When studying massive stars and their life cycles, rotation plays a key role. Hence, understanding the rotation of these stars
is crucial when determining their properties, or for constraining evolutionary models.
Aims. We examined the reliability of the standard methods to derive projected rotation speeds 3 sin i from photospheric spectra of hot
massive stars in the presence of high turbulent velocities.
Methods. We included rotation in the spectral synthesis of O stars by means of three-dimensional (3D) model atmospheres showing
significant photospheric turbulent velocities. We then used these as mock observations to back-test the Fourier transform (FT) and
goodness-of-fit (GOF) methods commonly used for empirical determination of 3 sin i when the turbulent velocity field is not known.
Results. When the expected 3 sin i > 3macro, with 3macro the macroturbulent velocity, FT 3 sin i determinations (most of the time)
give reasonable results. However, if 3 sin i < 3macro the method is no longer reliable. Results from the GOF method show that if one
parameter is not significantly larger than the other, empirical best-fit values may be located in a large region of the 3 sin i – 3macro
parameter space, independent of the true values. The degenerate region closely follows the shape of the empirical formula found by
Howarth et al. (2007), C =

1. Introduction
The influence of rotation on massive stars already begins during
the initial collapse of a rotating molecular cloud (Bodenheimer
1995; Schönke, J. & Tscharnuter, W. M. 2011; Dutta, Jayanta
2016), and lasts up to their brutal deaths as a core-collapse su-
pernova or hypernova (Nakamura et al. 2014). During its life as
a main-sequence star, and later on during the evolved stages, the
effects of rotation cannot be ignored (Meynet & Maeder 2000;
Maeder & Meynet 2012).
Even though rotation influences the entire life cycle of a mas-
sive star, we cannot directly measure the rotation rate in most
cases; an exception to this is a small number of strongly mag-
netic massive O stars (e.g. Sundqvist et al. 2013a). Hence, the
projected stellar rotation, 3 sin i, is commonly deduced (i be-
ing the inclination angle, in this work sometimes also referred
to as α) from the observed broadened line spectrum. This ap-
proach, however, has one primary issue, which is the potential
degeneracy between the rotational line broadening and the other
broadening processes in the line-formation regions. From obser-
vations of absorption lines in O stars, it has long been known
that a wide range of velocities must be present in their atmo-
spheres (Slettebak 1956; Conti & Ebbets 1977). The lines are
much broader (typical velocity dispersions σV ∼50 km/s) than
implied by thermal motions, and the observed line shapes can-
not be explained by stellar rotation alone (Conti & Ebbets 1977;
Howarth et al. 1997; Ryans et al. 2002; Simón-Díaz, S. & Her-
rero, A. 2007; Lefever, K. et al. 2007; Simón-Díaz et al. 2010;
Serebriakova et al. 2024). To overcome the issue of separat-
ing rotational broadening from this ‘turbulent’ contributor, em-
pirical techniques such as the Fourier transform (FT) method,
which looks for the first zero in the Fourier power spectrum to
determine 3 sin i (see Sect. 3.1), and the goodness-of-fit (GOF)
method (see Sect. 3.2), which uses the different shapes of the
rotational and turbulent broadening to find 3 sin i through a fit-
ting algorithm, have been utilised (Gray 2005; Simón-Díaz, S.
& Herrero, A. 2007). However, Sundqvist et al. (2013b) showed
that these methods may become unreliable at 3 sin i <∼50 km/s
due to the degeneracy of the broadening components (see also
Howarth et al. 2007; Markova et al. 2014).
Recent spectral synthesis directly from 3D model atmo-
spheres (Schultz et al. 2023; Delbroek et al. 2025) shows that
this ‘extra broadening’ indeed is naturally reproduced by the
high turbulent velocities present in such simulations of O-star
photospheres.1 These models, however, did not include rotation
and thus the question regarding separation between turbulent and
rotational broadening could not be addressed. In this letter we in-

2. Methods
The fundamental parameters for the O-star model studied in this
work can be found in Table B1. For the details and methodol-
ogy of the synthetic line profile calculations, more specifically,
the approximate non-local thermodynamic equilibrium2 and 3D
radiative transfer techniques (synthetic spectra computed from a
formal solution using long characteristics in a cylindrical coordi-
nate system, pointed at the observer) and the unified model atmo-
sphere with wind models, we refer to Delbroek et al. 2025. We
computed the line profiles directly from the 3D simulations, fo-
cusing on the typical optical absorption lines used to infer 3 sin i
and macroturbulence in O stars. The 3D RHD (radiation hydro-
dynamics) model has been mapped out to cover the complete
atmosphere and wind volume using the same technique as in
Delbroek et al. (2025)3. We added rotation to our 3D models
utilising azimuthal angular momentum conservation outside a
fixed inner boundary radius with rotation 3rot,in (see Appendix A,
here referred to as the inner boundary rotation method). We note
that this approach neglects dynamical feedback effects from the
rotation upon the atmospheric density and temperature structure,
which should be a reasonable approximation as long as the star is
not rotating too rapidly (e.g., Hillier et al. 2012; Sundqvist et al.
2012). We used radial averages in the static limit to mimic what
would come out of an equivalent 1D model photosphere anal-
ysis; to reproduce the equivalent widths of the 3D-based lines,
such 1D-based lines must also introduce microturbulence. For
the O III 5594 Å in focus here, one needs 3mic = 15 km/s, (Del-
broek et al. 2025). With these new sets of line profiles, we then
back-tested to see if we could find the correct 3 sin i values using
the FT and GOF methods. More specifically, we added rotation
to one 3D model and calculated the O III 5594 Å line flux pro-
file for different inclination angles. We then used these profiles
as mock observations to derive 3 sin i and macroturbulence using
the standard GOF and FT methods. Two remarks that should be
made are the following: i) In the 3D synthetic line profiles we
do not have the same noise levels as one would expect from real
observations; ii) The 3D line profiles are not necessarily sym-
metric, and can have irregularities. This cannot be reproduced
by the 1D models and methods, which may impact the results
somewhat.

3.1. Fourier transform 3 sin i determination
The FT 3 sin i results are shown in Fig. 4. The determination was
also done for the pole-on view (not included in the figure), for
which all determinations gave 3 sin i >∼30 km/s. The first thing
to note is that the FT method always gives a non-zero result, in-
dicating that fiducial minima are created by the presence of the
high turbulent velocities. Figure 4a and 4b show that as long as
3 sin i >∼3macro, the FT method produces reasonable results. How-
ever, even in this range there is significant scatter, such that some
best fits are less than satisfactory (e.g. top panel with i = 60◦).
If 3 sin i < 3macro, the measurements become much less reliable.
This is seen directly in Fig. 4c, where all measurements except
at the equator overestimate 3 sin i by at least 40 percent and up
to a factor of five, depending on which inclination angle is con-
sidered. This shows that one must be very careful when using
this method, especially when the macroturbulent velocity is not
known in advance, as also visible in Fig. 3 (in particular, the
right-hand side of bottom panel).

4. Conclusions
Both the FT and GOF methods have severe limitations for de-
termining 3 sin i in the presence of high atmospheric turbulent
velocities. If 3 sin i is not significantly higher than 3macro, an ac-
curate determination of the projected rotation rate is only possi-
ble if 3macro can be calibrated independently with sufficient con-
fidence (for example from 3D atmosphere simulations). If not
known independently, the macroturbulent velocity can only be
empirically determined if it dominates the broadening. This may
significantly alter previous determinations of observed rotation
rates for O stars, and a range of previous results must likely be re-
analysed and re-interpreted. This includes fiducial correlations
between 3 sin i and 3macro (Simón-Díaz et al. 2017) as well as the
statistical distribution of observed rotation speeds in O-star pop-
ulations (Ramírez-Agudelo et al. 2013).
