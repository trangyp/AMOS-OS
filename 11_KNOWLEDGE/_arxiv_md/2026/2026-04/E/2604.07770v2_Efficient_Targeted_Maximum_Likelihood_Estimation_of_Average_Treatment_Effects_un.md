Abstract: We study targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) of the average treat-
ment effect in a semiparametric regression model whose mean function is indexed by a
finite-dimensional parameter, while the additive error distribution is left unspecified apart
from mild regularity conditions and independence from treatment and baseline covariates.
The paper addresses a genuinely new causal problem: because the target depends on both
the regression parameter and the unrestricted marginal law of the covariates, the regression-
efficient score must be converted into a causal efficient influence function, semiparametric
efficiency bound, and targeting step for the average treatment effect itself. We derive those
objects, construct a cross-fitted TMLE, and establish asymptotic linearity and efficiency. In
simulations, the proposed estimator is most effective when the mean is correctly structured
but the error law is heavy-tailed or skewed. In these settings, it yields smaller root mean
squared error and shorter intervals than Gaussian working-model inference, a standard aug-
mented inverse-probability-weighted estimator, Bayesian additive regression trees, and a
forest-based TMLE benchmark. Misspecification experiments are included to clarify the
scope of the method rather than to claim universal superiority under broad mean-model
failure.

Keywords and phrases: average treatment effect, cross-fitting, efficient influence function,
semiparametric causal inference, semiparametric efficiency, targeted maximum likelihood
estimation, unknown error distribution.
1. Introduction
Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE; van der Laan and Rubin, 2006; van der Laan
and Rose, 2011; Gruber and van der Laan, 2012) is often developed in the fully nonparametric
causal model, where the outcome mean is treated as an unrestricted function of treatment and
baseline covariates. Here we consider a narrower question: what should the targeting step look
like when the outcome mean is deliberately modeled through a low-dimensional structure and
the main source of modeling uncertainty lies instead in the error distribution? In that setting,
the average treatment effect (ATE) remains the target, but the relevant fluctuation model is
inherited from efficient regression theory rather than from a generic nonparametric outcome
regression.
We study efficient average treatment effect estimation in a semiparametric regression sub-
model with unknown error distribution. The model assumes that the mean function has known
functional form up to a finite-dimensional parameter and that the remaining outcome varia-
tion can be represented by an additive error independent of treatment and baseline covariates.
No parametric restriction is imposed on the marginal distribution of the baseline covariates,

scientific structure lies in the mean function and residual variation is well described by a com-
mon, potentially non-Gaussian error law. Such settings arise naturally in applications where

heavy tailed, or otherwise irregular. In return for these restrictions, the nuisance tangent space
is smaller than in the fully nonparametric causal model.
A second motivation is comparative rather than purely theoretical. Modern nonparametric
regressors such as Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) can adapt automatically to
nonlinear mean structure, but they use the same flexibility to capture systematic trends and
irregular noise shape. When the dominant scientific structure is low-dimensional and most
complexity enters through heavy tails, skewness, or multimodality of the outcome, a fully
adaptive mean model can spend statistical effort on features that are better viewed as part
of the error law. The framework studied here separates these roles by constraining the mean
while leaving the residual distribution unrestricted.
The same comparative point applies to doubly robust estimators. AIPW remains one of the
most widely used doubly robust estimators for the ATE and is therefore a natural benchmark
in modern causal inference. In theory, it remains consistent if either the propensity score or the
outcome regression is consistently estimated. In practice, however, the finite-sample behavior
can still be poor when both nuisance components are hard to estimate accurately or when one
working model is materially misspecified, especially under skewed or heavy-tailed outcomes.
This is exactly the regime in which a structured semiparametric alternative can gain stability
by replacing the unrestricted outcome nuisance with a low-dimensional mean model while
leaving the error law unrestricted.
This perspective also clarifies how the paper should be read. We are not proposing a
universally robust alternative to the standard nonparametric ATE model. Rather, we study what
is gained when a scientifically interpretable mean structure is taken seriously and combined
with semiparametric treatment of the outcome error distribution. The resulting estimator
is most attractive when a low-dimensional regression model is substantively plausible, but
Gaussian or other simple parametric error assumptions are not.
At the technical level, the paper goes materially beyond a causal relabeling of Kim (2023).
That paper studies efficient estimation of regression parameters when the mean structure is
finite dimensional but the error shape is unknown. Our contribution lies in extending this
theory into a causal estimand framework by carrying out a novel semiparametric derivation
for the ATE itself. Since the average treatment effect is a smooth functional of both the
regression parameter and the unrestricted marginal distribution of W, the efficient score from
Kim (2023) becomes only one component of the causal efficient influence function. A new
marginal-W term must be introduced, the regression-efficient direction must be composed
with the gradient of the causal functional, and the resulting object must be operationalized as
a targeted substitution estimator. Put differently, the present paper does not merely cite that
regression theory; it changes the target from a regression coefficient to a causal functional and
derives a new EIF, efficiency bound, and TMLE from that underlying geometry.

2.3. Interpretation and scope of the model
Model (1) is best interpreted as a location-shift representation after any substantively appro-
priate outcome transformation. In many empirical settings, a transformation such as log(𝑌+𝑐)
or asinh(𝑌) makes the additivity assumption more plausible while preserving a meaningful
treatment contrast. The restriction 𝜀⊥(𝐴, W) therefore should not be read as claiming that
the raw outcome must be symmetric or homoscedastic; rather, it says that, after removing
the systematic component 𝑚(𝐴, W; 𝜷0), the remaining uncertainty is described by a common
error law whose shape need not be specified parametrically.
The mean model itself can still be fairly rich. The function 𝑚(𝑎, w; 𝜷) may contain spline
basis terms, prespecified nonlinear transformations, or domain-informed treatment-covariate
interactions, provided the parameter of interest remains finite dimensional. What distinguishes
the present framework from the standard nonparametric causal model is not linearity per se,
but the fact that the treatment effect is characterized by a low-dimensional regression parameter
rather than an unrestricted surface 𝜇0(𝑎, w). This dimensionality reduction is what makes the
resulting efficiency bound sharper and the corresponding √𝑛-rate semiparametric analysis
practically informative when the structured mean representation is supported by domain
knowledge.
The efficiency gains of this structure are predicated on the validity of the structural assump-
tions. If important treatment heterogeneity enters through unmodeled high-order interactions,
or if the residual scale or shape changes materially across treatment arms or covariate strata
even after the mean model is fitted, then the efficiency calculations below no longer correspond

This section derives the efficient influence function (EIF) for Ψ(𝑃0) under model (1). We build
on semiparametric efficiency theory for regression with independent errors and unknown error
distribution (see Bickel et al., 1998; van der Vaart, 1998; Tsiatis, 2006; Kim, 2023).
3.1. Regression-efficiency foundation for the causal derivation
The analysis begins with the semiparametric regression problem studied by Kim (2023). In
that setting, one observes (X,𝑌) satisfying
𝑌= 𝑚(X; 𝜷0) + 𝜀,

changes. We no longer target 𝜷0 itself. Instead, the parameter of interest is

6.4. Baseline and imbalanced-assignment results
The most revealing comparison is between the proposed estimator and BART in the second and
third regimes. There the mean model is correctly specified, so the structured regression signal
is genuinely low-dimensional, but the outcome noise is heavy-tailed or skewed, so Gaussian
likelihood-based procedures are poorly calibrated. In this regime, the theory developed above
predicts that the proposed estimator should translate its efficient regression correction into a
more precise causal estimator than a fully adaptive mean estimator that uses model flexibility
to absorb both signal and noise shape.
These results sharpen the intended comparison. In the Gaussian benchmark, Gaussian
OLS has the smallest RMSE (0.098), while the proposed estimator remains close (0.106)
and now achieves near-nominal coverage under repeated cross-fitting. In the heavy-tailed and
skewed-mixture regimes, where the mean model is correct but the error law is distinctly non-
Gaussian, the proposed estimator has the smallest empirical standard deviation, the smallest
RMSE, and the narrowest intervals in Table 1. The gain over BART is especially striking in
the skewed-mixture design, where the RMSE drops from 0.125 to 0.069, a reduction of nearly
one half, and the average interval width drops from 0.532 to 0.265, again by roughly a factor
of two. The mild misspecified-mean regime in Table 1 does not overturn this ranking: the
proposed estimator still has the smallest RMSE and the narrowest intervals, suggesting that in
this design the efficiency gain from error-law adaptation is not erased by a modest departure
from the working mean.

6.5. Overall interpretation
Tables 1 and 2, together with the supplementary graphical displays and auxiliary sample-
size tables, therefore support the main semiparametric message of the paper. When the
dominant treatment-outcome signal is genuinely low-dimensional and the main difficulty
lies in the error distribution, the proposed targeted estimator can turn that structure into a
substantial precision gain relative to Gaussian likelihood analysis, a flexible BART benchmark,
a standard doubly robust competitor, and even a forest-based TMLE benchmark. In that sense,
the proposed TMLE preserves the interpretability of a low-dimensional mean model while
remaining adaptive to heavy-tailed and skewed outcomes. With repeated cross-fitting, the
finite-sample calibration in the baseline design also becomes much more satisfactory: coverage
for the proposed estimator now lies between 0.942 and 0.952 across the four regimes, rather
than falling systematically below nominal. The mild misspecified-mean regime in Table 1
reinforces this interpretation rather than breaking it: the proposed estimator remains the
most precise method in that design, so the numerical advantage is not driven solely by
exact mean specification. The imbalanced-assignment comparison shows that this advantage
does not disappear when treatment assignment becomes meaningfully less balanced and
AIPW becomes the more natural causal benchmark. We therefore interpret the simulation
as evidence of both a real efficiency gain and a practical route to better calibrated inference,
not as a claim of universal superiority under automatic model discovery. Supplementary

8. Discussion
We have developed a theory for efficient ATE estimation under a semiparametric regression
model with a structured mean function and a common unknown error distribution. The main
conceptual message is that one need not choose between a rigid parametric error model and
a fully nonparametric outcome regression. By restricting only the component of the model
that is scientifically interpretable, namely the mean function, and leaving the residual law
unrestricted, one can obtain an estimator that remains adaptive to heavy-tailed or asymmetric
outcomes while still exploiting low-dimensional structure for efficiency.
These efficiency gains are meaningful precisely because the method is tied to a substantive
model choice. The proposed procedure is most convincing when the analyst can defend a
stable low-dimensional representation of the mean and when simple residual diagnostics do
not contradict a common-error approximation after transformation. In practice, it is therefore
useful to compare a small sequence of nested mean specifications and to examine residual
distributions across treatment strata or coarse covariate groupings. That is why the earlier
misspecification regime and the BART comparison are both essential complements. In this
sense, the procedure is best viewed as a precision-optimized specialist within a structured
semiparametric class, not as a general-purpose black-box replacement for every problem. The
comparison with BART is therefore substantively useful: it shows whether the additional
structure is buying precision without materially distorting the estimated effect, and it provides
a natural flexible reference for diagnostics and sensitivity analysis. It also clarifies the intended
scope of the method: the point is not uniform superiority in every large sample, but a precision
gain in the regimes where low-dimensional mean structure is real and the main challenge lies
in the error distribution. Across those regimes, the proposed TMLE preserves interpretability
while often improving RMSE and interval calibration relative to more flexible learners. In
our simulations, this advantage is visible not only against AIPW but also against BART and
a forest-based TMLE benchmark, all of which are less precise in the intended irregular-
error regime. Taken together, the numerical evidence supports a sharper conclusion: when
the structured mean model is substantively credible and the main complication lies in the
error law, the proposed TMLE is typically more precise than AIPW, BART, and the forest-
based TMLE benchmark considered here. A supplementary saturation-model experiment
strengthens this point by showing that the same qualitative ranking persists even when all
structured competitors are given the same practical natural-spline basis rather than the true
nonlinear form. That experiment is best read as a targeted stress test of whether the relative
precision ranking survives a mild nonlinear perturbation within the same common-error
model class; the spline approximation is deliberately mild, and the BART result is included as a
flexible reference rather than as a level-playing-field basis comparison. For practical inference,
our current evidence suggests that repeated cross-fitting is the most natural calibration device
within this framework, with percentile bootstrap serving as a useful supplementary sensitivity
analysis rather than as the default implementation.
The comparison with AIPW is also instructive. Because AIPW remains one of the main
standard benchmarks for ATE estimation, it is important to understand when its double-robust
protection is not enough. Double robustness is an important asymptotic safeguard, but it is not
a guarantee of good finite-sample behavior when the propensity score and outcome regression
are both difficult to estimate stably. Our simulations suggest that, in the regimes most relevant

here, the proposed TMLE can be materially more precise than AIPW precisely because it

mean while retaining semiparametric flexibility for the error distribution.
Seen from this angle, the paper contributes to a broader program initiated by Kim (2023):
semiparametric efficiency theory can be made substantially more useful in practice when mean
structure and error shape are treated as distinct statistical entities. The present article shows
that this principle extends to causal inference, but it does so through a genuinely new causal
construction rather than through a literal adaptation of the earlier regression theory. What
changes is not only the notation but the target itself: the regression score must be converted
into an influence function and a targeting step for an ATE functional that also depends on the
marginal distribution of W. The central novelty is therefore a causal TMLE construction with
its own EIF, efficiency bound, and targeting equation, built upon, yet fundamentally distinct
from, the earlier regression-efficiency theory.
Several extensions merit further study. The same logic should apply to related causal
targets such as the average treatment effect in the treated, transported treatment effects,
or policy contrasts defined through low-dimensional regression parameters. It would also
be valuable to develop heteroscedasticity-robust semiparametric targeting that relaxes the
common-error assumption toward covariate-dependent scale or more general semiparametric
location-scale models. Another natural direction would be a hybrid adaptive-structured TMLE
that replaces the low-dimensional working mean by adaptive learners such as BART or by
higher-dimensional specifications equipped with sparsity-inducing penalties, but that would
require a different efficiency analysis from the one developed here. Finally, a fuller empirical
and simulation study would clarify how the efficiency gains predicted by the present theory
manifest themselves in realistic sample sizes and under moderate misspecification.
