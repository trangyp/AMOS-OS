ECLIPSES ON ZIPPERS
KYEONGRO KIM
Abstract. Calegari and Loukidou introduced zippers, consisting of a disjoint pair of invariant
real trees in the boundary of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold group, which ensure the existence
of a universal circle. We study the action of π1(M) on a minimal zipper and prove a fixed point
dichotomy: every nontrivial element either fixes a unique point in each tree or acts freely on
both. This answers a question of Calegari and Loukidou. As a consequence, there exists an
element with exactly one fixed point in each tree.
1. Introduction
Motivated by Thurston’s vision of a universal circle [Thu22], Calegari–Dunfield proved the
universal circle theorem [CD03]. Since then, the theory has been extensively developed and
applied to a wide range of topological and geometric structures on 3-manifolds, including taut
foliations, quasi-geodesic and pseudo-Anosov flows, and veering triangulations. It now provides
a common framework connecting these structures.
Recently, Calegari and Loukidou [CL26] introduced a remarkably simple topological notion,
called zippers, which gives a sufficient condition for the existence of a universal circle: for a
closed hyperbolic 3-manifold M, a zipper is a pair Z± of disjoint, non-empty, path-connected
subsets of the Gromov boundary S2
∞= ∂∞π1(M) that are invariant under the action of π1(M).
The main result of this paper is the following:
Theorem A (Zipper Fixed Point Dichotomy). Let Z± be a minimal zipper for a closed hyperbolic
3-manifold M. Then for every g ∈π1(M) ∖{1}, one of the following holds:
●(hyperbolic-like) g acts freely on both Z±;
●(pseudo-Anosov-like) g fixes a unique point in each of Z±.
This provides an affirmative answer to the following question posed by Calegari and Loukidou
in the first version of their arXiv paper [CL24] (see Remark 2.27 in [CL26]):
Question 1.1 (Remark 2.21 in [CL24]). It is natural to expect that fixed points of group
elements play a key role in understanding the geometry and topology of zippers, and it would
be worthwhile to develop a clearer picture of this relationship. For instance: is it possible that
some element g ∈π1(M) has both fixed points in Z+? Or can there exist an element g ∈π1(M)
with one fixed point in Z+ and the other lying in neither of Z±?
1.1. Calegari’s Closed Orbit Conjecture. In [Fra13, Fra18], Frankel proved Calegari’s closed
orbit conjecture: every quasigeodesic flow on a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold M admits a closed
orbit. The key idea is to pass to the flowspace P, the orbit space of the lifted flow on ̃
M ≃H3.
This is a topological plane equipped with continuous endpoint maps e±∶P →S2
∞assigning to
each flowline its forward and backward limit points (see [Cal06]). The fibers of e± define two
decompositions P ± of P, and in this setting, closed orbits correspond to points of P fixed by
nontrivial elements of π1(M).
Calegari and Loukidou [CL26, Conjecture 3.10] conjectured that, given a minimal zipper Z±
for M, there exists a quasigeodesic pseudo-Anosov flow on M without perfect fits whose endpoint
maps satisfy e±(P ±) = Z±. In this context, it is natural to remark the following corollary:
Theorem B (Existence of a pseudo-Anosov-like element). Let Z± be a minimal zipper for M.
Then there exists g ∈π1(M) that fixes a unique point in each of Z±.

2.1. Topology on R-trees. Let T be a (topological) real tree. A subset γ of T is an arc,
ray or line in T if γ is the image of a continuous injective map f from [0,1], [0,1) or (0,1),
respectively. We call f an orientation of γ. If γ is a ray, then there is a unique orientation up to
parametrization. Note that an orientation f induces a natural linear order ≤on γ under which
f is increasing.
A subset γ of T is called a segment in T if γ is arc, ray or line. The interior Intγ of γ
is defined as the maximal line, contained in γ. We write ∂γ = γ ∖Intγ and call its elements
boundary points of γ. In particular, γ is said to be oriented if an linear order is fixed, and proper
if an associated orientation f is proper. We denote by [x,y] the unique arc between x and y in
T if x ≠y or the point {x} if x = y. We also define (x,y] = [x,y] ∖{x}, [x,y) = [x,y] ∖{y}, and
(x,y) = [x,y] ∖{x,y}.

proper with respect to ℓ. If r is a ray in T, then for any x ∈Intr, the subray r ∖[∂r,x) is called
the truncated ray of r at x.
For a point p in T, we define Tp as the collection of components of T ∖{p}. Each element of
Tp is called a branch at p. The point p is called
●a hard end if ∣Tp∣= 1;

2.2. Jordan segments. A Jordan arc in S2 is the image of an injective continuous map [0,1] →
S2, and a Jordan curve is the image of an injective continuous map S1 →S2. A Jordan segment
(resp. Jordan ray, resp. Jordan line) is a segment (resp. ray, resp. line) in a Jordan arc, viewed
as a real tree.
By the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem, every Jordan curve in S2 is carried by an orientation-
preserving homeomorphism of S2 to the equator. Moreover, there are no wild Jordan arcs in S2:
every Jordan arc is contained in a Jordan curve (see [New51, Chapter VI, Theorem 14.5] for an
elementary proof). It follows that every Jordan arc in S2 is carried by an orientation-preserving
homeomorphism of S2 to a subarc of the equator.
Consequently, every interior point x of a Jordan segment S has a neighborhood U that is a
Jordan domain, together with an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
φ∶(R2,R × {0}) →(U,U ∩S).
We call such a U a flat neighborhood of x relative to S. If S, regarded as a real tree, is oriented,
then φ may be chosen to be increasing on R × {0}. In that case, the sets φ(R × [0,∞)) and
φ(R × (−∞,0]) are called the left and right neighborhoods of x relative to S, and their interiors
the left and right open neighborhoods of x relative to S.
Now let J be a Jordan curve, and let D be a complementary Jordan domain of J . If S is a
Jordan segment, its closure S in S2 is a Jordan arc. Write
dS ∶= S ∖IntS.
Then dS consists of two points. We say that S crosses J through D if
S ∩J = dS
and
IntS ⊂D.

If S is oriented and crosses D, then S separates D into two Jordan domains. We denote them
by DL(S) and DR(S), and call them the left and right Jordan domains of S in D.

Proposition 2.2. If a Jordan curve J separates the endpoints of a Jordan arc A, then J ∩A ≠∅.

2.4. Landing rays. Let Z± be a zipper for M. Since M is closed and hyperbolic, every element
of G is loxodromic in PSL2(C). For g ∈G, write a(g) and r(g) for the attracting and repelling

A ray in Zα, where α ∈{+,−}, is called a landing ray if its image in S2
∞admits a compact-

3. Eclipses
Let Z± be a minimal zipper for M. To prove Theorem A, it suffices, by symmetry, to show
that there is no element g ∈G with r(g) ∈Z+ and a(g) ∉Z−. As indicated in Section 1.2, such an
element would determine a certain configuration in Z±, which we call an eclipse. In this section
we explain how an eclipse arises and establish its basic properties. In the following sections we
show that the existence of an eclipse would force an absurd configuration of points; in this sense,
the eclipse is only an illusion. This completes the proof of Theorem A.
3.1. Prongs and Fences. For an element g in G, a g-invariant ray in a zipper is called a
g-prong. When exactly one of Z± contains a fixed point p of an element g, there exists at most
one g-prong that starts at p:
Proposition 3.1 (Unique invariant prong). Let Z± be a zipper for M. Suppose that g ∈G fixes
a point p ∈Z+ and that Fix(g) ∩Z−= ∅. Then at most one g-prong in Z+ starts at p and lands
at the other fixed point q of g.
Proof. Suppose that there are two distinct g-prongs r1 and r2 in Z+ starting at p. Since both
land at q, the set r1 ∪r2 ∪{q} is a Jordan curve J ; see Lemma 2.5. Since the G-action on S2
∞
is minimal, Z−is dense in S2
∞. Hence we may choose points x,y ∈Z−lying in different Jordan
domains of J . The arc [x,y] ⊂Z−must then meet J . By the disjointness of Z+ and Z−, the
intersection point can only be q. Thus q ∈Z−, and since q ∈Fix(g), this gives q ∈Fix(g) ∩Z−,
contrary to the assumption.
□
A g-invariant prong gives rise to an g-invariant fence of Z±:
Proposition 3.2 (Invariant fence). Let Z± be a zipper for M. Assume that g ∈G acts freely
on Z−. If g admits a g-prong r in Z+, then the union of r, the axis ℓof g in Z−, and Fix(g) is

Proof. The description of J follows immediately from Theorem 2.1 and Lemma 2.5.
For the final statement, let B be a branch at a fixed point p ∈Z+ such that B ∩r = ∅. If
g(B) = B, then Proposition 2.6 gives a g-prong γ ⊂B ∪{p} starting at p. Since r is another
