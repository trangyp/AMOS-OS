Abstract. Let R be a finite commutative ring with identity, and let P be a
proper prime ideal of R. The prime ideal graph ΓP (R) has vertex set of R \ {0},
where two distinct vertices x and y are adjacent if and only if xy ∈P.
We
prove that ΓP (R) ∼= K|P |−1 ∨K|R|−|P |, so prime ideal graphs form a ring-induced
family of complete split graphs. Using this description, we determine the minimal
vertex covers and obtain an irredundant primary decomposition of the edge ideal
I(ΓP (R)). For every n ≥1, we characterize the minimal monomial generators of
the ordinary power I(ΓP (R))n: a monomial xαyβ belongs to G(I(ΓP (R))n) if and
only if |α| + |β| = 2n, |β| ≤n, and 0 ≤αi ≤n for all i. Consequently, we derive
a closed formula for µ(I(ΓP (R))n). We also prove that every ordinary power is
polymatroidal and hence has linear quotients and a 2n−linear resolution. Finally,
we interpret µ(I(ΓP (R))n) as the Hilbert function of the special fiber ring and
compute the analytic spread of I(ΓP (R)).
Keywords: Prime ideal graph, edge ideal, ordinary powers, polymatroidal ideal,
analytic spread.

1. Introduction
Edge ideals provide one of the most effective ways to translate combinatorial
information from a graph into algebraic information about a monomial ideal. Given
a finite simple graph G on vertices {z1, · · · , zr}, its edge ideal is
I(G) = (zizj : {zi, zj} ∈E(G)) ⊆k[z1, · · · , zr].
This construction, studied systematically by Villarreal, Simis, Vasconcelos, and oth-
ers, has become a central object in combinatorial commutative algebra because many
algebraic invariants of I(G) reflect structural features of the graph G [20, 21, 18, 15].
For example, minimal vertex covers describe the minimal primes of I(G), while ho-
mological invariants such as regularity, projective dimension, Betti numbers, and
linear resolutions are often controlled by the combinatorics of G [6, 4, 2, 10]. The
study of powers of edge ideals is especially important. Ordinary powers I(G)n encode
increasingly refined information about the interaction among edges of G, and their
behavior is closely related to regularity, associated primes, linear quotients, and as-
ymptotic invariants [12, 7, 5, 3]. In general, powers of monomial ideals can behave in
complicated ways: even when an ideal has a linear resolution, its higher powers need

Linear quotients provide another powerful method for proving linear resolutions,

an important role in this direction [9, 13]. Polymatroidal ideals form a particularly
tractable class of monomial ideals. Their combinatorial exchange property often
leads to strong algebraic consequences, including linear quotients and linear resolu-
tions. The algebraic theory of discrete polymatroids developed by Herzog and Hibi
provides the natural framework for this phenomenon [9, 11, 8]. Moreover, later work
on monomial localizations and polymatroidal ideals shows that polymatroidality is
closely related to robust homological behavior under localization and other mono-
mial operations [1]. Thus, when the ordinary powers of an edge ideal can be shown
to be polymatroidal, one obtains not only a structural description of the powers but
also immediate homological consequences. In parallel with these developments, sev-
eral graph families arising from algebraic or ring-theoretic constructions have been
studied through their associated edge ideals. Prime ideal graphs of commutative
rings were introduced by Salih and Jund [17]. If R is a finite commutative ring with
identity and P is a proper prime ideal of R, the prime ideal graph ΓP(R) has vertex
set R \ {0}, where two distinct vertices x and y are adjacent whenever xy ∈P.
Subsequent work investigated graph-theoretic properties of ΓP(R), including con-
nectedness, diameter, clique number, and chromatic number [14].
These studies
show that prime ideal graphs form a natural bridge between finite ring theory and
graph theory. However, their edge ideals and the ordinary powers of those ideals
have not yet been studied with comparable explicit algebraic detail.
The key observation in this paper is that the prime ideal graph has a very rigid
shape. Indeed, if
A = P \ {0},
B = R \ P,

and every element of A is adjacent to every element of B. Therefore
ΓP(R) ∼= Ka ∨Kb,
a = |P| −1,
b = |R| −|P|.

Betti numbers and homological invariants of edge ideals [19, 16]. Nevertheless, the
present setting has a distinctive feature: the parameters a and b are not arbitrary
graph parameters but arise canonically from the prime ideal P of the finite ring
R. Because ΓP(R) ∼= Ka ∨Kb, several qualitative homological features of I(ΓP(R))
are compatible with known results for split graphs, co-chordal graph classes, and
polymatroidal ideals. Thus, the main contribution of this paper is explicit rather

2. Preliminaries and the complete split graph model
In this section, we fix the notation and translate the ring-theoretic definition of
a prime ideal graph into a graph-theoretic model. The main point is that, after
decomposing the nonzero elements of R into P \ {0} and R \ P, the primality of
P forces ΓP(R) to be a complete split graph. This reduction is useful because the
subsequent algebraic arguments depend only on the complete split graph structure
and on the two numerical parameters a = |P| −1, b = |R| −|P|. We also determine
the minimal vertex covers and the corresponding primary decomposition of the edge
ideal, since these structural facts form the foundation for the study of ordinary
powers in the next section.
Throughout the paper, k denotes a field. If G is a finite simple graph on vertex
set {z1, · · · , zr}, its edge ideal is
I(G) = (zizj : {zi, zj} ∈E(G)) ⊆k[z1, · · · , zr].
If G and H are graphs on disjoint vertex sets, then the join G ∨H is obtained from
the disjoint union of G and H by adding all possible edges between a vertex of G
and a vertex of H. A split graph is a graph whose vertex set can be partitioned into
a clique and an independent set. We write Kb for the edgeless graph on b vertices.
Definition 2.1. [17] Let R be a finite commutative ring with identity, and let P be
a proper prime ideal of R. The prime ideal graph of R with respect to P, denoted

Proposition 2.2. With the notation above, ΓP(R) ∼= Ka ∨K b. In particular, the
vertices in A form a clique, the vertices in B form an independent set, every vertex
of A is adjacent to every vertex of B.
Proof. If ui, uj ∈A, then uiuj ∈P, since P is an ideal. Hence the vertices in A form
a clique. If ui ∈A and vt ∈B, then uivt ∈P again because P is an ideal. Thus
every vertex of A is adjacent to every vertex of B. Finally, if vs, vt ∈B and vsvt ∈P,
then primality of P implies vs ∈P or vt ∈P, which is impossible. Therefore B is
an independent set. Hence ΓP(R) ∼= Ka ∨Kb.

Proposition 2.3. Assume a ≥1. The minimal vertex covers of ΓP(R) are precisely
A and (A \ {ui}) ∪B (1 ≤i ≤a).
Proof. Since every edge of ΓP(R) has at least one endpoint in A, the set A is a vertex
cover. It is minimal because removing ui ∈A leaves the edge uiv1 uncovered. For
fixed i, the set (A\{ui})∪B is also a vertex cover: every edge inside A meets A\{ui},
and every edge uivt is covered by vt. This cover is minimal because removing any
uj with j ̸= i leaves uiuj uncovered, while removing any vt leaves uivt uncovered.
Conversely, let C be a minimal vertex cover. If some vt /∈C, then all vertices of A
must belong to C, because all edges uivt must be covered. By minimality, C = A.
If B ⊆C, then the clique on A forces C to contain at least a −1 vertices of A.
Minimality then gives C = (A \ {ui}) ∪B for some i.
□

5. Polymatroidal structure and linear powers
The goal of this section is to show that the explicit generator description from
Section 3 has strong homological consequences. Instead of proving linear resolutions
directly, we verify the polymatroidal exchange property for the minimal generators
of In.
The main constraint is that the exchange operation must preserve both
conditions appearing in the generator criterion: the total y-degree must remain
at most n, and no exponent of an x-variable may exceed n. We handle this by
considering separately exchanges among the x-variables and exchanges involving
the y-variables. Once polymatroidality is established, standard results imply that
each In has linear quotients and a 2n-linear minimal free resolution.

for any f ∈B2 \ B1, the set B1 \ e ∪f still contains an element of X, and hence
belongs to B. Thus B satisfies the basis exchange axiom and is the set of bases of a
matroid. Therefore I is matroidal. Since every matroidal ideal is polymatroidal, I
is polymatroidal.
□
Theorem 5.3. For every n ≥1, the ideal I(ΓP(R))n is polymatroidal.
Proof. By Proposition 5.2, the ideal I(ΓP(R)) is polymatroidal. It is known that
products, and in particular powers, of polymatroidal ideals are again polymatroidal [9].
Hence I(ΓP(R))n is polymatroidal for every n ≥1.
□
Remark 5.4. Although polymatroidality of the powers follows from the closure prop-
erties of polymatroidal ideals, Theorem 3.3 gives the explicit minimal generators of
In. This explicit description is essential for the closed formula for µ(In) and for the
computation of the analytic spread.
Corollary 5.5. Assume a ≥1. For every n ≥1, the ideal I(ΓP(R))n has linear
quotients and a 2n−linear minimal free resolution. In particular, reg(I(ΓP(R))n) =
2n, so I(ΓP(R)) has linear powers.
Proof. By Theorem 5.3, each In is polymatroidal. Standard results on polymatroidal
ideals imply that In has linear quotients with respect to a reverse lexicographic order
of its minimal generators [9, 1]. An ideal generated in a single degree and having
linear quotients has a linear resolution [13]. Since all generators of In have degree
2n, the resolution is 2n−linear, and therefore reg(In) = 2n.
□

7. Conclusion
Let R be a finite commutative ring and let P be a proper prime ideal of R. By iden-
tifying the prime ideal graph ΓP(R) with the complete split graph K|P|−1∨K |R|−|P|.,
we obtained an explicit ordinary-power description of the edge ideal I(ΓP(R)) in
terms of the two ring-theoretic parameters |P| −1 and |R| −|P|. We determined
the minimal vertex covers and the irredundant primary decomposition of I(ΓP(R)),
characterized the minimal monomial generators of I(ΓP(R))n for all n ≥1, derived
a closed formula for µ(I(ΓP(R))n). We also interpreted this counting formula as
the Hilbert function of the special fiber ring and computed the analytic spread.
The homological consequences follow from the polymatroidal structure of the edge
ideal. In particular, every ordinary power I(ΓP(R))n is polymatroidal and there-
fore has linear quotients and a 2n-linear minimal free resolution. Thus prime ideal
graphs provide a concrete ring-induced family for which ordinary powers of edge
ideals admit explicit combinatorial and algebraic descriptions. The present work is
restricted to ordinary powers of edge ideals arising from prime ideal graphs of finite
commutative rings. Natural directions for further work include graded Betti num-
bers, projective dimension, depth, Rees-algebra invariants, and extension to broader
split-graph families.
