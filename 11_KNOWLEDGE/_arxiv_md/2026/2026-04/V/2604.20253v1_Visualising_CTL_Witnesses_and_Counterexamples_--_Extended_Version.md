Visualising CTL Witnesses and Counterexamples
— Extended Version
Arend Rensink[0000-0002-1714-6319]
University of Twente, arend.rensink@utwente.nl
Abstract. One of the advantages of LTL over CTL is that the notion of
a counterexample is easy to grasp, visualise and process: it is a trace that
violates the property at hand. In this paper we propose a notion of evi-
dence for CTL properties on explicit-state models — which equally serves
as witness for satisfied properties and counterexample for violated ones
— and how to visualise it, with the main aim of (human) comprehension.
The main contribution consists of a formal model of evidence, a char-
acterisation of minimal evidence per temporal operator, and a concrete,
implemented proposal for its visualisation.
This is the extended version of [19], containing the proofs of all results.
Keywords: CTL Model Checking · Evidence · Witness · Counterexample ·
Visualisation

Introduction
Temporal logic is used to express properties of the evolution of a system over
time, such as “a certain property φ holds in the system until another property
ψ starts to hold”, or “provided φ keeps occurring again and again, ψ is never
reachable”. The family of temporal logics consists of two main branches, which
are incomparable in the sense that either can express properties that the other
cannot: Linear Temporal Logic (LTL, “linear time”) [17], and Computational Tree
Logic (CTL, “branching time”) [9,4]. A more powerful logic that combines the
features of these two is CTL∗[10], and a yet more expressive variant is the
modal µ-calculus [13,14].
Much has been said about the relative merits of LTL and CTL, regarding
expressivenes, computational complexity of model checking (i.e., the process of
finding out whether or not a temporal property holds in a given system model),
and usability in practice; see, e.g., [22]. In this paper, we address a relatively mi-
nor difference, namely the kind of information we can glean from the satisfaction,
respectively violation of a temporal property by a given model — besides the
bare, boolean fact itself. Such knowledge is naturally generated as a by-product
of the model checking process — or in sometimes, as in [20], is actually central
to the model checking algorithm itself. One of the reasons why LTL is found to
be useful is that, if a property φ is not satisfied, there is at least one (possibly
infinite) trace that violates φ, called a counterexample to φ. Counterexamples

are very useful because they can be further analysed, in order to understand how
the violation of φ came about. Another scenario is where one actually wants to
find a concrete trace that does satisfy some LTL property φ: this can be obtained
by checking ¬φ — a counterexample then serves as a trace of the desired kind.
In CTL, there is no such simple notion of counterexample. This is due to the
nature of the logic, which (as the respective adjectives “branching” and “linear”
imply) takes the model’s branching structure into account, rather than essen-
tially regarding it as a set of (finite and infinite) linear traces. In general, the
non-satisfaction of a property can therefore only be fully understood by taking
at least part of that branching structure aboard, making this conceptually more
complex than a single trace or even a set of them. What part of the branching
structure, and how to capture it, are questions we address in this paper.
Another difference between LTL and CTL we want to recall is that violating a
CTL property is equivalent to satisfying its negation. That is not the case for
LTL: e.g., considering the property φ = F p, which is satisfied by every trace
that reaches a state where p holds, we can easily imagine a system where neither
φ nor ¬φ is satisfied: it merely has to include one trace leading to a p-state,
and a second one that loops without every reaching such a p-state. The closest
alternative in CTL would be EF p (“there is a trace that reaches a state where p
holds”), but if this is violated by a given system then its negation ¬ EF p (being
equivalent to AG ¬p, “for all traces, ¬p holds all along”) is certainly satisfied.
As a consequence, in the case of CTL there is actually no reason to focus on
counterexamples for violated properties: we can simultaneously and equivalently
consider the dual notion of witnesses for property satisfaction. As an overarching
term, we use evidence for a property φ on a given model M: if M satisfies φ,
the evidence is a witness of that fact, whereas if M violates φ, the evidence is a
counterexample.
The overall question addressed by this paper is twofold: (i) what is the simplest
notion of evidence that precisely captures the reason for satisfaction or violation
of an arbitrary CTL formula, and (ii) how can we effectively visualise this notion?
With respect to (ii), we are obviously limited by the fact that visualisation
inherently stops being useful for human comprehension for any but small models.
After setting up the definitions in Section 2, the main technical contribu-
tion of this paper is given in Section 3. Sections 4 and 5 are mostly concerned
with visualisation. Sections 6 and 7 wrap up with related work and conclusions.
Appendix A contains the proofs of all results.

Definitions
We start off by presenting the concepts and notations used in this paper. Most
of them are standard, but we sometimes adopt alternative formulations to serve
the flow of the paper. The only technical novelties are those of closed states and
submodel (Definitions 2 and 4).

We use directed graphs ⟨X, R⟩, where X is countable and R ⊆X × X. Such
a graph is called cyclic if there are {(xi, xi+1)}1≤i≤n ⊆R for some n ≥1 such
that xn+1 = x1, and acyclic otherwise.
We use finite and infinite sequences over a set X, denoted X <ω and Xω,
as well as X ≤ω = X <ω ∪Xω. We write |σ| ∈N ∪{ω} to denote the length of
σ ∈X ≤ω, σ|i for its ith element (for any i ∈N such that 1 ≤i ≤|σ|) and
σ▷= σ||σ| for its last element (if σ ∈X <ω). We use [σ] = {σ|i | 1 ≤i ≤|σ|}
for the set of elements in σ, <σ = {(σ|i, σ|i+1) | 1 ≤i < |σ|} for the induced
ordering, and ⟨σ⟩= ⟨[σ], <σ⟩for its graph. σ is called acyclic if ⟨σ⟩is so. We
write ϵ for the empty sequence, σ1·σ2 for the concatenation of σ1 and σ2 (also
extended pointwise to sets of sequences), and σ ⪯σ′ to indicate that σ is a prefix
of σ′ (meaning that σ′ = σ·σ′′ for some σ′′).
We write f : X ,→Y to denote that f is a partial function from X to Y ;
dom f ⊆X then denotes its domain and ˆf = {(x, f(x)) | s ∈dom f} its graph;1
moreover, f|Z : X ,→Y for Z ⊆X denotes the restriction of f to the elements
in Z. The set of booleans is denoted B, with elements 0, 1.
To build formulas, we assume a set of basic propositions P and a set of logical
operators O. Every operator o ∈O has an arity αo ∈N. For instance, PL
(propositional logic) has basic operators OPL = {true(0), false(0), !(1), &(2), +(2)}
(where a superscript (i) indicates arity i),2 and CTL (Computation Tree Logic)
has OCTL = OPL ∪{AX(1), EX(1), AF(1), EF(1), AG(1), EG(1), EU(2), AU(2)}.
From propositions and operators we build formulas, as a set F such that
F ⊆P ∪(O × F<ω)).
Definition 1 (formula). The set of formulas F is the smallest set such that
P ⊆F and {(o, ¯φ) | o ∈O, ¯φ ∈F<ω, |φ| = αo} ⊆F.
We use chld(φ) to denote the set of arguments of φ, setting chld(p) = ∅for
all p ∈P. For any set of formulas F ⊆F, we use FP = F ∩P to denote
the subset of propositions, and FO = F ∩(O × F<ω) to denote the subset of
composite formulas. A set of formulas F is called subformula-closed if φ ∈F
implies chld(φ) ⊂F. The subformula-closed sets of formulas are collected in ↓F.
The semantics of CTL is usually defined in terms of Kripke structures [15], in
which the states (taken from some universe S) are labelled with the sets of
propositions that hold there. In this paper we use a variant, in which we add a
subset of closed states (which start playing a role in Definition 4), and use a more
general labelling function that (i) is partial, and (ii) includes arbitrary formulas
(and not just propositions). Hence, labellings are partial functions L: S×F ,→B
for some set of states S and set of formulas F. We use LP = L ∩(S × FP) and
LO = L ∩(S × FO) to denote the subfunctions for propositional and composed
formulas.

Definition 2 (model, path).
Let F ∈↓F. A model over F is a tuple M =
⟨S, C, R, L⟩where S is a set of states with subset C ⊆S of closed states, R ⊆
S ×S a binary relation over S and L: S ×F ,→B a labelling. M is called Kripke

A path in M is a sequence ρ ∈S≤ω such that (ρ|i, ρ|i+1) ∈R for all 1 ≤i <

Note that paths may contain repeated occurrences of states. The set of models

the set of direct successors of s, and S|φ,v ⊆S (with v ∈B) to denote the subset
of states s for which L(s, φ) = v, and analogous for C.
Given F ∈↓F and an arbitrary state set S, every labelling L: S × F ,→B
gives rise to a “discrete” model ⟨L⟩= ⟨S, ∅, ∅, L⟩∈M(F).

The distinction between open and closed states comes into play when we consider
submodels. A submodel of M consists of subsets of SM, CM, RM and LM, with
the proviso that a closed state of the submodel must already have all outgoing
transitions that it has in M. Formally:
Definition 4 (submodel).
Let F ∈↓F, and let M1, M2 ∈M(F). M1 is a
submodel of M2, denoted M1 ⊑M2, if S1 ⊆S2, C1 ⊆C2, R1 ⊆R2 and
L1 ⊆L2, and (s, s′) ∈R2 \ R1 implies s /∈C1.
Among other things, M1 ⊑M2 implies that none of the “new” states in S2 \ S1
are reachable from any of the closed “old” states in C1.
Definition 5 (soundness, consistency). Let F ∈↓F. A model M ∈M(F) is
called sound if it is full and for all (s, φ) ∈S × F, L(s, φ) = 1 iff s |=M φ (and
conversely, L(s, φ) = 0 iff s ̸|=M φ). M is called consistent if it has a sound
supermodel.
Note that every Kripke model in which dom L = S × FP is consistent. Hence,
the traditional task of model checking a formula φ over a given Kripke model
M comes down to finding the smallest sound supermodel N ⊒M (where the
qualification “smallest” implies that N is unique and has SN = SM, CN = CM
and RN = RM). Instead, in this paper, we rather focus on the equivalent task
of establishing whether (or actually: why) a given full model is sound.
3
Evidence
Given a labelling function L: S × F ,→B, we refer to the elements of ˆL (which
have the shape (s, φ, b)) as assertions, ranged over by a. Such an assertion should
be read as the claim that φ holds in s if b = 1, or fails to hold in s if b = 0. For
propositions (φ ∈P), assertions are axiomatic — they cannot be (dis)proved
but must be taken at face value. For compound formulas (φ ∈FO) however, the
correctness of an assertion depends on the subformulas of φ. We define evidence
for an assertion involving φ as sufficient information about the children of φ to
guarantee that the assertion is correct.
Definition 6 (evidence, witness, counterexample).
Let F ∈↓F and let
M ∈M(F) be consistent. M is evidence for an assertion a ∈S × FO × B if
(i) dom LM ⊆S×chld(φa) and (ii) a ∈ˆLN for every sound supermodel N ⊒M.
Moreover, M is called a witness for (s, φ) ∈S × F if it is evidence for (s, φ, 1),
and a counterexample for (s, φ) if it is evidence for (s, φ, 0).
For instance, starting with a sound model M and an arbitrary assertion a ∈
ˆL, a straightforward case of evidence is obtained by just taking the transition
structure of M and reducing the labelling to the children of φa.

Evidence may easily contain information that is not strictly required to explain
the assertion. This is already exemplified by Proposition 7: the model Ma shown
there to be evidence for an assertion a contains the entire transition structure
of the original model M, including states that are not even reachable from sa.
In the remainder of this section, we obtain more precise characterisations of
evidence for the core CTL formulas.
As a first step, Table 1 defines concrete “syntactic” conditions wts and ctr
that are sufficient and, for a certain class of formulas, necessary to ensure that
a model is evidence for a given assertion. We first state their sufficiency.
Theorem 8 (wts and ctr are sufficient).
Let F ∈↓F, let M ∈M(F) be
consistent and let s ∈S and φ ∈FO.
1. M is a witness for (s, φ) if wtsM(s, φ) holds;
2. M is a counterexample for (s, φ) if ctrM(s, φ) holds.
In general, wts and ctr are not necessary for M to be evidence: there exist
witnesses that do not satisfy wts, and counterexamples that do not satisfy ctr.
In a sense, this is not surprising. Consider the case where φ is a valid formula
(i.e., satisfied by any state of any sound model); then every consistent model
with dom L ⊆S × chld(φ) is a witness for (s, φ) (for any s), even the trivial,
⊑-minimum ⟨{s}, ∅, ∅, ∅⟩that contains no information whatsoever. A necessary
condition for being a witness would therefore have to check for validity, which is
more than can be expected from a simple “syntactic” condition such as wts.
In fact, there are even simpler examples of evidence that satisfy wts nor ctr.

One way to understand this example is that, although the model M does not

label it any other way and still remain consistent. In other words, the labelling

assign a particular value in order to give rise to a consistent model. We will see
that, in cases where the children of a formula are not constrained in this way,
wts and ctr are necessary conditions for witnesses, respectively counterexamples.

Definition 10 (constrained formula set).
Let F ∈↓F. A set of formulas
G ⊆F is constrained if there exists an inconsistent model M ∈M(F) with
dom L ⊆S × G ,→B.

Proofs and their Visualisation
The purpose of evidence visualisation is to aid the comprehension of model
checking outcomes. In terms of this paper, this translates to “understanding why
a given model is sound” — which in turn is a matter of providing evidence for
all its compound assertions. We formalise this in the notion of proof.
Definition 16 (proof).
Let F ∈↓F. A proof over F is a tuple P = ⟨M, π⟩
such that M ∈M(F) and π: ˆLO 7→{N ∈M(F) | N ⊑M} such that, for all
a ∈ˆLO, π(a) is evidence for a.
The following proposition makes clear that proofs are a tool to establish consis-
tency. Recalling that sound models are just consistent full ones, as a special case
we get that proofs are a tool to establish soundness.
Proposition 17. Let F ∈↓F. A model M ∈M(F) is consistent if and only if
M ⊑N for some proof ⟨N, π⟩over F.
Note 18 (complexity). Although in this paper we are not concerned with ques-
tions of complexity, it is worth pointing out that proofs can be constructed, at no

extra cost, as a byproduct of (explicit-state) CTL model checking. Very roughly,
to understand why this is the case, recall that checking a compound formula
ψ = (o, ¯φ) is a recursive procedure, in which first all ¯φ|i are checked, followed
by a state space traversal depending on the operator o, in which all states are
marked either 1 or 0 for ψ. Generating a proof (in the sense of Definition 16) is
a matter of recording the reason why a 1- or 0-mark is given.
For instance, to check ψ = E φ1 U φ2, the standard algorithm immediately
marks all states that satisfy φ2 with 1, after which (recursively) the incom-
ing transitions of all freshly 1-marked states are explored backwards, and their
sources are also marked 1 if they additionally satisfy φ1. After this stage has
terminated, all remaining states are marked 0. The proof generation starts with
all states of the system, none of which are closed, and no transitions. For the
positive (witness) part of the proof, all transitions that give rise to a 1-mark of
their source should be added. Witnesses are minimal if the backward exploration
is done breadth-first. For the negative (counterexample) part of the proof, every
0-marked state that satisfies φ1 should be closed and all its outgoing transitions
should be added. The labelling of the proof is obvious.

Combined Evidence
The visualisation of a CTL proof typically involves many individual snapshots,
each one showing the evidence for a given pair of state and temporal subformula.
Even if these can be called up by interactively selecting state/formula instances,
it is still hard to obtain a comprehensive understanding.
Combining different evidence models into a single static representation, on
the other hand, generally runs into the problem that (in particular for path-
based temporal formulas) each individual witness and counterexample is a model
of potentially unbounded size, which for different states will generally overlap.
The union of two evidence models may very well fail to be evidence, hence its
visualisation provides no useful information.

is actually possible: namely, for a given model and a single, fixed formula.

Related work
There is substantial prior work on the notion of evidence, some of which is
actually in the context of (much) more powerful logics, such as CTL enriched with
description logic [23], the modal µ-calculus [21] or even more general fixpoint
logics [7]. Comprehensive overviews of diagnostics for model checking can be
found in [2,12,8].

To start with, linear evidence (in our setting: evidence built on ⟨ρ⟩for some
ρ ∈S≤ω) has been studied in [5], where it was shown how such evidence can
be generated efficiently (using symbolic model checking) for fragments of CTL
and also CTL∗for which this type of evidence actually exists. In the same vein,
[1] studies the complexity of determining whether linear evidence (in this case
always a counterexample) exists for a given ACTL formula (the universal frag-
ment of CTL). Linear evidence was extended in [6] to tree-like evidence for ACTL
and certain ω-regular extensions. In [11] it is shown how to efficiently generate
minimal evidence, in this case for the dual existential fragment ECTL. (It should
be pointed out that, in all the cited papers, the terms “evidence”, “witness” and
“counterexample” do not refer to a single operator, as in this paper, but to the
entire nested structure that we have called “proof”. Moreover, minimality is de-
fined only relative to the set of states, and not to the labelling.)
In contrast, in this paper we have not concerned ourselves with the complexity
of generating evidence (except for the brief excursion in Note 18), but with its
(as precise as possible) characterisation. The essential technical novelty are the
closed states, which enable us to generalise to full CTL. For the fragments studied
in the papers above, our evidence essentially reduces to their linear and tree-like
counterpart, in which all states are open.
One further point of difference is that [6] is based on simulation of evidence
by the Kripke structures to which it applies, where we consider supermodels. In
principle, simulation is the more general concept, as it abstracts from state iden-
tities. Our motivation to concentrate on sub- and supermodels, rather than going
for a simulation-like relation, lies in the desire to obtain a human-comprehensible


## Page 16


explanation for model checking outcomes: the fact that the evidence is displayed
on the same states as the model that has been checked, rather than having to
be understood through the indirection of a simulation relation, is actually an
advantage in this context. In any case, the standard notion of simulation does
not work as required for our concept of evidence, since it does not take closed
states into account. For instance, consider once more the counterexample (say
M1) of Fig. 4b (ignoring the greyed-out part). Now imagine a model M2 that
extends M1 with a single transition from s0 to a new state s15 in which win is
satisfied. We would have that M2 simulates M1, while M2 does satisfy the prop-
erty E ! d1 U win to which M1 is supposed to be a counterexample. We briefly
return to this point in the future work discussion below.
In [23], the logic is extended by replacing atomic predicates by description
logic statements; however, the notion of evidence is essentially still restricted to
tree-like structures. In particular, witnesses for properties of the form AF φ are
(essentially) just given as 1 or 0, providing no useful structural information.
A very powerful notion of evidence, based on proof graphs for Parameterised
Boolean Equation Systems, is studied in [7,24,21] for logics that go far beyond
CTL in expressive power, up to the fixpoint logic in [7] that generalises the modal
µ-calculus. They show that such proof graphs can be generated effectively and, in
some cases, efficiently. However, with great power comes greater complexity; the
visualisation of general proof graphs has not been addressed systematically. With
respect to proof graphs, our closed states can be thought of as concisely encoding
a lot of negative information — namely, about the absence of transitions; as
argued before, this is a contributing factor to the relative ease of representing
evidence for path-existential operators. For instance, once more taking Fig. 4b
as an example, an equivalent proof graph would have to contain a node for each
of the closed states to explicitly represent the absence of a transition from that
state to the greyed-out state s12, as adding such a transition would result in a
model satisfying E ! d1 U win.
The work in [3] is motivationally quite close to ours, driven by similar consid-
erations of visualisation and comprehensibility of CTL model checking outcomes.
The visualisation framework presented there is in some ways more powerful than
ours, since it involves interaction during the generation of evidence. The nature
and visualisation of their counterexamples is close to the proof graphs cited in
the previous paragraph; there are no considerations of minimality. Finally, [16]
also pursues comprehensibility of model checking evidence, in this case for the
modal µ-calculus, but only for trace-like counterexamples.
7
Conclusion and future work
We have answered the questions posed in the introduction as follows.
(i) What is the simplest notion of evidence that precisely captures the reason for
satisfaction or violation of an arbitrary CTL formula?
We have given a formal characterisation of minimal evidence for individual
operators (Corollary 15), leading to proofs for entire formulas (Definition 16)


## Page 17


that map every state/subformula pair to corresponding evidence. The main
new concept here is that of closed states, concisely capturing the negative
information about the absence of further outgoing transitions.
(ii) How can we effectively visualise this notion of evidence?
We have implemented an interactive visualisation demonstrator [18], avail-
able as software artefact accompanying this paper, which shows the evidence
upon selecting any state/subformula pair (Section 4). To make the visual-
isation more effective, we have limited the number of individual snapshots
required to collectively represent a proof through local closure, in which the
evidence of non-temporal subformulas is added to that of temporal operators
without risk of confusion or loss of information. Furthermore, we have in-
troduced the concept of natural evidence to increase comprehensibility and
to rule out cases that, though minimal in a formal sense, are not intuitively
obvious. Finally, we have shown that it is actually possible to combine the
(natural) evidence for a given subformula over all states in a single model
(Section 5), such that the minimal evidence for a given state emerges as the
reachable fragment of that combined evidence. Thus, in the end, the number
of evidence models that collectively represent a complete proof is reduced to
one per temporal operator, rather than one per state/subformula pair.
We see two concrete avenues for future research.
More expressive logics. It is worth investigating to what extent the concepts of
this paper (especially the particular notion of evidence) remain useful in more
expressive logics. The primary candidate is CTL∗. Obviously, the difficulty lies
in the fact that the assertions are then not based on state/formula pairs, but on
path/formula pairs. However, since all such formulas are still bound by a univer-
sal or existential path quantifier, it might be possible to combine the evidence
for paths from a given state and so retain some of the advantages of the current
setup.
Simulation in the presence of closed states. In Section 6 we have recalled that
[6] uses simulation to relate evidence (in that case tree-like counterexamples)
and the Kripke structures to which that evidence applies: if a Kripke structure
simulates a counterexample, the property is violated in the Kripke structure. For
us, instead, evidence applies to any supermodel. We have sketched why simulation
not work “out of the box” in the setting of this paper: it allows the (simulating)
model to have transitions from states that are closed in the (simulated) evidence.
We hypothesise that our results can be generalised to a simulation-like relation
by requiring bisimulation on closed states.
