Abstract
The computational equivalence between approximate counting and sampling is well estab-
lished for polynomial-time algorithms.
The most efficient general reduction from counting
to sampling is achieved via simulated annealing, where the counting problem is formulated
in terms of estimating the ratio Q = Z(βmax)/Z(βmin) between partition functions Z(β) =
P
x∈Ωexp(βH(x)) of Gibbs distributions µβ over Ωwith Hamiltonian H, given access to a
sampling oracle that produces samples from µβ for β ∈[βmin, βmax].
The best bound achieved by known annealing algorithms with relative error ε is O(q log h/ε2),
where q, h are parameters which respectively bound ln Q and H. However, all known algorithms
attaining this near-optimal complexity are inherently sequential, or adaptive: the queried pa-
rameters β depend on previous samples.
We develop a simple non-adaptive algorithm for approximate counting using O(q log2 h/ε2)
samples, as well as an algorithm that achieves O(q log h/ε2) samples with just two rounds of
adaptivity, matching the best sample complexity of sequential algorithms. These algorithms
naturally give rise to work-efficient parallel (RNC) counting algorithms. We discuss applications
to RNC counting algorithms for several classic models, including the anti-ferromagnetic 2-spin,
monomer-dimer and ferromagnetic Ising models.

Introduction
A fundamental theme in randomized computation is the connection between sampling combinatorial
objects and counting those objects. This interplay gave rise to the Monte Carlo method and has
become a central paradigm in algorithm design. As an example, in theoretical computer science, a
classic result of Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani [17] established the equivalence between approximate
sampling and counting for self-reducible problems, up to polynomial-time factors.
A wide range of counting problems can be formulated as computing the partition function of
a Gibbs distribution. This task, in turn, can be approximated by leveraging samples from the
corresponding Gibbs distributions. Classic examples include volume estimation [6], approximation
of permanent [16], and approximately counting combinatorial objects [15, 20] or constraint satisfying
solutions [8]. Notably, many of these problems lack the self-reducibility property.

1.1
Estimating the Partition Ratio
The counting problem for Gibbs distributions can be formulated as the task of estimating the
partition ratio Q for chosen values βmin and βmax (often βmin = −∞). More specifically, we choose
βmin so that Z(βmin) is trivial, and estimate Z(βmax) by estimating the ratio Q. Our main goal is
to develop black-box algorithms to estimate Q using oracle access to the gross Gibbs distribution
µβ at query points β ∈[βmin, βmax]. This approach is often referred to as simulated annealing in
the literature, reflecting the process of gradually varying the parameter β from βmin to βmax.
We define the sample complexity to be the number of oracle queries. To measure the complexity,
we assume that we are given known parameters h, q ≥2 with
log Q ≤q,
Ex∼µβmax[H(x)] ≤h
Here, q is a known finite lower bound on the logarithm of the ratio. For example, when βmax ≤0,
we can take q = log |Ω|. Likewise, h is a known finite upper bound on the expectation; often we
can obtain such a bound from simplying knowing that H(Ω) ⊆{0} ∪[1, h].
Early non-adaptive simulated annealing algorithms, due to [6, 1], achieve sample complexity
O(q2 log h/ε2) for estimating Q to relative error ε. A long line of work has since focused on improv-
ing this complexity. The work of [22] introduced the first adaptive simulated annealing algorithm,
achieving a near-linear sample complexity O(q polylog(qh)/ε2). On the other hand, based on the
Tootsie Pop Algorithm (TPA) introduced by [13] and the Paired Product Estimator (PPE) intro-
duced by [11], a number of adaptive simulated annealing algorithms have been developed since
including [11, 18, 10].
Most recently, [10] achieved an upper bound of O(q log h/ε2) on space
complexity, while also showing a near-matching lower bound of Ω(q/ε2).1
A key limitation of the annealing algorithms achieving near-optimal sample complexity, however,
is that they are inherently sequential, or to put it another way, adaptive: each query to the sampling

1.2
Applications to parallel counting
Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 can be implemented in work-efficient RNC algorithms in a straight-
forward way. By combining our results with RNC sampling algorithms for Gibbs distributions,
we obtain RNC algorithms for approximate counting (i.e., estimating the partition function) for
several fundamental classes of Gibbs distributions, including:
• anti-ferromagnetic 2-spin systems (including the anti-ferro Ising model and the hardcore
model) up to the uniqueness threshold, beyond which approximate counting and sampling
become intractable even for sequential algorithms;
• the monomer-dimer model (matchings);
• the ferromagnetic Ising model with external fields.
The following theorems summarize (in slightly simplified form) our new results on parallel ap-
proximate counting. The formal definitions of the models are postponed to Section 5. Throughout,
we let G = (V, E) be a connected graph with n vertices, m edges, and maximum degree ∆.

• strictly anti-ferromagnetic and satisfying the uniqueness condition;
• satisfying the uniqueness condition, and G is ∆-regular.
