Abstract
The Fréchet distance is a popular distance measure between trajectories or curves in space, or between
walks in graphs. We study computing the Fréchet distance between walks in the d-dimensional grid
graphs, i.e. Zd where points share an edge if they differ by one in one coordinate.
We give an algorithm, that for two simple paths on n vertices, (1 + ε)-approximates the Fréchet
distance in time e
O(( n
ε )2−2/d + n). We complement this by a near-matching fine-grained lower bound:
for constant dimensions d ≥3, there is no O((ε2/d( n
ε )2−2/d)1−δ) algorithm for any δ > 0 unless the
Orthogonal Vector Hypothesis fails. Thus, our results are tight up to a factor ε2/d and log(n)-factors.
We extend our results to imbalanced lower and upper bounds, where the curves have n and m
vertices respectively, and also obtain near-tight bounds.
Driemel, Har-Peled and Wenk [DCG’12] studied realistic assumptions for curves to speed up
Fréchet distance computation. One of these assumptions is λ-low density and they can compute
a (1 + ε)-approximation between λ-low dense curves in time ˜O(ε−2λ2n2(1−1/d)). By adapting our
lower bound, we show that their algorithm has a tight dependency on n and a tight dependency on
ε as d goes to infinity. A gap remains in terms of λ.

Introduction
The Fréchet distance is a widely recognised measure of similarity between two curves
or trajectories. Conceptually, it can be considered the shortest leash length needed to
connect a human walking along one curve and a dog walking along the other, with both
moving forward without backtracking (see Figure 1).
The Fréchet distance is used in
various real-life applications, including signature verification [34], the study of coastline
retrievals [33], map-matching [39], protein structures [30], air traffic [5], and trajectory analysis
and clustering [1,6,9,10,11,21,27,31,32,36,37,42]. Alt and Godau [2] show a O(nm log(n+m))-
time algorithm for computing the Fréchet distance between two polygonal curves with n
and m vertices. Since then, there have been polylogarithmic-time improvements [13, 15],
but there is strong evidence that significantly faster algorithms cannot exist. Bringmann [7]
proved that, under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, no strongly subquadratic
algorithm (i.e., with running time O((nm)1−δ) for any δ > 0) exists to compute a 1.001
approximation of the Fréchet distance between curves of complexity n and m. Buchin,
Ophelders, and Speckmann [14] rule out a (3 −ε)-approximation in time O((n)2−δ) between
two n-vertex curves in R1 and recently, Blank [4] rules out a (1+
√
2−ε)-approximation (resp.
(3 −ε)-approximation) of the Fréchet distance between curves of complexity n and m in R2
under the ℓ2-norm (resp. ℓ∞-norm) in time O((nm)1−δ). Cheng, Huang, and Zhang [16]
obtain a randomised (7 + ε)-approximation in ˜O(nm0.99) time.
Restricted curve classes.
To obtain strongly subquadratic-time algorithms, one therefore
has to restrict the input. Gudmundsson, Mirzanezhad, Mohades, and Wenk [26] consider the
special case where the input curves consist exclusively of sufficiently long edges relative to
their Fréchet distance, and gave a near-linear time exact algorithm. Another way of obtaining
subquadratic results is to impose so-called realistic assumptions on the input — I.e., to
restrict the curve to not have geometric traits that are considered unrealistic. Examples
include κ-straight and κ-bounded curves [3], λ-low dense curves [35], and c-packed curves [20].
Driemel, Har-Peled and Wenk [20] showed, for these classes of curves, a near-linear time
algorithm to compute a (1 + ε)-approximation of the Fréchet distance. For c-packed curves
(and therefore c-straight and c-bounded curves) they can decide whether the Fréchet distance
between two curves is at most ∆in time O( c

the running time to O( c

Vector Hypothesis. Gudmundsson, Mai, and Wong [25] showed that it suffices to assume
that one curve is c-packed and show a ˜O(c3ε−8(n + m))-time algorithm. Conradi, van der
Hoog, van der Horst, and Ophelders [17] improve this running time to O( c
εn).
λ-low dense curves.
The realism assumption of λ-low density is of particular relevance to
this paper. A collection of edges in Rd is λ-low-density if for any ball B in Rd, the number
of edges of length at least radius(B) that intersect B is at most λ. It is a popular realism
assumption to speed up computations [12,19,20,24,28,29,35] with one compelling feature:
planar grid graphs are c-packed for c ∈Θ(n) but they are O(1)-low dense. Driemel, Har-Peled
and Wenk [20] show a ˜O(ε−2λ2n2(1−1/d))-time algorithm to compute a (1 +ε)-approximation
of the Fréchet distance between two n-vertex λ-low dense curves in Rd, which makes it the
only realism assumption discussed in [20] where the running time exponent depends on d.
Fréchet distance in grid graphs.
In this paper, we show near-tight bounds for approximating
the discrete and continuous Fréchet distance in d-dimensional grid graphs.

Fréchet Distance in d-Dimensional Grid Graphs
Driemel, van der Hoog, and Rotenberg [22] first studied the Fréchet distance in graphs.
Their input consists of a weighted graph G, where curves are paths in the graph (walks which
visit no vertex twice). The distance between any two vertices is the length of the shortest
path connecting them. They show a conditional quadratic-time lower bound on computing
the Fréchet distance between paths in a weighted graph, even when the graph is planar. Van
der Hoog, van der Horst, Rotenberg and Wulf [38] extend this lower bound by excluding
subquadratic 1.25 approximation algorithms for two disjoint paths in an unweighted planar
graph. They also identified a setting in which faster exact algorithms exist: when G is a
regular planar tiling, e.g., a planar grid graph, and P and Q are paths in G of length n and
m, then the exact Fréchet distance can be computed in time ˜O((n + m)1.5).
We note that, by subdividing each edge of the grid graph into 3 edges of equal length, any
path in a grid graph becomes O(1)-low dense without asymptotically increasing the curve
complexity. Thus, any lower bound for d-dimensional grid graphs is also a lower bound for
O(1)-low dense curves. Moreover, we can define for any grid graph a λ-path as any path that
traverses each vertex in the grid at most λ times. By the same analysis, any lower bound for
λ-paths in d-dimensional grid graphs is also a lower bound for λ-low dense curves in Rd.
Contribution.
We provide near-tight bounds for computing (1 + ε)-approximations of the
Fréchet distance in d-grid graphs. Our algorithmic input consists of two λ-paths P and Q in
the d-dimensional grid graph of complexities n and m, with n ≥m. One may either consider
the discrete Fréchet distance under the shortest path metric, or the continuous Fréchet
distance under the ℓ1-norm. Our lower bounds are conditioned on the Orthogonal Vectors
Hypothesis (OVH), and thus also hold unless the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis fails.
We first provide some context for our results: we show that computing an α-approximation
in time O((f(n, m))1−δ) with δ > 0 contradicts the Orthogonal Vectors Hypothesis. A lower
bound is balanced if it requires that n = m and imbalanced otherwise [4]. Imbalanced bounds
are typically more difficult to obtain. For example, for one-dimensional curves, no imbalanced
lower bound is known [4,14], and until recently [4], the best imbalanced lower bound in 2D
only ruled out a subquadratic 1.001-approximation [7], whereas the best balanced bound
rules out a subquadratic 3-approximation [14]. Our first result is a balanced lower bound for
dimensions d ≥3, and an imbalanced lower bound for dimensions d ≥4 that excludes strongly
subquadratic-time exact algorithms (Theorem 7). This complements the O(n1.5)-time exact
2D algorithm from [38], showing that similar techniques cannot extend to higher dimensions.

▶Definition 1. A λ-path is a walk in G that visits each vertex at most λ times.
Generalisations.
The above definition is but one of many ways to study the Fréchet distance
in regular tessellations. One immediate generalisation is to consider the discrete Fréchet
distance between λ-paths in any other regular d-dimensional tiling. For dimensions d ≥5
the only regular tessellation is the d-dimensional grid, sometimes referred to as the cubic
honeycomb [18], and so for dimensions d ≥5 our results are complete. Alternatively, one can
interpret the λ-paths P and Q as d-dimensional continuous curves under any ℓp-norm, and
consider the continuous Fréchet distance. We note in Appendix B that our results apply to
any such formulation, but for ease of exposition we restrict the main body to the discrete
Fréchet distance under the shortest path distance, which coincides with the ℓ1-metric.
OVH.
Given two sets of vectors U, W ⊆{0, 1}b of N and M vectors, the Orthogonal Vector
problem asks whether there exists vectors u ∈U and w ∈W such that u is orthogonal to w,
i.e., u · w = 0. If there exists a pair of orthogonal vectors, we will call it a YES instance.
▶Hypothesis (Orthogonal Vector Hypothesis (OVH) [40]). For all δ > 0, there exist constants
ω and 1 > γ > 0 such that the Orthogonal Vector problem for vectors of dimension b = ω log N
and M = N γ cannot be solved in O

time.
The Orthogonal Vector Hypothesis is implied by the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
(SETH) [41]. The lower bound we present is therefore also conditioned on SETH.

Lower Bounds
We provide balanced and imbalanced lower bounds for computing the Fréchet distance for
λ-paths in the d-dimensional grid graph. We make use of one of two existing lower bounds:
A balanced lower bound for the continuous Fréchet distance in R1, which states that a
(3 −ε)-approximation for complexity n curves in O(n2−δ) time [14] refutes OVH.
An imbalanced lower bound for the continuous Fréchet distance in R2, which states that
a (3 −ε)-approximation algorithm for complexity n and m curves under the ℓ1-norm with
running time O((nm)1−δ) [4] refutes OVH.1
Both of these constructions live in a bounded domain ([−10, 10] ⊂R1 and [−5, 5] × [−5, 5] ⊂
R2) and the construction decides whether the Fréchet distance is 1 or 3. Since the constructed
edges have constant length, they also rule-out faster discrete Fréchet distance computations.

the d-dimensional grid. Secondly, we consider a variety of Fréchet distance settings as we can

Approximating dG

Given two λ-paths P, Q in the d-dimensional grid graph G we give an approximate decider
that, for given ∆≥0 and ε > 0, determines whether dG

Throughout this section, we assume that the dimension d ≥2.
Free space matrix.
Given two walks P and Q of complexity n and m and some ∆≥0, we
wish to test whether ddF (P, Q) ≤∆. A common tool for solving this decision variant of
the problem is the ∆-free space matrix M∆: an n by m binary matrix where M∆[i, j] = 0 if
dist(pi, qj) ≤∆and M∆[i, j] = 1 otherwise. We follow geometric convention, where i denotes
the column of M∆, corresponding to some pi ∈P. Column entries with value 0 correspond
to qj ∈Q with dist(pi, qj) ≤∆. Eiter and Mannila [23] define a directed graph on M∆
where there is an edge from M∆[a, b] to M∆[c, d] if and only if c ∈{a, a + 1}, d ∈{b, b + 1},
and M∆[a, b] = M∆[c, d] = 0. They prove that ddF (P, Q) ≤∆if and only if there exists
a monotone path from (1, 1) to (n, m), i.e., iff there is a path from (1, 1) to (n, m) in the
constructed graph. Checking for the existence of such a path can be done in O(nm) time.
Switching cells.
There is an alternative approach for solving the decision variant. We call a
cell M∆[i, j] a switching cell [38] if M∆[i, j] = 0 and either M∆[i −1, j] = 1, M∆[i + 1, j] = 1,
or (i, j) lies on the boundary of M∆. Let S∆be the set of all switching cells and let f(S∆)
denote the time it takes to calculate the set S∆. One can decide whether ddF (P, Q) ≤∆in
O(|S∆| + f(S∆)) time as follows (see Figure 3). Firstly, we calculate the set of switching cells
using f(S∆) time. For each row, we probe one cell between every pair of consecutive switching
cells to identify all maximal horizontal intervals of zeroes. Two intervals are adjacent if they
occur in consecutive rows and their horizontal intervals overlap. A depth-first search on
this adjacency graph, when correctly accounting for monotonicity, determines whether a
monotone path exists from (1, 1) to (n, m) in M∆.

Approximate deciders.
Given ∆≥0 and ε > 0, an approximate decider correctly outputs
either ddF (P, Q) ≤∆(1 + ε) or ddF (P, Q) > ∆. Note that the algorithm may return either
outcome if ddF (P, Q) ∈(∆, (1 + ε)∆]. A sufficiently efficient approximate decider yields
(1 + ε)-approximation [20]. We construct such a decider using simplified curves.
Simplified curves
We view P as a curve, that is, as a continuous map P : [1, n] →Rd.
Driemel, Har-Peled, and Wenk [20] define a simplified curve Pα by some increasing sequence
Σ of integers it ∈[1, n] such that Pα is a sequence of points P(it) for it ∈Σ. Fix some
α, initialise i = 0, and add the vertex P(0) as the first vertex of Pα. We then traverse P
starting at P(i) until the first vertex P(i′) that lies outside the ball B(P(i), α) (see Figure
4). Add the vertex P(i′) to Pα, update i to i′, and repeat the process starting at the new i
until we reach i = n, which is added to Pα regardless. The resulting curve Pα consists of
edges of length at least α, except for possibly the last edge. Since all edges of P have unit
length, Pα has O(n/α) vertices. By construction, ddF (P, Pα) ≤α [20].
We choose α = ∆ε/2. While constructing the simplified curve Pα, we furthermore
maintain a table T of size n that records for each vertex P(k) of P the edge (P(it), P(it+1))
with k ∈[it, it+1). We say that this is the edge that P(k) is simplified to.

B
Generalisations
We briefly note that we believe that it is possible to extend our lower and upper bounds
to any d-dimensional regular tiling under the shortest path metric. Whilst we will not
provide a formal proof, nor a conclusive theorem, we observe that any tiling provides the
necessary properties for our lower bound. Specifically, we observe that it is possible to define
r-diagonals in any regular tiling (we elaborate slightly in the next subsection).
For our upper bound, we note that in any regular d-dimensional tiling, that for any ball
B(q, ∆) around a centre q with radius ∆, there are at most O(∆d−1) edges in a regular
tiling that have an endpoint incident to the boundary of B(q, ∆). Thus, we obtain the same
upper bound of having at most O(∆d−1λ) switching cells per column in our free-space matrix
and an upper bound algorithm with the same asymptotic complexity can follow from this
argument.

Proof. We will show that under the ℓ1-norm (resp. ℓ∞-norm) we can transform the paths P
and Q in such a way, that the discrete and continuous distances perfectly coincide.
Classically, the continuous Fréchet distance is realized by one of two events [2]: Either
a vertex-vertex distance, or a so-called monotonicity event defined by two vertices p and q,
and an edge e, where the monotonicity event is realized by the point along e for which the
distance to p and q is the same. In particular, this is realized by the intersection of e with
the bisector of p and q.
Now, as p, q and e are restricted to the grid-graph, e is a unit-edge. Let s and t be
the vertices of e. Similarly, p and q are vertices of the grid-graph. Suppose neither s nor t
are the point m ∈e for which dist(m, p) = dist(m, q). Then, by the nature of the ℓ1-norm
(resp. ℓ∞-norm) m must be exactly the mid-point of e. Hence we can transform P and Q by
bisecting every edge into two equally long edges. The resulting P and Q lie on a grid-graph
with half the edge length of that of the original grid-graph, and can after a scaling transform
be made part of the grid-graph. By the above discussion the discrete and continuous Fréchet
distance coincide, hence Theorem 17 concludes the proof.
