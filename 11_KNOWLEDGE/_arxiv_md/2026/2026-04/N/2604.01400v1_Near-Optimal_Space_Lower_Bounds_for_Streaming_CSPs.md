Near-Optimal Space Lower Bounds for Streaming CSPs
Yumou Fei∗
Dor Minzer†
Shuo Wang‡
Abstract
In a streaming constraint satisfaction problem (streaming CSP), a p-pass algorithm receives
the constraints of an instance sequentially, making p passes over the input in a fixed order,
with the goal of approximating the maximum fraction of satisfiable constraints. We show near
optimal space lower bounds for streaming CSPs, improving upon prior works:
1. Fei, Minzer and Wang (STOC 2026) showed that for any CSP, the basic linear program
defines a threshold αLP ∈[0, 1] such that, for any ε > 0, an (αLP −ε)-approximation can
be achieved using constant passes and polylogarithmic space, whereas achieving (αLP +ε)-
approximation requires Ω(n1/3/p) space. We improve this lower bound to Ω(√n/p), which
is nearly tight for a gap version of the problem.
2. For p = o(log n), we further strengthen the lower bound to Ω(n · 2−Oε(p)). Combined
with existing algorithmic results, this shows that αLP is not only the limit of multi-pass
polylogarithmic-space algorithms, but also the limit of single-pass sublinear-space algo-
rithms on bounded-degree instances.
3. For certain CSPs, we show that there exists α < 1 such that achieving an α-approximation
requires Ω(n/p) space.
Our proofs are Fourier analytic, building on the techniques of Fei, Minzer and Wang (STOC
2026) and the Fourier-ℓ1-based lower bound method of Kapralov and Krachun (STOC 2019).

Introduction
Constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) are among the most well studied problems in theoretical
computer science. Traditionally, they have been studied in the context of polynomial time com-
putation, and by now the tractability of their decision version [Bul17, Zhu20], as well as of their
approximation version [Rag08], are (for the most part) well understood. Over the last decade CSPs
have gained significant interest in the streaming community, with the goal of understanding what
approximation ratios efficient streaming algorithms can achieve. This is the subject of the current
paper, and our main result is a quantitatively stronger (and nearly optimal in some sense) space
lower bound for the classes of single pass and multi-pass streaming algorithms for CSPs.
1.1
Constraint Satisfaction Problems in the Streaming Model
A single-pass, space S streaming algorithm receives its input as a stream, and upon viewing each
element it is allowed to modify its memory arbitrarily. At the end of the stream, the algorithm is
supposed to output an answer. The study of CSPs in the streaming setting started in [KKS15],
who considered the max-cut problem. In this problem, the stream consists of the edges of some
graph G, and the algorithm is supposed to estimate the size of the largest cut in G. There is a
trivial O(log n) space algorithm achieving approximation ratio 1/2, and the work [KKS15] shows
that for all ε > 0, achieving approximation ratio 1/2 + ε requires Ωε(√n) memory.
The work of [KKS15] inspired a flourish of activity in the study of the performance of stream-
ing algorithms on optimization problems; see [GVV17, KK19, CGV20, AKSY20, AN21, CGS+22,
SSSV23, HSV24, CGSV24, SSSV25, FMW25a, FMW25b, STV25, Vel25, ABFS25] and [Sud22,
Ass23, Sin25] for surveys. All of these works consider constraint satisfaction problems, which is a
rich class of problems extending the max-cut problem, defined as follows.
Definition 1.1. Let k ∈N be a parameter, Σ be a finite alphabet and F ⊆{f : Σk →{0, 1}} be
a family of predicates. An instance I = (V, C) of CSP(F) (we write I ∈CSP(F)) consists of a set
of variables V and a collection of constraints C = (C1, . . . , Cm). Each constraint Ci is specified as
((vi,1, . . . , vi,k), fi), where vi,j ∈V is a variable for j = 1, . . . , k, and fi ∈F is a predicate.

All prior works on streaming CSPs either consider a specific problem (such as max-cut or max-

class of streaming algorithms on them (such as sketching algorithms, single-pass algorithms, mult-

of [CGSV24] gave an exact characterization of the power of the class of sketching algorithms, which
is a subclass of single-pass streaming algorithms. Another example, which is most relevant to our
paper, is the work of [KK19], who improved the result of [KKS15] and showed that any single-pass,
(1/2+ε)-approximation streaming algorithm for max-cut requires Ωε(n) memory. In that work the
authors show how to replace the L2-based lower bound approach of [KKS15] by a Fourier-ℓ1-based
lower bound approach, which gives better quantitative bounds.

Discussion: Approximation Resistance
A specific question that has guided prior research in streaming approximability of CSPs is approxi-
mation resistance. To explain this notion, we define for any predicate family F ⊆{f : Σk →{0, 1}}
the threshold

Before this work, it was not clear whether the lower bound in Theorem 1.7 can be improved. For
the specific problem of max-cut,2 [KK19] proved that any single-pass streaming algorithm solving

predicate families that satisfy a certain condition (stronger than supporting one-wise independence).
However, they were not able to generalize their lower bound to all predicate families that support
one-wise independence, due to technical complications (as explained in [CGS+22, Section 1.4]).
One of the technical components (see the discussion at the end of Section 1.3.4) in the proof of
Theorem 1.2 is to address these complications and generalize [KK19]’s result to all predicate families
supporting one-wise independence.

Theorem 1.7:
Theorem 1.8. Suppose F ⊆{f : Σk →{0, 1}} is a predicate family such that every predicate
f ∈F supports one-wise independence (as formally defined in Section 2.1). Then for any ε > 0,
any single-pass streaming algorithm solving MaxCSP(F)[1, ρ(F)+ε] must use Ωε(n) bits of memory.
Remark 1.9. Theorem 1.2 actually implies that this approximation resistance also holds against,
say, o(log n)-pass n0.99-space, or no(1)-pass n0.49-space streaming algorithms.
1.2.2
Discussion: Query-to-Communication Lifting
The dichotomy results of [Yos11, FMW25b] revealed a phenomenological connection between the
multi-pass streaming model and the “bounded-degree query model” from the property testing
literature. We do not attempt to fully elaborate on this connection here.3 Instead, we provide an
informal account of how it relates to the present work.
A standard approach to proving multi-pass streaming lower bounds for MaxCSP(F)[c, s] is to
consider a corresponding communication problem. In this formulation, a constant number of players
each hold a sequence of constraints over a common set of variables, and their goal is to distinguish
whether the value of the combined CSP instance is at least c or at most s. If this communication
problem requires f(n) bits of communication, then a standard reduction implies that any p-pass
streaming algorithm for MaxCSP(F)[c, s] must use Ω(f(n)/p) bits of space.
In communication complexity, a successful paradigm (e.g., [RM99, GPW17, GPW18]) is to first
establish a query lower bound for a suitably defined “query version” of the problem,4 and then
“lift” this bound to the communication setting.
Interestingly, the bounded-degree query model studied in [Yos11] serves as an appropriate query
analogue of the communication problem for MaxCSP(F)[c, s]. In this model, [Yos11] shows that for
any ε > 0, achieving an (αLP + ε)-approximation for CSP(F) requires Ωε(√n) queries. The work
of [FMW25b] lifts this query lower bound to a communication lower bound of Ωε(n1/3), thereby
yielding an Ω(n1/3/p) space lower bound for p-pass streaming algorithms. However, their lifting
incurs a loss in the exponent, reducing it from 1/2 to 1/3. Our Theorem 1.2 can be viewed as a
refinement of their query-to-communication lifting that avoids this polynomial loss.
Conversely, algorithms in the query model can usually be simulated by multi-pass streaming
algorithms. For example, the bipartiteness tester of [GR99, KKR04] implies the following result:5

1.2.3
Beyond √n Queries
There are also some MaxCSP problems for which the query complexity in the bounded-degree
query model is known to be Ω(n). For example, for the predicate family E3Lin6, [BOT02] showed
that MaxE3Lin[1, 1/2 + ε] requires Ωε(n) queries. By slightly extending our techniques for proving
Theorem 1.2, we are also able to lift [BOT02]’s query lower bound to a communication lower bound,
yielding the following result:
Theorem 1.11. Suppose F ⊆{f : Σk →{0, 1}} is a predicate family such that every predicate
f ∈F supports two-wise independence (as formally defined in Section 2.1). Then for any ε > 0,
any p-pass streaming algorithm solving MaxCSP(F)[1, ρ(F) + ε] must use Ωε(n/p) bits of memory.
A recent work [Fei25] showed that for any predicate family that has unbounded width,7 there
exists ε > 0 such that MaxCSP(F)[1, 1 −ε] requires Ωε(n) queries in the bounded-degree query
model. Using the same techniques, it is not hard to lift this lower bound also to the multi-pass
streaming model (answering [Fei25, Question 1.11]):
Theorem 1.12. For any predicate family F ⊆{f : Σk →{0, 1}} that has unbounded width, there
exists a constant ε > 0 such that any p-pass streaming algorithm for MaxCSP(F)[1, 1 −ε] must use
at least Ω(n/p) bits of memory.

The issue is that if we only consider the number of exposed edges in the structured part, we
are unable to tell the difference between a collection of t disjoint edges being exposed, and

with small discrepancy instead of considering the number of edges exposed.

Improving the Discrepancy Bound
To address the first issue raised at the end of Section 1.3.2, we again draw inspiration from [KK19].
The central argument of [KK19] analyzes how the distribution of the hidden partition x ∈ZV×[n]

By controlling the Fourier-ℓ1 mass of the probability density function of x, [KK19] improved the
single-pass space lower bound for MaxCut[1, 1/2+ε] from Ω(√n) (obtained in [KKS15] via Fourier-ℓ2
methods) to Ω(n).
Our situation appears analogous to the state of affairs prior to the breakthrough of [KK19].
In particular, we seek to strengthen the multi-pass space lower bound for MaxCut[1, 1/2 + ε] from
Ω(n1/3/p), as established in [FMW25a] using Fourier-ℓ2 techniques, to the near-optimal Ω(√n/p).
It is thus natural to suspect the Fourier-ℓ1-based techniques of [KK19] could be used to overcome
the first issue mentioned in Section 1.3.2, and yield a near-optimal multi-pass space lower bound.
However, it is not immediately clear how the techniques of [KK19] can be extended to establish
multi-pass streaming lower bounds. At a high level, two main obstacles arise:
1. To control the evolution of the Fourier-ℓ1 mass of the density function of x, [KK19] applies an
induction on the number of rounds in the protocol. At low Fourier levels, the ℓ1-mass bound
deteriorates by a constant factor with each additional message. While this degradation is
tolerable in the single-pass setting — where there are only a constant number of players,
each speaking at most once — it becomes prohibitive in the multi-pass setting, as it leads to
an exponential dependence on the number of rounds (and hence on the number of passes).
Consequently, this approach cannot yield an Ω(√n/p)-type lower bound.
2. The induction argument in [KK19] also critically relies on the fact that each player’s message
is generated from a fresh random labeled matching that is independent of the prior commu-
nication transcript. This independence assumption breaks down in the multi-pass setting,
where players may speak multiple times.
The strength of the structure-vs.-randomness framework of [FMW25a, FMW25b] lies precisely in
its ability to handle such obstacles. From the perspective of this framework, however, the argu-
ment of [KK19] has an intriguing feature: it does not rely on any explicit decomposition of the
players’ messages into “structured” and “pseudorandom” components. As noted in Section 1.3.3,
[KK19] does carry out a combinatorial analysis of the (highly structured) edge-exposure protocol
using the notion of weight ∥ζ∥. Importantly, this analysis is not invoked in their treatment of
general single-pass protocols. Instead, the Fourier-ℓ1 method developed in [KK19] can be viewed
as a generalization of this combinatorial analysis: it provides a unified approach that simultane-
ously captures both structured information and pseudorandom noise, which may coexist in general
protocols.
The key idea enabling us to incorporate the techniques of [KK19] into the framework of
[FMW25b] is to apply the Fourier-ℓ1-based induction argument of [KK19] only to the pseudo-
random component of each player’s message.
We begin by using the decomposition technique
from [FMW25b, Section 7] to cleanly separate the structured information in the messages from
the pseudorandom noise. The structured components of all players’ messages are then aggregated
and analyzed via the combinatorial method described in Section 1.3.3, while the pseudorandom
components are handled using the Fourier-ℓ1-based induction argument.
This approach overcomes the two high-level obstacles discussed above. Nevertheless, several
technical issues must still be addressed:

1.4
Open Problems
We finish this introductory section with a few open directions for future research.
1. While the multi-pass lower bound provided in Theorem 1.2 is tight in general, it can be
improved for some problems as in Theorems 1.11 and 1.12. It would be interesting to know if
there are a natural criteria dictating whether the space-pass tradeoff is eΘ(√n) or eΘ(n), and
even more interesting if there are intermediate behaviors. Perhaps the “query version” of this
question has to be answered first; see [Fei25, Question 1.13].
2. The algorithm (Theorem 1.6) of [SSSV25] that complements Corollary 1.5 only works for
bounded degree instances, because we cannot afford the degree-reduction step in [FMW25b]
as it requires more than a single pass. The recent work [ABFS25] shows that the bounded-
degree assumption in Theorem 1.6 can be removed for the specific problem of max-directed-
cut. For general CSPs, it remains open whether there is a single-pass n1−Ωε(1)-space algorithm
for (αLP(F) −ε)-approximation of CSP(F).
3. As noted in the first item above, it is unknown whether the space-pass tradeoff grows
from eΘ(√n) to eΘ(n) continuously or discretely as a function of the approximation ratio
α ∈[αLP(F), 1]. In the single-pass setting, the mystery lies below the LP threshold αLP(F):
for the specific problem of max-directed-cut (whose LP threshold equals 1/2), [CGV20] shows
that (4/9−ε)-approximation can be achieved by single-pass Oε(log n)-space algorithms, while
(4/9 + ε)-approximation requires Ωε(√n)-space. As shown by [KK19], achieving (1/2 + ε)-
approximation of max-directed-cut in the single-pass setting requires Ωε(n) space. It is un-
known whether the space complexity grows continuously as a function of the approximation
ratio α ∈[4/9, 1/2]. For general CSPs, it is also unknown whether there is a critical approx-
imation ratio at which the single-pass space complexity jumps from polylog(n) to nΩ(1).

Communication Lower Bound for DIHP
This section is devoted to the proofs of Theorems 3.14 and 3.17. As in [FMW25a, FMW25b], the
argument follows the standard structure-vs.-randomness framework in communication complexity,
and consists of the two main steps:
1. Given a protocol Π with low communication cost, the first step is to decompose each rectangle
induced by Π into smaller subrectangles.
Following [FMW25a, FMW25b], we show that
after this decomposition, most rectangles are “good”, in the sense that each encodes a well-
structured piece of information together with a controlled form of pseudorandom noise.
A key difference from [FMW25a, FMW25b] is that in our setting the goodness of these
rectangles must be established under two distinct sets of assumptions.
For the proof of
Theorem 3.14, we assume |Π| = o(√n).
In contrast, for the proof of Theorem 3.17, we
assume |Π| = o(n) and additionally require that Π uses few communication rounds.
The “decomposition lemma” needed under the first assumption was already established in
[FMW25b, Lemma 6.11]. Under the second set of assumptions, however, a new decomposition
lemma is required; this is provided by Lemma 4.14.
2. The second step is to show for each “good” rectangle R a discrepancy bound of the form12
Dyes(R) ⩾(1 −10−3) · Dno(R).
This is provided by our “discrepancy lemma,” Lemma 4.15. The “goodness” assumption in
our discrepancy lemma is weaker than its counterparts in [FMW25a, FMW25b], which is the
key reason we are able to obtain improved communication lower bounds.
These two steps are then combined to complete the proof of the communication lower bounds.
The new decomposition lemma (Lemma 4.14) will be proved in Section 5; its proof combines
ideas in [KK19, Section 5.2] and [FMW25b, Appendix A]. The proof of the discrepancy lemma
(Lemma 4.15) will take up Sections 6 and 7, where we combine ideas from [KK19, Section 7] and
[FMW25b, Section 7]. This section is devoted to laying out the overarching framework that connects
these components.
In particular, we formalize the notion of “good” rectangles in Sections 4.1

Lemma 4.13, we showed that the rectangle decomposition induced by any protocol Π with
