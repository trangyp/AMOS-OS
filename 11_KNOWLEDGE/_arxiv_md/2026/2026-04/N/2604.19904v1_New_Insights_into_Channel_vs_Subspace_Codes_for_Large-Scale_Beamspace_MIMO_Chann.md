New Insights into Channel vs Subspace Codes for
Large-Scale Beamspace MIMO Channel Sensing
Parthasarathi Khirwadkar, Robin Rajam¨aki, and Piya Pal
Abstract—This paper provides novel insights into channel
and subspace codes in nonadaptive channel sensing with a
single RF chain. Observing that this problem naturally maps
to a noncoherent decoding problem, we show that the sensing
performance of the maximum likelihood (ML) angle estimator—
which does not require knowledge of the typically unknown
channel coefficient—is governed by two key terms: the minimum
subspace distance and beam gain of the used beamformers.
We derive an exact expression for the subspace distance of
binary linear channel codes mapped to BPSK, which illuminates
the relationship between subspace and Hamming distance—
used to design subspace and channel codes, respectively. Our
result also reveals why good Hamming distance alone is in-
sufficient for sensing, and shows that well-known families of
channel codes such as Reed-Muller codes, yield zero subspace
distance and thereby poor sensing performance when used
naively without proper codebook pruning. Finally, we introduce
so-called beamspace subspace codes based on sparse antenna
selection patterns (Golomb rulers), which we show provide near-
optimal subspace distance. We demonstrate that this property
of judiciously designed sparse arrays can be leveraged together
with beamforming gain via convolutional beamspaces, enabling
hardware- and sample-efficient channel sensing with theoretical
guarantees in large-scale multiantenna communications.
Index Terms—Channel sensing; Subspace codes; Sparse ar-
rays; Reed-Muller Codes; Low hardware complexity
I. INTRODUCTION
Sensing will play a key role in 6G and beyond wire-
less networks due to the emergence of integrated sensing
and communications (ISAC) [1]–[3] and the utilization of
ever higher frequency bands [4], including millimeter-wave
(mmWave) bands [5]. These multiple-input multiple-output
(MIMO) systems are expected to leverage increasingly larger
antenna arrays [6], as well as advanced modulation and coding
techniques to meet stringent demands on spectral, energy and
cost efficiency [7].
The shift to higher frequencies and massive antenna arrays
presents pertinent new challenges, such as acquiring accurate
channel state information and managing pilot overhead [8]—
issues that demand novel low-complexity hardware solutions
and low-overhead channel estimation. A fundamental chal-
lenge is estimating angular parameters of channel paths (or
radar targets in ISAC) under hardware constraints. Indeed, due

Hence, a key challenge in 6G and beyond wireless networks
is designing sample-efficient channel estimation (beamform-

Several works address the channel sensing problem un-
der hardware constraints such as a limited number of RF
chains. In the non-adaptive setting, compressed-sensing-based
approaches have been proposed [11]–[13] to exploit the sparse
structure of mmWave channels, avoiding exhaustive beam
sweep and enabling sample-efficient sensing. Similarly, an-
tenna selection has recently been considered for channel sens-
ing and ISAC [14]–[16], which opens up exciting possibilities
for leveraging sparse arrays to improve spatial resolution
and identifiability [17]–[19]. Another line of research focuses
on adaptive channel sensing [20]–[22], which leverage feed-
back from previously acquired measurements to refine future
sensing decisions, thereby tailoring the probing process to
the channel conditions. More recently, deep learning–based
methods have also been proposed in both non-adaptive sensing
[21], [23], [24], e.g., to design site-specific codebooks [24],
and adaptive settings [25], [26] to learn sensing policies
directly from data.
A noteworthy body of recent works have explored tackling
the channel sensing problem with a single RF-chain using
ideas from error correcting codes (ECCs) [27]–[30]. These
works leverage the inherent error corrective capabilities of
EECs for improving sensing performance. The authors in [27]–
[30] established a connection between channel coding and
direction-of-arrival (DoA) estimation by using ECCs to embed
algebraic structure into beamformers. This structure enhances
robustness to noise in estimating DoA from received measure-
ments. In particular, the approaches in [28]–[30] assign each
angle (assumed to belong to a discrete grid) a unique codeword
which are collected into a codebook matrix. This codebook
matrix is then utilized to design beamformers for channel
sensing. In particular, [30] uses BPSK modulation to design
the beamformers such that its phase response is constructed

using Reed-Muller codes. Compared to compressed sensing-

framework provides two key advantages. First, it enables
deterministic design of the beamforming vectors, eliminating
the need for random beam patterns. Second, it allows the
derivation of performance guarantees grounded in the prop-

A separate body of work in signal processing has exploited
the structure of convolutional beamspaces in order to harness
the benefits offered by both antenna-space and beamspace
processing for channel sensing. Originally introduced in [31],
CBS constructs beamspace transformations through linear
shifts of a base beamforming filter. This approach preserves the
Vandermonde structure in the beamspace domain, enabling di-
rect application of classical subspace-based direction-of-arrival
(DoA) estimation methods to the transformed measurements.
Further expanding the CBS framework, [32] investigates its
utility in channel sensing under both uniform and non-uniform
decimation scenarios, while [33], [34] designed a two stage
strategy with CBS for improve angular resolution. More re-
cently, [35], [36] applied the CBS framework to the design
of beamforming and precoding matrices in mmWave channel
estimation as well as in near-field communication systems,
demonstrating its flexibility and scalability in emerging wire-
less communication scenarios.
Subspace Coding perspective and Open Questions: Despite
recent advances in ECC and CBS for beamspace channel
sensing with reduced RF chains, several questions regarding
their optimal design and performance remain unaddressed.
While ECCs have shown promising performance in sensing,
there is currently no general theory of which ECCs are suitable
for sensing. Indeed, as the performance of ECCs in their
designated task of detecting and correcting errors is typically
controlled by the Hamming distance between codewords, the
question arises whether codebooks with desirable Hamming
distance are also suitable for sensing? Similarly, the shift
structures of CBS and their impact on (maximum likelihood)
channel estimation error has not been analyzed. Such an
analysis is important for optimizing and adopting CBS in
emerging wireless applications.
A parallel line of recent work [37], inspired by so-called
analog subspace codes [38], has shown that the canonical
antenna-space sensing problem can be naturally re-formulated
as a subspace coding problem, since the unknown angles of
interest are encoded in the subspace spanned by the antenna
measurements. A key insight of this work is that the optimal
angle estimation performance of the maximum likelihood
decoder is controlled by the so-called minimum subspace
distance among all pairs of codewords (angles). This begs the
question: “How is subspace distance connected to Hamming
distance?” Furthermore, the choice of array geometry was
shown to control subspace distance in antenna-domain sensing
[37]. In particular, [37] showed that judiciously designed
sparse arrays provide superior subspace distance, and thereby
error performance, compared to conventional uniform arrays.
However, extending these results to the beamspace, especially
with fewer RF chains than antennas is a challenging task,
where many open questions remain, such as “What are
good sensing subspace codes for beamspace measurement
models?”, and “Can sparse array designs be leveraged to
improve subspace distance in this case as well?” Moreover,
hardware-constrained channel sensing naturally gives rise to
spatial selectivity and the question: “What is the impact of
beamforming on subspace distance and can sensing subspace
codes be designed to harness this beamgain?” As discussed
next, our contributions address these questions while providing
a unified treatment of both channel codes and CBS-based
beamformer design through the lens of beamspace subspace
coding.
A. Contributions
This paper investigates the fundamental relations between
channel and subspace codes in massive MIMO channel sensing
using a single RF chain by analyzing the Maximum Likelihood
(ML) decoder. Our results reveal several novel insights into
how the choice of the (nonadaptive) beamforming matrix—in
particular, its so-called minimum subspace distance—impacts
the performance of optimal angle estimation (ML decoding).
We further show that when channel codes are used for
designing beamformers, one has to consider the equivalent
subspace codes induced by them and focus on optimizing
the corresponding subspace distance (instead of Hamming
distance). Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Role of subspace distance in ML angle estimation: We
observe that the channel sensing problem naturally maps
to a noncoherent (de)coding problem, since the channel
coefficient α (nuisance parameter) is typically unknown,
and the ML estimator of the DoA (parameter of interest)
does not need to know α. This highlights the relevance of
sensing subspace codes [37] in the context of beamspace
channel sensing. We reveal the explicit interaction be-
tween two key terms controlling an upper bound on the
error exponent of the ML estimator (of the unknown
DoA): the minimum subspace distance and the beam
gain of the used beamforming matrix. Jointly designing
these two terms is crucial for achieving optimal sensing
performance. While this is a challenging task in general
with no known solutions, our results offer new insights
and near optimal designs when we restrict ourselves to
two families of beamformers, namely spatially isotropic
beams (compatible with channel coding based designs)
and convolutional beamspace.
• Subspace distance of channel codes: We derive an exact
expression for the minimum subspace distance of binary
linear (channel sensing) codes mapped to binary phase-
shift keying (BPSK) constellations, and establish that
such codes correspond to spatially isotropic filters. Our
results reveal an insightful connection between the sub-
space distance and the Hamming distance of the code, and
explains why good Hamming distance is not enough for
sensing, providing important guidelines for code selection
and codebook pruning. We specialize our results to Reed-
Muller codes, proving that these yield zero subspace
distance (when all codewords are used) and are hence
poorly suited for sensing when applied naively. This

III. NEW INSIGHTS INTO CHANNEL CODING BASED
SPATIALLY ISOTROPIC BEAMFORMERS
In recent work, Reed-Muller channel codes have been
utilized to design (non adaptive) beamformers for millimeter-
wave channel sensing [30]. In particular, these binary codes are
first mapped to a BPSK constellation and then these modulated
codewords are used to design the corresponding beamformers.
As we will show next, these codes naturally give rise to
spatially isotropic beamformers. We will therefore focus on the
minimum distance of the induced subspace codes, and relate
it to the Hamming distance of the underlying channel codes,
highlighting important distinctions between the two. These
results will shed new light into the efficacy of channel codes
(in particular Reed-Muller) for designing beamspace subspace
codes. As another example of isotropic beamformers, we will
investigate pure antenna selection strategies and show how
they produce near optimum d(s)
min(C(W, S)) in certain regimes
of T and Ng.

VI. CONCLUSIONS
This work explored the roles channel and subspace codes
in hardware-constrained nonadaptive channel sensing. This
problem naturally maps to a beamspace subspace (de)coding
problem, since knowledge of the unknown channel coefficient
is not needed by the ML estimator of the angular parameter
of interest. We demonstrated that sensing performance is
fundamentally governed by the minimum subspace distance
of the employed beamforming matrix, rather than the Ham-
ming distance of the associated code. This insight challenges
the direct use of channel codes for beamformer design and
highlights the need to consider their induced subspace prop-
erties. We provided a closed-form expression for the subspace
distance of BPSK-mapped binary linear codes, shedding light
on the role of the Hamming distance of such codes in
sensing. Our analysis shows that certain widely-used codes,
such as Reed-Muller, are poorly suited for sensing without
appropriate codebook pruning due to the presence of com-
plementary codewords in the codebook. Finally, we showed
that beamspace sensing subspace codes based on sparse arrays
and convolutional beamspaces offer both beamforming gain
and near-optimal subspace distance. Our work gives rise to
several new questions regarding the design of beamformers
with jointly optimal beam gain and subspace distance, pruned
channel sensing codebooks, as well as associated modulation
schemes. Exploring these questions will facilitate resource-
efficient channel sensing with theoretical guarantees in future
wireless networks employing large-scale multiantenna arrays.
