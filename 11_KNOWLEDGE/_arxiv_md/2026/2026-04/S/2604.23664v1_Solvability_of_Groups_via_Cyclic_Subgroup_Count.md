SOLVABILITY OF GROUPS VIA CYCLIC SUBGROUP COUNT
ANGSUMAN DAS AND KHYATI SHARMA
Abstract. In this paper, we provide new criteria for the solvability and supersolvability of
a finite group based on its number of cyclic subgroups. A finite group G is called n-cyclic if
it contains n cyclic subgroups. This paper also partially extends the classification of n-cyclic
groups for n ≥13.
1. Introduction
A major goal in the study of finite groups is to understand how far the structure of a
group can be recovered from the partial information attached to it. In many cases, quantities
obtained by counting certain algebraic objects or by studying probabilistic features of the group
can reveal important structural properties. This approach is very useful in finite group theory,
mainly because such invariants are often much easier to handle than more complex information,
such as the entire subgroup lattice or the character table.
Several important examples show the strength of this approach.
For instance, the sum
of element orders and the average element order were first introduced by Amiri, Amiri, and
Isaacs [1]. Later, these quantities were further studied deeply in connection with detecting
structural properties of finite groups [12, 3, 22, 13]. Another quantity considered in this con-
text is the order sequence [4]. Most recently, Lucchini [15] characterized solvability using the
nilpotent probability, that is, the probability that two randomly chosen elements of a group G
generate a nilpotent subgroup. In fact, one of the main objectives behind this study is to give
new criteria for the solvability of finite groups.
From this perspective, subgroup-based invariants arise quite naturally. A notable contribu-
tion in this direction is due to Das and Mandal [6], who obtained several criteria for solvability,
supersolvability, and nilpotency of a finite group by using subgroup counting. Their work shows
that subgroup counting, despite its elementary nature, can yield important structural conclu-
sions. However, counting all subgroups is often difficult in practice, which makes it desirable
to look for more accessible alternatives.
A natural choice is to focus on cyclic subgroups. They are among the simplest subgroups
of a finite group, yet their number often reflects meaningful information about the whole
group [9]. Moreover, the number of cyclic subgroups satisfies useful extremal bounds. A result
of Richards [18] states that if G is a finite group of order n, then c(G) ≥d(n), where c(G)
denotes the number of cyclic subgroups of G and d(n) is the number of positive divisors of n.
Moreover, equality holds if and only if G is cyclic. On the other hand, it is easy to see that
c(G) ≤|G|, and equality holds if and only if G is an elementary abelian 2-group. Thus, unlike
the counting of all subgroups, the number of cyclic subgroups comes with explicit lower and
upper bounds, making it more manageable.
This viewpoint has also led to several classification results. Recall that a finite group G
is called n-cyclic if it has exactly n cyclic subgroups. Zhou [26], Kalra [14], and Ashrafi and
Haghi [2] classified all n-cyclic groups for 3 ≤n ≤10. Reddy and Sharma [19, 20] classified
all n-cyclic groups for n = 11 and 12. Now, the natural question is: how is this counting

In this section, we prove that if 1 ≤c(G) ≤49 and c(G) ̸= 32, 49, then G is solvable. We
also classify the unique non-solvable groups with exactly 32 or 49 cyclic subgroups.
Lemma 3.1. Let G be a finite minimal simple group. Then c(G) ≥32 and equality holds if
and only if G ∼= PSL(2, 4) or A5.
Proof. By using the classification of finite simple groups, G is isomorphic to one of the following.
(1) PSL(2, 2p), where p is a prime.
(2) PSL(2, 3p), where p is an odd prime.
(3) PSL(2, p), where p > 3 is a prime such that 5 | p2 + 1.
(4) The Suzuki group Sz(2p), where p is an odd prime.
(5) PSL(3, 3).
If G ∼= PSL(2, 2p), where p is a prime, then except for p = 2 the number of involutions
in G ≥63 using Lemma 2.5. For p = 2, G = PSL(2, 4) or A5 and c(G) = 32. Again if
G = PSL(2, 3p), where p is an odd prime or G = PSL(2, p), where p > 3 is a prime such that
5 | p2 + 1, then also either the number of involutions in G are more than 32 using Lemma 2.6
or G ∼= PSL(2, 7). By simple calculation, we can check that c(PSL(2, 7)) = 79. If G = Sz(2p),
where p is an odd prime, or G = PSL(3, 3) then the |G| is 22p(22p + 1)(2p −1) or 24 · 33 · 5
respectively. By Richard’s theorem [18] c(G) > 32. Hence, the result holds.

Theorem 3.3. Let G be a group such that c(G) < 32, then G is solvable.
Proof. Let G be a minimum ordered non-solvable group such that c(G) < 32. We first show
that G must be simple.
It is easy to see that all proper subgroups of G are solvable, because G is the minimum
ordered group satisfying the above condition. If H is a proper normal subgroup of G, then
G/H must be non-solvable. Now, by the minimality of |G|, we have c(G/H) ≥32, which is
a contradiction by Lemma 2.3. Thus, G has no proper normal subgroup, and G is simple.
Among all finite simple groups, A5 has the least number of cyclic subgroups by Lemma 3.2.
Therefore, c(G) ≥32. Hence, the result holds.
□
Theorem 3.4. Let G be a non-solvable group such that c(G) = 32, then G ∼= A5.
Proof. Since G is non-solvable, then G has subgroups H and N such that N ⊴H and H/N
is a minimal simple group. If c(H/N) ≥33, then c(G) ≥33, which is a contradiction. Thus
c(H/N) = 32. If H is a proper subgroup of G, then c(G) ≥c(H) + 1 ≥c(H/N) + 1 = 33,
which is a contradiction. Therefore G = H and c(G/N) = 32. If N is a non-trivial subgroup of
G, then c(G) ≥c(G/N) + 1 = 33, which is also a contradiction. This implies that N is trivial,
and G/N ∼= G is a minimal simple group. Now, by Lemma 3.1, we get G ∼= A5.
□
