ABSTRACT
Stellar binaries may form through several formation pathways, including disk or core fragmentation.
Their spin-orbit angles are a signature of formation, although individual measurements for visual
binaries are limited and broad. A seminal work by A. Hale (1994) found that visual binaries with
separations ≲30 AU tend to be more aligned, which laid the groundwork for binary formation theories.
However, A. B. Justesen & S. Albrecht (2020) found that underestimated stellar radii lead to inaccurate
spin-orbit angles and that KS statistics do not provide meaningful population-level constraints even
with updated radii. Using a hierarchical Bayesian model to reanalyze their dataset, we find evidence
with a Bayes factor of 12 for two subpopulations of spin-orbit angles separated by a ∼31 −38 AU
cutoff. Binaries inside (outside) the cutoff are more (less) aligned, consistent with a Fisher distribution
with κ = 48 (κ = 6). We also find possible indications of a secondary cutoff at ∼10−17 AU, although
more data is required to resolve this prediction. These cutoffs may mark transitions between formation
pathways: closer-in binaries tend to form aligned in a shared protostellar disk, while wider binaries
tend to form less aligned through turbulent fragmentation.
Keywords: Visual binary stars (1777), Hierarchical models (1925), Astrostatistics (1882)
1. INTRODUCTION
In stellar binaries, the angle between a star’s spin axis
and orbital axis – the spin-orbit angle – is a tracer of
their formation history (M. R. Bate 2012; C. L. Smith
et al. 2024).
Stellar binaries that obtain their spin
and orbital angular momentum through formation in
a shared protostellar disk are expected to exhibit spin-
orbit alignment, while turbulent formation triggered by
gravitational instability can imprint a wide range of
spin-orbit angles (S. S. R. Offner et al. 2016).
An early landmark study by A. Hale (1994) found that
binary stars tend to have aligned spin-orbit angles for
semi-major axes ≲30 −40 AU, and randomly-oriented
angles at wider separations.
This result has become
foundational to our understanding of binary formation

Decades later, a follow-up study by A. B. Justesen &

existence of this semi-major axis cutoff, primarily due

Hale (1994).

tics, JA20 cannot reject either hypotheses that their
data came from random or perfectly aligned popula-
tions. That is, they find inconclusive evidence for an
isotropic or perfectly-aligned distribution of stellar spin-
orbit angles, potentially due to broad uncertainties. If
there is indeed no evidence for the 30 −40 AU cutoff,
this would call into question a widely cited observational
constraint on binary formation models.

2. STATISTICAL MODELING
Individual measurements of spin-orbit angles in visual
binaries are difficult to interpret because only the line-
of-sight component of the true angle in 3D space is ob-
servable (JA20). As a result, we can only determine a
lower limit on their intrinsic alignment or misalignment.
With sufficient data, population-level trends in these
projected angles can distinguish between truly aligned
and misaligned ensembles.
The seminal work of A. Hale (1994) analyzed 45 solar-
type binaries, and found that binaries with separations
< 20 AU exhibit small (< 20◦) line-of-sight projected
spin-orbit angles, while wide binaries with separation
> 30 AU exhibit a wide range of misalignment up to
∼60◦, consistent with random spin-orbit orientations.

3.4. Are there three populations?
We now modify the piecewise model to have two semi-
major axis cutoffs, allowing for three populations of
spin-orbit angles each described by their own κ.
We
refer to this model as the three population model. This
is done by modifying the piecewise condition in Eqn. 4
and the hyperprior Eqn. 5. Informed by the two peaks
in Fig. 5, we set the inner cutoff acutoff,1 = 15 −17 AU
and the outer cutoff acutoff,2 = 31 −38 AU.
In Appendix D, we present the corner plot for the
posterior distribution of this three population model.
Now, we compare the three population model with the
two population (piecewise) model previously developed,
again using the Bayes factor. We find that the Bayes
factor is 1.2, indicating that it is currently not possible
choose between the model with three or two populations.
Therefore, additional data is necessary to answer if there
are more than two spin-orbit angle populations.
4. DISCUSSIONS
4.1. Comparison with A. Hale (1994) and A. B.
Justesen & S. Albrecht (2020)
Comparing our results to A. Hale (1994), we find a
semi-major axis cutoff at acutoff = 31 −38 AU, consis-
tent with the previously proposed cutoff at ∼30 AU.
Furthermore, we likewise find that closer-in binaries are
more aligned. However, A. Hale (1994) found that wider
binaries have projected spin-orbit angles consistent with
isotropy, which we do not find. Our results disagree be-
cause neither the κ1 nor κ2 posterior have significant
probability density for small values (≪1), thus neither
population favors random spin-orbit angles.
In A. B. Justesen & S. Albrecht (2020), the authors
ask if their data is sufficient to reject either null hypothe-
ses, that the spin-orbit angles originate from purely ran-
dom (equivalent to κ = 0 in our formulism) or perfectly
aligned populations (κ →∞).
They found that nei-
ther null hypotheses can be rejected using the KS test,
implying that their data is too noisy.

4.2. Spin-orbit angles in context
To place the spin-orbit angles of visual binaries in con-
text, we compare them with spectroscopic binaries, as-
trometric binaries, and planetary systems.
At the smallest separations (orbital period ∼10 days),
M. L. Marcussen & S. H. Albrecht (2022) find that 40
out of 43 early-type and solar-type spectroscopic bina-
ries are consistent with alignment, although some uncer-
tainties are broad. This population is well represented
by a Fisher distribution with κ = 6.1 (corresponding to
a ψ distribution peaking at 23◦), and their preference
for alignment may be attributed to formation or tidal
interactions. While the JA20 dataset also favors align-
ment within ∼30 AU, such separations are too large for
tidal interactions to play a significant role (C. L. Smith
et al. 2024; M. L. Marcussen et al. 2024).
At intermediate separations (100 −3000 days or sepa-
rations 0.5 −5 AU), C. L. Smith et al. (2024) find that
early-type astrometric binaries have a 75% ± 5% spin-
orbit alignment fraction.
They conclude that aligned
75% population likely formed via disk fragmentation,
where spin-orbit angles are expected to start nearly
aligned, yet post-formation disk accretion may excite
spin-orbit angles moderately.
The remaining 25% in-
consistent with alignment instead likely formed via core
fragmentation and subsequent orbital decay, which nat-
urally misaligned spin-orbit angles.
These formation
pathways are similar to those expected for visual bina-
ries in the JA20 dataset, although weaker orbital decay
may allow them to remain at wider separations.
At wide separations comparable to the JA20 visual
binary dataset, but for planetary-mass companions, M.
Poon et al. (2025) find that spin-orbit angles (or plan-
etary obliquities) are consistent with isotropy, suggest-
ing that turbulent fragmentation may be the dominant
formation pathway.
Our results do not find any sub-
populaton with isotropic spin-orbit angles favored, indi-
cating that either turbulent fragmentation may not be
the dominant formation pathway, post-formation mech-
anisms may align initially misaligned visual binaries, or
that the data is not sufficient to resolve visual binaries
at the widest separations ≳100 AU.
Our analysis strongly suggests that there are at least
two subpopulations of visual binaries, separated by a
∼30 AU cutoff.
One potential interpretation for this
cutoff is that this is the location where different forma-
tion pathways dominate: closer-in binaries form aligned
in a shared protostellar disk, while wider binaries form
misaligned through turbulent fragmentation. Addition-
ally, our analysis hints towards a possible secondary cut-
off at ∼10 −17 AU, although this claim is currently
inconclusive and requires more data to verify.
5. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we present a hierarchical Bayesian model,
capable of inferring multiple sub-populations of stellar
spin-orbit angles (Sec. 2). With this framework, we find
the following results by analyzing the dataset in A. B.
Justesen & S. Albrecht (2020):
• If spin-orbit angles arise from a single one pop-
ulation, we model that population with a Fisher
distribution, yielding a concentration parameter of
κ = 11+10
−6 (Fig. 3). This indicates a moderately-
aligned population (in comparison, κ = 0 signifies
isotropy).
• If spin-orbit angles originate from two populations,
separated at a critical cutoff, then the cutoff is at

• We found that the two population model is

• Although we find some indications of another cut-

of 1.2. This implies that it is inconclusive to de-

current data.
Additional data is required to increase the Bayes fac-

model (a Bayes factor of 100 would be required to con-
clusively decide between the two models).
New data
(e.g., rotation period from TESS photometry for stel-

and rotational broadening from high-resolution spec-
troscopy) will also allow the opportunity to study if
there are more than two subpopulations for the spin-

A. SENSITIVITY ANALYSES
A.1. Orbital Inclination
We test the sensitivity of our results to uncertainties in the orbital inclination (io), since the JA20 dataset does not
report uncertainties on io (see their Table 1). To do this, we inject random Gaussian noise into io for each star in the
sample, adopting standard deviations of σ = 1◦, 5◦, 10◦. We then re-perform the Bayes factor analysis in Sec. 3.2,
which is shown in Fig. 8.
We find that with 1◦and 5◦uncertainty, the shape of the Bayes factor curve remains consistent with the no added
error curve (i.e., there remains a peak for acutoff at 31-38 AU and a secondary peak at ∼10 −17 AU). The confidence
in the model choice is reduced from strong to substantial evidence. Thus, our conclusions remain the same, albeit with
lower evidence.
A.2. Stellar Binaries Near Semimajor Axis Cutoff
We next test the sensitivity of our inferred κ1 and κ2 to potential outliers near acutoff = 31−38 AU, where the Bayes
factor drops steeply from BFpiecewise,single pop. ∼12 to < 1 at 40 −50 AU (Fig. 5).
To assess whether systems near the cutoff affect our conclusion of two subpopulations, we exclude all systems with
separations between 30−60 AU. We then apply the single-population model to each subsample (Fig. 9). The resulting
peak values of κ remain consistent with κ1 and κ2 inferred from the two-population piecewise model, indicating that
our conclusions are not sensitive to potential outliers near the semi-major axis cutoff.
