Origin of Sub-Neptune Aerosol Trends

ABSTRACT
Recent population-level studies of sub-Neptune atmospheres have identified a tentative parabolic
trend in transmission spectrum amplitude for planets with Teq ≈500−800 K. While the trend has
been commonly attributed to hydrocarbon aerosols, we lack a first-principles explanation of its under-
lying chemical mechanism. Previous work has focused on the role of methane photolysis and subsequent
polymerization, but with limited reaction networks that truncated at C2-species and couldn’t reproduce
the observed parabolic trend. In this work, enabled by a computer-automated, rate-based chemical
network generator, we construct the most comprehensive carbon reaction network for exoplanet atmo-
spheres to date. We explicitly model the formation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which
are well established as soot precursors in combustion chemistry. We calculate the chemical timescales
of hydrocarbon species through an eigenvalue timescale method and model their quenched abundances
across a range of C/O, metallicities, and Teq. In this framework, the deep atmosphere acts as a “soot
factory” analogous to a combustion engine, transporting the ingredients for hydrocarbon aerosol for-
mation to the JWST-observable region of the atmosphere, where it may be further augmented by
photochemistry. We find that the predicted abundances of PAHs peak near 600 K, and fall off toward
higher and lower Teq, consistent with the observed muted-spectra regime suggested in observational
studies by HST and JWST. We also show that PAH abundances are expected to vary with C/O and
metallicity, thus providing a natural explanation for observed diversity among planets with similar Teq.

1. INTRODUCTION
The formation of condensed-phase materials sus-
pended in gas, often referred to as aerosols, has been
studied across a wide range of environments, from can-
dle flames, where nanoscale carbonaceous particles form,
grow, and radiate through incandescence (Faraday 1825,
1861); to black smoke emitted by gasoline and diesel
engine exhaust (Clague et al. 1999); to Earth’s wa-
ter clouds and smog (Haagen-Smit 1952). Aerosols are
also prevalent beyond Earth, appearing for example in
the atmospheres of Jupiter (West et al. 1981), Saturn
(Tomasko & Doose 1984), and Titan (Tomasko 1980),
along with other solar system planets and moons. More
recently, strong circumstantial evidence has emerged
for the presence of aerosols in exoplanet atmospheres

(e.g., Pont et al. 2013; Sing et al. 2016; Gao et al.
2021). In particular, observational studies of exoplanets
have repeatedly been hindered by featureless or highly
muted transmission spectra, with a likely cause being
widespread aerosol particles that obscure atmospheric
signals (Knutson et al. 2014; Kreidberg et al. 2014).
Population-level studies of exoplanet atmospheres
have also emerged as a powerful technique for constrain-
ing the presence and properties of aerosols.
Recent
population-level studies of sub-Neptune atmospheric ob-
servations show that muted and flat transmission spec-
tra occur preferentially over a constrained range of plan-
etary equilibrium temperatures (Teq) between 500 and
800 K and follow a tentative parabolic spectral trend
with Teq between 200 and 1000 K (see the top panel
of Figure 1; Crossfield & Kreidberg 2017; Brande et al.
2024; Roy et al. 2025).
However, the existence and interpretation of this
Teq-dependent trend remain uncertain. Recent studies

for an upward shift in the transition temperature to
clear atmosphere conditions for more massive planets
(Ashtari et al. 2025), further indicating the potential
role of planet surface gravity or metallicity as a driver
of aerosol properties.
While the sub-Neptune parabolic trend is only tenta-
tive due to small number statistics and likely diversity
among the planet population, its confirmation and in-
terpretation have received considerable attention. It is
generally believed in the exoplanet community that this
temperature dependence reflects changes in the domi-
nant aerosol composition.
Aerosol microphysics mod-
els of giant exoplanet atmospheres suggest that hydro-
carbon hazes dominate at Teq ≤950 K, while silicates
and other metal oxides become the primary aerosol con-
stituents at Teq ≥950 K (Gao et al. 2020).
This
bifurcation arises from temperature-dependent differ-
ences in the aerosol production pathway. High tempera-
tures favor the condensation of silicates and metal oxide
clouds (e.g., Burrows & Sharp 1999; Visscher et al. 2010;
Mbarek & Kempton 2016), whereas the basic ingredi-
ents for hydrocarbon haze formation (i.e., methane and
its derivatives) become abundant at T ≲900 K (Zahnle
et al. 2009; Moses et al. 2013). From this perspective,
any spectral trend observed over Teq = 200–1000 K ap-
pears to be linked to hydrocarbon aerosol formation.
Indeed, multiple previous studies have pointed to the
formation of high-altitude hydrocarbon hazes driven by
CH4 photolysis, analogous to the hazy atmosphere of
Titan (Zahnle et al. 2009; Kempton et al. 2011; Morley
et al. 2013, 2015; Kawashima & Ikoma 2018). Among
these, Morley et al. (2013, 2015) made use of 1D pho-
tochemical modeling to predict the dependence of haze
“precursor” (C2Hx and HCN) column densities on equi-
librium temperature over the range Teq ≈450-1400 K.
Their models produce a peak in haze precursor abun-
dance near 800 K, suggestive of the trend shown in the
top panel of Figure 1. However, several limitations of
these existing calculations merit additional follow-up.
For example, the chemical networks truncated at C2
hydrocarbons and relied on highly simplified parame-
terizations to predict the conversion of these small gas-
phase molecules to aerosols. Furthermore, the existing
models do not extend down to the lowest Teq values of
JWST-observed sub-Neptunes (i.e., ∼260 K for K2-
18b; Madhusudhan et al. 2023), nor do they explain the
diversity in aerosol coverage seen among planets of sim-
ilar Teq (e.g., GJ 436 b vs. GJ 9827 d or LP 791-19 c
vs. TOI-270 d; Knutson et al. 2014; Benneke et al. 2024;
Piaulet-Ghorayeb et al. 2024; Roy et al. 2025).
Recent studies have also explored the role of poly-
cyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) in exoplanet at-
mospheres from both thermochemical and observational
perspectives. For example, Dubey et al. (2023) inves-
tigated PAH formation under thermochemical equilib-
rium in irradiated and non-irradiated giant-planet atmo-
spheres. Subsequent work has examined the detectabil-
ity of PAHs with JWST, both for individual targets
(Gr¨ubel et al. 2025) and across a broader range of at-
mospheric conditions (Arenales-Lope et al. 2025).
Interestingly, the combustion community has also
studied a similar problem of “soot” (hydrocarbon
aerosol) formation from gaseous fuel mixtures for sev-
eral decades. To avoid ambiguity, we define “soot” as
carbonaceous condensed-phase particles formed through
high-temperature
hydrocarbon
chemistry,
in
which
molecular growth proceeds via aromatic hydrocarbons
(AHs) and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
(Wang 2011; Frenklach & Mebel 2020; Martin et al.
2022). This is distinct from the organic hazes commonly
discussed in planetary atmospheres, which are typically
produced by photochemical processes in the upper at-
mosphere. In particular, laboratory analogs of such pho-
tochemical hazes, often referred to as tholins (Sagan
& Khare 1979), form through UV- or spark discharge-
driven chemistry of simple molecules (e.g., CH4, N2).
Although it is widely recognized that none of the exist-
ing soot models have full predictive power (Martin et al.
2022), largely due to uncertainties in gas-to-condensed
phase inception kinetics and subsequent particle growth,
this body of work has nevertheless produced extensive
theoretical and experimental insight into the physical
and chemical principles governing soot formation. One
broad and robust consensus is that PAHs act as the
key molecular precursors to soot particles (Wang 2011;
Frenklach & Mebel 2020; Martin et al. 2022). A cen-
tral remaining question is which molecular species and
reaction pathways control the transition from small gas
molecules to PAHs and then to soot particles.

explicitly modeling the reaction pathways from small
molecules to large PAHs and applying these results in
the context of exoplanet atmospheric observations.
2. METHODS
2.1. Model Parameter Grids
To explore the effects that govern PAH formation and
quenching behavior in sub-Neptunes, we generated a
large grid of model atmospheres, spanning a broadly
relevant parameter space. Our grid samples three key
parameters: equilibrium temperature, Teq, ranging from
350 to 1200 K with 10 grid points; metallicity, [M/H]3,
ranging from 0.0 to 4.0 with 9 grid points; and car-
bon to oxygen ratio, C/O, with discrete values of 0.2,
0.55, 1.0, and 2.5 as shown in Table A1.
The Teq
range is chosen to span the regime of sub-Neptune atmo-
spheres observed by HST and JWST where hydrocarbon
aerosols are expected to vary significantly. The lower
bound of 350 K avoids regimes where water condensa-
tion becomes important, while the upper bound of 1200
K extends into conditions where silicate and metal ox-
ide clouds may dominate. The metallicity range covers
solar ([M/H]=0) to highly metal-enriched atmospheres,
consistent with values inferred for sub-Neptune plan-
ets (e.g., [M/H] ∼3.5; Ohno et al. 2025), and extends
slightly beyond current observational constraints to al-
low broader exploration. The C/O range spans strongly
oxygen-rich to highly carbon-rich compositions.
This resulted in a total of 360 model grid points
(10 × 9 × 4).
For the standard solar metallicity case
[M/H] = 0, we adopted present-day solar abundances

2.2. The T–P profiles
In our framework, the efficiency of PAH formation and
quenching depends on the atmospheric thermal struc-
ture, as temperature primarily controls chemical reac-
tion timescales and molecular growth processes. To ex-
plore the sensitivity of these processes to variations in
thermal structure for realistic sub-Neptune conditions,
we generated a grid of one-dimensional (1-D) radiative-
convective equilibrium (RCE) models with the GPU-
accelerated HELIOS code (Malik et al. 2017, 2019). We
create a single 1-D temperature-pressure (T-P) pro-
file at each Teq–[M/H]–C/O grid point listed in Ta-
ble A1). The radiative-convective calculation addition-
ally requires the input of an intrinsic temperature (Tint),
which accounts for heat flux from the planet’s deep in-
terior, as detailed in Appendix A. Additional details on
the T-P profiles are provided in Appendix A.
2.3. Automatic Chemical Reaction Network
Generation for PAH-chemistry up to C16
We constructed a detailed chemical network describ-
ing complex PAH chemistry up to C16 using the Re-
action Mechanism Generator (RMG; Gao et al. 2016;
Liu et al. 2021b; Johnson et al. 2022), an open-source
Python package that automatically generates chemical
networks. Previous exoplanet atmosphere models have
generally limited hydrocarbon chemistry to small species
(i.e., C2Hx), whereas our approach explicitly tracks the
formation and growth of larger hydrocarbon molecules
relevant to soot precursors. RMG has been widely applied
to exoplanet atmosphere studies (Yang & Hu 2024a;
Damiano et al. 2024; Yang & Hu 2024b; Benneke et al.
2024; Hu et al. 2025; Bello-Arufe et al. 2025), and
more recently to modeling CO–CH4 chemistry in Jupiter
(Yang et al. 2026). The resulting chemical network con-
sists of 700 species and 8258 reactions and is provided in
the Supplementary Materials as a YAML file compatible
with Cantera (Goodwin et al. 2024), along with the cor-
responding species dictionary (soot network v1.yaml
and soot network v1.txt). Additional details on the
construction of the PAH chemical reaction network are
provided in Appendix B.
2.4. Chemical Network Species Classification
AH and PAH refer to classes of molecules rather
than individual species. Because evaluating the quench-
ing behavior of every species is computationally im-
practical, we assume that all AH species share the
same quenching behavior (quench pressure) as a sin-
gle grid-representative AH species, and likewise for PAH
species. To implement this approach, we classified the
700 species generated by RMG (see Section 2.3) into three
categories using the open-source cheminformatics toolkit
RDKit (Landrum et al. 2025): 313 no-ring species, 157
AH species (containing one ring), and 230 PAH species
(containing two or more rings). The full classification is
provided in the supplementary materials as a CSV file
(species ring classification.csv), accompanied by
SVG files showing the molecular structures of the species
constructed for this work.
We then identified representative species for the AH
and PAH classes. For each of the 360 grid points con-
sidered in Section 2.1, we computed the thermochemical
equilibrium abundances of all AH and PAH species along
the T-P profile computed in Section 2.2. Within each
class, the species with the largest equilibrium mixing
ratio at P = 1 mbar was selected as the representative
species, and its quench pressure was assigned to all other
species in the same class. For example, at C/O = 0.55,
[M/H] = 2.5, and Teq = 500 K, benzene (C6H6) and
indane (C9H10) are identified as the grid-representative
AH and PAH species, respectively (see the middle panel
of Figure C1). Consequently, the AH and PAH group
abundances inherit the quench pressures of benzene and
indane, as shown in the right panel of Figure C1.
2.5. 1D Quench Modeling Using a Characteristic
Chemical Timescale Approach
Vertical transport-induced quenching occurs when the
chemical timescale, τchem, becomes comparable to the
vertical mixing timescale, τmix, at a given pressure
level (Prinn & Barshay 1977).
Although the most
rigorous way to model quenching in exoplanet atmo-
spheres is to directly solve the full set of one-dimensional
chemical kinetic-transport differential equations (Moses
et al. 2011; Venot et al. 2012; Hu et al. 2012; Rim-
mer & Helling 2016; Tsai et al. 2017), this approach
becomes computationally impractical for large chemical
networks. This is because the computational cost scales
linearly with the number of reactions and quadratically
with the number of species (Schwer et al. 2002).
Given the large chemical network used in this work,
consisting of 700 species and 8258 reactions as described
in Section 2.3, we instead adopt a characteristic chem-
ical timescale approach.
This method is well estab-

Several numerical formulations exist, including

Using EVTS, described in detail in Appendix C, we
computed the characteristic timescales of the species of
interest along the T-P profiles described in Section 2.2.
We tested eddy diffusion coefficients of Kzz = 104–108

We therefore report only

2.6. 1D Photochemical Modeling of C2H2
Enhancement
The EVTS quench calculations described above ac-
count for disequilibrium via vertical mixing, but not
photochemistry. To explore the potential enhancement
of hydrocarbon formation due to photochemistry, we
performed 1D photochemical kinetic–transport model-
ing for a subset of 10 of the model atmospheres from
our total grid — those with C/O = 0.55 and [M/H] =
2.5, while varying the Teq from 350 to 1200 K. The sim-
ulations were carried out using EPACRIS (Yang & Hu
2024a). We adopted the photochemical network from
(Yang & Hu 2024b) and assumed a constant Kzz = 106
cm2/s as a nominal value for sub-Neptunes (Zhang &
Showman 2018). The stellar flux at each Teq was scaled
according to the bolometric luminosity, using the M4.5V
stellar spectrum of GJ 1214 from the MUSCLES survey4
(Loyd et al. 2016; France et al. 2016). We ran models
both with and without photochemical reactions to assess

3.2.1. A Thermochemistry Perspective
From left to right in Figure 3, we observe a general
trend with increasing Teq toward progressive molecular
growth of hydrocarbon species, from the simplest form,
CH4, to PAHs. At lower Teq ≤800 K, CH4 dominates
over CO due to its thermochemical stability in the deep
atmosphere, followed by quenching, a process that has
been well studied by Moses et al. (2011).
For C2 species, the saturated molecule C2H6 increases
with Teq up to approximately 800 K and then decreases
at higher Teq ≥800 K. This behavior arises because
C2H6 formation primarily proceeds through the recom-
bination of CH3 radicals stemming from CH4. At higher
Teq, CH4 becomes depleted (Moses et al. 2011), sup-
pressing CH3 production and, consequently, C2H6 for-
mation.
In addition, Teq above 800 K is sufficiently
high to promote H-abstraction and β-scission, which
thermally crack saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes such
as C2H6) into unsaturated species (alkenes and alkynes
such as C2H2) via hydrogen loss. As a result, unsatu-
rated C2 species such as C2H2 dominate over C2H6 at
higher Teq, as shown in the fifth column of Figure 3.
More broadly, the shift from saturated to unsaturated
C2 species highlights the role of multiple carbon-carbon
bonds in PAH formation.
Saturated hydrocarbons
with single C–C bonds are relatively inert toward ring
growth, whereas unsaturated species with C––C or C–––C
bonds, particularly C2H2, are chemically active inter-
mediates that efficiently drive aromatic growth through
hydrogen-abstraction/acetylene-addition (HACA) reac-
tions (Frenklach et al. 1983).
Beyond C2 species, the chemistry becomes more com-
plex and is no longer a simple monotonic function of
Teq. Instead, hydrocarbon growth results from a bal-
ance of thermochemical processes driven by either en-
thalpy or entropy, depending on temperature, pressure,
and initial composition. One good example that shows
this behavior is the experimental study of C11H22 (Jet-
A fuel) pyrolysis at T = 1300 K and P = 10 bar by
Wang et al. (2018).
Wang et al. (2018) showed that
early decomposition steps up to alkynes are entropy-
driven as large molecules fragment into smaller species,
whereas the first aromatic ring formation is enthalpy-
driven.
However, subsequent polycyclic aromatic hy-
drocarbon growth again becomes entropy-driven, as il-
lustrated in Figure 6 of Wang et al. (2018).
This complex thermochemical behavior implies that
soot precursor formation requires temperatures high
enough to sustain molecular growth, while excessively
high temperatures then fragment key intermediates
needed for PAH formation. This competition is what
drives the bell-shaped dependence of soot formation on
temperature observed in combustion experiments (bot-
tom panel of Figure 1).
In our model grid, the de-
pendence of AH and PAH abundances on Teq is non-

3.2.2. A Quenching Perspective
While atmospheric temperature is a key driver of plan-
etary atmospheric chemistry, vertical transport induced
by convection or diffusion can drive atmospheres away
from thermochemical equilibrium and into disequilib-
rium chemistry (Moses et al. 2011). Thus, it is impor-
tant to account for the effects of vertical mixing in ex-
oplanet atmospheric modeling. Figure 2b–d illustrates
quenching behavior under different Teq scenarios for ex-
oplanets with fixed C/O = 0.55 and [M/H] = 2.5.
One notable trend in Figures 2b is that as Teq in-
creases, chemical quenching occurs at higher altitudes
(i.e., lower pressures). At Teq ≳700 K, neither C2H2
nor PAHs achieve quenching over the entire modeled
atmosphere (see Figure 2c and d). This behavior arises
because at higher Teq, thermochemical reaction rates be-
come much faster than vertical mixing. In other words,
the chemical timescale (τchem) becomes much shorter
than the mixing timescale (τmix), as shown in Figure 2b.
As a result, the atmospheric composition closely fol-
lows thermochemical equilibrium throughout the model
domain.
This regime is typical of hot and ultra-hot
Jupiters, as demonstrated in previous modeling stud-
ies of HD 209458 b (Moses et al. 2011). We note that
our model assumes a uniform Kzz, which is unlikely to
be realistic for actual exoplanet atmospheres. However,


## Page 8


8
given that Kzz remains poorly constrained in planetary
science, this simplified treatment nonetheless captures
the essential qualitative quenching behavior of chemical
species and thus provides useful insight.
Turning to Figure 2d, the effects of quenching lead to
substantially enhanced vertical mixing ratios of PAHs at
1 mbar, the pressure level typically probed by JWST,
compared to their thermochemical equilibrium abun-
dances. As a result, PAHs are expected to be signifi-
cantly more abundant at lower Teq, with a pronounced
peak at Teq = 600 K. In contrast, C2H2 exhibits a more
monotonic dependence on Teq, with its mixing ratio in-
creasing with temperature and reaching a plateau at
Teq ≈700 K. This highlights that quenching plays an
important role in setting PAH abundances under the
grid conditions explored in this study. We remind the
reader that variations in Kzz (see Appendix C) have a
weaker effect on the bell-shaped trend than changes in
Teq.
3.2.3. A Photochemistry Perspective
Although photochemistry is known to play a major
role in shaping hydrocarbon abundances and initiat-
ing aerosol formation, as demonstrated by observations
of Titan’s atmosphere (Yung et al. 1984) and labora-
tory experiments simulating exoplanet conditions (He
et al. 2018; Fleury et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2021), most
full chemical kinetic models restrict haze precursors to
C2Hx species and HCN due to incomplete understand-
ing of photochemical hydrocarbon growth pathways and
computational limitations (Zahnle et al. 2009; Tsai et al.
2021; Huang et al. 2024). Consistent with these con-
straints, our photochemical network includes carbon-
bearing species only up to C2. While photochemistry
is also likely to significantly enhance the production of
PAHs, these processes remain poorly understood and
are beyond the scope of the present study. Accordingly,
in the following section, we mainly consider PAH forma-
tion through thermochemical pathways. Photochemical
effects on C2H2 are explicitly evaluated for the case with
C/O = 0.55, and [M/H] = 2.5, and the same photo-
chemical enhancement is assumed to apply across the
remainder of the parameter grid.
As shown in Figure 4, photochemistry enhances C2H2
abundances (red crosses) by up to 10 orders of magni-
tude at Teq ≤600 K compared to the thermochemistry-
only case.

dances obtained from quench chemistry alone, while the
red crosses show these same quench-chemistry abun-

effect of photochemistry on top of the quenched C2H2
abundances. The photochemical enhancement becomes
progressively weaker at higher Teq, where thermochem-
istry dominates (Yang et al. 2023). This enhancement
at lower Teq is therefore expected to substantially pro-

3.3. Sooting Propensity of Sub-Neptune Atmospheres
As discussed in the Section 1, there is a broad consen-
sus within the combustion community that alkynes such
as C2H2 and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
are positively correlated with the formation of soot par-
ticles, often described as “Sooting Propensity”, based
on combustion experiments (Martin et al. 2022). How-
ever, none of the existing soot models are predictive, as
aerosol formation involves a transition from gas-phase
to condensed-phase growth that is not well understood
by either experimental kinetics or first-principles the-
ory (Martin et al. 2022). In contrast to gas-phase reac-
tions, which can be treated using well-established first-
principles approaches such as transition state theory

trends in exoplanet atmospheric composition.

tic metric for the sooting propensity of sub-Neptune at-
mospheres, denoted by η, intended as a relative indicator

is plausible. In this regime, efficient hydrocarbon haze

low Teq. At the same time, LP 791-18 c is observed to

metallicity and C/O ratio must be considered when in-

temperate sub-Neptunes in forthcoming JWST studies.

4. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we combined modeling approaches from
planetary science and combustion chemistry to connect
sub-Neptune deep atmospheric thermochemistry with
upper atmospheric observations by JWST and HST. By
combining radiative transfer, chemical timescale analy-
sis, and rate-based automated chemical network gener-
ation, we provide a first-principles explanation that can
account for enhanced aerosol formation in Teq=500−800
K range, potentially contributing to the muted-spectra
regime suggested by some populational studies (e.g.,
Crossfield & Kreidberg 2017; Brande et al. 2024) and
identify its plausible origin in deep-atmospheric PAH
formation.
In this framework, the deep atmosphere acts as a
“soot factory” analogous to a gasoline or diesel com-
bustion engine, in which PAHs and soot are efficiently
produced and transported upward, amplified by photo-
chemistry, and ultimately manifesting as an observed
non-monotonic spectral trend.
This framework con-
sistently explains existing observations and establishes
both a basis for interpretation and a predictive model
for guiding future observations.
For example, our re-
sults indicate that, at a solar C/O ratio, GJ 1214 b–like
conditions with Teq ∼600 K and [M/H]∼3.5 maximize
soot propensity, making such planets a benchmark case
for hydrocarbon haze-rich atmospheres. More generally,
we find that in carbon-rich atmospheres (C/O≥1), soot
formation becomes largely insensitive to Teq and is in-
stead primarily controlled by composition, with high-
metallicity conditions ([M/H] ≥2.5) leading to persis-
tently hazy atmospheres, comparable to or more opaque
than GJ 1214 b, across a wide range of Teq.
Our work suggests that hydrocarbon haze production
is regulated not only by Teq, but also by atmospheric
metallicity and C/O. The C/O ratio plays a role anal-
ogous to the fuel-to-air ratio in combustion chemistry,
with higher C/O favoring incomplete combustion and
the formation of unsaturated hydrocarbons and PAHs,
thereby promoting haze formation. Increasing metallic-
ity likewise enhances haze production by increasing the
total carbon abundance. However, at very high metal-
licity ([M/H]≥4), the accompanying increase in oxygen
shifts the redox balance toward oxidized species such
as CO2, suppressing hydrocarbon formation in a regime
analogous to complete combustion. These compositional
axes add important nuance to the interpretation of sub-
Neptune transmission spectra and naturally explain the
differing degree of aerosol coverage inferred for planets
with similar Teq, such as the hazy LP 791-18 c compared
with the clear TOI-270 d (or K2-18 b).
Beyond providing a unified interpretive framework,
our sooting propensity metric also yields testable pre-
dictions.
Among moderate-to-high metallicity sub-
Neptunes, the presence or absence of hydrocarbon hazes
may serve as an indirect indicator of atmospheric C/O,
distinguishing carbon-rich “soot worlds” from “water
worlds” with sub-solar C/O. Because atmospheric C/O
is closely linked to formation location and accretion his-
tory (¨Oberg et al. 2011), haze availability may therefore
provide a practical observational constraint on planet
formation pathways (Bergin et al. 2023).
These pre-
dictions can be tested with JWST transmission spec-
troscopy by jointly constraining aerosol opacity and
molecular abundances.
Future work should extend the framework developed
in this Letter by incorporating a more detailed treat-
ment of photochemical effects on PAH formation and
by conducting a comprehensive kinetic investigation of
the transition from gas phase to condensed phase, com-
monly referred to as “particle inception”. The models
produced in this work can also be coupled in the future
to radiative transfer and aerosol microphysics calcula-
tions, in order to directly predict their observational con-
sequences — e.g., to determine what values of the soot-
ing propensity η are expected to produce clear, muted,
or flat transmission spectra.

We note that radiative transfer and disequilibrium
chemistry are not fully coupled in a self-consistent it-
erative framework.
This approximation is justified in
the deep atmosphere, where compositions follow ther-
mochemical equilibrium. At higher altitudes, disequi-
librium processes such as quenching can modify abun-
dances and, in principle, affect the thermal structure.
Previous work has shown that such effects are minimal
at pressures greater than ∼0.1 bar, with temperature
differences typically less than 100 K in the upper atmo-

We note, however, that PAHs and potentially soot
formed in the deep atmosphere, particularly under con-
ditions of elevated C/O≥1.0 and higher metallicity
([M/H] ≥3.5), may introduce additional opacity and ra-
diative feedback in the upper atmosphere, which could
lead to larger temperature deviations. The magnitude
of this effect remains highly uncertain, as the optical
properties of these species are poorly constrained and
require further laboratory measurements.
Fully self-
consistent coupling between radiative transfer and dise-
quilibrium chemistry remains an important direction for
future work, but is not yet standard in the field and is
beyond the scope of the current study.

However, an important distinction is that planetary

The cur-
rent implementation of RMG does not explicitly include
photochemical processes or transport when generating
reaction networks, which represents a key limitation
of this approach.
As a result, the network is best
suited for describing thermochemical processes in the
deep atmosphere rather than fully coupled photochem-
ical–transport systems.
Despite this limitation, the present study focuses on
deep atmospheric regions, where temperatures and pres-
sures are high, and chemistry is largely governed by
thermochemical kinetics and equilibrium. In these re-
gions, quenching typically occurs at pressures where ul-
traviolet photons cannot penetrate (e.g., P ≳17 bar for
Jupiter; Visscher et al. 2010), and photochemical effects
are therefore expected to be subdominant. Under these
conditions, the RMG-generated networks provide an ap-
propriate framework for modeling hydrocarbon growth
and PAH formation in thermal chemistry-dominating
deep atmospheres.
RMG has also been successfully applied in planetary
and exoplanetary contexts.
However, it is important
to distinguish between validation of the chemical net-
work construction and validation of the full atmospheric
modeling framework. In several studies, such as WASP-
39 b (Yang & Hu 2024a) and K2-18 b (Yang & Hu
2024b), RMG-generated networks were incorporated into
more complete photochemical kinetic–transport mod-
els. These results demonstrate that RMG can generate
chemically relevant reaction networks for planetary at-
mospheres, but do not directly validate the simplified
framework adopted in this work.
The most direct validation of the specific thermochem-
istry + quench-timescale framework used here is the re-
cent work application to Jupiter’s deep atmosphere, in
which RMG was used to model CO−CH4 chemistry to-
gether with the same EVTS-based method to reproduce
quenched CO constraints on the bulk oxygen abundance
inferred from the latest Juno MWR analysis (Yang et al.
2026). This agreement suggests that the EVTS approx-
imation can capture the dominant quenching behavior
in deep atmospheres, within the typical uncertainties of
current atmospheric constraints.
We emphasize that the present framework remains
an approximation to the full chemical kinetic–transport
problem. In particular, it does not include fully cou-
pled photochemistry or vertical transport, and therefore

exoplanet atmospheres. Rather, it provides a computa-
tionally efficient and physically motivated approach for

To capture the complex chemistry of PAH formation,
we benchmarked the most comprehensive CHO chemical
network to date, spanning C2 to C16 (Liu et al. 2021a).
Liu et al. (2021a) used the Reaction Mechanism Gener-

nism for acetylene (C2H2) pyrolysis, predicting PAH for-
mation from C2H2 to pyrene (C16H10). This model has
been shown to achieve good agreement with experimen-
tal data, with predicted mole fractions matching flow-
reactor C2H2 pyrolysis experiments to within a factor
of five for PAHs up to two rings (e.g., naphthalene and
indene) at 8 kPa (80 mbar) over the temperature range
1073–1373 K (Norinaga et al. 2008). The model also re-
produced qualitative agreement to trends for three-ring
PAHs (e.g., anthracene, phenanthrene, and acenaphthy-
lene) and four-ring PAHs (e.g., pyrene), with predicted

of magnitude, but did not capture PAHs larger than
pyrene due to computational limitations in automati-
cally generating larger PAH networks (Liu et al. 2021a).
These limitations include the rapidly increasing num-
ber of possible species and reactions, challenges in accu-
rately treating highly strained intermediates, and uncer-
tainties in thermochemical and kinetic data for higher-
order growth pathways (Liu et al. 2021a).
Because exoplanet atmospheres require chemistry ini-
tiated from C1 species, we did not directly use their
original chemical network, which is initiated from C2
species (Liu et al. 2021a).
Instead, we adopted the
Liu et al. (2021a) RMG input largely unchanged, but
added the Klippenstein Glarborg2016 library to rep-
resent C1–C2 chemistry, thereby generating a chemical
network with RMG spanning C1 to C16 species. The
Klippenstein Glarborg2016 library is based on the
methane-oxidation mechanism constructed by Hashemi
et al. (2016), which was extensively verified and bench-
marked across a wide range of conditions using various
experimental methane-oxidation studies (Hashemi et al.
2016). This library was previously incorporated to gen-
erate chemical networks for warm and hot Jupiter atmo-
spheres (Yang & Hu 2024a), for temperate sub-Neptune
atmospheres (Yang & Hu 2024b), and for Jupiter’s at-
mosphere (Yang et al. 2026). In both applications, it
successfully reproduced key atmospheric constraints for
WASP-39 b, WASP-80 b, K2-18 b, and Jupiter (Yang
& Hu 2024a,b; Yang et al. 2026). We assumed initial
elemental abundances of C, H, and O equal to 1000×
solar metallicity, temperatures of 300–2000 K, pres-
sures of 1 mbar to 100 bar, and an integration time of
3.154×1019 s (1012 yr), which is sufficient for the chem-
istry to reach steady state under these conditions. We
generated the reaction network at 1000× solar metal-
licity because this high-metallicity composition yields
a more comprehensive CHO network in RMG, retaining
minor intermediates and reaction pathways that may
not be included when the network is generated at lower
metallicities due to flux-based inclusion criteria.
The
resulting network therefore remains applicable across
lower-metallicity regimes (1–100× solar), where these
pathways naturally become inactive if the correspond-
ing species remain negligible. In principle, generating
a separate reaction network for each metallicity in the
model grid would be ideal; however, this approach is
computationally prohibitive.
Adopting a single, fixed
reaction network ensures internal consistency across the
full 1–10,000× solar metallicity grid while remaining
computationally tractable.
The RMG input file is pro-
vided in the Supplementary Materials (input.py). The
resulting CHO network contains 656 species and 7027
reactions.

sulfur-bearing species that can potentially affect the

To incorporate nitrogen- and

At each layer of the T–P profile (117 layers in to-
tal; Section 2.2), we compute thermochemical equi-
librium abundances using Cantera (Goodwin et al.
2024) with the chemical network described in Sec-
tion 2.3.
Using the resulting thermochemical equilib-
rium species mole fraction profiles, we construct the
Jacobian matrix, J, required for the EVTS calcula-
tion using Cantera’s Kinetics.net production rates
(Goodwin et al. 2024). In practice, this approach quan-
tifies how small perturbations to the equilibrium chemi-
cal state relax back toward equilibrium through the cou-
pled reaction network. By linearizing the chemical sys-
tem around equilibrium, the Jacobian matrix captures
how changes in the abundance of each species affect the
net production of all other species. The eigenvalues of
this Jacobian matrix, therefore, represent the charac-
teristic decay timescale of independent chemical modes,
from which we extract the dominant chemical timescale,
τchem(T, P), for each target species.
The Jacobian matrix was constructed numerically us-
ing a central finite difference scheme, with each species’
mole fraction perturbed by 50% of its equilibrium value.
From the resulting Jacobian eigenvalues, we compute
τchem(T, P) for the targeted species CO, CH4, C2H6,
C2H2, HCN, AHs (see Section 2.4), and PAHs (see Sec-
