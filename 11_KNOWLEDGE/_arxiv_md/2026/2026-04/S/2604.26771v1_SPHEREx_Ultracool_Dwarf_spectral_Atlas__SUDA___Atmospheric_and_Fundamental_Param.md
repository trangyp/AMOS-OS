ABSTRACT
We present the SPHEREx Ultracool Dwarf spectral Atlas (SUDA), a homogeneous sample of 1675
ultracool dwarfs with continuous 0.75–5 µm spectroscopy from SPHEREx QR2. Using the SAND and
ATMO2020++ atmospheric model grids, we derive atmospheric parameters and calculate bolometric
luminosities (Lbol). We combine the inferred Teff and radii with evolutionary tracks to estimate masses
and ages for the full sample. Evolutionary surface gravities (log g) are also reported for sources with
parallaxes. In the 1700–2500 K range, the atmospheric log g from spectral fitting are systematically
lower than the evolutionary log g, with a median offset of about 1.1 dex, likely reflecting residual
degeneracy between log g and metallicity in low resolution SPHEREx spectra. We also construct an
empirical spectral atlas by grouping the spectra in the adopted parameter space, using the Teff and
the evolutionary log g. The resulting atlas contains 52 parameter bins and spans Teff ≃700–3000 K.
Molecular indices reveal a coherent atmospheric sequence across the sample. H2O and CH4 strengthen
toward lower Teff, whereas CO and CO2 rise below ∼1500 K and turn over near ∼1000 K. Comparison
with model sequences shows that the CO2 index is a useful empirical metallicity tracer at Teff ∼800–
1300 K. Together, these results establish SUDA as a reference sample for linking observed 0.75–5 µm
spectral morphology to atmospheric and evolutionary trends in ultracool dwarfs.

1. INTRODUCTION
Ultracool dwarfs,
spanning the latest M dwarfs
through the L, T, and Y spectral classes, occupy the
coolest and lowest mass end of stellar and substellar
populations (J. D. Kirkpatrick et al. 1999; A. J. Bur-
gasser et al. 2002; M. C. Cushing et al. 2011). Their at-
mospheric parameters, including effective temperature
(Teff), surface gravity (log g), and metallicity ([M/H]),
together with derived physical properties such as bolo-
metric luminosity (Lbol), mass, radius, and age, are
central to understanding star formation at the lowest
masses, the boundary between stars and brown dwarfs,
and the connection between stars, brown dwarfs, and

giant exoplanets. These quantities are also relevant to
the radiation environments and potential habitability of
planets around low mass stars, since stellar activity and
ultraviolet emission in M dwarfs evolve with spectral
type and age and strongly affect circumstellar habitable
zone conditions (B. Stelzer et al. 2013; E. L. Shkolnik
& T. S. Barman 2014; X. Li et al. 2024, 2025). Because
ultracool dwarfs emit most of their energy in the near
and mid infrared, infrared spectroscopy is essential for
reliable physical characterization (S. K. Leggett & P.
Tremblin 2025; W. W. Meynardie et al. 2025; M. Voyer
et al. 2025; A. Lueber et al. 2026).
Among these quantities, the bolometric luminosity
plays a central and unifying role. Unlike atmospheric
parameters inferred from spectral fitting, Lbol is com-
paratively independent of atmospheric models and links
spectroscopy to derived physical properties through evo-
lutionary models (I. Baraffe et al. 2015; M. W. Phillips

et al. 2020; G. Chabrier et al. 2023). Accurate deter-
minations of Lbol are critical for constraining masses,
radii, and ages, and for breaking degeneracies among
Teff, log g, and metallicity that commonly arise in at-
mospheric fitting (A. Sanghi et al. 2023; S. A. Beiler
et al. 2024).
Improving the accuracy and homogene-
ity of bolometric luminosity measurements is therefore
essential for a more physically consistent view of the ul-
tracool dwarf population.
One persistent limitation has been wavelength com-
pleteness. As ultracool atmospheres cool, an increasing
fraction of the emergent flux shifts beyond ∼2.5 µm,
making the 3–5 µm region critical for both luminosity
estimates and atmospheric interpretation (D. Saumon
& M. S. Marley 2008; M. S. Marley et al. 2021). This
wavelength range also contains key molecular diagnos-
tics, especially CO and CO2, that are sensitive to chem-
istry, composition, and thermal structure in the coldest
brown dwarfs (B. W. P. Lew et al. 2024; E. Manjavacas
et al. 2024; J. K. Faherty et al. 2025).
Previous large ultracool dwarf spectroscopic studies
focused on optical and near infrared wavelengths short-
ward of ∼2.5 µm, with widely used libraries built pri-
marily from facilities such as IRTF/SpeX and Magel-
lan/FIRE (Z. Zhang et al. 2021; A. J. Burgasser et al.
2025; E. Mader et al. 2026). As a result, many luminos-
ity estimates still rely on heterogeneous SED assembly,
combining partial spectra with sparse mid infrared con-
straints and model interpolation or extrapolation across
unobserved regions (S. B. Dieterich et al. 2014; J. C. Fil-
ippazzo et al. 2015; A. Sanghi et al. 2023). For example,
A. Sanghi et al. (2023) derived atmospheric parameters
and physical properties for more than a thousand ultra-
cool dwarfs from optical to mid infrared SED compila-
tions and atmosphere model comparisons. That work
established an important benchmark, but the spectro-
scopic coverage was limited to 1–2.5 µm, with the re-
mainder of the SED constrained only by photometry.
The SPHEREx (Spectro-Photometer for the History
of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Ex-
plorer) mission provides a direct way to overcome this
limitation through all sky spectra from 0.75–5 µm with
continuous wavelength coverage (P. M. Korngut et al.
2018; B. P. Crill et al. 2020; J. J. Bock et al. 2025; R.
Akeson et al. 2025; SPHEREx Team 2025). Its combi-
nation of full sky coverage and continuous infrared spec-
troscopy enables homogeneous characterization of a vast
ultracool dwarf population.
In particular, SPHEREx
provides uniform access to the 3–5 µm regime, where
molecular opacity windows and thermal emission con-
tribute substantially to both bolometric flux and atmo-
spheric diagnostics.
A recent SPHEREx based study
has constructed the largest spectral sample of ultracool
dwarfs to date, demonstrating the value of these data for
population level studies of ultracool dwarf atmospheres
and spectral diversity (J. Gagn´e et al. 2026).
The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has
demonstrated the diagnostic power of 0.75–5 µm spec-
troscopy for cold brown dwarfs (K. L. Luhman et al.
2024; B. W. P. Lew et al. 2024; Z. Tu et al. 2025a,b),
but necessarily in relatively small targeted samples.
SPHEREx complements JWST by providing spec-
troscopy at lower resolution but for orders of magnitude
more objects in a homogeneous survey, enabling popu-
lation scale studies that were previously impractical.
In this work, we use SPHEREx quick release spec-
tra to construct the SPHEREx Ultracool Dwarf spec-
tral Atlas (SUDA) and derive a uniform set of atmo-
spheric parameters together with derived physical prop-
erties for a large sample selected from UltracoolSheet
(W. M. J. Best et al. 2024a). The key advance is the
combination of sample size and continuous 0.75–5 µm
coverage, which allows us to examine atmospheric pa-
rameters, bolometric luminosities, an empirical spectral
atlas, and carbon bearing molecular behavior within one
homogeneous sample.
The following sections describe
the data, sample construction, and fitting methodology,
and then present the resulting bolometric luminosities
and evolutionary constraints, the empirical spectral at-
las, and molecular index trends. Details of sample qual-
ity control, flux calibration, and supporting analyses are
provided in the appendices.
2. DATA AND SAMPLE
This section describes the SPHEREx spectral data,
the sample construction, and the screening steps that
define the final SUDA sample.
2.1. SPHEREx Spectral Data
SPHEREx provides the continuous 0.75–5.0 µm spec-
troscopy needed to study ultracool dwarfs within one
homogeneous survey.
The spectra used in this work
were retrieved from the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science
Archive (IRSA), and this wavelength coverage includes
the near and mid infrared regimes where ultracool stars
and brown dwarfs emit most of their bolometric flux, es-
pecially the critical 3–5 µm region. Observations are ob-
tained with the Wide Field Linear Variable Filter (LVF)
Imaging Spectrograph.
SPHEREx provides spectra with an angular resolution
of 6.15′′, corresponding to its photometric spatial reso-
lution. This enables systematic studies of isolated ultra-
cool stars and brown dwarfs across the sky, while mo-
tivating careful treatment of unresolved multiples and
crowded regions (Appendix A).

3. ATMOSPHERIC MODEL FITTING
We use a uniform two stage fitting pipeline to derive
atmospheric parameters for the full SPHEREx sample.
We first perform minimum χ2 fitting on model grids to
identify an initial solution for each source, then refine
that solution with nested sampling Bayesian inference.
3.1. Initial Grid Based χ2 Fitting
We first use grid based χ2 fitting to obtain an initial
atmospheric solution for each spectrum. Our primary
model grid is the Spectral ANalog of Dwarfs (SAND;
E. Alvarado et al. 2024), which spans 700–4000 K and
covers the broad surface gravity and metallicity range
required by this sample. Its demonstrated performance
across L/T transition spectra (Z. Tu et al. 2025b) also
makes it well suited to our heterogeneous data set.
We evaluated χ2 for all recommended6 SAND spec-
tra after convolving models to the SPHEREx resolution
(R = 40 over 0.7–3.8 µm and R = 110 over 3.8–5.0 µm).
Following Equations (6) and (7) in S. A. Beiler et al.
(2023), we solve for a wavelength independent flux scal-
ing and adopt the minimum χ2 parameter set as the
preliminary solution for each object.
For a subset of very cool sources, we also fit with the
ATMO2020++ model grid (A. M. Meisner et al. 2023),
which is optimized for late T/Y regimes and now spans
[M/H] = −1.0 to +0.3. For objects with preliminary
SAND fits of Teff ≤1300 K, we repeat the same mini-
mum χ2 procedure with ATMO2020++ and adopt the
model family with the smaller reduced χ2.
3.2. Bayesian Parameter Inference with Nested
Sampling
We then refine those initial solutions with Bayesian
inference to obtain the final parameter estimates and
uncertainties.
After the initial χ2 step, we use the
UltraNest nested sampling framework (J. Buchner
2016, 2019, 2021) for each source.
For each ob-
ject, we use the atmospheric model family (SAND or
ATMO2020++) preferred by the initial optimization.
For a given model grid, we linearly interpolate in
all available parameter dimensions in logarithmic flux
space.
For SAND, the native grid at Teff < 2500 K
covers log g = 4.0–6.0.
For sources with initial solu-
tions at Teff < 2500 K and log g = 4.0, we extend the
grid to log g = 3.5 by linear extrapolation in logarithmic

4. MODEL FITTING RESULTS
This section presents the atmospheric parameters,
bolometric luminosities, spectral types, and evolution-
ary quantities derived from the best fitting spectra and
models.
A sample of the catalog is provided in Ap-
pendix B (Table B1), including the best fit atmospheric
parameters, adopted model family, distance, literature
spectral type, and spectral type from this work. The
spectral types from this work are obtained with SPLAT
(A. J. Burgasser & the SPLAT Development Team 2017)
by minimizing χ2 against the standard spectral tem-
plates, and are left blank when the SPHEREx spectra
have insufficient S/N or incomplete blue wavelength cov-
erage for a reliable match. The classifications agree at
approximately the one subtype level, with a mean offset
of −0.3 subtype, a median offset of 0 subtype, and a
median absolute deviation (MAD) of 0.6 subtype. We
then use the best fitting spectra to derive Fbol, bolo-
metric luminosities, and the parameters inferred from
evolutionary models.
4.1. Bolometric Fluxes and Bolometric Luminosities
4.1.1. Bolometric Flux Estimation
We derive bolometric fluxes by integrating composite
spectra that combine the observed data with the best
fitting models.
The observed spectrum is used where
data are available, and the model is used outside the
observed wavelength range.
For sources for which SAND provides the best fit, the
observed spectrum replaces the model over the observed
range in the composite SED. Because the SAND grid
spans ∼0.1–999 µm, no extra end extrapolation is re-
quired.
For sources for which ATMO2020++ provides the
best fit, we use the same integration scheme but account
for the more limited model coverage (∼0.2–30 µm): a

Uncertainties in Fbol are propagated with Monte Carlo

and flux scaling are drawn from their posteriors, ob-
served fluxes are perturbed within reported errors, and
an additional 5% absolute calibration term is applied

composite integration procedure.

4.3. The Spectral Atlas
We present an empirical SPHEREx atlas built di-
rectly from the observed spectra of sources with par-
allaxes. Sources are grouped using the Teff fitted with
atmospheric models and the log g inferred from evolu-
tionary models, with bin widths of ∆Teff = 100 K and
∆log g = 0.5 centered on the atlas grid points. Within
each bin, we retain screened spectra, interpolate them
onto a common wavelength grid matched to the native
SPHEREx resolving power, normalize them in the 1.27–
1.33 µm region, and define the atlas spectrum as the
wavelength by wavelength median of the retained mem-
bers.
Bins with fewer than five acceptable members
are excluded, leaving 52 atlas bins constructed from 968
spectra of sources with parallaxes.
This construction is intended to preserve the charac-
teristic morphology of the observed spectra associated
with different regions of the adopted Teff and log g pa-
rameter space, while suppressing random noise, resid-
ual artifacts, and source specific peculiarities. Because
the atlas is assembled from normalized observations by
taking the median in each bin, it should be interpreted
primarily as an empirical reference sequence in the ob-
served 0.75–5 µm domain, rather than as an absolute
flux or purely model driven template set.
Figure 5 presents the resulting atlas as two vertically
offset spectral sequences, ordered primarily by decreas-
ing Teff. With this ordering, the dominant temperature
driven evolution of the SPHEREx spectral morphology
is seen directly: the broad molecular bands strengthen
and change shape systematically from the late M regime
through the T/Y regime. The labels retain the adopted
log g value for each bin, but the gravity dependence
is weaker than the temperature dependence in much
of the atlas.
In particular, at Teff ≳1800 K, spec-
tra with similar temperatures but different log g values
show only weak differences at the SPHEREx spectral
resolution, indicating limited gravity sensitivity for the
warmer sources.

At

perature dependence. Below ∼1500 K, both begin to
rise, reach maxima around ∼1000 K, and then decline
again toward lower temperatures, with CO2 exhibiting
the larger overall amplitude. At a given Teff, both in-
dices also show substantial dispersion, especially in the
CO2 panel of Figure 6.

A plausible physical interpretation of this shared be-
havior is that the two indices respond to related low
temperature atmospheric physics rather than to inde-
pendent trends.
The rise below ∼1500 K likely re-
flects changing thermal chemical structure across the
L/T regime, which strengthens the CO and CO2 bands
relative to the local continuum (S. K. Leggett et al. 2007;
C. V. Morley et al. 2012; T. Tsuji et al. 2011; S. Sora-
hana & I. Yamamura 2012). Below ∼1000 K, the de-
cline of both indices is most plausibly linked to carbon
chemistry, as carbon is increasingly partitioned into CH4
in the late T regime, reducing the conditions that favor
strong CO and CO2 absorption in the observable photo-
sphere (K. J. Zahnle & M. S. Marley 2014). Some indi-
vidual objects can nevertheless retain relatively strong
CO absorption below ∼1000 K, most plausibly because
vertical mixing quenches the CO abundance by dredging
up CO rich gas from deeper, hotter layers (C. Visscher
& J. I. Moses 2011).
Comparison with the model sequences clarifies both
the relative performance of the two model families and
the diagnostic value of these indices. For H2O and CH4,
the SAND and ATMO2020++ sequences are broadly
consistent with each other and both reproduce the main
observed temperature trend reasonably well.
For CO
and CO2, however, the two model families diverge much
more strongly, and ATMO2020++ provides the closer
overall match to the observed relation.
In these two
carbon bearing indices, the separation between different
metallicity model tracks explains most of the observed
dispersion, indicating that metallicity is the dominant
driver of the CO and CO2 index scatter. The CO2 index
is especially informative in this respect: in the approx-
imate range Teff ∼800–1300 K, it shows the strongest
metallicity sensitivity among the four indices while re-
taining a relatively coherent temperature dependence.
This makes the CO2 index a useful empirical metallicity
tracer for brown dwarfs in this temperature interval.
Overall, the molecular indices trace a clear atmo-
spheric sequence across the ultracool dwarf population.
H2O and CH4 mainly follow the temperature sequence,
while CO and CO2 are additionally sensitive to metal-
licity and to atmospheric physics that depends on the
adopted model in the 3–5 µm regime. Together, these
indices provide an empirical probe of both the dominant
temperature progression and the secondary atmospheric
parameters operating in ultracool dwarfs.
6. CONCLUSION
Our main results show how a homogeneous SPHEREx
sample links observed ultracool dwarf spectra to atmo-
spheric parameters, bolometric luminosities, evolution-
ary constraints, and molecular trends. The main con-
clusions are as follows.
1. We constructed the SPHEREx Ultracool Dwarf
spectral Atlas (SUDA) from SPHEREx QR2 data
and assembled a final sample of 1675 ultracool ob-
jects with reliable 0.75–5 µm spectroscopy. This
sample provides a homogeneous set of atmospheric
parameters, including Teff, log g, and [M/H], de-
rived within a uniform framework using the SAND
and ATMO2020++ model grids.
2. By combining the best fitting models with the
observed spectra, we directly calculated Lbol for
sources with parallaxes. For sources without par-
allax information, we used XGBoost to estimate
Lbol from Teff, log g, and [M/H]. We also used radii
together with Teff and C23 evolutionary models to
derive masses, ages, and evolutionary surface grav-
ities. The evolutionary log g differ systematically
from the atmospheric log g inferred from spectral
fitting in the 1700–2500 K range, where the fitted
atmospheric log g values are typically lower by a
median offset of about 1.1 dex. This discrepancy
likely reflects the limited ability of low resolution
SPHEREx spectra to separate log g from [M/H] in
L dwarf atmospheres, because gravity dependent
spectral changes are weak and can be partly ab-
sorbed by metallicity variations across the broad
SAND metallicity grid.
3. We constructed an empirical spectral atlas in the
Teff and log g diagram directly from the observed
SPHEREx spectra of sources with parallaxes. The
spectra were binned using the Teff fitted with at-
mospheric models and the log g inferred from evo-
lutionary models, providing an observational refer-
ence sequence for ultracool dwarfs over 0.75–5 µm
that links spectral morphology to the adopted
physical parameter scale.
4. The molecular index analysis reveals a coherent
atmospheric sequence across the sample.
H2O
and CH4 strengthen toward lower Teff, with a
steeper rise between ∼1300 and 1100 K, and the
SAND and ATMO2020++ model sequences re-
produce these trends in broadly consistent ways.
CO and CO2 show a shared nonmonotonic evolu-
tion: both rise below ∼1500 K, reach a turnover
near ∼1000 K, and decline at lower tempera-
tures. For these carbon bearing indices, the two
model families differ much more strongly, with
ATMO2020++ providing the closer overall match

11
to the observed relation. Their dispersion is ex-
plained primarily by metallicity, and the CO2 in-
dex is especially sensitive to metallicity in the
range Teff ∼800–1300 K, making it a useful empir-
ical tracer of metallicity for brown dwarfs in this
temperature interval.
Taken together, this work provides a homogeneous
empirical basis for studying ultracool dwarfs across the
0.75–5 µm range.
By combining a large spectral at-
las, uniformly derived atmospheric parameters, bolo-
metric luminosities, constraints from evolutionary mod-
els, and molecular index diagnostics within a single sam-
ple, SUDA links observed spectral morphology to at-
mospheric chemistry, substellar cooling, and the bound-
ary between stars and brown dwarfs. It therefore pro-
vides an observational basis for future population stud-
ies, model calibration, and further investigations of the
coolest and lowest mass sources with future infrared
space telescopes.
APPENDIX
A. SAMPLE QUALITY CONTROL
This appendix summarizes the screening and calibration steps that define the final SUDA analysis sample.
A.1. High S/N Source Screening
We applied quality and astrophysical cuts to isolate a reliable set of SPHEREx spectra for the main analysis.
First, we filtered individual spectral points using SPHEREx data quality flags. Following the SPHEREx Explanatory
Supplement7 (version 1.3, Table 10), we retained only flux measurements with flag value 221.
For each spectrum, we then computed the median S/N over retained wavelength points and excluded spectra
with median S/N < 2.
We also applied astrophysical cuts using UltracoolSheet metadata to reduce systemat-
ics from unresolved systems and complex environments.
Specifically, we removed unresolved binaries/multiples
(multiple unresolved in this table), sources flagged as problematic (flag), and objects associated with active
star forming regions (e.g., Taurus, Chamaeleon, Lupus, and IC 348) identified through age category.
After these cuts, we performed visual inspection and photometric consistency checks to mitigate contamination from
blended sources within the SPHEREx beam. Extracted spectra were compared against available 2MASS J, H, Ks (or
MKO J, H, K when 2MASS was unavailable) and WISE W1 photometry. Synthetic broadband fluxes were computed
from the spectra using filter profiles from the SVO Filter Profile Service8 and compared with catalog photometry.
When SPHEREx fluxes were significantly higher than catalog photometry, we inspected WISE and ZTF images to
assess contamination by nearby sources within 6–10′′ of the target; clearly contaminated objects were removed. When
SPHEREx fluxes were significantly lower, we checked SPHEREx images and source alignment to identify possible
positional offsets or centroid mismatches.
After all quality and selection cuts, the final sample contains 1675 ultracool stars and brown dwarfs with reliable
SPHEREx spectra spanning 0.75–5 µm.
A.2. Flux Calibration
We use external photometry to evaluate the absolute flux consistency of the SPHEREx spectra. We compare the
spectra with broadband photometry using 2MASS J, H, and Ks when available, MKO J, H, and K otherwise, and
WISE W1 to anchor the longer wavelength scale. For each source, synthetic photometry was computed in these bands
and used to derive a linear scaling factor R by minimizing P
i (Fphot,i −R Fsyn,i)2 , where Fsyn,i denotes the synthetic
flux computed from the SPHEREx spectrum through the i-th photometric bandpass, and Fphot,i is the corresponding
photometry value.

We identified a subset of spectra affected by short wavelength atmospheric emission line contamination.

objects show anomalously elevated short wavelength flux, most prominently in Band 1 (0.744–1.116 µm), relative to
the best fitting atmospheric models.
Inspection of the corresponding SPHEREx images indicates that this excess is caused by terrestrial atmospheric

D. MACHINE LEARNING DETAILS FOR log LBOL ESTIMATION
This appendix summarizes the XGBoost method used to estimate Lbol for sources without parallaxes.
D.1. Machine Learning Method and Validation
We train an XGBoost regressor to predict log(Lbol/L⊙) from Teff, log g, and [M/H]. The model is trained with a
squared error objective and evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE) in log(Lbol/L⊙). The regression target
is y = log(Lbol/L⊙), and the feature vector is x = (Teff, log g, [M/H]). These features are chosen because they are
available for essentially all objects and are physically linked to luminosity through the relation between radius and
temperature.
The training set is constructed from sources with reliable luminosities based on parallax. To mitigate contamination
by problematic entries (e.g., unphysical solutions driven by erroneous photometry or distances), we apply additional
quality cuts based on the inferred radius. Specifically, we retain only sources with radii in the range 0.7–2.5 RJup. Radii
are computed using the flux scaling parameter log(R2/D2) obtained from the nested sampling analysis in combination
with literature parallaxes. After these selections, the final training set contains 1041 sources. The trained model is
then applied to 550 sources without parallax measurements, which constitute the prediction sample of this study.
The training labels have heterogeneous uncertainties, which we incorporate through sample weighting. Each training
object is assigned a weight

(σ0 ≃0.026 dex in our training sample).
Hyperparameters are selected using ten fold cross validation on the training set. We use the out of fold (OOF)
MAE as the primary metric, since it summarizes typical prediction error for unseen objects drawn from the same
distribution. The final model achieves an OOF MAE of ≃0.12 dex. We optimize six hyperparameters and keep all
others at XGBoost defaults. The adopted values are γ = 0, learning rate = 0.02, maximum tree depth = 5, maximum
number of leaves = 64, number of estimators = 300, and λ = 10 (L2 regularization).
For sources without parallax, we propagate uncertainties in the input atmospheric parameters into the predicted
luminosities by Monte Carlo sampling. For each object, we draw 3000 realizations of (Teff, log g, [M/H]). The trained
XGBoost model is evaluated for each realization, yielding a distribution of predicted y = log(Lbol/L⊙). We adopt
the mean of the Monte Carlo prediction distribution as the predicted luminosity and use its standard deviation as the
corresponding uncertainty.
D.2. Training Results Evaluation
The trained XGBoost model reproduces the training labels with good overall accuracy, while showing mild bias
at the highest luminosities. The ML predictions have an MAE of 0.092 dex. Figure D1 summarizes the training
performance: the left panel shows predicted versus true log Lbol, and the right panel shows the residual distribution
∆log Lbol.
At the high Lbol end, the left panel shows a systematic underestimation, which shifts the center of the residual
distribution in the right panel toward negative values. This bias likely arises from two effects: (1) regression to the
mean in a regularized model, which pulls predictions toward the bulk of the training set and suppresses the brightest
tail; and (2) a biased distribution of training set Lbol in (Teff, log g) space, where the luminous objects occupy sparsely
sampled regions near the coverage boundary, leaving the model weakly constrained there.
