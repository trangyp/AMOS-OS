Lauritz van Luijk1,2, Henrik Wilming3
1Perimeter Institute for Theoretical Physics, Waterloo, Ontario, Canada

Abstract
We study the interconversion of families of quantum states (“statistical experiments”) via
positive, trace-preserving (PTP) maps and clarify its mathematical structure in terms of minimal
sufficient Jordan algebras, which can be seen to generalize the Koashi-Imoto decomposition to
the PTP setting.
In particular, we show that Neyman-Pearson tests generate the minimal
sufficient Jordan algebra, and hence also the minimal sufficient *-algebra corresponding to the
Koashi-Imoto decomposition. As applications, we show that a) equality in the data-processing
inequality for the relative entropy or the α-z quantum Rényi divergence implies the existence of
a recovery map also in the PTP case and b) that two dichotomies can be interconverted by PTP
maps if and only if they can be interconverted by decomposable, trace-preserving maps. We
thoroughly review the necessary mathematical background on Jordan algebras. As a step beyond
the finite-dimensional case, we also prove Frenkel’s formula for approximately finite-dimensional
von Neumann algebras.

Introduction and overview
Consider a pair of quantum states (ρ, σ), also called a “dichotomy”, on a finite-dimensional
Hilbert space1 H representing different preparations of the same system. It is a natural and
fundamental question to ask: How different, i.e., distinguishable, are the two states?
Let us discuss two different ways to address this question: The first, and maybe most natural
one, is via (Bayesian) hypothesis testing. That is, assuming that ρ is (assumed to be) prepared
with some prior probability p and σ with some prior probability 1 −p, we perform a binary
measurement represented by a positive operator-valued measure (x, 1 −x) with effect operator
0 ≤x ≤1. If the outcome is 0 (corresponding to x), then we guess that ρ was prepared; if
the outcome is 1 (corresponding to 1 −x) we guess that σ is prepared. Then (ρ, σ) are highly
distinguishable if the success probability is large, and are little distinguishable if the success
probability is low. The optimal test x is essentially unique and given by the projector [ρ > tσ]
onto the positive part of ρ −tσ, with t = (1 −p)/p [1], see also Section 8.
A particularly
important subtask of hypothesis testing is asymmetric hypothesis testing, where one tries to
minimize the error probability to wrongly guess ρ if the actual state is σ under the constraint
that the probability to correctly identify ρ if it is prepared is lower bounded by some value 1−ε.
The quantum Stein’s Lemma [2] shows that in the asymptotic limit of many independent copies,
the resulting error probability decreases exponentially with rate given by the quantum relative
entropy D(ρ∥σ) = tr(ρ log ρ) −tr(ρ log σ).
A different way to think about distinguishability is in terms of physical processes that are
applied to the system. Any physical process, applied after the respective preparations, should
only be able to reduce the distinguishability of ρ and σ. Any distinguishability measure D should
hence fulfill the data-processing inequality
D(ρ∥σ) ≥D(T ∗ρ∥T ∗σ),
(1.1)
for any completely positive, trace-preserving (CPTP) map T ∗.2 Examples are the success prob-
ability in hypothesis testing discussed above, or the quantum relative entropy D. In fact the
literature of quantum information theory exhibits a whole zoo of so-called divergences D (see
[3, 4]): Positive functions on pairs of density matrices, which in addition to (1.1) also fulfill
D(ρ∥σ) = 0 if and only if ρ = σ (and possibly have additional desirable properties, such as
additivity under tensor products). The problem of determining when one dichotomy can be
converted into another one (but not necessarily the other way around) has a long history dating
back to Alberti and Uhlmann [5], see also [6–11] for examples of recent work.
Two dichotomies (ρ1, σ1) and (ρ2, σ2) are clearly equally distinguishable if there are physical

we may and will assume in the following that statistical experiments are always faithful, i.e. if
a ≥0, then tr(aρθ) = 0 for all θ implies a = 0 (any statistical experiment is CPTP-equivalent
to a faithful one, see Section 3). We note here already that Section 2 collects the necessary
mathematical background that we need to establish our results and may be outside the usual
mathematical scope of quantum information theory.
1.1
Sufficiency and the structure of equivalent statistical experiments
A central notion of this work is sufficiency. Fix a faithful statistical experiment (ρθ) (we sup-
press the label set Θ) on L(H). A unital *-algebra3 A ⊂L(H) is called sufficient for the sta-
tistical experiment (ρθ) if there exists a unital, completely-positive (UCP) map T : L(H) →A
such that T ∗ρθ = ρθ for all θ ∈Θ [26, 27]. Here T ∗is the Hilbert-Schmidt dual defined via
tr(T ∗(a)b) = tr(aTb). This means that the states ρθ can be recovered from their restrictions to
the subalgebra A. In other words, all information about the statistical experiment is contained
in A.4 Among all CPTP-sufficient *-algebras, there is a minimal one, which we denote by A(ρθ)
and call the minimal sufficient *-algebra [28]. The minimal sufficient *-algebra is the same (up
to isomorphism) as the algebra obtained from the Koashi-Imoto decomposition of a statistical
experiment [28–30]. Petz neatly characterized sufficient *-algebras [31, 32], see also [26, 27].
In this work, we generalize from sufficient *-algebras. Let K ⊂L(H) be an operator system,
i.e., a unital, complex subspace closed under the adjoint. We say that K is (C)PTP-sufficient

1.4
Beyond finite dimensions
Our proofs are restricted to finite dimensions, but we believe that most of our results extend
to the setting of von Neumann algebras. In fact, we believe that they generalize to the setting

2.5
Structure theory of abstract J*-algebras
So far, we have considered J*-algebras as collections of operators on a given Hilbert space H.
It is sometimes better to regard J*-algebras as abstract objects and to regard the action on a
given Hilbert space as a representation. We make the following non-standard definition:17

Sufficiency encodes the idea that a strict subset of all possible measurements already encodes all
the relevant information of a statistical experiment (ρθ)θ∈Θ. As discussed in the introduction,
there are different ways to formalize this notion. If T is a (ρθ)-preserving UCP map on L(H),

T ∗ρθ = ρθ,
θ ∈Θ,
(4.1)

The range K = Ran(T) of a UP map T is an operator system (see Definition 2.1). An
operator system is linearly spanned by the effect observables it contains, i.e., K = span [0, 1]K,
where [0, 1]K = {x ∈K : 0 ≤x ≤1}. We make the following definition:

Bayesian hypothesis testing. Clearly, CPTP-sufficiency implies PTP-sufficiency. Unsurprisingly,

equivalent if K is a *-algebra [63].

A.1
Jordan symmetries vs unitary symmetries
An (anti-)unitary symmetry of a dichotomy (ρ, σ) on a Hilbert space H is an (anti-)unitary
operator u on H such that
uρu∗= ρ,
uσu∗= σ.
(A.1)
A unitary symmetry is trivial if u ∝1 is a scalar. The absence of non-trivial unitary symmetries
is easily characterized:
Lemma A.1. Let (ρ, σ) be a faithful dichotomy. The following are equivalent:
(a) (ρ, σ) only have trivial unitary symmetries;
(b) L(H) is the minimal sufficient *-algebra;
(c) the Koashi-Imoto decomposition H = ⊕jKj ⊗Lj consists of a single block K with trivial
multiplicity, i.e., dim L = 1.
(d) the pair (ρ, σ) is irreducible, i.e., all jointly invariant subspaces are trivial.
Proof. (a) ⇔(d) follows from the standard fact that a pair (a, b) of hermitian operators on a
finite-dimensional Hilbert space is irreducible if and only if the only unitaries commuting with
both a and b are the trivial ones. (b) ⇔(c) is explained in Remark 4.9. It follows from the
Koashi-Imoto decomposition (see Remark 4.9) that the group of unitary symmetries of (ρ, σ)
is the unitary group of the commutant of the minimal sufficient *-algebra A(ρ,σ). This unitary
group is trivial if and only if A(ρ,σ) = L(H). This shows (a) ⇔(b), which finishes the proof.
Proposition A.2. Let (ρ, σ) be a faithful dichotomy. The following are equivalent:
(a) (ρ, σ) has no anti-unitary symmetries and only trivial unitary symmetries;
(b) L(H) = J(ρ,σ) is the minimal sufficient J*-algebra;
(c) The following three conditions are met:
(c.1) (ρ, σ) only has trivial unitary symmetries,
(c.2) there is no basis relative to which both ρ and σ are real matrices,
(c.3) if dim(H) = 2n is even, it is not possible to decompose H as H = Cn ⊕Cn in such a
way that β(ρ) = ρ and β(σ) = σ, where β is defined as in Example 2.2.
Proof. Since all J*-automorphisms of L(H) are either implemented by unitaries or anti-unitaries
(this follows from Wigner’s theorem [84]), item (a) is equivalent to:
(a′) The identity is the only J*-automorphism of L(H) that leaves both ρ and σ invariant.
(a) ⇒(c) is clear.
(b) ⇒(a′): Let ϑ be a J*-automorphism of L(H) with ϑ(ρ) = ρ, ϑ(σ) = σ. Then the fixed-
point J*-algebra Fix(ϑ) is sufficient for (ρ, σ) (indeed, the conditional expectation E = 1
2(id +ϑ)
onto J is (ρ, σ)-preserving). Hence, Fix(ϑ) = L(H) because any sufficient J*-algebra contains
the minimal sufficient J*-algebra. This shows the claim since Fix(ϑ) = L(H) implies ϑ = id.

(c) ⇒(b): By Lemma A.1, item (c.1) implies that the minimal sufficient *-algebra is L(H).

A.2
Sufficiently many examples
We say that a J*-algebra J is 2-generated if there exist hermitian elements a, b ∈J such
that J = J*-alg(a, b). 2-generated J*-algebras are classified in Appendix B: A J*-algebra is
2-generated if and only if it is J*-isomorphic to a direct sum of the J*-factors L(Cd) (d ≥1),
L(Cd)t (d ≥2), and L(C2d)β (d ≥4). We conjecture that 2-generatedness is equivalent to being
minimal sufficient for some dichotomy. We are only able to prove this for J*-factors.
Proposition A.4. Let J be a J*-factor on a Hilbert space H. The following are equivalent:
(a) J = J(ρ,σ) is the minimal sufficient J*-algebra of a faithful dichotomy (ρ, σ) on H;
(b) J is 2-generated.
