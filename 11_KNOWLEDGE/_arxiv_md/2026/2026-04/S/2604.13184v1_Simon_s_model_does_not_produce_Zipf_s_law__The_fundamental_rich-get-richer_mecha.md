Power-law size rankings—from word frequencies to city sizes—are
among the most universal patterns in complex systems, yet their
canonical model, due to Simon, catastrophically fails in the Zipf’s
law limit. We derive the fundamental rich-get-richer mechanism that
correctly produces any power-law size ranking, uncovering that Zipf’s
law emerges when innovation decays as the inverse logarithm of the
number of types. Our dynamic innovation rate universally governs
type emergence in any such system, regardless of the underlying
mechanism.
Abstract:
Many complex systems are composed of disparate, interacting types
of varying ‘sizes’: Species abundances in ecosystems, firm sizes in
markets, city populations in countries, word counts in language, etc.
A longstanding mystery of complex systems is Zipf’s law, which is
the empirical observation that component size decreases as the
inverse of component rank—S ∝r−1— and its generalization
S ∝r−α for α ≥0. Herbert Simon’s 1955 theoretical rich-get-richer
mechanism for system growth has prevailed as capturing the essential
process. But Simon’s analysis is in fact flawed: In the limit of zero
innovation, the model leads to a winner-takes-all system with
α →∞, rather than α →1. Here, for pure rich-get-richer systems,
we derive the time-dependent innovation rate ρt that correctly
produces power-law size rankings across all α ≥0. To produce Zipf’s
law, we uncover that ρt must decay as the inverse of the log of the
number of types, 1/ ln N. We then show that our time-dependent
innovation rate governs type emergence in any system obeying a
power-law size-ranking, independent of the underlying mechanism.
We demonstrate agreement between our model’s output and word
rankings in a collection of famous novels, while Simon’s model fails.
Going forward, our dynamic innovation rate mechanism provides the
fundamental, Drosophila-like model for all rich-get-richer systems.

Introduction
Power-law size rankings—the empirical observation that
component size S decreases as an inverse power of
component rank r, S ∼r−α—are among the most
pervasive quantitative regularities in complex systems.
They appear in word frequencies in texts [1–4], species
abundances in ecosystems [5], city populations and firm
sizes in socioeconomic systems [6, 7], citation counts in
complex networks [8, 9], and many other contexts [10–14].
The special case α = 1, known as Zipf’s law, has attracted
particular attention for its near-universality in language
and beyond. Although the empirical universality of
power-law size rankings has been questioned in specific
domains [15–17], the pattern is sufficiently widespread
and consistent to demand a principled theory.
The most influential explanation is Herbert Simon’s 1955
rich-get-richer model [18], which builds on earlier work by
Yule [19] and has become the canonical theoretical
foundation for power-law size rankings. Simon’s
mechanism is elegantly simple: at each step, a single new
token is added to a growing system, either introducing a
novel type with probability ρ or reinforcing an existing
type with probability 1 −ρ, where each type attracts new
tokens in proportion to its current size. Simon’s
rich-get-richer process produces a power-law size ranking
with exponent α = 1 −ρ, and variants of the mechanism
have been identified in operation across a wide range of
real systems [9, 12, 20]. Simon’s model was ported to
evolving networks by Price for scientific citations in the
1960s [8], and later separately discovered for so-called
scale-free networks [21]. The major competing theory has
been Mandelbrot’s contemporary optimization argument,
which offered no mechanism, did not have a reason for the
exceptionality of Zipf’s law, and led to an extended back
and forth between Simon and Mandelbrot [22–27].
But Simon’s model contains a long missed critical flaw
that becomes catastrophic where it matters most, as
α →1−. As ρ →0, Simon’s analysis incorrectly showed
that the size-ranking exponent approaches α →1−,
recovering Zipf’s law. Instead, the first-mover advantage
inherent to rich-get-richer growth diverges as 1/ρ, so that
the leading type’s size is a factor of 1/ρ larger than all
others [28]. In the zero-innovation limit ρ = 0, the model
collapses entirely to a winner-takes-all outcome with the
degenerate case of α →∞rather than α = 1. As we show
in Fig. 1, this failure intensifies monotonically as ρ
decreases. Furthermore, the model is structurally
incapable of producing size rankings with 1 < α < ∞,
excluding an entire and empirically relevant region of the
parameter space.
Here, we derive the correct, time-dependent innovation
rate ρt,α that generalizes Simon’s rich-get-richer model to
produce power-law size rankings for any α ≥0. Our
generalized rate contains no discontinuities but rather

Concluding remarks
We have uncovered the minimal dynamic innovation rate
that corrects and extends Simon’s rich-get-richer model so
as to produce power-law size rankings across all
exponents, 0 ≤α < ∞. Having shown how Simon’s model
fails to actually produce Zipf’s law in the limit of ρ →0,
we determine the behavior of the dynamic innovation rate
that does: ρt,1 →1/ log Nt,1. Future work would explore
the possible origins of this form. For novels, we have
shown that our model accurately reproduces empirical
size rankings while Simon’s model does not. We have
shown a deep congruence between our mechanistic
rich–get–richer process and a general growing,
non-mechanistic model. Any mechanism that leads to a
growing power-law size ranking will functionally entail our
generalized innovation rate.
Our model is purposefully constrained by the assumption
of a proportionate rich-get-richer process, which, although
widespread, is not universal in empirical systems. While
our generalized innovation rate leads to the full range of
power-law exponents, additional mechanisms known to
generate power law size rankings could further expand the
framework’s scope and realism. Finally, for α > 1, the
power-law-in–power-law-out nature of the mechanism
invites explication of an underlying process that could
give rise to a power-law decaying innovation rate. These
limitations aside, our generalized innovation rate provides
a coherent reference for understanding power law size
rankings, repairs and goes beyond Simon’s model, and
will serve as a base model for all rich-get-richer systems.
