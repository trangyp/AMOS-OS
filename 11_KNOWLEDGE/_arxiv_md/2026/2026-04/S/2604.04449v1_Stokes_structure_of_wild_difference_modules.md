STOKES STRUCTURE OF WILD DIFFERENCE MODULES
YOTA SHAMOTO
Abstract. We formulate and prove a Riemann–Hilbert correspondence between
two categories: wild difference modules and wild Stokes-filtered Aper-modules.
This correspondence is motivated by the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence for
germs of meromorphic connections in one variable due to Deligne–Malgrange. It
also generalizes the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence for mild difference modules.
1. Introduction
In this paper, we establish a Riemann–Hilbert correspondence between two cate-
gories. The first is the category of additive analytic difference modules; the second
is the category of wild Stokes-filtered Aper-modules.
This correspondence is a generalization of the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence
[10] for mild difference modules, and can be regarded as a difference analogue of the
Deligne–Malgrange Riemann–Hilbert correspondence [1, 7] for germs of meromor-
phic connections in one variable. This generalization covers a new class of modules
called wild difference modules, as introduced in [12].
In this introduction, we describe the two categories in §1.1 and §1.2. We then
explain the Riemann–Hilbert functor between these categories in §1.3.
1.1. Wild difference modules. Let C({s−1}) be the field of convergent Laurent
series in s−1 with complex coefficients. We have an automorphism ϕ: C({s−1}) →
C({s−1}) defined by ϕ(f)(s) = f(s + 1).
A difference module is a pair (M , ψ)
consisting of a C({s−1})-vector space M and a C-linear automorphism ψ: M →M
which satisfies ψ(fv) = ϕ(f)ψ(v) for any f ∈C({s−1}) and v ∈M . A difference
module is called mild if it is isomorphic to a module of the form (C({s−1})r, A(s)ϕ)
where A(s) has holomorphic entries in s−1 whose constant term A(∞) is invertible.
A difference module which is not mild is called wild (cf. [12]). A typical example
of a wild difference module is (M , ψ) = (C({s−1}), s−1ϕ). A “solution” of this mod-
ule is the Gamma function Γ(s), which admits the following asymptotic expansion
as s →∞for any ε > 0, in the sector −π + ε < arg s < π −ε:

1.3. Riemann–Hilbert functor. The main difference between the mild case and
the wild case is the construction of the Riemann–Hilbert functor. The Riemann–
Hilbert functor defined in [10] for mild difference modules does not work well for
wild difference modules. The difficulty appears when we deal with the function ss
in the construction of the functor. Since ss grows or decays faster than any power
of u = exp(2πis), naive definitions of the de Rham functor cannot capture ss, or
else become too large to be manageable.
To overcome the difficulty, we introduce a new sheaf Q of rings on S1 as a subsheaf
of e
O. Roughly speaking, Q consists of holomorphic functions on sectors at infinity
that satisfy a suitable moderate growth condition in the u-direction. (see §3).

3.1.1. Notation. A pair (R, a) consisting of a commutative ring R and an automor-
phism a: R →R is called a difference ring. A typical example is a pair (C[s], Φ∗) of
the polynomial ring C[s] and an automorphism Φ∗defined as Φ∗(P(s)) = P(s + 1),
P(s) ∈C[s].
If R is a field, the difference ring is called a difference field.
Let

difference rings is a morphism of rings that is compatible with the automorphisms.
Let (R, a) be a difference ring. A pair (M, b) consisting of an R-module M and
an automorphism b: M →M is called a difference module over (R, a) if b(rm) =
a(r)b(m) holds for any r ∈R and m ∈M. For two difference modules (Mi, bi)
(i = 1, 2), a morphism of difference modules from (M1, b1) to (M2, b2) is a morphism
f : M1 →M2 of R-modules such that f ◦b1 = b2 ◦f. The set of morphisms is
denoted by Hom(R,a)(M1, M2). The subscript (R, a) is often omitted. The tensor
