ABSTRACT
Pebble drift plays a central role in modern planet formation models. In this work we carry out planet formation simulations
(including pebble accretion and migration) for a range of disc parameters to investigate (a) the impact of the snowline pebble
mass flux on final planet orbits and masses, and (b) the back-reaction of growing and migrating planets on the snowline pebble
fluxes in their natal discs. We find a strong correlation between the snowline pebble flux (at the time of protoplanet insertion)
and the final planet mass. The correlation is continuous in disks with high turbulence levels (𝛼= 10−3), but exhibits a step
function at lower turbulence (𝛼= 10−4), with giant planet formation requiring (initial) snowline pebble mass fluxes exceeding
100 M⊕Myr−1. We find qualitative agreement between pebble mass fluxes inferred for discs aged ∼1 Myr and our planet-
containing models, especially for larger disks (≥40 au), high 𝛼(10−3), and low 𝑣frag (3 m s−1). Additionally, giant planets in high
turbulence disks are found to perturb the snowline pebble flux only temporarily (for ≈105−6 yr) due to them quickly growing
and migrating across the snowline. Our simulations show that currently observed pebble fluxes can indeed be used to constrain
planet formation simulations, emphasizing that planet formation via pebble accretion is broadly in agreement with the currently
available constraints from disc evolution as provided by JWST.
Key words: protoplanetary discs – planet–disc interactions – planets and satellites: formation – planets and satellites: dynamical
evolution and stability
1 INTRODUCTION
Modern planet formation theory reserves a big role for peb-
ble drift, with the large-scale inward migration of marginally-
decoupled pebbles featuring centrally in models of planetesimal for-
mation (Drążkowska & Alibert 2017; Schoonenberg & Ormel 2017;
Drążkowska et al. 2023), planet growth and accretion (Johansen &
Lambrechts 2017; Ormel & Liu 2018), early solar system evolution
(Lichtenberg et al. 2021), and evolving disc chemistry (e.g., Booth
et al. 2017; Krijt et al. 2020; Mah et al. 2024; Williams et al. 2025).
At the same time, qualitative observational constraints on the timing
and magnitude of pebble drift have been challenging to obtain, with
measurements of e.g., dust disc mass or size evolution challenging
to interpret (Pascucci et al. 2016; Najita & Bergin 2018).
In this context there has been push in recent years to attempt to
estimate pebble fluxes at specific locations in discs (i.e., at major
snowlines, primarily those of CO, CO2, and H2O) by measuring
variations in the gas-phase abundance of major carbon and oxygen
carriers – and associated/attributing those changes to pebble drift and
ice sublimation (see Bosman et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019, 2020;
Williams & Krijt 2025). For water in particular, system-to-system
variations seen via infrared spectroscopy first with Spitzer and now

with the James Webb Space Telescope are frequently attributed to
variations in the icy pebble mass capable of reaching the inner disc
(Najita et al. 2013; Banzatti et al. 2020, 2023; Romero-Mirza et al.
2024; Sellek et al. 2025).
Most recently, Krijt et al. (2025) combined JWST MIRI/MRS and
ALMA continuum data for twenty-one protoplanetary discs to (a)
measure pebble mass fluxes across the water snowline (building on
the method outlined in Romero-Mirza et al. 2024) and (b) demon-
strate how those fluxes are shaped by the influence of gaps acting as
pebble traps in the outer disc – a process studied in various numerical
works (e.g., Kalyaan et al. 2023; Bitsch & Mah 2023; Stammler et al.
2023; Easterwood et al. 2024; Mah et al. 2024). Pebble mass flux
estimates ranged from ∼1−103 𝑀⊕/Myr in these ∼Myr-old systems,
broadly consistent with ranges estimated for the early solar system
and values assumed in planet formation models (Krijt et al. 2025;
Lichtenberg et al. 2021, Fig. 2). Given that models indicate that the
emerging masses (and architectures) of inner planetary systems are
very sensitive to the pebble mass flux arriving in the inner disc (e.g.,
Lambrechts et al. 2019; Drążkowska et al. 2021; McCloat et al. 2025;
Danti et al. 2025), what does this broad range of inferred snowline
pebble fluxes mean for the planets growing in the inner regions of
these systems? The aims of this study then are two-fold: (a) study
what planet formation models predict in terms of final planet masses
as a function of snowline pebble flux; and, at the same time (b) study

2 METHODOLOGY
2.1 Model
We use chemcomp1 (Schneider & Bitsch 2021a) to study the com-
bined evolution of discs, their dust/pebbles, and planet accretion
and migration. This is a semi-analytical 1D model which evolves a
viscous protoplanetary disk, including pebble drift, evaporation and
condensation, planetary growth and migration, and multiple chemi-
cal species. The midplane temperature of the disk is set at the start
and is calculated from viscous heating and irradiation from the star.
The chemcomp code is described in detail in Schneider & Bitsch
(2021a,b) and has in recent years been used to study, for example giant
planet formation (Savvidou & Bitsch 2023), the transport and ther-
mal decomposition of refractory organics (Houge et al. 2025b) and
the impact of volatile entrapment on evolving C/O ratios (Williams
et al. 2025). In the remainder of this section we discuss the disc
parameters we use, how the snowline pebble mass flux is obtained in
our modelling framework, and finally how planets are inserted and
evolve using a few illustrative examples.

2.5 Planet accretion and migration
Once injected, protoplanets grow by accreting drifting pebbles and/or
gas, and can migrate radially as they interact with the ambient disc.
For pebble accretion, chemcomp convolves the evolving pebble flux
at the planet’s location with a pebble accretion efficiency which
depends primarily on the pebble Stokes number, the turbulence, and
the protoplanet mass (see Schneider & Bitsch 2021a, Sect. 2.6.1 for
a detailed description). Both 2D and 3D regimes (see e.g., Johansen
& Lambrechts 2017; Ormel & Liu 2018) are considered.

mass, above which planets significantly perturb the local gas surface
density, preventing pebbles from reaching their location and bringing
a halt to pebble accretion. Based on Bitsch et al. (2018), chemcomp

3 RESULTS
We now turn to discussing the connection between the final planet
properties (e.g., final mass and position as indicated by the pink
points in Fig. 2) and snowline pebble flux (as depicted in Fig. 1). We
will follow two approaches that tell different parts of the story. First,
we compare planet outcomes to the snowline pebble flux measured
at the time of protoplanet insertion (Sect. 3.1). This approach high-
lights primarily the impact of the snowline pebble flux (and other disc
properties) on the planet’s ability to grow. Additionally, in Sect. 3.2,
we compare planet outcomes to the snowline pebble flux as eval-
uated around 1 Myr which allows for a more direct connection of
observations as this time is closer to the ages sampled in e.g., Krijt
et al. (2025). We discuss the impact of planets (if already sufficiently
massive by 1 Myr) on the snowline pebble flux in Sect. 3.3.
3.1 Pebble flux at the time of (proto)planet injection
Figure 3 shows final planet mass vs. the snowline pebble mass flux
(evaluated at the time of protoplanet injection 𝑡0) as a function of a
range of disc and protoplanet parameters (various colour bars). We
also highlight in each panel the positions of the four planets discussed
in Sect. 2.7 to aid in the interpretation.
First and foremost, just from the distribution of the points in any of
the panels, we see there is a clear correlation between the (maximum)
final planet mass and snowline pebble flux: protoplanets inserted
when the pebble flux is below ≈50−100𝑀⊕Myr−1 can not reach
masses exceeding a few 10𝑀⊕. This can be explained by planets in
discs with a lower pebble flux struggling to grow quickly early on,
and thus not being able to capture significant gas envelopes during
the gas disc lifetime.
On top of this general trend however there is considerable scatter.
Indeed, even in discs with pebble fluxes approaching ≈103 𝑀⊕Myr−1
there are planets that end up with masses of (just) 0.1𝑀⊕. There can
be various reasons for this. First, it is worth stressing that while the
pebble flux is measured at the water snowline (as we want to compare
fluxes inferred from JWST observations), protoplanets are inserted
with initial positions ranging from 0.5 and 20 au (see Sect. 2.7 and
Fig. 3A). As such, there may be differences between the pebble flux
at the planet position (most relevant for its growth) and the one at the
snowline, although we expect these differences to be minimal in the
inner ∼10au for most of the disc’s lifetime (see e.g. Mulders et al.
2021, Fig. 1). Second, and likely more important, is that the rate of
pebble accretion depends not solely on the pebble flux but also on
the pebble accretion efficiency, which itself is a complex function
of also the pebble Stokes numbers, the turbulence strength 𝛼, the
protoplanet mass (relative to the star) and the disc aspect ratio (e.g.,
Johansen & Lambrechts 2017; Ormel & Liu 2018) – all factors that
vary from disc to disc and planet to planet. We discuss some of these
factors in more detail here.
From Fig. 3C, we find that planet injection time plays a critical role
in the formation of gas giants with 𝑀p ≳102𝑀⊕: planets injected
early have a higher chance of experiencing high pebble fluxes (see
also Fig. 1) and thus a higher probability of growing into gas giants.
Similar results, i.e., earlier insertion times resulting in larger planets,
were found by Drążkowska et al. (2021, Fig. 3) (for static planets)
and Savvidou & Bitsch (2023, Fig. 5) (for migrating planets).
Most planets migrate inwards by considerable distances, owing to
rapid type II migration – especially in the 𝛼= 10−3 case (see also
Fig. 2). Those that start further out gain the most mass as they travel
through the disk. This is seen clearly Fig. 3B, which shows the final
positions of the majority of the planets to be ≲1 au. Additionally,

and migration can also be seen in Fig. 2, which shows giants planets
outside of 1 au only in the 𝛼= 10−4 case.
Similarly, plot F shows a clearer correlation between final planet
mass and snowline pebble flux for the 𝑣frag = 9 m s−1 case, while
for 𝑣frag = 3 m s−1 an additional population of low-mass planets
exists even for high snowline pebble fluxes >100 𝑀⊕/Myr. This
population of protoplanets, which struggles to grow despite high
pebble mass fluxes being present (at least during the time of insertion)
may be explained by low pebble accretion efficiencies as the high
turbulence levels (panel E) and low fragmentation velocity (and thus
lower Stokes numbers in the fragmentation regime) (panel F) both
contribute to decreased pebble accretion efficiencies (e.g., Ormel &
Liu 2018, Fig. 4). The late injection times for many of these planets
(panel C) also play a role, as these imply the pebble fluxes – while
high at 𝑡0 may start to decrease relatively quickly (see Fig. 1).

3.3 Pebble flux evolution in planet-hosting discs
Giant planets are expected to carve out gaps in the gas surface density
that can – locally – act as pebble traps/filters with various degrees of
efficiency (e.g., Weber et al. 2018; Bitsch et al. 2018; Stammler et al.
2023; Huang et al. 2025; Savvidou & Bitsch 2025), affecting also the
flux of pebbles arriving in the inner disc (e.g., Kalyaan et al. 2021,
2023; Mah et al. 2024; Pinilla et al. 2024; Easterwood et al. 2024).
To quantify this feedback for our simulations we show in Fig. 5 once
more snowline flux vs. time, in the same format as in Fig. 1 but
this time extracting the mass flux from the simulations with planets
for which the impact of the planets on the disc-wide pebble flux
is calculated self-consistently as the planets grow and migrate. For
context, we show also the 21 discs described in Krijt et al. (2025).
Current dust disc radii for these systems are known and fall between
≈30 −300 au (Krijt et al. 2025, Fig. 1), however, these radii – as
shaped by substructure – are expected to deviate from initial gas disc
characteristic sizes and we therefore plot all discs in each panel in
Fig. 5.
We see that the formation of a planet may greatly decrease the
pebble flux after a few 105 yr, by factors of up to and exceeding
two orders of magnitude. This particularly evident in larger disks,
where more mass is likely to be present exterior to the giant planet,
an effect captured in the analytical model of Krijt et al. (2025, Eq. 2).
Additionally, we see that in disks with lower 𝛼(blue lines) the pebble
flux is usually decreased essentially permanently, whereas in discs

4 DISCUSSION
We now turn to discussing how the above results can connect to
recently derived snowline pebble fluxes in a handful of ∼1Myr-old
protoplanetary disks, and the limitations within observations and our
models.
4.1 Connecting planet formation to JWST/MIRI-derived
snowline pebble fluxes
One of the main drivers behind this study was provided by the snow-
line pebble mass flux constraints derived from JWST/MIRI mea-
surements of cold water excess as presented in Romero-Mirza et al.
(2024) and Krijt et al. (2025).
For comparison, we show in Fig. 5 the pebble fluxes and ages of the
twenty-one discs studies studied in Krijt et al. (2025). It is important
to note here, however, that the system ages and inferred pebble fluxes
used for the comparison in Fig. 5 carry significant uncertainties: For
the ages the typical uncertainty is ∼0.5 Myr (discussed in Krijt et al.
2025, Sect. 2), while the pebble flux carries uncertainties associated
with (1) estimating the mass of the surface cold water reservoir4
and (2) converting that mass to a midplane pebble flux. The latter
in particular can be complicated as pebbles also deliver dust which
may shift the observable layers further up (see e.g., Houge et al.
2025a; Sellek et al. 2025). Nonetheless, our simulations, which self-
consistently solve for dust coagulation and transport in discs with a
(single) growing and migrating planet, appear in qualitative agree-
ment with the toy models presented in Krijt et al. (2025, Sect. 3.4) in
that the data seem to rule out the 𝑣frag = 9 m/s scenario as being dom-
inant as it fails to reproduce typical pebble fluxes around 𝑡≈1 Myr.
Instead; a relatively high turbulence and low fragmentation velocity
is favoured. This is in line with recent studies attempting to repro-
duce multi-wavelength ALMA continuum observations in outer disc
regions, which rule out combinations of high 𝑣frag and low 𝛼(e.g.,
Jiang et al. 2024; Tong et al. 2025; Williams & Krijt 2025).
The presence of (giant) planets outside the snowline does – as
expected (e.g., Bitsch et al. 2021) – act to reduce the pebble flux at

4.2 Model limitations and future extensions
We also comment briefly on some of the limitations of our model
as outlined in Sect. 2.1. First, our model is limited to one planet
per disc, and thus cannot capture direct or indirect planet-planet
interactions. An interesting (indirect) effect for example could be
that planets growing interior to any present giant planets will have
their pebble accretion rates decreased due to the reduced pebble flux.
This process was recently modelled by McCloat et al. (2025), who
found that growing masses in the outer disk are sufficient to prevent
inner masses reaching the pebble isolation mass. However, Eberlein
et al. (2024) found this to not be the case due to outer planets reaching
the pebble isolation mass later, when the inner planets would have
already been formed. This again brings in to question the timing of
protoplanet formation, and the way that process itself is regulated
by the drifting pebbles (e.g., Voelkel et al. 2020, 2021). The lack of
planet-planet gravitational interactions could also be a contributor to
the reason most of our giant planets end up very close to their star, as
we only see outward migration for smaller planets by construction,
due to the heating torque which causes outward migration for planets
up to a few Earth masses (e.g. Benítez-Llambay et al. 2015; Masset
2017).
Additionally, we note that the treatment of dust and pebble co-
agulation and drift in the chemcomp simulations we employed is
based on the two-population model of Birnstiel et al. (2012) and a
1D (vertically-integrated) disc model (Schneider & Bitsch 2021a).
Recent efforts that take into account the full dust size distribution
and/or are performed in 2D show that these approximations can lead
to variations in e.g., gap leakiness (e.g., Drążkowska et al. 2019;
Stammler et al. 2023; Pfeil et al. 2025), but we do not expect the
results to change qualitatively.
Finally, we do not self-consistently model the formation of plan-
etesimals or their subsequent growth into (proto)planets of masses
up to the mass given by Eq. 1. Instead, we effectively treat the planet
injection time and position as free parameters (see Sect. 2.7). Diverse
global (i.e., disc-wide) models predict very different formation times
and positions (as reviewed in Drążkowska et al. 2023, Sect. 3.1.4),
we expect these processes to depend on other disc parameters such as
𝛼and 𝑣frag (Birnstiel et al. 2016; Drążkowska & Dullemond 2018).
Thus, as some of the parameter combinations explored in this study
will be more or less likely then others, the synthetic planet popula-
tion resulting from them should not be compared directly to e.g., to
exoplanet occurrence rates.
5 CONCLUSIONS
We have used chemcomp (Schneider & Bitsch 2021a,b) to model
joint disc, dust, and pebble evolution (Fig. 1) together with planet
accretion and migration (Fig. 2) to investigate more quantitatively
the link between the snowline pebble flux on one hand, and final
planet properties (e.g., mass, orbital radius) on the other. Our main
conclusions can be summarized as follows:
(i) The (maximum) final planet mass strongly correlates with the
water snowline pebble flux at the time of protoplanet insertion, while
significant scatter is introduced when variations in disc parameters
(e.g., 𝛼, 𝑣frag, 𝑅0) and/or different protoplanet insertion locations are
considered (Fig. 3).
(ii) For turbulence levels of 𝛼= 10−4, the final planet mass vs.
pebble flux correlation is essentially continuous, while in discs with
𝛼= 10−3 the correlation behaves almost like a step function, with
snowline pebble mass fluxes exceeding 100 𝑀⊕/Myr (at the time of
protoplanet insertion) being necessary for the formation of gas giants
(Fig. 3E).
(iii) When comparing instead final planet masses to snowline peb-
ble fluxes specifically around 1 Myr (for which most of the current
constraints exist, see e.g., Krijt et al. 2025), the variation in planet
insertion times and various disc parameters allows for a broad range
of outcomes and the predictive power of the snowline pebble flux is
diminished (Fig. 4). Some of this degeneracy may be alleviated if
for example additional constraints on disc turbulence level, disc size,
present sub-structure, and/or typical protoplanet formation times can
be obtained.
(iv) Once grown to the pebble isolation mass, the planets evolving
in our simulations can significantly perturb the snowline pebble flux,
especially if they are located far out (i.e., well outside the snowline)
and form early enough to be able to block in their wakes a significant
mass of pebbles (cf. Fig. 5 and Fig. 1).
(v) Intriguingly, our one-planet-per-disc simulations for 𝛼= 10−3
result in the reduction of snowline pebble flux being only temporary,
with giant planet growing and migrating quickly, and being unable
to affect the snowline pebble flux once they have migrated to the
innermost 1 au (Figs. 5 and 2 and Sect. 4.1).
Generally speaking, the snowline pebble fluxes in our planet-
containing simulations broadly overlap with those inferred from
JWST/MIRI cold water excess studies (Fig. 5, and Romero-Mirza
et al. 2024; Krijt et al. 2025), but we refrain here folding in additional
constraints for all those discs (e.g., disc sizes, details of substructure,
etc.), focussing instead on the impact these pebble fluxes have on
(potentially) present protoplanets (Figs. 3 and 4) while Fig. 5 high-
lights how valuable additional pebble flux constraints – especially
for younger (≲0.5 Myr) and older (≳2 Myr) disks could be for dis-
criminating between models. To conclude, our simulations show that
the currently observed pebble fluxes can be used to constrain planet
formation simulations, and emphasize that diverse planet formation
via pebble accretion is in agreement with the currently available
constraints from JWST.
