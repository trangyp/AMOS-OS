Systematic Design of Local Rules for Directing Emergent Structure in Bottom-Up Systems
Andrew Slezak
Mechanical Science and Engineering, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Urbana, Illinois 61801, USA
Varda F. Hagh∗
Mechanical Science and Engineering & Beckman Institute for Advanced Science and Technology,
University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Urbana, Illinois 61801, USA
Many biological systems collectively construct complex, adaptive, and functional architectures, where function emerges from bottom-up
building processes rather than top-down planning or centralized control. However, general strategies for programming and controlling such
emergent function in engineered systems remain largely unexplored. In this work, we present a systematic framework for designing local
behavioral rule sets for simple builders such that, when adhered to, structures with targeted global properties emerge. Using a minimal
model inspired by tent caterpillars, we study how simple agents equipped with limited sensing and no memory or global knowledge construct
networked structures through local deposition of line segments. We base our framework on tuning local degrees of freedom in a complex system
to alter global behavior. By identifying the degrees of freedom that influence a given property and specifying how they are tuned through local
rules, we demonstrate that the corresponding global properties can be directed. We explore this through three geometric properties of the
agents’ resulting networks, in particular area coverage, average line density, and front curvature. We show that agents can reliably achieve
targeted values for these properties while maintaining low variability in the presence of stochasticity. These results establish a generalizable
approach for programming emergence in decentralized systems and suggest new pathways for designing adaptive materials and autonomous
construction strategies in complex, uncertain environments.
I.
INTRODUCTION
In nature, many insects collectively construct intricate, mul-
tifunctional architectures that are uniquely adapted to their
local environments. Across a wide range of species includ-
ing bees, wasps, ants, termites, and tent caterpillars, groups
of individuals build communal structures whose organization
ranges from ordered to highly amorphous forms [1–6]. Re-
markably, many of these builders, including tent caterpillars,
which provide the primary inspiration for this work, operate
without top-down planning, specialized tools, or engineering
calculations that guide most human-built systems. Yet they
reliably produce structures, such as those shown in Fig. 1, that
regulate temperature, retain humidity in arid environments,
and remain mechanically robust enough to protect colonies
during storms [7–9]. This global functionality has no explicit
designer; it instead arises from the local interactions through
which individuals manipulate and deposit material building
blocks.
Identifying and controlling the principles by which such lo-
cal interactions give rise to reliable global outcomes enables
new approaches for building adaptive structures in environ-
ments where precise information about local conditions or
sustained human oversight is not feasible. This includes dis-
aster zones, the deep ocean, and distant planetary surfaces.
Similarly inspired researchers in robotics have developed de-
centralized control systems [10–14] in which teams of robots
collectively build structures, in some cases using local infor-
mation and stigmergic interactions only [15–19]. While these
systems demonstrate the feasibility of autonomous collective
construction, general methods for systematic design of local
rules that direct the emergence of targeted global properties in
such systems remain largely unexplored.

In this work, we present a framework for designing and re-
fining local rules that produce target global properties while
remaining robust to nonlinearity and stochasticity. The frame-
work builds on concepts from tunable matter models in which
global material properties can be controlled by tuning local
degrees of freedom [20–36]. Inspired by tent caterpillars who
build silk networks collectively (Fig. 1), we study networks
that emerge through the deposition of line segments arranged
within branch-like scaffolds.
In our model, line segments
serve as the fundamental building blocks of the network, and
the properties of interest are geometric. The primary tunable
degrees of freedom correspond to the spatial placement and
length of these line segments, which define the edges of the
resulting network graph. Our objective is to determine agent
deposition behaviors that enable control over global properties
such as area coverage, average line density, and the curvature
of the advancing front in these networks. Although we focus
on geometric properties here and present results in 2D, the
conceptual framework can be extended to many other physi-
cal properties and is directly extensible to 3D.
A central challenge arises in creating and applying such a
framework due to the relationship between component-level
degrees of freedom and emergent system-level properties.
This mapping is rarely straightforward or deterministic due
to inherent nonlinearities. Moreover, perturbations and noise
introduce stochasticity into the construction process, which
propagate through the system and lead to non-trivial variabil-
ity in the resulting structures. As a consequence, even when
the same local rules are applied to the same set of degrees
of freedom, the resulting global outcomes may vary signif-
icantly. Conversely, different sets of local degrees of free-
dom may produce similar emergent properties through dif-
ferent rules. This many-to-many mapping between local de-
grees of freedom and global emergent properties, illustrated
in Fig. 2, makes it difficult to design tuning rules that reliably
achieve desired outcomes. To address this challenge, we intro-
duce a methodology that begins with heuristic local rules in-
formed by domain knowledge and systematically refines them
through statistical analysis of emergent properties across en-
sembles of realizations. Using this approach, we show that
simple agent behaviors can be tuned to direct global proper-
ties of the resulting networks, including area coverage, line
density, and front curvature. By directing these properties, we
mean not only that they approach a specified target value, but
also that ensembles of parallel realizations exhibit sufficiently
low standard deviation about that target.
II.
METHODOLOGY
In Fig. 3 we present our methodology for rule design in dia-
gram form, which we use throughout this work to achieve our
stated objectives. We begin by defining the tunable degrees
of freedom and selecting a target property or set of proper-
ties, whose emergence we aim to direct. We then specify an
initial set of governing rules along with associated input pa-
rameters. Although the space of possible rules and parameters
is large, we constrain the design space by first considering a
purely stochastic rule set to establish a baseline and identify
what emerges in the absence of additional specificity. We then
incorporate domain knowledge to define a tractable subset of
candidate rules informed by this initial exploration. For exam-
ple, because the structures formed by our agents are networks
and the target properties are geometric, we select the spatial
placement and lengths of edges as the primary degrees of free-
dom and initialize the system with a simple geometry-based
rule set, as described later in this section.
Using the defined system, we simulate the building process
via agent-based modeling under prescribed boundary condi-
tions. We then evaluate the resulting structures over an en-
semble of realizations to assess whether the desired prop-
erties emerge reliably. If the emergent properties are suffi-
ciently close to their target values, the rule design is consid-
ered achieved. If the property of interest emerges but its value
lies outside the acceptable range, we refine the input param-
eters until the target value is achieved. If the system shows
little to no progress toward the target properties or remains
outside the acceptable range, we instead modify the rules and
continue the iterative process until the desired behavior is ob-
tained. Selecting and modifying rules is inherently nontrivial,
as it depends sensitively on the discrepancy between current

Agent Design. To test our proposed framework, we define
a model for the agents. We assume that a building agent in-
teracts with its local environment through sensing and action.
In this work, sensing is restricted to distance measurements,
which are used to inform decisions but are not themselves con-
sidered actions. We model agents as simple, point-like entities
capable of moving within their environment and depositing
line segments, with each action requiring a single time step.
Each agent is assigned a fixed radius of vision Rv, which de-
fines the region of the environment within which it can per-
form measurements and execute actions. Within this radius,
the agent may move to or build toward any detected point. Af-
ter depositing a line segment, the agent’s position is updated
to the endpoint of the line, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Agents
do not retain memory of previously observed points; instead,
at each time step, they first sense (i.e., measure) their local
environment within Rv, then select an action based on these
measurements, and finally execute that action. Accordingly,
an agent samples its local environment at each time step and
selects either a build or move action. If no suitable build loca-
tion is identified, the agent continues moving until it reaches
a region where construction is possible or the allotted simula-
tion time is exhausted.
Using this model for agents, three initial input parameters
can be varied for any given rule to evaluate its effectiveness
within a given geometry: the radius of vision Rv, the building
time t, and the number of agents Na. To characterize the ef-
fect of the radius of vision without anchoring it to a specific
scale, we normalize it by the characteristic length scale L of
the environment, such that 0 < Rv/L ≤1. We fix the number
of agents at 2, representing the minimal collective, and set the
total number of time steps to 500 for all simulations unless
otherwise stated.
Note the geometry of the environment is intractable to eval-
uate comprehensively due to the vast space of possible con-
figurations, orientations, and structural elements, even when
a characteristic length scale is specified and fixed. We there-
fore fix the environment geometry throughout this study and
acknowledge that its coupling to emergent properties is not
fully explored. Instead, our analysis focuses on selecting and
refining rules that provide direct control over agent behavior
and the resulting structures.
Behavioral Rule Sets. With the initial parameters defined,
we study how behavioral rule sets can be determined to direct
agents to construct networks with target values for three geo-
metric properties: the covered area ratio, average line density,
and front curvature. Accordingly, we use geometry-based rea-
soning to formulate the rules within each set. A complete rule
set consists of at least three components: a build rule, which
determines the point to which an agent builds; a movement
rule, which determines the point to which an agent moves;
and a decision rule, which determines whether an agent moves
or builds at a given time step. Together, these rules provide
the minimal basis for describing agent actions during network
construction and offer independent mechanisms for modify-
ing agent behavior.
A.
Random Build, Random Movement, Random Decision
(RRR)
The purpose of this rule set is to establish what emerges
from the simplest possible rules where no additional direction
is provided to agents. It is in essence a baseline against which
everything else can be compared. For this rule set, agents
select a point uniformly at random from all detected points.
The decision to move or build to that point is similarly chosen
with uniform random probability.

B.
Maximum Length Build, Random Movement, Random
Decision (MRR)
Motivated by the results of the RRR rule set, we aim to un-
derstand if by simply modifying our build rule to always de-
posit maximum length segments we can direct agents to maxi-
mize the total area covered by the networks. Agents following
this rule set build only to points within their radius of vision
that are maximally distant from their current position; if mul-
tiple points satisfy this condition, one is selected uniformly at
random. As before, both movement and the decision to build
or move remain random.
C.
Area-based Build, Random Movement, Area Target
Decision (ARA)
Following our exploration of the RRR and MRR rule sets,
we develop a new rule set that reliably maximizes covered
area as quickly as possible using only local information. To
achieve this, agents estimate local area coverage using their
existing sensing capabilities and exclusively build in regions
where the local area is lower than a prescribed target. Under
this rule set, each agent is assigned a target local area ratio At,
a grid resolution Ncc, and an exclusion radius Rex. At each
time step, the agent discretizes the area within its radius of
vision into a uniform Cartesian grid of square cells with char-
acteristic length ℓc = Rv/Ncc and computes the local area ratio
Aℓ, as described in the Supplemental Information. This value
is then compared to the target At to determine whether the
agent moves or builds. If the local area ratio Aℓexceeds At,
the agent moves to a randomly selected point within its ra-
dius of vision until it identifies a region where the local area
ratio falls below the target. Otherwise, the agent considers
candidate line segments that connect its current position to
points located within Rv but outside the exclusion radius Rex.
Among these candidates, it deposits the segment that produces
the largest increase in local area coverage. The exclusion ra-
dius (Rex = 0.5Rv) is introduced to reduce computational cost
by excluding short segments, which are less likely to increase
area coverage significantly. If multiple candidate segments
yield the same maximal increase in area coverage, the first in
the list is selected.
D.
Area-based Build, Maximum Distance Movement, Density
Target Decision (AMD)
Building on the ARA rule set, which reliably produces net-
works with controllable area coverage, we introduce a mod-
ified rule set that preserves similar network structures while
also enabling control over the average line density. To achieve
this, agents are provided with an additional measure of lo-
cal density, computed using their existing sensing capabili-
ties. Under this rule set, each agent is assigned a target lo-
cal density ρt, a grid resolution Ncc, and an exclusion radius
Rex. Agents deposit line segments that result in the largest in-
crease in local area coverage and adopt a maximum-distance
movement strategy to explore the surrounding space more ef-
ficiently. The decision to build or move is determined by com-
paring the measured local density ρℓto the target value ρt (see
Supplemental Information for details on how ρℓis computed
from the number of line segments per unit area). If ρℓ< ρt,
the agent deposits a line segment of length Rex ≤l ≤Rv that
maximizes the increase in local area coverage. Otherwise, the
agent moves maximally until it reaches a region where ρℓ< ρt
and construction is possible. As in ARA, when multiple can-
didate segments yield identical maximal increases in area cov-
erage, the first in the list is selected.
III.
RESULTS
Using the four rule sets outlined above, we examine how
three global geometric properties can be directed as line seg-
ments are arranged within a given environment. These prop-
erties include the ratio of total area covered by a network, the
line density within the covered region, and the curvature of the
emerging front after the building process is completed. While
these properties are not unique, their coupling provides use-
ful insight into how rule sets can be adjusted to produce mul-
tifunctional structures with additional desired characteristics.
We also characterize the sensitivity of each rule set to initial
conditions by randomly assigning agent positions and repeat-
ing each simulation 100 times for each parameter set. This
allows us to quantify the variability of the emergent proper-
ties and assess the extent to which they can be directed using
a given rule set and its associated parameters.

agents within a given geometry over a fixed number of time
steps. A time step corresponds to a single update during which

The standard deviation is low for
smaller values of Rv (0.02L & 0.2L) because small Rv con-
strains agents’ ability to explore their surroundings at each
time step. In these cases, agents primarily construct along
a single scaffold branch for most of the simulation and only
occasionally encounter regions where connections to other
branches are possible.
As a result, only agents that begin
near, or subsequently reach, junctions in the geometry con-
tribute to expanding the network beyond segments co-linear
with the scaffold branches. This behavior is consistent with
exploration-exploitation dynamics observed in active matter
systems, where motion without directional bias limits spatial
exploration and leads to repeated sampling of previously vis-
ited regions [38–40].
To address this limitation, we introduce a bias into the
rule set by replacing the random build rule with a maximum
length construction rule (MRR). This modification increases
the length of each deposited segment, enabling agents to reach
new regions more often. As shown in Fig. 5F, this change
leads to a modest increase in the overall area coverage for
Rv = 0.2L and a substantial increase for larger values of Rv.
For sufficiently large Rv ≥0.6L, maximizing area coverage
becomes the dominant outcome, accompanied by a signifi-
cant reduction in the standard deviation. This improvement
arises because maximum length construction reduces the de-
pendence on initial agent positions by facilitating access to
previously unreachable regions. However, neither RRR nor
MRR reliably converge to maximal area coverage across all

B.
Average Network Line Density
Building on our analysis of area coverage, we now examine
how to direct the average network line density, defined as the
number of deposited line segments per control volume (e.g.,
pixel). Using the same grid discretization introduced earlier,
we count the number of line segments passing through each
pixel to obtain the local line density. With this definition, two
complementary averaging procedures can be used to charac-
terize the resulting networks across an ensemble of 100 paral-
lel realizations. The first averaging scheme captures how den-
sity is distributed in space, on average, for a given rule set and
value of Rv. For each pixel, we average its local line density
over 100 realizations to obtain an ensemble-averaged spatial
density field, visualized as heat maps in Figs. 8A–D. These
maps highlight the spatial heterogeneity in the constructed
networks and serve as the basis for our subsequent analysis
of front curvature.
The second averaging scheme provides a measure of how
the mean network density varies across independent realiza-
tions. For each individual network, we first average the local
line density over all nonzero pixels to obtain a single mean
density value, and then average these values over the 100 re-
alizations to compute ρ. This average value is shown as a
function of time steps in Figs. 8E–H.

ues of Rv, the mean density becomes more consistent across
realizations, but only over a comparatively narrow range with
no additional input parameters to modify this range.
The
ARA rule set similarly shows this trend at small Rv, with very
large variability, but sharply becomes more predictable once
Rv ≥0.3L. In this regime, ARA spans a relatively large range
of average densities with smaller standard deviations. This
improved consistency arises because for Rv ≥0.3L the system
rapidly reaches full area coverage, after which agents largely
stop depositing additional material. As a result, the average
line density associated with a given Rv approaches a repro-
ducible value. Accordingly, if the objective is to cover the full
area while reliably achieving a target average density within
the range identified by the ARA results, this rule set provides
a practical approach through appropriate selection of the Rv
values, though again is limited in modifying its range via pro-
vided input parameters.
The AMD rule set is distinct in that it produces relatively
small standard deviations without requiring full area cover-
age. This behavior arises from the additional control param-
eter ρt, which defines a local target density averaged over the
pixels within an agent’s radius of vision. By comparing the
local density in its sensing region to ρt, an agent moves and
builds in a way that maintains a more uniform local density
throughout the structure. In this sense, ρt sets a prescribed up-
per bound on the density within the radius of vision and pro-
vides more direct control over local material accumulation.
Agents that enforce this condition throughout the structure
tend to build more uniformly dense networks at the global
scale, yielding a more consistent global mean density. This
is reflected in Fig. 8H, where the standard deviations remain
small over a wide range of Rv across realizations.
Although the range of densities obtained over 500 time
steps remains relatively narrow for AMD when ρt is fixed, it
can be expanded by varying ρt, as shown in Fig. 9. By adjust-
ing the target density within the agents’ radius of vision, we
can systematically shift the resulting values of ρ while main-
taining consistency across 100 realizations. We report results
up to ρt < 0.7, since for ρt > 0.7 we find that 500 time steps
are insufficient for two agents to reach the imposed local den-
sity target. This limitation is not fundamental, and increasing
the construction time should permit access to higher values of
ρ.

networks composed entirely of linear segments is largely gov-

the maximum height at each horizontal location, these varia-

of feasible solutions is restricted, and the choice of rule set
and parameters must be made more carefully to achieve the
desired outcome.
IV.
CONCLUSION
In summary, we have explored how to systematically design
behavioral rules for simple agents so that, when they follow
these rules to manipulate and deposit building blocks locally,
they construct structures whose global properties emerge in
a controlled way. These properties arise not only from how
building blocks connect to one another, but also from how
agents interact with the evolving structure, their surrounding
environment, and the decisions they make during deposition.
Throughout this work, we have shown that these local be-
havioral rules can be designed and refined so that the emer-
gent properties of the resulting structures reliably approach
desired target values. Our approach relies on simple heuris-
tic rules grounded in domain knowledge. When the properties
of interest are geometric, the rules can be built from equally
simple geometric principles. Following this idea, we demon-
strated how properties such as area coverage, average line den-
sity, and front curvature can be directed in network structures,
when agents building them operate with minimal sensing and
limited information about their environment.
What we have studied here represents only a small fraction
of what is possible. In this work, we have primarily focused
on how to design rules for individual agents and have con-
sidered only minimal forms of collective behavior. Yet it is
in the presence of collective that the problem becomes richer.
How should agents be coupled so that they do not just build
alongside one another, but truly build together? What kinds
of structures become possible when agents share information,
coordinate their actions, or adapt to one another in real time?
There remains a wide and largely unexplored space in un-
derstanding how local interactions between agents, and not
just between agents and their environment, shape emergent
structure. More broadly, many fundamental questions remain
open. For instance, the mapping between available degrees of
freedom, the rules used to tune them, and the structures that
ultimately emerge is still poorly understood. What is the min-
imal set of degrees of freedom required to reliably produce a
desired emergent property? And how much information about
the environment is truly necessary to direct emergence?
We will pursue these questions in future work. The results
presented here point toward an alternative way of thinking
about how structures may be designed and built in environ-
ments where centralized control or continuous human super-
vision is not possible. Rather than exerting top-down speci-
fication over outcomes, the goal in such settings would be to
define proper constraints that guide the self-organization of
simple agents toward building functional structures. We view
this as a step forward in extending human capabilities for en-
gineering in unfamiliar and uncertain environments.
