Abstract
We analyze a system of bead–spring polymers interacting with Navier–Stokes turbulence to in-
vestigate chain–stretching physics and ﬁnite-time Lyapunov exponents in ultra–dilute solutions
with Weissenberg number Wi ≈80. Although the polymers do not alter the turbulent structure,
their hydrodynamic interactions generate sub–Kolmogorov–scale ﬂow ﬁelds, making the coupling
two–way. They stretch predominantly as material line elements, yet ﬁnite deviations arising from
elasticity and excluded–volume forces occur with measurable probability. The chain end–to–end
distance exhibits a power–law scaling regime. Polymers preferentially sample regions of axisym-
metric biaxial extension, where they reach their largest extensions and stretch most rapidly. The
degree of stretching is directly correlated with strain intensity, while relaxation events are concen-
trated in high–enstrophy regions. The chains align strongly with the second strain–rate eigenvector
and tend to anti–align with the third; consequently, the second eigenvalue contributes signiﬁcantly
to polymer compression, despite its magnitude typically being smaller than that of the third. Along
polymer trajectories, vorticity tends to align with both the ﬁrst and second eigenvectors, a behav-
ior that diﬀers from the corresponding Eulerian statistics and from Lagrangian vortex–stretching
phenomenology. After approximately ten large–eddy turnover times, the Lagrangian Lyapunov
exponent histories from diﬀerent chains appear to synchronise, consistent with convergence of the
mean logarithmic stretch rates. All Lyapunov–exponent probability density functions exhibit de-
partures from Gaussianity, and the intermediate Lyapunov exponent is positive in all realizations.
The ratio of the mean intermediate to largest exponents is E[λ2]/E[λ1] ≈2/7. The largest and
intermediate exponents are positively correlated, whereas the intermediate and smallest exponents
are anticorrelated.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Turbulence in simple ﬂuids is a central problem of nonlinear and statistical physics [1].
Important variations of the turbulence problem arise when ﬂuids are coupled to additional
structure (often after spontaneous symmetry breaking in a system) resulting in open prob-
lems of signiﬁcant conceptual depth and broad applications [2–4]. There are diﬀerent types
of microstructural motions in these, so called, complex ﬂuids, which correspond to microp-
olar (associated with rigid geometrical motions of microstructure), microstretch (similarity
2


## Page 3


transformations) and micromorphic (aﬃne transformations) ﬂuid media. A most important
example of the latter category is that of polymer ﬂuids and the associated turbulence phe-
nomena. The physics of polymer turbulence depend on the volume fraction of the polymers
in the solution, and in this work we will only consider dilute solution cases. Despite their
relative simplicity compared to semi-dilute, entangled or concentrated solutions, ﬂow struc-
ture in dilute solutions could be aﬀected by the elastic eﬀects of polymer deformations and
this, in turn, can have nontrivial eﬀects on ﬂow stability and turbulence structure, including
the important phenomena of lower drag levels exerted by polymer turbulence on solid walls
[5–9]. In this paper, we shall only present results for ultra-dilute turbulent solutions, when
the back-reaction of the polymers to the ﬂow does not alter the large scale structure of the
latter, although there are hydrodynamic interactions between the chains which induce a
microscopic ﬂow ﬁeld in the solvent.
The complexity of micromorphic continua makes a top–down, rational–mechanics for-
mulation as a statistical ﬁeld theory diﬃcult; accordingly, viscoelastic–ﬂuid models often
use bottom-up closures grounded in microscopic polymer physics [10–15]. More advanced
treatments still treat the solvent-polymer system as one ﬂuid, but instead of a statistical
closure they include a Fokker-Planck equation for the probability of polymer-chain confor-
mations, most often neglecting the hydrodynamic interactions between polymer chains [16].
Our approach diﬀers from such methodologies in that we have a clear separation between
the solvent ﬂuid and the microstructure, and in that we employ the Langevin equation
of polymer conformations in conjunction with the Navier-Stokes equation for the solvent.
This fully mesoscopic approach provides explicit trajectories of (coarse-grained) polymers,
and enables the investigation of intricate physics, as are the alignments between polymer
conformations and ﬂow strain-rates or the Lyapunov exponents along chain trajectories.
Our mesoscopic viewpoint is well established now, as a result of the work of many mi-
crohydrodynamics and polymer physics researchers that have developed and integrated nu-
merous basic methodologies [17–20] that enabled this powerful approach to polymeric ﬂows.
A major motivation for polymer turbulence mesoscopics was provided by the availability
of experimental methods for the control [21] and observation of individual polymer chains
[22]. When similar capabilities were extended to polymers in a ﬂow, the need for physics
of individual chains became a practical rather than pure research objective [23–26]. Indeed,
starting from simple stagnation ﬂows [27], there have been observations of single chains
3


## Page 4


in ﬂuctuating (albeit not fully developed turbulent) ﬂows [28], accompanied by theoretical
studies of passive polymer chains in such ﬂows [29–32]. The experimental studies focused
(among other) on polymer conformations during coil-stretching transition and elastic stress
levels, while the theoretical studies (with the exception of [32]) employed simpliﬁed polymer
models — for example, without hydrodynamic interactions, excluded volume eﬀects or mul-
timode conformation structure — to provide insightful analyses of the probability density
function (PDF) of polymer extensions. The geometry of strain-chain interactions was also
tackled [32].
By continuing along these lines, we investigate here chain stretching in ultra-dilute tur-
bulent polymer solutions with Kolmogorov scalings. We aim to examine the detailed ﬂow-
polymer interactions and their statistical measures by employing bead-spring polymer mod-
eling which includes all relevant physical eﬀects and by tracking individual chain trajectories
and the Lyapunov numbers along them. A special focus of this investigation is the correla-
tion between polymer dynamics and vorticity structure. Our viewpoint is similar to that of
[33], although their study emphasised the coil–stretch transition, which is not our concern
here, and—in contrast to our approach—neglected multi–body eﬀects in the hydrodynamic
interactions and in the Brownian forcing. Such eﬀects can play a signiﬁcant role in polymer
stretching by altering the drag forces acting on the chains. Indeed, as indicated by Zimm’s
analysis the inclusion of hydrodynamic interactions results in the polymer eﬀectively drag-
ging the solvent within its pervaded volume as it moves. So instead of having the Stokes
drag at each bead location (freely draining Rouse approximation), we have the viscous drag
on a pseudo-solid object of the size of the polymer’s pervaded volume [34]. These impor-
tant physics are expected to aﬀect polymer orientation with respect to the ﬂow, hence also
polymer stretching.
Our work adds to a body of important contributions, while diﬀering in scope. In par-
ticular, our approach diﬀers from the mesoscopic frameworks of [35–37] which, in seeking
to quantify the eﬀect of polymers on turbulence structure [38], employ computationally
tractable but not fully complete polymer models. These are typically dumbbells that ex-
clude excluded–volume interactions and inter–chain multibody eﬀects. Our calculation has
a narrower scope (we do not address alterations of the turbulence structure) but employs
a sophisticated polymer model that has been successfully tested against key rheological ex-
periments [23, 39, 40]. It is important to clarify that, by accounting for both intrachain and
4


## Page 5


interchain hydrodynamic interactions, our modelling includes the microhydrodynamic ﬂow
induced in the ﬂuid by polymer drag. The key diﬀerence from the aforementioned works is
that, in our case, the chains exert only weak feedback on the turbulence, so the turbulent
structure remains unaltered. In this respect, our formal system incorporates all important
physics in a consistent manner.
Investigations closely aligned in scope include [41], who employed a bead–spring model
without hydrodynamic interactions to assess the ability of FENE elasticity to reproduce
elastic stress levels in viscoelastic ﬂows.
Their study did not examine the geometry of
strain–polymer interactions or the PDFs of polymer extensions. In a similar milieu, [42, 43]
studied polymer dynamics in turbulence using dumbbells. Notably, [43] included the hy-
drodynamic interaction between the two beads and thus accounted for two–body eﬀects in
evaluating the Brownian force. By computing PDFs of polymer extension in the Batchelor
regime of the Kraichnan ﬂow and in Navier–Stokes isotropic turbulence, they concluded
that hydrodynamic interactions do not strongly aﬀect polymer stretching; however, these
conclusions are conditioned by the use of dumbbells. There was no geometrical analysis of
the stretching process or of Lyapunov exponents along the trajectories. The latter point is
important, because there is no a priori reason for polymers to move along material trajec-
tories and therefore to experience the same Lyapunov exponents as ﬂuid elements. Indeed,
the more complete the polymer model, the stronger the predicted deviation of polymer
trajectories from material ones.
II.
FORMAL SYSTEM
A.
Nondimensional numbers
Within the general mesoscopic polymer-ﬂuid dynamics framework, we need to set up an
ultra-dilute turbulent solution. The most systematic way of doing so is by identifying the
relevant nondimensional numbers and chose them accordingly. This removes any uncertainty
and provides a handle for rigorously deﬁning diﬀerent problems in the future. We pay special
attention to carefully deﬁne Wi, De and Lyapunov numbers pointing out their diﬀerent
physical content.
(1) The most basic nondimenional number is the volume fraction φ, the ratio of the

(8) As already mentioned, the polymer stiﬀness number S does not represent a measure
of elastic eﬀects in the ﬂow.
Indeed, a direct indicator of the linear or nonlinear
character of the dynamics is the magnitude of polymer deformation or strain. It is not

alternative but not equivalent measure is the Weissenberg number Wi = τR ˙γ, where ˙γ

B.
Physical System
The governing equations are a subset of a general formulation of polymer turbulence at all
volume fractions developed in [48]. The latter formulation included full entanglement eﬀects,
as well as hydrodynamic interactions by incorporating low Re number hydrodynamics within
wall and periodic boundary conditions. The method indicated a hitherto unnoticed subgrid
stress originating in the microhydrodynamic ﬂow ﬁeld. The nondimensional numbers conﬁrm
that we do not need the physical complexity of the full formulation. Indeed, since φ ≪1,
we can neglect entanglement eﬀects and since I ≪1, we can neglect the reaction from the
polymers to turbulence structure. We solve four diﬀerential equations: Navier-Stokes for
turbulence, Langevin equation for polymers, Stokes equations for intrachain and interchain
hydrodynamic interactions and Lyapunov dynamics for polymer stretching.

Langevin equation
The polymer physics are taken into account via a bead-spring model that was developed in
a series of papers [23, 39, 40], which should be consulted for detailed expositions of both the
employed formulae and their physical content. It has a couple of key features: (a) the model
has no adjustable constants. To set up the full polymer dynamics, it requires only material
properties as input: the θ temperature, the chain molecular molar mass M, the Kuhn
monomer molar mass MK, the Kuhn monomer length b and the number of monomers in
polymer strands in between entanglements in the melt state Pe. Pe is only required when the
solution is entangled. It has been tested with very good results in the context of single-chain
dynamics, where it has accurately predicted the longest relaxation time [40], and rheological
experiments, where it has reliably predicted elastic stress levels in extensional ﬂows [39]. The
former publication also showed that the model’s predictions are robust under variation of
the number of beads (i.e. of the coarse-graining level). This is because the model essentially
performs a heuristic renormalization group analysis of polymer physics that does not depend
of the chosen coarse-graining scale. So, one speciﬁes the number of beads per chain Nb and
the model’s equations automatically coarse-grain the dynamics at the corresponding spring
scale. We take advantage of these features and reduce the computational complexity by
choosing Nb = 5. The Langevin equation governing bead motion reads

many similarities. For example, both equations include the inertial force, and the pressure

models, since both originate from the potential ﬁelds of the underlying Hamiltonian system.
We have already mentioned the standard practice of neglecting the reaction of the polymer
Brownian force on the ﬂow—although, in principle, it should be included. Is this also the
case with polymer elasticity? There exist ﬂuctuating forces exchanged between the polymer
and the ﬂuid, corresponding to stochastic transitions between diﬀerent chain conformations,
such as trans–gauche torsion angle variations [34]. These microscopic force ﬂuctuations are
the underlying mechanism by which the polymer explores its conformational space, and are
fundamental to the entropy that gives rise to the observed mean elastic force. However, while
these forces are essential at the microscopic level, they average to zero over the time scales
of the bead–spring model and are not typically observed at the coarse-graining scale, where
only the mean elastic response is apparent. This is especially the case since, in deriving the
constitutive laws of polymer elasticity, we assume that polymer conformation equilibration
occurs much faster than drag-induced chain stretching, allowing thermodynamic arguments
to be applied to polymer elasticity.

III.
ANALYSIS OF THE SYSTEM
A.
Numerics
The numerical methods employed in this work have been discussed in detail elsewhere.
Here, we provide only a brief overview, emphasizing the mathematical properties that make
them particularly suitable for the present physical problem. However, we describe in detail
the SVD normalization method developed in this study, highlighting its geometric charac-
teristics and presenting the corresponding mathematical formulation.
The Navier-Stokes solver
We apply a staggered grid, fractional step, ﬁnite volume method [61, 62]. All spatial
partial derivatives are computed with second order accurate schemes. The method employs
an expicit, third order accurate, low storage Runge-Kutta (RK) method for the computation
of the advective terms. In addition, an implicit, second order accurate Crank-Nicolson (CN)
scheme is applied to the viscous terms. Since this scheme is implicit and the boundary con-
ditions are periodic, the method requires the solution of cyclic-tridiagonal, linear algebraic
equation systems. We have used the Sherman-Morrison formula to reduce the latter to much
easily solved tridiagonal systems. The CN scheme is incorporated into the RK steps and the
method becomes a hybrid RK/CN scheme. The pressure gradient is incorporated explicitly
into the RK steps, but in order to satisfy incompressibility, an additional calculation after
18


## Page 19


each RK substep projects the velocity ﬁeld onto the space of divergence-free vector ﬁelds
(Hodge projection). This latter computation is equivalent to a separate velocity upgrade
due to the pressure gradient. The net result of RK and Hodge projection procedures is that
— depending on the chosen scheme parameters — pressure gradient eﬀects are captured
with ﬁrst or second order in time accuracy. We have found the ﬁrst order accurate scheme
to be more stable, and we have employed it here.
The Langevin solver
The choice of numerical method for solving the Langevin equation is guided by the fact
that the polymer’s mass is very small compared with the magnitude of the ﬂow-induced forces
acting upon it. As the ﬂow ﬁeld changes, it exerts varying forces on the polymer that tend
to accelerate it. Because of the smallness of the polymer mass, variations in the magnitudes
of these forces in the Langevin equation induce very large chain accelerations. As a result,
polymer relaxation processes occur on timescales much shorter than those of viscous diﬀusion
in turbulence or Brownian diﬀusion of the chains. Over these diﬀusive timescales, a polymer
cannot resist changes in its velocity, and its motion is governed directly by the instantaneous
interplay of hydrodynamic drag, elastic, excluded-volume, and random Brownian forces.
By choice, we are not interested in resolving these rapid relaxation processes, which do
not inﬂuence polymer stretching. Hence, we neglect chain inertia and solve the Langevin
equation in the diﬀusive limit, where it takes the form of the overdamped Langevin equation
(Brownian dynamics), and we disregard the high-frequency components of the polymer’s
conformational response.
Methods for integrating the Langevin equation in the inertial
regime are discussed in [63].
The overdamped Langevin equation represents an algebraic stochastic force balance for
the bead velocity. To integrate it, one must select the appropriate stochastic calculus inter-
pretation, since two deﬁnitions of the stochastic integral are possible.
The Ermak–McCammon scheme [23, 64] is a ﬁrst-order, Itˆo-compatible extension of the
Euler–Maruyama method that includes a drift correction for position-dependent diﬀusion.
The Fixman scheme [40, 65] is a stochastic midpoint (Heun-type) integrator consistent with
the Stratonovich interpretation. The former is computationally eﬃcient but requires ex-
plicit drift evaluation, while the latter avoids this correction at the cost of roughly doubling
19


## Page 20


the computational work per step. For suﬃciently small timesteps, both yield equivalent
statistical results; however, Fixman’s method produces smoother, Stratonovich-type trajec-
tories, whereas the Ermak–McCammon trajectories reﬂect the characteristic roughness of
Itˆo processes owing to their strictly non-anticipative character.
We employ the Ermak–McCammon scheme for two reasons. First, our use of Rotne–
Prager–Yamakawa (RPY) hydrodynamics eliminates the Itˆo drift by construction, making
implementation straightforward and eﬃcient.
Second, we have adapted the predictor–
corrector algorithm of [66] to the Ermak–McCammon scheme, ensuring that its time-
accuracy properties are preserved. A signiﬁcant advantage of this combined approach is
that the Itˆo character of the dynamics is maintained, and the maximum chain-length con-
straint is enforced exactly. This is crucial for polymer dynamics in turbulent ﬂows, where
material lines can stretch indeﬁnitely but polymer chains cannot. The resulting method
improves numerical stability and allows larger timesteps [39].
It is important to note that without Itˆo drift, Brownian dynamics become an equation
for ﬁnding bead displacement via the equation drk = [u(rk) + uS(rk)]dt + thermal eﬀects.
Since uS in general is much smaller than u, it is reasonable to expect that the polymer is
predominantly stretched by turbulence as a material object. However, it is important to note
that the hydrodynamic interactions encoded in uS can have important eﬀects for polymers
stretched near their maximum length.
A material line would like to stretch indeﬁnitely
but a polymer developes a strong elastic force that limits stretching, thus increasing the
velocity of the beads relative to the ﬂow.
This indicates that the Lyapunov exponents
measured here are not necessarily those of a material object in the ﬂow, since polymer
trajectories are expected to deviate from material ones. Similarly, hydrodynamic interactions
and random displacements can alter the geometry of the alignment between the local strain-
rate and polymer conformations, compared to the corresponding geometry for material lines.
Thus, both the stretching and alignment statistics of polymers can diﬀer signiﬁcantly from
those of material lines, especially under conditions of strong extension, which are typical
of high Wi-number turbulent ﬂows. Our purpose is to focus on these issues, which, in the
context of ultra-dilute turbulent solutions, are expected to be universal, since the universality
of Navier–Stokes turbulence is combined with the universality of coarse-grained polymer
dynamics.

The Stokes solver
As already mentioned, we use the RPY solution of the Stokes equations, which in the Itˆo
interpretation of the overdamped Langevin equation has the advantage that its associated
drift is identically zero. Although the formulae themselves assume unbounded domains, we
can still apply periodicity to the bead positions, by ensuring that beads from the same chain
always remain close to each other, even if the chain is traversing a periodic boundary [40]. In
this way, we can retain the full Zimm eﬀects on the hydrodynamic drag levels (as contrasted
with those of the freely draining Rouse theory).
The tangent ﬂow solver
Equation (9) for the dynamics of the deformation gradient tensor F is solved with a
third order accurate in time RK method.
Although stable from the numerical analytic
viewpoint, large accumulated material deformations lead to ﬁnite precision arithmetic issues.
In particular, the Lyapunov exponents are computed via the logarithms of the singular values
of F. The latter can be computed via SVD but also much more easily via the spectral
decomposition of U2 matrix, since its eigenvalues are the squares of the singular values of
F. However, as more and more strain is accumulated in F for suﬃciently large times, its
singular values σi can become numerically extreme under ﬁnite precision arithmetic. For
example, very small σi values may lead to roundoﬀerrors that cause unphysical eﬀects, such
as slightly negative eigenvalues of F TF. We developed a method to resolve such issues by
ensuring that only well-behaved σi values appear in the computation, while preserving all
physical information carried by F. The method is based on the SVD of F.
To understand how this works, we reﬂect on the geometry of the SVD. The matrix F
acts on a material (reference) vector fR as follows: the matrix V is a rotation matrix whose
columns are the eigenvectors ri of F TF. The initial action V TfR rotates fR and expresses
its Cartesian components in the basis deﬁned by the ri. Next, Σ, a diagonal matrix with
entries σi, performs a pure stretch by scaling each of these components. Finally, the matrix
W, whose columns are the eigenvectors of FF T, rotates the stretched vector from the ri
basis to the Cartesian components of the spatial vector f.

Algorithmic procedure
Based on the above, the algorithmic steps are as follows:
(1) Specify initial conditions for turbulent ﬂow ﬁeld u, bead-positions rk, and deformation
gradients F of each chain.
(2) Increment time by the computational time step.
(3) Update turbulent velocity ﬁeld u with the Navier-Stokes solver.
(4) Using the current bead positions rk, compute the elastic and excluded volume forces.
(5) Insert the above forces in the overdamped Langevin equation to evaluate the hydro-
dynamic drag forces at rk.
(6) Using the RPY diﬀusion matrix and the hydrodynamic drag forces, calculate the
Stokes ﬂow uS(rk).
(7) Use the square root of the RPY diﬀusion matrix to calculate the Wiener process.
(8) Employ u(rk), uS(rk), and the Wiener process to update rk.
(9) Employing the updated rk positions, evaluate the velocity gradient tensor L at the
midpoint of the end-to-end distance of each chain, and update F.
(10) On prescribed time steps, perform the singular value decomposition of the deformation
gradient matrices, and normalise F.
(11) Repeat step (2) until the ﬁnal time is reached.

IV.
CONCLUDING REMARKS
(1) This investigation could be extended into the dilute regime, where, as indicated by
the interaction parameter I, several billion polymers could induce departures from
Navier–Stokes turbulence. Such calculations would require O(N) numerical methods
(with N the number of polymer beads in the system) and more general Stokes solvers
to accommodate periodic and wall boundary conditions.
In this context, Rotne–Prager–Yamakawa (RPY) hydrodynamics could be embedded
within more general PPPM (P3M) methods that handle arbitrary boundaries, for
example by employing boundary integral methods for the global Stokes ﬁeld [48]. It
is therefore imperative to pair O(N) numerics with massively parallel algorithms that
leverage modern GPU architectures.
(2) In our model, excluded–volume interactions are included, whereas the FENE elasticity
is an ideal–chain approximation. For consistency, a real–chain elasticity law would be
more appropriate in the presence of excluded volume [40]. This would allow us to test
whether such realism alters polymer dynamics in turbulence, particularly the location
and sharpness of the coil–stretch transition.
(3) Given the complexity of full–physics calculations in dilute and semi–dilute regimes,
a less detailed yet informative approach may be desirable. In particular, one could

develop a viscoelastic extension of Navier–Stokes vortex dynamics [55]. Such a reduced
description could guide and prioritise subsequent, more detailed computational studies.
