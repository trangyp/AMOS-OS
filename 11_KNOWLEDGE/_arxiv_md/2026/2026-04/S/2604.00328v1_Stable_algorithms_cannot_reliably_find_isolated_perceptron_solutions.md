Abstract
We study the binary perceptron, a random constraint satisfaction problem that asks to find a Boolean
vector in the intersection of independently chosen random halfspaces. A striking feature of this model is
that at every positive constraint density, it is expected that a 1 −oN(1) fraction of solutions are strongly
isolated, i.e. separated from all others by Hamming distance Ω(N). At the same time, efficient algorithms
are known to find solutions at certain positive constraint densities. This raises a natural question: can any
isolated solution be algorithmically visible?
We answer this in the negative: no algorithm whose output is stable under a tiny Gaussian resampling
of the disorder can reliably locate isolated solutions. We show that any stable algorithm has success proba-
bility at most 3

after a random perturbation of the disorder, the number of solutions located close to a pre-existing isolated
solution cannot concentrate at 1.

Introduction
The perceptron model is a simple one-layer neural network model that has attracted significant recent attention
at the interface of statistical physics, probability theory, learning theory, and theoretical computer science. It
was introduced by Cover [Cov65] and later studied extensively, notably by Gardner and Derrida [GD88] and
by Krauth and M´ezard [KM89]. Let ga ∈RN, for 1 ≤a ≤M, be i.i.d. random patterns. We say that these
patterns are storable if there exists a vector σ ̸= 0 such that ⟨ga, σ⟩≥0 for all 1 ≤a ≤M. The perceptron
may thus be viewed as a random constraint satisfaction problem (CSP), in which one seeks a vector satisfying
M random half-space constraints.
There are two widely studied versions of the perceptron model. In the spherical perceptron, the vector σ is
constrained to lie on the unit sphere SN−1. In the binary or Ising perceptron, one instead requires σ ∈ΣN :=
{−1, +1}N. For background on the spherical perceptron, we refer the reader to [Gar88, ST03, Tal11, Sto13b,
Sto13a, AS22]. In this paper, we focus on the binary perceptron.
A convenient way to formulate binary perceptron models is through an activation function U : R →{0, 1},
where a pattern ga is said to be stored by σ if U(⟨ga, σ⟩/
√

U(t) = 1{t≥κ} and U(t) = 1{|t|≤κ}. These correspond to the asymmetric binary perceptron (ABP) and the
symmetric binary perceptron (SBP), respectively. We describe these models in more detail below.

Satisfiability threshold.
An important question for the perceptron model is the satisfiability threshold, namely
the critical constraint density αSAT(κ) at which S(G, κ) transitions from being nonempty with high prob-
ability to empty with high probability. For the ABP, following the statistical physics prediction of Krauth
and M´ezard [KM89], substantial rigorous progress has been made on this question, including upper bounds
in [KR98, AT24], a sharp lower bound in [DS19], a matching sharp upper bound in [Hua24] (both sub-
ject to explicit numerical conditions), sharp-threshold results in [Tal11, Xu21, NS23], and a formula for the
free energy at low constraint density [BNSX22]. For the SBP, the satisfiability threshold was established in
[APZ19, PX21, ALS22b]. In addition, the model admits a more refined analysis, including contiguity results
[ALS22b] and detailed studies of the critical window [Alt23, SS23].
Algorithms.
Once satisfiability is understood, the next question is algorithmic: whenever solutions exist, is
there a polynomial-time algorithm that finds at least one solution? In the statistical physics perspective, this
algorithmic question is closely tied to the geometry of the solution space. Roughly speaking, if the set of
solutions is poorly connected inside the hypercube, so that typical solutions are far from one another, then one
expects algorithms or at least natural local dynamics to have difficulty finding solutions or moving between
them. This connection between geometry and algorithms is especially intriguing for perceptron models, and
will be directly relevant to the questions studied in this paper.
For the asymmetric binary perceptron, several efficient algorithms are known in certain regimes. Kim and
Roche [KR98] introduced a multistage majority algorithm that succeeds with high probability when κ = 0 and
α < 0.005. More recently, Li, Schramm, and Zhou [LSZ25] improved this guarantee to α ≤0.1 by developing
an algorithm based on discrepancy theory, and also obtained sharp algorithmic results in the large-|κ| regime.
In addition, a number of heuristic message-passing algorithms, particularly those based on belief propagation,
have been studied in the physics and machine learning literature; see e.g. [BZ06, BBBZ07, Bal09, BB15]. For
the symmetric binary perceptron, its close connection to discrepancy minimization leads to efficient algorithms
in certain parameter regimes, as well as hardness results in others; see, for example, [Ban10, CV14, LM15,
BS20, Pot20, ALS21, GKPX22, GKPX23, VV25].

Strong freezing.
To further understand the algorithmic question, one is naturally led to the geometry of
the solution space. In sparse random CSPs, two fundamental geometric properties of the solution space are
clustering and freezing. Clustering refers to the shattering of the solution space into exponentially many con-
nected components separated by linear distance, a picture formulated in [KMRT+07] and proved in [ACO08].
Freezing means that within a typical cluster, a linear fraction of variables take the same value across all so-
lutions. For example, in random graph coloring, the freezing threshold was established in [Mol12]; see also
[ZM08b, ZM08a, ZK11] for related results. In many such models, including random k-SAT, k-NAESAT, and
random graph coloring, both clustering and freezing occur well before the satisfiability threshold.
Perceptron models are unusual in that clustering and freezing are conjectured to occur at all positive con-
straint densities α > 0. Furthermore, it is expected that clustering occurs in a strong sense: [KM89] con-
jectured that in the ABP, typical solutions lie in clusters of vanishing entropy density (i.e., containing only
2o(N) solutions), with distinct clusters separated by linear Hamming distance. This prediction was strengthened
in [HWK13, HK14], which conjectured that typical solutions are genuinely isolated up to linear Hamming
distance. These predictions were later extended to the SBP in [APZ19, BDVLZ20].
This picture was rigorously confirmed for the SBP in [PX21, ALS22b], where it is shown that, with high
probability, all but an oN(1)-fraction of solutions are separated from every other solution by a linear Hamming
distance, for every α > 0. The same behavior is widely believed to hold for the ABP.
Why algorithms may still succeed.
At first glance, the strong-freezing picture seems difficult to reconcile
with the existence of efficient algorithms for finding perceptron solutions at positive density. Indeed, broadly
in disordered search and optimization problems, a long-standing belief from the statistical physics literature
is that the geometry of the solution space is closely linked to the computational tractability of the problem.
For example, [KMRT+07] conjectures that a broad class of Markov chain algorithms sample from the solution
space up to the clustering transition and no further, while an influential conjecture of [ACO08] suggests that the
clustering transition is the threshold beyond which any polynomial-time algorithm cannot find a solution.
However, for sparse random CSPs with bounded typical degree, it has been known since [AM02, ZK07]
that search algorithms can succeed beyond the clustering transition. This phenomenon is even more striking in
random perceptron models: even though strong freezing is expected at all constraint densities α > 0 for the
ABP (and has been proved for the SBP), many efficient algorithms are nevertheless known to find solutions at
some positive α. This creates an apparent violation of the heuristic that clustering of the solution space implies
hardness for algorithms.
A possible resolution was proposed in [BIL+15, BDVLZ20]: although a (1 −oN(1))-fraction of solutions
are strongly frozen, an exponentially small fraction may belong to clusters of exponential size, and efficient
algorithms may succeed by finding solutions in these rare but well-connected clusters. To turn this picture
into a rigorous explanation, one must establish two complementary facts. First, such rare but well-connected
clusters indeed exist. Second, strongly isolated solutions must themselves be hard to find algorithmically. The
first direction has seen recent progress; the second is addressed here. Supporting the first direction, Abbe,
Li, and Sly [ALS22a] proved that, for small α, with high probability there exists a cluster of nearly maximal
diameter; moreover, such a cluster can be found efficiently by a suitable multiscale majority algorithm in the
spirit of [KR98]. As further evidence for this picture, the branching overlap gap property (see Subsection 1.3.1
below) of Huang and Sellke [HS25b] rigorously links algorithmic tractability to the presence of a certain type
of dense solution cluster, namely a densely-branching ultrametric tree. Complementary to these results, several
recent works further investigate the structure of such atypical solution sets; see, for example, [BAKZ24, Bar25,
Bar26, Sto25].

1.2
Main results: algorithmic hardness of locating isolated solutions
The rare but well-connected cluster picture described above leaves one last piece of the puzzle: are isolated
solutions hard to find algorithmically? In other words, although efficient algorithms may succeed by finding
such atypical clusters, do they necessarily avoid the strongly isolated solutions that make up a 1−oN(1) fraction
of the solution space? This is the question we address in this paper.
This question arises implicitly in [PX21] from the strong-freezing picture, and was explicitly raised as an
open problem in [AG25]. In [AG25], it is proved that stable algorithms cannot produce an approximate sample
from the uniform measure over the solution space of the SBP model at any density α > 0. But this does not a
priori exclude the possibility that some special isolated solutions are algorithmically visible.
In this paper, we answer this question affirmatively. We formulate the obstruction in terms of stability under
a small Gaussian resampling of the disorder, which includes polynomial algorithms of degree o(N/ log N); see
Corollary 1.6. Our main results are as follows.
• Stable algorithms locate1 a strongly isolated solution with probability at most ≈0.84233 (Theorem 1.1).
• For any stable algorithm, including the algorithm of [KR98], that locates a solution with probability
1 −oN(1), the probability that it locates a strongly isolated solution is oN(1) (Theorem 1.4).
Similar arguments yield finer-scale k-isolation statements and extensions to perceptrons defined by a finite
union of intervals, including the SBP; though as we explain, the implications here are less clear. Our main
result shows hardness in the sense that algorithms succeed with probability at most ≈0.84233. Although one
would ideally like to show that algorithms succeed with probability oN(1), we remark that hardness results
with a non-vanishing success probability are common in the literature, and are still meaningful evidence of
computational intractability. Indeed, in random search and optimization problems, it is expected that in the
computationally easy phase, polynomial-time algorithms can succeed with probability very close to 1. Thus a
result that algorithms cannot succeed with probability larger than 1−Ω(1) still suggests computational hardness,
and [GJW20, GJW24, Wei22, BH22, GKPX22, LSZ25] show similar hardness results in related problems. We
leave the problem of strengthening the success probability to oN(1) as an important direction for future work.

1.3
Related work
Being concerned with algorithmic hardness, our result is related to two major approaches to such questions: the
overlap gap property (OGP) [Gam21] and the low-degree polynomial framework [Wei25].
1.3.1
Overlap gap property (OGP)
There is a long history of work on the solution geometry of disordered problems [MPS+84, MMZ05, ART06,
KMRT+07, ACO08], and it has long been expected that solution geometry has implications for algorithmic
tractability. The overlap gap property, introduced in [GS17a], provided an important framework to rigorously
link solution geometry and computational hardness. Since then, OGP has become a central tool for studying
computational hardness in random search and optimization problems; see, for example, [RV17, GL18, Wei22,
GKPX22, BH22, GKPX23, HS25b]. In its simplest form, an OGP roughly asserts that the overlap between
two solutions cannot lie in a certain intermediate range, so that any two solutions must be either far apart or
close together. This geometric obstruction has proved to be a powerful barrier for stable algorithms. Several
refinements of the original notion have since been developed, including multi-OGP [RV17, GS17b, GK23],
ladder OGP [Wei22, BH22], and branching OGP [HS25b, JMSS23, HS23, DGH25, BGG26, HSS26]. More
recently, OGP-based ideas have also been used to rule out online algorithms; see, for example, [GKPX23,
DGH25, GKW25, DKM25, BGG26]. Relatedly, recent work has also shown that the connection between
OGP and algorithmic hardness can be subtle: [LS25] exhibits algorithmically easy average-case optimization
problems that nevertheless satisfy OGP, while [HS25a] provides a refined rule of thumb for converting OGPs
into algorithmic hardness, and [MS25] gives a related counterexample for sampling.
Because OGP rules out stable algorithms through geometric properties of the solution space, it is natural
to ask whether the same framework can also rule out algorithms that locate isolated solutions in perceptron

route that does not rely on OGP; we are not aware of prior work showing hardness for a search problem against

1.3.2
Low-degree polynomials
A complementary approach is the low-degree polynomial framework. Originally developed in the study of
high-dimensional inference problems, it has since become a general framework for analyzing average-case
computational problems. In many such settings, low-degree polynomials capture the best known guarantees
achieved by algorithmic families such as spectral methods, approximate message passing, and subgraph counts;
see, for example, [Hop18, SW22, MWXY24, DDL25, CDGL26, BLY26]. Moreover, the framework also
provides strong evidence for algorithmic hardness. Indeed, a conjecture of Hopkins [Hop18] predicts that the
failure of degree-D polynomial algorithms implies the failure of “robust” algorithms running in time n e
O(D).
This is often referred to as the low-degree conjecture.
In recent years, the low-degree framework has also been brought to bear on random optimization problems
[GJW20, GJW24], including the largest independent set problem [Wei22], random k-SAT [BH22], and local
optima in spin glasses [HS25a]. Here (unlike the statistical estimation setting) the low-degree property is
relevant mainly due to its stability implications.

Stable algorithms cannot find isolated solutions.

Challenges and limitations of the OGP framework.
The standard route to hardness for stable algorithms
is through the overlap-gap property (OGP) [Gam21]. Consider the 2-OGP as a simple illustration. For a fixed
instance G, suppose there exist two strongly isolated σ1, σ2 ∈S◦(G, κ, ι). Since both are isolated solutions
of the same instance, their overlap is automatically constrained by isolation, namely
⟨σ1, σ2⟩≤(1 −2ι)N.
However, to use the 2-OGP to rule out stable algorithms, one must instead consider a pair of correlated instances
(G1, G2), where σj is an isolated solution for Gj. In this setting, the overlap ⟨σ1, σ2⟩is no longer controlled
for free by the isolation condition, and must itself be analyzed.
This leads to a substantial technical difficulty: one is forced to study the local geometry around σ1 under
conditioning on which nearby spin configurations are feasible for the corresponding instance. Such conditioning
introduces a complicated dependence structure that is difficult to handle. Moreover, even if one aims only for
a stability-based contradiction, it is not clear what form of uniform control should remain available, since
arguments involving correlated pairs (σ1, σ2) typically require union bounds over the solution set, which can
be prohibitively costly.

Discussion
We conclude this paper with some directions for future work. Strengthening the algorithmic success probability
ruled out by Theorem 1.1 is to us the most natural and interesting open problem.
Open problem 1. Show that stable algorithms cannot locate an isolated solution (in the sense of Theorem 1.1)
with probability more than oN(1).
We expect that achieving this will require new ideas. Indeed, a natural intuitive picture in the setting of
Theorem 1.1 would predict that after rerandomizing G slightly, the number of solutions near the algorithm
output is approximately a Poisson variable (since each point is unlikely to be a solution). A Poisson variable of
course cannot concentrate on 1. However, it can certainly place constant mass on 1 (in fact as much as 1/e), so
such a heuristic will not bound the success probability by oN(1).
The next problem pertains to Theorem 1.10.
Open problem 2. Show that in the generalized perceptron model considered in Theorem 1.10, stable algorithms
cannot kN-locate a kN-isolated solution with probability more than 1 −Ω(1), for some kN = ˜Ω(N).
Combined with Corollary 1.6, such a result would imply that degree ˜O(N) algorithms cannot kN-locate a
kN-isolated solution. The assumption kN = ˜O(
√
N) is required in the proof of Theorem 1.10 to ensure that,
when the perceptron constraints are defined by a finite union of intervals, at most one boundary constraint is
active for all solutions in a ball of radius kN; see (3.28). This step is the main obstacle to improving the bound
to kN = ˜Ω(N).
Finally, we ask if a similar hardness result can be proven for the spherical perceptron. Here one should
interpret “isolated solution” to mean a solution cluster of small diameter, which is separated from the rest of the
solution space by a significantly larger distance.
Open problem 3. Determine whether stable algorithms are able to locate a (suitably defined) isolated solution
in a spherical perceptron model.
