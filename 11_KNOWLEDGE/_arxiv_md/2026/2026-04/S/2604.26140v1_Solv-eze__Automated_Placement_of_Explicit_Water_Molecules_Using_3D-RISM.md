Abstract
Molecular
dynamics
(MD)
simulations
are
widely used to study biological systems, where
water molecules often play a critical role in
protein-ligand interactions.
In conventional
MD preparation protocols, water molecules are
typically added from a pre-equilibrated sol-
vent box and removed using conservative steric
cutoffs, an approach that can eliminate im-
portant interfacial waters that are often not
recovered during equilibration due to kinetic
barriers limiting exchange with bulk solvent.
In this work, we present an automated and
computationally efficient method for placing
water molecules around biomolecular solutes
using three-dimensional reference interaction
site model (3D-RISM) solvent density distri-
butions. By identifying regions of high solvent
probability, the method generates physically
meaningful initial hydration structures with-
out requiring extended sampling or special-
ized techniques such as grand canonical Monte
Carlo (MC) or hybrid MC/MD approaches, and
will be released as an update to AmberTools
26, enabling seamless integration into stan-
dard MD preparation pipelines.
We validate
the approach on a diverse set of protein-ligand
complexes with crystallographically resolved
bridging waters, showing that 3D-RISM-based
placement reproduces a large fraction of these
experimentally observed waters, while subse-
quent minimization further improves agree-
ment as crystallographic waters relax toward
positions consistent with those predicted by
our approach.
Overall, this method enables
more accurate and practical initialization of
interfacial hydration, improving the reliability
of MD simulations with modest computational
cost relative to routine system preparation.
Keywords
3D-RISM, water placement, molecular dynam-
ics, solvation, bridging waters, protein-ligand
interactions, AmberTools

Introduction
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is a fun-
damental tool for studying biomolecular sys-
tems, providing atomic-level insights into struc-
ture, dynamics, and thermodynamics.1–3 The
validity of thermodynamic properties computed
from MD trajectories relies on the ergodic hy-
pothesis4, which assumes that time averages
over sufficiently long trajectories converge to
ensemble averages. However, practical MD sim-
ulations are necessarily finite, and many biolog-
ically relevant processes occur on timescales far
exceeding accessible simulation lengths.

fundamental limitation poses significant chal-
lenges for systems in which slow relaxation pro-
cesses govern the equilibrium distribution of
molecular configurations.
Water molecules play an essential role in vir-
tually all aspects of biomolecular function.5–8
In protein-ligand binding, water molecules at
the interface can mediate recognition through
hydrogen-bonding networks, contributing sig-
nificantly to binding affinity and specificity.9
Crystallographic studies frequently reveal or-
dered water molecules that form bridging
hydrogen bonds between protein and ligand
atoms, underscoring their structural impor-
tance in molecular recognition.10,11 Standard
protocols12–16 for preparing solvated biomolec-
ular systems involve overlaying the solute with
a pre-equilibrated water box and subsequently
deleting water molecules that sterically over-
lap with solute atoms.
The overlap criterion
is typically defined using a distance cutoff of
approximately 4 Å, which significantly overes-
timates the true steric exclusion radius. This
conservative approach artificially creates re-
gions of zero water density, or vacuum, on
the solute surface, at protein-ligand interfaces,
and in buried cavities. Bridging water sites at
protein-ligand interfaces are of particular con-
cern, as the deletion protocol systematically
removes water molecules that should mediate
their interactions.
In standard protocols, after preparatory steps
including water deletion, the system undergoes
energy minimization and is relaxed through MD
simulation. These steps are employed with the
expectation that water molecules will diffuse
into underpopulated regions and establish dis-
tributions consistent with the target ensemble.
However, this assumption breaks down for wa-
ter molecules in occluded environments such as
structural waters buried within proteins, wa-
ters in gated cavities requiring conformational
changes for exchange, and, critically, bridging
waters between proteins and their bound lig-
ands.10,11,17–19 For bridging water sites to prop-
erly equilibrate through conventional MD, the
ligand would need to unbind to permit water
exchange, a dynamic process with timescales
often orders of magnitude longer than practi-
cal classical MD equilibration simulations.20
Several computational approaches have been
developed to address the challenge of water
equilibration in buried or occluded sites. Grand
canonical Monte Carlo21–23 (GCMC) methods
enable water insertion and deletion moves that
allow sampling of variable water occupancy
states24–26 which can be integrated with molec-
ular dynamics simulations. Ben-Shalom et al.19
introduced a hybrid MC/MD approach in Am-
ber27 that combines translational Monte Carlo
water moves with MD to exchange water be-
tween bulk and buried sites. While these meth-
ods are thermodynamically rigorous, they re-
quire substantial computational resources, spe-
cialized simulation protocols, and careful pa-
rameter selections, making them impractical for
rapid or automated simulation setup.
Three-dimensional reference interaction site
model28–32 (3D-RISM) theory offers an attrac-
tive alternative for characterizing the initial wa-
ter distributions around biomolecules.
As a
statistical mechanical theory of solvation, 3D-
RISM computes three-dimensional solvent den-
sity distributions that incorporate both solute-
solvent and solvent-solvent interactions using
the same force fields employed in MD simula-
tions. Importantly, 3D-RISM provides equilib-
rium solvent distributions without requiring dy-
namic equilibration, making it well-suited for
identifying high-probability water positions in
regions inaccessible to standard simulation pro-
tocols.
Here we present Solv-eze,
an automated
method for placing water molecules around
biomolecular solutes based on 3D-RISM sol-
vent density distributions. Our approach iden-
tifies regions of elevated water oxygen den-
sity from 3D-RISM calculations and system-
atically places water molecules at appropriate
positions, providing initial configurations that
better approximate equilibrium solvation. Un-
like GCMC or hybrid MC/MD methods, our
approach requires no extended sampling. The
generation of water distributions takes several
minutes for the solvation of typical proteins.
The method is fully automated and will be
released as an update to AmberTools 2616,
making it accessible to researchers preparing

Figure 1: User workflow.
Solv-eze automates the placement of explicit
water molecules on the surfaces of biomolecules
based on RISM theory as part of a stan-
dard system preparation protocol (Figure 1).
First, the user creates parameter and coor-
dinate files with periodic boundaries in the
Amber format.
These files can be con-
structed with any software package that sup-
ports output in the Amber format, such as
tleap16, CHARMM-GUI14,34, BioSimSpace35,
CPPTRAJ36, or ParmEd37,38.
Only coordi-
nates, Lennard-Jones parameters, and atomic
partial charges are required. solv-eze then cre-
ates a PDB file with high-probability water
molecules, to which remaining water and co-
solvent molecules can be added, and the system
can be parameterized using standard tools.
The Solv-eze workflow, represented as a di-
agram in Figure 2, begins by solving the 1D-
RISM equations for pure water to generate the
bulk solvent susceptibility. If the default val-
ues for 1D-RISM are used, the package will
use a precalculated solvent susceptibility re-
sult stored in a .xvv file.
The solvent sus-
ceptibility, parameter, and coordinate files are
then input into 3D-RISM16,39,40, which com-
putes the 3D number-density distribution of
the water oxygen and hydrogen atoms about
the solutes.
Once the number-density distri-
butions are solved, the package employs the
metatwist16,41 method to analyze the oxy-
gen site distribution function.
Specifically,
metatwist computes the Laplacian of the oxy-
gen density profile to identify regions of high
probability, corresponding to favorable sites.
Finally, the guess water hydrogen (gwh) tool is
used to generate plausible orientations of the
water molecules by assigning initial hydrogen
positions.16 The resulting structures can then
be used as starting configurations for molecular
dynamics simulations.

3
Validation method
The method was evaluated by running calcula-
tions on a set of 84 protein-ligand systems con-
taining bridging waters.33 Crystallographic wa-
ter molecules were considered bridging if they
were located between 2.5 and 3.6 Å from polar
atoms (S, N or O) in both the protein and the
ligand.
3.1
System Preparation and Pa-
rameterization
Each system was prepared using a combina-
tion of PyMOL43 and PDBFixer44 from their
respective RCSB entries.
Two copies of each
system were prepared: one to evaluate Solv-
eze water placement by removing all crystal-
lographic waters, and the other retained crys-
tallographic waters as a control case to com-
pare solv-eze against. PDBFixer was utilized
to replace nonstandard amino acids with their
standard equivalents, missing side chain atoms
were modeled by completing their respective
templates, and the protein structure was pro-
tonated at a pH of 7. Residues that were com-
pletely absent from the crystal structure were
not modeled during this step but were instead
treated as gaps in the structure. Finally, his-
tidine residues were renamed to reflect their
protonation states (HIE, HID, and HIP), and
capping residues (N-acetyl and N-methyl) were
added to both termini and gaps in the struc-
tures via an in-house Python script featuring
PyMOL function calls.
Ligand molecules were prepared using RD-
Kit45 to protonate and correct the bond orders
of organic compounds by cross-referencing the
co-crystal structure with its isomeric SMILES
entry in the corresponding RCSB entry. The
small molecule ligands were then parameter-
ized using Antechamber 22.
Atomic partial
charges were derived from the AM1-BCC46
charge model, and the Generalized Amber
Force Field 2 (GAFF 2)47,48 was used to de-
scribe the bonds, angles, and dihedral parame-
ters for each compound. Protein-ligand systems
were constructed with tleap16 using Amber 19
(FF19SB) protein parameters49, GAFF2 ligand
parameters, and standard monatomic ion pa-
rameters appropriate for use with the SPC/E
water model50.
After force field parameters
were applied, a system box was defined with
a 10 Å minimum distance between solute atoms
and the unit cell edges, suitable for 3D-RISM
implicit solvent calculations.

3.2
Water placement
As
described
in
Section2.1,
solv-eze
se-
quentially runs rism1d, rism3d.snglpnt39,40,
metatwist41, and gwh from the AmberTools
software suite16.
rism1d solved the dielectrically consistent
1D-RISM equations51,52 for pure water using
the SPC/E model at a concentration of 55.34 M,
dielectric constant of 78.4 and temperature of
298.15 K, with a residual tolerance of 10−10 on
a grid containing 16384 points and a spacing
of 0.025 Å. Solv-eze first solved the 1D-RISM
equations with the PSE-1 closure, and the re-
sults served as the initial guess for the subse-
quent calculations with the PSE-2 closure. The
1D-RISM equations were solved using the mod-
ified method of direct inversion in iterative sub-
space (MDIIS)53, utilizing 20 previous vector
solutions and an MDIIS step size of 0.3.
3D-RISM calculations with periodic bound-
ary conditions were carried out using a grid
spacing of 0.5 Å. The closure chain feature was
used to obtain solutions with the PSE-1 closure,
at a tolerance of 10−2, which was used as initial
guesses for the PSE-2 closure, which was solved
with a tolerance of 10−5. The MDIIS method
was employed to iteratively refine the solutions,
using five previous solutions and a MDIIS step
size of 0.7.
metatwist was applied in two steps to deter-
mine oxygen atom positions. First, the Lapla-
cian of the oxygen number density distribution
from 3D-RISM was computed with a convolu-
tion kernel of 1 Å. Then, a Laplacian blob anal-
ysis was carried out using the same bulk con-
centration of water as used for the 1D-RISM
calculations, and threshold values of 0.2, 0.3,
0.5, and 0.7 were applied.
Following this, gwh added hydrogen atoms to
the water oxygen atoms.
Default parameters
were used.
3.3
Minimization step
To assess the stability of both 3D-RISM pre-
dicted and crystallographic water molecules, an
implicit solvent minimization step was carried
out on both sets of water molecules, allowing
the water molecules to relax in their respec-
tive local environments. As mentioned in sec-
tion 3.1, these systems were prepared identi-
cally, except that the hydrogen positions for
the crystallographic waters were assigned us-
ing tleap, which gives the same orientation to
all water molecules. A steepest descent energy
minimization was carried out for 10,000 steps
using sander with the Onufriev-Bashford-Case
(OBC) version of the generalized Born implicit
solvent54, a surface tension of 0.007, a salt con-
centration of 0, a non-bonded distance cutoff of
100 Å, and harmonic restraints on all non-water
atoms with a 100 kcal/Å
2 force constant.
3.4
Comparison
with
crystallo-
graphic water
The oxygen atoms placed with Solv-eze were
compared with those identified as bridging wa-
ter molecules in the crystallographic structure.
The ones with the smallest distance were iden-
tified as the bridging water molecules predicted
by Solv-eze. This metric was defined to take
into account only crystallographic bridging wa-
ter molecules, since Solv-eze predicts additional
bridging waters that are not present in the crys-
tallographic structure.

4.1
Effect of metatwist threshold
on bridging water molecule
identification
To assess the quality of Solv-eze predictions,
we calculated the distance from each crystal-
lographic water oxygen to the nearest Solv-eze
oxygen atom. In Figure 3, we show the per-
centage of crystallographic water oxygens with
a predicted water oxygen within various cutoff
distances, including the van der Waals radius
(1.41 Å)55.
The van der Waals radius provides a metric
based on the effective size of a water molecule,
and for metatwist thresholds of 0.2 and 0.3,
more than 80% of predicted waters were within
1.41 Å and more than 90% were within 2.00 Å.

5
Conclusions
Modeling structures whose stability or interac-
tions are mediated by bridging waters requires
an accurate initial placement of these molecules
before performing molecular dynamics simula-
tions.
However, experimental structural data
may be unavailable, for example, in the case of
new drug proposals. In this work, we propose a
fast method to predict bridging water positions
based on the pair correlation functions acquired
from 3D-RISM calculations.
The results demonstrate that the Solv-eze
method provides accurate initial water posi-
tions when compared to crystallographic data.
For metatwist thresholds of 0.2 and 0.3, more
than 90% of the crystallographic bridging wa-
ters were predicted within 2.00 Å.
After en-
ergy minimization of both the Solv-eze predic-
tions and the experimental positions, we ob-
served an improvement in the overall agreement
of the water molecule positions, primarily be-
cause crystallographic water molecules moved
towards the Solv-eze predictions.
Therefore,
the proposed method produces predictions con-
sistent with the force field used and in good
agreement with crystallographic bridging water
molecules.
Thus, the proposed method accurately iden-
tifies key water molecules in the system, includ-
ing those that may not be captured by conven-
tional solvation with a pre-equilibrated water
box.
Although this study focuses on bridg-
ing waters at the ligand-protein interface, the
method can generate initial solvent placements
for arbitrary solutes and is broadly applica-
ble for preparing solvated systems for molecu-
lar dynamics simulations, particularly in cases
where standard approaches face substantial ki-
netic barriers that hinder equilibration toward
the correct solvent distribution. The package
developed in this work will be released as an
