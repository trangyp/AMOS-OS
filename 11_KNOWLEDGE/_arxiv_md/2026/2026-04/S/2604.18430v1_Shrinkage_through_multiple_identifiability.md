Shrinkage through multiple identifiability
Carlos Garc´ıa Meixide, David R´ıos Insua
Instituto de Ciencias Matem´aticas, Madrid, Spain
Abstract
We propose an empirical Bayes framework for aggregating estima-
tors obtained from several identification functionals associated to the
same causal parameter. The central object is a posterior mean that
pools a collection of asymptotically linear estimators of a scalar causal
target.
We establish consistency in two non-nested regimes: exact
identifiability, in which every functional identifies the same causal ef-
fect; and a second regime, in which individual functionals are biased
but the identification biases are mean-zero across functionals, and the
number of functionals grows with sample size. The dependence induced
by evaluating all estimators on the same sample is handled through a
working independence device that preserves consistency of the point
estimator. Inference is organized around a latent heterogeneity hyper-
parameter: when it vanishes, the functionals share a common target
and we report frequentist confidence intervals for that target via a
sandwich variance or subsampling; when it is strictly positive, each
functional targets a genuine draw from a mixing distribution and we
construct asymptotically valid Bayesian prediction intervals for the la-
tent target of a new functional. The two inferential outputs rest on
distinct assumption sets and are, therefore, complementary rather than
exclusive. We illustrate the framework in the context of augmenting
randomized controlled trials with observational evidence.

Introduction
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are widely regarded as the gold stan-
dard for internal validity, but they are costly, slow, and operationally con-
strained. Limited control-arm recruitment can inflate variance and erode
power even when identification is clear. Historical and real-world data are
frequently more abundant and may contain information relevant to the same
target question, motivating the augmentation of RCTs with external con-
trols or broader real-world data (Viele et al., 2014; Schmidli et al., 2014;

Galwey, 2017; Schmidli et al., 2020; Kojima, 2023). Uncritical borrowing,
however, can introduce bias when external and trial populations differ in
region, measurement, or care pathway, so precision gains must be balanced
against robustness to cross-study discrepancies (Burger et al., 2021; Bus-
gang et al., 2022).
Handling such discrepancies calls for causal-inference
methodology that accounts for hidden confounding.
A recurring feature of that methodology is that a given causal parame-
ter, defined in the counterfactual full-data world, is typically linked to the
observable data-generating distribution through more than one functional.
Multiple identifying functionals for the same causal parameter arise rou-
tinely: through distinct valid adjustment sets, through back-door versus
front-door formulas (Henckel et al., 2022; Gorbach et al., 2023; Mohammad-
Taheri et al., 2023), through inverse probability weighting versus outcome
regression under no unmeasured confounding, through several pre-treatment
cutoffs in regression discontinuity designs, through several candidate con-
trol groups in difference-in-differences, and through instrumental variables
indexing several data environments (Peters et al., 2016; Garc´ıa Meixide and
R´ıos Insua, 2025; Meixide and Insua, 2025; Meixide and van der Laan, 2025).
Under regular asymptotic linearity each such functional admits an efficient
influence curve with variance equal to the semiparametric efficiency bound
(Bickel et al., 1993; van der Vaart, 1998), and different functionals for the
same parameter generally induce different bounds, so no single choice is uni-
formly optimal across data-generating mechanisms (Robins and Rotnitzky,
1994; Hahn, 1998; Gorbach et al., 2023). Recent empirical Bayes propos-
als build on asymptotically linear estimators to borrow information across
related populations generating different samples (Law et al., 2023); our de-
velopment takes a related stance at the level of identification rather than
population. Wu et al. (2026) develops an empirical Bayes treatment of a
single experiment combined with many independent observational studies of
the same target, where the bias distribution is identified through auxiliary
calibration studies; in contrast, our estimators arise from multiple identify-
ing functionals evaluated on a single sample, so shrinkage borrows across
functionals.

1.1
Low-bias regime and minimax pooling
The question of when to pool RCT and observational data has been ana-
lyzed in a minimax framework by Chen et al. (2021). In their setting a single
RCT estimator ˆψrct is combined with a single observational estimator ˆψobs
of possibly biased mean, and the candidate pooled class consists of convex
combinations indexed by λ ∈[0, 1]. Let ∆denote the observational bias,
with an upper bound ¯∆assumed known. The oracle procedure is the infeasi-
ble rule that, given ∆, minimizes the mean squared error. It is dichotomous:
when the bias is large relative to RCT noise, ¯∆≳σrct/√nrct, the oracle sets
λ = 0 and uses the RCT alone; when the bias is small, it pools the two
sources as if ∆= 0. Chen et al. (2021) derive the minimax rate for mean
squared error and for confidence-interval length, and show that adaptivity
to an unknown bias has a fundamental limit: without prior information
placing the bias in the low regime, no uniformly valid confidence interval
can improve on the RCT-only interval by more than a constant factor.
Our framework specializes to the low-bias regime precisely when τ 2 = 0:
all identifying functionals—including the RCT and the IV-based function-
als indexed by subsets of environments—are asymptotically unbiased for
the same ψ∗. There the empirical Bayes combination ˆψEB collapses to a
precision-weighted average of J valid estimators and inherits the minimax
recommendation to pool. Beyond this regime, the centered-bias setting of
Theorem 4.2 extends the pooling recommendation to cases in which indi-
vidual functionals are biased but their biases average out across j, so long
as J →∞. When biases are systematic and uncentered, consistency is not
preserved and the minimax lower bound cautions against expecting both
uniform validity and short intervals without additional prior information.

A second source of DiD multiplicity, distinct from staggered timing,
arises when the analyst has several candidate control groups to contrast
against a single treated group and no principled rationale to prefer one over
the others. This situation is pervasive in comparative-effectiveness and phar-
macoepidemiologic studies. Consider, for instance, a new agent approved for
a chronic condition whose effect on a clinical outcome is to be estimated from
routinely collected records. The treated group is unambiguous—patients ini-
tiating the new agent at a known date—but the choice of comparator is not:
plausible controls include patients continuing on the previous standard of
care, patients switched to an alternative second-line therapy, patients in a
watchful-waiting arm, and patients meeting the indication but untreated for
access reasons. Each comparator rests on a different parallel-trends assump-
tion, reflecting a different assumed absence of differential secular trends in
outcome levels, and there is typically no design argument singling out one
comparator as exactly correct. Similar considerations arise in policy eval-
uation, where the counterfactual trajectory of a treated jurisdiction may
be proxied by neighboring jurisdictions, by demographically matched but
geographically distant ones, or by units that adopt the policy later. The fol-
lowing example formalizes this design; the empirical Bayes framework then
aggregates across the candidate comparators without forcing the analyst to
commit to one.

3
Identifiability, heterogeneity and maximum marginal
likelihood
Consider a collection ˆψ1, . . . , ˆψJ of estimators, each arising from a distinct
identifying functional of the same target and computed on a common sam-
ple. Because all of them are evaluated on the same observations, their joint
sampling distribution has non-diagonal covariance. To obtain a tractable
shrinkage rule we adopt a Gaussian hierarchical working model that treats
the ˆψj as conditionally independent given their latent targets ψj, with known
marginal variances vj. Working conditional independence is a nuisance de-
vice, not a claim about the true data-generating mechanism; it delivers a
closed-form empirical Bayes posterior mean, and the rest of the paper cor-

8
Discussion
We have developed an empirical Bayes aggregation framework for combining
estimators that arise from several identifying functionals of the same scalar
causal target. The central object is a precision-weighted estimator ˆψEB that
is consistent for ψ∗in two non-nested regimes.
Under exact identifiabil-
ity, every functional identifies ψ∗and aggregation serves a pure efficiency
purpose: the combined estimator borrows strength across functionals that
share the same identifying target but differ in their semiparametric efficiency
bounds. Under centered identification bias with a growing number of func-
tionals, no individual functional is consistent, yet the precision-weighted
average concentrates around ψ∗through a law of large numbers over the
functional index, provided the identification biases are mean-zero and the
precision weights do not asymptotically concentrate on a single functional.
The point estimator has the same closed form in both regimes; what changes
is the inferential guarantee.
The role of the heterogeneity hyperparameter τ 2 in the working model is
therefore twofold. At the estimation step, τ 2 controls the degree of shrinkage
and is estimated by the pairwise-difference estimator ˆτ 2. At the inferential
step, the value of τ 2 organizes a phase transition in what can be reported.
When τ 2 = 0, the de Finetti mixing measure underlying the hierarchical
prior degenerates to a point mass at ψ∗, there is no latent random varia-
tion across identifying targets, and the appropriate output is a confidence
interval for the fixed parameter ψ∗. Proposition 6.1 establishes global P-
orthogonality in this regime: the asymptotic distribution of n1/2( ˆψEB −ψ∗)
is invariant to the limit of ˆτ 2, and an observation-level sandwich variance—
together with a subsampling procedure that is robust to boundary behavior
of ˆτ 2 at zero—delivers valid confidence intervals. When τ 2 > 0, the mixing
measure is non-degenerate and the latent target ψJ+1 of a new identifying
functional is a genuine random quantity; the correlated sampling noise is
asymptotically dominated by the i.i.d. latent heterogeneity, and a split con-
formal procedure delivers asymptotically valid prediction intervals for ψJ+1
under the growth condition n−1 log Jcal →0.
The centering condition (C2) of Theorem 4.2 is substantive and should
27


## Page 28


be examined in each application. In the design of Example 2.5, in which
instrumental variables index several data environments, (C2) is plausible
whenever the set of environments is not systematically selected to favor
functionals that over- or under-estimate the causal effect; it would fail, for
instance, if weak first stages produce a common direction of finite-sample
bias shared across all subsets. Condition (C4) is automatically satisfied once
J = 2q −q −1 grows exponentially in the number of environments q, and
the convergence rate of the estimator is governed by (maxj Wj)1/2, which
approaches J−1/2 when the vj are comparable in size.
The framework connects naturally to the minimax data-fusion litera-
ture. Chen et al. (2021) show that in the low-bias regime pooling strictly
dominates RCT-only estimation, while Li et al. (2025) handle misalignment
through finite-dimensional selection-bias models. Our proposal complements
both by absorbing between-functional discrepancy into a single heterogene-
ity hyperparameter at the estimator level, so that precision-weighted pooling
is recovered in the low-bias regime while the centered-bias regime extends
the pooling recommendation beyond strict exchangeability of populations.
The price for this generality is that inference in the centered-bias regime
relies on resampling rather than on closed-form variance formulas, and uni-
form confidence-interval length reductions beyond a constant factor are not
available without prior knowledge of the bias.
Following the calibration device of Wu et al. (2026), a natural extension
of our framework is the introduction of calibration functionals: identify-
ing functionals applied to a contrast whose true causal effect is known a
priori to be zero, such as a placebo cohort, a pre-treatment period, or a
sub-population subject to a known null intervention. Calibration function-
als can identify the location µ = E[εj] of the bias distribution and remove
the centering condition (C2) of Theorem 4.2. Concretely, the working model
becomes ψj = ψ∗+ µ + εj with εj ∼N(0, τ 2), the marginal likelihood of the
calibration functionals is concave in µ given τ 2, and the resulting plug-in ˆµ
is subtracted from the substantive functionals before the precision-weighted
aggregation, restoring consistency under uncentered identification bias. The
illusion-return phenomenon of Wu et al. (2026)—in which jointly estimat-
ing (µ, τ 2) from the substantive estimators alone collapses ˆψEB onto the
minimum-variance functional—explains why a separate calibration channel
is essential rather than a stylistic preference.
A number of directions are left open. A systematic Monte Carlo study
under both the exact-identifiability and centered-bias regimes would quan-
tify finite-sample coverage of the sandwich, subsampling, and conformal
intervals across (J, n, τ 2). The efficiency loss of the working-independence
28


## Page 29


marginal likelihood relative to a generalized least squares objective with the
full J × J sampling covariance has not been quantified; this would clarify
the practical cost of the nuisance approximation. A complementary recov-
ery route arises when the identification biases εj are not mean-zero but their
signed structure is known, e.g., when some functionals deliver upper bounds
and others deliver lower bounds on ψ∗; a two-group combination that av-
erages group-specific empirical Bayes estimators restores consistency when
the group-level biases cancel, in the spirit of the doubly robust principle
(Bickel et al., 1993), and formalizing this construction as a partial identifi-
cation problem is a natural extension. Finally, the paper focuses on scalar
causal targets; extending the framework to vector-valued and functional esti-
mands, such as heterogeneous treatment effects indexed by covariate strata,
is of practical interest and is left for future work.
