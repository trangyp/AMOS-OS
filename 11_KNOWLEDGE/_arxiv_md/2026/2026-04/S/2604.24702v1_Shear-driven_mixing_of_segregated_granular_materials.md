Abstract.
As granular materials flow and settle, interactions among particles of different sizes or properties drive
mixing and segregation, producing rich dynamics that reshape systems ranging from industrial hoppers to planetary
surfaces. A hallmark of such polydisperse flows is shear-driven size segregation, whereby particles rearrange so that
larger grains migrate above smaller ones. Despite substantial progress in modelling granular flow and segregation, key
questions concerning the underlying mechanisms remain unresolved. In particular, the physics of granular mixing –
the natural counterpart of segregation – has received far less attention. Here, we investigate the dynamics of initially
segregated granular materials driven out of equilibrium by external shear. We ask: what controls the extent and rate of
segregation and mixing in a sheared granular flow? Answering this question is essential for understanding how external
forcing disrupts stable and unstable particle configurations and for optimising processes that require controlled mixing.
Using theoretical analysis and numerical experiments, we develop and validate a scaling framework that quantifies the
mixing dynamics. Our results provide new insight into the physics of granular flows and lay the foundation for
improved prediction and design in both natural and industrial settings.
Keywords: soft earth geophysics, granular flows, particle-size segregation, bidisperse mixing length.

1 Introduction
Polydisperse granular flows are governed by the competition between two antagonistic transport
mechanisms: size segregation, which sharpens concentration gradients through size-selective par-
ticle motion, and diffusive remixing, which smooths these gradients through random particle rear-
rangements.1,2 In dense sheared granular media, this competition determines whether an initially
stratified configuration remains stable, is transiently eroded into a mixed state, or relaxes back
towards a normally graded segregated state. It also sets the characteristic thickness of the polydis-
perse mixing layer that develops between segregated regions. However, a predictive description of
how this balance controls the relevant timescales and characteristic length scales remains incom-
plete.
This problem arises generically in granular materials, whose discrete and frictional nature gives
rise to strong coupling between particle rearrangement, frictional contacts, stresses, and bulk de-
formation.1–4 In polydisperse systems, shear commonly drives size segregation, with large grains
tending to rise while small grains percolate downwards.5,6 The resulting spatial sorting modifies
the internal structure, transport properties, and effective rheology of the material, with important
consequences across geophysical and industrial settings.4,7–12
Substantial progress has been made in understanding segregation in dense granular flows.
Mainstream mechanisms such as kinetic sieving and squeeze expulsion, together with continuum
convection–segregation–diffusion equations, have clarified how shear, pressure and particle-size
contrasts generate net segregation fluxes and stratified states.6,7,14–17 More recent studies have
quantified segregation rates, characterised intruder dynamics, and developed continuum descrip-
tions that link segregation to rheology and the mechanical energy budget of the flow.7,8,12,13 By
comparison, the transient dynamics of diffusive remixing remain much less well resolved. In most

Here, the flow is driven away from equilibrium, so that segregation no longer simply builds struc-

rebuild stratification. This competition is expected to be governed primarily by two parameters:
the particle-size ratio Rd, which sets the strength of size-selective percolation, and the P´eclet num-
ber Pe, which measures the relative importance of segregation and remixing. The problem is,
therefore, to determine how these parameters control the destruction, persistence, and recovery of
stratification. In particular, we raise the following questions:
(i) Does the degree of segregation systematically increase with increasing Rd and Pe?
(ii) Does the bidisperse mixing-layer thickness decrease systematically as segregation becomes
stronger relative to remixing?
(iii) Do the timescales for reaching maximum mixing and recovering the stable segregated state
follow distinct scalings with Rd and Pe?

Here, we address these questions by studying the evolution of an initially segregated bidisperse
granular column subjected to shear as a function of the parameter space (Rd, Pe). We combine
numerical simulations with asymptotic analysis to develop a scaling framework for the transient
dynamics. Our analysis identifies two characteristic timescales: a mixing timescale associated with
the erosion of segregation and the attainment of maximum mixing, and a segregation timescale
associated with the approach to the asymptotically stable stratified state. In parallel, we quantify
the mixing layer that develops between the large- and small-particle-rich regions and show how
its thickness reflects the local balance between segregation fluxes and diffusive remixing. Taken
together, these results provide a trackable analytical description of how transient granular mixing
and restratification depend on Rd and Pe, and shed light on the conditions under which segregated
states persist, are transiently disrupted, or are efficiently homogenised.

We numerically integrated the coupled nonlinear PDE system in (1) using the method of lines

The parameter values used to calculate h according to the µ(I)-rheology, along with other particle
parameters, were originally taken from,28 are detailed in table 1, and correspond to those used in
previous work.11,12
For the present study, the model was not coupled with a momentum equation, as done pre-
viously.11,12 This coupling was deemed unnecessary since transient momentum transfer does not
control the final mixing state, nor does it govern the segregation–diffusion balance. This was ev-
idenced by analysis of the energetic balance in previous work.12 The solution sets consisted of
varying the size ratio Rd for two different initial conditions (stable and unstable) of the small-
particle distribution ϕs. A first set of simulations was performed for an inversely graded (unstable)
initial condition with Rd ∈[1.1 : 0.1 : 3.2], and a second set for a normally graded (stable) ini-
tial condition with Rd ∈[1.1 : 0.1 : 5.0], yielding a total of 62 simulations. For this range of
Rd and the parameter values listed in table 1, the P´eclet number spans 0.8 ≤Pe ≤200. This
range is broad enough to capture the transition from diffusion-dominated remixing to segregation-
dominated transport. In all cases, the parameters in table 1 were held fixed, as was the initial
small–large particle interface, which was placed at z = h/2, or ˆz = 1/2. As an example, in
figure 2, we show the numerical solutions for Rd = 2.0 for both the unstable and stable initial

unstable case shows an intermediate (apparent) mixing state, not present in stable conditions, but
it is also a well-known feature of the inversely graded initial condition problem.2 Remarkably, this
apparently mixed state does not necessarily translate into wider or narrower mixing layers, as we
will explore later.

6 Concluding remarks and outlook
We have examined how the degree of segregation and mixing evolves in shear-driven bidisperse
granular flows, focusing on the interplay between advective segregation and diffusive remixing.
Using the convective–segregation-diffusive model2 under uniform shear, we performed systematic
numerical experiments to quantify the role of the particle-size ratio Rd = dl/ds > 1 and the P´eclet
number Pe, which measures the relative strength of the segregation flux to diffusive mixing. In
particular, we characterized: (i) the temporal evolution of the degree of segregation Sϕ(ˆt) and its
long-time asymptotic value S ∞
ϕ ; (ii) the thickness of the mixing layer ˆδm in which both species
coexist; and (iii) the timescale ˆτm required to reach the state of maximal mixing.

## Page 13


Across these metrics, we derived asymptotic scalings that explicitly depend on Pe and Rd, pro-
viding a reduced description of segregation dynamics in terms of a small set of control parameters.
These results clarify how segregation efficiency, interface sharpness, and transient mixing times
emerge from the competition between segregation and diffusive processes. The scaling relations
offer a predictive framework for estimating attainable segregation states, the extent of mixing lay-
ers, and corresponding timescales, with direct implications for the design and control of laboratory
experiments, numerical simulations (DEM), and industrial granular flows.
Several directions remain open. First, the present continuum description could be tested against
controlled laboratory experiments to assess its quantitative accuracy, particularly in regimes where
diffusion is non-Fickian or where shear is non-uniform. Second, DEM simulations would enable
systematic evaluation of the assumptions underlying the model, including the form of the segrega-
tion flux and the closure for diffusive remixing. Finally, extending the framework to polydisperse
systems, density-differentiated particles, non-uniform forcing, and coupled rheology–segregation
feedbacks would provide a more complete description of granular mixing in realistic flows.
