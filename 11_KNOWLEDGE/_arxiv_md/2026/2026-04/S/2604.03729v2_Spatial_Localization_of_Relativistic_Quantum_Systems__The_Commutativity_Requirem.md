Abstract
We study the role of commutativity in the representation of relativistic locality
for localization observables of relativistic quantum systems in Minkowski spacetime.
A celebrated no-go theorem by Halvorson and Clifton shows that the commutativ-
ity of localization effects associated with causally separated regions is incompatible
with other apparently natural assumptions about spatial localization. In particular,
commutativity is assumed to provide the mathematical representation of locality in
the Araki-Haag-Kastler formulation of quantum field theory. This raises the ques-
tion of whether commutativity follows from more elementary locality principles of
quantum theory. Adopting Busch’s operational analysis in terms of no-signaling
and relativistic consistency, we argue that, for a particle-like system, the commu-
tativity requirement does not follow from these principles. Under a natural local
detectability principle, elementary localization observables are not localized in ar-
bitrarily small spacetime neighborhoods of the corresponding spatial regions, but
rather in regions containing the entire rest space (a Cauchy surface) on which the
measurement is performed. This is a consequence of the very nature of a particle,
which is assumed to be localized at a unique position on a rest space completely
filled with ideal detectors. In this respect, there is no direct conflict with the Araki-
Haag-Kastler formulation of local quantum physics. On the other hand, we also
show that commutativity and localization may coexist when one refers to less ideal-
ized localization procedures. Indeed, we introduce conditional localization POVMs
associated with bounded spatial regions interpreted as laboratories. Owing to a
result in quantum information theory known as the gentle measurement lemma,
these observables describe conditional localization probabilities. In principle, these
observables satisfy commutativity when associated with causally separated labo-
ratories. As a consequence, they could be represented by local observables in the
sense of Araki-Haag-Kastler. Explicit examples of such local observables satisfying
the commutativity requirement will be presented in a forthcoming paper within the
framework of local quantum field theory.

Introduction
1.1
Localization, Causality, Commutativity
Spatial localization in relativistic quantum theory has long been a delicate issue, lying at the
intersection of quantum measurement theory, spacetime causality, and the very notion of particle.
In non-relativistic quantum mechanics, localization is naturally described by projection-
valued measures and, correspondingly, by selfadjoint position operators. This is a case of sharp
localization, since the projectors P(∆) of the spectral measure are labelled by arbitrarily small
regions ∆of the rest space Σ of an observer, and we can prepare states ρ for which the probability
of detecting a particle in a given such bounded region is tr(ρP(∆)) = 1.
In the relativistic setting, however, this scheme encounters severe difficulties.
The best-
known example is the Newton-Wigner construction, which provides a mathematically neat
notion of sharp position for relativistic particles, but whose physical interpretation has long
been debated. In particular, Newton-Wigner localization was proposed as a possible tool for
reinterpreting localization in (free) quantum field theory so as to avoid some of the puzzling
consequences related to the Reeh-Schlieder property [Fle00]. Nevertheless, it was later shown
that the counterintuitive features implied by the Reeh-Schlieder theorem persist also in this
reformulation [Hal01].

From the one-particle viewpoint, a further serious issue is raised by Hegerfeldt’s celebrated
analysis [Heg74, Heg85]. If energy, i.e., the selfadjoint generator of time translations, is positive
(or bounded from below), sharp localization described by projection-valued measures gives rise
to causality problems. In modern terms, and especially in the formulation later clarified by
Castrigiano, these problems concern the incompatibility of sharp localization with a natural
requirement of causal (i.e. non-superluminal) propagation for localization probabilities. This
strongly suggests that, in relativistic quantum theory, sharp notions of localization should be
abandoned in favour of unsharp ones described by POVMs. In this case, effects, that is, positive
operators bounded by the identity, 0 ≤A(∆) ≤I, rather than orthogonal projectors, are still
associated with arbitrarily small regions ∆in the rest space Σ of an observer, but the probability
of finding the particle in a given bounded region is strictly tr(ρA(∆)) < 1 for every state ρ, even
though sharp states can be approximated arbitrarily well.
Recent works [Cas17, Mor23, Cas24, DRMo24, Cas25, CDM26] have shown that this strat-
egy is in fact viable. A broad family of relativistic spatial localization observables has been
constructed and analyzed for various elementary systems, with full compatibility with Poincar’e
covariance and with natural causal requirements, especially in the unsharp setting when the
requirement of positive energy is assumed. These developments indicate that Hegerfeldt’s issue
does not rule out unsharp localization under the requirement of energy positivity, but rather
rules out an excessively rigid, projection-based notion of localization.
There is, however, another and subtler problem, which concerns not the propagation of
localization probabilities in time, but rather the representation of relativistic locality itself in
measurement theory. A central result in this direction is the no-go theorem by Halvorson and
Clifton [HaCl02], which may be regarded as the culmination of a line of investigation initiated
by Schlieder [Sch71] and Jancewicz [Jan77], sharpened by Malament [Mal96] for projector-
valued localization, and later extended by Busch [Bus99]. Roughly speaking, Halvorson and
Clifton proved that, under a family of assumptions that appear very general and natural for
a relativistic localization scheme, the localization effects (elements of an alleged localization
POVM) must be trivial. In this sense, the theorem seems to suggest a deep tension between
relativistic localization and relativistic locality. We stress that the theorem does not assume a
particular definition of the considered quantum system which, in principle, could be formalized
as a Wigner elementary particle, a composite system or even a quantum field. The assumptions
regard additivity, translation covariance, positivity of energy, and a microcausality requirement
expressed by commutativity of localization effects associated with suitably ”separated” regions
∆, ∆′:
[A(∆), A(∆′)] = 0 .
Actually, it is not even supposed that a whole unitary representation of the Poincar’e group
exists. The relativistic perspective comes into play only to physically justify commutativity of
localization effects associated with ”separated” regions, when interpreted as causally separated
regions in the relativistic sense.
The crucial point is that the commutativity requirement entering the Halvorson-Clifton the-
orem is usually regarded as self-evident from the perspective of local quantum physics, especially
within the Araki-Haag-Kastler framework [Haa96, Ara09], where locality is encoded precisely
by commutativity of observables associated with causally separated spacetime regions. If the
spatial region ∆of a rest space Σ where a particle is found at a certain time t belongs to the

spacetime region O where the localization-observable A(∆) is localized (in AHK sense), then the
commutativity assumption would indeed appear unavoidable. From this standpoint, Halvorson-
Clifton’s result would represent an insurmountable obstruction to any notion of position of a
quantum system at a given time in a reference frame. However, this line of reasoning already pre-
supposes a specific mathematical representation of locality. It is therefore natural to investigate
the interplay of commutativity and causal separation of the localization region, independently
of the assumption that localization observables A(∆) belong to AHK local algebras A(O), in
the strict sense, attached to arbitrarily small spacetime neighborhoods O of the corresponding
spatial regions: O ⊃∆. A first step in this direction was taken by Busch [Bus99], building on
a technical result with Singh [BuSi98], by analyzing the relation between commutativity and
locality directly within the general theory of quantum measurement. In Busch’s approach an
observable is localized in a region O of spacetime if it can be measured with instruments which
work therein. This is also in the spirit of AHK structure, but in contrast to that framework,
no further requirement about the structure (∗, C∗, von Neumann algebra) is assumed for the
family of observables localized in a region of spacetime. Similarly, no a priori requirements
for families of observables associated with distinct regions of spacetime are supposed. In that
setting, locality is formulated through operational principles such as the no-signaling condition
and, in a stronger form, what is often called relativistic consistency [Bec20], namely invariance
of the statistics of suitable consecutive measurements under interchange of their temporal order
when the measurements take place in causally separated regions. For broad classes of measure-
ment procedures – in particular for L¨uders measurements and related efficient schemes – these
principles imply commutativity of the relevant effects. This analysis is mathematically precise
and conceptually important because it does not assume locality in the algebraic form of micro-
causality (i.e. commutativity), but rather attempts to derive commutativity from independent
operational requirements.
The main purpose of this paper is to investigate whether this route can actually justify the
commutativity requirement for relativistic spatial localization observables. The answer we shall
propose is, in general, negative. To this end, following Busch’s approach, we adopt in particular
a natural local detectability principle which asserts that if instruments work in an open spacetime
region O and they are used to detect a particle in a spatial region ∆, then ∆⊂O.
The above negative answer is not due to a failure of no-signaling or relativistic consistency,
but rather to the physical meaning of localization for a quantum system which we define to be
a particle. Let us assume that a localization observable is meant to describe a system with the
propensity to be found in a unique place when an ideal position measurement is performed on a
whole rest space. Then the elementary observable associated with detection in a region ∆is not
naturally localized in an arbitrarily small spacetime neighborhood of ∆. On the contrary, once
one adopts the above stated local detectability principle, as we shall prove in Section 4.2, one
is led to the conclusion that the elementary observable A(∆), I −A(∆) is localized, in Busch’s
sense, in a region that must contain the whole rest space where the global localization experi-
ment is performed. As a consequence, two elementary localization observables associated with
spatially disjoint regions are not localized in causally separated spacetime regions, and therefore
the no-signaling and relativistic-consistency arguments leading to commutativity cannot even be
triggered. In this sense, the commutativity requirement for localization effects does not follow
from those more elementary locality principles, since the very hypotheses for employing these
principles are not satisfied. This conclusion is physically tied to the particle character of the

## Page 5


systems under consideration. A particle is assumed not to localize simultaneously in several
(causally) disjoint regions in an ideal exhaustive detection experiment, but rather in a unique
place. If one measures whether the particle is found in a region ∆within a complete position
measurement on a rest space Σ, then one automatically obtains information about the comple-
mentary region Σ\∆. From this viewpoint, the associated elementary observable is not confined
to ∆alone: it carries information about the global detection arrangement on Σ. The failure of
the commutativity argument is therefore not an exotic mathematical pathology, but rather a
direct consequence of the physical meaning attributed to localization of a particle. If we do not
accept this, we cannot accept the very notion of a particle.
These arguments provide a motivation for asserting that localization effects, for particles, do
not belong to local algebras in the sense of AHK formulation, in the case where they are parts
of a POVM which fills the whole rest space of a reference frame (a Cauchy surface).
The second aim of the paper is to indicate a possible way to make compatible spatial local-
ization and commutativity in more realistic localization experiments.
We introduce a notion of conditional localization associated with a bounded spatial region
∆0, interpreted as the spatial part of a laboratory. The idea is to construct, starting from a gen-
eral localization observable defined on a full rest space, a new POVM normalized on ∆0 which
describes the conditional probability of finding the system in a subregion ∆⊂∆0, given that the
system has been detected in ∆0. The mathematical possibility of such construction relies upon
a known result in quantum information theory called gentle measurement lemma. It permits one
to prove that a specific type of POVMs we introduce in a laboratory normalized on bounded
spatial region ∆0, measure the conditional probability of finding a particle in ∆⊂∆0 if we know
that the system was detected in ∆0. This is all the more true the closer the probability of finding
the particle in ∆0 is to 1. These conditional POVMs are intrinsically local to the laboratory
and, unlike the original localization observables on the whole rest space, are not affected by the
same problem that prevents the use of no-signaling or relativistic-consistency arguments. We
shall argue that pairs of such conditional POVMs associated with causally separated laboratories
may, in principle, satisfy commutativity and belong to local algebras concentrated around the
laboratory region in the AHK sense. This suggests that conditional localization may provide a
mathematically and physically appropriate notion of local position measurement in relativistic
quantum theory. Explicit examples of these local observables satisfying a commutativity re-
quirement and constructed in terms of local energies will be presented is a forthcoming paper
within the approach of local QFT.
1.2
Structure of the work
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we review the notion of relativistic spatial local-
ization observable and the relevant causal conditions, and we recall the Halvorson-Clifton no-go
theorem for unsharp localization systems. We also discuss the relation of the problem with the
Araki-Haag-Kastler framework. In Section 3 we summarize Busch’s operational analysis of local-
ity in terms of no-signaling and relativistic consistency, including the relevant results connecting
these principles with commutativity of effects. In Section 4 we apply that analysis to relativistic
localization observables and prove that, under a natural local detectability principle and for sys-
tems with particle-like localization behaviour, commutativity of causally separated localization
effects is not implied by those operational locality principles. In Section 5 we introduce condi-

Halvorson–Clifton’s no-go result for unsharp localizations
In what follows, we first introduce some types of spatial localization, based on the POVM for-
malism, which are compatible with Hegerfeldt’s locality constraint. We shall simultaneously
review Hegerfeldt’s causality conditions in the modern presentation due to Castrigiano. After-
wards, we shall present the Halvorson–Clifton no-go result [HaCl02]. Finally, we shall focus on
the crucial commutativity hypothesis assumed by Halvorson and Clifton from the standpoint of
Busch’s analysis of locality [Bus99] and of more recent developments on the subject, especially
those collected in Beck’s paper [Bec20].

the fourth one, which, on the other hand, seems to express an elementary locality requirement in
the quantum framework. If one proved that hypothesis (4) cannot be relaxed because relaxing
it would irremediably affect fundamental locality requirements, the conclusion would be that no
notion of spatial localization makes sense.
A problematic point in this investigation is that commutativity of observables localized in
causally separated regions is simply assumed as the basic description of locality in local quantum
physics, in particular in the formalization due to Araki and Haag-Kastler (AHK) [Haa96, Ara09]
(see the next section).
A deeper scrutiny, independent of the AHK approach, seems to be
necessary at this stage. In particular, where no requirement about the structure (∗, C∗, von
Neumann algebra) is assumed for the family of observables localized in a region of spacetime and
no a priori requirements for families of observables associated to distinct regions of spacetime
are supposed. This type of analysis was initiated by Busch [Bus99] and developed by further
authors, relying on the general theory of quantum measurement and on general local causality
requirements concerning consecutive measurement processes. This analysis will be summarized
in Section 3.
2.3
Relation with the Araki-Haag-Kastler framework
In the Araki-Haag-Kastler (AHK) formalism [Haa96, Ara09], the von Neumann algebra A of
physically relevant operators of a quantum physical system described in the Hilbert space H –
in particular the operators defining observables – is generated by local von Neumann algebras
A(O). There is a local von Neumann algebra A(O) for every open bounded set O ⊂M. Each

such algebra contains operations and observables that are somehow physically associated with O:
the corresponding physical operations and measurements are performed therein. The identity
operator I is obviously common to all local algebras. If O is open but not bounded, A(O) is the
von Neumann algebra generated by the family of bounded open subsets of O. Isotony holds:
A(O) ⊂A(O1) if O ⊂O1, and one of the fundamental assumptions is that operators belonging
to algebras associated with causally separated regions must commute:
[A1, A2] = 0
if A1 ∈A(O1), A2 ∈A(O2) and O1 ∩(J+(O2) ∪J+(O2)) = ∅.
The net of algebras is assumed to admit a strongly-continuous unitary representation of the
Abelian translation group V satisfying the spectral condition: the joint spectrum of the selfadjoint
generators must lie in V+. This representation is assumed to be extended to a full (continuous)
unitary representation of the orthochronous Poincar´e group. Finally the Hilbert space contains
a preferred Poincar´e invariant state, represented by a unit vector Ω, the vacuum vector state
which has the property to be cyclic: the subspace spanned by the vectors AΩwith A ∈A is
dense in H.
There are a number of further requirements and fundamental results arising from the AHK
approach. We shall, however, omit them all and stick to the elementary postulates above, since
they are enough for our discussion. The reader may consult [Haa96, Ara09] for classic general
treatments of the subject.
Most of the features of this approach can be generalized to the case where A and every A(O)
are unital ∗-algebras, in particular of operators in a given Hilbert space with a common invariant
domain.
As already suggested, it seems natural to postulate that the self-adjoint operators Av(∆) and
Av′(∆′) belong to the local algebras associated with open spacetime subsets arbitrarily narrowed
around ∆and ∆′, respectively. If these open sets are causally separated, adopting the AHK
formulation, then [Av(∆), Av′(∆′)] = 0. If ∆and ∆′ lie in a common 3-space Σ and the distance
between them is strictly positive, it is easy to prove the precise version of commutativity stated
in requirement (4) of Theorem 2.5 by means of this argument. We shall see in the next sections
that the hypothesis that Av(∆)8 is localized in a spacetime region arbitrarily concentrated
around ∆is, however, disputable when dealing with relativistic spatial localization observables
of particles: if these structures exist and can be constructed within the AHK framework, the
region of localization of the operators Av(∆) cannot be arbitrarily narrowed around ∆.
As is well known (see [Bec20] in particular), this negative result also arises from a funda-
mental result in local QFT known as Reeh-Schlieder theorem [Ara09, Haa96], if one interprets
Av(∆) as the mathematical representation of a detector localized in ∆. If assuming that this
operator is positive and that the vacuum state has zero expectation value (no particles stay in
that state!) then one faces a mathematical contradiction. For this reason, in the rigorous local
formulation of scattering theory in the Haag-Ruelle approach detectors are not assumed to be
local observables but just quasi-local observables [Ara09, Haa96].
The AHK formulation makes it possible, in particular, to exploit modular theory [Ara09,
Haa96] (in the one-particle space of a scalar free quantum field) when the net of observables is
formulated in terms of von Neumann algebras. More recently, this perspective has inspired an
alternative approach to localization, initiated by Lechner and de Oliveira [LedO26], based on

3
No-signaling, relativistic consistency, and commutativity
An important analysis of the validity of the commutativity hypothesis (4) in the Halvorson–
Clifton theorem – relying solely on general principles of measurement theory, and thus indepen-
dently of the AHK framework [Haa96, Ara09] – was presented by Busch in [Bus99] (drawing
on a previous technical paper [BuSi98] co-authored with Singh), prior to the Halvorson–Clifton
paper. In [HaCl02], Halvorson and Clifton justified their hypothesis (4) on the basis of Busch’s
analysis.
The next three sections present and examine Busch’s argument and some subsequent develop-
ments on the subject, in particular those due to S. Gudder and collaborators [GuNa01, AGG02].
An updated and thorough discussion, including further relevant results and important remarks,
appears in Section 2.2 of Beck’s comprehensive analysis developed in his PhD thesis [Bec20]. In
Section 2.6 of Beck’s analysis, it is explicitly stated that local commutativity does not seem to
be an entirely well-motivated requirement. We shall see that our results are consistent with this
view.
3.1
Localization of (generalized) observables according to Busch
Busch-Singh’s paper [BuSi98] and, more precisely, Busch’s work [Bus99] focus on a version
of Einstein causality called weak Einstein causality, also known as the no-signaling condition
(NSC). A related causality condition mentioned by Busch is what we shall call the relativistic
consistency condition (RCC), following Beck [Bec20]. To state them formally, we have to define
the physical notion of localization for a generalized observable, starting from [Bus99]: “[...] we
need to assume a physically meaningful association of observables with (bounded open) spacetime
regions, in the sense that such observables can be measured by means of operations carried out
within these regions”.
We stress that no further requirement about the structure (∗, C∗, von Neumann, algebra) of
the family of observables localized in a region is assumed contrarily to AHK approach. Similarly,
no a priori requirements for families of observables associated to distinct regions of spacetime
are supposed.
In this context, observables are POVMs, with PVMs as a special case. A selfadjoint operator
is not directly an observable; the associated observable is its PVM. An effect E of a POVM,
in spite of being selfadjoint, is not necessarily interpreted as an observable in its own right.
Indeed, the associated observable would be the PVM of E, which, however, plays no physical
role in the general case. Nevertheless, E defines an associated elementary observable, the POVM

4
Commutativity requirement and relativistic locality
We are now in a position to study the relation between Propositions 3.6, 3.7 and the validity
of the commutativity hypothesis (4) in Halvorson-Clifton’s theorem, also for relativistic spatial
localization observables and, in this case, also comparing effects with different tensorial indices v.
First, we prove that, in principle, these theorems – where NSC/RCC and L¨uders measurements
play a crucial role – imply commutativity for the effects of an unsharp localization system
associated with causally separated regions, provided some apparently natural hypotheses are
fulfilled. Next, we prove that causal separation hypotheses are, however, never satisfied simply
in view of the very physical nature of a particle and its propensity to localize in a unique
place. Hence the commutativity assumption does not arise by this route. In this case, failure of
commutativity is not in contradiction with locality principles such as NSC and RCC.

regions. Two localization regions, unlike the detection regions, are never causally separated,
and this does not permit one to trigger locality arguments relying upon NSC or RCC. The
apparently only alternative route is to abandon from the outset the physically natural principle
LDP, which played a crucial role in the proof of Proposition 3.7.
In summary, what we have established with Proposition 4.3 and Corollary 4.4 has a clear
physical meaning. We are considering systems which localize in a unique region, namely at a
single point of space in ideal detection procedures, and not in several regions simultaneously: in
other words, we are considering a particle. A particle has the propensity to localize in a single
place, unlike extended systems11. We are simply exploring the consequences in measurement
theory of this fundamental propensity characteristic of particle nature. Using a POVM on Σ of
a relativistic spatial localization observable means that we have filled the entire rest space Σ of
an inertial reference frame with infinitely many detectors. We turn them on at the instant t –
which defines Σ – and turn them off immediately afterwards. At that instant, the the particle
must necessarily be detected in a unique detector. If we focus our attention on an arbitrarily
chosen region ∆⊂Σ, the system may or may not be found there at time t. In the first case,
however, we are sure that it is simultaneously detected nowhere in ∆c; in the second case, we
are sure that it is simultaneously detected somewhere in ∆c. By observing what happens in
∆, we therefore have information about what is happening in the whole of Σ at the instant
t. In this sense, it seems natural that {Av(∆), I −Av(∆)} should be thought of as localized
everywhere in Σ, and there is no contradiction with locality because, as said above, we are using
the propensity of the considered quantum system, namely a particle, to localize somewhere and
in a unique place if its position is measured. If we admit that the system is allowed to localize
“simultaneously” in several places, i.e., in causally separated regions, we are no longer using the
notion of particle and the notion of relativistic spatial localization observable is not appropriate
for such a system. Whether or not particles exist, in the said sense, is simply a matter for
experimental investigation. In this work, we assume that they do exist. In a forthcoming paper
we shall more strongly assume that elementary particles in Wigner’s sense are of this sort, at
least massive scalar bosons.
This analysis can also be applied to the case where the regions ∆and ∆′ belong to different
rest spaces of different reference frames, provided these regions are causally separated. This is a
consequence of the fact that [DRMo24] (at least some) relativistic spatial localization observables
can be extended to the family of generic smooth spacelike Cauchy surfaces. Furthermore, for
spacelike separated (sufficiently regular) spacelike subsets ∆and ∆′, there is a spacelike Cauchy
surface S which contains both [BeSa06]. We can use S as our notion of rest space in which
to detect the particle, and we can recast the previous argument, this time assuming that theis
generalized rest space S is filled with detectors. The result is even more generalizable by taking ∆
and ∆′ of minimal regularity (Lipschitz achronal Borel sets) and exploiting a maximal achronal
set S which includes both [CDM26]. Finally, even in the situation where we switch on only two
detectors in causally separated regions ∆and ∆′ of a given rest space Σ, the above reasoning
could hold in a weaker version. If the particle is found in ∆, it cannot be found in ∆′ simply in
view of its propensity to localize in a unique place. Unlike the previous case, however, there is

H-C Theorem 2.5 cannot hold in general. Rather, we argue that, from a physical standpoint,
commutativity of effects of localization observables, if any, is not a consequence of NSC or
RCC (through Propositions 3.6,3.7, and 4.1 in particular). Indeed, there exist in the literature

not hold, because in general A(∆) ̸= 0. The reason is that the energy-boundedness requirement
(3) in Theorem 2.5 is not satisfied. Furthermore, upon restriction to the particle space, namely
the positive-energy sector, commutativity ceases to hold, and the observables are therefore
described by POVMs.
Nevertheless, if one insists on maintaining also the energy-boundedness requirement together
with additivity and translation covariance, then commutativity cannot hold simply because of
the Halvorson-Clifton result. However, for a quantum particle, understood as above, this fact
does not seem to pose a problem.

6
Conclusions and outlook
In this work, we studied the issue of commutativity in the representation of relativistic lo-
cality within the description of localization observables for relativistic quantum systems. The
celebrated no-go theorem of Halvorson and Clifton shows that the commutativity of causally
separated localization effects is incompatible with other apparently natural assumptions con-
cerning the notion of spatial localization. This implies, in particular, that localization operators
– elements of suitable families of POVMs associated with the rest spaces of inertial observers
– cannot belong to local algebras of observables in the sense of Araki–Haag–Kastler. Adopting
Busch’s analysis of commutativity, this paper established, in particular, that the commutativity
requirement is not, in this context, a necessary consequence of more elementary principles such
as no-signaling or relativistic consistency. This holds for elementary systems, i.e., particles, as
soon as one assumes that, in highly idealized experiments in which their position is searched
for throughout the whole rest space of a reference frame at a given time, they have the propen-
sity to localize at a unique place. In this sense, in the case under consideration, the failure of
commutativity does not represent a problem for causality.
On the other hand, we proved that, in principle, both commutativity and localization can be
restored when one refers to a more sophisticated, and perhaps more realistic, notion of conditional
localization. Here one tries to define POVMs normalized on a restricted region of space, namely
a laboratory. These POVMs correspond to the conditional probability of finding a particle in
a subregion of the laboratory, given that it has been detected there. A formal expression for
this type of POVM, for states of a particle with small probability of being detected outside the
laboratory, has been suggested in terms of general localization observables associated with full
rest spaces. It is argued that effects belonging to pairs of these conditional POVMs, associated
with causally separated laboratories, may in principle commute. A crucial technical result in
this interpretation is the so-called gentle measurement lemma.
34


## Page 35


It would be interesting to explore possible connections between these ideas and the modular-
theoretic approach developed in [LedO26]. This issue will be investigated elsewhere.
As a final remark, we observe that, in light of the results presented here – and also considering
the failure to describe localization by means of a self-adjoint operator – we may conclude that
the notion of position for a physical object in a fully developed relativistic quantum theory has a
rather peculiar status. In contrast with non-relativistic quantum theory, there does not appear to
be a preferred notion of position observables. Although one expects that, in the non-relativistic
regime, all such notions converge to the naive one – namely, the position operator (as shown, in
particular, for the relativistic localization observables constructed in [Mor23, DRMo24] – this is
no longer the case in the fully relativistic setting. Furthermore, any notion of spatial localization
must necessarily be unsharp. Position in a given reference frame cannot be identified with the
joint spectrum of a triple of self-adjoint operators. From this perspective, unsharp observables
assume a foundational role and should not be regarded merely as approximate or effective
versions of sharp ones. It is plausible that sharp observables, i.e., self-adjoint operators, are
instead to be interpreted primarily as generators of symmetries.
In a forthcoming paper, we shall construct explicit examples of general localization ob-
servables and conditional local position observables using the local AHK approach to QFT. In
particular, we shall see that this type of localization observable can be constructed from the
(smeared) normally ordered stress–energy operator, taking advantage of some known results on
quantum energy inequalities.

= A(∆)A(∆′ + ta) −(A(∆)A(∆′ + ta))† = 0.
The case t < 0 is analogous.
