Abstract
In this work, we benchmark tensor hypercontraction (THC)-accelerated fully self-
consistent GW (scGW) and vertex-corrected self-consistent GW (scGWΓ) methods
for predicting molecular first ionization potentials (IPs). The vertex function, Γ, is
inserted into the self-energy in a fully self-consistent manner, and representative scGW
and scGWΓ variants are assessed across the G0W0Γ29 and GW100 data sets.

Introduction
Many-body perturbation theory (MBPT) can be formulated in terms of Green’s functions,
which are particularly well-suited for studying excitation processes since the information
about the spectrum is intrinsically encoded in the Green’s function itself.1,2 To evaluate a
computationally tractable and physically justified one-body Green’s function using a dia-
grammatic framework, Hedin in Ref. 3 proposed a set of self-consistent equations that detail
the relationship among the Green’s function G, self-energy Σ, polarization propagator P,
screened Coulomb interaction W, and vertex function Γ.
However, even though GW is one of the simplest diagrammatic approximations, the exact
execution of this scheme, involving both the full self-consistency and vertex evaluation, has
remained out of reach for realistic systems for many years.Even for a model system such as
an electron gas, the exact vertex containing GW scheme was only executed in4 by Kutepov.
The difficulty with fully self-consistent execution, stemmed from the enormously chal-
lenging task of performing self-consistency using Green’s function defined on a real axis grid,
where a growing number of poles appear during each iteration.5,6 Thus, most commonly,
on real-axis scGW was approximated by the G0W0 scheme,7,8 which is the first iteration
of the fully self-consistent GW (scGW) method. Other quasi-self-consistent schemes such
as quasi-particle GW 9–11 or eigenvalue GW (evGW)12,13 etc. also exist. Commonly, G0W0
is performed using a mean-field reference orbitals resulting in the G0W0@reference scheme,
whose accuracy is severely reference dependent, affecting energy differences14–16 and ioniza-

tion potentials.17,18 Several of these partially self-consistent schemes have been systemati-
cally analyzed and compared in Refs. 6 and 19. Non-self-consistency might break theoretical
properties preserved by fully self-consistent methods. These violations can deteriorate nu-
merical estimates in practical calculations. For example, the lack of full self-consistency (the
violation of Kadamoff-Baym ”condition A”20) results in an ambiguous determination of the
Green’s function, often differing between implementations.
On the imaginary axis, the Green’s functions are smooth; thus, the growing number of
poles is of no concern. However, due to underdeveloped frequency grids, greater compu-
tational cost caused by the lack of separation into occupied and virtual orbitals, and the
necessity of analytic continuation to prepare the spectra, the scGW calculations were not
common for realistic molecules and solids.6,21,22
The development of modern frequency grids,,23–27 tensor decomposition techniques,28–33
and algorithmic developments of modern convergence techniques34,35 enabled relatively rou-
tine execution of the fully self-consistent GW method that approximated the vertex Γ as
a delta function δ. The GW self-consistency (without inclusion of vertex) results in en-
ergy conservation and gives a unique ability to evaluate total energy and energy differences,
which has already led to accurate determination of magnetic interactions in molecules36,37
and solids,38 N´eel temperatures,39 intermolecular interactions,40 and even charge-transfer
physics in semimetals.35
In Hedin’s hierarchy, the GW method plus vertex (GWΓ) is formulated through approx-
imations of the vertex function Γ. Γ introduces corrections to both the self-energy Σ and
the polarization Π. In the Hedin’s original scheme, such corrections are done using the bold
diagrams,1 meaning that the Dyson equation can be solved fully self-consistently. Numer-
ical results of theories of this nature, e.g. self-consistent G3W2, have been reported only
a few times.41–44 Due to the expensive computational cost, in practice, vertex corrections
have almost always been executed in non-self-consistent or stochastic ways.45–47 However,

In order to elucidate the performance of vertex correction in a controlled manner, our
aim is to eliminate the starting point dependence by implementing both GW and GWΓ self-
consistently. We tested the GW and GWΓ methods and compared their ability in simulating
the single-particle excitation processes. The first ionization potentials (IP) of small molecules
have been studied with reliable methods before, providing rich benchmark data sets.17,18,49
In our previous work, we found that the starting point dependence of G0W0Γ outweighs the
benefit of cutting costs.55 The GW generally provides accurate results that are comparable
to G0W0Γ, which relies on suitable Hartree-Fock (HF)/ Density Functional Theory (DFT)
calculations as starting points. When combined with a non-optimal mean-field reference, the
vertex correction can sometimes worsen the IP predictions for small molecules. A similar
effect has also been observed in past reports.53,54 To assess the genuine impact of the vertex
term in reducing the error introduced by the GW approximation, we evaluated the vertex
correction while maintaining full self-consistency.
In this work, we use a variety of fully self-consistent vertex correction schemes built
upon scGW, for which we use the term scGWΓ as a short-hand. We focus on four diagram-
matic approximations to the vertex contribution in self-energy: (i) the second-order exchange
(SOX);56 (ii) the second-order screened exchange (SOSEX);57,58 (iii) complete second-order
screened exchange (2SOSEX);59 (iv) the second order W in self-energy (G3W2).42,53 For
SOSEX and 2SOSEX, we further distinguish static and dynamic variants to assess the role

A central technical challenge is that fully self-consistent GWΓ becomes computationally
demanding when implemented in a straightforward orbital representation. The repeated
evaluation of screened interactions and the contractions required by the exchange-like di-
agrams lead to steep prefactors and substantial memory demands, which sharply restrict
tractable system size. A common approach to alleviating this problem is to compress the
electron repulsion integrals (ERIs) by employing density fitting (DF)61,62 or resolution-of-
identity (RI)63,64 techniques, as well as related Cholesky decomposition methods,65–70 all of
which transform the four-index ERIs into three-index quantities. However, while DF/RI is
highly effective for Coulomb-direct contractions, it does not change the effective four-index
structure that dominates exchange-like contributions, and the practical cost of vertex still
remains prohibitive in self-consistent settings.
To achieve computational speedup for exchange-like self-energies, we employ least-squares
tensor hypercontraction (THC) factorizations71–74 in which the collocation matrices are
obtained via interpolative separable density fitting (ISDF).28,74–77 We build on a finite-
temperature THC-scGW framework that serves as a common, efficient backbone for the
scGWΓ variants studied in this work. These variants include exchange-like diagrams that
are prohibitively expensive without THC factorizations. We present this article as follows. In
the Theory section, we introduce the THC-based finite-temperature scGW method; we then
develop multiple flavors of scGWΓ on that basis. In the Results and Discussion sections,
we describe the general protocol employed to compute molecular IPs for both scGW and
subsequent scGWΓ implementations. In these sections, we also present the data revealing
the major findings of this work. We summarize our comparison and share outlooks in the
Conclusions section.

Computational details
We used PySCF93–95 (version 2.6.2, commit hash b6bfced) to obtain converged unrestricted
Hartree–Fock (UHF) reference solutions, which served as starting points for all subsequent
THC-scGW and THC-scGWΓ calculations. Within the same framework, we generated all
one- and two-electron integrals and constructed dense Becke integration grids96 used to ini-
tialize the THC decomposition and the subsequent pruning. To ensure that cross-study com-
parisons are free of basis-set bias, we matched our orbital basis choices to those of the original
studies that reported the corresponding datasets: cc-pVQZ97–99 was used for the 29-molecule
(referred to as G0W0Γ29 in this work) set of Maggio et al.,,18 and def2-TZVPP100–104 was
used for the GW100 set.17 The choices of basis sets are consistent with previous works.55 DF
auxiliary bases for the static part of the self-energy Σ∞[G] were generated via the augmented
even-tempered-basis (ETB) procedure implemented in PySCF with βETB = 2.0, while THC
interpolating vectors for the dynamic part, ˜Σ[G](iωn), were constructed from dense Becke
integration grids following the approach of Ref.72 with the number of the THC interpolation
points per AO, αIpts of 10.
The THC decomposition is implemented using a pre-open-source, legacy version of the
CoQu´ı (Correlated Quantum ´ınterface) code,105 which performs the least-squares fitting of
DF three-index tensors to the separable ISDF/LS-THC form. All THC-scGW and THC-
scGWΓ calculations were conducted with the inverse temperature of β = 1000 a.u.−1 (corre-
sponding to about 316 K). For imaginary-time/frequency resolution we used high-precision
intermediate-representation (IR) grids from the sparse-ir package,106 characterized by the

To accelerate and stabilize the convergence of the Green’s function calculations, we com-

ϵ acting as an effective dielectric constant that weakens the bare interaction.
In this language, the SOX correction involves two unscreened interactions and therefore
yields the largest exchange-type subtraction from the GW self-energy, pushing the solution
toward a weaker net dynamical self-energy and correspondingly higher (less negative) total
energies. Replacing one of the bare interactions by W (SOSEX) reduces the magnitude
of this exchange correction, so SOSEX total energies fall between SOX and scGW. When
both interactions are screened (the G3W2-type exchange), the additional term is damped
even further and the energies remain close to scGW. Finally, the 2SOSEX approximation
can be understood as an overcorrection of G3W2, since it can be seen as G3W2 minus
SOX( ˜W, ˜W), thereby further lowering the total energy and placing it below the G3W2-type
exchange term, while remaining above scGW in the systems considered (however, Ref.60 has
an example for which the total energy of 2SOSEX is below scGW). Overall, the monotonic

strength of dynamical correlations through screened (or partially screened) exchange.

Conclusion
In this work, for the IPs of molecules from G0W0Γ29 and GW100 sets, we analyzed the effects
of adding the fully self-consistent vertex corrections to the GW self-energy in comparison with
the fully self-consistent GW alone. The calculations are enabled by the THC decomposition
of the Coulomb repulsion integrals with a moderate rank (αIpts = 10) achieving an excellent
cost–accuracy balance without introducing systematic bias in energies or IPs.
Self-consistent vertex insertions into self-energy produce a well-ordered and physically in-
terpretable hierarchy of total imaginary part of self-energy relative to scGW: where the mag-
nitude of self-energy is ordered from the least negative SOX > SOSEX > G3W2 > 2SOSEX >
scGW. This ordering is naturally explained by the effective-screening picture: SOX(v,v) con-
tains two bare interactions causing the largest exchange subtraction, resulting in the weakest
net dynamical self-energy, and finally yielding the highest (least negative) total energy. SO-
SEX(v,W) replaces one interaction by a screened one, causing reduced magnitude of exchange
and moving both self-energies and total energies closer towards scGW. G3W2(W,W) screens
both interactions, yielding smallest exchange correction resulting in self-energies that are
very close to scGW. 2SOSEX can overcorrect the amount of correlated exchange and may
approach or even cross scGW depending on screening strength. The systematic monotonic
trends across chemically different systems demonstrate that the dielectric-screening interpre-
tation is robust and transferable. We also observed consistent differences between static and
dynamic SOSEX (and 2SOSEX) that arise exclusively due to the treatment of the screened
interaction ˜W. Since || ˜W(iΩ)|| is typically maximal at Ω= 0, the dynamical screened-

in the overall smaller magnitude of self-energy ˜ΣSOSEX = ˜ΣSOX(v,W) = ˜ΣSOX(v,v) + ˜ΣSOX(v, ˜

Dynamic variants yield systematically lower (more negative) TrIm[Σ(iω)] self-energies. The
total energies depend on frequency integrals involving phases and cancellations between G
and Σ so energetic trends cannot be inferred from the norms of self-energies alone. However,
in many cases they trace the behavior of the self-energy.
From all the numerical observations done in this work, we conclude that all vertex cor-
rections, performed here, predominantly act as an approximately frequency-uniform vertical
renormalization of TrΣ(iω) rather than strongly reshaping its frequency dependence.
Moreover, adding the vertex corrections usually results in convergence difficulties for self-
consistent loops that are consistent with difficulties in converging methods such as Green’s
function second order (GF2).34 Such convergence difficulties can be mitigated with heating-
cooling methods34 or homotopy continuation.35 Additional details are provided in the Sup-
porting Information.
We observed the following trends in the accuracy of first IPs when comparing them
against experiments. For the G0W0Γ29 set, Hartree–Fock Koopmans values show signif-
icantly larger errors, confirming that dynamical screening is essential.
Both G0W0@HF

Intermediate Representation Grid choice
We selected the Intermediate Representation (IR) grid size Λ so that the grid spans
the full range of molecular-orbital energies. More specifically, we chose a positive integer n
such that Λ = 10n > β∆E, where β is the inverse temperature and ∆E = EMO
Max −EMO
Min is
evaluated at the Hartree–Fock level.
For a few cases in GW100 subset, THC-scGWΓ calculations exhibited large and progres-
sively increasing IR-grid leakage. For these systems, we increased Λ manually and repeated
the calculation. This behavior likely indicates that the original grid was not sufficiently
dense for those molecules.
For THC-scGW calculations, CDIIS was activated from iteration 5 with a maximum sub-
space size of 10, and a simple damping factor of 0.5 was used. For THC-scGWΓ calculations,
CDIIS was activated from iteration 10 with a maximum subspace size of 12, and a smaller
damping factor of 0.2 was used.
