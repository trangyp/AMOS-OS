Role of volatility mixing in wealth condensation transition
Jaeseok Hur,1, ∗Meesoon Ha,2, † and Hawoong Jeong1, 3, ‡

We study the role of heterogeneous volatility in a networked wealth dynamics model and its impact
on the wealth condensation transition. Extending the Bouchaud–M´ezard framework, we introduce
binary volatility in networks and investigate how its configuration affects the effective power-law
tail exponent of the wealth distribution. Using a stochastic block model, we control the mixing
between volatility groups and show that the effective exponent is governed not only by the global
parameter Λ = 2J/β2 but also by the volatility configuration in the network. We find that local
interactions between nodes with different volatility induce a neutralization of group-wise exponents,
which lowers the aggregate tail exponent and can drive a condensation transition across γc = 2.
Our results identify volatility mixing as another control mechanism for wealth condensation and
highlight the importance of noise heterogeneity in nonequilibrium systems on networks.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Heavy-tailed wealth distributions are a hallmark of so-
cioeconomic inequality, and the tail exponent γ plays
a central role in determining the degree of concentra-
tion. In the context of wealth dynamics, Bouchaud and
M´ezard [1] (BM) established a paradigmatic framework
showing that the stationary wealth distribution exhibits
a power-law tail whose exponent γ depends only on the
ratio between interaction strength and squared volatility,
Λ ≡2J/β2 (see Fig. 1), both in the mean-field (MF) case
and Erd˝os–R´enyi (ER) networks [2]. This framework sug-
gests that the condensation transition, signaled by γ ≤2,
is governed by a single control parameter.
However, real systems are rarely homogeneous: indi-
viduals may differ not only in growth rates but also in
volatility. Although heterogeneity in growth has been
studied [3, 4], the effect of heterogeneous volatility re-
mains largely unexplored. This is important because
volatility represents intrinsic fluctuations in wealth and
may interact with the network structure in ways that
cannot be reduced to a single effective parameter.
In this paper, we study how volatility configuration
affects wealth distribution and condensation in the BM
model. Using a stochastic block model (SBM), we tune
the mixing between high- and low-volatility groups while
keeping degree statistics nearly unchanged. This allows
us to isolate the volatility configuration effect itself.
We show that the effective tail exponent is determined
not only by Λ but also by how volatility is allocated on
the network. In particular, mixing between nodes with
different volatility induces a neutralization of group-wise
effective tail exponents, lowers the aggregate effective tail
exponent, and can drive the system across the conden-
sation threshold γc = 2. These results identify volatility

III.
RESULTS
For the SBM, the wealth distribution exhibits nearly
the same effective tail exponent ˆγ as that of the ER net-
work in the homogeneous limit ∆β →0, since the de-
gree distributions of the two networks are nearly iden-
tical. However, for ∆β ̸= 0, the effective tail exponents
are highly dependent on q. At q = 0, two blocks are
completely separated ER networks. Consequently, each
group has an effective tail exponent ˆγ± corresponding to
Λ1 = 2J/β2
+ and Λ2 = 2J/β2
−, as in the homogeneous
BM model [see Fig. 2(a)]. However, for q > 0, local in-
teractions between nodes with different volatility modify
γ±. Specifically, the difference between the two effective
tail exponents decreases, producing a neutralization ef-
fect.
This trend is consistent with the form of the interac-
tion term in Eq. (2), which redistributes wealth in pro-
portion to the difference in normalized wealth between
connected nodes. As q increases, the nodes with different
volatility interact more frequently, and the gap between
the group-wise distributions and the effective tail expo-
nent is reduced. Since this neutralization does not occur
in the MF model, it originates from local interactions on
the network.
In the homogeneous BM model, the effective tail ex-
ponent is determined solely by the control parameter Λ.
In the heterogeneous BM model, by contrast, the con-
figurational property of volatility acts as an additional
control dimension that determines the effective tail ex-
ponent. To demonstrate this, we fix Λ1 = 2J/β2
+ and
construct a phase diagram in the (q, Λ2) plane, where
Λ2 = 2J/β2
−. The effective aggregate tail exponent ˆγ
extracted from the overall distribution ρ(c) varies not
only with Λ2 but also with q. Moreover, for appropri-
ate values of (q, Λ1, Λ2), the system undergoes a wealth-
condensation transition across γc = 2, giving rise to a
phase boundary in the (q, Λ2) plane. As shown in Fig. 3,
increasing q lowers the effective tail exponent ˆγ, thus en-
hancing the overall inequality of the system.
The neutralization of ˆγ± does not affect the total distri-
bution symmetrically. In all parameter regimes we tested,
the group-wise distributions ρ±(c) show that the tail pop-
ulation is larger in the low-volatility group than in the
high-volatility group. Consequently, the tail of the aggre-
gate distribution ρ(c) is governed primarily by the tail
behavior of the β−group [see Fig. 3]. This is also re-
flected in the phase diagrams. Because the low-volatility
group contributes much more strongly to the tail, the
wealth shares of the two groups also differ substantially
[see Fig. A3]. This gap in wealth share decreases as q
increases, indicating that inter-block connections hinder
the net transfer of wealth from the high-volatility group
to the low-volatility group.
IV.
CONCLUSION
We have investigated the role of heterogeneous volatil-
ity in a networked Bouchaud–M´ezard model and shown
that the configuration of volatility fundamentally alters
the statistics of wealth distributions. In contrast to the


## Page 4


4
homogeneous case, where the tail exponent is uniquely
determined by the control parameter Λ, we find that the
effective exponent ˆγ depends additionally on the mixing
structure of volatility, controlled here by the inter-block
connection probability q. This configurational effect is
absent in the MF limit and emerges only when heteroge-
neous volatility interacts locally on a sparse network.
A key mechanism underlying this behavior is the neu-
tralization of the group-wise tail exponents through lo-
cal exchange between nodes with different volatility. As
volatility mixing increases, the disparity between subpop-
ulations is reduced at the group level, yet the aggregate
tail becomes heavier because the overall effective expo-
nent decreases. In this way, wealth condensation can be
induced not only by tuning a global parameter such as
Λ but also by changing the configurational correlation of
noise intensity. The phase diagram on the (q, Λ2) plane
and the complementary robustness checks in Appendix C
support this interpretation.
Our findings suggest a broader perspective on nonequi-
librium systems with heterogeneous noise. Although ear-
lier examples such as active matter [8, 9] and the random-
field Ising model [10, 11] emphasize correlations in the
noise itself, the present results show that correlations in
noise amplitude (i.e., correlated volatility) can also con-
trol macroscopic phase behavior even when the driving
noises remain mutually uncorrelated. In the context of
wealth dynamics, this identifies volatility configuration
as an additional control dimension for inequality forma-
tion and condensation.

Appendix C: Complementary numerical simulations
1.
Power-law test and size dependence
In this appendix, we examine the statistical test for
power-law tails and their robustness across parameter
regimes. Bouchaud and M´ezard [1] reported that a ran-
dom network with a mean degree ⟨k⟩= 4 exhibits a
power-law tail. Garlaschelli [13] discussed the relevance
of log-normal behavior in sparsely connected clusters,
whereas Souma et al. [14] showed that, even at sparse and
fixed mean degree, the shortcut effect makes small-world
networks exhibit power-law tails. Motivated by these ear-
lier observations, and going beyond visual inspection of
double-logarithmic plots of normalized wealth distribu-


## Page 6


6
tions, we performed statistical comparisons based on the
log-likelihood ratio test [12, 15].
For the GCC of an ER network with a mean degree
⟨k⟩= 4, whether the tail sector is better described by
a power-law or by a truncated log-normal distribution
depends both on the control parameter Λ and the sys-
tem size N. Numerically, we find that for N = 104 the
power-law is better for Λ ≥1, whereas for N = 8 × 104
it is better already for Λ ≥0.588. Thus, as N increases,
the parameter region in which the power-law fit is pre-
ferred becomes wider. We check that these numerical
consequences are also observed in the case of the HBM
model simulation on the SBM. On the other hand, for
smaller Λ, the empirical tail is better fitted by a truncated
log-normal form at finite N. However, the corresponding
MLE estimates (ˆµ, ˆσ) exhibit a clear dependence on the
system size, while the effective tail exponents ˆγ remain
well collapsed and show only a weak dependence on size;
see Fig. A2 for details.
These observations suggest that in the small-Λ regime,
the truncated log-normal fit may reflect finite-size round-
ing of the tail sector rather than the asymptotic form
itself. In particular, as N increases, the tail extends to
larger c and the fitted log-normal tail becomes progres-
sively flatter, while the effective exponent ˆγ remains
approximately stable. This trend is consistent with a
crossover to the power-law scaling in the thermodynamic
limit of N →∞. We therefore interpret ˆγ as an effective
exponent against scaling, while avoiding the claim that
finite-N samples are uniquely characterized by a pure
power-law over the entire fitted range. Within this in-
terpretation, the cases with ˆγ ≤2 are consistent with
condensation in the thermodynamic limit.
