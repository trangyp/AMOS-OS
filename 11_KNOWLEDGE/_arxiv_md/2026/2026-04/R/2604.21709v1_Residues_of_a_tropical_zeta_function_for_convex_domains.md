1. Introduction
1.1. Broader view. Gauss’ circle problem [16] is an archetypal problem at
the interface of analytic number theory and planar geometry. Although it
has been studied intensively for more than two centuries, it remains open,
despite substantial progress obtained by a wide range of methods and ideas
[3]. One aim of the present article is to introduce into this circle of ques-
tions another tool, arising from recent developments in what may be called
“non-algebraic tropical geometry” or “tropical optics”. Namely, to a com-
pact convex domain Ωone can canonically associate a function ZΩ(s) of
one complex variable, which we call its tropical zeta function. Just as the
zeros of the Riemann zeta function govern fine asymptotic properties of the
distribution of prime numbers [48, 43], we expect the poles of the tropical
zeta function to play a central role in obtaining sharp bounds on the error
term in lattice-point counting for dilates of the domain.
Indeed, for a planar convex domain, the main term in lattice-point count-
ing is quadratic and equals the area of the domain, whereas the general
bound for the error term is linear in the dilation parameter R. The co-
efficient of this linear term is determined by the straight segments of the
boundary, and therefore, in the strictly convex case, one can do better.
Assuming that the boundary is sufficiently smooth and has everywhere non-
vanishing curvature, van der Corput proved [7] about a century ago that the
error term is at most of order R2/3, see also [25, 21]. In terms of tropical zeta
functions, the first bound corresponds to the existence of a rightmost pole
at s = 1 for domains whose boundary contains rational straight segments,
with residue equal to the lattice perimeter. For domains with sufficiently
smooth boundary, the next pole, moving from right to left, appears at s = 2
3,
and the corresponding residue is universally proportional to the equiaffine
arc length of the boundary. This is the main result of the paper and the
technically most difficult part of the argument.
The definition of the tropical zeta function considered in this paper origi-
nates in the mathematical study of self-organized criticality. More precisely,
finite-point perturbations of the maximal stable state in the Abelian sandpile
model give rise to tropical analytic curves and to the tropical power series
defining them [32, 30, 29, 33]. One such series, corresponding to the action
of a point perturbation at the distinguished central point, is the tropical
distance function of a convex domain, while the associated tropical analytic
curve is the so-called tropical caustic [44, 51]. The convex domain itself
appears as the domain of convergence of this tropical series, which defines a
positive concave piecewise linear function on its interior. Integrating its s-th
power over the domain then produces a function of the complex variable s,
which we call the tropical zeta function of the domain. The central conjec-
ture for this class of functions is that they admit meromorphic continuation
to the entire complex plane.
One case in which the conjecture holds almost trivially is that of poly-
gons whose sides have rational slopes. Such polygons are naturally viewed as
moment polytopes of compact symplectic toric surfaces. From the algebro-
geometric point of view, these surfaces may be obtained from a minimal
model by a sequence of blow-ups at torus-fixed points. From the symplectic

Appendix B. Complex, symplectic and tropical geometries
Since the majority of the material in this article has a distinctly analytic
flavor, we decided to include in this appendix a very brief explanation of
geometric terminology that we have casually utilized in several places, espe-
cially in the broader introductory Section 1.1. Thus, subsections B.1 and B.2
cover basics on topological and symplectic aspects of toric surfaces, where,
although complex singularity theory in dimension two is not complicated,
we have chosen to focus on the non-singular case for the sake of simplic-
ity. B.3 provides a minimalistic overview of planar tropical wave fronts and
their caustics, with an emphasis on how their structure theory is expressed
in toric language. The content of B.4 is original, presenting an application
of the main result for the question of finding the asymptotic of the lattice
perimeter of the tropical wave front when time tends to zero.
B.1. Topology of complex toric surfaces. Compact smooth toric sur-
faces provide a tame and very well understood class of closed complex man-
ifolds7. The term “toric” comes from their defining property of being equi-
variant compactifications of the complex algebraic torus (C∗)2, where C∗
denotes the multiplicative group of non-zero complex numbers. A toric sur-
face is characterized by its fan represented by a collection of rational slope
rays (one usually constructs a smooth toric surface by means of explicit
gluings of affine plane charts dictated by the structure of the fan), which,
in the compact case, is complete, i.e. every point on the plane is a sum of
some vectors belonging to the rays, and, in the non-singular case, is uni-
modular, i.e. the primitive vectors spanning two consecutive rays form the
basis of the square lattice. Some fundamental examples of complete uni-
modular fans (represented by sets of primitive vectors generating the rays)
and corresponding surfaces are:
Example 1. a) {(−1, 0), (0, −1), (1, 1)} – projective plane CP 2;
b) {(−1, 0), (0, −1), (0, 1), (1, d)}, – CP 1-bundles (non-trial for d ̸= 0)
over CP 1, also known as Hirzebruch surfaces.
Let X denote a compact smooth toric surface. Then, then (C∗)2 is a dense
open subset of X, and its complement is a union of the so-called boundary
divisors {Dj}, each of which is a smooth embedding of the Riemann sphere
CP 1 to X. Moreover, the boundary divisors are in one-to-one correspondence
with the rays in the fan of X. In fact, the fan can be reconstructed as follows.
Take a non-zero real vector (a, b) and consider a path on (C∗)2 parametrized
as t ∈(1, +∞) 7→(ta, tb). Then, this path has a unique limit point in X for
t →+∞and this point belongs to a unique boundary divisor if and only if
(a, b) belongs to a ray of the fan corresponding to the divisor. More generally,
if (a, b) belongs to a connected component of complement to the union of
all rays in the fan bounded by two rays, the limit point of the path will be
the unique intersection point of the two boundary divisors corresponding to
the two rays.

Appendix D. Zeta functions for convex domains: a survey
Several classical zeta functions may be attached to a convex domain,
depending on which geometric profile one chooses to encode analytically.
The main examples relevant here are:
(1) gauge or lattice zeta functions, attached to lattice-point counting in
dilates of the body;
(2) distance and tube zeta functions, attached to the small-scale growth
of Euclidean neighborhoods;
(3) spectral zeta functions, attached to eigenvalue asymptotics of natural
operators on the domain or on its boundary.

with the zeta function studied in the main text.

To summarize, the tropical zeta function shares with the classical con-
structions the general Dirichlet–Mellin principle that poles encode asymp-
totic geometry, but it differs from them in three essential respects:
(1) its defining geometric input is the tropical distance function built
from primitive lattice support directions;
(2) in dimension 2, its singularities are governed by an exact boundary
Dirichlet series over Farey neighbors;
(3) for smooth convex domains with everywhere non-vanishing curva-
ture, its first nontrivial pole detects equiaffine arc length rather than
Euclidean boundary measure, volume, or spectral Weyl data.
This combination of lattice symmetry, boundary reduction, and affine
residue appears to be specific to the tropical zeta function introduced in
this paper.
The tropical zeta function thus places the geometry of convex domains
into a single analytic framework in which discrete lattice data and smooth
affine geometry appear as different singular regimes of the same object.
In the polygonal case, the first pole detects the lattice-visible boundary
through the tropical perimeter; in the smooth strictly convex case, the lead-
ing nontrivial residue is the equiaffine perimeter. The mechanism behind
this transition is the exact reduction of the interior Mellin integral to a
boundary Dirichlet series, whose arithmetic structure is governed by Farey
neighbors and whose local model is the primitive Mordell–Tornheim, equiv-
alently Witten SU(3), zeta function.
From this perspective, the pole at

of a tropical-lattice analytic theory of convexity. The broader continuation
problem, the higher-dimensional case, and the relation to lattice-point error
terms remain open, but the results obtained here already show that tropical
optics provides a natural zeta-theoretic passage from arithmetic boundary
data to equiaffine geometry.
