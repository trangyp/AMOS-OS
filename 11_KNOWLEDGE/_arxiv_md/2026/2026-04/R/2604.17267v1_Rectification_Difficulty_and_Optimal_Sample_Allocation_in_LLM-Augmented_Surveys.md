Large Language Models can generate synthetic survey responses at low cost, but their accuracy varies unpre-
dictably across questions. We study the design problem of allocating a fixed budget of human respondents
across estimation tasks when cheap LLM predictions are available for every task. Our framework combines
three components. First, building on Prediction-Powered Inference, we characterize a question-specific rec-
tification difficulty that governs how quickly the estimator’s variance decreases with human sample size.
Second, we derive a closed-form optimal allocation rule that directs more human labels to tasks where the
LLM is least reliable. Third, since rectification difficulty depends on unobserved human responses for new
surveys, we propose a meta-learning approach, trained on historical data, that predicts it for entirely new
tasks without pilot data. The framework extends to general M-estimation, covering regression coefficients
and multinomial logit partworths for conjoint analysis. We validate the framework on two datasets spanning
different domains, question types, and LLMs, showing that our approach captures 61–79% of the theoret-
ically attainable efficiency gains, achieving 11.4% and 10.5% MSE reductions without requiring any pilot
human data for the target survey.
Key words : Market Research, Survey Design, Large Language Models, Prediction-Powered Inference

1.
Introduction
Surveys play an important role in market research, from brand tracking and conjoint analysis to
willingness-to-pay estimation. However, fielding them is increasingly expensive. Large Language
Models (LLMs) offer a promising alternative: trained on massive text corpora, they can generate
plausible survey responses at negligible marginal cost, replicating aggregate human patterns across
a range of marketing and social-science tasks (e.g., Brand et al. 2023, Argyle et al. 2023, Wang
et al. 2024, Ye et al. 2025).
However, LLM responses are imperfect. Their accuracy varies sharply across topics, populations,
and question formats, with well-documented biases (Motoki et al. 2024, Peng et al. 2025). LLMs
are informative but biased, and the degree of informativeness varies across questions in ways
that are difficult to anticipate (Toubia et al. 2025). This observation motivates statistical tools

All of the above methods (PPI, PPI++, transfer learning, and the “mixed subjects design”
of Broska et al. (2025)) assume that human responses are already available for the question of
interest. In practice, however, researchers face the design problem before any human data have
been collected. Consider a market researcher planning a multi-question survey, or a conjoint study
with many choice sets: the LLM can cheaply generate predictions for every task, but the researcher
must decide how many human respondents to recruit for each task before the study goes to the
field. Our design decision is the allocation of a fixed human-label budget across estimation tasks,
not the content or phrasing of the tasks themselves.
This design problem has two distinguishing features. First, LLM reliability varies across ques-
tions, so uniform allocation is generally suboptimal. Second, the problem arises in a cold-start
setting: the quantities needed to determine the optimal allocation depend on human responses
that have not yet been collected. Existing approaches typically assume pilot data or previously
collected human responses from which to estimate these quantities. We propose a meta-learning
solution that learns from historical survey data which types of questions are hard for LLMs, and
transfers that knowledge to determine allocation for entirely new surveys, even before any pilot
data are collected.
An efficient survey design framework. We propose a framework with three components: (i) a
question-specific measure of LLM reliability called rectification difficulty, (ii) an optimal allocation
rule that directs human labels where they are most needed, and (iii) a meta-learning approach that
predicts rectification difficulty for new questions without pilot data. We describe each in turn.
We build on the PPI++ framework (Angelopoulos et al. 2023a,b), which uses a small human
sample to correct (or rectify) the LLM’s systematic errors. PPI++ introduces a question-specific
tuning parameter that automatically adapts the degree of LLM contribution, guaranteeing that
incorporating LLM data never harms precision relative to the human-only sample mean. When
synthetic LLM data are abundant, the asymptotic variance of the PPI++ estimator takes the
form Aq{nq, where nq is the human sample size and Aq, the rectification difficulty, measures how
hard it is to correct the LLM using human data for question q (formally defined in Section 4). This
variance characterization extends to general M-estimators (e.g., regression coefficients, multinomial
logit partworths) via a sandwich-type covariance formula (Section 5.3).

from historical data which questions are hard for LLMs and transferring that knowledge to unseen
survey questions.
Empirical validation. We validate the framework on two datasets. The first is the Twin-2K-500
digital-twin dataset (Toubia et al. 2025), a large-scale behavioral-economics benchmark in which
over 2,000 respondents each answer 500+ questions, each paired with an LLM-generated digital
twin. The fully observed population enables controlled evaluation of any allocation rule against
the infeasible best (allocation with known true Aq). The second is the CCES 2024 political survey
(Schaffner et al. 2025), covering 133 subquestions on economic perceptions, vote choice, and foreign
policy, with a different LLM as surrogate, demonstrating that the framework generalizes across
domains, question types, and LLMs.
Our analysis yields three main findings. First, the dominant source of efficiency gains is the
reallocation of human labels, not the PPI++ estimator itself: roughly three-quarters of the total
improvement comes from directing labels toward high-difficulty questions rather than spreading
them uniformly. Second, even without pilot data, our meta-learning approach captures 61–79%
of the oracle allocation gains, achieving 11.4% MSE reduction in Twin-2K-500 and 10.5% in the

Related Literature
Our work draws on and contributes to three streams of research: efficient experimental and survey
design in marketing, the use of LLMs as synthetic survey respondents, and Prediction-Powered
Inference for combining human and machine-generated data.
Efficient experimental and survey design. Marketing researchers have long studied how to allo-
cate scarce respondent effort to maximize inferential precision. In conjoint analysis, a foundational
line of work develops adaptive methods that select which product profiles or choice sets to present
to each respondent, tailoring question content in real time to maximize information about con-
sumer preferences (Toubia et al. 2003, 2004). Dzyabura and Hauser (2011) extend this idea using
machine learning to adaptively select the most informative questions for identifying consideration
heuristics. A parallel literature optimizes the static design of choice experiments (which profiles
to include and how to combine them) using prior information about respondent preferences to
improve efficiency (Huber and Zwerina 1996, S´andor and Wedel 2001). Toubia and Hauser (2007)
unify these ideas under the principle of “managerially efficient design”: experimental resources
should be directed toward improving the estimates that matter most for the decision at hand.
A distinct but related tradition studies split questionnaire and matrix sampling designs, where a
long survey is divided into modules administered to different subsets of respondents (Raghunathan
and Grizzle 1995, Adig¨uzel and Wedel 2008). The design challenge is to choose the assignment
of modules to respondent groups to minimize information loss, given that no single respondent
completes the entire instrument.
Our framework shares the design philosophy of this literature: allocate scarce respondent effort
where it yields the greatest improvement in statistical efficiency. The specific resource allocation
we study (how many respondents to recruit per question, given a fixed total budget) is a natural
complement to the question-content and module-assignment decisions above. Through our M-
estimation extension (Section 5.3), the framework applies to the same estimands that motivate these

designs, including multinomial logit partworths for conjoint analysis, linear regression coefficients
for demand estimation, and categorical response proportions. What makes non-uniform allocation
both feasible and valuable in our setting is the availability of cheap LLM predictions: they provide a

LLMs as synthetic survey respondents. A growing literature documents the ability of LLMs to
replicate aggregate human survey patterns across willingness-to-pay estimation, brand perception,
political opinion simulation, choice modeling, and other tasks (e.g., Brand et al. 2023, Li et al.
2024, Argyle et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2024, Ye et al. 2025, Ziems et al. 2024). At the same time,
LLM responses exhibit well-documented limitations, including political slant, ordering effects, and
insufficient within-population heterogeneity (Motoki et al. 2024, Brucks and Toubia 2023, Peng
et al. 2025). Crucially, the degree to which LLMs are informative varies across questions in ways
that are difficult to anticipate (Toubia et al. 2025, Peng et al. 2025). Our framework takes this
heterogeneity as the starting point for survey design: rather than asking whether LLMs can replace
human respondents, we ask how to optimally combine them.
Prediction-Powered Inference and hybrid human–LLM designs. Prediction-Powered Inference
(PPI; Angelopoulos et al. 2023a) and its PPI++ extension (Angelopoulos et al. 2023b) provide
a principled way to combine cheap machine predictions with gold-standard human labels, using
a small labeled sample to correct prediction bias while leveraging a large synthetic pool for vari-
ance reduction. Mozer (2026) shows that PPI estimators are algebraically equivalent to classical
survey sampling estimators (the difference estimator and the generalized regression estimator),
connecting PPI to the well-developed theory of optimal allocation in stratified sampling (Neyman
1934). PPI-style methods are increasingly applied outside survey research: Ji et al. (2025) revisit
the surrogate-outcome problem through the lens of modern AI predictions, and Vafa et al. (2025)
apply PPI to estimate wage disparities.
Several concurrent studies address hybrid human–LLM survey design. Broska et al. (2025) pro-
pose a mixed-subjects framework and use power analysis to determine the optimal ratio of human
to LLM respondents for a single estimand. Yin and Xin (2026) and Huang et al. (2025) study
how many synthetic LLM responses are needed to achieve a target precision, focusing on a sin-
gle question and assuming that some human data are already available. Our framework addresses
the across-question allocation decision—how to distribute a fixed human-label budget among het-
erogeneous questions—in a design phase where no human responses have yet been collected, and
introduces a meta-learning layer that predicts rectification difficulty from question text to enable
zero-shot allocation.

Problem Setup and Overview of Solution Concept
We study a hybrid survey-design problem in which LLM predictions are cheap to generate, while
human responses are costly. The researcher has two sources of information: (i) a historical corpus
H of previously fielded questions for which paired human and LLM responses are available, and
(ii) a new survey T whose questions have not yet been fielded to humans. The design problem is to
decide how many human responses to collect for each question in T , subject to a budget constraint,
while using LLM predictions as an auxiliary source of information.

3.3.
Overview of solution concept
A useful starting benchmark is the case in which LLM predictions are perfectly aligned with human
responses. In that case, the researcher could simply use the LLM predictions in place of collecting
new human data, and the survey-design problem would largely disappear. In practice, however,
a growing literature shows that while LLM predictions are often informative, they are not fully
accurate: they can be biased, and their reliability varies substantially across questions (Peng et al.
2025). Thus, the relevant problem is not whether to use LLMs or humans, but how to combine
cheap LLM predictions with scarce and costly human responses in a way that improves efficiency
while preserving valid inference.

Challenges. The researcher must decide how to allocate the human-label budget across the ques-
tions in the new survey T before any new human responses have been collected. This creates a
cold-start problem. At this point, LLM predictions are available, but the researcher does not yet
know which questions the LLM will handle well and which questions will require more human data,
because LLM reliability varies sharply across questions in ways that are hard to anticipate without
human data on the target survey itself.
More specifically, the framework must address three related challenges. First, given a fixed bud-
get, how should the available human effort be allocated across questions to maximize efficiency?
Second, before the survey is fielded, how can the researcher anticipate which questions will be
poorly handled by the LLM? Third, once human responses have been collected, how should biased
but informative LLM predictions be combined with those human responses for estimation?
Key Idea. Our key insight is that patterns in historical paired human–LLM data let us determine
where LLM surrogate responses are likely to be trustworthy on new survey questions, before any
human data on those questions are collected.

Empirical Analysis on Digital-Twins Dataset
We now turn to the empirical evaluation of our framework on the Twin-2K-500 digital-twin dataset
(Toubia et al. 2025). This dataset is especially well-suited to our analysis for four reasons. First, it
provides paired human and LLM responses at the respondent–question level, which is exactly the
structure needed to compute question-specific rectification difficulty. Second, it spans a broad range
of behavioral-economics tasks and response formats, creating the heterogeneity in LLM reliability
that our allocation framework is designed to exploit. Third, the large number of respondents per
question allows us to treat the full observed sample as a ground-truth population for controlled
Monte Carlo evaluation. Fourth, the multi-wave design, including the Wave 4 retest battery, enables
robustness checks on the stability of rectification difficulty across waves.

Where do the gains come from? The total 10.5% MSE reduction of PPI + Opt. (Pred.)
relative to SM + Uniform decomposes into two channels. The estimator effect—switching from
the sample mean to PPI++ while holding allocation fixed—yields a modest 1.5% MSE reduction.
The allocation effect—optimizing allocation while holding the estimator fixed—adds 9.0 percentage
points (from 1.5% to 10.5%), accounting for 86% of the total gain.
This decomposition is particularly instructive when compared to Twin-2K-500, where the esti-
mator effect was 3.6% and the allocation effect was 7.8 percentage points. In the CCES, allocation
accounts for an even larger share of the total gain because the estimator effect is weaker. This con-
trast between the two datasets reinforces the paper’s central message: the value of our framework
lies primarily in optimal allocation, not in PPI++ alone.
How does the allocation change? To illustrate, consider the allocations at B “ 5,000. Under
uniform allocation, each subquestion receives B{Q « 37.6 labels. The oracle allocation assigns as
few as 6.3 labels to the easiest sub-items and up to 105.4 to the hardest. The meta-learned allocation
exhibits the same qualitative pattern, ranging from 21.8 to 80.0 labels, with a Spearman rank
correlation of ρ “ 0.839 against the oracle allocation, confirming that even imperfect meta-learning
produces a reasonable ranking of subquestion difficulty.
The allocation pattern is substantively intuitive: factual and near-consensus items (e.g., voter
registration, employment status) receive the fewest labels because the LLM already provides a
reliable signal, while contested policy attitudes (e.g., student loan forgiveness, foreign policy) receive
the most because the LLM fails on these polarized topics, leaving the full population variance for
human data to resolve. Our framework automatically directs human effort toward the questions
where it is most needed.
Taken together, the CCES validation demonstrates that our framework generalizes across
domains, datasets, and LLMs. The weak LLM signal on political surveys makes the case for optimal
allocation stronger: because PPI++ alone delivers only modest gains, the only path to meaningful
improvement is through smarter allocation of human effort. The greater question heterogeneity in
the CCES is consistent with the square-root allocation logic of Theorem 1 and with the sensitivity
analysis in Section 6.5, both of which indicate larger gains when the dispersion in Aq is larger;
the 17.1% oracle MSE reduction is consistent with this pattern. With improved LLM predictions,
e.g., via domain-specific fine-tuning (Wang et al. 2025), both PPI++ and allocation gains would
increase further.

D.5.
Cross-dataset transferability of meta-learning
We test whether meta-learned question difficulty transfers across datasets by training on CCES
(133 questions, Gemini 2.5 Flash) and predicting on Twin-2K-500 (68 questions, GPT-4o). We
target VarpYqq rather than Aq because VarpYqq is LLM-independent, enabling transfer across dif-
ferent LLMs. Using PCA(5) and scale features on text-embedding-3-large embeddings, the
cross-dataset prediction achieves Spearman ρ “ 0.587 (p ă 0.001). Table EC.4 reports MC MSE
reductions: allocating Twin-2K-500 respondents with CCES-predicted VarpYqq and the PPI++
estimator yields a 9.6% MSE reduction—68% of the PPI + Opt. (Oracle) oracle (14.1%). This
is weaker than within-dataset meta-learning (62–79% of oracle), as expected given the different
survey domains (political vs. behavioral economics), but statistically significant, confirming that
text embeddings capture generalizable question-difficulty structure.
