RIBBON GRAPHS AND MEROMORPHIC FUNCTIONS
BORIS SHAPIRO
“The only things worthy of our attention are those which introduce order
in the complexity of the world and so make it accessible to us.” H. Poincar´e
Abstract. Let Y be a compact Riemann surface, ϕ : Y →CP1 a meromorphic
function, and Γ ⊂Y a ribbon graph avoiding the critical points of ϕ. Then
ϕ(Γ) is an immersed graph in CP1.
Conversely, given an immersion im : Θ →CP1 of an abstract multigraph
Θ without vertices of valence 1 or 2, we describe a construction of a compact
Riemann surface Y and a meromorphic function ϕim : Y →CP1 such that
ϕim(Γ) = im(Θ).
We further investigate the relation between the topology of Y and the
combinatorics of Γ. In particular, for a surface of genus g we construct span-
ning ribbon graphs whose underlying abstract graphs have arbitrary prescribed
graph genus g′ ≤g, including the planar case. As a consequence, the number
of self-intersections of ϕ(Γ) cannot, in general, be controlled solely by the genus
of Y . We establish general lower bounds for the number of self-intersections
and formulate several open problems, with emphasis on planar ribbon graphs.
1. Introduction
In what follows, all abstract multigraphs under consideration are assumed to
have no vertices of valence 1 or 2.
Definition 1.1. A ribbon graph is an abstract multigraph Γ, possibly with loops
and multiple edges, endowed with a cyclic order on the half-edges incident to each
vertex.
Ribbon graphs provide a combinatorial model for studying surfaces and their
embeddings.
Given an oriented compact Riemann surface Y and an embedded
multigraph Γ ⊂Y , the orientation of Y induces a natural ribbon structure on Γ
via the counterclockwise cyclic ordering at each vertex. Conversely, any abstract
ribbon graph Γ canonically determines a closed oriented surface YΓ obtained by
thickening Γ according to its cyclic structure and capping the resulting boundary
components by disks. The genus of YΓ is called the genus of Γ.
We say that an embedded multigraph Γ ⊂Y spans Y if the complement Y \Γ is
a disjoint union of topological disks. Spanning ribbon graphs encode the topology
of the ambient surface in a particularly efficient way.
In this paper, we investigate the interaction between ribbon graphs and meromor-
phic functions. Given a compact Riemann surface Y and a meromorphic function
ϕ : Y →CP1, any ribbon graph Γ ⊂Y avoiding the critical points of ϕ gives rise to

Conversely, starting from an immersion im : Θ →CP1 of an abstract multigraph

a triple (Y, ϕ, Γ) consisting of a compact Riemann surface, a meromorphic function,

this paper is to establish such a correspondence and to analyze its properties.
A central theme is the relationship between the topology of the surface and
the combinatorics of the underlying abstract graph. As shown in Section 2, this
relationship is highly flexible: for a surface of genus g, there exist spanning ribbon
graphs whose underlying abstract graphs have any prescribed graph genus g′ ≤g,
and in particular can be chosen to be planar (Proposition 2.1). This demonstrates
that the genus of the surface is not rigidly encoded in the combinatorics of the
graph.
This flexibility has important consequences for the study of immersions ϕ(Γ) ⊂
CP1. In particular, it explains why naive expectations relating the number of self-
intersections of ϕ(Γ) to the genus of Y fail in general. Instead, one is led to seek
more subtle invariants and lower bounds for the number of self-intersections, taking
into account both the ribbon structure and the analytic properties of ϕ.
The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we establish the basic exis-
tence results for ribbon graphs spanning surfaces with prescribed combinatorial
properties and develop the main constructions relating immersed graphs in CP1 to
meromorphic functions. We then investigate lower bounds for the number of self-
intersections of such immersions and discuss several open problems, particularly in
the case of planar ribbon graphs.
The results of this note are intended as a first step toward a systematic study of
immersed ribbon graphs arising from meromorphic functions.

before. Hence one can choose the above spanning graphs to be trivalent as well.

Problem 2.5. Find, or at least estimate from below, ♯([Γ], ϕ) for a given class [Γ].
We start with a basic simplification.
Lemma 2.6. For any class [Γ], there exists a representative eΓ ∈[Γ] such that
ϕ(eΓ) has no self-intersections of individual edges. Furthermore, any representative
Γ∗∈[Γ] realizing the minimum ♯([Γ], ϕ) has this property.
Proof of Lemma 2.6. Let eΓ ∈[Γ] be a generic representative, which exists by
Lemma 2.3. Suppose that some edge e ⊂eΓ has a self-intersection in the image.
Then there exist two distinct interior points p, q ∈e such that ϕ(p) = ϕ(q) = x,
and since the graph is generic, both points are regular for ϕ.
Choose pairwise disjoint small discs Up, Uq ⊂Y around p and q such that
ϕ|Up : Up →V,
ϕ|Uq : Uq →V
are homeomorphisms onto the same sufficiently small disc V ⊂CP1 centered at x.
Inside V , the two arcs ϕ(e∩Up) and ϕ(e∩Uq) meet transversely at x. Since ϕ|Up is
a homeomorphism, one may isotope the arc e∩Up inside Up, fixing its endpoints on
∂Up, so that its image under ϕ becomes a nearby arc joining the same endpoints in
V but disjoint from ϕ(e ∩Uq). Choosing the neighborhoods small enough ensures
that no new intersections with the rest of ϕ(eΓ) are created.
Thus one removes one self-intersection of the image of an edge without chang-
ing the class [Γ]. Repeating the procedure finitely many times eliminates all self-
intersections of individual edges. In particular, if a representative realizing ♯([Γ], ϕ)
had such a self-intersection, the above move would strictly decrease the number of
intersections, a contradiction.
□

The lower bound in Proposition 2.7(i) is not the whole story. The underlying
abstract graph itself may force extra intersections.
Proposition 2.8. Let Γ ⊂Y be a ribbon graph and let G be its underlying abstract
multigraph. Then for any meromorphic function ϕ : Y →CP1,
♯([Γ], ϕ) ≥max{g(Y ), cr(G)},
where cr(G) is the planar crossing number of G.
Proof of Proposition 2.8. For any generic representative eΓ ∈[Γ], the image ϕ(eΓ) is
an immersion of the underlying abstract multigraph G into CP1. Hence it has at
least cr(G) transverse double points by the definition of the planar crossing number.
Therefore
♯([Γ], ϕ) ≥cr(G).
Combining this with Proposition 2.7(i), which gives ♯([Γ], ϕ) ≥g(Y ), we obtain the
required bound.

For planar trivalent ribbon graphs there is another way to encode their ribbon
structure in CP1 besides using immersed images. This may substantially reduce
the number of intersections of edges. We call an immersed graph 2-coloured if its
vertices are coloured black and white. The ribbon structure induced by a 2-coloured
trivalent immersed graph is defined by taking the clockwise cyclic order at white
vertices and the counterclockwise cyclic order at black vertices.
Problem 2.21. Given a trivalent ribbon graph Γ ⊂Y of genus g, find lower bounds
on the number of intersections of edges if Γ is realized in CP1 as a 2-coloured
immersed graph.
Proposition 2.22. Let Γ be a trivalent ribbon graph of genus g(Γ). Any 2-coloured
immersed realization of its underlying abstract graph in CP1 inducing the given
ribbon structure has at least g(Γ) transverse edge intersections. More generally, if G
is the underlying abstract graph, then the number d of transverse edge intersections
satisfies
d ≥max{g(Γ), cr(G)}.
Proof. Replace each transverse intersection of edges by a new 4-valent vertex. This
produces an embedded graph H ⊂CP1, and the ribbon surface associated with H
has genus 0. Undoing one such replacement restores a crossing and can increase
the genus of the thickened surface by at most 1. After all d crossings are restored,
one recovers the original ribbon graph Γ, hence
g(Γ) ≤d.
On the other hand, any immersed realization of the underlying abstract graph G
in the sphere has at least cr(G) double points by definition of the planar crossing
number. Combining the two bounds yields the claim.
□

3. Final remarks
The preceding discussion shows that the direct and inverse constructions are ro-
bust, but also that the naive expectation ♯([Γ], ϕ) = g(Y ) is too optimistic without
further restrictions. Several of the open questions admit partial answers: the degree
