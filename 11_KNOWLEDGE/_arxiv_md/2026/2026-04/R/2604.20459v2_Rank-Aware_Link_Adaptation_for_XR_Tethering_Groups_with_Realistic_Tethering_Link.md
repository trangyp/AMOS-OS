the gains of the TGr remain at 165-180% under realistic high-

practical viability of TGr based cooperation for XR capacity
enhancements within existing cellular resources.

I. Introduction
XR encompassing virtual reality (VR), augmented re-
ality (AR) and mixed reality (MR) is considered as
a defining application for next-generation wireless sys-
tems, imposing stringent and simultaneous demands on
latency, reliability and throughput that far exceed those
of conventional mobile broadband services [1]. Meeting
these demands within the constrained radio resources
of a cellular network significantly limits the number of
XR users that can be simultaneously supported within a
5th Generation-Advanced (5G-A) cellular cell [2], [3]. To

is required but also higher spectral eﬀiciency, making
spatial multiplexing through higher-rank transmission a
natural and necessary tool for increasing the number of
simultaneously supportable XR users. However, realiz-
ing spatial multiplexing gains in XR scenarios is non-
trivial, the stringent latency and reliability constraints
imposed by XR quality of service (QoS) requirements
demand accurate link adaptation. Moreover, the inter-
ference suppression capability of conventional receivers
such as minimum mean square error-interference rejection
combining (MMSE-IRC) can degrade under higher-rank
transmissions [4] particularly in inter-cell interference
(ICI) limited environments. These challenges motivate the
investigation of transmission schemes that can exploit spa-
tial multiplexing while preserving the decoding reliability
required for XR services.
Multi-connectivity has been explored as a means to
simultaneously improve both reliability [5], [6] and spectral
eﬀiciency [7], [8] for XR. Among emerging approaches, XR
TGr, in which an XR UE is cooperatively paired with
a nearby conventional UE via a short-range TL, have
demonstrated compelling capacity gains using only sub-6
GHz resources, without requiring additional cellular spec-
trum [7], [8]. The cooperative reception mechanism within
a TGr, combining joint hybrid automatic repeat request
(HARQ) processing, soft combining, and joint OLLA,
improves post-combining SINR and reduces effective block
error rate (BLER) for a given channel condition. Crucially,
this enhanced decoding reliability not only improves link
robustness but also creates more favorable conditions for
higher-rank transmission, enabling TGr to operate at
higher modulation and coding scheme (MCS) and rank
than would be feasible for single-link legacy UEs under
the same interference conditions. Whether and to what
extent TGrs can exploit this advantage to achieve higher
XR capacity through spatial multiplexing remains an open
and practically important question.
LA plays a critical role in realizing these gains by
selecting appropriate transmission parameters based on
channel conditions [2], [9]. Existing studies on XR TGr
have primarily focused on rank-1 transmission and ideal
TLs [7], [8], where a joint OLLA mechanism was shown to
improve MCS selection within TGr, effectively translating
cooperation gains into spectral eﬀiciency improvements.

However, the performance of TGrs under higher-rank
transmission remains largely unexplored.
The authors of [10] studied XR capacity for legacy
UEs under higher-rank (rank-2) transmissions but did
not compare their results against rank-1 scenarios. More-
over, higher-rank transmission can degrade interference
suppression capability of the MMSE-IRC receiver [4]. For
legacy UEs, this degradation in interference suppression
can outweigh the spatial multiplexing gains, leading to
suboptimal or even negative capacity outcomes from
higher-ranks. For TGrs, however, the joint OLLA and
HARQ algorithms enable operation at higher spectral
eﬀiciency, which in turn reduces ICI and may mitigate
this limitation. Nevertheless, the net impact of higher-rank
transmission on TGr performance, particularly in terms
of XR capacity, cannot be assumed a priori, especially in
ICI limited environments such as Indoor Hotspot (InH)
deployments.
Furthermore, conventional rank adaptation methods
are typically based on greedy throughput maximization
[11] and do not explicitly account for OLLA [12]. Even
when OLLA is incorporated, a single-offset is applied
uniformly across all ranks. This is fundamentally prob-
lematic because different transmission ranks operate at
different SINR regimes and exhibit different effective
BLER characteristics. A single-offset leads to throughput
prediction errors and suboptimal rank and MCS selection,
as different ranks exhibit distinct SINR operating regimes.
For XR services, where such ineﬀiciency directly reduces
the number of supportable users per cell, this limitation
has significant practical consequences.
Beyond LA, the practical deployment of TGr also raises
important questions about the TL. When cooperation
is utilized, the TL delay may impact the stop-and-
wait (SAW) channel utilization of the network and more
importantly increases the application layer delay, which
may affect the performance gains of the TGr. Whereas, the
delay over the TL largely depends on the TL throughput
and its load. Despite its practical relevance, the impact
of realistic TL constraints, including limited bandwidth
and delay, on TGr performance has not been previously
characterized.
To address these gaps, this paper investigates higher-
rank transmission for XR TGr in the InH scenario and
analyzes the impact of realistic TL delays. In addition,
we propose an MO-OLLA framework that enables rank
aware SINR correction for improved LA.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized
as follows
• We investigate the performance of XR TGrs under
higher-rank PTM transmission and quantify the re-
sulting XR capacity gains relative to legacy XR UEs.
The results demonstrate that TGrs can effectively
exploit spatial multiplexing to achieve substantially
higher XR capacity than is attainable by single-link
UEs with rank adaptation, an advantage rooted in the
TGr’s ability to overcome the spatial multiplexing vs
interference trade-off that limits conventional UEs.
• We propose MO-OLLA, a rank aware LA scheme that
maintains independent OLLA offsets per rank to en-
able more accurate SINR estimation and throughput
prediction.
• We model the TL using a WiFi based delay abstrac-
tion and conduct the first systematic analysis of how
practical TL constraints, including limited PHY rate
and queuing delay, interact with network’s HARQ
resources and XR capacity, establishing a feasibility
boundary for TGr deployment under realistic tether-
ing conditions.

II. System Model
We consider a downlink (DL) cellular network compris-
ing multiple cells, where each cell serves multiple XR TGrs
with a uniform load distribution, i.e., the same number of
XR TGrs is connected to each Next-Generation Node B
(gNodeB). Each XR TGr consists of two closely located
UEs, an XR UE (UE-X) and a conventional UE (UE-
T), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Both UEs are associated
with the same gNodeB. In addition, the two UEs are
connected to each other via a short-range TL, which
may be implemented using a wireless local-area network
interface or sidelink [13].
The network configures both UEs of the TGr into a
tethered-assisted session. The tethered-assisted session is
conceptually analogous to a 5th generation (5G) multicast
session [14]. In this session, DL data is delivered using
PTM transmission, in which the same transport block
(TB) is transmitted to both UEs of the TGr using a single
set of cellular radio resources. Uplink (UL) transmissions
remain UE specific and are independently used for HARQ
feedback and channel state information (CSI) reporting,
in accordance with standard specifications.
Asynchronous HARQ is assumed and both UEs in a
TGr provide HARQ feedback prior to any cooperation,
relative to the initial TB decoding attempt [15]. This
initial HARQ feedback is unaffected by the TL delay.
However, UE-X may provide an additional, second HARQ
feedback when soft combining is applied just like a UE
does when it receives a retransmission from gNodeB. This
second feedback is affected by the TL delay and may delay
retransmissions from the gNodeB if UE-X fails to decode
the TB even after soft combining and sends a negative
acknowledgement (NACK). HARQ feedback from both
UEs is processed using a joint HARQ processing algorithm
to support retransmissions within the tethered-assisted
session [7]. According to this algorithm, the gNodeB
retransmits only if the TGr fails to decode the TB even
after cooperation, depending on the cooperation scheme

A. Cooperative Reception Model
Each XR TGr operates according to a Selection/Soft
Combining Scheme (SSCS) [8]. Both UEs initially attempt
to decode the received TB. Depending on the decoding
outcomes at UE-X and UE-T, multiple scenarios may
arise, as detailed in [8]. Ultimately, the TGr decodes the
TB according to one of the following three scenarios.
1) Decoding at UE-X only (DX): If User Equipment-
extended reality (UE-X) successfully decodes the
TB, it accepts the decoded data and any correspond-
ing information forwarded by User Equipment with
Tethering (UE-T) is discarded. This scenario incurs
no TL delay.
2) Decoding after selection combining (DSC): If UE-
X fails to decode the TB but UE-T succeeds, UE-
T forwards the decoded TB over the TL to UE-X
using a decode-and-forward algorithm [16], which
applies selection combining. In this case, the TL
delay does affect the TB delay and is calculated
using the decoded TB.
3) Decoding after soft combining (DSoftC): If both UEs
fail to decode the physical downlink shared chan-
nel (PDSCH) but successfully decode the physical
downlink control channel (PDCCH), UE-T forwards
the soft-TB (log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) [17] cor-
responding to that TB) to UE-X [18]. UE-X then
performs element-wise soft combining [19] followed
by a second decoding attempt. This scenario also
incurs an additional delay approximately equal to
the TL delay of the quantized soft-TB.
B. Tethering Link
The TL is assumed to operate on a carrier frequency
different from that used by the cellular network for DL
and UL transmissions. It may utilize WiFi or sidelink [13]
or any other proprietary solution.

Applying a single OLLA offset uniformly across all
ranks is suboptimal, as the SINR associated with each
rank requires different degrees of correction to achieve
the TBLERT . This mismatch produces a cross-rank
contamination in throughput prediction, which in turn
results in suboptimal rank and MCS selection.
The fundamental limitation of single-offset OLLA under
multi-rank operation is best understood by examining
the two distinct timescales on which the offset and rank
selection operate. The OLLA offset ∆OLLA is updated
at the fast timescale of HARQ feedback, after every
TB transmission, the HARQ outcome (ACK or NACK)
updates the offset as

B. Simulation Results
1) Ideal Tethering Link: Fig. 3 presents the empirical
cumulative distribution function (eCDF) of the average
Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs) utilization in the net-
work, along with the distribution of the selected trans-
mission rank, for both legacy UEs and TGrs. Results are
presented for three configurations R-1 (rank-1), R-4 (up to
rank-4 with single OLLA offset) and R-4 MO-OLLA (up
to rank-4 with MO-OLLA). For legacy UEs, R-4 results in
higher PRBs utilization than R-1, reflecting the impact of
suboptimal rank selection. In contrast, TGr do not exhibit
this behavior owing to the cooperative gains. TGr improve
decoding reliability and reduce ICI by operating at higher
spectral eﬀiciency (i.e., higher-rank and MCS) thereby
requiring fewer PRBss. Nevertheless, even for TGr, a
single OLLA offset does not guarantee optimal rank
selection. Enabling MO-OLLA improves rank selection
accuracy for both legacy UEs and TGrs, resulting in
reduced PRBs utilization under R-4 MO-OLLA compared
to R-4. For TGr, this gain is more pronounced at higher
load (12 TGrs per cell) than at lower load (5 TGrs
per cell), since ICI depends jointly on the number of
active users and their resource utilization eﬀiciency. TGr
tends to operate at higher-ranks than legacy UEs due
to cooperation gains, which are effectively captured by
the joint OLLA algorithm. For example, with 5 users
(legacy UEs/TGrs) per cell and MO-OLLA, legacy UEs
select rank-4 in fewer than 1% of transmissions, whereas
TGr select rank-4 in approximately 67% of transmissions.
Consequently, at the 50th percentile with 5 users per cell
and MO-OLLA, PRBs utilization is 58% for legacy UEs

capacity gains over legacy single-link UEs. Under ideal
tethering conditions, combining higher-rank transmission
with MO-OLLA yields XR capacity gains of 180-200%

effect of enhanced decoding reliability, reduced ICI, and

standards. A key question for practical deployment is how
much of this gain survives under realistic TL constraints.
The results provide a clear and encouraging answer: with
PHY rates of approximately 2.4 Gbps and above, TGrs
provide 165-180% XR capacity gains relative to legacy
UEs, with only minor degradation relative to the ideal
case. This robustness stems from the fact that, at these
PHY rates, mean TL delays of 1-1.64 ms keep the delayed
HARQ feedback from UE-X well within the limit, thereby
avoiding the worst-case retransmission chains that drive
application layer delay beyond the PDB. Importantly,
HARQ channel occupancy remains well below saturation
across all evaluated WiFi configurations, even at the
100th percentile, The one exception is the combination
of WiFi-5 equivalent tethering (PHY rate 1.2 Gbps) with
a 10 ms PDB constraint, where TL delays can trigger
retransmissions that push end-to-end delay beyond the
PDB, causing TGr performance to fall below that of legacy
UEs. However, the TGrs with TL PHY rates of 2.4 Gbps or
above preserve the cooperative advantage effectively and
providing gains approximately near to ideal case. Taken
together, these findings confirm the practical viability of
TGr based cooperation for XR capacity enhancement for
future XR deployments.
V. Conclusion
This paper investigated the performance of multi-
connected XR devices enabled through TGr under higher-
rank PTM transmission, with a focus on link adapta-
tion accuracy and practical TL constraints. The results
demonstrate that, beyond reliability improvements, TGr
operation can effectively leverage spatial multiplexing
better than conventional single-link UEs. This leads to
substantially more eﬀicient resource utilization and a
significant increase in XR capacity. The proposed MO-
OLLA framework addresses a fundamental limitation of
conventional single-offset OLLA, its inability to provide
accurate SINR correction across ranks. By maintaining
independent offsets per rank, MO-OLLA improves rank
selection accuracy and yields up to 20% improvement
in TGr XR capacity with no modifications to existing
standards, making it a practical enhancement for deploy-
ment. System-level evaluations confirm that TGr achieves
substantial XR capacity gains of 180–200% over single-
link XR UEs under ideal TL. Analysis of the realistic
TL model reveals that tethering induced delays have a
limited impact on HARQ resource utilization, and do not
significantly degrade system performance provided the TL
offers suﬀicient PHY rate. Specifically, TLs operating at
Wi-Fi-6 or Wi-Fi-7 equivalent PHY rates ( 2.4 Gbps and
above) are shown to provide 165–180% XR capacity gains
relative to legacy UEs, confirming that the cooperation
benefits of TGr are not merely theoretical, 90% of the
TGr gains remain intact under realistic deployment con-
ditions. These findings highlight the practical viability
of TGr based cooperation as an effective mechanism for
supporting higher XR capacity within existing cellular
network resources, and provide actionable guidance for TL
selection and device pairing in future XR deployments.
