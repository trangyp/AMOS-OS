Gray tin (α-Sn) layers with 30 nm thickness were grown on InSb (001) substrates using molecular beam
epitaxy. The surface preparation of the substrates was adjusted to achieve either n-type or p-type doping in
the α-Sn layer. Fourier-transform infrared ellipsometry was used to ﬁnd the temperature-dependent dielectric
function of the α-Sn layers from 0.03 to 0.8 eV and from 10 to 300 K. Because of the inverted band structure
of α-Sn, the spectra show a strong absorption peak at 0.45 eV due to transitions from the inverted Γ−
7
“electron” valence band to the Γ+
8 heavy hole valence band. Applying the Thomas-Reiche-Kuhn f-sum rule,
the integrated oscillator strength of this peak was used to calculate the heavy hole concentration as a function
of temperature. For a nearly intrinsic α-Sn layer, the heavy hole concentration agrees well with predictions
based on degenerate Fermi-Dirac statistics. Deviations from the intrinsic α-Sn carrier concentrations are
attributed to substrate surface preparation leading to the diﬀusion of donor or acceptor ions into the α-Sn
layer causing n-type or p-type doping.
Keywords: Gray tin, infrared ellipsometry, interband transitions, Fermi-Dirac statistics, Dirac semimetal,
molecular beam epitaxy
I.
INTRODUCTION
Because of strong relativistic eﬀects in heavy elements
(Darwin shift), the band structure of α-Sn is inverted.
In comparison to germanium, the Γ−
7 “electron” band
moves between the degenerate Γ+
8 heavy and light “hole”
bands and the Γ+
7 split-oﬀhole band. It thus becomes a
valence band with a negative curvature. In the absence
of strain, the Γ+
8 heavy hole and light “hole” bands are
degenerate. This makes α-Sn a zero-gap semimetal. The
heavy hole band is curved like a valence band, while the
light “hole” band is a conduction band.1–4 See Fig. 1 in
Ref. 1 or Fig. 1 in Ref. 5 for a schematic band structure.
Epitaxial growth of α-Sn on InSb stabilizes the diamond-
like crystal structure at room temperature and avoids
transformation into the metallic β-Sn phase.6 The small
lattice mismatch of −0.15% between α-Sn and InSb leads
to an antisplitting of the Γ+
8 bands equal to 12 meV for
growth of α-tin on an InSb (001) surface. Technically, α-
Sn on InSb is a Dirac semimetal.7 However, this splitting
is small compared to the energy scale investigated here.
For our purposes, it is suﬃcient to treat α-Sn on InSb as
a topologically trivial zero-gap semimetal.
This inverted band structure allows optical interband
transitions from the Γ−
7
“electron” band to the Γ+

heavy and light “hole” bands, which have traditionally
been observed using magnetoreﬂection, magnetotrans-
mission, and thermoreﬂectance measurements in α-Sn
and HgTe.8–11 A strong infrared ¯E0 peak due to these
transitions was recently found at 0.45 eV in Fourier-
transform infrared ellipsometry measurements of the α-
Sn optical constants.7 A careful analysis of the band
structure of α-Sn showed12 that this infrared peak is
mostly due to transitions from Γ−
7 into the Γ+
8 heavy
hole band because of its large eﬀective mass.
Transi-
tions into the Γ+
8 light “hole” band are much weaker
(because of the smaller eﬀective mass). The ¯E0 energy
shows the usual decrease13 with increasing temperature
for HgTe,10,11 but is nearly independent of temperature
for α-Sn.8
The purpose of this article is to apply the Thomas-
Reiche-Kuhn f-sum rule14–16 to calculate the heavy hole
concentration from the integrated oscillator strength of
the infrared ¯E0 peak at 0.45 eV. For a nearly intrinsic
(undoped) α-Sn layer, the resulting heavy hole concen-
trations from 10 to 300 K are in excellent agreement with
a degenerate Fermi-Dirac carrier statistics model, which
considers the electrons located at the nearly degenerate
L-valleys.12 (The simple two-band approximations given
by Ref. 4 are not accurate because of the small indirect
gap.)

strate before growth, donor or acceptor doping of α-Sn
with Sb or In can be achieved. This leads to observable
changes in the infrared spectra and the resulting heavy

One might also wonder if doping or thermally activated
carriers in intrinsic α-Sn might cause changes to the in-
terband transitions at higher energies, especially the E1
and E1 + ∆1 critical points.7,17 A detailed study of the
temperature dependence of the energies and broadenings
of the critical points of intrinsic α-Sn was already per-
formed, but no anomalies were found in comparison to
Ge.17,18 For very high electron concentrations in the L-
valley, a Fermi level singularity19 and Pauli blocking20 of
the E1 and E1 + ∆1 transitions are expected. These ef-
fects become noticeable in Ge at electron concentrations
of the order of 1020 cm−3, about one order of magnitude
or more higher than those considered here. Such stud-
ies of the interband critical points in the range of 1 to 6
eV require a completely diﬀerent equipment set and we
therefore did not pursue them for our epilayers.
II.
EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
A.
Molecular beam epitaxy and characterization
The α-Sn ﬁlms were grown by molecular beam epi-
taxy on nominally undoped InSb(001) (Wafer Technol-
ogy Ltd.)
with an indium rich c(8×2) reconstruction
(AE225) and with an antimony rich c(4×4) reconstruc-
tion (AE227) of the InSb substrate. The substrates were
gallium-bonded to molybdenum substrate holders.
The InSb(001)-c(8×2) reconstruction is prepared via
atomic hydrogen clean (MBE Komponenten GmbH) to
remove oxides at 285◦C followed by a light anneal at
360◦C to smoothen the surface.
The cleaning recipe
was periodically veriﬁed using scanning tunneling mi-
croscopy. Temperature measurements are uncalibrated.
The InSb(001)-c(4×4) reconstruction was prepared by
annealing the InSb(001)-c(8×2) substrate in a primarily
Sb4 overpressure above 430◦C and then rapidly cooling.
For growth on both InSb reconstructions, the substrate
was allowed to cool to room temperature prior to initia-
tion of α-Sn growth.
Desired reconstructions were conﬁrmed by reﬂection
high energy electron diﬀraction (RHEED) immediately
prior to the initiation of α-Sn growth. The α-Sn growth
rate was monitored by RHEED oscillations and periodi-
cally calibrated by Rutherford backscattering spectrom-
etry. Further details on the growth details and chemistry
of the α-Sn epilayers can be found in Ref. 6.
High resolution x-ray diﬀraction (HRXRD) was used
to measure ω−2θ scans using the Empyrean x-ray diﬀrac-
tometer from Malvern PANalytical.21 The Empyrean is
a ﬁve-axis x-ray diﬀractometer that uses an in-plane ver-
tical goniometer and a horizontal sample stage. X-rays
are generated from a copper target using a 45 kV poten-
tial. The incident beam optics consists of a two-bounce
Ge(220) hybrid monochromator with a 10 mm beam
mask and a 1/32◦divergence slit. The diﬀracted beam
optics includes a programmable anti-scatter slit (PASS),

C.
Analysis of ellipsometry spectra
The ellipsometric angles22,23 ψ and ∆for a nominally
undoped α-Sn layer on InSb (sample AE225) are dis-
played in Fig. 2.
ψ shows a monotonic increase with
photon energy and two peaks around 0.2 eV (from the
band gap of the InSb substrate) and at 0.45 eV (due to
the ¯E0 intervalence band transition in the α-Sn layer).
The same two structures also show up as wiggles in the
ellipsometric angle ∆.
The band gap of the substrate
decreases with increasing temperature.25 The ¯E0 peak
of the α-Sn layer is quite prominent at room tempera-
ture and disappears at lower temperatures. The same
experimental data is also displayed as a pseudodielectric
function in Fig. 2. Because of interference eﬀects, the
absorption peaks in the substrate and in the α-Sn layer
appear in the real (not imaginary) part of the pseudodi-
electric function.
Determining the optical constants (dielectric function
ǫ) of the α-Sn layer requires two pieces of informa-

tion as input: (1) The thickness of the α-Sn layer was
found to be 29.7±0.7 nm using high-resolution (004) x-
ray diﬀraction.5 (2) The temperature dependence of the
dielectric function of the InSb substrate was obtained
previously.25 This information allows a direct inversion
of the Fresnel equations to ﬁnd ǫ of α-Sn at each photon
energy.23 To avoid large point-to-point ﬂuctations, it is
more convenient to expand ǫ using Kramers-Kronig con-
sistent basis spline (B-spline) functions26,27 with a node
spacing of 20 meV. The imaginary part ǫ2 was forced to
be nonnegative. Figure 2 shows the experimental data
(symbols) in comparison to the B-spline ﬁt (lines). The
same procedure was used at all temperatures and for all
samples. This B-spline ﬁt is more predictive and less bi-
ased than another commonly used method, in which the
dielectric function of the layer is expanded as a series of
oscillators.22
Like many digital ﬁlters, our B-spline ﬁt introduces os-
cillations in the data. These oscillations are on the order
of 0.1 on the scale of ǫ2 (in Fig. 4) or 5×1017 cm−3 for the
heavy hole concentration (in Fig. 5). These oscillations
can be minimized through a careful choice of the node
spacing of the B-spline ﬁt with a statistical method28 or
with a Fourier analysis of the spectra.29 We did not do
this here and instead used a ﬁxed node spacing of 20 meV,
which visually seemed to provide the best results. Our
method therefore has much worse sensitivity than typi-
cal electrical measurements, which can determine carrier
concentrations as low as 1015 cm−3, see Refs. 30,31.
III.
RESULTS
The dielectric function for a nominally undoped α-Sn
layer on InSb (sample AE225) resulting from a B-spline
ﬁt is displayed in Fig. 2. There are three artifacts in this
dielectric function: First, there is a feature near 0.2 eV
because of the incomplete removal of interference eﬀects
from the InSb substrate. (It is common to ﬁnd such ar-
tifacts in the dielectric function of a layer near a critical
point of the substrate. They are the result of improper
assumptions in the data analysis, such as abrupt inter-
faces and homogeneous, plan-parallel layers.)
Second,
we did not consider surface overlayers, such as surface
roughness, oxidation of α-Sn, or (even in UHV at 10−8
Torr) the condensation of residual gases on the surface.
Ignoring such overlayers causes pseudo-absorption. This
means that ǫ2 shown in Fig. 2 is only an upper limit. If an
overlayer correction was performed, ǫ2 would be reduced.
The impact of surface overlayers on ǫ1 is usually smaller
than for ǫ2. Finally, the free carrier concentration of the
substrate (which is not known for this particular sample)
might inﬂuence the spectra at the lowest energies.
Despite these uncertainties, three results stand out in
the dielectric function of α-Sn shown in Fig. 2: First, ǫ1
decreases and ǫ2 increases at the lowest energies. This
divergence is more pronounced at higher temperatures.

Finally, the most prominent feature in the spectra
(least impacted by uncertainties in the data analysis) is
the ¯E0 peak at 0.45 eV. The shape of this peak and its
magnitude at 300 K have been analyzed elsewhere.12 This
peak is due to direct intravalence band transitions from
the Γ−
7 inverted “electron” valence band to empty states
(holes) in the Γ+
8 heavy hole valence band. These tran-
sitions can only occur in the presence of holes in the Γ+
8
band. Therefore, for an intrinsic sample, they are less
pronounced at low temperature than at room tempera-
ture. The heavy hole density can also be tuned (increased
or decreased) by doping the α-Sn layer with In or Sb.
The E0 peak is therefore sample-dependent. An ex-
ample of a dielectric function for an Sb-doped α-Sn layer
is shown in Fig. 3 (sample AE227). Because of a lower
hole concentration in this n-type sample, the ¯E0 peak is
much weaker in this sample than in p-type or intrinsic
α-Sn layers. Other examples can be found in Ref. 7. We
note that the ¯E0 peak in the ellipsometric angles and
in the pseudodielectric function increases with thickness,
but is independent of thickness in the dielectric function.
IV.
DISCUSSION
The dielectric function ǫ of isotropic materials must
meet many requirements due to physical constraints that
originate from Maxwell’s equations. For example, ǫ must
be an analytic complex function.
It must also satisfy
causality, time-reversal symmetry, and the reality of the
ﬁelds.14 There are also other frequency-averaged condi-
tions that restrain the optical constants. For example,
the average refractive index (averaged over all frequen-
cies) is equal to unity.16 These conditions are often re-
ferred to as sum rules.
Since the complex dielectric function of a material de-
scribes the collective polarizability of charges under the
inﬂuence of an electromagnetic wave with photon energy
ω, it is plausible that the magnitude of the dielectric func-
tion is related to the total charge density in the material.
This is the so-called f-sum rule15,16,33,34

As written, Eq. (1) should yield the valence electron
density (e.g., four electrons per atom, about 1.8×1023
cm−3 for a covalently bonded semiconductor such as Ge)
if m0 is taken as the free electron mass. Unfortunately,
Eq. (1) can rarely be used to obtain the valence electron
density due to practical limitations:
(1) Ellipsometry
data for ǫ2 are rarely available above 10 eV. Therefore,
typical experiments do not probe all charges.35 The lim-
ited spectral range requires ﬁtting the available experi-
mental data with a dispersion model followed by extrap-
olation to 50 eV, see Ref. 36. For example, an integration
over ωǫ2 for AlSb up to 5.8 eV yields only 2.4 electrons
per atom.37 (2) When integrating up to very large ener-
gies, the dielectric function might also include the con-
tributions from transitions involving core electrons. This
will overestimate the valence electron density. (3) Due
to surface layers, such as roughness or native oxides, the
experiment might underestimate the value of ǫ2 in the
ultraviolet spectral region.

sum rule, with well-deﬁned lower and upper limits of the

the eﬀect of the periodic crystal potential by replacing
the free electron mass with the eﬀective mass of the elec-

CdTe also yielded heavy hole concentrations in the mid-
1018 cm−3 range just below room temperature.30
A second sample (AE227) yielded much lower heavy
hole concentrations, reaching only 1018 cm−3 at 300 K.
We conclude that this layer, grown on an Sb-rich sub-
strate surface, is doped with electrons from Sb donors,
which reduce the thermal population of heavy holes at
low temperature.

V.
SUMMARY
In summary, Hall measurements of α-Sn layers grown
by Hartmut H¨ochst’s group in Wisconsin using molecular
beam epitaxy showed many years ago30 that α-Sn layers
grown on In-doped CdTe substrates result in p-type α-Sn
layers while those grown on undoped CdTe tend to be in-
trinsic or n-type. Growth of α-Sn on InSb substrates has
also resulted in p-type α-Sn layers.7,38,39 It is diﬃcult to
reproduce such Hall measurements on modern samples,
because producing Hall bars on thin α-Sn layers6 and
Hall eﬀect data analysis3 tend to be cumbersome. There-
fore, we have demonstrated the use of a non-destructive
all-optical technique, which produces similar hole concen-
tration results as Hall measurements. Our method only
determines the hole concentration. Its accuracy is about
5×1017 cm−3. It is not aﬀected by the complicated con-
duction band structure caused by the near degeneracy of
the Γ- and L-bands.2
Second, we were able to show that not only the choice
of the substrate7,30 can modulate the electron and hole
doping levels, but also the surface preparation of the InSb
substrate before growth. An In-rich InSb substrate sur-
face produces p-type or nearly intrinsic α-Sn layers, while
Sb-rich InSb substrate surfaces can result in n-type lay-
ers.
