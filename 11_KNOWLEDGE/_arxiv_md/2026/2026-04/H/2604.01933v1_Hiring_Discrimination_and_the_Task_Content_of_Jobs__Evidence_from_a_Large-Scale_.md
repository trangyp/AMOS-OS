Abstract
We conducted a large-scale r´esum´e audit of 36,880 applications to 9,220 job advertisements
for new college graduates across the United States. Firms express task preferences through
job-advertisement text, which we link to occupation-level task measures from O*NET and the
American Community Survey.
We develop a model in which discrimination increases with
evaluative discretion, defined as the share of hiring decisions driven by subjective rather than
verifiable assessment.
Callback gaps vary systematically with the task content of jobs.
In
management occupations, callbacks are 28 to 43 percent lower for Black men, Black women,
White women, and Hispanic men than for otherwise identical White men. Broad occupation
categories conceal important variation in task demands. When jobs are grouped by task in-
tensity, discrimination concentrates in positions combining high analytical and interpersonal
demands with low routine content. Decomposing task content into subjective-evaluation and
objective-precision components, we find that subjective evaluation widens callback gaps while
objective precision compresses them.
Customer contact amplifies this divergence, widening
gaps in non-routine jobs but not in routine jobs. Randomly assigned r´esum´e credentials that
increase callbacks on average reduce gaps in low-discretion jobs but not in high-discretion jobs.
Early-career exclusion from high-return task bundles may entrench long-run demographic gaps
in employment outcomes.

Introduction
Racial and gender disparities in task allocation are stubborn features of the U.S. labor market.
While Black–White differences in routine, manual, and social-contact tasks have narrowed consid-
erably since the 1960s, important gaps remain in advanced analytical and social-reasoning tasks,
which are increasingly valued in the modern economy (Hurst et al., 2024). Similarly, after rapid
progress through the 1980s, women’s entry into high-status occupations such as medicine, law,
and business slowed markedly during the 1990s (Goldin, 2021; Blau, 2024). These differences in
task allocation translate into earnings disparities, accounting for a substantial share of wage gaps
for both women (Blau and Kahn, 2017) and Black workers (Gray et al., 2025). Whether hiring
discrimination contributes to unequal access to these task bundles remains an open question.
We address this question by developing a framework linking discrimination to the evaluative
demands of different jobs. Discrimination is more pronounced when hiring depends more heavily
on subjective evaluation and less pronounced when evaluation is constrained by standardized,
verifiable criteria. We argue that jobs with high analytical and interpersonal demands require
greater employer judgment at the screening stage, while routine-intensive jobs are more amenable
to structured evaluation.
The framework predicts that callback gaps should be larger in jobs
combining high analytical and interpersonal intensity with low routine content, and that contact
tasks should amplify discrimination in jobs where evaluative discretion is already high.
We evaluate these predictions using data from a randomized experiment embedded within the
naturally occurring task structure of the U.S. labor market for new college graduates. Between
2016 and 2017, we submitted 36,880 fictitious r´esum´es to 9,220 unique job advertisements posted by
4,968 firms across the United States. Focusing on new college graduates limits heterogeneity arising
from long and complex work histories and provides a clear view of entry-level hiring decisions. To
map jobs to task content, we classify each advertisement into one of 175 detailed occupations based
on its text and then link those occupations to task-intensity measures derived from O*NET and
the American Community Survey.
We document that callback gaps vary systematically with the task content of jobs. The dispar-
ities are particularly large in management occupations, where callbacks are 28 to 43 percent lower
for Black men, Black women, White women, and Hispanic men than for otherwise identical White
men. Black men face a callback gap of 5.1 percentage points in management compared with 1.7
percentage points in office and administrative support. This pattern extends beyond occupation
labels. When we group jobs by task profiles using K-means clustering, discrimination concentrates
in jobs combining high analytical and interpersonal intensity with low routine content regardless
of their major occupation classification. We formalize this relationship through a discretion index
that summarizes the relative importance of subjective evaluation for each job. Decomposing this
index into its subjective noise and objective precision components, we find that the two channels
pull in opposite directions, with the difference statistically significant at the five percent level. The
gradient appears across most demographic groups and across alternative specifications.
Randomly assigned r´esum´e credentials sharpen this pattern. Credentials with the largest call-
back returns, social internships and programming skills, narrow minority callback gaps by 3.7
to 4.1 percentage points in low-discretion jobs, effectively closing the gap in those positions. In
high-discretion jobs, the same credentials provide no differential benefit to minority applicants.

Verifiable signals of quality help minority applicants gain access to routine-intensive positions but
do little to reduce barriers to the analytically and interpersonally demanding roles that carry higher
wage returns and serve as entry points into management tracks.
Customer contact sharpens the distinction between evaluative discretion and customer preju-
dice. The framework predicts that contact amplifies discrimination only in jobs where analytical
or interpersonal demands create scope for subjective evaluation to differ across groups. In purely
routine jobs, contact should have no effect. The data match this prediction. The gap between sub-
jective noise and objective precision effects on callbacks is −4.3 percentage points and statistically
significant in high-contact jobs but indistinguishable from zero in low-contact jobs. This pattern is
difficult to reconcile with a pure customer-prejudice account, under which contact would increase
discrimination regardless of task content.
Our analysis extends the literature in three directions. First, a growing literature documents
demographic differences in the tasks workers perform and the wages attached to those tasks (Gray
et al., 2025; Hurst et al., 2024), but observational data cannot isolate whether hiring discrimi-
nation contributes to those differences. By randomizing applicant characteristics within job ad-
vertisements, we provide the first direct evidence that callback gaps vary with the task content
of jobs, complementing firm-level audit studies (e.g., see Kline and Walters, 2021; Kline et al.,
2022) that identify discriminatory employers but do not examine how task content shapes discrim-
ination patterns. Second, the decomposition of the discretion index into its subjective noise and
objective precision components contests a generic screening-difficulty account in which any form of
task complexity widens discrimination. Third, the credential attenuation test exploits within-job
randomization and therefore does not rely on cross-job variation in task content, providing an
independent check on the mechanism. Because task content itself is not randomly assigned, the re-
sults identify treatment-effect heterogeneity across job types rather than the evaluative-discretion
mechanism per se. Taken together, the three sets of results are difficult to reconcile with a simple
account in which job complexity mechanically increases discrimination.
By focusing on new college graduates, we study discrimination at a career stage when early
access to task bundles may shape longer-run earnings trajectories. Initial labor market conditions
can have persistent effects on subsequent earnings and career paths (von Wachter, 2020; Oreopoulos
et al., 2012), and task-specific experience accumulated early in a career accounts for a substantial
share of later wage gaps (Stinebrickner et al., 2026; Deming, 2023). If discrimination concentrates
in entry-level jobs that build valuable task-specific human capital, the systemic effects documented
by Bohren et al. (2025) are amplified. Heterogeneity in callback discrimination across job types
may therefore matter for longer-run inequality even when the outcome under study is limited to
the callback stage.

Theoretical Framework
2.1
Related Literature and Motivation
2.1.1
Task-Based Production and Discrimination
Task-based production models conceptualize occupations as bundles of tasks that can be performed
by either workers or capital (Autor et al., 2003; Autor and Dorn, 2013; Acemoglu and Autor, 2011;
Autor and Handel, 2013). This framework has proven powerful for understanding technological
change and wage polarization, but its implications for discrimination have only recently received
attention.
Recent extensions suggest that discrimination is not evenly distributed across task
domains. Hurst et al. (2024) show that while barriers have diminished in roles emphasizing routine
customer and coworker contact, disparities remain pronounced in analytical reasoning tasks, which
we argue below are characterized by greater evaluative discretion. This pattern is consistent with
Gray et al. (2025), who document persistent racial disparities in task transitions over the twentieth
century, with Black workers less likely to move into non-routine analytic occupations and more
likely to remain in or transition into physically intensive work. Dicandia (2021) link persistent
wage gaps to routine-biased technological change. Together, these findings suggest that the locus
of discrimination has shifted toward precisely those non-routine tasks (analytical and interpersonal)
that are increasingly central to economic opportunity (e.g., see Deming, 2017, 2021, 2023).
2.1.2
Evidence from Audit Studies
R´esum´e audit studies provide causal evidence of discrimination by randomizing applicant attributes
(Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004; Lippens et al., 2023). A central puzzle in this literature is
that both the direction and magnitude of discrimination vary substantially across occupational
contexts in ways that existing models struggle to explain. Gender discrimination, for example,
can reverse sign depending on occupational composition. Booth and Leigh (2010) find bias against
women in female-dominated occupations in Australia, Ahmed et al. (2021) find bias favoring women
in female-dominated occupations in Sweden, Arnoult et al. (2021) detect discrimination against
women in lower-skilled jobs but against men in French management occupations, and Kline et al.
(2022) find discrimination against women in male-dominated occupations in the U.S. Racial gaps
present a similar puzzle.
They sometimes widen with candidate quality (Nunley et al., 2015)
and intensify when subjective visual assessment enters the evaluation process (Arceo-Gomez and
Campos-Vazquez, 2014).
The varying findings in the literature are difficult to reconcile with models in which discrim-
ination reflects a fixed employer trait. They are, however, consistent with a framework in which
the scope for discrimination depends on the evaluative demands of the job. Two recent strands
of research point toward this interpretation. Bartoˇs et al. (2016) show that group identity affects
information acquisition before the selection decision. In selective labor-market settings, negatively
stereotyped minority names reduce employers’ effort to inspect r´esum´es, whereas in a less selective
rental-housing market minority names increase information acquisition. Their evidence implies
that discriminatory screening is context-dependent. Kessler et al. (2019) show that discrimination
in r´esum´e evaluation reflects not only hiring interest but also beliefs about offer acceptance, high-
lighting the role of subjective judgment in screening. By contrast, the firm-level analysis pioneered

by Kline and Walters (2021) and Kline et al. (2022) identifies specific discriminatory firms but
does not examine how task content shapes within-firm discrimination patterns, leaving the puzzle
of occupational heterogeneity unresolved.
Bohren et al. (2025) formalize why the pattern of discrimination across jobs matters beyond
the immediate hiring decision. In their framework, direct discrimination at an early career stage
can generate systemic discrimination at later stages by changing the human capital, experience,
or signals that workers carry forward.
Using an iterated audit design, they show that racial
discrimination in entry-level hiring compounds through experience gaps into larger disparities in
subsequent hiring rounds. The consequences of direct discrimination therefore depend on what is
at stake at the career stage where it occurs. If discrimination concentrates in entry-level jobs that
build valuable task-specific human capital, the systemic effects are amplified. If it concentrates
in jobs where accumulated experience matters less, the downstream consequences are smaller.
Identifying where direct discrimination concentrates in the task space is therefore a prerequisite
for understanding its systemic reach. This requires separating demand-side discrimination from
supply-side sorting, which we turn to next.
2.1.3
Occupational Self-Selection
Labor market segregation reflects not only demand-side discrimination but also supply-side self-
selection into occupations (Pager and Pedulla, 2015; Le Barbanchon et al., 2021). Likewise, same-
demographic role models influence educational and employment choices (Kofoed, 2019; Porter and
Serra, 2020; Altindag et al., 2025), and beliefs about returns to different fields shape career decisions
(Wiswall and Zafar, 2015; Zafar, 2013).
Recent research suggests that women are more likely to avoid “greedy jobs”, occupations that
consume more time but are also more remunerative, as a possible explanation for the gender wage
gap (Goldin and Katz, 2016; Goldin, 2021). Stinebrickner et al. (2026) provide direct evidence that
these supply-side forces have lasting consequences. Using longitudinal individual-level task data
from the Berea Panel Study, they show that gender differences in task-specific work experience,
particularly in high-skilled information tasks, account for roughly 35 percent of the gender wage
gap within ten years of college graduation. Precollege aptitude predicts task sorting along lines
more consistent with comparative advantage than with discrimination.
Because our audit design randomizes applicant characteristics within job postings, applicant
self-selection does not confound our estimates, although it may influence which jobs are posted.
The distinction matters for interpretation. Observed occupational segregation reflects the equi-
librium outcome of both supply- and demand-side forces. Our experiment isolates the demand
side by holding applicant characteristics fixed and exploiting cross-job variation in task content,
allowing us to test whether callback gaps vary systematically with proxies for evaluative discretion
independently of worker sorting.

The Experiment
3.1
Design
We conducted identical r´esum´e audits in 2016 and 2017.3 The audits ran from April through July in
each year. Using a large online job search board, we submitted randomly generated r´esum´es to job
advertisements drawn at random from a bank of postings compiled by our research team. Employer
responses were monitored through dedicated email and voice accounts to identify callbacks.4
The job bank includes advertisements from six categories: account executive, banking, cus-
tomer service, finance, insurance, and marketing.
These categories were selected because they
generate a large and consistent flow of postings suitable for an audit study of entry-level labor
markets. Ads requiring extensive prior experience, specialized credentials, or occupational licenses
were excluded.
Because the fictitious applicants represent new college graduates, the r´esum´es
primarily reflect internship and part-time experience during college. Restricting the job bank to
positions compatible with this profile avoids additional design complexity associated with licensing
requirements and other specialized qualifications that vary across states.
Unlike many audit studies that focus on specific cities or local labor markets (Bertrand and
Mullainathan, 2004; Kroft et al., 2013; Lahey, 2008; Nunley et al., 2015), we audit the national
labor market and impose no geographic restrictions on job advertisements. After constructing the
job bank, research assistants applied to openings randomly selected from this set. Four randomly
generated r´esum´es were submitted to each advertisement, yielding 36, 880 applications to 9, 220
unique job postings.5

3.2
Template and Detection Bias
A key concern in audit studies is “template bias”, as well as the possibility of detection by au-
dited firms.8 Template bias arises when r´esum´e characteristics are systematically linked, making
it difficult to isolate the effect of individual attributes. To prevent this, we independently and
randomly assign r´esum´e characteristics across applications. One exception is the joint assignment
of university and residential address. This linkage does not affect identification, as the design en-
sures that no two r´esum´es listing the same university are submitted to the same job advertisement,
eliminating any potential confounding arising from this pairing.
Detection by audited firms cannot be fully ruled out and is unobserved by the researcher. How-
ever, existing evidence suggests that such detection attenuates measured discrimination. Balfe et al.
(2023) show that repeated exposure to infrequent identity signals can alter employer behavior, re-
ducing observed bias. Because we submit four r´esum´es to each job opening, detection by employers
is possible. To mitigate this risk, we take several steps to reduce the likelihood of detection:
1. Most firms are audited only once. Approximately 70 percent of firms in our sample receive
applications for a single job posting, and only four r´esum´es are submitted to each advertise-
ment.
2. Applications are staggered over time so that no advertisement receives more than one r´esum´e
submission per day. Research assistants apply to each job over a 4–7 day period.
3. Each application submitted to a job lists a different university. This reduces the likelihood
that firms detect similarities across r´esum´es when applicants share other characteristics, such
as majors.
4. Restricting the design to six job categories allows us to construct realistic r´esum´es. Focusing
on new college graduates further supports this by limiting work histories to short and plausible
experiences.9
5. Advertisements requiring company-specific application forms are excluded. Open-ended ap-
plication questions are difficult to standardize across applicants and may increase the likeli-
hood of detection.
As a further check, we estimate specifications with firm fixed effects substituted for advertisement
fixed effects, as detection efforts are more likely to occur at the firm level (see end of Section 5.1).
While most firms are audited once, approximately 30 percent appear multiple times. Our results
are robust to using firm fixed effects.

3.4
Employer Responses
Our outcome of interest is the callback rate, defined as an employer response expressing interest
in the applicant, including invitations to interview or requests for additional information (e.g.,
availability to discuss the position). The overall callback rate is approximately 15 percent, with
substantial variation across occupation groups and task-intensity groupings. Callback rates are
lowest in business and financial operations (5 percent) and highest in sales (25 percent), with
intermediate values in management (12 percent) and office and administrative support (10 percent).
Across task dimensions, callback rates are similar for occupations above and below the median
in analytical and interpersonal intensity. In contrast, substantial differences emerge for other tasks:
routine cognitive (7 versus 22 percent), routine manual (11 versus 20 percent), physical (13 versus
18 percent), and contact (18 versus 12 percent). The absence of unconditional differences along
analytical and interpersonal dimensions, despite their importance in the discrimination results
below, underscores that callback levels and callback gaps need not move together.
A limitation of audit studies is that callbacks capture only the initial screening stage, and
discrimination at this stage does not determine whether discrimination occurs at subsequent stages.
However, existing evidence suggests that callback gaps likely understate later-stage disparities.
Lanning (2013) embed hiring discrimination in a search model calibrated to audit data and show
that such discrimination can generate wage gaps. Quillian et al. (2020) document larger majority-
minority differences at the job-offer stage than at the callback stage, implying that audit studies
of callbacks may provide conservative estimates of discrimination.

The experiment was designed in 2014, prior to the widespread adoption of pre-analysis plans (PAPs)

did not submit a PAP. The risk of data mining is mitigated by three features of the design. First,

clinical trials study heterogeneous treatment effects across pre-treatment characteristics such as
age or baseline health without requiring those characteristics to be randomized.
The standard concern with treatment-effect heterogeneity is that the moderating variable may
correlate with omitted factors. Jobs that differ in task content may also differ in pay, firm type,
applicant pool composition, screening technology, and selectivity. Job-advertisement fixed effects

this concern. First, the gradient is robust across many alternative classifications of task content,

emphasizes, audit studies identify differences in employer responses holding observable qualifica-

therefore reflect the combined effects of level-based and variance-based discrimination. Our objec-

Conclusion
In this paper, we show that hiring discrimination depends on the task content of jobs. Using a large-
scale r´esum´e audit of 36,880 applications to 9,220 job advertisements for new college graduates, we
find that callback gaps are concentrated in jobs with high analytical and interpersonal demands
and low routine content. The pattern appears across occupation groups, task-based clusters, and
ad-level text measures, indicating that the structure of work helps predict where discrimination
concentrates.
We propose a theoretical framework based on evaluative discretion that organizes these pat-
terns. The model predicts that discrimination widens when subjective assessment dominates hiring
decisions and narrows when objective criteria constrain evaluative discretion. Several pieces of evi-
dence support this framework. Subjective evaluation widens callback gaps while objective precision
compresses them, informative credentials attenuate gaps only in low-discretion jobs, and alterna-
tive task measures yield similar patterns. Together, these findings are difficult to reconcile with a
generic account in which job complexity uniformly increases discrimination.
We emphasize that our design identifies treatment-effect heterogeneity across job types rather
than a direct causal test of the evaluative-discretion mechanism. Jobs that differ in task content

Our findings complement the literature on demographic differences in task allocation by pro-
viding experimental evidence that hiring discrimination varies systematically with the task content
of jobs. While prior work documents differences in the tasks workers perform, our results show
that discrimination itself contributes to these patterns at the point of entry into the labor market.
These patterns are economically important because discrimination concentrates in task bundles
associated with higher wages and career advancement. Entry-level jobs differ in the opportunities
they provide for accumulating task-specific human capital, and access to analytically and interper-
sonally intensive roles is particularly consequential for long-run earnings trajectories. If exclusion
from these positions limits the accumulation of valuable experience, initial hiring disparities may
compound into larger long-run inequality. More broadly, the results suggest that the consequences
of hiring discrimination depend not only on its average magnitude but also on where in the task
distribution it occurs.
