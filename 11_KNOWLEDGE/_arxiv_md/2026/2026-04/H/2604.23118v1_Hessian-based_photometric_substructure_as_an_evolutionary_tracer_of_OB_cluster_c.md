ABSTRACT
Using Hubble Space Telescope images from the PHAT and PHAST surveys, we construct an updated catalogue of 747 OB
cluster (OBC) candidates. We introduce a dimensionless structural metric, the trace coefficient of variation (𝐶𝑉tr), derived from
the Hessian matrix in four HST bands, to quantify the internal photometric substructure of partially resolved OBC candidates.
Cross-matching with the subset of M31 clusters that have independent colour–magnitude diagram (CMD) age estimates yields
247 objects in common. We find statistically significant anti-correlations between 𝐶𝑉tr and age in the UV and blue bands,
suggesting a progressive smoothing of the light distribution as clusters evolve. Bootstrap resampling confirms the robustness of
these trends. Forward modelling of synthetic clusters analysed with the same pipeline recovers a monotonic 𝐶𝑉tr–age relation
under simplified but physically motivated assumptions. These results show that second-order photometric structure contains
measurable evolutionary information within the CMD-calibrated regime (∼10–300 Myr).
Key words: galaxies: individual: M31 – galaxies: star clusters: general – galaxies: star clusters: star formation
1 INTRODUCTION
Star clusters are gravitationally bound stellar systems formed in
nearly coeval star-forming events, and serve as fundamental labora-
tories for studying star formation, stellar feedback, stellar evolution,
and galactic dynamics (Krumholz et al. 2019; Krause et al. 2020).
In particular, the early evolution of star clusters remains an open
problem. Despite extensive observational and theoretical efforts, the
dynamical state and long-term survival of young stellar systems are
still uncertain (Adamo et al. 2020).
In the Milky Way, the proximity of young star clusters allows
individual stars to be resolved, enabling detailed analyses based on
colour–magnitude diagrams (CMDs) and tailored 𝑁-body modelling
(Allen et al. 2007; Jaehnig et al. 2015; Kuhn et al. 2015; Zhong et al.
2022; Tarricq et al. 2022). Theseapproaches providesome of the most
direct constraints on cluster ages, structures, and dynamical states. By
contrast, star clusters located in galaxies beyond several megaparsecs
provide little information on their internal structure, and are therefore
typically treated as integrated light sources. Their physical properties
are commonly inferred from simplified morphological, photometric,
or spectral models (King 1966; Elson et al. 1987; Barmby et al. 2009;
Ryon et al. 2017; Brown & Gnedin 2021; Maschmann et al. 2024;
Thilker et al. 2025).
With the increasing spatial resolution of modern imaging surveys,
star clusters in nearby galaxies are now observed in an interme-

A number of recent studies have already begun to explore such
approaches. One example is the pixel colour–magnitude diagram
(pCMD) technique, which exploits pixel-level flux distributions to
extract statistical information from partially resolved stellar popula-
tions (Cook et al. 2019b, 2020). In parallel, large surveys such as
LEGUS and PHANGS have investigated young star clusters by treat-
ing them as extended two-dimensional objects, incorporating struc-
tural and morphological measurements derived from high-resolution
imaging (Adamo et al. 2017; Cook et al. 2019a; Wei et al. 2020;
Hannon et al. 2023). While these studies demonstrate the potential
of moving beyond purely integrated descriptions, they primarily rely
on zeroth- or first-order structural information and do not fully exploit
higher-order features associated with internal substructure.
To address this challenge, we introduce a Hessian-based frame-
work for analysing the internal photometric structure of partially
resolved star clusters. The Hessian matrix, a second-order differen-
tial operator sensitive to curvature and filamentary structure, has been
widely applied in image processing (Lowe 2004; Xiao et al. 2019),
oceanography (Loose & Heimbach 2021), seismology (Yang et al.
2025), quantum physics (Dawid et al. 2021), and medical imaging
(Yang & Cheng 2014; Lavín-Delgado et al. 2024). In astronomy,

The performance of the Hessian-based framework depends sensi-
tively on image resolution: insufficient spatial sampling suppresses
detectable substructure, while excessively large image sizes increase
computational cost without proportionate gains in structural informa-
tion. For young clusters with typical half-light radii of a few parsecs
(Brown & Gnedin 2021), the clusters typically subtend of order a
few–tens of pixels in radius (at ∼0.04–0.05′′ pixel−1) at distances of
1–3 Mpc. Consequently, Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the nearest large
spiral galaxy, provide an ideal data set for developing and testing our
framework. It hosts a wide variety of star clusters that span a range of
ages and masses, with varying resolutions in available imaging data.
Some of these clusters are available for color-magnitude diagram
age determination, which can be cross-validated with the structural
analyses provided by our Hessian-based method.
As our goal is to investigate the early evolutionary stages of star
clusters, careful sample selection is essential. Young clusters domi-
nated by O- and B-type stars provide particularly valuable laborato-
ries in this context, owing to their short lifetimes, high masses, and
strong stellar feedback. Over the past decade, the PHAT (Dalcanton
et al. 2012; Williams et al. 2014, 2023) and PHAST (Chen et al.
2025) surveys have delivered extensive multi-band imaging of M31,
enabling detailed cataloguing and analysis of cluster properties, in-
cluding ages, masses (Beerman et al. 2012; Fouesneau et al. 2014),
and formation efficiencies (Johnson et al. 2016, 2017), as well as
the construction of homogeneous cluster catalogues (Johnson et al.
2012, 2015). Despite these advances, most existing analyses rely on
integrated light measurements or global structural parameters, and
thus largely overlook the internal structural complexity of young or
morphologically irregular clusters, particularly in crowded environ-
ments (de Meulenaer et al. 2017; Naujalis et al. 2021; Krišči¯unas
et al. 2023).
In our previous work (Liang et al. 2025), we identified a popula-
tion of OB cluster (OBC) candidates in M31 based on their compact
morphology and UV-bright stellar content. In the present study, we
first extend that catalogue by adopting improved selection criteria
and cross-match our catalogue with the catalogue of Johnson et al.
(2016), which provides CMD-based ages for a resolvable subset of
M31 clusters. We then apply the Hessian-derived trace coefficient
of variation (𝐶𝑉tr) to quantify the internal photometric substruc-
ture of OBC candidates. The analysis reveals statistically significant
correlations between 𝐶𝑉tr and cluster age within the CMD-calibrated
regime. Finally, to investigate the physical plausibility of the observed
trends, we construct synthetic cluster models spanning a broad range
of ages and process them through the same measurement pipeline.
This forward-modelling approach allows us to evaluate whether the
empirical 𝐶𝑉tr–age relation can naturally arise from simplified but
physically motivated assumptions about stellar population evolution
and structural fading.
The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, we describe the
observational datasets, present the algorithms for improving the OBC
candidate catalogue, and introduce the Hessian-based structural in-
dicators. Section 3 shows the updated catalogues and the spatial and
evolutionary trends of the structure indices. In Section 4, we evaluate
the catalogue robustness and assess the performance of the Hessian-
based indicators. Section 5 summarises our findings and outlines
future directions.
2 DATA AND METHODOLOGY
2.1 Data
This study is based on imaging from the Panchromatic Hubble An-
dromeda Treasury (PHAT; Dalcanton et al. 2012) and the Panchro-
matic Hubble Andromeda Southern Treasury (PHAST; Chen et al.
2025) surveys. Together, these programmes provide near-contiguous,
high-resolution coverage of approximately two-thirds of the M31
disc, extending from the central bulge to galactocentric radii of
∼20 kpc.
The PHAT survey covers ∼0.5 deg2 in the north-eastern disc us-
ing 828 HST orbits, while PHAST expands the footprint by an
additional ∼0.45 deg2 along the southern major axis. Both pro-
grammes include imaging in four broadband filters (F275W, F336W,
F475W, and F814W) obtained with ACS and WFC3. Observing
strategies were coordinated to ensure consistent spatial resolution
(∼0.05 arcsec pixel−1 in the optical bands) and photometric depth,
with Nyquist-sampled imaging in F475W and F814W to enable ac-
curate reconstruction of point sources.
The combined dataset resolves more than 1.5 × 108 stars across
ultraviolet, optical, and near-infrared wavelengths (Dalcanton et al.
2012; Williams et al. 2014, 2023; Chen et al. 2025). Photomet-
ric completeness in the F475W band reaches ∼27.9 mag in the outer
disc and declines to ∼26.0 mag in more crowded inner regions. Com-
pleteness and photometric uncertainties were quantified using arti-
ficial star tests and crowding simulations; detailed procedures are
described in Dalcanton et al. (2012) and Chen et al. (2025).
2.2 Identification via MeanShift and Photometric Measurement
Young cluster candidates are initially identified using F275W imag-
ing, which is particularly sensitive to the strong ultraviolet emission
from massive stars. Longer-wavelength bands (F336W, F475W, and
F814W) are subsequently used for morphological verification, back-
ground rejection, and structural characterisation. Our identification
and photometric measurement procedures follow Liang et al. (2025),
with two updates: (i) an extended set of spatial scales for the Mean-
Shift search and (ii) a semi-automated estimate of the full-light radius,
𝑅full. We summarise the key steps below.
Candidate clusters were identified using the F275W images from
the HST PHAT and PHAST surveys, publicly available via MAST.1
The images were processed with the standard HST pipelines. To
enhance sensitivity to local overdensities on a broader range of phys-
ical scales, we adopt a set of MeanShift search radii spanning 25–200
pixels, extending the 25–150 pixel range used in Liang et al. (2025).
We applied the MeanShift algorithm (Fukunaga & Hostetler 1975;
Comaniciu & Meer 1997; Bradski 1998; Comaniciu & Meer 1999;
Li & Zhao 2009) to identify density peaks. Each candidate overden-
sity is required to be associated with at least five detected sources,
where sources are defined as local intensity maxima identified with
Photutils (Bradley et al. 2024). This detection strategy does not
require sources to be fully resolved or isolated, which is advan-
tageous in the partially resolved regime. Foreground contaminants
were removed via cross-matching to Gaia DR3 (Gaia Collaboration
et al. 2021; Evans et al. 2023), and all candidates were subsequently
verified by visual inspection.
The half-light radius, 𝑅eff, is estimated as the average of two para-
metric fits: a King profile (King 1966) and an EFF profile (Elson et al.

To determine the photometric aperture, we developed a semi-
automated procedure to estimate the full-light radius, 𝑅full, improving
consistency relative to manual measurements. The cluster centre is
given by the MeanShift peak, and we extract a 4′′ cutout motivated
by the typical M31 cluster size distribution (Liang et al. 2025). We
compute cumulative fluxes in concentric circular apertures and define
𝑅full as the smallest radius at which the growth curve converges, i.e.,
𝐹(𝑟)/𝐹(𝑟+ 1) > 0.98 for two consecutive steps, where 𝐹(𝑟) is the
cumulative flux enclosed within radius 𝑟which is sampled in steps
of one pixel. We further impose an upper limit of 𝑅full < 2′′ to
prevent noise-driven divergence of the growth curve in crowded or
low surface-brightness cases. While empirical, this criterion provides
a practical and reproducible definition of cluster extent for irregular
morphologies.
Photometry is performed within 𝑅full. Local backgrounds are es-
timated from annuli spanning 1.2 𝑅full to 3.4 𝑅full. Aperture cor-
rections for flux beyond 𝑅full assume a King profile with 𝑐= 7
(Liang et al. 2025). The total flux is computed either using a di-
rect 𝑅total measurement where feasible or using the empirical scal-
ing 𝑅total = 2.5 𝑅full. Magnitudes are calibrated using the standard
WFC3/UVIS zeropoints. All measurements are visually inspected to
exclude spurious sources and instrumental artefacts.
2.3 Structural Curvature from the Hessian Matrix
Higher-order spatial derivatives of an image, such as curvature, are
intrinsically sensitive to fine-scale variations in surface brightness
and therefore provide a useful probe of internal structural complexity.
In the context of young star clusters, such complexity is expected to be
most pronounced at early evolutionary stages, when massive O- and
B-type stars dominate the light and produce strong spatial intensity
contrasts, particularly in the ultraviolet (UV) bands.
A cluster exhibiting strong curvature in the UV is therefore likely to
host a substantial population of massive stars and to be at a relatively
young evolutionary stage, given their short lifetimes. Curvature mea-
sured at longer wavelengths may also encode structural evolution,
as photometric substructure can persist beyond the phase in which
massive stars dominate the integrated UV emission.
Motivated by these considerations, we adopt curvature as a quanti-
tative structural metric to characterise and compare the evolutionary
states of star clusters. In practice, curvature is measured through
the trace of the Hessian matrix, Tr(H), which captures the local
isotropic curvature of the surface-brightness distribution and is sen-
sitive to rapid intensity variations associated with compact cores,
extended haloes, and embedded substructures. We note that the ab-
solute normalisation of the curvature statistic is band-dependent due
to differing pixel scales and noise properties; our interpretation there-
fore focuses on monotonic trends within each band rather than on
cross-band absolute values.

3.2 Trace Coefficient of Variation vs. Ages of clusters
Most star clusters at 1–3 Mpc remain unresolved, which limiting
CMD-based age dating for the majority of compact or crowded sys-
tems. We therefore cross-match our extended catalogue with the sub-
set of M31 clusters for which CMD-based ages are available from
Johnson et al. (2016), and use these ages as an external calibration
to test whether 𝐶𝑉tr carries evolutionary information. In total, we
identify 247 clusters in common with the catalogue of Johnson et al.
(2016).
Figure 4a shows the relation between 𝐶𝑉tr and CMD age in four
bands, with interquartile ranges computed within 0.2 dex age bins. A
systematic decline of median𝐶𝑉tr with increasing age is evident in the
near-UV and blue bands (F275W, F336W, and F475W), indicating
that the UV/blue light distribution becomes progressively smoother
and less dominated by high-contrast substructure as clusters evolve.
The Spearman rank correlation coefficients derived from the
unbinned data are 𝜌= −0.53 (F275W), −0.52 (F336W), −0.45
(F475W), and −0.12 (F814W), with corresponding 𝑝-values of
2.1 × 10−19, 6.8 × 10−19, 1.8 × 10−13, and 5.2 × 10−2, respectively.
These values indicate a moderately strong and highly significant
anti-correlation between 𝐶𝑉tr and age in the UV/blue bands, while
the correlation in F814W is much weaker, although still statistically
significant given the sample size.
Beyond the statistical significance, the binned trends in Fig. 4a
indicate that the decline of 𝐶𝑉tr is not confined to the youngest age bin
but persists across the full CMD-calibrated range. In the UV band,
the median 𝐶𝑉tr decreases gradually with increasing age, without
any abrupt transitions, supporting a broadly monotonic behaviour
consistent with the non-parametric nature of the Spearman statistic.
The interquartile ranges further show that the scatter in 𝐶𝑉tr remains
substantial at all ages, but appears systematically larger at younger
ages in the UV bands. This suggests that clusters at early evolutionary
stages exhibit a wider diversity of internal structural complexity,
while older clusters tend to occupy a narrower range of smoother
morphologies. The presence of significant scatter also emphasises
that 𝐶𝑉tr is not expected to provide a deterministic age estimator
for individual objects, but rather acts as a statistical structural proxy
when applied to ensemble populations.
To test the robustness of these correlations against sampling fluc-
tuations, we performed bootstrap resampling (1000 realizations) of
the cluster sample and recomputed the Spearman coefficient for each
band. The resulting bootstrap statistics are summarised in Fig. 4b.
In all bands, the bootstrap medians are consistent with the original
measurements, and the 16th–84th percentile ranges remain narrow
(Δ𝜌≲0.05 for F275W–F475W), demonstrating that the observed
anti-correlations are not driven by a small subset of objects. Notably,
the correlations in the UV/blue bands remain consistently negative
across all bootstrap realizations, reinforcing the statistical robustness
of the monotonic 𝐶𝑉tr–age relation.

relation:

We further assess whether the observed 𝐶𝑉tr–age relation could be
driven by variations in cluster mass. Adopting the CMD-based mass
estimates from Johnson et al. (2016), which span 102.5 to 104 M⊙, we
compute Spearman rank correlations among 𝐶𝑉tr, log 𝑡, and log 𝑀.
We find a moderate positive correlation between age and mass (𝜌=

somewhat more massive. The correlation between 𝐶𝑉tr and mass is
weaker, with 𝜌= −0.22 in F275W and F336W, 𝜌= −0.10 in F475W,

𝐶𝑉tr and age remains stronger in the UV/blue bands (𝜌≃−0.5).

analysis, the dependence of 𝐶𝑉tr on age remains robust. For instance,
the correlation coefficient in F275W changes negligibly from 𝜌=
−0.53 to 𝜌partial = −0.51, while in F814W, it shifts slightly from 𝜌=
−0.21 to −0.22. This result confirms that the observed morphological
evolution is intrinsically linked to cluster aging and is not a secondary
effect of cluster mass.
Taken together, these tests disfavour a purely mass-driven origin
for the observed trend. These relative correlation strengths suggest
that while cluster mass may introduce a secondary contribution to
the observed structural variation, the dominant monotonic trend of
𝐶𝑉tr with age cannot be attributed solely to the underlying mass
distribution within the CMD-calibrated sample.
4 DISCUSSION
4.1 Improvements and Limitations of the Current OBC
Candidate Catalog
The OBC candidate catalog in M31 has been updated and the number
of candidates increases from 578 to 747 due to a finer spatial search
grid, while preserving the search algorithm and overall spatial dis-
tribution pattern reported in previous studies (Liang et al. 2025).
The algorithm is used primarily as a pre-selection step, with final
candidate identification based on visual inspection. We also refine
the definition of 𝑅full using a non-parametric method based on the
convergence of cumulative flux curves, which is better suited for can-
didates with irregular morphologies (e.g. Ryon et al. 2017). This re-
duces reliance on subjective visual estimation, though all boundaries
are still visually reviewed. This refinement generally leads to more
consistent 𝑅eff values compared to nearby galaxy catalogs (Brown &
Gnedin 2021).
However, even with the latest PHAST data (2025 release), only
about two-thirds of M31 is covered, as shown in Fig. 2. The south-
western quadrant—particularly beyond 14 kpc and between 210◦and
330◦in azimuth-contains large gaps. We note that this incomplete
coverage introduces substantial sampling bias, especially in dust-
rich regions along the ring, limiting the statistical completeness of
the catalog.
4.2 Forward Modeling of Photometric Structural Evolution
Motivated by the empirical wavelength-dependent trends in Sec-
tion 3.2, we construct synthetic cluster images to demonstrate the
plausibility of the observed 𝐶𝑉trage trends under simplified but phys-
ically motivated assumptions, we construct synthetic cluster images
and process them through the same measurement pipeline. Each
mock cluster is assigned 𝑀= 103–104 𝑀⊙, log(age/yr) = 6.5 −9.2,
and a characteristic radius of 1–4 pc. Internal substructure is intro-
duced through multiple fragmentation cores whose number scales
with cluster mass. Stellar masses are drawn from a Kroupa IMF
(Kroupa 2001) over 0.5–30 𝑀⊙; to suppress extreme stochastic re-
alizations, we impose observational constraints on the number of
the most massive stars. Multi-band luminosities are assigned using
solar-metallicity MIST isochrones (Choi et al. 2016; Dotter 2016)
in the same HST filters as the observations. The mock clusters are
projected to the distance of M31 (0.78 Mpc) with random orienta-
tions. Synthetic images are generated with GalSim (Rowe et al. 2015)
by converting stellar fluxes to detector counts and convolving with
instrument-specific PSFs (WFC3: STScI calibration products; ACS:
empirical PSF from isolated stars; Sahu 2021). We further inject
Gaussian noise with an amplitude of 0.5 times the median non-zero
pixel value and repeat the analysis for ten noise realizations, finding
𝐶𝑉tr–age relations indistinguishable from the noise-free case.
Figure 5 summarizes 38 independent mock realizations analyzed
with the same measurement procedure as the observations. In all
bands, the median𝐶𝑉tr decreases monotonically with age (Figure 5a),
with realization-to-realization scatter small compared to the overall
dynamic range. The Spearman coefficients 𝜌remain consistently
negative across realizations (Figure 5b), with median values between
−0.94 and −1.00 and 16th–84th percentile intervals that do not cross
zero. This demonstrates that a monotonic 𝐶𝑉tr–age relation can be
robustly recovered in the forward-modelling framework under sim-
plified but physically motivated assumptions.
We emphasise that the empirical validation presented in this work
is constrained to the CMD-calibrated range (∼10–300 Myr), while
the extension to later ages in the simulations is intended as an
exploratory theoretical demonstration rather than an observational
claim. The stronger correlations in the simulations likely reflect the
controlled nature of the forward models and the absence of several
real-world complications (e.g. crowding, complex backgrounds, and
stochastic sampling at the high-mass end) that can dilute trends in
the observational data. Accordingly, we interpret the simulations as
a plausibility test for the emergence and monotonicity of the trend,
rather than as a quantitative match to the observed slope or scatter.

fading and disappearance naturally enhance the evolution of light-

band primarily traces longer-lived intermediate- and low-mass stars,
for which luminosity evolution over the CMD-calibrated range is
comparatively modest. As a result, age-dependent structural signa-

as a failure of the modelling framework, but as an indication that 𝐶𝑉tr

than to purely dynamical smoothing of the underlying stellar mass
distribution.
5 CONCLUSION
In this work, we present a Hessian-based approach to quantify the
internal photometric substructure of partially resolved OB cluster
(OBC) candidates in M31, and assess its evolutionary relevance using
independent CMD age estimates and forward modelling.
First, we refine and extend our previous OBC candidate sample
(Liang et al. 2025) using PHAT and PHAST imaging, yielding an
updated catalogue of 747 candidates. The revised pipeline includes
an expanded MeanShift search over a broader range of spatial scales
and a semi-automated, growth-curve-based definition of the full-light
radius 𝑅full, leading to more consistent size measurements across the
survey footprint.
Second, we quantify internal photometric substructure via the
Hessian-derived trace coefficient of variation, 𝐶𝑉tr, measured con-
sistently in four HST bands. By cross-matching our catalogue with
the subset of M31 clusters that have CMD-based ages from Johnson
et al. (2016), we identify 247 objects in common and find statistically
significant anti-correlations between 𝐶𝑉tr and age in the UV/blue
bands. This supports the interpretation of 𝐶𝑉tr as a structural proxy
for relative evolutionary stage within the CMD-calibrated regime.
Finally, we construct synthetic cluster images spanning a broad
range of ages and analyse them with the same measurement pipeline.
The forward-modelling experiments robustly recover a monotonic
decline of 𝐶𝑉tr with age under simplified but physically motivated
assumptions, demonstrating that the observed trend is plausibly ex-
plained by stellar population evolution and the associated fading of
high-contrast substructure. We emphasise that the empirical valida-
tion in this work is limited to the CMD-calibrated range (∼10–
300 Myr), while the extension to later ages in the simulations is
intended as a theoretical demonstration rather than an observational
claim.
Future work will first focus on strengthening the empirical founda-
tion of the curvature–age relation. In particular, incorporating more
realistic forward modelling that accounts for observational noise,
crowding, and instrumental effects will help to clarify the robustness
of the observed trends. Extending the analysis to larger and more
diverse cluster samples within M31 will also be important as addi-
tional homogeneous age estimates become available, including those
derived from spectral energy distribution modelling (e.g. Prospector,
Leja et al. 2017; Johnson et al. 2021).
On the methodological side, further refinement of curvature-based
diagnostics may improve sensitivity to internal structural evolution
in the partially resolved regime. The exploration of alternative or
higher-order Hessian-derived metrics, beyond the trace alone, could
provide additional discriminatory power. We also note that the trace
coefficient of variation is naturally sensitive to strong localised lu-
minosity fluctuations. In the UV bands, where massive OB stars
dominate the light budget, the presence of a small number of high-
luminosity stars can enhance local curvature contrasts and broaden
the distribution of |Tr(H)|, potentially increasing 𝐶𝑉tr. This sug-
gests that, beyond its evolutionary sensitivity, the trace coefficient of
variation may serve as a statistical indicator of clusters whose light
is dominated by massive stars, particularly in the UV regime. An
analysis combining spectroscopic classifications or resolved stellar
content would be required to test this possibility in detail.
In this context, a more systematic investigation of mass-dependent
structural signatures will also be necessary. While the present analysis
indicates that the curvature–age relation is not solely driven by cluster
mass, the moderate correlation between mass and age suggests that
mass-dependent sampling effects, particularly in the UV regime,
may modulate the amplitude and scatter of 𝐶𝑉tr. A study designed to
disentangle stochastic massive-star sampling from secular structural
evolution will therefore be pursued in future work.
Finally, the availability of larger cluster samples in nearby galaxies,
enabled by forthcoming wide-field facilities such as the China Space
Station Telescope (CSST)(Zhan 2021), will allow curvature-based
diagnostics to be tested across broader environmental conditions. A
more comprehensive exploitation of the full Hessian matrix, includ-
ing its eigenvalue structure and anisotropy properties, may ultimately
contribute to a unified morphological framework for studying extra-
galactic star clusters beyond simple structural smoothing signatures.
