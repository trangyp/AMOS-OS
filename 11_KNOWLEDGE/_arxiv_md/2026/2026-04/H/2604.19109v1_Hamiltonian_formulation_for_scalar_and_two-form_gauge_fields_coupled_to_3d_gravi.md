We develop a systematic Hamiltonian formulation for a gravitating topological matter system in
three-dimensional spacetime, coupling a scalar gauge field and a rank-2 antisymmetric gauge field to
Einstein–Cartan gravity. We perform the Dirac–Bergmann analysis, systematically finding the full
structure of the constraints, classifying them into first- and second-class ones, and computing their
Poisson bracket algebra. Furthermore, we write down the explicit expression for the Hamiltonian
generator of gauge symmetries on the full set of canonical variables, containing the exact number of
gauge parameters, and demonstrate that, through a mapping of the gauge parameters, these gauge
transformations reproduce on-shell the spacetime diffeomorphism and local Poincar´e symmetries,
thereby establishing the full symmetry structure of the coupled model.
Our canonical analysis
further reveals that the reduced phase-space admits exactly three reducibility conditions for the
first-class constraints, which guarantee the consistency of the gravitating matter system by ensuring
a correct count of physical degrees of freedom. The fundamental symplectic structure on the reduced
phase-space is established through the explicit computation of the Dirac brackets.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Lower-dimensional field-theoretic models provide a powerful framework for gaining deeper insight into the funda-
mental interactions of Nature. For instance, unlike four-dimensional (4d) general relativity, 3d pure gravity—described
by the Einstein–Hilbert action—exhibits no local propagating physical degrees of freedom (DoF) and, consequently,
features neither Newtonian forces nor gravitational waves [1, 2]. Furthermore, with or without a cosmological constant,
the theory admits a first-order formulation as a gauge theory where the action—the Einstein-Cartan action—is purely
topological, making both Lorentz and diffeomorphism invariance manifest [3, 4]. This topological nature—meaning
the action is independent of the background metric—allows it to be expressed equivalently as a Chern–Simons action
[4–8], at least classically. These remarkable features significantly simplify the analysis of 3d gravity relative to its 4d
counterpart, resulting in an exactly solvable theory that can be quantized through various approaches [9–12]. In addi-
tion, since 3d and 4d gravity share a common foundational structure—characterized by diffeomorphism invariance, the
absence of background time, and constraint-encoded dynamics—3d gravity serves as a robust toy model for exploring
the complexities of the gravitational field itself and several issues in quantum gravity [13]. These include the nature
of time in quantum gravity [14], the meaning of diffeomorphism-invariant observables [15], and foundational questions
such as whether gravitational interactions may have a finite range [16] and the specific ways in which quantum gravity
might be useful [17].
Going further, any consistent quantum gravity formalism is expected to address some of the most profound open
problems in theoretical physics. These include the resolution of spacetime singularities [18], the late-time accelerated
expansion of the Universe [19], black hole evaporation and the information paradox [20, 21], and the gravitational
field sourced by matter in spatial superposition [22, 23], among others. Since all these questions involve matter in one
way or another, pure gravity models are insufficient for shedding light on such inquiries and are therefore of limited
applicability in these contexts. Bearing this in mind, we must search for interacting theories that consistently couple
the structural versatility of 3d gravity to the dynamics of matter fields. In particular, a complete understanding of
their Hamiltonian formulation, including their full gauge content, is paramount for canonical approaches to quantum
or modified gravity with matter fields.
Furthermore, in recent years, gravity in three dimensions has proven highly useful for describing the mechanisms
behind exotic physical phenomena realized in certain condensed matter systems (see, e.g., Refs. [24–27]). This kind
of application establishes a profound link between gravitation and modern condensed matter physics, suggesting
that their interplay could elucidate fundamental aspects of both fields. However, coupling matter to gravitation in
three dimensions in the traditional way—either through the metric in the second-order formalism or through the
dreibein field in the first-order formulation—generally spoils the topological properties of pure Einstein theory and
possibly introduces local dynamics. As a result, the quantization process faces many of the same obstacles as its 4d
counterpart. A remarkable exception lies in certain special classes of topological field theories that couple matter

We focus on one such exceptional class: three-dimensional first-order gravity coupled with topological matter.
Importantly, due to its interdisciplinary nature and the unusual electromagnetic and gravitational effects it often
exhibits, topological matter represents one of the most active research areas in modern physics [36–38]. It serves as
an ideal playground where ideas and techniques from various areas, ranging from electromagnetism and high-energy
physics to atomic-molecular optics and condensed-matter physics converge. Theoretically, these systems involve not
only point-like quasi-particles—such as fermions, bosons, and anyons—but also extended objects like quasi-strings
and quasi-membranes [39]. These extended objects can be conventionally described by rank-2 tensor (2-form) gauge
fields through effective gauge potentials with topological terms [40]. In our specific setup, the topological matter
sector consists of a scalar field and a rank-2 antisymmetric tensor field—also known as a Kalb–Ramond field1, whose
coupling to gravity preserves the solvability of the pure 3d theory.
While a spacetime-dependent effective action can describe both topological and dynamical aspects of topological
matter, a phase-space treatment has proven to be more convenient and efficient for capturing all its fundamental
aspects [52–54].
Furthermore, although the lack of local DoF is well understood in pure 3d gravity, coupling to
matter—even of a topological nature—requires careful verification that no local excitations emerge from the interaction
terms. Given that dynamical systems of this type are of topological origin, the analysis of DoF would be strengthened
by explicitly demonstrating that the potential degrees of freedom of the matter sector are entirely gauged away through
the gravitational interaction.
Thus, it is highly desirable to employ a phase-space approach that systematically
provides a robust method to count the number of local physical DoF without resorting to linearization—that is, by
taking into account the full set of physical constraints and gauge invariances. This approach would also be helpful in
establishing the relationship between the constraint structure and the symmetries of such theories. In this context, a
deep understanding of the dynamical content of topological matter models, coupled with 3d general relativity, requires
constructing and analyzing its Hamiltonian formulation [55–60].
A Hamiltonian description of any physical system consists of three fundamental components: a phase-space identi-
fying the dynamical degrees of freedom, the Poisson–Dirac brackets defining a symplectic structure, and the Hamilto-
nian functional, which generates the evolution of the system from given initial conditions by specifying a curve in this
phase-space. Nevertheless, as in any covariant theory, the general covariance of the action gives rise to a constrained
Hamiltonian system; specifically, the action is first order in the time derivatives, and the Hamiltonian functional turns
out to be a linear combination of the constraints. In this constrained scheme, the dynamics of the system is entirely
dictated by the constraints; that is, they define a physical constraint surface within the original phase-space that re-
stricts the system. The subsequent classification of the full set of constraints into first-class and second-class provides
the necessary framework to explicitly count the physical DoF. Furthermore, the first-class constraints allow for the
construction of the generator of Hamiltonian gauge symmetries—with the prescription of initial data on a Cauchy
surface—while the second-class constraints lead to the derivation of the corresponding Dirac brackets. Ultimately, this
systematic Hamiltonian treatment enables us to characterize both the dynamical content and the symmetry structure
of the model in a definitive manner (see, e.g., Ref. [61, 62] for details).
Taking the previously mentioned observations as inspiration, the purpose of this paper is to employ the Dirac–
Bergmann protocol to develop in detail a Hamiltonian description of a topological field theory containing a scalar
field and antisymmetric tensor field non-minimally coupled to Einstein-Cartan gravity in three spacetime dimensions
[63]. These matter fields are coupled to gravity via a potential that represents a special case of a general class of
topological field theories first analyzed by Horowitz [34]. This model is remarkably rich: it contains unusual black-hole
solutions—the so-called exotic BTZ black holes—in which the roles of mass and angular momentum are reversed; it
admits an interpretation as a generalized Chern–Simons theory with fermionic topological matter fields; and it also
admits a description as a higher gauge theory [64, 65]. The constrained Hamiltonian analysis `a la Dirac-Bergmann
presented here provides a compelling and complementary view to the Lagrangian framework established in Ref. [63].
In addition, we hope that this approach will be useful for analyzing the initial-value problem and facilitating the
search for numerical solutions. Finally, although beyond the scope of this work, this treatment represents the first
step toward the construction of a quantum theory, where the relevant constraints and symmetries of the system play
a crucial role in providing a non-perturbative handle that enables us to limit the quantization ambiguities.

VI.
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we have set up a complete and consistent Hamiltonian formulation of a gravitating topological
matter model. It features a tensor (Kalb-Ramond) gauge field and a scalar gauge field with a topological interaction
term non-minimally coupled to Einstein-Cartan gravity through the Lorentz connection [63]. In order to obtain the
best description of this matter-gravity system in the full phase-space, the Dirac-Bergmann algorithm for constrained
systems was rigorously applied. This procedure results in a larger set of primary constraints (19). Consequently, the
time evolution of these constraints allowed us to determine the full set of secondary constraints (25) as well as several
Lagrange multipliers (26). This model does not have tertiary constraints.
A crucial aspect of our analysis has been the study of the constraint algebra. For instance, by using the linearly
independent zero-modes (30) of the matrix (29) of Poisson brackets among all secondary constraints AI, BI, CaI and
the primary constraints ξa, ϕaI, ψabI, θI, we determined the full structure of the secondary constraints eAI, eBI, eCaI
(31). Thereafter, with the complete structure of the constraints and their algebra, we easily classified all constraints
into first and second class. The results showed that the system possesses 24 first-class constraints (38) and 18 second-
class constraints (39).
Furthermore, with the help of first-class constraints, we have displayed the Hamiltonian generator (42), containing
the exact number of gauge parameters, for the gauge symmetry transformations of this theory on the entire phase-
space (44). These are new in that the generator and the symmetries are realized on the full set of phase-space variables.
From these, we then extracted the covariant form of gauge symmetry for the dynamical fields (45) and successfully
recovered the full diffeomorphism (47) and Poincar´e (49) symmetries through appropriate gauge parameter mappings,
but modulo terms proportional to the field equations, confirming the on-shell nature of spacetime symmetries in this
first-order formulation.
Finally, the correct classification of all the constraints, in turn, allowed us to revise the counting of the degrees of
freedom. We have found that the number of first- and second-class constraints does not match the topological nature
of the model. Namely, the resulting classification of constraints has suggested an inconsistent phase-space dimension
of −6. Nevertheless, we found that some first- and second-class constraints satisfy non-trivial relations (52), indicating
that they are not all independent. We demonstrated that these relations, when treated with Dirac brackets, transform
into reducibility conditions on the first-class constraints (53). These additional conditions are necessary and sufficient
to confirm the model as a consistent topological field theory with zero local degrees of freedom per space point.
As a final remark, our analysis provides a solid Hamiltonian description for this model of gravitating topological
matter. The constraints, their algebra, the Dirac brackets, the gauge generator, and the symmetries are the best
guidelines for performing the quantization. For instance, symmetries provide, thanks to Noether’s theorem, a non-
perturbative handle that enables us to limit the quantization ambiguities by demanding that such symmetries are
preserved upon quantization. A natural next step would be to explore the non-perturbative quantum theory, for
instance, within the framework of loop quantum gravity [10, 11, 13, 80, 81] or the deformation quantization (Wigner-
Weyl quantization) program [52, 54, 82–85]. It would also be interesting to investigate if this model and its Hamiltonian
description serve as theoretical tools in the interpretation and exploration of certain topological phases supporting
extended objects such as quasi-strings and quasi-membranes. These objects are often described by rank-2 tensor
gauge fields and have recently attracted considerable attention in the context of fracton phases and higher-rank gauge
theories.
