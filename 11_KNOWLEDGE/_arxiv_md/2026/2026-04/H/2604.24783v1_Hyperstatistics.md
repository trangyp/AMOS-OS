Abstract
We propose a general approach, named by us hyperstatistics, to treat complex
systems, in which Boltzmann-Gibbs statistics breaks down in domains of the sys-
tem. Hyperstatistics preserves the concavity of nonadditive q-entropy. We obtain
analytical closed-form expressions for the here proposed q-generalized Boltzmann
factor Bq considering uniform, γ, Log-normal, F, and the q-γ probability dis-
tribution functions. Remarkably, for all investigated distribution functions, Bq
reduces to a q-exponential-type function. To demonstrate the applicability of
hyperstatistics, we use a table top experiment of the discharge of a capacitor
considering γ-distributed relaxation times, the pressure decay over time associ-
ated with the pumping of 4He lines of a closed cycle cryostat, midrapidity data
for p-Pb collisions at the LHC, as well as data set for acceleration distribution in
turbulent systems. Furthermore, we deduce the power-law-like dielectric response
using the q-γ-distribution function. Our proposal is applicable to systems with
inherent non-Boltzmann-Gibbsian statistics in domains of the system.
Keywords: hyperstatistics, Mellin transform, q-exponential, Γ(n) function,
midrapidity, turbulent systems, dielectric response.

1 Introduction
The Boltzmann-Gibbs statistical framework is widely employed in systems at equilib-
rium and where short-range forces are dominant. As pointed out by Cohen [1], “...
The first classical non-Boltzmann statistics of physical systems was proposed by Tsal-
lis ...” aiming to treat a variety of complex systems, cf. Refs. [2, 3] and references cited
therein. When dealing with systems that lie in nonequilibrium, the approach of super-
statistics has been employed, which considers fluctuating physical quantities, such as
temperature, that are distributed throughout the system [4]. Even though q-statistics
and superstatistics have been employed successfully in the description of a variety of
systems, cf. Refs. [3, 5–7], a general formalism capable of addressing complex systems
in terms of probability distribution functions, hereafter PDFs, with extremely high
accuracy and mathematical simplicity is still lacking. In the present work, we propose
such a new approach, which encapsulates situations whose Boltzmann-Gibbs equilib-
rium in domains of the system is no longer valid, giving rise to what we named as
hyperstatistics. The latter was chosen because it is reminiscent of superstatistics and
also because of the unprecedented high accuracy and versatility in the description of
experimental data with inherent non-Boltzmann-Gibbsian statistics. Our proposal is
not to be confused with a generalization of superstatistics. As a matter of fact, it is
distinct from superstatistics in the sense that we consider a distribution of Boltzmann
factors within domains of the system, cf. detailed discussions below. Of course, the
superstatistics approach also provides a very good description of a variety of systems.
Recently, superstatistics was successfully employed to describe chemical reactions as
a function of the density of reactants [6]. Also, it is remarkable that superstatistics,
proposed over than 20 years ago, also captures and describes properly the behaviour
of turbulent systems [5]. As a matter of fact, superstatistics served as the ground of
the present work. In this context, hyperstatistics can be seen as an upgraded super-
statistics framework. The use of the term hyperstatistics is also not intended to cover
a generalization of statistical mechanics, cf. proposed in Ref. [2]. Instead, as it will be
discussed below, hyperstastistics is fully aligned with the q-entropic functional [2] (Sup-
plementary Section 1). To introduce our proposal, we start by discussing its genesis in
terms of the Gamma function Γ(n), which is addressed in the following. The classical
Γ(n) function appears frequently in Physics as the mathematical ground underlying
the normalization and computation of exponential-type distribution functions. It turns
out that when the latter are replaced by power-law-type distributions, as required
by systems with inherent long-range interaction, disorder, or strong fluctuations, the
canonical Γ(n) function is no longer appropriate to be used. This motivates the pro-
posal of a “q-deformed” Γ(n) function, where q refers to the entropic index [2]. It is to
be noted that proposals of generalization of the Γ(n) function have been reported in the
literature in the form of q-analogues associated with q-series and hypergeometric func-
tions [8], as well as degenerated Γ(n) function [9], among others [10, 11]. As discussed
into more details below, the here proposed q-generalized Γ(n) function, i.e., Γ(n, q),
is distinct, since we have defined it as the Mellin transform of the q-exponential func-
tion. We propose thus a natural generalization of Γ(n) in terms of the q-exponential
function, hereafter q-exp, with the preservation of the mathematical structural role

system is obtained from the velocities distribution. Our proposal goes a step further.
First, we consider a γ-distribution of Boltzmann factors at the level of domains, which
leads to a q-generalized Boltzmann factor for each domain of the system, cf. Fig. 2 b).
Note that each Boltzmann factor inside every domain of the system is related to the
probability of the particle be found in a particular βi. Then, in a second step, a dis-
tribution of such q-generalized Boltzmann factors for all the domains of the system
is considered, yielding a Bq(E) for the system in terms of an average temperature,
cf. Fig. 2 f). The consideration of a given distribution of q-generalized Boltzmann fac-
tors regarding all domains within the system can thus be seen as the estimate of the
“probability of the probability”. In other words, we may say hyperstatistics treats the
γ-distribution of the canonical distribution [15]. Employing Eq. 14 for the particular

have deduced a distribution of Boltzmann factors in terms of ⟨β⟩, being the latter
indeed the observable accessible experimentally. In other words, our approach takes
into account a distribution of Boltzmann factors within every domain of the system
leading to a q-exp factor for each of them, i.e., non-Boltzmann-Gibbsian statistics at
the level of domains, cf. illustrated in Figs. 2 a) and b). Essentially, our approach,
based on q and n, enables us to quantify both the deviation from the standard expo-
nential decay through q and the deviation of β from its corresponding average, i.e.,
⟨β⟩, through n. The latter in turn comes from the Mellin transform of the q-exp func-
tion, cf. Eqs. 3 and 4. In other words, q and n can be interpreted as quantifiers of the
degree of complexity in a given system. In particular, considering the role played by
the parameter n in the mathematical expression of the γ-distribution, larger values of
n may be associated with higher complexity and, consequently, with a higher accu-
mulation of entropy. This is sustained when comparing n = 1.020 for the discharge
of a capacitor with n = 4.87 for the midrapidity data set, cf. discussions in Section 4,

distinct from Beck and Cohen approach [4]. Although each PDF employed for com-
puting ⟨β⟩gives rise to distinct Bq, cf. Table 1, it becomes evident that our approach
is unique in the sense that it is dictated by q-exp-type functions with closed form.
Thus, an advantage of using hyperstatistics, refers to the fact that the specific distri-
bution that works for the considered dataset is naturally embedded in Bq. In other
words, upon employing Bq we obtain an appropriate description of the dataset regard-
less of the PDF considered as usual in other frameworks. Also, our approach should
not be confused with the so-called hyperensembles [30], which considers distinct PDFs
for the constituents of the system, named by Crooks as metaprobability. In contrast,
hyperstatistics considers a unique PDF that describes the distribution of Boltzmann
factors within a given domain, resulting in a q-generalized Boltzmann factor described
by a q-exp function in terms of ⟨βi⟩. It is worth mentioning that the hyperstatis-
tics approach can be naturally applied to the case of a distribution of probabilities
[5], yielding a q-Gaussian function in terms of ⟨β⟩. Following previous discussions, it
becomes clear that superstatistics is based on Boltzmann-Gibbs equilibrium for each
one of the domains, while hyperstatistics considers them to follow non-Boltzmann-
Gibbsian statistics. As a consequence, instead of assuming that fluctuations act on
intensive quantities, hyperstatistics considers that fluctuations affect directly the sta-
tistical weights, i.e., we consider a distribution of Boltzmann factors in the domains
composing the system. This is totally in line with the fact that the presence of the
so-called multiplicative noise leads to nonextensive statistical mechanics [31]. Also,
the auto-correlation function plays a key role in the context of hyperstatistics, since
it quantifies memory effects within a given system, which are in turn associated with
the long-tailed decays discussed in the present work. In this regard, as an additional
direct application of hyperstatistics, we refer to the Griffiths-like phase [32–34]. The
latter is characterized by a long-tail exponential decay of the auto-correlation func-
tion [35], which is captured by magnetization measurements as a function of time in
the vicinity of the transition temperature, see, e.g., Ref. [36]. Yet, hyperstatistics is in
line with the observation of non-Boltzmann-Gibbsian statistics right at critical points
[37, 38], being that the block size may be seen as analogous to the domains composing
the system.
4 Discussions and applications of hyperstatistics
In what follows, we demonstrate the applicability of hyperstatistics by presenting
experimental results for the discharge of a capacitor considering a γ-type distribution
of relaxation times; the pressure decay over time upon pumping 4He lines of a cryostat;
fits of the midrapidity data for p-Pb collisions at the LHC; and acceleration in turbu-
lent systems, as well as the deduction of the universal power-law dielectric response
of non-homogeneous systems. Astonishingly, our approach accurately captures the
evolvement of all these experimental data set. It is worth emphasizing that Beck and

a broad distribution of velocities. As a matter of fact, since the system is kept at
room-temperature, gas particles already exhibit a distribution of thermal velocities.

quite accurate. Instead, it is appropriate to consider a distribution of velocities of the
gas particles being pumped. Hence, based on such physical arguments, a distribution
of effective escape velocities can be naturally inferred, which makes our proposal to
properly describe the behaviour of the pressure versus time, cf. bottom panel of Fig. 3.

resistor since it hinders the gas flow through the system, so that the flow of electrons in
the RC circuit upon discharging the capacitor is analogous to the flow of gas particles
during the pumping of the 4He lines. Note that for the pressure decay experimental

suggesting that the pressure decays more slowly during the pumping process than

4.3 Hyperstatistics and turbulence: Bodenschatz experiment
Hyperstatistics can be straightforwardly applied to the acceleration of a fluid particle
in a turbulent flow in the frame of the Bodenschatz experiment [50]. Essentially, the
acceleration of a fluid particle in turbulent flows arises from pressure gradients [50],
being properly described within the framework of Kolmogorov’s scaling theory [27, 28].
In simple words, in such a theory a domain is considered homogeneous when the PDF
does not depend on spatial coordinates, time, or velocity [27, 28]. In our approach,
based on Fig. 2, we consider a non-homogeneous domain characterized by a distri-
bution of fluid particle accelerations. Figure 5 presents experimental data extracted
from Ref. [50] showing the PDF versus the transverse acceleration normalized by its
standard deviation for distinct Reynolds numbers. Note that, within the framework
of hyperstatistics, the turbulence data shown in Fig. 5 can be precisely fitted using a
expq (−⟨β⟩E), cf. Table 1, regardless of the PDF considered. In contrast, within the
superstatistics framework, a proper fit of the data set depicted in Fig. 5 requires the
choice of the underlying PDF, cf. Fig. 3.2 in Ref. [5]. This is because the author in
Ref. [5] employed the superstatistics approach to consider a distribution of probabili-
ties. In the frame of our approach, however, the distribution of Boltzmann factors in
the scale of domains already incorporates a distribution of probabilities. The proper
use of the hyperstatistics formalism to turbulent systems, together with the other
applications discussed in previous sections, highlights that the hyperstatistics frame-
work can be applied to a wide variety of physical systems. Yet, using Bq(E), we can
analyze how the value of q is affected by distinct turbulence data sets corresponding
to different Reynolds numbers (Supplementary Section 7). In terms of the so-called
Taylor microscale Reynolds number Rλ, the range 140 < Rλ < 970 corresponds to
Reynolds numbers Re 1300 < Re < 63000 [50], which is indeed in the turbulent regime.
Upon analyzing the experimental results depicted in Fig. 5, the data set is properly fit-
ted by making use of the hyperstatistics approach. Note that the experimental results
depicted in Fig. 5 refer to an overlap of the data for various values of Rλ, being the
obtained value of q slightly modified upon analyzing distinct Rλ values independently
(Supplementary Section 7).

5 Conclusions and outlook
We have introduced the Γ(n, q) function as the Mellin transform of the q-exp function.
We have fixed the recurrence relation and convergence conditions of Γ(n, q). As the
highlight of the present work, we have proposed a new framework, named by us hyper-
statistics, upon considering non-Boltzmann-Gibbsian-type statistics for each domain
of the system, whose corresponding entropic functional is the one proposed in Ref. [2].
We have probed our approach experimentally upon considering a γ-type distribution
of relaxation times in the discharge process of a real capacitor in a standard series
RC circuit, the pumping of the 4He circulation lines in a cryostat, midrapidity data
set for p-Pb collisions at the LHC, as well as acceleration in turbulent systems. Also,
we have deduced the power-law-type dielectric response function of non-homogenous
systems [44, 52]. Beyond the specific examples discussed here, hyperstatistics provides
a unified description for relaxation and response functions in complex systems where
Boltzmann-Gibbs statistics is no longer applicable. This includes critical phenomena,
response functions, e.g., dielectric response, non-homogeneous systems, among oth-
ers. Our approach can be also employed to other scenarios, such as in cases involving
non-Gaussian distributions, e.g., temperature fluctuations in the cosmic microwave
background [53], luminosity calibration in charged particle colliders [54], power-law in
both velocity distributions [55] and trapped ions [56], as well as for the standard map
[57] and quantum decays [58–60].

Methods
The RC electrical circuit consists of a resistor with R = 1 MΩand an electrolytic
aluminium capacitor of C = 470 µF supplied by JWCO group, so that τ0 = 470 s. We
have employed a Keithley 617 electrometer as a voltage source and a nanovoltmeter
Keithley 2182A. The data set shown in the bottom panel of Fig. 3 was recorded using a
MercuryiTC controller provided by Oxford Instruments and the pumping was carried
out employing a Hi-Cube vacuum pump supplied by Pfeiffer attached to the 4He
circulation lines of a Teslatron-PT cryostat from Oxford Instruments.
