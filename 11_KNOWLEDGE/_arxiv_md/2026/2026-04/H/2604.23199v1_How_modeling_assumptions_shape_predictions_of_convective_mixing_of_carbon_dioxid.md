Abstract
We investigate how models of fluid properties and boundary conditions influence predictions
of convective mixing in confined porous media, with relevance to subsurface carbon diox-
ide storage. Using high-resolution simulations at high Rayleigh-Darcy numbers (O(104)),
we analyze miscible fluids with linear, nonlinear, and non-monotonic density-concentration
relationships under fixed- and free-interface in 2D and 3D. We show that, across all cases,
mixing is governed by the mean scalar dissipation, providing a unifying framework for con-
vective–diffusive interactions. The density-concentration relationship affects mixing via the
effective density contrast driving convection and the position of the maximum density. Free
interfaces enhance early-time mixing through deformation, while long-term behavior de-
pends on fluid properties and dimensionality.
We demonstrate that simplified modeling
assumptions (e.g., monotonic density laws or 2D flow) can lead to deviations in predicted
mixing rates of up to O(10–100)%. These results offer guidance for model selection and
improving predictions of convective mixing in geophysical systems.
Plain Language Summary
When carbon dioxide is injected underground for long-term storage, it can dissolve
into salty groundwater and mix through a process driven by small density differences. This
mixing process helps trap the carbon dioxide safely, but predicting how fast it occurs is
challenging because it depends on how we model the fluids and their interactions. In this
study, we use numerical simulations to explore how different assumptions—such as how
fluid density depends on concentration, whether the interface between fluids can move, and
whether the flow is modeled in two or three dimensions—affect mixing. We find that mixing
is controlled by how quickly concentration differences are smoothed out, which is quantified
by a quantity called “scalar dissipation.” Our results show that some common simplifications
can lead to inaccurate predictions. For example, allowing the interface between fluids to
move can speed up mixing early on, while realistic three-dimensional flows can behave
differently from simpler two-dimensional models. We also identify which fluid properties
lead to faster mixing. These findings help improve predictions of carbon dioxide storage
in underground formations and provide guidance for designing models of mixing in natural
systems.
1 Introduction
Convective mixing in porous media plays a central role in a wide range of geophysical
processes, including the long-term storage of carbon dioxide (CO2) in subsurface geological
formations (Schrag, 2007; Szulczewski et al., 2013). When CO2 dissolves into brine, small
density differences arise, which can trigger buoyancy-driven instabilities, enhancing mixing
and accelerating dissolution (Huppert & Neufeld, 2014; Emami-Meybodi et al., 2015). This
process is widely recognized as a key mechanism controlling the security and efficiency of
geological CO2 storage. A distinctive feature of CO2–brine systems is the non-monotonic
dependence of density on solute concentration, with the density of the mixture exceeding
that of both pure fluids, leading to complex and potentially counter-intuitive flow dynamics
(Hidalgo et al., 2015; Imuetinyan et al., 2026).
Previous studies have established that mixing in such systems results from the in-
terplay between diffusion and convection, often quantified through the mean scalar dissi-
pation (Hidalgo et al., 2012).
Mixing rates depend sensitively on fluid properties, such
as the density-concentration relationship, and on the configuration of the system, includ-
ing boundary conditions and interface dynamics (Hewitt et al., 2013). In particular, free
(mobile) interfaces between CO2 and brine can enhance mixing through deformation and
entrainment, while stable density contrasts may suppress interface motion. However, most
existing studies have considered simplified configurations, typically assuming monotonic
density laws (De Paoli, Zonta, et al., 2025), fixed interfaces (Hewitt et al., 2013; A. Slim,

2014), or two-dimensional flow (Hidalgo et al., 2012, 2015), and their combined impact on
mixing predictions remains unclear. A key open question is therefore how modeling as-
sumptions (such as the choice of density-concentration relationship, boundary conditions,
and flow dimensionality) affect predictions of convective mixing in realistic geophysical set-
tings. This issue is particularly relevant for CO2 storage, where fluid composition, chemical
interactions, and geological conditions can alter density profiles and flow behavior (Thomas
et al., 2016). Understanding the sensitivity of mixing dynamics to these factors is essential
for developing reliable predictive models.
In this work, we address this gap by systematically investigating convective mixing in
confined porous media using high-resolution numerical simulations. We consider both fixed-
and free-interface configurations, linear and non-monotonic density-concentration relation-
ships, and two- and three-dimensional flows at high Rayleigh-Darcy numbers, and obtain a
unique database that we make available (De Paoli & Pirozzoli, 2026). Our analysis reveals a
unifying framework in which mixing dynamics are controlled by the mean dissipation, while
fluid properties and interface dynamics determine the efficiency and temporal evolution of
mixing. The results provide new physical insight into buoyancy-driven mixing and quantify
the limitations of commonly adopted modeling simplifications. More broadly, they offer
practical guidelines for selecting appropriate physical models and fluid properties to opti-
mize mixing in applications such as geological CO2 storage and other subsurface transport
processes.

3 Fixed interface system
We consider the system in Figure 1(a-ii), in which the boundary conditions correspond
to Equation (8). Here we briefly recall the flow dynamics, previously discussed for the linear
case (see De Paoli, Zonta, et al. (2025) and references therein), and explore the role of

3.1 Flow dynamics
The flow dynamics is characterized by three main regimes (A. C. Slim et al., 2013;
A. Slim, 2014; Hewitt et al., 2013; Hidalgo et al., 2015; De Paoli et al., 2017; De Paoli,
Zonta, et al., 2025).
During the first regime, the domain is initially saturated with fluid at C = 0, with a
constant concentration (density) at the top equal to C = 0 (ρ = 1). A diffusive boundary
layer develops at the top and thickens, eventually becoming unstable and leading to the
formation of finger-like structures (Figures S3a,b,c,d-i). These fingers promote downward
transport of high-concentration fluid, increasing the bulk concentration.
Once developed, these fingers grow at a nearly constant speed, and the flow enters
the second regime. In this convection-dominated phase, fingers grow vertically, increasing
the bulk concentration (see Figures S3e,f). The 2D and 3D dynamics are similar (compare
Figures S4a,b and Figures S4c,d), with slightly faster finger growth in 3D. However, the ρ(C)
considered influence the finger growth rate: the fluid density increases more rapidly in the

4 Free interface system
We consider a system defined by the boundary conditions (9), initialized with two layers
of different concentration (see Text S3 in Supporting Information S1). The interface between
the layers moves as mixing occurs. We analyze flow and mixing by disentangling the effects
of density contrast between the two layers (β), shape parameter (α), and dimensionality.
4.1 Flow dynamics
The flow dynamics closely follows the evolution discussed in Section 3.1. Initially, the
flow is diffusion-controlled, and a high-concentration, high-density layer develops at the
fluid–fluid interface. When this layer becomes sufficiently thick and unstable, finger-like
structures form and merge into larger, persistent fingers, which transport solute downward
in a quasi-steady fashion, increasing the bulk concentration below the interface. This process
ends when the concentration distribution in the vicinity of the interface increases, reducing
the driving force of the system, i.e., the concentration contrast between the upper layer and
the near-interface boundary layer. The additional degree of freedom in this configuration,
associated with the mobile and deformable interface, controls the flow evolution and mixing.
We first consider the effect of the parameter α, which indicates how close the concen-
tration of the top layer, C+ = α/(1 −α), is to the interfacial concentration (Cm = 0). To
this purpose, the evolution of B4, B5, and B6 is shown in Figures S7(a), S7(b), and S7(c),
respectively, for a fixed value of density contrast (β = 1.5). We observe that the smaller α
(and hence C+), the faster the flow evolution: small values of C+ produce larger density
differences for the same concentration difference. To obtain the same density difference,
which drives convective motions and mixing, smaller concentration gradients are required
when α is low, resulting in faster mixing. In these cases, the interface is weakly deformed
and nearly independent of α.
To analyze the effect of the density contrast (β), we fix C+ = 0.7 (α = 0.4) and
consider simulations C4–C6 reported in Figures S7(d–f). The interface is strongly affected
by the density contrast: the larger the density difference (and hence β), the more stable
the interface.
It is harder for the fluid to deform the interface when ρm −ρ−is large.

4.2.2
Convection-dominated dynamics
The diffusive regime is followed by the thickening of the interfacial boundary layer,
which eventually becomes unstable, leading to the formation of fingers.
This process is
first characterized by an increase in dissipation, which departs from the diffusive behavior
(0.1 < t < 0.2), due to the formation of fingers that carry solute away from the interface.
Later (0.2 < t < 0.3), fingers interact and reorganize; the flux diminishes, and fingers
eventually merge, forming larger descending structures during a phase of slowly decreasing
dissipation, similarly to what observed in Figure S5(b). In the Rayleigh–Taylor–Darcy case,
in contrast, the dissipation increases with time (De Paoli, Yerragolam, et al., 2025; De Paoli
et al., 2019).
We analyze in Figure 2(b) the local dissipation |∇C|2 for simulation B3 at different
times. High values of dissipation are mostly localized in two distinct regions: at the bound-
aries of the fingers (Gopalakrishnan et al., 2017) and at the fluid–fluid interface.
The
interfacial contribution is by far more dominant than that associated with the fingers, as
shown in Figure 2(b). As suggested by Hidalgo et al. (2015), the interface is compressed

interface deformation occurring in 2D. We confirm here the same dynamics, see for instance
simulations C1 and C4, for which α = 0.4 and β = 0. In this case, the interface is much
more deformed in 2D than in 3D (see a direct comparison in Figure S11), and as a result
the dissipation is larger in 2D (Figure 2c-i). However, the effect of dimensionality on the
evolution of mixing is difficult to characterize due to its transient nature: for instance, C1
exhibits smaller dissipation than C4 prior to the convection-dominated phase. Therefore, for
a thorough description of the role of dimensionality, α and β should be considered together
with specific values of time or degree of mixing. Details are provided in Section 5.
4.2.3
Shutdown dynamics
When the concentration in the bulk increases, and so does in the boundary layer below
the interface, the driving progressively reduces. As a result, mixing (quantified by χ in
Figure 2c-i) also decreases. The time at which this convective shutdown occurs is 7 < ts <
9, and it also marks a different distribution of low/high dissipation regions in the flow.
Large concentration gradients, indicating large values of |∇C|2 and initially localized at the
finger boundaries and in the interfinger interfacial regions (Figure 2b-i,b-ii), are now mostly
concentrated at the roots of the fingers (Figure 2b-iii).
The key role of interfacial dynamics has stimulated the development of physical models
capable of predicting its behavior and, simultaneously, describing mixing and bulk concen-
tration. The model proposed by Hewitt et al. (2013), which we test in detail in Text S5.3
in Supporting Information S1, provides an excellent prediction of the fixed-interface system
and also of the free-interface system, but only in the presence of partially miscible fluids and
large values of C+, when the interface remains mostly flat and the corresponding boundary
layer thickness is uniform. In our case, in contrast, fluids are fully miscible, and the interface
topology evolves following a complex dynamics that this model fails to capture.

5 Discussion and conclusions
The dynamics during the shutdown regime is key to predicting the long-term flow
behavior and fluid mixing, as it is, by far, responsible for the major proportion (50% to 80%)
of overall mixing. This is quantified via the degree of mixing in the main panel of Figure 2(c-
iv), which depends on the history of the system. Therefore, it is difficult to interpret the
late-time flow dynamics without accounting for the previous phases contributing to mixing.
To analyze and compare the role of different effects, we consider the time t% required to
achieve a prescribed degree of mixing M%.
For instance, t30% corresponds to the time
required to reach M = 0.3.
The results reported in Figure 3 highlight the role of modeling assumptions (monotonic
ρ(C), 2D, fixed interface) relative to the most complete model considered here (3D, non-
monotonic ρ(C), free interface). The following effects emerge:
i) Interface model: Despite the dependence of M(t) on α and β, mixing is initially
more efficient in the free-interface systems (Figure 2c-iv). However, at long times
this is, in general, no longer true and depends on the flow parameters. 3D results in
Figure 3(a,b) confirm this, indicating that t30% is smaller for the free-interface case
compared to the fixed-interface case. For higher degrees of mixing (t50%, t70%), the
threshold value for which the free- and fixed-interface systems exhibit the same t%
depends on the fluid parameters (α, β), with α playing a leading role.
ii) Dimensionality: The onset of convection occurs earlier in 2D than in 3D (see Fig-
ure 2c-i), and this effect is clearly reflected in the time ratio at t1% reported in
Figure 3(c). The dissipation during the convection-dominated phase may be higher
in 3D than in 2D, or vice versa (e.g., for α = 0.5 and β = 0, in agreement with
Imuetinyan et al. (2026)), depending on the fluid parameters (α, β) (see Figure 2c-ii).
The cumulative mixing in these regimes yields a complex, time-dependent picture:
for all times considered (t20%–t70%, Figure 3c), two distinct regions of the parameter
space (α, β) exist in which mixing may be faster (up to 20%) or slower (up to 6%) in
3D than in 2D.
iii) Fluid properties: The most effective parameter combination for faster mixing can
be derived from Figures 3(a,b). The dominant parameter is α: the smaller α, the
sooner a given degree of mixing is achieved, in agreement with previous observations

by Hidalgo et al. (2012); Soboleva (2025).
However, for intermediate times (e.g.,

density law considered in the fixed-interface case may substantially influence mixing
at intermediate stages (t30%, t40%), while no major difference is observed for larger
degrees of mixing (t70%).
One simple outcome of this work is that optimal conditions for mixing are achieved for small
α, β (see Figures 3(a,b)), for which the interface is highly deformed.
The results presented in this work, together with the unique database we make freely
available to the community (De Paoli & Pirozzoli, 2026), provide a tool to understand
the role of different numerical modeling assumptions. In addition, they can help interpret
experiments involving miscible or partially miscible fluids in porous media (Backhaus et al.,
2011; Neufeld et al., 2010; Imuetinyan et al., 2024a, 2024b; Xu et al., 2026).
5.1 Limitations and future developments
When compared to convective mixing in geological CO2 sequestration sites, the condi-
tions considered in the present study are idealized in several aspects, including the assump-
tions of homogeneity (Chen & Yan, 2015; Hidalgo et al., 2022; Hu & Yang, 2024), absence
of fractures (Keim & Class, 2025; Ulloa et al., 2025), dispersion (Hidalgo & Carrera, 2009;
De Paoli, Yerragolam, et al., 2025; De Paoli et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2018; Wen et al.,
2018), and chemical reactions (De Wit, 2004; Jotkar et al., 2019; De Wit, 2020; Strauch et
al., 2025). Moreover, fluids are considered fully miscible, whereas CO2 and brine are only
partially miscible. As a result, the large density gradient across the resulting CO2–brine
interface (corresponding to a large β in the present model) remains sharp, inducing a nearly
flat interface behavior (see, for instance, simulation E7 in Figure 1b-vii). Partially misci-
ble fluids have been modeled in 2D by Li et al. (2022, 2023) using a phase-field method
(Darcy–Cahn–Hilliard). When comparing miscible and partially miscible cases, they ob-
served that in the convective phase mixing is higher in the miscible case, and convective
shutdown occurs earlier. Their results suggest that any extension of findings from miscible
to partially miscible systems should be treated with caution. A possible development of this
work therefore consists in analyzing partially miscible systems and investigating the role of
dimensional effects on flow and mixing evolution.
