When structure does not imply symmetry
Skyler R. St. Pierre1, Thibault Vervenne2, Ethan C. Darwin1, Ellen Kuhl1
1Department of Mechanical Engineering, Stanford University, Stanford, CA 94305, United States
2Department of Mechanical Engineering, KU Leuven, Leuven, Belgium
Fungal protein materials exhibit inherently anisotropic microstructures formed by networks of
hyphae, which suggest a natural pathway to replicate the fibrous texture of animal meat. We probe
whether this structural anisotropy translates into macroscopic mechanical and sensory anisotropy.
Using orthogonal tension, compression, and shear experiments on three fungi-based materials, we
identify distinct symmetry classes that range from strongly anisotropic to effectively isotropic be-
havior. Automated model discovery reveals that fiber-dependent invariants emerge only when me-
chanically relevant, and enables direct identification of material symmetry from data. These results
demonstrate that microstructural anisotropy does not universally imply anisotropic mechanics or
perception and establish a data-driven framework to infer symmetry in complex soft materials.
When
structure
does
not
imply
symmetry.
Continuum theories often assume that microstructural
alignment dictates the symmetry of the mechanical
response [13].
From early theories of rubber elasticity
[2] to modern descriptions of fiber-reinforced solids [3],
scientists have linked macroscopic behavior to underlying
microstructure through invariant-based constitutive laws
[4]. In this framework, anisotropy arises when internal
architecture introduces preferred directions that break
rotational symmetry and produce direction-dependent
stiffness, strength, and deformation. Invariant-based for-
mulations express this behavior through scalar measures
[8], I1, the total stretch, I2, shear-like distortion, and I3,
volume change, for the isotropic response, and I4, the
fiber stretch squared and I5, nonlinear fiber stretch for
the anisotropic response [7].
This paradigm motivates
the widespread assumption that visible structural align-
ment implies anisotropic mechanical symmetry.
Yet,
many soft materials, including polymers with oriented
molecular chains [7], cartilage with collagen networks [8],
and brain with axonal fiber bundles [9], challenge this
assumption:
microstructural
organization
does
not
always translate into direction-dependent macroscopic
response.
Model system—Fungal protein materials provide a
compelling system to test this assumption [10].
Their
microstructure consists of interconnected hyphae that
form hierarchical networks with pronounced directional
organization across multiple length scales [11], from
micron-scale filaments to millimeter-scale assemblies
(Fig. 1).
These architectures resemble classical fiber-
reinforced materials and suggest an inherent capacity
for anisotropic mechanical behavior, consistent with
established descriptions of fungal ultrastructure and cell
wall organization [12]. At the same time, these materials
have emerged as promising alternatives to animal meat,
where fibrous texture governs sensory perception and
consumer acceptance [13]. This dual role places fungal
systems at the intersection of soft matter physics,
biological materials, and food science.
This raises a
central question: does anisotropic microstructure nec-
essarily produce anisotropic mechanical response—and
does this response control perceived fibrousness? More
fundamentally, does structure dictate symmetry, or does
symmetry only emerge through the response?
Experimental strategy—To address this question, we
combine controlled experiments with data-driven model
discovery to probe how mechanical symmetry emerges
in structured soft materials.
We study three fungi-
based protein systems derived from distinct structural
origins–mushroom root mycelium, the fruiting body, and
a protein mycelium blend—which provide a spectrum
of microstructural organization and symmetry classes.
We prepare samples in two orthogonal orientations
relative to the dominant structural direction: in-plane,
parallel to the apparent fiber alignment, and cross-plane,
perpendicular to it.
For each material and direction,
we perform n = 10 tension, compression, and shear
experiments up to 10% strain, which enables direct
comparison across loading modes and orientations [9].

Continuum framework—We interpret all experiments
within a finite-deformation continuum framework that
links the microstructural architecture to the macroscopic
response
through
invariant-based
constitutive
laws.
We characterize deformation through the deformation
gradient and construct scalar invariants that encode the
symmetry of the response [13].
In isotropic materials,
the strain energy depends solely on three invariants,
the total stretch I1, shear-like distortion I2, and volume
change I3. In anisotropic materials, additional invariants
like the fiber stretch squared I4 or nonlinear fiber stretch
I5 introduce preferred directions and reduce symmetry.
Classical invariant theory provides the formal basis for
this representation [8], while modern formulations of
fiber-reinforced soft materials extend it to biological and
engineered systems [2]. In this framework, the selection
of invariants determines the effective material symme-
try. Here, instead of a priori prescribing the symmetry
group, we discover symmetry through automated model
discovery using a constitutive neural network [3] that
expresses the strain energy as a sparse combination of
invariant-based terms [5].

Conclusion—We show that anisotropic microstructure
in fungal protein materials does not universally translate
into anisotropic mechanics or perception. While fibrous
architecture often suggests directional behavior, this link
is not universal: similar filamentous networks can exhibit
distinct nonlinear responses [18, 19], structure alone
fails to predict dynamics in disordered systems [20],
and symmetry can emerge from mechanics rather than
geometry itself [21].
Instead, the mechanical response
defines effective material symmetry, and data-driven
model discovery identifies the correct symmetry class.
These results challenge the assumption that structure
determines symmetry and show that visual anisotropy
can mask fundamentally isotropic behavior.
Similar
discrepancies arise in polymeric solids [2], hydrated
soft matter [8], and brain tissue [9].
This framework
provides a general route to identify governing sym-
metries—defined through invariant-based constitutive
response—and shows that structure alone cannot predict
macroscopic behavior.

SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL
In this study,
we test three fungi-based materials:
mycelium, fruiting body, and a protein mycelium blend.
The materials originate from commercially available
products (Meati Foods, Boulder, CO; OMNI, New York,
NY; Beyond, El Segundo, CA), but we refer to them here
solely by their structural origin to emphasize generality.
We test each material in two orthogonal directions, the
in-plane direction, parallel to the plane of the steak, and
the cross-plane direction, perpendicular to the plane
(Fig. 2).
Microscopy—We cut each material in multiple orien-
tations and then freeze the samples in Optimal Cutting
Temperature compound (Fisher Scientific, Waltham,
MA). We cryosection 40 µm thin slices and then image
the samples with a Leica SP8 microscope (Leica Mi-
crosystems, Wetzlar, Germany) with 10× magnification
under white light (Fig. 3).
Test sample preparation—For each material and each
direction, in-plane and cross-plane, we prepare samples
for quasi-static tension, compression, and shear testing
with n = 10 samples each, resulting in a total of n = 180
tests. For tension testing, we cut rectangular samples of
10×10×20 mm both in-plane, parallel to the apparent
fiber alignment, and cross-plane, perpendicular to it.
For compression and shear testing, we use a biopsy
punch to extract cylindrical samples of 8 mm diameter
and 10 mm height, both in-plane and cross-plane.
Tension,
compression and shear testing—For the
tension tests (Fig. 4, first column), we use an Instron
5848 (Instron, Canton, MA) and glue the samples to
glass slides, which we mount in custom testing grips [9].
We mount the samples and extend them to a stretch of
λ = 1.1 at a stretch rate of ˙λ = 0.002/s, which translates
to a total loading time of t = 50 s. For the compression
and shear tests (Fig. 4, second column), we use an
HR20 discovery hybrid rheometer (TA Instruments,
New Castle, DE) and mount the samples between a
40 mm diameter base plate and a 8 mm diameter parallel
plate, both sandblasted to avoid slippage [14]. For the
compression tests, we mount the samples and compress
them to a stretch of λ = 0.9 at a stretch rate of ˙λ =
0.002/s, which translates to a total loading time of t =
50 s. For the shear tests, we mount the samples, fix them
at λ = 0.9 compression, and then shear them to a shear
strain of γ = 0.1 at a shear rate of ˙γ = 0.002/s, which
translates to a total loading time of t = 50 s.
Across
all testing modes, we hold the samples with a small
pre-load force between 0.01-0.05 N. For each material,
each direction, and each loading mode, we calculate
the mean and standard error of the mean across all
n = 10 tests and illustrate the mean as solid curve and
the error as the shaded region around it (Fig. 4). The
tension, compression, and shear tests serve as the basis
for the linear mechanical analysis and for the nonlinear
automated model discovery.
Mechanical analysis—To characterize the linear elastic
behavior, we perform a linear regression on the quasi-
static tension, compression, and shear data throughout
10% tension, 10% compression and 10% shear to extract
the compressive, shear, and mean stiffnesses [9].
We
postulate a linear stress-strain relation, σ = E · ε, and
determine the tensile stiffness, Eten = (ε · σ)/(ε · ε),
from the recorded strain-stress pairs {ε; σ} using linear
regression.
We use the same method to determine
the compressive stiffness Ecom.
Similarly, we postu-
late a linear shear stress-strain relation, τ
= µ · γ,
convert the shear modulus µ into the shear stiffness,
Eshr = 2 [ 1 + ν ] µ = 3 µ, and determine the shear
stiffness, Eshr = 3 (γ · ˆτ)/(γ · γ), from the recorded
shear strain-stress pairs {γ; τ}. Finally, we average the
stiffnesses in tension, compression, and shear to obtain
the mean stiffness, Emean = (Eten + Ecom + Eshr)/3.

Sensory survey—For the sensory survey, we heat the
plain fungi-based steaks, without spices or condiments,
and prepare bite-sized samples. In accordance with our
previous studies [1, 9, 10, 14], we recruited n = 16,
n = 21, and n = 16 participants to participate in the
Food Texture Survey to characterize mycelium from
Mushroom Root Classic Steak (Meati Foods, Boulder,
CO), fruiting-body from Lion’s Mane Mushroom Steak
(OMNI, New York, NY), and protein mycelium from
Beyond Steak Filet (Beyond, El Segundo, CA). We
instruct participants to eat samples of the product
and rank its texture features on a 5-point Likert scale
with twelve questions. Each question starts with “this
food is ...”, following by established sensory texture
descriptors [6, 11]:
soft, hard, brittle, chewy, gummy,
viscous, springy, sticky, fibrous, fatty, moist, meaty. The
scale ranges from 5 for strongly agree to 1 for strongly
disagree. The participants first eat and rank the steak
samples in the cross-plane direction, with the fiber
direction perpendicular to the chewing direction, and
then in the in-plane direction, with the fiber direction
parallel to the chewing direction. Between the samples,
we ask participants to cleanse their palate with water
to minimize residual flavor, neutralize their taste, and
standardize the sensory environment.
We follow a
fixed evaluation protocol consistent with our prior
studies [1, 9, 10, 14] to enable direct comparison across
datasets. This research was reviewed and approved by
the Institutional Review Board at Stanford University
under the protocol IRB-75418.
