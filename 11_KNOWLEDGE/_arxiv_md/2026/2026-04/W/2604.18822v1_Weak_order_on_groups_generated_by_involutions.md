WEAK ORDER ON GROUPS GENERATED BY INVOLUTIONS
FABRICIO DOS SANTOS, CHRISTOPHE HOHLWEG, AND ALEKSANDR TRUFANOV
Abstract. In this article, we propose to initiate the general study of involu-
tion systems. An involution system, that is, a group W generated by a set of
involutions S, is naturally endowed with a weak order arising from orienting
the Cayley graph of (W, S). In the case of a Coxeter system (W, S), Bj¨orner
showed that the weak order is a complete meet-semilattice. This fact has many
important consequences for Coxeter systems and their related structures. In
this article, we discuss the following question: For which involution systems is
the weak order a complete meet-semilattice?
The class of involution systems that satisfies this condition is larger than the
class of Coxeter systems (it contains, for instance, Cactus groups). In the case
of an involution system with sign character, we provide a finite presentation
by generators and relations and a classification in rank 3. We also obtain new
characterizations of Coxeter systems in terms of the weak order, and prove a
number of results on certain subclasses of these involution systems. Finally, we
discuss further works and open problems in relation to biautomatic structures,
geometric representations, mediangle graphs, and more.

In this article, we propose to initiate the general study of involution systems.
Involution systems are naturally endowed with the weak order (W, ≤R), a poset
defined by u ≤R v if a reduced word for u is a prefix of a reduced word for v, or
equivalently, if a geodesic in the (right) Cayley graph of (W, S) from the identity
e to v passes through u. In the case of a Coxeter system, Bj¨orner [4] showed that
the weak order is a complete meet-semilattice (for a proof, see [5, Theorem 3.2.1]).
This fact has many important consequences for Coxeter systems and their con-
nected structures, such as in the study of: the Tamari lattice, Cambrian fans and
generalized associahedra, see for instance [43]; 0-Hecke algebras in the case of the
symmetric group, see for instance [49] and the references therein; the represen-
tation theory of quivers and their torsion classes [19]; Garside families and Shi
arrangements; see for instance [18, 22] and the references therein; biautomaticity
for Coxeter systems [44, 21]; root systems in Lie theory and reflection ordering in
Kazhdan-Lusztig theory, see for instance [11, 24].
In this work, we consider the following question: For which involution systems
is the weak order a complete meet-semilattice?
We start by discussing involution systems in general and some of their basic
properties in §2. In particular, we discuss sign characters for an involution sys-
tem and define the notion of even involution systems (EIS for short), which are
involution systems admitting a sign character.
In §3, we describe the weak order on involution systems. Moreover, we give many
examples of meet involution systems (MIS for short), i.e., involution systems whose
weak order is a complete meet-semilattice.
In particular, we prove that cactus
groups, and more generally, involution systems with median Cayley graphs [28]
(called right-angled mock reflection groups in [48]) are MIS.
We then restrict our attention in §4 to even meet involution systems (EMIS for
short), i.e., involution systems that are both EIS and MIS, and prove our first
main result.
Theorem 1.1 (Theorem 4.1). Any involution system (W, S) with a sign charac-
ter whose weak order is a complete meet-semilattice (i.e., an EMIS) admits the
following finite presentation:
W = ⟨S | r2 = e, ss1 · · · sk = tt1 · · · tk ∀r ∈S and s, t ∈S bounded in (W, ≤R) ⟩,
where ss1 · · · sk = tt1 · · · tk are reduced words for the join s∨R t of bounded s, t ∈S.
The proof is based on the study of irreducible cycles in the Cayley graph G of
an EMIS. These are elements of the cycle space of G (or, equivalently, the first
singular homology of G over Z2) that cannot be decomposed into a sum of cycles
of strictly smaller length. In particular, we show in Proposition 4.10 that the set
of irreducible cycles of G weakly intersects, meaning that the intersection of any
two distinct irreducible cycles does not contain consecutive edges (in fact, we prove
something stronger in Corollary 4.13). Then, to prove Theorem 1.1, we show that
the relations of an EMIS are given by the words labeling all irreducible cycles

Theorem 1.2 (Corollary 4.4). Let (W, S) be an involution system. Then the fol-

(1) (W, S) is a Coxeter system;
(2) (W, S) is an EMIS and for any s, t ∈S bounded, the reduced words

We also introduce in §4 the notions of companion graph and Coxeter companion
of an EMIS (W, S).
In particular, the companion graph of (W, S) is a graph
encoding for each pair s, t ∈S the length of the unique irreducible cycle containing
the edges {e, s} and {e, t} in the Cayley graph of (W, S). The Coxeter companion of
(W, S) is then defined as the Coxeter system having as Coxeter type the companion
graph of (W, S).
We also define the Coxeter type of an involution system in a
similar way as for a Coxeter system (see Definition 2.5). This leads to another new
characterization of Coxeter systems.
Theorem 1.3 (Corollary 4.18). Let (W, S) be an EMIS. Then (W, S) is a Coxeter
system if and only if its Coxeter type and companion graph are the same.
In §5, we introduce the notion of a 2-recognizable presentation (Definition 5.2) as
a means to describe the words appearing in Theorem 1.1. The idea is to encode the
weak intersection of irreducible cycles in terms of combinatorics of words. Roughly
speaking, a 2-recognizable is a presentation ⟨S | {s2 | s ∈S}∪R0⟩where the words
of R0 cannot overlap by two consecutive letters. This condition can be rephrased
in terms of small cancellation theory (over free products) [38], see Remark 5.5.
Unfortunately, it is not the case that every 2-recognizable involution system (i.e.,
an involution system given by a 2-recognizable presentation) is an EMIS, see §5.4
for a discussion. However, we show this is true for rank 3 in §6, and the proof
depends on the classification of 2-recognizable involution systems of rank 3 we

(1) (W, S) is an EMIS;
(2) (W, S) admits a 2-recognizable presentation;
(3) (W, S) admits one of the presentations in Theorem 1.4;
(4) (W, S) is a quasi-Coxeter system.

2. Involution systems
An involution system is a pair (W, S) where W is a group with identity e and
S ⊆W is a set of involutions1 that generates W. The cardinality |S| is the rank of
(W, S). We say that the involution system (W, S) is finite (or spherical) if W is a
finite group.
Amongst examples of involution systems are Coxeter systems, which we discuss
in §2.3 below; see for instance [35, 5, 1] for more details on Coxeter systems. This
section is inspired by [23].
2.1. Length function. Let (W, S) be an involution system. The length on (W, S)
is the function ℓ(W,S) : W →N defined by ℓ(W,S)(e) = 0 and for w ̸= e,
ℓ(W,S)(w) = min{k ∈N∗| w = s1s2 · · · sk, si ∈S}.
If there is no possible confusion, we simply write ℓ:= ℓ(W,S).
Denote by S∗the free monoid on the alphabet S. For simplicity, we denote by
s1 . . . sk both the concatenation of letters in S and the product of generators of S
in W. A word s1 · · · sk in S∗is a reduced word for w ∈W if w = s1 . . . sk in W and
k = ℓ(w).
For simplicity, we say that w = s1 . . . sk is a reduced word.
We denote by
Red(W, S) ⊆S∗the set of all reduced words for (W, S) and by Redw(W, S) the set
of reduced words for w ∈W. Moreover, for u, v, w ∈W, we say that:
• w = uv is a reduced product if ℓ(w) = ℓ(u) + ℓ(v), i.e., the concatenation of any
reduced word for u with any reduced word for v is a reduced word for w;
• u is a prefix of w if a reduced word for u is a prefix of a reduced word for w;
• v is a suffix of w if a reduced word for v is a suffix of a reduced word for w.
Observe that if w = uv is reduced, then u is a prefix of w and v is a suffix of
w. The following straightforward proposition states basic properties for the length
function.
Proposition 2.1. Let (W, S) be an involution system and w, w′ ∈W.
(1) ℓ(w) = 1 if and only if w ∈S.
(2) The inverse of a reduced word is a reduced word: ℓ(w) = ℓ(w−1).
(3) ℓ(ww′) ≤ℓ(w) + ℓ(w′).
(4) ℓ(ww′) ≥|ℓ(w) −ℓ(w′)|.
(5) ℓ(w) −1 ≤ℓ(sw) ≤ℓ(w) + 1, for any s ∈S; or equivalently ℓ(w) −1 ≤
ℓ(ws) ≤ℓ(w) + 1.
(6) All prefixes and suffixes of a reduced word are reduced.
The left descent set DL(w) and right descent set DR(w) of an element of w ∈W
are defined by

2.2. Sign character and Even Involution Systems (EIS). It might happen
that, for some involution systems, ℓ(sw) = ℓ(w) for some w ∈W and s ∈S. For a
Coxeter system, it is well-known that ℓ(sw) = ℓ(w)±1 for all s ∈S and all w ∈W.
In fact, this is a consequence of the existence of a sign character for (W, S); see for
instance [23].

Proposition 2.2. Let (W, S) be an involution system. Then the following state-
ments are equivalent:
(1) (W, S) admits a sign character, that is, there is a group epimorphism ε :
W →{±1} such that ε(s) = −1 for all s ∈S;
(2) ℓ(sw) = ℓ(w) ± 1 for all w ∈W and s ∈S;
(3) ℓ(ws) = ℓ(w) ± 1 for all w ∈W and s ∈S.
In this case ε(w) = (−1)ℓ(w) for all w ∈W and the sign character is unique.
Proof. Assume there is a sign character for (W, S). Therefore ε(w) = (−1)ℓ(w) for
all w ∈W. In particular, for s ∈S and w ∈W, we have ϵ(sw) = ϵ(s)ϵ(w) =
−(−1)ℓ(w) = (−1)ℓ(w)±1. Therefore ℓ(w) ̸= ℓ(sw) and we conclude by Proposi-
tion 2.1 (5).
Conversely, assume that ℓ(sw) = ℓ(w)±1 for all w ∈W and s ∈S. We prove that
the function ε : W →{±1} defined by ε(w) = (−1)ℓ(w) is a group epimorphism.
Since ε(e) = 0 and ε(s) = −1 for all s ∈S, this function is surjective. Let w ∈W
and s ∈S, then:
ε(sw) = (−1)ℓ(sw) = (−1)ℓ(sw)±1 = −(−1)ℓ(w) = ε(s)ε(w).
We conclude that ε(uv) = ε(u)ε(v) by induction on ℓ(u). The equivalence between
(2) and (3) follows directly from Proposition 2.1.

Definition 2.3. An involution system (W, S) is an even involution system, or EIS
for short, if (W, S) has a sign character.
Any Coxeter system is an EIS. However, there are involution systems that do
not have a sign character: for instance the alternate group A5 generated by three
involutions as in §3.4 is not an EIS.
Proposition 2.4. Let (W, S) be an involution system and R ⊆S∗a set of relations,
i.e., W = ⟨S | R ⟩. Then (W, S) is an EIS if and only if any word w in R has an
even number of letters in S.
Proof. Assume that (W, S) is an EIS with sign character ε. Let w = s1 . . . sk ∈R,
then when considering w as an element of W, we have ε(w) = (−1)k = ε(e) = 1 by
Proposition 2.2. Hence k is even. Conversely, consider the function f : S →{±1}
defined by f(s) = −1. Consider the free group FS over S and recall that S∗is
a submonoid of FS. By the universal property of free groups, there is a unique
epimorphism ψ : FS →{±1} such that ψ(s) = −1 for all s ∈S. By assumption
R ⊂ker ψ. So there is a unique epimorphism ε : W →{±1} such that ε(s) = −1
for all s ∈S, which is a sign character by definition.
□

3. Weak order on involution systems
Let (W, S) be an involution system. Recall that the (right) Cayley graph2 of
(W, S) is the edge-labeled graph Cay(W, S) whose vertices are the elements of W
and the edges are {w, ws} with label s, where w ∈W and s ∈S. The Cayley graph
has a metric defined by:
d(W,S)(u, v) = ℓ(W,S)(u−1v).
As with the length, we denote d := d(W,S) if there is no possible confusion. More-
over, W acts by isometries on Cay(W, S) by left-multiplication. It is also known
that for any w ∈W the set of reduced words Redw(W, S) is in bijection with the
set of geodesics in Cay(W, S) from e to w; see for instance [26] for more details.
3.1. The weak order. We define the relation ≤R on W by:
u ≤R w ⇐⇒u is a prefix of w ⇐⇒u lies on a geodesic from e to w.

Proposition 3.1. The relation ≤R is a partial order on W with rank function
ℓ: W →N∗.

So any EMIS with median Cayley graph is mediangled; see §3.3 for the discussion
on median graphs and EMIS. Moreover, all examples of EMIS presented in this
article, and all the ones we have investigated so far, have mediangle Cayley graph.
This leads to the following question, which is the subject of ongoing research with
Tatiana Smirnova-Nagnibeda and Megan Howarth.
Question 6. If (W, S) is an EMIS, is Cay(W, S) a mediangle graph?
In [29, Definition 1.4], the only properties that one has to show to answer Ques-
tion 6 in the affirmative are the cycle condition and the intersection of even cycles
condition.
Let (W, S) be an EMIS. The intersection of even cycles condition can be restated
as the condition that the set of convex cycles weakly intersects.
With Tatiana
Smirnova-Nagnibeda and Megan Howarth, we already have a proof of this fact.
The cycle condition can be restated in terms of the weak order:
The cycle condition for an EMIS. (W, S) satisfies the cycle
condition if for any w ∈W and s ̸= t ∈S such that ws, wt ≤R w
the cycle containing w, ws, wt and ws ∧R wt is convex.
The isometry w−1 on Cay(W, S) maps a cycle containing w, ws, wt and ws∧R wt
to a cycle containing e, s, t and s ∨R t. So to show that (W, S) satisfies the cycle
condition one can show that Question 2 has a positive answer.
7.8. On CAT(0) cellular structures for EMIS. Every Coxeter group W acts
properly and cocompactly by isometries on a contractible CAT(0) piecewise Eu-
clidean cell complex Σ called the Davis complex of W [16, 42]. This has many
important consequences for the geometric and topological study of Coxeter groups.
This leads to the following question.
Question 7. Is it possible to construct an analog of the Davis complex for an
EMIS? Are EMIS CAT(0)?
In [14], the authors show that any bipartite mediangle graph can be endowed
with the structure of a contractible cell complex. Hence, if it is the case that Cayley
graphs of EMIS are mediangle, one would obtain such a contractible complex for
an EMIS. However, it is unclear if one can endow these complexes with a CAT(0)
metric.
7.9. On convex subgraphs of Coxeter systems. By [33], any median graph
can be realized as a convex subgraph of the Cayley graph of some right-angled
Coxeter system. One natural generalization of this result that Genevois asks in [29]
is: Which bipartite mediangle graphs can be realised as convex subgraphs of the
Cayley graph of some Coxeter system?
Even though we still don’t know if the Cayley graph of every EMIS is mediangle,
all examples of EMIS in this paper satisfy this property. That is, for any EMIS
(W, S) appearing in this work we can find some Coxeter system (W, S) such that
Cay(W, S) is a convex subgraph of Cay(W, S). This lead us to ask the following
question.

Question 8. Can the Cayley graph of any EMIS be realized as a convex subgraph
of the Cayley graph of some Coxeter system?
If the answer to Question 8 is positive, this would have notable consequences.
For instance, it would yield a positive answer to Question 5 and give the notion
of parabolic subgroups of EMIS. Another consequence would be the finiteness of
cone types of an EMIS, thus giving a positive answer to the first part of Question 3
and the question of the regularity of the language Red(W, S).
