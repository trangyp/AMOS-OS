When Reasoning Models Hurt Behavioral Simulation: A Solver-Sampler
Mismatch in Multi-Agent LLM Negotiation
Sandro Andric
sandro.andric@nyu.edu
Abstract
Behavioral simulation and strategic problem solving are dif-
ferent tasks. Large language models are increasingly be-
ing explored as agents in institutionally structured simu-
lations relevant to policy-facing use, but stronger reason-
ing does not necessarily improve behavioral sampling. In
the sharpest counterexample to the view that more reason-
ing improves simulation, DeepSeek native reasoning in the
grid-curtailment transfer reaches action entropy 1.256 and a
concession-arc rate of 0.933 yet still ends in authority de-
cision in 15 of 15 runs. A direct OpenAI extension shows
the same pressure at provider breadth: GPT-5.2 with na-
tive reasoning ends in authority decisions in 45 of 45 runs
across three negotiation environments. Budget-matched no-
reflection controls at 1024 tokens and two orthogonal private-
state controls remain rigid, while the negotiation-structured
scaffold condition stays compromise-heavy. These diagnos-
tics screen for clear sampler failure within a fixed negotiation
grammar; they do not establish external behavioral realism
or policy-forecasting validity. We study this solver-sampler
mismatch in three multi-agent negotiation environments: an
ambiguous fragmented-authority trading-limits scenario, an
ambiguous unified-opposition trading-limits scenario, and a
grid-curtailment case in emergency electricity management.
Across the two primary model families, native reasoning
and often no reflection collapse to authority-heavy outcomes,
while the negotiation-structured scaffold used here is the
only condition that consistently opens negotiated outcomes
alongside richer action distributions and concession behav-
ior. Gemini and GPT-5.2 run scaffold and native at matched
1024-token floors. The controls show that neither more out-
put space nor generic extra state rescues solver-like sampler
failure; only the negotiation-structured scaffold changes the
sampled distribution here. For responsible institutional simu-
lation, solver strength and sampler qualification are different
objectives: models should be qualified for the behavioral role
they are meant to play.
Introduction
Behavioral simulation and strategic problem solving are dif-
ferent tasks. A model that performs well on benchmarks re-
warding correct reasoning, long-horizon planning, or strate-
gic optimization may still be a poor instrument for simulat-
ing human behavior. The stakes are practical in institution-
ally structured simulation settings relevant to policy-facing
use: distorted outputs can shape judgments about gover-
nance, coordination, and intervention. Recent work increas-
ingly studies LLM agents in synthetic societies, negotia-
tion simulators, policy-analysis tools, and economic games
(Bonabeau 2002; Epstein and Axtell 1996; Aher, Arriaga,
and Kalai 2023; Horton 2023). In these settings, the prac-
tical goal is often not to discover the optimal strategy from
first principles. It is to sample a plausible distribution of ne-
gotiation trajectories under uncertainty and institutional con-
straint.
If the target population negotiates under uncertainty and
institutional constraint, then a model optimized for extended
reasoning may become misspecified as a simulator. Instead
of producing diverse, path-dependent trajectories shaped by
incomplete information, pressure, and timing, it may infer
the strategic structure too cleanly and converge on a narrow
policy. In a multi-agent setting, that shift can collapse the
very variation that simulation is supposed to reveal.
We study this tension as a measurement problem. Recent
work on generative social simulation argues that validation,
rather than raw capability, is the central challenge for this
entire research area (Larooij and T¨ornberg 2026; Collins,
Koehler, and Lynch 2024; Wallach et al. 2025; Zhou et al.
2024). We call the specific failure mode analyzed here the
solver-sampler mismatch: the mismatch between the capa-
bilities that make a model a stronger strategic solver and the
properties required for it to act as a good behavioral sam-
pler. Conceptually, solver-sampler mismatch is the tension
between optimizing for strategic resolution and matching a
target trajectory distribution for boundedly rational negoti-
ation under a fixed grammar. A good solver seeks domi-
nance, consistency, and efficiency. A good sampler, by con-
trast, must preserve behavioral variation under uncertainty.
It must allow agents to concede late, misread leverage, set-
tle on suboptimal but plausible arrangements, or fail to con-
verge at all. When those behaviors disappear, simulation can
become cleaner while becoming less behaviorally represen-
tative.
Sampler qualification is anchored in this negotiation
grammar and in published negotiated-rulemaking and bar-
gaining evidence, not in an abstract realism target.
From an AI ethics and governance perspective, that is
a measurement problem with downstream consequences: if
institutional simulations are used to explore policy choices,
organizational responses, or social risk, then clean but be-

The paper makes four contributions.
1. It formalizes the distinction between solver quality and
sampler qualification in multi-agent simulation.
2. It proposes a practical metric framework for behavioral

3. It presents evidence from three scenario-based, institu-

Why Solvers Are Not Necessarily Samplers
The core mistake in many LLM simulation pipelines is ob-
jective slippage. Simulation asks one question: what range
of plausible trajectories might agents produce under a struc-
tured set of conditions? Strategic problem solving asks an-
other: what is the best action sequence under those condi-
tions? These are related but not identical objectives.
If a model is rewarded or tuned for coherent long-horizon
optimization, it may reinterpret a simulation prompt as a task
to be solved. In multi-agent settings, this often manifests as:
• repeated use of the same dominant action
• suppressed concession behavior
• rapid convergence on a single normative interpretation of
the game
• failure to preserve the frictions, misalignments, and par-
tial commitments that create plausible negotiation trajec-
tories
For social or policy simulation, that is an instrument-
selection problem. A model can be a strong strategic rea-
soner and still be the wrong sampler for the negotiation be-
havior of interest. The practical question is which model
matches the behavioral target, not which one is smartest. A
strong decision-support model may be the wrong simulation
model, while a weaker but more behaviorally diverse model
may be the better simulator.
Behavioral Diversity, Concession Dynamics,
and Sampler Qualification
We evaluate behavioral sampling through behavioral diver-
sity, concession dynamics, and negotiation responsiveness.
Sampler qualification asks whether a model clears basic
checks against rigid, non-negotiated behavior within this
fixed negotiation grammar.
Outcome quality alone is insufficient for this purpose.
A model can arrive at an apparently sensible final agree-
ment while still producing a behaviorally implausible trajec-
tory. For that reason, we evaluate candidate samplers with
trajectory-level diagnostics rather than endpoint accuracy
alone.
These diagnostics should be read as necessary but not suf-
ficient conditions for sampler qualification. High action en-
tropy alone does not prove a good sampler. Near-zero diver-
sity, zero concession, and universal turn-budget exhaustion
are inconsistent with any plausible account of boundedly ra-
tional negotiation in the environments studied here. In that
sense, the metrics are best understood as falsification tools
for sampler failure rather than as complete validation crite-
ria.
Primary Metrics
We use three primary metrics.
1. Action entropy: measures behavioral diversity within tra-
jectories. Low entropy indicates repetitive strategic play;
higher entropy indicates a richer mix of negotiation ac-
tions.
2. Concession arc rate: measures whether a run contains
meaningful concession behavior. Operationally, a run
counts as containing a concession arc when the same
agent first rejects or counters and later concedes or sup-
ports. This captures whether agents soften, adapt, or par-
tially revise positions during negotiation rather than sim-
ply repeating hardline counters.
3. Max-turn exhaustion rate: measures how often interac-
tions hit the turn budget rather than resolving earlier.
High exhaustion is consistent with rigid play and poor
adaptive behavior.
Empirical Reference Points for Institutional
Negotiation
The diagnostics above are anchored to published work on
negotiated rulemaking and bargaining impasse rather than
to an abstract notion of realism. Empirical studies of ne-
gotiated rulemaking report that convened committees often
reach consensus or near-consensus even when downstream
implementation remains contested (Langbein and Kerwin
2000). Reviews of bargaining impasse likewise place non-
agreement well below one hundred percent across heteroge-
neous negotiation settings (Schweinsberg, Thau, and Pillutla
2022). Those literatures do not provide a ground-truth target
distribution for our environments, and they are not a substi-
tute for direct human calibration in any specific institutional
case. They do support a weaker falsification claim: a condi-
tion that ends in authority decision in 15 of 15, 30 of 30, or

Experimental Setting
Environments
The completed study contains three scenario-based multi-
agent experiments. The first two vary structural conditions
within the same trading-limits case family. The third pre-
serves the institutional negotiation skeleton while transfer-
ring the experiment into a new emergency grid-curtailment
domain.
Experiment 1 uses an ambiguous fragmented-authority
trading-limits scenario designed to create nontrivial bargain-
ing pressure across multiple parties.
This environment is well suited to the main claim because
it requires:
• multi-party negotiation rather than bilateral optimization
• partial convergence rather than simple win-loss scoring
• concession and coalition dynamics rather than one-shot
choice
• boundedly rational adaptation under ambiguity
Experiment 2 uses an ambiguous unified-opposition sce-
nario from the same trading-limits case family. This pre-
serves ambiguous authority while changing the coalition
structure from fragmented opposition to unified opposition.
It therefore tests whether the main result depends on the ex-
act fragmentation structure of Experiment 1 while staying
inside the same institutional negotiation regime.
Experiment 3 uses a new grid-curtailment case. This envi-
ronment shifts the substantive domain from financial-market
restrictions to emergency electricity curtailment while pre-
serving the core experimental grammar:
• multiple institutional actors with heterogeneous man-
dates
• ambiguous authority and fallback decree pressure
• issue bundling rather than single-issue optimization
• path-dependent concessions under time pressure
• compromise, consensus, and authority-imposed terminal
outcomes
This design tests whether the solver-sampler mismatch
survives domain transfer when the negotiation architecture
stays fixed.
Reflection Conditions
We compare three conditions.
1. No reflection No structured private reflection beyond the
main prompt context.
2. Negotiation-structured scaffold condition A short struc-
tured private ledger that organizes private state around
negotiation-relevant variables. In the actual experiment
stack, that ledger contains five fields tracking one’s own
concessions, the other side’s concessions, current state,
opponent assessment, and open issues. This is not open-
ended chain-of-thought. It is a constrained memory scaf-
fold.
3. Native reasoning Provider or model native reasoning
mode. In the completed experiments, Gemini native used
medium reasoning effort, while DeepSeek native used
provider-native reasoning explicitly enabled; the corre-
sponding DeepSeek no-reflection and scaffold conditions
explicitly disabled that path.
The negotiation-structured scaffold condition and native
long-horizon reasoning should not be treated as the same
intervention. They are different mechanisms with different
behavioral consequences. Exact provider settings for Gem-
ini, DeepSeek, and the OpenAI extension are listed in the
protocol appendix so that native reasoning is inspected as
a provider-specific treatment rather than treated as a single
homogeneous mechanism.
Model Families
The primary experiment matrix uses two model families:
• Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite Preview
• DeepSeek V3.2
Each family was evaluated under no reflection, the
negotiation-structured scaffold condition, and native reason-
ing, with 15 runs per condition.
After freezing that core matrix, we ran a direct-OpenAI
extension through the same three experiments:
• GPT-4.1 under no reflection and the negotiation-
structured scaffold condition
• GPT-5.2 under no reflection, the negotiation-structured
scaffold condition, and native reasoning
We treat these OpenAI runs as a breadth extension rather
than as the paper’s primary identification layer. They were
added after the primary matrix was complete, but they use
the same environments, protocol, and aggregation schema.
The provider-specific native settings are simple. Gemini
used no explicit reasoning payload in no-reflection and scaf-
fold mode, and medium native reasoning effort in the na-
tive condition. DeepSeek explicitly disabled provider rea-
soning in no-reflection and scaffold mode, then enabled it in
the native condition. GPT-5.2 used the OpenAI Responses
API, with reasoning disabled in no-reflection and scaffold
mode and high reasoning effort in the native condition. To-
ken floors, timeout floors, and API-path details appear in the
protocol appendix.

Analysis Procedure
All runs were aggregated through a common schema and
summarized with bootstrap confidence intervals. Opera-
tional failures were separated into provider-error turns and
format-error turns to avoid conflating instrumentation break-
downs with behavioral outcomes. An anonymized supple-
mentary PDF provides exact prompts, scaffold and control
texts, provider settings, parser rules, outcome taxonomy,
transcript excerpts, and expanded robustness tables.
The full primary matrix is 2 primary model families x 3
reflection conditions x 3 experiments x 15 runs = 270 core
runs, plus 225 direct-OpenAI extension runs, for 495 com-
pleted runs in the main study matrix. A separate 15-run GPT-
5.2 crossed mechanism check brings the overall completed
run count to 510.
The public action schema is fixed across experiments:
• Support: endorse the current proposal or direction
• Oppose: reject the proposal without offering a concrete
counter
• Concede: soften an earlier position or accept another ac-
tor’s demand
• Counter: reject while proposing a modified alternative
• Exit: leave the negotiation or force escalation
The terminal outcome taxonomy is likewise fixed:
• Compromise: negotiated middle-ground resolution be-
fore the turn cap
• Consensus: broad agreement with no material unre-
solved dispute
• Authority decision: fallback institutional decision after
unresolved issues remain
• Deadlock: no agreement and no constructive fallback
resolution; in the completed matrix this category re-
mained unused because unresolved runs flowed into the
common authority-decision fallback
Deadlock remains part of the shared taxonomy, but no
completed run in the 495-run main matrix ended in dead-
lock; unresolved trajectories in these experiments instead
flowed into authority decision under the common fallback
rule.
That fallback is related to, but not identical with, max-turn
exhaustion. Max-turn exhaustion is a process diagnostic that
records whether a run reached the configured turn cap. Au-
thority decision is the terminal outcome label applied when
compromise or consensus has not been reached and the des-
ignated authority remains active under the shared classifier
rule. Many rigid runs exhibit both, but they should not be
read as the same variable.
In compact form, the common fallback rule is:
1. no compromise or consensus has yet been detected
2. at least five turns have elapsed
3. the designated authority remains active in the interaction
If those conditions hold at termination, the shared de-
terministic classifier assigns authority decision. Concession
arcs are also detected without an LLM judge: the same agent
must first emit OPPOSE or COUNTER and later emit CON-
CEDE or SUPPORT.

Results
The Main Pattern
The main result is that native reasoning, and often no reflec-
tion, collapse toward authority-heavy outcomes. Gemini and
GPT-5.2 give the cleanest matched-token comparison be-
cause scaffold and native both run at 1024-token floors. The
negotiation-structured scaffold matters here mainly because
it is the only condition that opens negotiated outcomes.
Across the two primary families, the main pattern is:
• native reasoning can reintroduce some internal variation
but still behaves like a poor sampler at the outcome level
• no reflection often lands in the same rigid authority-
driven endings as native reasoning
• the negotiation-structured scaffold condition is the only
one that reliably opens negotiated outcomes under the
same grammar

Cross-Condition Interpretation
The cross-condition story is not that reasoning simply low-
ers entropy. Native reasoning and often no reflection con-
verge on the same authority-heavy terminal pattern, while
the negotiation-structured scaffold condition is the only in-
tervention here that breaks it consistently:
• native reasoning can reintroduce some internal variation
without recovering negotiated closure
• no reflection often lands in the same authority-driven ter-
minal state with fewer provider-specific execution fail-
ures
• the negotiation-structured scaffold condition increases
diversity, concession, and negotiated resolution
The main problem is not reflection itself. It is the mis-
match between open-ended strategic reasoning and the ne-
gotiation behavior we want to simulate.

Experiment 3: New-Domain Grid Curtailment
Transfer
The third experiment asks whether the effect survives a sub-
stantive domain transfer when the experimental structure re-
mains the same.
It does. This is not merely another scenario variant. The
grid-curtailment case replaces financial-market actors with
electricity-system and public-interest actors, changes the
substantive stakes from trading restrictions to emergency
load shedding, and introduces a different time-pressure
structure tied to near-term infrastructure reliability rather
than market policy. The experimental grammar remains the
same, but the domain semantics and institutional roles are
genuinely different.
In the emergency grid-curtailment environment, the same
cross-condition pattern reappears across both model fami-
lies. Gemini ends in 15 of 15 authority decisions under no
reflection, shifts to 13 compromise and 2 authority-decision
runs under the negotiation-structured scaffold condition, and
returns to 15 of 15 authority decisions under native reason-
ing. DeepSeek follows the same terminal pattern: 15 of 15
authority decisions under no reflection, 12 compromise, 2
consensus, and 1 authority decision under the negotiation-
structured scaffold condition, and 15 of 15 authority deci-
sions under native reasoning. The run-level metrics again fa-
vor the negotiation-structured scaffold condition. For Gem-
ini, action entropy rises from 0.163 under no reflection to
0.887 under that condition, while native reasoning falls back
to 0.141. For DeepSeek, the negotiation-structured scaffold
condition yields the strongest entropy and exhaustion pro-
file (1.457 entropy; 0.400 exhaustion), while native rea-
soning preserves substantial local variation (1.256 entropy;
0.933 concession-arc rate) without recovering negotiated
outcomes.
Local Variation Without Negotiated Resolution
Experiment 3 also contains the sharpest falsification of the
idea that more internal variation automatically implies bet-

Targeted Robustness Checks
The first targeted robustness question after the three main
experiments is whether the negotiation-structured scaffold
condition only works because it injects more randomness.
A compact temperature sweep on the Gemini anchor family
argues against that reading. In Experiment 1, changing tem-
perature from 0.3 to 0.7 to 1.0 does not alter the outcome
pattern: no reflection remains 10 of 10 authority decisions
at all three temperatures, the negotiation-structured scaffold
condition remains 10 of 10 compromises, and native rea-
soning remains 10 of 10 authority decisions. Action entropy
shifts somewhat within conditions, but the terminal distribu-
tion does not.
A direct token-budget control addresses the remaining
scaffold-versus-none confound in the Gemini anchor. Re-
running Experiment 1 under no reflection with the output
budget raised from 384 to 1024 tokens stayed rigid: all 15
runs still ended in authority decision, entropy moved only
from 0.024 to 0.072, concession arcs rose from 0.000 to
0.133, and max-turn exhaustion remained 1.000. We then
extended the same no-reflection control at 1024 tokens to
the remaining five confounded cells in the primary matrix,
and all five also stayed at 15 of 15 authority decisions.
So the scaffold-versus-none difference is not explained by
output-space asymmetry anywhere in the primary Gem-
ini/DeepSeek matrix.
The more revealing robustness question is what kind of
private scaffold is doing the work. Matched 15-run reruns of
the current ledger and a neutral-label ledger keep the Gem-
ini anchor compromise-heavy, but three orthogonal five-
slot controls collapse back to 15 of 15 authority decisions.
The third preserves slot count, cadence, and token budget
while tracking only message-form variables such as prior-
turn recap, style/tone target, clarity check, format check,
and message-shape plan; it also stays rigid, with action en-
tropy 0.085, concession arcs 0.067, and max-turn exhaus-

orthogonal-process anchor shows the same pattern. Generic
extra private state therefore does not rescue sampling; the
effect is tied to negotiation-structured state semantics rather
than to extra memory in the abstract.

Mechanism and Interpretation
The data support a mechanism we can describe cautiously
as game-solving pressure. Native reasoning appears to push
agents toward coherent strategic resolution of the situation
rather than toward plausible boundedly rational enactment.
In Experiment 1, this pressure shows up as:
• concentration on authority-driven endings
• suppressed negotiated compromise
• frequent exhaustion of the available interaction horizon
• elevated protocol noise relative to the negotiation-
structured scaffold condition
A short qualitative contrast makes the mismatch con-
crete. In Experiment 1 under no reflection, Gemini repeat-
edly emits counter-actions across the full horizon and falls
into the authority fallback. In Experiment 3 under DeepSeek
native reasoning, the trace contains both countering and sup-
port moves and a high concession-arc rate, yet still ends in
authority decision in every run. Local variation can survive
while negotiated closure still collapses.
Those authority-decision endings are not all the same.
Gemini none in Experiment 1 looks like escalation by
repetition: the regulator and then other actors keep emit-
ting COUNTER despite some semantic movement in the
prose, so the action layer never loosens before the deadline.
DeepSeek native in Experiment 3 looks different: support
and counter moves coexist around a partially shared two-
tier framework, but carveouts and cost-recovery demands
never close into a concession cascade. One subtype is hard-
line strategic escalation; the other is active but unresolved
bargaining. Both miss the sampling target because scaffold
runs under the same grammar routinely reach negotiated clo-
sure. A lightweight coding pass over three anchor authority-
decision cells shows the same split: Gemini none and GPT-
5.2 native collapse into counter-repetition deadlock, whereas
DeepSeek native Experiment 3 stays in active unresolved

new controls show that this is not generic bounded mem-

the primary matrix, and orthogonal private buffers in the
Gemini anchor plus a GPT-4.1 orthogonal anchor also stay

ity in the abstract; it is a scaffold that organizes private state
around negotiation-relevant variables. Crossing that scaffold
with native reasoning then reveals a provider-specific inter-

it only modestly. The analysis stays at the level of observed
behavioral distributions, not provider internals.
Implications
The distinction matters when simulation outputs are used to
explore policy options or institutional risk. In an electricity-
curtailment exercise, for example, a utility operator or en-
ergy ministry may use simulated stakeholders to test whether
an emergency load-shedding plan will be renegotiated once
hospitals, industrial users, and grid operators begin trading
concessions. If the simulator defaults to authority decision
when the real process would often produce partial accom-
modation, the exercise can bias planners toward decree-first
responses and miss compromise paths. Model qualification
for simulation should therefore differ from model qualifi-
cation for decision support. For decision support, one may
reasonably want stronger strategic coherence, better opti-
mization, and tighter reasoning consistency. For behavioral
simulation, one may instead want higher trajectory diversity,
concession dynamics consistent with boundedly rational ne-
gotiation, and boundedly rational inconsistency without ex-
tra protocol fragility. These objectives are not the same. A
model that is excellent for advising a policymaker or nego-
tiator may be inappropriate for simulating the negotiators
themselves. Simulation pipelines should qualify models as
samplers, not assume that stronger reasoning automatically
improves behavioral sampling.
Related Work
The paper sits at the intersection of simulation valida-
tion, LLM-based social simulation, and negotiation-focused
LLM studies. Classic simulation and agent-based-modeling
work treats validation as a core scientific obligation, and re-
cent LLM-simulation reviews argue that it is now the central
bottleneck because apparent behavioral realism can mislead
when the measurement target is underspecified (Bonabeau
2002; Epstein and Axtell 1996; Collins, Koehler, and Lynch
2024; Larooij and T¨ornberg 2026; Wallach et al. 2025; Zhou
et al. 2024).
Adjacent work on synthetic societies and simulated hu-
mans argues that language models can generate plausible
social behavior at scale, while bargaining studies show both
promise and instability in interactive strategic settings (Park
et al. 2023; Aher, Arriaga, and Kalai 2023; Horton 2023;
Fan et al. 2024; Akata et al. 2025; Kwon et al. 2024; Hua,
Qu, and Haffari 2024). Our result adds a narrower qualifi-
cation layer: locally plausible dialogue is not sufficient in
institutionally structured negotiation if the endpoint distri-
bution still collapses to authority decision, as the DeepSeek
grid-curtailment result shows. The negotiation framing is
grounded in the longer behavioral tradition around bounded
rationality, concession behavior, and compromise forma-
tion (Simon 1955; Kahneman 2003; Rubinstein 1998; Pruitt
1981; Walton and McKersie 1965; Raiffa 1982).
Limitations
The study has important limitations. The empirical base is
still narrower than a full benchmark suite: we study three
scenario-based multi-agent experiments, including one do-
main transfer, plus a provider-breadth extension. Provider-
native reasoning is only partially observable and is not stan-
dardized across Gemini, DeepSeek, and the OpenAI exten-
sion, so the paper’s causal claim is behavioral rather than ar-
chitectural. Protocol reliability also complicates interpreta-
tion because some native/noise effects may reflect provider-
side tooling constraints in addition to behavioral mismatch.
The paper does not claim that reasoning is universally
harmful. In tasks where the objective is optimization, plan-
ning, or advice, native reasoning may be preferable. Here
the claim is about sampler qualification in boundedly ratio-
nal multi-agent settings. Human-grounded calibration would
still be required for claims of external behavioral realism or
downstream policy prediction, and the literature-based an-
choring is not a substitute for direct human comparison. The
earlier scaffold-versus-none token asymmetry is now better
constrained: rerunning no reflection at 1024 tokens in all six
primary-matrix cells still produced 15 of 15 authority deci-
sions in every case.
Each cell contains 15 runs, which is enough to ex-
pose stable directional patterns but still leaves a multiple-
comparison burden. The positive mechanism claim is also
narrower than “generic bounded memory helps in general.”
Two orthogonal scaffold controls and a GPT-4.1 orthog-
onal anchor all collapsed back to authority-decision out-
comes, so generic extra state does not rescue the sampler.
The negotiation-structured scaffold used here is not norma-
tively neutral, and the crossed scaffold-native interaction is
measured directly only on Experiment 1.
Conclusion
More reasoning does not necessarily produce better behav-
ioral sampling in negotiation simulation. Across the primary
matrix, no-reflection controls at 1024 tokens and orthogo-
nal scaffold controls show that neither more output space
nor generic extra private state rescues solver-like sampler
failure; DeepSeek native in the grid-curtailment transfer is
the sharpest within-matrix counterexample, and GPT-5.2 na-
tive extends the same pattern to 45 of 45 authority-decision
runs across three environments. The negotiation-structured
scaffold changes the sampled distribution, but in provider-
specific ways, so behavioral simulation should qualify mod-
els as samplers instead of assuming that benchmark strength
alone improves the fit.

Broader Impact and Ethics
The main risk raised by this paper is misuse of LLM sim-
ulations in high-stakes settings. If policymakers, firms, or
researchers treat stronger reasoning models as automatically
better social simulators, they may over-trust clean but be-
haviorally distorted outputs and understate boundedly ra-
tional frictions in real institutions. In the load-shedding
scenario above, a decree-biased simulator could understate
negotiable capacity among hospitals, industrial users, and
grid operators, leading planners to pre-commit to manda-
tory curtailment when voluntary arrangements might have
held. The risk is strongest when simulation outputs are used
upstream of planning, consultation design, or contingency
analysis rather than as pure thought experiments. In those
settings, a simulator that makes negotiated accommodation
look too rare can bias institutions toward command-and-
control responses and away from staged bargaining, ex-
emption design, or fallback agreements. The safer use is
comparative and diagnostic: qualify models for the behav-
ioral role they are meant to play, document scaffold and
outcome-classification choices, and treat simulated trajecto-
ries as stress tests of assumptions rather than as forecasts
of stakeholder behavior. The negotiation-structured scaffold
used here should not be read as neutrality-restoring; it is a
research intervention that changes the sampled distribution
and itself requires scrutiny. Human expertise and domain-
specific empirical checks remain necessary before such sys-
tems are used to justify real institutional interventions.
