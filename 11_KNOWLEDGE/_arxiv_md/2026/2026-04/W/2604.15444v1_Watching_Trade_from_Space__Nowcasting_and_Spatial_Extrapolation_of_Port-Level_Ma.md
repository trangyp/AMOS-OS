Watching Trade from Space: Nowcasting and
Spatial Extrapolation of Port-Level Maritime
Trade Using Satellite Imagery ‗
Yonggeun Jung†
This version: March, 2026
Abstract
Satellite data are increasingly used to measure economic activity, yet port-level trade
remains largely unmeasured from space. This paper combines synthetic aperture
radar imagery, nighttime lights, and port characteristics to measure monthly port-
level maritime trade using only publicly available data. The model achieves strong
out-of-sample accuracy for U.S. ports, with satellite signals and port attributes playing
complementary roles. While absolute levels are difficult to extrapolate beyond the
training domain, percentage changes are reliably recovered, as we confirm through a
leave-one-region-out exercise and Monte Carlo simulation. Applying the framework
to Russian ports after the 2022 sanctions, we detect shifts consistent with trade
reorientation toward the Far East. The approach complements AIS-based methods
by remaining robust to strategic signal manipulation.

Introduction
Satellite data have become a central tool for measuring economic activity where con-
ventional statistics are delayed, incomplete, or unavailable. Beginning with the use of
nighttime lights as proxies for income and growth (Jean et al., 2016; Chen and Nordhaus,
2011; Elvidge et al., 1997), the economics literature has progressively expanded the range
of remote-sensing applications to include urbanization, agricultural output, and infras-
tructure development (see Donaldson and Storeygard, 2016, for a survey). More recently,
Yang et al. (2025) demonstrate how combining multiple geospatial data sources within
a machine learning framework can yield accurate measures of local economic activity
at high spatial and temporal resolution. Despite these advances, the measurement of
international trade flows from space remains largely undeveloped, even though trade
statistics are among the most frequently delayed and strategically suppressed forms of
economic data.
This paper addresses that gap by developing a satellite-based framework to measure
port-level maritime trade. Official trade statistics are typically released with substantial
delays and may be unreliable in environments characterized by weak statistical capacity
or strategic data suppression (see, e.g., Taipliadis, 2025; Tyazhelnikov and Romalis, 2024).
A salient example is Russia, which suspended reporting to UN Comtrade following
economic sanctions in February 2022. Alternative approaches based on the Automatic
Identification System (AIS) have proven informative when transponder signals are
available (Arslanalp et al., 2025), but AIS-based methods are inherently limited when
vessels intentionally disable signals, a key challenge in contexts involving sanctions, illicit
trade, or military conflict (see, e.g., Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2025). Our framework
circumvents these limitations by relying exclusively on publicly available remote-sensing
data and port-level characteristics that do not depend on voluntary signal transmission.
The core intuition is that port activity leaves measurable physical footprints visible
from space. We combine three data sources: synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imagery
from Sentinel-1, which captures vessel movements and container presence regardless of
weather or daylight; nighttime lights (NTL) from VIIRS, which proxy for continuous port
operations; and detailed port characteristics from the World Port Index (WPI). These
inputs are mapped to monthly port-level trade values and weights using XGBoost, a
flexible supervised learning model suited to the tabular, moderate-sample structure of
our data (Chen and Guestrin, 2016). We deliberately focus on publicly available data
and tabular features to ensure transparency and reproducibility, though the framework
is modular and can incorporate proprietary inputs such as AIS or bill-of-lading data.

Our empirical results demonstrate strong predictive performance. In out-of-sample
time-series evaluation using U.S. ports from 2016 to 2024, the model achieves an
𝑅2 of 0.945 for trade value and 0.880 for trade weight. A port-characteristics-only
specification performs comparably for cross-sectional levels (𝑅2 = 0.933), confirming
that static infrastructure pins down level variation, while satellite signals capture within-
port temporal dynamics that time-invariant attributes cannot.
Placebo tests verify
that predictive power derives from meaningful satellite signals rather than spurious
persistence.
A key contribution is to demonstrate how satellite-based measures can track maritime
trade in environments where both official statistics and vessel-level monitoring break
down. We show that absolute levels are difficult to recover when extrapolating outside
the spatial support of the training data—consistent with the spatial machine learning
literature (Ploton et al., 2020)—but that a simple anchoring strategy restores accuracy, and
that percentage changes are reliably estimated even without anchoring. A Monte Carlo
simulation formalizes this result: when the outcome is expressed as Δ𝑌= ¯𝑌post −¯𝑌pre,
port-specific fixed effects cancel algebraically, yielding near-perfect recovery of true
changes (slope = 0.988, correlation = 0.997). Applying the framework to Russian ports
after February 2022, we detect predicted declines at European-facing ports and increases
at Far Eastern ports, consistent with existing evidence on sanctions circumvention
through dark shipping (Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2025; Tyazhelnikov and Romalis,
2024).
Our paper contributes to several literatures. First, it advances the satellite-based
economic measurement agenda (Henderson et al., 2012; Donaldson and Storeygard,
2016; Yang et al., 2025) by extending it to high-frequency, port-level trade—a domain
where conventional data are particularly prone to delay and suppression. Second, it
complements AIS-based trade nowcasting (Arslanalp et al., 2025; Fernández-Villaverde
et al., 2025) by providing a framework robust to strategic signal manipulation. Third, it
contributes to the spatial machine learning literature by formalizing the conditions under
which extrapolation succeeds (changes) versus fails (levels), with practical implications
for applied measurement in spatially disjoint settings.
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the data sources
and feature construction. Section 3 presents nowcasting results. Section 4 examines
spatial extrapolation using Hawaii and applies the framework to Russian ports. Section
5 concludes.

Data and Methodology
We use XGBoost, proposed by Chen and Guestrin (2016), as our prediction model. This
choice is motivated by the nature of our data: rather than fully raw inputs such as images,
our dataset consists of partially preprocessed tabular data, and the sample size is not
particularly large. Since the model is already well established in the literature (see, e.g.,
Mullainathan and Spiess, 2017; Yang et al., 2025), we focus on the data we use and how
we handle it.1 For details regarding data acquisition and processing, see Appendix A.
We rely exclusively on publicly available data. Instead of AIS data,2 which are
commonly used for trade nowcasting or for identifying hidden trade flows (see, e.g.,
Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2025; Arslanalp et al., 2025; Cerdeiro et al., 2020) but often rely
on proprietary sources with limited accessibility and reproducibility, we use Sentinel-1
satellite imagery, nighttime lights (NTL), the World Port Index (WPI), and U.S. Census
data. While AIS-based approaches are highly informative when vessel transponders are
active, they are inherently limited when ships intentionally disable AIS signals, a key
challenge emphasized in the hidden-trade literature (e.g., Fernández-Villaverde et al.,
2025; Bunwaree, 2023). Our approach circumvents this limitation by relying on remote
sensing and port-level information that do not depend on voluntary signal transmission.
Although our objective is to conduct the analysis using publicly available data, the
framework is designed to be modular and can readily incorporate richer inputs when
available. In particular, proprietary bill-of-lading data, AIS data, or higher-resolution
commercial satellite imagery could be integrated without altering the core structure of
the analysis, potentially improving predictive performance and spatial resolution.
Throughout the analysis, we define an area of interest (AOI) for each port, denoted
by 𝑎, as a square buffer with a radius of 3 km centered at the port coordinates provided
by the WPI.3

Sentinel-1 satellites operate day and night and acquire synthetic aperture radar (SAR)
imagery, enabling data collection regardless of weather conditions.5 SAR sensors detect
objects by transmitting radar signals and analyzing the reflected backscatter, which is
typically visualized in grayscale images.6 Sentinel-1 has a nominal revisit cycle of six
days, with global coverage available since October 2014 and largely stable image quality
after 2016.7
Sentinel-1 provides multiple polarization modes; we focus on VV and VH, which
ensure uniform global coverage across ports.8 The VV polarization is suited for detecting
objects, whereas the VH polarization is informative about metallic object shape and
structure, particularly for angular and textured features such as container stacks (see
Figure B1).

Port-level trade and port characteristics.
We use monthly port-level trade data from the
U.S. Census, which report the value and weight of U.S. imports and exports transported
by vessel at the port level.17 These data provide information on maritime trade flows by
port and month, measured in both monetary value and physical weight. We aggregate
vessel-based imports and exports to construct monthly measures of total port-level trade
activity.
To capture time-invariant port characteristics, we use the WPI provided by the
National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA).18 The WPI is a comprehensive global
database covering more than 3,800 ports across 195 countries and provides detailed
information on port geography, infrastructure, services, and navigational features. It
reports over 100 port attributes, including physical characteristics such as channel depth
and berth dimensions, as well as operational features such as cargo handling facilities
and port services.19 We use port coordinates and selected port characteristics from the
WPI (December 2025) to complement the satellite-based measures and to control for
cross-port heterogeneity.
Sample restriction and scope.
In this study, we restrict our analysis to ports in the
United States. This choice is driven primarily by data availability at the port level:
while some countries provide limited port-level trade statistics, the U.S. offers a rich
and reliable environment with consistent monthly data on vessel-based imports and
exports. Focusing on U.S. ports therefore allows us to evaluate our framework in a setting
with high-quality ground truth. In principle, global port-level trade flows could be
constructed using bill-of-lading data (e.g., Li et al., 2024), which are commonly employed
in the trade and logistics literature; however, such data are typically proprietary and
not publicly accessible. We therefore mention this possibility only to highlight potential
extensions.
We further restrict the sample based on port size classifications provided by the WPI,
which applies a four-category classification: Large, Medium, Small, and Very Small.20
According to the December 2025 WPI release, of the 666 U.S. ports listed, 21 are classified
as Large, 38 as Medium, 132 as Small, and 475 as Very Small. Since “Very Small” ports

with delays of several months or more; our framework enables timely measurement
using publicly available satellite and port-level information without relying on AIS data.

From an intuitive perspective, one might expect trade weight, as a more direct
proxy for physical quantities, to be predicted more accurately at the time of observation.
However, this need not be the case in practice. When trade weight is used as the
dependent variable, predictive performance is slightly lower. In the specification that
includes the full set of port characteristics, the model achieves an 𝑅2 of 0.880 and a
correlation of 0.938, while the satellite-only specification yields an 𝑅2 of 0.776 and
a correlation of 0.881. Bulk commodities and raw materials, for instance, are heavy
but often non-metallic, making them harder to identify using SAR-based measures.
In addition, when port characteristics are included, the model may implicitly learn
information related to unit values through port infrastructure and specialization, which
can improve predictions of trade value relative to weight.
Finally, to assess whether the model is simply exploiting time-series persistence in the
dependent variable rather than information contained in satellite measures, we conduct
a placebo test using the satellite-only model for trade value. Specifically, we randomly
shuffle the satellite-based explanatory variables across observations. Under this placebo
exercise, predictive performance collapses, with an 𝑅2 of −0.019 and a correlation of
−0.026. This result indicates that the satellite variables carry meaningful information
for explaining port-level trade outcomes, rather than the model relying on spurious
temporal patterns.

Spatial Extrapolation: Leave-one-region-out
4.1
Hawaii
In this section, we evaluate our model’s ability to predict outcomes in regions that lie
outside the training domain. While one option would be to incorporate data from a
new country, we instead exploit the fact that Hawaii is geographically isolated from
the U.S. mainland and exhibits markedly different natural and economic characteristics.

Motivated by this insight, we refine our approach to mitigate excessive extrapolation.
Specifically, we restrict the training sample to ports on the U.S. mainland that belong to
the same size category as Hawaiian ports, based on the WPI size classification. Within our
sample, Hawaii contains two ports, Hilo and Honolulu, both classified as medium-sized.
Accordingly, we train the model using only medium-sized mainland ports, excluding
large ports to avoid bias driven by scale differences.
Under this specification, the out-of-sample prediction performance for Hawaii,
measured in trade value,28 yields an 𝑅2 of −1.380 and a correlation of 0.896. While
the correlation remains high, the negative 𝑅2 indicates that the model performs worse
than a simple mean benchmark in terms of level prediction. A closer inspection reveals
that predictions for the Port of Honolulu are relatively accurate, whereas predictions
for the Port of Hilo are systematically too high. This reflects the absence of mainland
ports with characteristics comparable to Hilo, even within the medium-size category.
In particular, when conditioning on the dependent variable, no mainland port exhibits
values comparable to those observed for Hilo. As a result, the model captures the overall
temporal comovement, leading to a high correlation, but fails to recover the correct level.

institutional, or infrastructural characteristics outside the support of the training data. In
other words, machine learning models are well suited to recovering temporal fluctuations

detectable physical signatures in satellite imagery.

physical activity at ports regardless of its commercial nature, the framework can detect
behaviors that deliberately obscure conventional data sources, but it cannot distinguish
trade-related from non-commercial maritime activity.
5
Conclusion
This paper develops a satellite-based framework for measuring port-level maritime trade
using only publicly available data. By combining SAR imagery, nighttime lights, and port
characteristics within a machine learning model, we show that monthly trade activity
can be measured with high accuracy even in the absence of vessel-level tracking data. A
decomposition across model specifications reveals that port characteristics and satellite
signals serve complementary roles: the former capture cross-sectional levels, while the
latter track within-port temporal dynamics that static attributes cannot.
The paper also clarifies the conditions under which satellite-based trade measures
generalize spatially. While absolute levels are difficult to recover outside the training
domain, percentage changes are reliably estimated, a result we formalize through Monte
Carlo simulation. This insight motivates our application to Russian ports following the
17


## Page 19


2022 sanctions, where we detect shifts in port-level activity consistent with a reorientation
toward Far Eastern ports, despite the absence of official statistics or reliable AIS coverage.
Several limitations point to directions for future research. The framework currently
operates at the aggregate port level and cannot distinguish bilateral flows or commodity-
level composition. Incorporating proprietary data such as AIS or bill-of-lading records,
or higher-resolution commercial satellite imagery enabling vessel-level detection, could
improve both accuracy and granularity. Extending the training data beyond the United
States to countries with weaker statistical systems remains an important step toward
global applicability. Finally, while the model is designed for measurement rather than
causal inference, future work could explore how satellite-based trade indices can be
integrated into structural models of trade policy or sanctions enforcement.
