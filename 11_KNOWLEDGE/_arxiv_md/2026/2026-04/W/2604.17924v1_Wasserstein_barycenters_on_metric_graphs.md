Introduction
This paper is devoted to the study of Wasserstein barycenters over a metric graph G. By metric
graph, we mean a finite or countable set of vertices connected by edges viewed as actual paths
homeomorphic to compact intervals of the real line. We always assume this graph to be connected
and to have finite index at each point. We equip this graph with a length distance and its associated
1d-Hausdorff measure which turns G into a proper, complete, and separable geodesic space. We
refer to Section 2 for precise definitions.

Existence of a barycenter on a non-proper metric space may fail, see [22] for a rather complete
collection of counter-examples, but barycenters over (W2(X), dW ) do exist whenever the underlying
metric space (X, d) is proper, complete, and separable [20]. Such barycenters are called Wasserstein
barycenters.
The problem of studying Wasserstein barycenters (aka the Wasserstein barycenter problem)
and its regularised version have drawn a lot of interest for its many applications in statistics, image
processing, and data science [24]. In statistics, Wasserstein barycenter can be used as a mean on a
non-linear space, and, among other things, results analogous to the law of large numbers do exist,
see for instance [20, 8]. Wasserstein barycenters are also of primary importance in the field of metric
geometry as a tool to characterise synthetic curvature bounds. Indeed to a probability measure
P = (1 −λ)δµ0 + λδµ1 corresponds a collection of barycenters for λ varying in [0, 1] which induces a
geodesic curve (µλ)λ∈[0,1] in the Wasserstein space, and k-convexity of Entropy-like functionals on
the Wasserstein space characterises lower Ricci curvature bound, see for instance [34, 4, 32, 14] for
precise statements and (much) more on this subject.
Uniqueness of the barycenter is false in general. A classical example is that of P = 1/2(δx +δ−x)
where x and −x are two antipodal points on a unit sphere equipped with its canonical metric
(X, d) = (S2, can), in that situation any point of the equator relative to x and −x is a barycenter
of P.
Uniqueness of the barycenter holds if the space (X, d) is a metric space of nonpositive
curvature thanks to strong convexity properties satisfied by (x, y) 7−→d(x, y) [31]. For Wasserstein
barycenters, while nonnegative curvature bound is preserved from the base space (X, d) to its
Wasserstein space (W2(X), dW ), this is not the case for nonpositive curvature bound despite some
nonpositive curvature like properties that do hold on (W2(X), dW ) when (X, d) is a nonpositively
curved metric space [34, 7].

Organisation of the paper: In Section 2, we first define the metric graphs to be considered in
this paper. We also introduce the 1d-Hausdorff measure on such a graph as the canonical reference
measure. We briefly recall and/or prove the main properties of metric graph. In the second part, we
recall classical properties of Wasserstein spaces, putting the emphasis on the measurable selection
properties available on this space. Finally, we describe extra properties in the case of the Wasserstein
space over the real line. In this very specific situation, the Wasserstein space W2(R) is flat, namely
isometric to a convex subset of a Hilbert space. Through this isometry, we prove the folklore result
that the barycenter of a measure is the standard average of the measures with respect to the given
probability measure P. We refer to Theorem 2.26 for a statement.
In Section 3, taking advantage of the tools introduced in Section 2, we first prove the restriction
property for barycenters. Generalising the fact that the restriction of an optimal plan remains
optimal, Proposition 2.31 is given in a broad set-up since we believe it can be useful in other
contexts. This statement will allow us to reduce the proof of our main theorem to the ”mesoscopic”
scale where a Wasserstein barycenter is assumed to be supported in a edge. In the last part, we
also define our branched covering map and then prove its main properties that will allow us to
somehow transfer the Wasserstein problem over G to one such problem over the real line. It is to
be emphasized that the presence of cyles in G does not prevent the branched covering map to have
these powerful features.
Finally, in Section 4.2 we prove our main result. The main result proof is in two steps as men-
tioned above. First, we prove the result in the case when the Wasserstein barycenter is supported
in an edge, then building on the restriction property, we prove our main result holds without the
restriction on the support.

If µ˚e = µP, then, according to Corollary 4.7, Φ(µ˚e) is the unique barycenter of Q := Φ#P which is
absolutely continuous since Q gives mass to absolutely continuous measures on R by Theorem 2.27.
It follows that µP is absolutely continuous when µ˚e = µP. We now prove that µ˚e is absolutely
continuous when µ˚e ̸= µP. For the decomposition µP = λ µ˚e + (1 −λ)µ2 with λ := µP(˚e) ∈(0, 1)
and µ2 ∈W2(G), Corollary 2.32 provides us with two measures P1, P2 ∈W2(W2(G)) such that µ˚e
is a barycenter of P1 and µ2 is a barycenter of P2. Moreover, Corollary 2.32 implies that both P1
and P2 give mass to absolutely continuous measures since P does so. We then have µ˚e = µP1 is

Finally, since the directed edge ⃗e is arbitrarily chosen, our theorem is proven.
