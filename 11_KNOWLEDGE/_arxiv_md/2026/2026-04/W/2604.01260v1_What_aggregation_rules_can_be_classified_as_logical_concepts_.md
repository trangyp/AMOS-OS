Abstract In this paper, we study aggregation rules with nontrivial symmetric classes
of invariant sets (restricted domains), assuming that they, unlike others, have a logical
nature. In the simplest case, we provide a complete classification of such rules. Our
primary tools are methods of universal algebra and the theory of closed classes of
discrete functions.
Keywords Social Choice Theory · Logic of Social Choice · Impossibility Theorem ·
Possibility Theorem · Aggregation Rule · Locality · Neutrality · Restricted Domain ·
𝑘-valued Logic · Discrete Function · Closed Class · Clone
1 Introduction
In this paper, we study aggregation rules with symmetric (closed under isomorphisms)
classes of invariant domains. The primary motivation for this research is the belief that
these rules stand out from the rest due to their logical nature. Although the division
between the logical and the non-logical is rather arbitrary, Alfred Tarski’s idea can serve
as a guiding thread: “...a notion (individual, set, function, etc.) based on a fundamental
universe of discourse is said to be logical if and only if it is carried onto itself by
each one-one function whose domain and range both coincide with the entire universe
of discourse”, [1]. A detailed discussion and development of this approach can be
found in [2,3]; see also [4]. Extending this idea to the field of social choice theory, we
will consider classes 𝒟of sets of individual preferences (or choices of variants) and
aggregation rules 𝑓that satisfy the following conditions: (a) the class 𝒟is closed under
natural isomorphisms, and (b) every set 𝐷∈𝒟is closed under the rule 𝑓. It can be
noted that such a situation allows for a logical formalism in which the propositions are
individual preferences, and 𝑓plays the role of an inference rule. Similar constructions

to assume that the model of individual choice (if we wish to view it as a logical object)

The process of collective decision-making is formalized by an aggregation rule.
We will understand an aggregation rule as any function 𝑓: 𝐶𝑛→𝐶satisfying the
unanimity condition:
𝑓(𝑐, 𝑐, . . . , 𝑐) = 𝑐
(no matter how general the approach to collective decision-making procedures, it is
hard to find any argument against the unanimity condition).
The structures obtained in this way can be considered as logical concepts; however,
a number of known results suggest that under certain natural assumptions, we have no
nontrivial examples. The famous Arrow theorem [15] implies that for 3 ≤|𝐴| < 𝜔, the
set 𝐶of rational choice functions admits only dictatorial rules within the class of local
aggregation rules. From the works [7,16] it follows that this ”impossibility principle”
extends quite widely. Namely, except for a few exotic cases, for 3 ≤|𝐴| < 𝜔the model
of local aggregation of single-choice functions on the set of 𝑟-element subsets of 𝐴
leads to exactly two situations: either the rule 𝑓is dictatorial, or the set 𝐶consists of
all admissible choice functions (here one can see an analogy with two degenerate cases
in logic: the empty and the inconsistent theory).
The above observations lead us to the following concept. For lack of a better alterna-
tive, we will assume that the model of individual choice (𝐴, 𝐵, 𝐶, ∗) is maximally broad
(and therefore ”trivial”), i.e., it contains, among others, all ”absurd” (unacceptable for
various reasons) choice functions that can be obtained via aggregation, and we will
focus on aggregation rules 𝑓: 𝐶𝑛→𝐶that preserve some natural set of ”reasonable”
choice functions. Many positive results (possibility theorems) are known in this area.
A description of the class of invariants (restricted domains) for the majority rule can be
found in the works of Sen [18], Kaneko [19], and others. The specific class of single-
peaked domains (see [20]) has been studied in detail in [21–23]. Restricted (invariant)
domains for the nonlocal aggregation procedure according to the level 1 consensus rule
can be derived from [24], etc.
With this approach, in what case can an aggregation rule 𝑓: 𝐶𝑛→𝐶be called
logical? We believe that the minimal condition for this is the existence of a class 𝒟of
nontrivial invariant sets 𝐷⊆𝐶for 𝑓that is closed under permutations of the set of
options, i.e., together with each set 𝐷it also contains the set {𝑑𝜎: 𝑑∈𝐷} for every
permutation 𝜎of 𝐴. Intuitively, this means that a logical aggregation rule effectively
”works” on some nontrivial restricted domains 𝐷, and this fact is due only to the general
structure of the domain 𝐷(without hidden ”privileges” for any specific options). It is
clear that the condition of nontriviality of a set 𝐷⊆𝐶cannot be precisely formalized
in the general case. However, the classes 𝐷consisting of all choice functions 𝑐∈𝐶
that coincide on some set 𝑋⊆𝐵should certainly be considered trivial, since they are
preserved by every aggregation rule 𝑓: 𝐶𝑛→𝐶(in particular, all singletons {𝑐},
𝑐∈𝐶, and the set 𝐶itself are trivial). We will see below that in some simple cases this
is sufficient to obtain a characterization of logical aggregation rules. All of the above
leads us to the following definitions.

5 Concluding remarks
The universal algebraic approach offers hope for extending the results of Theorem 1
to more general situations. However, such research will likely prove significantly more
difficult. In particular, for aggregation rules on a set 𝐶of choice functions 𝑐: [𝐴]𝑟→
𝐴, 𝑟> 2, the analogue of Proposition 3 is not true (for this case, it is known that
local aggregation rules do not have symmetric invariants 𝐷⊆𝐶if 5 ≤|𝐴| < 𝜔,
see [7, 16]). Another problem is the possible emergence of “foreign” combinatorics.
For example, Theorem 2 allows us to classify all logical local aggregation rules on
symmetric functional sets 𝐶⊆𝐴𝐵with the condition ∀𝑏∈𝐵|{𝑐(𝑏) : 𝑐∈𝐶}| ≤2 (in
addition to the one considered, such a problem may arise, for example, if we study the
aggregation of “choice functions” on multi- or fuzzy sets). In addition to neutral rules,
we obtain here the rules given by Item 1 of Theorem 2, which, apparently, are of little
interest in social choice theory. This suggests that the generalization of the obtained
results should be accompanied by additional restrictions on the aggregation rules or an
expansion of the concept of triviality of a set 𝐷⊆𝐶.
