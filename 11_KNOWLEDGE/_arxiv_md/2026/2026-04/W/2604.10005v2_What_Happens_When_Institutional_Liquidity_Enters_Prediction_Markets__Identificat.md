What Happens When Institutional
Liquidity Enters Prediction Markets:
Identification, Measurement, and a
Synthetic Proof of Concept
Shaw Dalen∗
Daedalus Research Team†
Abstract
Prediction markets are starting to look less like crowd polls and more like elec-
tronic markets.
The central question is therefore no longer only whether these
markets forecast well, but what happens when institutional liquidity enters: do
spreads tighten, does price discovery improve, and do those gains actually reach
the traders who are slowest to react when information arrives?
This paper offers a research design for answering that question. It defines a
broad market-quality lens, separates the main channels through which institutional
liquidity enters, and maps the identification problems that arise in live venue data.
It also uses a synthetic microstructure laboratory as a proof of concept for the
measurement pipeline.
The main lesson of the synthetic exercise is deliberately narrow. Market-maker
coverage, liquidity incentives, and automation do not have to work through the
same channel; average liquidity gains do not have to translate into equal gains for
all traders; and the sharpest welfare losses are most likely to appear in shock states,
when slower takers receive the least pass-through of tighter quoted markets. The
synthetic results are useful because they stress-test the design, not because they
settle the live empirical question.

Introduction
Prediction markets have long been studied as mechanisms for aggregating dispersed infor-
mation into probabilistic forecasts [1, 3, 11, 28, 31, 35, 37, 38]. In the classic formulation,
the main question is whether market prices summarize collective beliefs more effectively
than polls, committees, or experts. That literature established a durable result: under
many conditions, market prices are informative, reasonably calibrated, and practically
useful for forecasting [1, 35, 37].
The problem this paper focuses on is more specific and more contemporary. Once
institutional liquidity enters prediction markets through maker programs, exchange-run
incentives, and automated quoting, the market can look easier to trade on average while
still becoming harsher for slower or less informed traders. A narrower quoted spread does
not automatically mean that every participant actually receives better execution. The
key economic question is therefore simple: when institutional liquidity enters prediction
markets, who really gets the benefit?
A concrete example helps. Imagine a contract on whether a CPI release will come
in above consensus. Before the announcement, market-maker support and automated
quoting may compress the displayed spread. At the release time, however, some partic-
ipants react almost instantly while others arrive late. The same market can therefore
look more liquid in the book and yet become less forgiving for slower takers exactly when
information arrives. This paper is organized around that tension.
The market that now matters in practice is also richer than the one emphasized in
the earliest forecasting literature. The U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission has
recently treated prediction markets and event contracts as an explicit regulatory object
[9, 10]. Kalshi publicly documents both a market-maker program and a liquidity incentive
program, while also exposing order-book and public-trade feeds through its API [20–
23]. Polymarket similarly documents CLOB data and public order-book access [33, 34].
Institutional interest has also become more visible through strategic ICE investments in
Polymarket [18, 19]. Together, these developments move prediction markets toward the
domain of electronic market design and market microstructure.
This paper answers the resulting question in a deliberately scoped way. It develops
a research design with a synthetic proof of concept: the aim is to define the relevant
outcome family, separate the channels through which institutional liquidity enters, clarify
what the live empirical paper must measure, and demonstrate that the pipeline can be
implemented end-to-end before private or venue-level data are attached.

Contribution and scope.
The paper makes three contributions. First, it argues that
prediction markets should be evaluated with a joint market-quality lens spanning liquid-
ity, information efficiency, forecast quality, internal consistency, and trader-level welfare.
Second, it replaces an omnibus institutionalization dummy with a channel-based treat-
ment map: market-maker coverage, liquidity-incentive eligibility, automation/API inten-
sity, and participant-composition shifts. Third, it states the live-data empirical agenda
explicitly, including staggered-adoption identification, mediator measurement, richer het-
erogeneity, and welfare decomposition. The synthetic laboratory serves only to illustrate
the estimands, tables, and figures that such a live paper should ultimately deliver.
What public data can already tell us.
Even without account-level data, a live
public-data paper can say a great deal about quoted spreads, effective spreads, realized
spreads, pass-through, and several forms of internal consistency. What it cannot cleanly
sign on its own are informed-trader rents and genuine risk-sharing gains. Bringing that
distinction forward helps clarify both the ambition and the limits of the empirical agenda.

Institutional Liquidity, Treatment Channels, and
Hypotheses
Prediction markets as electronic markets.
A modern event contract is a binary
claim paying $1 if a specified event occurs and $0 otherwise.
Unlike equities, event
contracts have bounded payoffs and deterministic convergence to 0 or 1 at settlement.
They are also unusually shock-driven: macro releases, debate performances, court rulings,
injuries, and protocol announcements can all move beliefs discontinuously.
For that
reason, market quality in prediction markets cannot be assessed from one dimension
alone. Spread, depth, resiliency, price impact, forecast calibration, and cross-contract
consistency all matter.

Why institutional liquidity entry must be decomposed.
The phrase “institu-
tional participation” is too broad for causal work. To study institutional liquidity entry,
the live empirical paper should instead distinguish at least four separable channels:
1. Designated market-maker coverage. Formal maker status, quoting obligations,
or program enrollment.
2. Liquidity-incentive eligibility. Exchange subsidy rules for displayed orders or rest-
ing quotes.
3. Automation/API intensity. The ability and willingness of participants to quote or
react through public or private infrastructure.
4. Participant-composition shifts. A change in the share of professional, systematic,
or cross-venue-capable flow.
The first three are candidate entry channels or treatments; the fourth is better viewed
as a mediator or equilibrium outcome. This distinction is important. A convincing live
paper should estimate what maker designation, incentive eligibility, and automation ac-
cess do separately, and then ask whether they work through a reweighting of participant
composition.
Conceptual trade-off.
The microstructure intuition is standard. More institutional
liquidity can tighten spreads, replenish depth, and reduce transitory price impact [4, 13,
15, 16, 24, 27, 30]. Modern algorithmic participation can also improve liquidity in other
electronic markets [17, 29]. But the same infrastructure can sharpen toxicity filters and
concentrate information rents against less-informed traders. Prediction markets should
therefore exhibit a familiar tension between better average execution and more selective
extraction in shock states.
Hypothesis 1 (Liquidity). Market-maker coverage, liquidity incentives, and automation
intensity improve average displayed liquidity, but the magnitude need not be the same
across channels.
Hypothesis 2 (Efficiency). At least some institutional-liquidity channels improve price
discovery and internal consistency, but forecast-quality gains may be weaker than execution-
quality gains.
Hypothesis 3 (Selection). Execution-quality improvements can coexist with worse shock-
state adverse selection for slower takers.
Hypothesis 4 (Heterogeneity). The gains from institutional liquidity vary with infor-
mation density, hedgeability, resolution clarity, time-to-resolution, and participant mix,
not just with coarse event categories.

Empirical Agenda and Identification
Takeaway. This section is not about methodological ornament. Its purpose is to make
clear where the live paper will be hardest to identify and what must be measured before
any claim about institutional liquidity is credible.
The empirical agenda is most persuasive when stated explicitly.
Step 1: build a modular market panel.
The live paper should combine market
metadata, order-book snapshots, trade prints, resolution outcomes, and treatment meta-
data from venue documentation or platform-side records [20, 23, 33, 34]. The key differ-
ence from the earlier draft is that maker designation, liquidity-incentive eligibility, and
automation proxies should be encoded separately.
Step 2: identify staggered treatment correctly.
Because the treatment channels
are likely to be staggered and heterogeneous, the main live estimators should not rely on
a single off-the-shelf two-way-fixed-effects regression.
The natural toolkit is now the
staggered-adoption difference-in-differences literature: decomposition diagnostics [14],
interaction-weighted event studies [36], and group-time average treatment effects [6]. The
live paper should pair those with matched controls, randomization-style placebo dates
where feasible, and cluster-robust inference, ideally including wild bootstrap variants
when the effective number of treated clusters is small [7, 8, 26].
Step 3: define richer heterogeneity.
The prior category split — politics, macro,
sports, crypto — is too coarse on its own. A stronger paper should study heterogene-
ity by information density, resolution ambiguity, hedgeability, time-to-resolution, and
the availability of related markets or cross-venue hedges. Those dimensions are more
economically interpretable than raw category labels.
Step 4: take welfare seriously.
The best version of the paper will not stop at spread
and price impact. It will measure how gains from institutional liquidity are distributed
across maker profits, taker execution, informed-trader rents, and inventory-risk sharing.
That requires trader segmentation or at least credible proxies for trader type, order size,
and speed.
This is where venue-level identifiers or internal tags would be especially
valuable.
Step 5: measure participant-composition shifts as mediators.
Participant-composition
changes should not be coded as a primitive treatment if they are themselves caused by

maker programs, incentives, or automation. Instead, the live paper should measure them
directly and treat them as mediators or equilibrium outcomes. Useful proxies include
the share of message traffic with high cancel-to-trade ratios, the share of quote updates
that replenish within a short latency window after best-price moves, the fraction of flow
synchronized across related markets, the prevalence of cross-venue basis reactions after
public news, and the share of volume accounted for by accounts with repeated multi-
market quoting behavior.
These proxies are informative but highly endogenous. High cancel-to-trade ratios,
fast replenishment, and synchronized cross-market reactions can reflect professional liq-
uidity, but they can also reflect venue rules, event intensity, or latent order-flow toxi-
city. The live paper therefore needs a contamination strategy as well as a proxy list:
compare proxy behavior inside and outside scheduled-news windows, validate against
markets with known differences in automation access, exploit abrupt program-eligibility
thresholds when available, and report robustness across “strict” and “broad” mediator
definitions. The goal is not to claim that any single proxy cleanly identifies profession-
alism, but to show whether several imperfect measures move together in ways consistent
with a participant-composition channel.
A practical validation hierarchy would separate core proxies from supplemental prox-
ies. Core proxies are the ones closest to continuous quoting technology, such as repeated
multi-market quoting, short-latency replenishment, and cross-market synchronization im-
mediately after public news. Supplemental proxies include cancel-to-trade ratios, mes-
sage intensity, and cross-venue basis reactions, which are informative but more easily
contaminated by venue mechanics and temporary toxicity. In the live paper, the strictest
specifications should begin with the core set, then show how conclusions move when the
broader proxy bundle is layered in.
Step 6: classify shock states rather than using one black-box indicator.
The
paper’s main welfare claim is inherently state dependent, so the live design should dis-
tinguish at least three shock classes: scheduled public news (for example, macro releases
or known debate times), semi-scheduled or quasi-public events (such as court windows
or lineup announcements with uncertain timing), and unscheduled breaking news. Those
classes differ both in adverse-selection mechanics and in proxy contamination risk. Sched-
uled shocks are the cleanest environment for testing how institutional-liquidity channels
affect pass-through because the event clock is known in advance. A CPI release market
is the natural example: everyone knows when the information will arrive, but not every-
one can react at the same speed. Breaking-news windows are economically important,
but they are also where mediator proxies, participant composition, and toxicity become
hardest to disentangle.

Step 7: write down the robustness checklist before touching live data.
A min-
imal live-data robustness set should include: alternative treatment timestamps; strict
versus broad mediator definitions; exclusion of scheduled macro windows; matched-
market subsamples; alternative quote-sampling frequencies; placebo treatment dates; and
contract-family fixed effects. Writing this checklist into the design paper makes the even-
tual live study look less like an ex-post robustness search and more like a pre-analysis
blueprint.
Step 8: make the identification map explicit.
Figure 1 summarizes the live-paper
logic: maker programs, incentive eligibility, and automation act as channels through
which institutional liquidity enters; participant-composition shifts operate as mediators;
shock states and venue mechanics affect both mediator proxies and outcomes; and wel-
fare is identified primarily through pass-through and execution-cost objects rather than
through fully observed surplus decomposition.

4.2
Channels of institutional liquidity entry
The synthetic panel separately assigns a market-maker activation time, a liquidity-incentive
activation time, an automation-intensity path that ramps up after API-style adoption,
and an endogenous professional-share proxy that responds to the first three channels.
This is a better match to the live agenda because it prevents all institutional changes
from being collapsed into one treatment dummy.
4.3
Synthetic outcomes
Spread, effective spread, depth, price impact, no-arbitrage gaps, and forecast-quality
statistics are generated from the latent state, the shock process, and the modular channels
through which institutional liquidity enters.
The synthetic design deliberately allows
some channels to help execution much more than calibration. It also allows quality gains
to depend on hedgeability, information density, and resolution clarity.
Panel dimensions.
The synthetic panel contains 320 markets and 57,600 market-time
observations. Its purpose is illustrative rather than evidentiary, so the panel is calibrated
to generate richer variation across information density, hedgeability, and resolution am-
biguity than the earlier bundle-only design.

Maker profits may be partially recoverable from realized spreads and inventory prox-
ies, but informed rents and risk-sharing gains are much harder to sign cleanly without
account-level labels, strategy traces, or inventory data. The live paper should therefore
distinguish between components that can be estimated directly, components that can
only be bounded, and components that remain qualitative.
Because the current proof-of-concept lacks live trader identifiers, it uses illustra-
tive archetypes instead: small slow takers, small fast takers, large hedged traders, and
informed-like traders. Table 6 and Figure 6 therefore focus on pass-through rather than
levels alone. In the synthetic design, calm-state benefits are highly uneven, and slow
takers can lose them entirely in shock states. That is the closest synthetic analogue to
the claim that institutional liquidity may finance average spread compression through
more selective extraction from slow flow when information arrives.

A disciplined public-data pa-

compression, pass-through by trader-size or speed proxy, and the shock wedge in pass-
through. Only after those have been established should it move to partial maker-profit
recovery or more speculative statements about informed rents. This ordering keeps the
welfare section anchored in what the data can actually bear.

Related Literature
This paper sits at the intersection of prediction markets, market microstructure, and elec-
tronic market design. The classic prediction-market literature established the information-
aggregation and forecasting value of market prices [1, 35, 37, 38]. The microstructure lit-
erature provides the language for adverse selection, price impact, spreads, and inventory

## Page 18


management [2, 4, 13, 15, 16, 24, 27, 30]. Recent work on algorithmic or high-frequency
market making is particularly relevant because the arrival of institutional liquidity in pre-
diction markets may look more like the arrival of new electronic market makers than like
a simple change in participant counts [17, 29]. On the prediction-market side, recent work
has begun to study market-maker economics, prediction-market microstructure, calibra-
tion, and law-of-one-price issues on contemporary platforms [5, 12, 25, 32]. The present
paper’s main distinction is to center institutional liquidity as a modular empirical object
and to connect it explicitly to measurement, mediator coding, and welfare incidence.
10
Conclusion
Prediction markets are moving into a more professional, electronically intermediated
regime. Studying that transition requires more than asking whether prices are accurate
on average. It requires following the joint movement of liquidity, information efficiency,
forecast quality, internal consistency, and trader-level welfare, while treating maker des-
ignation, incentive design, automation intensity, and participant-composition shifts as
distinct empirical objects.
The main value of the current paper is to organize that agenda into a concrete em-
pirical blueprint. The synthetic proof of concept shows what the tables and figures look
like, how moderator and mediator analysis fit together, and why the strongest live claims
will likely concern execution and shock-state incidence rather than dramatic calibration
gains. Just as importantly, the paper makes the measurement burden explicit: which
objects are tractable with public data only, which mediator proxies are likely to be con-
taminated, which consistency gaps are prediction-market-specific, and which robustness
checks the live version should treat as non-negotiable.
Three points should remain central. First, institutional liquidity is the core object
of interest, while maker programs, incentives, and automation are best understood as the
channels through which it enters. Second, the first and clearest gains from institutional
liquidity are more likely to appear in execution quality than in forecasting performance.
Third, the most important welfare question is whether those gains are actually passed
through to the traders who are slowest, smallest, or least able to hedge when information
arrives.
