Multipliers and Disjointness from Mixing
Sohail Farhangi,
Joel Moreira and
Rigoberto Zelada
Abstract In 2005, Parreau proved that if a measure preserving system is not strongly mixing then it contains a non-
trivial factor that is disjoint from every strongly mixing system. Taking this construction as the starting point, we
develop the complementary notions of U-generated and U-mixing systems, for a set U of ultrafilters, and use them to
recover several classical results in ergodic theory as special cases of a unified framework.
We prove that a system is U-mixing if and only if it is disjoint from all U-generated systems. In fact, we show that if Y
is a U-generated system and Z is disjoint from every U-mixing system, then any joining of Y and Z remains disjoint
from all U-mixing systems. We also show that every partially rigid system is a finite extension of some U-generated
system.
1 Introduction
Disjointness of measure-preserving systems, introduced in the seminal work of Furstenberg [31] in 1967, can be used
to characterize many classical dualities in ergodic theory. In particular, we have
Theorem 1 (cf. [52, Remark 10] or [36, Page 145]) Let X := (𝑋, B, 𝜇,𝑇) be a measure-preserving system.
(i) X is ergodic if and only if it is disjoint from every identity system.
(ii) X is weakly mixing if and only if it is disjoint from every rotation on a compact abelian group.
(iii) X is mildly mixing if and only if it is disjoint from every rigid system.
(iv) X has completely positive entropy if and only if it is disjoint from every zero entropy system.
We recall that if the systems X and Y have a nontrivial common factor Z, then the relatively independent joining
of X and Y over Z is a nontrivial joining, so X and Y are not disjoint. However, it is possible for systems X and Y
not to have a common factor, but still have a nontrivial joining, i.e., to not be disjoint (cf. [36, Example 7.15]).
A class C of measure-preserving systems is a characteristic class if it is closed under factors and countable joinings.

Another important class of systems in ergodic theory is the class S of strongly mixing systems. The question of
whether strongly mixing systems can be characterized as those disjoint from a certain class, in the spirit of Theorem 1,
remained open until the 2000’s. A positive answer was essentially found by Parreau [55] (see also [50, Theorem 11]),
who proved that any system that is not strongly mixing contains a non-trivial factor disjoint from all mixing systems. In
the present paper we explicitly describe and study a class of systems, which we call U-generated systems and denote
by G (cf. Definition 1), which, in view of Parreau’s theorem, allows one to characterize strongly mixing in terms of
disjointness as in Theorem 1. The following is an immediate consequence of [55].
Theorem 2 A system X is strongly mixing if and only if it is disjoint from every system from G.
We will see in Lemma 9 that the class G is closed under countable joinings, but Examples 5-7 will show that it
need not be closed under passage to factors, so G is not a characteristic class. Nonetheless, it is still the case that X is
strongly mixing if and only if it has no factor in G. Indeed, we will see in Corollary 1 below that G is "closed under
subfactors", meaning that for any system X ∈G, and any non-trivial factor Y of X, there is a non-trivial factor Z of Y
for which Z ∈G. As discussed in Remark 15, it follows that the class G of systems that are a factor of some member
of G is a characteristic class.
The classical notion of disjointness is often called Furstenberg disjointness in order to distinguish it from the stronger
notion of spectral disjointness (defined in Section 2.3). It is worth noting that parts (i)-(iii) of Theorem 1 are still true if
the word “disjoint" is replaced by “spectrally disjoint". In particular, the mean ergodic theorem can be reinterpreted as
the spectral disjointness of identity systems I and ergodic systems E, the Jacobs-de Leeuw-Glicksberg decomposition
can be reinterpreted as the spectral disjointness of rotations on compact abelian groups K and weakly mixing systems
W, and the spectral disjointness of rigid systems R and mildly mixing systems M can be deduced from [65, Lemma
1] (see also [9, Theorem 0.4]). All systems X of completely positive entropy have countable Lebesgue spectrum [23],
and many systems of zero entropy also have countable Lebesgue spectrum, so the dichotomy present in Theorem 1 (iv)
cannot be expressed through spectral disjointness. We will see in Examples 4-7 that systems in G need not be spectrally
disjoint from all strongly mixing systems. Nonetheless, there is a strong relation between strong mixing, U-generated
systems, and spectral disjointness, described in Theorem 3.
Another important topic in the theory of disjointness is that of multipliers. Given a class D of systems, we let D⊥
denote the class of systems that are disjoint from all systems in D. We let M (D⊥) denote the class of systems X, such
that for any Y ∈D⊥, every joining of X and Y is again in D⊥.2 For any class of systems D, we have that M (D⊥) is
a characteristic class [42, Section 2.1].
Letting D denote the class of measurably distal systems, Furstenberg [31] had shown that D ∩E ⊆W⊥and W =
K⊥. However, we are far from a complete understanding of W⊥. Glasner and Weiss [37] showed that D ∩E ⊊W⊥.
Later, Glasner [35] showed that D ∩E ⊊M (W⊥), then Lemańczyk and Parreau [51] showed that M (W⊥) ⊊W⊥.
Recently Berk, Górska, and de la Rue [15] showed that I = M (E⊥) ⊊E⊥, and a complete characterization of E⊥
was obtained by Górska, Lemańczyk, and de la Rue [39].

That (ii) =⇒(i) holds can be deduced from Remark 14. The implication (i) =⇒(iii) follows from Corollary 3. That
(iii) =⇒(ii) is a consequence of Theorem 10. Moreover, we provide two different approaches to the proof of Theorem
10. The first of these approaches is based on Parreau’s original proof of Theorem 2 and it relies on the concept of
relatively independent joinings. Our second approach still employs some of the main ideas presented in [55] but avoids
the use of relatively independent joinings by employing a variant of the van der Corput trick introduced in [13]. This
second approach may provide a useful approach to extend Theorem 10 to the context of noncommutative groups.
The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section 2 we collect relevant facts about measure-preserving systems,
joinings and disjointness, spectrum and spectral disjointness, the Image-Kernel decomposition of a normal operator
on a Hilbert space, the algebra of the Stone-Čech compactification, and ultrafilter limits of operators. In Section 3
we define U-mixing for a family of ultrafilters U, discuss how this recovers the classical notions of weak, mild, and
strong mixing, and classify which systems are spectrally disjoint from all U-mixing systems. In Section 4 we define
U-generated and U-generated factors, prove some of their basic properties, and provide many examples. In Section 5
we study partially rigid systems and show that they are always a finite extension of their U-generated factor. In Section
6 we study the multiplier property and prove Corollaries 6 and 7, which are two of the main results of this article. In
Section 7 we give an alternative proof of Theorem 10 using the van der Corput difference trick. In Section 8 we state
some open questions and conjectures.

1.2 Previous work
After an initial version of the present paper was shared with various experts in the field, it was brought to our attention
that Parreau’s work [55] and many of its generalizations and consequences were announced and promoted in various
talks in the mid 2000’s (see [54], for example). Many of the results proved in this paper were already known at that
time and include results originally proved by Lemańczyk [53,54] and Tehungue [62]. For instance, they showed that
the construction of Parreau yields systems in M (S⊥), and Theorem 9 appears in [53,54].
Lemańczyk defines the Parreau factor of a system X to be the largest factor contained in M (S⊥), and in [53] shows
that an ergodic IET is its own Parreau factor, hence a member of M (S⊥). The proof given there easily generalizes
to any ergodic partially rigid system (cf. Corollary 7 part (iii)). Furthermore, in [53, 54] it is shown that Parreau’s
construction can be generalized to recover the duality between identity and ergodic systems, rotations on compact
abelian groups and weakly mixing systems, as well as rigid and mildly mixing systems. Furthermore, it yields a class
of systems that are related to singular spectrum systems and are members of M (A⊥) (see also [50, Page 17]). The
Parreau factor always contains the U-generated factor of a given system (in view of Corollary 7), but in some cases it
is strictly larger: indeed, any nilsystem is its own Parreau factor, but its U-generated factor is the Kronecker factor (cf.
Example 1).
While many of the results in this paper were known before, we hope to bring renewed attention to this interesting
topic and provide an accessible and detailed presentation.

8 Questions and Future Work
The study of the spectral properties of Interval Exchange Transformations has long been a topic of interest. In 1967
Katok and Stëpin [44] showed that almost every 3-IET has simple spectrum and is weakly mixing, and in 1980
Katok [43] showed that no IET is strongly mixing. In 1984 Veech [64] showed that almost every IET is totally ergodic,
rigid, rank 1, and has simple spectrum. While Veech had also shown that many IETs are weakly mixing, it was a break
through when Avila and Forni [3] showed that almost every IET is either weakly mixing or an irrational rotation. As
discussed in [50, Page 15] and [26], it remains an open question as to whether or not every IET has singular spectrum.
The following related question is also open.
Question 1 Is every IET spectrally disjoint from every strongly mixing system?
We observe that a positive answer to Question 1 would imply that every IET has singular spectrum.

We worked with abelian groups because we required 𝑇𝑝to be a normal operator to use the results of Section 2.4.
Furthermore, if 𝐺is abelian, then 𝑇𝑝is the Markov operator associated to a self-joining of X, and this fact was used
in the proof of Theorem 10. Nonetheless, the definitions of U-mixing, U-generated system, and U-generated factor
extend to the setting of countably infinite groups without any changes. Thus, we are left with the following questions.
Question 3 Let 𝐺be a countably infinite noncommutative group, X a measure-preserving 𝐺-system, and ∅≠U ⊆𝐺∗.
(i) Is the factor XU also a U-generated system? In other words, do we still have XU = (XU)U?
(ii) If X is a U-generated system, is X disjoint from all U-mixing systems?
It seems unlikely that our proof of Theorem 10 presented in Section 6 will help in the noncommutative setting, but
the methods of Section 7 may help study the situation when 𝐺is nilpotent.
In the introduction we saw that M (E⊥) ⊊E⊥and M (W⊥) ⊊W⊥. We have not yet determined whether or not
M (M⊥) ⊊M⊥, M (S⊥) ⊊S⊥, or M (A⊥) ⊊A⊥, which leads us to the following:
Question 4 Does there exist a class of systems D ⊆W that is closed under factors, countable direct products, and
inverse limits, for which we also have M (D⊥) = D⊥?
We have seen that U-mixing encapsulates many but not all notions of mixing that are of interest. Another well-
studied notion of mixing in the setting of Z-systems is that of light mixing. The Z-system X is lightly mixing if for
every 𝐴, 𝐵∈B+, we have lim inf𝑛→∞𝜇(𝐴∩𝑇𝑛𝐵) > 0. Light mixing sits strictly inbetween mild mixing and partial
mixing. Furthermore, Kingman [45] showed that the class of lightly mixing systems is closed under direct products
and inverse limits. Since Adams [2] showed that light mixing on a dense algebra of sets does not necessarily imply
light mixing, we are led to the following:
Question 5 Is there any U ⊆Z∗for which SU coincides with the class of lightly mixing systems?
