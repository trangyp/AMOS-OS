Abstract 
Membrane wrapping underlies nanoparticle uptake during endocytosis, whereas the reverse 
process of membrane unwrapping accompanies particle expulsion and membrane fusion events. 
Existing theoretical descriptions typically focus on adhesion and bending energies within the 
particle–membrane contact region and often neglect the deformation energy of the membrane 
outside the contact zone. This approximation is valid only in the limit of vanishing membrane 
tension, where the non-contact membrane assumes a catenoid-like configuration with negligible 
bending energy. However, at finite tension the deformation of the non-contact membrane becomes 
a dominant energetic contribution. Here we show that this tension-dependent non-contact energy 
governs the progression of particle wrapping. By analysing the variation of the total membrane 
energy with wrapping degree, we uncover a competition between particle adhesion, membrane 
tension and particle size that determines whether wrapping proceeds, stalls, or reverses into 
spontaneous unwrapping. This framework reveals a stalling boundary separating regimes of 
particle uptake and expulsion. To capture the non-contact deformation efficiently, we derive a 
compact analytical approximation that accurately reproduces the full numerical solution of the 
membrane shape. The resulting energetic map provides a unified physical description of particle 
wrapping and unwrapping, with implications for endocytosis, membrane fusion, and nanoparticle 
design. 
Keywords: membrane wrapping; membrane tension; particle–membrane adhesion; wrapping–
unwrapping transition; membrane mechanics; nanoparticle uptake & fusion

Introduction 
Nanoparticles (NPs) are essential in modern medicine for targeted drug delivery (e.g., cancer 
therapies), diagnostic imaging (MRIs), rapid tests, and vaccine delivery (e.g. COVID-19 vaccines). 
These processes are often induced through intracellular mechanisms. One critical mechanism is 
endocytosis, where the cell membrane gradually wraps around and engulfs the NP. Endocytosis 
regulates drug uptake and virus infection. In addition to that, expulsion mechanisms (particles 
exiting the membrane) include exocytosis (unwrapping/fusion expulsion) and budding (wrapping 
expulsion). Over the past 50 years, biomechanics researchers have developed theoretical models 
to describe such membrane processes, as direct nanoscale experiments are extremely difficult. 
These models are largely based on a competition between adhesion energy (driving wrapping) and 
penalties from membrane bending and surface tension (Helfrich 1973, Seifert et al. 1991). If 
adhesion exceeds the penalties throughout the endocytosis process, wrapping occurs 
spontaneously. Otherwise, it may stall (stop midway) or fail to begin at all. 
Accurate modeling and prediction is vital for improving NP uptake in drug delivery, as it can 
dictate important factors such as wrapping efficiency, energy barriers, optimal NP design (size, 
shape, and surface chemistry), and cell-type selectivity (preferential uptake by target cells over 
healthy ones). The total energy includes contributions from the contact (C) region and the non-
contact (NC) region (the deformed membrane away from the NP), as shown in Figure 1. While the 
C-region energy is straightforward, as it can be described by an analytical formula, the NC-region 
energy requires solving a complex variational problem, which first involves minimizing an energy 
integral through delicate ordinary differential equations called the membrane’s shape equation. 
The solution to these shape equations allows us to find the membrane’s optimal configuration, 
through which we can then calculate the NC energy. To find this minimum-energy configuration, 
one would start by guessing a range of initial curvature values, iterating through them until all 
boundary conditions (BCs) are satisfied (Julicher & Seifert 1994, Deserno 2004a). Unfortunately, 
this process is computationally expensive, time-consuming, and extremely numerically sensitive 
(Deserno 2004b). 
The literature typically handles NC energy by neglecting it or approximating it as a simple function 
of the wrap angle θ (Zhang et al. 2015, Bacca 2022, Agostinelli et al. 2022). Foundational works 
such as Gao et al. (2005) have assumed that the membrane is perfectly flat in the NC-region, 
therefore neglecting any deformations that may arise for that region. Neglecting the NC 
contribution is only valid at the start or end of wrapping and for zero-tension membranes (σ = 0), 
where the membrane assumes a catenoid-like shape (Deserno 2004a, Deserno & Bickel 2003, Auth 
& Gompper 2009). In all other cases, the NC energy shows a non-monotonic trend and is 
significant enough that ignoring it would lead to errors in predicting points of stalling or cases of 
incomplete wrapping. Many studies have analyzed the effects of the NC-region in their overall 
energetics (Yi et al. 2011, Napoli and Goriely 2020), albeit with no analytical expression or 
separate focus discussing the energetics in the NC-region.  
In standard membrane wrapping models, the NP is posed as either a rigid or soft (deformable) 
structure. Common NP shapes include spheres, rods, cubes, cylinders, and ellipsoids, often used 
as diagnostic or therapeutic agents in modern practices (Zhang et al. 2015). In most studies, the 
membrane is deformable and much larger than the NP, with membranes often treated as infinite 
and inextensible. When both the membrane and NP are soft, the attachment point of the membrane 
to the NP is unknown, increasing the complexity of finding the optimum membrane configuration 
(Yi et al. 2011). Some models can also require the need to define a fixed area for the membrane to

Results and Discussion 
Here we first examine the physically relevant range of the dimensionless tension 𝜎[ = 𝜎𝑅"/𝐵. 
Rangamani (2022) provided experimental membrane tension values for different cell types, 
typically measured by tether pulling (Dai & Sheetz 1999, Lieber et al. 2013) or optical methods 
(Popescu et al. 2003). The reported range is 𝜎= 0 −4.5 × 10HM 𝑁/𝑚. Deserno (2004a) cites a 
representative tension value of 𝜎= 2 × 10HN 𝑁/𝑚. As for the bending rigidity 𝐵, Helfrich (1973) 
estimated 𝐵≈10H'" 𝑒𝑟𝑔 (~ 23.4 𝑘#𝑇). Subsequent studies have commonly adopted 𝐵= 20 𝑘#𝑇 
as the reference value (Deserno 2004a, Gao et al. 2005).  
In the tension-range experiments discussed above, the value of 𝐵 can either be taken as a constant 
or obtained through additional measurements and implementing the theory (experiment-
dependent). The values are reported to be in the range 𝐵 ~ 1 −35 𝑘#𝑇 for the tension range 
discussed above. Despite this range, the experiments that examined the low 𝐵≈1 −2 𝑘#𝑇 values 
also had small 𝜎 on the order of 𝜇𝑁/𝑚. In fact, the upper limit of 𝜎 corresponds to 𝐵≈35 𝑘O𝑇.

Conclusion 
In this work, we investigated the role of membrane deformation in the NC region during tension-
assisted wrapping of a rigid spherical NP. By solving the full axisymmetric membrane shape 
equations and mapping the total free-energy variation with wrapping degree, we showed that the 
energy stored in the NC region is a dominant and non-monotonic contribution to the total energy 
landscape. This contribution peaks at intermediate wrapping and can account for a substantial 
portion of the total energy barrier, demonstrating that neglecting the NC energy leads to incorrect 
predictions of wrapping pathways, stalling points, and critical adhesion thresholds. 
By expressing the condition for spontaneous wrapping in terms of the rate of energy change with 
respect to wrapping degree, we constructed an adhesion–wrapping map that reveals distinct 
regimes of spontaneous wrapping and spontaneous unwrapping. The transition between these two 
regimes defines a stalling condition, where the system is unable to proceed toward either full 
wrapping or full unwrapping and instead becomes trapped in a partially wrapped configuration. 
This stalling behavior arises from the non-monotonic variation of the NC membrane energy, which 
generates an energy barrier that separates the wrapping and unwrapping pathways. As a result, 
wrapping and unwrapping are intrinsically asymmetric processes and may follow different 
energetic paths, leading to metastability and hysteresis. The framework therefore provides a 
unified description of particle uptake and expulsion in terms of adhesion–tension competition and 
the energetics of the NC membrane. 
In the high-tension limit, the NC region shrinks and becomes localized near the detachment point, 
but its bending energy does not vanish and instead concentrates in a narrow boundary layer. This 
indicates that even in the limit of large tension the deformation energy of the NC membrane cannot 
be neglected, and classical descriptions based solely on adhesion and tension may not fully capture 
the energetic barriers governing wrapping and unwrapping transitions. 
To make these results readily usable in analytical models, we provided a compact closed-form 
approximation for the NC energy that accurately reproduces the numerical solutions across the 
parameter space. This analytical expression enables rapid evaluation of wrapping and unwrapping 
conditions without the need to repeatedly solve the membrane shape equations.  
Overall, these results show that membrane wrapping is governed not only by the competition 
between adhesion and membrane tension, but also by the energy stored in the NC region, which 
controls the energy barriers and therefore determines whether wrapping proceeds, stalls, or 
reverses. This energetic mapping provides a physical framework for understanding endocytosis, 
membrane fusion, and NP expulsion, and may be useful for the design of NPs and vesicle-based 
delivery systems where intermediate metastable states and stalling phenomena play a critical role.
