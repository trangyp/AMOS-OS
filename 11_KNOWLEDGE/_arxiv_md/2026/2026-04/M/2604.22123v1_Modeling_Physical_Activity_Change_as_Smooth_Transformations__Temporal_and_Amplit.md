Abstract
Background: Minute-level accelerometer data capture rich diurnal physical
activity (PA) patterns, but conventional summary metrics can obscure clini-
cally meaningful changes in when and how PA is accumulated across the day.
Building on our previous Riemannian framework, which models longitudinal PA
change as diffeomorphism between PA diurnal pattern curves from different vis-
its, we integrate multivariate functional principal component analysis (MFPCA)
to identify main modes of longitudinal PA change in older women and examine
their associations with physical function (PF).
Methods: The analysis included a subset of women from OPACH (Objective
Physical Activity and Cardiovascular Health) as baseline followed in two WHISH
(Women’s Health Initiative Strong and Healthy) waves (W1, W2), yielding three
accelerometer measurements; each participant’s diurnal PA at each visit was
represented as a smooth functional curve. Change between consecutive visits
(defined as transition periods: baseline–W1 and W1–W2) was modeled as a Rie-
mannian deformation jointly capturing changes in PA timing and magnitude.
Deformations were parameterized by initial momenta and summarized using
MFPCA; participant-level changes were characterized by principal component
(PC) scores and deformation energy, a metric of overall pattern change. Associ-
ations with PF, measured by the RAND-36 PF instrument, were assessed using
linear mixed-effects models adjusted for baseline PF and key covariates.
Results: Mean PA deformation in both periods showed overall downward shifts
in PA magnitude with temporal redistribution between 10 a.m. and 7 p.m. Top
15 PCs explained ≥90% of variability in both periods; PC1 represented a pattern
of PA increase/decrease throughout the day, explaining 22.4% (baseline–W1)
and 20.8% (W1–W2). Among participants with complete PF scores and base-
line covariates (N=1,157), an increase in PA in the mode of PC1 was positively
associated with PF (p <0.0001). The interaction between deformation energy
and period was significantly associated with PF (p=0.003), with a larger positive
effect in W1–W2 than baseline–W1.
Conclusion: Modeling longitudinal PA change as Riemannian deformations
and summarizing variability via MFPCA produced clinically interpretable phe-
notypes of diurnal PA change beyond standard cut-point metrics. The leading
deformation mode was significantly associated with PF, and deformation energy
showed a stronger association with PF in the later transition period.
Keywords: Riemannian Deformation, Multivariate Functional Principal Component
Analysis (MFPCA), Physical Activity, Physical Function, Accelerometer, Functional
Data Analysis
1 Introduction
Regular physical activity (PA) promotes health and longevity [1, 2], whereas physical
inactivity and sedentary behavior (SB) are associated with higher risks of cardiovas-
cular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality [3–9], and have been linked to cancer,
obesity, and declines in physical and cognitive function [10–13] in older adults. Under-
standing how PA and SB evolve with aging is therefore a clinically important goal,
2


## Page 3


particularly in older adults who experience heterogeneous trajectories of functional
decline and cardiometabolic risk [14–16].
Accelerometers provide directly measured, high-resolution PA information in the
free-living setting, yet common analytic pipelines reduce minute-level time series to
day-level summaries (e.g., minutes/day in magnitude based on cut-points) [8, 17–
23]. Such aggregation improves interpretability and comparability but may not fully
capture within-day temporal structure or clinically relevant diurnal features such as
PA timing, amplitude changes, and shifts in routines.
Functional principal component analysis (FPCA) offers a principled alternative
by representing minute-level profiles as functions and extracting dominant modes
of variation [24–31]. However, standard FPCA decomposes PA curves from all sub-
jects and visits jointly, so that change is inferred indirectly, e.g., as differences in
PC scores between visits. This combines two distinct phenomena: (i) baseline het-
erogeneity among subjects and (ii) within-subject longitudinal change. Furthermore,
without explicit time-warping, standard FPCA does not isolate changes in PA timing
(horizontal phase shifts) from changes in magnitude (vertical amplitude shifts). An
alternative framework is needed that directly models the visit-to-visit transformation,
representing change itself as the primary analytical object.
Zou et al. [32] proposed such a framework, modeling each smoothed diurnal PA pro-
file as a one-dimensional Riemannian manifold and representing between-visit change
as a diffeomorphic deformation using a Riemannian geometry formulation [33–35]. By
modeling deformations directly, the approach preserves within-day temporal structure
and characterizes PA change in terms of coupled shifts in activity timing (phase; x-
domain) and magnitude (amplitude; y-domain), a representation well-suited to aging
cohorts where decline and adaptation manifest as coordinated changes in both when
and how much activity occurs [36, 37]. Cohort-level heterogeneity is then summarized
by applying FPCA to these deformations.
In this paper, we build on Zou et al. [32] by integrating multivariate FPCA
(MFPCA) [38] to jointly model longitudinal PA change in the phase (x-domain)
and amplitude (y-domain) directions as a genuinely multivariate functional object
rather than relying on univariate FPCA of the concatenated x- and y-domain changes
as in Zou et al. [32]. We apply this framework to free-living accelerometer data
from OPACH (Objective Physical Activity and Cardiovascular Health) and WHISH
(Women’s Health Initiative Strong and Healthy), examining longitudinal PA change
in older women with repeated accelerometer and physical function (PF) assessment
to characterize clinically meaningful heterogeneity and its association with PF, a key
pathway linking PA to aging-related outcomes, including CVD, mortality, and loss of
mobility [39].

2.1 Study design and participants
The OPACH study was a Women’s Health Initiative (WHI, initiated in 1992 [40])
ancillary study that enrolled 7,048 ambulatory, community-dwelling women aged

63–99 years between 2012 and 2014 [4, 7, 41]. Among surviving OPACH partici-

whether increasing PA reduced the risk of primary CVD events in older women.
Together with the OPACH baseline assessment, this design afforded up to four waves

informed consent, and the studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board
at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center [41, 43].
The present analyses focus on the first three accelerometer assessment waves:
OPACH baseline (original n0 = 6,489), WHISH wave 1 at 6 months post-
randomization (W1; n1 = 1,934), and WHISH wave 2 at 18 months post-randomization
(W2; n2 = 1,677). The average interval between baseline to W1 was 3 years and
the average between W1 to W2 was 1 year. We refer to this analytic sample as the
OPACH–WHISH sub-cohort. Data from the WHISH wave 3 at 36-month (W3; n3 =
1,398) were excluded due to substantial loss to follow-up, to minimize potential bias
in FPCA estimation and subsequent association analyses. At the time of the present
analysis, WHISH randomization assignments (usual activity versus PA intervention)
for OPACH–WHISH sub-cohort members had not yet been made available to the
study team; implications are addressed in the Concluding Remarks (5).

[41, 42]. The device captures acceleration along three orthogonal axes: vertical, hori-
zontal, and perpendicular (VA, HA, PPA) [45, 46]. 15-second epoch count files were
generated via ActiLife software (v.6) [41, 42]. Counts from each axis were summed
over four consecutive 15-second intervals to obtain minute-level counts, then combined
across axes to compute the vector magnitude (VM) as

Inclusion criteria and valid-day definitions. For each wave, minute-level

2.3 Physical function outcome
Physical function (PF) reflects the capacity to perform daily activities requiring
locomotion, balance, and muscular endurance, and its decline is a well-established
precursor to disability, loss of independence, and mortality in older adults [51, 52].
Importantly, low PF is a risk factor for CVD [53, 54] and predicts incident CVD events
after adjustment for traditional risk factors [55, 56]. There is also compelling evidence
that PA is central to preserving PF and mobility in later life [57–63]. Given its dual
role as a key pathway through which PA influences aging-related outcomes and as an
independent predictor of incident CVD, PF served as the primary outcome of this
study.
PF was assessed using RAND-36 Physical Functioning subscale, which quantifies
health-related limitations in daily physical activities on a 0–100 scale, with higher
values indicating better functioning [64]. Because participants’ RAND-36 assessments
and accelerometer wear wave did not always occur simultaneously, the first day of
accelerometer wear at each of W1 and W2 was matched to the nearest RAND-36
assessment within a ±190-day window. This window was chosen to minimize the time
discrepancy between two different instruments while maximizing participant retention.

or less, some college, college graduate or higher), alcohol consumption (nondrinker,
< 1 drink/week, ≥1 drink/week, unknown), current smoking status (no, yes), and

3.2 Multivariate functional principal component analysis on
changes in PA
To extract the dominant modes of longitudinal PA change and to preserve the dis-
tinction between these two clinically interpretable x (Temporal) and y (Amplitude)
domains, we applied multivariate functional principal component analysis (MFPCA)
using the approach of Happ and Greven [38].
In this framework, univariate eigenfunctions and scores were first estimated sepa-
rately on each domain, and the multivariate eigenfunctions and scores were obtained
as linear combinations of their univariate counterparts with a weight factor. The esti-
mation steps were described in more details in Appendix (A) and [38]. MFPCA was
applied separately to each transition period with identical parameter settings.
An alternative, simpler strategy is to concatenate the x- and y-components of
the initial momenta into a single vector function and apply standard univariate
FPCA (UFPCA) [69]. If the measurement on both domains had identical grids (time-
points), comparable variance, and the principal modes were not highly correlated
across domains, concatenated UFPCA can approximate MFPCA reasonably. Other-
wise, naive concatenation risks producing blended components in which temporal-shift
modes are absorbed into amplitude modes (or vice versa), obscuring domain-specific
interpretability. We therefore report results from both approaches and quantify their
differences.

3.3 Association between changes in PA and health outcomes
Three sets of predictors were derived in earlier sections to characterize longitudinal
changes in diurnal PA patterns: (i) MFPCA principal component scores (Section 3.2),
which capture the dominant modes of change in both temporal shift and amplitude;
(ii) ∆net-AUC(η) (Section 2.2), which summarizes net directional change (positive
versus negative) in PA without information on temporal shift, and (iii) initial deforma-
tion energy d(η)
i
(Section 3.1), which summarizes overall amount of PA change without
directionality in timing and amplitude.
Because PC1 and ∆net-AUC were highly collinear in both periods (Pearson r =
0.9), they were not included in the same model. In contrast, deformation energy showed
weak correlations with PC1 (|r| ≤0.15) and with ∆net-AUC (|r| ≤0.30) in both
periods, so deformation energy was modeled jointly with PC1 and, separately, with
∆net-AUC. Two LMMs were carried out with participant-specific random effects to
account for repeated PF outcome at W1 and W2: Model 1 included the stacked
deformation-based change features (PC scores) and deformation energy, together with
baseline PF scores and other covariates; Model 2 replaced the PC scores with the
scalar change summary, ∆net-AUC, while retaining deformation energy, baseline PF
and covariates. Inclusion of interaction terms between period and the predictors were
determined using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs) comparing nested models. To reduce
overfitting and improve interpretability, we applied LASSO [70] to select covariates and
PCs in the LMM via R package glmmLasso[71]. The LASSO penalty parameter was
determined by Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the significance threshold
for two LMMs was adjusted to α = 0.025 by Bonferroni method.
All statistical analyses were conducted in the R statistical programming environ-
ment (version 4.3.1) [72], except for the initial momenta estimation and optimization
steps, which were implemented using the MATLAB package fshapesTK [35] and exe-
cuted on the National Research Platform Nautilus Kubernetes Cluster [73]. R package
nlme for LMM is available from [74] and package MFPCA is available from [75].

4.2 Associations between longitudinal PA change and PF
For association analyses, two LMMs were described in Section (3.3) and LRT revealed
significant deformation-energy-by-period interaction in both models (p < 0.01). In
Model 1, LASSO removed PC2, PC3, PC4, baseline smoking status, and DBP; in
Model 2, baseline smoking status and DBP were removed as well.
Because PF and accelerometer assessments were not collected concurrently, PF
was missing for 260 participants at W1 and 338 at W2 even after allowing a ±190-day
matching window. Several baseline covariates also had substantial missingness (e.g.,
423 missing Reynolds Risk Score and 329 missing cholesterol/glucose/blood pressure
measures). Furthermore, 106 participants with previous CVD diagnosis at baseline
were excluded. After restricting to participants with complete data on PF and all

5 Concluding Remarks
Prior OPACH analyses have established associations between accelerometer-measured
PA and mortality [4, 7], CVD and heart failure [8, 9], cancer [11], incident falls [76],
and blood glucose [29]. A longitudinal OPACH–WHISH study reported significant
PA decline over 7 years, with the largest decline observed for MVPA [77]. Recent
work from the Women’s Health Accelerometry Collaboration (WHAC) showed that
higher accelerometer-measured PA was associated with improved odds of survival to
age 90 with and without mobility disability [78, 79]. Cross-sectional evidence further
supports associations between sensor-based PA and physical fitness [80, 81], as well
as between PA and PF measured by the Short Physical Performance Battery [21, 82].
In these previous analyses, the accelerometer output typically was processed using
conventional framework resulting in averaged VM count time-series data, which might
obscure important shifts in habitual PA amounts and patterns throughout the day.

Our present work extends this literature by shifting the analytic target from static
PA levels to longitudinal change in diurnal PA patterns. Rather than relying solely
on scalar summaries, we modeled visit-to-visit change as Riemannian diffeomorphic
deformations between diurnal PA curves and used MFPCA to obtain clinically inter-
pretable change phenotypes. After removal of the mean function, PC1 represented the
residual dominant mode of diurnal PA change (increase/decline) across the day, espe-
cially between 10 a.m. and 7 p.m.. It was strongly associated with PF after adjustment
for baseline PF and covariates, supporting the clinical relevance of deformation-derived
change features. We also observed a positive association between PF and ∆net-AUC,
a signed scalar measure of total activity change, but ∆net-AUC does not distinguish
whether change is driven by timing reallocation, magnitude shifts at specific times of
day, or both. Deformation energy provided a complementary scalar index of overall
PA change, and its association with PF was stronger in W1–W2 than in baseline–W1,
indicating greater sensitivity of later-life function to recent changes in daily activ-
ity routines. Together, these findings underscore that how activity changes across the
day carries information relevant to functional aging that is not captured by standard
aggregate metrics.
There were several limitations. First, OPACH–WHISH participants are older
women; results may not generalize to other populations. Second, missing data in the
LMMs may have reduced statistical power and could introduce some degree of bias
in parameter estimates. Third, the randomization status was unavailable at the time
of analysis; incorporating a group indicator to assess whether PF changes differed
between intervention arms is an important future direction. Fourth, PF was measured
by self-reported RAND-36 rather than performance-based metrics (e.g., gait speed);
however, in the national WHI cohort, within which the OPACH-WHISH subset resides,
RAND-36 is highly correlated with performance-based PF measures and shows similar
discrimination of mortality outcomes [83]. Fifth, we pooled weekdays and weekends
13


## Page 14


when constructing each participant’s diurnal PA curve, which may mask day-type-
specific patterns of change; separately modeling weekday and weekend deformations
is a natural future direction of this work. Finally, MFPCA was applied separately to
each period rather than as a single multi-level MFPCA. Although multi-level models
offer advantages such as borrowing strength across periods and separating between-
and within-subject components, existing frameworks are largely limited to small sam-
ple sizes due to computational challenges [84–88]. An important future direction is to
develop scalable multi-level MFPCA method that is applicable to large cohorts like
OPACH-WHISH.
