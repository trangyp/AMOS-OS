Introduction
In 1986, Lambek and Scott wrote [LS86, Hist. comments on §II.20]:
The question in the text concerning the projectivity of N in the
free topos, equivalently, the countable rule of choice in pure
type theory, has of course been solved by proof-theoretical
techniques (see Friedman and Scedrov 1983). Makkai in unpub-
lished notes (1980) cast the logician’s proof into a categorical
(actually 2-categorical) mould.
Unfortunately, neither of the cited proofs ever saw the light of day, and the
proofs have generally been considered lost [Oos06].

In December 2015, Michael Makkai visited Stockholm, where the first two
authors were then postdocs. We pressed him on this topic; once back home,
he unearthed his original (marvellously lucid) notes and kindly gave us his
blessing to write up the proof for eventual publication. After barely another
decade, here it is.
In the end, our treatment diverges substantially from Makkai’s original —
primarily due to the subsequent development of the field, partly of course
just from personal taste, and finally from the desire to give as self-contained
and elementary an exposition as possible. In particular, we hope this should
be accessible for essentially anyone familiar with elementary toposes and a
little of their basic development.
Proof sketch. Like many constructive systems, intuitionistic higher-order lo-
gic (IHOL𝑁), the logic of elementary toposes, enjoys various meta-theorems
saying that existence proofs can be distilled into constructions. Most fun-
damental is the existence property (EP): if IHOL𝑁proves “∃𝑥:𝐴𝜑(𝑥)”, then
there is some IHOL𝑁-definable 𝑎:𝐴for which IHOL𝑁proves “𝜑(𝑎)”.
Categorically, this says that in the free topos, every cover of the terminal
object [[ 𝑥:𝐴| 𝜑(𝑥) ]]

gluing argument gives a very clean, purely categorical proof of this fact.
Our main goal is to strengthen this, to show that the rule of count-

Certainly, if IHOL𝑁proves “∀𝑛∃𝑥𝜑(𝑛,𝑥)”, the existence property tells

Outline. We start in Section 1 by collecting a grab-bag of background
material as required for later chapters, and fixing notation and terminology
for the main notions we work with: first some general categorical logic,
then some basics of essentially algebraic (aka cartesian) theories, and lastly
the construction of the free topos itself.
In Section 2, we define ranked toposes, categorical analogues of the bounded-
rank fragments of higher-order logic, and develop their essential properties.
In Section 3, we then develop the results on internal ranked toposes
required for the main proof; we use the framework of indexed categories to
handle the interaction between external and internal ranked toposes, and
of locoses/arithmetic universes to construct and analyse internal free ranked
toposes. The results there on exact completion and free models in arithmetic
universes may be of independent interest (Theorem 3.34, Corollary 3.35).
With this machinery in place, Section 4 gives the endgame, putting to-
gether the main reflection results for projectivity of 𝑁and admissibility
of RC𝜔as sketched above (Theorem 4.24, Corollary 4.25), along with ana-
logues for dependent choice (Theorem 4.27, Corollary 4.28). Finally, we look
back over the proof, and discuss possible alternative approaches and related
literature.

1. Preliminaries
We begin by recalling some background material, tailored to the forms we
will require. Sections 1.1 and 1.2 cover general category theory and essentially
algebraic theories, respectively; these serve mostly to fix notation and note
a few theorems we require, so the impatient reader may safely skip them.
Section 1.3 introduces the free topos and Freyd’s gluing argument; this serves
additionally as a warm-up for the ranked versions of these in Sections 2
and 3. Two further topics, indexed categories and arithmetic universes, we
leave until they appear later in the story.
1.1. General category theory. We assume some background in general
category theory — essentially, familiarity with the notions involved in ele-
mentary toposes and their basic theory. The early chapters of any text in
topos theory should certainly suffice — [Joh02, §A1], say. For everything
beyond this, we recall at least in outline the necessary definitions and results.
In most choices of definitions and terminology, we follow the Elephant
[Joh02]. One peculiarity in particular deserves setting out from the start:
Convention 1.1 ([Joh02, §A1.2]). Whenever we speak of structures satisfy-
ing existence conditions (e.g. categories with limits, essentially surjective
functors), we mean these to include a choice of witnesses for the existence
(chosen limits, chosen lifts-up-to-isomorphism, and so on). We do not how-
ever ask morphisms to preserve these choices, unless explicitly specified.
Assuming the Axiom of Choice, this is of course inconsequential. Our
main motivation is for uniformity with internal category theory, where the
chosen-structure version is almost always advantageous since it presents
structured categories as essentially algebraic notions.

4.4. History and related work. The early history of work on RC𝜔is
somewhat murky, with more folklore than published literature; the following
is the best understanding we have been able to reach, based in part on
correspondence with Phil Scott and conversations with Michael Makkai. For

Section 4.5
The first proof of admissibility of RC𝜔was for second-order arithmetic
(HAS), in Troelstra [Tro73, Thm. 4.5.12], couched in traditional proof-theoretic
language (and with a technical error corrected by Hayashi [Hay77, App. 3]).5

4.5. Comparison with proof-theoretic presentations. In the introduc-
tion we outlined the argument in terms of IHOL𝑁; then we actually proved
it entirely categorically, and extracted the IHOL𝑁version as a corollary. As
usual, the categorical treatment could be compiled down into a more tradi-
tionally proof-theoretic treatment working syntactically in IHOL𝑁, along
the lines of the introductory sketch.
In such a development, our reflection theorems (Section 4) would turn out
essentially like Lemmas 4.5.8–9 of [Tro73], and our endgame much like the
proof of Thm. 4.5.12 there (all adapted from HAS to HAH/IHOL𝑁). The most
substantial difference from Troelstra/Hayashi’s approach is that they obtain
EP by normalisation techniques, where we used Freyd’s gluing argument.
As shown in Scedrov and Scott [ŠS82], Freyd’s gluing topos corresponds on
the syntactic side to a variant of the Kleene slash due to Friedman6 ; our
proof thus corresponds to suitable internalisations of the Friedman slash for
fragments of IHOL𝑁/HAH.

intuitively and expositorily simpler. However, the bureaucratic encoding
