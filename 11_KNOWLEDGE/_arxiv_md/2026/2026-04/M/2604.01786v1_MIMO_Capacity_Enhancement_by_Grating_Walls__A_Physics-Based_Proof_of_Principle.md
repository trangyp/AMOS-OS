MIMO Capacity Enhancement by Grating Walls:
A Physics-Based Proof of Principle
Xiaolu Yang, Oscar C´espedes Vicente, and Christophe Caloz, Fellow, IEEE
KU Leuven, Department of Electrical Engineering, Kasteelpark Arenberg 10, 3001 Leuven, Belgium
Abstract—This paper investigates the passive enhancement of
MIMO spectral efficiency through boundary engineering in a
simplified two-dimensional indoor proof-of-principle model. The
propagation channel is constructed from the electromagnetic
Green’s function of a room with boundaries modeled as free
space, drywall, perfect electric conductor (PEC), or binary grat-
ings. Within this framework, grating-coated walls enrich the non-
line-of-sight (NLoS) multipath field, reduce channel correlation,
and enhance spatial multiplexing over a broad range of receiver
locations. Comparisons with the drywall and PEC reference cases
further reveal that the observed capacity enhancement arises
not from diffraction alone, but from the combined effects of
effective wall reflectivity, which confines and reradiates energy
within the room, and diffraction-induced angular redistribution,
which enriches the channel eigenstructure.
Index Terms—MIMO capacity, environment engineering, grat-
ing walls, multipath propagation, spatial multiplexing, diffrac-
tion, statistical channel modeling.
I. INTRODUCTION
Multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems increase
spectral efficiency by exploiting the spatial eigenstructure of
the propagation channel [1]. When the environment supports
several sufficiently distinct propagation paths, the channel
matrix can sustain several effective eigenchannels and hence
a larger multiplexing gain. In most wireless-system designs,
this capability is improved primarily through array design,
beamforming, coding, or signal processing. A complementary
possibility is to engineer the propagation environment itself.
Electromagnetic metasurfaces [2] and reconfigurable intelli-
gent surfaces [3] have recently attracted considerable attention
as tools for environment-assisted wireless communication.
Most of that literature focuses on directional link enhancement
through beam steering or wave redirection toward a known
receiver [4]. Much less attention has been paid to a different
question that is central to MIMO: whether engineered bound-
aries can improve capacity by enriching multipath, reducing
channel correlation, and reshaping the singular-value distribu-
tion of the channel matrix.
This paper studies that question in a deliberately simpli-
fied but physically transparent setting. We consider a two-
dimensional empty-room model with scalar TEz fields and
line-current sources, and we compare four boundary cases:
free space, drywall, perfect electric conductor (PEC), and
binary gratings [5]. Within this setting, the channel matrix is
obtained from the Green’s function of the environment, and the
corresponding MIMO spectral efficiency is evaluated through
singular-value decomposition and water-filling.
The main contributions are as follows. First, we provide a
physics-based formulation that connects the MIMO channel
matrix to the Green’s function of the engineered environment.
Second, we show numerically that grating walls can improve
MIMO spectral efficiency over a broad region of receiver lo-
cations relative to free space and drywall. Third, we show that
the relevant mechanism is not diffraction alone: the observed
enhancement is governed by the interplay between effective
wall reflectivity and diffraction-induced angular redistribution.
Finally, we complement the field and capacity results with
mode-level and statistical interpretations.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion II introduces the Green’s-function channel formulation
and the corresponding MIMO capacity calculation. Section III
presents the system configuration. Section IV describes the
four wall models. Section V presents the field, capacity,
and mode-level results. Section VI provides a complementary
statistical interpretation. Section VII discusses grating-period
effects. Section VIII concludes the paper.

III. SYSTEM CONFIGURATION
Figure 1 illustrates the geometry considered in this work.
The propagation region is a rectangular room of dimensions
ℓroom × wroom. Its boundaries are modeled as free space,
drywall, PEC, or grating walls. A transmitter array and a
receiver array are placed inside the room. Their separation
is denoted by dTR, their orientations by θT and θR, and the
distances from the transmitter to the walls along the −x and
−y directions are denoted by dTWx, dTWy.
The analysis is performed in a two-dimensional TEz setting.
Each antenna element is modeled as an out-of-plane time-
harmonic line current source, which radiates a cylindrical wave
in the x–y plane. This choice is deliberate: it simplifies the
model enough to isolate the boundary-induced mechanisms
while retaining a rigorous wave-based channel formulation.
A key implication of the geometry is that the receiver
operates in the radiative near field of the surrounding walls.

V. NUMERICAL RESULTS
Based on the electromagnetic models established in Sec. IV,
we now examine how different wall types affect the field
behavior and the resulting communication capacity.
A. Electric Fields in Different Walls
Figure 2 shows the spatial distributions of electric-field
magnitude for SISO and MIMO transmission under the com-
mon parameter set. In SISO, free space shows the expected
smooth cylindrical decay. Drywall introduces weak ripples
through partial reflection. The grating wall produces a sub-
stantially more irregular field because the periodic boundary
redistributes energy into additional reflected directions. PEC
produces the strongest oscillations because repeated lossless
reflections generate a dense interference pattern. In MIMO,
the free-space field is strongly directional because the array
forms a dominant forward beam. Drywall perturbs this beam
only moderately. The grating wall distorts it more strongly
and creates a richer interference pattern, while PEC produces
the most oscillatory response. These field maps indicate that
the walls reshape the propagation environment globally rather
than only locally, but field complexity alone is not sufficient
to infer MIMO performance. That requires explicit evaluation
of the channel matrix and its singular values.

VII. DISCUSSION
In this section, we analyze the MIMO capacity achieved
by grating walls with different periods, and then relate the
observed trends to the corresponding field behavior in the
actual system. Special attention is given to the effects of
cylindrical-wave illumination and finite room size, which
cause the actual diffraction behavior to deviate from the ideal
predictions of the grating equation.
Figure 8 shows the MIMO capacity enhancement of grating
walls with different periods relative to the drywall reference.
The enhancement is largest for the subwavelength grating
p = 0.25λ. Although the overall tendency is a decrease
in capacity enhancement with increasing grating period, the
dependence on p is clearly non-monotonic. To examine this
behavior more closely, we select three representative grating
periods, as marked in Fig. 8, namely p = 0.25λ, p = 2λ, and
p = 30λ. Denoting by N the number of unit cells along the
30λ-long room wall, these three cases correspond to N = 120,
N = 15, and N = 1, respectively.
To interpret the capacity trend observed above, Figs. 9 and
10 compare the corresponding total fields, scattered fields,
and angular spectra5. for these three representative periods.
Two regimes should be distinguished. In the subwavelength
regime, the grating behaves approximately as a homogenized
high-reflectivity boundary. The large capacity enhancement of
the p = 0.25λ case is therefore attributed mainly to strong
confinement and reradiation of energy inside the room. How-
ever, because such a perfectly closed cavity does not permit
electromagnetic energy exchange with the exterior, it does
not correspond to a practical indoor–outdoor communication
scenario.
In the non-homogenized regime, diffraction-induced angular
redistribution becomes more important. Among the periods
considered, p = 2λ shows the richest directional redistribution
in the finite-room configuration. By contrast, p = 30λ corre-
sponds effectively to only one unit cell along the wall and
therefore cannot support well-developed periodic scattering.
The fact that p = 2λ outperforms p = 30λ even though both
are less metal-like than p = 0.25λ shows that reflectivity alone
does not determine capacity enhancement.
The mechanism can therefore be summarized as follows:
effective reflectivity governs how much energy is confined and
reradiated inside the room, while diffraction-induced angular
redistribution governs how richly that reflected energy is
distributed across directions and hence across channel modes.
The grating advantage is produced by the interplay of both
effects.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper has presented a physics-based two-dimensional
study of MIMO capacity enhancement by grating-engineered
walls. By constructing the channel from the Green’s function
of the propagation environment, the work connects elec-
tromagnetic boundary behavior directly to channel singular
values and to MIMO spectral efficiency. Within the simplified
empty-room model, grating walls improve capacity relative to
free space and drywall over a broad range of receiver positions.
The results further show that the governing mechanism is
not diffraction alone, but the combined action of effective re-
flectivity and diffraction-induced angular redistribution, which
together reshape the channel eigenstructure.
The present study is a two-dimensional proof of principle
designed to isolate the physical role of boundary engineering.
Extensions to three-dimensional propagation, vector electro-
magnetic fields, realistic antennas, broadband signaling, and
cluttered indoor scenarios are left for future work.
