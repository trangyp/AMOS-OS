ABSTRACT
The outer Galaxy presents an optimal setting for investigating molecular clouds and star formation in environments with low metal-
licity. A total of 72 Galactic edge clouds were surveyed using the CO (2–1) line with the IRAM 30 m telescope, leading to the
identification of 112 CO clumps within molecular clouds with linear resolutions of 0.5–0.9 pc. Parameters such as size, mass,
surface density, and velocity dispersion of these CO clumps, derived from CO (2–1) observations, exhibit ranges of 0.6–3.4 pc,
34–8250 M⊙, 12–1025 M⊙pc−2, and 0.3–1.7 km s−1, respectively. Over the Galactocentric distance range of 14–23 kpc, no sys-

1. Introduction
Outskirts of the Galaxy, known as low-density and metal-poor
environments, provide an ideal laboratory to study molecular
clouds and star formation (Kobayashi et al. 2008; Yasui et al.
2008). However, molecular clouds at the edge of the Galaxy ex-
hibit a spatially extended yet sparse distribution across the sky,
owing to their remote locations. The pressure exerted by the
inter-cloud medium is expected to be low compared to that in
the inner Galaxy. External triggers for star formation are either
absent or only weakly present, with spiral arms and supernova
remnants being less significant (Brand & Wouterloot 1995). Ob-
servational studies of molecular gas in the outer Galaxy have
been carried out and star formation activity has been found to be
present (e.g., Fich & Blitz 1984; Mead & Kutner 1988; Wouter-
loot et al. 1988; Wouterloot & Brand 1989; Digel et al. 1994;
Heyer et al. 2001; Du et al. 2016; Su et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2020,
2024a,b; Ma et al. 2021, 2022; Braine et al. 2023; Urquhart et al.
2024; Cheng et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025; Luo et al. 2025). This
includes star-forming molecular clouds (e.g., Gong et al. 2016;
Izumi et al. 2017, 2022, 2024; Ikeda et al. 2025), stellar clus-
ters (e.g., Yasui et al. 2006, 2008; Kobayashi et al. 2008; Izumi
et al. 2014), H ii regions (e.g., de Geus et al. 1993; Rudolph et al.
1996; Anderson et al. 2015), and maser lines (e.g., Armentrout
et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2018b). Nevertheless, the Galactic edge
clouds have not been subjected to the same level of detailed ob-
servational studies as nearby clouds.
Distinct boundaries and hierarchical structures have been ob-
served in molecular clouds (e.g., Goodman et al. 1998; McKee
& Ostriker 2007; Rosolowsky et al. 2008; Yuan et al. 2021;
Shen et al. 2024). Molecular cloud structures can be catego-
rized into clouds (∼10 pc), clumps (∼1 pc), and cores (∼0.1 pc).
These entities are successively smaller in size and higher in den-
sity (Williams et al. 2000; Kauffmann et al. 2013). The major-
ity of stars originate from the dense regions within molecular
clouds, where gravity is sufficiently strong to induce gas col-
lapse (Bergin & Tafalla 2007). The clump properties reflect the
initial conditions for stellar cluster formation (Mok et al. 2021).
Increasing evidence suggests that the physical properties of the

The three scaling relations proposed by Larson (1981) serve
as fundamental observational constraints on the dynamics of
molecular clouds. Larson (1981) established a power-law rela-
tion between velocity dispersion (σv) and cloud size (L) with
an index of 0.38, an inverse correlation between the mean den-
sity of the cloud (n(H2)) and L, and the relation 2GM/σ2

tween gravitational and kinetic energy densities (Heyer & Dame
2015). Notably, these relations are interdependent, where the va-
lidity of any two expressions algebraically implies the third. This
interconnection is encapsulated in the unified expression equat-

original value of ∼0.38 derived by Larson (1981).
Numerous surveys utilizing multiple molecular tracers
across diverse scales in both our Galaxy and external galaxies
have significantly contributed to the advancement and enhance-
ment of these scaling relations. These surveys offer a consis-
tent dataset with extensive samples of molecular clouds encom-
passing a wide range of environmental conditions. The velocity
dispersion-size relation (hereafter σv–R relation) has been in-
vestigated in different environments, including the Milky Way
(e.g., Larson 1981; Solomon et al. 1987; Heyer et al. 2001, 2009;
Shetty et al. 2012; Heyer & Dame 2015; Rice et al. 2016; Sun
et al. 2017, 2024a; Miville-Deschênes et al. 2017; Ma et al.
2021; Feng et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2024a,b) and nearby galaxies
(e.g., Wong et al. 2017, 2019, 2022; Sun et al. 2018a; O’Neill
et al. 2022b; Saldaño et al. 2023; Finn et al. 2024; Krahm et al.
2024). Observational results suggest that the power-law index
predominantly varies from 1/3 to 1/2 between velocity disper-
sion and size (e.g., Liu et al. 2012; Colombo et al. 2014; Maeda
et al. 2020). Giant molecular clouds (GMCs) are typically de-
fined as molecular gas structures with masses exceeding 105 M⊙
and spatial extents of ∼10–100 pc (Blitz 1993). Within GMCs,
the power-law index approximating 1/2 is indicative of super-
sonic turbulence, while the power-law index of 1/3 suggests the
presence of subsonic turbulence (McKee & Ostriker 2007; Inde-
betouw et al. 2013).
The relation between mass and size (hereafter M–R relation)
in GMCs also exhibits a power-law scaling of M ∝Lq. Previ-
ous studies indicate that the q value typically falls within the
range of 1 to 3 (e.g., Larson 1981; Solomon et al. 1987; Shetty
et al. 2010; Kauffmann et al. 2010a,b; Kauffmann & Pillai 2010;
Miville-Deschênes et al. 2017; Faesi et al. 2018; Traficante et al.
2018; Ballesteros-Paredes et al. 2019; Lada & Dame 2020; Li
& Zhang 2020; Ma et al. 2021; Xing & Qiu 2022). q = 2 implies
that molecular clouds maintain a nearly constant average column
density or surface density as a function of size. Nevertheless, the
M–R relation within individual clouds can not be adequately rep-
resented by a single power-law, as q demonstrates variation with
radius (Kauffmann et al. 2010a,b). The empirical threshold of
the M–R relation for massive star formation generally adheres to
M(R) > 580M⊙(R/pc)1.33 (Kauffmann & Pillai 2010).
As mentioned above, while numerous studies have been con-
ducted on the physical properties of molecular clouds, obser-
vational biases are unavoidable owing to limited spatial resolu-
tion and sensitivity. To mitigate these biases, observations span-
ning a larger dynamic range are required. The outer regions of
the Galaxy may be younger than its inner counterparts, as ev-
idenced by the presence of young stellar populations (Martig
et al. 2016). The outer Galaxy exhibits characteristics akin to
those of dwarf galaxies and the Milky Way in the early phase
of formation (Izumi et al. 2024). Previous observations sug-
gest that about half of the molecular gas is CO-dark in the so-
lar neighborhood (e.g., Paradis et al. 2012; Pineda et al. 2013;
Chen et al. 2015). This contribution is expected to be even more
pronounced in the low metallicity environment of the Galactic
edge clouds (Pineda et al. 2013; Langer et al. 2014; Luo et al.
2024c). The physical properties and scaling relations of molec-
ular clouds have been studied in the outer Galaxy (e.g., Mead
& Kutner 1988; Brand & Wouterloot 1995; Wouterloot & Brand
1995; May et al. 1997; Brand et al. 2001; Heyer et al. 2001; Su
et al. 2016; Sun et al. 2017, 2024a; Ma et al. 2021; Urquhart et al.
2024; Yuan & Yang 2025). However, such clump-scale studies
at the edge of the Galaxy remain limited. Recently, a large num-
ber of molecular clouds in the outer Galaxy have been identified
by the Milky Way Imaging Scroll Painting (MWISP) project, an
unbiased CO survey of the northern Galactic plane with an an-
gular resolution of ∼1′ (Sun et al. 2015, 2024a,b; Su et al. 2019;
Yang et al. 2026). While MWISP provides an excellent census
of outer-Galaxy molecular clouds with Galactocentric distances
up to ∼26 kpc, its angular resolution is insufficient to resolve in-
dividual molecular clumps at the edge of the Galaxy. Mapping
these molecular clouds with higher sensitivity and angular reso-
lution is therefore essential for characterizing clump-scale phys-
ical properties and star formation at the edge of the Galaxy.
The present study aims to observe Galactic edge clouds at
high resolution (∼11′′) in an effort to investigate the physical
properties of molecular clumps and to explain their equilibrium
state by scaling relations. In Sects. 2 and 3, we introduce our
observations, data reduction, and describe the main results. The
discussion of the clump’s dynamical state is presented in Sect. 4.
Our main conclusions are summarized in Sect. 5. This paper is
part of the "Molecular Clouds at the Edge of the Galaxy" project,
which focuses on the investigation of the physical and chemical
properties of molecular clouds and star formation located in the
outskirts of the Milky Way.

2. Observations and data reduction
2.1. Observations
An extension of the Scutum-Centaurus arm has been detected
beyond the Outer arm (Dame & Thaddeus 2011; Sun et al. 2015,
2024b). Sun et al. (2015) catalogued 72 molecular clouds located
in the second Galactic quadrant (100◦< l < 150◦) of this arm,
which is situated at the edge of the Galaxy (Rg ∼14 –22 kpc, see
also Fig. 1 in Luo et al. 2025). The kinematic distances of these
Galactic edge clouds were determined by Sun et al. (2015) us-
ing the Galactic rotation curve from Reid et al. (2014). In the
present study, we recalculated these distances employing the up-
dated Galactic rotation curve model from Reid et al. (2019). The
derived results are listed in Table C.1. The mean Galactocentric
distance of these Galactic edge clouds is ∼16.7 kpc, correspond-
ing to a metallicity of ∼0.3 Z⊙(Z⊙signifies the Solar metallic-
ity; Méndez-Delgado et al. 2022). Utilizing the IRAM 30 m tele-
scope1, high-sensitivity, high-resolution maps of the CO (2–1)
line were obtained towards these 72 Galactic edge clouds. Maps
of size ∼100′′ × 100′′ are generated by the On-The-Fly (OTF)
observing mode. The Half Power Beam Width (HPBW) is ∼11′′
at a central frequency of ∼230 GHz. The heliocentric distances

3. Results
3.1. Overview
The CO (2–1) line has been detected in all 72 observed molec-
ular clouds at the edge of the Milky Way. The integrated in-
tensity distributions are presented in Figs. 1 and A.1. Morpho-
logical structures of these clouds have been resolved by the
CO (2–1) data at linear resolutions of 0.5–0.9 pc. Overall, 25
clouds exhibit compact morphologies (e.g., G139.116−1.475),
25 show diffuse structures (e.g., G145.808−1.817), and 22 dis-
play intermediate morphologies between these two categories
(e.g., G101.992+3.016; see Fig. 1 and Table C.1 of Luo et al.
2025). Typical examples of these three cloud structure classes
are also illustrated in Fig. A.1 of Luo et al. (2025). In compact
clouds, 81 clumps have been identified, whereas in intermedi-
ate clouds and diffuse clouds, the numbers stand at 28 and 3
(see Sect. 3.2), respectively. The clumps predominantly reside in
compact clouds, with their presence diminishing in intermedi-
ate and diffuse clouds. Clouds exhibiting clumpy morphology
demonstrate a higher degree of star formation (Neralwar et al.
2022; Luo et al. 2025). The morphology of molecular clouds at
the edge of the Galaxy exhibits similarities to those observed in
the less extreme outer Galaxy, notably characterized by filamen-
tary, clumpy, and extended structures (Yuan et al. 2021).

4. Discussion
4.1. Clump parameters as a function of distance
Previous observations indicate that molecular clouds in the outer
Galaxy, observed in CO, are generally smaller, less massive,
and exhibit lower densities and narrower linewidths compared to
those in the inner Galaxy (e.g., Brand & Wouterloot 1995; May
et al. 1997; Heyer & Dame 2015; Sun et al. 2024a; Lin et al.
2025). As mentioned in Sect. 1, the differences in physical prop-
erties between the inner and outer Galaxy can be attributed to
variations in the surrounding environment. In the outer Galaxy,
CO-dark H2 gas may contribute more significantly than locally
to the total molecular mass, potentially introducing systematic
uncertainties in mass-related estimates (see Sect. 4.5). A radial
gradient has been reported in cloud velocity dispersion (Rg = 0–
24 kpc; Sun et al. 2024a; Lin et al. 2025). Observed surface
densities also appear to show a radial decrease, diminishing
from 1800 M⊙pc−2 near the Galactic center to 200 M⊙pc−2 in
the molecular ring (Rg ∼3–5 kpc) and further down to about
30 M⊙pc−2 in the outer Galaxy (θbeam ∼45′′; see Fig. 8 in Heyer
& Dame 2015). In contrast, Urquhart et al. 2024 report no dis-
cernible difference in the surface density of clumps between the
inner and outer Galaxy (θbeam ∼30′′). The distributions of clump
mass and luminosity seem to show a minimum near the Solar
circle (∼8 kpc), but this is attributable to the particularly high
sensitivity and resolution accessible for sources near the Sun
(Urquhart et al. 2024). Beyond ∼14 kpc from the Galactic center,
the CO luminosity no longer appears to exhibit a clear declining
trend due to the increasing detection threshold with heliocentric
distance (Heyer & Dame 2015).
Compared to previous studies, our data provide a substan-
tially higher angular resolution (θbeam ∼11′′), which is partic-
ularly relevant for size- and density-related parameters. Tak-
ing into account the variation of the CO-to-H2 conversion fac-
tor with Galactocentric distance, as outlined in Sect. 3.3, the
ranges of size, mass, and volume density of our Galactic edge
clumps are found to be 0.6–3.4 pc, 34–8250 M⊙, and 100–
16100 cm−3, respectively (see Tables 1 and C.1). Over the Galac-
tocentric distance range of 14–23 kpc, we find no compelling
evidence for systematic radial trends in clump size, mass, vol-
ume density, velocity dispersion, luminosity, or surface density
(see Fig. 3). No clear dependence of these properties on he-
liocentric distance is observed over Ds ∼9–16 kpc. We observe
marginal increases in clump size and mass at the largest dis-
tances (Rg ∼22–23 kpc, corresponding to Ds ∼15–16 kpc; see
Fig. 3), while the volume density remains approximately con-
stant. Notably, the mean clump mass in the outermost Galacto-
centric bins (∼1863 M⊙; see Fig. 3 (c)) is nearly an order of mag-
nitude higher than the median mass of the full sample (216 M⊙;

4.2. Velocity dispersion-size relation
As mentioned in Sect. 1, the first Larson relation demonstrates
a power-law form between velocity dispersion and size, repre-
sented as σv = v0R m
eff. The σv–R relation is considered as a result
of multi-scale turbulent motions within the interstellar medium.
Large-scale turbulence promotes the fragmentation of molec-
ular clouds, subsequently leading to the formation of clumps.
Conversely, small-scale turbulence may suppress star formation
by sustaining gravitational collapse (Larson 1981). This relation
gains increasing credibility due to the utilization of multi-tracers
and large cloud samples. The σv–R relation of molecular clouds
has been studied in various tracers, including CO, H2CO, HCO+,
HCN, N2H+, CS, and NH3 (e.g., Larson 1981; Fuller & My-
ers 1992; Goodman et al. 1998; Shetty et al. 2012; Kauffmann
et al. 2017; Gong et al. 2023; Luo et al. 2024b). Furthermore,
it has been studied through observations on various scales, in-
cluding cloud scale (e.g., Larson 1981; Solomon et al. 1987),
clump scale (e.g., Traficante et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2024b), and
core scale (e.g., Fuller & Myers 1992; Caselli & Myers 1995;
Barnes et al. 2021; Li et al. 2023). The σv–R relation in low
metallicity galaxies has been found to be consistent with that of
the Milky Way, where the coefficient v0 is highest for the Milky
Way and the index m is highest for dwarf galaxies (Rubio et al.
2015). Typical values found in previous studies are v0 ∼0.4–3.0
and m ∼0.3–0.6, depending on the tracer, spatial scale, and en-
vironment.

4.4. Virial stability
The equilibrium state of the clumps is typically characterized
by the ratio of internal kinetic energy to gravitational potential
energy (i.e., virial parameter αvir; see Eq. 12), a concept known
as the second Larson relation (Larson 1981). The critical virial
parameter (αcri) is used to determine whether a molecular cloud
can maintain equilibrium. In the ideal case of virial equilibrium,
αvir ≈1, while values up to αvir ∼2 are often considered consis-
tent with gravitationally bound structures when accounting for
non-ideal effects such as turbulence and external perturbations
(Kauffmann et al. 2013; Rigby et al. 2019). Approximately 80%
of cores situated within 70 µm dark high-mass clumps in infrared
dark clouds are gravitationally bound (αvir < 2; Li et al. 2023).
However, the existence of gravitationally bound dense cores
does not necessitate that the parent cloud be globally bound.
The virial parameter of our Galactic edge clumps spans a wide
range from 0.6 to 15.3, with most values concentrated between
1 and 4 (see Fig. 2 and Table 1). The median virial parameter is
2.8 ± 0.6, indicating that the majority of clumps possess kinetic
energies exceeding their gravitational binding energies and are
therefore not gravitationally bound. Only 28% of the clumps ex-
hibit αvir values between 1 and 2, while merely 5% have αvir < 1
(see Table C.1). Clumps with αvir < 2 are predominantly asso-
ciated with compact clouds (74%), compared to intermediate
(20%) and diffuse clouds (6%), suggesting that clump stability
is closely linked to cloud compactness. Although uncertainties
in the CO-to-H2 conversion factor and the presence of CO-dark
molecular gas in the low-metallicity Galactic edge may affect
the absolute values of αvir (see Sect. 4.5), most clumps remain
clearly supervirial even under conservative assumptions.

4.5. CO-to-H2 conversion factor
The XCO factor exhibits variation across different environments
(e.g., Arimoto et al. 1996; Leroy et al. 2011; Bolatto et al. 2013;
Amorín et al. 2016; Schruba et al. 2017; Saldaño et al. 2023;
Lee et al. 2024; Deng et al. 2025). However, determination of
the XCO value is challenging. The assumption of a constant XCO
is frequently employed in the calculation of physical parame-
ters for molecular clouds. The virial masses of the outer Galaxy
clouds appear to be 2-3 times larger than those derived from
the CO luminosity (Sodroski 1991). It is more likely that XCO
is not constant but increases with Galactocentric radius due to a
decrease in metallicity (Abdo et al. 2010; Tibaldo et al. 2011).
Consequently, the luminous mass might be underestimated in the
outer Galaxy, given that the XCO factor is quantified in the Solar
neighborhood. The virial parameter was examined to decrease
with increasing Galactocentric radius (refer to Sect. 4.4 or Lin
et al. 2025). However, the virial parameter remains consistent in
both inner and outer Galaxy, owing to the assumption of a con-
stant XCO factor (Miville-Deschênes et al. 2017). Furthermore,
the decreased surface density of molecular clouds in the outer
Galaxy may be partially attributed to a variation in the XCO fac-
tor.
In the absence of other reliable tracers, the XCO factor can be
estimated by a virial-based approach (Arimoto et al. 1996). The
correlation between Mvir and LCO for the Galactic edge clouds is
depicted in Fig. 11. The figure illustrates a one-to-one correspon-
dence between the XCO and αCO values. A mean of XCO factor
within the Galactic edge clumps is 20 × 1020 cm−2 (K km s−1)−1
at ∼0.6 pc resolution. However, the median XCO factor is only
6 × 1020 cm−2 (K km s−1)−1 for our Galactic edge clumps even
with the relationship XCO ∝Z−1.0 (see Sect. 3.3). Thus, the XCO
factor for these clumps appears to be underestimated. Neverthe-
less, the XCO factor estimated by a virial-based approach depends
on the virial parameter. A more plausible hypothesis could sug-
gest that the clouds do not represent an unusually high αCO but
rather are gravitationally unbound. The mean αCO value in our
Galactic edge clumps is approximately 43 M⊙(K km s−1 pc2)−1,
which is ten times greater than those found in the Solar neigh-
borhood (4.3 M⊙(K km s−1 pc2)−1). Similarly, high αCO values
(10–28 M⊙(K km s−1 pc2)−1) are observed in metal-poor galax-
ies (e.g., SMC; Saldaño et al. 2023). The αCO values show a clear
increasing trend with greater Galactocentric distance (Urquhart
et al. 2024) and lower metallicity (Wilson 1995; Arimoto et al.

5. Summary
We carried out CO (2–1) mapping observations, with an angu-
lar resolution of ∼11′′, towards 72 Galactic edge clouds utilizing
the IRAM 30 m telescope. Based on these data, we investigated
cloud-scale morphologies and derived the physical properties
and scaling relations of molecular clumps within these clouds.
The main results are the following:
1. Among the observed clouds, 25 exhibit compact formations,
25 manifest diffuse configurations, and 22 demonstrate an
intermediate morphology bridging the characteristics of the
aforementioned categories.
2. A total of 112 CO clumps have been discerned within our ob-
served Galactic edge clouds. The parameters of size, mass,
surface density, and velocity dispersion of these clumps, as
derived from CO observations, exhibit ranges of 0.6–3.4 pc,
34–8250 M⊙, 12–1025 M⊙pc−2, and 0.3–1.7 km s−1, respec-
tively.
3. The size, velocity dispersion, surface density, and CO lumi-
nosity of Galactic edge clouds, as determined from CO (2–
1) emission, show no discernible systematic variation across
the Galactocentric distance range of 14 to 23 kpc. However,
the virial parameters display a decline described by an expo-
nential relation αvir = 33.0(± 10.4) e−Rg/6.7(±0.9) from Rg = 14
to 23 kpc in the outer Galaxy, conforming previous observa-
tional results.
4. The velocity dispersion-size relation of the Galactic edge
clouds is fitted as σv = 0.69(±0.03)R0.36(±0.10)
eff
, suggesting
that turbulent motions are present in the Galactic edge
clumps. This finding mirrors that observed in inner Galactic
disk clouds. It hints at a potential for star-formation activity
within molecular clouds located at the Galactic edge.
5. The mass-size relation of the Galactic edge clouds is fit-
ted as Mlum = 196(±17)R 2.18 (±0.26)
eff
. This indicates that the
clumps maintain a roughly constant average column density
(∼2.9 × 1021 cm−2) that is nearly independent of clump size
for our Galactic edge cloud sample.
6. The virial parameters within the Galactic edge clumps range
from 0.6 to 15.3, with a mean of 3.4 ± 0.8 and a median of
2.8 ± 0.6. The virial parameters for the majority of clumps
surpass 2, indicating that the clumps within the Galactic edge
clouds are likely unbound. Nevertheless, the external pres-
sure required to bind the clumps appears to be low at the
edge of the Galaxy.
The outer Galaxy presents metal-poor environments con-
ducive to exploring the physical properties and scaling relations
of molecular clouds. It is crucial to conduct further observations
of additional molecular lines, expand the cloud sample size, and
improve resolution to facilitate a comprehensive investigation.
