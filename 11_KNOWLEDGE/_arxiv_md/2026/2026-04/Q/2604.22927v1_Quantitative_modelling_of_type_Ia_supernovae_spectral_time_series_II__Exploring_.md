ABSTRACT
Observations of type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) have led to suggestions of multiple progenitor and explosion scenarios. Distinguishing
between scenarios and tying specific SNe Ia to individual scenarios however has so far been challenging. Constraints on the
explosion physics are often achieved through empirical modelling of SNe Ia spectra and qualitative assessments of the level of
agreement. While this approach has provided useful insights, it cannot be scaled up to large numbers of SNe Ia in a robust and
systematic way. riddler is a machine learning based framework for automated and quantitative fitting of SNe Ia designed to
overcome these limitations. Neural networks are used as radiative transfer emulators and, in conjunction with nested sampling,
emulated spectra are fit to observations of SNe Ia to determine the best-fitting input parameters and explosion scenario. In this
work, we present recent improvements to riddler, including a significantly expanded training dataset covering pure deflagrations,
delayed detonations, double detonations, gravitationally confined detonations, and violent mergers. We show that despite the
increased complexity and variety of our training data, riddler is able to accurately recover the input parameters and explosion
scenario of spectra unseen during training. Using riddler, we fit observations of three SNe Ia covering different sub-classes:
SN 2011fe, SN 2005hk, and SN 2018byg. We detail a number of limitations and assumptions that should be considered when
applying similar approaches. Nevertheless, the benefits of this approach and riddler will result in automated fitting playing an
increasingly important role in the coming years.

1 INTRODUCTION
Increasingly large spectroscopic datasets of type Ia supernovae
(SNe Ia) have proved invaluable in furthering our understanding
of thermonuclear explosion physics (Liu et al. 2023a; Ruiter &
Seitenzahl 2025). While candidate progenitor scenarios and explo-
sion mechanisms have been identified in a handful of cases (e.g.
Kromer et al. 2013a; De et al. 2019; Galbany et al. 2019; Dimitriadis
et al. 2023), the origins of the majority of SNe Ia remain unknown. It
is widely believed that they are the result of thermonuclear explosions
of white dwarfs in binary systems, but multiple pathways to produc-
ing such events have been proposed and distinguishing between them
is non-trivial.
To understand the diversity and observable signatures of different
explosion mechanisms, highly sophisticated and multi-dimensional
explosion simulation and radiative transfer codes have been devel-
oped (Hoeflich et al. 1995; Höflich 2003; Röpke & Hillebrandt 2005;
Kasen et al. 2006; Kromer & Sim 2009; Springel 2010; Hillier &
Dessart 2012; van Rossum 2012; Ergon et al. 2018). Beginning from
the progenitor white dwarf(s), the explosion can be triggered under
the specific desired criteria, the evolution of the explosion followed,
and synthetic observables generated at various phases in the SN evo-
lution. These synthetic observables can then be compared directly

given SN is consistent with a given explosion scenario. Significant
advances have resulted from this approach (e.g. Baron et al. 2012;
Dessart et al. 2014; Röpke & Sim 2018; Polin et al. 2019; Shen
et al. 2021; Pakmor et al. 2024), but the high computational expense
associated with such modelling means it is not suitable for generat-
ing large suites of models or performing detailed characterisation of
newly observed SNe Ia.
Alternatively, simplified radiative transfer codes have also been
developed (Mazzali & Lucy 1993; Kerzendorf & Sim 2014). Rather
than beginning from the explosion of a white dwarf, modelling with
these codes instead follows a more empirical approach and does
allow for detailed characterisation. For a given SN, a customised
ejecta model is constructed and radiative transfer performed to pro-
duce individual snapshot spectra. Input parameters can subsequently
be iterated upon to improve agreement and modelling of multiple
spectra at different phases enables a more complete picture of the SN
ejecta than single epochs. The speed and flexibility of these codes
means that they can be used to rapidly converge towards models that
reproduce observations and this approach has been highly success-
ful in providing constraints on the explosion physics of individual
SNe Ia and samples (Stehle et al. 2005; Mazzali et al. 2007, 2008;
Tanaka et al. 2011; Mazzali et al. 2014; Sasdelli et al. 2014; Ashall
et al. 2016; Heringer et al. 2017; Magee et al. 2017; Heringer et al.
2019; Harvey et al. 2023; Yadavalli et al. 2024). This approach is

Overcoming the limitations of both approaches and producing ro-
bust comparisons between explosion or empirical models and obser-
vations of SNe Ia has been a challenging prospect, but is required to
understand the explosion mechanism(s). Sasdelli et al. (2015) present
a new metric space for characterising SNe Ia samples through princi-

pal components and other observable properties of the SN. Sasdelli
et al. (2017) expand this analysis to include explosion simulations and
show that, within this metric space, all models considered have coun-
terparts within the observed SN Ia sample, but reconstructions of the
explosion model spectra show some disagreement. Direct spectral
comparisons through quantitative means have also shown promise.
Ogawa et al. (2023) present a large suite of simulated spectra and
define multiple metrics to asses the level of agreement with obser-
vations. These metrics are based on the velocity offsets, equivalent
widths, and FWHM of various spectral features, and the overall spec-
tral slope. These metrics are then weighted to broadly reproduce the
approach of visual inspection in a semi-automatic way.
Machine learning-based techniques have been increasingly ap-
plied to fully automate fitting of stellar (Czekala et al. 2015; Ness
et al. 2015; Ting et al. 2019; Badenas-Agusti et al. 2024, 2025) and
supernovae spectra (Vogl et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020; Kerzendorf
et al. 2021; O’Brien et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2024; Magee et al.
2024; O’Brien et al. 2024). Typically this involves generative models
or emulators producing large numbers of spectra that are compared
against observations and physical properties, such as the chemical
composition, inferred. Chen et al. (2024) use custom neural networks
designed to emulate tardis radiative transfer simulations (Chen et al.
2020) to constrain the 56Ni abundances of SNe Ia, while O’Brien et al.
(2024) use the tardis emulator dalek (Kerzendorf et al. 2021) to
investigate differences between the ejecta of normal and 91T-like
SNe Ia (Li et al. 1999). Magee et al. (2024) present riddler, a ma-
chine learning-based method for automatic and quantitative fitting
of SNe Ia spectra. Unlike previous works that focus on fitting indi-
vidual spectra, riddler is designed to fit multiple spectra of a given
SN Ia (up to shortly after maximum light) simultaneously. In addi-
tion, rather than empirical models, the training data used by riddler
is more directly based on explosion model predictions. Magee et al.
(2024) show how, with riddler, it is therefore possible to assess the
relative likelihood of explosion scenarios based on their agreement
with observed spectra. The training data used by Magee et al. (2024)
however was limited to only two explosion scenarios and did not
cover the broad range of scenarios proposed to produce SNe Ia.
In this paper we describe recent improvements made to riddler
and highlight differences with respect to Magee et al. (2024). This
includes a significantly expanded training dataset and updated neural
network architectures with more robust uncertainty estimates. Our
expanded training dataset is presented in Sect. 2 and used to train
the new neural networks discussed in Sect. 3. In Sect. 4 we discuss
fitting spectra with riddler and the fitting performance. Section 5
applies this method to observations. Finally, we discuss our results in
Sect. 6 and present our conclusions in Sect. 7. The updated version
of riddler is publicly available1. A companion paper, Magee 2026,

2 TRAINING MODELS
The initial training datasets presented by Magee et al. (2024) included
spectra generated based on the ejecta structures (density profiles and
compositions) predicted by explosion simulations, but were limited
to only two underlying explosion scenarios: the W7 pure deflagration
(Nomoto et al. 1984) and the N100 delayed detonation (Seitenzahl
et al. 2013) models. In order to allow each explosion scenario to fit a
variety of SNe Ia, the kinetic energy (𝐾𝐸) for each ejecta model was
treated as a free parameter, enabling some differences in structure
among the models within each of the respective training datasets.
With this work, we aim to further expand the diversity of objects
that can be fit with riddler to include both normal and peculiar
SNe Ia across a wide range of luminosities and spectral features.
To that end, we present updated training datasets based on predic-
tions from an increased set of explosion scenarios. This includes
pure deflagrations (DEF), delayed detonations (DDT), double det-
onations (DOD), gravitationally confined detonations (GCD), and
violent mergers (VM). In addition, our new pure deflagration and de-
layed detonation models are no longer based on a single realisation
of each explosion scenario (see Sect. 2.1 for further details).
To generate new ejecta models based on each underlying explo-
sion scenario, we develop a pipeline to analyse and process the one-
dimensional, angle-averaged explosion model predictions available
from HESMA2 (Kromer et al. 2017). Each explosion model is as-
sumed to contain a ‘core’ and a ‘shell’ (although we note that in
most cases the shell mass is 0 𝑀⊙, see Sect. 2.1 for further details),
the abundances within which may be updated independently. This
process ensures that explosion scenarios are treated in a consistent
manner regardless of their complicated underlying structures. The
pipeline is designed to be as flexible as possible and allow for new
training datasets to be easily generated and added to riddler in
future releases.

The additional parameters used in this work allow for significantly
more variation among models within each of the training datasets
than those presented by Magee et al. (2024). Our approach is de-
signed to broadly capture the variation in ejecta properties predicted
by different explosion scenarios, but also to allow for enough flex-
ibility in composition that predictions from our fits could be used
to further refine future explosion simulations. We note however that
each training dataset is still directly tied to predictions from explo-
sion simulations and does not have the freedom to arbitrarily choose
any combination of, for example, ejecta mass, 56Ni mass, etc. In
addition, with the exception of 56Ni, the relative abundances of ele-
ments within a specific group (IGEs, IMEs, C/O) are fixed to those
predicted by the explosion scenario. For example, while our random
sampling and scaling can change the total fraction of IMEs within
the ejecta, the relative fraction of silicon to sulphur will remain the
same for a given explosion strength. In Sect. 2.1 we briefly describe
each of the underlying explosion scenarios and the process of gener-
ating new ejecta structures based on their predictions. Figure 1 shows
indicative ejecta structures for each explosion scenario.

et al. 2024). In addition to the structure of the ejecta, to generate
each model spectrum tardis requires the time since explosion, 𝑡exp,
luminosity, 𝐿, and inner boundary velocity, 𝑣𝑖. The luminosity is de-
termined by the ejecta structure parameters and randomly sampled

drawn by randomly sampling a velocity at peak and velocity gradient.

• Rise time, 𝑡𝑟,
• Luminosity at maximum light, 𝐿max,

2.1 Ejecta structures
2.1.1 Pure deflagrations (DEF)
The pure deflagration explosion scenario (Nomoto et al. 1984; Rei-
necke et al. 2002a,b; Gamezo et al. 2003; Röpke et al. 2006, 2007)
invokes subsonic burning of a near-Chandrasekhar mass white dwarf,
known as a deflagration. Within this scenario, the slow speed of
burning results in some expansion of the white dwarf fuel and the
production of IMEs at lower densities. Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities
are formed due to the outward propagation of the deflagration front,
accelerating the front and leading to significant mixing of burned
and unburned material. Early one-dimensional models of pure de-
flagrations, such as the W7 model, showed promise in reproducing
observations of normal SNe Ia around maximum light, but were
tuned with a parameterised description of the deflagration front and
its propagation speed (Nomoto et al. 1984). Unlike Magee et al.
(2024), here we do not include the W7 model in our training dataset
and instead focus on more realistic multi-dimensional simulations.
Fink et al. (2014) present a suite of 3D pure deflagration explosions
of Chandrasekhar mass white dwarfs. For these models, the explo-
sion is ignited centrally through a number of ignition sparks, ranging
from 1 – 1600. The strength of the explosion is directly correlated
with the number of ignition sparks – more sparks result in stronger
deflagrations and more energetic explosions. For weak deflagrations
the explosion is insufficient to completely unbind the white dwarf,
producing only a small amount of 56Ni and ejecta, and leaving be-

2.1.2 Delayed detonations (DDT)
While simulations invoking pure deflagrations of Chandrasekhar
mass white dwarfs are not suitable for normal SNe Ia, those that
eventually undergo a detonation (i.e. the delayed detonation or
deflagration-to-detonation transition, DDT, scenario) have shown
more promise in reproducing observations (Khokhlov 1991a,b; Hoe-
flich et al. 1995, 2017; Blondin et al. 2011, 2013; Sim et al. 2013).
In this scenario, the explosion progresses in a similar way to the
pure deflagrations described in Sect. 2.1.1 – subsonic burning of the
white dwarf results in some expansion of the fuel and a decrease
in density. A subsequent detonation burns through the pre-expanded
fuel and the remainder of the white dwarf (Khokhlov 1989, 1991a;
Hoeflich et al. 1995; Gamezo et al. 2004, 2005). Trigger mechanisms
for the detonation are unclear, but it is believed to result from strong
turbulent velocity fluctuations (Niemeyer & Woosley 1997; Woosley
2007; Woosley et al. 2009). Compared to pure detonations of Chan-
drasekhar mass white dwarfs, the pre-expanded fuel of the delayed
detonation scenario results in the production of less 56Ni and more
IMEs (Seitenzahl & Townsley 2017).
Magee et al. (2024) develop a training dataset for the delayed deto-
nation scenario based purely on the N100 model published by Seiten-
zahl et al. (2013). Similar to Fink et al. (2014) however, Seitenzahl
et al. (2013) present a suite of models exploring different explosion
strengths, which are again controlled by the number of sparks used
to ignite the initial deflagration phase and range from 𝑁𝑘= 1 – 1600.
Contrary to the Fink et al. (2014) pure deflagration models, in the
delayed detonation scenario the explosion strength and production of
56Ni is inversely related to the number of sparks. Ignition with more
sparks results in a more vigorous deflagration phase, overall lower
densities of the fuel at the moment of detonation, and therefore a
less energetic explosion and less 56Ni being produced. As with the
deflagration models, the structure parameters described in Sect. 2.1
are highly correlated with the number of ignition sparks. Here we use
an expanded set of delayed detonation models from Seitenzahl et al.
(2013), covering 10 – 150 ignition sparks, as the basis of our training
dataset. Initial testing showed that our neural networks struggled to
learn features of the spectra across the full range of 𝑁𝑘presented
by Seitenzahl et al. (2013), but we note that models with 𝑁𝑘be-
low/above this range are generally over/under-luminous relative to
normal SNe Ia. Our selected range of 𝑁𝑘= 10 – 150 should therefore
be suitable for the bulk of the SNe Ia population.

2.1.3 Double detonations (DOD)
In the double detonation explosion scenario, a sub-Chandrasekhar
mass white dwarf accretes material from its companion and ac-
cumulates a helium shell on its surface. Under certain conditions,
this helium shell may spontaneously ignite and trigger a detonation.
Early double detonation models required relatively large helium shell
masses (≲0.2 𝑀⊙) to initiate the detonation (Livne 1990; Livne &
Glasner 1991; Woosley & Weaver 1994), but recent models have
shown that detonations can also be achieved with lower shell masses
(∼0.004 𝑀⊙; Bildsten et al. 2007; Fink et al. 2010; Kromer et al.
2010; Shen & Moore 2014; Polin et al. 2019; Gronow et al. 2020).
Once triggered, the detonation front sweeps around the shell and
burns it to heavier elements. A secondary detonation of the white
dwarf core may then be triggered via multiple mechanisms (e.g. con-
verging shock, edge-lit, or scissors mechanisms; Livne 1990; Livne
& Glasner 1991; Moll & Woosley 2013; Gronow et al. 2020). This
secondary detonation in the core completely disrupts the white dwarf.
Burning in the helium shell can produce a significant amount of IGEs
in the outer ejecta that result in strong line blanketing and observables
that do not agree with the majority of normal SNe Ia (Kromer et al.
2010; Polin et al. 2019; Collins et al. 2022). Some peculiar SNe Ia
however, are consistent with this scenario (Jiang et al. 2017; De et al.
2019; Jacobson-Galán et al. 2020; Dong et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023b;
Padilla Gonzalez et al. 2023). Models with thin or polluted helium
shells generally predict lower amounts of IGEs (or no IGEs) are pro-
duced in the outer ejecta and similar observables to normal SNe Ia
(Shen et al. 2018; Polin et al. 2019; Townsley et al. 2019).

2.1.4 Gravitationally confined detonations (GCD)
The gravitationally confined detonation scenario is a variation of
the delayed detonation scenario and was first suggested by Plewa
et al. (2004). Similar to the pure deflagration and delayed detonation
scenarios, the explosion begins as the subsonic deflagration of a
Chandrasekhar mass white dwarf. As the burning products from the
deflagration rise towards the white dwarf surface, they wrap around
the core and converge on the opposite side. Compression and heating
of unburned fuel at this point may trigger a detonation (Plewa 2007;
Meakin et al. 2009). Generally these models produce relatively large
amounts of 56Ni and are somewhat over-luminous compared to most
SNe Ia, but also show some similarities with bright SNe Ia and
peculiar 91T-like SNe Ia (Kasen & Plewa 2007; Seitenzahl et al.
2016; Lach et al. 2022b).

2.1.5 Violent mergers (VM)
The violent merger scenario involves the merging and subsequent
detonation of two sub-Chandrasekhar mass white dwarfs (Iben &
Tutukov 1984; Webbink 1984; Pakmor et al. 2010). In this scenario,
the (typically lower mass) secondary white dwarf is disrupted and
accreted violently onto the primary. This process results in compres-
sional heating of the surface of the primary, leading to the formation
of hot spots and a subsequent detonation that completely disrupts the
primary. Early merger simulations typically invoked white dwarfs
with significantly different masses, resulting in a merger product ex-
ploding above the Chandrasekhar mass or collapsing to a neutron
star (Iben & Tutukov 1984; Webbink 1984; Nomoto & Kondo 1991).
Pakmor et al. (2010) show that mergers of white dwarfs with equal
masses can result in detonations of the primary during the merger pro-
cess and therefore at sub-Chandrasekhar masses. These simulations
result in observables that are generally consistent with sub-luminous
91bg-like SNe Ia. Subsequent simulations covering white dwarfs of
similar (but not necessarily equal) masses also showed that this sce-
nario could produce observables more comparable to normal SNe Ia
(Pakmor et al. 2012).

2.2 Simulation parameters
To define the simulation parameters for each tardis model, we use a
combined approach based on observed samples of SNe Ia, literature
tardis models, and the explosion models discussed in Sect. 2.1. As
in Magee et al. (2024), for each model we uniformly sample the time
since explosion, 𝑡exp, but with a slightly expanded range of 5 – 30 d.
Magee et al. (2024) use an empirical approach to calculate the lu-
minosity 𝐿of each tardis simulation based on observed bolometric
light curves of SNe Ia and an assumed rise time of 18.5 d. Given
the increased diversity of our training datasets in this work, here we
adopt an alternative approach that is again more directly related to the
explosion model predictions. Determining the necessary luminosity
for each simulation requires a light curve from which we can calcu-
late the luminosity at the randomly sampled 𝑡exp. For each model, we
interpolate the light curves from HESMA to the appropriate explo-
sion strength parameter discussed in Sect. 2.1. Within each explosion
scenario, the luminosity at maximum light, 𝐿max, is proportional to
the mass of 56Ni within the ejecta, therefore we scale the light curve
to match the luminosity expected for the new 56Ni mass of each
model 𝑥. In addition we also allow for changes in the rise time, 𝑡𝑟, to
ensure that our models are able to match observed luminosities. For
the pure deflagration, double detonation, and violent merger mod-
els, 𝑡𝑟is proportional to 𝑀ej. We calculate a new 𝑡𝑟,𝑥for each model
based on the sampled 𝑀ej,𝑥, again allowing for additional scatter. For
our pure deflagration, double detonation, and violent merger models
we set the upper limits of this scatter to 50 per cent, 30 per cent, and
±10 per cent, respectively. Within the delayed detonation and grav-
itationally confined detonation scenarios, all models have the same
ejecta mass of 𝑀ej,𝑥= 1.4 𝑀⊙. The delayed detonation models do
not show any correlation between rise time and explosion strength,
therefore we uniformly sample 𝑡𝑟,𝑥between 16 – 23 d, which broadly
covers the range of rise times observed for normal SNe Ia (e.g. Miller
et al. 2020). In the gravitationally confined detonation scenario, 𝑡𝑟
is proportional to 𝐸def
nuc, therefore we select a new 𝑡𝑟,𝑥based on the
sampled 𝐸def
nuc,𝑥allowing for scatter of ±10 per cent. All interpolated
light curves are then stretched to match 𝑡𝑟,𝑥and the luminosity at the
required phase, 𝐿𝑥, is calculated.
Unlike tardis, the radiative transfer codes used to simulate the
models discussed in Sect. 2.1 do not assume a sharp inner bound-
ary separating optically thick and thin regions. As such, we cannot
rely purely on the existing radiative transfer simulations to calculate
the appropriate inner boundary velocity, 𝑣𝑖, for our tardis simula-
tions. For all models, we determine the velocity at peak and sam-
ple a velocity gradient, from which we determine the appropriate
𝑣𝑖,𝑥for the sampled 𝑡exp,𝑥through linear extrapolation. Magee et al.
(2019) present a number of tardis simulations based on the Fink
et al. (2014) pure deflagration models at maximum light. The inner
boundary velocities of these models are highly correlated with 𝑁𝑘.
We therefore use the relation between 𝑣max and 𝑁𝑘to interpolate to
our selected 𝑁𝑘,𝑥and allow for scatter of ±30 per cent. For all other
models, we use an empirical approach based on observed SNe Ia.
Silverman et al. (2012b) present Si ii 𝜆6 355 velocities at maximum
light (ranging from ∼8 000 – 16 000 km s−1) and velocity gradients
(ranging from ∼10 – 500 km s−1 d−1) for a sample of SNe Ia ob-
served as part of the Berkeley Supernova Ia Program (Silverman et al.
2012a). Overall, these velocities show significant scatter. No strong
correlation is observed with light curve stretch, or indeed between
the velocity and velocity gradient. The inner boundary velocity in
tardis simulations is typically correlated with, but generally lower
than, the Si ii 𝜆6 355 velocity. We therefore uniformly sample a ve-
locity at peak between 6 000 – 13 000 km s−1. The velocity gradient
for all models (including pure deflagrations) is uniformly sampled
between 50 – 250 km s−1 d−1 and the resulting 𝑣𝑖,𝑥calculated for

2.3 Pre-processing
We follow a similar pre-processing approach as in Magee et al.
(2024), although with an expanded wavelength range. All model
spectra are first resampled to 2 000 log-spaced wavelength bins be-
tween 2 000 – 10 000 Å (c.f. 1 000 log-spaced bins between 3 000
– 9 000 Å in Magee et al. 2024). We take log10 of the flux in each
wavelength bin and transform using the standard scaler in sklearn.
Input parameters are also processed by taking log10 and applying the
standard scaler. This process is applied to each training dataset
independently.

4.2 Performance
In Fig. 6 we show riddler fits to template spectra randomly selected
from the evaluation datasets of each explosion scenario. For each fit,
we allow the explosion epoch to vary by up to ±5 d and assume a
2 per cent flux error. Based on our fits, we find that riddler is able to
produce good agreement with the templates, matching the overall flux
level, spectral shape, and many prominent spectral features across the
full wavelength range. As discussed in Sect. 3.2 however, some weak
features are not as well reproduced. For example, Fig. 6 shows that
our best-fitting DOD model fails to produce the weak C ii 𝜆6 580
feature present in the template spectrum and the faint structure in the
Ca ii NIR feature. Nevertheless, we find mean FEs between our neural
network reconstructions and templates range from ∼1 – 3 per cent.
Figure 6 also shows the posterior distributions of fitted and derived
parameters, along with the true values of the templates. Despite the
significant increase in complexity of our models, we find that riddler
is generally able to recover the true input parameters of the templates.

5 APPLICATION TO OBSERVATIONS
In the following section, we use riddler to fit observations of
SNe 2011fe, 2005hk, and 2018byg. These SNe were selected due
to the availability of multiple spectra and existing comparisons with
explosion models (see Sect. 6.2). Fits were performed with each
explosion scenario independently. Following Magee et al. (2024),
due to the photospheric approximation used by tardis wavelengths
>6 500 Å are excluded from the fit, but shown for reference. All
spectra were obtained from WISeREP (Yaron & Gal-Yam 2012), cali-
brated to contemporaneous photometry, and corrected for Milky Way
extinction. We assume a flat Universe with 𝐻0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1
and Ω𝑀= 0.3.
We again stress that tardis simulations are not sensitive to material
below the photosphere and therefore riddler cannot constrain the
entire ejecta. Although we quote the values of ejecta masses, 56Ni
masses, etc., we do not claim that these are the true values for any
of the SNe fit here. Rather, the quoted values should be treated with
caution. They are simply the values of our model for which the
material above the photosphere best reproduces the observations.
5.1 SN 2011fe
We fit the HST spectra of SN 2011fe presented by Mazzali et al.
(2014). Based on the low extinction inferred (𝐸(𝐵−𝑉) = 0.014; Patat
et al. 2013), we do not correct for host-galaxy reddening. Following
from Shappee & Stanek (2011), we set a Gaussian prior on the
distance modulus of N (29.04, 0.19). We set a uniform prior on the
explosion epoch of MJD = 55 790.0 – 55 797.2
Figure 9 shows the results of our fits to SN 2011fe. Overall we find
that the VM scenario is heavily favoured (based on the Bayes factor)
compared to all other explosion scenarios. At −15.3 d the VM model
generally matches the velocities and relative strengths of numerous
features. This includes the Ca ii H&K feature, the Mg ii and Fe ii
blend around ∼4 300 Å, and the Si ii 𝜆5 972 and 𝜆6 355 features.
The spectral shape however is clearly discrepant with wavelengths
≳5 200 Å showing a systematically higher flux than observed. The
model also fails to reproduce the S ii W feature around ∼5 200 –
5 400 Å. The best-fitting DEF model fails to reproduce almost all
spectral features and indeed has the lowest evidence. Our DOD and
GCD models perform somewhat better, reaching broadly similar
levels of agreement and producing a few features comparable to
the observations, but overall are not good matches to the spectra.
The DDT model provides reasonable agreement, again producing
numerous features with comparable velocities and relative strengths.
Indeed, the DDT model also provides better agreement with the
overall spectral shape ≳5 200 Å, but performs somewhat worse in
the near-UV.
Between −15.3 – 10.1 d, the spectra show only minor changes.
The Si ii 𝜆5 972 feature predicted by the VM model has weakened
by −13.1 d and the strength relative to Si ii 𝜆6 355 is in better agree-
ment with SN 2011fe, but the S ii W feature remains absent. The
DDT model shows quite significant evolution during this time. Most
noticeably, the Si ii velocities are clearly offset from the observa-
tions. At −6.9 d, the S ii W feature has begun to develop in the VM
model, but is much weaker than observed. The model also shows
single absorption troughs around ∼3 800 Å and ∼4 300 Å, rather
than the complex features observed. Nevertheless, the Si ii 𝜆5 972
and 𝜆6 355 velocities and strengths are now in closer agreement with
SN 2011fe. In addition, the VM model also matches the complex
blend of Si iii, Fe iii, and Co iii features observed in the near-UV, but
the blend around ∼3 300 Å is somewhat too strong. At this epoch, the
DEF model again fails to reproduce any spectral features and instead
our best-fitting parameters predict a mostly featureless spectrum. The
DDT, DOD, and GCD models all predict the S ii W feature, but none
are able to match the observed velocity with the DDT being too
high and the DOD and GCD too low. Indeed, many of the features
produced by the DDT model show velocities that are too high.
At −2.9 d, the VM model has developed a weak feature consistent
with C ii 𝜆6 580. This feature is also predicted by the DDT and GCD
models, but in all cases is stronger than observed in SN 2011fe. The
DOD model now shows generally stronger similarities to SN 2011fe
than at previous epochs or indeed the DDT model. The Si ii 𝜆5 972
and 𝜆6 355 features are reasonably well reproduced although the
velocities are somewhat too high. The model also generally matches
the S ii W feature and the broad features around ∼3 800 Å and
∼5 000 Å, although the features ≲3 500 Å are not well reproduced.
For our final spectrum at maximum light, the VM model again
shows good agreement with Si ii 𝜆5 972 and 𝜆6 355, and the S ii W
features. As with previous spectra however, the VM model shows
only a single absorption feature around ∼4 800 Å, rather than the
complicated blend observed. This feature has previously been par-
tially attributed to high velocity Fe ii (Branch et al. 2006). Although
generally reproducing the spectral features, by this epoch our VM
model over-predicts the flux in the near-UV. The DOD and GCD
models also generally reproduce SN 2011fe around maximum light.
In particular, both models show comparable levels of agreement with
the Si ii 𝜆5 972 and 𝜆6 355 and S ii W features.
Figure 9 also shows the posterior distributions for our best-fitting
input and derived parameters. We find significant variations in pa-
rameters across the different explosion scenarios considered here.
Depending on the model, the explosion epoch can vary by up to
∼3 days. For the VM model we find the earliest explosion epoch of
MJD = 55794.15±0.19, while the GCD model favours a later explo-
sion epoch that falls on our upper limit of MJD = 55 797.2. The VM
model also shows the lowest distance modulus of 28.27±0.02. Inter-
estingly, we find that the DEF model produces the highest distance
modulus and indeed the largest predicted 56Ni mass. Although it may
seem counter-intuitive that our DEF model is intrinsically brightest,
this likely results from the need for a stronger explosion that can pro-
duce a slower light cure evolution more comparable to that observed
in SN 2011fe since most DEF models will evolve much faster. Our
DEF model shows 𝑁𝑘∼20, which is at the upper limit of the defla-
gration models in our training dataset, and naturally results in higher
ejecta (∼1.4 𝑀⊙) and 56Ni (0.651±0.026 𝑀⊙) masses. For the VM
model, our best-fitting parameters indicate the ejecta of SN 2011fe
is most similar to a VM ejecta containing 0.253±0.002 𝑀⊙of IGE
(of which 0.240±0.002 are 56Ni), 0.624±0.006 𝑀⊙are IME, and the

5.3 SN 2018byg
We use the spectra of SN 2018byg presented by De et al. (2019). From
De et al. (2019) we also set 𝐸(𝐵−𝑉) = 0.0. Based on the redshift of
𝑧= 0.066, we assume a distance modulus prior of N (37.36, 0.30).
We set explosion epoch limits to MJD = 58 236 – 58 241.
The results of our fits to SN 2018byg are shown in Fig. 11. While
we find that the DOD model is favoured overall, the difference in ev-
idence relative to the GCD model (log BF ∼1) indicates only a slight
preference. The −10 d spectrum of SN 2018byg is generally not well
reproduced by any model. This spectrum shows strong absorption
features at ∼4 100 Å and ∼4 800 Å, due to Ti ii and other IGEs, with
few other optical features including Si ii 𝜆6 355. The DOD model
produces both features with approximately the correct velocities and
strengths, but a flatter absorption profile from ∼4 800 – 5 000 Å. This
model also predicts broad features at ∼5 500 Å and ∼6 200 Å that are
not observed. Although not included in the fit, we note that the DOD
model also predicts lower velocities for the Ca ii NIR triplet. The
GCD model does not reproduce the Ti ii feature at ∼4 100 Å but pro-
vides better agreement with the feature at ∼4 800 Å. The spectrum at
wavelengths ≳5 200 is generally featureless, similar to SN 2018byg,
with the exception of a feature at ∼6 000 Å. Our best-fitting DEF
model is also mostly featureless at these wavelengths, but predicts
other features at 𝜆≲5 200 Å that are not observed. Both the DDT
and VM models predict strong Si ii features, in disagreement with
SN 2018byg.
By −5 d the spectrum of SN 2018byg is no longer featureless and
has developed a few strong features. The DOD model shows good
agreement with the sharp decrease in flux below ≲5 200 Å and the
features from ∼3 800 – 4 800 Å. Beyond ∼5 200 Å, the overall flux
predicted by our DOD is clearly much higher than in SN 2018byg
and the features are much stronger. Similar results have previously
been shown by Gronow et al. (2020) and Collins et al. (2022) (see
Sect. 6.2.3). Despite the difference in flux level, some of the observed
features are also reproduced by the model with the correct velocities,
including Si ii 𝜆6 355. The Ca ii NIR feature predicted by the DOD
model at this epoch shows improved agreement with SN 2018byg, but
is not as broad as observed. The GCD model provides a better match
to the overall flux level, but fails to reproduce most spectral features,
including what may be a single broad feature from ∼5 400 – 6 400 Å.
As with the earlier spectrum, none of the other models are able to
reproduce the observed features and typically over-predict the flux in
the UV. At −2 d the level of agreement is similar to the previous −5 d
spectrum. The DOD model matches the UV line blanketing and some
absorption features, although the flux level is higher than observed.
The GCD model matches the flux level, but does not reproduce most
absorption features. Likewise none of the other models reproduce
the features observed.
For SN 2018byg, we find that the best-fitting explosion epoch
varies by up to ∼5 d, with the DDT and VM models preferring
explosion epochs at our lower limit of MJD ∼58 236, while our
DOD model prefers a value close to our upper limit of MJD ∼58 241.
The distance modulus also shows considerable variation of ∼3 mag,
ranging from 35.85±0.05 for the DOD model to 38.50±0.04 for the
DDT model. The best-fitting ejecta structure of our DOD model is
based on a WD core mass of 0.859±0.002 𝑀⊙and a shell mass of
0.038±0.001 𝑀⊙, giving a total ejecta mass of 0.897±0.002 𝑀⊙.
Within the WD core, this model contains 0.221±0.005 𝑀⊙of IGE
(of which 0.150±0.004 𝑀⊙are 56Ni), 0.373±0.005 𝑀⊙of IME, and
0.265±0.005 𝑀⊙of unburned C/O. The 56Ni mass of this model
is towards the lower end of our prior distributions (Fig. 2), which
could explain the over-predicted flux if our models do not cover
the appropriate parameter space for SN 2018byg (see Sect. 6.2.3).
The strong line blanketing produced in the DOD model results from
the burned material in the shell. Approximately half of the mass of
the shell is unburned material, while the remainder is comprised of
0.014±0.001 𝑀⊙of IGE and 0.008±0.001 𝑀⊙of IMEs. The GCD
model predicts a similarly low 56Ni mass of 0.188±0.010 𝑀⊙in the
core and a large shell mass of 0.716±0.012, with 0.159±0.031 𝑀⊙of
56 Ni in the shell, indicating a significant fraction of burned material
at high velocities in the outer ejecta.
For SN 2018byg, we find comparable levels of agreement with
our DOD and GCD models although the DOD model is slightly
preferred. Both models predict some amount of burned material in
the outer ejecta, resulting in significant line blanketing in agreement
with SN 2018byg. While the DOD model is slightly preferred, it
generally over-predicts the optical flux. Future models with lower
WD masses may improve agreement.

6 DISCUSSION
6.1 Limitations and assumptions
While the updated version of riddler presented in this work im-
proves upon that presented by Magee et al. (2024), a number of
key limitations and assumptions still remain. Care must therefore
be taken when making inferences. Section 5 discusses how tardis,
and therefore riddler, is not sensitive to material below the photo-
sphere. We reiterate this point again here and stress that the masses
of various elemental groups within the models should not be taken
as measurements of the true masses for a given SN. Rather than
measuring elemental masses, the purpose of riddler is to determine
the overall preferred explosion scenario for a given SN and best-
fitting model parameters for that scenario. As with any comparison
between observations and models however, our results depend on
both the assumed priors for the model and the likelihood function
with which the comparison is performed. Changes to either of these
could produce vastly different results (see Sect. 6.2.1 and Magee et al.
2024). Although this problem is not unique to riddler, and indeed
is likely more significant for the classical visual inspection approach,
it should be considered when performing automated fitting of SNe
spectra. With an explicit likelihood function (e.g. Eqn. 2), automated
fitting is at least easily reproducible and fits to observations can be
performed in a systematic way.
Fits with riddler are based on emulated spectra of radiative trans-
fer simulations. All applications invoking machine learning in similar
ways are limited by their training data to some extent and riddler
is no different. In this work we expand the training data of riddler
to a wider range of parameters and explosion scenarios than those
considered by Magee et al. (2024). We therefore expect that riddler
should be applicable to a broader range of both normal and peculiar
SNe Ia. Nevertheless, using existing explosion simulations as the
basis for our radiative transfer simulations comes with advantages
and disadvantages. Our training models are more physically realistic
than completely ad-hoc, custom models and therefore can provide
useful constraints on the explosion physics, but they will almost cer-
tainly not be able to provide perfect matches with observations. If
models do not predict the correct element at the correct velocity then
they will never be able to reproduce certain spectroscopic features.
Some SNe Ia may also simply fall outside of the prior ranges or
explosion scenarios considered here. We also note that all of our
training data are based on publicly available models from HESMA,
but many alternative literature models for the scenarios considered
here are available from other groups. Even where our double deto-
nation models, for example, may fail to reproduce certain features or
SNe Ia, models from other groups may fare better (see Sect. 6.2.3).
Indeed, one of the key considerations when fitting with riddler
is that it does not find the ‘correct’ explosion scenario for a given
SN Ia. Nor does it quantify whether an explosion scenario provides
a ‘good’ match to the observations at all. Formally speaking, with
the inclusion of the nuisance parameter 𝑓, all of our fits are ‘good’
matches in terms of their reduced 𝜒2 (𝜒2
𝜈∼1). Removing this pa-
rameter would result in none of the fits being classed as ‘good’
(𝜒2
𝜈≫1). The same is also true for the underlying models upon
which our training data are based and, we suspect, all existing empir-
ical spectral models – a simple 𝜒2
𝜈would produce values ≫1. The
evidence values calculated by riddler and ultranest are therefore

only informative in a relative sense when considering each explosion
scenario for a given SN. Evidences should not be compared across
multiple SNe Ia to determine whether, for example, the delayed deto-
nation scenario provides a better match to one SN or another. Instead,
for any given SN, evidences can be used to determine whether the
delayed detonation scenario produces a better match than the vio-
lent merger scenario, for example. riddler is simply able to identify
which model is preferred among those considered in the training data
(for a given likelihood and priors), but otherwise does not consider
the quality of agreement.
The primary limitation for observations is the requirement of flux
calibrated spectra. Fits with riddler are performed in physical flux
units and therefore the absolute and relative (i.e. as a function of
wavelength) flux calibration will impact the results. Indeed, this lim-
itation is also applicable to all similar spectral comparisons between
models and observations. Calibrating an observed spectrum to pho-
tometry at the same epoch could follow one of two approaches. The
spectrum can be scaled by a single value such that the synthetic
magnitudes are on average equal to the photometry. Alternatively, a
wavelength dependent scale factor could be applied and the spectrum
mangled such that the synthetic magnitudes are exactly equal to the
photometry in each band. The former case implicitly assumes that the
relative flux calibration of the spectrograph is accurate, which is not
necessarily the case. Synthetic magnitudes in the latter case should
provide better matches to the photometry in the same band, but inter-
polating to bands not observed or extrapolating beyond wavelengths
covered by photometry could introduce a bias or systematic offset
into the calibrated spectrum. Accurately accounting for all of the
systematic uncertainties associated with this process is non-trivial.
Here we adopt the most simplistic approach of assuming a single
scale factor.
As mentioned, some of the limitations highlighted in this section
also apply to the classical visual inspection approach. Although auto-
mated fitting with riddler does not fully overcome these limitations,
it is nevertheless a more robust and repeatable approach than visual
inspection that can be readily applied to large numbers of SNe Ia
spectra.
6.2 Comparison with previous results
6.2.1 SN 2011fe
The proximity and luminosity of SN 2011fe has resulted in an ex-
tensive dataset and comprehensive analyses to try and uncover the
progenitor and explosion mechanism. Here we compare the results
from our riddler fits to existing explosion simulations and empirical
models for SN 2011fe.
From our riddler fits we find that the VM model produces the
overall best agreement, which could indicate that SN 2011fe is con-
sistent with a sub-Chandrasekhar mass progenitor. The distance mod-
ulus resulting from this fit (𝜇= 28.27±0.02) is ∼4𝜎below the mean
reported by Shappee & Stanek (2011) and hence our model is some-
what under-luminous compared to typical SNe Ia. The underlying
ejecta structure is based on the merger of a ∼0.94 𝑀⊙primary white
dwarf with a ∼0.77 𝑀⊙secondary. Our model is therefore similar to
the merger presented by Kromer et al. (2013b) for the under-luminous
and slowly evolving SN 2010lp, although with a slightly higher 56Ni
mass (0.24 𝑀⊙c.f. 0.18 𝑀⊙). Kromer et al. (2013b) argue that the
merger of a 0.90 𝑀⊙primary and a 0.76 𝑀⊙secondary provides
reasonable agreement with the low luminosity, slowly evolving light
curves, colours, and spectral features of SN 2010lp. In particular,
late time (≳260 d) spectra of SN 2010lp show narrow emission lines
identified as [O i]. Such features could naturally be explained by the
presence of unburned material in the centre of the ejecta as a result of
the merger process (Taubenberger et al. 2013). SN 2011fe does not
show a similar feature at these phases, but Taubenberger et al. (2015)
tentatively identify [O i] in the spectrum of SN 2011fe at ∼1 000 d,
although this is debated (Tucker et al. 2022). Whether our VM model
would show a similar feature is not clear, but again we note that we
cannot constrain the inner ejecta.
Mergers of ∼0.9 𝑀⊙white dwarfs, similar to those favoured by
our riddler fits, have generally been argued to be consistent with
under-luminous SN 1991bg-like SNe Ia (Filippenko et al. 1992; Pak-
mor et al. 2010) as opposed to normal SNe Ia. Indeed, our VM model
shows some features in disagreement with SN 2011fe that are more
commonly observed in fainter SNe Ia, such as the strong Si ii 𝜆5 972
feature at maximum light. The density of the primary white dwarf at
the moment of ignition determines the mass of 56Ni produced dur-
ing the explosion (Pakmor et al. 2011) and mergers involving higher
mass primaries (∼1.0 – 1.1 𝑀⊙) have instead been favoured for nor-
mal SNe Ia. Pakmor et al. (2012) present the merger of a 1.1 𝑀⊙
primary and a 0.9 𝑀⊙secondary and find that this scenario produces
reasonable agreement with SN 2003du, a normal SN Ia (Stanishev
et al. 2007), around maximum light. Compared to SN 2003du, this
model produces good agreement with the observed Si ii features and
continuum, although the model is somewhat too red and does not re-
produce the S ii W feature observed. Conversely, our VM model does
reproduce the Si ii 𝜆6 355 and S ii W features observed in SN 2011fe,
but over predicts Si ii 𝜆5 972 and C ii 𝜆6 580. Both models also fail
to reproduce the absorption features around ∼4 500 – 5 200 Å for
different reasons. The Pakmor et al. (2012) model predicts a feature
that is too broad and flat compared to SN 2003du, whereas our model
predicts a single, strong feature and does not reproduce the complex
shape observed in SN 2011fe. Similar variations are seen among pure
detonation models of sub-Chandrasekhar mass white dwarfs, indi-
cating these discrepancies may be related to the mass of the primary
(Shen et al. 2018).
Various arguments for and against sub-Chandrasekhar mass pro-
genitors for SN 2011fe have been presented in the literature (e.g.
Mazzali et al. 2015; Dimitriadis et al. 2017; Kerzendorf et al. 2017;
Shappee et al. 2017). Although our fiducial fit favours such pro-
genitors, the model clearly has some short comings. As discussed
in Sect. 6.1, the assumed priors and likelihood function can signif-

The differences between the best-fitting models in these four sce-
narios highlight the importance of an explicit likelihood function,
something which is not captured with visual inspection. Indeed, for
all but one of these scenarios the DDT model is favoured over the VM
model statistically, but one could argue that the VM model provides
comparable or better visual agreement at some epochs (particularly
around maximum). Again we note that we take an agnostic approach
of weighting every wavelength (⩽6 500 Å) and phase equally. With
log-spaced wavelength bins however, approximately half of our bins
occur in the region ∼2 000 – 4 500 Å with the remainder spanning
a larger wavelength range of ∼4 500 – 10 000 Å. These bins were
selected to ensure that all spectral features are equally resolved in
velocity space, but will also result in a larger contribution of smaller
wavelengths to the likelihood. Similarly, if a spectral sequence con-
tains multiple spectra at early times and fewer at later times (or vice
versa) then the fit will be biased towards specific phases and may not
necessarily reflect the full ejecta. The modeller employing a classical
visual inspection approach may be aware of these limitations and
adjust their model accordingly, but again this is not explicit and diffi-
cult to replicate. Similar results may be obtained through automated
fitting if the wavelength and phase weights are also adjusted in an
attempt to remove these biases. This is an arbitrary decision however
and it is not clear what (if any) alternative weights should be used.
These limitations are apparent when comparing our results to the
SN 2011fe fits presented by Mazzali et al. (2014). Our DDT models
struggle to replicate both the UV and optical features of SN 2011fe
simultaneously and our model including the UV is clearly not a good
fit beyond the first few days immediately after explosion. Mazzali
et al. (2014) present empirical models based on the density pro-
file of the one-dimensional WS15DD1 delayed detonation model
(Iwamoto et al. 1999), assuming a fixed 𝜇= 29.04, and fitting for the
best-matching composition. Mazzali et al. (2014) show that the rel-
atively large amount of material at high velocities in the WS15DD1
model results in UV features with velocities that are too high. In-
stead, Mazzali et al. (2014) favour a model in which there is a lower
density tail towards high velocities. Our DDT models including the
UV show qualitatively similar results. Although the overall flux level
is (naturally) in better agreement when the UV is included, the veloc-
ities are somewhat higher. Conversely, when the UV is excluded the
best-matching model favours a lower kinetic energy (∼1.1×1051 erg
c.f. 1.7 × 1051 erg) that provides better agreement and reduces the
density of the outer ejecta. Although the velocities of the features in
our DDT models are in good agreement with SN 2011fe, the opti-
cal features in particular (𝜆≳4 000 Å) are clearly much weaker at
maximum light. This is typically not the case for the Mazzali et al.
(2014) models, which produce strong features with generally com-
parable relative strengths to those in SN 2011fe. These differences
are likely caused by the fact that the inner boundary velocities in our
models are higher than those of Mazzali et al. (2014) resulting in
lower optical depths for lines.
SN 2011fe was also previously modelled with an earlier version of
riddler by Magee et al. (2024). This version included only a single
realisation of a delayed detonation explosion, represented by N100
(0.604 𝑀⊙of 56Ni), and is most directly comparable to our models
in which the UV is excluded and 𝜇= 29.04. Compared to the Magee
et al. (2024) N100 model, the 𝑁𝑘∼10 models favoured here produce
better agreement with the early spectra. In particular, the Magee
et al. (2024) model predicted a single strong feature at ∼4 800 Å
and did not produce a S ii W feature at −13.1 d. Conversely, our
model reproduces many of optical features from ∼4 500 – 6 500 Å
with the correct velocities and relative strengths. Both models predict
similarly high flux around ∼3 000 – 3 500 Å. At −6.9 d, the Magee
et al. (2024) model continues to over-predict the flux in this region,
while our model now shows good agreement. At the same time,
the Magee et al. (2024) model reproduces the absorption features
around ∼4 200 – 4 500 Å that are weaker in our model. Finally,
as mentioned, the features in our model at maximum light are also
weaker than observed in SN 2011fe, while those in Magee et al.
(2024) show good agreement.
Overall, our riddler fits to SN 2011fe highlight the benefits
and challenges associated with automated fitting and where caution
should be applied. None of the models considered here are simulta-
neously able to reproduce all of the UV and optical features. Careful
consideration must be given to the priors and likelihood function
when applied to fitting SNe Ia, and these should be made explicitly
clear when fitting. This is not possible with a classical visual inspec-
tion approach, but automated fitting similar to that shown here offers
new possibilities for constraining the explosion physics of SNe Ia in
a systematic way and testing the impact of various assumptions.
6.2.2 SN 2005hk
Kromer et al. (2013a) present comparisons between the Fink et al.
(2014) N5def (𝑁𝑘= 5) pure deflagration model and observations
of SN 2005hk. Kromer et al. (2013a) find that the N5def model
broadly reproduces the observed features of SN 2005hk, including
the peak absolute magnitude, colours at maximum light, and low
velocities. The N5def model has an ejecta mass of 0.372 𝑀⊙, of
which 0.158 𝑀⊙is 56Ni, 0.222 𝑀⊙is IGEs, and 0.042 𝑀⊙is IMEs.
From our fits with riddler, we find that a model based on 𝑁𝑘∼10
is preferred. Compared to the canonical N10def model presented by
Fink et al. (2014), our best fitting model has a slightly lower ejecta
mass of 0.434±0.008 𝑀⊙(c.f. 0.478 𝑀⊙), higher 56Ni and IGE
masses of 0.224±0.009 𝑀⊙and 0.322±0.022 𝑀⊙(c.f. 0.183 𝑀⊙and
0.267 𝑀⊙), respectively, and a lower IME mass of 0.038±0.002 𝑀⊙
(c.f. 0.0581 𝑀⊙). The increased 56Ni mass of our best-fitting model
(and therefore higher luminosity) compared to N5def is compensated
by the higher distance modulus found by our fits (𝜇= 33.70 ± 0.04
c.f. 𝜇= 33.46 ± 0.27).
Kromer et al. (2013a) show that at −6.3 d, the N5def model is
somewhat fainter and redder than SN 2005hk. In addition, N5def
does not reproduce both absorption features at ∼3 500 and ∼3 700 Å,
but does produce the strong Fe iii 𝜆4 404 absorption observed. Con-
versely, our riddler fits show closer agreement with the spectrum
flux level and shape, and reproduce the strong features observed. Nei-
ther N5def nor our model however produce significant Si ii 𝜆6 355
absorption and instead over-predict the C ii 𝜆6 580 feature. By −3.3 d,

SN 2005hk has also been subject to multiple empirical modelling
analyses that provide useful comparison points for our riddler mod-
els. Sahu et al. (2008) use the code presented by Mazzali et al. (1993)

that best reproduces the observations. This model is based on the W7

(0.18 𝑀⊙) and kinetic energy (0.30×1051 erg). The kinetic energy of

W7 ejecta mass (∼1.4 𝑀⊙). The W7 ejecta mass is also a factor of

that this model extends to later times than our riddler fits and there-
fore is able to probe deeper into the centre of the ejecta. Barna et al.
(2018) model the spectra of SN 2005hk using tardis and customised
density and composition profiles based on the Fink et al. (2014) de-

reproduced by assuming a steeper density profile in the outer ejecta
of the N5def model, which is qualitatively similar to the lower kinetic
energy found by our fitting. Overall, the models show broadly similar
levels of agreement.
While some previous works have focused on comparing
SN 2005hk to the N5def model in particular, Fink et al. (2014)
argue that the N5def and N10def models are promising candidates
for SNe Iax, including SN 2005hk. Our fits support this claim, but
suggest that some differences relative to the Fink et al. (2014) mod-

a brighter model that simultaneously has a lower kinetic energy is
in better agreement with SN 2005hk than the standard Fink et al.
(2014) models. The Lach et al. (2022a) pure deflagration models test
various parameters affecting the initial ignition of the deflagration,
including the size of the offset from the centre of the white dwarf

0.97×1050 erg, respectively). Models ignited with larger offsets from
the white dwarf centre show smaller variations in their kinetic ener-
gies and predict overall smaller 56Ni masses. An offset of 10 km is the
lowest value considered by Lach et al. (2022a) therefore we speculate
that even smaller ignition offsets may show larger variations in their
predicted ejecta as other parameters are varied and could produce
pure deflagrations similar to those that appear to be favoured by our
fits.
6.2.3 SN 2018byg
Our double detonation models are based on the suite presented by
Gronow et al. (2021). Collins et al. (2022) generate synthetic observ-
ables of these models and perform comparisons with multiple SNe Ia,
including the SN 2018byg spectra presented in Fig. 11. Collins et al.
(2022) show that the Gronow et al. (2021) models display signifi-
cant viewing angle variation, due to the location at which the helium
shell is ignited. None of the models are able to match all of the
features observed in SN 2018byg however, regardless of viewing an-
gle. Models with relatively low mass white dwarfs and helium shells
(𝑀𝐶= 0.8 𝑀⊙, 𝑀𝑆= 0.03 – 0.05 𝑀⊙) provide somewhat reasonable
agreement with the spectrum at −10 d when viewed offset (𝜃≳130°)
from the shell ignition point. These viewing angles experience min-
imal line blanketing and approximately match the luminosity and
colour, but do not reproduce many of the spectral features. At later
epochs, such viewing angles clearly do not reproduce the strong flux
suppression for wavelengths ≲5 000 Å. When viewed closer to the
ignition point (𝜃≲90°), the early spectra instead show line blanket-
ing that is too strong and do not match the colour of SN 2018byg at
−10 d. Qualitatively, the strong line blanketing observed at −5 – −2 d
in these models is similar to that seen in SN 2018byg, but many of
the spectral features are too strong and the flux level and colour are
not well reproduced. Unsurprisingly, our best-fitting DOD model for
SN 2018byg shows many of the same shortcomings as the Gronow
et al. (2021) models, despite the increased variation. In particular,
Collins et al. (2022) show that these models have a similarly high flux
at longer wavelengths and later epochs as that observed in Fig. 11.
Recently, Collins et al. (2025) presented updated artis simula-
tions for the M2a (𝑀𝐶= 1.05 𝑀⊙, 𝑀𝑆= 0.05 𝑀⊙) double detona-
tion model presented by Gronow et al. (2020) using a full non-LTE
treatment (compared to the approximation previously used). These
models were calculated in 1D using slices of the full 3D ejecta pro-
files along different viewing angles. Collins et al. (2025) show that
the updated non-LTE treatment results in significantly less viewing
angle variation in the synthetic observables than predicted by Collins
et al. (2022). Whether this would also result in better agreement with
observations of SN 2018byg should be investigated in the future.
Our best-fitting DOD model is based on an ejecta structure with
𝑀𝐶∼0.859 𝑀⊙and 𝑀𝑆∼0.038, which is similar to the models

by Polin et al. (2019). De et al. (2019) find that a model with

the observations, in particular the 𝑟-band evolution. The large helium
shell mass of this model however produces a strong early flux excess
in the 𝑟-band light curve, with an initial decline of ∼0.4 mag before
the light curve begins to rise again. Such a decline is not observed

produces better agreement with SN 2018byg than the Gronow et al.
(2021) models or indeed our best-fitting DOD model. At −10 d, the
model broadly matches the flux and colour of the observed spec-
trum, although some spectroscopic features are still too strong. At
−2 d, the model again reproduces the observed flux and colour, and

7 CONCLUSIONS
riddler is a machine learning based method for automated, quantita-
tive, and repeatable fitting of SNe Ia spectral time series up to shortly
after maximum light (Magee et al. 2024). In this work, we presented
an updated version of riddler, rewritten in pytorch with a signifi-
cantly expanded training dataset, broader wavelength coverage, and
more robust uncertainty estimates.
Training data for riddler is based on predictions from existing
literature explosion simulations. Here we added a wider range of
explosion scenarios to riddler, including pure deflagrations (Fink
et al. 2014), delayed detonations (Seitenzahl et al. 2013), double det-
onations (Gronow et al. 2021), gravitationally confined detonations
(Lach et al. 2022b), and violent mergers (Pakmor et al. 2010, 2011;
Kromer et al. 2013b). For each scenario we considered a broad set
of input parameters and generated 300 000 synthetic spectra with
tardis (Kerzendorf & Sim 2014) that were used to train neural net-
works. The neural networks act as emulators of the tardis radiative
transfer simulations, predicting fluxes and flux uncertainties from
2 000 – 10 000 Å in a fraction of the time. Our emulators show typi-
cal accuracies of ∼1 – 3 per cent. Nested sampling is performed with
ultranest (Buchner 2021) and the trained emulators to determine
the best-fitting set of parameters for each explosion scenario. Com-
paring the overall likelihoods of different explosion scenarios also
allows for a quantitative determination of the best-matching scenario
for a given SN Ia.
Using template spectra generated following the same procedure
as our training data, but not seen during training, we showed that
riddler is able to accurately recover the correct input parameters
and explosion mechanism of the template. We tested the impact of
the signal-to-noise ratio and spectral resolution on our fits and found
that signal-to-noise ratios ≳10 and resolutions ≳300 (at 6 000 Å) are
required for robust results. Above these limits, fits showed no signif-
icant variation in the best-fitting parameters or explosion scenarios.
We applied riddler to fit observations of the normal SN Ia
SN 2011fe (Nugent et al. 2011), the SN Iax SN 2005hk (Phillips
et al. 2007), and the peculiar, line-blanketed SN Ia, SN 2018byg
(De et al. 2019). We showed that the violent merger scenario pro-
duces the overall best agreement with SN 2011fe, but this requires
a sub-luminous model and a distance modulus that is ∼4𝜎be-
low that reported by Shappee & Stanek (2011) (𝜇= 28.27 ± 0.02
c.f. 29.04±0.19). Alternatively, using a fixed distance modulus of
𝜇= 29.04 and/or excluding the UV region from the fit favours the
delayed detonation scenario overall and produces results comparable
to previous analyses. Our fits to SN 2005hk are also consistent with
previous works. Specifically, we find that the pure deflagration sce-
nario produces the best agreement and indicates a somewhat lower
kinetic energy for a given 56Ni mass is favoured. We argue that pure
deflagration explosion simulations ignited closer to the centre of the
white dwarf may produce similar ejecta structures to those favoured
by our fits. For SN 2018byg, we find that the double detonation sce-
nario broadly reproduces the strong line-blanketing observed, but our
models show some disagreement with features at early times and the
flux level around maximum light.
All fits performed with riddler are only valid given the assumed
priors and likelihood function. Changes to either of these can sig-
nificantly impact the best-fitting parameters, as demonstrated by our
fits to SN 2011fe. In addition, although riddler provides a quanti-
tative means with which to perform model comparisons, it does not
measure the overall goodness of fit. Therefore the best-fitting model
may in fact represent an overall poor fit to the data. Indeed, riddler
does not find the ‘correct’ explosion scenario for a given SN Ia, but
merely allows one to quantitatively determine which scenario among
the training data provides the best match under the priors and like-
lihood function used. Typically, similar fits to SNe Ia spectra are
performed manually, using qualitative visual inspection to judge the
fit quality. While automated fitting comes with a number of assump-
tions and limitations, these are generally also applicable to qualitative
fitting. Automated fitting nevertheless has the benefit that the priors
and likelihood function can be made explicit, and large samples of
SNe Ia can be treated in a systematic and reproducible way.
Automated fitting of SNe Ia spectra similar to that shown here will
play an increasingly important role within the coming years. Thou-
sands of SNe Ia spectra already exist, far exceeding our capability to
model them individually with a qualitative approach. The time and
computational expense of this method means that we must move to an
automated approach in order to fully exploit the possibilities of these
large datasets. In a companion paper, Magee 2026, we use riddler
to fit spectra of cosmological SNe Ia observed as part of the Zwicky
Transient Facility survey (Bellm 2014; Bellm et al. 2019; Rigault
et al. 2025). Systematically applying riddler to new and upcoming
surveys, such a TiDES on 4MOST (Swann et al. 2019; Frohmaier
et al. 2025), will provide new constraints on thermonuclear explosion
physics that have so far been out of reach.
