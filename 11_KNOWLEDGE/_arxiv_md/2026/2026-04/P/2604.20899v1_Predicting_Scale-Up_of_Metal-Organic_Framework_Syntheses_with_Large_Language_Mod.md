Abstract
Scalable synthesis remains the gate between MOF discovery and industrial deploy-
ment, as scale-up know-how is fragmented across disparate reports. We introduce ESU-
MOF, a literature-mined dataset and a positive-unlabeled learning strategy that fine-tunes
large language models to predict scalability potential with 91.4% accuracy, enabling rapid
data-driven triage for industrial MOF discovery.
1
Introduction
Metal-organic frameworks (MOF) have, hitherto, benefitted from the expansive design space
opened by reticular chemistry [1, 2, 3, 4], and the literature now measures progress by the tens
of thousands of structures [2, 5, 6, 7, 8], yet the path from crystalline discovery to industrial
material remains slow and not easily transferable across scales [9, 10, 11, 12]. A skilled chemist
may progress from single-crystal synthesis to milligram production, then to sub-gram, gram,
and eventually kilogram or larger batches [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]; however, this intuition is hard
to be generalized as it is distributed across disparate synthesis records. Considering the rising
industrial interest in MOFs and the growing proximity between discovery and application [10,
19, 20, 21, 22], it is increasingly important to ask whether scalability itself can be predicted
from the early synthesis record.
Herein, we report a data-centric workflow that connects the latent synthesis knowledge
embedded in the literature to the prediction of scale-up plausibility for MOFs. Our workflow
contains a large language model (LLM) agent for data mining, and the resulting Experimental
Scale-Up (ESU) dataset was used to fine-tune another LLM under positive-unlabeled (PU)
learning [23, 24] to give binary classification for reaction procedures. Our central goal is that,
given a newly reported MOF with a small-scale synthesis description, can we predict whether
the protocol exhibits credible potential for gram-scale or larger production, and can we do so
early enough to guide experimental prioritization and industrial engagement.

Results and Discussion
In our workflow, we combined LLMs with PU learning to construct a literature-scale workflow
for scalable synthesis prediction, ultimately bridging the critical gap between laboratory dis-
covery and industrial manufacturing. At the onset of this study, we reasoned that the literature
already contains the ingredients for such a model, although they are scattered in forms that are
difficult to unify. Many MOFs have been reported both as initial small-scale solvothermal crys-
tallizations and, later, as gram-scale or kilogram-scale preparations under related or modified
conditions. Conceptually, these linked reports constitute an apprenticeship archive of synthesis
scale-up.
To this regard, we assembled two literature pools from Web of Science to reflect the asym-
metric nature of scale-up evidence. A possible-positive pool, denoted P, was collected using
the search keywords “scale-up”, “gram-scale”, “kilogram”, and “pilot” in title or abstracts,
under “metal-organic framework” peer-reviewed literatures between 1995 and 2026, intention-
ally with broad scope so that relevant protocols would not be lost at the search stage; this
search yielded 117 candidate paper groups. An unlabeled pool, denoted U, was collected using
“metal-organic framework” and “synthesis” with the same year range, and was then restricted to
solvothermal syntheses containing one principal metal source and one principal linker, follow-
ing the method established in our earlier MOF text-mining study[25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32],
and this selection criteria yielded 946 candidate paper groups.

From the obtained papers, we text-mined reaction-level synthesis records [25, 28] from
both the main manuscripts and associated supporting information files, if presented, organizing
them into a machine-readable dataset (see Supporting Information). The overall workflow is
shown in Fig. 1 (a). In particular, an LLM was first deployed as a data-mining agent; analysing

the full text of a paper, it extracted essential fields—including the primary metal precursor,
organic linker, modulators, solvent systems, and reaction conditions—yielding the structured
protocol records exemplified in Fig. 1 (b). The subsequent flow of these curated protocols,
label constructions, and data splits is summarized in Fig. 1 (c) and Supporting Information
Section S1. In addition, the representative LLM prompts and the resulting binary predictions
were demonstrated in Fig. 1 (d) and Supporting Information, Section S3.
While structure identifier (e.g., MOF names and CCDC number [33, 34]), reagent mass and
volume, post-processing conditions (e.g., solvent washing time and activation temperature), and
reaction outcomes (e.g., isolated product mass and yield), were also considered and extracted
under same schema, they were optional items and kept blank if the authors of the selected
papers did not explicitly report these values (Supporting Information, Sections S2.3 & S2.4). A
random 10% of selected papers from both corpora were manually verified by comparing with
ground truth annotated by the human expert, giving an extraction accuracy of 97.6% for key
reaction parameters (precursor, solvent, time, temperature), which is consistent with the high
accuracy of chemical data extraction in LLM-driven data mining [25, 27, 28, 35, 36].
The resulting dataset, termed the Experimental Scale-Up ESU-MOF dataset, contains 3,568
synthesis protocols, with labels defined as follows: strong positives (Ps, protocols with explicit
scale-up evidence), auxiliary positives (Pa, protocols in the unlabeled corpus that match Ps
by MOF name and metal/linker identity, representing small-scale preparations of a MOF later
shown to be scalable), unlabeled (U, containing unknown positives and true negatives), and a
small negative set (N) used for a gold-standard evaluation, which was curated by human expert.
After deduplication, the ESU-MOF dataset contains 2,684 unlabeled protocols (75.2%), while
Ps and Pa together contribute 723 positives (20.3%), and N contributes 161 (4.5%). Protocols
were split by paper-level group identifiers, which serve to prevent data leakage, in a 70:15:15
ratio so that no single publication contributes to more than one of train, validation, or test sets,
reducing overfitting and giving 662, 142, and 143 groups across three sets.
For model training, the PU subset contained 288 Ps, 255 Pa, and 1,965 U protocols in the
training split, whereas N examples were withheld from fitting and used only for evaluation,
as shown in Fig. 1 (c). The predictive model was then structured by fine-tuning a base LLM
on JSON representations of these synthesis protocols [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 37, 38, 39]. In
training, both Ps and Pa were mapped to the positive label P, whereas U remained unlabeled
and N was excluded from training. This construction is based on the assumption that absence of
scale-up evidence in literature does not imply failure, but only incomplete knowledge. Positive-
unlabeled learning therefore is suitable for this classification task [24], where input is a “reac-
tion card” and output is an single-token “P” or “U” label, and the complex nature of reagent
names and reaction apparatus make LLMs a good fit for the model shown in Fig. 1 (d).
For the PU correction, we adopted the classical perspective that the observed positive la-
bel in the literature is a biased sampling of true positives. Consequently, the raw probabilities
output by the model may inherently underestimate a protocol’s true scalability, necessitating a
mathematical calibration. Operationally, we obtained the model’s raw score as the token-level
probability of emitting label P, denoted q(x), derived from log probabilities of the actual output
token for an input protocol x. In practice, we then estimated c, the mean of q(x) over vali-
dation positives Ps in the validation gold set, and this factor was computed to be 0.837. This
constant implies that while the labelled literature captures the majority of scalable protocols,
approximately 16% of genuinely scalable MOFs remain undocumented and hidden within the
unlabelled corpus. Because industrial screening is fundamentally a ranking problem, we also
apply a calibration layer via Platt scaling [40] on the gold validation set to produce a final score
whose threshold may be selected for a desired trade-off between missed opportunities and false

leads, and the resulting validation threshold was determined to be 0.39.
Upon the model fine-tuning, the first evaluation was designed to test whether the model can
distinguish explicit scale-up protocols from unlikely ones under controlled conditions. On the
gold benchmark, which compares Ps against N, the validation set contained 65 protocols and
the test set 73 protocols. Using the raw observed-positive score alone, the balanced accuracy
was 82.4%. After PU correction and calibration, balanced accuracy increased to 91.4%, with
a F1 score of 93.2% and Matthews correlation coefficient [41, 42] of 82.8%, while ROC-AUC
reached 95.8% (Fig. 2 (a); Supporting Information, Section S4.1), which shed light on a key
point that the remaining errors are limited and balanced rather than concentrated in one failure
mode. Bootstrap analysis further supported the robustness of this performance, with the 95%
confidence interval for balanced accuracy spanning 83.7 to 97.2% and that for F1 spanning
87.4 to 97.9%.
A more demanding and, in our view, more useful test asks whether the model can surface
promising small-scale protocols from the large body of ordinary literature where scalability is
not explicitly declared. For this reason, we evaluated deployment performance on Pa versus U.
The validation deployment set contained 441 protocols, whereas the held-out test deployment
set contained 367 protocols composed of 43 ancillary positives and 324 unlabeled candidates.
On this test, the final calibrated model achieved an ROC-AUC of 94.5%. Additionally, precision
at the top of the ranking was strong, reaching 80.0% at 10. At the level of held-out papers,
which more closely resembles how a researcher screens a family of related syntheses, the model
achieved a top-3 hit rate of 88.9% across 9 test papers. It is envisioned that such a ranking mode
is especially valuable for industrial triage, where one rarely needs a model to certify every
protocol; rather, one needs it to move the most plausible candidates to the front of the queue.

in a general-purpose model (Fig. 3). On the gold test benchmark, the balanced accuracy of fine-
tuned model (91.4%) is significantly higher than those of the base models at 78.5% and 77.6%,

respectively. On the more practically relevant deployment test, fine-tuning widened the margin
further as average precision rose to 65.8%, whereas the base model only reached 28.2 to 28.7%.
Even at the paper level, where untuned models retained some within-document ranking ability,
the fine-tuned model still led with a mean average precision of 72.2% and a top-1 hit rate of
77.8%, illustrating high utility for down-selection in an industrial discovery pipeline.
Notably, this predictive power also substantially eclipses traditional machine learning base-
lines (Supporting Information, Section S4.1), which struggle to capture the nuanced, contextual
heuristics of reaction conditions without extensive manual feature engineering. Nevertheless,
the untuned models did preserve partial ranking structure within some papers, which suggests
that chemistry language models already encode fragments of synthetic plausibility, yet without
the calibration needed to separate a merely feasible solvothermal recipe from one with cred-
ible scalability. Thus, it provided evidence that fine-tuning on a literature-derived ESU-MOF
dataset containing experimental knowledge enables the LLM to organize synthesis context into
a much sharper ranking of industrially relevant candidates.
Importantly, these results should be interpreted against the reality that scalability is not
a single property of a framework but a property of a protocol, and hence the model is as-
sessing procedural plausibility rather than intrinsic crystallographic “goodness.” As supported
by our results, often, scalability is written into solvent choice, reaction severity, and oper-
ational simplicity. Water-based or benign solvent systems, moderate reaction temperatures,
shorter durations, and the absence of complex modulators can correlate with practical scale-up
routes [10, 43, 44]. Likewise, from engineering point of view solvent burden, long solvothermal
times, and workup intensity can be barriers to sustainable manufacturing, which reinforces the
idea that these fields act as weak yet learnable proxies for process viability [11].

In this study, it can be demonstrated that such scalability intuition, expressed neither as a
single heuristic nor as a hand-built descriptor set, can become a learned judgment grounded in
literature-scale experimental data. To this regard, the present study also sheds light on a broader
role for large language models in materials chemistry, wherein they do more than extract facts
and instead help estimate whether a new material can move beyond discovery into practical ac-
cessibility. Nevertheless, it should also be acknowledged that a limitation of the present work-
flow is that the input precursors is limited to one in our dataset, therefore it is unable to predict
the scalability of multivariate MOFs that contain more than one metals or linkers [45]. Besides,
the set of confirmed successful scale-up protocols remains only in the hundreds, and thus the
positive class, while informative, is still modest relative to the breadth of MOF chemistry. It is
envisioned that, as more gram-scale, kilogram-scale, and pilot-oriented procedures appear in
the literature, the ESU-MOF dataset should become more chemically diverse and more sharply
calibrated across families of metals, linkers, and solvent systems.
3
Conclusion
In summary, we show that scalability prediction for MOFs can be formulated as a literature-
grounded positive-unlabeled learning problem and addressed effectively with LLMs. By con-
structing the ESU-MOF dataset and training on small-scale synthesis context, we obtained a
model that identifies scalable MOF candidates with strong gold-benchmark accuracy (91.4%)
and, more importantly, high deployment ranking power. Conceptually, the study suggests that
synthesis procedures contain latent information about future manufacturability well before in-
dustrial development begins. It is therefore conceivable that, when a new MOF is discovered,
one may already estimate whether its path toward practical use is narrow or promising. Be-
sides, for existing reported MOF protocols, this workflow can potentially identify overlooked
ones that contain high scale-up potential and thus commercial interest. In this regard, LLM
prediction of MOF scalability may become part of how the field decides which discoveries are
truly ready to move from the laboratory toward commercial and industrial realization.

S2
Workflow Implementation Details
The workflow consists of seven sequential Steps. In this section we provide details of each step
with reference to the source code modules. All data and codes are available at https://github.
com/zzhenglab/MOF-Scaleup/
S2.1
PDF Text Extraction
Full-text PDFs (main manuscripts and supporting information) are parsed into plain text using
pypdf. As most documents exceed the usable context length of the extraction LLM, we apply
a keyword-guided truncation scheme to retain passages in synthesis conditions and scale-up
details.
The extractor uses a curated set of domain-specific keywords (e.g., “solvothermal”, “auto-
clave”, “gram-scale”, “metal”, “linker”, “modulator”, “yield”, “scale-up”, “kilogram”) as an-
chors to identify relevant regions. The extraction procedure consists of the following steps:
1. Scan all pages to locate keyword occurrences.
2. Define context windows around each occurrence.
3. Merge overlapping windows.
4. Extract the merged text regions, subject to a configurable maximum character limit.
This ensures that synthesis procedure sections receive priority over unrelated content (e.g.,
characterisation data, discussion), while respecting the token budget of the extraction LLM.
Main text and supporting information are processed independently and then combined before
being passed to the extraction agent, enabling cross-referencing between dispersed procedural
details.
Keyword selection planner (PdfConfigPlanner).
The keyword inventory and trunca-
tion parameters are stored in pdf config.json. To initialize this configuration, human
experts provide a small set of high-priority seed terms. These seeds are then expanded by a
dedicated PdfConfigPlanner, by caling GPT-5.4 to propose additional synthesis-relevant
keywords for PDF pre-selection.
The planner takes any existing keywords as input and is instructed to expand them to
MIN FOCUS KEYWORDS distinct entries, spanning experimental section markers, reactor or
vessel types, scale-up and quantity indicators, solvent and reaction medium descriptors, work-
up terminology, and yield/characterisation language.
Numeric truncation parameters (e.g., window sizes, character limits) are treated as engi-
neering constants. The planner is invoked at runtime if pdf config.json is missing or the
keyword count falls below the required threshold, leading consistent coverage without manual
intervention.
S2.2
Structured Extraction
An ExtractionAgent class invokes a large language model with schema-constrained out-
put to extract structured synthesis records. The agent currently supports two API providers:
• OpenAI: Uses the Responses API with structured output mode, where the output schema
is enforced by the API itself.
• Anthropic: Uses the Messages API with JSON output parsing at the application level.
Default to OpenAI (GPT-5.4).
14


## Page 15


Retry-with-feedback mechanism.
When the LLM output fails Pydantic schema validation,
the validation error is appended to the conversation as a user message (e.g., “Your output had
error: {error}. Fix and retry.”), and the model is prompted to self-correct. This loop runs for
up to 2 retries with increasing backoff (1.0 × (attempt + 1) seconds). In our practice, the retry
mechanism is triggered for approximately 8 −10% of papers, and after retries, the overall
extraction success rate exceeds 99%.
User prompt template.
Each extraction call receives a user prompt that includes: (i) the
file identifier (DOI or filename), (ii) the full article text (truncated), and (iii) the supporting
information text (if available), delimited by explicit start/end markers. Full prompts see S3.
Session memory via a content-addressable extraction cache.
We introduce a content-addressable
session memory, ExtractionCache (mof scaleup.cache), to reduce redundant structured-
extraction calls, one of the dominant cost in the agent loop. Unlike the coarse --skip-existing
shortcut, the cache keys each episode by its full reasoning context,
⟨provider, model, prompt version, file id, sha256(main pdf∥si pdf)⟩,
with 8-byte length-prefixing before hashing to ensure collision-safe separation between manuscript
and SI. This enforces strict episodic isolation: reuse occurs only when both input bytes and in-
ference configuration match. On recall, cached outputs are revalidated via ArticleExtraction
.model_validate(). Any stale or schema-incompatible entry is evicted before falling back
to a fresh LLM call. Successful extractions are written back with full configuration meta-
data and a UTC timestamp, yielding a replayable audit trail. The mechanism is opt-in via
--cache-dir <path> and otherwise leaves the pipeline fully stateless. When enabled,
recall costs only a hash over raw PDF bytes, negligible relative to a single model invocation.
The plain-JSON storage design keeps this strategy of low external denpendency. It enables
straightforward extensions such as TTL policies, drift-aware invalidation, or long-horizon de-
cision logs.
S2.3
Protocol Normalisation
Extracted records undergo three post-processing steps:
(a) Solvent canonicalisation.
A lookup table of most commonly known solvent name/abbre-
viation mappings is used to standardise solvent identifiers. For example, “DMF”, “dimethylfor-
mamide”, and “N,N-DMF” are all mapped to the canonical form “N, N-dimethylformamide”
with abbreviation “DMF”. The lookup table uses normalised keys (lowercased, whitespace/punc-
tuation removed, Greek letters transliterated) for robust matching.
Solvent table Planner (SolventAliasPlanner). The alias table is stored in solvent_aliases
.json and maintained by a SolventAliasPlanner class that calls GPT-5.4 to enumer-
ate alias-to-canonical mappings. Each entry maps a sovent alias (full name, abbreviation, or
spelling variant) to a two-element array [canonical full name, canonical abbreviation].
The planner augments the existing table to MIN ALIAS ENTRIES entries, covering common
MOF solvents (water, DMF, DEF, DMAc, EtOH, MeOH, DMSO, MeCN, THF, 1,4-dioxane,
acetone, NMP, ldots), halogenated and aromatic solvents, acid/amine modulators used as co-
solvents, fluorinated solvents, and alternate IUPAC names or common misspellings. Existing

## Page 16


entries always take precedence, and the LLM fills gaps only. The planner is invoked automati-
cally at runtime if the alias file has fewer than MIN ALIAS ENTRIES.
The solvent system is compacted so that empty slots are removed and single-solvent sys-
tems receive a default ratio of 1.
(b) Stoichiometry computation.
If the metal-to-linker molar ratio is not explicitly reported
by the authors, it is computed from the extracted molar amounts (mmol, mol, or µmol) of the
primary metal source and linker. The ratio text is formatted in M :L notation (e.g., “1:2”). The
stoichiometry source is tracked as explicit, computed, or not reported.
(c) Yield parsing.
If a yield percentage was reported as text (e.g., “85%”) but the numeric
field was left empty by the extraction, a regex-based parser extracts the numeric value.
(d) Scale evidence reconciliation.
If any synthesis record within a paper has a scale label of
“gram”, “multi-gram”, or “kilogram”, the article-level flag gram scale reported is set to
“yes”. Product masses ≥1.0 g also trigger this flag.

S2.7
Calibrated Scoring
Referring to [28], the post-processing of fine-tuned model outputs is implemented in the ScoringPipeline
class, which provides a fit-once / transform-many interface.
For each input protocol x, the pipeline performs three sequential operations.
1. Extracts a raw score from the token-level log-probabilities of the two candidate output
tokens (“P” and “U”), yielding a continuous confidence value in [0, 1].
2. This score is corrected under the PU-learning framework to account for incomplete pos-
itive labelling in the literature-derived training set.
3. The corrected score is optionally calibrated by Platt scaling on the validation gold split,
and a decision threshold selected on the same split is used to obtain the final binary
prediction.
In addition to the scalar score, the log-probability extraction module records diagnostic
metadata, including the generated token sequence, top-k alternative tokens at the classification
position, and warnings when the emitted label disagrees with the highest-probability token.
These records are used for debugging and sanity checking, but do not alter the final score.
Mathematical formal expressions of the raw score, PU correction, calibration procedure,
and threshold selection are provided in Section S5.5.

Model outputs on all evaluation splits were collected via an asynchronous batch runner AsyncEvalRunner

S5
Positive-Unlabeled (PU) Learning Framework
S5.1
Motivation
Standard binary classification requires both positive and negative training labels. In the MOF
scale-up prediction task, however, the absence of scale-up evidence in a publication does not
imply that the synthesis is fundamentally non-scalable , the authors may simply not have at-
tempted or reported a larger batch. This label asymmetry means that the unlabeled pool U
is a mixture of true negatives and latent positives (i.e., genuinely scalable protocols that lack
published scale-up data). Training a standard classifier on such data would systematically un-
derestimate the scalable class and produce biased probability estimates.
Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning[24] addresses precisely this setting: the learner has access
to a set of verified positives and a larger set of unlabeled examples, but no confirmed negatives.
The framework provides a principled correction that recovers calibrated posterior probabilities
from a model trained on {P, U} labels alone.
S5.2
Problem Formulation
Let f : X →{0, 1} be the true (unknown) binary label, where f(x) = 1 denotes a genuinely
scalable protocol and X is the space of synthesis protocol descriptors (metal, linker, solvent,
conditions, etc.). Let s(x) ∈{0, 1} denote whether the protocol was selected (i.e., reported
with explicit scale-up evidence in the literature). In the PU setting the learner observes:
• A set of labelled positives P = {x : s(x) = 1}, drawn from p(x | f(x) = 1, s(x) = 1).
In our dataset, P comprises both strong positives (Ps, protocols with direct scale-up
evidence) and auxiliary positives (Pa, small-batch preparations of MOFs later shown
to be scalable).
• A set of unlabeled examples U = {x : s(x) = 0}, drawn from the marginal p(x), which
contains an unknown fraction of latent positives alongside true negatives.
Training label mapping. During training, both Ps and Pa are mapped to the positive label “P”,
while U examples retain the label “U”. Crucially, the expert-curated negative set (N, n = 161)
is entirely excluded from training and reserved for the gold evaluation benchmark (Section S4),
ensuring that the model is trained under the strict PU assumption with no access to confirmed
negatives. The resulting training set contains 2,508 examples (288 Ps + 255 Pa + 1,965 U).
S5.3
The SCAR Assumption
The key Selected Completely At Random (SCAR) assumption[24] states that, among truly
scalable protocols, the probability of being labelled (i.e., reported with scale-up evidence) is a
constant c independent of the protocol features:
p(s = 1 | x, f = 1) = c ∈(0, 1]
(S1)
This means that the labelled positives P are an unbiased sample of all true positives , which ones
get reported with scale-up data is random with respect to the synthesis conditions themselves.
Justification in the MOF context.
Whether a research group reports scale-up results is
driven primarily by factors orthogonal to the synthesis conditions themselves , funding priori-
ties, application targets, journal scope, and industrial collaborations , rather than by the specific
metal/linker/solvent/temperature combination. A protocol using Zr/BDC in DMF at 120 °C is

at 80 °C, even though both may be equally scalable. While SCAR is an idealisation (publica-
tion bias may introduce mild deviations), it provides a tractable and well-studied framework
that is substantially more appropriate than the naive assumption that all unlabeled protocols are
negative.
