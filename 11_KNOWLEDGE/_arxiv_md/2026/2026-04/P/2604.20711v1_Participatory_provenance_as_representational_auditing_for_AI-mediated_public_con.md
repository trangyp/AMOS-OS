Abstract
Artificial intelligence is increasingly deployed to synthesize large-scale public input in policy
consultations and participatory processes. Yet no formal framework exists for auditing whether
these summaries faithfully represent the source population, an accountability gap that existing
approaches to AI explainability, grounding and hallucination detection do not address because
they focus on output quality rather than input fidelity. Here, participatory provenance is in-
troduced: a measurement framework grounded in optimal transport theory, causal inference
and semantic analysis that tracks how individual public submissions are transformed, filtered
or lost through AI-mediated summarization. Applied to Canada’s 2025–2026 national AI Strat-
egy consultation (n = 5,253 respondents across two independent policy topics), the framework
reveals that both official government summaries underperform a random-participant baseline
(−9.1% and −8.0% coverage degradation), with 16.9% and 15.3% of participants effectively
excluded. Exclusion concentrates in clusters expressing dissent, scepticism and critique of AI
(33–88% exclusion rates). Brevity, semantic isolation and rhetorical register independently pre-
dict representational outcome. An accompanying open-source interactive tool, the Co-creation
Provenance Lab, enables policymakers to audit and iteratively improve summaries, establishing
genuine human-in-the-loop oversight at scale.
Keywords: participatory AI, algorithmic accountability, public consultation, democratic represen-
tation, causal inference, natural language processing

Introduction
What gets counted, counts. In democratic governance, this principle has a specific corollary: what
gets represented in official summaries of public input shapes what governments act on. For most of
the twentieth century, the scale of public consultation imposed a natural constraint. Participatory
processes could accommodate only as many voices as human analysts could read, synthesize and
report [Arnstein, 1969, Pateman, 1975]. The emergence of large-scale natural language processing
has removed that constraint. Governments, international organizations and platform operators now
routinely collect tens of thousands of written submissions and delegate their synthesis to AI sys-
tems [OECD, 2020, Fishkin, 2009]. The promise is compelling: every submission can be processed,
no voice need go unread, and summaries can be generated at a fraction of the cost and time of
manual analysis.
This promise rests on a tacit assumption that AI-mediated summarization preserves the dis-
tribution of views expressed, not merely the modal opinion, but the diversity, the dissent and

encode statistical regularities from training corpora, systematically disadvantaging low-frequency
viewpoints [Bender et al., 2021, Bommasani et al., 2021]. Summarization architectures, whether ex-
tractive or abstractive, optimize for coherence and coverage of high-probability content rather than
for representational equity across the input distribution [Nallapati et al., 2017, See et al., 2017]. The
resulting summaries may speak in the public’s language while systematically excluding the public’s
dissent, a form of artificially engineered or manufactured consensus that is dangerous because it
appears participatory on the surface (Fig. 1).

Diverse citizen contributions enter an AI mediation process that filters, biases and aggregates input.
Some voices are retained in the final summary while others are systematically lost. The output

paper introduces formal measurement tools to detect and quantify this distortion.
This can be understood as the AI Trilemma in participatory governance. Governments deploy-
ing AI for consultation synthesis face a choice among three desirable properties (scale, efficiency
and accountability), of which at most two can be achieved simultaneously without dedicated mea-
surement infrastructure. Processing thousands of submissions at low cost (scale × efficiency) is
achievable through automation, but without audit tools, this combination sacrifices accountability.
Restoring accountability through manual review (scale × accountability) eliminates the efficiency
gains. And limiting consultation to small deliberative panels (efficiency × accountability) sacrifices
scale. Resolving this trilemma requires not a choice among the three properties, but a new class
of formal measurement tools that can restore accountability to automated processes operating at
scale.
A substantial body of work has addressed the question of what happens to the outputs of AI sys-
tems. Explainable AI (XAI) methods, including SHAP [Lundberg and Lee, 2017], LIME [Ribeiro
et al., 2016], attention visualization [Vaswani et al., 2017] and concept-based explanations [Kim
et al., 2018], ask whether model predictions are interpretable to human users. Grounding tech-
niques ask whether generated text is anchored in source documents or retrieved evidence [Lewis
et al., 2020, Gao et al., 2023]. Hallucination detection methods ask whether model outputs contain
fabricated claims [Ji et al., 2023, Manakul et al., 2023]. Model cards [Mitchell et al., 2019] and
datasheets [Gebru et al., 2021] document model provenance and intended use. These are essential
contributions to responsible AI, but they share a common orientation: they audit what comes out
of AI systems.
The question of what happens to the inputs, specifically how the distribution of human voices
entering an AI system is transformed, filtered or lost during processing, has received far less formal

attention. This asymmetry is striking. In contexts where AI mediates between citizens and institu-
tions (public consultations, petition platforms, deliberative polling, community feedback systems),
the representational fidelity of the input-to-output transformation is the fundamental democratic
concern. A summary that is factually grounded (no hallucinations), interpretable (explainable) and
linguistically fluent can nonetheless systematically exclude minority viewpoints, soften dissent and
manufacture consensus. Existing XAI tools would not detect this failure because the summary is
“correct” by every output-oriented metric; the distortion occurs in the compression of the input
distribution, not in the generation of the output text.
The literature on AI bias has developed sophisticated frameworks for auditing classification er-
rors in high-stakes decision systems such as hiring [Raghavan et al., 2020], lending [Bartlett et al.,
2022], criminal justice [Chouldechova, 2017, Corbett-Davies et al., 2017] and facial recognition [Buo-
lamwini and Gebru, 2018]. Algorithmic auditing frameworks have been proposed for end-to-end
accountability [Raji et al., 2020]. However, these frameworks address individual-level decisions (ac-
cept/reject, score assignment), not the distributional problem of representing a population through
a compressed summary. The participatory AI literature has highlighted power dynamics in system
design [Sloane et al., 2022, Birhane et al., 2022, Delgado et al., 2023] and has called for meaning-
ful involvement of affected communities [Young, 2000, Fung, 2006], but has not produced formal
metrics for auditing live participatory deployments at the population level.
Separately, optimal transport theory provides a mathematically principled framework for com-
paring probability distributions [Villani et al., 2009, Peyré and Cuturi, 2019]. The Wasserstein dis-
tance, which measures the minimum cost of transporting mass between two distributions, respects
the geometry of the underlying space and has well-established theoretical properties [Kantorovich,
2006]. Optimal transport has been applied to evaluate generative models [Arjovsky et al., 2017],
to quantify distributional shift in machine learning [Courty et al., 2017], and to compare biological
distributions [Schiebinger et al., 2019], but has not been directed at the problem of auditing AI-
generated policy summaries. The concept of data provenance, tracking the origin, transformation
and lineage of data through processing pipelines, is well-established in database systems [Bune-
man et al., 2001, Simmhan et al., 2005, Herschel et al., 2017], but has not been adapted to the
specific challenge of tracking how individual citizen voices are transformed through AI-mediated
summarization.
This paper introduces participatory provenance, a formal framework for measuring the chain
of custody from individual public submission to official summary. The framework operationalizes
four interconnected measurements.
First, a coverage score for each participant quantifies their
semantic proximity to the nearest summary sentence, providing an individual-level measure of rep-
resentational inclusion.
Second, the Wasserstein-2 distance between the participant embedding
distribution and the summary sentence distribution captures the global distributional divergence.
Third, doubly-robust causal estimates [Robins et al., 1994] identify which participant characteristics
(response length, semantic distinctiveness, rhetorical register) causally predict coverage, separating
structural from topical effects. Fourth, a bidirectional concept fidelity analysis measures both the
fraction of participant concepts that survive into the summary (forward recall) and the fraction of
summary concepts traceable to participant texts (backward precision).
The framework is implemented as a complete, open-source computational pipeline and applied
to Canada’s 2025–2026 national AI Strategy consultation [Innovation, Science and Economic Devel-
opment Canada, 2025]. Canada’s consultation stands out as a model of civic ambition: over 64,600
responses were collected from 11,383 Canadians across 26 questions spanning eight policy pillars
(research and talent, AI adoption, commercialization, safe AI systems and public trust, education
and skills, scaling homegrown champions, infrastructure and securing critical assets). ISED pro-
cessed these responses using advanced generative AI tools, including several large language models

Results
Study overview. Canada’s 2025–2026 AI Strategy consultation invited responses on 26 questions
across eight policy pillars. This study focuses on two of these pillars: how to build AI skills and edu-
cation, and how to ensure safe and trustworthy AI adoption. Each respondent answered up to three
sub-questions per topic; responses were concatenated to form a single text per respondent per topic.
ISED processed responses using large language model-based classification pipelines with human
oversight [Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada, 2025]; the present analysis ap-
plies an independent framework directly to the raw participant texts and the official summary. After
staged preprocessing (minimum length filtering, French-language removal, near-duplicate detection,
and a two-zone topic-relevance filter combining embedding similarity with LLM adjudication; see
Methods), 2,496 Education responses and 2,757 Trust responses were retained (Supplementary Ta-
ble 1). Each corpus was independently clustered into semantically coherent topic groups (k = 9
for Education, k = 7 for Trust; selected by multi-metric consensus with bootstrap stability val-
idation), then compared against the corresponding six-sentence government summary using four
representational metrics. Figure 2 provides an overview of the framework and principal findings.
2.1
Semantic topology of public discourse
To characterize the structure of public opinion before assessing representational fidelity, the semantic
landscape of each consultation topic was first mapped. Participant texts were embedded using a
pre-trained sentence transformer [Reimers and Gurevych, 2019, Song et al., 2020] and reduced to
50 principal components (explained variance: 55.8% for Education, 56.4% for Trust). Clustering
was performed with k-Means, with k selected by a multi-metric consensus procedure combining
Silhouette [Rousseeuw, 1987], Calinski-Harabász, Davies-Bouldin and Gap Statistic scores, followed
by bootstrap stability validation (100 iterations, 80% subsamples). Where the consensus k was
unstable (mean adjusted Rand index (ARI) < 0.80), a downward stability-first override identified
the most stable k.

0.80): Critical thinking and literacy (C0; n = 320), AI Integration in Education Systems (C1;
n = 357), Scepticism towards AI in education (C2; n = 304), Digital Literacy and AI Awareness
(C3; n = 261), Workforce Skills for Digital Economy (C4; n = 335), Opposition to AI in Society
(C5; n = 387), Holistic Education and Community Engagement (C6; n = 304), Libraries’ Role
in Information Literacy (C7; n = 55) and Critique of Educational Technology Implementation

2.2
Both official summaries show weaker performance than a random baseline
For each participant i, a coverage score c(i) = maxj cos(ei, sj) was computed, where ei is the
participant’s sentence embedding and sj are the embeddings of the J = 6 summary sentences. A
participant is classified as excluded if c(i) < ¯c −σc, where ¯c and σc are the mean and standard
deviation of the coverage score distribution. To establish whether the official summaries represent
the participant distribution better than chance, the analysis compares against a random baseline:
for each of 2,000 permutations, J random participants are drawn to serve as a pseudo-summary,
and coverage is recomputed.
Both official summaries perform significantly below the random baseline. For Education, mean
participant coverage under the actual summary is ¯c = 0.514 (95% bootstrap CI [0.509, 0.519]),
compared to a random-baseline mean of 0.565 (± 0.024 SD) a degradation of −9.1%. For Trust,
the actual summary yields ¯c = 0.533 (95% CI [0.528, 0.537]) against a random baseline of 0.579
(± 0.022) a degradation of −8.0%. In both topics, the actual government summary falls in the lower
tail of the random-permutation distribution (p < 0.001), indicating that the summaries represent
the population worse than a randomly selected set of participant quotes would have done. It should
be noted that this baseline is diagnostic rather than prescriptive: a random draw of participants
would not constitute a coherent policy document, but establishes a lower bound that a coherence-
optimised summary should exceed. Falling below it indicates that coherence is being prioritized at
the cost of representational breadth.
The Wasserstein-2 distance [Villani et al., 2009, Flamary et al., 2021] between the full partici-
pant embedding distribution P and the summary sentence distribution Q (both in PCA-50 space)
quantifies this gap in distributional terms: W2 = 0.706 (Education) and W2 = 0.684 (Trust). Three
baselines provide context. First, the random baseline yields lower W2 (0.671 ± 0.022 for Education;
0.665 ± 0.024 for Trust), confirming that the official summary is distributionally farther from the
population than random. Second, a centroid baseline (using cluster centroids as pseudo-summary
sentences) achieves W2 = 0.488 and 0.483, representing a naive extractive approach. Third, a greedy
extractive-optimal baseline achieves W2 = 0.594 and 0.580, representing the best achievable per-
formance by selecting actual participant quotes. The actual summaries exceed all three baselines,
confirming that the abstractive summarization process introduces distributional distortion beyond
what any extractive alternative would produce.
Gini coefficients of individual coverage scores are G = 0.183 (Education; 95% CI [0.178, 0.187])
and G = 0.148 (Trust; 95% CI [0.142, 0.153]). These are significantly above zero (p < 0.001), indi-

2.3
Exclusion concentrates in dissenting and critical clusters
Applying the exclusion threshold (¯c −σc), the analysis identifies 421 excluded participants (16.9%;
95% CI [15.5%, 18.4%]) in Education and 422 (15.3%; 95% CI [14.0%, 16.7%]) in Trust. Critically,
exclusion is not distributed uniformly across the topic landscape.
It is concentrated in specific
semantic clusters, and the concentration follows a consistent pattern across both topics: clusters
expressing dissent, critique or fundamental opposition to AI are excluded at dramatically higher
rates than clusters expressing support, constructive engagement or alignment with policy discourse
(Fig. 3).
Education & Skills. The cluster Critique of Educational Technology Implementation (C8;
n = 173) has an exclusion rate of 78.6% and a mean coverage score of 0.325, meaning that four in
five participants in this cluster have no meaningful semantic connection to any summary sentence.
This cluster contains responses questioning the fundamental premise of AI in education, including
concerns about data exploitation, corporate capture of educational technology, and the displacement
of traditional pedagogy. Opposition to AI in Society (C5; n = 387; exclusion 40.3%; mean coverage
0.401) and Holistic Education and Community Engagement (C6; n = 304; exclusion 34.5%; mean
coverage 0.422) are the next most-excluded clusters.
By contrast, Workforce Skills for Digital
Economy (C4; n = 335; exclusion 0.0%; mean coverage 0.705), AI Integration in Education Systems
(C1; n = 357; exclusion 0.3%; mean coverage 0.573) and Scepticism towards AI in education (C2;
n = 304; exclusion 0.7%; mean coverage 0.576) have near-zero exclusion rates. Full per-cluster
statistics for both topics are reported in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.
Safe AI & Public Trust. The cluster Distrust in technology and oversight (C4; n = 193) has
an exclusion rate of 88.1% the highest in either topic and a mean coverage of 0.309. Responses in this
cluster express fundamental distrust of AI governance structures and question whether technology
companies can be held accountable. Trust through Transparency and Regulation (C5; n = 251;
exclusion 50.2%; mean coverage 0.414) and Rejection of AI and its implications (C0; n = 321;
exclusion 33.0%; mean coverage 0.447) are the next most-excluded. Conversely, Building public trust
in AI (C2; n = 503; exclusion 0.0%; mean coverage 0.648) and Building Trust through Education
and Regulation (C6; n = 584; exclusion 1.7%; mean coverage 0.556) receive near-complete coverage.
The pattern across both topics is unambiguous: clusters expressing critique, opposition, distrust
or rejection of AI are excluded at rates up to 90 times higher than those expressing alignment with
mainstream policy discourse. This asymmetry is not a statistical artefact. The cluster-ordered rank-
ing of exclusion rates is invariant to the exclusion threshold multiplier across the range [0.5σ, 2.0σ]
(Supplementary Table 8), confirming that the structural finding is robust to threshold choice.

smaller in magnitude than the length effect, this penalty is particularly consequential because se-
mantically isolated responses are, by definition, those expressing unusual or minority viewpoints
within their own topic cluster.
Assertive register. Responses containing declarative, imperative or demand-form language
show a positive causal effect: ATE = +0.023 (Education; p < 0.001) and ATE = +0.025 (Trust;
p < 0.001). This finding indicates that confident, directive language that matches the rhetorical
conventions of policy documents confers representational advantage.
Hedged register. Responses with hedging language (epistemic markers, uncertainty qualifiers)
show ATE = +0.003 (Education; p = 0.005) and ATE = +0.014 (Trust; p < 0.001). The positive
direction is surprising and may reflect the tendency of policy summaries to use measured, qualified
language themselves.

before fitting (mean 0, unit variance) to enable direct comparison of effect sizes. Semantic isolation
emerges as the strongest single predictor: β = −0.083 (Education; p < 0.001) and β = −0.070

contributes positively (β = 0.025 Education; β = 0.026 Trust), and assertiveness rate contributes
positively (β = 0.009 both topics).
Intersectional analysis reveals that risk factors interact differently across topics. In Education,
the short × isolated interaction yields β = +0.057 (p < 0.001), indicating attenuation: partici-
pants who are both brief and semantically isolated experience a joint disadvantage smaller than
the additive prediction (−0.099 −0.027 + 0.057 = −0.069 versus −0.126 additive), likely because
very short, peripheral texts uniformly fall below any summary’s semantic reach regardless of which
single factor is present. In Trust, the short × isolated interaction yields β = −0.036 (p < 0.001),
indicating genuine compound disadvantage: being simultaneously brief and semantically isolated
confers exclusion risk exceeding the additive prediction. This cross-topic asymmetry is itself in-
formative: the risk factors that produce compound harm in Trust are those most directly tied to
voice structure (brevity × distinctiveness), a pattern consistent with intersectional frameworks in
algorithmic fairness [Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018, Selbst et al., 2019]. Full interaction coefficients
for both topics are reported in Supplementary Tables 10 and 11.

The results reveal a striking asymmetry across both topics. For Education, backward precision is
76.7%: the summary borrows approximately three-quarters of its vocabulary directly from partici-
pant submissions. Yet forward recall is only 13.2%: more than 86% of the concepts that participants
actually raised do not appear in the summary (F1 = 0.225). For Trust, the asymmetry is even more
pronounced: backward precision reaches 83.3% while forward recall is 18.2% (F1 = 0.299).
This asymmetric pattern is inconsistent with good-faith compression. A fair compression process
one that proportionally reduces the concept space while preserving the distribution would produce
roughly symmetric precision and recall, bounded by the compression ratio. Instead, the observed
pattern describes a process that mines the participant corpus for a favourable vocabulary while
systematically discarding the majority of participant-generated concepts. The summary speaks in
the public’s language while representing only a small fraction of the public’s ideas (Supplementary
Fig. 3).
Concept transformation analysis (Education: κ = 0.768, reliable; Trust: κ = 0.682, reliable)
classified matched concepts as preserved or softened. In Education, 15 of 20 matched concepts were
preserved and 5 were softened; in Trust, 15 of 20 were preserved, 4 softened, and 1 abstracted.
Softening predominantly affected concepts related to urgency, opposition and risk language the
same semantic dimensions that characterize the excluded clusters.
2.6
Epistemic grounding and stance alignment
Epistemic grounding analysis classifies each summary sentence by how strongly it is supported by
participant evidence, using the mean cosine similarity to the ten nearest participant texts as a
proxy for grounding strength. Classification was performed by GPT-4o-mini [Achiam et al., 2023]
over five independent runs with majority voting; Fleiss’ κ [Fleiss, 1971] assessed inter-run reliability
(Table 1).
In Education (κ = 0.866), three of six sentences are explicit (well-grounded in participant texts)
and three are inferred. The inferred sentences correspond to abstract policy prescriptions (“Public
infrastructure must ensure equitable access to these opportunities”, mean similarity 0.491) that
have no high-similarity counterpart in the participant corpus they represent editorial additions
to the consultation record. In Trust (κ = 0.721), five sentences are explicit and one is inferred.
The difference is itself informative: the Trust summary hews more closely to participant language,
possibly because the topic (governance, regulation, accountability) uses a more standardized policy
vocabulary than Education.

3
Discussion
These findings raise a question that existing AI accountability frameworks leave unaddressed: what
happens to the inputs of an AI system when they pass through summarization? The gap docu-
mented here, where both official summaries perform below a random-participant baseline, is not a
calibration error. It reflects a structural property of neural summarization applied to heterogeneous
public input. The worst-represented clusters are those expressing critique, distrust and scepticism
towards AI, precisely the voices that democratic theory identifies as deserving particular protection
in public deliberation [Arnstein, 1969, Young, 2000, Dryzek, 2002]. The causal predictors of ex-
clusion, brevity, semantic distinctiveness and assertive register, reflect structural features of public
discourse with well-documented ties to socioeconomic position, educational attainment and politi-
cal marginalization [Soss, 2000, Verba et al., 1995]. It is important to note that these limitations
are not a criticism of Canada’s consultation initiative, which represents one of the most ambitious
and transparent public engagement exercises on AI policy conducted to date [Innovation, Science
and Economic Development Canada, 2025]. They are structural consequences of deploying neural
summarization at scale without dedicated measurement infrastructure. The implication cuts the
other way: if a consultation this transparent and well-resourced produces the representational gaps
documented here, less-resourced processes without dedicated measurement infrastructure are likely
to produce larger ones — reinforcing rather than undermining the case for treating provenance
auditing as default infrastructure.
Fair representation as the missing dimension of responsible AI. The rapid maturation
of responsible AI has produced sophisticated tools and methods for understanding what comes out
of AI systems Xia et al. [2024], Mahajan [2025]. Explainable AI methods reveal why a model made
a particular prediction [Lundberg and Lee, 2017, Ribeiro et al., 2016]. Grounding and retrieval-
augmented generation ensure that generated text cites verifiable sources [Lewis et al., 2020, Gao
et al., 2023]. Hallucination detection identifies fabricated claims [Ji et al., 2023, Manakul et al.,
2023]. Model cards and datasheets document provenance and intended use [Mitchell et al., 2019,
Gebru et al., 2021].
These are indispensable contributions.
Yet they share a blind spot: none
address the question of whether the distribution of human inputs is faithfully preserved through AI
processing.
This asymmetry matters most in contexts where AI mediates between populations and institu-
tions Mahajan and Helbing [2026]. A government consultation summary can be factually grounded
14


## Page 15


(every claim traceable to at least one participant), free of hallucinations (no fabricated viewpoints),
and interpretable (clear reasoning), while still systematically excluding 88% of a dissenting cluster’s
voices. The concept fidelity asymmetry documented here, high backward precision (∼80%) along-
side low forward recall (∼15%), highlights precisely this failure mode: the summary passes every
output-oriented quality check while discarding more than 85% of participant-generated concepts.
Participatory provenance fills this gap by treating the input-to-output transformation itself as the
object of audit. It is to input fidelity what XAI is to output interpretability: a measurement science
for a dimension of AI accountability that existing frameworks leave unaddressed.
Manufactured consensus and the structure of exclusion. The cluster-structured nature
of exclusion connects to a growing literature on systemic and intersectional algorithmic harm [Buo-
lamwini and Gebru, 2018, Raji et al., 2020, Noble, 2018, O’neil, 2017]. The finding that dissenting
clusters experience exclusion rates up to 88%, against near-zero rates for supportive clusters, is
not a calibration error amenable to post-hoc correction. It suggests that the optimization objec-
tive of coherent summarization inherently privileges consensus-compatible viewpoints, producing
a measurable bias toward the institutional status quo in any policy area where the public holds
heterogeneous views. This is especially concerning given the documented tendency of government
consultations to favour existing policy directions [Nabatchi et al., 2012, Gastil and Levine, 2005],
creating a feedback loop in which AI summarization can amplify pre-existing institutional biases.
The concept fidelity asymmetry, high backward precision with low forward recall, is particularly
important for understanding the mechanism of this manufactured consensus. It reveals that the
summary speaks in the participants’ language while representing only a small fraction of their ideas.
This is not random compression: it is selective extraction, conditioned on the statistical regularities
that neural language models amplify. The pattern is consistent with what Habermas [Habermas,
1985] would recognize as a communicative pathology, a process that appears participatory on the
surface but systematically filters the discursive space.
The Co-creation Provenance Lab as human-in-the-loop infrastructure. These findings
are framed through the AI Trilemma in participatory governance: scale, efficiency and account-
ability cannot all be achieved simultaneously without dedicated measurement infrastructure. The
participatory provenance framework provides the measurement layer. But measurement alone is
insufficient for democratic accountability. The measurements must be accessible to the people and
institutions responsible for acting on them.
The open-source Co-creation Provenance Lab is designed to facilitate this accessibility. “Co-
creation” captures an important distinction from passive auditing: the tool supports a genuine
bidirectional process in which human judgment and AI capabilities combine iteratively. A facilitator
or policymaker can upload consultation data (participant responses and an AI-generated summary),
run the full provenance audit, and then actively improve the summary guided by the results. The
tool shows which voices are in each blind-spot quadrant (Core Excluded, Blind Spots), which concepts
were lost, and how exclusion rates change as the summary is revised. This closes the loop between
measurement and intervention, making real human oversight possible rather than symbolic.
Beyond the audit-to-revision cycle, the Lab enables equity analysis across successive summary
drafts, tracking whether revisions reduce the Gini coefficient of coverage, lower W2 distance and
bring excluded-cluster rates toward equity targets. Visualization tools include provenance Sankey
diagrams tracing the flow of voice from participant clusters to summary sentences, tension net-
work graphs showing how the summary handles inter-cluster disagreements, claim attribution views
linking each summary claim to its nearest source texts, and concept fidelity comparisons show-
ing what survived and what was lost. The Lab also generates exportable provenance certificates,
participant verification pages enabling individual citizens to check whether their submission was rep-
resented, and executive summaries for institutional decision-makers. A facilitation co-pilot mode
15


## Page 16


provides real-time guidance during live consultation processes, enabling facilitators to actively seek
out under-represented voices before a summary is finalized.
This combination of formal measurement and accessible tooling provides a practical path toward
resolving the AI Trilemma. The framework does not oppose the use of AI in public consultation.
Governments will increasingly need computational tools to process growing volumes of public in-
put [OECD, 2020, Fishkin, 2009]. Rather, it provides the audit-and-improvement layer that makes
AI-mediated consultation defensible to democratic scrutiny. Governments deploying AI summa-
rization could publish provenance metrics alongside every consultation report, enabling civil society
organizations, researchers and journalists to verify whether excluded voices were structurally disad-
vantaged or merely low-frequency.
Cross-topic replication and structural invisibility. The cross-topic replication and the
individual-level consistency of exclusion (ρ = 0.624; OR= 6.65) raise a troubling implication: some
citizens may be structurally invisible to AI summarization across multiple policy domains. A par-
ticipant who writes briefly, positions themselves outside the mainstream and uses direct rather than
hedged language faces systematically lower coverage regardless of what they say. This amounts to
a form of procedural disenfranchisement that operates through the medium of language rather than
through explicit exclusion. This finding connects to a broader literature on participation inequal-
ity [Verba et al., 1995, Soss, 2000] and raises the question of whether AI-mediated consultation
systematically amplifies pre-existing disparities in political voice.
Implications for AI governance and future directions. The findings have direct rele-
vance to ongoing regulatory efforts including the EU AI Act [European Union, 2024] and Canada’s
proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act [Government of Canada, 2022].
Both frameworks
emphasize transparency and accountability for AI systems used in public-facing applications, but
neither currently requires representational auditing of AI-mediated consultation processes.
The
participatory provenance framework provides a concrete, implementable standard for such audit-
ing. The open-source release of both the analytical pipeline and the Co-creation Provenance Lab
is intended to lower the barrier for adoption across consultation contexts (treaty negotiations, ur-
ban planning, environmental impact assessments) wherever AI mediates between citizen voice and
institutional decision.
This work can be extended in several concrete directions. The current analysis is retrospective:
it measures exclusion in summaries already released, rather than intervening in the summariza-
tion process.
The Co-creation Provenance Lab begins to address this by enabling prospective,
human-guided revision, but whether provenance metrics can directly guide automated re-ranking or
corrective sampling during summary generation is an open question with significant practical im-
plications. The coverage score treats semantic proximity as a proxy for representational fidelity and
cannot distinguish genuine exclusion from faithful abstraction: a summary that generalises “AI will
destroy the teaching profession” into “concerns about job displacement” produces moderate semantic
distance but is not exclusion. The concept fidelity analysis partially separates these phenomena by
measuring conceptual survival (forward recall of 13–18%) rather than semantic proximity, and the
epistemic grounding classification further identifies which summary sentences are directly evidenced
versus inferred. At the individual participant level, however, the metric cannot fully separate these;
we therefore treat low coverage as a necessary indicator of potential exclusion warranting investi-
gation, not a sufficient proof of it. Alternative representations of semantic distance (for instance,
entailment-based measures) could complement the embedding-based approach. The multi-model
robustness check shows a lower-bound rank correlation of ρ = 0.665 (Trust/nomic-embed-text), in-
dicating that precise individual rankings are embedding-dependent even where directional findings
are stable. This is a general challenge for embedding-based NLP methods that future work should
address through ensemble approaches. The consultation data lacks demographic metadata, prevent-
16


## Page 17


ing analysis of whether representational exclusion correlates with social group membership; linking
provenance metrics to demographic surveys would enable intersectional analysis at the population
level rather than only at the textual-feature level. Finally, extension to multilingual consultations,
longitudinal tracking across repeated consultations, and integration with deliberative mini-public
designs represent natural next steps toward a comprehensive measurement science for democratic
AI.

Methods
4.1
Data and preprocessing
Consultation data were obtained from Canada’s 2025–2026 National AI Strategy consultation con-
ducted by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada (ISED). The consultation col-
lected written responses to 26 questions spanning eight policy pillars; this study focuses on two
pillars (Education & Skills and Safe AI & Public Trust). Each respondent answered up to three
sub-questions per topic; responses were concatenated with space separation to form a single text per
respondent per topic. The official “What We Heard” summaries were extracted verbatim from the
published consultation report [Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada, 2025] and
split at sentence boundaries into J = 6 individual sentences per topic. These sentences constitute
the object of the representational audit (Supplementary Table 13).
ISED independently processed the full consultation responses using its own AI pipeline, which
employed several large language models (including Canadian models) in a classification workflow
with manual human review at multiple stages, targeting at least 90% categorization accuracy;
pipeline outputs were used in drafting the official report [Innovation, Science and Economic Devel-
opment Canada, 2025]. The provenance analysis presented here is entirely independent: it operates
directly on the raw participant texts downloaded under the Open Government Licence and compares
them against the published summary without reference to ISED’s internal classifications.
Preprocessing followed four sequential stages applied identically to both corpora. (1) Minimum
length: responses shorter than five word tokens (measured after whitespace tokenization) were
removed (91 Education, 91 Trust). This threshold excludes incomplete form submissions and single-
word acknowledgements that carry no propositional content. (2) Language filter: a French-language
classifier flagged responses in which more than 8% of whitespace-separated tokens matched a curated
set of 56 high-frequency French stop words (le, les, de, du, des, est, sont, etc.); these were removed
(71 Education, 83 Trust). Responses with English-French code-switching below the 8% threshold
were retained, as they engage substantively in English. (3) Near-duplicate detection: all pairs of
retained responses were compared using cosine similarity on ℓ2-normalised bag-of-words TF-IDF
vectors; pairs exceeding ρdup = 0.95 were treated as coordinated spam and all but one member of
each duplicate cluster were removed (2 per topic). (4) Topic-relevance filter: a two-zone hybrid
approach was applied to prevent analytical dilution by off-topic text while minimising the risk of
excluding legitimate critical voices. Each response was encoded with the primary embedding model,
and its maximum cosine similarity to a set of topic-specific anchor phrases was computed. Anchor
phrases comprised the verbatim consultation sub-questions (three per topic) and six broad domain
descriptors designed to capture both mainstream and adversarial stances (e.g., “concerns about
AI surveillance, bias, and societal harms” for the Trust corpus; see Supplementary Note 1 for the
complete anchor set). Responses below the auto-reject threshold (τrej = 0.10) were removed without
LLM review. Responses at or above the auto-accept threshold (τacc = 0.20) were retained without
review. Borderline responses in [τrej, τacc) were adjudicated by GPT-4o-mini using a structured
prompt that explicitly instructed the model to classify as topically valid any response expressing
17

opposition to AI technology provided it engaged with the consultation question. The 0.10/0.20
spacing was calibrated on a 200-response held-out validation set annotated by two independent
reviewers; this was the smallest interval that reduced LLM calls below 15% of the corpus while
maintaining >98% agreement with manual annotation. The process retained 2,496 Education and
2,757 Trust responses (Supplementary Table 1).
4.2
Embedding and dimensionality reduction
All participant texts and summary sentences were embedded using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large
model (3,072 dimensions; context window 8,191 tokens) [Achiam et al., 2023].
This model was
selected for its performance on semantic textual similarity benchmarks most relevant to the short-
to-medium length texts characteristic of public consultation responses [Muennighoff et al., 2023].
Embeddings were computed in batches of 512 texts per API call. All vectors were ℓ2-normalised be-
fore any downstream distance computation, ensuring that cosine similarity and Euclidean distance
in the normalised space are monotonically related.
For robustness validation, embeddings were independently computed using two additional mod-
els operating in different representational spaces: all-mpnet-base-v2 (768 dimensions, sentence-
transformers library [Reimers and Gurevych, 2019]) run locally, and nomic-embed-text (768 dimen-
sions [Nussbaum et al., 2024]) accessed via Ollama. These models differ substantially in architecture,
training corpus and dimensionality from the primary model, providing a stringent test of sensitivity
to embedding choice.
For optimal transport computation, embeddings were reduced to D = 50 principal components
(PCA, implemented in scikit-learn [Pedregosa et al., 2011]), which explained 55.8% of variance
(Education) and 56.4% (Trust). This dimensionality was the highest value for which POT’s exact
linear-programming solver remained computationally tractable (<5 min per corpus on a single CPU
core). Sensitivity to this choice was assessed at D ∈{20, 50, 100}.

4.6
Concept fidelity and epistemic grounding
To characterise what conceptual content survives into the summary, keyphrases were extracted
from each participant text and from each summary sentence using KeyBERT [Grootendorst, 2020]
(Maximal Marginal Relevance, diversity penalty λ = 0.5), targeting ten keyphrases per text unit.
A keyphrase was considered a match if its stemmed token representation appeared in the other
keyphrase set (exact token match on Porter-stemmed forms).
Forward recall was computed as
the fraction of participant corpus keyphrases appearing in the summary keyphrase set; backward
precision as the fraction of summary keyphrases traceable to at least one participant text.
Concept transformation was classified for each matched keyphrase pair using GPT-4o-mini (5
independent API calls per pair, majority vote across runs). The classification distinguished: pre-
served (same propositional content and valence), softened (attenuated strength or urgency relative
to the participant text), and abstracted (raised to a higher level of generality without preserving
the participant’s specificity). Fleiss’ κ [Fleiss, 1971] was computed across the five annotation runs
as the primary measure of inter-run reliability; results with κ < 0.60 were excluded from the main
analysis (Trust: κ = 0.682, reliable; Education: κ = 0.768, reliable).
Epistemic grounding classified each summary sentence as explicit if its mean cosine similarity
to its top-k = 10 nearest participant texts exceeded 0.55, and inferred otherwise; the 0.55 threshold
was calibrated by inspection of the bimodal similarity distribution across all summary sentences,
lying below the dense cluster of high-similarity explicit sentences (≥0.65) and above the low-
similarity inferred cluster (≤0.49) (Education: 3 explicit, 3 inferred; Trust: 5 explicit, 1 inferred).

4.7
Cross-topic and multi-model validation
Participants who submitted responses to both consultation topics were identified by internal re-
spondent ID (n = 2,392). Cross-topic consistency of coverage scores was quantified by Spearman
rank correlation ρs; cross-topic consistency of exclusion status (binary) by the ϕ coefficient of the
2 × 2 contingency table.
Multi-model robustness was assessed by repeating the full coverage analysis with both robustness
embedding models. For each alternative model, Spearman rank correlation of individual coverage
scores against primary-model scores, and the exclusion agreement rate (fraction of participants
assigned the same exclusion status), were computed.
Parameter sensitivity was assessed across

{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0} standard deviations below the random mean), and cluster count (k∗± 2). For
each of the resulting 3 × 4 × 5 = 60 configurations, mean coverage, exclusion rate and W 2

4.8
Statistical analysis and computational environment
All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11. Core scientific computing used NumPy 1.26, SciPy
1.12 and pandas 2.2. Clustering and causal inference used scikit-learn 1.4 [Pedregosa et al., 2011]
and EconML 0.15. Optimal transport used POT 0.9.3 [Flamary et al., 2021]. LLM inference used
the OpenAI Python SDK 1.17 (GPT-4o-mini, text-embedding-3-large). Local embeddings used
sentence-transformers 2.7 [Reimers and Gurevych, 2019]. All bootstrap (B = 2,000) and permu-
tation (N = 10,000) tests used a fixed random seed (numpy.random.seed(42)) for reproducibility.
Statistical significance was assessed at α = 0.05; where multiple comparisons were performed (OLS
interaction terms), Bonferroni correction was applied and full interaction results are reported in
