Perceived Social Norms under Uncertainty
Senran Lin
***Preliminary draft***

Abstract
This paper proposes a belief-based framework for social norms in environments where in-
dividuals choose a single action. Relaxing the assumption that the appropriateness standard is
common knowledge, the framework allows individuals to be uncertain about this standard and
to hold heterogeneous assessments and beliefs about others’ assessments. Within the frame-
work, perceived injunctive social norms, personal values, and empirical expectations, while
distinct, are systematically connected through a common informational structure. The frame-
work further clarifies how disclosed information shapes perceived norms: its effect depends on
what is disclosed, whether it is publicly or privately revealed, and how the disclosed statistic
encodes underlying private cues.
1
Introduction
The literature on social norms distinguishes three closely related concepts: personal value, per-
ceived injunctive social norm, and empirical expectation. Empirically, these measures are often
correlated, yet existing work has mainly emphasized their differences (Bicchieri, 2006; Bicchieri
and Xiao, 2009). However, there is no simple formal framework that brings these concepts together
within a unified structure.
This paper develops a belief-based framework that connects these concepts. The starting point
is to relax the typical assumption that the relevant appropriateness standard is commonly known,
and instead allow individuals to be uncertain about a latent appropriateness standard and to hold
heterogeneous beliefs due to imperfect observation. This relaxation reflects the norm-perception
view that individuals respond to their subjective perceptions of social norms, rather than directly
observing a community-wide norm (Tankard and Paluck, 2016). We therefore treat the appropri-
ateness standard as a latent benchmark and focus on how individuals form beliefs about it. Ac-
cordingly, perceived social norms are understood as beliefs about how the reference group assesses
appropriateness.
We focus on environments in which individuals choose a single action, as in many norm-
elicitation and behavioral experiments. This restriction keeps the appropriateness standard one-
dimensional and makes belief formation and updating transparent. The standard need not be a fea-
sible action; rather, it serves as a reference point against which feasible actions are evaluated. For

To provide a tractable information structure, the framework draws on a noisy-inference idea

noisy private cues.
In this framework, personal value captures an individual’s own assessment of what is appro-
priate, while the perceived social norm reflects the individual’s belief about the reference group’s
average assessment. The framework shows that, although conceptually distinct, the two are sys-
tematically related, as both are formed from beliefs about the same underlying appropriateness
standard.
The framework also clarifies the relation between the descriptive and injunctive components.
An empirical expectation, or descriptive norm, is an individual’s belief about how others will be-
have, whereas the perceived (injunctive) social norm concerns what others regard as appropriate.
These two distinct concepts can be systematically related when behavior is influenced by perceived
social norms, as beliefs about others’ actions can be derived from beliefs about others’ perceived
social norm. Consequently, the framework provides a unified representation in which personal
value, perceived social norm, and empirical expectation can be connected through the same la-
tent appropriateness standard, while differing in the perspective taken on it and in how individual
assessments are aggregated and used to infer others’ views.
A further contribution is to clarify how disclosed information, in particular information ob-
served from a previous group, enters the updating of perceived norms. In the framework, such
information matters because it shifts the individual’s inference about the underlying standard and
about others’ assessments of appropriateness. Crucially, different types of disclosed information,
such as elicited norms, personal values, or observed actions, do not provide the same signal about
this underlying standard. They encode underlying private cues in different ways and therefore in-
duce different degrees of belief updating for the observer. Moreover, public and private disclosure
affect how beliefs and higher-order beliefs adjust as well. Hence, how disclosure affects beliefs
and behavior depends both on what is revealed and how it is revealed.
This paper relates to several strands of the norms literature. Bicchieri’s expectation-based
account provides a natural conceptual benchmark, as it distinguishes normative expectations, em-
pirical expectations, and personal normative beliefs (Bicchieri, 2006; Bicchieri and Chavez, 2010).
Krupka and Weber (2013) provide a leading experimental approach to elicit shared judgments of
social appropriateness and show that these judgments predict behavior. Closest in spirit is d’Adda
et al. (2020), who study a utility function in which the injunctive norm enters individual decision-

Norms as beliefs about appropriateness
Injunctive social norms concern appropriateness: what behavior is regarded as appropriate in a
given situation. To formalize this idea, we introduce an appropriateness standard, denoted by S,
which serves as a situation-specific benchmark against which actions are evaluated.2 For example,
in a dictator game, S may be interpreted as an appropriate transfer share; in a public goods game,
as an appropriate contribution rate; in a tax compliance setting, as an appropriate declaration level;
and in an honesty task, as an acceptable lying threshold.
We depart from the assumption that this standard is commonly known. Instead, individuals
do not directly observe it, may be uncertain about it, form different assessments, and hold hetero-
geneous beliefs about how others assess it. We model S as an exogenous random variable and
assume
S ∼N(µs, νs),
where µs is the prior mean of the appropriate standard, and νs captures the uncertainty about it,
with the distribution of S being environment-specific. The variance νs reflects the appropriateness
uncertainty: it is higher when the situation admits a more controversial, complex, ambiguous, or
weakly defined appropriateness benchmark. This specification captures the idea that individuals
may share a common but imprecise prior view of what is appropriate in a given situation.3

Public disclosure of previous-group information
We now turn to how perceived social norms respond to additional information. Specifically, we
examine how publicly disclosed previous-group information shapes individuals’ beliefs about what
the current group regards as appropriate. Evidence shows that norm-relevant information can shift
perceived social norms and subsequent behavior, not only beliefs about behavior (Paluck, 2009;
Paluck and Green, 2009; Tankard and Paluck, 2016; Bicchieri and Chavez, 2010; Bursztyn et al.,
2020; d’Adda et al., 2020).
In this framework, this effect arises from how individuals interpret previous-group information:
rather than treating it as a direct signal of the appropriateness standard S, they view it as reflecting
others’ noisy inferences about S, and hence as an endogenous outcome.

6
Conclusion and discussion
This paper develops a belief-based framework for social norms by relaxing the assumption that the
relevant appropriateness standard is commonly known. Individuals instead receive noisy private
cues about a latent standard S and form beliefs about how others assess it. Within this framework,
personal value captures an individual’s own assessment of appropriateness, perceived social norm
captures her belief about the reference group’s average assessment, and empirical expectation cap-
tures her belief about others’ behavior. The three objects are therefore distinct perspectives on
the same latent appropriateness standard: an individual’s own assessment, her belief about others’
assessments, and her belief about others’ behavior.
The first implication is that personal value and perceived social norm are systematically related
but need not coincide. When individuals observe only their private cues, the perceived social norm
is a convex combination of the prior mean and the individual’s personal value, and is therefore
pulled toward the prior relative to her own assessment. The model also predicts that perceived
social norms are less dispersed than personal values, and that the two become more closely aligned
when appropriateness uncertainty is higher or private cues are more precise.
When perceived social norms affect individuals’ behavior, the same belief structure links em-
pirical expectations to perceived social norms. Empirical expectations concern what others do, but
if behavior responds to perceived social norms, beliefs about others’ actions can be expressed in
13


## Page 14


terms of those norms. This implies that the two may be correlated while remaining conceptually
distinct.
The framework also clarifies how previous-group information shapes perceived norms. Such
information matters because it updates beliefs about S and about others’ assessments of S, not
merely because behavior is mechanically related. A key implication is that the effect of a disclosed
statistic depends on how it encodes underlying private cues. If individuals could directly infer the
previous group’s private signals, the effect of those signals would follow the usual signal-precision
intuition. However, when the disclosed statistic is itself an elicited belief, such as an average
perceived norm or an average personal value, the comparative statics can reverse. In this case, a
given change in the reported statistic implies a larger inferred change in the underlying private cues
when appropriateness uncertainty is lower or private cues are noisier, leading to a stronger effect.
The analysis further shows that the same statistic has a weaker effect under private disclosure
than under public disclosure. Under private disclosure, the information affects only the individual’s
own updating about S. Under public disclosure, she also accounts for the fact that others update
from the same information. This explains why publicly disclosed norm-relevant information can
have stronger effects than privately received information.
This mechanism can be viewed as a socially generated signal-extraction problem. As in noisy-
representation models, responses depend not only on the underlying information but also on how
that information is encoded.11 Here, the encoding is social rather than purely cognitive: reported
statistics summarize previous individuals’ private cues only through their beliefs. Their influence
therefore depends on how strongly those beliefs respond to the underlying cues.
In sum, the framework provides a belief-based account of how perceived social norms are
formed, how they relate to personal values and empirical expectations, and how they respond to
different forms of social information. It therefore contributes not only a conceptual unification
of key norm-related measures, but also testable implications for how norm-relevant information
should affect beliefs and behavior.
