Abstract
The notion of a two-dimensional word arises naturally in the study of combinatorics
on words, while the iterative construction of pedal triangles results in a rich dynamical
system in the study of geometry.
At first, these two classes of objects seem to be
unrelated.
However, it is known that for all n ≥1, the number of primitive two-
dimensional words of dimension 2 × n over a binary alphabet agrees with the number
of triangles whose first similar pedal triangle is their nth pedal triangle. We construct
a finite four-symbol coding of the sorted pedal map and use the resulting branch
itineraries to give a bijection between these two classes.
Key words and phrases: Formal language theory, pedal map, pedal triangle, symbolic
dynamics, two-dimensional word.
MSC2020 classes: 37B10 (primary); 51M15, 68Q45 (secondary).

Introduction
A two-dimensional word is an array or matrix of symbols drawn from some finite alphabet Σ,
and we say that a two-dimensional word with m rows and n columns has dimension m × n.
If we cannot decompose a given two-dimensional word W into some repeated concatenation
of a smaller two-dimensional word, then we say that W is a primitive two-dimensional word.
There exists a formula to enumerate all primitive two-dimensional words of dimension m×n
over a binary alphabet Σ; call this value ψ2(m, n) [2, 11].
A pedal triangle is constructed by starting with a triangle T, taking the three points where
the altitudes dropped at each vertex of T intersect with the opposite (possibly extended)
side, and joining these points. We can iterate the process of constructing pedal triangles in
the usual way: we construct the nth pedal triangle of T by constructing the pedal triangle
of the (n −1)st pedal triangle of T; the ‘zeroth’ pedal triangle is T itself. If T is similar
to its nth pedal triangle, where triangle similarity is determined by internal angles, then we

Preliminaries
In this section, we fix some notation and establish key properties about the objects studied
in this paper. For more details about two-dimensional formal language theory in particular,
see the article by Giammarresi and Restivo [3] or the book by Rosenfeld [9].
2.1
Two-Dimensional Words
Let Σ = {0, 1, . . . , k −1} denote a finite k-ary alphabet for k ≥1. In one dimension, a
word w = a0a1 . . . an−1 is a sequence of concatenated symbols ai ∈Σ, and the length of w is
denoted |w| = n. A language over Σ is then a set of words over Σ.
Just as we may concatenate symbols to form words, we may concatenate words to form
longer words. If we concatenate a word w with itself m times, we obtain the word power
wm. A word w is said to be periodic if it can be written as w = vm where v is a nonempty
word and m ≥2. If w is not periodic, then it is said to be primitive.

Just as we may concatenate words in one dimension in the usual manner, we may concate-

by joining W and V in such a way that the topmost row of V is adjacent to the bottommost
row of W. On the other hand, if m1 = m2 = m, then the column concatenation of W and
V is the two-dimensional word of dimension m × (n1 + n2) produced by joining W and V in
such a way that the leftmost column of V is adjacent to the rightmost column of W.
By repeatedly concatenating two-dimensional words, we may generalize the notion of
a word power to two dimensions.
Suppose W ∈Σm×n.

denoted W p×q, is the two-dimensional word of dimension pm × qn with the property that
W p×q[i, j] = W[i mod m, j mod n].
Lastly, defining two-dimensional word powers allows us to define primitivity and period-
icity in two dimensions. A two-dimensional word W is said to be periodic if it can be written
as W = V p×q where V is a nonempty two-dimensional word and p ≥2 or q ≥2 (or both).
If W is not periodic, then it is said to be primitive.
2.2
Pedal Triangles
Let T be a triangle formed by joining vertices L, M, and N. If we drop the altitude from
vertex L to intersect the (possibly extended) line segment MN and mark this point of
intersection as X, then do the same for vertices M and N intersecting line segments LN and
LM to mark points Y and Z, respectively, then the triangle formed by joining X, Y , and
Z is known as the pedal triangle of T. Figure 1 depicts an example construction of a pedal
triangle.
Remark. The geometric object produced according to our definition of a pedal triangle ought
to more properly be referred to as an orthic triangle. The most general definition of a pedal
triangle is one constructed by joining the three points where the perpendiculars drawn from
an arbitrary point P intersect each side of a triangle; our definition arises by taking P to

triangle for which some pedal iterate is right-angled, as being pedally degenerate.
If a triangle T is similar to its nth pedal triangle Tn, then we say that T has pedal period
dividing n. Moreover, if T is similar to Tn but not to any other pedal triangle Tm where
1 ≤m < n, then we say that T has exact pedal period n.
Our objects of interest in this paper are triangles whose pedal iterates are similar to the
original triangle. Since triangle similarity is determined by interior angles, we will represent
triangles as triples of angles. Suppose a triangle T has angles A, B, and C, where A+B+C =

and c = C/π to obtain values a, b, and c, where a + b + c = 1. We may then define the set

C = {(a, b, c) ∈R3 | a ≥b ≥c ≥0, a + b + c = 1}

C = {(a, b, c) ∈R3 | a ≥b ≥c > 0, a + b + c = 1}.
Any element (a, b, c) ∈C therefore refers to the similarity class of triangles whose interior
angles are aπ, bπ, and cπ, in any order.

Constructing the Bijection
Having established via Theorem 3 that the counts of our objects of study are the same,
we now turn to constructing the bijection between these two sets of objects. Interestingly,
nothing about the structure of either object itself leads to the bijection; rather, as we will
show, both sets of objects are related by way of the same finite symbolic dynamical system.

4.2
Sorted Pedal Map
Next, we shift our focus to pedal triangles. Hobson [4] was the first to provide angle formulas
for constructing pedal triangles, while Kingston and Synge [5] corrected errors in Hobson’s
work and studied the sequences that arose by iteratively constructing pedal triangles accord-
ing to the corrected angle formulas. Denoting the original triangle by T and its corresponding
triple by (a, b, c) ∈∆, the sequence develops according to the following pedal mapping:
• If T is acute, then its pedal triangle corresponds to the triple (1 −2a, 1 −2b, 1 −2c).
• If T is obtuse at angle A, then its pedal triangle corresponds to the triple (2a−1, 2b, 2c).
• If T is obtuse at angle B, then its pedal triangle corresponds to the triple (2a, 2b−1, 2c).
• If T is obtuse at angle C, then its pedal triangle corresponds to the triple (2a, 2b, 2c−1).
Kingston and Synge [5] observed that the process of iteratively applying this pedal mapping
gives rise to a discontinuous four-to-one mapping of a simplex to itself, where each triple
(a, b, c) gives the barycentric coordinates of a point interior to this simplex. Lax [6] later
showed that this pedal mapping is ergodic, while Ungar [12] represented this pedal mapping
as a shift.
Here, we will use a similar idea to develop a pedal map for our sorted triples in C. Before
we proceed, let C∗= {(a, b, c) ∈C | a ̸= 1/2}; we may interpret this set C∗as the set of
sorted triples for which at least one step of the pedal map is defined. We exclude sorted
triples where a = 1/2 because this only occurs when T is a right triangle and is therefore
pedally degenerate.

4.4
Branch Itineraries
For n ≥1, let Tn denote the set of sorted triples corresponding to triangles having exact
pedal period n. Using our pedal map notation, we may write this definition as
Tn = {p ∈C | P j(p) is defined for all 0 ≤j < n, P n(p) = p, and P d(p) ̸= p for any 1 ≤d < n}.
As one of the final steps toward our bijection, we define a so-called itinerary map that
serves to record the sequence of branches of our sorted pedal map produced by some sorted
triple p corresponding to a triangle in Tn.
Definition 11. Take ι: Tn →Γn to be an itinerary map, and for any p ∈Tn, take the branch
itinerary to be the word ι(p) = w0w1 . . . wn−1, where P j(p) ∈Rwj for all 0 ≤j < n.
In other terms, the itinerary map ι encodes branches arising from the partition given in
Definition 6 as words over a four-symbol alphabet. Specifically, the symbol wj records which
of the four branches of the sorted pedal map is used at the jth step of the period. This
particular idea is not entirely novel; Alexander [1] similarly used a four-symbol alphabet to
assign to ordered triangles an infinite “obtuseness sequence”, and pedal iteration corresponds
to a left shift being applied to this sequence. By contrast, here we sort the triple of angles
after each pedal iteration and record via the branch itinerary which branch of the sorted
pedal map was used. Thus, we essentially make use of a finite sorted analogue of Alexander’s
infinite coding.

Since p ∈Tn, we have that P n(p) = p, and so p = Iw(p).
The second technical result connects branch itineraries of sorted triples to words over
Γ of the form {1, 2}n; recall that this is one of the forbidding conditions we discussed in
Section 4.1.
Lemma 13. If p ∈Tn, then ι(p) ̸∈{1, 2}n.
Proof. Let p = (a, b, c) ∈Tn and suppose by way of contradiction that ι(p) ∈{1, 2}n. Then at
each point, we must follow either the branch corresponding to R1 or the branch corresponding
to R2. On both of these branches, the third coordinate is doubled: P(a, b, c) = (2a−1, 2b, 2c)
in the case of R1, or P(a, b, c) = (2b, 2a−1, 2c) in the case of R2. After n iterations, the third
coordinate becomes 2nc. However, since p ∈Tn, we know that P n(p) = p. Hence, c = 2nc.
Since n ≥1, this forces c = 0, which means that p ̸∈C and therefore p ̸∈Tn.
The third technical result connects branch itineraries of sorted triples to the notion of
periodicity, which again is one of the forbidding conditions mentioned in Section 4.1.
Lemma 14. If p ∈Tn, then ι(p) is a primitive word over Γ.
Proof. Let w = ι(p), and suppose by way of contradiction that w is periodic. Then w = vm
for some v ∈Γℓwith ℓ≥1 and m ≥2. Since w = ι(p), we have that n = ℓm where ℓ< n.
By Lemma 12, we know that p = Iw(p), and since w = vm, we may rewrite the inverse
branch as Iw = (Iv)m and therefore p = (Iv)m(p). By Lemma 10, we know that Iv has a
unique fixed point. Additionally, since Iv is contractive, (Iv)m is also contractive and, by the
Banach fixed-point theorem, has the same fixed point as Iv. Thus, we may write p = Iv(p)
by itself.
Since the first ℓsymbols of the branch itinerary are v itself, performing the inverse branch
calculation demonstrated in the proof of Lemma 12 will produce p = Iv(P ℓ(p)). By Lemma 9,
we know that Iv is injective, and so taking both p = Iv(p) and p = Iv(P ℓ(p)) together implies
that p = P ℓ(p). However, this means that p cannot have exact pedal period n, and therefore
p ̸∈Tn.
Lastly, we combine the previous results to establish some helpful properties about the
itinerary map.
Lemma 15. The itinerary map ι: Tn →Un is well defined and injective.
Proof. Let p ∈Tn. By Definition 11, we have that ι(p) ∈Γn. Since the four regions R0,
R1, R2, and R3 form a disjoint partition of the domain of the sorted pedal map according
to Definition 6, each iteration of the pedal map P j(p) falls into a unique region Ri, and
hence ι(p) is uniquely determined. Then, by Lemma 13 and Lemma 14, we know both that
ι(p) ̸∈{1, 2}n and that ι(p) is a primitive word, so ι(p) ∈Un. Therefore, ι is well defined as
a map from Tn to Un.

4.5
Main Theorem
We now have enough to prove the main theorem of the paper.
Theorem 16. For each n ≥1, the map η ◦ι is a bijection between Tn and Wn.
Proof. By Lemma 15, we know that ι: Tn →Un is injective. Furthermore, we know by
Lemma 5 that the substitution η restricts to a bijection between Un and Wn. Therefore, by
composition, we have that η ◦ι: Tn →Wn is injective.
Now, by Theorem 3, we know that ψ2(2, n) = χ(n) for all n ≥1. We also know that
|Wn| = ψ2(2, n) and |Tn| = χ(n) by definition, so the sets Wn and Tn have the same
cardinality.
Since η ◦ι is an injection between two finite sets having the same cardinality, it is a
bijection as desired.
It is a rather straightforward exercise to illustrate, using Theorem 16, that a given tri-
angle having exact pedal period n determines a unique primitive two-dimensional word of
dimension 2 × n over a binary alphabet, and that such a two-dimensional word itself cor-
responds to a unique triangle having exact pedal period n. Starting with a triangle having
exact pedal period n, for instance, we sort the triple corresponding to the triangle to obtain
p ∈Tn, record the branch itinerary, and then apply the substitution η. Conversely, starting
with a two-dimensional word W ∈Wn, we compute w = η−1(W), then find the associated
triangle obtained by solving the fixed-point equation Iw(p) = p in C.
