Planar 1-ended graphs can be periodically coloured
Luke Waite
Abstract
We conclude an investigation of Abrishami, Esperet, Giocanti, Hamman, Knappe and M¨oller studying the
existence of periodic colourings of locally finite graphs. A colouring of a graph Γ is periodic if the resulting
coloured graph has a finite number of orbits under its colour-preserving automorphisms, as such it is natural to
consider those quasi-transitive graphs with finite quotient. In the case that the graph is planar and has 1-end
we prove that it always permits a periodic proper vertex colouring. This is shown by constructing isometry
respecting embedded maps into the Euclidean and hyperbolic planes and leveraging known properties of
Euclidean and hyperbolic isometry groups. Moreover, in the case that a graph is Euclidean we show that this
can always be done in 5 colours.

Introduction
The main purpose of this paper is to complete a recent investigation started by Abrishami, Esperet, Giocanti,
Hamman, Knappe and M¨oller in [AEG+25]. Therein the authors look at when highly-symmetric graphs can
be endowed with an additional highly-symmetric structure that respects some of the original symmetries. In
particular, in this paper we shall focus on whether highly-symmetric planar graphs permit the assignment of a
highly-symmetric proper vertex colouring. A highly-symmetric vertex colouring can be taken to mean one such
that there are a finite number of orbits under the colour-respecting automorphisms of the resulting coloured
graph; such a vertex colouring is called periodic. One requirement for such a colouring is that the original graph
has a finite number of vertex orbits under its automorphism group to begin with, it is natural to ask which of
these quasi-transitive graphs permit periodic colourings. Abrishami and collaborators ask:
Problem 1.1 ([AEG+25], Problem 1.11). Do all locally-finite quasi-transitive graphs permit periodic colourings?
It is not difficult to construct examples of these colourings; the 2-colouring of the Cayley graph of Z2 shown
in Figure 1 is periodic. Moreover, any proper colouring of a square section of the graph can be extended to a
periodic colouring of the whole graph by translation. In fact for any sufficiently ‘nice’ finitely generated group, a
periodic colouring of its Cayley graph may be found by colouring the finite quotient graph induced by the action
of a finite index proper subgroup, and lifting with respect to the quotient map.
However, in general they show quasi-transitivity is not sufficient for existence of such colourings, and hence prove
the negative to Problem 1.1
The authors achieve this by constructing 1-ended (non-planar) counterexamples. Norin and Przytycki are ad-
ditionally credited for finding counterexamples that are also Cayley graphs (and hence vertex transitive). By
restricting the hypotheses of the question, the authors also show that all locally-finite quasi-transitive graphs Γ
with bounded pathwidth do permit a periodic colouring, and hence proving a positive answer for all locally-finite,
quasi-transitive graphs with 2 ends. In addition, Abrishami and collaborators construct several ∞-ended planar
graphs that do not permit periodic colourings. Following [Bab97, Proposition 2.1], which tells us every infinite
locally-finite quasi-transitive graph has 1, 2 or ∞-many ends, this leaves only one case outstanding.
Problem 1.2 ([AEG+25], Problem 6.6). Are there locally finite, quasi-transitive planar graphs with 1-end that
do not a permit periodic colouring?

Of these highly symmetric graphs, those that are planar are of particular interest as they naturally arise as
periodic tilings of the planar geometries. As such they induce embeddings of their finite quotients onto well-
defined quotient surfaces. In this paper we prove the answer to Problem 1.2 in the negative. That is:
Theorem 1.1. Let Γ be a locally-finite, quasi-transitive planar graph with 1-end. Then Γ permits a periodic
proper vertex colouring.
Combined with the work in [AEG+25], this completes the discussion of periodic vertex colourings for locally-finite,
quasi-transitive planar graphs. That is
Corollary 1.2. Let Γ be a locally-finite, quasi-transitive planar graph with finitely many ends. Then Γ admits a
periodic proper vertex colouring.
We close this introduction by giving a brief survey of extant results. We shall make use of the following theorem
of Babai, which asserts in the case that the graphs are 3-connected we may identify their automorphism groups
with groups of Euclidean or hyperbolic plane isometries.
Theorem 1.3 ([Bab97], Theorem 4.2). Let Γ be a locally-finite 3-connected quasi-transitive planar graph with at
most one end. Then Γ has an embedding on a natural geometry such that all automorphisms of Γ are induced by
isometries of the geometry.
In a paper of Tim´ar concerning colourings of random planar maps, a similar question to that of Problem 1.2 is
asked ([Tim11], Question 4.8) and a sketch argument is provided for graphs that embed into the Euclidean plane
under Theorem 1.3. A crutch of Tim´ar’s argument is a powerful theorem of Thomassen concerning 5-colourability
of certain maps on closed orientable surfaces.
Theorem 1.4 ([Tho93]). Let Γ be an embedded graph on a closed orientable surface of genus g > 0 such that all
non-contractible cycles contain at least 214g+6 distinct edges. Then Γ admits a proper 5-colouring.
As such, Tim´ar’s argument provides us with the following strengthening of the main result for those 3-connected
graphs that embed into the Euclidean plane under Theorem 1.3.
Theorem 1.5. Let Γ be a 3-connected, locally-finite, quasi-transitive planar graph with 1-end that embeds into
the Euclidean plane under Theorem 1.3. Then Γ permits a periodic 5-colouring.
Moreover, in §5 when dealing with those graphs that are not 3-connected, we are able to extend this result to a
larger class of graphs that embed into the Euclidean plane with the property that a finite index subgroup of the
group of automorphisms is induced by Euclidean isometries.
We shall proceed by leveraging properties of Euclidean and hyperbolic planar isometry groups, in particular the
existence of finite index torsion free subgroups. In the Euclidean case we reproduce Tim´ar’s argument. By finding
torsion free subgroups, which in the Euclidean case are simply 2-generator translation groups, we may define an
embedding of the quotient graph onto a sufficiently large torus and apply Theorem 1.4. Using classical results on
the subgroups of Fuchsian groups we make an analogous construction in the hyperbolic case, though due to the

Preliminaries and General properties
Graphs and maps: We define a graph to be pair Γ = (V, E), where V is the vertex set, and E the set of edges
represented by unordered pairs {u, v} ⊂V so that u and v are joined by an edge. In cases where it is useful to
specify the edge and vertex sets of a specific graph Γ we will write V (Γ) = V and E(Γ) = E. A graph Γ is said
to be infinite if its vertex set is infinite. A graph is said to be locally-finite if the degree at each vertex is finite.
All graphs considered will be infinite, locally-finite, undirected, and without multiple edges or loops.
A map M is a graph embedded in a surface X so that the vertices v ∈V (M) are distinct points of X, the edges
{u, v} ∈E(M) are curves αu,v : [0, 1] →X such that αu1,u2 and αv1,v2 intersect at x ∈V (M) if and only if
ui = vj = x for some i, j ∈{1, 2} and, if we cut the surface along the set of all such curves what remains (X −M)
is a disjoint union of connected components called faces, all of which are homeomorphic to open discs. A graph
Γ is said to be planar if there exists a map M ⊂E2 and an embedding φ : Γ →E2 into the Euclidean plane so
that φ(Γ) = M. It will be useful to distinguish between a combinatorial graph Γ and a corresponding embedded
map M in a surface X.
A path in a graph Γ from a vertex v0 (called its initial vertex) to vertex vm (its terminal vertex) is a sequence
of incident edges (v0, v1), (v1, v2), . . . , (vm−1, vm) such that every vertex is visited only once, that is vi ̸= vj for
all i, j ≤m, i ̸= j. A path is called a cycle if its initial and terminal vertices coincide. A ray r in an infinite
graph Γ is an infinite one-way path. We say that two rays r1, r2 are end-equivalent if and only if there exists some
third ray r3 that contains infinitely many vertices of both r1 and r2. Equivalently we may say that r1 and r2 are
end-equivalent if and only if there are infinitely many disjoint paths connecting vertices of r1 to vertices of r2.
An equivalence class of rays in Γ is called an end of Γ, and a graph with exactly k ends is said to be k-ended. In
practice, given any infinite sequence of finite subgraphs Σ1, Σ2, . . . ⊂Γ such that V (Σi+1) ⊊V (Σi), the number
of ends of Γ can be found as the limit of the number of infinite connected components of Γ −Σi as i →∞. A
graph is said to be connected if there exists a path between any pair of distinct vertices. Throughout, unless
otherwise stated all graphs will be assumed to be connected. If the removal of any set of less than q vertices
leaves Γ connected, then Γ is said to be q-connected. A map is said to be locally-finite, k-ended or q-connected,
if its respective graph is.
For graphs Γ1, Γ2 a graph isomorphism is a map f : V (Γ1) →V (Γ2) that respects edge adjacency, that is for all
{u, v} ∈E(Γ1) we have that (f(u), f(v)) ∈E(Γ2). A graph automorphism is a graph isomorphism where Γ1 = Γ2.
The set of all graph automorphisms for a graph Γ forms a group under composition, which we will write Aut(Γ).
For a map M in a surface X, a map automorphism is a homeomorphism f : X →X such that f(V (M)) = V (M)
and the induced action on V (M) is a graph automorphism.
Groups and colourings: Given a group G acting on a graph Γ, we say that G acts transitively if for all u, v ∈
V (Γ) there exists g ∈G so that gu = v. An orbit of a vertex v ∈V (Γ), is defined as the set Gv := {gx : g ∈G}.
In other words a group acts transitively if there is a single orbit of the vertices under the action of G. A group G

An
immediate consequence is that, if {u, v}, {x, y} ∈E(Γ) are in the same Aut(Γ) orbit, then the corresponding edge
curves in M, αu,v and αx,y are isometric. Inline with the terminology of [Bab97] we define the following;
Definition 2.1. Suppose a graph Γ embeds into a manifold X, with embedded map M ⊂X. X is said to be
plane with respect to M if every cycle in M partitions X in two parts. An embedding M in X of a graph Γ is a
plane embedding if the manifold X is plane with respect to M.
Both E2 and H2 can be considered as plane manifolds, and the embedding inferred by Theorem 1.3 a plane
embedding. Recall in a topological space X, a family of subsets {Si}i is said to be a locally finite collection if for
any compact set K ⊂X, K has non-empty intersection with at most finitely many Si. For a map M on a closed
surface X, we say that V (M) is a locally finite collection of vertices if the family of singleton subsets of V (M) is
a locally finite collection.
Lemma 2.2. Let Γ be a locally-finite, 3-connected, quasi-transitive planar graph with 1-end that embeds as a map
M on a natural geometry X (X = E2 or H2) under Theorem 1.3. Then V (M) is a locally finite collection of
vertices.
Proof. By the construction of Babai’s embedding it follows that M contains no faces with infinitely many sides
[Bab97, Lemma 2.2].
Moreover since M is quasi-transitive and Aut(M) acts by isometries of X, the set of
distances between adjacent vertices E := {ρX (u, v) : {u, v} ∈E(M)} is finite, and the set of lengths of the edge
curves αu,v is also finite. Hence we may conclude all faces of M have compact closure. For a simple closed loop
C : [0, 1] →X, let C ⊂X be the induced compact set such that ∂C = C. Observe that in X, any compact set K

for any simple closed loop C : [0, 1] →X, the induced compact set C contains at most finitely many vertices of
M.
First suppose that C coincides with a cycle in M. Then as M has 1-end the induced subgraph on V (M) −V (C)
consists of two connected components Q1, Q2 such that V (Q1) is finite and V (Q2) is infinite. Since C is compact,
it has finite area, so that C can contain only a finite natural number of faces of M. As the boundary of each face
consists of finitely many vertices, we have that
V (M) ∩C = V (C) ∪V (Q1) < ∞.
Let C now be an arbitrary loop in X, and suppose that we have at least one vertex x ∈V (M) ∩C. Since M has
1-end, and E is finite, it follows that for any given vertex x, and some r > 0, we can find a cycle C′ in M such
that C′ contains the open ball B(x, r). Then we may choose r sufficiently large so that C ⊂B(x, r) ⊂C′, and

Graphs that embed in the Euclidean plane
We now consider only the case where X = E2. Discrete groups of isometries of the Euclidean plane are well studied
and classified. A discrete group of isometries such that there exist two linearly independent translations is called
a wallpaper group, of which it is known there are 17 up to isomorphism. A discrete group of isometries with a
single translation up to linear dependence is known as a Frieze group, of which there are 7 up to isomorphism
(see [CBGS08] for a full exposition on these groups).
For a discrete group of isometries G acting on a metric space X, a fundamental domain F ⊂X is an open
connected set so that;
(i) S
g∈G g ¯F = X, and
(ii) for G ∋g ̸= 1, F ∩gF = ∅.
The set ∂F = ¯F −F is called the boundary of F. In addition, it is well known that for any point x ∈X, the
intersection ∂F ∩Gx is empty or finite.
A Euclidean isometry t ∈Isom(E2) that acts without fixed points is called a translation, and is completely
determined by its direction and translation length. A group of isometries T ≤Isom(E2) that, with the exception
of the identity, consists entirely of translations is called a translation group. It is a fact of linear algebra that any
translation group acting on the Euclidean plane can have at most two linearly independent generating translations.
Let T = ⟨t1, t2⟩be a two-dimensional translation group generated by linearly independent translations t1 and t2.
Fix some x ∈E2, and define line segments si : [0, 1] →E2, such that si(0) = x and si(1) = ti(x) for i ∈{1, 2}.
Then each si is parallel to the action of the respective ti, and has length equal to the translation length of ti. It
then follows that the interior of any parallelogram defined by line segments s1, s2 is a fundamental domain for
T.

Graphs that embed in the hyperbolic plane
We proceed in the Poincar´e disc model D for the hyperbolic plane, where D := {z ∈C : |z| < 1}. Let D denote
the interior of the disc, ∂D denote the principal circle ‘at infinity’ and D = D ∪∂D its closure with the boundary
at infinity. We shall write ρD(·, ·) to denote the appropriate hyperbolic metric on D. A degree of familiarity with
elementary hyperbolic geometry is assumed here, for an introductory approach see [And05].
For a discrete group of isometries G ≤Isom(H2), and a point α ∈D that is not a fixed point for any element of
G, a Dirichlet domain D(α) is a fundamental domain such that D(α) = T
γ∈G{z ∈D : ρD(z, α) < ρD(z, γ(α))}.
A Dirichlet domain is a convex hyperbolic polygon whose boundary consists of geodesic arcs, these are referred to
as the sides of D(α), and the point where two incident sides meet as a vertex of D(α). In the case that a Dirichlet
domain is an unbounded set, we may consider its closure ‘at infinity’ D(α) in D. Each point where a side of D(α)
intersects ∂D is called an ideal vertex of D(α), and each section of the boundary ∂D contained in D(α) an ideal
side. For a point x ∈D and r > 0 let C(x, r) denote the hyperbolic circle with centre x and hyperbolic radius r.
For a connected subset A ⊆D let µ(A) denote its hyperbolic area.
An orientation-preserving discrete group of isometries G+ ≤Isom+(H2) is called a Fuchsian group. Every discrete
group of hyperbolic isometries G ≤Isom(H2) has an orientation-preserving subgroup G+ ≤G of index at most
2 (index 1 if G itself is orientation-preserving). It follows immediately that if a group G acts quasi-transitively
on a map M, then G+ also acts quasi-transitively on M (with at most twice as many orbits). Moreover, if F is
a fundamental domain for G and F + a fundamental domain for G+, so that [G : G+] = k ∈{1, 2}, we have that
µ(F +) = k · µ(F) ([Rat19], 6.7.3). Fuchsian groups are well studied, as such it will often be useful to consider the
orientation-preserving subgroup of a given group of isometries.

Graphs that are not 3-connected
We turn now to the case that Γ is a locally-finite, planar connected graph with 1-end that is not 3-connected. A
subdivision of a graph X is a graph obtained by adding finitely many vertices of degree 2 to each edge of X. A
topological subgraph Y of a graph X is a graph such that some subdivision of Y is a subgraph of X.
To analyse the Γ that are not 3-connected we will make use of known reduction technique first introduced by Mac
Lane [Lan37], and used in [Bab75], [Bab97], [HT72] among many others2. This technique produces a topological
subgraph of Γ that is 3-connected and well behaved under the inherited action of the automorphisms of Γ.

Though this technique is described in detail in the above mentioned papers we will outline it here for completeness
and ease of reference. First we introduce a number of notions; for a subset U ⊆V (Γ) the boundary ∂U of U is
the set of vertices not contained in U that are adjacent to a vertex in U. If Γ is κ-connected, and F ⊊V (Γ) a
proper subset such that F induces a connected subgraph, then it follows that |∂F| ≥κ. If |∂F| = κ then F is
called a fragment and the set C = ∂F a minimal cutset. An inclusion minimal fragment is called an atom. That
is, a fragment F is an atom if and only if there are no fragments F ′ such that F ′ ⊊F.
The reduction constructs a sequence of graphs Γ = Γ0, Γ1, . . . , Γm, so that Γi+1 is a topological subgraph of Γi
and;
1. each Γi is connected,
2. each Γi has 1-end,
3. V (Γi) ⊇V (Γi+1), and
4. Aut(Γi) acts quasi-transitively on V (Γi+1)
(see [Bab97, Theorem 4.1]). The reduction from Γi to Γi+1 goes as follows; We first assume that Γi is 2-connected.
For each atom A ⊂Γi remove the vertices and incident edges of V (A), and replace them by an edge between
the two vertices of ∂A. If Γi is 1-connected but not 2-connected, then delete all atoms to obtain Γi+1. Let
G := Aut(Γ) and Gi := Aut(Γi) for i ≤m. In addition to (iv) above, we may further conclude from the inherited
structure of each Γi+1 that, the induced action of Gi on V (Γi+1) is via graph automorphisms, implies there
exists some subgroup of Gi+1 that acts freely on V (Γi+1), and with equivalent action to that of Gi on V (Γi + 1).
Moreover, we note that given any subgroup H of G, we have the inherited action on V (Γ) and in turn this defines
an induced action by graph automorphisms on V (Γi) for all i ≤m.
It is known that locally-finite, planar, vertex transitive graphs with 1-end are 3-connected [Bab75, Theorem 1].
Hence this reduction sequence is guaranteed to conclude in a 3-connected graph Γm in m < ∞steps, as the
number of vertex orbits strictly reduces with each step. As Γm is a locally-finite, quasi-transitive, 3-connected
planar graph with 1-end there exists an embedded map Mm in X such that all automorphisms are induced by
isometries of X. As such Gm can be identified with a group of discrete isometries of X.

Discussion and open problems
Edge colourings and orientations. In addition to periodic vertex colourings [AEG+25] also discusses the
existence of periodic edge colourings and orientations. For brevity we do not include the precise definitions here,
and direct the interested reader to the article in question. Observe that if there exists a periodic vertex colouring
for a given graph, a periodic orientation can be defined by any total ordering on the set of colours. Together with
the work in [AEG+25] we may conclude:
Corollary 6.1. Let Γ be a locally-finite, quasi-transitive planar graph with finitely many ends. Then Γ permits
a periodic orientation.
For the existence of periodic edge colourings, we would like to follow a similar argument as [AEG+25, Corollary
5.5] (the analogous result for 2-ended graphs) and prove existence by passing to the line graph. However, since
the line graph of a planar graph is not necessarily planar there are restrictions on this approach. We have the
following two results of Sedl´aˇcek, and Greenwell and Hemminger that give two equivalent conditions for a graph
to have a planar line graph.
For two graphs Γ1, Γ2 we define the join Γ1 + Γ2 to be graph with vertex set
V (Γ1 + Γ2) = V (Γ1) ∪V (Γ2), and edge set E(Γ1 + Γ2) = E(Γ1) ∪E(Γ2) ∪{{u, v} : u ∈V (Γ1), v ∈V (Γ2)}.
Theorem 6.2 ([GH72]). A graph Γ has a line graph L(Γ) that is planar if and only if it has no subgraph
isomorphic to K3,3, K1,5, P4 + K1 or K2 + ¯K3.
Theorem 6.3 ([Sed64]). A graph Γ has a line graph L(Γ) that is planar if and only if the maximum degree of
any vertex is 4, and each vertex of degree 4 is a cut vertex.
Corollary 6.4. If Γ is a locally finite, quasi-transitive planar graph with 1-end satisfying the hypotheses of
Theorems 6.3 or 6.2, then there exists a periodic edge colouring of Γ.
Proof. Observe that the line graph L(Γ) is also locally finite, connected and quasi-transitive, and by our hypotheses
must be planar. In addition the rays of Γ and L(Γ) are in correspondence, and similarly given any pair of rays
r, r′ in Γ and their respective line graph rays L(r), L(r′) in L(Γ), the infinite set of disjoint rays between vertices

of r and vertices of r′ are in correspondence with an infinite set of disjoint rays between vertices of L(r) and
vertices of L(r′). Hence we may conclude that L(Γ) also has 1-end. Thus we conclude by applying Theorem 5.5

It is certainly conceivable an alternative construction of a periodic edge colouring exists that is not dependent on
the planarity of the line graph. For example by following similar techniques employed in this paper but focussing

Problem 6.1. For a locally-finite, quasi-transitive planar graph Γ with 1-end is it possible to construct a periodic
edge colouring without requiring the planarity of the line graph?
Constructibility of colourings.
Of course for any existence statement as we have proved above, and in
particular for graph colourings, one should comment on the constructibility of said colourings.
Suppose we
have a graph Γ and its automorphism group G. The first stage to examine is Babai’s automorphism respecting
embeddings; for a given locally-finite, quasi-transitive 3-connected graph with 1-end the construction laid out in
[Bab97] can be reduced to assigning an automorphism respecting rotation system to each of the vertices, and the
geometry is then determined by the curvature of the induced 2-dimensional cell complex. The process of finding a
maximal torsion free subgroup of a 2-dimensional discrete Euclidean or hyperbolic isometry group, is well studied,
and not hard to identify up to its signature3 and isomorphism class (see [EEK82],[Sch78]). In the Euclidean case,
given the maximum edge length it is not hard to derive a subgroup with sufficient minimum translation length in
order that the quotient map satisfies the hypotheses of Thomassen’s theorem. However we are not able to define
an explicit colouring from Thomassen’s theorem, the proof is a non-constructive proof-by-contradiction and relies
in part on the planar four colour map theorem.
In the hyperbolic case, we rely on the residual finiteness (or alternatively the stronger property of being locally
extended residually finite or LERF) of Fuchsian groups to infer the existence of normal (or not necessarily normal)
subgroups of sufficiently large minimum translation length. Whichever property we use, the desired subgroups
are found as finite index subgroups of surface groups (which are themselves finite index in Fuchsian groups).
Although constructing each subgroup Ti, not containing some ti, would be a long path to tread, and require
some translation of hyperbolic isometries to elements of the fundamental group of a quotient surface, the proofs
provided in [Hem72] and [Lop94] describe how to construct subgroups not containing a chosen element. However
the process to identify the full list of all the elements to remove, that is elements whose translation lengths are
too small, is not clear to the author.
Note that a similar approach can be applied to construct explicit periodic colourings in the Euclidean case, though
of course with the number of colours depending on the index of the translation subgroup. Finally, for graphs
that are not 3-connected, the reduction technique to a 3-connected topological subgraph is explicitly constructive,
with the algorithm provided as its definition in §5.
Reducing the number of colours in the hyperbolic case. The application of Thomassen’s Theorem 1.4 in
the Euclidean case is remarkably powerful here, providing a uniform bound on the chromatic number of all such
graphs. In the hyperbolic case, we are not so fortunate. By relying on residual finiteness to construct our desired
finite index subgroups it means that we have very little control over the size of the index, and consequently over
the number of colours (see Remark 4.1). For any integer B > 0 it is not hard to construct maps in the hyperbolic
plane with the property that any subgroup with sufficiently large minimum translation length has index at least
B. Then in contrast with the Euclidean case, for an arbitrarily large integer C > 0 there exist graphs that embed
into the hyperbolic plane such that the periodic colouring defined by the methods above uses at least C colours,
for arbitrarily large C. As such it is desirable to be able to apply Theorem 1.4 to those graphs that embed into
the hyperbolic plane. However constructing an embedding M and a subgroup H ≤Aut(M) so that the quotient
map M/H on the quotient surface H2/H has non-contractible cycles of sufficient size is unclear. In the Euclidean
case, although 214g+6 is large, whenever we take a torsion free wallpaper group T ≤Isom(E2) the quotient surface
is always a torus with genus g = 1. This means one can continue to take larger index translation subgroups,
and the genus of the quotient surface always stays constant. This fixes a minimum non-contractible cycle size to
