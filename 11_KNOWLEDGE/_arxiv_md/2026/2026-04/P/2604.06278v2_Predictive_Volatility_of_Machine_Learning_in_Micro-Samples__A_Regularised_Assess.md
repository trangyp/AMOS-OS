Abstract
Identifying the structural drivers of poverty in regional datasets is frequently
hindered by small sample sizes and high multidimensional collinearity, which can
result in unstable and misleading policy advice. This paper evaluates the provin-
cial causes of poverty in Indonesia by addressing these specific statistical hazards.
We employ a rigorous model-comparison framework designed for small samples
(n = 34) with high collinearity, comparing standard linear models with frequentist
penalisation, Bayesian shrinkage priors, an adjusted spatial intrinsic conditionally
autoregressive (ICAR) model, and complex machine learning ensembles. To ensure
a robust evaluation, we measure predictive performance using strict Leave-One-Out
Cross-Validation (LOOCV). The results demonstrate that algorithmic complexity is
inherently risky in regional datasets: simple linear shrinkage models (Ridge, Elastic
Net, LASSO) achieve the superior out-of-sample prediction, whereas complex en-
sembles like BART suffer from severe overfitting. Across all successful regularised
models, ICT skills consistently emerge as the most stable proxy for lower provincial
poverty. The primary contribution of this paper is demonstrating that, in data-
constrained regional analysis, parametrically regularised linear shrinkage provides a

Introduction
Indonesia is the world’s fourth most populous country and remains one of South-East
Asia’s most dynamic emerging economies. Nonetheless, its provincial development profile
is notably uneven. Recent data from 2024 indicates that poverty rates range from 4.53%
to 26.80%, whilst ICT skills vary from 29.82% to 92.36%. Whilst identifying the drivers of
these disparities is essential for regional equity, the pursuit of “place-based policy” is often
built on fragile empirical foundations. When sample sizes are small and predictors are
deeply intertwined, standard statistical tools can produce “false positives” that misdirect
public investment. These gaps matter not only for equity but for the practical design
of policy, reflecting long-standing structural challenges in the Indonesian developmental
trajectory (World Bank, 2023; United Nations, 2024; Suryahadi et al., 2012).
Despite the importance of these trends, much of the existing literature on Indonesian
development continues to rely on unpenalised regressions, often overlooking how collin-
earity and limited data points can distort results. Analysing these drivers presents three
fundamental methodological difficulties that motivate this study. First, the sample is
constrained: with only 34 provinces, parameter uncertainty is substantial, and complex
models risk severe out-of-sample overfitting (Hastie et al., 2009; Varma and Simon, 2006).
Second, the predictors are highly correlated; provinces with stronger ICT skills also tend
to possess better sanitation and higher life expectancy. This multicollinearity threatens
the stability of coefficient estimates and can lead to erroneous sign inversions, making it
difficult for policymakers to discern which interventions actually reduce poverty (Belsley
et al., 2005; Greene, 2018). Third, raw poverty rates are spatially clustered, meaning
geographic proximity often serves as a proxy for unobserved regional dependencies (Anselin,
1988; Cliff and Ord, 1981). These three issues, i.e., small samples, collinearity, and spatial
bias, create a “perfect storm” for statistical unreliability.
We therefore adopt a structured model-comparison strategy to determine which frame-
work offers the most reliable guidance for regional analysis. Rather than the indiscriminate
application of complex algorithms to see what fits, a process that often leads to biased
inferences in micro-samples (Wolpert, 1996), we systematically evaluate a portfolio of
conceptual approaches: linear baselines, frequentist and Bayesian regularisation (shrink-
age), spatial models, and tree-based ensembles (BART, Random Forest, XGBoost). Our

objective is to establish a rigorous predictive framework for analysing small-sample regional
data where the costs of model-misidentification are high.
Three methodological findings emerge. First, simple linear shrinkage proves to be the
optimal predictive framework. Under strict Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV),
Ridge regression, Elastic Net, and LASSO achieve the highest out-of-sample accuracy.
Whilst models such as Random Forest and XGBoost offer moderate improvements over
unpenalised OLS, highly unconstrained algorithms such as BART suffer from severe
overfitting. Second, within these optimal shrinkage frameworks, ICT skills serve as the
most stable negative structural proxy for provincial poverty, remaining robust even under
Horseshoe shrinkage and spike-and-slab selection. Third, although raw poverty rates
show spatial clustering, robust Monte Carlo residual diagnostics do not indicate remaining
spatial dependence; geographic proximity acts largely as a proxy for shared socioeconomic
covariates rather than a distinct spillover effect.

2
Theoretical Framework and Literature Review
2.1
Poverty as a multidimensional and spatially embedded phe-
nomenon
Modern poverty analysis has moved beyond a purely income-based notion of deprivation. In
the capability framework of Sen (1999), poverty reflects shortfalls in substantive freedoms,
including education, health, and social participation. Alkire and Foster (2011) formalise
this multidimensional view by aggregating deprivations across domains. In Indonesia,
these domains are unequally distributed across space, with marked provincial disparities
in schooling, health access, infrastructure, and digital capability.
The spatial dimension remains conceptually important. Provinces do not develop
in isolation. Geographic distance, connectivity, and regional spillovers may matter for
market access, service delivery, and the diffusion of technology (Calderón and Servén, 2010;
Banerjee et al., 2014). Yet a statistical distinction is needed between raw spatial clustering
and residual spatial dependence after observed provincial characteristics are controlled for.
The former may be strong while the latter is statistically invisible.

2.2
Human capital, health, inequality, infrastructure, and digital
capability
Human capital theory predicts that schooling raises productivity and earnings capacity
(Becker, 1964; Psacharopoulos and Patrinos, 2018). Health matters both directly, through
productivity, and indirectly, through vulnerability to shocks and treatment costs (Wagstaff,
2002; Sparrow et al., 2013). Inequality may weaken the poverty-reducing effect of growth
when gains are unevenly shared (Bourguignon, 2004; Yusuf et al., 2014). Infrastructure,
including sanitation, water, and electricity, affects health, time allocation, and labour-
market participation (Calderón and Servén, 2010).
Digital capability has become increasingly central. ICT skills can improve job search,
access to information, take-up of public services, and integration into formal markets (Aker
and Mbiti, 2010; Monde, 2021). But it is vital to recognise that the relationship between
digital infrastructure and poverty is highly endogenous.
Wealthier, more developed
provinces naturally attract and sustain better digital infrastructure and foster more
digitally literate populations. In a country as geographically fragmented as Indonesia,
ICT acts primarily as an accurate composite proxy for a wider, unobserved bundle of
provincial advantages, including institutional quality and economic integration, rather
than functioning as an isolated causal lever.
2.3
Why a model portfolio is needed
The provincial poverty problem is not well suited to a single-model strategy. OLS is fragile
under collinearity and small samples. Penalised estimators such as ridge, LASSO, and
elastic net help to stabilise coefficient estimates (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970; Tibshirani,
1996; Zou and Hastie, 2005). Bayesian shrinkage priors allow regularisation while retaining
full posterior uncertainty (Gelman et al., 2013; Carvalho et al., 2010). Spike-and-slab
selection provides posterior inclusion probabilities that are easy to interpret (Mitchell and
Beauchamp, 1988; George and McCulloch, 1993). Finally, nonparametric methods such
as BART and tree ensembles are often deployed to detect threshold effects (Chipman et
al., 2010; Breiman, 2001; Chen and Guestrin, 2016), though their utility in micro-samples
requires strict out-of-sample validation.
The advantage of a model portfolio is not that every specification must perfectly align,
but rather that it systematically filters out specification-sensitive noise while explicitly
controlling for overfitting.
2.4
Statistical regularisation and the bias-variance tradeoff
The primary challenge in provincial-level analysis is the unfavourable ratio of predictors to
observations (p/n). In such settings, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimators are often
4


## Page 5


unbiased but suffer from high variance, leading to poor out-of-sample generalisation. This
instability is exacerbated by multicollinearity, where the inclusion of highly correlated
predictors, such as ICT skills and sanitation, inflates the variance of the coefficient estimates
(Belsley et al., 2005).
To mitigate these risks, the statistical literature has increasingly moved towards
regularisation. Frequentist penalisation techniques, such as Ridge regression (Hoerl and
Kennard, 1970) and LASSO (Tibshirani, 1996), introduce a small amount of bias into
the model in exchange for a significant reduction in variance. Whilst Ridge regression
shrinks coefficients towards zero to handle collinearity, LASSO performs simultaneous
variable selection by forcing some coefficients to exactly zero. The Elastic Net (Zou and
Hastie, 2005) provides a compromise between these two, which is particularly useful when
predictors are grouped in high-correlation blocks, as observed in our Indonesian dataset.
2.5
Bayesian shrinkage and the search for stable proxies
An alternative to frequentist penalisation is the use of Bayesian shrinkage priors. Unlike
hard-thresholding methods, Bayesian frameworks allow for the incorporation of prior
information regarding the sparsity of the data (Gelman et al., 2013). The Horseshoe
prior (Carvalho et al., 2010) and Spike-and-Slab selection (Mitchell and Beauchamp,
1988; George and McCulloch, 1993) have become the gold standard for high-dimensional
inference. These methods are designed to identify “signals” (stable structural drivers)
amongst “noise” (spurious correlations), providing posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs)
that offer a more nuanced measure of variable importance than traditional p-values.
2.6
The limitations of algorithmic complexity in micro-samples
In recent years, tree-based machine learning ensembles such as Random Forest (Breiman,
2001), XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016), and Bayesian Additive Regression Trees
(BART) (Chipman et al., 2010), have gained popularity due to their ability to capture
non-linearities and complex interactions without explicit functional specifications. However,
their performance in “micro-samples” (n < 50) remains a subject of debate.
Whilst these algorithms are powerful for large-scale informatics, they are prone to
“learning the noise” in small cross-sectional data. Consequently, their inclusion in our
model portfolio is not to seek a superior fit, but to test the limits of algorithmic complexity.
By comparing these “black-box” models against regularised linear frameworks using strict
Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV), we can determine whether the underlying
structure of provincial poverty is truly non-linear, or if complexity simply introduces
predictive volatility (Wolpert, 1996; Vehtari et al., 2017).

2.7
Spatial dependency: process vs. proxy
Finally, the inclusion of spatial modelling, specifically the BYM2 ICAR specification
(Riebler et al., 2016), addresses the potential for geographic spillover. A common pitfall in
regional science is assuming that spatial clustering in the outcome variable necessitates a
spatial model. However, if the spatial pattern is entirely explained by the distribution of
observed covariates, the overhead of a spatial random effect becomes redundant (Anselin,
1995). Our blueprint explicitly tests this by comparing the predictive accuracy of spatial
versus non-spatial models to ensure the most parsimonious interpretation of the Indonesian
poverty landscape.

Spatial autocorrelation and the case for parsimony
Raw poverty rates exhibit significant positive spatial autocorrelation across the Indonesian
archipelago. As illustrated in the choropleth visualisation (Figure 2), there is a pronounced
longitudinal poverty gradient, with higher intensities concentrated in the eastern provinces,
specifically within the Nusa Tenggara and Papua clusters.
Given the small sample size (n = 34), we eschew standard asymptotic assumptions
and calculate empirical p-values for the statistic originally proposed by Moran (1950) via
999 Monte Carlo permutations. The resulting empirical Moran’s I is 0.358 (p = 0.009),
providing formal evidence of the geographic clustering observed in the map.
Nonetheless, the conditional picture is markedly different. Once the nine socioeconomic
covariates are included in an OLS framework, the residual Moran’s I falls to −0.058
(p = 0.566). This sharp distinction between raw clustering and residual independence
strongly implies that geographic proximity acts primarily as a surrogate for underlying
socioeconomic fundamentals, such as shared infrastructure or educational levels, rather than
representing a distinct spatial spillover process (Anselin, 1995). Consequently, whilst we
include the BYM2 spatial model (M11) in our portfolio for completeness, this exploratory
evidence suggests that the spatial random effect may be statistically redundant.

Statistical Models and Methods
This section sets out the modelling strategy adopted in the study. A quantitative model-
comparison framework was employed because the empirical problem combines three well-
known statistical hazards: a very small sample size (n = 34), substantial multicollinearity
among the socioeconomic predictors, and possible spatial patterning in the outcome. In
order to evaluate which modelling framework provides the most reliable guidance in such a
setting, we estimated and compared a portfolio of linear, penalised, Bayesian, spatial, and
non-linear machine-learning models under a common out-of-sample validation protocol.
More specifically, the analysis was designed to answer two related questions. First,
which modelling framework yields the most stable and accurate prediction of provincial
poverty when the number of observational units is small relative to the complexity of the
predictor set? Second, which covariates remain most robustly associated with poverty
after accounting for regularisation, posterior uncertainty, and possible spatial dependence?
For the purposes of this study, predictive reliability rather than in-sample fit was treated
as the principal criterion for model assessment.

were evaluated under the same out-of-sample logic as the non-Bayesian models.

changes the neighbourhood graph itself, so perfect equivalence between exact LOOCV and
the original spatial topology cannot be maintained. For that reason, the spatial model’s

slightly greater caution than the corresponding comparisons for the non-spatial models.

4.13
Inference and model interpretation
The inferential summaries differ by model class. For OLS and the penalised frequentist
models, the main outputs are the estimated coefficients after rescaling to the original units.
For the Bayesian Gaussian and shrinkage models, posterior means, posterior standard
deviations, 95% credible intervals, and posterior probabilities of direction were examined.
For the spike-and-slab model, attention centres on posterior inclusion probabilities. For
Beta regression, inference concerns the sign and magnitude of the linear predictor on the
logit scale together with the implied predictive behaviour on the original poverty scale.
For tree-based and Gaussian-process models, the emphasis is not on single structural
coefficients but on predictive performance and variable-importance or feature-attribution
summaries.
Because the study is based on a single cross-section, all coefficients and predictive
associations are interpreted as conditional correlational relationships rather than causal
effects. Put differently, a negative coefficient for ICT skills indicates that provinces with
higher ICT skills tend, on average and conditional on the remaining variables in the
model, to exhibit lower poverty rates. It does not by itself establish that exogenous
improvement in ICT skills would mechanically generate the same numerical reduction in
poverty. This distinction is especially important in a setting where development indicators
are structurally bundled and may proxy broader underlying advantages.
4.14
Model diagnostics and robustness checks
Several diagnostics were used to ensure that the methodology was not driven by a
single specification choice. First, multicollinearity diagnostics were used to document
the extent of predictor dependence in the raw design matrix. Second, raw and residual
Moran’s I statistics were used to distinguish descriptive spatial clustering from unexplained
residual dependence.
Third, Bayesian convergence diagnostics were inspected, with
particular attention to rank-normalised ˆR, effective sample size, and the absence of
divergent transitions in the more demanding shrinkage models. Fourth, posterior predictive
checks were used for selected Bayesian specifications to assess whether the fitted models
reproduced the observed poverty distribution adequately. Fifth, prior sensitivity was
examined for the weakly informative Gaussian-prior Bayesian model in order to determine
whether the main substantive conclusion depended on a particular prior scale.
Taken together, these diagnostics were intended to strengthen the credibility of the
empirical comparison. The aim was not simply to fit many models, but to assess, as
rigorously as possible, which conclusions remain stable after confronting the data with
alternative regularisation structures, distributional assumptions, and validation schemes.

4.15
Summary of methodological logic
The methodological logic of the paper may therefore be summarised as follows. The
analysis begins with a common provincial dataset and a common set of nine theoretically
relevant predictors. It then compares a sequence of model classes ranging from unpenalised
linear regression to strong shrinkage, bounded-response modelling, spatial random effects,
and flexible non-linear learners. All models are evaluated under a common leave-one-out
framework, and all key modelling terms are defined so that the substantive interpretation
of the results remains transparent. This design allows the study to distinguish between
effects that appear only under fragile specifications and effects that remain visible even
after aggressive regularisation and strict out-of-sample scrutiny.

6
Discussion
Across the full model portfolio, one substantive conclusion is the most stable: ICT skills
emerge as the strongest negative structural correlate of provincial poverty in Indonesia.
This result is broadly consistent with the wider development literature, which increasingly
argues that digital capability can support poverty reduction by improving access to
information, labour-market matching, market participation, education, and public services
(Aker and Mbiti, 2010; Galperin and Viecens, 2017; Lechman and Popowska, 2022). It also
complements the Indonesian evidence in Monde (2021), who shows that digital disadvantage
and poverty are spatially intertwined. However, our results add an important qualification
to that literature. In large cross-country or panel settings, ICT is often interpreted as
one channel through which growth and inclusion improve. In our much harsher provincial
small-n setting, ICT survives not merely as one correlate among many, but as the only
predictor that remains consistently stable after aggressive regularisation. This suggests that
ICT skills should be interpreted less as a narrowly isolated treatment variable and more
as a summary proxy for a wider bundle of provincial advantages, including institutional
quality, educational readiness, service accessibility, and economic integration.
This distinction matters because several development indicators that are theoretically
33


## Page 34


important in the literature, such as schooling, sanitation, electricity, and life expectancy,
do not display the same degree of stand-alone statistical stability once entered jointly.
That does not imply that these variables are unimportant in substantive reality. Rather,
it indicates that in provincial data they move together so strongly that naive regression
coefficients cannot be trusted to cleanly separate their marginal contributions. The sign
reversals between OLS and shrinkage models illustrate exactly the instability warned
about in the collinearity literature (Belsley et al., 2005; Zou and Hastie, 2005; Carvalho et
al., 2010). In this sense, our findings refine rather than contradict the broader poverty
literature: the standard developmental fundamentals remain relevant, but their individual
coefficients become fragile in small, highly collinear regional samples. The methodological
implication is that empirical poverty studies at the provincial level should be far more
cautious about ranking determinants from unpenalised regression output alone.
A second contribution concerns the interpretation of spatial structure. Many studies
of regional poverty, including Indonesian applications, begin from the observation that
poverty is geographically clustered and then proceed directly to spatial specifications.
Our results support only the first half of that logic. Raw provincial poverty is indeed
spatially autocorrelated, but once the socioeconomic covariates are included, the residual
dependence vanishes under exact Monte Carlo testing. This pattern is consistent with the
distinction in spatial econometrics between spatial process and spatial proxy: geography
may matter because neighbouring places share observable fundamentals, without necessarily
implying an additional residual spillover process after those fundamentals are controlled
for (Anselin, 1995). Relative to empirical studies that interpret clustering itself as evidence
of a remaining spatial mechanism, our findings suggest a more parsimonious conclusion
for the Indonesian provincial case: at this level of aggregation, much of the apparent
geography of poverty is compositional. This does not rule out genuine spillovers at finer
administrative levels, but it does show that province-level spatial random effects are not
automatically warranted.
The third and most important methodological lesson concerns algorithmic complexity
in micro-samples.
The machine-learning validation literature has repeatedly warned
that when samples are limited, predictive performance estimates become volatile and
easily overstated, especially when model tuning and evaluation are not cleanly separated
(Vabalas et al., 2019; Varoquaux, 2018; Neunhoeffer and Sternberg, 2019). Our empirical
results are highly consistent with that warning. Under strict LOOCV, BART performs
extremely poorly out-of-sample, and Gaussian Process regression also fails to generalise
well. Random Forest and XGBoost improve modestly on OLS, but neither surpasses the
best linear shrinkage models. Hence, the present application does not support the common
presumption that more flexible algorithms necessarily uncover deeper structure in social
data. On the contrary, the evidence indicates that in an n = 34 regional cross-section,
additional flexibility primarily increases variance.
34


## Page 35


This point also affects how the tree-based variable-importance profiles should be
interpreted.
The comparatively strong Random Forest importance assigned to ICT
skills is substantively plausible, but it must be treated cautiously because the random-
forest literature shows that standard importance measures are biased in the presence of
correlated predictors (Strobl et al., 2007, 2008; Gregorutti et al., 2017). In other words,
the machine-learning outputs do not overturn the shrinkage-based conclusion; if anything,
they reinforce the need for caution when covariates come in tightly correlated blocks.
The relative flatness of the BART importance profile is therefore also informative: it
suggests that once regularisation is imposed through many weak learners, the model diffuses
importance across a bundle of related provincial characteristics rather than identifying
many sharply distinct non-linear thresholds.
Taken together, these comparisons place our findings in a more precise position within
the literature. Substantively, the results align with prior work that identifies digital
capability as a relevant poverty correlate, but they sharpen the claim by showing that
ICT is the most stable predictor once small-sample uncertainty and multicollinearity
are properly confronted. Methodologically, the results support the growing argument
that careful regularisation and strict out-of-sample validation are more informative than
indiscriminate algorithmic complexity in small regional datasets. For applied regional
science, this is a consequential point: when the number of observational units is fixed and
low, better inference may come from disciplined shrinkage and conservative validation
rather than from increasingly flexible black-box architectures.
Because the analysis remains cross-sectional, these findings should not be interpreted
causally. Reverse causality and omitted-variable bias remain serious concerns: richer
provinces are better positioned to invest in digital capability, infrastructure, and service
provision, whilst those same underlying conditions also reduce poverty. We therefore do
not claim that increasing ICT skills alone would mechanically produce the full poverty
reductions implied by the fitted coefficients. Rather, the policy interpretation is that
provinces with stronger ICT capability also tend to possess a broader development package
associated with lower poverty. A realistic policy implication is thus not “ICT alone”, but
integrated regional upgrading in which digital inclusion is pursued alongside education,
health access, and basic service quality.
Several limitations remain. First, with only 34 provinces and nine predictors, uncer-
tainty is inevitably large, even after shrinkage. Second, province-level analysis may smooth
over important within-province heterogeneity and more localised spatial spillovers. Third,
whilst LOOCV is appropriate here, the small-sample validation literature reminds us that
all error estimates in limited datasets retain non-trivial uncertainty (Vabalas et al., 2019;
Varoquaux, 2018). These limitations do not overturn the main conclusion, but they do
reinforce the need for modest inferential claims and for future work using longer panels or
finer geographic units.

7
Conclusion
This paper has presented a comparative analysis of provincial poverty in Indonesia using
a model portfolio explicitly designed for a difficult empirical setting: small sample size,
strong predictor collinearity, and potential spatial dependence. Evaluated under strict
Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation, three conclusions emerge.
First, ICT skills are the most robust provincial correlate of lower poverty. This result
is consistent with a growing literature that links digital capability to poverty reduction
through information access, labour-market inclusion, education, and service connectivity
(Aker and Mbiti, 2010; Galperin and Viecens, 2017; Lechman and Popowska, 2022).
However, our contribution is more specific. In contrast to broader macro-level studies, we
show that in a high-collinearity provincial setting ICT is the only predictor that remains
clearly stable across frequentist shrinkage, Bayesian shrinkage, and spike-and-slab selection.
The most credible interpretation is therefore that ICT skills capture a wider package of
structural development advantages rather than a clean, isolated causal mechanism.
Second, spatial modelling adds little once provincial socioeconomic composition is
controlled for. Although raw poverty rates are geographically clustered, residual spatial
dependence disappears after covariate adjustment, and the BYM2 ICAR specification
does not improve predictive performance. For this Indonesian provincial application, the
evidence therefore favours a parsimonious reading in which geography primarily proxies
observed development disparities rather than an additional residual spillover process.
Third, and most importantly for applied statistical practice, highly flexible machine-
learning models are not automatically superior in small regional datasets. Our results
closely match the recent validation literature showing that limited samples make predictive
assessment unstable and leave complex models especially vulnerable to overfitting (Vabalas
et al., 2019; Varoquaux, 2018; Neunhoeffer and Sternberg, 2019). In this application,
predictive dominance belongs to simple linear shrinkage estimators—Ridge, Elastic Net,
and LASSO—not to BART or other unconstrained non-linear architectures. Thus, the
main methodological contribution of the paper is to demonstrate that, in small-n regional
poverty analysis, disciplined regularisation and strict cross-validation provide a more
reliable inferential foundation than either naive OLS or complexity-heavy machine learning.
More broadly, the paper offers a practical blueprint for empirical work in regional
science when the data environment is constrained: regularise aggressively when predictors
are structurally bundled, validate out-of-sample with procedures that penalise overfitting,
distinguish raw spatial clustering from residual spatial dependence, and frame substantive
claims conservatively when only cross-sectional evidence is available. For Indonesian
poverty policy, the message is correspondingly cautious but actionable: digital capability
appears central, yet it should be advanced as part of a broader place-based development
strategy rather than as a stand-alone intervention.
