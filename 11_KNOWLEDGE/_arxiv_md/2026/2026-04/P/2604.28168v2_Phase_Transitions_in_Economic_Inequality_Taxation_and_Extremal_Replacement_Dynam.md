Abstract
We present a minimal agent-based model of interacting agents characterized by their wealth to
study taxation and inequality in a non-conservative economy. Wealth evolves through an extremal
stochastic replacement process in which the poorest agent has its wealth replaced by a new random
value, financed through a collective taxation mechanism. We explore taxation regimes ranging from
regressive to progressive schemes and tune the overall redistribution strength. Under regressive
taxation, the system self-organizes into two distinct stationary phases when changing the total
tax collected: a non-ergodic, high-inequality regime characterized by wealth condensation in a
subset of agents that permanently escape replacement, and a more homogeneous ergodic phase
in which all agents participate in the dynamics.
Increasing taxes drives an abrupt transition
between these phases.
The transition is discontinuous and exhibits hysteresis and bistability,
consistently detected through the Gini index, the Top 1% wealth share, the entropy, and the Binder
cumulant. In contrast, neutral and progressive taxation suppress persistent wealth concentration,
preventing the emergence of strongly unequal states and eliminating hysteretic behavior. These
results show that minimal stochastic redistribution mechanisms alone can produce discontinuous
transitions, metastability, and non-ergodicity, demonstrating that taxation structure can determine
the emergence and stability of macroscopic inequality.

I.
INTRODUCTION
Economic inequality has long been studied not only in economics and sociology, but
also in political philosophy. In his theory of justice, John Rawls proposed that social and
economic inequalities may be acceptable only if they benefit the least advantaged members
of society, a principle known as the difference principle [1]. This perspective shifts attention
from aggregate wealth to extremal outcomes, suggesting that the dynamics of inequality
may be driven by agents at the lower tail of the distribution.
From a modeling perspective, this focus on the least advantaged agent has been naturally
incorporated in agent-based models inspired by extremal dynamics. The model we present
here draws inspiration from the work by Pianegonda et al. [2], itself motivated by the
extremal dynamics model of extinction of species by Bak and Sneppen [3]. In the original
framework, each agent is assigned an initial wealth value drawn uniformly from the interval
[0,1), with the focus on the poorest agent, namely the one holding the minimum wealth. This
agent attempts to improve its economic standing through a stochastic action: its wealth is
reassigned by drawing a new value uniformly from [0,1). This update can also be interpreted
as the poorest agent defaulting, being removed from the economic system, and replaced by
a new agent with random wealth.
Unlike the Bak–Sneppen model, the Pianegonda et al. model is conservative, hence its
name: the Conservative Exchanges Market Model (CEMM). Within the CEMM, the change
in wealth of the poorest agent is exactly compensated by redistributing the opposite amount
in equal fractions among a subset of other agents. This group may range from just one or
two agents to the entire population. Therefore, any improvement in the economic condition
of the poorest agent necessarily comes at the expense of others, who collectively lose the
same total amount of wealth. This replacement mechanism captures individual economic
mobility under uncertainty while preserving computational simplicity.
Variants of this model, differing in the number of agents involved in exchanges, or in the
network structure [4], display consistent features: nearly all agents reside above a poverty
threshold (whose exact value depends on model details), with those below it most likely
being the minimum-wealth agents, whose state will change in subsequent simulation steps.
Additionally, the wealth distribution rises from zero at the poverty line, peaks sharply, and
then decays exponentially, with an exponent proportional to the square of the wealth.

In the present work, we invert the logic of the inspirational model. Rather than modifying
the poorest agent’s wealth and redistributing the difference among neighbors, we impose a
tax on all agents in this hypothetical society. The collected revenue is not distributed for
the benefit of a subset of agents, as commonly implemented in other models [5–8], but is
instead directed exclusively to the poorest agent through a replacement mechanism. Fur-
thermore, the agent’s previous wealth is discarded upon replacement, making the dynamics
non-conservative: except when the poorest agent is initially in debt, total wealth decreases
after each step. As shown below, this latter case is rare, so the dominant effect is a systematic
loss of total wealth, which we renormalize to avoid numerical underflow.
These seemingly small modifications, namely collecting taxes collectively and direct-
ing them exclusively to the poorest agent, together with abandoning wealth conservation,
generate qualitatively new collective behavior. This sets our model apart from the con-
servative CEMM and from most kinetic or asset-exchange models studied in statistical
physics. In standard exchange models with pairwise interactions, such as those introduced in
Refs. [9, 10], wealth is redistributed through local transactions that conserve total resources
and typically lead to stationary distributions whose inequality varies smoothly with model
parameters. Extensions incorporating saving propensity heterogeneity [11], multiplicative
growth [12], risk strategies [13], or network structure [14] likewise preserve this continu-
ous dependence. More recent formulations, including those with taxation, redistribution, or
stochastic growth mechanisms [15–19], introduce additional economic realism while retaining
the same qualitative feature: macroscopic inequality indicators change gradually as control
parameters are tuned. By contrast, the non-conservative extremal dynamics considered here
produces abrupt transitions, hysteresis, and bistability under regressive taxation. We show
that the structure of taxation alone can trigger macroscopic reorganizations of wealth anal-
ogous to first-order transitions in physical systems. In the following, we characterize this
transition using multiple macroscopic observables.
The outline of the article is as follows: In the next section, we describe the model in
detail and define the indicators used to characterize inequality; we then present the numer-
ical results, discussing the emergence of distinct dynamical regimes and phase transitions
under different taxation schemes. Finally, we conclude with a summary of the results and a
discussion of their broader implications.

A.
Characterization of the model dynamics
To explore the dynamical behavior of the system, we first analyze in Fig. 1 the temporal
evolution of the Gini index for different combinations of the control parameters, recalling
that γ is related to the new wealth of the poorest agent and the total tax collected, and α sets
the structure of such a tax. In all cases, the system reaches a stationary state, as defined by
the Gini-based criterion introduced in the previous section, after a transient whose duration
depends on the parameters. The approach to stationarity is typically faster for progressive
taxes (α > 1) and moderate values of γ, while for regressive taxation (α < 1) the relaxation
becomes slower due to the emergence of large fluctuations in the wealth distribution. All
curves eventually satisfy the stationary condition, yielding asymptotic Gini values that span

After the system reaches a steady state, the dependence of inequality on the model
parameters can be examined through the Gini index. Figure 2 shows the stationary Gini
index as a function of γ for different values of α. The curves reveal three distinct regimes
associated with the taxation regime.
For regressive taxation (α < 1), the Gini index exhibits a discontinuous jump from a high-
inequality state to a much lower value once γ exceeds a parameter-dependent threshold. This
abrupt change indicates a transition between distinct stationary regimes, analyzed in detail
in the next section. The large error bars observed in this region for α < 1 suggest the
presence of multiple dynamically accessible stationary states.
For progressive taxation (α > 1), the Gini index remains close to zero up to γ ≈1. In this
regime of progressive taxation and moderate redistribution strength, the system reaches a
state of almost perfect equality, in which all agents hold approximately the same wealth. For
γ > 1, the Gini index displays a slow but monotonic increase with γ, remaining at relatively
low inequality levels. The smooth evolution observed for α ≥1 contrasts sharply with the
abrupt transition found for α < 1, highlighting the stabilizing effect of progressive taxation
on the system’s wealth dynamics.
We have also verified that the stationary inequality
indicators are essentially independent of system size for N ranging from 500 to 104 (see
Appendix A).
The neutral case (α = 1) displays an intermediate behavior between the progressive and
regressive regimes. The Gini index decreases with increasing γ up to γ ≈1, where it reaches
a minimum, and then increases smoothly for larger γ, without exhibiting a discontinuous
transition. Interestingly, the minimum occurs close to γ = 1, where the maximum attainable
wealth after replacement becomes comparable to the mean wealth of the system, a feature
that will become relevant later in our analysis.

This behavior can be understood by examining the replacement mechanism around γ ≃1.
Since the poorest agent receives a new wealth drawn from the interval [0, 2T) = [0, γ⟨w⟩), the
parameter γ determines whether the replacement can lift that agent above the bulk of the
distribution. For γ ≤1, the maximum attainable wealth after replacement does not exceed
the mean wealth ⟨w⟩. In the progressive regime, the distribution is tightly clustered around
⟨w⟩, forming a sharp bulk (see Fig. 5 for γ = 0.8). Only a very small fraction of agents,
sometimes even a single one, have wealth w < ⟨w⟩and are therefore repeatedly selected as
the poorest. Because the replacement cannot promote them into the bulk, the identity of the
poorest agent remains essentially confined to this small subset. For γ > 1, the replacement
interval extends beyond ⟨w⟩, allowing the poorest agent to re-enter or even overshoot the
bulk of the distribution. The identity of the poorest agent then changes over time, and the
replacement mechanism propagates across the entire population. In this regime the system
becomes ergodic, the wealth distribution acquires a finite and increasing width (as shown
in Fig. 6), and inequality grows as a direct consequence of the broadened distribution. This
explains the increase of the Gini index for γ ≥1.

redistribution dominates the dynamics and the system converges to a unique stationary state
characterized by low wealth concentration, high entropy, and Binder cumulant values close
to zero, consistent with weak, approximately Gaussian fluctuations.

γ branch.
This indicates that, although both branches may sustain a wealthy elite, its
quantitative dominance differs markedly: in the decreasing branch, wealth is concentrated
in fewer and more extreme individuals, whereas in the increasing branch the upper tail
remains comparatively less pronounced. However, the Top 1% wealth share is intrinsically
insensitive to changes occurring in the rest of the population. Since this observable integrates
only over the very richest agents, it does not capture how wealth is distributed among
the remaining 99% of agents. As a consequence, configurations that differ substantially in

The enhanced sensitivity of the entropy at small values of γ can be traced back to its
dependence on the effective number of agents contributing to the wealth distribution. Even
when the Gini index or the Top 1% wealth share take similar values along the forward and
backward branches, the redistribution of wealth across the bulk of the population leads to
significant variations in H. Entropy therefore distinguishes between stationary configura-
tions that appear similar at the level of average inequality but differ in their overall structural
organization.
The Binder cumulant provides complementary information by probing higher-order mo-
ments of the distribution. In the hysteretic regime, its value depends strongly on the dy-
namical branch followed. Configurations dominated by rare, extremely wealthy agents dis-
play strongly negative values of UL. The separation between the increasing and decreasing
branches indicates that these higher-order structural differences persist even when average
inequality measures take comparable values, confirming the existence of distinct dynamically
stable microscopic states.
Taken together, these results show that entropy and Binder cumulant measurements un-
cover aspects of the hysteretic behavior that are not fully captured by average inequality
measures alone. In particular, they indicate that the branch-dependent behavior observed
at low and intermediate values of γ originates from the persistence of extreme wealth con-
centration and from metastable configurations selected by the direction of variation of γ.

Under regressive taxation (α < 1), the system undergoes a qualitative change as the
tax strength parameter γ is varied. For sufficiently small γ, a non-ergodic regime emerges,
characterized by the formation of a class of never-poor agents that permanently escape
replacement and retain a considerable fraction of total wealth. This regime corresponds to
a “frozen” economic state dominated by entrenched elites, in contrast to a “mobile” regime
in which all agents eventually renew their wealth.
As γ increases, ergodicity is restored and all agents participate in the replacement dy-
namics. The wealth distribution becomes more homogeneous and inequality is reduced. The
transition between these regimes is abrupt and accompanied by hysteresis, indicating the
existence of two distinct stationary states over a finite range of γ. This bistable behavior is
reminiscent of first-order phase transitions in physical systems, where metastable states are
separated by finite barriers. In economic terms, this implies that once inequality becomes
entrenched, stronger redistributive action is required to reverse it, highlighting the persis-
tence and resilience of wealth concentration under regressive taxation. We also verified that
the discontinuous drop in the stationary Gini index and the location of the transition are
essentially unchanged when increasing N from 500 to 104.
The hysteretic character of the transition is consistently captured by different macro-
scopic observables. In addition to the Gini index, we find that the Top 1% wealth share, the
entropy of the wealth distribution, and the Binder cumulant all display separated increasing
and decreasing branches over the same interval of γ. Although these quantities probe dif-
ferent aspects of the distribution, they identify a common region of bistability, underscoring
the robustness of the observed transition. Importantly, entropy and the Binder cumulant
provide complementary information by being sensitive to the internal structure of the wealth
distribution and to the presence of extreme wealth fluctuations, rather than only to overall
inequality levels.
At small values of γ, entropy proves particularly effective in discriminating between sta-
tionary configurations that may exhibit a similar Gini index.
This enhanced sensitivity
stems from its dependence on the effective number of agents contributing to total wealth:
the presence of a few extremely wealthy agents strongly reduces entropy, even when stan-
dard inequality measures remain comparable. The Binder cumulant captures a related effect

events in the non-ergodic regime.
A noteworthy feature appears in the behavior of the Top 1% wealth share at intermediate
values of γ (0.5 ≲γ ≲1.5), where the increasing and decreasing branches nearly coincide.
In this regime, both dynamical paths sustain a wealthy elite concentrating a similar fraction
of the total wealth, and the Top 1% observable alone does not clearly distinguish between
them. However, this apparent overlap contrasts with the behavior of the entropy and the
Binder cumulant, which remain clearly separated over the same range of γ. This indicates
that, although the wealth held by the very richest agents is comparable along both branches,
the overall organization of the system differs substantially. In particular, the redistribution
of wealth across the bulk of the population and the higher-order structural properties of the
distribution depend on the dynamical history, revealing the persistence of hysteresis at a
more microscopic level. For smaller values of γ (γ ≲0.5), the situation changes: the Top 1%
wealth share clearly separates the two branches, reflecting the emergence of extreme wealth
concentration along one of the dynamical paths. In this regime, the observable becomes
highly sensitive to the dynamics and directly captures the dominance of a small number of
exceptionally wealthy agents.
In contrast, under progressive taxation (α ≥1), no abrupt transition is observed. Wealth
distributions remain comparatively homogeneous, and no persistent elite capable of escaping
replacement emerges. This behavior is consistent with a continuous evolution or crossover
between regimes and highlights the central role of tax progressivity in sustaining economic
mobility and preventing the formation of entrenched wealth hierarchies.
Overall, our results demonstrate that even minimal stochastic redistribution rules can
generate rich collective dynamics, including non-ergodicity, bistability, hysteresis, and abrupt
changes in inequality.
The emergence of never-poor agents provides a clear microscopic
mechanism for persistent wealth concentration and its resistance to redistribution. This
framework offers a simple yet powerful lens through which to understand how the structural
form of taxation interacts with redistribution to shape long-term economic inequality.
