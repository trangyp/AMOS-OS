Introduction
Hawking’s prediction of black hole evaporation [1] was derived for black holes that form
from gravitational collapse. In practice, most calculations of quantum effects in black hole
spacetimes have employed the Unruh state [2] on an eternal Schwarzschild background, which
reproduces the late-time Hawking flux without requiring a model of collapse. A fundamental
question is how quickly the quantum state established by collapse converges to the Unruh
state, and what mechanism governs the approach. (We compare with the Unruh state rather
than the Hartle-Hawking state [22] because the Unruh state is the one that reproduces the

In two spacetime dimensions the convergence is exponential in time [3], because the
wave equation has no effective potential and no scattering. In four dimensions the situation
is qualitatively different. Gholizadeh Siahmazgi, Anderson, and Fabbri [4] computed the
spherically symmetric modes of a massless minimally coupled scalar field for both the in-
vacuum state in a collapsing null-shell spacetime and the Unruh state in Schwarzschild
spacetime. They found that the individual mode differences decay as a power law t−3
s
at
late Schwarzschild times, preceded by a period of quasinormal-mode oscillations.
They
conjectured that at least some components of the renormalized stress-energy tensor exhibit
the same power-law approach, but left the proof as an open problem.
The mode-level
exponent −3 is the Price-law exponent [6] for ℓ= 0 scalar perturbations, originating from
a ω2 ln ω branch-point singularity in the Wronskian of the radial wave equation at zero
frequency.
This non-analyticity is a manifestation of the threshold resolvent singularity
identified in [7], where inverse-cube curvature decay on a spatial Cauchy slice was shown to
mark the onset of infrared sensitivity for curvature-coupled Laplace-type operators.
The gap between mode-level decay and stress-tensor decay is not trivial. The renormal-
ized stress tensor is built from a bilinear mode sum integrated over frequencies, summed over
angular momenta, acted on by a differential operator, and evaluated in a coincidence limit.
Each of these operations could in principle alter the decay rate. Establishing that none of
these pathologies occur is one part of this paper. Establishing that the leading coefficient is
nonzero, so the decay rate is exactly t−3
s
and not merely an upper bound, is the other.

5.5
The sign of Cuu
The sign of Cuu is determined by the product of real constants in (14). A direct evaluation
requires specifying the normalizations of the zero-frequency radial solutions. We do not carry
out this evaluation here.
A physical argument constrains the sign. Before collapse (us < u0), ⟨Tuu⟩in = 0 while
⟨Tuu⟩U = LH > 0, giving ∆⟨Tuu⟩= −LH < 0. After collapse, ∆⟨Tuu⟩→0. If Cuu > 0,
the flux difference would cross zero and become positive, the in-state would temporarily
radiate more than the Unruh state. The numerical mode data of [4] show no evidence of
such overshoot, and the physical expectation is that particle creation approaches the thermal
rate from below. We therefore expect Cuu < 0, consistent with [4].
Proving the sign rigorously would require establishing that the spectral deficit δnωℓ≤0
holds at all frequencies, which in turn requires controlling the interference terms in the 4D
Bogoliubov transformation between the transmitted and reflected pieces of the in-modes.
This is an open problem; the physical intuition that barrier reflection only reduces particle
creation is compelling but has not been verified as an exact inequality. We note that in certain
analog black hole models, interference effects can enhance particle production in specific
frequency bands (see, e.g., the discussion in [24]); whether this occurs for Schwarzschild is
unknown. The numerical data of [4] are consistent with δnω,0 < 0 for all frequencies tested
at late times, but a complete frequency-by-frequency verification has not been reported.
Consistency check. The reality of D0 at leading order can also be verified from the symmetry
relation D0(−ω∗) = D0(ω)∗, which for real ω gives D0(−ω) = D0(ω)∗. If D0 = ig with g
real, this forces g odd, while (13) gives D0 ∼cω2 (even), producing a contradiction.
6
Discussion
6.1
Physical interpretation
The sign of the flux deficit has a transparent physical meaning: the in-state always radiates
less than the Unruh state because particle creation turns on at collapse rather than being
present for all time. The power-law rate at which the deficit closes is set by the Price-tail
mechanism: the branch-point singularity in the Wronskian at zero frequency produces a t−3
s
tail that governs the final approach to thermal equilibrium.
The QNM oscillations observed by [4] at intermediate times correspond to the discrete
poles of the retarded Green function in the lower half-plane. These are exponentially damped
and subleading relative to the branch-cut tail. The fundamental ℓ= 0 scalar QNM has
Im ω0 ≈−0.10/M, giving a damping time τQNM ∼10M. The transition to the tail regime
at us ∼50M therefore corresponds to approximately five e-folding times, after which the
QNM contribution is suppressed by a factor ∼e−5 ≈7 × 10−3 relative to the tail.
6.2
The 2D/4D distinction
In two dimensions the potential vanishes, so there is no branch point and no power-law tail.
The approach is exponential, governed by the surface gravity κ [3, 15]. A related power-
law approach was observed for particle detector responses during collapse [18]. The present
9

framework makes the distinction structural: the potential barrier creates the branch point
that converts exponential convergence to power-law convergence.
6.3
Robustness beyond the null-shell model
The present analysis uses the null-shell collapse model of [4], but the key ingredients are
more general. The βI −= 0 condition (Sec. 3) holds for any collapse model in which the in-
state is defined by positive-frequency modes on I −: the scalar product depends only on the
behavior of the modes at I −, which lies in the asymptotically flat region regardless of the
collapse details. The Price-tail mechanism (Sec. 2) depends on the late-time Schwarzschild
potential, not on the near-field collapse geometry. The main model-dependent ingredient is
the Cauchy-surface data δµ on H, which encodes the collapse geometry through the matching
of modes across the shell. For more general collapse models (e.g., Oppenheimer-Snyder dust
collapse), δµ will differ in detail but the qualitative structure, nonzero cross-correlations
between I −and H sectors, with the H-leg carrying the Price tail, is expected to persist.
6.4
Extension to finite radius
Proposition 1 provides the O(t−3
s ) upper bound at any exterior radius. The sign argument
of Proposition 2 applies directly only at I +, where the flux has a natural spectral decompo-
sition. At finite r, the nonvanishing of the coefficient C(r) in the Cauchy-surface formula (8)
can be established by explicit evaluation of the regulated spectral density, combining all
mode sectors in a single frequency variable. The numerical mode data of [4] at r = 3M are
consistent with C(3M) ̸= 0.
6.5
Extensions to other fields and Kerr
For higher-spin fields on Schwarzschild, the branch-point order in the Wronskian remains
ω2ℓ+2 ln ω with the appropriate ℓmin for each spin. The classical Price-law exponent −(2ℓmin+
3) is therefore −7 for gravitational perturbations (ℓmin = 2). We expect the upper bound

6.7
Connection to the resolvent threshold singularity
The branch-point structure controlling the late-time tail is a manifestation of the threshold
resolvent singularity identified in [7]: inverse-cube curvature decay |Riem| ∼r−3 produces
a non-analytic threshold singularity in the weighted resolvent of the spatial operator at
zero energy.
To be precise about the division of contributions: Ref. [7] establishes the
resolvent singularity and its connection to the ω2ℓ+2 ln ω Wronskian structure for general
curvature-coupled operators on asymptotically flat backgrounds; the present paper applies
that framework to the specific problem of quantum state convergence, providing the Cauchy-
surface decomposition, the ℓ-sum convergence estimates, and the spectral computation of
Cuu that are new. The classical Price-tail mechanism (Leaver [8], Ching et al. [10]) predates
both works and is the common foundation. As argued in [7], the same non-analyticity is
suggestively connected to the soft graviton expansion and gravitational memory. Whether
these connections can be made precise at the level of the quantum stress tensor remains an
open question.
