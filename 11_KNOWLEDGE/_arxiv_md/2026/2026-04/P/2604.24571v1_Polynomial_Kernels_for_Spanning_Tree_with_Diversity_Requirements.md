Abstract
Given a connected undirected graph G, a spanning tree is a subgraph T of G such that V (T) = V (G)
and T is a tree. A collection of ℓspanning trees T1, . . . , Tℓis pairwise k-diverse if for every i ̸= j,
|E(Ti)△E(Tj)| ≥k. Given a connected undirected graph G and integers p, q, k, ℓ, Leaf&Internal-
Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees asks whether there are ℓdistinct spanning trees T1, . . . , Tℓ
of G that are pairwise k-diverse such that each tree has at least p leaves and at least q internal
vertices. Similarly, Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees takes a con-
nected undirected graph G, VNT ⊆V (G), and three integers p, k, ℓ, and asks if G has ℓspanning
trees that are pairwise k-diverse, and each has at least p leaves and contains the vertices of VNT as
internal. We consider these two problems from the kernelization perspective and provide polynomial
kernels for Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees and Leaf&Non-terminal-
Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees, when parameterized by p+q+k+ℓand p+|VNT|+k+ℓ,
respectively.

Introduction
The diversity paradigm in the Parameterized Complexity framework was formulated by
Fellows and Rosamond [22], but the concept was known even before, e.g., in Integer Pro-
gramming [14]. The paradigm is motivated by the common situation that arises in various
applications when it is not suﬃcient to ﬁnd just one high-quality solution to a combinatorial
optimization problem due to numerous side constraints. Furthermore, it makes no sense to
try to ﬁnd all optimal solutions because typically there are too many — it is either compu-
tationally infeasible to list all of them or, if the ﬁrst task is somehow achieved, impossible
to analyze the huge list of solutions. Instead, it is preferable to work with a reasonably
sized sample of suﬃciently good solutions, which may be analyzed by making use of side
information. Such a sample should represent the variety of solutions, and the natural way
to achieve this is to demand that the solutions be “far apart”. This “far apart” notion can
be mathematically expressed in various ways. However, when dealing with subset problems,
it is common to measure the diversity of a sample as either the minimum pairwise Hamming
distance between solutions, or the sum of all pairwise Hamming distances, or the cardinality

In our paper, we consider the problem of ﬁnding diverse spanning trees with additional
constraints on leaves and internal vertices.
Spanning trees are fundamental in Graph Theory and Computer Science, and various
types of spanning tree problems naturally arise in applications. One of these variants is the
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree problem [6, 17, 21, 34, 39, 44]. Formally, Max-Leaf Spanning
Tree asks, given a graph G and an integer p, whether G has a spanning tree with at least p
leaves. The Max-Internal Spanning Tree problem [4, 8, 23, 31, 35, 37, 38, 42, 43] is dual
to Max-Leaf Spanning Tree, and given a graph G and an integer q, asks whether G has
a spanning tree with at least q internal vertices. The input to Non-Terminal Spanning
Tree [31] contains a graph G with a set of non-terminal vertices VNT ⊆V (G), and the
question is whether G contains a spanning tree with the vertices of VNT being internal.
We consider the “diverse” variants of these problems. For an integer k ≥0, two spanning
trees T1 and T2 are said to be k-diverse if |E(T1)△E(T2)| ≥k. Respectively, a collection
of spanning trees T1, . . . , Tℓis pairwise k-diverse if for every i ̸= j, Ti and Tj are k-diverse.
Since our approach for the considered variants of Max-Leaf Spanning Tree and Max-
Internal Spanning Tree is based on similar arguments, we combine the requirements
for leaves and internal vertices in the Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning
Trees (Leaf&Internal-CDST) problem.
Leaf&Internal-CDST
Input: A connected graph G = (V, E), and integers p ≥0, q ≥0, k ≥1, and ℓ≥1.
Question: Decide whether G has ℓpairwise k-diverse spanning trees T1, . . . , Tℓsuch
that every tree has at least p leaves and q internal vertices.
Similarly, Max-Leaf Spanning Tree and Non-Terminal Spanning Tree are gener-
alized in Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees (Leaf&Non-
Term-CDST).
Leaf&Non-Term-CDST
Input: A connected graph G, a subset of non-terminal vertices VNT ⊆V (G), and
integers p ≥0, k ≥1, and ℓ≥1.
Question: Decide whether G has ℓpairwise k-diverse spanning trees T1, . . . , Tℓsuch
that the vertices of VNT are internal in every tree and each tree has at least p leaves.
Our results.
As Max-Leaf Spanning Tree is the dual of the Connected Dominating
Set problem, it is NP-complete [28]. Also, Max-Internal Spanning Tree and Non-
Terminal Spanning Tree generalize Hamiltonian Path and s-t-Hamiltonian Path,
respectively, which are well-known to be NP-complete [28]. On the positive side, these prob-
lems are well-studied from the perspective of parameterized algorithms. In particular, Max-
Leaf Spanning Tree [6, 21, 34] and Max-Internal Spanning Tree [23, 37, 42, 43]
are FPT when parameterized by p and q, respectively, and, furthermore, admit polyno-
mial kernels for these parameterizations. The same holds for Non-Terminal Spanning
Tree [31] parameterized by the size of the given set of non-terminal vertices. Our ﬁrst
result demonstrates that the kernelization carries over to the common diverse variant.

Related work.
The study of diverse spanning trees was initiated by Hanaka et al. [33].
However, they considered the diﬀerent diversity measure – the sum of all pairwise Hamming
distances. They proved that ℓspanning trees maximizing this measure can be found in
polynomial time. Moreover, this result can be extended to the problem of ﬁnding diverse
matroid bases; we remind that the edge sets of spanning trees are bases of graphic matroids.
The result is obtained by reducing ﬁnding diverse bases to the problem of ﬁnding disjoint
bases, which could be solved in polynomial time by the classical results of Nash-Williams [41]
and Edmonds [20]. We note that this approach fails for our case when we maximize the
minimum Hamming distance between trees. Diverse matroid bases when the diversity mea-
sure is the minimum Hamming distance were investigated by Fomin et al. in [25]. They
proved that the problem of ﬁnding ℓpairwise k-diverse bases admits a polynomial kernel for
the parameterization by ℓand k. Deciding whether a given graph has ℓpairwise k-diverse
spanning trees is a special case of this problem. In fact, Fomin et al. proved their result
for the more general weighted problem where the diversity is deﬁned as the weight of the
symmetric diﬀerence of two bases.
The ﬁrst FPT algorithm for Max-Internal Spanning Tree parameterized by the
number of leaves p was given by Downey and Fellows [17], and a faster algorithm was
provided by Bonsma et al. [6]. The ﬁrst polynomial kernel was given by Estivill-Castro et
al. [21]. The kernel with at most 5.5p vertices was established by Jansen [34]. Approximation
algorithms for the problem were given in [39, 44].
As we are interested mainly in the
parameterized complexity for the parameterization by the number of leaves, we do not
discuss here the results for the Connected Dominating Set problem, where the task is
to minimize the number of internal vertices of a spanning tree.
The Parameterized Complexity study of Max-Internal Spanning Tree was initiated
by Prieto-Rodriguez and Sloper in [42, 43] who gave a ﬁrst FPT algorithm for the parameteri-
zation by the number of internal vertices q and proved that the problem admits a polynomial
kernel. The currently best FPT algorithm was given by Björklund et al. [4]. Fomin et al. [23]
demonstrated that Max-Internal Spanning Tree admits a kernel with at most 3q ver-
tices, and Li et al. [37] got the improved kernel with at most 2q vertices. Non-Terminal

Preliminaries
Sets, Numbers, and Graph Theory:
We use N to denote the set of all natural numbers.
Given an integer r ≥1, the set [r] denotes the set {1, . . . , r}. Given two sets A and B,
the symmetric diﬀerence between A and B is denoted by A∆B which equals to the set
(A \ B) ∪(B \ A). We use standard graph theoretic terminologies from Diestel’s book [16].
For a graph G = (V, E), let V (G) = V denote the set of vertices and E(G) = E denote the
set of edges of G. If the graph is clear from the context, we use n to denote the number
of vertices, i.e.
|V (G)|, and m to denote the number of edges, i.e.
|E(G)|.
For a set
X ⊆V (G), we use G[X] to denote the subgraph induced by the vertex subset X. Given a
vertex v ∈V (G), let degG(v) denote the degree of v in G, i.e. the number of neighbors of
v in G. A pendant vertex is a vertex of degree exactly one. A pendant edge is the unique
edge that is incident to a pendant vertex. A tree is a connected graph without cycles. Given
a graph G, a spanning tree T of G is a tree subgraph of G whose set of vertices is V (G).
For a tree T, pendant vertices are leaves and the other vertices are internal. A path P in
a graph is a sequence of distinct vertices (v0, . . . , vℓ) such that for every i ∈[ℓ], vi−1vi is
an edge; ℓis the length of P. The vertices v0 and vℓare the end-vertices, and the other
vertices are internal. A degree-2-path P = (v1, . . . , vℓ) is a sequence of distinct vertices with
only possible exception v0 = vℓsuch that v1, . . . , vℓ−1 are vertices of degree two in G; in
the same way as for standard paths, v0 and vℓare end-vertices and the other vertices are
internal, and ℓis the length.
Parameterized Complexity and Kernelization:
A parameterized problem L is a set of in-
stances (x, k) where x ∈Σ∗over a ﬁnite alphabet Σ and k ∈N is the parameter.
A
parameterized problem L is said to be ﬁxed-parameter tractable if given (x, k) ∈Σ∗× N,
there exists an algorithm A that runs in f(k)|x|O(1)-time, where f is a computable function,
and correctly decides whether (x, k) ∈L or not. We call this algorithm A a ﬁxed-parameter
algorithm (or FPT algorithm in short). Note that we allow combinatorial explosion in the
parameter k while the algorithm runs in time polynomial in |x|. We say that two instances
(x, k) and (x′, k′) of a parameterized problem L are equivalent when (x, k) ∈L if and only

some computable function g : N →N. If g(k) is kO(1), then L is said to admit a polyno-

5
Conclusions and Future Research
Our results in this paper provide a comprehensive framework on polynomial kernelizations
for the variants of the Spanning Tree problem with constraints on the number of leaves
and internal vertices in the diversity paradigm. It would be interesting to consider other
variants of the Spanning Tree problem. First, we would like to point out that, while
disjoint spanning trees [41, 20] or spanning trees with the minimum sum of all pairwise
Hamming distances [33] can be found in polynomial time, the computational complexity of
ﬁnding pairwise k-diverse spanning trees is open. If k = 2, then pairwise k-diverse spanning
trees are distinct trees. Because distinct spanning trees of a given graph can be counted
by Kirchhoﬀ’s matrix tree theorem (see, e.g., [5]), it can be decided in polynomial time
whether a graph has ℓpairwise 2-diverse spanning trees. However, for k > 2, it is unknown
whether ﬁnding three pairwise k-diverse spanning trees is polynomial or NP-hard.
The
other set of open problems stems from directed graphs. What is the complexity of ﬁnding
diverse spanning trees (i.e., outbranchings) in directed graphs? Furthermore, it is interesting
to investigate the directed analogs of Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning
Trees and Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees. We remark
that the Parameterized Complexity of the Directed Max-Leaf Spanning Tree and
