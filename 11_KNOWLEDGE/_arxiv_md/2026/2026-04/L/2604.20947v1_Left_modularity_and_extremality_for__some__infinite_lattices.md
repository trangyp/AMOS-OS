LEFT MODULARITY AND EXTREMALITY FOR (SOME)
INFINITE LATTICES
SOTA ASAI, OSAMU IYAMA, KAVEH MOUSAVAND, CHARLES PAQUETTE
Abstract. For some important families of complete infinite lattices, we study
some generalizations of two fundamental notions which are mostly treated for
finite lattices. Specifically, for well-separated κ-lattices, and also for weakly
atomic completely semidistributive lattices, we generalize the notions of left
modularity and extremality. These two families of lattices coincide if restricted
to finite lattices, but are distinct when infinite lattices are also included. For
both families, we prove that extremality and left modularity imply each other.
Furthermore, for weakly atomic completely semidistributive lattices, we give
several conceptual characterizations of left modular elements, and show that
the set of left modular elements form a complete distributive sublattice. Our
results, combined with some recent work on finite lattices, imply that the
weakly atomic completely semidistributive lattices that are left modular (or
extremal) generalize the semidistributive trim lattices; from finite to infinite
lattices. We then apply our results to the lattice of torsion classes of finite
dimensional algebras, which are known to fall in the intersection of the two
families treated in our work. For an algebra A, we obtain that the lattice of
torsion classes is left modular (equivalently, extremal) if and only if A is brick-
directed. This leads to an abundance of concrete examples and non-examples.

1. Summary and main results
1.1. Summary. Here we briefly present our motivations and main contributions.
For notation, terminology, and background material, see Section 2 and references
therein. Throughout, unless otherwise specified, (L, ≤) denotes a complete lattice,
which is not necessarily finite. When there is no confusion, we simply denote a
(complete) lattice by L. The treatment of arbitrary lattices is known to be formi-
dable, and most studies assume some additional conditions on the lattice. Some
notable recent developments in lattice theory have been inspired by the remarkable
properties of lattices of torsion classes of finite dimensional algebras, leading to
important results beyond finite lattices; see [DI+, RST] and the references therein.
Distributive lattices are known to satisfy many nice properties, but they form a
rather small family. For finite lattices, several generalizations of the distributivity
property have been studied; see [Mu] and the references therein. Notably, extremal
lattices and left modular lattices are two of the most studied generalizations of
finite distributive lattices; respectively introduced in [Ma] and [LS].
Neither of
these properties implies the other one. However, for a semidistributive finite lattice
L, recent results of [TW, Mu], together, show that L is left modular if and only if L
is extremal. That being the case, L is a trim lattice [Th]. This establishes elegant
connections between some generalizations of distributive lattices; albeit under the
assumptions that L is finite and semidistributive. Meanwhile, as proved in [Ma],
every finite lattice can be embedded as a subinterval into an extremal lattice, hence
a complete classification of extremal lattices seems out of reach.
Thanks to the fruitful links between representation theory and lattice theory, a
rich collection of lattices arises from the former area, more specifically in connection
with the study of torsion classes, bricks, and related topics.
The treatment of
lattice of torsion classes has led to many fundamental results in a broader context,
including “The fundamental theorem of finite semidistributive lattices”; see [RST].
For any finite dimensional associative k-algebra A, the lattice of torsion classes in
mod A, denoted by (tors A, ⊆), is known to be a completely semidistributive weakly
atomic well-separated κ-lattice [DI+]. For a lattice (L, ≤), these properties can be
seen as a kind of discreteness of L; however, tors A is not necessarily finite, nor even
countable. In fact, tors A is finite if and only if A is brick-finite (see [AIR, DIJ]).
Building upon our recent work [AI+] on the lattice theory of torsion classes,
we go beyond the representation-theoretical context and study some families of
infinite lattices that possess a subset of the properties of (tors A, ⊆). More specifi-
cally, we are primarily interested in infinite lattices and treat those which are well-
separated κ-lattices, and also those lattices that are completely semidistributive
weakly atomic. For both classes, we generalize the classical notions of extremality
and left modularity. Moreover, in our setting, we study the implications between
our generalized notions of left modularity and extremality. Before presenting our
main results and putting our work and some more recent progress into a broader
perspective, we give an outline of this manuscript.

1.3. Main Results. Throughout, (L, ≤) always denotes a complete lattice. An
element x of L is called left modular if for any y ≤z, we have (y∨x)∧z = y∨(x∧z).
By LM(L), denote the set of all left modular elements in L. Following [LS], a finite
lattice is called left modular if it has an inclusion-maximal chain of left modular
elements. Moreover, a finite lattice is said to be extremal if it has exactly n join-
irreducibles as well as n meet-irreducibles, and a chain of length n (namely, the
chain has n+1 elements); see [Ma]. All terms that are not explicitly defined in this
section are recalled or introduced in Section 2.
We propose an extension of left modularity and extremality beyond finite lat-
tices.
Our proposed generalization of left modularity is the obvious candidate:
We say a (possibly-infinite but complete) lattice L is left modular if it has an
inclusion-maximal chain of left modular elements. In contrast, our generalization
of extremality is a bit more technical (for some clarification on the technicality and
simplification of this notion in some cases, see Remark 3.3 and Section 4): We say
an arbitrary lattice L is extremal if there exist a bijection λ : JIc(L) →MIc(L)
and a maximal chain X in L such that, for each x ∈X, if j ∈JIc(L) satisfies j ̸≤x,
then x ≤λ(j), and further, if x ⋖y for two elements x, y of X, then there is a
unique completely join-irreducible j which is below y (i.e., j < y) but not below x,
and a unique completely meet-irreducible m which is above x (i.e., x < m) but not
above y, and λ(j) = m. If L is finite, it is evidently left modular in our sense if and
only if it is left modular in the classical sense. Also, L is extremal in our sense if
and only if it is extremal in the usual sense; Lemma 3.4.
In Section 3, for the well-separated κ-lattices, we prove that our more general
notions of extremality and left modularity are equivalent. Then, in Section 4, we
show a similar result for weakly atomic completely semidistributive lattices, where
the notion of extremality is realized via a noticeably less technical condition. Before
we state our first result, let us emphasize that, the two families of lattices treated
in Sections 3 and 4 are different; see Remark 4.11. More specifically, neither of the
assertions of the following theorem implies the other one.

2. Preliminaries and background
In this section, we briefly recall some facts and terminology from lattice theory
and representation theory used in our work. For more details and rudimentary
materials in lattice theory, see [Gr], [DP], and references therein. Moreover, [ASS]
and references therein contain the required materials from representation theory
2.1. Lattice theory. Let (P, ≤) be a non-empty (possibly infinite) partially or-
dered set. Recall that x < z in P is said to be a cover relation, denoted by x ⋖z,
provided that for each y ∈P, if x ≤y ≤z, we have either x = y or y = z. Moreover,
Hasse(P) denotes the Hasse quiver of P, which is a directed graph whose vertex
set is P, and it has an arrow x →z for each cover relation x ⋖z.
Recall that (P, ≤) is a complete lattice if for any subset S in P, there exists a
unique element of P, smallest with the property of being larger than or equal to all
elements of S, called the join of S and denoted by W S; as well as a unique element
of P, largest with the property of being smaller than or equal to all elements of S,
called the meet of S and denoted by V S. To denote a lattice, we often use (L, ≤),
and for x and y in L, the join and meet are respectively denoted by x ∨y and
x∧y. In the following, unless specified otherwise, (L, ≤) always denotes a complete
lattice, and ˆ1 and ˆ0 respectively denote the greatest element and the least element
of L (sometimes called the maximum and minimum of L); that is, ˆ1 := V ∅= W L
and ˆ0 := V L = W ∅. When there is no confusion, we often suppress ≤in the
notation and simply use L instead of (L, ≤). We also recall that L is said to be

weakly atomic if, for all x < y, there exist u and v with x ≤u⋖v ≤y (equivalently,
Hasse[x, y] contains at least one arrow). Every finite lattice is weakly atomic.
A sequence x0 < x1 < · · · < xi < · · · in L is said to be a chain, and is called a
finite chain if the sequence consists of only finitely many elements. In particular,
the length of x0 < x1 < · · · < xr is defined to be r. Moreover, the length of the
lattice L is defined as the maximum of the lengths of finite chains, if there exists
such a maximum; otherwise, L is said to be of infinite length. We say a chain
x0 < x1 < · · · < xi < · · · is maximal if it cannot be further refined to a chain by
inserting more elements; this is the case if and only if xj ⋖xj+1, for each j.
We recall that x ∈L is join-irreducible if, for each y and z in L with y, z <
x, we have x ̸= y ∨z. By convention, ˆ0 is not considered as a join-irreducible
element.
Moreover, x ∈L is said to be completely join-irreducible if for every
subset S ⊆{y ∈L | y < x}, we have x ̸= W
y∈S y. Note that x is completely
join irreducible if and only if there is an element x∗such that y < x if and only if
y < x∗. Provided x is join-irreducible but not completely join-irreducible, such an
element x∗does not exist. Let JI(L) denote the set of all join-irreducible elements in
L, and JIc(L) denotes the subset of JI(L) consisting of completely join-irreducible
elements. The notion of (completely) meet-irreducible elements, and consequently
MI(L) and MIc(L), are defined dually. Note that, by convention, ˆ1 does not belong
to MI(L). In particulr, m belongs to MIc(L) if and only if there is an element m∗
such that m < y if and only if m∗< y.
A lattice L is called semidistributive if, for all x, y, z ∈L, if x ∨y = x ∨z, then
x ∨(y ∧z) = x ∨y, and, furthermore, x ∧y = x ∧z implies x ∧(y ∨z) = x ∧y.
Consequently, for any cover relation x ⋖y, the set {a ∈L | x ∨a = y} has a unique
minimal element. A complete lattice L is completely semidistributive if, for every
x ∈L and each S ⊆L, the following hold:
• If x ∨y = x ∨z for all y, z ∈S, then x ∨(V S) = x ∨y, for all y ∈S.
• If x ∧y = x ∧z for all y, z ∈S, then x ∧(W S) = x ∧y, for all y ∈S.
A lattice L is said to be spatial if every element x in L can be written as a
(possibly infinite) join of completely join-irreducible elements; that is, x = W
j≤x j,
where j ∈JIc(L). The lattice L is called bi-spatial if it is spatial and the dual
condition holds; namely, every x ∈L can be written as a join of completely join-
irreducible elements, as well as a meet of completely meet-irreducible elements.
An important subfamily of bi-spatial lattices consists of those which admit par-
ticular bijections between JIc(L) and MIc(L). More specifically, a bi-spatial lattice
L is said to be a κ-lattice if there are inverse bijections κ : JIc(L) →MIc(L) and
κ−1 : MIc(L) →JIc(L), respectively defined by κ(j) := max{x ∈L | x ∧j = j∗}
and κ−1(m) := min{y ∈L | y ∨m = m∗}. Provided L is a finite lattice, then L is
semidistributive if and only if it is a κ-lattice (for instance, see [RST, Section 2.5]).
A κ-lattice L is called well-separated if whenever x ̸≤y, there exists j ∈JIc(L) such
that j ≤x and y ≤κ(j). The following theorem puts the above definitions into a
better perspective. Meanwhile, we emphasize that for an arbitrary complete lattice,
the condition of being a well-separated κ-lattice neither implies nor is implied by
being completely semidistributive (see Remark 4.11). For a detailed comparison of
the above lattice-theoretical conditions, see [RST, Sections 3.1 and 3.3].

Theorem 2.1 ([RST, Theorem 3.1])
For a complete lattice L that is completely
semidistributive, the following hold:
(1) If L is weakly atomic, then L is bi-spatial.
(2) L is bi-spatial if and only if L is a κ-lattice.
For an arbitrary lattice L, recall that an element x in L is said to be left modular
if for every y ≤z in L, we have the equality (y ∨x) ∧z = y ∨(x ∧z). Observe that,
for a pair of elements y ≤z in L, the inequality (y ∨x) ∧z ≥y ∨(x ∧z) holds for
all x ∈L; often known as modular inequality. Therefore, to verify whether x in L is
left modular, one only needs to verify the non-trivial inequality. Let LM(L) denote
the set of all left modular elements of L.
For finite lattices, the study of left modular elements, as well as the length of a
lattice and its maximal chains, have inspired many works. This has ultimately led
to various generalizations of (finite) distributive lattices. For instance, in [St2] the
author introduced supersolvable lattices as graded lattices which have a maximal
chain consisting entirely of left modular elements (see also [St1]). Moreover, in
[LS] left modularity is treated as a generalization of the more restrictive notion of
modular elements, where the authors gave some alternative characterizations of left
modular elements [LS, Theorem 1.4], and generalize some earlier results of [BS].
An arbitrary finite lattice (not necessarily graded) L is said to be left modular if it
possesses a maximal chain consisting entirely of left modular elements. Every finite
distributive lattice is obviously left modular.
Another generalization of (finite) distributive lattices is given in terms of the
length of L compared to |JI(L)| and |MI(L)|. Following [Ma], a lattice L of length
m is said to be extremal if |JI(L)| = |MI(L)| = m. We remark that the inequalities
|JI(L)| ≥m and |MI(L)| ≥m always hold. For a detailed comparison between the
notions of left modularity and extremality (and some related notions) in the setting
of finite lattices, we refer to [Mu]. We state the following result, which clarifies the
connections between left modularity and extremality in the setting of finite lattices.
For more recent studies on finite extremal semidistributive lattices, see [Se] and the
references therein.
Theorem 2.2 ([Mu, TW])
Let L be a finite semidistributive lattice. Then L is
left modular if and only if L is extremal.
We finally recall that a finite lattice L is called trim if L is both extremal and
left modular. This family also gives another generalization of finite distributive
lattices; an analogue of distributivity for ungraded lattices (for details, see [Th]).
We note that a trim lattice is not necessarily semidistributive. That being the case,
the preceding theorem also gives a characterization of semidistributive trim lattice.
More specifically, a finite semidistributive lattice L is trim if and only if L is left
modular, if and only if L is extremal.

3. Left modularity and extremality for well-separated κ-lattices
Recall that for a lattice (L, ≤), an element x of L is called left modular if for any
y ≤z, we have (y ∨x) ∧z = y ∨(x ∧z). A finite lattice is said to be left modular
if it has an inclusion-maximal chain of left modular elements. We also recall that
a finite lattice is said to be extremal if the length of its longest chain equals the
number of join-irreducibles and the number of meet-irreducibles. In this section,

the definition of left modularity is with respect to the property of the elements

Definition 3.1

inclusion-maximal chain of left modular elements.

(1) In the proof of part (2), we use the well-known structure tors kQ, for the
Kronecker quiver Q. However, we can alternatively use the generalized bar-

In fact, a decisive point in the proof is the existence of an infinite semib-

hereditary or biserial; see [MP1]. Another key component of the proof is the
brick-directedness, which guarantees (and is equivalent to) left modularity
of tors A. As noted in Section 2.3, brick-directed algebras conceptually gen-
eralize classical representation-directed algebras. It is known that for any
rep-directed algebra A, the lattice tors A is left modular and extremal, but it
is always a finite lattice; [TW, Corollary 1.5]. However, unlike rep-directed
algebras, which are always of rep-finite type, brick-directed algebras include
various types of algebras, of tame and wild types; [AI+, Corollary 1.6].
(2) In connection with some important open questions in representation theory,
we formulate the following problem in a more lattice-theoretical setting.
Question: Let k be an algebraically closed field of cardinality ℵ, and
assume that A is a brick-directed k-algebra. Are the following equivalent?
(I) A is a brick-infinite algebra.
(II) There exist 2ℵmaximal chains in tors A, each of size ℵand consisting
of left modular elements.
Note that (II) ⇒(I) follows from the definition, but (I) ⇒(II) is unknown
to us. It is notable that this open implication closely relates to the Strong
Semibrick Conjecture; see [MP2, Remark 3.5]. Although this conjecture is
still open in full generality, it is known to hold for all hereditary and all
biserial algebras (and some other families). Hence, for these families, we
can give an affirmative answer to the above questions. For several closely
related open conjectures on bricks, see [MP2] and references therein.
