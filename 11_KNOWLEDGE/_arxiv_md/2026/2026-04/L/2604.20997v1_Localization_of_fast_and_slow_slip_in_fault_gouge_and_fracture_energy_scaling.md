Abstract
The localization of slow and fast slip in fault gouges may play a crucial role in un-
derstanding the mechanics of earthquakes and slow slip events. Here, we investigate the
fracture energy accompanying this localization and the subsequent thermal weakening.
We develop an analytical framework, complemented by numerical simulations, for a gouge
governed by rate-and-state-dependent friction with ﬂash-heating at high strain rate and
thermal pressurization of pore ﬂuids. The model captures the transition from initially
distributed shearing to a co-seismic principal slip “surface” at slip δloc ≈γch, and yields
a decomposition of the fracture energy, G = Gloc(h)+∆G(δ). The minimum, localization-
related component Gloc scales with gouge thickness h, which in turn scales linearly with
fault size. Flash heating is activated only upon localization for fast earthquake slip, pro-
ducing an abrupt strength drop, and contributing to the magnitude of Gloc. The post-
localization term ∆G increases with co-seismic slip due to eﬃcient thermal pressuriza-
tion and is insensitive to h. Localization is predicted to occur for both rate-weakening
and rate-strengthening gouges because transient state evolution drives apparent weak-
ening after a slip-rate increase. These results unify ﬁeld, laboratory, and seismological
observations of shear band thickness, critical slip, and fracture-energy scaling, and they
clarify why small events can be governed by scale-dependent Gloc whereas large ruptures
become increasingly fault-invariant as ∆G dominates. Our framework provides testable
predictions for the relation of gouge thickness to lower bounds of co-seismic fracture en-
ergy, and the mechanics of slow-slip transients and fast earthquakes.
Plain Language Summary
Earthquakes and slow-slip events release the energy built up in the crust by tec-
tonics to overcome fault friction. Part of that work becomes seismic waves or dissipated
as heat. The rest, called fracture energy, controls how ruptures start, grow, and stop.
We show that when a fault’s crushed rock (“gouge”) is sheared, deformation ﬁrst spreads
across the whole layer, then abruptly narrows to a thin band just a few grain diameters
thick. That grain-scale localization sets a minimum energy cost that grows with the gouge
thickness, which in turn increases with fault maturity and size. Localization also turns
on two heat-driven weakening processes, ﬂash heating at microscopic grain contacts and
thermal pressurization of pore ﬂuids, that continue to reduce strength as slip accumu-
lates. Their slip-dependent contribution makes the total fracture energy less sensitive
to fault size for larger events. This two-part energy budget links ﬁeld, lab, and seismo-
logical observations and helps explain why small earthquakes scale diﬀerently from large
earthquakes globally.

1 Introduction
Localization of shear deformation manifests in nature across a wide range of spa-
tial and temporal scales. Examples include the formation and growth of faults over ge-
ological timescale to accommodate long-term shear deformation of the Earth’s crust (e.g.,
Vermilye & Scholz, 1998; Scholz, 2019; Milliner et al., 2025), the localization of discrete
seismicity that is initially distributed over subsidiary-fault networks hundreds of kilo-
meters wide during preparation for a large earthquake (Bouchon et al., 2011; Ellsworth
& Bulut, 2018; Kato & Ben-Zion, 2021; Ben-Zion & Zaliapin, 2020; Almakari et al., 2026),
laboratory compression of initially intact rock specimens (e.g., Lockner et al., 1991; Thomp-
son et al., 2009; Mokni & Desrues, 1999), gouge friction experiments (e.g., Marone & Kil-
gore, 1993; Mizoguchi et al., 2009; Reches & Lockner, 2010; Kitajima et al., 2009; Proc-
tor et al., 2014), and coseismic localization of deformation within fault cores (e.g. F. M. Chester
& Chester, 1998; J. S. Chester et al., 2005; Heermance et al., 2003; De Paola et al., 2008;
F. M. Chester et al., 2004; Savage & Rowe, 2024) (Fig. 1a,b).
Here we focus on coseismic localization on natural faults and view localization within
the broader structural context of the Principal Slip Zone (PSZ), a weak natural fault gouge
unit embedded within, or constituting, the fault core (Fig. 1a). Across faults of diﬀer-
ent cumulative displacement and maturity, the PSZ can span thicknesses from millime-
ters to about a meter (Fig. 1c, (F. M. Chester & Chester, 1998; Wibberley & Shimamoto,
2003; Mizoguchi et al., 2008; Mitchell & Faulkner, 2009; Zoback et al., 2011)). Field and
microstructural observations further indicate that coseismic slip is accommodated on a
much narrower Principal Slip Surface (PSS) embedded within a wider PSZ (F. M. Chester
& Chester, 1998; J. S. Chester et al., 2005; Smith et al., 2011; Collettini et al., 2014) (Fig.
1b), although the extent to which exhumed fault rocks preserve a unique record of seis-
mic slip remains an active question (Rowe & Griﬃth, 2015).
Throughout experimental studies and theoretical modeling, localization is eﬀectively
synonymous to gouge weakening during slip. However, the physical origin of the weak-
ening that drives localization remains debated. In the localization theory of Rudnicki
and Rice Rudnicki and Rice (1975) for pressure-sensitive, non-associative-plastic geoma-
terials (see also (Vardoulakis & Sulem, 1995)), incipient localization is linked to the im-
pending transition in plastic response from strain hardening to strain weakening. By con-
trast, several recent theoretical studies (Rice et al., 2014; Platt et al., 2014, 2015; Bar-
ras & Brantut, 2025; Wang & Heimisson, 2026) have attributed localization in fault gouge
to shear-heating-driven thermal pressurization of pore ﬂuids under conditions of distributed
gouge shear. Although the latter is a plausible mechanism for fast co-seismic slip, shear
heating is largely inactive in low-velocity friction experiments (Marone & Kilgore, 1993;
Beeler et al., 1996) and in quasi-static triaxial and biaxial compression experiments on
intact rock and sand, where localization is nevertheless prominent (e.g. Lockner et al.,
1991; Thompson et al., 2009; C. Han & Vardoulakis, 1991; Mokni & Desrues, 1999). This
suggests that a more general weakening mechanism, or a set of mechanisms, governs in-
cipient localization in fault gouge.
Evolution of the state of frictional contacts (Dieterich, 1979; Ruina, 1983) in fault
gouge is likely one such ubiquitous weakening mechanism. In response to a shear-strain-
rate transient, for example imposed by a rupture front propagating along the fault, it
produces apparent strain-weakening of the gouge and thereby leading to incipient local-
ization, irrespective of whether slip is slow or fast. This is similar to the localization driven
by hardening-to-weakening transition in strain-dependent, pressure-sensitive plasticity
(Rudnicki & Rice, 1975; Vardoulakis & Sulem, 1995). However, such strain weakening
by itself does not determine the internal structure of the shear band: in a purely local
formulation it leads either to unbounded localization onto a surface of vanishing thick-
ness (Rudnicki & Rice, 1975; Vardoulakis & Sulem, 1995) or, in patterned solutions, to
a ﬁnite thickness linked to the characteristic band spacing (I. Garagash, 2006). Previ-
ous work relating shear-band width to grain size within the framework of second-order-

Once localization occurs, the resulting high shear strain rates within a narrow shear
band promote eﬃcient shear heating and activate a host of additional thermal weaken-
ing mechanisms (Rice, 2006). These include ﬂash heating at grain or asperity contacts
(e.g., Rice, 2006; Goldsby & Tullis, 2011; Rempel & Weaver, 2008; Brantut & Platt, 2017),
which produces rapid frictional weakening over small slip distances set by the thickness
of the localized shear band; thermal pressurization (TP) of pore ﬂuids (e.g., Lachenbruch,
1980; Mase & Smith, 1987; Rice, 2006; Badt et al., 2020); thermal decomposition (R. Han
et al., 2007; Hirose & Bystricky, 2007; Brantut et al., 2008; Sulem & Famin, 2009; Bran-
tut et al., 2010); and melting (Hirose & Shimamoto, 2005; Di Toro et al., 2011). These
processes promote continued weakening over larger slip distances controlled by the hydro–thermo–chemo-
poroelastic properties of the gouge and are largely independent of PSZ gouge thickness.
This co-event shear localization may later be reversed. Shear strain rate may de-
localize within the gouge during the inter-event period through gouge healing and/or creep
(Zoback et al., 2011), or during the late stages of slip through through deceleration, pore-
ﬂuid and heat diﬀusion, or rheological changes such as melting. Laboratory slip-hold-
slip friction experiments (Beeler et al., 2022; Bedford et al., 2023) and observations from
natural earthquake gouge (Savage & Rowe, 2024) provide support for both inter-event
and late-stage co-event delocalization, respectively. This suggests that natural slip tran-
sients may commonly initiate from a relatively delocalized state, with coseismic re-localization
following the onset of dynamic slip. This framework is relevant across the full spectrum
of observed fault slip behavior, from slow slip events (SSEs) to fast earthquakes (Rogers
& Dragert, 2003; Ide et al., 2007; Peng & Gomberg, 2010; Behr & B¨urgmann, 2021).
Fracture energy is a key energetic characteristic of fault weakening during slip and
governs rupture initiation, propagation, and arrest for a given fault and surrounding bulk
rock loading (e.g. Palmer & Rice, 1973; Abercrombie & Rice, 2005; Weng & Ampuero,
2019; Kammer et al., 2024; Cocco et al., 2023). Fracture energy in both natural and lab-
oratory earthquakes is known to scale with ruptured fault size and coseismic slip (e.g.
Abercrombie & Rice, 2005; Viesca & Garagash, 2015; Nielsen et al., 2016; Gabriel et al.,
2024). Because shear localization is synonymous with weakening, and therefore likely gov-
erns dynamic frictional weakening and the fracture energy dissipated during coseismic
slip, the goal of this study is to interpret fracture energy observations in the context of
the physics of localization and fault gouge properties, in particular their scaling with fault
maturity and size.
Building on seismological inferences of coseismic fracture energy, Gabriel et al. (2024)
showed that the total fracture energy can be decomposed into two additive parts,
G = Gc(R) + ∆G(δ),

2.3.3
Constraints on fully-localized shear band thickness h∗and ﬂash-
heating activation strain-rate ˙γw
Friction experiments on artiﬁcial gouges with varied grain-size distributions (Marone
& Kilgore, 1993) show values of state-evolution slip distances dc ranging between 1 and
10 µm when measured at suﬃciently large cumulative strain presumably after undergone
strain localization. Given the relation dc = γch∗and assuming the state-evolution strain
γc to be in the range between 0.1 to 1, the fully-localized shear band thickness h∗can
be indirectly estimated to fall in the range from 10 to 100 µm.
This estimate is consistent with direct experimental observations showing that shear
bands typically span from a few to a few tens of grains across (see review by Rice (2006)
references therein). Natural ultracataclastic gouges in principal slip zones (PSZs, Fig. 1a)
of mature faults commonly have a median grain size of ∼1 µm, with distributions span-
ning from nanometers to tens of microns (Wilson et al., 2005). Consequently, they may
host very thin localized bands (h∗∼10 µm). Microstructural observations from the
Punchbowl fault principal slip surface (PSS) are consistent with such extreme localiza-
tion (F. M. Chester & Chester, 1998; J. S. Chester et al., 2005) (Fig. 1b). In contrast,
localization bands in many artiﬁcial gouge experiments are typically thicker (h∗≳100 µm,
(e.g., Proctor et al., 2014)) commensurate with their larger median grain size (≳10 µm).
This also suggests that h∗in artiﬁcial gouge may evolve with ongoing comminution.
Flash heating is expected to occur once a critical contact-scale strain rate, ˙γw, is
exceeded (Beeler et al., 2008; Platt et al., 2014; Proctor et al., 2014). This implies that
the corresponding critical slip velocity Vw scales with the localized band thickness h∗as
˙γw = Vw/h∗. Laboratory constraints suggest Vw ∼0.1 m s−1 on bare rock surfaces
(Kohli et al., 2011; Goldsby & Tullis, 2011; Di Toro et al., 2011), whereas sheared rock
gouges require higher values, Vw ∼1 m s−1 (Proctor et al., 2014; Yao et al., 2015). For
bare surfaces, h∗can be approximated by the asperity size observed in microstructures
(h∗∼10 µm), whereas artiﬁcial gouge experiments report localized bands with h∗∼
100 µm after substantial grain-size reduction to ≲20 µm. Both cases yield a consistent
estimate of ˙γw ∼104 s−1, supporting the notion that the onset of ﬂash heating is fun-
damentally controlled by a strain-rate threshold. Consequently, ﬂash-heating is expected

2.4 Grain-scale physics and structure of a localized shear band
The following discusses the grain-scale physics that prevents the otherwise “run-
away” (unbounded) localization onto a mathematical plane, thereby deﬁning the ﬁnite
thickness and internal structure of the localized shear band.
2.4.1
Impending run-away localization in a local frictional rheology
Although in the frictional rheology formulation in Sec. 2.3 we invoke gouge gran-
ularity and the existence of a minimum, irreducible localized shear thickness h∗, this frame-
work remains inherently local, meaning it depends only on the strain rate and state vari-
ables deﬁned at a single point. Consequently, it is expected to lead to an unabated lo-
calization process inconsistent with the irreducible ﬁnite shear band thickness h∗. Un-
der steady-state rate-weakening conditions, either due to a low-strain-rate weakening regime
(a −b < 0) or due to localization activation of ﬂash-heating weakening at suﬃciently
high imposed slip velocity, this local framework would predict unbounded shear local-
ization, collapsing onto a mathematical plane and resulting in inﬁnite strain rates. Fur-
thermore, even in the case of steady-state rate-strengthening gouge friction, the inher-
ent weakening driven by the evolution of the frictional state following a transient increase
in slip velocity also drives the localization onto a mathematical plane.
2.4.2
Non-local friction rheology
In reality, the gouge granularity (ﬁniteness of grain sizes) frustrates the otherwise
unbounded localization. This manifests as a departure from the purely local gouge rhe-
ology at the large spatial gradients in strain rate intrinsic to the localization process. The
granularity is expected to lead to a ﬁnite thickness h∗> 0 of the localized shear band
and non-local rheological eﬀects exercised during localization (e.g., Daub & Carlson, 2008;
Rattez et al., 2018b, 2018a; Pranger et al., 2022; Casas et al., 2025), as also documented
in granular physics (e.g., Ertas & Halsey, 2002; Bonamy et al., 2002; Pouliquen, 2004;
Mills et al., 2008).

2.6 Numerical methodology
We numerically solve the coupled frictional, hydraulic, and thermal evolution equa-
tions to investigate strain localization and frictional weakening within fault gouge. Rec-
ognizing that non-local eﬀects are prevalent only at the grain-scale and that the local-
ized shear band thickness in a fully non-local analysis is governed by the grain-size, we
adopt a simpler, local rheological model that inherently predicts unlimited localization.
To approximate the ﬁnite thickness of the localized shear band in our numerical solu-
tions, we set the minimum grid size equal to the irreducible localized shear-band thick-
ness obtained from the non-local solution for the internal shear-band structure (Eq. 12),
h∗= 2πλ.
Thus, the maximum localized strain-rate in our numerical simulations remains phys-
ically bounded, as in nature, reaching a peak value of ˙γloc ≈V/h∗within a single (min-
imum size) grid spacing of size h∗. The numerical solution obtained with this “grid-limited”
local framework is approximately dynamically equivalent to that of the full non-local frame-
work, with our choice of λ = h∗/2π. This is evidenced by the nearly identical predicted
shear-strain-rate distributions outside the localized band in the two frameworks (Fig. 2b),
and follows from the matching steady-state gouge strength within the localization band
in the two solutions.
This means that the local strain rate in the grid-limited local framework, ˙γloc, matches
the non-local strain rate in the non-local framework, ˙γλ, within the shear band (Fig. 2c).
Likewise, the friction within the band is the same in the two approaches. As a result,
the two modeling frameworks predict nearly identical evolution of the gouge frictional
strength and breakdown energy (Suppl. Text S4).

In summary, our derived expressions establish that the total fracture energy can
be decomposed into a fault-size-dependent minimum component, Gloc ∝h, and a slip-
dependent component, ∆G(∆δ), arising from post-localization thermal weakening. This
framework quantitatively links the mechanics of shear localization and the eﬃciency of
subsequent thermal weakening to the fault energy budget, providing a physical basis for
the observed scaling of fracture energy with both the fault size and the coseismic slip
(Abercrombie & Rice, 2005; Viesca & Garagash, 2015; Gabriel et al., 2024).
4 Discussion
4.1 From distributed shear in the principal slip zone to a localized prin-
cipal slip surface
Our results suggest a robust, three-phase evolution (Fig. 3a) of co-seismic shear strength
when slip in a ﬂuid-saturated gouge is driven by a rapid velocity increase (passing front
of a slow or fast rupture). Slip begins delocalized within the principal slip zone of thick-
ness h and weakens slowly under classical rate–state friction, potentially aided by weak,
undrained thermal pressurization. Once the frictional state has evolved by a strain ∼
γc, slip abruptly localizes at the grain/asperity scale h∗triggering ﬂash heating if the
slip is fast enough; thereafter, fully localized slip continues to weaken by eﬃcient ther-
mal pressurization on an eﬀectively mathematical plane. This cascade holds for both steady-
state rate-weakening and rate-strengthening gouges; in the latter, the transient state evo-
lution still drives instability and localization (see Suppl. Figs. S1-S4).

The strong ﬂash-heating-induced friction drop at localization contributes to Gloc

shear bands, h∗∼100 µm, as observed in laboratory experiments (Proctor et al., 2014;

weakening with a characteristic slip scale L∗that is independent of h and set by hydrother-

Together, these results reconcile small-event fracture energy, dominated by Gloc(h),
with large-event behavior where ∆G(∆δ) renders G increasingly fault-invariant with grow-
ing slip (Gabriel et al., 2024).
4.3 Rectifying the range of the state-evolution strain γc
The state evolution strain is the key gouge property controlling the localization strain,
slip and fracture energy. It is therefore important to verify that our assumed range, γc ∼
0.1–1, is consistent with observed localization strains in frictional and compressional fail-
ure tests, when interpreted through our localization model. Biaxial undrained compres-
sion of dense sands yields γloc ∼0.1 (C. Han & Vardoulakis, 1991; Mokni & Desrues,
1999), whereas triaxial compression of intact rocks indicates γloc ∼0.01 (Rummel et al.,
1978). Using the approximate relation γloc ∼(0.1–0.5) γc emerging from our analysis,
these measurements are consistent with γc ∼0.1–1. This range ensures that δloc ∼(0.1–0.5)γch
remains small compared with typical coseismic slip on mature faults, yet large enough
to carry a measurable Gloc that scales with h. It also ensures that thermal pressuriza-
tion, which becomes eﬃcient only at the much larger strain scale γw ≫1, has negli-
gible inﬂuence on the localization process, in contrast to previous studies that neglect
frictional state evolution in the gouge.
4.4 Relation to previous localization models
Our results extend and revise earlier analyses of gouge localization under thermal-
pressurization weakening. Prior kinematic and dynamic treatments of distributed shear
with a rate-only (velocity-strengthening) frictional rheology found localization to be driven
by thermal pressurization at a strain γloc comparable to the undrained-adiabatic char-
acteristic strain γw = ρc/(fΛ) ≫1 (Rice et al., 2014; Platt et al., 2014, 2015; Barras
& Brantut, 2025; Wang & Heimisson, 2026). By incorporating the full rate–and–state
response, we show that the transient state evolution is the primary driver of localization,
yielding a much smaller localization strain, γloc ∼γc ≲1, negligible TP eﬀects prior
to localization, and drastically smaller δloc and Gloc than inferred previously with rate-

only rheologies. Our conclusions regarding the driver of localization, namely the evolu-
tion of frictional contact state in the gouge leading to apparent mechanical weakening
with slip or strain, are more consistent with studies that invoke similar gouge-weakening
mechanisms, including pressure-sensitive, non-associative, hardening-softening plastic-
ity (M¨uhlhaus & Vardoulakis, 1987; Vardoulakis & Aifantis, 1991; Rattez et al., 2018b,
2018a; Stathas & Stefanou, 2023) and shear-transformation-zone inelastic theory (Daub

In our framework, TP becomes decisive after localization, governing ∆G(∆δ), whereas

ing δloc) in fast transients. This framework also clariﬁes why localization occurs irrespec-
tive of whether the low-velocity steady-state friction branch is rate-weakening or rate-
strengthening (Suppl. Figs. S1-S4), since the non-steady state evolution behind the rup-
ture front provides the apparent weakening required to localize strain. In contrast, steady-
state rate strengthening behavior eventually promotes delocalization in the tail of slow
transients that fail to activate ﬂash heating (Suppl. Fig. S2f).
4.5 Heterogeneity, repeated ruptures, and (de-)localization within the
principal slip zone (PSZ)
Natural PSZs are heterogeneous (e.g., the SAFOD core), and initial slip within a
low-velocity strengthening segment may be partitioned across sub-bands within a delo-
calized PSZ.
Inter-event healing, the homogenization of gouge strength and structure, will con-
trol whether a subsequent transient must re-localize or instead inherits a previously formed
principal slip surface. Where slip transients occur frequently (e.g., as triggered or episodic
slow slip events), healing may be incomplete, favoring persistent localization across events.
Conversely, substantial interseismic aging promotes initially delocalized slip, requiring
renewed localization during each fast event (Beeler et al., 2022). Resolving these com-
peting tendencies is essential for assessing the generality and persistence of co-event lo-
calization in mature faults.
4.6 Implications for earthquake scaling and observables
Our approximately linear scaling Gloc ∝h, exempliﬁed by Gloc ≈10 [MPa] ×
γc h in Fig. 6b and Suppl. Fig. S5b, implies a fault-size dependence of the small-slip frac-
ture energy because h increases with both cumulative fault displacement D and fault size
R. Using h ≈10−2D for unsaturated growth (Fig. 1c) and D ≈10−2R (Dawers et al.,
1993; Scholz, 2010), the predicted Gloc ≈1000 [Pa] × γc R scales linearly with fault
dimension and helps explain seismological inferences of an approximately linear lower-
bound G-vs.-size trend for small earthquakes and small-slip sub-events, Gc ≈400 [Pa]×
R (Fig. 8, and Gabriel et al. (2024)). Full agreement between the localization theory pre-
diction Gloc and the observed lower-bound Gc is obtained for a realistic value of the state-
evolution-strain, γc ≈0.4. At large slip, the fault-size-invariant contribution ∆G(∆δ)
increasingly dominates, consistent with observations that G becomes less sensitive to fault
size and more sensitive to coseismic slip in large crustal and subduction events (Abercrombie
& Rice, 2005; Viesca & Garagash, 2015). Microstructural evidence for extreme coseis-
mic shear localization within narrow PSSs embedded in wider PSZs (F. M. Chester &
Chester, 1998; J. S. Chester et al., 2005; Rice, 2006) supports the mechanistic plausibil-
ity of this scaling.

The localization solutions developed here for an advancing rupture front are kine-
matic, based on slip-rate-step solutions. Extending this work to fully elasto-dynamic mod-

Dynamically incorporating such non-local eﬀects, together with dilatancy, poroe-

et al., 2021) could quantify how often natural ruptures re-use pre-existing principal slip
surfaces versus (re-)localize within the principal slip zone, and how this choice controls
macroscopic earthquake characteristics such as rupture speed, high-frequency radiation,
and event-to-event variability.
5 Conclusions
We show that localization in gouge is a transiently state-driven process that (i) sets
a minimum fracture-energy budget, Gloc(h), that depends on principal-slip-zone thick-
ness, and (ii) triggers rapid ﬂash heating followed by eﬃcient thermal pressurization, which
together provide a co-transient, slip-dependent contribution ∆G(∆δ). This decomposi-
tion uniﬁes ﬁeld, laboratory, and seismological observations across small and large events,
and it provides a concrete mechanistic pathway from initially distributed shear to a co-
seismic principal slip surface.
In detail, we ﬁnd that:
1. In faults governed by rate-and-state friction, slip transients initially distribute shear
across the fault gouge and subsequently localize it to the asperity or grain scale.
This transition is a fundamental mechanism governing earthquake rupture.
2. The slip distance to localization, δloc, scales approximately linearly with gouge thick-
ness h, and increases weakly with transient velocity V and with decreasing am-
plitude of the initial strain-rate perturbation within the gouge. The associated frac-
ture energy, Gloc, scales similarly to δloc, but with a marked dichotomy in its de-
pendence on V , depending on whether the transient is strong enough to activate
ﬂash-heating.
3. Localization leads to continued fault weakening with post-localization slip ∆δ, and
thus to a continuously increasing fracture-energy contribution ∆G(∆δ). Rapid slip
enhances thermal weakening through (i) ﬂash heating at frictional grain contacts
within the shear band, which occurs nearly instantaneously upon localization and
contributes to the localization fracture energy Gloc; and (ii) delayed thermal pres-
surization, together with extended ﬂash-heating weakening after localization, mod-
–20–


## Page 21


manuscript submitted to JGR - Solid Earth
ulated by heat and ﬂuid diﬀusion. The latter drives continued weakening with slip
and the slip-dependent fracture-energy increment ∆G(∆δ).
4. Coupled with the scaling of fault gouge thickness h with fault size R (Fig. 1), this
localization theory provides a physical mechanism underlying the decomposition
of coseismic fracture energy into G = Gc(R) + ∆G(δ), proposed by Gabriel et
al. (2024) based on seismological observations, in which the fault-size-dependent
component can be understood as Gc(R) = Gloc(h(R)).
5. In our model, localization occurs on both rate-weakening and rate-strengthening
faults. The underlying driver is the apparent weakening with slip associated with
evolution of the frictional state variable in response to a fault slip-rate perturba-
tion, from the peak friction associated with the direct eﬀect to a residual, steady-
state value. Neglecting state-evolution in some of the previous localization mod-
els leads to strong overestimation of both the localization slip and the associated
fracture-energy contribution.
