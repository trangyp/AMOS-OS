Abstract
We study the complexity of inverse cellular automata on configurations
of bounded size. Deciding injectivity in this setting is co-NP-complete by
a theorem of Durand [6]. We give a simpler proof of this theorem by a
direct reduction from UNSAT to this problem, avoiding more complicated
intermediate constructions.
We also show that one direction of the reduction can be formalized in
the weak theory of bounded arithmetic V 0.
Durand’s coNP-completeness result allows one to view inverse cellular
automata acting on bounded size configurations as propositional proofs,
cf. Cavagnetto [2, 3], and we prove lower bounds on their size. The proof
uses known lower bounds for bounded-depth Frege systems together with
the Paris–Wilkie translation of arithmetic proofs into propositional proofs,
which allows us to transfer proof complexity lower bounds to our setting.

Introduction
Cellular automata are simple models of systems that change in steps, widely
used in computer science and physics. They capture basic features of nature
like local interactions, uniform rules, and parallel updates. A key concept is the
Garden of Eden, a configuration that cannot arise from any previous state. The
Garden of Eden theorem characterizes when such configurations exist. A cellular
automaton is surjective if and only if every configuration has a predecessor;
that is, if and only if it has no Garden of Eden configuration. This is further
linked to local injectivity: two finite patterns are called twins if they can be
swapped anywhere without affecting future configurations; the automaton is
locally injective if it has no twins. The theorem states that surjectivity and
local injectivity are equivalent. In two dimensions, Kari [7] showed that checking

injectivity or surjectivity is undecidable, meaning no algorithm can solve it in

However, the situation changes when we restrict attention to bounded con-
figurations. In this setting, the problem becomes decidable but remains compu-
tationally difficult. In particular, Durand [6] proved that deciding injectivity of
two-dimensional cellular automata on bounded configurations is coNP-complete,
implying that, unless P = NP, this problem is computationally hard. His proof
uses a complex multi-step reduction from the nondeterministic halting problem
via tiling systems.
In this paper, we give a more direct reduction. More precisely, we introduce a
polynomial reduction of the coNP-complete set UNSAT (the set of unsatisfiable
CNF formulas) to the set of injective two-dimensional cellular automata with
von Neumann neighbourhood on configurations of bounded size.
Moreover,
we show that one direction of this reduction (namely, that if the formula is
satisfiable, then the cellular automaton is not injective) can be formalized in the
theory V 0, a weak theory of bounded arithmetic. Since V 0 is extremely weak,
formalizing such a complexity-theoretic result within it is not straightforward.
While several unprovability results are known for V 0, few non-trivial theorems
of this kind have been shown to be provable inside it.
Durand [6, Sec. 4] posed the question of whether the inverse automaton
to a cellular automaton operating on bounded configurations, whose transition
function is computed by a simple algorithm, is also computed by a simple al-
gorithm. Cavagnetto [2, Sec. 3.3] showed, using the computational complexity
hypothesis that a one-way function exists, that this is not the case if by an
algorithm one means a Boolean circuit. In addition, he showed that Durand’s
coNP-completeness results still hold in such a setup. The question of the size of
inverse cellular automata for the original representation of the transition func-
tion by its table was left open in both [6, 2]. We shall use proof complexity to
solve this problem.
The motivation for our approach comes from the PhD thesis of Stefano
Cavagnetto [2], which was later published in [3]. In that work, inverse cellular
automata were viewed as propositional proofs in the sense of Cook–Reckhow [5].
We follow this idea.
We show that, when we restrict ourselves to configurations of bounded size,
cellular automata in our construction have the property that inverse automata
to them cannot be in general of subexponential size. Indeed, their size is expo-
nential, and, in particular, their neighborhood must grow polynomially.
To prove this, we consider the unsatisfiable CNF formula φ = ¬ontoPHPk,
which expresses that there is a bijection between k + 1 and k. This formula
has size polynomial in k but requires exponential-size bounded-depth Frege
refutations by known strengthenings of a theorem of Ajtai [1]. Then we use the
Paris–Wilkie translation [9] to simulate our inverse cellular automaton proof
system by a bounded-depth Frege system Fd, thereby transferring this lower
bound to our setting.
For background on cellular automata, see for example [7], and for background
on bounded arithmetic and proof complexity, see [8].

Preliminaries
Throughout the paper we use the notation [n] ∶= {0,1,...,n −1}.
2.1
Cellular automata
We briefly recall the basic notions of cellular automata that will be needed
later. The definitions are standard and follow [6], but we include them here to
fix notation and terminology. We also record a simple bound on the size needed
to encode a two-dimensional cellular automaton with a fixed neighbourhood.
Definition 2.1 (Cellular automaton).
A cellular automaton is formally defined as a quadruplet (d,S,N,f):
• The integer d ≥1 is the dimension of the space on which the cellular
automaton operates.
• S /= ∅is a finite set called the set of states.
• The neighbourhood N is a v-tuple of distinct vectors of Zd.
For N =
(x1,...,xv), vectors xi are the relative positions of the neighbour cells
with respect to a given center cell. The states of these neighbours are
used to compute the new state of the center cell.
• The local function of the cellular automaton f ∶Sv →S gives the local
transition rule.
A configuration is a map C ∶Zd →S. For a configuration C and a position
y ∈Zd, the value C(y) is called the state of the cell at position y. We refer to
the position y itself as a cell.
If C ∶Zd →S is a configuration, we identify C with its graph
{((i,j),C(i,j)) ∣(i,j) ∈Zd }.
All automata considered in this paper are two-dimensional, i.e. d = 2.
Sometimes, a state q for which f(q,q,...,q) = q is distinguished in S and is
called a quiescent state. A finite configuration is a configuration which has only
a finite number of cells in a non-quiescent state. If there exist two integers i
and j such that all non-quiescent cells of the configuration are located inside a
rectangle with coordinates in [i]×[j], then we say that the finite configuration is
inside [i]×[j]. We shall be interested in how automata operate on configurations
inside a fixed rectangle.
Lemma 2.2. ([6]) For fixed N (of size v) the size of a binary string sufficient
to code a cellular automaton of dimension d = 2 is bounded above by sv log s,
where s is the size of S.
Proof. We have d = 2 and a fixed neighbourhood N (lexicographically ordered),
so the local function can be represented by a table that has sv rows. Any value
of the function is encoded by log s bits, so sv log s bits in total suffice to encode
the cellular automaton.

2.2
Propositional proof complexity
Propositional proof complexity studies the length and structure of proofs of
propositional tautologies in different proof systems. In this subsection we recall
the notion of a propositional proof system and introduce Frege and bounded-
depth Frege systems. These concepts will later be linked to bounded arithmetic
via the Paris–Wilkie translation.
The language we use in this subsection is the standard DeMorgan propo-
sitional language, consisting of propositional variables x,y,z,..., the logical
constants ⊺and , the connectives ¬, ∧, and ∨, and parentheses.
2.2.1
Proof systems
Let TAUT denote the set of propositional tautologies and let SAT denote the set
of satisfiable propositional formulas. We identify formulas with binary strings
that encode them.
Definition 2.3 (Propositional proof system [5, Definition 1.3]). A relational
propositional proof system is a binary relation Q ⊆{0,1}∗×{0,1}∗, where {0,1}∗
denotes the set of all finite binary strings, such that:
• Q is p-time decidable;
• for any w,α, if Q(w,α) holds then α ∈TAUT;
• for any α ∈TAUT, there is a w ∈{0,1}∗such that Q(w,α) holds.
The second condition gives the soundness of Q and the third its completeness.
We will often shorten the terminology propositional proof system to proof
system.
A refutation of a formula φ in Q is a proof w ∈{0,1}∗such that
Q(w,¬φ)
holds. Equivalently, a refutation of φ is a proof of the tautology ¬φ.
The size of a proof w (respectively, a formula φ) is the number of symbols
it contains.
To compare the strength of different propositional proof systems, we use the
notion of polynomial simulation.
Definition 2.4 (p-simulation). Let P and Q be two propositional proof sys-
tems. A p-time function f ∶{0,1}∗→{0,1}∗is a p-simulation of Q by P if and
only if for all strings w,α,
Q(w,α) →P(f(w,α),α).
If such f exists, we say that P p-simulates Q.

We now give a basic and well-known example from propositional proof com-
plexity. The pigeonhole principle is a standard source of hard formulas and is
often used to show lower bounds for proof systems, in particular for bounded-
depth Frege systems.
Example 2.6 (Pigeonhole principle). Consider the following CNF formula
¬ontoPHPn, consisting of clauses over propositional variables pij, where
i ∈[n + 1] and j ∈[n]:
1. ⋁j pij, one clause for each i;
2. ¬pi1j ∨¬pi2j, one clause for each triple i1 < i2 and j;
3. ¬pij1 ∨¬pij2, one clause for each triple i and j1 < j2;
4. ⋁i pij, one clause for each j.
The variable pij is intended to express that pigeon i is assigned to hole j.
The clauses in (1) and (4) assert that every pigeon is assigned to some hole and
that every hole contains at least one pigeon, respectively. Clauses in (2) and (3)
enforce that no two pigeons are assigned to the same hole and that no pigeon
is assigned to more than one hole.
Clearly, ¬ontoPHPn is unsatisfiable, since the number of pigeons exceeds
the number of holes.
In fact, the formula remains unsatisfiable even if only
clauses (1) and (2) are retained. This weaker principle is known as the weak
pigeonhole principle, denoted ¬PHPn.
We introduce this example because of the following well-known result due
to Ajtai, which establishes super-polynomial lower bounds for bounded-depth
Frege proofs.

Theorem 2.7 (Ajtai’s theorem). [1] Let d ≥2 and let 0 < δ < 5−d. Then for all

From CNF to cellular automaton
We now turn the computation tables introduced in the previous section into
objects that can be checked locally by a cellular automaton.
We define a cellular automaton Aφ that will operate inside [n + 1] × [m + 2]
whose purpose is to verify whether a given configuration represents a correct
computation of the truth value of φ for some assignment a. These configurations
are intended to encode computation tables for φ as defined in the previous
section. However, the cells will contain not only the information used for the
verification of the correctness but also an additional bit called label.
The idea is that Aφ performs a local verification of the correctness. It in-
spects the neighbourhood of each cell and checks whether the local configuration
is consistent with a correct computation. If yes, the automaton updates the la-
bel component of the cell’s state according to the labels of its neighbors. The
consistency requirements are formalized by a collection of local correctness rules,
which will be specified later in this section. On each cell Aφ will possibly modify
only the label component of the cell state, leaving all other symbols unchanged.
In order to define the cellular automaton Aφ, we first formalize the notions
introduced in the previous section and fix some of its parameters. The remaining
components will be specified later.
In particular, we fix:
• the dimension, which is d = 2;
• the neighbourhood, which is the von Neumann neighbourhood
N = {(0,0),(0,1),(0,−1),(1,0),(−1,0)}.

Lower bound for inverse automata
Following the idea in Chapter 4 of [2] we shall interpret the inverse automaton
to Aφ as a propositional refutation of φ.
Definition 7.1 (PCA). We define a propositional proof system PCA as follows.
Let φ be a CNF formula. A refutation of φ in PCA is a cellular automaton
B = (2,S,M,g)
such that:
1. S = Sn,m is the set of states determined by φ as in Section 6;
2. if t ∶= ∣B∣and µ is the number of cells in M, then Inv(n,m,Aφ,B,t) holds.
Lemma 7.2. PCA is a sound and complete proof system.
Proof. The provability predicate is ΣB
0 and hence p-time decidable.
Suppose Inv(n,m,Aφ,B,t) holds. By Lemma 6.3 and modus ponens, this
implies that Aφ is injective. By Theorem 4.16, Aφ is injective if and only if φ
is unsatisfiable. Therefore PCA is sound.
For completeness, assume that the neighbourhood M is large enough so
that for every cell c ∈[n + 1] × [m + 2] the set M(c) contains the whole region
[n + 1] × [m + 2]. Thus the function g ”sees” the whole configuration C.
If Aφ is injective, then for every configuration C ∈Aφ(Config) there exists
exactly one configuration C′ ∈Config such that
Aφ(C′) = C.
We take this configuration C′ and define g so that, when it is applied to the
states of the cells in M(c) as they appear in the configuration C, it outputs the
state of the cell c in C′.
Thus g reconstructs the predecessor configuration cell by cell, so it is the
inverse of Aφ. Hence Inv(n,m,Aφ,B,t) holds. Therefore PCA is complete.

Open problems
There are two open problems which may be interesting to explore:
1. Can the inverse implication in Theorem 4.16 be formalized in V 0? We
note that it can be formalized in V 1 (see [4] for its definition). It remains
open whether the whole argument can be carried out in a theory weaker
than V 1.
2. What is the strength of the proof system PCA? In particular, does PCA
p-simulate resolution, or even bounded-depth Frege systems Fd for some
d ≥2?
