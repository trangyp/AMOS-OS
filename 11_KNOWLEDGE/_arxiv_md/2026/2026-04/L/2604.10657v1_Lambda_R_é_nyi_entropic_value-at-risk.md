Abstract
This paper introduces the Lambda extension of the R´enyi entropic value-at-risk (Λ-EVaR), a novel
family of risk measures that unifies the flexible confidence level structure of the Λ-framework with the
higher-moment sensitivity of EVaR. We define Λ-EVaR, establish its foundational properties including
monotonicity, cash subadditivity, and quasi-convexity, and provide a complete axiomatic characterization
showing that convexity, concavity in mixtures and cash additivity hold only when Λ is constant. A dual
representation and an extended Rockafellar-Uryasev-type formula are derived, enabling efficient compu-
tation. We further analyze the worst-case behavior of Λ-EVaR under Wasserstein and mean-variance
uncertainty, obtaining closed-form expressions that reveal its robustness properties. The proposed mea-
sure bridges the gap between adaptive risk tolerance and moment-sensitive risk assessment, offering a
versatile tool for modern risk management.

Introduction
Risk measures serve as the cornerstone for quantifying, managing, and regulating risk in finance, insur-
ance, and decision theory. Among the plethora of proposed measures, Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected
Shortfall (ES) have achieved paradigmatic status due to their intuitive interpretation and regulatory adoption
(e.g., Basel Accords). Both are defined with respect to a fixed confidence level α ∈[0, 1], which determines
the portion of the loss distribution considered. While this fixed-level paradigm offers simplicity, it has been
widely criticized for its rigidity. A single α cannot reflect the nuanced risk tolerance of a decision-maker who
may wish to be highly conservative for moderate, more frequent losses, yet more accepting of truly extreme,
catastrophic events—a preference pattern often observed in practice and embedded in progressive regulatory
frameworks.
To address this limitation, the innovative Lambda (Λ) risk measure framework was introduced. It replaces
the constant α with a function Λ : R →[0, 1], mapping each potential loss threshold x to a local confidence
level Λ(x). This generalization allows the risk assessment to adapt dynamically to the loss size, formally

encoding the intuition that “how much risk we see depends on how bad the loss could be.” The framework
originated with Λ-VaR, where Λ is taken as increasing, linking higher loss thresholds with higher confidence
levels (Frittelli et al., 2014). Λ-VaR has been shown to satisfy several properties that are useful in finance,
including elicitability (Bellini and Bignozzi, 2015), robustness (Burzoni et al., 2017), and quasi-star-shapeness
(Han et al., 2025). Bellini and Peri (2012) further provided an axiomatic characterization of Λ-VaR, which
particularly justifies the choice of Λ as a (weakly) decreasing function. From a practical perspective, Λ-VaR
has also been studied in various applied contexts, such as estimation and backtesting (Hitaj et al., 2018;
Corbetta and Peri, 2018), distributionally robust optimization (Han and Liu, 2026), risk sharing (Liu, 2025;
Xia and Hu, 2025), and optimal insurance design (Boonen et al., 2025).
Subsequently, the Λ framework was recently extended from VaR to ES, employing a decreasing Λ function
by Bellini et al. (2025). This modification aligns more naturally with economic and regulatory reasoning:
for extreme tail losses (high x), insisting on an extremely high confidence level (Λ(x) ≈1) may lead to
prohibitive and unrealistic capital requirements; thus, allowing Λ(x) to decrease for large x provides necessary
flexibility. Both Λ-VaR and Λ-ES preserve essential properties such as monotonicity, law invariance, and,
notably, cash subadditivity—a relaxation of cash additivity pertinent in environments with stochastic or
ambiguous interest rates (El Karoui and Ravanelli, 2009; Cerreia-Vioglio et al., 2011). Moreover, Bellini et
al. (2025) demonstrated that Λ-ES is the smallest quasi-convex and law-invariant risk measure dominating
Λ-VaR, and provided both a dual representation and a Rockafellar-Uryasev-type formula (Rockafellar and
Uryasev, 2002) for Λ-ES.
While Λ-VaR and Λ-ES offer valuable flexibility in confidence levels, they nonetheless inherit fundamen-
tal limitations from their classical counterparts. VaR is not a convex risk measure, failing to incentivize
diversification (Artzner et al., 1999). ES, while coherent (convex, monotone, cash additive, and positively
homogeneous), is a spectral risk measure that depends solely on the tail expectation, disregarding informa-
tion about the shape of the loss distribution beyond the α-quantile Acerbi (2002). In modern finance, where
assets often exhibit heavy tails, skewness, and kurtosis, a risk measure sensitive to higher moments is crucial
for accurate risk assessment and capital allocation. This raises a natural and pressing question: Can we
construct a Λ-type risk measure that is both convex (or quasi-convex) and sensitive to higher-moment tail
behavior?
The R´enyi Entropic Value-at-Risk (EVaR) emerges as an ideal candidate to answer this question. In-
troduced and further developed within a rigorous dual framework by Pichler and Schlotter (2020) and an
adjusted version by Zou et al. (2023), EVaR is a coherent risk measure defined on Lp spaces. For a fixed α,
EVaRp
α is derived from an optimization problem involving the R´enyi entropy of order q (where 1/p+1/q = 1),
effectively imposing a constraint on the divergence of a probability measure from the reference measure P. Its
primal representation admits a computationally convenient Rockafellar-Uryasev-type formula (Rockafellar
and Uryasev, 2002). Crucially, EVaRp
α dominates ES and incorporates information from the p-th moment
of the loss tail. As p increases, it interpolates between ES (at p = 1) and more conservative, and higher-
moment sensitive measures, making it a powerful and flexible tool for applications where tail dispersion
matters (Pichler and Schlotter, 2020; Dentcheva et al., 2010).
Building upon the success of the Λ-framework for VaR and ES, and motivated by the superior theoretical

2.1
Basic terminology
In this subsection, we briefly review the basic concepts and properties of risk measures used throughout
the paper. For a thorough introduction to the theory of risk measures, we refer the reader to the monographs
Delbaen (2012); F¨ollmer and Schied (2016).
A risk measure is a functional ρ : X →R, where X is a suitable space of financial positions (typically
Lp), which represents the minimal amount of capital that has to be raised and injected in a financial position
X to ensure that X is acceptable (safe). To characterize the behavior of a risk measure in a mathematically
rigorous and economically meaningful way, several axiomatic properties have been widely adopted in the
literature. The most commonly considered axioms for risk measures include the following: for X, Y ∈X,
(A1) Monotonicity: ρ(X) ≤ρ(Y ) if X ≤Y ;
(A2) Cash additivity: ρ(X + m) = ρ(X) + m for all m ∈R;
(A3) Subadditivity: ρ(X + Y ) ≤ρ(X) + ρ(Y );
(A4) Positive homogeneity: ρ(λX) = λρ(X) for λ ≥0;
(A5) Convexity: ρ(λX + (1 −λ)Y ) ≤λρ(X) + (1 −λ)ρ(Y ) for all λ ∈[0, 1];
(A6) Law invariance: ρ(X) = ρ(Y ) if X
d= Y .
Note that the axiomatic properties listed above are not mutually independent; some may be implied by
others depending on the chosen framework. Different selections of these properties lead to distinct classes of
risk measures commonly studied in the literature. For instance:
• A risk measure satisfying monotonicity (A1) and cash additivity (A2) is called a monetary risk measure.;
• If, in addition, it satisfies convexity (A5), it is referred to as a convex risk measure.;
• A coherent risk measure is a monetary risk measure that also fulfills positive homogeneity (A3) and
positive homogeneity (A4).
In empirical and practical applications, it is essential that a risk measure can be estimated from observable
data. This requirement is formally captured by the property of law invariance (A6), which ensures that the
risk measure depends only on the distribution of the financial position. Throughout this paper, we restrict
our attention to law invariant risk measures, as is standard in most applied and statistical settings.
To account for environments with non-constant interest rates, cash additivity has been relaxed to the
weaker property of cash subadditivity. Specifically, a risk measure ρ is called cash subadditive if ρ(X +m) ≤
ρ(X) + m for all X ∈X and m ∈R+ (El Karoui and Ravanelli, 2009). While cash subadditivity reflects the
impact of varying discount rates, the diversification effect in this setting is characterized by quasi-convexity
rather than convexity. That is, ρ(λX + (1 −λ)Y ) ≤max{ρ(X), ρ(Y )} for all X, Y ∈X and λ ∈[0, 1]
(Cerreia-Vioglio et al., 2011). More recently, Han et al. (2025) studied the broader class of cash subadditive
risk measures that satisfy only monotonicity and cash subadditivity, without requiring quasi-convexity,
thereby extending the framework to accommodate a wider range of risk-assessment practices.

2.2
Risk measures and their Lambda counterparts
The concept of Lambda risk measures generalizes traditional risk measures by allowing the confidence
level to depend on the loss threshold. Instead of fixing a single confidence level α ∈[0, 1], one uses a function
Λ : R →[0, 1], where Λ(x) reflects the risk-tolerance at the loss level x. This idea was originally introduced
for Value-at-Risk (VaR) by Frittelli et al. (2014), who considered an increasing function Λ, and was later
extended to Expected Shortfall (ES) by Bellini et al. (2025) who adopted a decreasing specification of Λ.
Here, as throughout the paper, the terms “increasing” and “decreasing” are understood in the non-strict
(weak) sense.

While the Λ-framework in principle allows for both increasing and decreasing specifications, the decreasing
choice aligns naturally with economic intuition and regulatory practice. When the loss threshold x is low,
the decision-maker is typically concerned with relatively frequent, moderate losses and may therefore adopt a
high confidence level (i.e., Λ(x) close to 1). Conversely, for very high thresholds x that correspond to extreme
loss, a lower confidence level (i.e., Λ(x) close to 0) is often more appropriate, as insisting on extremely high
confidence may lead to unrealistically conservative capital requirements. This decreasing pattern mirrors
the common regulatory philosophy that allows for more flexibility in the treatment of extreme tail risks.
Moreover, a decreasing Λ guarantees that the resulting Λ-risk measure retains desirable properties such as
elicitability and the axiomatic justification for Λ-VaR (Bellini and Bignozzi, 2015; Bellini and Peri, 2012). For
these reasons, we restrict our attention to decreasing Λ throughout the paper, which is also the convention
adopted in the recent literature on Λ-ES.

Conslusion
This paper introduces and studies the Lambda extension of the R´enyi entropic value-at-risk (Λ-EVaR), a
flexible family of risk measures that allows the confidence level to vary with the loss threshold via a decreasing
function Λ.
The main contributions are threefold.
First, we have defined Λ-EVaR and established its fundamental properties, including monotonicity, cash
subadditivity, quasi-convexity, and Lp-continuity.
A key result (Proposition 3.5) shows that convexity,
concavity in mixtures and cash additivity of Λ-EVaR are all equivalent to Λ being constant, confirming that
the Lambda framework genuinely extends the classical EVaR.
Second, we have derived a dual representation (Theorem 4.1) and an extended Rockafellar-Uryasev
formula (Theorem 4.4) for Λ-EVaR. The latter provides a tractable two dimensional minimization problem
that facilitates numerical computation and optimization.
Third, we have examined worst-case values of Λ-EVaR under two standard models of distributional
uncertainty: Wasserstein balls and mean-variance sets. For Wasserstein uncertainty, the worst-case Λ-EVaR
admits a closed-form expression (Proposition 4.3) that cannot be reduced to a single Λ-EVaR with a new
fixed Λ. Under mean-variance constraints, the worst-case values of Λ-VaR, Λ-ES and Λ-EVaR (with p = 2)
coincide and are given by a simple Cantelli-type bound (Proposition 4.5). This coincidence, however, does
not extend to higher-order moment constraints or to general p ̸= 2.
The Λ-EVaR family thus offers a unified framework and incorporating the sensitivity to higher moments
afforded by the R´enyi entropy. Future research could explore its statistical estimation, its performance in
portfolio optimization under ambiguity, and its potential applications in regulatory capital frameworks where
varying confidence levels are economically justified.
