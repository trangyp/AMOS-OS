Deﬁning a causal estimand for a longitudinal outcome trun-
cated by death is challenging, because the outcome may be
undeﬁned at the end of follow-up. Although a range of es-
timands and several estimators have been proposed, guid-
ance on the underlying causal assumptions and on the con-
texts in which each estimand is most appropriate remains
limited.
We propose a framework to clarify the challenges of
deﬁning causal estimands in a longitudinal setting with cen-
soring due to death. Within this framework, we review
existing estimands and make explicit the assumptions re-
quired for their identiﬁcation and estimation. We develop
Bayesian estimators for each estimand and compare their
behavior in a simulation study. Finally, we illustrate the pro-
posed approach using data from a randomized controlled
trial in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.
We show that the main diﬃculty arises from the lack
of a natural notion of ordering and distance for outcomes
truncated by death. This leads to an inherently multifacto-
rial problem. In this context, the stratiﬁed average causal ef-
fect, combined with restricted mean survival time, provides
a more complete characterisation of treatment eﬀects.

INTRODUCTION
Quality of life (QOL) and patient-reported outcomes (PROs), typically measured longitudinally, are key endpoints in
clinical analysis and are increasingly regarded as essential even in settings with high mortality [1]. In such contexts,
however, a non-negligible proportion of patients may die before the end of follow-up, rendering the longitudinal
outcome ill-deﬁned. In randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this feature complicates the formulation of a scientiﬁcally
meaningful causal estimand for assessing treatment eﬀects on such outcomes.
Some causal estimands have been proposed to address this issue, e.g., [2, 3, 4], but their use in practice remains
limited, partly owing to the absence of a framework for comparing and interpreting them. In parallel, a range of esti-
mators has been developed to tackle this problem, notably based on landmarking [5] and joint modeling approaches
[6]. However, despite the central role of estimands emphasized in the ICH E9 addendum [7] and in recent work ad-
vocating a shift in focus away from modeling alone [8], these methods are typically not accompanied by an explicit
formulation of the causal estimand they estimate. Consistently with this, a recent review of methods for handling
death in RCTs summarized several analytical strategies, yet without framing them in terms of causal estimands [1].
In this context, our contribution is twofold. First, we propose a general causal framework to formalize the chal-
lenges arising from truncation due to death when deﬁning causal estimands for longitudinal outcomes. Within this
framework, we review several existing estimands and assess their interpretability. Second, for each selected estimand,
we make explicit the assumptions required for the estimation of an unbiased estimate, and develop Bayesian estima-
tors to investigate their statistical behavior. After validating these methods through simulation, we illustrate their
application using data from a randomized controlled trial in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), a disease for which
the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requires trials to assess treatment eﬀects on both overall survival and
longitudinal outcome capturing functional abilities [9].

Treatment Eﬀect for Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
The motivating example considered in this paper is the estimation of a treatment eﬀect on a longitudinal functional
score in ALS. ALS is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive loss of motor function and eventual
death from respiratory failure [10]. The median survival time from disease onset is approximately 3–4 years [10].

Functional ability in daily activities, intended to capture patients’ functional decline, is most commonly assessed using
the revised ALS Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRSr) [11], a longitudinal score ranging from 48 to 0, with lower scores
indicating more severe disease.
Longitudinal outcomes truncated by death pose a central challenge in ALS controlled trials, motivating the de-
velopment of several methodological approaches. In its 2019 guidance, the FDA recommended “an analysis method
that combines survival and function into a single overall measure.” [12]. In response, several composite estimands tai-
lored to ALS have been proposed, including the Combined Assessment of Function and Survival (CAFS) [13] and the

rank-based estimands, such as the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney estimand (further discussed in Section 4.4.2), for which
a causal interpretation is not straightforward. More recently, a model-based estimand has also been proposed [15].
However, this approach departs from European Medicines Agency (EMA) recommendations [7] and from general prin-
ciples of causal inference, in that it deﬁnes the estimand through strong modeling assumptions rather than directly in
terms of potential outcomes.

Challenges with Death Truncation
The central diﬃculty induced by truncation due to death is that the longitudinal potential outcome is no longer deﬁned
after death has occurred. As a consequence, two fundamental questions typically addressed in randomized controlled
trials become diﬃcult to answer: (i) "Are treated patients in better health?" and (ii) "By how much are they better?".
In the absence of truncation by death, the ﬁrst question can be addressed using the natural ordering of the longi-
tudinal scale: for example, an ALSFRSr score of 47/48 is unambiguously better than 40/48. However, when death
occurs, one must compare outcomes for living and deceased patients, a situation for which no intrinsic ordering ex-
ists. This issue is particularly acute in chronic and fatal diseases, where quality of life is a key outcome and where, in
some settings, end-of-life decisions such as euthanasia may be available. Thus, the ﬁrst challenge posed by truncation
by death is the lack of a natural ordering between survival and longitudinal health outcomes.
The second question, “By how much are patients better?”, relies on settings without truncation on the natural met-
ric of the longitudinal outcome (e.g., 47 −40 = 7 points of ALSFRSr score). Under truncation by death, however, one
must compare quantities measured on fundamentally diﬀerent scales: score unit versus survival time. There is no
intrinsic metric that allows these two quantities to be meaningfully combined, so truncation by death also removes
the natural notion of distance of the longitudinal outcome.
In this article, we estimate and interpret the causal estimands proposed for handling truncation by death with respect
to these two requirements: the existence of a meaningful ordering and of a meaningful distance.

Method
We analyze data from a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in patients with ALS, estimating the eﬃ-
cacy of olesoxime in 512 individuals [40]. The trial showed no eﬀect on survival based on the log-rank test, and only
a modest improvement in ALSFRSr score at 9 months in favor of olesoxime, which was not sustained at 18 months,
assessed using an inappropriate naive eﬀect (see Section 3.3.1).
We compute the estimators deﬁned in Section 4 at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 18 months. Identiﬁcation relies on the assump-
tions of SUTVA, no unmeasured confounding, positivity, and principal ignorability. To assess the plausibility of these
assumptions, we report measures of reconstruction performance. For the longitudinal outcome, reconstruction accu-
racy for missing potential outcomes is estimated using the mean absolute error (MAE). For survival, reconstruction
performance is assessed using the cumulative dynamic area under the ROC curve (cdAUC), integrated from 2 to 17
months at monthly intervals as a measure of discrimination, and the integrated Brier score (IBS), which captures cali-
bration of reconstructed survival probabilities. Survival metrics are computed using the sksurv Python package [41].
As in the simulation study, we specify identical priors under treatment and control, reﬂecting the absence of prior
information on treatment eﬀects, together with weakly informative priors for covariate eﬀects and for the residual
variability of the longitudinal model (see Section 5.1). For the longitudinal slope, we use β0
t (W ) ∼N(−1, 3), con-
sistent with the disease progression rate of approximately 0.92 units per month reported in randomized trials [42].
Patients typically die 3–4 years after symptom onset [10], and were enrolled on average 1.5 years after onset (Ta-
ble 7), corresponding to an expected survival of 18 to 30 months from trial baseline. Following the approach used in
the simulation study (see Section 5.1), we specify λj (W ) ∼Γ(0.035, 0.1).

DISCUSSION
In this paper, we proposed a uniﬁed framework to deﬁne, interpret, and compare causal estimands for longitudinal
outcomes truncated by death, together with Bayesian estimators for their estimation.
We showed that the main diﬃculty arises from the absence of a natural notion of ordering and distance for
outcomes truncated by death. This setting is inherently multifactorial, as estimands either provide disaggregated
information on survival and the longitudinal outcome or aggregate these dimensions into a single measure. Stratiﬁed
Average Causal Esimand, combined with Restricted Mean Survival Time, enables a decomposition of these objectives
at the cost of yielding multiple estimands. By contrast, scalar estimands require additional assumptions to deﬁne
an ordering between survival and the longitudinal outcome. Among these, the Pairwise Comparison estimand is
particularly appealing, as it corresponds to a probability of a better outcome, is well-deﬁned mathematically, and
remains identiﬁable across scenarios, in contrast to the Survival-Incorporated Median.
Identiﬁcation of the proposed estimands relies, in addition to standard causal assumptions (SUTVA, no unmea-
sured confounding, and positivity), on the principal ignorability assumption. This is a strong assumption, requiring
both substantive knowledge of the disease and the availability of appropriate measured covariates. We assessed its
plausibility in the ALS setting with discrimination and calibration metrics of survival reconstruction.
We developed Bayesian estimators that robustly estimate uncertainty, and compare their performance in a sim-
ulation study. The results conﬁrm that the pairwise comparison estimand captures the trade-oﬀbetween survival
and longitudinal outcomes in settings with opposing treatment eﬀects, whereas principal stratiﬁcation and restricted
mean survival time provide a more complete but inherently multidimensional characterization. We have pointed to
the long-lasting role of prior as posterior uncertainty does not vanish asymptotically in this setting, highlighting the
persistent inﬂuence of prior speciﬁcations, as discussed for principal stratiﬁcation by Richardson et al. [43]. Our imple-


## Page 19


Ortholand et al.
19
mentation relies on relatively simple parametric models, which could be extended in future work. We illustrated the
proposed methods using data from a randomized controlled trial in ALS, where longitudinal outcomes are truncated
by death, thereby providing a concrete example of their practical application. Code for the proposed estimators is
available at https://github.com/JulietteOrtholand/TBDEstimates, together with a simple example to facilitate
their use.
Although our focus is on truncation by death as a speciﬁc intercurrent event, the underlying issues—particularly
the need to deﬁne ordering and distance—also arise in other settings, such as treatment non-adherence, where the
proposed framework may provide useful insights.
In summary, we provide a comprehensive framework for analyzing longitudinal outcomes truncated by death. We
recommend the joint use of the Stratiﬁed Average Causal Eﬀect (SACE) and Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) to
disentangle survival and longitudinal components and to reﬂect the inherently multifactorial nature of the problem.
More broadly, this work aims to support more coherent and interpretable analyses of randomized controlled trials in
ALS and other life-threatening diseases.
