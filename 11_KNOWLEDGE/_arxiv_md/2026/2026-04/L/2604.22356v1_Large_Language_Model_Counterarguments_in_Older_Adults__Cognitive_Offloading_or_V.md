Abstract 
This study examined whether counterarguments generated by large language models (LLMs) 
influence the moral judgments of younger and older adults and whether these effects vary as a 
function of dilemma type, cognitive functioning, trust in AI, and prior experience using LLMs. 
Using the switch and footbridge trolley dilemmas, 130 participants (56 younger adults and 74 
older adults) were presented with ChatGPT arguments that opposed their initial judgments. 
Results revealed that more than 30% of participants reversed their moral judgments in both 
dilemmas (32.31% in the switch dilemma and 36.92% in the footbridge dilemma), suggesting 
that LLMs possess substantial persuasive power. Older adults tended to be more likely than 
younger adults to reverse their judgments, and they showed a significantly greater degree of 
judgment change in the switch dilemma. Notably, in the emotionally aversive footbridge 
dilemma, older adults with lower cognitive functioning were significantly more likely to align 
with the LLM-generated counterargument. General trust in AI and prior experience with LLMs 
did not predict judgment reversal, supporting a disconnect between trust and persuasion. Instead, 
individual factors such as lower initial confidence and higher perceived task difficulty were 
associated with greater susceptibility to AI influence. These findings suggest that, although 
LLMs may serve as tools for cognitive offloading that compensate for age-related cognitive 
decline, they may also pose a risk of undue persuasion for cognitively vulnerable individuals. 
Keywords: Moral judgment; AI trust; Algorithm aversion; Aging; Cognitive function

1. Introduction 
Large language models (LLMs) have been used as tools of persuasive communication 
that influence people’s thoughts, choices, and behaviors in domains such as political 
communication and marketing (Hölbling et al., 2025). At the same time, they have been 
criticized for their potential to generate and disseminate hate speech, misinformation, and 
political propaganda (Salvi et al., 2025). Although a meta-analysis on AI persuasiveness found 
no consistent difference in persuasive effectiveness between humans and LLMs, it has 
emphasized the need to focus more closely on individual characteristics and the psychological 
factors underlying persuasion (Hölbling et al., 2025). The present study examines whether 
individuals reverse their judgments and accept LLM-generated counterarguments when the LLM 
presents a position opposite to their original judgment. It also investigates whether the 
acceptance of such counterarguments is influenced by individual attributes, such as age, as well 
as psychological factors, such as cognitive function and trust in AI. 
In the present study, the switch dilemma and the footbridge dilemma are used as 
decision-making tasks without objectively correct answers to examine whether people’s 
judgments change in response to AI-generated counterarguments. The switch dilemma is a task 
in which participants judge whether it is appropriate to pull a switch to divert a trolley, thereby 
sacrificing one person in order to save five people who would otherwise be killed if the trolley 
continued on its current course. The footbridge dilemma is a task in which participants judge 
whether it is appropriate to push a man off a footbridge to stop the trolley and save five people. 
These two trolley dilemmas are equivalent in that both involve sacrificing one life to save five 
others. However, previous research has shown that many individuals make a utilitarian judgment 
in the switch dilemma, favoring the sacrifice of one person in order to save five because doing so


## Page 4


4 
 
maximizes benefits and minimizes costs. In contrast, in the footbridge dilemma, individuals tend 
to make a deontological judgment, showing a moral aversion to the act of pushing a person to his 
death (Greene et al., 2001). 
One theoretical framework that explains the difference in judgments between these two 
dilemmas is the dual-process theory of moral judgment (e.g., Greene, 2009). According to this 
model, moral decision-making is shaped by two qualitatively distinct processes: an emotional 
and automatic process, and a controlled cognitive process that can override or modify judgments 
based on the former. In the switch dilemma, individuals are thought to rely more heavily on 
controlled cognitive processing because the judgment requires weighing the costs and benefits of 
sacrificing one person to save five others. In contrast, in the footbridge dilemma, the act of 
directly pushing a person to their death elicits a strong negative emotional response, leading 
individuals to rely more on emotional and automatic processing. 
1.1. Does the Persuasive Effect of LLM-Generated Counterarguments Depend on Dilemma 
Type? 
The present study examines the extent to which judgments change when an LLM 
presents a counterargument to an individual’s original judgment. Previous research has shown 
that judgments in moral dilemmas can be influenced by the opinions of LLMs (Krügel et al., 
2023). However, in that study, the LLM’s response was presented as advice and did not 
necessarily function as a direct counterargument to the participant’s initial judgment. Therefore, 
in judgments without objectively correct answers, such as moral dilemmas, it remains unclear 
how many individuals will reverse their judgments and accept an LLM-generated 
counterargument. In addition, the present study investigates whether the persuasive effect of


## Page 5


5 
 
LLM-generated counterarguments differs between the switch dilemma and the footbridge 
dilemma, which are assumed to involve different cognitive processes. This makes it possible to 
examine whether the persuasive effect of LLMs varies as a function of task characteristics. Prior 
research has suggested that it is relatively rare for people to reverse moral judgments that are 
strongly grounded in emotional and automatic processes (Haidt, 2001). Based on this reasoning, 
the following hypothesis was proposed. 
H1: Individuals will be more likely to accept LLM-generated counterarguments and change their 
judgments in the switch dilemma than in the footbridge dilemma. 
 
1.2. Does Age Influence the Acceptance of LLM-Generated Counterarguments? 
The present study focuses on age as an individual characteristic that may influence the 
acceptance of LLM-generated counterarguments. Because aging is associated with declines in 
long-term memory, processing speed, working memory, and executive functioning (Salthouse, 
2010, 2019), older adults often experience greater difficulty with decisions that require the 
evaluation of complex options or probabilities, rapid judgment, information search, and novel 
decision making (Mather, 2006; Peters et al., 2007). Given that aging affects decision-making 
processes, age may be an important individual characteristic to consider when examining the 
influence of LLM-generated counterarguments on decision making. For example, older adults 
tend to strategically avoid processing information that is not personally important or engaging in 
effortful thought in order to conserve cognitive resources (Hess, 2014). Accordingly, older adults 
may be less likely to engage in demanding cognitive strategies, such as carefully evaluating 
counterarguments from an LLM and acknowledging the possibility that their own initial


## Page 6


6 
 
judgment was incorrect. If so, they may be less likely than younger adults to accept LLM-
generated counterarguments and revise their judgments. 
In addition, older adults have been shown to exhibit a greater tendency toward deontological 
judgment than younger adults and may be more reluctant to act in ways that conflict with moral 
idealism, which emphasizes adherence to socially defined norms (McNair et al., 2019). 
Therefore, compared with younger adults, older adults are expected to be less likely to accept 
LLM-generated counterarguments in the footbridge dilemma, which elicits stronger negative 
emotional responses. Taken together, these considerations lead to the following hypothesis. 
H2a: Older adults will be more likely than younger adults to reject LLM-generated 
counterarguments and maintain their original judgments, with this tendency expected to be 
especially pronounced in the footbridge dilemma. 
On the other hand, an alternative hypothesis can be derived from the perspective of 
cognitive offloading. Cognitive offloading refers to the use of external tools to reduce cognitive 
load, including the use of notes, reminders, and digital tools such as AI (Gerlich, 2025; Risko & 
Gilbert, 2016). For example, in prospective memory tasks, older adults have been shown to 
engage in cognitive offloading by using reminders more frequently than younger adults to reduce 
cognitive demands (Gilbert, 2015). In addition, it has been reported that the use of LLMs during 
information search in decision-making contexts can reduce the perceived difficulty of decision 
making among older adults (Ishibashi et al., 2025). On the basis of this perspective, the 
following alternative hypothesis was also proposed.


## Page 7


7 
 
H2b: Older adults will be more likely than younger adults to change their judgments in response 
to LLM-generated counterarguments, and this tendency will be especially strong among those 
with lower cognitive functioning. 
Most prior research on interactions between LLMs and humans has relied on data from 
younger populations, particularly university students and participants in online surveys, whereas 
studies involving older adults remain extremely limited (Ishibashi et al., 2025). Moreover, to the 
best of the authors’ knowledge, no previous study has directly assessed cognitive functioning 
and examined whether cognitive decline influences changes in judgment in response to 
information provided by LLMs. 
 
1.3. What Individual Factors Influence the Acceptance of LLM-Generated Counterarguments? 
In addition to age, other individual factors may also influence the acceptance of LLM-
generated counterarguments. Research on the effects of advice from others on decision making 
has shown that individuals are less likely to accept advice when they have greater confidence in 
their own initial judgment, whereas they are more likely to accept advice when they have higher 
trust in the source of that advice (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006). Accordingly, the present study also 
examines the effects of trust in AI and prior experience using LLMs. 
Furthermore, when comparing human and AI decision makers, people often exhibit 
algorithm aversion, that is, a tendency to prefer human judgment over algorithmic judgment. 
Algorithm aversion is especially pronounced in tasks that are open to interpretation and rely on 
personal intuition (Castelo et al., 2019). Because moral dilemma tasks, particularly the


## Page 8


8 
 
footbridge dilemma, elicit strong negative emotions, whether individuals accept a logical 
counterargument from an LLM may depend in part on their degree of trust in AI. 
H3: Individuals with lower confidence in their own judgments, prior experience using 
LLMs, and higher trust in AI will be more likely to accept LLM-generated counterarguments. 
In summary, the purpose of the present study is to clarify the extent to which individuals 
revise their initial moral judgments when an LLM presents a counterargument and to examine 
how this persuasive effect varies as a function of task characteristics (the switch dilemma vs. the 
footbridge dilemma) and individual factors, including age, cognitive functioning, trust in AI, and 
prior experience using LLMs. Through this investigation, the study aims to provide a 
multifaceted evaluation of both the potential usefulness of LLMs as external resources that may 
compensate for age-related cognitive decline and the possibility that constraints on cognitive 
resources may increase conformity to, or vulnerability to, the influence of LLMs. 
 
2. Methods  
2.1. Participants 
Participants were recruited from a pool of individuals who had participated in the 
authors’ previous studies and were invited to take part in the present experiment. The final 
sample consisted of 130 individuals who provided informed consent. Specifically, the sample 
included 56 younger adults (M = 21.34 years, SD = 1.78) and 74 older adults (M = 74.23 years, 
SD = 5.06). Upon completion of the experiment, older adults received 10,000 yen and younger 
adults received 6,000 yen as compensation.


## Page 9


9 
 
Cognitive functioning was assessed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Fourth 
Edition (WAIS-IV; Wechsler, 2008). All indices fell within the normal range (mean Full Scale 
IQ = 115.84, SD = 14.95), and no participant was suspected of having dementia. In addition, 
none of the participants had a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. 
2.2. Design and Sample Size 
Age group was treated as a between-subjects factor, whereas moral dilemma (switch 
dilemma vs. footbridge dilemma) and timing of the AI counterargument (pre vs. post) were 
treated as within-subjects factors.  
The primary dependent variable in the present study was whether participants reversed 
their judgment after being presented with the LLM-generated counterargument, that is, whether 
their judgment shifted from appropriate to inappropriate, or vice versa. Because this outcome 
was binary (reversal = 1, no reversal = 0), the data were analyzed using a generalized linear 
mixed model (GLMM) with a logistic link function. Age group, moral dilemma condition, and 
their interaction were entered as fixed effects, and participant ID was included as a random 
intercept. 
The adequacy of the sample size was evaluated after data collection through a post hoc 
sensitivity analysis based on the observed data using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007). In the present 
study, 130 participants responded to two moral dilemma conditions, yielding a total of 260 
observations. However, because these observations were repeated measures nested within 
participants, they were not treated as independent. Instead, the design effect was taken into 
account based on the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.227). As a result, the effective 
number of observations was estimated to be approximately 212, corresponding to an effective


## Page 10


10 
 
sample size of 106 participants. Furthermore, under a simplified set of assumptions in which the 
response probability in the reference category was set at .50, the significance level was set at .05, 
and statistical power was set at .80, the minimum detectable effect size was estimated to be 
approximately an odds ratio of 3.45. According to the criteria proposed by Chen et al. (2010), 
this value is close to a medium effect size (odds ratio = 3.47), indicating that the present study 
had sufficient statistical power to detect an effect of approximately medium magnitude. 
In addition, to examine the extent of judgment change following the presentation of 
counterarguments, a two-way analysis of variance was conducted with age group and moral 
dilemma condition as factors and the absolute change score, calculated by subtracting the pre-
counterargument judgment rating from the post-counterargument judgment rating, as the 
dependent variable. A power analysis indicated that, assuming a statistical power of .80 and a 
medium effect size (f = 0.25), the required sample size was 98 participants. Because the present 
study included 130 participants, this criterion was satisfied, confirming that sufficient statistical 
power was secured for this analysis. 
2.3. Moral Dilemma Tasks 
The present study employed two moral dilemma tasks, the switch dilemma and the 
footbridge dilemma (Greene et al., 2004). Participants first completed a pre-counterargument 
phase in which they made their own judgments about each dilemma. In the pre-counterargument 
phase, the moral dilemma scenario was presented on a screen, and participants were instructed to 
read it aloud. They were then asked to judge whether it was appropriate to sacrifice one person 
in order to save five others, using a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (definitely inappropriate) to 6 
(definitely appropriate). After making their judgment, participants were asked to rate their


## Page 11


11 
 
confidence in that judgment and the subjective difficulty of the judgment using the items “I am 
confident in this judgment” and “This judgment was difficult,” respectively, on 5-point scales 
ranging from 1 (do not think so at all) to 5 (strongly think so). The order of presentation of the 
switch dilemma and the footbridge dilemma was counterbalanced across participants. 
In the post-counterargument phase, participants who had indicated in the pre-
counterargument phase that sacrificing one person to save five others was appropriate (i.e., 
ratings of 4, somewhat appropriate, to 6, definitely appropriate) were presented with a 
counterargument asserting that sacrificing one person to save five others was inappropriate. 
Conversely, participants who had indicated in the pre-counterargument phase that such a 
sacrifice was inappropriate (i.e., ratings of 1, definitely inappropriate, to 3, somewhat 
inappropriate) were presented with a counterargument asserting that sacrificing one person to 
save five others was appropriate. These counterarguments were generated and presented using 
ChatGPT-4o (hereafter, ChatGPT). The specific prompts and the full counterarguments are 
provided in the Supplementary Material.  
To ensure that the counterarguments presented by ChatGPT were identical across 
participants and trials, a video showing the ChatGPT interface generating the response text was 
prepared in advance and presented on a monitor. After viewing the ChatGPT response, 
participants were again asked to judge whether it was appropriate to sacrifice one person to save 
five others. They then rated the statement, “The AI’s counterargument was persuasive,” on a 5-
point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) to examine whether the 
perceived persuasiveness of the counterarguments differed between the two dilemmas.

2.4. Cognitive Function 
Cognitive function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence 
Scale–Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV; Wechsler, 2008). The WAIS-IV consists of 10 subtests 
designed to measure four domains of cognitive functioning: the Verbal Comprehension Index 
(VCI), the Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI), the Working Memory Index (WMI), and the 
Processing Speed Index (PSI). The VCI comprises the Similarities, Vocabulary, and Information 
subtests; the PRI comprises the Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, and Visual Puzzles subtests; 
the WMI comprises the Digit Span and Arithmetic subtests; and the PSI comprises the Symbol 
Search and Coding subtests. In addition to these subtest scores, the present study calculated the 
Full Scale IQ (FSIQ). 
2.5. Trust in AI 
To assess trust in AI, the Trust Scale for Artificial Intelligence (Katase, 2021) was 
administered. This scale consists of two factors: Trust in the Social Benefit of AI, which includes 
five items such as “AI generally produces correct results,” and Distrust in the Fidelity of AI, 
which includes five items such as “AI sometimes deceives people.” Responses were recorded on 
a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). 
2.6. Procedure 
The experiment was conducted individually. After the purpose and content of the study 
had been explained, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. On the first 
day, the WAIS-IV was administered. On the second day, participants completed the decision-
making tasks and were then asked to complete the measure assessing trust in AI.

3. Results 
3.1. Age Differences in Study Variables 
Table 1 presents the means, standard deviations, and results of between-group 
comparisons for cognitive function and trust in AI by age group. With regard to cognitive 
function, younger adults scored higher than older adults on all subtests except Vocabulary. 
Because verbal knowledge has been shown to remain relatively preserved in older adulthood, 
this pattern is consistent with the typical age-related profile of cognitive function. With respect 
to trust in AI, no significant age-group differences were observed. In addition, for both the 
switch dilemma and the footbridge dilemma, older adults reported significantly greater 
confidence in their judgments than younger adults, whereas no significant age-group differences 
were found in subjective decision difficulty.  
Prior experience using LLMs also differed markedly by age group: 47 of the 56 younger 
adults (83.93%) reported prior experience using LLMs, whereas only 9 of the 74 older adults 
(12.16%) did so. Younger adults were significantly more likely than older adults to have prior 
experience using LLMs (χ²(1) = 66.96, p < .001).

In the switch dilemma, 64.61% of participants judged it appropriate to sacrifice one

elicited more utilitarian judgments, whereas the footbridge dilemma elicited more deontological 
judgments. 
3.3. Judgment Change Following LLM Counterarguments 
First, to confirm that the perceived persuasiveness of the LLM-generated

the footbridge dilemma (M = 3.35, SD = 1.38). The results indicated no significant difference in 
the persuasiveness of the LLM counterarguments between the two dilemmas (t(129) = 1.76, p 
= .08, Cohen’s d = .15).

3.4. Judgment Reversal Following LLM Counterarguments and Cognitive Function 
To compare cognitive function between participants who reversed their judgments in 
response to the LLM-generated counterarguments and those who did not, separate one-way 
MANOVAs were conducted for each dilemma within each age group, with the WAIS-IV subtest 
scores as the dependent variables. Among younger adults, the multivariate effect was not 
significant for either the switch dilemma (F(10, 45) = 0.84, p = .60, ηp² = .16) or the footbridge 
dilemma (F(10, 45) = 0.64, p = .78, ηp² = .12), indicating no significant differences in cognitive 
functioning between those who reversed their judgment and those who did not. Among older 
adults, the multivariate effect was not significant in the switch dilemma (F(10, 63) = 0.67, p 
= .75, ηp² = .10), but it was significant in the footbridge dilemma (F(10, 63) = 1.98, p = .05, ηp² 
= .24). Follow-up ANOVAs (Table 2) showed that, with the exception of Coding, older adults 
who reversed their judgments had significantly lower cognitive function scores than those who 
did not.

showed that, in the switch dilemma, judgment reversal was more likely among older adults than

4. Discussion 
The purpose of this study was to examine whether LLM-generated counterarguments can 
change people’s judgments in decision-making situations with no objectively correct answers, 
whether acceptance of such counterarguments differs between younger and older adults, and 
whether prior experience using LLMs and trust in AI influence judgment change in response to 
those counterarguments. 
4.1. Judgment Change Following LLM Counterarguments 
The results showed that 32.31% of participants in the switch dilemma and 36.92% of 
those in the footbridge dilemma accepted the LLM-generated counterarguments and reversed 
their judgments. Previous research has suggested that people tend to exhibit egocentric advice 
discounting, that is, a tendency to incorporate only approximately 20% to 30% of others’ advice 
into their own judgments (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006). In this context, the complete judgment 
reversals observed in more than 30% of participants in the present study suggest that LLMs may 
exert substantial influence on people’s moral judgments. 
In the present study, we hypothesized that participants would be more likely to accept 
LLM-generated counterarguments and change their judgments in the switch dilemma than in the


## Page 18


18 
 
footbridge dilemma (H1). However, there was no significant difference in the proportion of 
participants who changed their judgments in response to the LLM-generated counterarguments 
between the two dilemmas; therefore, H1 was not supported. Landes et al. (2026) argued that, 
when evaluating LLM output, people check for inconsistencies or obvious errors, but if the 
response contains no fatal flaws and is sufficiently convincing, or “seems good enough,” they 
may defer their moral judgments to LLM. The present findings suggest that, regardless of 
dilemma type, participants evaluated the presented counterarguments using a heuristic of seems 
good enough, thereby attenuating differences between the two dilemmas. In addition, previous 
research has shown that people tend to trust AI in objective and numerical tasks, whereas 
algorithm aversion is more likely to emerge in subjective tasks that require judgments based on 
personal opinions or intuition (Castelo et al., 2019). Because both the switch dilemma and the 
footbridge dilemma used in the present study involved subjective tasks with no objectively 
correct answer, participants were required to rely on their own opinions and intuitions in both 
cases. It is therefore possible that a similar degree of algorithm aversion emerged across the two 
dilemmas, thereby contributing to the absence of differences in the influence of the LLM-
generated counterarguments. 
4.2. Age Differences and Cognitive Offloading 
In the present study, we proposed two competing hypotheses regarding age-related 
differences between younger and older adults. 
H2a: Older adults will be more likely than younger adults to reject LLM-generated 
counterarguments and maintain their initial judgments, with this tendency expected to be 
especially pronounced in the footbridge dilemma.


## Page 19


19 
 
H2b: Older adults will be more likely than younger adults to change their judgments in response 
to LLM-generated counterarguments, and this tendency will be especially strong among those 
with lower cognitive functioning. 
The results showed that older adults were more likely than younger adults to reverse their 
judgments in response to the LLM-generated counterarguments. In particular, in the switch 
dilemma, older adults exhibited a greater degree of judgment change than younger adults. These 
findings support H2b. 
The finding that older adults were more strongly influenced by the LLM-generated 
counterarguments than younger adults may be understood in terms of cognitive offloading, 
defined as the use of external tools to reduce cognitive burden (Gerlich, 2025). Because aging is 
associated with declines in processing speed and working memory, older adults may be more 
likely to avoid engaging in complex thought in order to conserve cognitive resources (Hess, 
2014). In the present study, the counterarguments generated by the LLM may have served as an 
external aid that reduced the cognitive burden associated with independently reasoning through 
morally difficult decisions for which there is no objectively correct answer. It is also noteworthy 
that, in the footbridge dilemma, which is thought to rely on emotional moral judgments that are 
relatively resistant to revision through logical reasoning (Haidt, 2001), older adults with lower 
cognitive functioning were more likely to reverse their judgments. This finding suggests that, 
even when the LLM presented a counterargument involving the emotionally aversive act of 
pushing one man to his death, older adults with lower cognitive function may have been less 
willing or less able to sustain the cognitive effort required to continue justifying their initial 
judgment, thereby becoming more likely to align with the LLM’s counterargument. Older adults, 
who may have had fewer cognitive resources available to resolve the conflict between their own


## Page 20


20 
 
emotional or intuitive judgment and the AI’s rational counterargument, may therefore have 
engaged in excessive cognitive offloading by relying on a heuristic of seems good enough 
(Landes et al., 2026). 
Older adults exhibited a greater degree of judgment change than younger adults in the 
switch dilemma. The switch dilemma is considered to rely on cognitive control processes 
involved in weighing costs and benefits, and before exposure to the LLM-generated 
counterarguments, older adults, like younger adults, tended to make utilitarian judgments. At the 
same time, however, older adults have been shown to favor deontological judgments, such as 
adherence to rules and moral ideals, more than younger adults (McNair et al., 2019). It is 
therefore possible that, when the LLM generated counterarguments from a deontological 
perspective in the switch dilemma, older adults were more strongly influenced by these 
counterarguments than younger adults. 
4.3. Individual Factors Predicting Acceptance of LLM Counterarguments 
In the present study, we proposed H3, that individuals with lower confidence in their own 
judgments, prior experience using LLMs, and higher trust in AI would be more likely to accept 
LLM-generated counterarguments. However, the results did not support H3, as neither trust in 
AI nor prior experience using LLMs influenced judgment change. This finding is consistent with 
the disconnect between trust and persuasion, whereby higher-order beliefs, such as trust in AI, 
do not necessarily affect persuasive impact (Landes et al., 2026). In tasks such as moral 
judgment, where careful consideration of the specific features of a given situation is emphasized 
(Castelo et al., 2019), people may evaluate and respond to the content of the LLM-generated


## Page 21


21 
 
counterargument itself rather than deciding whether to accept it based on their general trust in 
AI.  
The absence of an effect of prior LLM use may be explained by the fact that participants 
were likely to have had little experience using LLMs in the context of unusual tasks such as 
moral dilemmas. Consequently, prior experience with LLMs may not have been meaningfully 
related to the evaluation of LLM-generated counterarguments in the present task. 
In contrast, subjective evaluations of one’s own judgment, such as confidence and 
perceived difficulty, did influence whether participants subsequently reversed their judgments in 
response to the LLM-generated counterarguments. Research on advice taking has shown that the 
more confident individuals are in their own judgments, the more likely they are to prioritize 
those judgments over external advice (Yaniv, 2004). It has also been shown that task difficulty 
moderates advice use, such that advice tends to be weighted more heavily when tasks are more 
difficult (Gino & Moore, 2007). Similarly, a study on the persuasive effects of LLMs (Ikeda, 
2024) reported that individuals with lower confidence in their own decisions are more 
susceptible to LLM advice, and that reliance on LLM-generated advice is not necessarily driven 
by trust in AI. Ikeda (2024) further suggested that, under conditions of high uncertainty, LLM 
advice may function as a reference point. 
The present findings suggest that acceptance of LLM-generated counterarguments is 
determined less by factors related to general evaluations of AI, such as trust in AI or prior 
experience using LLMs, than by individual factors such as perceiving the task as difficult, 
having low confidence in one’s own judgment, and having sufficient cognitive resources to resist 
counterarguments that appear rationally compelling.

5. Limitations 
The present study has several methodological limitations. First, it examined only the 
short-term effects of LLM-generated counterarguments. Future research should investigate 
whether the judgment changes induced by such counterarguments are temporary or lead to 
longer-term changes in moral beliefs. Second, the study did not include direct bidirectional 
interaction between participants and the LLM. Because requiring older adults to operate the 
LLM directly was considered difficult, participants were shown only a video of ChatGPT-4o 
generating responses, and no real-time interaction took place between participants and the LLM. 
However, previous research has suggested that dialogic interaction may enhance persuasiveness 
(Hölbling et al., 2025; Salvi et al., 2025). Therefore, the present findings may underestimate the 
persuasive power of LLMs. Third, the study employed an unusual moral dilemma as the 
decision-making task. Specifically, the trolley problem was used to examine whether acceptance 
of AI-generated counterarguments differs depending on whether judgments rely more heavily on 
cognitive control processes or on emotional and automatic processes. However, the extent to 
which these findings generalize to more naturalistic settings remains unclear. Further research is 
needed using tasks that more closely reflect everyday situations in which LLMs are likely to be 
used. 
 
6. Conclusion 
The present study demonstrated that, in moral dilemmas with no objectively correct 
answer, LLM-generated counterarguments can powerfully influence and even reverse human


## Page 23


23 
 
judgment. Overall, our findings highlight the double-edged role of LLMs in human decision-
making. For older adults, LLMs may function as a supportive tool for cognitive offloading that 
compensates for age-related decline, yet this same mechanism may also create a vulnerability to 
undue persuasion when cognitive resources are limited. In addition, regardless of age, the results 
caution that, when individuals perceive a judgment as difficult or lack confidence in their 
decision, information provided by LLMs may exert undue persuasive influence. In an 
increasingly AI-mediated society, it will be essential to develop ethical AI systems that take 
users’ individual characteristics into account and to promote digital literacy education 
concerning the risks of using LLMs in decisions for which there is no single correct answer.

Switch dilemma: Counterargument to the judgment that sacrificing one person to save five 
is appropriate 
 
Prompt: 
In the switch dilemma, please write a counterargument against the judgment that sacrificing one 
person to save five is appropriate. Take the position that sacrificing one person to save five is 
inappropriate. Organize the response into five short sections with subheadings, and explain each 
reason in approximately 60 words. 
 
1. The Value of Human Life Cannot Be Measured by Numbers 
The judgment that it is acceptable to sacrifice one person in order to save five is based on a 
numerical calculation. However, the value of human life should not be determined solely by 
numbers. If sacrificing a minority for the benefit of the majority is justified, moral standards may 
become distorted and could lead to dangerous consequences. 
 
2. Moral Action Depends on Intention 
Pulling the lever to sacrifice one person involves a deliberate intention to bring about that 
person’s death. Intentionally taking a life carries a profound moral responsibility. By contrast, 
choosing not to pull the lever, even if five people may die as a result, can be regarded as morally 
less serious because it does not involve a direct intention to kill.


## Page 37


3 
 
3. Responsibility for Active Intervention 
Pulling the lever and sacrificing one person means taking deliberate action and thereby assuming 
responsibility for the outcome. This differs from merely allowing events to unfold, because it 
directly brings about the victim’s death. For that reason, the moral responsibility in this case is 
especially weighty. 
 
4. It Violates the Rights of the Victim 
In the switch dilemma, the person who is sacrificed has not consented to losing their life for the 
sake of others. Taking a person’s life for the benefit of others while disregarding that 
individual’s rights constitutes a violation of fundamental human rights and cannot be morally 
justified. 
 
5. It Is Important to Maintain Moral Consistency 
In situations such as the switch dilemma, it is important to make judgments based on universal 
moral principles rather than focusing only on immediate outcomes. If sacrificing human life for 
short-term benefit becomes normalized, moral standards may become blurred and create even 
greater problems in the future.

Switch dilemma: Counterargument to the judgment that sacrificing one person to save five 
is inappropriate 
 
Prompt: 
In the switch dilemma, please write a counterargument against the judgment that sacrificing one 
person to save five is inappropriate. Take the position that sacrificing one person to save five is 
appropriate. Organize the response into five short sections with subheadings, and explain each 
reason in approximately 60 words. 
 
1. A Rational Judgment Based on Numerical Reasoning 
Choosing to sacrifice one person in order to save five is a rational judgment grounded in 
numerical reasoning. Accepting a small sacrifice in order to save a greater number of lives 
reflects a utilitarian perspective and represents an important choice for maximizing overall 
welfare. If one instead chooses to save the single person, five lives will be lost, resulting in a 
greater overall loss to society. 
 
2. Reconsidering Moral Responsibility 
Pulling the lever to sacrifice one person is an active intervention, but it can also be understood as 
a morally responsible act compared with passively allowing five people to die. When we have an 
opportunity to save others, failing to act may itself be regarded as a morally blameworthy


## Page 39


5 
 
omission. From this perspective, choosing not to pull the lever can also be seen as morally 
problematic. 
 
3. Fairness in Sacrifice 
If some sacrifice is unavoidable regardless of the choice made, then that sacrifice should be 
minimized as much as possible. Allowing five people to die is less justifiable than sacrificing 
one person. From the standpoint of society as a whole, choosing the option that saves more lives 
at the cost of fewer victims is the fairer and more reasonable course of action. 
 
4. Moral Responsibility Through Action 
Pulling the lever and sacrificing one person is an active response to the situation and may reflect 
a sense of moral responsibility. Rather than passively leaving the situation unresolved, taking 
action to reduce the overall harm demonstrates a commitment to helping others. For this reason, 
pulling the lever can be regarded as the more morally appropriate course of action. 
 
5. The Legitimacy of Consequentialism 
From a consequentialist perspective, what matters most is that five lives are saved. Choosing not 
to pull the lever and thereby allowing more people to die runs counter to a moral judgment that 
prioritizes outcomes. Therefore, the decision to sacrifice one person can be justified as morally 
appropriate because it leads to the greater overall good.

Footbridge dilemma: Counterargument to the judgment that sacrificing one person to save 
five is appropriate 
 
Prompt: 
In the footbridge dilemma, please write a counterargument against the judgment that sacrificing 
one person to save five is appropriate. Take the position that sacrificing one person to save five 
is inappropriate. Organize the response into five short sections with subheadings, and explain 
each reason in approximately 60 words. 
 
1. Sacrificing a Person Is Morally Wrong 
Intentionally taking a person’s life is morally wrong under any circumstances. Even if the goal is 
to save five others, pushing one person to their death constitutes intentional killing and violates 
both ethical principles and human rights. The value of a human life is immeasurable, and it is 
never morally permissible to impose such a sacrifice on another person. 
 
2. Treating a Person as a Means Is Inhumane 
Pushing one person off the bridge means treating that individual as a mere means to benefit 
others. According to Kantian moral philosophy, human beings are ends in themselves and should 
never be treated solely as means. No matter how many lives might be saved, it is morally 
unacceptable to use one person in this way.


## Page 41


7 
 
3. The Danger of Undermining Law and Social Order 
If pushing one person to their death were considered permissible, it could erode moral and legal 
order. Once circumstances are accepted in which killing can be justified, similar actions that 
devalue human life may spread to other situations, threatening social stability. Because the law 
prohibits intentional killing, this principle should not be overridden even in extreme cases. 
 
4. Responsibility Cannot Be Avoided 
If one chooses to push a person off the bridge, the responsibility for taking that life rests entirely 
with that decision. Even if five people are saved, one cannot escape responsibility for having 
deliberately chosen to kill another person. Deciding with one’s own hands that someone should 
die is a grave moral wrong. 
 
5. One Must Not Ignore Another Person’s Will 
The person being pushed has not consented to being sacrificed. To disregard that person’s will 
and the dignity of their life, and to take their life based on one’s own judgment, is a serious 
violation of personal freedom and autonomy. No matter how noble the goal may appear, acting 
in a way that ignores another person’s will is ethically unacceptable.

Footbridge dilemma: Counterargument to the judgment that sacrificing one person to save 
five is inappropriate 
 
Prompt: 
In the footbridge dilemma, please write a counterargument against the judgment that sacrificing 
one person to save five is inappropriate. Take the position that sacrificing one person to save five 
is appropriate. Organize the response into five short sections with subheadings, and explain each 
reason in approximately 60 words. 
 
1. Sacrificing One Person to Save Five Can Be Morally Justified 
If pushing one person off the bridge would certainly save the lives of five others, then that action 
can be regarded as morally justified. Because more lives are saved, the overall welfare and 
benefit to society are maximized. When an individual sacrifice is unavoidable and necessary to 
prevent a greater loss of life, refusing to make that choice may instead result in far greater harm. 
 
2. One Must Also Bear Responsibility for the Consequences of Inaction 
Choosing not to push one person can be seen as allowing five people to die when their deaths 
could have been prevented. Avoiding direct action does not necessarily remove moral 
responsibility. A decision not to act still has consequences, and those consequences may include 
the loss of multiple lives. From this perspective, taking action to save five people can be 
understood as the more responsible moral choice.


## Page 43


9 
 
 
3. From an Altruistic Perspective, Sacrifice Can Be Justified 
From an ethical perspective that prioritizes the welfare of others, sacrificing one person can be 
justified if doing so saves a greater number of lives. In a situation where five lives are at stake, 
pursuing the greatest good for the greatest number may be regarded as a moral obligation. 
Although the choice is tragic, it may still be ethically defensible when the goal is to minimize 
total harm. 
 
4. We Have a Duty to Protect Others 
Ignoring people who are in immediate danger of losing their lives may itself be ethically 
problematic. Choosing to push one person in order to save many others can be understood as 
fulfilling a duty to protect human life wherever possible. From this perspective, the option that 
saves the greatest number of people may be regarded as the most responsible course of action. 
 
5. Rational Judgment Should Prevail Over Emotional Reaction 
It is understandable to feel strong emotional resistance to pushing one person off the bridge. 
However, in a situation such as this, calm and rational judgment is required in addition to 
emotional intuition. Allowing emotional hesitation alone to determine the decision may place 
even more lives at risk, whereas choosing the option that saves five people may be seen as the 
most reasonable way to minimize overall harm.
